Experiences, barriers, and enablers of integrated HIV and sexual and reproductive health services in Nigeria: a qualitative study of service users and healthcare providers
Authors: Priscilla Nemieboka, Folahanmi T Akinsolu, Olunike R Abodunrin, Oliver C Ezechi, Yannis Pappas, Gurch Randhawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrating HIV and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services is a global priority for improving access to comprehensive, equitable, and people-centred care. However, people living with HIV (PLHIV) continue to experience unmet SRH needs, and evidence on how integrated services are experienced, delivered, and utilised in routine care settings remains limited in Nigeria. This study explored service users’ and healthcare providers’ experiences of integrated HIV–SRH services, including patterns of service delivery and utilisation, barriers, facilitators, and recommendations for strengthening integration.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in 2025 in Lagos and Oyo States, South-West Nigeria. In-depth interviews were conducted with HIV service users and healthcare providers involved in delivering or accessing integrated HIV–SRH services. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using thematic analysis. A hybrid inductive–deductive coding approach was applied, supported by Quirkos software.ResultsParticipants described strong and sustained engagement with HIV services but more selective and fragmented use of SRH services. While healthcare providers articulated a clear understanding of HIV–SRH integration as a holistic and client-centred model of care, many service users had limited awareness of integration as a formal care approach. Integrated service delivery was enabled by supportive and non-judgmental staff attitudes, effective follow-up systems, client-centred relationships, and social and family support. However, multiple intersecting barriers constrained effective integration, including chronic staffing shortages and long waiting times, inconsistent availability of essential resources, inadequate infrastructure, stigma and discrimination, sociocultural norms, limited provider training, and reliance on donor funding. Despite these challenges, user satisfaction with HIV services remained high, largely driven by positive provider–client interactions. Participants recommended strengthening integration through sustained free access to medications, workforce expansion and capacity building, improved service coordination, community engagement, and increased domestic resource mobilisation.ConclusionIntegrated HIV and SRH services are widely valued by both service users and healthcare providers in South-West Nigeria and contribute to patient-centred care and stigma reduction. However, gaps in user awareness, health system constraints, and persistent social barriers limit the full potential of integration. Addressing these challenges through targeted health system strengthening, sustained community engagement, and equitable financing is essential for improving access, quality, and sustainability of integrated HIV–SRH services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14645-5.Keywords: HIV, Sexual and reproductive health, Service integration

Background

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) needs among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Nigeria remain substantial yet under-addressed, reflecting broader deficiencies in structural and programmatic weaknesses within the national health system [1, 2]. While the national HIV prevalence among adults aged 15–49 years remains relatively low at 1.3% [3], approximately 1.8 million Nigerians were living with HIV in 2022 [4]. Legal and structural barriers further constrain access to essential HIV and SRH services [5]. Notably, the Same-Sex Marriage (Prohibition) Act of 2014 criminalizes same-sex relationships, exacerbating stigma and limiting access to care for men who have sex with men (MSM), with implications for HIV transmission within this population and beyond [6].

Despite global momentum toward the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets aimed at diagnosing 95% of PLHIV, treating 95% of those diagnosed, and achieving viral suppression in 95% of those treated [7], Nigeria’s progress as of 2021 stood at 90-85-93 [8]. These gaps highlight persistent challenges in case identification, antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, and sustained viral suppression [9]. Beyond clinical outcomes, HIV imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden, contributing to catastrophic health expenditures, household income loss, and deepening cycles of poverty at the community level [10]. These challenges are compounded by pervasive stigma and discrimination against PLHIV, which impede access to testing and treatment while undermining fundamental human rights [11].

Crucially, PLHIV in Nigeria also face amplified and often overlooked SRH risks that intersect with the country’s broader SRH crisis (ref). Nigeria records some of the poorest SRH indicators globally, including a maternal mortality ratio exceeding 1,047 deaths per 100,000 live births, a modern contraceptive prevalence rate of only 12%, and an estimated 2.5 million unsafe abortions annually [12–14]. Many of these outcomes are preventable through access to quality maternal and emergency obstetric care, comprehensive contraceptive services, and safe abortion provision [15]. However, entrenched sociocultural norms, gender inequality, and a fragmented health system continue to undermine equitable access to these life-saving services [16–18].

Within this broader context, PLHIV in Nigeria face additional SRH challenges shaped by both biological vulnerability and fragmented service delivery. Women living with HIV (WLHIV) face elevated risks of cervical cancer, unintended pregnancy complications, and vertical HIV transmission, while retaining the same fundamental rights as the general population to safe childbirth, voluntary contraception, protection from sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and support for gender-based violence (GBV) services [14, 19, 20]. In practice, however, these rights are often constrained by fragmented services, provider discrimination, weak referral pathways, and broader sociocultural barriers to care [21]. Unsafe abortion poses particular risks for WLHIV, as unregulated procedures increase vulnerability to severe complications and potential HIV transmission [22]. HIV diagnosis may also reshape reproductive decision-making, influencing fertility intentions, contraceptive preferences, disclosure, and strategies to reduce sexual and perinatal transmission risks [23].

These intersecting challenges highlight the importance of integrating HIV and SRH services within the Nigerian health system [24]. In a setting where many PLHIV continue to experience unmet SRH needs alongside ongoing barriers to HIV care, integration offers a practical approach for improving continuity, efficiency, and person-centred care [25]. In Nigeria, populations most affected by HIV often also face substantial unmet SRH needs, including gaps in access to safe abortion care, GBV support, and reproductive health education [2, 14]. Embedding SRH services such as contraception, cervical cancer screening, STI management, and counselling within HIV platforms may reduce missed opportunities for prevention and treatment, while strengthening referral systems and reducing stigma associated with seeking HIV-specific services [24, 26]. However, although integration is widely promoted in policy and programme discussions, evidence remains limited on how such services are actually experienced, delivered, and navigated by service users and healthcare providers in routine care settings in Nigeria [27].

While Nigeria continues to contend with a dual burden of HIV and poor SRH outcomes, most existing research has focused on SRH service utilization among the general population, limited attention to the specific SRH needs and experiences of PLHIV [28]. Studies examining HIV-SRH intersections have largely emphasized prevention-of-mother-to-child-transmission (PMTCT) outcomes or HIV testing within antenatal care, often treating SRH primarily as a platform for HIV control rather than recognizing the independent and intersecting SRH rights and needs of PLHIV [29, 30]. Consequently, critical SRH concerns affecting PLHIV, including safe conception, contraceptive choice, cervical cancer screening, GBV, and safe abortion access, remain under-researched, despite heightened biological and social vulnerabilities, particularly among WLHIV [23].

Reviews of HIV-SRH integration efforts in Nigeria have highlighted systemic challenges, including vertical funding streams, provider-level training gaps, and weak referral systems [24, 31]. However, these assessments typically adopt a broad national or general population perspective, offering limited insight into the specific barriers PLHIV face in accessing integrated services. This gap is particularly salient in Southwest Nigeria, where sociocultural and health system dynamics may uniquely shape service access and quality.

Given the limited understanding of how sociocultural and health system factors shape access to integrated HIV and SRH services in Southwest Nigeria, a qualitative approach is essential to capture the complex, context-specific realities that influence service integration. Such an approach allows for in-depth exploration of the attitudes, experiences, and relational dynamics of those delivering and receiving care. Therefore, this study uses a qualitative design to examine the barriers, facilitators, and underlying mechanisms shaping HIV-SRH integration in Southwest Nigeria from the perspectives of healthcare providers and WLHIV.

Methods

Study design and epistemological approach

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design to explore how service users and healthcare providers experience, perceive, and make meaning of integrated HIV and SRH services in South-West Nigeria. A qualitative descriptive approach was appropriate because the study aimed to generate practice-relevant and policy-relevant insights into how HIV–SRH integration was understood, delivered, and accessed in routine care settings. Rather than seeking to produce a purely phenomenological account of lived experience, the study focused on capturing participants’ perspectives on service delivery processes, barriers, facilitators, and opportunities for strengthening integration [32].

The study was informed by an interpretivist orientation, which recognizes that experiences of integrated care are socially constructed and shaped by interactions between service users, healthcare providers, and the broader health system context. This orientation enabled the study to examine how HIV–SRH integration was understood and enacted in everyday clinical practice, while remaining attentive to the relational, organisational, and sociocultural factors shaping service access and delivery.

Study setting and site selection

The study was conducted in accredited ART centres across selected Local Government Areas (LGAs) in Lagos and Oyo States. Facilities were selected from the official list of health facilities accredited by the Nigerian Ministry of Health and the National Agency for the Control of AIDS (NACA) [7, 8].

Although many facilities in Lagos and Oyo States provide comprehensive HIV care, facilities were purposively selected for this study based on their demonstrated provision of both HIV and core SRH services within the same facility. Eligibility criteria required facilities to offer comprehensive HIV care, including ART, viral load monitoring, and PMTCT, alongside SRH services such as family planning counselling/provision and STI management.

This selection strategy was adopted to ensure that participants had direct experience with service environments where HIV–SRH integration was operational or actively attempted, thereby enabling in-depth exploration of how integration was implemented, experienced, and perceived in practice. Facilities that provided HIV services alone, without routine or structured SRH service delivery, were excluded, as they would not allow meaningful examination of integration processes, barriers, and facilitators.

In addition, facilities were selected across urban and peri-urban LGAs to capture variation in service delivery contexts, patient volume, and health system capacity, thereby strengthening the analytic richness and transferability of findings.

Sample size was guided by the principle of thematic saturation rather than formal sample size calculation. In this study, we prioritised meaning saturation, defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer generated substantively new insights, interpretive depth, or variation relevant to the study aims, rather than simple repetition of codes alone [34]. Saturation was assessed iteratively through ongoing team debriefings, comparison of emerging patterns across participant groups, and review of transcript content during data collection. Core themes began to recur consistently by the later stages of data collection across both service users and healthcare providers, and data collection ceased when the team judged that sufficient conceptual depth and diversity had been achieved to address the study objectives.

We conducted 40 KIIs and 40 IDIs to ensure a comprehensive exploration of the integration of HIV and SRH services. Saturation was determined iteratively during data collection through regular debriefs and content assessments by the research team. Data collection ceased when it was judged that further interviews were unlikely to yield additional insights [34].

Data collection

Two researchers (PN-Woman and Masters) and (FTA-Man and PhD), fluent in local languages and familiar with the sociocultural context, were trained for three days on qualitative interviewing, research ethics, and study objectives. Interviews were conducted in private rooms within the facilities to ensure confidentiality.

Data collection commenced in April 2025 following ethical and administrative approvals. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted face-to-face using interview guides developed from a prior systematic review and relevant literature (Supplementary File 1). Interviews lasted approximately 35–60 min. Repeat interviews were not conducted.

Interviews were conducted primarily in Yoruba, with options for Igbo and Hausa. All tools were professionally translated and back translated to ensure conceptual equivalence across languages.

Written and verbally informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. The participants were informed that they could opt out when they felt uncomfortable with the questions. With permission, interviews were audio-recorded and supplemented with detailed field notes to capture contextual observations and non-verbal cues.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to explore how service users and healthcare providers understood, experienced, and navigated HIV and SRH service integration. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English prior to analysis. All transcripts and field notes were managed using Quirkos qualitative data analysis software and analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [35].

Analysis began with repeated reading of transcripts and field notes to support familiarisation with the data. Initial coding was led by PN and focused on participants’ accounts of service delivery, service use, perceptions of integration, barriers, facilitators, and recommendations. Codes were generated primarily inductively from the data, while deductive interpretation was introduced later to consider the findings through an implementation lens. A subset of transcripts was reviewed by additional members of the research team to enhance interpretive consistency. Related codes were grouped into candidate sub-themes and broader themes based on conceptual similarity and patterned meaning across interviews. Differences in coding or theme interpretation were discussed during analytic meetings and resolved through reflexive dialogue and consensus.

Attention was given to both convergent and divergent accounts across service users and healthcare providers, including instances that challenged dominant interpretations. Throughout analysis, reflexive dialogue among the research team supported critical examination of assumptions, interpretation of meaning, and refinement of thematic structure.

Attention was given to both convergent and divergent accounts across service users and healthcare providers, including instances that challenged dominant interpretations. To strengthen the applied relevance of the findings, the final themes were subsequently interpreted through an implementation lens using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), particularly in relation to service processes, inner setting constraints, and outer setting influences [36].

Data collection, analysis, and reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [37, 38].

Reflexivity and trustworthiness

The study team comprised public health researchers with experience in HIV and sexual and reproductive health research in Nigeria. This background provided contextual familiarity with HIV service delivery, stigma, and health system constraints, but also meant that the researchers were likely to approach the study with prior assumptions about the value of service integration and the structural barriers affecting access to care. The interviewers were trained research assistants who had no clinical or supervisory relationship with participants, which helped minimise power imbalances and reduce the likelihood of socially desirable responses.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout data collection and analysis. After interviews, the research team held regular debriefing sessions to reflect on how interviewer positioning, prior professional experience, and emerging assumptions might shape questioning, interpretation, and theme development. Field notes and reflexive memos were used to document contextual observations, initial impressions, and potential biases, including tendencies to foreground health-system constraints or interpret participants’ accounts through prior knowledge of HIV/SRH programming. During analysis, these reflections were discussed within the team to challenge dominant interpretations, consider divergent views, and ensure that themes remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researcher expectations [39, 40].

Trustworthiness was enhanced through multiple, complementary strategies aligned with established qualitative rigor criteria. Credibility was supported through the inclusion of diverse participant groups (service users and healthcare providers) across multiple facilities and LGAs, prolonged engagement with the data, and regular analytic debriefings among the research team. Dependability was strengthened through a transparent and systematic analytic process, including iterative coding, repeated review of emerging themes, and discussion of interpretive differences. Confirmability was supported through the maintenance of an audit trail comprising coded transcripts, field notes, reflexive notes, and analytic memos, allowing findings to be traced back to the original data [38, 41]. Transferability was supported through thick description of the study context, service delivery environments, and participant experiences, enabling readers to assess the relevance of the findings to similar HIV and SRH service settings in Nigeria.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the National Code of Health Research Ethics in Nigeria. Research ethics approval was obtained from the Lagos State Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committee (LASG: LSMH/6038/1/122) and the Oyo State Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committee (OYMOH: NHREC/OYOSHRIEC/10/11/22). Because the study was conducted in public health facilities under the jurisdiction of state health authorities, ethical approval was obtained from the Lagos State and Oyo State Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committees, which have regulatory oversight for research conducted within state health systems. Social approval was also obtained from the Lagos and Oyo State Primary Health Care Boards, which oversee public primary health facilities, as well as administrative permission from all participating ART facilities.

All participants provided written and verbal informed consent in their preferred language prior to participation. The consent process emphasized voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequence. All interview data were anonymized; personal identifiers were removed from transcripts, and all digital and hard-copy materials were stored securely with restricted access.

Given the sensitive nature of HIV and sexual and reproductive health topics, interviewers were trained to recognize and respond to participant distress and to provide information on available counselling and support services when needed. Service users received a modest transport reimbursement of ₦10,000 (approximately US$6.3 at the prevailing official exchange rate during data collection in April-2025) to offset participation costs. Healthcare providers received no financial compensation. While the reimbursement was intended to compensate for transportation and related participation costs, it is possible that it may have influenced willingness to participate and contributed to some degree of response bias.

Service providers

Among the 40 service providers (Table 2), ages ranged from 22 to 58 years, with most (60%) aged 31–45 years. The majority were married (77.5%), had a tertiary education (85%), and were of Yoruba origin (60%). Professional roles included HIV testers, mentor mothers, data staff, and program coordinators. Themes and subthemes

The findings are presented as experiential themes that capture how service users and healthcare providers perceive, experience, and interpret the integration of HIV and SRH services within routine care settings. Six major themes emerged from the analysis, reflecting how integrated HIV–SRH services are perceived, delivered, accessed, and experienced by both service users and healthcare providers. Together, these themes illustrate the interaction between individual awareness, service organisation, sociocultural context, and health system capacity in shaping HIV–SRH integration.

Participants’ perceptions of integration revealed varying levels of awareness and understanding of how HIV and SRH services are linked, alongside differing views on the value of providing care in a more holistic and coordinated manner. These perceptions were closely connected to how integration was implemented within health facilities, where healthcare workers described their roles and responsibilities and how integrated service delivery influenced patient care, continuity of treatment, and perceived health outcomes. Service users’ accounts further highlighted how integration was experienced in practice, revealing patterns of utilisation, selective engagement with SRH services, and generally high levels of satisfaction with care, even when awareness of formal integration was limited.

At the same time, multiple structural and sociocultural barriers constrained the effectiveness of integrated care, including staff shortages, long waiting times, transportation costs, stigma and discrimination, inadequate infrastructure and supplies, limited training, and restrictive cultural and religious norms. In contrast, a range of facilitators supported engagement with integrated services, including supportive and non-judgmental healthcare workers, effective follow-up and appointment reminders, social and family support, collaboration between facilities and external stakeholders, and community-based health education. Building on these experiences, participants also articulated clear recommendations for strengthening HIV–SRH integration, emphasising the need for free and accessible medications, improved staffing and capacity building, stronger service coordination, sustained community engagement, and greater investment in resource mobilisation to ensure long-term sustainability. Theme 1: Perceptions and awareness of HIV–SRH integration

Participants’ perceptions of HIV and SRH integration revealed a striking disconnect between how services were organised and how they were experienced. While healthcare providers consistently described HIV–SRH integration as a deliberate, holistic model of care, most service users experienced it in a fragmented and often passive manner, with limited awareness of how HIV and SRH services were meant to function together.

Sub-theme 1.1: Service users’ perceptions of integration

Service users held mixed and often unclear views about HIV–SRH integration. Although some were aware that SRH services such as family planning or cervical cancer screening were available within their treatment facilities, access to these services was frequently described as inconsistent, dependent on personal requests, or delivered without explicit explanation. For many, integration was not something they actively recognised but something that occurred, if at all, in the background of routine HIV care.

Some participants described receiving SRH-related services without being fully aware that these were part of an integrated model. As one participant noted:

HIV and SRH services are done separately. They provide cervical screening… we don’t need to request it before it’s provided. (IB_SU_14, 58yrs)

This account reflects a passive form of service delivery, where care is received without explanation or contextual framing. Although the service is technically integrated at the point of delivery, the absence of communication means that users do not conceptualise it as such.

Others highlighted that information about SRH services was inconsistently communicated and weakly reinforced:

They used to inform us about other services… It’s just that they don’t enforce it. (IB_SU_02, 39yrs)

Here, integration appears to exist at the level of information provision but lacks consistent follow-through, limiting uptake and sustained engagement.

Several users perceived HIV and SRH services as fundamentally separate, reinforcing the absence of a unified care experience:

In this facility, they provide care based on what you are requesting… I don’t use SRH services here except for HIV treatment.(IB_SU_11, 45yrs)

You have to request it before it’s provided. (LA_SU_12, 46yrs)

These narratives suggest that access to SRH services is contingent on clients’ awareness and assertiveness, rather than embedded into routine HIV care pathways.

Limited awareness of integration was widespread. Many users described accessing HIV services exclusively, without knowledge of co-located SRH care:

I only access HIV services. I don’t know about others (IB_SU_16, 35yrs)

I don’t know. I’ve never asked (LA_SU_03, 54yrs)

This lack of clarity sometimes extended to misconceptions about eligibility for SRH services, such as cervical cancer screening:

I thought cervical screening is for people who have cervical cancer (IB_SU_10, 33yrs)

Such misconceptions point to missed opportunities for preventive care and reflect gaps in client education within integrated service models.

In response to uncertainty or limited guidance within HIV clinics, some users sought SRH services elsewhere:

I purchase family planning supplies outside (LA_SU_02, 44yrs)

Collectively, these accounts point to a pattern of “integration by default,” where services may exist within the same facility but are not consistently understood or accessed as part of a coordinated care model.

Sub-theme 1.2: Healthcare providers’ perceptions of integration

In contrast, healthcare providers articulated a clear and positive understanding of HIV–SRH integration, describing it as central to holistic, patient-centred care, stigma reduction, and improved health outcomes. Providers viewed HIV and SRH as inherently linked, particularly in relation to sexual behaviour, reproduction, and transmission prevention.

As one provider explained,

I think the two HIV and sexual reproductive health, they work hand in hand… to prevent unwanted stories. (IB_SP_12, 47yrs)

while another emphasised the importance of addressing sexuality directly:

We need to talk more about sex… that’s a primary issue when it comes to HIV (IB_SP_08, 30yrs).

Providers also emphasised the educational value of integration in challenging misconceptions about HIV and family life:

Being HIV positive doesn’t mean you can’t have a spouse or children. (LA_SP_04, 45yrs)

Operationally, integration was described as normalising HIV care and reducing stigma, particularly by embedding HIV services within broader SRH workflows:

Now, anybody with an STI will just come in… there’s no room for anybody to suspect that this is HIV. (LA_SP_06, 55yrs)

The rate of discrimination has decreased compared to before. (IB_SP_06, 58yrs)

Several providers reported observable reductions in discrimination within integrated service environments:

The rate of discrimination has decreased compared to before (IB_SP_06, 58yrs)

From a clinical perspective, providers described multiple ways in which integration improved care delivery, including co-locating HIV commodities and SRH services. Examples included placing antiretroviral drugs in labour wards, equipping family planning units with HIV test kits, and offering cervical cancer screening during routine visits:

We equip the family planning unit with test kits… ARVs are in labour rooms for timely administration. (IB_SP_14, 22yrs)

We screen for cervical cancer… and encourage family planning during medication pickups. (IB_SP_08, 30yrs)

Integration was also seen as critical for managing serodiscordant couples through partner testing and prophylaxis:

We administer prophylaxis to the negative partner to prevent transmission. (LA_SP_03, 44yrs)

Despite these strengths, some providers acknowledged that existing arrangements reflected only partial integration and called for a more comprehensive model:

There is already partial integration, and it would be better to fully integrate HIV services into SRH. (IB_SP_11, 45yrs)

This admission highlights provider awareness of structural and operational limitations that constrain the full realisation of integrated care.

Sub-theme 1.3: Gaps in awareness and understanding of integrated care

A key finding was the disconnect between provider-level implementation of integrated services and user-level awareness of those services. While providers described structured, coordinated care pathways linking HIV testing, STI management, partner notification, and SRH counselling, many service users remained unaware that such integrated systems existed within the facilities they attended.

Even among users who recognised that HIV and SRH services were offered in the same setting, integration was perceived as weakly promoted and inconsistently applied. As one participant observed,

They offer HIV and SRH services together… but they don’t enforce it. (LA_SU_17, 46yrs).

This limited visibility of integration contributed to confusion about what services were available and who was eligible to receive them. Some users held misconceptions about key SRH services, such as cervical cancer screening:

I thought cervical screening is for people who have cervical cancer. (IB_SU_10, 33yrs).

Others bypassed clinic-based SRH care altogether because they were uncertain how to access it within HIV services, explaining:

I purchase family planning supplies outside. (LA_SU_02, 44yrs)

In contrast, healthcare providers articulated a clear and systematic understanding of integrated care delivery, describing formalised pathways designed to address both HIV and SRH needs simultaneously. One provider outlined how integration was embedded in routine clinical practice:

In this facility, when anyone, whether male or female, has a sexually transmitted infection (STI), they are referred to this unit. We listen to their complaints, diagnose the issue, and provide HIV testing. If an individual is HIV positive, we check their sexual and reproductive health to ensure they adhere to treatment. We also conduct index testing, where we test their partners to prevent reinfection. Without addressing both HIV and sexual health, a positive patient engaging with an infected partner may not achieve good health outcomes. The two cannot be separated. (IB_SP_11_45yrs_F)

This contrast underscores that the challenge of HIV–SRH integration lies less in provider intent or service availability and more in how integration is communicated, routinised, and experienced by service users. The findings suggest that integration remains largely provider-driven and system-facing, with insufficient emphasis on client awareness, engagement, and empowerment.

Theme 2: Delivery of integrated HIV–SRH services

Accounts from healthcare providers revealed that HIV–SRH integration was enacted through a deliberately collaborative and multi-layered service delivery model. Rather than being confined to a single cadre or department, integration was distributed across multiple roles and points of care, reflecting an organisational logic in which HIV and SRH responsibilities were shared, overlapping, and mutually reinforcing. Providers consistently framed integration not as a standalone intervention, but as an embedded practice shaped by teamwork, communication, and client-centred engagement.

Subtheme 2.1: Role and responsibilities of healthcare workers

Healthcare providers described a differentiated but interconnected division of labour in the delivery of integrated HIV–SRH services. While some roles focused primarily on HIV testing and clinical management, others functioned as bridges between HIV services and SRH platforms such as family planning, immunisation, and maternal health services. This distribution of responsibilities enabled integration to occur across the care continuum rather than within a single service point.

One provider described how integration was operationalised through routine clinical encounters across departments:

I foster collaboration across healthcare units like family planning and immunization to help integrate HIV screening during other routine visits. (IB_SP_14, 22yrs).

This account illustrates how integration relied on opportunistic entry points, where non-HIV services were deliberately leveraged to expand HIV screening and early identification, thereby reducing missed opportunities for diagnosis.

Lay adherence counsellors were positioned as central actors in the linkage process, particularly at the point of diagnosis. Their role extended beyond test administration to include emotional support, risk communication, and structured enrolment into care:

Once the test is reactive, we engage them in counselling… and enroll them in care. (LA_SP_02, 39yrs).

Here, counselling is portrayed as both a psychosocial and clinical intervention, mediating the transition from diagnosis to long-term engagement with treatment and SRH services.

Mentor Mothers emerged as especially critical to sustaining engagement among pregnant and postpartum women living with HIV. Drawing on shared lived experience, they addressed fear, uncertainty, and adherence challenges across different stages of the maternal journey. One Mentor Mother described this role in depth:

As a Mentor Mother, I follow up with clients regularly to check if they are taking their medication as prescribed… I help ensure they stay on treatment and feel encouraged. We teach them how to care for their babies, what to eat, and what to avoid until the child is at least one year and six months old. By then, the child is considered to have outgrown the critical period, and standard procedures would have been followed to protect the child. In HIV program centers, there are different stages. When a woman is pregnant and HIV-positive, she often becomes fearful wondering, Now that I’m carrying this baby, will my child also have HIV? A lot runs through her mind. As a mentor mother, you use your own experience as an example.

This narrative highlights how integration extended beyond biomedical care to encompass emotional reassurance, peer modelling, and sustained accompaniment across time. Mentor Mothers functioned as trusted intermediaries who translated clinical guidance into relatable, experience-based support.

Taken together, these overlapping roles formed a continuum of care that linked HIV testing, SRH counselling, maternal health, and long-term adherence support. Integration was thus enacted through human relationships and coordinated practices rather than solely through facility-level structures.

Subtheme 2.1: Perceived outcomes of integrated care

Healthcare providers consistently linked integrated HIV–SRH service delivery to improved health outcomes, particularly through enhanced counselling, adherence support, and sexual risk reduction. Integration enabled providers to address HIV not as an isolated condition but as part of a broader sexual and reproductive health context, facilitating more meaningful and sustained engagement with clients.

One provider described how combined HIV–SRH counselling translated into observable clinical improvements.

HIV management is working in this community. Regarding sexual and reproductive health, we counsel patients… When we counsel them to start protecting themselves sexually, and they adhere to our counselling, their viral load improves. They do well, and looking at them, you wouldn’t even know they’re HIV-positive. (IB_SP_08_30yrs_F),

This account suggests that integration enhanced the effectiveness of HIV treatment by embedding adherence and risk-reduction counselling within broader discussions of sexual behaviour and reproductive health.

Understanding clients’ sexual histories and reproductive intentions was also described as essential to safe and responsive clinical care, particularly during pregnancy and childbirth:

When you talk to them, see what they have done before, then they will be able to talk. We take precautions when we are taking their delivery. (LA_SP_09_52yrs_F).

Here, integration enabled providers to tailor clinical decisions based on clients’ lived realities, reducing risk during delivery and improving maternal and infant outcomes.

Partner engagement emerged as a key mechanism through which integration supported prevention and family health. Providers emphasised partner testing as central to protecting future children and preventing reinfection:

We encourage HIV-positive patients to bring their sexual partners for testing to protect future children. (IB_SP_09_40yrs_F)

When clients adhere to counselling on sexual protection, their viral load improves, and they appear healthy. (LA_SP_19_30yrs_F)

Talking with clients about their history allows us to take precautions during delivery. (IB_SP_06_58yrs_F)

These accounts illustrate how integrated services facilitated shared responsibility for prevention within relationships, rather than placing the burden solely on individual clients.

Medication adherence was repeatedly identified as a central pathway linking integration to improved outcomes. Providers described actively motivating clients to remain on treatment, framing adherence as both a health-preserving and prevention-oriented practice:

One thing I do is to tell patients to stick with their medication because they won’t die from HIV if they keep taking their drugs. I emphasize that they need to take their medication to stay healthy and prevent transmission. (IB_SP_08_30yrs_F).

This framing positions integration as a relational process, where sustained provider–client interaction supports long-term behaviour change and health maintenance.

These findings demonstrate that integrated HIV–SRH service delivery functioned not merely as a structural arrangement, but as a set of relational, communicative, and clinical practices. Through coordinated counselling, partner engagement, and adherence support, providers were able to address sexuality, reproduction, prevention, and treatment in an interconnected manner, contributing to improved health outcomes and sustained engagement in care.

Theme 3.0: Utilization and experience of integrated services

Service users’ accounts revealed a nuanced pattern of care utilisation characterised by strong, sustained engagement with HIV services alongside more selective, episodic, or fragmented use of SRH services. Although many participants lacked explicit awareness of formal HIV–SRH integration, their narratives illustrated how personal health histories, perceived relevance of services, and the organisation of care shaped utilisation decisions. These findings suggest that service utilisation was driven less by awareness of integration as a policy goal and more by lived experiences of need, trust, and perceived benefit.

Subthemes 3.1: Patterns of service utilization

Most participants reported consistent and routine engagement with HIV services, including medication refills, viral load monitoring, and clinical follow-up. HIV care was described as predictable, dependable, and embedded into daily life. In contrast, SRH service use was more variable and often disconnected from HIV care, reflecting individual preferences and interpretations of relevance.

Several participants deliberately separated HIV care from other health needs, preferring to access SRH services in different settings:

I personally prefer to visit my family hospital for other things related to my health (IB_SU_15, 46yrs),

Others restricted their engagement strictly to HIV services, viewing SRH care as unnecessary or unrelated to their immediate priorities:

Just HIV, nothing more, nothing less (IB_SU_01, 31yrs).

These accounts suggest that while HIV services were perceived as essential and non-negotiable, SRH services were seen as optional or situational, rather than integral to ongoing care.

Past experiences also played a significant role in shaping SRH utilisation. Negative reactions or dissatisfaction with family planning methods discouraged continued engagement:

Family planning never worked well for me, my body doesn’t react well to it (LA_SU_02, 44yrs)

This highlights how embodied experiences and side effects can override clinical recommendations, leading users to disengage from specific SRH services even when they remain engaged in HIV care.

In some cases, SRH counselling extended beyond biomedical guidance to include intimate partner dynamics. One participant described advice that addressed relationship stability rather than solely reproductive health:

They advised me to let my husband ejaculate so that he won’t engage in extramarital affairs (IB_SU_03, 53yrs).

This account reflects the complexity of counselling within integrated settings, where providers navigate sexual health, relationship norms, and prevention in culturally situated ways.

Despite fragmented use of SRH services, participants consistently valued the interpersonal quality of care they received at HIV clinics. Friendly, respectful staff and active follow-up encouraged continued engagement:

They attend to me well… that makes me feel comfortable (IB_SU_15, 46yrs)

Staff call to remind me of appointments (IB_SU_16, 35yrs)

We are like a big family… they are very supportive (LA_SU_17, 46yrs).

These relational elements functioned as powerful facilitators of continued HIV service utilisation, even when SRH services were accessed selectively.

Some participants reported that SRH services, particularly cervical cancer screening and family planning, were provided proactively within HIV clinics.

Some SRH services, particularly cervical cancer screening and family planning, were reportedly offered proactively within HIV clinics:

We don’t need to request for it before it’s provided (IB_SU_05, 45yrs).

However, even where SRH services were available, some users still chose to obtain SRH commodities elsewhere without expressing dissatisfaction with clinic-based care:

I buy my family planning drugs outside… but I’m satisfied (IB_SU_02, 44yrs).

This suggests that external use of SRH services did not necessarily reflect service failure but rather convenience, habit, or perceived adequacy of alternative sources.

For users who engaged with integrated services, perceived health benefits were substantial. Participants described improved wellbeing, comprehensive diagnostics, and responsive clinical care:

I’ve been healthier since I started using these services (IB_SU_13, 56yrs)

They take good care of us at no cost (IB_SU_12, 46yrs)

If I cough, they do a TB test… check my blood count and viral load (LA_SU_05, 53yrs).

Despite these benefits, some users remained uncertain whether their care was formally integrated:

I haven’t noticed much difference (LA_SU_15, 56yrs).

This reinforces the finding that integration was often experienced pragmatically rather than conceptually, users benefited from coordinated care without necessarily recognising it as an integrated model.

Subtheme 3.2: Satisfaction with integrated HIV–SRH services

Overall satisfaction with care was high and closely tied to the emotional support provided by healthcare workers, particularly during the initial stages following HIV diagnosis. Participants described counselling as transformative, helping them move from fear and despair to acceptance and hope:

I thought I would die… I was made to understand that once I follow treatment, I would be fine (IB_SU_08, 30yrs)

Thanks to the health workers who helped me through that phase (IB_SU_15, 46yrs)

Most participants reported minimal challenges in accessing services, characterising care as reliable and well-organized:

Everything runs smoothly (IB_SU_05, 45yrs)

High satisfaction was reflected in long-term engagement and expressed loyalty to services:

I’m almost 100% satisfied… If it weren’t, I’d have moved elsewhere (LA_SU_20, 35yrs)

I’ve been using this service for 13 years (IB_SU_05, 45yrs).

Taken together, these findings suggest that even in the absence of clear awareness of formal HIV–SRH integration, service users experienced care that was compassionate, consistent, and responsive. This relational quality of care supported sustained engagement with HIV services and, for some users, selective uptake of SRH services based on perceived relevance and personal experience.

Theme 4: Barriers to integrated HIV–SRH services

Despite broad recognition of the benefits of integrating HIV and SRH services, participants described a constellation of interrelated barriers that limited both access to care and the effective delivery of integrated services. These barriers operated simultaneously at structural, institutional, interpersonal, and sociocultural levels, often reinforcing one another. Rather than functioning as isolated challenges, they collectively shaped how integration was implemented, experienced, and sustained within health facilities.

Subtheme 4.1: Staffing shortages and waiting times

Staffing constraints emerged as one of the most immediate and visible barriers to integrated service delivery. Both service users and providers described overcrowded clinics and limited personnel, resulting in prolonged waiting times and physical exhaustion for staff. One service user explained:

There will be a lot of crowd… we have to wait for a long time. Even the staff will be tired (IB_SU_01, 31yrs).

This account highlights how workforce shortages affect not only service efficiency but also the perceived quality of care. Providers echoed these concerns, emphasising that inadequate staffing undermined the sustainability of integration efforts:

Manpower is still inadequate… we can’t sustain screening efforts (LA_SP_10, 34yrs).

Beyond numerical shortages, providers identified gaps in skill mix and training as additional constraints. Integration requires staff who are competent across both HIV and SRH domains; however, this capacity was uneven,

Not all staff working in HIV services are well trained in SRH (IB_SP_11, 45yrs).

As a result, participants called for both increased recruitment and targeted training to distribute workload more equitably:

Employ more employees, so the workload is not on a single person (LA_SP_07, 42yrs).

These findings suggest that staffing challenges weaken integration not only by increasing waiting times, but also by limiting providers’ ability to deliver comprehensive, cross-cutting care.

Subtheme 4.2: Geographic and financial access barriers

Physical distance and transportation costs emerged as significant barriers to service use, particularly for clients travelling from remote or peri-urban areas. Service users described the financial and logistical strain of repeated clinic visits:

I come from a far distance… sometimes the transportation fare is challenging (IB_SU_02, 39yrs)

The only difficulty is travelling from Lagos (LA_SU_06, 48yrs).

Providers noted that these burdens often resulted in missed appointments or delayed care, especially for clients requiring frequent follow-up. Distance-related barriers therefore intersected with service frequency demands, reducing continuity of care and undermining retention in integrated service models.

Subtheme 4.3: Stigma and discrimination

Stigma remained a powerful deterrent to engagement with HIV–SRH services. Providers described persistent societal judgment toward people living with HIV:

Social stigma is a major issue… people condemn those who are HIV positive (LA_SP_01, 41yrs).

Fear of rejection within intimate relationships further limited disclosure and partner testing:

Women are afraid to disclose… due to fear of rejection and conflict (IB_SP_01, 35yrs).

Service users also internalised stigma, reporting emotional distress and concealment of their status:

Sometimes I used to feel stigmatized, even when I talk to my co-workers (LA_SU_02, 44yrs).

To avoid recognition and judgement, many clients chose to access services far from their communities, thereby compounding transportation barriers and disrupting continuity of care. Stigma thus operated both as a social constraint and as a driver of structural access challenges.

Subtheme 4.4: Infrastructure and resource constraints

Inadequate physical infrastructure and inconsistent availability of basic supplies significantly disrupted integrated service delivery. Overcrowded and undersized facilities limited privacy and patient flow:

This place is very small… some people are coming from far places (IB_SP_02, 41yrs),

Shortages of consumables further constrained service provision:

We receive test kits but lack gloves, or vice versa (LA_SP_14, 27yrs).

These resource gaps forced providers to prioritise specific populations, particularly pregnant women:

We focus mainly on pregnant women (IB_SP_07, 50yrs).

Such rationing undermined the principle of comprehensive integration and restricted the reach of SRH services within HIV care settings.

Subtheme 4.5: Funding and financial constraints

While HIV treatment itself was largely free, users reported unexpected out-of-pocket costs for ancillary services such as laboratory tests:

We are now told to pay for blood test (LA_SU_13, 39yrs).

For clients facing economic hardship, even minimal fees disrupted adherence and retention:

Some were crying they didn’t even have drugs to use (IB_SP_01, 35yrs).

Providers expressed deep concern about declining international donor funding, particularly from U.S. sources, and the implications for sustainability:

Once this program ends, there will be a need to get drugs out of pocket (LA_SP_10, 34yrs)

Donald Trump has cut funding… affects retention (LA_SP_13, 42yrs).

These anxieties highlight the fragility of integration efforts that rely heavily on external funding and the risk of service disruption if domestic financing is not strengthened.

Subtheme 4.6: Patient reluctance and non-compliance

Patient reluctance, particularly regarding partner involvement, emerged as a persistent barrier. Providers described resistance to partner testing and disclosure:

Some are hesitant to follow our recommendations about bringing in partners (IB_SP_09, 40yrs).

Although confidentiality protections were essential for safeguarding patient rights, they sometimes limited providers’ ability to engage partners proactively. Limited health literacy further compounded these challenges:

Many of our clients are not well educated… they don’t take it seriously (IB_SP_13, 38yrs).

These findings suggest that non-compliance often reflected structural and informational barriers rather than deliberate refusal of care.

Subtheme 4.7: Sociocultural norms

Cultural and religious norms strongly shaped attitudes toward SRH services, particularly family planning. Providers described resistance rooted in religious doctrine and gender dynamics:

Islamically, family planning is considered wrong… My husband will not accept this (IB_SP_08, 30yrs).

Religious beliefs also affected service uptake through objections to blood sampling and clinic attendance during fasting periods:

Some clients believe blood sampling breaks their fast (IB_SP_13, 38yrs).

These sociocultural constraints limited clients’ autonomy and required providers to navigate care sensitively within deeply held belief systems.

Subtheme 4.8: Limited provider training

Finally, insufficient training emerged as a critical barrier to effective integration. Several providers reported learning about integration informally rather than through structured programs:

I have not received formal training on integration, but am learning on the job (IB_SP_11, 45yrs).

Others described sporadic or superficial training opportunities:

I’ve only participated in step-down sessions (IB_SP_14, 22yrs)

It has been long now since the last training (IB_SP_06, 58yrs).

These gaps undermined provider confidence and consistency, limiting the quality and fidelity of integrated HIV–SRH service delivery.

Theme 5: Facilitators to integrated HIV-SRH service use

Participants identified multiple facilitators that enabled access to and sustained engagement with integrated HIV and SRH services. These facilitators operated across interpersonal, organisational, and community levels, often counteracting the structural and sociocultural barriers described earlier. Rather than functioning in isolation, these enablers worked synergistically to support trust, continuity of care, and long-term service utilisation.

Subtheme 5.1: Supportive and client-centred provider relationships

Supportive, compassionate, and non-judgmental staff attitudes emerged as one of the most influential facilitators of service use. Service users consistently described healthcare workers as respectful and emotionally attuned, creating a care environment that reduced fear and encouraged continued engagement. One participant explained,

The healthcare workers are nice. They treat us well despite knowing our status (IB_SU_07, 45yrs).

This account highlights how respectful treatment counteracts anticipated stigma and reassures clients of their dignity within the healthcare setting.

Positive staff behaviour was also directly linked to clinic attendance and retention:

The behaviour of the staff encourages me to visit the clinic… they are accommodating and nice (LA_SU_13, 42yrs).

For some participants, the clinic environment extended beyond a biomedical space to become a source of emotional safety and belonging:

We are like a big family… I can call to ask questions anytime (IB_SU_08, 30yrs).

Such descriptions suggest that relational continuity and accessibility fostered trust, enabling clients to seek guidance beyond scheduled appointments.

Emotional support was particularly critical during the period immediately following HIV diagnosis, when vulnerability and distress were highest. One user recalled,

The first time I came here, I broke down but… I was able to cope (IB_SU_01, 31yrs).

This illustrates how empathetic staff interactions functioned as an early stabilising mechanism, shaping clients’ willingness to remain engaged with care over time.

Subtheme 5.2: Follow-up and continuity mechanisms

Organisational follow-up mechanisms, particularly appointment reminders and phone calls, were central to sustaining engagement and supporting adherence. Service users described consistent outreach from healthcare workers:

They call us every time as a reminder for treatment (IB_SU_14, 58yrs);

Staff call to remind me of appointments (LA_SU_02, 44yrs).

These reminders reduced missed appointments and reinforced routine engagement, particularly for clients managing competing social and economic demands.

Providers framed follow-up not as monitoring or enforcement but as an expression of genuine care:

We follow-up with our clients and show we genuinely care beyond rendering health services (IB_SP_11, 45yrs).

This framing underscores how follow-up systems strengthened relational trust and signalled provider commitment, transforming administrative processes into supportive care practices.

Subtheme 5.3: Social and family support

Support from family members and intimate partners emerged as a critical facilitator of service utilisation and adherence. Emotional encouragement and practical assistance made it easier for clients to attend appointments and remain on treatment. One participant shared:

Having a strong support system… makes a huge difference. My twin sister is very supportive (IB_SU_01, 31yrs).

This account highlights the protective role of trusted family relationships, particularly in contexts where disclosure is selective:

Husbands remind their pregnant wives to take medication because they care (LA_SP_10, 33yrs).

Such involvement redistributed responsibility for care and reduced the emotional burden placed solely on women living with HIV.

Subtheme 5.4: Stakeholder collaboration

Institutional collaboration with government agencies and non-governmental organisations was essential for sustaining integrated service delivery. Providers described how these partnerships enabled continuity of care during resource shortages:

We notify the Ministry of Health… and SACA when we run out of supplies (IB_SP_14, 22yrs).

External partners also provided critical operational and logistical support:

We receive a lot of support from NGOs like APIN and the national government (LA_SP_01, 52yrs).

These collaborations strengthened system resilience, ensuring that integration efforts could continue despite fluctuating resources.

Cross-departmental collaboration within facilities, such as integrated testing points in gynaecology and family planning units, further facilitated seamless referrals and continuity of care, reinforcing integration at the operational level.

Subtheme 5.5: Client-centred relationships

Trust-based, client-centred relationships between providers and clients were central to sustained engagement with integrated services. Providers described how relational closeness encouraged openness and disclosure,

Clients see us as friends and confidants, which helps them open up (IB_SP_12, 47yrs).

This relational dynamic enabled providers to address sensitive issues related to sexuality, adherence, and partner involvement more effectively.

Providers also responded flexibly to clients’ material vulnerabilities, recognising that social needs directly affect health behaviours:

When clients lack food or transportation, we… sometimes provide transport support (IB_SP_03, 40yrs).

These gestures reinforced trust and demonstrated a holistic approach to care that extended beyond clinical treatment.

Subtheme 5.6: Community health education and awareness

Community-level education and outreach were critical in addressing sociocultural resistance and increasing awareness of integrated HIV–SRH services. Providers described how health talks on cervical cancer screening and HIV improved acceptability and facilitated service integration:

That one will make it easier for us if we want to combine the two together (IB_SP_10, 49yrs).

Mass media and engagement with community leaders were also identified as effective strategies:

Cultural resistance has reduced significantly through radio education programs (LA_SP_04, 45yrs).

Importantly, providers emphasised respectful communication tailored to community norms:

We bridge gaps by creating awareness… while respecting cultural sensitivities (IB_SP_01, 35yrs).

These efforts positioned integration as culturally responsive rather than externally imposed, supporting broader acceptance and uptake.

Theme 6: Recommendations for strengthening integration

Participants articulated a set of forward-looking recommendations aimed at addressing the structural and social constraints identified in earlier themes. These recommendations were grounded in lived experience and reflected a pragmatic understanding of what is required to improve access, quality, and sustainability of integrated HIV and SRH services. Rather than proposing isolated solutions, participants emphasised system-wide strengthening, encompassing financial protection, workforce capacity, community engagement, and sustainable financing.

Subtheme 6.1: Free and accessible medications

Maintaining free access to HIV treatment and related SRH services emerged as a central priority, particularly for economically vulnerable clients. Participants framed affordability as a non-negotiable requirement for continued engagement with care, highlighting the precarious economic realities faced by many service users. One participant explained,

They should keep supporting us with free drugs. I don’t even have enough food to eat, so I cannot afford to buy medications (IB_SU_14, 58yrs).

This account illustrates how financial hardship intersects with health needs, making out-of-pocket payments untenable and threatening treatment continuity. Another participant reinforced this point:

Drugs should continue to be given to us at no cost because that’s what we need (IB_SU_06, 48yrs).

For participants, financial protection was not merely about reducing immediate costs but about safeguarding adherence, preventing treatment interruption, and preserving health outcomes over time. Free access was therefore viewed as foundational to the success of integrated HIV–SRH services.

Subtheme 6.2: Strengthening service integration

Participants consistently called for deeper, more visible, and better-coordinated integration of HIV and SRH services within healthcare facilities. These recommendations reflected dissatisfaction with partial or implicit integration and a desire for a more seamless and proactive care experience. One participant stated,

These two services should be well integrated and promoted, as I believe they work best together (IB_SU_10, 49yrs),

This comment underscores the perceived synergy between HIV and SRH services and the need for clearer communication to ensure users understand and access the full scope of care available.

Another participant emphasised the importance of embedding integration into routine clinical processes:

Vitals should be checked regularly and HIV and SRH services should be integrated together (LA_SU_08, 35yrs).

These recommendations suggest that participants viewed effective integration as extending beyond co-location to include coordinated workflows, routine screening, and proactive service delivery. Strengthening integration was therefore framed as both an organisational and communicative challenge.

Subtheme 6.3: Staff shortages and capacity building

Staffing shortages and limited training were identified as major threats to service quality and integration. Service users highlighted how understaffing translated into long waiting times and fatigue among providers:

Maybe more staff… we have to wait for a long time. Even the staff will be tired (IB_SU_01, 35yrs).

Providers echoed these concerns and emphasised the need for both recruitment and ongoing professional development:

We need more hands… More training for health workers… it will keep us on our toes (IB_SP_06, 58yrs).

Capacity building was framed as essential for maintaining quality and consistency in integrated service delivery:

Ongoing training and orientation for staff will help the integration of the two services (IB_SP_14, 22yrs).

These accounts indicate that effective integration depends on a well-resourced and adequately trained workforce capable of delivering HIV and SRH services in a coordinated manner.

Subtheme 6.4: Community engagement and sensitization

Participants underscored the importance of sustained community engagement to address stigma, improve health literacy, and increase service uptake. Involving trusted community actors was seen as critical for legitimising integration efforts:

Our community gatekeepers, religious leaders, youth leaders, and political figures must be engaged (IB_SP_14, 22yrs).

Education was perceived as most effective when delivered consistently rather than as one-off campaigns:

People need to hear about HIV repeatedly to drive away stigma (IB_SP_07, 50yrs)

Consistent, clear reminders help people remember both the positives and the precautions (LA_SP_12, 29yrs).

Participants also emphasised the need for practical, everyday health education that addressed non-sexual routes of transmission:

If you go for a manicure or pedicure, bring your own tools. It’s safer (IB_SP_12, 47yrs).

Subtheme 6.5: Resource mobilization and support

Finally, participants highlighted the need for greater and more sustainable investment in integrated HIV–SRH services from both government and development partners. Ongoing funding was seen as essential for maintaining awareness campaigns and service delivery:

Funding and support… are needed to sustain sensitization and awareness (IB_SP_12, 47yrs).

There was particular concern about heavy reliance on external donors, especially USAID:

If USAID does not support this program again, things will be worse. The government should take this program seriously (IB_SP_02, 41yrs).

These concerns reflect an awareness of the fragility of donor-dependent health systems and a call for stronger domestic ownership and financing to ensure the long-term sustainability of integrated care.

Discussion

This study contributes new qualitative evidence on how integrated HIV and SRH services are experienced by service users and healthcare providers in Southwest Nigeria. Existing literature in Nigeria has largely examined HIV–SRH linkages through specific service domains, particularly prevention of mother-to-child transmission and antenatal HIV testing, with limited attention to how integration is experienced in routine service delivery [24, 31]. Globally, integration is widely promoted as a strategy to improve continuity of care and reduce stigma, yet implementation is often assessed in terms of service availability rather than how services are communicated, accessed, and navigated in practice [42]. By combining perspectives of service users and providers, this study extends existing evidence by demonstrating that the lived experience of integration differs substantially from its policy intent.

A central and novel finding is the persistence of what we describe as “integration by default rather than by design”, where HIV and SRH services coexist operationally within facilities but remain poorly understood, inconsistently promoted, and unevenly accessed by service users [42]. Although some users receive SRH services during routine HIV visits, these services are often delivered without explicit communication, limiting awareness of available care and placing the burden of navigation on clients. This dynamic reproduces inequities, as individuals with greater health literacy or confidence are more likely to benefit from available services [43]. This finding challenges the implicit assumption in integration literature that co-location alone translates into effective, person-centred care, highlighting instead that integration must be actively communicated, coordinated, and experienced as a coherent care pathway.

From an implementation perspective, these findings point to gaps in service integration processes and communication, particularly insufficient communication, weak service promotion, and lack of structured navigation, all of which limit the visibility and usability of integrated care. These challenges are consistent with CFIR domains related to implementation process and inner setting [36]. The findings also highlight constraints within the inner setting, especially staffing shortages and high workload, which undermine providers’ ability to deliver integrated services consistently. Although healthcare providers demonstrated strong commitment and created supportive care environments, the effectiveness of integration remained constrained by chronic human resource limitations. This aligns with prior evidence showing that integrated service delivery depends not only on service design but also on adequate workforce capacity and organisational support [44]. Reliance on provider goodwill, while important, is not a sustainable foundation for system-level integration.

Financial and logistical barriers further highlight the influence of outer setting factors on service access. Transportation costs, long travel distances, and user fees for ancillary services disproportionately affected economically vulnerable clients [45]. In addition, inconsistent availability of commodities such as HIV test kits and consumables disrupted service continuity and forced providers to ration care, effectively reintroducing service fragmentation [46]. Even where core HIV services such as ART are free, indirect costs continue to shape access, reinforcing the well-established link between socioeconomic status and health outcomes [47–49]. Taken together, these findings point to constraints across both the outer setting, particularly financial and geographic barriers, and the inner setting, including limited organisational resources and supply continuity, as conceptualised in CFIR [36].

At the same time, the study highlights important relational and process-level facilitators of effective integration. A key finding is the central role of supportive and non-judgmental healthcare providers in creating psychologically safe clinical environments. Service users consistently described compassionate provider attitudes as a major driver of continued engagement with care, suggesting that relational quality is a core mechanism through which integration functions. This aligns with the People-Centred Care framework, which emphasises dignity, respect, and empowerment as central to high-quality health systems [50]. Trust and emotional security enabled disclosure, adherence, and long-term engagement with care, which are critical for managing HIV as a chronic condition [51, 52].

Similarly, proactive follow-up systems, such as telephone reminders, strengthened continuity of care by bridging clinic and community contexts [53]. These interactions were perceived as supportive rather than intrusive, reinforcing trust and collaboration between clients and providers [54]. In addition, social and community-level support, including partner involvement and family engagement, played an important role in sustaining adherence and promoting health-seeking behaviour [55]. Together, these findings suggest that effective integration operates not only through structural arrangements, but also through relational continuity and community support systems.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this study have several important implications for strengthening HIV–SRH integration in Nigeria. First, integration needs to be made more visible and explicit to service users. These findings suggest the value of more structured communication strategies, including routine counselling on available SRH services during HIV visits, use of patient education materials, and clearer referral pathways within facilities. Without deliberate communication, integration may remain under-recognised and underutilised. Second, strengthening health workforce capacity is likely to be important for improving integration. Addressing staffing shortages, workload distribution, and provider training in integrated service delivery may help support more consistent and proactive delivery of HIV–SRH services. Task-sharing approaches and integration-specific training may also reduce the burden on overstretched providers. Third, reducing financial and logistical barriers may improve equitable access. These findings suggest that efforts to reduce transportation burdens, minimise user fees for ancillary services, and ensure more consistent availability of essential commodities could help reduce fragmentation of care. Fourth, integration efforts should continue to prioritise relational and people-centred approaches. Supporting healthcare providers to maintain respectful, non-judgmental interactions and strengthening systems for follow-up and patient engagement may enhance trust, retention, and long-term outcomes. Finally, integration strategies may benefit from extending beyond facilities to include community and social support systems, recognising the role of family, partners, and community networks in shaping health behaviours and service utilisation.

Overall, these findings suggest that HIV–SRH integration is most effective when it operates across three interconnected levels: structural (health system capacity and resources), relational (provider–client interactions), and community (social support systems). Integration that focuses solely on co-location or service availability, without addressing these broader dimensions, risks remaining symbolic rather than transformative.

This study has several limitations. Findings are context-specific and may not be generalisable beyond Southwest Nigeria. Recruitment through health facilities may have introduced selection bias by capturing the experiences of individuals already engaged in care, while excluding those who had disengaged from services and who may face even greater barriers to HIV–SRH integration. In addition, because interviews were conducted in facility settings and addressed sensitive issues related to HIV and SRH services, some participants may have provided socially desirable responses, particularly when describing provider behaviour or satisfaction with care. Policymakers were also not included, limiting insight into financing and governance dynamics that shape system-wide implementation. In addition, the modest transport reimbursement provided to service users may have influenced willingness to participate and could have contributed to response bias, although it was intended only to offset participation-related costs.

Conclusion

Integrated HIV and SRH services are widely valued by both healthcare providers and service users in Southwest Nigeria for supporting holistic, person-centred care and reducing stigma. However, this study demonstrates that integration is often experienced as fragmented and poorly communicated, limiting its full potential. Addressing structural constraints, strengthening workforce capacity, improving service visibility, and leveraging relational and community-based support systems will be critical to translating HIV–SRH integration from policy aspiration into effective and sustainable practice.
