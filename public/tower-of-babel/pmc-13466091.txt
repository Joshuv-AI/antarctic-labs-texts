MOTS-c: How a secreted mitochondrial microprotein may become a potential treatment for inflammatory lung diseases
Authors: Carlos A. Amado, Juan Agüero, Mayte García-Unzueta, Ana Berja, Bernardo A. Lavín, Paula Martín-Audera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMOTS-c is a mitochondrial-derived microprotein (mitokine) encoded within the 12 S rRNA gene that exerts intracrine, paracrine, and endocrine effects across multiple tissues. Although initially described as a regulator of metabolic homeostasis, growing evidence indicates that MOTS-c also modulates oxidative and toxic stress, inflammation, autophagy, cell death (apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, among others), mitochondrial dysfunction, and immune responses—key mechanisms involved in acute and chronic respiratory diseases.Main bodyAvailable experimental and clinical studies suggest that circulating MOTS-c levels are reduced in different forms of acute respiratory distress, while exogenous administration attenuates lung injury in preclinical models. Remote ischemic preconditioning appears to exert part of its protective effects through MOTS-c release. In contrast, chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and asthma are characterized by decreased MOTS-c concentrations, reflecting severe mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced cytoprotective capacity. Preliminary observations suggest increased MOTS-c levels in lung cancer, potentially related to NRF2-mediated antioxidant responses, although these findings require further validation.ConclusionsCurrent evidence positions MOTS-c as a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic candidate in respiratory medicine. Well-designed translational and multicenter clinical studies are needed to determine whether modulation or supplementation of MOTS-c can influence disease progression and clinical outcomes in inflammatory lung diseases.

Introduction

Mitochondria are highly dynamic, double‑membrane organelles that perform essential functions extending far beyond the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell’s primary bioenergetic “currency” [1]. They participate in glucose, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism; the synthesis of key molecules such as heme and iron–sulfur clusters; the regulation of intracellular calcium and reactive oxygen species (ROS); autophagy; programmed cell death; inflammation; and immune responses [1].

According to the classical endosymbiotic theory, mitochondria originated from a symbiotic event in which a bacterium “infected” primordial eukaryotic cells, giving rise to a new nucleated cell equipped with a highly efficient organelle for ATP generation [2]. The establishment of this symbiosis required extensive co‑evolution, adaptation, and functional integration of mitochondrial and nuclear DNA and proteins to ensure coordinated metabolic and cellular processes. As reviewed by Picard et al. [3], mitochondria can sense a broad spectrum of endogenous and environmental stressors and integrate these signals through dynamic physical and chemical networks. The resulting output signals modulate the activity of other organelles and elicit systemic adaptive responses. One such output signal is the microprotein Mitochondrial Open Reading Frame of the 12 S rRNA‑type c (MOTS‑c), with potent antioxidant, detoxifying, and anti-inflammatory effects, which is the focus of this review.

Cellular injury of diverse etiologies can lead to a decline or complete loss of essential mitochondrial functions. The magnitude of the stressor—its type, intensity, and duration—plays a decisive role in determining cellular fate [4]. Restoration of basal cellular homeostasis requires the removal of damaged molecules via autophagy and the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria through mitophagy. However, when the energetic cost of repair exceeds its potential benefit, cells activate programmed death pathways such as apoptosis or ferroptosis. The term mitochondrial dysfunction is commonly associated with impairment of the organelle’s most prominent function: bioenergetic failure and reduced ATP production [5]. This dysfunction encompasses disturbances in mitochondrial energy metabolism, calcium homeostasis, membrane depolarization or loss of membrane potential, and aberrant ROS generation. All these processes can be assessed through histological and biochemical analyses [1, 3].

A wide range of pulmonary disorders have been clearly associated with impaired mitochondrial activity [6–8], which has stimulated considerable interest in the potential involvement of mitokines—cytokines whose secretion is directly or indirectly regulated by mitochondrial function—in their pathophysiology and treatment. Our group has provided the first data in the literature on serum concentrations of Humanin (the first identified mitokine), MOTS‑c, Fibroblast Growth Factor 21 (FGF21), and Growth Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF15) in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as their clinical implications [9–11].

The objective of the present paper is to offer an up‑to‑date (as of March 2026), comprehensive, and critical review of the potential roles of MOTS‑c in the pathophysiology and clinical dimensions of pulmonary diseases, particularly those characterized by well‑documented oxidative stress and prominent inflammation. Beneficial effects of MOTS-c have been demonstrated in many inflammatory diseases, but the data about inflammatory pulmonary diseases are few. While this manuscript was under review, we became aware of the insightful article by Behan et al. [12], which examines the roles and implications of mitochondrial‑derived microproteins (MDPs) in lung diseases. Our review expands and complements their paper by incorporating additional diseases not addressed in their analysis and offering potential explanations for several questions raised in that paper. Based on the available evidence, we propose that impaired MOTS-c secretion within the framework of the mitohormetic dose–response of mitochondrial dysfunction may play a significant role in the onset and progression of these disorders. This perspective may provide a unifying framework for understanding many previously reported abnormalities in key biochemical pathways (e.g., AMPK, NRF2) implicated in these diseases. Furthermore, we review in detail the potential utility and limitations of MOTS-c as a biomarker and as a therapeutic agent in inflammatory pulmonary disease.

MOTS-c

Discovery and mitochondrial origin of MOTS‑c

Approximately a decade ago, Cohen and colleagues identified a previously unrecognized 16–amino acid microprotein encoded within mitochondrial DNA (13). This peptide is translated from a small open reading frame (sORF) embedded in the 12 S rRNA gene (MT‑RNR1) and was therefore designated Mitochondrial Open Reading Frame of the 12 S rRNA type‑c (MOTS‑c). In their initial report, the authors described a wide range of intracrine, paracrine, and endocrine actions attributed to this molecule [13]. Early investigations primarily linked MOTS‑c to metabolic regulation, although that aspect is beyond the scope of the present discussion.

Microproteins—peptides typically composed of fewer than 100–150 amino acids—are encoded by sORFs located in either nuclear or mitochondrial DNA. Their biological relevance was initially overlooked during early genomic analyses, largely because a minimum ORF length of 300 base pairs (100 codons) had been arbitrarily established to reduce false positives arising from non‑coding RNAs (14). Advances in sequencing and proteomic technologies have since revealed numerous functional microproteins, including at least ten encoded by mitochondrial sORFs and more than one hundred encoded in the nuclear genome.

MOTS‑c is expressed and functionally active in a wide variety of metabolically demanding, mitochondria‑rich tissues, including cardiac and skeletal muscle, liver, kidney, intestinal epithelium, vascular endothelium, airway epithelial cells, alveolar cells, monocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, and primary bone marrow stem cells [13]. Importantly, translation of MOTS‑c occurs in the cytoplasm using the standard genetic code, as the mitochondrial genetic code does not support the synthesis of this peptide. Consequently, the mitochondrial MOTS‑c transcript must be exported to the cytoplasm for translation. The first 11 amino acids of MOTS‑c are highly conserved across species—from humans to zebrafish—highlighting their evolutionary and functional significance [13].

In vitro studies demonstrate that MOTS‑c expression is upregulated under conditions of mitochondrial stress, including glucose restriction, serum deprivation, hypoxia, and oxidative stress, although the precise molecular mechanisms governing this induction remain unclear [13]. Under such stress, mitochondria initiate mitonuclear (retrograde) signaling to activate compensatory autocrine responses. Ultimately, MOTS‑c exerts systemic effects, as it is capable of crossing cellular membranes and is detectable in the circulation [13]. Through these mechanisms, MOTS‑c contributes to cytoprotection by reprogramming cellular metabolism, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and function, and promoting antioxidant, detoxifying, and anti‑inflammatory pathways [13].

Mechanisms of action of MOTS‑c

MOTS‑c modulates a broad array of cytosolic, mitochondrial, and nuclear metabolic pathways. The interactions among these pathways are complex, context‑dependent, and influenced by factors such as dosage, temporal dynamics, and tissue‑specific mitochondrial phenotypes (“mitotypes”). Despite significant progress, the integrative mechanisms through which MOTS‑c orchestrates these metabolic responses remain incompletely understood.

Cytoplasmic activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) by MOTS‑c

The original characterization of MOTS‑c demonstrated that both endogenous and exogenous forms of the peptide modulate the methionine–folate cycle in skeletal muscle [13]. Specifically, MOTS‑c reduces intracellular concentrations of 5‑methyl‑tetrahydrofolate (5‑MeTHF), a change associated with a striking ~ 20‑fold accumulation of 5‑aminoimidazole‑4‑carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) and a concomitant inhibition of de novo purine biosynthesis [13]. The precise biochemical mechanisms underlying this pronounced rise in AICAR remain unresolved. Since 1995, it has been known that AICAR is an AMP analogue capable of allosterically activating AMPK, although with much lower potency (40–50 times less) [13, 15]. The availability of this compound made it possible to investigate the effects of AMPK activation in vitro and in vivo through intravenous or intraperitoneal administration. However, its very low oral bioavailability prevented its development as an orally administered drug in humans [16].

AMPK activation proceeds through the canonical AMPK → Sirtuin‑1 (SIRT1) → Peroxisome proliferator‑activated receptor‑γ coactivator‑1α (PGC‑1α) axis, a signaling cascade also robustly induced by physical exercise. Recent reviews provide comprehensive analyses of these AMPK‑dependent mechanisms [17–20]. The γ‑subunit of AMPK contains four tandem cystathionine‑β‑synthase (CBS) domains that function as energy‑sensing modules by binding AMP, ADP, and ATP. Under conditions of elevated AMP/ADP and reduced ATP—such as exercise, nutrient deprivation, or mitochondrial dysfunction—AMPK becomes activated.

Once activated, AMPK orchestrates a coordinated metabolic response across multiple tissues. These effects include enhanced skeletal muscle glucose uptake, suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipid synthesis, initiation of autophagy, optimization of mitochondrial function, and activation of antioxidant and anti‑inflammatory programs. These processes are mediated through both direct phosphorylation events (AMPK) and downstream transcriptional reprogramming involving SIRT1 and PGC‑1α [17–20].

Mitochondrial activation of BH3 domain of the anti‑apoptotic protein B‑cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl‑2) by MOTS‑c

A notable mechanistic advance was reported by Lu et al. [21], who demonstrated that MOTS‑c directly interacts with Bcl‑2. Bcl‑2, located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, prevents mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening and thereby inhibits intrinsic apoptosis. MOTS‑c binding stabilizes Bcl‑2 by suppressing its polyubiquitination, resulting in increased Bcl‑2 protein levels in hepatocytes exposed to palmitic acid and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF‑α).

In vivo, MOTS‑c administration ameliorated non‑alcoholic steatohepatitis in rat models, further supporting its role in mitochondrial self‑preservation under metabolic and inflammatory stress [21]. Although compelling, these findings remain preliminary and require validation in human studies to determine their translational relevance.

Nuclear translocation of MOTS‑c and coactivation of NRF2‑mediated transcription

Subsequent work from the same California research group revealed that MOTS‑c is capable of translocating to the nucleus, where it enhances the transcription of a broad set of genes containing antioxidant response elements (AREs) [22]. In this context, MOTS‑c functions as a coactivator of nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2), a master regulator of cellular antioxidant and detoxification programs [23]. Activation of the NRF2 pathway leads to the induction of numerous cytoprotective genes that collectively reduce ROS and toxins, and mitigate oxidative damage.

Two recent studies [24, 25] have elucidated the molecular mechanism underlying MOTS‑c nuclear translocation. In the cytoplasm of skeletal muscle cells, MOTS‑c binds to the α‑subunit of casein kinase 2 (CK2; formerly protein kinase CK2) and activates it. Activated CK2 phosphorylates serine 1943 of myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9), a cytoskeletal motor protein essential for intracellular cargo transport. This phosphorylation event enables the formation of a MOTS‑c–phosphorylated MYH9 complex that is actively transported across the nuclear membrane. AMPK activation also contributes to MOTS‑c nuclear import, although the precise and complex interplay between AMPK‑dependent and CK2‑dependent mechanisms remains to be fully clarified.

Cytoprotective effects of MOTS-c

MOTS‑c regulates multiple processes central to the pathogenesis of inflammatory and degenerative diseases, including aging, oxidative stress, toxic exposures, inflammation, autophagy, programmed cell death, and immune responses [7, 22–27]. Oxidative stress arises when ROS production exceeds the antioxidant capacity of the cell. Excess ROS —derived from exogenous sources such as cigarette smoke, pollutants, and infections, or endogenous sources such as metabolic dysfunction and primary mitochondrial defects — react with and damage carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, as well as organelles including mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and the nucleus.

Damaged mitochondria release mitochondrial damage‑associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs), including mitochondrial DNA, cardiolipin, N‑formyl peptides, ATP, and succinate, into the cytosol. These mtDAMPs activate inflammatory signaling pathways such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)-inflammasome, and the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)– Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) axis, leading to the production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (interleukins -IL‑1β, IL‑6, etc.-tumor necrosis factor -TNF‑α-) and chemokines (CXCL2, CXCL8, among others [26, 27]. Concurrently, autophagy is initiated to sequester and degrade damaged organelles and macromolecules. Unc-51-like autophagy activating kinase 1/2 (ULK1/2), a central autophagy‑initiating kinase regulated by AMPK, plays a key role in this process.

Through its activation of AMPK and NRF2, MOTS‑c exerts broad regulatory control over these pathways. AMPK activation induces antioxidant transcription factors (NRF2, Forkhead box O 1 (FOXO1), antioxidant enzymes such as manganese superoxide dismutase (SOD2) and catalase (CAT), and autophagy machinery, while also enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and function via PGC‑1α. NRF2 activation suppresses ROS‑generating enzymes such as NADPH oxidases (NOX), and induces a wide array of antioxidant and detoxifying enzymes, including heme oxygenase‑1 (HO‑1), glutathione synthesis enzymes, glutathione peroxidases, thioredoxin reductase, and tobacco‑smoke detoxification enzymes such as NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase1 (NQO1), Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and UDP glucuronosyltransferase family 1 member A6 (UGT1A6) etc. NRF2 also dampens NF‑κB activation, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine production [17–20, 26, 27] (Table 1). When these cytoprotective mechanisms are insufficient, cells progress toward programmed cell death, with the specific pathway determined by the surrounding microenvironment.

Over the past five years, the anti‑inflammatory properties of exogenously administered MOTS‑c have been consistently demonstrated across numerous experimental systems. These include in vitro models of oxidative injury—such as hydrogen peroxide–induced cellular stress [28]—and a wide range of in vivo models that recapitulate human inflammatory diseases. Beneficial effects have been reported in the mouse formalin test [29], inflammatory pain models (30), dextran sulfate sodium–induced colitis [31], lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–induced septic cardiomyopathy [32], diabetic cardiomyopathy [33, 34], non‑alcoholic steatohepatitis [21], and osteoarthritis [35]. Despite this extensive preclinical evidence, no published studies have yet evaluated the anti‑inflammatory actions of MOTS‑c in humans.

Recent findings indicate that MOTS‑c also possesses direct antibacterial [36] and potentially antiviral activities [37], a feature of particular relevance for infectious pulmonary diseases. Interestingly, the discovery of MOTS‑c is linked to earlier work by Tsuzuki et al. [38], who showed that interferon‑γ stimulation of mitochondrial DNA led to the production of a novel molecule—later identified as MOTS‑c [13]. This connection suggests that MOTS‑c may be an integral component of the innate immune response to infection.

In murine models, MOTS‑c markedly attenuates the pathological consequences of methicillin‑resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infection [39]. Cohen’s group demonstrated that MOTS‑c, when added directly to suspensions of Escherichia coli or MRSA, induces bacterial aggregation and membrane disruption through interactions mediated by its ionized hydrophobic residues [36]. Notably, this effect is selective, as MOTS‑c does not interact with Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Salmonella typhimurium. In vivo, MRSA inoculation is typically lethal in mice; however, co‑administration of MOTS‑c reduced mortality to 16% and significantly blunted the associated cytokine storm [39.

Both in vitro and in vivo studies indicate that MOTS‑c enhances monocyte activation and promotes their differentiation into macrophages with robust antibacterial capacity [36]. Human monocytes exhibit a similar response in vitro. Moreover, exposure of primary bone marrow cells to MOTS‑c induces the emergence of a distinct subset of mature macrophages characterized by the expression of genes involved in antigen presentation [36]. Simultaneously, MOTS‑c suppresses the expression of genes associated with interferon synthesis and interferon‑mediated signaling. Together, these findings suggest the presence of a finely tuned feedback loop between MOTS‑c and interferon‑γ, enabling dynamic regulation of monocyte–macrophage function in accordance with physiological demands. This interplay may represent an evolutionarily conserved mechanism linking mitochondrial stress responses to innate immunity.

In addition to its antibacterial properties, MOTS‑c has recently been shown to inhibit hepatitis B virus (HBV) replication in both in vivo experimental models and in vitro human hepatocyte cultures [37]. This antiviral effect is associated with enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and activation of the mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) pathway, a central component of innate antiviral immunity [37]. These findings position MOTS‑c as one of the first described mitochondrial DNA–encoded molecule with direct antibacterial and antiviral activities, while simultaneously enhancing innate immune defenses and modulating adaptive immune responses.

A significant immunomodulatory role for MOTS‑c has been demonstrated in autoimmune disease. In non‑obese diabetic (NOD) mice—the most widely used model of human type 1 diabetes—MOTS‑c administration delays autoimmune destruction of pancreatic islets and postpones the onset of hyperglycemia [40]. This protective effect is mediated by alterations in T‑cell populations: MOTS‑c increases CD4⁺ T‑cell numbers while reducing CD8⁺ T cells; within the CD4⁺ compartment, pro‑inflammatory Th1 cells (CD4⁺ IFNγ⁺) are diminished, whereas regulatory T cells (Tregs; CD4⁺ CD25⁺ FOXP3⁺) are expanded (40). These findings suggest that MOTS‑c may have therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases, including those associated with secondary (autoimmune) pulmonary fibrosis.

Factors modulating blood MOTS-c levels

Exercise‑induced MOTS‑c production

Physical exercise robustly increases MOTS‑c secretion in skeletal muscle and elevates circulating MOTS‑c levels, producing both systemic and muscle‑specific benefits [13, 41]. These include enhanced glucose uptake and glycolysis, improved exercise tolerance, prevention of immobilization‑induced muscle atrophy, and regulation of muscle fiber composition [41]. Gunawan et al. [4] have proposed that MOTS‑c functions as an “exercise mimetic” and mitohormetic hormone.

Hormesis and mitohormesis

Hormesis is now recognized as a fundamental biological principle with broad implications across molecular, cellular, tissue, and organismal levels [42, 43]. It describes a biphasic dose–response relationship in which low doses of a stressor elicit beneficial adaptive responses, whereas high doses are detrimental. Hormetic responses occur in molecules (e.g., ROS, antioxidants), organelles (e.g., mitochondria), cells (e.g., stem cells), tissues, organs, and whole organisms [42, 43].

The most recent definition describes hormesis as a nonlinear adaptive process in which cells respond to increasing doses or durations of a stressor [44]. At low or moderate levels, the stressor activates signaling networks that promote biochemical and physiological adjustments, reaching a peak adaptive response [44]. Beyond a critical threshold, defense mechanisms become saturated or dysregulated, and the response shifts toward toxicity or inhibition [44].

Mitohormesis—hormesis specifically involving mitochondria—refers to the phenomenon whereby mild mitochondrial stress triggers compensatory mitochondrial and cellular responses that enhance resilience and protect against future insults. However, chronic or excessive mitochondrial stress leads to maladaptive responses, including cell death, organ dysfunction, and disease progression [4, 45]. This response is mediated by ROS and other metabolites. A recent study by Kang et al. [46] demonstrated that inducing either low or high degrees of mitochondrial dysfunction—achieved through heterozygous versus homozygous deficiency of CR6-interacting factor 1 (CRIF1), a mitoribosomal protein essential for coordinating the insertion of mitochondrially encoded proteins into electron transport chain complexes—within hypothalamic proopiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons elicits a characteristic hormetic pattern in MOTS-c production. This biphasic response is mediated by ROS, since exogenous antioxidants prevent this effect. Notably, an analogous increase in MOTS-c is observed following moderate-intensity endurance exercise, indicating that physiological stressors of differing origins can converge on similar mitochondrial signaling pathways [46].

Age‑related decline in MOTS‑c

Advancing age is associated with a progressive decline in circulating MOTS‑c levels [47]. Reduced MOTS‑c is increasingly viewed as a biomarker of aging and age-related diseases [47]. Some mitochondrial single nucleotide polymorphisms (mtSNPs) seem to be involved [47].

MOTS‑c in inflammatory respiratory diseases

MOTS‑c in acute respiratory failure (ARF), lung transplantation and remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC)

Before the discovery of MOTS‑c, several experimental studies had already shown that activation of AMPK by exogenous AICAR in mice reduced the severity of acute respiratory failure (ARF) induced by Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [48] or lipoteichoic acid (LTA) [49].

The first direct link between respiratory disease and MOTS‑c was reported in 2020, when Xingqiang and colleagues [50] demonstrated that intraperitoneal administration of MOTS‑c prior to endobronchial LPS instillation in healthy mice markedly attenuated LPS‑induced lung injury. MOTS‑c treatment improved pulmonary inflammation and reduced circulating concentrations of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and cell‑adhesion molecules. These effects were associated with increased AMPK and SIRT1 activity. Collectively, these findings provided the first evidence that MOTS-c may theoretically represent a potential therapeutic strategy for ARF.

In 2022, Ahmad et al. [51] presented new direct evidence that AICAR reduced lung injury and mortality when administered to mice after bromide gas exposure. This protection was mediated through AMPK phosphorylation and upregulation of heme oxygenase‑1.

Subsequent clinical and experimental studies have further strengthened the hypothesis that MOTS‑c exerts beneficial effects in ARF. In 2023, Lu et al. [52] reported that patients undergoing off‑pump coronary artery bypass surgery exhibited higher postoperative MOTS‑c levels compared with preoperative values; however, those who developed postoperative ARF had significantly lower MOTS‑c concentrations than patients without respiratory failure. In the same study, the authors used a rat model of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion, a condition known to induce pulmonary ferroptosis. MOTS‑c administration reduced ferroptosis and activated the NRF2 and PPAR‑γ pathways both in vitro and in vivo. Similar protective effects were observed in a murine model of ARF induced by a bacterial formylated peptide [53]. In 2025, the same research group confirmed that patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass also exhibited reduced MOTS‑c levels when postoperative respiratory distress developed [54].

A comparable pattern has been described in lung transplantation, a clinical context in which acute respiratory complications substantially contribute to morbidity, mortality, and chronic graft dysfunction. Wang et al. [55] observed decreased circulating MOTS‑c levels in patients who developed postoperative ARF compared with those who did not. Moreover, MOTS‑c concentrations were markedly higher in patients who had undergone remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC). RIPC—brief cycles of ischemia and reperfusion applied to a distant tissue such as the arm—induces a systemic protective response and has been shown to mitigate ischemia–reperfusion injury in multiple organs, although the mediators of this protection have remained largely unidentified. Zhao et al. [56] provided compelling experimental evidence that MOTS‑c is one such mediator, acting through the PGC‑1α and NRF2 pathways.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with a blunted circulating MOTS‑c response in acute respiratory distress compared with the response of animals or patients who did not develop ARF across diverse clinical and experimental settings. This growing body of evidence strongly supports the need for clinical trials to evaluate the therapeutic potential of MOTS‑c in human ARF, given its robust efficacy in preclinical models [57].

MOTS‑c in COVID‑19

SARS‑CoV‑2, the coronavirus responsible for severe acute respiratory syndrome type 2, is known to induce mitochondrial dysfunction through excessive production of free radicals generated during the inflammatory response. This pathophysiological mechanism prompted interest in evaluating circulating MOTS‑c levels in affected patients. In 2023, Saracaloglu et al. [58] reported a marked elevation in blood MOTS‑c concentrations in patients with COVID‑19 both at diagnosis and after seven days of treatment with favipiravir. Notably, MOTS‑c levels did not correlate with any clinical or biochemical parameters assessed in the study.

Although no further studies have evaluated MOTS‑c in the context of acute COVID‑19, there has been increasing interest in its role in the post‑acute sequelae of SARS‑CoV‑2 infection, particularly among individuals presenting with neuropsychiatric symptoms. Goetzl et al. [59] observed significant reductions in MOTS‑c levels within total extracellular vesicles derived from a small cohort of patients acutely infected with SARS‑CoV‑2 who subsequently developed neuropsychiatric sequelae.

MOTS‑c in lung cancer and thoracic radiotherapy

Information regarding serum MOTS‑c levels in patients with cancer remains limited. To date, only one preliminary study has evaluated MOTS‑c in individuals with unresectable non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)—specifically, 22 squamous cell carcinomas and 13 adenocarcinomas, stages IIIA/IIIB—treated with paclitaxel‑based chemotherapy and radiotherapy [60]. Baseline MOTS‑c concentrations were elevated in cancer patients compared with healthy controls, and levels increased further following radiotherapy. However, interpretation of these findings is limited by the absence of detailed information on the timing of sample collection within the study protocol.

Additional investigations across diverse cancer types are clearly needed to validate these preliminary observations and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. The activation of MOTS‑c in cancer warrants particular attention, as this peptide stimulates antioxidant and detoxification pathways that could theoretically support the high metabolic demands of proliferating tumor cells and potentially interfere with chemotherapeutic efficacy. Moreover, because radiotherapy‑induced injury to healthy lung tissue can lead to pneumonitis, it became relevant to assess the possible preventive role of MOTS‑c in any type of radiotherapy.

In 2024, Zhang et al. [61] demonstrated that MOTS‑c treatment significantly improved oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, alveolar cell apoptosis, inflammation, and the histological tissue injury caused by radiation in the lung in a murine model. The same protective effects were observed in vitro in MLE‑12 mouse lung epithelial cells. These findings were associated with increased NRF2 expression and enhanced translocation of NRF2 to the nucleus. In NRF2‑knockout mice, this beneficial response was absent. In the same year, other work [62] also showed that the protective effect of pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) against radiation‑induced lung injury was accompanied by increased activity of the MOTS‑c–NRF2 pathway. When NRF2 was inhibited, PQQ had no effect on irradiated lung tissue, indicating that NRF2 plays an essential role in these actions of MOTS‑c.

MOTS-c in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

Before the discovery of MOTS-c extensive evidence demonstrated that the AMPK/SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways were dysfunctional in COPD. In 1995 Lee et al. [63] demonstrated that AMPKα1-deficient mice exhibited increased susceptibility to lung inflammation and emphysema following CS exposure. These pathological changes were mitigated by treatment with AICAR, an AMPK activator, and exacerbated by compound C, an AMPK inhibitor. In 2017, Cheng et al. [64] confirmed these findings in human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS‑2B) and small airway epithelial cells (SAECs) treated with cigarette smoke extract (CSE) in vitro. AICAR reduced, whereas compound C enhanced, inflammatory responses and the expression of genes involved in cellular senescence. Furthermore, Yao et al. [65, 66] reported that SIRT1 expression was reduced in the lungs of cigarette smoke (CS)-exposed mice and in patients with COPD. Pharmacological or genetic activation of SIRT1 attenuated lung inflammation and injury, decreased oxidative stress, and restored the balance between Tissue Inhibitor of Matrix Metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1) and Matrix Metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9). In 2009 Sussan et al. [67] had demonstrated that an activator of NRF2 was able to attenuate cigarette smoke-induced emphysema in mice. Therefore, there was important evidence of inadequate function of the AMPK/SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways in COPD.

In 2022, our group [9] found that serum levels of Humanin (HN)—the first identified mitokine, which exerts cytoprotective effects through pathways distinct from those of MOTS‑c—were elevated in patients with stable COPD compared with age‑ and sex‑matched smokers without COPD. Moreover, higher HN levels predicted the risk of COPD exacerbations over the subsequent two months. In a subsequent study [10], we observed decreased circulating MOTS‑c levels in patients with stable COPD compared with smokers without COPD. Among COPD patients, MOTS‑c levels below the median were associated with greater oxygen desaturation and with walking less than 350 m in the 6‑minute walk test, indicating reduced exercise capacity.

In a third study [11], we measured circulating MOTS‑c and Humanin levels in patients with stable COPD and compared them with those in patients hospitalized for acute exacerbations, whose samples were collected on the day of admission. Patients with exacerbated COPD exhibited lower MOTS‑c levels than those with stable disease, whereas Humanin levels did not differ between groups. In contrast, levels of GDF15 and FGF21—two stress‑responsive molecules encoded in the nucleus but regulated by mitochondrial signals—were elevated during exacerbations. MOTS‑c and GDF15 showed a negative correlation. Collectively, these findings indicate that mitokine responses to COPD exacerbations are heterogeneous yet partially coordinated. As COPD progresses—through exacerbations triggered by smoke, bacteria, fungi, or viruses—and as patients age, physical inactivity further aggravates the condition, contributing to an additional decline in circulating MOTS‑c levels. These observations support the rationale for investigating whether MOTS‑c administration could mitigate the progressive clinical deterioration observed in COPD.

MOTS‑c in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)

In 2021, Baylan et al. [68] reported for the first time that circulating MOTS‑c levels were lower in patients with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) than in healthy controls, with the most pronounced reductions observed in individuals with severe disease. Moreover, the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) was independently associated with body mass index (BMI), the HOMA index, and MOTS‑c concentrations. Subsequently, Luo et al. [69] confirmed these findings in patients with moderate or severe OSA, although no significant differences were detected in those with mild disease. MOTS‑c levels correlated with BMI, AHI, and the oxygen desaturation index, independently of age.

MOTS-c in asthma

In a small cohort of patients with asthma (n = 20), reduced circulating MOTS‑c levels were observed [70], and these reductions were associated with increased markers of oxidative stress. Similarly, low MOTS‑c levels were detected in cultured human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS‑2B) and in mice with allergic asthma induced by exposure to house dust mites [70]. In asthmatic mice, treatment with MOTS‑c improved oxidative stress parameters, reduced pulmonary inflammation at both anatomical and histological levels, attenuated disruption of the bronchial epithelial barrier, and prevented mitochondrial damage in airway epithelial cells. Comparable effects were observed in BEAS‑2B cells, where MOTS‑c enhanced mitochondrial function, reduced apoptosis, and preserved epithelial barrier integrity. Notably, NRF2‑knockout asthmatic mice did not exhibit these improvements following MOTS‑c treatment, further supporting that the protective effects of MOTS‑c are mediated through activation of the NRF2 pathway [70].

MOTS‑c in interstitial lung disease

No published studies to date have specifically examined MOTS‑c levels in interstitial lung disease. However, evidence indicates reduced expression of sirtuins 1 and 3 in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, in those with systemic sclerosis–associated lung fibrosis, and in models of bleomycin‑induced pulmonary fibrosis [71]. Additionally, growth and differentiation factor‑15 (GDF‑15)—a protein upregulated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction—is elevated, whereas PGC‑1α levels are decreased, in cachectic patients with pulmonary fibrosis [72]. The potential antifibrotic effects of MOTS‑c in these conditions remain unexplored and warrant further investigation.

MOTS-c in hyperoxia-induced bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) can also be added to the list of pulmonary disorders in which MOTS‑c appears to play a role. Premature infants frequently require supplemental oxygen to maintain adequate arterial oxygen saturation. However, due to their immature lungs, insufficient pulmonary surfactant, and underdeveloped antioxidant enzyme systems, oxygen therapy leads to excessive production of ROS. These molecular disturbances induce cellular dysfunction, apoptosis, and structural alterations throughout the bronchopulmonary system [73]. In recent years, dysregulated SIRT1 signaling has been shown to contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of BPD [73].

Furthermore, Chen et al. [74] recently reported that MOTS‑c levels were reduced in a mouse model of hyperoxia‑induced BPD. In this model, exogenous MOTS‑c administration markedly inhibited oxidative stress and inflammation both in vivo and in vitro. Most of the protective effects of MOTS‑c were dependent on NRF2 activation, as they were abolished by an NRF2 inhibitor in vitro and were absent in NRF2‑deficient mice. These findings further support the central role of the NRF2 pathway in mediating the cytoprotective actions of MOTS‑c.

Potential utility and limitations of MOTS-c as a biomarker and therapeutic agent in inflammatory pulmonary diseases

At first glance, MOTS‑c appears to be a highly attractive biomarker and potential therapeutic agent for inflammatory pulmonary diseases. However, numerous challenges must be addressed before it can be translated into clinical practice. Although clinical evidence remains preliminary, mechanistic studies in animal models provide highly promising insights.

MOTS‑c is a small peptide for which several commercial and in‑house ELISA assays have been developed. These assays, however, require substantial standardization, as reported “normal” circulating concentrations vary widely across studies. This heterogeneity suggests that the immunoreactive species detected by different kits may not be identical. Advances in mass spectrometry for small‑protein detection may help resolve this issue. Furthermore, the stability of MOTS‑c at room temperature and after freezing remains unknown. Its circulating half‑life, the enzymes responsible for its degradation, and the potential need for sample preservatives have not yet been characterized. The influence of sex, fasting status, diet, sampling time (circadian rhythm), recent physical activity, pulsatility of secretion, concomitant medications, and comorbidities is only partially understood. Consequently, numerous technical and methodological issues must be resolved before MOTS‑c can be reliably used as a biomarker.

Furthermore, we suggest that an alternative explanation for the apparent contradictions in reported MOTS‑c levels of acute vs. chronic pulmonary inflammatory diseases could be interpreted within the framework of the mitohormetic dose–response relationship. This framework helps explain why acute inflammatory respiratory diseases (e.g., ARF, COVID‑19) are associated with elevated MOTS‑c levels, whereas chronic conditions such as COPD or asthma typically show reduced levels. In both acute and chronic settings, exogenous MOTS‑c administration could theoretically alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction through activation of AMPK and NRF2. This conceptual model appears applicable not only to acute inflammatory diseases but also to other chronic disorders characterized by high oxidative stress, such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus [75] or chronic renal failure [76]. Nevertheless, substantially more empirical evidence is required to validate this hypothesis. In this sense, for example, it would be of interest to evaluate the time course of MOTS-c and ROS levels every 6 h during the early postoperative period after cardiopulmonary bypass to establish if this model is correct and its possible utility as a prognostic factor of ARF. Calabrese et al. [77] have demonstrated that activation of NRF2 follows this type of curve using numerous oxidative stressors.

Regarding the possible utility of MOTS-c as a therapeutic agent in these diseases we need to study the pharmacokinetics of this mitokine, to evaluate optimal delivery strategies (intravenous, intramuscular, oral, bronchial instillation, among others), developing long-term or orally active analogs, and to conduct well-designed controlled clinical trials, especially in ARF, COPD, Asthma, OSA and pulmonary fibrosis, considering possible contraindications (other medications or diseases) and synergies with other mitokines.

Conclusions

MOTS‑c has rapidly evolved from a metabolic regulator to a multifunctional microprotein with profound implications for pulmonary biology. Its roles in oxidative and toxic stress, inflammation, autophagy, programmed cellular death, mitochondrial homeostasis, immune modulation and antimicrobial effects position it as a promising biomarker and therapeutic candidate across a spectrum of respiratory diseases.
