Frequently hypercyclic composition operators on the little Lipschitz space of a rooted tree


Abstract
We characterize the strictly increasing symbols φ : ℕ 0 ⟶ ℕ 0 \varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0} whose composition operators C φ C_{\varphi} satisfy the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion on the little Lipschitz space ℒ 0 ​ ( ℕ 0 ) \mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0}) . With this result we continue the recent research about this kind of spaces and operators, but our approach relies on establishing a natural isomorphism between the Lipschitz-type spaces over rooted trees and the classical spaces ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} and c 0 c_{0} . Such isomorphism provides an alternative framework that simplifies and allows to improve many previous results about these spaces and the operators defined there.

Introduction

Within the domains of Functional Analysis and Operator Theory, the field of Linear Dynamics has established itself over the past five decades as a highly active and influential area of research, with a primary focus on the notion of
hypercyclicity
(i.e. existence of a dense orbit). The first examples of hypercyclic operators acting on Banach spaces were the
Rolewicz operators

[
33
]
. These are the multiples
\lambda B
for any
|\lambda|>1
of the backward shift
B:X\longrightarrow X
. We recall that
B
acts on the classical sequence spaces
X=\ell^{p}(I)
or
c_{0}(I)
, indexed by the set of positive integers
I=\mathbb{N}
or equivalently by the set of non-negative integers
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, as follows: for each
x=(x_{j})_{j\in I}\in X
one has

x=(x_{j})_{j\in I}\longmapsto B(x):=(x_{j+1})_{j\in I}.

Since then, the more general
weighted shifts
on sequence spaces indexed by
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{N}_{0}
have become one of the main sources of examples in Linear Dynamics (see
[
25
, Chapter 4]
and the references therein).

These classical weighted shifts were later generalized into the so-called
weighted shifts on trees
, for which the underlying spaces are again sequence Banach (or even Fréchet) spaces but this time indexed by what is called a
directed tree

T
(see Section
2
for a precise definition). In fact, and among others, references such as
[
1
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
30
,
31
]
have focused on this topic by letting the underlying spaces to be the respective
\ell^{p}(T)
or
c_{0}(T)
, being these natural generalizations of
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.

Recently, two apparently new sequence Banach spaces indexed by a tree
T
have appeared in the literature: the
Lipschitz space

\mathcal{L}(T)
and the
little Lipschitz space

\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
. These Banach spaces were introduced in the 2010 paper
[
16
]
and, since then, many works have been published studying how shift, multiplication and composition operators behave there (see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
32
]
). The main objective of this paper is to provide examples of frequently hypercyclic composition operators on the little Lipschitz space of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, addressing the following open question left in
[
19
, Subsection 6.1]
:

Question 1.1
.

Are there some both sufficient and necessary conditions for the hypercyclicity of a composition operator
C_{\varphi}
acting on a little Lipschitz space
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
of a rooted tree
T
?

In this paper we solve Question
1.1
for the particular case of composition operators whose symbols are strictly increasing self-maps of the set of non-negative integers. Our main result shows that these operators are hypercyclic if and only if they satisfy the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notation used and we establish an isometric isomorphism between the Lipschitz-type spaces
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, of each rooted tree
T
, and the spaces
\ell^{\infty}(T)
and
c_{0}(T)
. This isomorphism allows us to compute the norms of the composition, multiplication and shift operators on
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, improving all the norm estimates given in the literature for these operators (see Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
). In Section
3
we again use the established isomorphism, together with the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion, in order to characterize those strictly increasing symbols
\varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
for which the composition operators
C_{\varphi}
are hypercyclic on
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. We conclude the paper in Section
4
, where we briefly discuss further applications of the isomorphism established above and formulate several open problems.

2
Notation, a useful isomorphism, and some consequences

Following
[
19
]
, a
directed tree
(or just a
tree
) will be a locally finite, connected, and simply-connected graph
T
that as a set we identify with the collection of its vertices. We will say that two distinct vertices
v\neq w
are
adjacent
, and we will denote it by
v\sim w
, if
v
and
w
are connected by an edge of the graph
T
. The
degree
of a vertex
v\in T
is the number of vertices in
T
that are adjacent to
v
, and a
path
between two vertices
v\neq w
will be a finite sequence of distinct vertices
[v,v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{k-1},w]
in the tree
T
and fulfilling that
v\sim v_{1}\sim v_{2}\sim\cdots\sim v_{k-1}\sim w
(note that in a connected and simply-connected graph there always exists a unique path between two distinct vertices).

Also as in
[
19
]
, every tree
T
considered in this paper will be a
rooted tree
, i.e. there will be a distinguished vertex
\mathfrak{o}\in T
that will be called the
root
of
T
. Given two vertices
v\neq w
in a rooted tree
T
we will say that
v
is a
descendant
of
w
, or that
w
is an
ancestor
of
v
, if the vertex
w
lies in the unique path between
\mathfrak{o}
and
v
. We will write
T^{*}:=T\setminus\{\mathfrak{o}\}
and given any vertex
v\in T^{*}
we will denote by
v^{-}
the unique vertex in
T
that is “an ancestor of” and “adjacent to”
v
. In this case
v^{-}
is called the
parent
of
v
, and
v
is said to be a
child
of
v^{-}
. In addition, given any vertex
v\in T
we will denote by
\text{Chi}(v)
the set formed by all the children of
v
(note that
v\in T
could be the root
\mathfrak{o}
this time).

Given
v,w\in T
we will denote by
\operatorname{dist}(v,w)
the number of edges in the unique path between
v
and
w
, calling it the
distance
between
v
and
w
, and setting
\operatorname{dist}(v,w)=0
whenever
v=w
. Moreover, for each
v\in T
we will denote by
|v|:=\operatorname{dist}(\mathfrak{o},v)
the distance between the root
\mathfrak{o}
and the vertex
v
. All the trees considered in this paper will be rooted trees for which only the root
\mathfrak{o}
may have degree equal to one (so that all these trees will be infinite). Finally, also as in
[
19
]
, for each
v\in T
we will denote by
S_{v}
the
sector of
v
, which is the set consisting of
v
and all its descendants (note that
S_{\mathfrak{o}}=T
).

A
function

f
on a tree
T
will be a complex-valued function defined on the set of its vertices. The, the
Lipschitz
and the
little Lipschitz spaces
of a rooted tree
T
are defined as

\mathcal{L}(T):=\left\{f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\ ;\ \sup_{v\in T^{*}}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|<\infty\right\},

\mathcal{L}_{0}(T):=\left\{f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\ ;\ \lim_{|v|\to\infty}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|=0\right\}.

It was proved in
[
16
]
that
\mathcal{L}(T)
is a Banach space with the norm

\|f\|:=|f(\mathfrak{o})|+\sup_{v\in T^{*}}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|\quad\text{ for each }f\in\mathcal{L}(T).

(1)

It was also proved in
[
16
]
that
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
is always a closed and separable subspace of
\mathcal{L}(T)
. However, following
[
19
,
32
]
, we will use in both spaces
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
the equivalent norm

\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}}:=\max\left\{|f(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{v\in T^{*}}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|\right\}\quad\text{ for each }f\in\mathcal{L}(T).

(2)

The norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
allows us to find an interesting
isometric isomorphism
, which will be our main tool in this paper (see Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
and Section
3
):

Remark 2.1

(
A useful isomorphism
)
.

Given a rooted tree
T
, and following
[
23
,
24
,
26
,
30
]
, consider the well-known sequence spaces

\ell^{\infty}(T):=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\mathbb{C}^{T}\ ;\ \sup_{v\in T}|x_{v}|<\infty\right\}

and

c_{0}(T):=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\mathbb{C}^{T}\ ;\ \lim_{|v|\to\infty}|x_{v}|=0\right\},

both of them endowed with the
supremum norm

\|x\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{v\in T}|x_{v}|\quad\text{ for each }x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\ell^{\infty}(T).

The spaces
(\ell^{\infty}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and
(c_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
are sequence Banach spaces, and it is not hard to check that the map
D:(\mathcal{L}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})\longrightarrow(\ell^{\infty}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, defined for each
f\in\mathcal{L}(T)
as

f\longmapsto D(f)=([D(f)]_{v})_{v\in V}\in\ell^{\infty}(T)\quad\text{ with }\quad[D(f)]_{v}:=\begin{cases}f(\mathfrak{o}),&\text{ if }v=\mathfrak{o},\\[5.0pt]
f(v)-f(v^{-}),&\text{ if }v\in T^{*},\end{cases}

is indeed an isometric isomorphism. In fact, the inverse operator
D^{-1}:(\ell^{\infty}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{L}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})
can be easily computed and expressed for each
x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\ell^{\infty}(T)
as

x\longmapsto D^{-1}(x)\in\mathcal{L}(T)\quad\text{ with }\quad[D^{-1}(x)](v)=\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]}x_{w}\right)\quad\text{ for every }v\in T,

where
[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]
is the unique path in
T
between the root
\mathfrak{o}
and the vertex
v
. Since we will focus on the little Lipschitz space we will use the restriction
D:(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})\longrightarrow(c_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, which by abuse of notation will still be denoted by
D
. Note that
D
is also an isometric isomorphism between
(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})
and
(c_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, and that the expression of the respective inverse map
D^{-1}:(c_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})
coincides with that of
D^{-1}:(\ell^{\infty}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{L}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}})
.

Considering the mathematical objects introduced in Remark
2.1
, let us establish some further notation that will help us to clarify how
D
and
D^{-1}
act on
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
:

–

For each
w\in T
we will denote by
e^{(w)}:=(\delta_{w,v})_{v\in T}\in c_{0}(T)
the
w
-th element of the canonical Schauder basis of
c_{0}(T)
, i.e.
\delta_{w,v}=1
when
w=v
and
\delta_{w,v}=0
otherwise. We thus have that

D(\chi_{S_{w}})=e^{(w)}\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad D^{-1}(e^{(w)})=\chi_{S_{w}},

where
S_{w}
is the sector of the vertex
w
, and
\chi_{S_{w}}:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the corresponding indicator function.

–

For each
w\in T
we will use the notation
\partial^{(w)}:=e^{(w)}-\sum_{u\in\text{Chi}(w)}e^{(u)}\in c_{0}(T)
. We thus have that

D(\chi_{\{w\}})=\partial^{(w)}\quad\text{ or equivalently }\quad D^{-1}(\partial^{(w)})=\chi_{\{w\}},

where
\chi_{\{w\}}:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the corresponding indicator function.

Moreover, the following linear subspaces of
c_{0}(T)
, defined as

c_{00}(T):=\operatorname{span}\{e^{(w)}\ ;\ w\in T\}

and

\Sigma_{00}(T):=\operatorname{span}\{\partial^{(w)}\ ;\ w\in T\},

will play a fundamental role in our main result (see Theorem
3.1
below). Actually, if for each vector
x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\ell^{\infty}(T)
we denote its
support
by
\operatorname{supp}(x):=\{v\in T\ ;\ x_{v}\neq 0\}
and we define

|\operatorname{supp}(x)|:=\begin{cases}\sup\left\{|v|\ ;\ v\in\operatorname{supp}(x)\right\},&\text{ if }\operatorname{supp}(x)\neq\emptyset,\\[5.0pt]
0,&\text{ if }\operatorname{supp}(x)=\emptyset,\end{cases}

then it is not hard to check the equalities

c_{00}(T)=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in c_{0}(T)\ ;\ |\operatorname{supp}(x)|<\infty\right\}

and

\Sigma_{00}(T)=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in c_{00}(T)\ ;\ \textstyle\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]}x_{w}=0\ \text{ whenever }|v|=|\operatorname{supp}(x)|\right\},

where
[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]
is again the unique path in
T
between the root
\mathfrak{o}
and the vertex
v
. These last alternative expressions for
c_{00}(T)
and
\Sigma_{00}(T)
allow us to easily check that they both are dense linear subspaces of
c_{0}(T)
. As a consequence, having in mind the isomorphism established in Remark
2.1
together with the direct computations
D^{-1}(e^{(w)})=\chi_{S_{w}}
and
D^{-1}(\partial^{(w)})=\chi_{\{w\}}
for each
w\in T
, one can deduce the results
[
16
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
19
, Lemma 2.3]
in a direct way.

Other results from the previous works on these Lipschitz spaces
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
32
]
can also be directly obtained via the isometric isomorphism established in Remark
2.1
. In fact, the findings about their Banach-space structure, such as
[
18
, Proposition 3.1]
where it is proved that
\mathcal{L}(T)
is not separable, or
[
18
, Proposition 4.2]
where it is proved that the dual of
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and the pre-dual of
\mathcal{L}(T)
is an
\ell^{1}
-type space, are now obvious. Also, the results about certain inequalities satisfied by every function
f\in\mathcal{L}(T)
, such as
[
16
, Lemma 3.4]
or
[
32
, Lemma 2.2]
, are indeed direct if we note that
f(v)
is a sum of
|v|+1
coordinates of a vector in
\ell^{\infty}(T)
, namely
D(f)\in\ell^{\infty}(T)
. In the aforementioned works, the map
D
was introduced as a tool to compute the
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
-norm of elements in
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, but none of these references addressed the natural isometric isomorphism, nor the explicit form of the inverse operator
D^{-1}
as presented in Remark
2.1
. Using both
D
and
D^{-1}
we can obtain alternative expressions for the operators defined in
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
. See Section
4
for more comments.

2.1
Composition operators

Given a rooted tree
T
and a map
\varphi:T\longrightarrow T
, if we denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of all functions
f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we have that
C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}
defined as
C_{\varphi}(f):=f\circ\varphi
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a linear map. This map
C_{\varphi}
is called the
composition operator
with symbol
\varphi
. By the Closed Graph Theorem
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}(T)
if and only if
C_{\varphi}(\mathcal{L}(T))\subset\mathcal{L}(T)
. By
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
this holds when
\varphi
is
Lipschitz
, i.e. when

L_{\varphi}:=\sup_{v\neq w\in T}\frac{\operatorname{dist}(\varphi(v),\varphi(w))}{\operatorname{dist}(v,w)}<\infty,

where
L_{\varphi}
is said to be the
Lipschitz constant
of
\varphi
. The Closed Graph Theorem shows again that
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
if and only if
C_{\varphi}(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T))\subset\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T))
. However, the fact that
\varphi
is Lipschitz is not sufficient to ensure this last inclusion (though the Lipschitz condition is necessary) and a complete characterization of when
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
was established in
[
19
, Section 3]
. When
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded operator, the following observation is a direct consequence of Remark
2.1
:

Remark 2.2

(
A useful conjugacy
)
.

Given a rooted tree
T
and a map
\varphi:T\longrightarrow T
such that
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded operator in
\mathcal{L}(T)
, then one can find a natural
\ell^{\infty}(T)
-operator that is
topologically conjugate
to
C_{\varphi}
(see
[
25
, Section 1.1]
for the precise definition of “conjugacy”). In fact, letting

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x):=(D\circ C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)\quad\text{ for each }x\in\ell^{\infty}(T),

where the map
D
is the isomorphism established in Remark
2.1
, we have that the diagram

\begin{CD}\mathcal{L}(T)@>{C_{\varphi}}>{}>\mathcal{L}(T)\\
@V{D}V{}V@V{}V{D}V\\
\ell^{\infty}(T)@>{\widehat{C_{\varphi}}}>{}>\ell^{\infty}(T)\end{CD}

is commutative, so that
C_{\varphi}
and
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}
are conjugate via the isometric isomorphism
D
. Note that the same still holds if we replace
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\ell^{\infty}(T)
with the respective spaces
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and
c_{0}(T)
, when
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
. Thus, these operators share all the dynamical properties that are preserved under conjugacy such as hypercyclicity, density of periodic points and Devaney chaos among others.

Using Remarks
2.1
and
2.2
we can improve
[
19
, Theorem 3.7]
:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\varphi
be a self-map of a rooted tree
T
. If the composition operator
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in the space
\mathcal{L}(T)
, then its operator norm fulfills the equality
\|C_{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\max\{1+|\varphi(\mathfrak{o})|,L_{\varphi}\}
, where
L_{\varphi}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
. Moreover, if
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in the space
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, then the same equality holds for its respective norm as an operator on
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
.

Proof.

By
[
19
, Theorem 3.7]
we already have the following inequalities

1+|\varphi(\mathfrak{o})|\leq\|C_{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq\max\{1+|\varphi(\mathfrak{o})|,L_{\varphi}\},

so it is enough to check that
\|C_{\varphi}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\geq L_{\varphi}
when
L_{\varphi}\geq 1
. We will prove that
\|\widehat{C_{\varphi}}\|_{\infty}\geq L_{\varphi}
, where
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}
is the respective operator obtained from Remark
2.2
. This would complete the proof because both operators are topologically conjugated via the isometric isomorphism
D
from Remark
2.1
.

By
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
we can find a vertex
u\in T
fulfilling that
\operatorname{dist}(\varphi(u),\varphi(u^{-}))=L_{\varphi}\geq 1
. Thus, let
[\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,\varphi(u)]
and
[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,\varphi(u^{-})]
be the unique paths in
T
between the root
\mathfrak{o}
and each of the vertices
\varphi(u)
and
\varphi(u^{-})
, and consider the subsets
U,V\subset T
with
U:=\{\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,\varphi(u)\}
and
V:=\{\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,\varphi(u^{-})\}
. Consider
x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\mathbb{C}^{T}
defined as

x_{v}:=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1,&\text{ if }v\in U\setminus V,\\
-1,&\text{ if }v\in V\setminus U,\\
\phantom{-}0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is clear that
x\in c_{00}(T)\subset c_{0}(T)\subset\ell^{\infty}(T)
, and that
\|x\|_{\infty}=1
because both
U\setminus V
and
V\setminus U
can not be empty at the same time (recall that
\varphi(u)\neq\varphi(u^{-})
). We claim that
\|\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x)\|_{\infty}\geq L_{\varphi}
, which would complete the proof. In fact, from Remarks
2.1
and
2.2
we have that

[(C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)](u)=\sum_{v\in[\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,\varphi(u)]}x_{v}\quad\text{ and }\quad[(C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)](u^{-})=\sum_{v\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,\varphi(u^{-})]}x_{v},

and, since
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x):=(D\circ C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)
, we have that

[\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x)]_{u}=\left(\sum_{v\in[\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,\varphi(u)]}x_{v}\right)-\left(\sum_{v\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,\varphi(u^{-})]}x_{v}\right)=\left(\sum_{v\in U\setminus V}1\right)-\left(\sum_{v\in V\setminus U}-1\right).

Since the unique path between
\varphi(u)
and
\varphi(u^{-})
is formed by the vertices in
(U\setminus V)\cup(V\setminus U)
and a common ancestor of
\varphi(u)
and
\varphi(u^{-})
, it is not hard to check the cardinal equality

\text{card}(U\setminus V)+\text{card}(V\setminus U)=\operatorname{dist}(\varphi(u),\varphi(u^{-})).

Hence,
[\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x)]_{u}=\text{card}(U\setminus V)+\text{card}(V\setminus U)=\operatorname{dist}(\varphi(u),\varphi(u^{-}))=L_{\varphi}
, and the result follows.
∎

The next example shows an operator from the class of operators considered in our main result:

Example 2.4
.

Let
T
be indexed by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
0\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as its root and with
k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
being adjacent if and only if
|k-l|=1
. Consider
\varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
defined as
\varphi(j):=j+1
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and note that the respective composition operator
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
by
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
. Given any
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, using the map
D
from Remark
2.1
for this particular case we have that

[D^{-1}(x)](j)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}x_{k}\quad\text{ and hence }\quad[(C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)](j)=\sum_{k=0}^{j+1}x_{k}\quad\text{ for each }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Finally, from the conjugacy established in Remark
2.2
but for this particular case, we have that

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x):=(D\circ C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)=(x_{0}+x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},...).

Hence
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}=P_{0}+B
, where
P_{0}
is the projection on the
0
-coordinate and where
B
is the classical backward shift in
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
as denoted at the Introduction for the Rolewicz operators. This operator admits a representation as an infinite-matrix
A_{\varphi}=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
when acting on
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
as

A_{\varphi}=\begin{matrix}\text{\tiny$i=0$}\\
\text{\tiny$i=1$}\\
\text{\tiny$i=2$}\\
\vdots\\
\text{\tiny$i$}\\

with two consecutive

j=i+1

2.2

,

,

,

,

. Following the spirit of Corollary

, with our “isomorphic approach” we can compute the precise norm of the mentioned operators. In particular, we improve

Remark 2.5

(
Multiplication operators
)
.

As in
[
16
]
, given a rooted tree
T
and a complex-valued map
\psi:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, if we again denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of all complex-valued functions
f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
then we can consider the linear map
M_{\psi}:\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}
defined as
M_{\psi}(f):=\psi\cdot f
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Such a map
M_{\psi}
will be called the
multiplication operator
with symbol
\psi
.

By
[
16
, Theorem 3.6]
we know that
M_{\psi}
is well-defined and a bounded operator in
\mathcal{L}(T)
, if and only if
M_{\psi}
is well-defined and bounded operator in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, if and only if

\sup_{v\in T}|\psi(v)|<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left(|v|\cdot|\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})|\right)<\infty.

As in Remark
2.2
, if
M_{\psi}
is a bounded operator in
\mathcal{L}(T)
, then one can find a natural
\ell^{\infty}(T)
-operator that is
topologically conjugate
to
M_{\psi}
by letting

\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x):=(D\circ M_{\psi}\circ D^{-1})(x)\quad\text{ for each }x\in\ell^{\infty}(T),

where the map
D
is again the isomorphism established in Remark
2.1
. We thus have that the diagram

\begin{CD}\mathcal{L}(T)@>{M_{\psi}}>{}>\mathcal{L}(T)\\
@V{D}V{}V@V{}V{D}V\\
\ell^{\infty}(T)@>{\widehat{M_{\psi}}}>{}>\ell^{\infty}(T)\end{CD}

is commutative, so that
M_{\psi}
and
\widehat{M_{\psi}}
are conjugate via the isometric isomorphism
D
. The same still holds if we replace
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\ell^{\infty}(T)
with the respective spaces
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and
c_{0}(T)
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\psi:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a complex-valued map on a rooted tree
T
. If the multiplication operator
M_{\psi}
is bounded in the space
\mathcal{L}(T)
, or equivalently in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, then its operator norm is

\|M_{\psi}\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\max\left\{|\psi(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left(|\psi(v)|+|v|\cdot|\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})|\right)\right\}.

Proof.

As in Corollary
2.3
we will directly compute the norm of
\|\widehat{M_{\psi}}\|_{\infty}
, which coincides with
\|M_{\psi}\|_{\mathcal{L}}
by the isometry condition of
D
. Firstly, note that given any
x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\ell^{\infty}(T)
we have

[(M_{\psi}\circ D^{-1})(x)](v)=\psi(v)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]}x_{w}\right)\quad\text{ for every }v\in T.

Thus, since
\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x):=(D\circ M_{\psi}\circ D^{-1})(x)
, we have that
[\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x)]_{\mathfrak{o}}=\psi(\mathfrak{o})\cdot x_{\mathfrak{o}}
, and that

\displaystyle[\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x)]_{v}

\displaystyle=\psi(v)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]}x_{w}\right)-\psi(v^{-})\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v^{-}]}x_{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi(v)\cdot x_{v}+\left(\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v^{-}]}x_{w}\right),

(3)

for every
v\in T^{*}
. Taking modulus on the previous expressions and using the triangle inequality in a standard way one can easily show, when
\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, that

\left|[\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x)]_{v}\right|\leq\max\left\{|\psi(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left(|\psi(v)|+|v|\cdot|\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})|\right)\right\}\quad\text{ for all }v\in T.

Thus,

\|\widehat{M_{\psi}}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\|\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x)\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{|\psi(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left(|\psi(v)|+|v|\cdot|\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})|\right)\right\}.

Conversely, if for each
u\in T^{*}
we have that
[\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,u]
is the unique path from
\mathfrak{o}
to
u
, then we can construct a vector
x^{(u)}\in c_{0}(T)\subset\ell^{\infty}(T)
as follows:

–

let
x^{(u)}_{\mathfrak{o}}=x^{(u)}_{u_{1}}=x^{(u)}_{u_{2}}=...=x^{(u)}_{u^{-}}=\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|=1
and
\lambda\cdot\left(\psi(u)-\psi(u^{-})\right)=\left|\psi(u)-\psi(u^{-})\right|
;

–

pick
x^{(u)}_{u}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|x^{(u)}_{u}|=1
and such that
\psi(u)\cdot x^{(u)}_{u}=|\psi(u)|
;

–

and let
x^{(u)}_{w}=0
for all
w\in T\setminus\{\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,u\}
.

Then we have that
\|x^{(u)}\|_{\infty}=1
, but also that
\left|[\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x^{(u)})]_{\mathfrak{o}}\right|=|\psi(\mathfrak{o})|
, and by (
3
) that

[\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x^{(u)})]_{u}=\psi(u)\cdot x^{(u)}_{u}+\left(\psi(u)-\psi(u^{-})\right)\cdot|u|\cdot\lambda=|\psi(u)|+|u|\cdot|\psi(u)-\psi(u^{-})|,

for each
u\in T^{*}
. Thus,

\|\widehat{M_{\psi}}\|_{\infty}\geq\sup_{u\in T^{*}}\|\widehat{M_{\psi}}(x^{(u)})\|_{\infty}\geq\max\left\{|\psi(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{u\in T^{*}}\left(|\psi(u)|+|u|\cdot|\psi(u)-\psi(u^{-})|\right)\right\},

finishing the proof.
∎

In
[
16
]
, the equivalent norm
\|\cdot\|
defined in (
1
) was used instead than
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
defined in (
2
). The reader should be careful since in
[
16
]
the symbol “
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
” is used to denote the norm
\|\cdot\|
. However, also when one uses
\|\cdot\|
, similar computations to those employed in Corollary
2.6
lead to

\|M_{\psi}\|=|\psi(0)|+\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left(|\psi(v)|+|v|\cdot|\psi(v)-\psi(v^{-})|\right)

for the norm of
M_{\psi}
as an operator from
(\mathcal{L}(T),\|\cdot\|)
to itself, or from
(\mathcal{L}_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|)
to itself. This last equality is our real improvement of
[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
. The “isomorphic approach” developed here may be used to compute the exact norm of the multiplication operators considered in
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
17
]
.

Remark 2.7

(
The backward shift
)
.

As in
[
32
]
, given a rooted tree
T
, if we again denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of all functions
f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we can consider the linear map
B:\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}
defined for
f\in\mathcal{F}
as

[B(f)](v):=\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(v)}f(w)\quad\text{ for each }v\in T.

This map is called the
backward shift
on
\mathcal{F}
. The reader must distinguish this map from the original backward shift mentioned at the Introduction of this paper (these operators act in different spaces).

Following
[
32
]
, given a vertex
v\in T
we will denote by
\gamma(v)
the cardinality of the set
\text{Chi}(v)
of children of
v
. Moreover, a rooted tree
T
is said to be
homogeneous by sectors
(
at level
N
) if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that for all
v\in T
with
|v|=N
we have that
\gamma(v)=\gamma(w)
for every
w\in S_{v}
. In this case, by
[
32
, Theorem 4.10]
, we know that
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
is well-defined and a bounded operator from
\mathcal{L}(T)
to itself;

(ii)

B
is well-defined and a bounded operator from
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
to itself;

(iii)

the tree
T
is homogeneous by sectors;

(iv)

we have that
\sup\left\{|v|\cdot|\gamma(v)-\gamma(v^{-})|\ ;\ v\in T^{*}\right\}<\infty
.

In addition, when these conditions hold one has that
\|B\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq\max\left\{2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}),\Lambda(T)\right\}
, where

\Lambda(T):=\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\left\{\gamma(v)+\gamma(v^{-})-1+\left|\gamma(v)-1\right|+|v|\cdot|\gamma(v)-\gamma(v^{-})|\right\}.

As in Remarks
2.2
and
2.5
, when
B
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}(T)
, then one can find a natural
\ell^{\infty}(T)
-operator that is
topologically conjugate
to
B
by letting

\widehat{B}(x):=(D\circ B\circ D^{-1})(x)\quad\text{ for each }x\in\ell^{\infty}(T).

Here, the map
D
is again the isomorphism established in Remark
2.1
. We thus have, one more time, that the diagram

\begin{CD}\mathcal{L}(T)@>{B}>{}>\mathcal{L}(T)\\
@V{D}V{}V@V{}V{D}V\\
\ell^{\infty}(T)@>{\widehat{B}}>{}>\ell^{\infty}(T)\end{CD}

is commutative, so that
B
and
\widehat{B}
are conjugate via the isometric isomorphism
D
. The same still holds if we replace
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\ell^{\infty}(T)
with the respective spaces
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and
c_{0}(T)
. In particular, given any vector
x=(x_{v})_{v\in T}\in\ell^{\infty}(T)
we have for each
v\in T
that

[(B\circ D^{-1})(x)](v)=\sum_{u\in\text{Chi}(v)}\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v,u]}x_{w}\right)=\gamma(v)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},v_{2},...,v]}x_{w}\right)+\sum_{u\in\text{Chi}(v)}x_{u}.

Thus, since
\widehat{B}(x):=(D\circ B\circ D^{-1})(x)
, we have that

[\widehat{B}(x)]_{\mathfrak{o}}=\gamma(\mathfrak{o})\cdot x_{\mathfrak{o}}+\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(\mathfrak{o})}x_{w},

(4)

and that

\displaystyle[\widehat{B}(x)]_{v}

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma(v)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},...,v]}x_{w}\right)+\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(v)}x_{w}\right)-\left(\gamma(v^{-})\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},...,v^{-}]}x_{w}\right)+\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(v^{-})}x_{w}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(v)}x_{w}\ \ -\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(v^{-})\setminus\{v\}}x_{w}+(\gamma(v)-1)x_{v}+\left(\gamma(v)-\gamma(v^{-})\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{w\in[\mathfrak{o},v_{1},...,v^{-}]}x_{w}\right),

(5)

for each
v\in T^{*}
.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree. If the backward shift operator
B
is bounded in the space
\mathcal{L}(T)
, or equivalently in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
, then its operator norm in these spaces is
\|B\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\max\left\{2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}),\Lambda(T)\right\}
.

Proof.

As in Corollaries
2.3
and
2.6
we will directly compute the norm of
\|\widehat{B}\|_{\infty}
, which coincides with that of
\|B\|_{\mathcal{L}}
by the isometry condition of
D
. By
[
32
, Theorem 4.10]
, we already know that

\|\widehat{B}\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}),\Lambda(T)\right\}.

For the converse we start considering the vector
x^{(\mathfrak{o})}\in c_{0}(T)\subset\ell^{\infty}(T)
defined as
x^{(\mathfrak{o})}_{w}=1
when
w\in\{\mathfrak{o}\}\cup\text{Chi}(\mathfrak{o})
and
x^{(\mathfrak{o})}_{w}=0
otherwise. It follows that
\|x^{(\mathfrak{o})}\|_{\infty}=1
, and from (
4
) we have that

[\widehat{B}(x^{(\mathfrak{o})})]_{\mathfrak{o}}=\gamma(\mathfrak{o})+\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(\mathfrak{o})}1=2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}).

Moreover, if for each
u\in T^{*}
we have that
[\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,u]
is the unique path from
\mathfrak{o}
to
u
then we can construct a vector
x^{(u)}\in c_{0}(T)\subset\ell^{\infty}(T)
as follows:

–

let
x^{(u)}_{\mathfrak{o}}=x^{(u)}_{u_{1}}=x^{(u)}_{u_{2}}=...=x^{(u)}_{u^{-}}=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&\text{ if }\gamma(u)-\gamma(u^{-})\geq 0,\\[1.0pt]
-1&\text{ if }\gamma(u)-\gamma(u^{-})<0;\end{cases}

–

let
x^{(u)}_{u}=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1&\text{ if }\gamma(u)-1\geq 0,\\[1.0pt]
-1&\text{ if }\gamma(u)-1<0;\end{cases}

–

let
x^{(u)}_{w}=-1
for all
w\in\text{Chi}(u^{-})\setminus\{u\}
;

–

let
x^{(u)}_{w}=1
for all
w\in\text{Chi}(u)
;

–

and let
x^{(u)}_{w}=0
for all
w\in T\setminus\left(\{\mathfrak{o},u_{1},u_{2},...,u\}\cup\text{Chi}(u^{-})\cup\text{Chi}(u)\right)
.

It follows that
\|x^{(u)}\|_{\infty}=1
and, by (
5
), that

\displaystyle[\widehat{B}(x^{(u)})]_{u}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(u)}1\right)-\left(\sum_{w\in\text{Chi}(u^{-})\setminus\{u\}}-1\right)+|\gamma(u)-1|+|\gamma(u)-\gamma(u^{-})|\cdot|u|

\displaystyle=\gamma(u)+\gamma(u^{-})-1+\left|\gamma(u)-1\right|+|u|\cdot|\gamma(u)-\gamma(u^{-})|,

for each
u\in T^{*}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{B}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{u\in T}\|\widehat{B}(x^{(u)})\|_{\infty}=\max\left\{\|\widehat{B}(x^{(\mathfrak{o})})\|_{\infty},\sup_{u\in T^{*}}\|\widehat{B}(x^{(u)})\|_{\infty}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}),\sup_{u\in T^{*}}\left\{\gamma(u)+\gamma(u^{-})-1+\left|\gamma(u)-1\right|+|u|\cdot|\gamma(u)-\gamma(u^{-})|\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{2\gamma(\mathfrak{o}),\Lambda(T)\right\}.\qed

In
[
32
]
the underlying tree
T
was allowed to have
leaves
(i.e. vertices with no children). However, the proof of Corollary
2.8
remains valid for trees with leaves since the inequality “
\gamma(u)-1<0
” has been considered. Moreover, Corollary
2.8
resolves all the questions raised in
[
32
, Section 4]
concerning the sharpness of the estimates for the norm
\|B\|_{\mathcal{L}}
obtained there. For comparison, see
[
34
]
, where this result is proved by a considerably more involved argument than the one presented here.

3
Frequently hypercyclic composition operators on
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)

In this section we address Question
1.1
for the particular case of composition operators whose symbols are strictly increasing self-maps of the set of non-negative integers (see Theorem
3.1
below). Recall first that given a bounded linear operator
A:X\longrightarrow X
on a separable Banach space
X
we say that:

–

the operator
A
is
hypercyclic
if there exists
x\in X
for which the set
\{A^{n}(x)\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
X
;

–

the operator
A

satisfies the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion
if there exist a dense subset
X_{0}\subset X
and a (not necessarily linear nor continuous) map
B:X_{0}\longrightarrow X_{0}
such that, for any
x\in X_{0}
,

(I)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{n}(x)
converges unconditionally;

(II)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B^{n}(x)
converges unconditionally;

(III)

AB(x)=x
, i.e.
B
is a right-inverse for
A
.

All the hypercyclic operators that we are going to consider in this paper do satisfy this version of the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion, which is one of the strongest properties (dynamically speaking) that a linear operator may present. In fact, let us recall that satisfying the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion implies being
frequently hypercyclic
(and hence hypercyclic), and that with the form presented here it also implies being
Devaney chaotic
and
topologically mixing
(see
[
25
, Proposition 9.11]
), having the so-called
operator specification property
(see
[
7
]
), but also admitting
ergodic and invariant measures with full support
(see
[
2
]
but also
[
20
,
28
]
), among many other strong dynamical properties.

We are not going to give the precise definitions of the dynamical notions mentioned above, and we refer the reader to the textbooks
[
9
,
25
]
for more on Linear Dynamics. Actually, with the previous paragraph we only wanted to remark that the operators we are about to consider present a much more interesting dynamical behaviour than just hypercyclicity. Recall, though, that the main result of this section came originally motivated by Question
1.1
, where only hypercyclicity was considered:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
be a strictly increasing map with
\varphi(0)>0
and assume that the composition operator
C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
is bounded, where
T
is the rooted tree indexed by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
0\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as its root and with two vertex
k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
being adjacent whenever
|k-l|=1
. Hence, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

C_{\varphi}
satisfies the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion;

(ii)

C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic;

(iii)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j-1))=\infty
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
;

(iv)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j-1))=\infty
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
;

(v)

for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
there exists some
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n_{j}}(j),\varphi^{n_{j}}(j-1))\geq 2
.

Before starting the proof recall that with the notation from Section
2
, but for the tree considered in the statement of Theorem
3.1
, we have the equality
j^{-}=j-1
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is well-known (see
[
25
, Chapter 9]
). For (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) note that, if
C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic, then it follows from
[
19
, Proposition 5.4]
that for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
the set of distances
\{\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j^{-}))\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is unbounded. This fact trivially implies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j^{-}))=\infty\quad\text{ for every }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Let us prove (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv)
\Leftrightarrow
(v) using that
\varphi
is strictly increasing. Actually, the increasing condition implies that
\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j^{-}))\leq\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n+1}(j),\varphi^{n+1}(j^{-}))
for every pair
n,j\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j^{-}))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j),\varphi^{n}(j^{-}))\quad\text{ for every }j\in\mathbb{N},

and (iv) holds as soon as (iii) does. The implication (iv)
\Rightarrow
(v) is trivial. Finally, if (v) holds we can briefly justify that (iii) also holds as follows: fix any arbitrary index
j\in\mathbb{N}
, name it as
j_{0}:=j
and note that by (v) there is
n_{j_{0}}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}),\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}^{-}))\geq 2
; letting
j_{1}:=\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0})
, noticing that
\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}-1)<j_{1}^{-}
, and since
\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}(j_{1}),\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}(j_{1}^{-}))\geq 2
for some
n_{j_{1}}\in\mathbb{N}
by (v), then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}+n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}),\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}+n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0})\right),\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}(j_{1}),\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}(j_{1}^{-})\right)+\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}(j_{1}^{-}),\varphi^{n_{j_{1}}}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{0}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2+1=3.

A trivial recursive argument, in which one lets
j_{k+1}:=\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, shows the existence of a sequence
(j_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and by (v) a corresponding sequence
(n_{j_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
fulfilling that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0}),\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}\left(\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0})\right),\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}\left(\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}(j_{k}),\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}(j_{k}^{-})\right)+\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}(j_{k}^{-}),\varphi^{n_{j_{k}}}\left(\varphi^{\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}n_{j_{l}}}(j_{0}^{-})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2+k\quad\text{ for each }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Thus,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(j_{0}),\varphi^{n}(j_{0}^{-}))=\infty
. The arbitrariness of
j_{0}=j\in\mathbb{N}
implies (iii).

To finish the proof of Theorem
3.1
it is then enough to show (iv)
\Rightarrow
(i). Assume (iv) and let us show that then the respective operator
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}:c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\longrightarrow c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, obtained from Remarks
2.1
and
2.2
applied to the tree
T=\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the map
\varphi
described in the statement of Theorem
3.1
, satisfies the previously introduced Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion. We start noticing that given any
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, using the map

from Remark

as in Example

but for this general case, we have that

[D^{-1}(x)](j)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}x_{k}\quad\text{ and then }\quad[(C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)](j)=\sum_{k=0}^{\varphi(j)}x_{k}\quad\text{ for each }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Hence, from the conjugacy established in Remark
2.2
we have that

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}(x):=(D\circ C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})(x)=\left(\sum_{k=\varphi(j^{-})+1}^{\varphi(j)}x_{k}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},

(6)

where by abuse of notation we are setting
\varphi(0^{-}):=-1
. Moreover, a similar calculation also shows that for any
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C_{\varphi}}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=(D\circ C_{\varphi}\circ D^{-1})^{n}(x)=(D\circ C_{\varphi}^{n}\circ D^{-1})(x)

\displaystyle=(D\circ C_{\varphi^{n}}\circ D^{-1})(x)=\left(\sum_{k=\varphi^{n}(j^{-})+1}^{\varphi^{n}(j)}x_{k}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\quad\text{ for each }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(7)

Again, by abuse of notation we are setting
\varphi^{n}(0^{-}):=-1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, consider the dense linear subspaces
\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\subset c_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\subset c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
as defined after Remark
2.1
, and note that for this particular case we have the equality

\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})=\left\{x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in c_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\ ;\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{|\operatorname{supp}(x)|}x_{k}=0\right\}.

Let

be the (linear) map acting on each

(8)

Note that the restriction

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}

(I)

Consider any
x=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})

\varphi(0)>0
and we are assuming (iv), it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|\varphi^{n}(0)|=\infty
so that we can choose

\varphi^{n}(0)\geq|\operatorname{supp}(x)|

and by (
7

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}^{n}(x)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\varphi^{n}(0)}x_{k},\sum_{k=\varphi^{n}(0)+1}^{\varphi^{n}(1)}x_{k},\sum_{k=\varphi^{n}(1)+1}^{\varphi^{n}(2)}x_{k},...\right)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{|\operatorname{supp}(x)|}x_{k},0,0,...\right)=(0,0,0,...),

because
x\in\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. It trivially follows that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{C_{\varphi}}^{n}(x)
converges unconditionally.

(II)

Now we claim that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B^{n}(x)
converges unconditionally for every
x\in\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. To check it recall the notation after Remark
2.1
from which
e^{(l)}=(\delta_{l,j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
for each
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. With this notation, since
B
is a linear map originally defined on
c_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, since
\Sigma_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\subset c_{00}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, and since
B(e^{(0)})
can be expressed as a linear combination of the vectors
e^{(1)},e^{(2)},...,e^{(\varphi(0))}
, then the proof of (II) will be complete as soon as we check that:

–

the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B^{n}(e^{(l)})
converges unconditionally for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
.

Thus, let us fix some
l\in\mathbb{N}
. Given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, using (
8
) inductively it follows that

\operatorname{supp}\left(B^{n}(e^{(l)})\right)=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{n}(l^{-})+1\leq k\leq\varphi^{n}(l)\}.

(9)

On the other hand, using assumption (iv) it is not hard to recursively construct a sequence of non-negative integers
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
n_{0}=0
and such that

\operatorname{dist}\left(\varphi^{n_{j}}(k),\varphi^{n_{j}}(k^{-})\right)\geq 2\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\varphi^{n_{j-1}}(l^{-})+1\leq k\leq\varphi^{n_{j-1}}(l),

(10)

where we are taking
\varphi^{n_{0}}=\varphi^{0}
as the identity map in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, i.e.
\varphi^{n_{0}}(l^{-})+1=l=\varphi^{n_{0}}(l)
. Hence, using (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) one can easily prove by induction that

\left\|B^{n}(e^{(l)})\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{\varphi^{n}(l^{-})+1\leq k\leq\varphi^{n}(l)}\left|[B^{n}(e^{(l)})]_{k}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2^{j}}\quad\text{ whenever }\sum_{i=0}^{j}n_{i}\leq n<\sum_{i=0}^{j+1}n_{i}.

(11)

By (
9
) and the increasing condition of
\varphi
we have that the supports of
B^{n}(e^{(l)})
and
B^{m}(e^{(l)})
do not intersect for
n\neq m
. Thus, since by (
11
) we have that
\|B^{n}(e^{(l)})\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, it follows that the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B^{n}(e^{(l)})
converges to some element in
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, and this convergence is unconditional because
(e^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an unconditional Schauder basis for
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
.

(III)

Combining the expressions (
6
) and (
8

\widehat{C_{\varphi}}B(x)=x
for every

Since
C_{\varphi}
and

are topologically conjugate via an isometric isomorphism, it follows that
C_{\varphi}
satisfies the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion and the proof of Theorem
3.1
is now complete.

.

The statement and proof of Theorem

admit several remarks:

(a)

Note that Question

4
Further comments and future lines of research

The main objective of this paper was to exhibit simple examples of frequently hypercyclic composition operators acting on the little Lipschitz space of a rooted tree. To do so, we established in Section
2
an isometric isomorphism between the Lipschitz-type spaces under consideration and the corresponding sequence spaces
\ell^{\infty}
and
c_{0}
. We then used this identification to obtain alternative expressions for the operators studied. In the present section, we further explore the scope of this isometric representation and we also formulate several open problems about the operators we considered in this paper.

We proceed by examining additional applications of the isometry established in Section
2
. Actually, we recall that for a directed tree
T
with a root
\mathfrak{o}\in T
as defined in Section
2
: in
[
16
]
, the spaces considered in this paper
\mathcal{L}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
were introduced for the first time; in
[
5
]
, it was defined the so-called
weighted Lipschitz space

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(T)
and the respective
little space

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{w},0}(T)
; and in
[
4
]
, a generalization called the
iterated logarithmic
(and the respective
little
)
Lipschitz spaces
appeared. If now we let
X
be any of these Lipschitz spaces, and
X_{0}
the associated little space, in each case there exists a concrete sequence
c=(c_{v})_{v\in T}
of positive real numbers with
c_{\mathfrak{o}}=1
for which

X=\left\{f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\ ;\ \sup_{v\in T^{*}}\ c_{v}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|<\infty\right\},

and

X_{0}=\left\{f:T\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\ ;\ \lim_{|v|\to\infty}\ c_{v}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|=0\right\},

both of them endowed with the norm

\|f\|:=|f(\mathfrak{o})|+\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\ c_{v}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|\quad\text{ for each }f\in X.

It is not hard to check that, if one considers the equivalent norm

\|f\|_{X}:=\max\left\{|f(\mathfrak{o})|,\sup_{v\in T^{*}}\ c_{v}|f(v)-f(v^{-})|\right\}\quad\text{ for each }f\in X,

then the maps
D:(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})\longrightarrow(\ell^{\infty}(T,c),\|\cdot\|_{\infty,c})
and
D:(X_{0},\|\cdot\|_{X})\longrightarrow(c_{0}(T,c),\|\cdot\|_{\infty,c})
defined as in Remark
2.1
are again isometric isomorphisms, where

\ell^{\infty}(T,c):=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\mathbb{C}^{T}\ ;\ \sup_{v\in T}\ c_{v}|x_{v}|<\infty\right\}

and

c_{0}(T,c):=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\mathbb{C}^{T}\ ;\ \lim_{|v|\to\infty}\ c_{v}|x_{v}|=0\right\}

are the classical weighted
\ell^{\infty}
and
c_{0}
spaces as defined in
[
25
, Section 4.1]
,
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
, both of them endowed with the
weighted supremum norm

\|x\|_{\infty,c}:=\sup_{v\in T}\ c_{v}|x_{v}|\quad\text{ for each }x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\ell^{\infty}(T,c).

In addition, these sequence spaces are isometrically isomorphic to
(\ell^{\infty}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
and
(c_{0}(T),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
as defined in Remark
2.1
. See
[
25
, pages 96 and 353]
for further details.

As a consequence of these observations, although these Lipschitz-type spaces over rooted trees were originally motivated by the well-known and extensively studied
Bloch space
(formed by holomorphic functions whose derivative grows no faster than the density of the Poincaré metric; see
[
16
, Section 1]
), the isometric identification established above shows that they can be naturally realised as concrete representations of the classical sequence spaces
\ell^{\infty}
and
c_{0}
. From this perspective, several results obtained in
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
32
]
admit alternative proofs, and in some cases more direct arguments, by means of the correspondence described after Remark
2.1
. In particular, the identities

D(\chi_{S_{w}})=e^{(w)},\quad D^{-1}(e^{(w)})=\chi_{S_{w}},\quad D(\chi_{\{w\}})=\partial^{(w)},\quad D^{-1}(\partial^{(w)})=\chi_{\{w\}},

for each
w\in T
, allow one to transfer well-known structural properties of the classical spaces
\ell^{\infty}
and
c_{0}
or about their duals to the Lipschitz-type spaces under consideration.

On the other hand, the study of operators on these Lipschitz-type spaces presents several natural features of interest. First, such operators are typically defined through simple symbols (such as
\psi
for multiplication or
\varphi
for composition operators, see
[
16
,
18
,
19
]
), which makes their formulation particularly transparent. Secondly, via the map
D
, these operators admit equivalent representations as operators acting on the classical sequence spaces
\ell^{\infty}
and
c_{0}
, as illustrated in Sections
2
and
3
. This correspondence makes it possible to analyse and classify them in terms of shift, multiplication and composition operators on sequence spaces. The availability of different, yet equivalent, representations of the same operator often provides additional structural insight, as the examples and arguments developed in Sections
2
and
3
demonstrate. Thus, it appears natural to continue the systematic study of the operators considered so far on these Lipschitz-type spaces over rooted trees, and to extend the analysis to related classes, such as weighted composition or weighted shift operators.

From the perspective of Linear Dynamics, many natural problems can be posed. For the rest of the paper we leave some concrete open questions regarding the composition operators
C_{\varphi}
considered in this paper, but also for their complex multiples
\lambda C_{\varphi}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. We start asking about the possibility of distinguishing some of the main dynamical properties considered in the literature:

Problem 4.1
.

Can the composition operators (or their complex multiples) acting on little Lipschitz spaces be used to distinguish the notions of mixing, Devaney chaos and frequent hypercyclicity?

Since the little Lipschitz spaces are isomorphic to certain
c_{0}
-spaces as argued above, Problem
4.1
seems a relevant question because the first operator that distinguished frequent hypercyclicity from chaos was a very specific backward shift acting on
c_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. This backward shift is frequently hypercyclic, but neither mixing nor chaotic (see
[
8
, Section 5]
and then
[
22
]
for examples of the converse).

Remark 4.2
.

We include in this remark what we known about Problem
4.1
:

(a)

The hypercyclic composition operators considered in this paper fulfill the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion, so that they can not be used to solve Problem
4.1
.

(b)

About the composition operators considered in
[
19
]
and the two criteria given there:

–

The proof of
[
19
, Theorem 6.1]
, combined with that of
[
19
, Proposition 6.2]
, can be strengthened to show that the symbols
\varphi
fulfilling the hypothesis of
[
19
, Theorem 6.1]
are such that the operator
\lambda C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
satisfies the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion for all
|\lambda|>1
. In particular, these operators
\lambda C_{\varphi}
can not be used to solve Problem
4.1
.

–

The hypothesis in
[
19
, Theorem 6.6]
seem less demanding that those of
[
19
, Theorem 6.1]
, and it is not completely clear if satisfying such result implies or not satisfying the Frequent Hypercyclicity Criterion. Clarifying this fact could be the starting point to solve Problem
4.1
.

In addition, the notion of (usual) hypercyclicity for composition operators and their multiples has not been distinguished from that of mixing. It could be interesting to start by distinguishing them. One should probably consider trees more sophisticated than
\mathbb{N}_{0}
to address these problems.

As a second question, and in view of
[
19
, Proposition 6.2, Corollary 6.3 and Theorem 6.6]
, we would like to propose the following:

Problem 4.3
.

Let
C_{\varphi}
be a composition operator on a little Lipschitz space
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and assume that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic for some
|\lambda|\geq 1
. Is
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}
a finite set for every vertex
v\in T
?

By
[
19
, Proposition 5.3]
we have that every symbol
\varphi
fulfilling that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, is an injective map. Note that, given any vertex
v\in T
and any positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we only have two possibilities for the set
\varphi^{-n}(\{v\}):=\{w\in T\ ;\ \varphi^{n}(w)=v\}
when
\varphi
is injective:

–

we either have
\varphi^{-n}(\{v\})=\{u\}
for some vertex
u\in T
;

–

or we have that
\varphi^{-n}(\{v\})
is empty.

Thus, when
\varphi
is injective, it follows that “
the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}
is finite for every
v\in T
” if and only if “
the set
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi^{-n}(\{v\})
is finite for every
v\in T
”. Conversely, but also when
\varphi
is injective, given any vertex
v\in T
we have that

\text{{the set $\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi^{-n}(\{v\})$ is infinite}}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\text{{$\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{N}$}}.

Our conjecture is that Problem
4.3
has a positive answer. In fact, if
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{N}
for a certain vertex
v\in T
, it seems that all the
\lambda C_{\varphi}
-orbits of the functions in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
that are near enough to the characteristic function
\chi_{\{v\}}\in\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
would hardly approach the zero-vector of the underlying space for
|\lambda|\geq 1
. However, we can not discard the possibility of constructing a sufficiently complex map
\varphi
, acting on a sufficiently complex tree
T
, to answer Problem
4.3
in the negative.

Although the mentioned condition “
the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}
is finite for every
v\in T
” was relaxed in
[
19
, Theorem 6.6]
, all the hypercyclic operators of the type
\lambda C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
provided by the authors in
[
19
]
fulfilled such condition. Also, every map
\varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
considered here in Section
3
fulfills that the set
R_{j}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{j\})\neq\emptyset\}
is finite for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Indeed, since
\varphi
was assumed to be strictly increasing with
\varphi(0)>0
, it follows that the cardinality of the set
R_{j}
is at most
j
. In view of these comments, we consider worth it to provide an example of composition operator
C_{\varphi}
whose symbol
\varphi
fulfills that the set
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{v\})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{N}
for some
v\in T
but such that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|\lambda|<1
in view of Problem
4.3
:

Example 4.4
.

Consider the rooted tree
T
indexed by
\mathbb{Z}
, with
0\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as its root, and where two vertices
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
are adjacent if and only if
|k-l|=1
. Consider now the map

\varphi(j):=\begin{cases}j+1,&\text{ if }j<0,\\[5.0pt]
2j+1,&\text{ if }j\geq 0.\end{cases}

Note that
\varphi
is Lipschitz with constant
L_{\varphi}=2
and that
\lim_{|j|\to\infty}|\varphi(j)|=\infty
, which shows that
C_{\varphi}
is bounded in
\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
by
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
. Moreover, we have the equality

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi^{-n}(\{0\})=\{-n\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}\quad\text{ which shows that }\quad\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{0\})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{N}.

This is an infinite set, contrary to what happened for the symbols considered in Section
3
. Note also that
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ \varphi^{-n}(\{j\})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{N}
for each negative integer
j\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}
and for the positive integers of the form
j=2^{n}-1
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
. One can check that
\varphi
fulfills the assumptions of
[
19
, Theorem 6.6]
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\tfrac{1}{2}<|\lambda|<1
(consider
c=1
in
[
19
, Theorem 6.6, Condition (2)]
). It follows that the operator
\lambda C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
is mixing whenever
\tfrac{1}{2}<|\lambda|<1
.

For the next and last questions we use the
essential spectrum
. It is known that a weakly-mixing bounded linear operator
A:X\longrightarrow X
on a separable Banach space
X
admits a
hypercyclic subspace
(i.e. a closed infinite-dimensional subspace formed by hypercyclic vectors but the zero-vector) if and only if its essential spectrum
\sigma_{e}(A)
intersects the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(see
[
27
]
). In particular, if
\ker(A)
is infinite-dimensional, then
0\in\sigma_{e}(A)\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(see
[
27
, Corollary 7.1]
). For our composition operators it is not hard to check that if
T\setminus\varphi(T)
is an infinite set, then
\ker(C_{\varphi})
is infinite-dimensional. Indeed, note that
\{\chi_{\{v\}}\ ;\ v\in T\setminus\varphi(T)\}
is a linearly independent set contained in
\ker(C_{\varphi})\subset\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
. Thus, we can ask the following natural questions:

Problem 4.5
.

Let
C_{\varphi}
be a composition operator on a little Lipschitz space
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and assume that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\leq 1
. Does it follow that
T\setminus\varphi(T)
is an infinite set?

Problem 4.6
.

Let
C_{\varphi}
be a composition operator on a little Lipschitz space
\mathcal{L}_{0}(T)
and assume that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\leq 1
. Does
\lambda C_{\varphi}
admit a hypercyclic subspace?

In Problems
4.5
and Problem
4.6
we have avoided the option
|\lambda|>1
because the composition operator considered in Example
2.4
fulfills that:

–

The map
\varphi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
is such that
\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\varphi(\mathbb{N}_{0})=\{0\}
, i.e.
T\setminus\varphi(T)
is finite in this case.

–

The operator
\lambda C_{\varphi}:\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
can be checked to be hypercyclic and even mixing for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|>1
by applying
[
19
, Theorem 6.1]
.

–

The operator
\lambda C_{\varphi}
does not admit a hypercyclic subspace when
|\lambda|>1
. Indeed, since
\widehat{C_{\varphi}}=P_{0}+B
as argued in Example
2.4
, we have that
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is topologically conjugate via an isometric isomorphism to the compact perturbation
\lambda P_{0}+\lambda B
of the Rolewicz operator
\lambda B
. It is well-known that such operator does not admit a hypercyclic subspace (see
[
27
, Remark 5.9 and Corollary 7.2]
).

Our conjecture is that Problem
4.5
should have a positive answer, and this could probably be achieved using that the set
\{|\lambda|^{n}\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\varphi^{n}(v),\varphi^{n}(v^{-}))\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded whenever the operator
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is hypercyclic by
[
19
, Proposition 5.4]
. However, note that a positive answer to Problem
4.5
does not directly imply a positive answer to Problem
4.6
, unless one shows that every hypercyclic operator of the type
\lambda C_{\varphi}
is weakly-mixing (see
[
27
, Proposition 7.9]
for more details).

To conclude, we would like to mention other possible lines of research apart from that related to hypercyclicity. In fact, recall that in
[
18
]
the authors showed that the multiplication operators acting on little Lipschitz spaces (but also their adjoints) can not be hypercyclic. Hence, we propose to study for these operators (and also for the shifts considered in
[
32
]
) other dynamical notions that have recently appeared in Linear Dynamics such as (
mean
)
Li-Yorke
and
distributional chaos
(see
[
10
,
11
]
),
shadowing
,
hyperbolicity
and
structural stability
(see
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
) or
recurrence
(see
[
14
,
21
,
29
]
). The isomorphism and conjugacies established in this paper seem good tools to look into these topics.
