Synergistic corrosion protection and bacterial control in a UV-cured UA coating containing TiO2–polyaniline–calixarene nanocomposite
Authors: Shakiba Dadkhah, Ali Gharieh, Fatemeh Rafimanzelat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An innovative nanocomposite architecture—TiO2–polyaniline‑doped sulfonated calixarene‑GMA (TiO2–PA–GSC)—is engineered for integration into UV‑curable waterborne urethane‑acrylate coatings. This design delivers concurrent improvements in cross‑link density, thermal stability (ΔT₅₀% = +31 °C), and electrochemical barrier properties with low‑frequency impedance rising by up to two orders of magnitude during long‑term immersion. The synergistic mechanism comprises: (i) polyaniline‑mediated formation of a redox‑active passive layer, (ii) TiO2 nanoparticles imparting tortuous diffusion pathways and interfacial charge‑trapping, and (iii) sulfonated calixarene facilitating homogeneous dispersion, cross-linking, and selective metal‑ion complexation. Furthermore, the coating demonstrates Gram‑positive‑selective antibacterial efficacy, exhibiting complete inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus (95.6%, 12 ± 1 mm zone) with negligible activity against Escherichia coli (≈ 10% inhibition). This integrated multifunctional platform advances protective coating technology by simultaneously addressing corrosion degradation and microbial colonization in demanding saline environments.

Introduction

Metallic components constitute the structural backbone of contemporary marine infrastructure, yet their operational longevity is persistently compromised by synergistic degradation mechanisms involving corrosion and microbial colonization. Corrosion not only undermines mechanical integrity and safety but also imposes substantial economic burdens through maintenance and replacement costs. Concurrently, bacterial adhesion and subsequent biofilm formation accelerate material deterioration, increase hydrodynamic drag, and complicate maintenance protocols1–4. Given that microbial attachment represents the primary initiation point for surface degradation, protective strategies targeting bacterial inhibition offer disproportionate benefits in extending service life and reducing lifecycle expenditures. In this context, the development of smart multifunctional coatings has emerged as a key trend in the advancement of organic coatings5. These coatings transcend passive barrier functions by actively resisting corrosion and preventing biological attachment. Consequently, there is escalating demand for advanced coating systems that integrate robust corrosion protection with active antibacterial functionality to address the multifaceted challenges of harsh marine environments6.

Historically, solvent-based organic coatings have been the industry standard for corrosion protection7,8. Although effective in many contexts, these systems raise environmental and health concerns due to volatile organic compound emissions, and some formulations still fall short in multifunctional performance and long-term outdoor durability9. In response, UV-curable, waterborne coating technologies have emerged as an attractive alternative: they combine rapid, energy-efficient curing with reduced emissions and can be engineered to meet tighter performance requirements10. Among UV-curable binders, urethane–acrylate (UA) resins offer a favorable balance of mechanical properties and chemical resistance, but neat UA films can be limited by insufficient hardness, thermal stability, and outdoor aging resistance for demanding marine applications11.

Incorporating nanoscale additives has proven to be one of the most effective routes to overcome these limitations12. Nanoparticles and conductive polymers can simultaneously enhance mechanical strength, barrier properties, and functional responses such as corrosion inhibition and antimicrobial activity13,14. Polyaniline (PANI), in particular, has attracted sustained interest because it combines facile synthesis, environmental benignity, and electrochemical activity that contributes to corrosion mitigation15. Doped PANI (emeraldine salt form) can also provide antibacterial effect via electrostatic interactions with microbial membranes and by enabling redox- or current-driven inactivation pathways16,17. Complementing conductive polymers, semiconductor photocatalysts like TiO2 can generate electron–hole pairs under illumination; the resulting photocurrents and reactive oxygen species disrupt biofilms and inactivate microorganisms18,19. Thus, hybrid systems that pair PANI with semiconductor nanoparticles can exploit both redox and photocatalytic mechanisms for enhanced, light-assisted antibacterial performance18.

Despite these promising synergies, practical application demands careful attention to compatibility, dispersion, and curing chemistry in the polymer matrix. Functional dopants and surface modifiers can play a decisive role: they improve nanoparticle dispersion, promote interfacial bonding with the polymer matrix, and sometimes introduce additional functions such as metal-ion complexation or crosslink enhancement20. Sulfonated calixarenes grafted with reactive groups are attractive in this context because they can act simultaneously as dopants, compatibilizers, and UV-reactive crosslinking sites — bridging inorganic nanoparticles, conductive polymers, and acrylate-based networks21.

Motivated by the need for low-emission, multifunctional protective films for marine and industrial applications, this study develops a UV-curable, waterborne nanocomposite coating that integrates these complementary strategies. We synthesized a novel titanium-oxide–polyaniline system doped with sulfonated calixarene- glycidyl methacrylate (TiO2-PA-GSC) and dispersed it into a waterborne UA matrix. Our design leverages (i) TiO2 for photocatalytic charge generation and bactericidal action, (ii) PANI for redox-active corrosion protection and electrostatic antibacterial, and (iii) sulfonated calixarene- glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) for improved compatibility, enhanced cross-link density during UV cure, and metal-ion complexation to further inhibit corrosion. This study aligns with the current advancement trend in organic coatings by developing a smart multifunctional system that effectively integrates corrosion resistance and antibacterial functionality. We hypothesize that the synergistic interaction of these components — even at low loadings — will produce coatings with markedly better corrosion resistance and antibacterial efficacy than unmodified UA.

EXPERIMENTAL

Materials and methods

All reagents and solvents were used as received unless otherwise stated. Major suppliers were: Sigma–Aldrich (trimethylolpropane, polycaprolactone diol 2000 (PCL), isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI), 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), p-tert-butylphenol, diphenyl ether, formaldehyde, sulfuric acid, dried dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), glycidyl methacrylate (GMA), aniline), Merck (sodium hydroxide, sodium chloride, ethyl acetate, acetic acid, acetone, toluene, ethanol, methanol, triethylamine, hydrochloric acid, ammonium persulfate, titanium dioxide), Shanghai Chemical Reagents Co. (dibutyltin dilaurate, DBTDL), and Evonik (Tegomer® D3403). Irgacure® 819 was used as the photoinitiator (PI). Substrates were Q235 steel plates (C ≤ 0.22 wt%, Mn ≤ 1.40 wt%, Si ≤ 0.35 wt%, P ≤ 0.045 wt%, S ≤ 0.045 wt%), cut to 20 × 20 × 1 mm.

Chemical structure and functionalization were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). FT-IR spectra were recorded on a BRUKER IFS 48 spectrophotometer using KBr pellets. 1HNMR and 13C NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance II spectrometer at 500 MHz. Samples were dissolved in solvents selected to maximize spectral clarity and solubility: dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for UA resin and PA-GSC nanocomposite, deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) for CA, and deuterium oxide (D2O) for SCA and GSC, facilitating accurate structural elucidation. Crystallographic information was obtained by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips PW 3710 diffractometer (Cu Kα, 30 kV, 35 mA). Thermal stability was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on a Netzsch TGA 209 F1 under nitrogen from room temperature to 600 °C at 10 °C min− 1. Morphology and dispersion of the nanocomposites were investigated by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific Quanta 450 FEG) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; CM120). Gel fraction (gel content) was determined by Soxhlet extraction in tetrahydrofuran (THF) at its reflux temperature (~ 66 °C) for 10 h, with the sample contained within a cellulose thimble. Adhesion of cured coatings was measured by pull-off testing according to ASTM D454122.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using an Ivium Vertex potentiostat/galvanostat. Steel specimens were polished sequentially with abrasive papers from 80 to 2000 grit, rinsed with deionized water, air-dried, and degreased with acetone. A 1 cm² exposed area was defined and the remaining specimen surface was masked with a 3:1 beeswax: colophony barrier. Electrochemical measurements were conducted after 24 h of immersion to allow the system to reach steady‑state open‑circuit potential (OCP). EIS spectra were acquired using a conventional three‑electrode configuration: a saturated Ag/AgCl reference electrode (+ 0.197 V vs. SHE at 25 °C), a graphite auxiliary electrode, and uncoated/coated steel specimens as working electrodes. All reported potentials are referenced to this Ag/AgCl electrode. For leachate‑based assessment, 0.1 g of TiO2-PA-GSC nanocomposite was dispersed in 100 mL of saline (0.9 wt% NaCl) and stirred at ambient temperature for 24 h; the resulting extract served as the electrolyte for immersion tests. Samples were immersed in the extract solution, and EIS measurements were performed at OCP over a frequency range of 10 mHz to 100 kHz with a 10 mV sinusoidal perturbation amplitude. Parallel electrochemical impedance evaluations were also carried out in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution at room temperature. Data fitting and equivalent‑circuit modeling were executed using ZSimpWin software. All electrochemical experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility; representative spectra and corresponding fitted models are presented.

Antibacterial activity was evaluated by three complementary methods: broth macrodilution, agar diffusion, and time-kill assays, using Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922. Bacterial suspensions were prepared to 0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1.5 × 108 CFU mL− 1). For broth macrodilution, TiO2-PA-GSC was tested at 1 wt% (relative to the assay volume). Agents were serially diluted in 2 mL nutrient broth, inoculated with bacterial suspensions, and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. Growth inhibition was assessed by measuring optical density at 600 nm. Agar diffusion (zone of inhibition) tests were used to evaluate UA (unmodified UA) and NC4 (UA containing the nanocomposite) films. Bacterial lawns were prepared on agar plates, and after brief drying, 1 cm2 film samples were placed on the inoculated surface under sterile conditions. Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 18–24 h and zones of inhibition were measured. Time-kill assays for NC4 were conducted by placing 1 cm2 pieces of film into flasks containing bacterial suspensions at 5 × 105 CFU Ml− 1. At predefined time points, 100 µL aliquots were plated on nutrient agar (lawn formation) and colonies were counted after 24 h to determine log-reduction relative to the initial inoculum. All biological assays were performed in triplicate.

Synthesis of UV-curable waterborne urethane-acrylate (UA)

The UV-curable waterborne UA resin was prepared in a two-step synthesis (Fig. 1). In step 1, PCL (30 g), Tegomer® D3403 (15.8 g) and TMP (1.3 g) were charged to the reactor and heated to 60 °C. IPDI (18.9 g) and DBTDL (0.002 g) were then added and the mixture was heated to 95 °C and maintained for 4 h. In step 2, the reaction mixture was cooled to 50 °C and BHT (0.2 g), additional DBTDL (0.006 g) and HEMA (6 g) were added; the reaction was continued at 50 °C for 2 h. The resulting resin was processed and stored according to standard laboratory practice.

Fig. 1Schematic illustration of two‑step UA resin synthesis.

Schematic illustration of two‑step UA resin synthesis.

Modification of sulfonated calix4 arene with GMA (GSC)

Calix4 arene (CA) was prepared and sulfonated to give sulfonated calix4 arene (SCA) following literature procedures23,24. A sample of SCA (0.2 g) was dispersed in 20 mL dried DMSO under mechanical stirring. GMA (0.2 g) and triethylamine (0.14 g) were added and the mixture was stirred at 65 °C for 24 h. The obtained product (GSC) was precipitated with acetone, collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol and acetone, and oven-dried at 50 °C. The reaction in the aprotic DMSO medium proceeds via transesterification of GMA, as reported previously (Fig. 2)25,26.

Synthesis of GSC nanocomposite.

Preparation of TiO2-polyaniline doped GSC nanocomposite (TiO2-PA-GSC)

To synthesize PA-GSC, aniline (1 g) and GSC (1.4 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of 0.05 M HCl and stirred at room temperature. Polymerization was initiated by slow addition of ammonium persulfate solution (10.7 mL of 0.93 M) over ~ 20 min to control heat evolution. The reaction color changed from blue to green over 24 h; the green precipitate was filtered, washed repeatedly with cold distilled water and methanol until the filtrate was colorless, and dried. As illustrated in Fig. 3, for the TiO2-PA-GSC nanocomposite, TiO2 nanoparticles were introduced at the beginning of polymerization at 5 wt% relative to polyaniline mass, ensuring uniform distribution throughout polymer matrix development.

Fig. 3Schematic of TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite synthesis.

Schematic of TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite synthesis.

Fabrication of nanocomposite coatings

UA coatings containing different loadings of the nanocomposite (Table 1) were prepared by ultrasonic dispersion of the measured nanocomposite in 5 g of UA (150 W, 37 kHz, Adeeco) for 20 min. Irgacure® 819 (PI, 0.1 g) was then added and mixed. Steel substrates were polished with emery papers (500, 1000, 2000 grit), degreased with ethanol, and dried. Coatings were applied using a manual drawdown bar applicator to achieve a target dry film thickness of ~ 100 μm, which was verified using a magnetic induction thickness gauge. The coatings were then cured under a medium-pressure mercury UV lamp (365 nm, 154 mW cm⁻²) for 2 min. Unless otherwise noted, all experiments were performed at least in triplicate and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

RESULTS and DISCUSSION

Synthesis and structural characterization of the UA resin

The chemical structure of the synthesized UA was confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 4) and FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 5). The 1H NMR spectrum of UA ((Fig. 4-a) reveals distinct peaks corresponding to various proton environments. The peak at 0.80 ppm is attributed to the methyl groups of TMP (H1), while the peaks between 0.84 and 0.97 ppm correspond to the methyl groups of IPDI (H8, H9). The methylene proton signal associated with the -CH2-NH-C = O group is observed at approximately 2.84 ppm (H10), with the cis and trans conformations of the NH moiety appearing at 6.76–7.67 ppm (H4). Additionally, the methine proton resonance in the -CH-N- group within the cyclohexane ring of IPDI appears around 3.58 ppm (H5). The methylene protons in the (-CH2-CH(NCO)) and (-CH2-CH(NH)C = O) groups in the cyclohexane ring of IPDI are observed at 1.42 ppm (H7) and 1.07 ppm (H6), respectively9. Peaks at 1.59 and 3.99 ppm correspond to the methylene protons in (CH3-CH2-C) and (-C-CH2-O) of TMP (H2, H3), while the signals at 3.62 and 3.99 ppm are assigned to the methylene protons in the (-O-CH2-C) segment of PCL (H17, H16, H11). For the PCL chain, the triplet at 2.29 ppm corresponds to the methylene protons in the –O–CO–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2– (H15) segment, whereas the peak at 1.58 ppm is assigned to the –O–CO–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2– (H12, H14) methylene protons, and the peak at 1.32 ppm corresponds to the same –O–CO–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2–CH2– (H13) region. In the Tegomer component, peaks at 0.83 and 3.40 ppm are attributed to the methyl groups in (-CH2-CH3) and (-O-CH3) (H20, H23), respectively, while the peaks at 1.32 and 4.00 ppm correspond to the methylene protons in (C-CH2-CH3) and (O-CH2-C) (H19, H18). The resonances at 5.67 and 3.54 ppm are associated with the methylene groups in (-C-CH2-O-) and (O-CH2-CH2-O-) (H21, H22)27,28. Furthermore, the spectrum shows peaks corresponding to the protons of the HEMA molecule, with the CH3 (H25) group observed at 1.90 ppm, and the CH2=C- (H26) groups at 5.67 ppm and 6.07 ppm. Lastly, the methylene groups attached to the -O-C(= O)-C = C and -OC(= O)-N groups of HEMA are identified at 4.10 ppm (H24)9.

Finally, the peak at 61.8 ppm corresponds to the methylene groups in (–O–CH2–CH2) (C32), with ethylene groups of HEMA appearing at 135.90 and 125.3 ppm (C34, C36). FTIR analysis of UA (Fig. 5) depicts broad absorption band at 3600–3400 cm− 1 indicates N–H stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of urethane bonds. Peaks at 1280 cm− 1 and 1244 cm− 1 correspond to N–H bending/C–N stretching and C–O stretching vibrations, respectively. The absence of a characteristic peak near 2270 cm− 1 confirms the complete reaction of NCO groups from IPDI. Peaks at 2940 and 2866 cm− 1 are attributed to C–H stretching in the Tegomer and PCL segments, while the C–O–C stretching at 1100 cm− 1 and the C = O stretching at 1725 cm− 1 confirm PCL incorporation. Peaks at 1466 and 1362 cm− 1 correspond to CH₂ bending vibrations, and the presence of acrylate groups (C = C) is confirmed by a peak at 1640 cm− 128.

Taken together, the 1H and 13C NMR assignments and the FT-IR fingerprint confirm successful synthesis of the target UA resin bearing urethane linkages and terminal acrylate functionality suitable for subsequent UV curing and nanocomposite formulation.

Structural and spectroscopic characterization of TiO₂-PA-GSC nanocomposites

The chemical composition and intermolecular interactions of the prepared nanocomposites was analyzed using multiple techniques. Figure 6 shows the FTIR spectra of CA, SCA, GSC, PA-GSC, and TiO2-PA-GSC. The spectrum of CA displays O–H stretching at ~ 3240 cm− 1 and aliphatic C–H stretching at 2957 and 2868 cm− 1. Aromatic C–H and C = C stretches appear near 3052 and at 1598, 1485, and 1455 cm− 1, respectively. C–O stretching is observed at 1203 cm− 1, tert-butyl C–H bending at 1395 and 1363 cm− 1, while out-of-plane C = C and C–H bending occurs at 783 and 871 cm− 1. These bands are consistent with the reported fingerprint of substituted calixarenes31,32.

Sulfonation (SCA) introduces strong S = O absorptions at ≈ 1190 cm− 1 (asymmetric) and 1054 cm− 1 (symmetric). Broad O–H stretching bands appear at ≈ 3447 and 3235 cm− 1, the higher-wavenumber component reflecting hydrogen-bonded OH groups associated with the sulfonate moiety (SO3⁻). Additional features include O–H bending near 1380 cm− 1, aliphatic C–H stretching at 2955 cm− 1, aromatic C = C stretches at 1629, 1476 and 1457 cm− 1, =C–H bending at 630 cm− 1 and a C–S band around 890 cm− 1, supporting successful sulfonation33,34. GMA-modified SCA (GSC) shows spectral changes consistent with grafting of glycidyl methacrylate. A weak carbonyl band at ≈ 1728 cm− 1 (C = O) and C = C stretching at 1636 cm− 1 are observed, together with an out-of-plane = C–H bend at ≈ 805 cm− 1. Importantly, the intensity of the broad OH stretching and the O–H bending bands (3235–3447 and 1380 cm− 1) is reduced relative to SCA, indicating consumption or chemical modification of hydroxyl functionalities during the GMA transesterification/transetherification reaction25.

The FTIR spectrum of PA-GSC confirms polyaniline formation and strong interaction with the GSC dopant. Characteristic PANI bands appear at ≈ 1572 and 1498 cm− 1, assigned to quinonoid and benzenoid ring stretching, respectively, indicating the expected conjugated backbone. The C–N aromatic stretching mode is observed at 1305 cm− 1, while the band at 1231 cm− 1 is indicative of the protonated (emeraldine salt) form of polyaniline. In-plane and out-of-plane aromatic C–H bending modes are present at 1153 and 823 cm− 1, respectively. Broad absorptions at ≈ 3445 and 3232 cm− 1 are attributed to N–H (from PANI) and residual O–H (from GSC), consistent with hydrogen bonding in the composite. The S = O stretching of the sulfonate dopant appears at ≈ 1036 cm− 1, and a band at ≈ 876 cm− 1 is assigned to NH+···SO3⁻ electrostatic interactions between protonated PANI and the sulfonated calixarene dopant, providing spectral evidence for strong dopant–polymer coupling in PA-GSC13,35.

The TiO2 spectrum exhibits characteristic Ti–O stretching vibrations with a main band near 712 cm− 1 and additional strong absorptions centered around 500 cm− 1, consistent with anatase-type TiO2. In the TiO2-PA-GSC composite, the Ti–O band is slightly shifted (≈ 700 cm⁻¹) and its intensity is attenuated. This shift and decreased intensity likely result from two effects: (i) overlap and partial masking of the Ti–O vibrations by adjacent PANI/GSC absorptions, and (ii) interfacial interactions (electrostatic and hydrogen bonding) between TiO2 surface sites and the PANI/GSC matrix, which perturb the local Ti–O vibrational environment. Together, the FT-IR data demonstrate successful synthesis of the hybrid TiO2-PA-GSC material and strong molecular-level interactions among TiO2, polyaniline and the sulfonated calixarene dopant that are expected to influence dispersion, charge transfer and functional performance in the cured coating18,36.

The 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra of CA, SCA, GSC, and PA-GSC nanocomposites are presented in Fig. 7. In the 1H NMR spectrum of CA (Fig. 7a), a peak at 1.29 ppm corresponds to the tert-butyl protons (H5), while peaks at 3.52, 3.56, 4.39, and 4.42 ppm are attributed to methylene bridge protons (H4, H3). Aromatic protons (H2) and phenolic protons (H1) appear at 7.22 and 9.68 ppm, respectively. In the ¹³C NMR spectrum (Fig. 7b), peaks at 31.49 and 34.03 ppm represent tert-butyl carbons (C6, C5), while methylene bridge carbons appear at 33.21 ppm. Aromatic carbons (C1–C4) resonate at 125.54, 128.72, 14.73, and 14.63 ppm32.

The 1H NMR of SCA (Fig. 7c) shows methylene bridge protons appear at 3.97 ppm (H1), and aromatic protons at 7.42 ppm (H2). A peak at 4.71 ppm is due to the D₂O solvent, which causes the phenolic OH proton to exchange and disappear. Notably, the tert-butyl proton resonances observed in CA are no longer visible in SCA, consistent with chemical modification during sulfonation. In the 13C NMR (Fig. 7d), the methylene bridge carbons (ArCH2Ar) resonate at 30.65 ppm, while aromatic carbons (C1–C4) are detected at 153.52, 135.19, 128.18, and 126.20 ppm32,37. In the 1H NMR of GSC (Fig. 7e), new signals attributable to grafted GMA are evident. A methyl resonance of the methacrylate unit appears at ~ 1.8 ppm (H2), and vinyl protons of the GMA moiety are observed at 4.66 and 4.76 ppm (H1). By comparing the integrals of the GMA vinyl/methyl signals (H1, H2) with those of the remaining SCA aromatic/methylene protons (H3, H4), the degree of modification is estimated to be ~ 50% of the original phenolic sites, indicating substantial but partial functionalization by GMA38.

The 1H-NMR spectrum of the PA-GSC composite, presented in Fig. 7f, contains resonances attributable to both the GSC dopant and the polyaniline chain. Signals at 3.46 ppm (H3) and 7.45 ppm (H4) arise from the GSC component, while the polyaniline backbone shows aromatic resonances near 6.98 and 7.11 ppm and broad features at 6.56 and 7.92 ppm that can be assigned to proton environments influenced by NH (H5) and the conjugated PANI network (H6). The PANI resonances are somewhat broadened, as commonly observed for conducting polymers due to limited solubility and electronic delocalization; nevertheless, the presence of both GSC and PANI signals confirms successful incorporation of GSC as a dopant and formation of the polyaniline phase in the composite35.

Figure 8 shows the XRD patterns of SCA, GSC, PA-GSC, TiO2-PA-GSC and commercial TiO2. The pristine SCA exhibits a sharp basal reflection at 2θ ≈ 6.9°, which corresponds to an interlayer spacing of ≈ 12.8 Å (Bragg’s law). Additional features near 2θ ≈ 13° and ≈ 20° likely arise from higher-order reflections or from localized ordering within the three-dimensional packing of the calixarene scaffold39. Functionalization with GMA (GSC) causes the sharp 6.9° reflection to disappear and produces broad, low-intensity features, consistent with disruption of the original lamellar order by bulky GMA substituents and the formation of a more disordered (poorly ordered or amorphous) phase. This structural perturbation is ascribed to the introduction of bulky GMA moieties, which induce steric hindrance and reduce the packing efficiency of the calixarene layers.

The XRD pattern of the TiO₂ powder matches anatase (JCPDS No. 21–1272), with characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 25.28°, 37.81°, 47.99°, 53.95°, 55.00°, 62.90° and 75.00° corresponding to the (101), (004), (200), (105), (211), (204) and (215) planes, respectively. The sharpness and intensity of these reflections indicate a high degree of crystallinity. Using the (101) reflection and the Debye–Scherrer Eqs. where D is the mean crystallite size, K is the shape factor (0.9), λ is the Cu Kα wavelength (1.5406 Å), β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM, in radians) and θ is the Bragg angle, the mean crystallite size was estimated (from our data) to be approximately 156 nm40.

Comparing PA-GSC and TiO2-containing samples shows a clear influence of TiO2 on the diffractive signature of the polymer phase. PA-GSC alone displays broad, weak features (typical of semi-crystalline or largely amorphous conducting polymers) around 2θ ≈ 21°–24°. In the TiO2-PA-GSC composite these broad polymer peaks are no longer apparent; instead, the pattern is dominated by the distinct anatase reflections. This observation indicates that (i) TiO2 retains its crystalline anatase structure within the composite and (ii) the presence of TiO2 during PANI formation restricts or perturbs PANI ordering, promoting a more semi-amorphous polymer morphology. Such confinement of PANI chains on TiO2 surfaces—together with strong interfacial interactions—can reduce polymer crystallinity and alter the overall microstructure of the nanocomposite. These structural changes are important because they affect charge transfer, dispersion and the functional properties (photocatalytic and electrochemical) of the final material41.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and its derivative (DTG) curves, presented in Figure 9, were used to compare the thermal decomposition behavior of CA, SCA, GSC, PA–GSC, TiO2–PA–GSC and pristine TiO2, with the corresponding thermal decomposition events detailed in Table 2. CA shows a small mass loss (≈6%) below 230 °C, attributable to desorption of weakly bound solvent and moisture. A major weight-loss step occurs between ~330–420 °C (≈40%), consistent with cleavage of tert-butyl substituents and evolution of volatile fragments. Further decomposition between ~430–600 °C (≈30–32%) corresponds to breakdown of the macrocyclic framework and phenolic fragments, yielding the low char residues typical of unsubstituted calixarene backbones. SCA displays an early two-step loss (total ≈21%) below 200 °C, which we assign to removal of adsorbed and coordinated water at the sulfonate sites. Decomposition of the sulfonic acid functionalities is observed near ~300 °C, followed by extensive degradation of the calixarene core above 400 °C. The presence of oxygenated and sulfonate groups in SCA leads to a higher carbonaceous residue compared with CA, consistent with increased char-forming propensity of sulfonated organics42,43. GSC exhibits a three-stage thermal profile. The first event (≈6% mass loss between 25 and 250 °C) is attributed to decomposition of grafted GMA moieties and removal of low-molecular-weight volatiles. The second stage, observed between ‎~‎280–380 °C, corresponds to the decomposition of sulfonic acid functionalities. The third stage (≈400–600 °C) corresponds to degradation of the calixarene backbone and phenolic fragments. Overall, GSC shows slightly improved thermal resistance and a greater residual char than SCA, which is consistent with incorporation of the thermally more stable methacrylate segments. The PA–GSC composite demonstrates a multistep decomposition typical of doped conducting-polymer systems. An initial mass loss from ~50–130 °C is attributable to desorption of moisture and any loosely bound or unreacted monomers. The principal mass-loss stage between ~250–370 °C reflects decomposition of polyaniline oligomers and side-chains, while the final high-temperature event (≈400–580 °C) corresponds to progressive breakdown of the conjugated polymer backbone and associated organic fragments15. Pristine TiO2 shows only minimal weight loss, limited to removal of surface-adsorbed water, as expected for an inorganic oxide. Incorporation of TiO2 into the PA–GSC matrix (TiO2–PA–GSC) produces a composite with improved thermal stability: the overall mass loss is reduced and the onset of major degradation events is shifted to higher temperatures relative to the polymer alone. This stabilizing effect is attributed to the thermally inert TiO2 phase and to interfacial interactions between TiO2 surface sites and the polymer matrix, which can retard chain scission and volatile evolution by acting as a physical barrier and by dissipating thermal energy44.

The microstructure and elemental distribution of SCA, GSC, PA–GSC and TiO2–PA–GSC were examined by SEM, TEM and elemental mapping (Fig. 10). SEM micrographs show that pristine SCA possesses a smooth, plate-like morphology consistent with the ordered packing inferred from XRD (Fig. 10a)33. After GMA grafting (GSC), the surface morphology changes markedly: the original plates become covered by densely packed, nanoscale aggregates (Fig. 10b). This transformation is consistent with covalent attachment of bulky GMA groups to surface hydroxyls, which introduces steric disruption and localized heterogeneity and is expected to increase the material’s specific surface area and provide more anchoring sites for subsequent polymerization. The morphology of the polyaniline-containing samples differs substantially depending on whether TiO2 was present during polymerization. PA–GSC prepared without TiO2 displays a relatively irregular, particulate/porous texture (Fig. 10c). When TiO2 is introduced at the start of aniline polymerization, the resulting TiO2–PA–GSC composite exhibits well-developed, cauliflower-like structures and a more uniform surface organization (Fig. 10d). At the resolution of our SEM imaging, no significant TiO2 agglomerates are visible. We attribute this improved morphological coherence to heterogeneous nucleation and confinement of PANI growth on TiO2 surfaces when the inorganic particles are present during polymer formation, which promotes homogeneous dispersion and interfacial bonding45,46.

Physico-mechanical properties of the prepared coatings

The effect of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite on the cross-linking behavior of UA was assessed by measuring the gel content of UV-cured coatings. As summarized in Table 4, pristine UA exhibited a gel content of 89%, which is consistent with typical values reported for UV-cured acrylate systems15. Previous work indicates that the sol fraction in such systems often results from limited polymer-chain mobility, which hinders double-bond conversion and thus lowers the final gel content of the coating48. Incorporation of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite (NC3) increased the gel content, likely because acrylate functionalities introduced by GSC-doped polyaniline participate in the UV-induced cross-linking, increasing network density15. However, increasing the TiO2–PA–GSC loading from 1 wt% to 1.5 wt% produced a counterintuitive decrease in gel content; this decline is probably due to particle agglomeration, which reduces effective interaction with the UA matrix and impairs uniform cross-linking. Coatings containing the GSC nanocomposite (NC1) showed a pronounced reduction in gel content, indicating markedly lower cross-linking efficiency. This effect can be attributed to hydrophilic sulfonate (SO3⁻) groups that promote nanoparticle agglomeration during film formation and disrupt the homogeneity of the cured network45. Additionally, the PA–GSC-based coating (NC2) exhibited lower gel content than its TiO2–PA–GSC counterpart (NC4), which supports the hypothesis that TiO2 nanoparticle promote more uniform dispersion of PANI in the polymer matrix and thereby facilitates more efficient cross-linking36.

Table 4 presents pull‑off adhesion results for the UV‑cured coatings on steel substrates. Incorporation of the TiO₂–PA–GSC nanocomposite yielded an approximately 22% improvement in adhesion strength compared to the neat UA coating. The obtained adhesion values fall within the typical range reported for UV‑curable water‑based polyurethane systems13,49. This enhancement is attributed to increased cross‑linking density via acrylate‑functionalized PANI and stronger nanocomposite–matrix interactions. Failure analysis consistently showed adhesive failure at the coating–substrate interface for all samples, confirming that separation occurred between the film and the metal substrate.

Introduction of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite raised the decomposition temperature of the hard-segment population, an effect likely caused by covalent or strong interfacial interactions between the nanocomposite and the unsaturated moieties of the UA matrix that improve thermal resistance15. These thermal-stability trends are consistent with the gel-content and adhesion results. Finally, the superior thermal performance of NC4 compared with NC2 further highlights the beneficial role of TiO2 in the nanocomposite, where TiO2 helps reinforce the polymer network and enhance heat resistance under thermal stress52.

Conversely, the steel specimen immersed in the TiO2-PA-GSC extract-containing solution exhibits a transient decrease in charge-transfer resistance (Rct) during the initial hour of exposure relative to the control. Following 48 h of immersion, the total resistance of the steel in the TiO2-PA-GSC extract solution rises markedly to 5096 Ω.cm2, underscoring the extract’s superior long-term corrosion-inhibitive performance59. Anti-corrosion performance of the prepared coatings

EIS was performed to assess the corrosion resistance of the coatings, with long-term measurements confirming the results. Figure 15 presents the Bode and Nyquist plots for coatings immersed in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution for up to 50 days. Protective efficiency correlates with the low-frequency impedance modulus, |Z|0.01 Hz53. Over the 50-day exposure, composite coatings containing PA–GSC and TiO2-PA-GSC (NC2–NC5) maintained significantly higher |Z|0.01 Hz values than the UA (Fig. 15a). This effect is most pronounced for NC4: |Z|0.01 Hz decreased only slightly from 6.06 × 1010 Ω.cm2 to 3.25 × 109 Ω.cm2 after 50 days. NC4 also exhibited the most favorable phase-angle response, consistent with a denser coating that effectively fills pores and minimizes ionic pathways. By comparison, NC3 and NC5 showed lower protection after 50 days, with |Z|0.01 Hz values of 1.65 × 108 Ω.cm2 and 5.24 × 108 Ω.cm2, respectively. The relatively poor long-term performance of NC3 is attributable to insufficient TiO2-PA-GSC loading, while NC5’s reduced efficacy is linked to nanocomposite aggregation that creates channels and defects, facilitating electrolyte ingress. Additionally, the intrinsically hydrophilic character of the GSC dopant (Fig. 15b) may detract from barrier performance when not adequately balanced by TiO2 and cross-link density. Overall, the combined electrochemical and morphological evidence indicates that the optimized TiO2-PA-GSC content (1 wt%, NC4) yields the best balance of dispersion, cross-linking, and barrier properties, delivering superior long-term corrosion protection in saline environments.

Model A describes coatings where corrosive species do not reach the metal surface, whereas Model B applies when they do reach the steel surface15. The fitted parameters are given in Table 9. Rc is a reliable indicator of barrier performance: higher Rc values reflect a stronger barrier to electrolyte penetration. Coatings containing PA-GSC and TiO2-PA-GSC nanocomposites exhibited much smaller reductions in arc diameter over time than the neat polymer, and no clear penetration-failure of the coatings was observed during the 50-day immersion. This behavior indicates that the pathways for corrosive species were not fully opened and that the composite coatings retained effective protection of the steel substrate. The semicircle diameter increased with TiO2-PA-GSC loading up to 1 wt% (NC4), with Rc ≈ 3.42 × 109 Ω.cm2, but excessive loading that causes particle aggregation can compromise coating integrity by creating additional pathways for electrolyte ingress. Among the samples, NC4 showed the largest semicircular arc, consistent with the highest active anticorrosion efficiency. By contrast, NC1 (containing only GSC) displayed a decreasing arc diameter with time and a distinct penetration event after 5 days, suggesting early electrolyte access to the coating/metal interface.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial performance of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite was evaluated using macrodilution and agar‑diffusion assays. Macrodilution measurements revealed strong, selective activity: Assessment against Staphylococcus aureus demonstrated that the control group displayed a substantial increase in optical density from 0.05 to 1.12 over a 24‑hour period, whereas the nanocomposite sample maintained a consistent level at 0.05 (Table 10), indicating 95.6% inhibition of bacterial growth. Similarly, evaluation of Escherichia coli showed that the control group also exhibited growth enhancement, rising from 0.05 to 1.18 within the same timeframe. However, the nanocomposite sample produced only a modest increase to 1.06, representing a mere 10.16% reduction in bacterial growth (Table 10), demonstrating markedly lower efficacy against this Gram‑negative species. Agar-diffusion assays (Fig. 17a-d) produced consistent results. The neat UA film showed no antibacterial activity against either organism (no inhibition zone). In contrast, the NC4 film (UA containing 1 wt% TiO2–PA–GSC) produced a clear inhibition zone against S. aureus with a diameter of 12 ± 1 mm, but no inhibition zone was observed for E. coli. This zone arises because reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite at the film surface diffuse into the agar, establishing a concentration gradient that inhibits S. aureus growth where the ROS concentration exceeds the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Together, the macrodilution and agar-diffusion data confirm the selective antibacterial behavior of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite, likely reflecting differences in cell-envelope architecture and permeability between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.

The antibacterial activity of the TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite and the NC4 film arises from a synergistic combination of PANI and TiO₂. The antibacterial activity of Polyaniline is mainly attributed to surface-mediated contact with bacterial cells, leading to membrane disruption and loss of cellular integrity. In addition, as a conductive and redox-active polymer, PANI may induce limited oxidative stress at the bacteria–material interface under ambient conditions, which can further contribute to bacterial inactivation62,63.

TiO₂ contributes to the antibacterial performance mainly through surface-related effects within the nanocomposite. In TiO₂/PANI systems, polyaniline can act as a conductive polymer that facilitates interfacial charge transfer with TiO₂, which may improve charge separation at the interface and modify the electronic structure of the composite. As a result of this synergistic interaction, limited formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may occur under ambient conditions. However, no external UV irradiation was applied in the present study; therefore, UV-induced photocatalytic ROS generation is not involved under the experimental conditions58,64.

The time-kill assay revealed a time-dependent bactericidal effect of the NC4 film against Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive): the viable cell count decreased substantially over the exposure period (Fig. 17e), consistent with inhibition of biofilm formation on the film surface. By contrast, NC4 showed no bactericidal activity against Escherichia coli (Gram-negative); viable counts increased with time under the same conditions. The observed selectivity is likely due to structural differences between the two groups: Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer membrane that restricts penetration of TiO2 nanoparticles and other agents, whereas Gram-positive bacteria lack this barrier and are therefore more vulnerable to membrane disruption and oxidative damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by TiO2. Protonated PANI may additionally enhance activity against Gram-positive cells by promoting electrostatic adhesion to the negatively charged cell envelope and facilitating localized ROS-mediated or contact-mediated membrane disruption65. Together, these effects explain the selective, time-dependent antibacterial performance of NC4.

Conclusion

A TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite was incorporated at 1 wt% into a UV-curable waterborne polyurethane-acrylate matrix to create multifunctional protective coatings. The optimized formulation (NC4) exhibited significantly enhanced corrosion resistance, mechanical properties, thermal stability, and selective antibacterial activity compared to the neat polymer and other loadings. After 50 days in 3.5% NaCl, electrochemical tests showed over 100-fold increase in low-frequency impedance and markedly reduced corrosion rates. Equivalent circuit analysis revealed a high coating resistance (Rc ≈ 3.42 × 109 Ω.cm2), indicating improved barrier properties and durability. These improvements result from synergistic effects: polyaniline forms a redox-active passive film and inhibits corrosion contact; TiO2 nanoparticles increase tortuosity, fill voids, and trap charge at the PANI/TiO2 interface; and the GSC dopant enhances dispersion, cross-linking, and iron ion complexation to block corrosion pathways. Higher nanocomposite loadings caused aggregation and reduced protection. Antibacterial tests confirmed selective activity against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus, due to combined PANI-induced membrane disruption and TiO2-generated reactive oxygen species. Overall, the 1 wt% TiO2–PA–GSC nanocomposite offers an optimal balance of multifunctional performance, making it a promising coating for harsh saline environments requiring simultaneous corrosion and antibacterial protection.
