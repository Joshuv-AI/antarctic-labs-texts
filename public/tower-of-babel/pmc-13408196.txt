Phytochemical profiling and in-vitro antibacterial activity of Croton macrostachyus Hochst. ex Delile leaf and stem bark extracts against selected human pathogens
Authors: Getayawukal Yohanis, Krishna Moorthy Sivalingam, Gedif Meseret Abebe, Mukunthan Sankar Narayanan, Fekredin Argaw Bartuga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedicinal plants have long been utilized for their antiseptic, antiallergic, antibacterial, and antiviral properties in the treatment of human diseases. Croton macrostachyus is a prominent medicinal plant widely used in traditional African medicine for the treatment of various diseases like malaria, tuberculosis, cough, diabetes, rabies and dysentery. This study aimed to investigate the in-vitro antibacterial activity of ethanol, methanol and ethyl acetate extracts derived from the leaves and stem bark of C. macrostachyus against selected human bacterial pathogens. Crude extracts were prepared using the maceration method with the aforementioned solvents. Antibacterial activity, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were determined following clinical laboratory standards institute guidelines. Additionally, qualitative phytochemical analyses were also performed using the standard protocols. All extracts exhibited the inhibitory against Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus, with S. aureus showing the highest susceptibility. The antibacterial effect was concentration dependent, and leaf extracts demonstrated greater activity compared to stem bark extracts. Among the tested solvents, the methanolic crude extracts ahowed the strongest antibacterial activity. The MIC values of leaf extracts ranged from 0.625 to 2.25 mg/mL while MBC values ranged from 1.25 to 5 mg/mL. Phytochemical analysis of leaf extracts revealed the presence of tannins, alkaloids saponins, flavonoids, glycosides and phenolic compounds in ethanol and methanol based extraction, whereas alkaloids were absent in ethyl acetate based extraction. In contrast stem bark extracts contained glycosides across all solvents, while alkaloids, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds were absent. Tannins and saponins were detected in ethanol and ethyl acetate extracts but not in methanol extracts. Therefore, the present research concludes that the leaf extracts of C. macrostachyus exhibited highest antibacterial activity compared to stem bark extracts. Introduction

Medicinal plants constitute a rich source of naturally occurring nutrients and biologically active compounds synthesized during growth and, in part, accumulated from their surrounding environment. These phytochemicals including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolics, and terpenoids are associated with a wide range of pharmacological activities. Beyond their nutritional value, many medicinal plants are consumed as part of the human diet and have long been utilized in traditional medical systems in diverse therapeutic formulations. Consequently, they represent an important reservoir of bioactive substances and continue to serve as valuable resources for the development of novel therapeutic agents1.

In Ethiopia, up to 80% of the population relies on traditional medicine for primary healthcare. This dependence is largely driven by strong cultural traditions, community trust in the therapeutic efficacy of traditional remedies, limited access to modern healthcare services, and the relative affordability of plant-based treatments. However, the widespread perception that medicinal plants are inherently safe due to their natural origin is often misleading. Although their long history of use contributes to this belief, safety and efficacy are not always supported by rigorous scientific evidence, particularly in the absence of comprehensive regulatory frameworks2. Furthermore, the extensive use of diverse plant species by traditional healers underscores the need for systematic scientific validation, safety assessment, and standardization3.

Croton macrostachyus Hochst. ex Delile is a deciduous pioneer tree belonging to the Euphorbiaceae family, which comprises approximately 300 genera and 8,000–10,000 species predominantly distributed in tropical regions. The species is native to several African countries, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda. It is widely used in traditional medicine for the management of gastrointestinal disorders, infectious diseases such as malaria and typhoid, as well as wounds and skin infections. In addition, it is employed in ethno-veterinary practices. These diverse applications highlight its potential pharmacological significance and the need for further scientific investigation4.

The rise of antibiotic resistance is primarily driven by the overuse and misuse of antibiotics, along with limited development of new drugs due to economic and regulatory challenges5. As a result, medicinal plants are increasingly recognized as important sources of natural antimicrobial compounds with potential to combat resistant bacterial infections. Bacterial infectious diseases continue to pose a major global public health challenge, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic options6.

Bacterial pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are responsible for as wide range of human infections7. The increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance, along with concerns related to drug toxicity, has limited the effectiveness of conventional antimicrobial agents8. This has rehabilitated interest in exploring plant based antimicrobials, which are considered to offer comparable efficacy with potentially improved safety profiles9,10. Medicinal plants would be the best source to obtain a variety of drugs and therefore, present study aims to evaluate the in vitro antibacterial activity of leaf and stem bark extracts of Croton macrostachyus against selected human bacterial pathogens, thereby contributing to the scientific validation of its traditional use.

Materials and methods

Study design

An in vitro experimental study was conducted at Wolaita Sodo University, Ethiopia from January 2022 to April 2023. The leaf and stem bark plants were collected and the antibacterial activity of the plants was conducted in the Post Graduate Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Biology, Wolaita Sodo University. Antibacterial activities were determined by using agar well diffusion, minimum inhibitor concentrations, and minimum bactericidal concentrations (broth dilution) method. The extraction of plants was done by maceration methods using three different solvents; ethanol, methanol, and ethyl acetate. A 2.5% solution of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as negative control to conform that the solvent had no inhibitory effects against the tested bacterial pathogens, and tetracycline (30 µg) was used as positive control to monitor the antibacterial activities of stem bark and leaf extracts of plants against human bacterial pathogens.

Permission and ethical compliance statement

Plant sample used in this study was collected in compliance with all relevant national and institutional regulations. Permission for sample collection was obtained from the appropriate local landowners prior to sampling. The study did not involve endangered or protected species, and no protected areas were accessed.

The conservation status of Croton macrostachyus was assessed according to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species. Croton macrostachyus is currently categorized as Least Concern (LC) under the IUCN Red List criteria, indicating that it is not considered at risk of extinction in the wild at the global level. Sample collection for this study was conducted in accordance with IUCN guidelines and did not involve species listed as threatened, nor did it adversely impact natural populations.

Plant material collection and identification

The plant samples of Croton macrostachyus collected around Humbo Larena St. Rufael church located at a latitude and longitude of 6°46’45"N and 37°45’43"E respectively in Larena village. The fresh plant leaf and stem bark samples of C. macrostachyus were collected in plastic bags and transported to the Department of Biology, Wolaita Sodo University. The little bit collected plant specimens were used for authentication process. The collected sample was identified as Croton macrostachyus by Dr. Melaku Wendaferash a botanist in the Department of Plant Biology, at Addis Ababa University, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia as per the IUCN Guidelines. The voucher specimens were deposited to the National Herbarium of Ethiopia with Code No. (GY001).

Preparation of crude extract

Plant extracts were prepared in accordance with methods described by Basri and Fan11. Briefly, the dried leaf and stem bark samples were ground to a fine powder using an electrical grinder. The powder was stored in an airtight container and kept in the refrigerator at 4 °C until used for further processing. The crude extracts of leaf and stem bark were prepared by using three different solvents such as Ethanol, ethyl acetate, and methanol. For the preparation of crude extracts, 80 g of leaf and stem bark powdered was mixed with 400 ml of solvent individually. All the flasks were marked properly with the name of the solvent and type of plant materials. The flasks containing the mixture were wrapped in aluminum foil to avoid contamination12. All the flasks were incubated under a shaker incubator at 170 rpm for 72 h. The resulting mixture after the incubation period was filtered with Whatman No1 and air-dried for seven days. The resultant dried extracts were considered as crude extracts. The yield of the crude extracts was calculated and preserved with 10 ml of DMSO at 4 °C for further analysis13,14.

Phytochemical screening tests

Preliminary phytochemical screening of the crude extract was carried out using the following standard methods to detect the presence of major bioactive constituents such as tannins, saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, and phenolics.

Foam test for saponins

Approximately 0.5 g of crude extract was mixed with 10mL of distilled water and shaken vigorously for 2 min. The persistence of stable foam for at least 30 min was considered indicative of the presence of saponins16.

Mayer’s test for alkaloids

About 0.5 g of powdered crude extract was boiled with 10mL acidified alcohol for 2 min. After cooling, the mixture was filtered. A 5mL aliquot of the filtrate was treated with 2mL of dilute ammonia solution, followed by the addition of 5mL of chloroform and mixed gently. The resulting solution was then treated with Mayer’s reagent. The formation of a yellowish creamy precipitate indicated the presence of alkaloids17.

Keller killiani test for glycosides

Approximately 0.5 g of crude extract was mixed with a 2mL of Glacial acetic acid, few drops of ferric chlorides (FeCl3) solution and 1mL of concentrate sulphuric acid. The formation of a reddish brown ring at the interface indicated the presence of glycosides18.

Ammonia test for phenols

About 3 mL of crude extract was put in a test tube and treated with a few drops of dilute ammonia solution. Bluish-green coloration indicated the presence of phenols19.

Alkaline reagent test for flavonoids

About 3 mL of crude extract was treated with a few drops of 10% sodium hydroxide solution and incubated in a hot water bath for 10 min. The presence of flavonoid was identified by the formation of an intense yellow color that becomes colorless with the addition of few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid20.

Ferric chloride test for tannins

Approximately 0.5 g of powdered crude extract was mixed with 10mL of distilled water and heated to boiling. The mixer was filtered while hot, and a few drops of 0.1% ferric chloride solution were added to the filtrate. The formation of brownish green or blue-black color indicated the presence of tannins20.

Antibacterial activity test

Bacterial culture collection and preparation

The American type culture collection bacterial strains such as Escherichia coli O157:H7, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC27853), and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC25923) were obtained from Ethiopia Biodiversity Institute, Addis Abeba, Ethiopia. All the above-mentioned cultures were checked properly for their purity and viability before use. The loop full of purified bacterial colony was transferred into a test tube containing sterilized normal saline and standardized with sterile saline to a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland using a UV Visible spectrophotometer at 600nm21.

Crude extract dilution

A Stock Solution at 200 mg/ml was prepared by reconstituting 1 g of each of the extracts in 5 mL of DMSO (Dimethyl Sulfoxide). Two-fold serial dilutions were then performed to using sterile DMSO to obtain the concentrations of 100, 50, 25, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, and 0.625 mg/ml16,22.

Antibacterial assay

Antibacterial activity of crude leaf and stem bark extracts was determined by agar well diffusion assay23. The sterilized Mueller Hinton agar (MHA) was transferred to the sterilized Petri dishes and allowed to solidify. After the solidification, perfect 6 mm diameter wells were prepared and 0.1 ml (10− 7 CFU/mL) of the selected bacterial cultures was inoculated on the MHA. All the wells were filled with 50 µl of the prepared crude leaf and stem bark extracts at different concentrations such as 100, 50, and 25 mg/ml. The antibiotic tetracycline (30 µg/disc) was used as a positive control, and 50µL of DMSO solution was considered as a negative control. All the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and then examined for the zone of inhibitions24. The zone of inhibition measured in millimeter (mm) and classified in to three categories based on the inhibition zone of positive control tetracycline (30 µg/disc): sensitive or susceptible (≥ 19 mm zone), intermediate (15–18 mm), and resistant (≤ 14 mm)25.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC is defined as the lowest concentration where no visible turbidity is observed in the test tube (bacteriostatic concentration). The broth dilution technique was employed to determine the MIC of the crude extracts. A stock solution was prepared at 200 mg/L in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subjected to two-fold serially dilution using nutrient broth to obtain the working concentrations such as 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, and 0.625 mg/ml. Each tube was inoculated with 0.2 ml of the test organism suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. The least concentration that inhibited the visible turbidity was observed and noted as the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value26,27.

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MBC was determined by nutrient agar plating techniques (Re-culturing) using the test tube cultures from the MIC experiment. In this technique, a loop full of bacterial culture from the test tubes obtained from the MIC was streaked on the antimicrobial agents free nutrient agar media, and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. The lowest concentration of the extract which showed no bacterial growth was noted and recorded as the MBC28.

Data analysis

The mean ± standard deviation (SD) values of the C. macrostachyus were compared using descriptive statistics. All statistical analyses (mean and standard deviation) were performed using the Statistical Package of Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. Differences were considered statistically significant at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Discussion

Nature has provided an immense diversity of plant species with significant therapeutic potential. Medicinal plants have long been recognized as valuable sources of bioactive compounds used in the prevention and treatment of human disease, Microbial infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and the rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance has become a serious global public health concern. The development of multi-drug resistant microorganisms has reduce the efficacy of currently available antibiotics, emphasizing the urgent need to discover novel, safe, and effective antimicrobial agents from plant sources29,30.

The present study evaluated the antibacterial activity of crude leaf and stem bark extracts of C. macrostachyus prepared using three different solvent systems. The extraction yield varied depending on solvent polarity and plant part. Methanolic extracts of both leaf and stem bark produced higher yields compared to ethanolic and ethyl acetate extracts, with leaf and stem bark yield of 1.89 g (2.3%) and 3.33 g (4.1%) respectively. This observation confirms that polar solvents enhance extraction efficiency by dissolving a broader range of phytochemicals31. Variations in extraction yield compared to other studies many be attributed to environmental factors, plant age, and methodological differences32.

Phytochemical screening revealed that methanolic and ethanolic leaf extracts contained a wide range of secondary metabolites, including tannins, saponins, flavonoids, glycosides, phenolics and alkaloids, whereas alkaloids were absent in the ethyl acetate leaf extract. In contrast, stem bark extracts lacked alkaloids and flavonoids, and ethyl acetate extracts showed minimal phytochemical presence. These finding suggest that both solvent polarity and plant part significantly influence phytochemical composition. The detected phyto-constituents are known to possess diverse pharmaceutical properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antimicrobial activities33–35. In particular, compounds such as flavonoids, saponins, tannins, and alkaloids are reported to contribute to antimicrobial activity through mechanisms such as cell membrane disruption, enzyme inhibition, and interference with microbial metabolism36–38.

The antibacterial activity results demonstrated that leaf extracts exhibited stronger activity compared to stem bark extracts. Among the solvents, methanolic leaf extract showed the highest antibacterial activity, particularly at 100 mg/mL concentration. The largest zone of inhibition (25.47 ± 0.90 mm) was observed against Staphylococcus aureus, followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (21.80 ± 0.83 mm) and Escherichia coli (19.61 ± 0.33 mm). This indicates that gram-positive bacteria were more susceptible than gram-negative bacterial pathogens, likely due to structural differences in their cell walls, where the outer membrane in gram-negative bacteria acts as a permeability barrier. These finding are consistent with previous report demonstrating higher susceptibility of S. aureus compared to E. coli31,32. Notably, the inhibition zone produced by methanolic extract at higher concentration was comparable to those of standard antibiotic, indicating substantial antibacterial potential. Consistent with present findings, previous studies have reported that methanolic leaf extracts of C. macrostachyus exhibit antibacterial activity against both gram negative and gram positive bacteria. In agreement with these findings, the methanolic leaf extracts of C. macrostachyus produced antibacterial activity against E. coli. Similarly, the methanolic leaf extracts demonstrated larges inhibition zone against E. coli and S. aureus, suggesting a high antibacterial potency of the plant derived compounds39,40. This further reinforces the superior efficacy of methanolic extracts observed in the current study.

Ethanolic leaf extracts also showed considerable antibacterial activity, although slightly lower than methanolic extracts, while ethyl acetate extracts exhibited minimal activity, with most bacterial strains showing resistance even at higher concentrations. This suggests that the effectiveness of extraction solvents is closely related to their ability to solubilize active phytochemicals. These findings are in agreement with earlier studies reporting superior antimicrobial activity of methanolic extracts, while other solvent system produced less activity41,42. However, the low activity observed in ethyl acetate extracts contradicts the findings of Kebede and Shibeshi43 who reported higher antibacterial activity for ethyl acetate extracts of Ricinus communis, indicating that solvent effectiveness may vary depending on plant species and chemical composition.

In contrast to leaf extracts, stem bark extracts exhibited relatively lower antibacterial activity across all solvents. Although methanolic stem bark extract showed moderate (Intermediate) activity against S. aureus and E. coli at higher concentrations, most inhibition zones fell within resistant to intermediate ranges. Ethyl acetate stem bark extracts were largely ineffective, showing minimal inhibition. This observation contrast with the finding of Ogueke et al.44, who reported higher antibacterial activity of stem bark extracts of Alstonia boonei, but aligns with findings indicating lower antibacterial potency in C. macrostachyus stem bark45 who reported that crude ethanolic stem bark extracts produced lower zone of inhibition against Escherichia coli and Listeria monocytogenes and no activity was observed against Staphylococcus aureus.

The MIC and MBC data further support the efficiency of methanolic leaf extract of C. macrostachyus, which exhibited the lowest inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations (MIC: 0.625–1.25 mg/mL; MBC: 1.25 mg/mL) against all tested pathogens. In contrast, stem bark extracts required higher concentrations to achieve similar effects, indicating comparatively lower potency. These findings are notably stronger than several previous reports. For example, Wagate et al.46 documented MIC values ranging from 15.6 to 250 mg/mL for methanolic extracts of C. macrostachyus, while stem bark extracts reported to exhibit MIC values between 125 mg/mL, and 250 mg/mL, consistent with finding by Suffredini et al.47. Similarly, obey et al.48 reported high MIC values for methanolic bark extracts against E> coli (250 mg/mL) and K. pneumonia (500 mg/mL), indicating weaker antibacterial activity compared to the present study. Conversely, the MIC value obtained in this study are comparable with lower values reported by Ayelate et al.40 and Romha et al.49 against E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus. The MBC results obtained in the present study (1.25–5 mg/mL) demonstrates that C. macrostachyus extracts, particularly methanolic leaf extracts, possess strong bactericidal activity at relatively low concentrations. These values are notably lower than those reported for some combination studies, where the synergistic used of C. macrostachyus with other medicinal plants such as Calpurina aurea and Ocimum gratissimum resulted in MBC values ranging from 6.25 mg/mL to 25 mg/mL depending on the bacterial species39.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that extraction solvent and plant part significantly influence the antibacterial activity of C. macrostachyus. Methanolic extraction yielded the highest amount of crude extract and recovered a broad range of phytochemicals, particularly in leaf extract. The methanolic and ethanolic leaf extracts exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity in a concentration dependent manner, with Staphylococcus aureus being the most susceptible organism. In contrast, stem bark showed comparatively lower activity. The low MIC (0.625 mg/mL) and MBC (1.25 mg/mL) values of methanolic leaf extracts further confirm their antibacterial potency. Overall, the findings indicate that methanolic leaf extracts of C. macrostachyus are a promising source of bioactive compounds and support the traditional use of this plant in the treatment of bacterial infections. Further research should focus on the identifying and characterizing the specific phytochemical responsible for the observed antibacterial activities, as well as evaluating their therapeutic indices to assess safety and efficacy, thereby supporting their potential for pharmaceutical development.

Limitations

Due to lack of finance and poor laboratory infrastructures, the study was not extended to the level of advanced phytochemical identification process like NMR, GCMS and LCMS. This facility is not available throughout out our country.
