Sensitivity of ODE Solutions and Quantities of Interest with Respect to Component Functions in the Dynamics Thanks: To appear in SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis
Jonathan R. Cangelosi, Matthias Heinkenschloss

Abstract
This work analyzes the sensitivities of the solution of a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and a corresponding quantity of interest (QoI) to perturbations in a state-dependent component function that appears in the governing ODEs. This extends existing ODE sensitivity results, which consider the sensitivity of the ODE solution with respect to state-independent parameters. It is shown that with Carathéodory-type assumptions on the ODEs, the Implicit Function Theorem can be applied to establish continuous Fréchet differentiability of the ODE solution with respect to the component function. These sensitivities are used to develop new estimates for the change in the ODE solution or QoI when the component function is perturbed. In applications, this new sensitivity-based bound on the ODE solution or QoI error is often much tighter than classical Gronwall-type error bounds. The sensitivity-based error bounds are applied to a trajectory simulation for a hypersonic vehicle.

Introduction

Many applications are modeled by systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
in which some solution-dependent component functions are not exactly known.
In such cases, it is crucial to determine the sensitivity of the ODE solution or of a
quantity of interest (QoI) with respect to perturbations in the component functions. If bounds for the errors in the component functions are known, this sensitivity information can be used to estimate the error
in the ODE solution or the QoI.
For example, the trajectory of an aircraft can be modeled by a system
of ODEs including lift and drag coefficients, which are functions that themselves depend on the trajectory of the
aircraft. Often only values of lift and drag coefficients at some points are available, e.g., from experiments or
computationally expensive CFD simulations, and approximate lift and drag coefficients
are obtained from interpolation or regression for numerical solution of the ODEs.
See, e.g.,
[
4
, Sec. 6.2]
or

[
6
]
.
In this case, one wants to estimate the error between the solution of the ODE system with the true component function
and the computable solution of the ODE system with the approximate component function.

In this paper, we first establish the Fréchet differentiability (in suitable function spaces)
of the ODE solution with respect to these component functions.
This result is then used to provide a new sensitivity-based estimate for the error between ODE solutions computed with the
approximate and true component functions
and a corresponding sensitivity-based estimate for the error in a QoI depending on the ODE solution.
These error estimates are crucial to determine whether the given approximate component function
is of sufficient quality. If it is not, then the error estimate could be used to determine in which regions of the solution
space the approximate component function needs to be improved.
In applications, our new sensitivity-based error estimates can produce superior estimates compared to classical
ODE perturbation estimates, which depend exponentially on the logarithmic Lipschitz constant of the ODE system
and on the length of the time interval considered.

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}\Big(t,\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{g}\big(t,\mathbf{x}(t)\big)\Big),

\displaystyle\mbox{almost all (a.a.) }t\in I,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=x_{0},

Sensitivity analyses of the solution of an ODE

At the heart of our analysis is the continuous Fréchet differentiability of a (somewhat nonstandard) superposition or Nemytskii operator.

In

, we will use the sensitivity results of
section

to establish a new approximate upper bound for the error

\|\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})\|
(in some suitable norm or seminorm) given a pointwise bound for
|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}|
(understood componentwise).
Classical ODE perturbation results such as those in

[
9
, Sec. I.10]
,
[
13
]
provide
a bound for the error
\mathbf{x}(t;\mathbf{g}_{*})-\mathbf{x}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})

. However, this bound can be very pessimistic, especially
when

becomes larger. In our example shown in
section

, this

. This has motivated the sensitivity-based bound
we will develop in

3.2
. The idea is to approximate

\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})\approx\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}),

where
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\mathbf{g}\mapsto\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g})
at
\mathbf{g}=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
,
then use a bound of the error in the component function along the
computed trajectory
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})
to obtain an upper bound for the error estimate
\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})\|
(in some appropriate norm or seminorm), which is an approximate upper bound of
\|\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})\|
when
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}
is relatively small.
Specifically, if

\big|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)-\mathbf{g}_{*}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\big|\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I,

where the absolute value and the inequality are applied componentwise and
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}}
is an error bound for the component function, we formulate and solve a linear quadratic optimal control
problem to obtain an approximate upper bound for
\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})\|
using
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)
,
t\in I
.
In our examples shown in
section

4
, this new bound provides excellent estimates for the error

\|\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})\|
provided
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)
is a relatively tight upper bound
for
\big|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)-\mathbf{g}_{*}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\big|
and
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
is relatively close to
\mathbf{g}_{*}
.

Our new (approximate) bound for the ODE solution error
\|\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})\|

comes at the cost of solving a linear quadratic optimal control problem, which has some theoretical shortcomings that will be discussed in
section

3.2
.
However, in applications where the evaluation of the true
\mathbf{g}_{*}
is computationally expensive
but the evaluation of an approximate surrogate
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
is not, the extra expense of solving the
linear quadratic optimal control problem is less expensive than working with the true
\mathbf{g}_{*}
and
yields good estimates for the ODE solution error in practice.
We are utilizing this in other work to adapt surrogate models for
\mathbf{g}_{*}
from evaluations of
\mathbf{g}_{*}
at
points
(t,x)\in I\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
along the current trajectory.

The linear quadratic optimal control problem can be avoided if only an estimate for the error in a
quantity of interest
\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g}):=q\big(\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}),\mathbf{g}\big)
is desired. Instead of sensitivities,
a so-called adjoint equation can be used to express the Fréchet derivative of
\mathbf{g}\mapsto\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g})
.
As we show in
section

3.3
, our approximate upper bound for the error
|\widetilde{q}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g}_{*})|
can be obtained by solving a linear program with a simple analytical solution at the expense of one linear adjoint ODE solve. This approach avoids the theoretical issues associated with the linear quadratic optimal control problem and yields a much more easily computable error bound.

Problem Setting

We seek solutions of the IVP

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{G}^{k}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}:=\big\{\mathbf{g}:I\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}}\;:\;

of (

Suppose that we can only access an approximation

of (

In this section we discuss estimates of the size of the solution

|\widetilde{q}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g}_{*})|=\big|q\big(\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}}),\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big)-q\big(\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*}),\mathbf{g}_{*}\big)\big|

in a quantity of interest (

Assume that all possible state trajectories are known to be contained in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
.
This allows to incorporate
a priori
knowledge of the system, but
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is possible.
Furthermore, we assume that we have a componentwise error bound

and all
x\in\Omega
.

Classical ODE perturbation theory provides an estimate for the solution error

given a norm error bound

\|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(t,x)-\mathbf{g}_{*}(t,x)\|\leq\epsilon(t,x),\quad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I\mbox{ and all }x\in\Omega,

(3.2)

which will be reviewed next. Unfortunately, in many cases, this classical solution error bound
is extremely pessimistic and useless in practice. This has motivated our new
estimates based on sensitivity analysis, which will be presented in

section

3.2
and
section

3.3

3.2
Sensitivity-Based Error Estimation for ODE Solution

We will use sensitivities to estimate the error

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}:=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\big(\mathbf{x}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(t;\mathbf{g}_{*})\big)^{T}\mathbf{Q}(t)\big(\mathbf{x}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(t;\mathbf{g}_{*})\big)\,dt

(3.7)

with a user-specified matrix-valued function
\mathbf{Q}\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
such that for almost all
t\in I
the matrix
\mathbf{Q}(t)
is symmetric positive semidefinite, with corresponding (semi)norm
\|x\|_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}=\sqrt{x^{T}\mathbf{Q}(t)x}
.

Recall from Theorem
9
that under Assumption
3
, the solution
\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g})\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}

of (
1.1
) is continuously Fréchet differentiable with respect to
\mathbf{g}\in\big(\mathcal{G}^{2}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
.
We approximate

\mathbf{x}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{g}_{*})=\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})+\int_{0}^{1}\big[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{g}_{*}+s(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}))-\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\big](\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})\,ds\approx\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}).

(3.8)

If we knew
\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}:=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}
, then
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}
could be computed as
the solution of (
2.11
) with the current
\mathbf{g}=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
and
\delta\mathbf{g}=\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}
.
However, the sensitivity equations (
2.11
) require
\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)
,
t\in I
, which is expensive,
but from the componentwise error bound (
3.1
) we
know that

\big|\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t))\big|\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I.

(3.9)

We assume that
t\mapsto\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
.
Motivated by the error estimate (
3.8
), the
sensitivity equation (
2.11
) with the current
\mathbf{g}=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
and
\delta\mathbf{g}=\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}
,
and the model error bound (
3.9
), we consider the following optimization problem to obtain an approximate upper bound on the error measure (
3.7
):

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta\mathbf{x},\delta\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\|\delta\mathbf{x}(t)\|_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}^{2}\,dt

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(t)\delta\mathbf{x}(t)+\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)\delta\mathbf{g}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I,

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=0,

\displaystyle-\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\leq\delta\mathbf{g}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I,

where
\widehat{\mathbf{A}},\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
are given by (
2.8
) with
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{g}
replaced by
\widehat{\mathbf{x}},\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
respectively.

The idea behind (
3.10
) is that we consider all possible model perturbations that obey the pointwise error bound along the nominal trajectory and use the sensitivity equation to determine the worst-case perturbation in the corresponding ODE solution.
The composition
\delta\mathbf{g}\big(\cdot,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(\cdot)\big)
can be replaced
by a function
\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
, yielding the linear quadratic optimal control problem

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\delta}}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\|\delta\mathbf{x}(t)\|_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}^{2}\,dt

(3.11a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(t)\delta\mathbf{x}(t)+\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)\boldsymbol{\delta}(t),

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I,

(3.11b)

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=0,

(3.11c)

\displaystyle-\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\leq\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I.

(3.11d)

Theorem 15
.

The problems (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) are equivalent.

Proof.

Since the objective function depends only on
\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
, it is sufficient to show that
every feasible point of (
3.10
) corresponds to a feasible point of (
3.11
) and vice versa.
If the pair
\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
,
\delta\mathbf{g}\in\big(\mathcal{G}^{2}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
is feasible for (
3.10
),
then define
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t):=\delta\mathbf{g}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)
. The pair

\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
is feasible for (
3.11
).
On the other hand, if the pair
\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
is feasible for (
3.11
),
then define
\delta\mathbf{g}(t,x):=\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)
. The pair
\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
,
\delta\mathbf{g}\in\big(\mathcal{G}^{2}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
is feasible for
(
3.10
).

The problem (
3.11
) is a convex linear quadratic optimal control problem,
but we seek a
supremum
rather than an infimum; therefore, standard techniques for establishing existence of solutions cannot be applied here.
Moreover, if a solution exists, it is not unique; for instance, if
\boldsymbol{\delta}
solves (
3.11
), then
-\boldsymbol{\delta}
does as well.
After discretizing (
3.11
), the resulting finite-dimensional linearly constrained quadratic program (LCQP) has a solution.
However, the finite-dimensional LCQP is NP-hard,
as it is a convex maximization problem and therefore has optimal solutions at the vertices of the feasible polyhedron, the number of which grows exponentially
with the problem dimension. See, e.g.,
[
3
]
,

[
5
]
,
[
10
]
.
Despite these issues, for discretizations of (
3.11
),
we can often compute an approximate discretized state-control pair
(\delta\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\delta})
whose objective value is close
to the maximum
using a tailored interior point method that exploits the symmetry of the discretized box constraints (
3.11d
).

Because we assume
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
has already been computed and the error bound function
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}}

can be evaluated inexpensively for almost all
t\in I
and all
x\in\Omega
,
the linear quadratic optimal control problem (
3.11
) can be set up inexpensively.

If an optimal solution to (
3.11
) exists, then the optimal objective function value
is an upper bound of the size of the error estimate

\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})
.

Theorem 16
.

If
\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\big(L^{\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}
and
\delta\mathbf{x}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}
solve (
3.11
),
then

\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\big\|[\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})](t)\big\|_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}^{2}\,dt\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\big\|\delta\mathbf{x}(t)\big\|_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}^{2}\,dt.

Proof.

Because of the model error bound (
3.9
),

\boldsymbol{\delta}(t):=\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)-\mathbf{g}_{*}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)

satisfies (
3.11d
), and

\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}\in\big(W^{1,\infty}(I)\big)^{n_{x}}

is a corresponding feasible state for (
3.11
).
Thus, their objective function value is less than or equal to the optimal objective function value,
which is the desired inequality.

In cases where (
3.11
) has no solution, we may at best obtain an approximate upper bound by taking a sufficiently fine discretization of (
3.11
) and solving the resulting LCQP.
In the numerical examples shown in Section
4
and in the supplement, we still obtain excellent estimates for the solution error despite this theoretical shortcoming.

Remark 17
.

If, instead of the componentwise error bound (
3.1
), we have
a norm error bound (
3.2
), then the control constraints (
3.11d
)
have to be replaced by

\|\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\|\leq\epsilon\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),\quad\mbox{ a.a.}\;t\in I.

(3.12)

Theorem
16
remains valid after this change of control constraints.
However, depending on the choice of norm in (
3.12
), the
resulting optimal control problem may be more difficult to solve than (
3.11
),
which is why we have focused on componentwise error bounds (
3.1
).

3.3
Sensitivity-Based Error Estimation for Quantity of Interest

In the previous two subsections the goal was to analyze the solution error
\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{x}(\cdot\,;\mathbf{g}_{*})

Often, however, we are interested in a quantity of interest (

(3.13)

We proceed as in the previous section.

Under the assumptions of

11

\mathbf{g}\in\big(\mathcal{G}^{2}(I)\big)^{n_{g}}

If we knew
\delta\mathbf{g}_{*}=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}

Specifically, if the matrices
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(t)

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}

where

\displaystyle-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(t)^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(t)+\nabla_{x}l\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)+\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{x}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)^{T}\nabla_{g}l\Big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\Big),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(t_{f})

\displaystyle\max_{\delta\mathbf{g}}\quad

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\Big[\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(t)+\nabla_{g}l\Big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\Big)\Big]^{T}\delta\mathbf{g}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\,dt\,\Big|

(3.15a)

s.t.

\displaystyle-\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\leq\delta\mathbf{g}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\leq\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big),

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\Big[\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(t)+\nabla_{g}l\Big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\Big)\Big]^{T}\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\,dt\,\Big|

3.16

Theorem 18
.

The problems (
3.15
) and (

The linear program (

\pm\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\Big|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)^{T}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(t)+\nabla_{g}l\Big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t),\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\Big)\Big|^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\big(t,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\big)\,dt,

Proof.

The equivalence of (
3.15

3.16

5
Conclusions and Future Work

We have applied the Implicit Function Theorem in an appropriate function space setting to obtain rigorous sensitivity results for the solution of an ODE with respect to a
state-dependent component function, and we used these findings to develop sensitivity-based error estimates and bounds for the ODE solution and for a quantity of interest that depends on the ODE solution.
The sensitivity-based error bound for the ODE solution was shown to significantly outperform error bounds from classical ODE perturbation theory.

The sensitivity-based upper bounds for the ODE solution error and QoI error given (resp.) by
theorem

16
and

theorem

19
give a computationally inexpensive way to assess the quality of the computed solution to (
1.1
)
when an approximation
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
is used in place of the true model
\mathbf{g}_{*}
and an inexpensive pointwise error bound is available for
|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*}|
.
This can be leveraged to develop a
sensitivity-driven
method for adaptively constructing surrogate models from high-fidelity data, which can be used to simulate a dynamical system using surrogates while still ensuring a high-quality solution; this will be explored in a forthcoming paper. Future work will also focus on obtaining sensitivity analysis results for solutions of optimal control problems with surrogates entering in the dynamics. The function spaces used for sensitivity analysis in this paper were chosen with these future extensions in mind.

The reason why the true QoI error and the sensitivity differ by a factor of approximately two for small
\epsilon
is that

\widetilde{q}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\frac{d}{dV}\big(V-\mathbf{V}_{*}(t)\big)^{2}\big|_{V=\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})}\delta\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\,dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}2\big(\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{V}_{*}(t)\big)\delta\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\,dt,

where
\delta\mathbf{V}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}}):=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{g}}(\cdot\,;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})
is the sensitivity of the voltage function.
If
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
is close to
\mathbf{g}_{*}
, then
\delta\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})\approx\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{V}_{*}(t)
, implying that

\widetilde{q}_{\mathbf{g}}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}-\mathbf{g}_{*})\approx 2\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\big(\mathbf{V}(t;\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{V}_{*}(t)\big)^{2}\,dt=2\,\widetilde{q}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})=2\big(\widetilde{q}(\widehat{\mathbf{g}})-\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g}_{*})\big),

where the latter equality follows from the fact that
\widetilde{q}(\mathbf{g}_{*})=0
. However, when these sensitivities are used, e.g.,
for surrogate model refinement, it is often sufficient in practice for the sensitivity to be roughly proportional to the actual error, which is the case here.
