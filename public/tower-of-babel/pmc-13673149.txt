A rare instance of Ogilvie syndrome in a pediatric patient with a complex medical history: A case report
Authors: Kirby Lampe, Victoria Sarata, Aileen Har, Fernando Zapata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute colonic pseudo‐obstruction or Ogilvie's syndrome is an acute dilatation of the large bowel without an obstructive lesion. Symptoms include abdominal distension, nausea and vomiting, constipation, and occasional dyspnea. The disorder most commonly involves the cecum and right colon and is most frequently seen in male patients above the age of 60. It is rare for Ogilvie's to occur in the pediatric population though some infrequent instances have been reported. Here, we report a case of a patient with significant abdominal distension and gaseous distension seen on X‐ray, which was diagnosed and treated as Ogilvie's syndrome. He was unresponsive to conservative and medical management and ultimately required endoscopic decompression.Keywords: colonic decompression, dilatation, neostigmine, pseudo‐obstruction

INTRODUCTION

Acute colonic pseudo‐obstruction (ACPO) or Ogilvie's syndrome is a disorder that most commonly occurs in the cecum and right colon but can extend to the rectum and is noted as an acute dilatation without presence of an obstructive lesion. 1 Those patients who are usually diagnosed with this disorder are above the age of 60 and have a predisposing risk factor such as hospitalization, long‐term care facilities, severe illness, post‐surgery, or metabolic/medication causes like antipsychotics and chemotherapy medications. 2 There is an association with hypothyroidism but otherwise there are no pathognomonic laboratory findings. 3 Abdominal computed tomography (CT) will show proximal colonic dilatation which can extend to the rectum with an intermediate transitional zone. Initial treatment of choice is supportive care, but if there are severe symptoms, Neostigmine can be used. If Neostigmine fails, then the patient will undergo colonoscopic decompression or surgical intervention. 4 There is limited data describing Ogilvie's syndrome in children, but one article does describe the use of Neostigmine for treatment of Ogilvie's syndrome in pediatric hematologic malignancies. 5 The case described below is that of a medically complex teenager admitted for septic shock and acute respiratory failure, whose course was complicated by Ogilvie's syndrome.

CASE REPORT

The patient is a 16‐year‐old male with static encephalopathy, intractable epilepsy, global developmental delay, spastic quadriparesis, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), chronic multifocal osteomyelitis, hyponatremia, and gastric tube dependence admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for decompensated septic shock and acute respiratory failure secondary to multifocal pneumonia requiring intubation and pressor support. While in the ICU, the patient had a CT abdomen due to distension, which started on hospital Day 5. CT showed moderately distended fluid‐filled right colon. Serial abdominal X‐rays (KUB) were performed due to abdominal distension showing diffuse gaseous distention of the colon with rectal air present. Initial KUB can be seen in Figure 1. The abdominal distention was contributing to difficulty with ventilation. Repeat CT showed gaseous distension of the colon with no focal dilation of the bowel concerning for physical obstruction. Exam notable for hypoactive bowel sounds and severe generalized abdominal distension. Radiology, surgery and gastroenterology (GI) team then discussed concern for Ogilvie syndrome. Gastric tube was vented every 3–4 h and Erythromycin, 5 mg/kg, three times a day, was initiated and continued for 48 h. Daily KUB did not show any improvement after starting Erythromycin. Two doses of Neostigmine were given at 2 mg each, 24 h apart, and did not show significant improvement in gaseous distension with only 200 mL of stool out. Bedside rectal decompression measures (rectal tube, irrigation, and enemas) were discussed with family and surgical team but were not pursued due to proximal colonic dilation limiting expected benefit. The decision was made to proceed with unprepped colonoscopy and rectal tube. The patient was taken to the operating room on day of admission 10. Colorectal surgery was made aware and was on stand‐by if complications arose. A guidewire was passed through the scope and advanced to the splenic flexure, where a 24 French Foley catheter was placed. The foley catheter had six openings made by scissors. The catheter had a distance of 7 cm outside of the anus with approximately 33 cm left in the colon. The colonoscope was then pulled out after suctioning and decompressing the colon. The patient continued a bowel regimen of MiraLAX two times daily. Repeat KUB can be seen in Figure 2 demonstrating decreasing gaseous distension after decompression. The rectal tube was removed on day of admission 14 (4 days after placement) as the patient had significant improvement in distension. Over the next several days, he was able to wean off the ventilator, sedation medications and pressor support. He was transferred to general pediatrics on day of admission 17 and discharged home on day 25 with bowel regimen of MiraLAX 1 cap a day and suppositories as needed.

DISCUSSION

ACPO is a rare cause of abdominal distension in pediatrics but should not forgotten about in complex hospitalized patients. Common risk factors in adults include non‐operative trauma, infection, malignancy, and cardiac disease. In patients who are not treated, 3%–15% of cases may lead to intestinal perforation and increased mortality rate up to 50%. 5 Diagnosis takes clinical suspicion as it is uncommon in the pediatric population. In our case, the patient did present with significant distension and CT findings concerning for ACPO. He was then treated using adult recommendations including Erythromycin, Neostigmine, and ultimately colonic decompression. Though we know it is most effective in the gastric antrum and proximal duodenum, Erythromcyin was trialed as patient had generalized distension on exam. There are case reports in adults showing effectiveness of Erythromycin in ACPO as well. 6 In a study published by Lee et al., 10 pediatric patients with malignancy were diagnosed with ACPO. In this study, all of the patients were treated with Neostigmine after supportive care was ineffective. Half of the patients showed improvement after two doses and there was an overall positive response to Neostigmine 80% of the time. 5 As for the decompression procedure, there are limited guidelines but according to the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, the procedure should be performed using water infusion or minimal to no insufflation of carbon dioxide. The colon should be unprepped and the distal transverse colon should be reached with extensive suctioning. There is a 40% risk of recurrence, especially in patients where decompression tube is not left in place. 7 We followed these guidelines.

CONCLUSION

Taking into consideration this case in addition to other reports such as above in Lee et al., it is important for gastroenterologists to recognize signs and symptoms that are suggestive of ACPO and intervene quickly due to increased risk of mortality. Often in collaboration with primary teams, patients such as in this case, we can initiate medical treatment that may lead to resolution of symptoms early and prevent need for additional invasive procedures.
