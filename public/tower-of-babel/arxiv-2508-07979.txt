Well-posedness for a fourth-order nonisothermal tumor growth model of Caginalp type


Abstract
We introduce a nonisothermal phase-field system of Caginalp type that describes tumor growth under hyperthermia. The model couples a possibly viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation, governing the evolution of the healthy and tumor phases, with an equation for the heat balance, and a reaction-diffusion equation for the nutrient concentration. The resulting nonlinear system incorporates chemotaxis and active transport effects, and is supplemented with no-flux boundary conditions. The analysis is carried out through a two-step approximation procedure, involving a regularization of the potential and a Faedo–Galerkin discretization scheme. Under stronger regularity assumptions, we further establish the existence of strong solutions and their uniqueness via a continuous dependence result. Key words: nonlinear initial-boundary value problem, Cahn–Hilliard equation, nonisothermal model, well-posedness, regularity results, tumor growth. AMS (MOS) Subject Classification: 35K61, 35K35, 35D30, 35B65, 35A02, 35Q92, 92C50.

Introduction

Despite the advances in modern medicine, cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide (see, e.g.,
[
Fer+21
]
). Moreover, due to the actual rates in population growth and aging, the number of new cases per year is set to increase, further intensifying the global burden of the disease (see, e.g.,
[
Bra+24
]
). As a result, there is a great
interest in combining existing therapeutic strategies to enhance treatment efficacy and improve patient outcomes. Mathematical modeling plays a crucial role in this context, providing a powerful framework for exploring the complex interplay between biological processes and different therapies. In particular, it enables a systematic and quantitative investigation of treatment combinations,
dosages, and scheduling, which is not always possible in clinical trials, providing valuable insights. In this work, we focus on the effects of
hyperthermia therapy, a treatment approach that has been explored for several decades (see, e.g.,
[
Mal+16
]
and the references therein). Hyperthermia consists of raising the local, regional, or whole-body temperature above 39°C for 30 to 60 minutes, using techniques such as radio wave, microwave, or focused ultrasound heating, perfusion, and thermal chambers (see, e.g.,
[
Hab+11
]
). It is sometimes used
alone, but most frequently is combined with other primary therapies, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Depending on the temperature, it has different effects.

1.

Moderate hyperthermia (below 42°C) increases tumor perfusion, primarily due to heat-induced vasodilatation. This, in turn, improves the delivery of chemotherapeutic or immunotherapeutic drugs.

2.

High hyperthermia (i.e., temperatures between 42°C and 50°C) exerts direct cytotoxic effects and induces vascular damage. Moreover, it impairs DNA repair mechanisms, thereby potentially enhancing the susceptibility of tumor cells to other treatments such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

3.

Thermal ablation (above 50°C), applied directly in situ, results in irreparable cellular damage and consequent apoptosis and necrosis of tumor tissue.

The result depends on several factors, including treatment timing, heat, and nutrient distribution within the tissue. To address these questions, we derive a model that couples the dynamics of tumor growth, nutrient
diffusion, and localized thermal treatment. We aim to rigorously investigate the analytical properties of the resulting system, such as the existence of weak solutions, regularity, and continuous dependence on the problem’s data.

The PDE system.
We consider a smooth domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=2,3
and final time
T>0
. The PDE system reads as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\theta+\ell\varphi)-\Delta\theta=u,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\mu=(\lambda_{P}\sigma-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mu=\tau\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi+\beta(\varphi)+\pi(\varphi)-\chi\sigma-\Lambda\theta,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta(\sigma-\chi\varphi)=-\lambda_{C}\sigma\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)+\lambda_{B}(\sigma_{B}-\sigma)-\lambda_{D}\sigma\mathbbm{k}(\theta),

(1.1d)

in the parabolic cylinder
Q\coloneqq\Omega\times(0,T)
, coupled with the boundary conditions

\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\theta=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\varphi=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mu=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sigma=0

(1.2)

on
\Sigma\coloneqq\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
, and with the initial conditions

\theta(0)=\theta_{0},\quad\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\quad\sigma(0)=\sigma_{0}

(1.3)

in
\Omega
.
The subsystem (
1.1a
)–(
1.1c
) is of Caginalp type, and is made of the second-order parabolic equation (
1.1a
), ruling the evolution of
\theta
—the relative temperature with respect to a certain critical value normalized to 0—and the (possibly) viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.1b
)–(
1.1c
), governing the evolution of the phase-field variable
\varphi
, which is the difference in volume fraction between tumor and healthy cells.

Within this framework, in place of a sharp interface separating tumor and healthy tissues, there is a narrow transition layer, where both cell types coexist. The result is a model well-suited for describing topological changes such as coalescence or breaking up phenomena, which frequently occur during the early stages of tumor development.
From the mathematical point of view, at least in principle,
\varphi
has values in the interval
[-1,1]
, where
\{\varphi=-1\}
identifies the tumor region,
\{\varphi=1\}
the healthy region, and
\{-1<\varphi<1\}
the diffuse interface (see
Remark

3.1
).

Finally, the parabolic equation (
1.1d
) prescribes the dynamics of the nutrient concentration
\sigma
, a chemical species dissolved in the blood (such as oxygen or glucose) and consumed only by tumor cells.

Before delving into the derivation of the model introduced above, allow us to recall some of the related literature. The widely studied and now well-understood Cahn–Hilliard equation is usually set when assuming constant temperature, which is not always reasonable from the modeling point of view (see
[
Mir19
]
for further details). Therefore, a number of nonisothermal phase transition models have been proposed (see, e.g.,
[
Cag88
,
PF90
,
AP92
]
and the more recent
[
MS05
,
MS21
,
De +24
]
).
In particular, the Caginalp model has been discussed and analyzed over the years, also recently: on that, we may quote
[
Col+23
,
Col+24
]
and references therein.

As for tumor growth models based on the Cahn–Hilliard equation, the literature is extensive (see, e.g.,
[
FGR15
,
Gar+16
,
GL17
,
EGN21
]
). The nutrient dynamic is typically taken into account, but various works also incorporate the evolution of other biological quantities that may affect cancer
development, such as relevant biomarkers, elastic effects, and fluid flows (see, e.g.,
[
Col+20
,
Cav+26
,
GLS21
,
GKT22
,
Gar+16
,
KS22
]
and the references therein).

However, to the best of our knowledge, only the recent contribution
[
Ipo22
]
by Ipocoana incorporates temperature effects. In the paper
[
Ipo22
]
, a nonisothermal diffuse interface model for tumor growth is proposed and analyzed. The system tries to capture the complex coupling between temperature variation, nutrient transport, cellular proliferation, and apoptosis within a Cahn–Hilliard-type framework. The model is formulated in terms of an entropy balance and is rigorously studied in a suitable weak setting. In particular, the author establishes the existence of solutions for the associated initial-boundary value problem. While our approach shares with
[
Ipo22
]
the interest in coupling phase-field dynamics with thermal effects and a tumor growth process, we deal with a different system and follow another strategy in terms of mathematical structure and analytical results.

Derivation of the model.
The model (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) is derived following the approach from Caginalp (see

[
Cag88
,
Cag90
]
but also
[
BS96
, Chapter 4, Example 4.4.2]
). The evolution of temperature is governed by the heat balance equation

\partial_{t}(\theta+\ell\varphi)-\Delta\theta=u,

(1.4)

where
u
is a given heat source, and
\ell
is a constant related to the tissue’s latent heat. This equation is coupled with the mass balance

\partial_{t}\varphi+\diver(-\nabla\mu)=U^{\varphi},

(1.5)

where
U^{\varphi}
denotes a phenomenologically chosen source term, and
\mu
is the chemical potential of the system. Following the formulation in
[
Nov88
]
, the chemical potential is defined as

\mu\coloneqq\tau\partial_{t}\varphi+\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\varphi}(\theta,\varphi,\sigma),

(1.6)

where the first addend provides a viscous, dissipative contribution with
\tau\geq 0
, while the second term is the variational derivative of the total free energy functional, which is assumed to be given by

\mathcal{F}(\theta,\varphi,\sigma)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\widehat{\beta}(\varphi)+\widehat{\pi}(\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}|\sigma|^{2}-\chi\sigma\varphi-\Lambda\theta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1.7)

The term
\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\widehat{\beta}(\varphi)+\widehat{\pi}(\varphi)
is of classical Ginzburg–Landau type: the gradient accounts for the interfacial energy, while the remaining part is a double-well potential related to the interaction between the two phases.
The term
\frac{1}{2}|\sigma|^{2}
reflects the energetic cost associated with the presence of nutrients, implying that high concentrations increase the system’s free energy.
The two final addends couple the Cahn–Hilliard equation with the nutrient and with the temperature equations. The nonnegative constants
\chi
and
\Lambda
represent, respectively, a transport coefficient (modeling effects such as chemotaxis and active transport) and a parameter related to the tissue’s latent heat. The nutrient equation is also obtained by a mass balance, choosing the flux as the variational derivative of the free energy
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\sigma
:

\partial_{t}\sigma+\diver\bigg[-\nabla\bigg(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\sigma}(\theta,\varphi,\sigma)\bigg)\bigg]=U^{\sigma},

(1.8)

for a nutrient source
U^{\sigma}
, again phenomenologically chosen.
We observe that the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.5
) includes the term
\diver(\chi\nabla\sigma)
, accounting for chemotaxis, i.e., the tendency of tumor cells to migrate toward regions with higher nutrient concentrations. Additionally, the nutrient equation (
1.8
) contains the term
-\diver(-\chi\nabla\varphi)
, accounting for the so-called active transport, which models the preferential flow of nutrients toward tumor cells. This is motivated by the biological observation that tumor cells often overexpress specific membrane transporters facilitating nutrient uptake (for more details, see the
[
Gar+16
,
EGN21
]
).

Finally, we introduce the mass and nutrient sources that we are going to employ throughout the paper. Starting from
U^{\varphi}
, we assume

U^{\varphi}(\theta,\varphi,\sigma)\coloneqq(\lambda_{P}\sigma-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi).

According to the literature (see
[
GL17
]
), we assume that the proliferation of tumor cells is only limited by the quantity of nutrients available, and that they die only because of apoptosis. Moreover, here we assume that
\theta
has cytotoxic effects proportional to the temperature. The function
\mathbbm{h}
guarantees that the phenomena we have just described are proportional to the tumor cells available in a certain area. For example,
\mathbbm{h}
may be a monotone increasing function which is 0 where
\{\varphi=-1\}
and 1 where
\{\varphi=1\}
. On the other hand, we assume the nutrient source
U^{\sigma}
to be of the form

U^{\sigma}(\theta,\varphi,\sigma)\coloneqq-\lambda_{C}\sigma\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)+\lambda_{B}(\sigma_{B}-\sigma)-\lambda_{D}\sigma\mathbbm{k}(\theta).

The first two addends are again compliant with
[
GL17
]
. The term
-\lambda_{C}\sigma\mathbbm{h}(\varphi)
models the fact that tumor consumption is higher where tumor cell density is higher, while
\lambda_{B}(\sigma_{B}-\sigma)
accounts for nutrient supply from the preexisting capillaries. A novel aspect of the model is the inclusion of the term
-\lambda_{D}\sigma\mathbbm{k}(\theta)
, which describes the influence of temperature on nutrient absorption—e.g., due to vasodilation effects that enhance nutrient transport in warmer tissue regions.
We couple the system (
1.1
) hereby obtained with the no-flux conditions (
1.2
), which reflect the idealization that the system is effectively isolated from its surroundings, and with the initial
conditions (
1.3
).

Notation and preliminaries

Notation.
For any Banach space
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
we employ
(X^{*},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X^{*}})
for its topological dual. We denote the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces over
\Omega
respectively as
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
. In the special case of
p=2
, we set
H^{k}(\Omega)\coloneqq W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. The norm of the Bochner space
W^{k,p}(0,T;X)
is indicated as
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{W^{k,p}(X)}
, omitting the time interval
(0,T)
for the sake of brevity. If the final time differs from
T
, it will be written explicitly to avoid ambiguity. With the notation
C^{0}([0,T];X)
, we mean the space of continuous
X

C^{0}_{w}([0,T];X)
denotes the space of functions

that are weakly continuous from
[0,T]
to

.
For convenience, we introduce the notation

W\coloneqq\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega)\,|\,\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\},\qquad V\coloneqq H^{1}(\Omega),\qquad H\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega).

We use
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
for the internal product in

and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
for the dual pairing between
V^{*}
and
V
. As always in this setting, we identify
H
with its dual space

and as a subspace of
V^{*}
through

\langle w,v\rangle_{V}=(w,v)_{H}

for every
w\in H
and
v\in V
. This way we obtain the usual Hilbert triplet

V\subset\subset H\subset\subset V^{*},

where the embeddings are dense and compact. For every

, we introduce its (generalized) mean value as

\overline{v^{*}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\langle v^{*},1\rangle_{V}

for every

The Neumann–Laplace operator.
We introduce the
-\Delta
operator with Neumann boundary condition restricted to the space
V_{0}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,V_{0}\to V_{0}^{*}\text{ s.t.}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{R}v,w\rangle_{V}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall w\in V_{0}.

\mathcal{R}
is an isomorphism, and we denote its inverse by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{R}^{-1}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,V_{0}^{*}\to V_{0}.

The operators
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{N}
enjoy several well-known properties, which we enlist here for the reader’s convenience:

\langle v^{*},\mathcal{N}w^{*}\rangle_{V}=\langle w^{*},\mathcal{N}v^{*}\rangle_{V}=(\nabla\mathcal{N}w^{*},\nabla\mathcal{N}v^{*})_{H}
for all
v^{*},w^{*}\in V_{0}^{*}
,

(2.1)

(w,v)_{H}=(w-\overline{w},v)_{H}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\mathcal{N}v\,\mathrm{d}x
for all
w\in V,\ v\in H_{0}
,

(2.2)

\lVert v\rVert_{H}\leq\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{H}^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{N}v\rVert_{H}^{1/2}
for every
v\in V_{0}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{$\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}v^{*}(t),\mathcal{N}v^{*}(t)\rangle_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{N}v^{*}(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}$}\\
&\quad\quad\text{for a.e. $t\in(0,T)$, for all $v^{*}\in H^{1}(0,T;V^{*}_{0})$.}\end{split}

(2.4)

Since
\mathcal{N}
is defined over the space
V_{0}^{*}
but the functions we work with do not have, in general, zero mean value, it is useful to notice that the standard norms over
W
,
V
,
H
, and
V^{*}
are equivalent to the following ones:

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{W}

\displaystyle\simeq(\lVert-\Delta v\rVert_{H}^{2}+\overline{v}^{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{V}

\displaystyle\simeq(\lVert\nabla v\rVert_{H}^{2}+\overline{v}^{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\simeq(\lVert v-\overline{v}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\overline{v}^{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\lVert v\rVert_{V^{*}}

\displaystyle\simeq(\lVert v-\overline{v}\rVert_{V^{*}}^{2}+\overline{v}^{2})^{1/2}\simeq(\lVert\nabla\mathcal{N}(v-\overline{v})\rVert_{H}^{2}+\overline{v}^{2})^{1/2}.

Useful inequalities.
In our proofs, we frequently rely on a set of classical inequalities. In particular, we make repeated use of the Poincaré, the Hölder, and the Young inequalities, the latter of which reads

ab\leq\eta a^{2}+\frac{1}{4\eta}b^{2}\quad\text{for every }a,b\in\mathbb{R},\ \eta>0.

Another important tool is Ehrling’s Lemma (see, e.g.,
[
LM12
, Theorem 16.4, p. 102]
), a well-known compactness result which states that, given three Banach spaces
X\subset\subset Y\subset Z
, for every
\eta>0
there exists a
C_{\eta}>0
such that

\lVert v\rVert_{Y}\leq\eta\lVert v\rVert_{X}+C_{\eta}\lVert v\rVert_{Z}

for every
v\in X
. In our setting, we apply it for
V\subset\subset L^{p}(\Omega)\subset V^{*}
and
p=2,4
, which is true in dimension
d=2,3
.

Finally,
C
is a constant depending only on the problem’s data and whose value might change from line to line or even within the same line. If we want to highlight a dependency on a certain parameter, we put it as a subscript (e.g.,
C_{\tau}
indicates a constant that depends on
\tau
,
C_{0}
a constant that depends on the initial data, etc.).

Hypotheses and main results

Throughout the paper, we consider a bounded domain
\Omega
of class
C^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=2,3
, and we fix a final time
T>0
.

1.

We assume that

\displaystyle\ell,\Lambda,\chi\text{ are positive constants},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\tau\text{ and }\lambda_{P},\lambda_{A},\lambda_{E},\lambda_{C},\lambda_{B},\lambda_{D}\text{ are real nonnegative constants}.

(3.2)

2.

The assigned functions
u
and
\sigma_{B}
enjoy the regularity

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(Q)\ \text{ with }\ \lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq M,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sigma_{B}\in L^{2}(Q)

(3.4)

for a nonnegative constant
M
.

3.

Regarding the nonlinearities
\mathbbm{h}
and

, we require that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{h},\mathbbm{k}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}),\text{ and}

(3.5)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbbm{h}\leq\mathbbm{h}^{*},\ 0\leq\mathbbm{k}\leq\mathbbm{k}^{*},

(3.6)

for two nonnegative constants
\mathbbm{h}^{*},\ \mathbbm{k}^{*}
.

4.

We consider a potential
\widehat{f}=\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\pi}
split into the sum of a convex part and a nonconvex perturbation. Explicitly, we suppose

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta},\,\widehat{\pi}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}\text{ is convex and nonnegative, with }\widehat{\beta}(0)=0.

(3.8)

Moreover, denoting its derivative as
f\coloneqq\beta+\pi
where
\beta\coloneqq\widehat{\beta}^{\prime}
and
\pi\coloneqq\widehat{\pi}^{\prime}
, the following growth conditions hold:

\displaystyle|\beta(r)|\leq C_{\beta}(\widehat{\beta}(r)+1),

(3.9)

\displaystyle|\pi^{\prime}(r)|\leq C_{\pi}

(3.10)

for all

, where
C_{\beta}
,

are given nonnegative constants. We also point out that

\beta(0)=0.

(3.11)

Remark 3.1
.

It follows that
\widehat{\pi}
has at most quadratic growth and
\widehat{\beta}
has at most exponential growth. A meaningful example covered by our hypotheses is the regular quartic potential

\widehat{f}_{reg}(r)=\frac{1}{4}(r^{2}-1)^{2}.

Notice that the logarithmic potential, as well as the double obstacle potential, is instead excluded. In particular, we are not able to guarantee that the order parameter
\varphi
assumes values in the physically relevant interval
[-1,1]
.

Remark 3.2
.

We observe that the assumption that
\widehat{\beta}
attains its minimum value
0
at
0
(see (
3.8
) and (
3.11
)) is not restrictive. Indeed, if this were not the case, one could modify
\widehat{\beta}
by subtracting its tangent line at
0
, so that the new function
\widehat{\beta}
remains convex and nonnegative, and additionally attains its minimum
0
at
0
.
Moreover, there is no issue in adding a linear term to
\widehat{\pi}
; doing so still preserves the Lipschitz continuity of
\pi
.

a.e. in

Theorem 3.6

.

Assume that the hypotheses

\theta_{0}\in V\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega),\quad\varphi_{0}\in W\cap H^{3}(\Omega),\quad\sigma_{0}\in V.

Then, there exists a weak solution
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
with the additional regularity

\displaystyle\theta\in H^{1}(0,T;H)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;W)\cap L^{\infty}(Q),

(3.22)

\displaystyle\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;V^{*})\cap H^{1}(0,T;V)\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;W),

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mu\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;W).

(3.24)

In particular,
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
is a strong solution, i.e., it satisfies the system (
1.1
) a.e. in
Q
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
that depends on
M
and on the other problem’s data, such that the following estimate holds

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta\rVert_{H^{1}(H)\cap L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)\cap L^{\infty}(Q)}+\lVert\varphi\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}(V^{*})\cap H^{1}(V)\cap L^{\infty}(W)}\\
&\quad+\lVert\mu\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}\leq C_{2}.\end{split}

(3.25)

Remark 3.7
.

We point out that (
3.21
) in particular yields (
3.18
) and
\widehat{\beta}(\varphi_{0})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Moreover, it is worth noting that, by standard Sobolev embedding results in dimensions

, the order parameter
\varphi
belongs to

.

The

-bound for the component

of the solution, combined with the local Lipschitz continuity of

, plays a crucial role in establishing a continuous dependence result, which, in particular, ensures the uniqueness property stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8

(Continuous dependence)
.

Suppose that the hypotheses
1
–
4
are fulfilled, and that the initial data comply with (

). Then, for every assigned functions

), if we denote by

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(V)}+\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(W)}\\

4
Existence of weak solutions

The existence of weak solutions is proved through two levels of approximation. First, we introduce an approximated problem in which
\beta
is replaced by a suitable Lipschitz continuous function
\beta_{\varepsilon}
. Second, to show the existence of weak solutions of the approximate PDE system, we employ a Faedo–Galerkin scheme. Finally, thanks to some a priori estimates, we separately pass to the limit, first in the Faedo–Galerkin scheme, and then as
\varepsilon\to 0
, finding a solution to the original problem. Notice that we need to introduce
\beta_{\varepsilon}
even if
\beta
is smooth due to the wide class of possible growth that we are allowing: without it, we would not be able to pass to the limit in the corresponding term of the discretized system.

4.1
The approximated problem

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we define the Moreau–Yosida approximation of
\widehat{\beta}
as

\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)\coloneqq\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|s-r|^{2}+\widehat{\beta}(s)\bigg\}\qquad\forall r\in\mathbb{R},

(4.1)

and the Yosida regularization of
\beta
as

\beta_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\bigl(\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{\prime}.

(4.2)

They enjoy the following properties:

1.

\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
is a
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
convex function with

0\leq\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)\leq\widehat{\beta}(r)\qquad\text{for all $r\in\mathbb{R}$,}

(4.3)

2.

\beta_{\varepsilon}
is monotone increasing and Lipschitz continuous with
\beta_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
and with Lipschitz constant bounded by
\varepsilon^{-1}
,

3.

they satisfy the inequality (
3.9
) with the same constant
C_{\beta}
, i.e.,

|\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)|\leq C_{\beta}(\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)+1)\quad\text{ for all $r\in\mathbb{R}$.}

(4.4)

In view of
4
,
the properties
1
–
2
are well known and can be
found in
[
Bré73
, Proposition 2.6, p. 28 and Proposition 2.11, p. 39]
. To prove

3
, we need to introduce the resolvent operator associated with
\beta
,
defined as

J_{\varepsilon}(r)=({\rm{Id}}+\varepsilon\beta)^{-1}(r),

(4.5)

i.e.,
J_{\varepsilon}(r)
satisfy

J_{\varepsilon}(r)+\varepsilon\beta(J_{\varepsilon}(r))=r

for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. It can be proved (see
[
Bré73
, Proposition 2.11, p. 39]
) that
J_{\varepsilon}=\rm{Id}-\varepsilon\beta_{\varepsilon}
.
It trivially follows that

\beta(J_{\varepsilon}(r))=\frac{r-J_{\varepsilon}(r)}{\varepsilon}=\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)\quad\text{ for all $r\in\mathbb{R}$.}

(4.6)

Moreover, again by
[
Bré73
, Proposition 2.11, p. 39]
, we know that

\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)|^{2}+\widehat{\beta}(J_{\varepsilon}(r)),

thus
\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)\geq\widehat{\beta}(J_{\varepsilon}(r))
. Putting these elements together and employing the inequality (
3.9
), we obtain

|\beta_{\varepsilon}(r)|=|\beta(J_{\varepsilon}(r))|\leq C_{\beta}\left(1+\widehat{\beta}(J_{\varepsilon}(r))\right)\leq C_{\beta}\left(1+\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(r)\right),

so
3
is proved.

The approximate problem is obtained from (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) replacing
\beta
with
\beta_{\varepsilon}
and its weak solutions are defined consequently as in
3.3
.

4.2
Existence of weak solutions for the approximated problem

As already anticipated, we prove the existence of weak solutions of the approximated system through a Faedo–Galerkin space discretization.

Faedo–Galerkin discretization.
We introduce the nondecreasing sequence of eigenvalues
\{\gamma_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of the Laplace operator with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
and the associated sequence of eigenvectors
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which is a complete orthonormal system in
H
, orthogonal in
V
and
W
. Explicitly, for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\begin{cases}-\Delta e_{j}=\gamma_{j}e_{j}&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}e_{j}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

with the additional properties

(e_{i},e_{j})_{H}=\delta_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{if }\,i=j\\
0&\hbox{if }\,i\not=j\end{cases},\qquad(\nabla e_{i},\nabla e_{j})_{H}=\gamma_{i}\delta_{ij},

for every
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Recall that, by standard elliptic regularity results,
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are smooth functions. We define

V^{n}\coloneqq\text{span}\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n}\}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then,
\{V^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a nondecreasing sequence of subspaces, whose union is dense in
V
as well as in
H
. With our notation,
\gamma_{0}=0
,
e_{0}=|\Omega|^{-1/2}
, and
V^{0}
is the space of constant functions. We also introduce the projection of
H
onto
V^{n}
as

P^{n}(v)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{n}(v,e_{j})_{H}e_{j}

for all
v\in H
. Notice that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\lVert P^{n}(v)\rVert_{X}\leq C\lVert v\rVert_{X}\quad\hbox{ for all $v\in X$, where $X=H,V,W$.}

(4.7)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we aim at finding a quadruplet
(\theta^{n},\varphi^{n},\mu^{n},\sigma^{n})
of the form

\begin{split}\theta^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta^{n}_{j}(t)e_{j}(x),\quad\varphi^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi^{n}_{j}(t)e_{j}(x),\\
\mu^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu^{n}_{j}(t)e_{j}(x),\quad\sigma^{n}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma^{n}_{j}(t)e_{j}(x)\end{split}

such that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}(\theta^{n}+\ell,\varphi^{n}\rangle){v}_{V}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta^{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}uv\,\mathrm{d}x,

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\varphi^{n},v\rangle_{V}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu^{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{P}\sigma^{n}-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta^{n})\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})v\,\mathrm{d}x,

(4.8b)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\langle\tau\partial_{t}\varphi^{n},v\rangle_{V}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi^{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left(\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{n})+\pi(\varphi^{n})-\chi\sigma^{n}-\Lambda\theta^{n}\right)v\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\mu^{n}v\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{aligned}

(4.8c)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\langle\partial_{t}\sigma^{n},v\rangle_{V}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\sigma^{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x-\chi\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi^{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\left(-\lambda_{C}\sigma^{n}\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})+\lambda_{B}(\sigma_{B}-\sigma^{n})-\lambda_{D}\sigma^{n}\mathbbm{k}(\theta^{n})\right)v\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{aligned}

(4.8d)

a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, for all
v\in V^{n}
, and that fulfill the initial conditions

\theta^{n}(0)=\theta_{0,n}\coloneqq P^{n}(\theta_{0}),\quad\varphi^{n}(0)=\varphi_{0,n}\coloneqq P^{n}(\varphi_{0}),\quad\sigma^{n}(0)=\sigma_{0,n}\coloneqq P^{n}(\sigma_{0}).

(4.9)

Notice that, owing to the properties of the projection
P^{n}
, the following inequalities hold

\lVert\theta_{0,n}\rVert_{H}\leq C\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H},\quad\lVert\varphi_{0,n}\rVert_{V}\leq C\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{V},\quad\lVert\sigma_{0,n}\rVert_{V}\leq C\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{V},

(4.10)

as well as the convergences

\displaystyle\theta_{0,n}\to\theta_{0}

strongly

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }H,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\varphi_{0,n}\to\varphi_{0}

strongly

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }V,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0,n}\to\sigma_{0}

strongly

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }V.

(4.13)

Here, we neglect the dependence on
\varepsilon
in the notation
(\theta^{n},\varphi^{n},\mu^{n},\sigma^{n})
on purpose, for brevity.

Local-in-time existence.
We test each equation of the system (
4.8
)–(
4.9
) by
e_{j}
for
j=0,\dots,n
, obtaining the following ODEs system

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}(\theta^{n}_{j}+\ell\varphi^{n}_{j})+\gamma_{j}\theta^{n}_{j}=(u,e_{j})_{H},

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\varphi^{n}_{j}+\gamma_{j}\mu^{n}_{j}=((\lambda_{P}\sigma^{n}-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta^{n})\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n}),e_{j})_{H},

(4.14b)

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\varphi^{n}_{j}+\gamma_{j}\varphi^{n}_{j}+(\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{n})+\pi(\varphi^{n}),e_{j})_{H}-\chi\sigma^{n}_{j}-\Lambda\theta^{n}_{j}=\mu^{n}_{j},

(4.14c)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\sigma^{n}_{j}+(\gamma_{j}+\lambda_{B})\sigma^{n}_{j}-\chi\gamma_{j}\varphi^{n}_{j}=(\left(-\lambda_{C}\sigma^{n}\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})+\lambda_{B}\sigma_{B}-\lambda_{D}\sigma^{n}\mathbbm{k}(\theta^{n})\right),e_{j})_{H},

(4.14d)

\displaystyle\theta^{n}_{j}(0)=(\theta_{0,n},e_{j})_{H},\quad\varphi^{n}(0)=(\varphi_{0,n},e_{j})_{H},\quad\sigma^{n}_{j}(0)=(\sigma_{0,n},e_{j})_{H}.

(4.14e)

Notice that
\mu^{n}_{j}
is an auxiliary variable and can be removed from the system by substituting
\mu^{n}_{j}
, whose expression is given by equation (
4.14c
), into equation (
4.14b
). Then we can recover the expression for
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\varphi^{n}_{j}
and replace it in equation (
4.14a
). Hence, we obtain a
3n
-equations first-order ODEs system in the variables
\theta^{n}_{j}
,
\varphi^{n}_{j}
, and
\sigma^{n}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n
with locally Lipschitz continuous nonlinearities. Thus, the existence of
H^{1}(0,T_{n})
solutions follows from Carathéodory existence theorem, for a certain
T_{n}\leq T
. The solution is not global because the nonlinearities are not globally Lipschitz continuous. Then, we retrieve
\mu^{n}_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T_{n})
by equation (
4.14c
).
In the following, we will derive some a priori estimates independent of
n
that will allow us to extend the solution to all
[0,T]
, and, at the same time, to recover enough compactness to pass to the limit as
n
goes to infinity. Keeping in mind that our final goal is passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we will also be careful in tracking the dependence on
\varepsilon
.

First a priori estimate.
We integrate in time
equation (
4.8a
) for a fixed
v\in V^{n}
, obtaining the equation

\int_{\Omega}(\theta^{n}+\ell\varphi^{n})v\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla(1*_{t}\theta^{n})\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(1*_{t}u)v+\int_{\Omega}(\theta_{0,n}+\ell\varphi_{0,n})v\,\mathrm{d}x.

(4.15)

Here, the notation
*_{t}
denotes the convolution with respect to time, i.e.,

1*_{t}\theta^{n}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{n}(\cdot,s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad 1*_{t}u\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}u(\cdot,s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

We take
v=\theta^{n}
in equation (
4.15
) and multiply each side of the equality by a constant
R>0
, fixed but yet to be determined. We choose
v=\varphi^{n}
in (
4.8b
),
v=-\Delta\varphi^{n}
in (
4.8c
), and
v=\sigma^{n}
in (
4.8d
). We add the resulting equalities and, after some simplification, we obtain:

\displaystyle R\int_{\Omega}|\theta^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{R}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(1*_{t}\theta^{n})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\tau}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=R\int_{\Omega}(1*_{t}u)\theta^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x+R\int_{\Omega}(\theta_{0,n}+\ell\varphi_{0,n})\theta^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x-R\ell\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{n}\theta^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{P}\sigma^{n}-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta^{n})\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})\varphi^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\pi(\varphi^{n})(-\Delta\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}\chi\sigma^{n}(-\Delta\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}\theta^{n}(-\Delta\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda_{C}\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})+\lambda_{D}\mathbbm{k}(\theta^{n})\right)|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{B}(\sigma_{B}-\sigma^{n})\sigma^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x.

After applying the Hölder and the Young inequalities to the right-hand side, and rearranging some terms, we end up with

\displaystyle R\int_{\Omega}|\theta^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{B}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(R\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(1*_{t}\theta^{n})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\tau\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle\quad=R\int_{\Omega}\left(1*_{t}u+\theta_{0,n}+\ell\varphi_{0,n}-\ell\varphi^{n}\right)\theta^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{E}\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})\varphi^{n}\theta^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{P}\sigma^{n}-\lambda_{A})\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})\varphi^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left(-\pi(\varphi^{n})+2\chi\sigma^{n}+\Lambda\theta^{n}\right)(-\Delta\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda_{C}\mathbbm{h}(\varphi^{n})+\lambda_{D}\mathbbm{k}(\theta^{n})\right)|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{B}\,\sigma_{B}\,\sigma^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq\bigg(\frac{R}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{2}\bigg)\int_{\Omega}|\theta^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+C_{R}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\theta_{0,n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi_{0,n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|1*_{t}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+C\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\sigma_{B}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+1\bigg),

where we have exploited the boundedness of
\mathbbm{h}
and

given by hypothesis

(cf. (
3.10
)). Now we choose

such that

, move the first two addends of the right-hand side to the left-hand side, and integrate in time over the interval

. Moreover, we recall that
\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\theta^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\

&\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left(|1*_{t}u|^{2}+|\sigma_{B}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left(|\varphi^{n}|^{2}+|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg).\end{split}

Applying the Gronwall inequality leads to

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T^{n};H)}+\lVert 1*_{t}\theta^{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T^{n};V)}+\lVert\varphi^{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T^{n};H)\cap L^{2}(0,T^{n};W)}\\

First a priori estimate consequences.

Second a priori estimate.

-dependence starts to appear in the third a priori estimate, and then, starting from it, it spreads out. Thus, we need to re-perform it, focusing on the problematic term, which now becomes

\int_{\Omega}\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(0))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Taking into account that
\beta_{\varepsilon}
can be bounded by
\widehat{\beta}
(see the inequality (
4.3
)), and that
\widehat{\beta}(\varphi_{0})
is integrable by assumption (
3.18
), this integral is estimated as follows

\int_{\Omega}\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(0))\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\widehat{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\widehat{\beta}(\varphi_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty,

independently from
\varepsilon
. Thus, also the third a priori estimate and its consequences hold with a constant independent of
\varepsilon
. By summarizing, we have that

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(V^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(V)}+\lVert\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(V^{*})\cap L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}\\
&\quad+\lVert\tau^{1/2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(H)}+\lVert\mu_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{2}(V)}+\lVert\sigma_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(H)\cap L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}\leq C\end{split}

(4.45)

for some constant
C
depending on
M
and on the problem’s data.

Now we can derive an additional estimate, proceeding by comparison in the version of (
3.16c
) written for
\beta_{\varepsilon}
(in place of
\beta
) and
(\theta_{\varepsilon},\varphi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})
. In fact, in view of the regularity of the solution, we can write the equivalent equation

\tau\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}+\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})+\pi(\varphi_{\varepsilon})-\chi\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\Lambda\theta_{\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}\qquad\hbox{a.e. in }Q

(4.46)

and then comparing the terms in the light of (
4.45
). Hence, we can recover a uniform estimate for
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})
, namely

\lVert\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}\leq C.

(4.47)

4.4
Passage to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0

From the estimates (
4.45
), (
4.47
) and standard compactness results, there exists a quadruplet
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
satisfying the regularity properties in
3.3
such that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, along a nonrelabelled subsequence, the same convergences we had in (
4.34
)–(
4.43
) hold, with the additional

\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\to\beta(\varphi)\qquad\text{weakly}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H).

(4.48)

In fact, since
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H)
it converges, along a subsequence, to a certain
\xi\in L^{2}(0,T;H)
. Moreover, we know that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}\to\varphi
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;H)
. Thus, we may identify
\xi
with
\beta(\varphi)
because of the strong-weak closeness of the graph of the maximal monotone operator
\beta
(see
[
Bré73
, Proposition 2.5, p. 27]
). This is enough to pass to the limit in the approximate system, showing that the limit

(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
is a weak solution to the PDE system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). Moreover,
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
satisfies the estimate (
3.20
).

We aim to check that

5.22
), we can conclude that the limit
(\theta_{\varepsilon},\varphi_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon})
we found in the proof of the existence theorem as
n\to\infty
, enjoying the convergences (
4.34
)–(
4.43
), satisfies the additional estimate

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(H)\cap L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}+\lVert\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}(V^{*})\cap H^{1}(V)\cap L^{\infty}(W)}\\
&\quad+\lVert\tau^{1/2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}(H)}+\lVert\mu_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)\cap L^{2}(W)}\leq C\end{split}

(5.23)

which passes to the limit because it is independent of
\varepsilon
.
Note that, by the Sobolev embedding
W\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
(
5.23
) entails as well that
\lVert\varphi_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq C
. Moreover, by comparison in the equation
(
4.46
) we find that

\lVert\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)}\leq C.

(5.24)

Passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, all the estimates are preserved and therefore satisfied by the limit
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
. Note that
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\to\beta(\varphi)
weakly-
\ast
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H)
due to (
5.24
).
Finally, we observe that, since the right-hand side in the equation
(cf. (
1.1a
) and (
3.3
))

\partial_{t}\theta-\Delta\theta=u-\ell\partial_{t}\varphi

lies in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
, and
\theta_{0}
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then it turns out that

\lVert\theta\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq C

by maximal parabolic regularity (see
[
LSU68
, Chapter III, Theorem 7.1,
p. 181]
). This concludes the proof
of
3.6
.

6
Continuous dependence

In order to prove
3.8
, we consider two pairs
\{(\theta_{i},\varphi_{i},\mu_{i},\sigma_{i})\}_{i=1,2}
of strong solutions corresponding to the initial data
\{(\theta_{0,i},\varphi_{0,i},\sigma_{0,i})\}_{i=1,2}
and to the assigned functions
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1,2}
. For convenience, in the following, we will employ the shorter notation

\displaystyle\theta\coloneqq\theta_{1}-\theta_{2},\quad

\displaystyle\varphi\coloneqq\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle\mu\coloneqq\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma\coloneqq\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2},

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\coloneqq\theta_{0,1}-\theta_{0,2},\quad

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}\coloneqq\varphi_{0,1}-\varphi_{0,2},\quad

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\coloneqq\sigma_{0,1}-\sigma_{0,2},\quad

\displaystyle u\coloneqq u_{1}-u_{2}.

Moreover, we recall the notation
f=\beta+\pi
that we are going to use from now on.
First of all, we observe that
(\theta,\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
satisfies

\displaystyle\theta+\ell\varphi-\Delta(1*_{t}\theta)=\theta_{0}+\ell\varphi_{0}+(1*_{t}u),

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\mu=(\lambda_{P}\sigma-\lambda_{E}\theta)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})+(\lambda_{P}\sigma_{2}-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}theta_{2})(\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{2})),

(6.1b)

\displaystyle\tau\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi+f(\varphi_{1})-f(\varphi_{2})-\chi\sigma-\Lambda\theta=\mu,

(6.1c)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta(\sigma-\chi\varphi)+\lambda_{B}\sigma=&-\lambda_{C}\sigma\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\lambda_{C}\sigma_{2}(\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{2}))\\
&-\lambda_{D}\sigma\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{1})-\lambda_{D}\sigma_{2}(\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{1})-\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{2})),\end{aligned}

(6.1d)

a.e. in
Q
. This system is obtained straightforwardly by taking the difference of the systems (in the strong form) satisfied by
\{(\theta_{i},\varphi_{i},\mu_{i},\sigma_{i})\}_{i=1,2}
, and integrating the temperature equation in time. We multiply (
6.1a
) by
R\theta
, where
R
is a positive (big) constant yet to be determined, (
6.1b
) by
\varphi
, (
6.1c
) by
-\Delta\varphi
, and (
6.1d
) by
\sigma
. We sum all these equalities and integrate over
\Omega
, finding

\begin{split}&R\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{R}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(1*_{t}\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\tau^{1/2}\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\lambda_{B}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad=R\int_{\Omega}(\theta_{0}+\ell\varphi_{0})\theta\,\mathrm{d}x+R\int_{\Omega}(1*_{t}u)\theta\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad-R\ell\int_{\Omega}\varphi\theta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{P}\sigma-\lambda_{E}\theta)\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}(\lambda_{P}\sigma_{2}-\lambda_{A}-\lambda_{E}\theta_{2})(\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{2}))\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad-\int_{\Omega}(f(\varphi_{1})-f(\varphi_{2}))(-\Delta\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x+2\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma(-\Delta\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}\theta(-\Delta\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}\left[-\lambda_{C}\sigma\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\lambda_{C}\sigma_{2}(\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{1})-\mathbbm{h}(\varphi_{2}))\right]\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}\left[-\lambda_{D}\sigma\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{1})-\lambda_{D}\sigma_{2}(\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{1})-\mathbbm{k}(\theta_{2}))\right]\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(6.2)

Then, recalling assumptions
3
,
4
, and the regularity estimate (
3.25
), we exploit the following facts:

•

\mathbbm{h}
and
\mathbbm{k}
are bounded and Lipschitz continuous;

•

\theta_{2}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(Q)
;

•

\sigma_{2}
is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;W)
, and it holds that
\|\sigma_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\sigma_{2}\|_{W}
a.e. in
(0,T)
;

•

f
is locally Lipschitz continuous, and
\varphi_{1}
,
\varphi_{2}
are bounded in
L^{\infty}(Q)
.

Using Young’s inequality and the bounds above, the right-hand side of (
6.2
) can be estimated by

\begin{split}&\frac{R}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\/R\left(\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|1*_{t}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+C\left(1+\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+8\chi^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\Lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+C\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}+|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|\/|\sigma|\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(6.3)

Let us briefly comment on the fact that, at this stage, the constants
C
used above depend on the norms of the solutions
\{(\theta_{i},\varphi_{i},\mu_{i},\sigma_{i})\}_{i=1,2}
through the estimate (
3.25
). However, as a consequence of the proof we are currently carrying out, we will establish the uniqueness of the solution. This implies that any solution must coincide with the one constructed in
3.5
and
3.6
. Therefore, it satisfies the a priori estimates derived therein, which ensure that the norm of each component of the solution can be bounded by a constant depending only on the data of the problem—such as the final time
T
, the domain
\Omega
, the constant
R
, the initial data… As a result, the same conclusion applies to the constants
C
appearing above.
Most of the terms in the previous expression (
6.3
) do not need any further treatment because, after time integration, we are going to apply the Gronwall Lemma. Only the last one requires some additional calculations. By the Young inequality, we find

\begin{split}C\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|\/|\sigma|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(6.4)

Now we collect the contibutions (
6.2
)–(
6.4
), fix
R>\frac{R}{2}+1+2\Lambda^{2}
(cf. the coefficients of the terms with
\theta
on the right-hand side), then move to the left-hand side the terms in
\theta
and
-\Delta\varphi
, and integrate in time over
(0,t)
, obtaining:

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(1*_{t}\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\tau^{1/2}\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|-\Delta\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sigma^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad\leq C\bigg[\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\tau^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|1*_{t}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}^{2}\bigr)\int_{\Omega}|\sigma|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg].\end{split}

Hence, since the function
t\mapsto 1+\lVert\sigma_{2}\rVert_{W}^{2}
is bounded in
L^{1}(0,T)
, by the Gronwall Lemma we infer that

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}+\lVert\nabla(1*_{t}\theta)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)}^{2}+\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(W)}^{2}\\
&\quad\quad+\lVert\tau^{1/2}\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(V)}^{2}+\lVert\sigma\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)\cap L^{2}(V)}^{2}\\
&\quad\leq C\left(\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\tau^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\rVert_{V}^{2}+\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert 1*_{t}u\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(6.5)

Finally, we want to improve this result for the temperature variable
\theta
. To do so, we consider the equation satisfied by
\theta
a.e. in
Q
, which is the following
(cf. (
1.1a
))

\partial_{t}(\theta+\ell\varphi)-\Delta\theta=u.

We multiply it by
\theta+\ell\varphi
and integrate over
\Omega\times(0,t)
, finding

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta+\ell\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta_{0}+\ell\varphi_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}u(\theta+\ell\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s-\ell\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

We apply the Young inequality and the previously proved inequality (
6.5
) to treat the terms on the right-hand side, leading to

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\theta+\ell\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\theta|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad\leq C\bigg(\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\theta|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\\
&\quad\leq C\bigg(\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\tau^{1/2}\lVert\nabla\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg).\end{split}

Then, we end up with

\begin{split}&\lVert\theta+\ell\varphi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(H)}+\lVert\nabla\theta\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}\\
&\quad\leq C\left(\lVert\theta_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\varphi_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert\tau^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\rVert_{V}^{2}+\lVert\sigma_{0}\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(H)}^{2}\right)\end{split}

(6.6)

and the continuous dependence inequality (
3.26
) follows easily from the estimates (
6.5
)–(
6.6
).
Therefore,
3.8
is completely proved.
