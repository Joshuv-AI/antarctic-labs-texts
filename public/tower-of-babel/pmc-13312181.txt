In ovo dexamethasone elicits an increase in ACTH-stimulated glucocorticoid release but no effects on fear behaviors in Pekin ducks
Authors: N.K. Wilson, J.M. Schober, E.M. Oluwagbenga, I.C. von Seggern, G.S. Fraley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat stress is among the leading causes of stress to poultry during their productive life, leading to the loss of profit at each stage of production. This event can have profound impacts on a duck’s physiology, leading to changes in their behavior and an increased response to future stressors. We have previously shown that breeder ducks exposed to heat stress deposit increased quantities of glucocorticoids in their eggs, and that is correlational to altered phenotype of the offspring. We sought to replicate those findings by increased glucocorticoid content in ovo without a parental heat stress (HS) event. Eggs were injected with either dexamethasone (Dex; = 100/treatment (1ug/ul]) or saline (Con = 100/treatment (0.1 mg/egg)), sealed with silicon glue and set to incubate and hatch following industry standards. Ducklings were separated into their respective groups (n = 50/treatment/pen). At 3 weeks of age, ducks were then given an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH; 0.0625 mg/kg cosyntropin in 1.0 ml saline) challenge (n = 6/treatment) or control (1.0 ml saline per bird). Blood was collected at 0,1,2, and 4 h after injection. An isolation fear test was performed on the ducks at week 3.5 and novel environment test on weeks 1 and 5. Heterophil to lymphocyte ratio (HLR), ELISA for glucocorticoids, feed conversion ratio (FCR), and weekly body weights were all calculated and recorded. No significant differences were observed in FCR, body weights, or isolation fear test metrics such as latency to first vocalization. There were significant differences in circulating corticosterone in the Con-ACTH and Dex-ACTH groups each time point. The Con-ACTH had a significant (p < 0.05) increase after hour 1, and the Dex-ACTH groups showed a further significant (p < 0.05) increase above all other groups. Similar results were obtained with cortisol and HLR. The novel environment test showed only an age effect between weeks 1 and 5. Our results show that the in ovo glucocorticoids impact physiological, but not necessarily behavioral responses.

Introduction

Annual periods of episodic heat stress cause monetary loss to poultry producers, and the duck industry is no exception. In the United States, the poultry industry suffers losses of $128-165 million due to heat stress (St-Pierre et al., 2003). On a global level, the losses can reach over $1 billion annually (Government Review Draft Third NCA Front Matter (v. 22 November 2013) GOVERNMENT REVIEW DRAFT THIRD NCA ii). These annual heat stress events are particularly detrimental to breeder ducks due to their heavy down feathers. Heat stressed breeder ducks show not only increased circulating corticosterone levels but also lay fewer eggs, have reduced fertility and hatchability, and considerably reduced welfare (Oluwagbenga et al., 2022a; Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). Eggs laid by the heat stressed breeder ducks showed an increase in glucocorticoids deposited into the eggs, and the offspring from these eggs exhibited a heightened hormonal stress response and increased fear behaviors (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). Although correlational, it is not known if the increase in egg glucocorticoid is the main driver of the phenotypic changes in the F1 behavioral and hormonal stress responses.

The hormonal stress response in birds refers to an activation of the septal-hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal axis (SHPA) (Nagarajan et al., 2017). In ducks that experience temperatures that rise above 35°C (95°F) during the day and do not drop below 29.4°C (85° F) at night, this activation leads to the production of corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) and arginine vasotocin hormone (AVT) from the hypothalamus and septum, respectively (Kuenzel and Jurkevich, 2010; Nagarajan et al., 2017; Kadhim et al., 2019). These two neurohormones act on the anterior pituitary to stimulate the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH then stimulates adrenal cortical cells to synthesize and secrete the glucocorticoids (GC), corticosterone and cortisol. Glucocorticoids have numerous functions that include metabolic, immune, and stress response regulation. During stress, these steroid hormones cause hyperglycemia and lead to an overall increase in the heterophil to lymphocyte ratio (HLR) in birds. The increase in HLR is due to an apparent increase in heterophils with a concurrent apparent decrease in lymphocytes via diapedesis; although the exact mechanism by which GC stimulates diapedesis is not known. Parental exposure to chronic stressors may alter the physiological response to stress and behavior in their offspring, possibly via GC deposited into the egg.

GC levels in the egg may reflect stressful environmental conditions that may also alter either pre-hatch or post-hatch development (Love et al., 2005; von Engelhardt and Groothuis, 2011). The potential of GC as a mediator of maternal effect on offspring can be validated through in ovo administration of GC into the albumen or yolk (reviewed by von Engelhardt and Groothuis (2005)). Some effects of in ovo GC may include reduced hatchability, poor growth and development, and increased fear (Saino et al., 2005; Janczak et al., 2006, 2007; Eriksen et al., 2010; Peixoto et al., 2020). Hayward and Wingfield (2004) showed a decrease in body weight and an increase in SHPA response in offspring of Japanese quail treated with chronic GC. Previous studies from our lab have shown that chronic treatment of GC in adult ducks increased GC deposition into the egg (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). We have further shown that a long-term exposure of heat stress also increases GC deposition in the egg (Oluwagbenga et al., 2022a). In the offspring of those breeders, we observed an increase in GC and HLR to an ACTH challenge, as well as an increased fear response (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). These effects shown in commercial ducks are similar to those reported in other species such as the Scrub Jay (Small and Schoech, 2015), Great Tit (Van Der Meer and Van Oers, 2015), and European Pied Flycatchers (Tomotani et al., 2016). Thus, there appears to be a correlation between increased egg GC, altered physiology, and behavior in the F1 generation of heat stressed parents, however, a causative relationship between egg GC and altered phenotypes needs to be explored.

The purpose of our experiment was to determine if in ovo GC could replicate our previous findings but without a parental heat stress component. We hypothesized that ducks hatched from eggs injected with GC will show increased physiological stress after an ACTH challenge as well as increased fear responses. We found that the GC-treated group showed an enhanced response to the ACTH challenge, very similar to the F1 from HS parents (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a). However, the offspring from our current study failed to show increased fear behaviors as seen in the Oluwagbenga et al. (2023a) studies. Our results suggest that there is a transgenerational effect due to an altered environment for the parents and that GC may be causative to the enhanced hormonal stress response but not the behavioral components.

Materials and methods

Animals

Two hundred fertilized Pekin duck eggs were obtained from Maple Leaf Farms, Inc. (Leesburg, IN USA). After hatching, ducks were fed via feed restriction in the typical manner and kept on a 18:6 LD lighting schedule. Birds were fed commercial (proprietary) starter and finisher diets provided by Maple Leaf Farms Inc. Birds were allowed ad liberum access to water via nipple lines (4 ducks per nipple), per industry standards. Temperatures were maintained at 18°C at 55% relative humidity (Chen et al, 2021). Ducks were placed into floor pens that were 50% pine shavings and 50% raised plastic flooring under the water lines. Ducks were housed with increasing size of pen to maintain industry standard density (232–1393 cm2/bird; weeks 1–5, respectively; Chen et al.​, 2021). All protocols were approved by Purdue University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PACUC# IPROTO0424002501).

Experimental design

Prior to setting, a trephine hole was drilled into the end of the egg away from the air cell and was then injected with either 0.1 ml of 1 µg/µl of dexamethasone (Dex) in saline or 0.1 ml saline as a control (n = 100/treatment) into the albumen. Trephine holes were sealed with a silicone gel and allowed to fully set. After the silicone gel had set, eggs were incubated at industry standard temperature and humidity (Chen et al., 2021; NatureForm 2340 incubator/hatcher, Florida, USA). Eggs were candled at days 7 and 14 and dead embryos were removed. On incubation day 24, eggs were placed into hatcher trays, and returned to the same incubator for hatching. On the day of hatch, live hatchlings were weighed and ducklings were placed in separate pens in the same room by in ovo treatment at N = 50/treatment/pen. Control group hatch numbers were culled to match those of the Dex group. All ducks were measured weekly for body weight and feed intake were recorded to determine FCR.

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test

During week 3 (∼3.5 kg) ducks from each treatment group were injected with intramuscularly ACTH (cosyntropin, ACTH 1-24, 0.04 mg/kg in saline, at N = 6/treatment) or saline at 0.1 ml/kg saline with 0.01% mannitol (n = 6/treatment). This dose of ACTH has been utilized by our lab in several previous studies in chickens and ducks (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023b; Abraham et al., 2025). The dose of DEX was chosen based upon previous studies in chickens. Groups were formed as such, Con-Saline, Con-ACTH, Dex-Saline, or Dex-ACTH depending on original treatment status. Blood was collected from all ducks at 0,1,2,3 h after injected. Blood smears were also prepared for complete blood count (CBC) analyzes. Blood smear analyzes were performed by avian pathologists (Antech Diagnostics Inc. Chicago, IL) unaware of treatment groups. Remaining blood was centrifuged in serum separator tubes and spun at 3500 x g for 15 min with serum being collected and stored at −20°C for later GC analyzes.

Isolation fear test

At 4 weeks of age, ducks were subjected to an isolation fear test. One duck per treatment was placed in a typical 5-gallon bucket immediately outside the home pen for three minutes. Investigators stood 3 meters away from test bucket. All birds from each group were subjected to this test. Video recording started as soon as the duck was placed into the bucket and allowed to continue until either the duck successfully escaped or the full three minutes had elapsed. The latency to first escape attempt, the number of escape attempts, and the total time in the bucket were recorded. iPhones mounted on tripods were used to record tests. Resultant mp4 files were used to assess behavior times by individuals unaware of the treatment groups.

Novel environment test

At weeks 1 and 5 of age, offsetting by week as to not interfere with the ACTH stimulation, 2 ducks from each treatment group were placed into one of two identical anechoic chambers and vocalizations recorded for 3 min. All birds were used from each treatment group. Details of vocalization recording and analyzes have been reported by our lab previously (Schober et al., 2024). After each recording, treatment groups to each chamber was switched to avoid a room effect. Briefly, we used a Zoom H5 recorder (Zoom North America, Hauppauge, NY), an Audio Technica AT4022 omnidirectional microphone (Audio-Technica, Stow, OH) to record vocalizations. Once the ducks were recorded in the sound chamber, we analyzed their vocalizations via the use of Adobe Audition and Praat software. Number of vocalizations and latency to first vocalization were counted and calculated.

Glucocorticoid analyzes

Serum was analyzed in duplicate for free corticosterone using a commercially available kit (Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, MI USA). This kit was thoroughly validated previously by our lab to be specific for either corticosterone or cortisol (Tetel et al., 2022a, 2022b; Tonissen et al., 2022). Assays for both GCs were completed following manufacturer recommended protocols.

Statistical analyzes

All data were checked for normality and statistics run using using SAS (MacJMP vers18). All data were analyzed by a Welch’s t-test or Student’s t-test as appropriate. ACTH-stimulation data were analyzed by a two-way repeated measure ANOVA with time as the repeated measure. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Our objective was to determine if glucocorticoids delivered in ovo could replicate our previous findings of stress events leading to increased fear and physiological effects, without a parental heat stress component. We hypothesized that ducks hatched from the eggs given glucocorticoids would exhibit an increased fear response both behaviorally and physiologically. We tested this hypothesis by injecting eggs with either Dex or saline, and administered an ACTH challenge and behavior tests. We found that the Con-ACTH group showed increased corticosterone compared to both saline-treated controls. The Dex-ACTH group showed increased corticosterone and cortisol compared to all other groups as well. For the novel environment test, there were not significant differences for number of vocalizations or time to first latency. Our findings suggest that increased in ovo GC may be causative to phenotypic changes associated with ACTH-stimulated corticosterone release and downstream effects on HLR. However, in ovo GC does not appear to be involved with phenotypic changes in expression of fear. Our data suggests that GC may be involved with transgenerational changes in physiology, but perhaps not behavior. However, further research is required to confirm this possibility.

Behavioral tests such as the novel environment test are becoming increasingly common in poultry research. These tests are performed to determine stress responses outside of physiological analysis. The novel environment test quantifies how birds illicit a stress response in a new unfamiliar environment. The more a bird vocalizes coupled the shortened onset of vocalization while in this new environment, the more fearful they are (Marx et al., 2001; Herborn et al., 2020). Another fear response test is the novel object test. This is similar to the novel environment test as the ducks are given an unfamiliar object and their tendency to approach the object is recorded. As reported by Oluwagbenga et al. (2023a) the F1 generation stemming from parents exposed to heat stress had increased fear response to a novel object. These ducks avoided the object when walking or rejected approaching the object compared to offspring from non-heat stressed parents. In our current study we did not see any effects on behavior. Our observations suggest that GC may not play a role in transgenerational changes in fear behavior. This lends to the idea that there may be other pathways at play that cause these shifts in fear behavior following parental stress. Previous studies from our lab also show changes in biogenic amines in the egg from HS parents (Lyte et al., 2023). These amines and their respective pathways could possibly influence trans-generational behavioral changes, for example.

Following exogenous administration or endogenous secretion of ACTH, adrenocortical cells within the inner zone of the avian adrenal parenchyma will produce the majority of corticosterone needed to elicit a hormonal response to a stressor. After secretion, corticosterone is transported into the blood after binding to either corticosteroid binding globulin (CBG) or albumin with CBG being the transporting agent with the highest and most specific binding affinity. Receptor cells in target tissues are capable of unbinding corticosterone from CBG to thus increase bioavailability for use. Receptors for GC can be found in a wide variety of tissues such as brain, liver, kidney, thymus and intestine among others. It is at these target tissues that GC can either interact directly with receptors or be modulated through hormones such as norepinephrine. Once the GC interacts with its respective receptor, changes in metabolism, immunity, electrolyte balance, and behavior may occur (Thompson and Lippman, 1974). As its namesake suggests, one major change is the effect it has on gluconeogenesis and increased circulation of glucose. During a stressful event, GC release leads to an increase in hepatic gluconeogenesis but is coupled with a decrease in cellular glucose uptake (De Matos, 2008). It has been determined that GC inhibit glucose absorption in the gut and uptake in peripheral tissues (Norris, 1985). Concurrently, lipolysis in adipose tissue leads to increased plasma fatty acids. Both events increase energy sources for the bird to utilize to cope with the stimulus causing stress (Braganza et al., 1997). However, these mechanisms are typically short lived and not meant to be sustained for long periods of time as to not cause a lengthened state of glucose insensitivity (Kahn et al., 1978). Under typical circumstances, GC promote negative feedback on the SHPA axis, slowing the synthesis and release of GC, reducing the fear response and restoring physiological homeostasis. In times of chronic stress, however, this negative feedback is dampened, and GC propelled changes remain (Munck, 1971; Kahn et al., 1978). In previous studies, it has been shown that the F1 of HS parents show an exaggerated GC response to an ACTH stimulation (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023a); similar findings have also been shown in wild bird populations (Bowers et al.,). It is thought that the enhanced GC response in offspring of stressed parents may help prepare the offspring for a stressful environment, through the mechanisms outlined above. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these changes in the offspring have yet to be elucidated.

In poultry, the prolonged GC can lead to a drop in production variables such as egg count or carcass quality, reproduction metrics such as fertility and hatchability, and an overall decreased welfare of the flock across multiple species such as chickens and ducks (Hardy et al., 2005; Quinteiro-Filho et al., 2012; Oluwagbenga et al., 2022a). In our current study, the Dex-ACTH group showed an increased physiological stress response to the ACTH challenge, though they hailed from non-heat-stressed parents, lends to the idea that GC deposited into eggs from hens will influence the physiology of offspring. While the mechanism of this deposition is not completely understood, egg GC nonetheless leads a heightened SHPA axis activation later in life. The lack of expected behavioral changes also implies that there are most likely different pathways and hormones involved and GC alone are not sufficient to develop an enhanced behavioral fear response. However, we did also observed enhanced HLR response in Dex-ACTH-treated ducklings.

Immune cell mobilization measured via HLR also is used to confirm physiological stress within birds. Heterophils are a type of phagocytic leukocyte unique to avians with an analogous function to mammalian neutrophils. As part of the innate immune system, heterophils serve to activate an acute inflammatory as a defense against a potential or current infection (Kaspers et al., 2022). This influx of heterophils is coupled with the migration of lymphocytes from blood into tissues. Lymphocytes are part of the adaptive immune system with B cells that produce antibodies and killer T cells that destroy infected cells. During times of stress, GC cause an increase in the HLR as heterophils move into the blood and lymphocytes migrate into tissues (Davis et al., 2008). An increase in HLR is closely tied to an increase in circulating corticosterone. Multiple stressors such as fasting, heat stress, transportation, and shackling cause an increase in corticosterone and a subsequent increase in the HLR (Gross and Siegel, 1983; Bedanova et al., 1997; Al-Aqil et al., 2013). These are endogenous changes in HLR propelled by an increase in GC and a HLR change can occur from pharmacological manipulation, most notably by dexamethasone administration REF. A sustained increase in HLR leads to a sustained immune response in preparation for potential tissue damage associated with a stressor. Our results suggest that in ovo GCs are involved in some of the transgenerational changes in phenotype associated with a chronic stressor, such as heat stress. These changes may also help prepare the offspring for an increased stress environment, but again, the mechanism of these changes have yet to be elucidated. However, epigenetics are possible.

Epigenetic changes are one possibility to explain how a heat stress event catalyzes change in the physiology of respective offspring. Epigenetics is defined as the changes seen on offspring two generations past the stressor in the parent generation REF. Many mechanisms such as DNA methylation and post-translational histone modifications have been identified to facilitate these genetic changes (Liu et al., 2023; Kolli and Jayasundara, 2024). Both mechanisms mark specific regions of DNA to either be or not be transcribed in offspring, resulting in a different response to stimuli compared to the parent generation. While our results begin to follow the pattern described by an epigenetic change, it cannot truly be defined as an epigenetic change due to the lack of a second generation, other non-epigenetic mechanisms such as reactive oxygen species accumulation and cytokine generation could play a role in the changes observed in our current study. It is also a possibility that these physiological and hormonal but not behavioral changes may play a role in transgenerational changes as well as epigenetic changes, however further research is necessary to confirm this possibility.
