The BNP-to-Albumin Ratio (BAR) as a Predictor of All-Cause Mortality in Elderly Patients with Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Xiran Zeng, Yunhong Yang, Dajin Liu, Baotong Hua, Tao Shi, Qin Fu, Zhimin Zheng, Yujuan Peng, Xinuo Ma, Lixing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of the BNP-to-albumin ratio (BAR) for all-cause mortality in AF patients aged ≥60 years.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included clinical data from 1458 AF patients treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between December 2019 and December 2023. Using the optimal cutoff value determined by the restricted cubic spline, patients were divided into a low BAR group (BAR < 6.05) and a high BAR group (BAR ≥ 6.05). The discriminatory performance of the BAR and various predictive models was evaluated and compared using Kaplan‒Meier survival curves, Cox proportional hazards regression models, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. No external validation was performed.ResultsThe study ultimately included 842 patients with AF. Kaplan‒Meier analysis revealed that among elderly AF patients, the all-cause mortality rate was higher in the high-BAR group. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that the BAR is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for predicting all-cause mortality was 0.778, indicating the superior predictive performance of the BAR compared with that of individual BNP and albumin levels.ConclusionIn elderly patients with AF, high BAR levels are independently associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, suggesting that BAR may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common type of arrhythmia in clinical practice and is linked to a range of adverse consequences, including a fivefold increase in stroke incidence, left ventricular dysfunction, and heart failure.1,2 Additionally, it plays a role in cognitive decline, vascular dementia, depression, and reduced quality of life,1,2 making it a significant factor in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.3,4 Its incidence has increased with the increasing aging population, resulting in a substantial disease burden.3,5,6 The onset and progression of AF involve a series of “upstream pathways”, including inflammation, oxidative stress, fibrosis, and metabolic disorders.1 Atrial fibrosis is one of the key pathological foundations for the occurrence of AF.7 Preventing atrial fibrosis is considered a potential therapeutic target for the development of AF and its associated complications.8 Current therapeutic strategies for AF are broadly divided into rhythm control approaches (such as catheter ablation and antiarrhythmic drugs) and strategies targeting complications and pathophysiology, including anticoagulation, left atrial appendage occlusion, and upstream therapies against the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis.1 Nevertheless, the risk of all-cause mortality in patients with AF may be twice that of the general population.3 Therefore, how to improve the long-term prognosis in these patients is worth considering.

B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) is a serum cardiac biomarker and a multifunctional hormone that,7,9 in addition to its typical effects on sodium excretion, vasodilation and antagonism of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system,9 has recently been found to inhibit fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition in cardiac tissue.10 In addition, it participates in systemic metabolism by activating lipolysis and promoting lipid oxidation.10 Compared with clinical risk scores, natriuretic peptides themselves demonstrate superior prognostic discriminatory ability for cardiovascular events and progression to AF.11

Albumin is the major protein in blood and is considered to be the most important antioxidant in whole blood,12,13 with several important physiological functions: maintaining colloid osmotic pressure and vascular integrity, regulating metabolism and vascular activity, providing substance-binding ligands and exhibiting anticoagulant and antiplatelet aggregation activity.13,14 Hypoproteinemia may indicate the presence of an underlying inflammatory state,12 which may accelerate cardiac and organ failure, thereby increasing the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes and reducing survival.15 There is evidence that lower serum albumin levels in older adults are associated with a greater risk of new-onset cardiovascular disease.15

Previous studies have suggested that the BAR—a composite indicator comprising BNP, which reflects cardiac neurohormonal activation,16 and albumin, which reflects inflammatory-metabolic and vascular homeostasis12,14,17—combines two independent predictors of AF severity, and that this ratio is associated with stroke risk in patients with AF.18 Given the significant heterogeneity in the elderly population regarding physiological reserve, comorbidities, and geriatric syndromes,19 the prognostic value of this ratio in elderly patients with AF has not yet been specifically evaluated. Therefore, this study focuses on the elderly population and aims to assess the association between BAR and long-term prognosis in elderly patients with AF.

Methods

Study Population

A retrospective study was performed on 1458 consecutive patients aged sixty years or older with AF who were admitted to the Heart Center and the Department of Geriatric Cardiology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between December 2019 and December 2023. The exclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 90 years; absence of necessary data, such as BNP or albumin levels; lack of follow-up data (loss of contact or refusal to cooperate during telephone follow-up); active bleeding; malignancy, including hematologic malignancies such as leukemia, multiple myeloma, and lymphoma; valvular heart disease-associated AF; severe infection (sepsis, septic shock, or bacteremia); severe anemia (hemoglobin < 60 g/L); uremia or dialysis dependence; decompensated cirrhosis; or in-hospital death. By excluding these extremely high-risk patients, we confined our analysis to those with a relatively low-risk profile and a modifiable disease window, thereby enabling the accurate detection of early and specific prognostic markers for AF.20 After applying these criteria, 616 patients were excluded, and a total of 842 patients were included in the final analysis. Detailed information on the study population, including subgroup distribution and comparisons of baseline characteristics between patients with and without missing data, is provided in the supplementary tables (Supplementary Tables 1, 3 and 4). This retrospective cohort study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement and the RECORD guideline21 (Supplementary Table 7).

Data Collection

Patient demographic and clinical information was collected at admission. The demographic characteristics included age, sex, history of smoking, alcohol consumption, and family history of cardiovascular disease. Anthropometric data included pulse and heart rate, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Clinical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure, peripheral vascular disease (PVD), a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, cardiomyopathy, and chronic kidney disease. All laboratory data, including results of routine blood tests including white blood cell (WBC), lymphocyte percentage (LY%), red blood cell (RBC),platelet (PLT), neutrophil, monocyte, creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), cardiac troponin (cTn), myoglobin, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), total protein (TP), albumin, total bilirubin (STB), conjugated bilirubin, cholinesterase (ChE), uric acid (UA), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), serum potassium, serum sodium, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), thyroid function tests, and antibody tests, were used. All blood samples were based on the most recent plain blood before and after admission, and emergency blood was used if unavailable and then sent to the laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University for testing according to standard operating procedures.

Adjunctive examinations included cardiac ultrasound data such as left atrial diameter (LAD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd), right atrial long‑axis diameter (RAd), interventricular septal thickness, left ventricular posterior wall thickness, left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), and other data.

Pharmacological and interventional treatments during hospitalization included warfarin, new oral anticoagulants, aspirin or indobufen, clopidogrel or ticagrelor, statins, β-blockers, digoxin, diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin receptor blocker/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ACEI/ARB/ARNI), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), amiodarone/dexamethasone, and SGLT2 inhibitors, as well as atrial fibrillation ablation (AF ablation), left atrial appendage occlusion(LAAO), and pacemaker implantation. The CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores were also recorded. BAR was calculated by dividing the log10-transformed BNP (multiplied by 100) by the albumin level.

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Survival data were collected through telephone follow-up with patients or their family members. Lost to follow-up was defined as failure to establish contact with both the patient and at least one family member after three or more unsuccessful attempts on separate days. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of discharge (index date) to the date of all-cause mortality, with censoring at the last known alive date. The study endpoint was defined as all-cause mortality (ACM).

Statistical Analysis

Patients were categorized into a low-BAR group (BAR < 6.05; n = 419) and a high-BAR group (BAR ≥ 6.05; n = 423) based on optimal cutoff values determined by restricted cubic splines (RCSs). Alternative grouping strategies (tertiles and quartiles) were explored in supplementary analyses to further characterize the threshold effect.(Supplementary Table 6). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the mean (standard deviation), and comparisons between groups were performed using the t test. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed are expressed as medians (P25, P75), and comparisons between groups were made using the Mann‒Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test.

The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of ACM. We first carried out univariable analyses to identify potential prognostic candidates. Given the limited number of outcome events (137 deaths) and the availability of over 20 candidate predictors, we applied a clinically-oriented hierarchical variable-selection strategy to construct the final multivariable Cox model. This approach prioritized well-validated baseline predictors, including demographic features, routine laboratory measurements, cardiac structural-functional parameters, established thromboembolic-bleeding risk scores, and key interventional covariates.22–25 Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were fitted for ACM, with adjustment for sex, age, BMI, heart failure, CHA2DS2‑VASc score, HAS‑BLED score, AFCA, LAAO, LAd, LVEF, platelet count, serum sodium, and eGFR. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and met for all covariates (global test: Chisq = 9.774, df = 14, P = 0.779) (Supplementary Table 5). Missing data were addressed via three sensitivity analyses: multiple imputation for missing covariates (m = 10, pooled by median), inverse probability weighting for missing BAR, and an extreme bound analysis assuming immediate death for lost‑to‑follow‑up cases (with worst‑case BAR assignment), as detailed in Supplementary Table 2. To further evaluate the robustness of the association between the BAR and ACM, hierarchical multivariate models were constructed with incremental adjustment for confounders, using the unadjusted model as the reference. The results are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The predictive value of the BAR for ACM was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval were calculated, and comparisons were made among different biomarkers. Model 1 (M1) was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 (M2) was further adjusted for Lad, LVEF, BMI, Heart failure, Platelet, Sodium, and eGFR. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for the BAR based on Model2. Using a calibration curve generated via bootstrap resampling (B = 200), we evaluated the calibration of the full model (including BAR) and the clinical model (excluding BAR). The full model included Age, Sex, BMI, heart failure, LAd, LVEF, Platelet count, serum sodium, eGFR, and BAR; the clinical model included the same variables except BAR. Model performance was assessed using the C-statistic and the Brier score. The Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) and Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) were used to evaluate the incremental predictive value of BAR beyond BNP. The reference model included Age, Sex, BMI, heart failure, LAd, LVEF, Platelet count, serum sodium, eGFR, and BNP; the full model additionally included BAR. All the analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 31.0 and R 4.6.1. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline Patient Characteristics

This study ultimately included 842 patients with AF. The mean age of the study participants was 72.67 (7.73) years, with males accounting for 55.1% of the cohort. The median follow-up duration, calculated retrospectively from the date of discharge to the date of death or last censoring, was 916 days (IQR, 706–1325 days), during which 137 deaths (16.3%) were recorded. Compared with those in the low-BAR group, patients in the high-BAR group were significantly older and exhibited elevated heart rates (HRs) as well as increased levels of troponin, LgBNP, hs-CRP, total bilirubin, conjugated bilirubin, UA, and serum potassium. Echocardiographic assessments revealed significantly increased LAd, LVDd, RAd and LVESV (all P < 0.05). Within the CHA2DS2-VASc score range (1–9 points), a significantly greater proportion of patients in the high-BAR group were classified into sex-specific high-risk categories according to guideline definitions; they also exhibited higher prevalence rates of heart failure and cardiomyopathy, along with higher prescribed doses of beta-blockers (all P < 0.05). Conversely, the high-BAR group had lower SBP, BMI, LY%, RBC, hemoglobin, total protein, albumin, cholinesterase, total cholesterol, LDL-C, eGFR, and LVEF and lower rates of prior AF ablation and LAAO history (all P < 0.05). The median follow-up duration was longer in the high-BAR group (P < 0.05). Associations Between the BAR and Endpoints

As shown in Figure 1, Kaplan‒Meier analysis demonstrated that ACM was significantly greater in AF patients with higher BAR levels than in those with those with lower BAR levels (ACM: log-rank χ2 80.677; P < 0.001). As shown in Figure 2, the relationship between the BAR and ACM in AF patients was flexibly modeled and visualized using restricted cubic spline regression. This analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between the BAR and ACM (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinear = 0.0024). Overall, the BAR was positively associated with the risk of ACM, and the curve exhibited a clear threshold effect: the risk of death remained relatively stable below the threshold but increased sharply once the threshold was surpassed. Discussion

This retrospective study is the first to show that the BAR has a significant effect on the prognosis of elderly patients with AF. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the all-cause mortality rate in the high-BAR group was significantly higher than that in the low-BAR group. After adjustment using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, the BAR remained an independent risk factor for ACM. In stepwise Cox regression analyses, adjustment for age reduced the HR from 6.785 to 5.582; further adjustment for sex resulted in minimal change (5.647). Inclusion of BMI and heart failure substantially attenuated the HR to 4.067, whereas additional adjustment for the CHA2DS2-VASc score, HAS-BLED score, ablation history, left atrial appendage occlusion, LAd, LVEF, and eGFR only modestly attenuated the HR to 3.639. These results indicate that heart failure, BMI, and age are the major confounders in the BAR-mortality association.

ROC curve analysis further revealed that the area under ROC curve (AUC) for the ability of the BAR to predict all-cause mortality was 0.778 (95% CI: 0.737–0.819). Its predictive efficacy is superior to that of BNP alone or albumin alone. In supplementary analyses, we performed non‑parametric tests (Mann‑Whitney U-test) comparing both raw BAR and log10‑transformed BAR between survivors and non‑survivors. Both metrics showed significant inter‑group differences (BAR‑log10: U = 21,402.000, Z = −10.323, P < 0.001; BAR‑raw: U = 24,632.000, Z = −9.098, P < 0.001). We further conducted ROC analyses for raw BAR and log10‑transformed BAR, which yielded AUCs of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.703–0.788) and 0.778 (95% CI: 0.737–0.819), respectively (DeLong test: Z = −4.043, P < 0.001). (Supplementary Figure 1) These findings suggest that although log‑transformation moderately improves discriminatory ability, BAR retains statistically significant and clinically meaningful predictive value under both forms. Overall, these research findings endorse the utilization of BAR as an adjunctive tool for risk stratification in elderly patients with AF.

Inflammation is the most important factor in the development of AF, second only to atrial infarction and subsequent tissue fibrosis.7,26 A study finds inflammatory cytokines selectively regulate BNP gene.27 Inflammation and hemodynamic stress, which may be important pathophysiologic underpinnings of AF onset and left atrial appendage thrombosis, both trigger natriuretic peptide production.28 BNP expression is primarily regulated by mechanical stretch, ischemia-hypoxia, and neurohumoral factors, among which mechanical stretch is considered the most important.9 In addition to being a marker of inflammation and hemodynamic stress,10 natriuretic peptides have a wide range of cardioprotective effects. Through the NPR-A/cGMP pathway, BNP exerts anti-myocardial hypertrophy and anti-fibrotic effects, suggesting its role in counteracting AF-related cardiac remodeling.29

Enhanced BNP synthesis is a response to the increased ventricular load.30 Elderly people often have increased left ventricular wall thickness and left ventricular mass, as well as elevated total peripheral resistance and systemic blood pressure.31 Accordingly, plasma BNP and its precursors serve as useful biomarkers for excluding severe left ventricular dysfunction.27 In addition, BNP is independently associated with AF burden, risk of AF progression,11,32 and its elevation may also reflect increased left atrial pressure and impaired left ventricular filling.32 Thus, elevated plasma BNP concentrations have important prognostic value in elderly patients.31

Low serum albumin levels are associated with the increased risk of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, AF, stroke, and venous thromboembolism,13 as well as an approximately twofold higher risk of cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality.33 The pathophysiologic basis of these associations lies in albumin’s critical roles in inflammation and thrombosis. During systemic inflammation and metabolic stress, serum albumin—a negative acute-phase protein—decreases significantly due to reduced hepatic synthesis and increased capillary leakage into the extravascular space.15,34,35 This is clinically relevant because inflammation itself is a key mechanism in the initiation and maintenance of AF12. Second, reduced albumin levels can further aggravate disease progression. Albumin inhibits platelet activation via binding free fatty acids. Lower albumin concentrations disrupt this homeostatic balance and favour a prothrombotic state. Meanwhile, accumulated free fatty acids may reciprocally amplify oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.17 In addition, albumin possesses notable neuroprotective properties in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Such protective effects are mainly mediated by multiple pathways, including ameliorating cerebral microcirculation, suppressing thrombus formation, and inhibiting leukocyte adhesion to vascular endothelium.36

These findings suggest that the pathophysiological processes reflected by these two markers are complementary, with serum albumin primarily reflecting systemic nutrition, inflammation, and hypercoagulability, and BNP primarily reflecting myocardial stress and metabolic function. The calibration curve of the BAR model demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes, with a C-statistic of 0.8222. Notably, the addition of BAR significantly improved risk reclassification relative to BNP alone, as reflected by significant IDI and NRI.

Given that biomarkers capable of integrating multiple pathophysiological mechanisms of AF are critical for optimizing risk stratification and prognostic assessment,37 composite indices combining inflammatory and metabolic parameters have garnered increasing research interest. For instance, the Composite Inflammation Index (CTI) exhibited superior predictive performance than individual biomarkers including TyG and CRP for cardiovascular disease among middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults.38 Likewise, the red cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RAR) and advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) carry prognostic implications in stable heart failure; specifically, ALI predicts long-term outcomes, whereas RAR forecasts 1-year mortality.39 Prior work has shown that BAR and heart rate can predict stroke risk among hospitalized patients with AF.18 More recently, the NT-proBNP-to-albumin ratio (NTAR) – a biomarker functionally analogous to BAR – has emerged as a useful risk-stratification tool for patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, with strong discriminatory performance for predicting prolonged hospital admission and mortality.40 Similarly, in patients with chronic heart failure, NTAR reflects the combined burden of hemodynamic stress and reduced systemic metabolic reserve, and independently predicts in-hospital mortality.41 Building on these findings, our results show that the BAR --a composite index incorporating BNP (neurohormonal activation) and albumin (nutritional-inflammatory status) – confers additional prognostic value in elderly patients with AF. As a low-cost and routinely measurable biomarker, the BAR may therefore help estimate ACM risk, identify high-risk individuals, and guide personalized treatment and follow-up.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective study with inherent limitations. First, as a single-center study, the applicability of its results to a broader population requires the consideration of multicenter data. Second, the BAR was based on a single measurement at admission and thus could not capture dynamic changes following therapeutic interventions or longitudinal biomarker variations. Additionally, despite adjustment for multiple confounders, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Third, the median follow-up duration was longer in the high-BAR group, which may reflect survivor bias; however, the Cox model, which uses time as a scale, can partially account for this bias. Fourth, the BAR cutoff of 6.05 was selected based on the optimal threshold identified by restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. While this threshold showed an incremental predictive signal in our cohort, it may not represent a biologically or clinically optimal cutoff and should therefore be considered exploratory, warranting external validation. Finally, even though the model’s discriminatory power has improved, whether the BAR can guide clinical decision-making (such as risk stratification and follow-up strategies) still requires further clarification through validation in external cohorts and prospective intervention studies.

Conclusion

In summary, based on routine clinical measurements of serum albumin and cardiac biomarkers, this study revealed that the BNP/albumin ratio (BAR) is independently associated with all-cause mortality in elderly patients with AF. The potential prognostic value of this ratio may stem from the integration of two core pathophysiological dimensions: cardiac stress, as reflected by BNP, and systemic inflammation and metabolic burden, as reflected by albumin.
