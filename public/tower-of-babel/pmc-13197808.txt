Carp collagen membrane for guided bone regeneration: evaluation in a rat femoral defect model
Authors: Michele Bujda, Tomáš Suchý, Martin Bartoš, Jitka Lunáčková, Jaroslava Dušková, Margit Žaloudková, René Foltán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlight•Carp-derived collagen membrane supports bone healing in a rat femoral defect model with good biocompatibility.•Membrane-only treatment showed a consistent trend toward higher bone formation and healing scores.•Addition of Bio-Oss® did not improve healing outcomes in this non–critical-size defect model.•Further studies in clinically relevant models are required to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a well-established approach for promoting bone formation in sites with insufficient bone volume, most commonly applied in maxillofacial surgery prior to dental implant placement. A key component of GBR is the barrier membrane, whose primary function is to maintain a protected space for healing and to stabilize the blood clot by preventing micromovements during the early phases of regeneration [1]. Beyond its barrier function, membrane properties such as biocompatibility, degradation profile, porosity, and mechanical stability may substantially influence the quality and predictability of bone healing [2,3].

Collagen-based membranes are widely used in GBR due to their favorable biological properties and resorbability. However, most commercially available membranes are derived from mammalian sources (porcine and bovine), which may raise concerns related to potential zoonotic disease transmission (e.g., bovine spongiform encephalopathy), religious restrictions (e.g., avoidance of porcine products in Islam and bovine products in Hinduism), as well as economic and ethical considerations [4,5]. Alternative collagen sources therefore represent an area of growing interest. Fish-derived scaffolds have been shown to be biocompatible and exhibit lower immunogenicity when compared to bovine collagen [6]. They also minimize the risk of zoonoses transmission and avoid religious constraints. They represent cost-effective option, as they can be produced from waste products (skin, scales, fins, etc.) [7].

In our previous work, we introduced a collagen membrane derived from European carp skin and characterized its properties, biocompatibility, and degradation behavior in a rat subcutaneous model [8], demonstrating a degradation profile comparable to commonly used collagen membranes [9].

A study by other authors have demonstrated that this collagen material can be effectively loaded with gentamicin [10] and allows its gradual release during collagen degradation. In the field of bone tissue engineering, incorporation of bioactive molecules into collagen scaffolds is a commonly used strategy to enhance regenerative outcomes, including growth factors (e.g., BMPs, VEGF), inorganic components such as hydroxyapatite, and pharmacological agents such as bisphosphonates [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. More recently, advanced approaches involving nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) and exosomes have emerged as promising strategies for bone regeneration [[15], [16], [17]]. However, prior to such modifications, it is essential to establish the baseline in vivo performance of the native carp collagen membrane in bony environment.

Evaluation of membrane performance in a bone defect model is essential to assess its functional behavior under conditions relevant to GBR. The present study aimed to investigate the behavior of the carp collagen membrane in a standardized femoral bone defect model in rats. Bone healing was assessed using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histological scoring systems, allowing evaluation over time. In addition, local soft-tissue response was analyzed to further characterize the biocompatibility of the material in a bone-associated environment.

Specifically, the primary objective was to determine whether coverage of a bone defect with the carp collagen membrane influences bone healing compared with spontaneous healing of an untreated defect. A secondary objective was to compare the performance of the carp collagen membrane with that of a widely used porcine collagen membrane (Bio-Gide®) when both were applied in combination with a bovine xenograft material (Bio-Oss®) in a typical GBR configuration.

Rats represent a commonly used and well-characterized model for experimental bone-healing studies. Although rodent bone lacks a classical Haversian system, the sequence and biological principles of bone repair have been shown to resemble those observed in larger animals [18,19]. Wistar rats, in particular, have shown similarities to human anatomy and are therefore the optimal first-line model due to their manageable size and reduced ethical and economic burden [20].

Methods

Carp collagen membrane preparation used in experiment

The preparation of the carp collagen membrane has been described in detail in our previous study [8]. For the present experiment, a membrane identical to membrane type A from the previous work was used. Here we describe its production process briefly. Aqueous collagen dispersion (4 wt%) was homogenized and left for 60 min at a temperature of 20 °C. The resulting dispersion was placed in separate containers frozen at −70 °C for 5 h and then lyophilized (BenchTop 4KZL, VirTis, CA, USA). Collagen was crosslinked subsequently by N-ethyl-N'-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (Sigma-Aldrich, MI, USA) at an EDC/NHS ratio of 4/1 (wt/wt) for 24 h. Finally, the membranes were washed, frozen (−30 °C) and lyophilized. The isolation procedure of carp collagen is described in detail by Lukáč et al. [10].

All membranes were packed in indicator bags, sealed, and sterilized by gamma irradiation (nominal dose 25 kGy; BIOSTER, Veverská Bítýška, Czech Republic). Immediately before implantation, membranes were cut under sterile conditions to a standardized size of 8 × 6 × 2 mm.

Cross-sections of the membrane after sterilization is shown in Supplementary Figure. S1.

Experimental animals and ethical approval

Twelve-week-old male Wistar rats (provided by Velaz, s.r.o, Prague, Czech Republic) were used as the experimental model. Animals were housed and all surgical procedures were performed at the Center for Advanced Preclinical Imaging (CAPI), Prague.

All procedures complied with Act No 246/1992 Coll. on the Protection of Animals Against Cruelty. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Faculty of Medicine, Charles University, and the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sports of the Czech Republic (Approval No MSMT-26,149/2022–4). The experiments followed Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Experimental design and surgical procedure

A total of 21 rats were randomly assigned to three experimental groups (n = 7 per group). Each animal underwent surgery under general anesthesia induced by 3% isoflurane in air delivered to the enclosed chamber. After sedation, the animal was placed on heated operating table, secured, and anesthesia was maintained using 1.5–2% isoflurane administered through a rodent face mask.

All surgical procedures were performed under sterile conditions. Both hind limbs were shaved on the dorsal aspect and disinfected with Skinsept Mucosa (Ecolab Deutschland GmbH, Germany). On each lateral femoral region, a longitudinal skin incision extending from the knee to the pelvic region (approx. 4 cm, depending on each animal size) was made. The quadriceps muscle heads were bluntly separated through the fascia to expose the lateral surface of the femur from knee joint to the proximal metaphysis, taking care to avoid damage to surrounding structures. Subsequently, cortical bone defects measuring 4 × 2 mm were created using a 1 mm round burr at low rotational speed under continuous saline irrigation. The depth of the defect corresponded to the thickness of the cortical bone; penetration into the cancellous bone was confirmed by a sudden loss of drilling resistance. In each animal, two defects per femur were created (four defects in total). One defect was placed in the proximal metaphysis (within the trochanteric region) and one in the distal metaphysis.

Each defect received a predefined treatment, resulting in four treatment groups. The proximal defect of the right femur was left untreated (empty defect control). The distal defect of the right femur was covered with the experimental carp collagen membrane. In the left femur, both defects were filled with bovine-derived xenograft material (Bio-Oss®, Geistlich Pharma AG, Switzerland); the proximal defect was subsequently covered with the carp collagen membrane, while the distal defect was covered with a porcine collagen membrane (Bio-Gide®, Geistlich Pharma AG, Switzerland). Assignment of treatments to fixed anatomical locations was chosen to minimize the risk of cross-contamination between adjacent defects.

Following defect treatment, all wounds were closed in layers. The skin was closed using a running intradermal suture with buried knots to prevent postoperative self-removal.

Postoperative analgesia was provided by subcutaneous administration of ketoprofenum (Ketonal, Sandoz Pharmaceuticals d.d., Ljubljana, Slovenia) at dose of 5 mg/kg. Animals were returned to their individual cages and allowed to recover under standard housing conditions. No animals showed signs of postoperative infection, necrosis, or systemic illness, and no animals were excluded from the experiment due to postoperative complications.

Animals were euthanized at predefined time points, group 1 at 4 weeks, group 2 at 12 weeks, and group 3 at 16 weeks after surgery.

Evaluation

After euthanasia, the femurs together with overlying soft tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde solution (Medilab, Czech Republic) at room temperature until further processing.

micro-CT

The formaldehyde-fixed specimens (14 femora per group; 42 femora in total) were scanned using a desktop micro-CT device SkyScan 1272 (Bruker micro-CT, Belgium). The scanning parameters were as follows: camera binning 4 × 4, pixel size 21 μm, source voltage 100 kV, source current 100 μA, filter Cu 0.11 mm, rotation step = 0.6°, 180° rotation, and total scanning time per specimen 2.5 h. Due to the size of the samples, 3 vertical connecting scans in central position of the camera were performed.

The micro-CT data was processed using Bruker micro-CT software as described below. The projection images were reconstructed using NRecon, and the visualizations were acquired using DataViewer. Measurements were performed in CTAn software.

The micro-CT data was subjected to semiquantitative analysis. The healing of bone defects was scored from 1 (poor) to 4 (excellent). Additionally, a regeneration ratio was calculated for the treatment groups containing Bio-Oss®. For the regeneration ratio, the area of the bone augmentation and regeneration was measured in 2D using CTAn. During micro-CT evaluation, fractures at the defect site were identified in four animals. Three animals exhibited a fracture in one distal femoral defect (two right, one left), and one animal presented fractures of both distal femora. Data from fractured defects were excluded from both micro-CT and histological analyses and were not included in the statistical evaluation. Reduced sample sizes (n < 7) are indicated in the text.

Histological analysis

After micro-CT analysis, specimens were transferred to the Department of Pathology, General University Hospital in Prague. Two transverse sections perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of each femur were obtained. The excised segments, including overlying soft tissues, were decalcified in Crutsay solution (distilled water 920 ml, ferric chloride pentahydrate 20 g, hydrochloric acid 80 ml). Decalcification was monitored after 24 h and was typically completed within 24–48 h.

Following decalcification, specimens were dehydrated in an automated tissue processor and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections (5–7 µm thick) were prepared using a Leica SM2010 R microtome and dried in at 56 °C. Sections were stained according to the protocol of Nowalk et al. [21] and evaluated using the bone healing scoring system described by Han et al. [22].

Four parameters were assessed: newly formed bone, newly formed cartilage, newly formed fibrous tissue, and remnant defect size. Newly formed bone and remnant defect size were normalized to the original defect area, whereas newly formed cartilage and fibrous tissue were normalized to the remnant defect size. Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 to 10, where higher scores reflect greater quantities of bone or cartilage and lower scores reflect increased fibrous tissue. The final healing score was calculated as the sum of all four parameter scores, yielding a total possible score between 0 and 40. A maximum score of 40 indicated complete osseous healing of the defect, whereas a score of 0 represented a defect without any newly formed tissue or evidence of healing.

Soft tissue overlying the femoral defects were evaluated using an adapted scoring system by Lindner et al. [23] to assess the local irritation potential of implanted biomaterials. The empty defect served as an internal control for each animal. Its value marked as „control irritancy score“ reflected tissue response to surgical trauma alone, while the „irritancy score“ of treated defects reflected the combined effect of trauma and implanted materials.

The „final irritancy score“ for each treatment was calculated as the difference between the „irritancy score“ of the treated defect and the corresponding „control irritancy score“. Based on the „final irritancy score“, materials were classified as non-irritant (0.0–2.9), slight irritant (3.0–8.9), moderate irritant (9.0–15.0), or severe irritant (> 15.0). Negative values were interpreted as zero i.e. non-irritant [23].

Not all defects were evaluable in histological analysis. In several cases, the defect region was not adequately captured in the section plane or the sections were not interpretable due to technical limitations of sample preparation. In total, 14 defects were excluded from histological evaluation, including five defects (in four animals) that were excluded due to femoral fractures identified on micro-CT imaging. Consequently, the number of evaluable defects (n) varies between treatment groups and time points.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in RStudio (version 2025.09.2 + 418) [24]. The sample size (7 animals per group) was established in accordance with the principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement (3R). The number of animals was selected as the minimum compatible with maintaining methodological validity and enabling effect-size-based interpretation.

Data were evaluated as non-normally distributed. Descriptive statistics are presented as median values with interquartile range (IQR), and the number of observations (n) is reported for each group. No outliers were present. Individual defects (within the same animal) were treated as independent experimental units due to anatomically separated healing environments. Comparisons between treatment groups at each time point, as well as temporal comparisons within each treatment group, were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. When statistically significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Holm correction. In cases of non-significant results, effect sizes were calculated using η2 according to the formula η2 = (H - k + 1)/(n - k), where H is the Kruskal-Wallis statistic, k is the number of groups, and n the total sample size. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (η2 < 0.06), medium (0.06 ≤ η² < 0.14), or large effects (η² ≥ 0.14).

At 4 weeks, no statistically significant differences in healing scores were observed among the treatment groups (p = 0.055). Healing scores ranged from 2 to 4 across all evaluable defects. Median healing scores were 3 (IQR 3–3) in the untreated control group (n = 7), the carp collagen membrane group (n = 6), and the Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane group (n = 7), while the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group (n = 6) showed a slightly lower median value of 2.5 (IQR 2–3). A large effect size was observed (η² = 0.20).

At 12 weeks, differences between treatment groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.060), although a large effect size was observed (η² = 0.19). Healing scores ranged from 2 to 4 across all evaluable defects. Median healing scores were 3 (IQR 3–4) in the untreated control group (n = 7), 4 (IQR 4–4) in the carp collagen membrane group (n = 6), and 3 (IQR 3–3) in both the Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane (n = 7) and Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® groups (n = 7).

At 16 weeks, no statistically significant differences in healing scores were observed among the treatment groups (p = 0.731), with a moderate effect size (η² = 0.08). Healing scores ranged from 2 to 4 across all evaluable defects. Median healing scores were 3 (IQR 3–4) in the untreated control group (n = 7), 4 (IQR 3.25–4) in the carp collagen membrane group (n = 6), 3 (IQR 3–4) in the Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane group (n = 7), and 3.5 (IQR 2.25–4) in the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group (n = 6).

Analysis of healing scores over time revealed a statistically significant change only in defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone (p = 0.012). Post-hoc testing demonstrated significant increases in the scores between 4 and 12 weeks (p = 0.010) and between 4 and 16 weeks (p = 0.020). No statistically significant temporal changes were detected in the untreated control group (p = 0.27), the Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane group (p = 0.36), or the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group (p = 0.17); effect sizes in these groups were small.

Histological healing scores of femoral bone defects at 4, 12, and 16 weeks.

Values are presented as median [IQR], with the number of evaluable defects (n) indicated for each group. The healing score represents a composite semi-quantitative measure based on the relative proportions of newly formed bone, cartilage, fibrous tissue, and residual defect size, as described in the Methods section. Higher scores indicate a greater degree of defect healing, with a maximum possible score of 40. Variations in sample size reflect exclusion of specimens due to technical limitations (e.g., femoral fractures or insufficient histological sections), as described in the Methods section. At 12 weeks, a statistically significant difference between groups was observed (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.020), with post-hoc analysis identifying a significant difference between defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone and those treated with Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane (p = 0.007). No statistically significant differences were observed at 4 or 16 weeks.

Histological analysis at 4 weeks demonstrated no statistically significant differences in healing scores between treatment groups (p = 0.064), with a large effect size (η² = 0.18). The highest median healing score (32 of a maximum of 40) was observed in defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone. In five of six evaluable cases in this group, the defect was 80–90% filled with newly formed bone (score 9 in category „newly formed bone“). The lowest median healing score (17 of 40) was observed in untreated control defects. Differences in newly formed bone score among treatment groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.29) and were associated with a small effect size (η² = 0.03).

At 12 weeks, differences in healing scores between treatment groups reached statistical significance (p = 0.020). Post-hoc analysis identified a significant difference between defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone and those treated with the combination of Bio-Oss® and the carp collagen membrane (p = 0.007). Defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone again showed the highest median healing scores. In five of six evaluable cases, ≥90% of the defect area was filled with newly formed bone (score 10 of 10). Differences in newly formed bone score among treatment groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.073) but were associated with a large effect size (η² = 0.24).

At 16 weeks, differences between treatment groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.28), with a small effect size (η² = 0.03). The median score for newly formed bone in the carp collagen membrane group reached the maximum value (10 of 10). However, differences in newly formed bone among treatment groups remained non-significant (p = 0.060) but were associated with a large effect size (η² = 0.18).

Analysis of healing scores over time revealed a statistically significant change only in the untreated control group (p = 0.025). Post-hoc comparisons indicated a significant increase from 4 to 12 weeks (p = 0.010). Although median scores continued to rise from 12 to 16 weeks (17.0 → 24.5 → 25.0), the difference between 4 and 16 weeks did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12), likely due to inter-subject variability. No statistically significant temporal changes in healing scores were observed in any of the treated groups (carp collagen membrane: p = 0.56; Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane: p = 0.76; Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide®: p = 0.58); effect sizes were small to moderate. Similarly, no statistically significant temporal changes in newly formed bone were detected in any treated group, whereas a significant increase was observed in the untreated control group between 4 and 12 weeks (p = 0.010).

SOFT tissue reaction TO implanted biomaterials

Histological analysis was performed to asses the surrounding soft tissues and their reaction to the implanted biomaterials. Results are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Defects covered with the carp collagen membrane alone were classified as non-irritant at all evaluated time points. Median irritancy scores were 0 at 4 weeks (n = 6) and 12 weeks (n = 6) and 2 at 16 weeks (n = 5). A statistically significant increase in irritancy score over time was observed in this group (p = 0.017), with post-hoc differences between 4 and 16 weeks (p = 0.012) and between 12 and 16 weeks (p = 0.018).

Defects treated with the combination of Bio-Oss® and the carp collagen membrane were classified as slight irritants (median irritancy score = 6) at 4 weeks (n = 7). Irritancy scores decreased to median value of 2 at 12 weeks (n = 5) and to 1 at 16 weeks (n = 5), corresponding to classification as non-irritant at later time points. Changes over time were not statistically significant (p = 0.17), and the effect size was moderate (η² = 0.11).

Irritancy scores in defects treated with Bio-Oss® and Bio-Gide® remained within the non-irritant range throughout the experiment, with median scores of 2.5 at 4 weeks (n = 6), 3 at 12 weeks (n = 7), and 1 at 16 weeks (n = 6). Differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.47), and the effect size was small (η² = −0.03).

Comparisons between treatment groups revealed statistically significant differences in irritancy scores at 4 weeks (p = 0.025) and at 12 weeks (p = 0.018), whereas no differences were detected at 16 weeks. Post-hoc analysis at 4 weeks demonstrated a significant difference between defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone and those treated with Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane (p = 0.011), and a borderline non-significant difference between the carp collagen membrane alone and the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group (p = 0.085). At 12 weeks, significant differences were observed between the carp collagen membrane alone and the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group (p = 0.0075), and a borderline difference between the carp collagen membrane alone and the Bio-Oss® + carp collagen membrane group (p = 0.079). No statistically significant differences were detected between the two Bio-Oss®–containing groups at any time point.

Discussion

This study evaluated a collagen membrane derived from European carp skin in a rat femoral defect model, addressing the emerging evidence available for non-mammalian collagen materials in bone regeneration. In contrast to conventional porcine- and bovine-derived membranes, fish-derived collagen represents a potentially safer and more sustainable alternative, with minimal risk of zoonotic disease transmission and fewer ethical or religious constraints.

Across all evaluated time points, defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone showed consistently favorable healing characteristics, including a higher proportion of newly formed bone and smaller residual defect size compared with other groups. Composite healing scores were highest in this group at 4 and 12 weeks, while at 16 weeks they were comparable to those observed in the Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® group.

Despite the absence of statistically significant differences, several comparisons were associated with moderate to large effect sizes, indicating consistent trends in favor of the carp collagen membrane when used alone, particularly at earlier time points. In contrast, these trends were not observed when the carp collagen membrane was combined with Bio-Oss®. These findings suggest that, under the conditions of a non–critical-size metaphyseal defect, the membrane alone may provide sufficient stabilization of the healing environment and support early bone formation without the need for additional graft material. This observation is consistent with the current understanding that the primary function of GBR membranes extends beyond passive barrier formation, including mechanical stabilization of the clot and creation of a microenvironment conducive to cellular infiltration and early tissue organization [3].

Similar findings have been reported by Araújo et al. [25], who demonstrated that Bio-Oss® did not enhance new bone formation compared with controls in an animal model. In a clinical context, a study by Heberer et al. [26] further showed that xenograft placement in extraction sockets could even impair bone healing compared with ungrafted sites. Another study by Chen et al. [27] demonstrated that treatment with a barrier membrane alone was sufficient to achieve healing of periodontal intrabony defects and that the additional use of a bone graft did not result in a measurable improvement in healing outcomes. These observations indicate that in small, well-contained defects, the presence of slowly resorbing graft particles may delay complete defect resolution rather than promote it.

In defects treated with Bio-Oss® in combination with either the carp collagen membrane or Bio-Gide®, bone healing progressed more slowly and did not reach the degree of defect fill observed in membrane-only defects within the studied timeframe. Early healing in Bio-Oss®–treated defects was more pronounced than in untreated controls, which is in line with previous studies reporting initial space-maintaining effects of particulate grafts [28,29]. When comparing graft-based approaches, defects treated with Bio-Oss® in combination with the carp collagen membrane consistently showed lower healing scores, reduced newly formed bone, and larger residual defect size compared with defects treated with the standard Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® combination. Although these differences were not statistically significant, the observed trends showed the carp collagen membrane did not achieve comparable performance to the established porcine membrane when used in combination with particulate graft material.

Histological evaluation revealed differences earlier and more distinctly than micro-CT analysis, particularly at the 4-week time point. This discrepancy is expected, as histology allows detection of early trabecular bone formation that may remain below the resolution threshold of micro-CT imaging. In addition, micro-CT analysis in this study was partially constrained by the presence of overlying soft tissues, which were intentionally preserved to enable subsequent evaluation of soft-tissue reaction.

Evaluation of soft-tissue reaction demonstrated excellent biocompatibility of the carp collagen membrane. When used alone, the membrane was classified as non-irritant at all time points, with only a slight increase in median irritancy score observed at 16 weeks. This finding is consistent with our previous subcutaneous implantation study [8], confirming that favorable biocompatibility is maintained in a bone-associated environment.

In contrast, defects containing Bio-Oss® showed higher irritation scores at early time points compared with defects treated with the carp collagen membrane alone. This early response likely reflects the combined effect of surgical trauma and the presence of xenogeneic particulate material. Importantly, irritation scores decreased over time in all graft-containing groups, and no differences between biomaterials were detectable at 16 weeks, indicating resolution of the initial inflammatory response.

Several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. The number of animals per time point was limited, and fractures at some defect sites further reduced the number of evaluable samples. Additionally, histology captured fewer evaluable defects than micro-CT due to the limited number of available sections, which led to further data loss. The metaphyseal defects used in this study were not critical-size defects, as evidenced by substantial spontaneous healing in untreated controls by 16 weeks. This reduced intergroup separation at later time points and limited the ability to detect differences between treatment modalities. However, enlargement of the defect dimensions in the rat femur would substantially increase the risk of mechanical instability and fracture, which was observed in a limited number of cases in the present experiment and led to exclusion of five defects from the analysis. This constraint represents a well-recognized limitation of small-animal long-bone models and influenced the choice of defect size in order to preserve structural integrity and animal welfare.

The creation of multiple defects per animal introduces partial pseudoreplication. Although defects were anatomically separated and represented localized healing environments, systemic factors may still influence bone repair, reducing statistical independence. Proximal and distal metaphyseal regions differ in vascular supply and local bone turnover, which may have affected healing dynamics independently of the applied treatment. Furthermore, the four-walled metaphyseal defect does not replicate the clinical scenarios encountered in regenerative dentistry, where vertical or horizontal augmentation of atrophic ridges is typically required.

Future studies should focus on large-animal models with clinically relevant critical-size defects and true augmentation scenarios to determine whether these observations translate to more demanding regenerative conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, a collagen membrane derived from European carp skin was shown to support bone healing in a non–critical-size defect model. When used alone, it demonstrated higher median values across evaluated outcomes, including new bone formation and defect fill, although these differences were not statistically significant. Less favorable results were observed when combined with particulate graft material compared with the standard Bio-Oss® + Bio-Gide® configuration.

These findings suggest that fish-derived collagen may represent a promising alternative membrane material for guided bone regeneration, particularly in indications where membrane function alone is sufficient. Further studies in larger and more clinically relevant models are required to confirm these observations.
