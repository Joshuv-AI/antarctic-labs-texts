O-arm navigation vs. freehand screw placement in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: A comparative analysis of screw size and reoperation rates
Authors: Rahul Ramanathan, Connor Luck, Özgür Dede, Timothy Ward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Pedicle screw fixation is the gold standard for surgical correction of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). Prior to the adoption of intraoperative O-arm navigation, surgeons relied on freehand pedicle screw insertion. While freehand insertion demonstrates acceptable safety, comparative data on screw size selection between techniques remain limited. Screw size selection, screw size variability, and complication rates between O-arm navigation and freehand pedicle screw techniques in AIS surgery were compared.Methods:This was a retrospective comparative cohort study of AIS patients treated with either freehand or navigated pedicle screw insertion by a single senior surgeon. Two cohorts of consecutive AIS patients were analyzed: freehand (n = 137; February 2014-November 2018) and navigated (n = 137; September 2018-August 2024). Data were extracted from a prospectively maintained surgical registry. Measured outcomes included: (1) screw diameter distribution per vertebral level; (2) pedicles deemed too small to cannulate; (3) intra- and intervertebral screw size variability; (4) intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak rates; and (5) reoperation for symptomatic screws. Screw diameter distributions were analyzed by region and vertebral level. Variability in screw size was evaluated within vertebrae and between adjacent vertebrae. Rates of non-cannulatable pedicles, CSF leaks, and symptomatic screw reoperations were compared using chi-square and t-tests.Results:Operative time was longer in the navigated cohort (312 ± 54 minutes) compared to the freehand cohort (279 ± 48 minutes, P < .001). Navigation resulted in a broader range of screw diameters. Screws sized 4.0-4.5 mm were used in 49.6% of navigated cases vs. 0.4% of freehand cases (P < .0001), while screws ≥7.0 mm were used in 1.5% vs. 0%, respectively (P = .003). Use of 5.0 mm screws was similar (18.0% vs. 13.7%, P = .16), while screws sized 5.5-6.5mm were more common in freehand cases (85.9% vs. 30.8%, P < .0001). Intravertebral screw size differences ≥1.5 mm were seen in 3.6% of navigated vertebrae vs. 0.2% in freehand (P < .0001), and intervertebral (adjacent-level) differences occurred in 6.9% vs. 0% (P < .0001). Pedicles too small to cannulate were identified in 5.0% of navigated cases vs. 1.9% freehand (P < .0001), with greater right-sided prevalence in both cohorts. Intraoperative CSF leaks occurred in 2.7% of navigated cases vs. 0.7% freehand (P = .397). Symptomatic screw-related reoperations were infrequent in both cohorts (1.5% navigation vs. 3.6% freehand, P = .44).Conclusion:Navigation was associated with broader screw size utilization, greater intra- and intervertebral screw diameter variability, and improved identification of pedicles too small to safely instrument. Although complication and reoperation rates were low in both groups, there was a non-significant trend toward fewer symptomatic screw-related reoperations in the navigated cohort. These findings suggest that navigation enhances intraoperative matching of screw size to pedicle anatomy.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Multisegmental pedicle screw fixation remains the standard of care for surgical management of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). Historically, pedicle screw size selection in AIS surgery has been guided primarily by adult morphometric literature and empirical data, lacking standardization and validation specific to the pediatric AIS population.1-3 The advent of computed tomography (CT) has more recently allowed for quantification of pedicle dimensions in adolescents and children.4-6 Despite these advancements, there is still a lack of characterization of pedicle size variability in AIS, particularly when comparing traditional freehand screw selection to modern navigation-guided insertion. Consequently, evidence-based guidelines for optimal screw sizing in AIS surgery remain limited.

Descriptions of optimal pedicle entry points and trajectories also vary in the literature, leading to inconsistencies and nuances in surgical technique.1,7 Most surgeons traditionally use screw diameters between 5.0 and 5.5 mm, based largely on empiric practice. Freehand pedicle screw insertion relies heavily on anatomic landmarks and surgeon experience. Extensive literature supports that freehand pedicle screw insertion can be performed safely in AIS.5,8-12 However, postoperative CT analyses demonstrated that up to 40% of freehand thoracic screws may breach the cortex, particularly in the upper thoracic spine where pedicles are smallest.13-15 O-arm navigation has improved placement accuracy,4,13-18 but it is unclear if this translates into fewer symptomatic misplacements or reoperations. Notably, even with high breach rates, clinically significant screw-related complications remain low.10,19-21 This paradox of high breach rates but low complications partially explains the ongoing controversy regarding the routine use of navigation in AIS surgery.22

Several studies have compared navigation and freehand techniques with a focus on screw placement accuracy and breach rates, but no prior study has directly quantified how navigation impacts screw diameter selection or intraoperative identification of pedicle size variability at each level in AIS. It was hypothesized that navigation would result in a broader range of screw size utilization, more precise intraoperative matching of screw size to pedicle anatomy, and better detection of small or asymmetric pedicles that may not be safe to cannulate freehand.

Material and methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pittsburgh (IRB protocol STUDY20070337; modification approval MOD20070337-008) on March 20, 2025. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Given the retrospective design, informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Two consecutive retrospective cohorts of AIS patients treated by a single senior surgeon were identified: the last 137 cases performed freehand (February 2014–November 2018) and the first 137 cases performed with O-arm navigation (September 2018–August 2024). All procedures were single-surgeon posterior spinal fusions using pedicle screw instrumentation. Planned implant density and type of anchor implant were not standardized between the 2 cohorts. In both groups, if a planned pedicular screw could not be safely inserted, a hook was substituted, or the level was left uninstrumented. The upper instrumented vertebra frequently had dual hooks inserted in both cohorts. It is important to acknowledge this experimental design, as comparing a surgeon’s final cases using a technique they have optimized to their first cases using a developing technique introduces an inherent bias that may affect results. Specifically, for surgeons adopting O-arm navigation, as it has been proposed that a volume of 15-30 cases is required prior to acclimation of this technique,23,24 and this design may fail to account for the outcomes of O-arm navigation once proficiency is reached.

Patient selection

Inclusion criteria were patients under 18 years old undergoing primary posterior spinal fusion for AIS using pedicle screw instrumentation. Onset scoliosis, revision surgeries, and cases with syndromic, congenital, or neuromuscular scoliosis were excluded. Patients were included regardless of curve magnitude, curve type, or sex. Minimum follow-up was set at 6 months for assessment of reoperation rates and symptomatic screw complications; the navigation cohort included cases through August 2024, with the longest follow-up approaching 5 years.

Surgical technique

Freehand screws were placed using standard anatomic landmarks for entry. In the thoracic spine, the starting point was at the intersection of a vertical line extending from the lateral border of the exposed superior facet and ipsilateral pars, and a horizontal line bisecting the transverse process. In the lumbar spine, the pedicle screws were placed at the junction of the transverse process and superior facet. No intraoperative fluoroscopy was used for freehand cases beyond occasional plain radiographs for level localization and alignment.

In navigated cases, the O-arm and Stealth Station were used to determine entry point, trajectory, and optimal screw diameter by overlaying virtual screws to maximize endosteal fill without cortical breach. A high-speed burr or pedicle probe was used to cannulate the pedicles under navigation guidance. For extremely thin pedicles, an in-out-in technique was occasionally employed.7 All patients underwent neuromonitoring, with continuous somatosensory-evoked potential monitoring and motor-evoked potential checks performed after each screw was inserted. Triggered electromyography was not routinely used.

Rod constructs were composed of cobalt chrome rods in both groups, with diameters ranging from 5.5 to 6.0 mm depending on surgeon preference and deformity correction requirements.

Screw size determination

Screw size was determined based on the pedicle’s endosteal diameter. In navigated cases, this was visualized intraoperatively with the O-arm using virtual templating to select the largest possible screw that maintained cortical integrity. In freehand cases, size selection was guided by the surgeon’s tactile feedback during probing, standard anatomical landmarks, and empiric preference for 5.0- or 5.5-mm screws unless the pedicle felt too small to accept that size. There were no predefined standard sizes per vertebral level, and screw size was selected intraoperatively on a case-by-case basis.

Pedicles were deemed non-cannulatable if the surgeon judged that even a 4.0 mm screw would risk cortical breach, at which point the level was skipped or a hook was used instead.

Additional variables

For both cohorts, operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and radiation exposure were reviewed when available from the medical record. However, radiation exposure data were not consistently documented during the early freehand cases.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize screw size distributions, pedicle cannulation outcomes, and screw size variability. Categorical comparisons between cohorts were performed using chi-square tests, including analysis of screw diameter categories, intravertebral (left-right) screw size differences ≥1.5 mm, intervertebral (adjacent level) screw size differences ≥1.5 mm, and non-cannulatable pedicle rates.

Continuous variables such as age, number of screws per patient, and anchor density were compared using independent samples t-tests. Standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals were reported where appropriate. Regions with 0 expected frequencies were excluded to meet chi-square assumptions.

A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python (v3.11) with SciPy and Seaborn packages.

Results

General cohort comparisons

Although this was not a randomized study, the navigation and freehand cohorts were similar in baseline characteristics (Table 1). The mean age at surgery was 15.0 ± 2.1 years, with 80% female predominance. There was no significant difference in preoperative curve magnitude (largest curve in double major; P = .66), curve location (P = .20), average number of levels fused (P = .27), or number of screws per patient (P = .37).

The mean number of hooks per patient was higher in the freehand cohort (1.4 ± 0.8 vs. 1.1 ± 0.6, P = .01). The navigated cohort had a higher total anchor density (21.1 ± 3.1 vs. 19.7 ± 2.9, P = .001). More screws were placed in the navigated group overall (2739 navigated vs. 2487 freehand), primarily due to fewer skipped levels and higher planned pedicle screw density. Implant density was higher in the navigated group (1.68 ± 0.12 vs. 1.55 ± 0.14, P = .002).

Operative time, blood loss, length of stay, and radiation exposure

Mean operative time was longer in the navigated cohort (312 ± 54 minutes) compared to the freehand cohort (279 ± 48 minutes, P < .001). Estimated blood loss was similar between groups (510 ± 190 mL navigation vs. 495 ± 185 mL freehand, P = .51). Length of hospital stay did not differ significantly (4.1 ± 1.2 days navigation vs. 4.0 ± 1.3 days freehand, P = .64). Radiation exposure to the surgical team was not measured directly but was presumed higher in the navigated cohort due to O-arm use.

Screw size utilization by region

Upper thoracic pedicles (T1-T5)

Navigation significantly changed screw size selection in the upper thoracic spine. Screws sized 4.0 or 4.5 mm accounted for 79.5% of navigated screws at T1-T5, compared to 0% in the freehand group (P < .0001). Conversely, 99.2% of freehand screws in this region were 5.0 or 5.5 mm (P < .0001). Pedicle screws larger than 5.5 mm were not used in the upper thoracic spine in either group. The navigated cohort identified and avoided cannulation in small pedicles at a higher rate, resulting in more frequent use of hooks at these levels (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 1).

Apical thoracic pedicles (T6-T9)

In the apical thoracic region, navigated screw size selection was similarly shifted toward smaller diameters. Screws sized 4.0 or 4.5 mm were used in 70.8% of navigated cases compared to 0.6% of freehand cases (P < .0001). Conversely, 97.1% of freehand screws in this region were 5.0 or 5.5 mm (P < .0001) (Figure 2).

Lower thoracic pedicles (T10-T12)

Navigation resulted in a broader range of screw sizes in the lower thoracic spine. Screws sized 4.0 or 4.5 mm were used in 25.2% of navigated cases compared to 0.7% of freehand cases (P < .0001). Screws sized 7.0 or 7.5 mm were used in 2.7% of navigated cases and never in freehand cases. The majority of screws in both cohorts remained in the 5.0 to 6.5 mm range, but use of these sizes was lower in the navigated group (72.1% navigation vs. 99.3% freehand, P < .0001) (Figure 3).

Lumbar pedicles (L1-L4)

In the lumbar spine, navigated cases again showed greater screw size variability. Screws sized 4.0 or 4.5 mm were used in 25.8% of navigated cases, while no freehand cases used screws of this size (P < .0001). Screws sized 7.0 or 7.5 mm were used in 3.5% of navigated cases compared to 0% in freehand. Both groups primarily used screws between 5.0 and 6.5 mm, though the navigated group used these sizes less frequently (70.7% vs. 100%, P < .0001) (Figure 4).

Intra- and intervertebral screw size variability

Navigated cases demonstrated significantly greater screw size variability both within individual vertebrae and between adjacent levels. In 3.6% of navigated vertebrae, left-to-right screw sizes differed by ≥1.5 mm, compared to 0.2% in freehand cases (P < .0001). There was a significant difference in the distribution of ≥1.5 mm screw width between regions (P = .003), with the highest rates of asymmetry in the lower thoracic spine of the navigated cohort (Figure 5).

Adjacent vertebral segment screw size differences ≥1.5 mm occurred in 7.7% of navigated segments versus 0% of freehand cases (P < .0001). These differences were most frequent at the thoracolumbar junction, particularly between T9-T10, T12-L1, and L2-L3 (Figure 6).

Non-cannulatable pedicles

Pedicles deemed too small to cannulate (unable to accommodate a 4.0 mm screw) were more common in the navigated cohort (5.0% navigation vs. 1.9% freehand, P < .0001). This was especially pronounced on the right side, where 7.6% of pedicles in the navigated group were non-cannulatable versus 2.7% in the freehand group (P < .0001). On the left side, rates were 1.7% and 1.0%, respectively (P = .21).

The most frequently non-cannulatable levels in the navigated group were T3 (41.7%), T4 (26.4%), and T5 (12.8%). In the freehand group, T4 (17.7%) and T5 (3.6%) were most common.

Complications and reoperation rates

Intraoperative CSF leaks occurred in 4 patients (2.7%) in the navigated group and 1 patient (0.7%) in the freehand group (P = .397). All CSF leaks were addressed intraoperatively with dural repair or sealant application, and only 1 required postoperative revision for persistent leak.

Five patients experienced symptomatic screw-related reoperations in the freehand cohort (3.6%) compared to 2 patients in the navigated group (1.5%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .44). In the navigated group, 1 patient required revision for a persistent CSF leak and another for postoperative sensory changes due to medial breach. All symptoms resolved after screw removal.

In the freehand cohort, 5 patients underwent reoperation for symptomatic screws involving a total of 15 screws (fourteen 5.5 mm screws and 1 6.5 mm screw). Presentations included chest wall radiculopathy, sensory deficits, and 1 transient lower extremity weakness. All cases resolved following screw removal.

No cases of screw pull-out, mechanical failure, or hardware-related reoperation were identified in either cohort during the follow-up period.

Discussion

As far as is known, this is the first study that has analyzed in detail the variance in screw width utilization between O-arm navigated and freehand pedicle screw insertion for AIS surgery. In this series, 51% of navigated screws were <5.0 mm or >6.5 mm, sizes that were essentially never used freehand. This confirms the hypothesis that navigation enables more precise pedicle screw size selection. Through navigation, it appeared more likely to keep the screws within the confines of the pedicle walls, potentially improving fixation strength, especially in the small subset of pedicles in which 7.0- or 7.5-mm screws were inserted. Such precise sizing is difficult to achieve consistently with the freehand technique, regardless of surgeon experience.

Non-cannulatable pedicles were identified at all thoracic levels but were most common from T3 to T5, where 21% of navigated pedicles were deemed too small (<4 mm) for safe screw insertion compared to only 0.8% in freehand cases. These findings strongly suggest that many upper thoracic pedicles that accepted 5.0- or 5.5-mm screws with the freehand technique likely experienced cortical breach or plastic deformation. Navigation reveals these limitations intraoperatively, enabling surgeons to avoid unsafe cannulation. These observations align with prior studies indicating improved accuracy and fewer cortical breaches with navigation techniques.4,13-15,25

It was further observed that thoracolumbar pedicle widths differed by at least 1.5 mm between left and right sides in 3.6% of vertebrae and ≥1.5 mm between adjacent levels in 7.7% of segments in navigated cases. While these percentages are relatively low, they underscore clinically relevant anatomical variability that can affect surgical decisions regarding screw sizing. Such subtle asymmetries may be overlooked with freehand insertion, leading to inadvertent pedicle breach or suboptimal screw sizing, potentially compromising fixation or safety. This data highlights the ability of navigation to detect this previously underappreciated variability, thereby guiding safer and more tailored screw placement.

The ideal starting point and trajectory for pedicle screw insertion are straightforward with navigation, as the axial and sagittal directions are clearly displayed on the navigation monitor. In contrast, neither the starting point nor trajectory is as intuitive for freehand pedicle screw insertion. Fluoroscopy or intraoperative radiographs, commonly recommended for freehand techniques,4,13,14 may reduce breach rates, but they provide less comprehensive anatomical information compared to navigation. However, while navigation likely ensures better pedicle confinement of screws, this increased precision did not translate into a statistically significant reduction in clinically meaningful complications or reoperations.10,19

Prior literature has documented a “safe zone” of pedicle wall violation up to 2-4 mm without significant clinical symptoms.26,27 However, it remains unclear whether this safety margin exists consistently across all AIS patients and vertebral levels.28 Rather than relying on theoretical safe zones, it is contended that it is safer to contain screws fully within the undilated pedicle bone, leveraging navigation to precisely match screw size to the individual patient's pedicle anatomy.

Intraoperative blood loss and length of hospital stay did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that neither freehand nor navigation techniques result in increased perioperative complications that require monitoring after surgery. The navigation group, however, experienced a significantly longer operative duration than the freehand group, a finding that should be considered for surgeons who elect to use navigation to correct AIS, as prolonged operative time is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications.29

Clinically, these findings imply that navigation may enhance safety margins for surgeons less experienced with pedicle screw insertion, those working in anatomically challenging cases, or when training residents and fellows. While reoperation rates were low in both groups, there was an observational trend of lower reoperation rate with navigation (1.5% vs. 3.6%). This study was likely underpowered to detect a significant difference, which merits further investigation with larger cohorts.

Several limitations must be clearly acknowledged. This study is retrospective, non-randomized, and represents the experience of a single senior surgeon. The single-surgeon design eliminates inter-surgeon variability as a confounder but may limit generalizability, as freehand technique varies widely among surgeons. Additionally, this study compared the surgeon’s matured freehand technique to the initial cases performed using O-arm navigation, potentially underestimating the full benefits achievable after navigation proficiency is reached.23,30-33 Planned implant density and type of anchor implant were not standardized between the cohorts, therefore introducing variability among cases and limiting the ability to directly compare findings across cohorts. Follow-up duration differed between cohorts, and although the minimum follow-up was sufficient to detect early symptomatic screw complications, longer follow-up might reveal differences in hardware failure or delayed complications not observed in this study. Furthermore, because O-arm navigation increases radiation exposure compared to freehand technique,12,34-37 the absence of quantitative radiation exposure data in this study is a notable limitation. Recent adoption of low-dose pediatric imaging protocols can significantly reduce radiation exposure, and such strategies should be considered standard practice in pediatric spinal deformity surgery moving forward.

In practical terms, this study supports navigation as an optional enhancement rather than mandatory standard practice, particularly beneficial for surgeons prioritizing precision, patient-specific pedicle sizing, or training surgical residents. Future prospective studies should evaluate whether navigation-driven precision translates into improved long-term outcomes or reduced mechanical complications and should also seek to mitigate navigation-related downsides, notably radiation exposure.

Conclusion

During its initial adoption, O-arm navigation in AIS surgery enables more accurate and tailored pedicle screw sizing, revealing a wider range of pedicle dimensions than appreciated with the freehand technique. Navigated screws were more fully contained within pedicles, suggesting fewer breaches by inference. Additionally, fewer symptomatic screw complications requiring reoperation were observed in the navigated cohort, though this was not statistically significant and the overall incidence of such events remained low in both groups. Despite these findings, it is important to consider the surgeon’s inexperience in O-arm navigation relative to their matured freehand technique, and how this impacted the results. Further investigation into the merits of a developed O-arm navigation technique remains warranted. While navigation offers advantages in precision and pedicle size assessment, experienced surgeons using freehand techniques can achieve similarly low complication rates. Thus, navigation should currently be viewed as a valuable adjunctive tool rather than a mandatory standard. Surgeons prioritizing optimal screw-pedicle matching, training residents, or operating in anatomically challenging cases may particularly benefit from adopting O-arm navigation. Future studies should further investigate whether navigation's increased precision translates into meaningful clinical outcomes or reduced hardware-related complications over longer-term follow-up and continue addressing methods to minimize radiation exposure.
