Abstract
. Collective operations are cornerstones of both HPC application and large-scale AI training and inference. Yet, comprehensive, systematic and reproducible performance evaluation and benchmarking of said operations is not straightforward. Existing frameworks do not provide sufficiently detailed profiling information, nor they ensure reproducibility and extensibility. In this paper, we present PICO (Performance Insights for Collective Operations), a novel lightweight, extensible framework built with the aim of simplifying collective operations benchmarking.



1.
Introduction

While recent High Performance Computing (HPC) systems have reached and even surpassed exascale performance
(
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory 2025
;
TOP500 Project 2025
)
, the growing disparity between computational capability and data movement remains a critical obstacle. Processor performance continues to improve at a faster pace than memory and interconnect technologies, increasing the relative cost of communication. As HPC systems scale to ever-larger sizes, the efficiency of data transfer increasingly determines both application performance and overall system scalability
(
ke Liao et al. 2018
;
Lucas et al. 2014
;
Lu et al. 2022
)
.

Collective operations, being among the most communication-sensitive components of distributed-memory applications, are particularly affected by this trend and face mounting performance challenges on large-scale HPC clusters
(
Hwang and Xu 1998
)
. Together with point-to-point communication, they form the backbone of traditional HPC workloads as well as AI training and inference tasks
(
Weingram et al. 2023
)
. At large scales, the cost of collective communication often dominates application runtime
(
Laguna et al. 2019
;
Weingram et al. 2023
;
Balaji et al. 2009
)
, making the design and optimization of efficient collective algorithms a key research priority
(
Sensi et al. 2024
;
Sewell et al. 2024
;
Bienz et al. 2022
;
Basu et al. 2024
;
Sack and Gropp 2015
;
Lim and Kim 2025
)
.

Understanding and optimizing the performance of collective communication remains challenging due to multiple sources of complexity. Collective operations combine computation, memory, and network activity, and isolating the impact of each component is crucial for identifying system and algorithmic bottlenecks. Additional difficulties arise from modern supercomputers employing both scale-up and scale-out networks with diverse topologies, the coexistence of multiple user-level libraries (e.g., MPI implementations, *CCL
(
NVIDIA Corporation 2025a
;
Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. 2025
;
He et al. 2021
;
Weingram et al. 2023
)
), and heterogeneous network software stacks that vary in version and low-level APIs such as OFI and UCX
(
The Unified Communication X Library [n. d.]
;
OpenFabrics Interfaces Working Group (2025) OFIWG
)
.

Variability in physical interconnect technologies further contributes to unpredictability. Beyond hardware and software heterogeneity, dynamic runtime conditions, including network congestion
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, job allocation policies
(
Qureshi et al. 2020
)
, and task-to-node mappings
(
Kovalov et al. 2017
)
, cause performance fluctuations that are difficult to predict, reproduce, or control. These challenges hinder accurate performance characterization and complicate the design, evaluation, and reproducibility of new collective algorithms. Consequently, systematic and comprehensive benchmarking frameworks are essential to capture realistic behavior and guide optimization and innovation effectively.

Although several benchmarking tools such OSU Micro-Benchmarks (OMB)
(
The Ohio State University [n. d.]
)
, NCCL Test
(
NVIDIA Corporation [n. d.]
)
, Intel MPI Benchmark suite
(
Intel Corporation 2021
)
, and CommBench
(
Hidayetoglu et al. 2024
)
, have been developed to evaluate collective communication performance, as detailed in Section
5
, they only partially address the challenges outlined above. Namely, they do not provide fine-grained profiling of the different phases taking place in a collective operation, nor do they allow for easy comparison of different algorithms for the same collective operation (including custom ones).

For these reasons, we introduce
PICO
(Performance Insights for Collective Operations), a lightweight, extensible, and user-friendly framework for benchmarking collective operations across diverse communication libraries and hardware platforms.
PICO
supports MPI, NCCL, and user-defined collectives across multiple interconnect stacks, simplifying the entire benchmarking process, from test creation and execution to data collection and analysis. It features a guided Terminal User Interface (
TUI
) for intuitive test setup, while also supporting full command-line automation for scripting and integration.
PICO
automatically captures detailed metadata, including environment variables, runtime parameters, hardware and software configurations, and network-stack settings, ensuring reproducibility and traceability. Fine-grained instrumentation enables performance analysis at the algorithmic step level, and integrated analysis tools provide immediate insights through visualization, comparison, and tracing capabilities. These qualities make
PICO
suitable for researchers developing new algorithms, system administrators tuning deployments, and application developers exploring performance-critical configuration parameters.

To motivate the design and implementation of the

2.1.
Use Case 1: Algorithm Design

Interconnect technology is becoming increasingly complex. Modern supercomputers often feature multi-GPU nodes, where GPUs are connected through
scale-up
networks (e.g., NVLink
(
Merritt 2023
)
, InfinityFabric
(
Schor 2018
)
, UALink
(
Ames and Lowman 2025
)
, UnifiedBuffer
(
Zuo et al. 2025
)
, or Scale-Up Ethernet (SUE)
(
Broadcom Inc. 2025
)
), which provide up to an order of magnitude higher bandwidth
(
De Sensi et al. 2024
)
than the
scale-out
networks (e.g., InfiniBand
(
Buyya et al. 2002
)
, Slingshot
(
De Sensi et al. 2020
)
, or UltraEthernet
(
Ultra Ethernet Consortium 2025
)
) that connect GPUs across different nodes. The size and scope of scale-up networks are rapidly expanding, now supporting up to 72
(
NVIDIA Corporation 2025b
)
or even 384
(
Zuo et al. 2025
)
GPUs within a single domain. Meanwhile, scale-out networks are frequently tapered, as in Dragonfly
(
Kim et al. 2008
;
Zwinger et al. 2023
;
Atchley et al. 2023
;
De Sensi et al. 2020
)
, Dragonfly+
(
Turisini et al. 2023
;
Shpiner et al. 2017
)
, or tapered fat-tree topologies
(
Banchelli et al. 2025
;
Forschungszentrum Jülich, Jülich Supercomputing Centre 2025
;
Sensi et al. 2022
)
.

This architectural diversity introduces significant heterogeneity, with communication performance between two endpoints depending on whether they share the same scale-up network and, for the scale-out network, whether they reside within the same local switch domain or in different, more distant groups (e.g., in Dragonfly or Dragonfly+ groups)
(
De Sensi et al. 2024
;
De Sensi et al. 2020
)
. Consequently, traditional collective algorithms, which typically assume homogeneous interconnects, often underperform. To address this, several hierarchical and topology-aware algorithms have been developed to better exploit available network resources
(
De Sensi et al. 2025
;
Bienz et al. 2022
;
Kandalla et al. 2009
;
Träff 2006
)
.

Designing and evaluating new collective algorithms requires simple and reliable ways to benchmark them against state-of-the-art implementations. Existing frameworks such as OSU
(
The Ohio State University [n. d.]
)
, IMB
(
Intel Corporation 2021
)
, NCCL Test
(
NVIDIA Corporation [n. d.]
)
, and CommBench
(
Hidayetoglu et al. 2024
)
do not provide explicit, portable mechanisms for algorithm selection. While users can manually control algorithm choice through environment variables or configuration files, this approach is error-prone, adds unnecessary complexity, and may not be consistent across different library versions. Moreover, comparing the newly designed algorithm against those provided by different MPI and *CCL libraries would also add additional complexity requiring, for example, to recompile the benchmark everytime with a different MPI library (e.g., once with Open MPI, once with MPICH, etc…).

PICO
addresses these limitations by automating algorithm selection through its benchmark orchestrator once the configuration is defined, and by providing a guided Terminal User Interface (
TUI
) that clearly presents the available algorithms and assists in creating portable test scenarios across supported MPI and *CCL libraries (see Sec.
3.1
). In addition,
PICO
includes an integrated analysis pipeline that organizes, processes, and visualizes benchmark results, enabling efficient comparison of algorithms and identification of performance trends.

A collective operation can often be decomposed into multiple phases (for example, an
Allreduce
can be implemented as a
Reduce-scatter
followed by an
Allgather
), and each phase is further divided into several steps or rounds. Profiling each phase and step individually is crucial to identify sources of inefficiency. Moreover, collective operations typically involve three main components: computation (e.g., in
Reduce
,
Reduce-scatter
, and
Allreduce
collectives), memory allocation and data movement (e.g., copying data to temporary or destination buffers), and network communication (transferring data between ranks). Profiling these components separately enables algorithm designers to focus their optimization efforts on the parts that most significantly affect performance. It also allows users and application developers to determine whether observed bottlenecks originate from the network, memory subsystem, or CPU/GPU computation.

2.2.
Use Case 2: Tuning

The performance of collective operations depends on several factors, with the choice of the communication library being one of the most critical. Although *CCL libraries are often assumed to outperform MPI for collectives, primarily because they are optimized for GPU-to-GPU communication, this is not always the case. Depending on the number of nodes, message sizes, and CPU/GPU models, certain MPI implementations can outperform *CCL
(
De Sensi et al. 2024
)
. Additionally, even for a single MPI implementation, collectives can run on different backends (e.g., UCX
(
The Unified Communication X Library [n. d.]
)
, OFI/libfabric
(
OpenFabrics Interfaces Working Group (2025) OFIWG
)
, UCC
(
Venkata et al. 2024
)
, etc…), with different performance.

Performance is also highly sensitive to library and backends parameters and environment variables, which, when properly tuned, can yield speedups of 3–4x
(
De Sensi et al. 2024
)
. This is especially relevant for GPU-resident buffers, where settings such as the number of channels in *CCL (i.e., CUDA blocks used for transfers
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
), MPI staging buffer size, the number of
queue pairs
per connection, or DMA enablement can substantially affect performance
(
De Sensi et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
. Similarly, UCX
(
The Unified Communication X Library [n. d.]
)
tuning parameters, including the number of rails and the rendezvous protocol threshold, have a significant impact.

Determining the most suitable library for a given application, based on its message sizes and number of ranks, while navigating dozens of parameters with many possible values, is a challenging and time-consuming task. Existing benchmarking frameworks provide no assistance in this process, leaving users to rely on trial and error and to manually collect and organize results.
PICO
addresses these limitations by automating parameter sweeps across multiple values, capturing both performance data and detailed metadata for systematic analysis.

2.3.
Use Case 3: Regression Testing

Regression testing in HPC environments is complicated by the deep software stack underlying collective operations. As discussed before, within a single MPI implementation, collective operations may still run on different backends or vendor-specific transports, with each of these layers introducing its own set of tunable parameters, version-specific behaviors, and GPU-aware extensions. Changes to and updates to any layer, can cause non-trivial shifts in performance, making it difficult to pinpoint the source of regressions. This complexity is amplified when systems deploy multiple MPI or *CCL implementations, where subtle differences in defaults or internal optimizations can lead to inconsistent results.

Existing benchmarking suites offer only limited support for detecting and diagnosing such regressions. Tools like OSU, IMB, NCCL Test, and CommBench can measure collective performance but typically require manual configuration and lack mechanisms for systematically capturing metadata about the software stack. As a result, even when a performance change is detected, it is often unclear whether it stems from an MPI update, a backend library version, or modifications in low-level transport layers. Furthermore, most benchmarks provide no automated way to compare results across different runs or library versions in a reproducible and structured way.

PICO
addresses these gaps by offering reproducible, metadata-rich benchmarking tailored to regression testing. It automatically captures environment variables, software versions, backend configurations, and relevant runtime parameters alongside performance data, enabling systematic tracking of changes across updates. Its guided test creation and automation features simplify benchmarking across MPI, NCCL, and user-defined collectives on different backends.
PICO
’s integrated analysis tools allow system administrators to quickly identify when and why performance has shifted, streamlining the process of regression testing in complex HPC software environments.

3.1.
Configuration and Orchestration

A central design goal of
PICO
is to streamline the benchmarking process, making it accessible and repeatable without extensive scripting or manual setup. Tests can be launched, results recorded, and analysis generated with minimal human intervention making
PICO
suitable for system validation and scalable performance studies (Sec.
2.2
-
2.3
). The configuration of
PICO
is specified through declarative
JSON
files, which fall into two primary categories: the ones that define the characteristics and configuration of the target machine, and those that specify the details of an individual test execution. Those files are fed into a shell-based testing script that prepares the environment, compiles the necessary backend-specific executables, sumbits a
sbatch
script, and manages execution and result collection.

3.1.1.
Evironment Configuration

It consists of a set of
JSON
files that define the characteristics of a specific cluster. These files describe available MPI/*CCL versions, their backend libraries, hardware details, relevant module settings, and SLURM job parameters. This configuration is defined once per cluster and reused across multiple tests. As a result, once the environment is properly set up, the end user benefits from a streamlined and guided testing process.

Fig.
2
shows one of the required configuration files, in this case, the one describing the communication libraries. As shown, the environment configuration files contain part of the metadata relevant to a benchmark, which will be stored alongside tests’ results themselves to improve reproducibility.

Test Configuration

The test is defined via a
JSON

While the

3.1.3.
Benchmark Orchestration

The execution of benchmarks in
PICO
is managed by a dedicated orchestration script that automates all steps from configuration parsing to result collection. The process begins by reading the benchmark configuration from the
test.json
file and the environment description from the
env.json
files. Based on these inputs, the orchestrator loads the required software modules and compiles the benchmark code, performing a separate build for each communication library under test to ensure clean isolation.

Once compiled, the orchestrator submits the benchmark job via
sbatch
, automatically setting the appropriate job parameters for the selected benchmark. It also applies cluster-specific settings, such as specifying QoS requirements where necessary. Before execution, the script prepares a structured output directory for the test run. When multiple libraries are being evaluated in sequence, the orchestrator performs a complete environment reset (
module purge
) before each run. It then reloads the correct set of modules, and records the current software environment, including detailed outputs from tools such as
ompi-info
and
ucx_info
, to ensure reproducibility (to be discussed in Sec.
3.3.3
). The benchmark executable is then launched with the appropriate runtime parameters (the number of iterations, message sizes, and datatypes) defined in the configuration. For each iteration the orchestrator ensures that the selected algorithm is updated correctly between iterations when required.

3.2.
PICO_core
and
libpico

PICO_core
is a benchmarking harness that includes backends for MPI, and NCCL. It supports conditional compilation via preprocessor directives, allowing the script to generate multiple backend-specific executables via a single source code. While binary compatibility can be possible in some cases (e.g., when compiling Open MPI 4.x and 5.x), the system favors full recompilation per backend to ensure robustness and maintain clean isolation between libraries. Users can test different libraries or versions by selecting them through the
TUI
/
CLI
, which triggers compilation and execution of the corresponding benchmark binary.
PICO_core
serves as the central execution engine, responsible for running each benchmark instance and storing the results in the directories designated by the orchestrator.

The
libpico
module complements this core by providing reference implementations of common collective algorithms (e.g., ring, rabenseifner…), reimplemented using plain
MPI_Send/Recv
operations. Adapted from major MPI libraries such as Open MPI and MPICH, these versions serve as standardized, backend-agnostic baselines that enable consistent comparisons independent of vendor-specific optimizations. This same mechanism also supports user-defined algorithms, enabling developers to prototype and benchmark custom collective strategies within the unified
PICO
framework. All
libpico
algorithms, whether reference or custom, integrate seamlessly with
PICO
’s execution pipeline, result capture, and post-processing tools.

In addition,
libpico
supports lightweight instrumentation macros that can be inserted into algorithm implementations to measure the performance of individual phases, such as memory allocation, computation, or specific communication rounds. Implementing these macros outside complex MPI internals makes them easy to deploy and adapt for experimental analysis. The design and usage of these macros are detailed in Sec.
3.4.3
and in Sec.
4.1
.

3.3.3.
Metadata Logging and Reproducibility

To ensure reproducibility and support detailed analysis,
PICO
captures a comprehensive set of metadata for each test. This includes:

•

the complete
test.json
configuration file used to define the benchmark;

•

runtime environment variables, including both defaults and user-defined overrides;

•

a snapshot of the software environment (e.g., loaded modules, library versions, and outputs of tools such as
ompi-info
);

•

resource allocation data, such as node-to-rank and GPU mappings (recorded in
alloc.csv
).

This metadata is saved in dedicated files within the test directory, rather than being embedded in the result
CSV
s. This separation preserves clarity while enabling modular parsing, analysis, and visualization. For example, environment dumps are stored in plain-text logs, while rank mappings are kept in structured
CSV
format.

Since the environment configuration file defines the exact software stack (via module names and versions), and all runtime information is logged at both compile-time and run-time, each test directory contains all the necessary artifacts to reproduce or audit a benchmark. Results and metadata live side-by-side in a structured hierarchy, simplifying integration with post-processing scripts and making long-term archival and comparison practical.

To aid in understanding how collective communication maps onto network hardware,
PICO
includes a tracer tool that estimates data movement across a system’s interconnect. Given a saved resource allocation (e.g., from
alloc.csv
) and a provided topology description of the target machine, the tracer infers traffic volumes across local versus global links, identifying potential bottlenecks or congestion-prone paths, and maps the placement of ranks onto network domains or groups.

3.4.3.
Instrumentation-Based Profiling

For algorithm developers and advanced users,
PICO
offers a set of lightweight instrumentation hooks that enable fine-grained profiling of specific phases within a collective. These hooks can be manually inserted into any custom collective or into the provided reference implementations within the
libpico
module (Sec.
3.2
), when compiled with appropriate instrumentation flags. They allow the capture of timing information for operations such as memory copies or buffer management, communication phases and synchronization points or barriers. The collected data can be aggregated and visualized, using the tools described in Sec.
3.4.1
, as phase-level breakdowns, offering deep insight into the internal behavior of collective implementations. This enables precise identification of bottlenecks, a clear assessment of optimization benefits, and meaningful comparisons of algorithmic strategies at a fine-grained execution level.

4.1.
From Observation to Collective Tuning

When exploring
Allreduce
performance on Leonardo (Open MPI), LUMI (Cray MPICH), and Mare Nostrum5 (Open MPI), we observed that the algorithm automatically selected by the MPI libraries was not always the most efficient choice. This behavior is expected: default algorithm selection in MPI implementations is designed to be conservative and broadly portable, relying on internal heuristics that may not reflect the characteristics of a specific machine. Those findings, summarized in Fig.
6
, quantifies performance drop of the default algorithm choice on those systems. Each cell in the heatmaps reports the median performance ratio between the default algorithm and the best-performing alternative for a given message size and node count; values below
1.0
(shaded in red) indicate the existence of a better algorithm for that node–size interval that is not being chosen. The patterns are strikingly consistent: across all three systems, there are distinct regions, often at larger scales or for specific message sizes, where the default algorithm choice falls well short of optimal, typically by around
30
\sim
40\%
, and in the worst cases achieving only
\frac{1}{5}
of the optimal performance.

The detailed per-algorithm performance data gathered by
PICO
provides a clear basis for tuning the library to the target system. By systematically sweeping algorithm choices and parameters,
PICO
produces exactly the kind of per-case performance map shown here, enabling targeted tuning of collective behavior. In Open MPI, this can be achieved through the
coll_tuned
dynamic decision file mechanism
(
The Open MPI Community 2025
)
, in which a text file specifies, for each collective, the algorithm to be used as a function of communicator size and message size. When dynamic decision mode is enabled, Open MPI consults this file at runtime to override its internal selection logic. Other MPI implementations, as well as *CCL libraries, expose similar capabilities via configuration files or environment variables.

Today,
PICO
enables users to create these tuning artifacts manually by translating measured performance data into rule files. In future releases, we plan to integrate an automated workflow that will generate library-specific tuning files directly from the post-processing results, closing the loop from measurement to deployment-ready optimization.

4.2.
Algorithmic Differences

Cost models are a common tool for reasoning about the expected performance of collective communication algorithms. The most widely used cost model for collective operations is the
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
model:

(1)

A\alpha+B\beta+C\gamma

where
A
is the number of steps of the algorithm,
\alpha
the latency cost of the communication,
B
is the communication volume per step,
C
and
\gamma
are used to model reduction operations

4.3.
Network Layer Parameters

Many factors in network communication libraries are often overlooked when running collective benchmarks, yet they can have a decisive impact on results and, if uncontrolled, can hinder test reproducibility.
One example is
UCX_MAX_RNDV_RAILS
, a UCX runtime parameter that controls the maximum number of active network rails (multiple ports/links) used by the UCX Rendezvous protocol for large-message transfers. Increasing the number of rails can, in some scenarios, improve throughput by exploiting multiple NICs for a single MPI process; in others, it can introduce contention or additional synchronization overhead.

To illustrate its effect, we ran an
Allreduce
benchmark on 2048 nodes of the Leonardo supercomputer, fixing the algorithm to
Rabenseifner
to remove algorithmic variability. Two runs were performed with identical configurations except for
UCX_MAX_RNDV_RAILS
, set to its default cluster value of
2

This example highlights only one of many possible sources of variability. In UCX alone, parameters such as
UCX_RNDV_THRESH
(threshold from eager to rendezvous),
UCX_MEMTYPE_CACHE
(enable/disable UCX-specific memory caching), and

(

)
, to name just a few, can produce measurable performance changes. While this list is drawn from the UCX-based network stack of Leonardo, similar effects occur across other systems and communication libraries. Most importantly, the list is far from exhaustive; in practice, almost any runtime parameter can influence benchmark outcomes. Knowing the exact network configuration during a run is therefore essential for correct interpretation of test results.

The metadata logging capabilities of

) address this need by capturing detailed runtime information alongside benchmark results. This enables post-hoc identification of the parameters in effect for any given test, and
PICO
further simplifies running controlled experiments in which only specific parameters are varied, making it straightforward to map their performance impact in a reproducible and systematic way.

5.
Related Work

As mentioned in Sec.
1
, a variety of tools have been developed to benchmark collective communication in modern HPC systems, each with different trade-offs in terms of generality, usability, and analytical depth. In this section, we review several prominent tools used in the HPC community and discuss their capabilities and limitations in the context of scalability, reproducibility, and ease of integration into modern software stacks.

OSU Micro-Benchmarks (OMB)

The OSU Micro-Benchmarks suite
(
The Ohio State University [n. d.]
)
is one of the most widely adopted tools for evaluating communication performance in MPI environments. It provides comprehensive support for point-to-point and collective operations and includes a broad set of standard tests.
However, it is primarily designed around MPI and offers only limited support for other communication standards such as NCCL. Moreover, OMB reports only aggregated performance metrics giving only a summary of real data captured, and does not capture detailed runtime metadata. Users are required to rely on custom scripting to automate experiments or to perform in-depth analysis.
Notably, the selection of specific collective algorithms is not integrated into the benchmark interface. Instead, users must manually configure environment variables or modify scripts, which complicates controlled comparisons between algorithmic variants of the same collective operation.

Intel MPI Benchmark (IMB)

The Intel MPI Benchmark (IMB) suite
(
Intel Corporation 2021
)
offers functionality similar to OMB and is commonly used in Intel-based environments. Like OMB, it focuses exclusively on MPI and lacks cross-library compatibility. While it includes a broad set of collective tests, it suffers from similar drawbacks in terms of extensibility, metadata capture, and automation.

NCCL Test

NCCL Test
(
NVIDIA Corporation [n. d.]
)
is a lightweight utility provided by NVIDIA for benchmarking collective operations in NCCL-based GPU environments. While it is useful for assessing NCCL performance in isolation, the tool is tightly coupled to the NCCL library and offers no support for cross-library comparisons.
It also lacks advanced data management or analysis capabilities and requires manual configuration to adjust environment settings, select algorithms, or automate large-scale test campaigns.

CommBench

CommBench
(
Hidayetoglu et al. 2024
)
is a more recent effort that aims to unify benchmarking across multiple collective communication libraries. It introduces a library-agnostic API and supports the generation of communication tests across heterogeneous platforms, including both MPI and *CCL-based collectives. CommBench represents a significant step toward extensibility and portability in benchmarking.
However, it still presents certain limitations that may affect its usability in practice. For instance, metadata logging is relatively minimal, making it difficult to capture detailed contextual information such as job placement, network topology, or low-level runtime state. The tool is also primarily geared toward GPU systems and assumes a one-process-per-GPU execution model, which may not generalize well to other scenarios. Additionally, CommBench requires users to write low-level benchmarking logic, resulting in a steep learning curve and additional manual effort, which can be a barrier for those aiming to perform rapid, exploratory testing.

6.
Conclusions

We presented
PICO
, a lightweight and extensible framework for benchmarking collective communication operations across heterogeneous HPC and AI systems. Unlike existing tools,
PICO
integrates fine-grained profiling, rich metadata collection, and automated orchestration to support reproducible, system-aware performance analysis. Through its modular architecture,
PICO
enables portable comparisons across MPI, *CCL, and user-defined algorithms, while its integrated post-processing tools facilitate both high-level performance summaries and detailed algorithmic phase breakdowns.

Our case studies demonstrated how PICO can (i) reveal suboptimal default algorithm selections and guide library tuning, (ii) highlight subtle performance trade-offs between closely related algorithms, (iii) quantify the impact of backend parameters, and (iv) identify hidden bottlenecks through targeted instrumentation.

These examples underscore
PICO
utility for algorithm designers, application developers, and system administrators alike. By bridging systematic benchmarking with actionable insights,
PICO
aims to become a foundational tool for advancing the reproducible performance analysis of collective communications in next-generation computing systems.
