Refining left bundle branch block criteria for improved left bundle branch area pacing response in heart failure
Authors: Hao Huang, Yu Jiang, Bingqi Fu, Tianxin Long, Sijing Cheng, Juwei Yang, Chi Cai, Xuhua Chen, Min Gu, Michael R. Gold, Hongxia Niu, Wei Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft bundle branch block (LBBB) on the surface electrocardiogram (ECG) is a key criterion for selecting patients suitable for cardiac resynchronization therapy. However, the diagnostic criteria for LBBB vary across guidelines, and their impact on the efficacy of left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the correlation among different LBBB-related ECG criteria, as well as ECG characteristics, on the efficacy of LBBAP in heart failure (HF).MethodsConsecutive patients with systolic HF with wide QRS complexes who successfully underwent LBBAP were enrolled. Preoperative standard 12-lead ECG was assessed. This included LBBB-related parameters, including time to notch/slur in lateral leads. The associations among different LBBB criteria (2009 American Heart Association guidelines, 2013 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial–Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy, and Strauss criteria) were evaluated. The endpoints were post-LBBAP electrical synchronization, echocardiographic response, and clinical outcomes.ResultsAmong 149 patients included in the analyses (baseline left ventricular ejection fraction 32.6% ± 7.0%; QRS duration 170.8 ± 20.3 ms), substantial variability in LBBB classification was observed among different criteria. The Strauss criteria demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy for QRS narrowing (area under the curve 0.710) and showed favorable performance for identifying echocardiographic responders (area under the curve 0.631). Patients who met the Strauss LBBB criteria had a significantly lower risk of composite clinical endpoint than those without LBBB (adjusted hazard ratio 0.216; 95% confidence interval 0.057–0.810; P = .023). Combining the Strauss criteria with delayed time to notch/slur in lead V5/V6 (>60 ms) further improved the predictive value of ECG-based assessment.ConclusionLBBAP provides the most clinical benefit among patients with HF with Strauss-defined LBBB. The combination of the Strauss criteria with delayed notch/slur in lead V5/V6 maximizes the predictive value of the ECG.

Key Findings ▪Among the 5 evaluated criteria, the Strauss definition demonstrated superior performance for long-term clinical outcomes.▪Strauss criteria also performed better for the other endpoints including QRS duration narrowing, left ventricular ejection fraction improvement, and short-term clinical outcomes.▪Time to notch/slur in lead V5/V6 of ≥60 ms was also strongly and independently associated with outcomes among patients whose electrocardiogram was also classified as a left bundle branch block (LBBB) by the Strauss criteria.▪Discordant T waves regardless of LBBB criteria are an intrinsic adverse marker, independent of the therapeutic effects of physiological pacing.

Introduction

Left bundle branch block (LBBB) is often secondary to structural heart diseases, such as coronary artery disease, valvular disease, and cardiomyopathy. These comorbidities are recognized as predictors of adverse outcomes among patients with heart failure (HF).1 Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) demonstrated favorable therapeutic effects, particularly among patients with LBBB.2 However, those patients with intact His–Purkinje activation and intraventricular conduction delay (IVCD) may derive less benefit from LBBAP.3 Accordingly, refining the definition of LBBB on the surface electrocardiogram (ECG) is important to select candidates most likely to respond to LBBAP.

Several landmark trials4,5 and guidelines6, 7, 8 of cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) proposed different ECG criteria for LBBB. However, these criteria yield considerable variability in classifying patients eligible for CRT.9 Moreover, specific ECG features, either associated with procedural injury during transcatheter aortic valve interventions or reversible by His–Purkinje system pacing, are considered more indicative of true LBBB.10 Nevertheless, it remains unclear to what extent these criteria correlate with electrical resynchronization after LBBAP and whether they can predict clinical outcomes.

The present study aimed to evaluate the associations among various LBBB definitions, their component ECG criteria, and the treatment efficacy among consecutive patients receiving LBBAP. In particular, we investigated the ability to predict QRS narrowing, left ventricular (LV) reverse remodeling, and adverse clinical events. These results may help identify the most reliable ECG markers for selecting optimal candidates for LBBAP in HF.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective observational study enrolled consecutive patients successfully undergoing LBBAP for cardiac resynchronization at Fuwai Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. All patients met the indications for LBBAP implantation according to the 2023 ACC/American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for physiological pacing,11 which included those with New York Heart Association functional class II–IV despite optimal medical therapy, an LV ejection fraction (LVEF) of <50%, and a QRS duration of >120 ms. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (2) previous implantation of a pacemaker or CRT device (n = 33), (2) LBBAP failure rescued by biventricular pacing (n = 26), (3) left bundle branch (LBB)–optimized CRT (n = 10), (4) poor ECG signal quality (n = 3), and (5) a lack of follow-up data (n = 4). All patients provided a written informed consent. The research reported in this paper adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was waived owing to the use of retrospective and deidentified data.

ECG acquisition and analysis

Baseline 12-lead ECGs were digitally stored in the electronic medical record system. Automated ECG analysis was used to extract basic parameters. In paced rhythm, QRS duration was measured from the pacing stimulus to the end of the QRS complex. Other parameters (ie, time to notch) were measured using ImageJ software (version 1.54, National Institutes of Health, United States) based on the ECG image files. These additional measurements, as well as the assessment of ECG characteristics suggestive of LBBB, were conducted by 2 physicians (H.H. and H.X.N.) working independently, followed by joint review to resolve any measurement discrepancies. For the analysis of time to notch in lateral (I/aVL/V5/V6) leads, the interval from the onset of the QRS complex to the nadir of any notch present (or the midpoint of a slur) was measured. If no distinct notch was observed within the QRS duration, the time to notch was recorded as 0 ms. In cases where multiple notches were present, the latest notch was selected. This measurement was performed consistently for all patients included in the study.

The diagnosis of LBBB morphology was determined according to 5 different definitions derived from major guidelines and clinical trials: (1) the 2009 AHA/American College of Cardiology Foundation/Heart Rhythm Society guideline,6 (2 and 3) the 2013 and 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines,7,8 (4) the Multicenter Automatic Defibrillator Implantation Trial–Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (MADIT-CRT) criteria,4 and (5) the LBBB criteria proposed by Strauss et al.12 T-wave discordance is defined as the T-wave direction being opposite to the main QRS direction in at least 2 of the leads I, aVL, and V6.13 The detailed criteria are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Implantation procedures

Under the fluoroscopic right anterior oblique 30° view, the fixed-curve sheath (C315HIS, Medtronic) was introduced to the right ventricle over a hydrophilic guidewire. The ventricular pacing lead with fixed helix (SelectSecure model 3830, 69 cm, Medtronic) was then advanced from the right side of the interventricular septum toward the LBB area, while simultaneously recording intracardiac electrograms and 12-lead ECGs. LBBAP was successful when the unipolar paced QRS morphology demonstrated a Qr or qR pattern in V1 along with any of the following: (1) demonstration of transition from nonselective to selective LBB or LV septal capture during threshold testing, (2) R-wave peak time in leads V6 of <90 ms, or (3) V6–V1 interpeak interval of >33 ms.14 Based on the abovementioned criteria, the lead was positioned at the optimal LBB area during the implantation procedure and was considered to achieve maximal correction of electrical dyssynchrony, ensuring the best achievable therapeutic effect under the given procedural conditions.

Follow-up and outcomes

All patients were followed at the cardiac device clinic or via telephone at 3–6 months postoperatively and annually thereafter. Follow-up evaluations included assessments of clinical symptoms, ECG, device interrogation, transthoracic echocardiography, and procedure-related complications. QRS narrowing was defined as a ≥20 ms reduction in paced QRS duration compared with baseline. QRS normalization was defined as a paced QRS duration of <120 ms. Echocardiographic response was defined as an absolute LVEF improvement of ≥5% from baseline within 12 months of follow-up. Functional normalization was defined as an absolute LVEF of ≥50% on follow-up assessment. The primary endpoint was a composite clinical endpoint of all-cause mortality, heart transplantation, HF rehospitalization, and malignant ventricular arrhythmias. HF rehospitalization was defined as any hospital admission or emergency care visit requiring intensive treatment for HF with intravenous diuretics or intravenous inotropic medications. Malignant ventricular arrhythmia was defined as any sustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, or appropriate defibrillator therapy, including antitachycardia pacing or shock. The secondary endpoints consisted of the individual components of the primary composite endpoint. The last follow-up was completed in February 2025.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Given that patients could fulfill more than 1 LBBB criterion, statistical comparisons of baseline variables across LBBB-defined subgroups were performed using linear mixed-effects models to account for repeated inclusion of individuals.

Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient was used to evaluate the agreement among different LBBB definitions. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to assess the performance of different LBBB criteria in identifying patients with ECG and echocardiographic recovery (including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio, and negative likelihood ratio). Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association among different LBBB criteria/ECG parameters with ECG and echocardiographic outcomes. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to assess the relationship between different LBBB criteria/ECG parameters and the clinical endpoint, with log-rank tests used to determine P values. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association of the Strauss criteria and other ECG characteristics with clinical composite endpoints.

For individual ECG characteristics, we applied a 2-step selection process before including it in a multivariate model to avoid redundancy and overfitting: (1) prescreening based on clinical relevance and consistency, in which ECG features associated with at least 4 of the predefined outcomes (including QRS narrowing, echocardiographic response, QRS normalization, functional recovery, and clinical events) were retained, and (2) least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was applied to select the remaining ECG features most strongly associated with clinical endpoints, while excluding collinear variables with variance inflation factors of >10. The maximal selected rank statistics method was used to determine optimal cutoff values for time to notch, via the surv cutpoint function in the survminer R package. This approach identifies the threshold that maximizes the log-rank statistic for dichotomizing the continuous variable into high- and low-risk groups based on survival outcomes. The DeLong test was performed to compare whether the area under the curve (AUC) differed significantly between individual LBBB criteria and those supplemented with additional ECG features.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3). 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Distribution of various LBBB definitions and associated ECG characteristics in the study population

The mean QRS duration, QT interval, and QTc interval in this cohort were 170.8 ± 20.3 ms, 449.9 ± 41.2 ms, and 494.7 ± 35.7 ms, respectively. Sinus rhythm was documented in 89.9% of patients (134 of 149), and 67.8% had a normal axis in the frontal plane before the procedure.

The distribution of LBBB according to different criteria is presented in Figure 2A. The AHA and ESC 2021 criteria were the most stringent, classifying only 22.8% and 25.5% of patients as having LBBB, respectively. The ESC 2013, MADIT-CRT, and Strauss criteria showed comparable classification rates at 86.6%, 81.9%, and 83.2%, respectively. Notably, only 16.1% of patients (24 of 149) met all 5 LBBB definitions. The agreement among different LBBB criteria varied substantially (Figure 2B). The highest Cohen’s κ coefficient was observed between the ESC 2013 and MADIT-CRT criteria (κ = 0.82), whereas the lowest concordance occurred between the ESC 2013 and ESC 2021 criteria (κ = 0.06).Figure 2Prevalence and concordance across LBBB diagnostic criteria. Notched or slurred R waves appeared in 82.6%, 63.1%, 47.7%, and 73.8% among these leads. The R-wave peak time measured 66.3 ms in lead I, 81.8 ms in aVL (significantly longer than leads I and V5), 68.4 ms in V5, and 76.7 ms in V6. The mean time to notch/slur showed a different pattern, which was longest in lead I (70.6 ms) and V5 (65.7 ms), significantly shorter in aVL (56.0 ms), with V6 demonstrating an intermediate value (62.6 ms) (Figure 3E–3H).

Leads V1 and V2 predominantly demonstrated QS or rS patterns, with prevalence rates reaching 95.3% and 96.6%, respectively. In the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), notching or slurring was observed in 54.4%, 75.8%, and 72.5%, respectively, with 45.6% of patients exhibiting notches across all 3 leads. The aVR lead showed a QS pattern in 76.5% of cases, and a dominant Q wave was present in 90.6%. A negative concordant ST-T segment was observed in 32.9% of patients.

Predictive value of different LBBB definitions for electrical resynchronization and echocardiographic response

The average QRS duration in this cohort decreased from 170.8 ± 20.3 ms at baseline to 137.4 ± 22.9 ms after the procedure. QRS narrowing rates were approximately 80% among different LBBB criteria: 79.4% (AHA), 78.3% (ESC 2013), 76.3% (ESC 2021), 80.3% (MADIT-CRT), and 83.1% (Strauss). The mean QRS narrowing durations were −41.6 ms (AHA), −36.4 ms (ESC 2013), −39.3 ms (ESC 2021), −38.3 ms (MADIT-CRT), and −39.6 ms (Strauss). As presented in Table 2, the Strauss criteria had the best predictive performance for QRS narrowing (AUC 0.710; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.629–0.791), with the highest sensitivity, followed by MADIT-CRT (AUC 0.650; 95% CI 0.568–0.731). The AHA and ESC 2021 criteria had higher specificity but lower sensitivity. At the last follow-up, the mean paced QRS duration remained stable at 137.4 ± 23.8 ms, with 25.7% of patients having normalized QRS (<120 ms). Both the Strauss and MADIT-CRT criteria had the highest predictive value for long-term QRS normalization. The mean LVEF significantly increased from a baseline of 32.6% ± 7.0% to 43.3% ± 11.0% in 1 year. The overall echocardiographic response rate was 75.8%, with the response rates ranging approximately 80%–83% according to different LBBB criteria. The ESC 2013, MADIT-CRT, and Strauss criteria demonstrated comparable performance in predicting echo response, with AUC ranging from 0.625 to 0.657. These 3 criteria shared characteristics of high sensitivity but relatively low specificity (Table 2). The median time of the last echocardiographic follow-up was 568.5 days (interquartile range 315.5–962). At the last follow-up, the mean LVEF showed continued improvement to 47.7% (95% CI 45.4–50.0), with 53.9% achieved functional normalization (LVEF ≥50%). Although all the criteria demonstrated limited predictive value for complete functional recovery (AUC range 0.505–0.575), the largest response of complete recovery was 58.3% of patients who had Strauss LBBB. Moreover, the Strauss criteria indicated superior performance than the others, exhibiting the highest AUC value, as well as optimal positive and negative predictive values (Supplemental Table 2).

Predictive value of different LBBB definitions for clinical outcomes

During a median follow-up of 601 days (interquartile range 361–1035), 2 cardiac transplantations, 13 HF rehospitalizations, and 9 malignant arrhythmic events were observed. Discussion

The present study systematically reviewed the ECG characteristics of patients with HF and QRS prolongation who successfully underwent LBBAP. There were 4 major results from these analyses. First, among the 5 evaluated criteria, the Strauss definition demonstrated superior performance for long-term clinical outcomes. Second, the Strauss criteria also performed better for the other endpoints including QRS duration narrowing, LVEF improvement, and short-term clinical outcomes. Third, time to notch/slur in lead V5/V6 of ≥60 ms was also strongly and independently associated with outcomes among patients whose ECG was also classified as an LBBB by the Strauss criteria. Fourth, discordant T waves regardless of LBBB criteria are an intrinsic adverse marker, independent of the therapeutic effects of physiological pacing. From a clinical perspective, these findings may also help identify nonresponders. Patients without Strauss-defined LBBB, or with shorter time to notch/slur or absence of notch/slur in the lateral leads, may be less likely to benefit from LBBAP. Identifying such patients may help to refine patient selection and avoid ineffective interventions.

The other LBBB criteria were variable regarding their predictive value. The MADIT-CRT and ESC 2013 definitions also exhibited appreciable predictive value, primarily for short-term electrical and echocardiographic responses. The AHA and 2021 ESC criteria were characterized by high specificity.

Significant variability among LBBB definitions

LBBB has been widely recognized as a key ECG presence for CRT response.4,15 However, considerable discrepancies exist among LBBB definitions across different clinical trials and guidelines, resulting in significant variation in the reported prevalence among CRT cohorts.16 In the present study, the proportion of patients classified as LBBB varied from 23% to 87%, depending on the criteria applied, with only 16% meeting all definitions simultaneously. The AHA and ESC 2021 definitions are the most stringent and complex, thus identifying the smallest proportion of patients as having LBBB. Consequently, applying the ESC 2021 or AHA criteria potentially enhances specificity for genuine electrical dyssynchrony. However, these 2 criteria may risk missing patients with considerable electrical remodeling that could benefit from early conduction system pacing.

Impact of the LBBB criteria on prognosis in patients with HF

Although the prevalence of LBBB varies, most studies consistently observed prognostic differences between patients with and without LBBB undergoing resynchronization therapy. In a post hoc analysis of the REVERSE trial, Gold et al15 found that the 2-year event rate among patients with LBBB receiving CRT was 10% compared with 26% in patients without LBBB. Similarly, a secondary analysis of the MADIT-CRT trial showed that, during a 3-year follow-up, the rate of the composite endpoint of death or HF was 16% in patients with LBBB vs 23%–33% in those without LBBB; mortality rates alone were 7% and 12%–15%, respectively.4

In the present cohort, 83% of patients met the Strauss criteria for LBBB. Over a follow-up period of nearly 2 years, the incidence of death or HF hospitalization was as low as 8.7%, supporting the effectiveness of selecting patients for LBBAP using the Strauss criteria. The superiority of this criterion has been found in the CRT population17 and lies primarily in its ability to predict acute QRS narrowing accurately, with an AUC of 0.710 when used as a single predictor. Compared with the AHA and ESC definitions, the Strauss criteria demonstrated better sensitivity (0.945) and both high positive and negative predictive values (0.831 and 0.760). A possible underlying explanation is that patients with Strauss LBBB morphology tend to have lower septal scar burden than those without LBBB; the absence of septal scar is correlated with successful LBBAP implantation and better clinical outcomes.2,18 These findings suggest that patients not meeting the Strauss criteria should be carefully evaluated preoperatively such as with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, given that the likelihood of electrical resynchronization by LBBAP may be limited. In such cases, coronary sinus lead placement with or without LBBAP may be required to optimize CRT outcomes.

Predictive value of LBBB-related ECG features

A hallmark feature of LBBB, as defined by the Strauss, AHA, and ESC 2021 criteria, is the presence of notch or slur within the QRS complex. Classically, the first notch corresponds to the endocardial breakthrough of activation on the left side of the interventricular septum, whereas the subsequent notch reflects activation reaching the epicardial lateral wall of the LV.12 The incidence of this feature differs significantly between the AHA and ESC 2021/Strauss criteria, given that the AHA definition requires the presence of a broad R wave with notching or slurring in all lateral leads, whereas the Strauss criteria only require 2 contiguous leads among the lateral leads and leads V1 or V2. This difference largely explains the lower positivity rate of LBBB under the AHA definition. As noted earlier, the presence and extent of notched/slurred lateral leads showed a significant association with electrical and structural cardiac remodeling, as well as long-term clinical outcomes, which underscores their critical importance for predicting the therapeutic efficacy of LBBAP.

With a narrow QRS, the initial activation of the interventricular septum typically produces an initial Q wave in the lateral leads owing to left-to-right activation. The AHA, ESC 2013, and MADIT-CRT criteria all require the absence of an initial Q wave in the lateral leads. However, Strauss et al12 allowed that, in cases of myocardial infarction involving the septum, unopposed right ventricular activation can also generate a Q wave. The Strauss criteria, which allowed the presence of an initial Q wave, do not diminish the efficacy of LBBAP. The low proportion of ischemic cardiomyopathy and the correspondingly low incidence of initial Q waves may have influenced the positive detection rate. Nevertheless, even in lead aVL, which had the highest incidence of initial Q waves, this ECG feature was not significantly associated with outcomes.

Typically, the ST-T segment in LBBB exhibits a direction opposite to that of the QRS complex, known as discordant LBBB. This criterion is particularly emphasized in the AHA and ESC 2021 guidelines. Previous studies have linked discordant LBBB with poorer general condition, neurohormonal alterations, and adverse clinical outcomes.19,20 A post hoc analysis of the MADIT-CRT trial demonstrated that, among patients with LBBB receiving CRT, those with discordant LBBB had higher rates of mortality and HF events than those with concordant LBBB.13 This suggests that conventional CRT may not confer superior benefits for patients with discordant LBBB relative to those with concordant LBBB. In the current LBBAP cohort, the proportions of leads I, aVL, and V6 showing discordant T waves were 73.2%, 75.2%, and 58.4%, respectively, with an overall prevalence of 74.5%. 67.1% patients met the AHA criterion of no negative concordance, which was comparable with the MADIT-CRT cohort. Although LBBAP achieved similar electrical and echocardiographic resynchronization in both discordant and concordant T-wave groups, we observed that patients with discordant T waves experienced a higher incidence of composite clinical endpoints than those with concordant T waves (log-rank P = .048). Furthermore, each additional lateral lead exhibiting discordant T waves was associated with a 1.07-fold increased risk of clinical events (HR 2.07; 95% CI 1.08–3.94; P = .028). Taken together, these findings suggest that discordant T waves may serve as an intrinsic adverse marker, independent of the therapeutic effects of physiological pacing.

As a single predictor, time to notch in lead V5/V6 yielded AUC values of 0.729, 0.659, 0.706, and 0.794 for acute QRS narrowing, echocardiographic response, and composite clinical endpoints at 1 year and 2 years, respectively. When combined with the Strauss criteria, the predictive value can further improve. Therefore, measuring time to notch in V5/V6 leads may offer better clinical utility in identifying suitable candidates for LBBAP than the conventional R-wave peak time of >60 ms criterion recommended by the 2021 AHA/ESC guidelines.

Clinical implication

This work offers important insights for optimizing both patient selection and intraoperative strategies for CRT. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with IVCD often show limited response to conduction system pacing, given that their wide QRS complex cannot be fully corrected.21 When evaluating candidates for CRT, substantial evidence indicates that patients exhibiting an LBBB pattern on ECG derive greater benefit than those with IVCD or right bundle branch block patterns,4,15 whereas patients with IVCD with prolonged QRS duration have a better response than those with right bundle branch block.22 This distinction is particularly critical when considering conduction system pacing. Patients with increased septal scar were less likely to present the Strauss LBBB morphology and were associated with worse outcomes for LBBAP than traditional CRT.23 Therefore, in patients who do not meet the Strauss criteria for LBBB, lack a notch or slur in the lateral leads, or have an early notch with a duration shorter than 60 ms, biventricular pacing or a hybrid strategy combining conduction system and coronary venous pacing may represent a more appropriate therapeutic approach.

Limitation

This cohort primarily consisted of nonischemic cardiomyopathy patients, so caution should be used in extrapolating these data to ischemic cardiomyopathy, in whom myocardial scar burden, particularly in the septum, may influence both LBBB morphology and response to LBBAP. Another potential variable that affects outcomes is LBBAP lead location. Although it may strongly influence time to peak ventricular activation (LV activation time), with more apical sites giving a shorter LV activation time, the improvement in QRS duration or clinical outcomes seems similar.24 Systematic differentiation between LBBAP with true LBB capture and LV septal pacing was not applicable in the present study owing to its retrospective design, which may influence the degree of electrical resynchronization and subsequent clinical outcomes. In addition, the assessment of QRS notching or slurring may involve a degree of subjectivity, despite the use of predefined criteria, which may introduce measurement variability. Furthermore, although the overall follow-up duration varied among patients owing to continuous enrollment, echocardiographic response was assessed at a standardized 6-month time point, and time-to-event analyses were applied for clinical outcomes; nevertheless, residual bias related to follow-up variability cannot be entirely excluded. Given that these parameters can hardly be controlled in a retrospective analysis, they remain a question for future prospective research.

Conclusion

Compared with current guideline-based criteria, patients with LBBB who meet the Strauss definition derive the greatest benefit from LBBAP. Moreover, combining the Strauss criteria with a 60 ms time to notch in leads V5/V6 further enhances the predictive value of ECG in identifying LBBAP responders.
