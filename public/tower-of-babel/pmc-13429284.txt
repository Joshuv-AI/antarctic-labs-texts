The evolution of informed consent

Globally, despite extensive written guidance, landmark legal cases, and evolving clinical practices, the landscape of informed consent remains uncertain for both patients1 and doctors.2 The notion of informed consent was thrust upon medicine by the law, rather than originating as a ‘medical philosophy’,3 or being independently adopted by virtue of the increased recognition of ethical principles within medicine. Key time-honoured works from which ethical perspectives within medicine were derived, such as those from Hippocrates and Percival, did not visit the notion of informed consent in a meaningful way,4 despite an underlying appreciation of the “voluntary […] relationship between physicians and patients”.5

Medical ethics was initially focused upon nondisclosure rather than truthful disclosure, and the so-called Hippocratic ethic did not apportion sufficient weight to the patient’s agenda.6 The World Medical Association’s Declaration of Geneva in 1948, despite demonstrating a shift towards patient-centredness, gave little specific mention of the concept of informed consent or the underlying ethical principle of respect for patient autonomy. Autonomy in the setting of obtaining consent for a medical intervention refers to the acknowledgement that patients have “a right to hold views, make choices, and to take actions based upon their values and beliefs”.7

Among the first legal cases to recognize the notion of informed consent were the Canadian case of Reibl v Hughes8 and the American case of Salgo v Leland Stanford Jr. University Board of Trustees.9 In Reibl, the plaintiff suffered a stroke and resultant paralysis, following a competently performed endarterectomy. The patient claimed that the risks of stroke and paralysis were not discussed, and thus consent was not informed. The case highlighted that a patient must be allowed to make an informed choice when consenting to a medical procedure. In Salgo, the plaintiff underwent angiography, which was complicated by serious lower limb paralysis; it was subsequently claimed that the risks associated with the procedure had not been properly discussed before intervention. The judgment in Salgo found that a duty to disclose risks to a patient already existed in the law, as an aspect of the physician’s responsibility to describe the general character of an intervention; thus this judgment set a much-needed precedent that offered greater patient protection.4 In the UK, the case of Chatterton v Gerson10 helped define the principle of informed consent. Chatterton involved a patient who was experiencing postoperative pain and sought treatment from a specialist clinician. The clinician advised further surgery, however failed to warn of the risk of numbness following treatment. A sensation deficit eventuated, and a subsequent claim of negligence was sought. Despite suggesting the notion of informed consent, when the judge stated that “the duty of the doctor is to explain what [they] intend to do, and its implications”,11 it showed that a paternalistic view still overshadowed the principle of patient self-determination.

This germinal version of the doctrine of informed consent matured to some extent over the following years, and an appreciation of the ethical obligations of the notion developed. This acquisition of a certain moral significance was a consequence of a societal drift away from a healthcare structure regulated predominantly by unbridled paternalism, towards one that began to value patient self-determination.12 This was largely influenced by the “legal development of the Human Rights Act 1998”.3 Articles 2 and 8 of the Human Rights Act 1998 broadly promoted the ethical principle of respect for autonomy;13 as such, this legislation challenged the version of informed consent in which “the doctor decides [what the patient] should know”.14

The increasing ethical significance of the concept of informed consent was accompanied by an ebbing of the case law towards this realization. In 1957, the case of Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee provided the medical profession with the Bolam standard,15 whereby “a doctor is not negligent if he acts in accordance with a practice accepted at the time as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion”.16 The case of Sidaway v Board of Governors of the Bethlem Royal Hospital drew heavily from Bolam.17 Sidaway concerned a surgeon who had not warned the patient of an inherent risk of spinal cord injury related to a recommended operative intervention; subsequently the complication occurred and the patient claimed that the lack of disclosure amounted to negligence. The judgment in Sidaway found that the Bolam standard was the pertinent standard to utilize to assess risk disclosure, and on application of the Bolam test, the clinician’s lack of risk disclosure had not been negligent. Sidaway therefore left the doctrine of informed consent in a state of limbo; far from ushering in the era of patient self-determination, the judgment reinforced the role of paternalism in risk disclosure, despite the sociocultural change of direction within the UK, towards a recognition of the importance of the ethical principle of respect for autonomy within the patient-doctor setting.

The professional standard of risk disclosure, as it emerged in Chatterton and was confirmed in Sidaway utilizing Bolam, depicts a situation where the nature and degree of information provided by a clinician to a patient is evaluated by the clinician. Following Sidaway, the professional standard with respect to information disclosure was reaffirmed in the cases of Pearce v United Bristol Healthcare NHS Trust,18 and Chester v Afshar.19 However, in 2015, the UK case of Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board rejected this standpoint and brought about the shift from a professional to a patient standard of disclosure.20 Montgomery highlighted “the patient’s right to make [their] own decision”,21 and stated that “the doctor is under a duty to take reasonable care to ensure that the patient is aware of any material risks”.22

The patient standard of risk disclosure can be additionally classified as subjective, objective, or a mixed stance that lies somewhere in between. In the subjective patient standard, the patient in question determines the sufficiency of disclosure. This position may be vulnerable to hindsight, and as the judgment in Canterbury v Spence alluded to, would be practically very challenging for clinicians. Thus, due to its reliance on physicians knowing every patient’s idiosyncratic beliefs, few courts adopt this standpoint. Montgomery moved away from a purely subjective patient standard via the use of “a reasonable person in the patient’s position”.22

In the objective patient standard, the acceptability of disclosure is established utilizing the perspective of a theoretical typical patient. Aside from the issue that this position may trivialize the unique set of circumstances that influences a patient’s health, it also opens a door for over-reliance on medical opinion, as occurred at first instance in the case of Maynard v West Midlands Regional Health Authority.23 Montgomery withdrew from a purely objective approach, and the difficulties associated with this, when it qualified that a clinician’s duty of disclosure is bound to the particular case and patient.

A lasting legal ramification of the Montgomery decision is therefore the adoption in the UK of a mixed subjective-objective patient standard, with respect to risk disclosure during the process of obtaining consent.

Since Montgomery, the courts appear to be making a concerted effort to increase the practical utility of the concept of informed consent. An example of such an effort can be found in the case of Duce v Worcestershire Acute Hospitals NHS Trust.24 Here, it was reiterated that a doctor is not required to disclose a risk that they cannot reasonably be aware of. This position may effectively narrow the scope of disclosure and make informed consent discussions more practicable for clinicians.

Materiality of risk

The idea of materiality of risk is whether, within the parameters of a specific case, a hypothetical patient would deem a risk to be significant.25 However, it must acknowledge the integral yet delicate difference between the specific patient in question and a “hypothetical reasonable patient”.26 Materiality must be “fact sensitive, and sensitive also to the characteristics of the patient”,22 but how do surgeons practically determine this within a consent discussion?

First and foremost, to uphold the individuality and autonomy of each patient, engaging in meaningful discussions that go beyond mere procedural formalities should be a priority. Understanding a patient’s perspective allows insight to identify and discuss material risks more effectively. A clinician must acknowledge this form of epistemic justice, and accept that the patient is an expert in their own life, and how their ill health fits in to this.27

Concerning the differentiation between theoretical and material risk, perhaps greater acknowledgement of the probability of disease occurrence, without over-reliance, may increase the practical utility of information disclosure discussions. The case of Rogers v Whittaker,28 where a one in 14,000 risk of blindness was deemed material due to its significance to the patient, made it clear that materiality could not solely be about percentage chance of risk. However, the case of A v East Kent Hospitals University NHS Foundation Trust29 acknowledges the fact that incidence of risk may affect materiality status.30 Clearly, the oversimplistic use of percentages to determine materiality of risk is not a method to champion true patient comprehension and autonomy, however the incidence at which a risk occurs is surely relevant to informed consent discussions. A middle ground must be found, which may involve threshold percentages, not only related to rate of risk occurrence, but perhaps also related to the detrimental effect of the risk on the specific patient’s life. This may help a particular patient to contextualize a risk within their own life, and differentiate theoretical risks from those that are truly significant to them.

Alternative treatments

Concerning disclosure of alternative treatments during the process of informed consent for a medical intervention, “it is not possible to consider a particular medical procedure in isolation from its alternatives”.31 Surgeons have a duty to discuss these. While some clarification of this aspect of the consent process was provided in McCulloch v Forth Valley Health Board,32 the scope and subjectivity of the duty remains uncertain.33 McCulloch found that a clinician should discuss reasonable alternatives, with reasonability determined via a derivation of the Bolam standard. This judgment, through its subjectivity, may simultaneously frustrate patient autonomy and decrease practical achievability of informed consent. Clarification is necessary to provide clinicians with a workable model upon which discussions about reasonable alternative treatments can be framed. Although individualized patient-doctor discussions are paramount, perhaps there is an opportunity for the Surgical Speciality Associations in the UK, and their equivalents worldwide, to compile a list of reasonable alternatives for each surgical procedure, upon which the reasonable variations discussion could then be framed. The omission of a listed alternative treatment from the discussion, or the addition of another variant treatment to the dialogue, may be appropriate and ultimately should still be determined according to the particular patient. This suggestion would satisfy McCulloch,32 reduce clinician-led subjectivity, and improve the feasibility of such discussions.

In the setting of a patient requesting a procedure that the surgeon feels would be clinically suboptimal, the first port of call must be patient-doctor discussion. The clinician should first endeavour to explain the rationale behind the inferiority of the procedure. Following this, the offer of a referral for a second opinion may improve patient autonomy and trust, while providing an acknowledgement of the complexity and experiential subjectivity of surgical decision-making.

Shared decision-making

Shared decision-making involves a ‘partnership’ between patient and doctor.3 This explores the risks and benefits of management options, discusses the choices each patient has, and reaches an informed outcome together.34 One strategy to foster improved shared decision-making could be the use of an open-ended question to conclude the consent discussion: ‘Is there anything else you want to tell me that may be important, or you wish we had discussed?’ Such an open-ended question may invite insight into the patient’s own concerns, and could uncover considerations that might otherwise go unaddressed, and further strengthen the patient’s role in their care. Another option to promote patient autonomy could be, at the end of a meaningful and collaborative discussion about the surgery, to ask the patient to voice the final affirmation, and confirm: Doctor, can you please do my operation?

It could be argued that in order to consistently fulfil the obligations of material risk disclosure, a clinician is required to regularly reach an impractical level of intimate knowledge about their patient.35 The scope of disclosure could be vast for even the most basic medical procedure, and this scope may differ profoundly between patients. The conclusion of perceived materiality of a risk or a reasonable alternative may also vary with the parties involved in a discussion, the tone of said discussion, and subtle nuances that emanate from the dialogue. If the scope of disclosure in a practical setting could be so erratic, is it realistically achievable for surgeons to always obtain informed consent? An idealistic viewpoint may suggest that in the process of communication between patient and doctor, with the provision of meaningful information and genuine patient comprehension, material risks and reasonable alternatives would simply materialize. While this may be the case occasionally or even frequently, it simply may not be possible to achieve this every time. Surgeons must acknowledge this and, instead of reacting with despondency or perceived legal vulnerability over the lack of feasibility, use methods that help to strive for flawlessness. Indeed, the recently updated guidance on decision-making and consent from the General Medical Council acknowledges the impact of time constraints and resource availability, while emphasizing dialogue and patient autonomy.36

Conclusion

In conclusion, the concept of informed consent has evolved over many years, and surgeons should strive to achieve a patient standard of disclosure. This can only be achieved by authentic collaborative discussion, which may be augmented by multiple modes of information provision and multiple timepoints as required. The notion of materiality must differentiate between theoretical and material risk without reverting to an over-reliance on percentages, while still acknowledging that risk incidence is relevant for patients. Perhaps a cautious role for threshold percentages related to both risk occurrence and detrimental effect of the risk, may refine the concept and facilitate patient comprehension. The scope of the duty to discuss reasonable alternatives remains unclear, and could possibly be crystallized by the involvement of Surgical Speciality Associations. Shared decision-making could be promoted via the use of a carefully phrased invitational question at the culmination of the risk disclosure discussion, and asking the patient to vocalize their intention to proceed with surgery. By refining these elements, surgeons could move towards a more transparent and achievable patient-centred approach to informed consent.
