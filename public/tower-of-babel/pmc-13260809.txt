Peritectic engineering enhanced thermoelectrics for smart thermal messaging devices
Authors: Jiwu Xin, Ziwang Luo, Wang Li, Pengyu Zhang, Chengyun Xu, Shixing Yuan, Wulong Li, Long Chen, Tianzhu Zhou, Yuntian Wang, Abdul Basit, Yubo Luo, Junyou Yang, Ting Zhang, Lei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutonomous, bio-integrated electronic systems, such as smart prosthetics and functional electronic skins, require materials combining energy harvesting with perception. Although Indium Antimonide is well established in high-speed electronics owing to its high electron mobility, yet its large intrinsic thermal conductivity has limited its use in thermoelectric energy harvesting. Here, we introduce a peritectic engineering strategy to reduce the thermal bottleneck. Thermodynamic control of the peritectic reaction generates hierarchical InBi@(Bi, Sb) core–shell nanostructures that reduce the room-temperature lattice thermal conductivity from 13.1 to 6.84 W m-1 K-1. This microstructural manipulation raise the power factor by 98% at 473 K, yielding a marked decoupling of electron and phonon transport. A compact, self-powered InSb-InBi/Cu3InSnSe5 module drives commercial electronics under moderate thermal gradients. The module also functions as a zero-power thermo-tactile interface for prosthetic limbs, enabling covert thermal messaging via Morse code decoded by a transfer learning algorithm a transfer learning algorithm. This platform enables the integration of thermoelectric materials into intelligent human-machine interfaces, advancing the development of self-powered sensory systems.Subject terms: Materials for devices, Thermoelectric devices and materials

Introduction

The proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoT) and bio-integrated electronics has created a growing need for autonomous sensory nodes that operate without bulky power supplies. Thermoelectric technology1–6, which directly converts thermal gradients into electricity, offers a viable route to self-powered perception, particularly for converting body heat or industrial waste heat into functional signals7,8. While traditional active sensors offer high precision, their dependence on external batteries limits their scalability, sustainability, and integration into lightweight wearable systems9, such as smart prosthetics10 and electronic skins11. Consequently, harvesting ambient energy to drive passive sensing interfaces represents a critical frontier in modern electronics.

Indium antimonide (InSb), a quintessential III–V semiconductor, is renowned for its high electron mobility (~77000 cm²/V s) and narrow bandgap (~0.17 eV), underpinning its use in high-speed electronics and infrared detection12–16. Recently, InSb has emerged as a promising lead-free candidate for mid-temperature thermoelectric applications due to its favorable electronic transport properties. However, a fundamental bottleneck hinders its practical deployment: its intrinsically high lattice thermal conductivity (κl) precludes the establishment of the large temperature gradients necessary for efficient energy harvesting and sensitive thermal detection17. Although strategies such as eutectic engineering (e.g., InSb–Sb) have been employed to introduce phonon-scattering boundaries18–20, eutectic reactions typically occur at high temperatures (~763 K) and result in thermodynamically restricted microstructures, limiting their efficacy in regulating transport properties across the broad operating ranges required for wearable applications.

Distinct from previously reported eutectic engineering strategies (operating at ~763 K), here we report the realization of a peritectic engineering strategy in InSb-based thermoelectrics. Controlling nucleation at ~405 K yields hierarchical InBi@(Bi, Sb) core-shell nanostructures (Fig. 1a). This approach improves the power factor in the near-room-temperature regime and extends the utility of InSb beyond its traditional roles in infrared and magnetic sensing. This peritectic architecture introduces a high density of coherent phase boundaries and dislocations that selectively scatter phonons while preserving the high electron mobility of the InSb matrix. The resulting n-type InSb–InBi (ISB) material exhibits a robust power factor (Fig. 1b) and a significantly suppressed thermal conductivity (κ) comparable to state-of-the-art counterparts6,19–32 (Fig. 1c).

We then translate these transport improvements into a functional device. Through full-parameter multiphysics modeling, we designed and fabricated a high-efficiency thermoelectric module by pairing our n-type ISB with a thermally matched p-type Cu3InSnSe5 (CISS). The integrated ISB/CISS device drives commercial electronics using moderate temperature differences (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, the device also serves as a thermo-tactile interface for smart prosthetics, capturing transient thermal signals for burn prevention and decoding covert Morse code messages. This work demonstrates peritectic engineering as a practical strategy for semiconducting thermoelectrics and provides a reference for integrating energy harvesters into autonomous human–machine interfaces.

Results

Design and synthesis of ISB peritectic structures

Tailoring nucleation dynamics through element selection allows for the engineered formation of eutectic or peritectic structures within an InSb matrix. Unlike eutectic solidification, peritectic reactions typically yield a distinct solid phase encapsulating the nucleus, generating a core–shell architecture. This heterogeneous nucleation creates abundant phase boundaries critical for effective, broad-spectrum phonon scattering, an advantage not readily achieved in conventional eutectic systems33,34. Thermodynamic analysis of the In–Sb–Bi ternary system identifies a critical peritectic reaction isotherm at 405 K (Supplementary Fig. S1). Accordingly, trace secondary phases of Bi and InBi were detected in InSb1-xBix (x = 0.01–0.04) samples (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Fig. S3). Differential thermal analysis (DTA) further reveals a distinct peritectic endotherm between 383 and 403 K, alongside the sharp melting peak of the InSb matrix at ~803 K (Supplementary Fig. S4). This thermal signature suggests that InBi peritectic phases form via the reaction of excess Bi with InSb at lower temperatures.

To suppress high-temperature Bi alloying (Talloying > Tperitectic) and promote incomplete peritectic crystallization, we developed a synthesis route incorporating Bi nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. S5), sintering at the peritectic temperature (Tperitectic = 405 K), and subsequent consolidation (Fig. 2a). The resulting bulk samples (InSb + z% Bi; z = 0, 1, 3, 6) exhibit X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns well-matched to the zinc-blende InSb structure (Fig. 2b). The high-temperature in situ XRD of the 3% Bi sample reveals an inverse correlation between Bi consumption and InBi formation as temperatures evolve from 300 to 750 K (Fig. 2c), consistent with the dynamic stability of the peritectic phase.

Electron probe micro-analysis (EPMA) and EDS mapping (Supplementary Fig. S6) show InBi phases encased by a (Bi, Sb) solid solution alloy layer at the matrix interface, consistent with a core–shell morphology. Based on the localized stoichiometry, the specific peritectic reaction is inferred as L(Bi, Sb) + InSb → InBi@(Bi, Sb). The formation of this (Bi, Sb) alloy shell is driven by the rejection of Sb from the matrix into the Bi-rich liquid phase during the peritectic transformation, followed by the precipitation of the stable solid solution. The electrical properties were modeled using self-consistent effective medium theory (SCEMT; Supplementary Note 1) to interpret the transport behavior. The experimental electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient deviate from SCEMT predictions (Fig. 2d,e). The experimental electrical conductivity increases with Bi content up to x = 0.03 but shows a slight reduction at x = 0.04 (Fig. 2d). To understand this, Hall effect measurements were conducted (Supplementary Fig. S7). The results indicate that while the carrier concentration (n) increases with Bi alloying, the carrier mobility (μ) drops at x = 0.04 due to intensified alloy scattering and lattice distortion. This mobility degradation dominates the transport behavior at high Bi content, leading to the observed decrease in total electrical conductivity. Despite these variations, the overall experimental electrical conductivity exhibits a positive deviation from the SCEMT baseline predictions (which assume standard random inclusions). This deviation suggests, at a phenomenological level, that the peritectic interfaces possess high electronic coherence. This contrasts with conventional composites, where resistive interfaces suppress carrier transport. Consequently, the incorporation of the metallic tetragonal InBi phase (resistivity ~3.92 μΩ m)35 reduces the electrical resistivity of the alloyed samples relative to the pristine matrix (Fig. 2f), with only a minor compromise in the Seebeck coefficient (Fig. 2g). Together, these effects yield a peak power factor of 29.7 μW cm-1 K-2 at 473 K (Fig. 2h).

Direct observation of peritectic core–shell architectures and lattice defects

The peritectic microstructure was examined by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Low-magnification TEM and electron diffraction patterns confirm that the spark plasma sintered (SPS) composites maintain the high crystallinity of the cubic InSb matrix (Fig. 3a), with In and Sb atoms clearly resolved in the lattice (Fig. 3b,c). The elemental mapping reveals a distinct phase segregation characterized by dispersed (Bi, Sb) solid solutions and InBi phases embedded within the InSb matrix, forming a hierarchical core–shell architecture (Fig. 3d). A high density of these nanoinclusions is distributed along the grain boundaries (Fig. 3e), consistent with the nucleation sites expected from the peritectic reaction.

Higher-magnification imaging resolves these core–shell structures (Zones I and II), which exhibit typical dimensions ranging from 300 to 500 nm (Fig. 3f, g). The atomic intensity contrast and lattice spacing in the HAADF-STEM images identify the core nanostructures as heavy-atom-rich InBi phases (Fig. 3h). The peritectic reaction also introduces significant lattice imperfections. The HAADF-STEM imaging captures collapsed dislocations (Fig. 3i), attributed to the incomplete crystallization and precipitation of InBi from the liquid phase; this dynamic process lowers the dislocation formation energy of the system36,37. Furthermore, inverse FFT filtering and geometric phase analysis (GPA) reveal that incoherent twinning centers induce substantial linear strain fields along the εyy tensor (Fig. 3j, k). These atomic-scale defects appear as well-aligned strain centers in the elastic strain map and are expected to scatter phonons effectively, contributing to κl suppression.

Mechanisms of phonon transport suppression via peritectic engineering

Reducing κl is key to decoupling electrical and thermal transport. The introduction of InBi@(Bi, Sb) core–shell architectures, coupled with the high density of dislocations identified in Fig. 3, results in a substantial reduction in both κ and κl compared to incompletely crystallized InSb0.97Bi0.03 samples (Supplementary Fig. S8).

To probe the atomistic origin of this κl suppression, we performed phonon dynamics calculations using density functional perturbation theory (DFPT). These calculations address the pristine phases to evaluate the intrinsic phonon properties of the constituent materials. The phonon dispersion relations for InBi exhibit lower frequencies and flatter optical branches compared to InSb, indicating phonon mode softening that promotes scattering of medium- and long-wavelength acoustic phonons (Fig. 4a). Consequently, InBi shows markedly lower phonon group velocities (ν), reducing the heat-carrying capacity of its acoustic modes (Fig. 4b).

Given that acoustic impedance is governed by phonon velocity (Z = ρ vg), this distinct velocity contrast quantifies the large intrinsic acoustic impedance mismatch between the stiff InSb matrix and the soft InBi phase. While this baseline calculation does not model interfacial strain, it is consistent with the InBi phase acting as an intrinsic phonon damper, minimizing κl in the heterostructured system.

Experimentally, this microstructural engineering substantially reduces lattice heat transport: the κl of the ISB system drops from 13.1 W m-1 K-1 to 6.84 W m-1 K-1 at room temperature, and from 3.55 W m-1 K-1 to 0.63 W m-1 K-1 at high temperatures. To quantify the underlying scattering mechanisms, we evaluated the total scattering parameter (Γtotal), incorporating both mass field fluctuations (Γm) arising from Bi alloying and strain field fluctuations (Γs) induced by the peritectic nanostructures, calculated using the Klemens-Drabble model (Fig. 4c). The theoretical κl, calculated using the Debye–Callaway formalism incorporating Klemens-Drabble scattering terms (Supplementary Note 2 and Table S1), aligns closely with experimental data at elevated temperatures (e.g., 523 K), suggesting that the peritectic-induced scattering dominates in this regime (Fig. 4d). At 523 K, the measured κl (~3.55 W m-1 K-1) remains above the theoretical minimum κl (κl,min ~ 0.44 W m-1 K-1, Cahill limit). This confirms that in this temperature regime, the heat transport is still governed by well-defined propagating phonons rather than diffuson-like hopping observed in amorphous limits. Consequently, the phonon-based Debye–Callaway approach therefore remains physically appropriate here.

We further deconstructed the spectral contributions of distinct scattering centers (Supplementary Note 3), including Umklapp (U), electron–phonon (EP), and grain boundary (GB) scattering (Fig. 4e). The spectral κl (κl,spectral) analysis reveals that while Bi nanoparticles (GB_1μm to GB_10μm) scatter low-frequency phonons, a gap remains in the phonon spectrum (Fig. 4f). By modeling the transport with a T−1 dependence, we isolated the specific role of the peritectic structure (PS) as a scattering center. As visualized in the zoomed frequency spectrum (ω/ωD < 0.6, Fig. 4g and Supplementary Note 4), this analysis shows that the peritectic interface scatters low-to-medium frequency phonons effectively at high temperatures. This scattering, indicated by the low spectral conductivity region, bridges the gap between point-defect scattering at high frequencies and grain boundary scattering at low frequencies, reducing κl across the working temperature range. Therefore, the InSb–InBi peritectic system achieves a κl lower than state-of-the-art InSb-based thermoelectric materials developed to date (Fig. 4h)6,19,22,23,25,28–32,38. To benchmark the overall thermoelectric performance, we evaluated the figure of merit (zT). As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S9a, our peritectic system demonstrates a competitive zT profile compared to representative Pb/Te-free counterparts, including sulfides and oxides39–44. Critically, for the targeted sensing application, the room-temperature power factor of the InSb–InBi peritectic structure reaches ~20 μW cm-1 K-2, outperforming many eco-friendly alternatives by a factor of 3–10 (Supplementary Fig. S9b). This electrical performance supports high-fidelity signal generation and provides the material basis for the device integration described below.

Device engineering and system integration for efficient heat capture

Although InSb is a premier thermoelectric candidate, translating this material performance into practical mid-temperature device application remains non-trivial. Specifically, realizing efficient heat capture requires not only robust n-type performance but also a thermally matched p-type counterpart and rigorous geometric optimization to minimize parasitic losses. We therefore paired peritectic n-type ISB with p-type CISS, an emerging eco-friendly thermoelectric semiconductor recognized for its mid-temperature performance45.

Device design began with multiphysics simulations multiphysics simulations (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Note 5) to optimize the module geometry based on the intrinsic properties of the ISB and CISS legs (Supplementary Fig. S10). The calculated power generation conversion efficiency (η) as a function of the thermal conductivity ratio (Kn/Kp) confirms that CISS is matched to ISB, minimizing thermal stress and enhancing efficiency (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, we systematically decoupled the impacts of interfacial contact resistivity (Ricr) and leg height (l) on the power output (Pout) and power reduction ratio (ΔP/P) (Supplementary Fig. S11 and Fig. S12). These simulations show that high output power requires tight control of Ricr and an optimized leg aspect ratio. Consequently, we targeted a cross-sectional area ratio (Ap/An) of 1.2 and a l of ~7 mm to maximize device performance (Fig. 5b,c).

Based on these parameters, single ISB legs and unicouple ISB/CISS modules were fabricated (Fig. 5d and inset). Three-dimensional finite element method (3D-FEM) simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics were conducted using the experimental parameters to validate the design. The experimental output voltage for both the single-leg and unicouple modules exhibits a linear relationship with the temperature difference (ΔT), indicating ohmic contact behavior and structural stability (Fig. 5e). The experimental data aligns with the 3D-FEM predictions, confirming the fidelity of the physical model (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. S13, and Supplementary Videos S1,S2). At a ΔT of 120 K, the single ISB leg generated an open-circuit voltage of 8.2 mV (Fig. 5g), while the ISB/CISS module achieved an output power density of 8.5 Wm-2 (Fig. 5h). The simulated electrical potential distribution confirms the effective establishment of a uniform electric field (Fig. 5i), consistent with the expectation that a well-matched n/p assembly improves electrical output. We integrated the module with a custom impedance-matched passive DC/DC converter (Fig. 5j; see Supplementary Fig. S14 for circuit performance). The low internal resistance of the InSb-based legs enables a short-circuit current density (>1800 A m-2), providing the essential high-current input to efficiently drive the voltage booster. The system powers commercial electronics (such as an LED and a digital watch) through harvesting thermal energy (Fig. 5k and Supplementary Video S3). Reliability testing shows that the device retained >95% of its initial output power after 3 months of ambient storage and maintained stable internal resistance under continuous thermal operation (Supplementary Fig. S15).

Thermo-tactile perception and intelligent interaction

Thermo-tactile perception is central to restoring environmental awareness in prosthetic users. Beyond basic temperature logging, passive thermo-tactile interfaces that support both burn prevention and Morse code communication via thermal rhythm sensing represent an important direction for smart prosthetic interfaces. With the hardware foundation established, we evaluated the specific sensing characteristics critical for this application. Characterization of the sensing properties reveals favorable temporal response for both ISB (Supplementary Fig. S16a,b) and ISB/CISS (Supplementary Fig. S16c,d) devices. The device maintains high linearity in response to small ΔT (~5 K) even against a high ambient background of 423 K (Supplementary Fig. S16e,f). This stability confirms the device's suitability for wearable use in complex thermal environments, including real-time overheat detection upon contact with hot objects.

Figure 6a illustrates the primary application scenario: thermo-tactile Morse code communication. The system employs thermal pulse sequences as a covert communication channel, offering prosthesis users an intuitive method for information exchange without requiring visual attention. Signal transmission fidelity was assessed by monitoring the dynamic temperature difference across the device alongside the real-time output voltage (Fig. 6b). The results show that the ISB/CISS device exhibits a stable, reliable response with negligible hysteresis. This high dynamic range and hysteresis-free behavior are critical for capturing transient thermal signals and decoding the temporal patterns inherent to Morse code. Linearity tests at room temperature (Tambient ~ 298 K) demonstrate high linearity for ΔT < 130 K (Fig. 6c). While deviations attributed to thermal convection occur above this threshold, signal readout accuracy within the conventional interaction range remains unaffected. Improved thermal encapsulation would reduce these convective effects.

To assess performance in a realistic prosthetic setting, the ISB-based sensing module was integrated with a custom wireless transmission unit mounted on a dummy prosthetic hand (Fig. 6d). Specifically, the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) interface was configured in high-impedance voltage mode (~20 kΩ). This limits the loop current to <0.01 μA, eliminating electrical loading and preserving signal fidelity. This integrated setup allows for real-time signal acquisition and processing without the constraints of tethered power sources. We evaluated the telemetry performance under static thermal conditions; infrared thermograms confirmed the establishment of distinct thermal gradients across the device when interfaced with varying temperature sources (Fig. 6e). These thermal variations were mirrored by the wirelessly transmitted current data (Fig. 6f), confirming the high fidelity of the signal conditioning and transmission circuit. We then examined the device response to physiological signals. Figure 6g displays the current signals generated when a finger at human body temperature periodically touches the sensor. The device exhibits high sensitivity to the transient thermal contact, with signal peaks and widths corresponding to different tactile durations (representing the ‘dots’ and ‘dashes’ of Morse code). To decode these time-domain thermal signals into semantic information, we employed a transfer learning framework illustrated in Fig. 6h. The model leverages source domain derived from basic thermal, object, and stress inputs (Supplementary Fig. S17), adapting to the target domain of specific human touch patterns, incorporating additional layers to analyze the tactile period and touch duration. Learning curves for single-symbol recognition show rapid convergence of training and validation accuracy (Fig. 6i and Supplementary Fig. S18a). The resulting confusion matrix highlights high classification accuracy for individual Morse code symbols corresponding to letters such as ‘H’, ‘I’, ‘N’, ‘T’, and ‘U’ (Fig. 6j). Furthermore, we extended the model’s capability to recognize continuous character sequences necessary for word-level communication. The learning curves for symbol and word recognition confirm the model’s stability (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Fig. S18b). The system successfully decoded the composite thermal message ‘HI-NTU’ (Fig. 6l, Supplementary Fig. S18c,d, and Supplementary Video S4, S5), confirming that ISB-based passive sensors can support covert thermal communication in prosthetic applications.

While these results demonstrate high decoding accuracy, the current study is a proof-of-concept. The model was evaluated on a target dataset of approximately 1510 samples, and real-world prosthetic deployment will face broader challenges. Factors such as inter-user variability (e.g., differences in skin temperature and finger contact pressure), environmental thermal noise, and long-term baseline drift could influence signal stability. Future work will address dataset diversity and adaptive calibration to improve robustness under real-world conditions.

Discussion

In this work, we have shown that peritectic microstructural engineering can connect fundamental thermoelectric materials design to practical, intelligent sensing. Controlling nucleation dynamics within the InSb system produces a hierarchical InBi@(Bi, Sb) core–shell architecture. This microstructure, coupled with dense dislocation arrays, decoupling electron and phonon transport without compromising electrical performance. At the device level, we realized the transition from laboratory capability to system-level utility. Multiphysics-guided pairing of n-type ISB with p-type CISS yielded an efficient unicouple module. Integrated with a custom impedance-matched DC/DC converter, this system harvests medium-grade heat to drive commercial electronics, supporting battery-free operation.

The application scope extends beyond energy harvesting to active human–machine interaction energy harvesting to active human–machine interaction. By embedding these modules into smart prosthetics, the module restores thermo-tactile perception for prosthetic users. The system not only provides essential safety features like burn prevention but also introduces a covert communication channel via thermal Morse code, decoded by transfer learning algorithms. The combination of peritectic engineering, self-powered device integration, and machine learning-assisted signal decoding provides a practical solution for autonomous sensory systems in wearable and prosthetic applications.

Methods

Materials synthesis

Polycrystalline InSb samples were synthesized using high-purity elements: In (99.99%), Sb (99.99%), and Bi (99.999%). The stoichiometric mixtures were sealed in evacuated quartz tubes (residual pressure <10-4 Torr) and heated to 973 K at a rate of 117 K/h. The melt was held at this temperature for 6 h, followed by furnace cooling to room temperature. The resulting ingots were pulverized into fine powders using an agate mortar and ball milling. To induce the peritectic structure, Bi nanopowders were refined via ball milling and ultrasonic dispersion in alcohol for 6 h. These refined Bi nanoparticles were then homogeneously mixed with the InSb matrix powders. The mixture was annealed at the peritectic temperature of 405 K for 6 h to promote core–shell formation, followed by consolidation via spark plasma sintering (SPS-3T-3-MIN) at 733 K for 10 min under an axial pressure of 60 MPa in a ϕ20 mm graphite mold. For the p-type Cu3InSnSe5 counterpart, stoichiometric amounts of Cu (99.99%), In (99.99%), Sn (99.9%), and Se (99.99%) were subjected to high-energy ball milling for 270 min. The resulting powders were consolidated into dense pellets using SPS at 60 MPa for 10 min.

DFT and phonon dynamics calculations

First-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)46 based on density functional theory (DFT). The structural geometry was fully relaxed until the Hellmann–Feynman forces on all atoms converged to less than 10-5 eV/Å, with a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV. A 2 × 2 × 2 supercell with a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 3 k-point grid was employed for the Brillouin zone integration to calculate total energies. Phonon dynamics were subsequently computed using the DFPT method as implemented in the Phonopy package interfaced with VASP.

Microstructures characterizations

Phase purity was analyzed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and in situ temperature-dependent PXRD using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5418 Å). Rietveld refinements were performed using the Whole Pattern Fitting (WPF) module. Microstructural evolution and elemental distribution were examined using an Electron probe micro-analysis (EPMA-8050G) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging and high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning TEM (STEM) analyses were conducted on a Thermo Scientific Talos F200X system.

Transport property measurements

The temperature-dependent electrical resistivity and Seebeck coefficient were measured simultaneously using a commercial system (ZEM-3, ULVAC-RIKO) under a helium atmosphere. Room-temperature Hall effect measurements were performed using a physical property measurement system (ECOPIA HMS-5500) with a four-probe van der Pauw configuration to determine the carrier concentration and mobility. Sound velocities, including longitudinal (νl) and transverse (νt) components, were determined using an ultrasonic pulse-receiver (Olympus 5072PR). Particle size distribution was analyzed using a laser diffraction particle sizer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical). The relative density of bulk samples was measured via the Archimedes method. Thermal diffusivity (D) was measured using the laser flash method (LFA 427, Netzsch), and specific heat capacity (Cp) was derived using the Debye model (Supplementary Note 6).

Module fabrication and assembly

The n-type InSb–InBi and p-type Cu3InSnSe5 bulk materials47 were machined into rectangular legs with dimensions of 3 × 4 × 7 mm and 3.6 × 4 × 7 mm, respectively. To prevent diffusion and ensure ohmic contact, a 0.2-mm-thick Ni barrier layer was deposited onto the contact surfaces via vacuum evaporation. The n- and p-type legs were assembled onto AlN ceramic substrates patterned with Cu electrodes. Bonding was achieved by sintering a 0.4 mm Cu buffer layer and soldering with a Sn–Ag-based alloy at elevated temperatures to ensure reliable mechanical and electrical contact.

Thermoelectric performance evaluation

Device performance was evaluated using a custom-built, programmable temperature gradient platform. K-type thermocouples were integrated into the hot and cold sides to monitor temperatures at both sides. A feedback-controlled water circulation system maintained the cold-side temperature. The output power, current–voltage (I–V) characteristics, and open-circuit voltage (Voc) were recorded using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E, CH Instruments) and a nanovoltmeter (Keithley 2182 A).

Finite element multiphysics simulation

Three-dimensional finite element method (3D-FEM) simulations were conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics to model the power generation characteristics. A 3D geometric model representing the single ISB leg and the unicouple ISB/CISS module was constructed. The simulation coupled the Heat Transfer in Solids, Electric Currents, and Thermoelectric Effect modules. Geometric parameters and material properties were input based on experimental measurements and full-parameter calculations. The model structure included a 1 mm AlN ceramic plate, 0.4 mm Cu electrodes, 0.1 mm solder layers, 0.2 mm Ni barrier layers, and the semiconductor legs. The following boundary conditions were applied: (1) Thermal: All lateral surfaces were assumed to be adiabatic. The outer surface of the cold-side ceramic was maintained at a constant temperature (Tcold = 298 K), while the hot-side temperature was prescribed to establish the temperature gradient. The finite thermal contact resistances were modeled at the ceramic-electrode interfaces using thermal contact conductance coefficients (CTEh and CTEc) derived from full-parameter calculations (values listed in Supplementary Table S2). (2) Electrical: All lateral boundaries were electrically insulating. One electrode terminal was grounded (0 V), and the counter electrode was designated as the current/voltage terminal coupled to a parametric load resistance to evaluate the power output characteristics.

Wireless signal acquisition system

A custom wireless telemetry unit was designed based on an ARM Cortex-M microcontroller integrated with a low-noise instrumentation amplifier. Thermal voltage signals were digitized via a 12-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) at a sampling rate of 50 Hz, adequate for resolving transient Morse code signals. Data packets were transmitted via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE 5.0) to a mobile terminal for real-time visualization and logging.

Transfer learning algorithm

The signal decoding framework was implemented using TensorFlow. The dataset included a source domain (Ds, containing 500 laboratory samples of basic thermal/mechanical stimuli) and a target domain (Dt, containing 1510 human touch samples). In the learning process, the dataset was randomly split into trainset, validation set, and testset according to an 8:1:1 ratio. The model architecture integrated a 1D-CNN for feature extraction and a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) layer to capture temporal dependencies (tactile duration). A transfer learning strategy was adopted by pre-training the 1D-CNN on Ds and subsequently fine-tuning the 1D-CNN and the high-level LSTM layers on Dt using the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1 × 10−3) and cross-entropy loss.
