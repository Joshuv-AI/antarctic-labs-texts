A Unified Substitution Method for Integration (DRAFT)
Emmanuel Antonio José García

Abstract
This paper introduces a Unified Substitution Method (USM) that systematically integrates expressions involving inverse trigonometric functions, radicals, and related forms. By synthesizing classical techniques, such as Euler substitutions, hyperbolic parameterizations, and complex exponential identities, into a single framework, the USM streamlines the evaluation of integrals containing terms such as tan ⁡ ( csc − 1 ⁡ ( y ) 2 ) , ( x + b ) 2 ± a 2 , or x + p x + q . \tan\left(\frac{\csc^{-1}(y)}{2}\right),\qquad\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}\pm a^{2}},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sqrt{\frac{x+p}{x+q}}. Central to the method are novel Euler-like identities that rigorously connect trigonometric, hyperbolic, and algebraic substitutions while preserving domain consistency. These transformations reduce complex integrands to rational or polynomial-like forms in new variables, bypassing ad hoc substitutions and the memorization of a multitude of standard formulas. Demonstrations include integrals resistant to standard Computer Algebra Systems (CAS), such as those mixing csc − 1 \csc^{-1} and sec − 1 \sec^{-1} , and comparisons with classical Euler substitutions reveal the USM’s broader scope and conceptual cohesion. The method not only enhances computational efficiency but also clarifies domain-specific sign choices, offering a robust educational and practical tool for calculus.



1
Introduction

Integration techniques often rely on fragmented substitution rules tailored to specific integrand forms—Euler substitutions for radicals, trigonometric identities for angular functions, or hyperbolic parameterizations for quadratic expressions. While effective individually, these methods lack a unifying framework, forcing practitioners to memorize case-specific recipes and navigate opaque domain restrictions. This paper addresses these limitations by introducing the
Unified Substitution Method (USM)
, a cohesive approach that generalizes and systematizes classical tools through exponential and hyperbolic reparameterizations.

The USM originated from efforts to extend geometric formulas [4], which unexpectedly yielded trigonometric-hyperbolic identities for quadratic roots [2]. These identities bridge the tangent half-angle substitution [7] and complex exponentials [3], traditionally viewed as distinct techniques. For instance, integrals involving
\csc^{-1}(y)
or
\sec^{-1}(y)
map to rational functions via substitutions like
t=e^{\pm i\alpha}
, while radicals such as
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}\pm a^{2}}
simplify through hyperbolic or exponential parameterizations. Crucially, the USM’s theoretical foundation (Theorems 1–5) rigorously governs domain-specific sign choices, eliminating ambiguities that complicate traditional methods.

Key motivations for the USM include its ability to resolve integrals where standard CAS tools falter (e.g., Example 6) and its capacity to reduce radicals like
\sqrt{\frac{x+p}{x+q}}
to polynomial-like forms (Example 5). Unlike Euler substitutions, which require memorizing separate rules for
\sqrt{x^{2}\pm a^{2}}
or
\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}
, the USM unifies these cases under a single principle: expressing
x+b
in terms of
e^{\pm i\alpha}
or
e^{\theta}
, with sign conventions dictated by interval domains. This coherence extends to inverse trigonometric integrands, trigonometric/hyperbolic forms, and more, all handled systematically.

The paper proceeds as follows: Sections 2–7 detail the USM’s transformation formulas, supported by proofs and examples. Section 9 contrasts the USM with Euler substitutions, showing how the latter emerge as special cases within the unified framework. By consolidating techniques, clarifying domain logic, and broadening applicability, the USM offers educators and practitioners a versatile, theoretically grounded alternative to ad hoc integration strategies.

Some Euler-like identities

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
and
\beta=\csc^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are complex numbers and
x
,
a
, and
b
are real numbers with
a>0
. Then the following relation holds:

e^{\pm i\alpha}=\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right),

(1)

where the upper sign is used when
x\in[a-b,\infty)
, and the lower sign is used when
x\in(-\infty,-a-b]
.

Proof.

Consider the left-hand side (LHS) of equation
(1)
. We simplify
e^{\pm i\alpha}
using Euler’s formula:

\displaystyle e^{\pm i\alpha}

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\alpha\right)\pm i\sin\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x+b}{a}\pm i\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)^{2}}.

Let us define:

y=\dfrac{x+b}{a}.

Then the LHS becomes:

e^{\pm i\alpha}=y\pm i\sqrt{1-y^{2}}.

Next, we simplify the right-hand side (RHS) of equation
(1)
. Using trigonometric identities [6], we have:

\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)=\frac{1-\cos\left(\beta\right)}{\sin\left(\beta\right)}.

Since
\beta=\csc^{-1}(y)
, we have:

\sin\left(\beta\right)=\frac{1}{\csc\left(\beta\right)}=\frac{1}{y}.

\cos\left(\beta\right)=\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}\left(\beta\right)}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{y^{2}}}=\frac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{|y|}.

Therefore, the RHS becomes:

\displaystyle\tan\left(\dfrac{\beta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{|y|}}{\dfrac{1}{y}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{|y|-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{|y|}\right)y

\displaystyle=y-\text{sgn}(y)\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

Since
a>0
, we consider two cases based on the value of
y
:

Case 1:

y\geq 1
(i.e.,
x\geq a-b
). In this case,
y\geq 1
and
|y|=y
, so:

\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)=y-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

Also,
\sqrt{1-y^{2}}
is imaginary since
y^{2}\geq 1
. Therefore,

\sqrt{1-y^{2}}=i\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

So the LHS becomes:

e^{i\alpha}=y-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

Thus,

e^{i\alpha}=\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right).

Case 2:

y\leq-1
(i.e.,
x\leq-a-b
). Here,
y\leq-1
and
|y|=-y
, so:

\tan\left(\dfrac{\beta}{2}\right)=y+\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

The LHS becomes:

e^{-i\alpha}=y+\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

Thus,

e^{-i\alpha}=\tan\left(\dfrac{\beta}{2}\right).

Therefore, the identity holds with the appropriate sign depending on the interval of
x
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
and
\gamma=\sec^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
, where
\alpha
and
\gamma
are complex numbers and
x
,
a
, and
b
are real numbers with
a>0
. Then the following relation holds:

e^{\pm i\alpha}=\dfrac{1-\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)}{1+\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)},

(2)

where the upper sign is used when
x\in[a-b,\infty)
, and the lower sign is used when
x\in(-\infty,-a-b)
.

Proof.

We proceed similarly to the previous theorem. Let
y=\dfrac{x+b}{a}
. Then
\gamma=\sec^{-1}(y)
. Since
\sec\left(\gamma\right)=y
, we have:

\cos\left(\gamma\right)=\dfrac{1}{y},\quad\sin\left(\gamma\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{|y|}.

Using the half-angle formula [6]:

\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)=\dfrac{\sin\left(\gamma\right)}{1+\cos\left(\gamma\right)}.

Case 1:

y\geq 1
(i.e.,
x\geq a-b
). The expressions are defined, and we have:

\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)=\dfrac{\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{y}}{1+\dfrac{1}{y}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{y+1}.

Case 2:

y<-1
(i.e.,
x<-a-b
). The expressions are defined except at
y=-1
, which we exclude.
We have:

\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)=\dfrac{\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{-y}}{1+\dfrac{1}{y}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}{-y+1}.

Following similar steps as before, we find:

- For
y\geq 1
:

\dfrac{1-\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)}{1+\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)}=y-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}=e^{i\alpha}.

- For
y<-1
:

\dfrac{1-\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)}{1+\tan\left(\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\right)}=y+\sqrt{y^{2}-1}=e^{-i\alpha}.

Therefore, the identity holds with the appropriate sign depending on the interval of
x
.

∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. For each
x\in[-a-b,a-b]
, define

y=\frac{x+b}{a}.

Then, with
\alpha=\cos^{-1}(y)
taken in the principal branch (so
\alpha\in[0,\pi]
), the following identity holds:

e^{-\,i\,\alpha}=y-\sqrt{\,y^{2}-1}.

Proof.

Since
x\in[-a-b,a-b]
and
a>0
, we have

-1\leq\frac{x+b}{a}\leq 1.

Set
\alpha:=\cos^{-1}(y)
. By definition of the principal branch [1],
\alpha\in[0,\pi]
.
Then

\cos(\alpha)=y,\quad\sin(\alpha)=\sqrt{\,1-y^{2}}\quad\left(\text{since }\sin(\alpha)\geq 0\right).

Hence

e^{-\,i\,\alpha}=\cos(\alpha)-i\,\sin(\alpha)=y-i\,\sqrt{\,1-y^{2}}.

When
-1\leq y\leq 1
, note that

y^{2}-1=-\left(1-y^{2}\right),

and so in the principal branch for the square root,

\sqrt{\,y^{2}-1}=\sqrt{\,-\left(1-y^{2}\right)}=i\,\sqrt{\,1-y^{2}}.

Therefore,

y-i\,\sqrt{\,1-y^{2}}=y-\sqrt{\,y^{2}-1}.

Combining the above, we get

e^{-\,i\,\alpha}=y-i\,\sqrt{\,1-y^{2}}=y-\sqrt{\,y^{2}-1}.

∎

Theorem 4
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi=\sec^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
and
\displaystyle\psi=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
, where
\phi
and
\psi
are (possibly complex) angles, and
x,a,b
are real numbers with
a>0
. Then the following identity holds:

e^{\pm i\,\phi}=\tan\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\psi\right),

where the “
+
” sign is used when
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[0,1]
and the “
-
” sign is used when
\tfrac{x+b}{a}\in[-1,0]
.

Analogous steps to those in the proofs of Theorems 1 and 2 show this result. As before, one applies Euler’s formula
e^{\pm i\,\phi}=\cos(\phi)\pm i\,\sin(\phi)
, then uses
\sec(\phi)=\frac{x+b}{a}
and
\sin(\psi)=\frac{x+b}{a}
to express
\cos(\phi)
and
\sin(\phi)
in terms of
\phi
and
\psi
. A short trigonometric manipulation verifies that it matches
\tan\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\,\psi\bigr)
. The choice of sign
\pm
depends on the interval for
\frac{x+b}{a}
, just as in Theorem 1.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi=\sec^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
and
\displaystyle\chi=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
. Under the same assumptions on
x,a,b
(with
a>0
) and corresponding domain considerations for
\frac{x+b}{a}
, we have:

e^{\pm i\,\phi}=\frac{1-\tan\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\chi\right)}{1+\tan\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\chi\bigr)},

where the “
+
” sign applies to
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[0,1]
, and the “
-
” sign applies to
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[-1,0]
.

As with Theorem 4, the proof follows the same pattern as Theorems 1 and 2: write out
e^{\pm i\,\phi}=\cos(\phi)\pm i\,\sin(\phi)
, then substitute
\sec(\phi)=\frac{x+b}{a}
and
\cos(\chi)=\frac{x+b}{a}
. Standard half-angle identities confirm that

\frac{1-\tan\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\chi\right)}{1+\tan\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\chi\right)}

matches
e^{\pm i\,\phi}
for the indicated domain. The sign selection
\pm
again parallels that of Theorem 2.

3
Transformation for Integrals Involving
\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)
and
\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)

We want to transform an integral of the form

\int f\left(x,\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right),\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\,dx,

where (from the theorems):
\alpha=\cos^{-1}(y)
,
\beta=\csc^{-1}(y)
leads to
\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)=e^{\pm i\alpha}
(Theorem 1),
\gamma=\sec^{-1}(y)
leads to
\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)=\frac{1-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{1+e^{\pm i\alpha}}
(Theorem 2). Since
y=\frac{x+b}{a}
, we have

x+b=a\cos\left(\alpha\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=a\cos\left(\alpha\right)-b.

Hence,

dx=-\,a\,\sin\left(\alpha\right)d\alpha.

Thus everything is now expressed in terms of
\alpha
. Hence

\int f\left(x,\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right),\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\right)\,dx=\int f\left(a\cos\left(\alpha\right)-b,e^{\pm i\alpha},\,\frac{1-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{1+e^{\pm i\alpha}}\right)\left(-a\sin\left(\alpha\right)\right)\,d\alpha.

Recall the identities

\sin\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{e^{\,i\alpha}-e^{-\,i\alpha}}{2\,i},\qquad\cos\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{e^{\,i\alpha}+e^{-\,i\alpha}}{2}.

Hence

-\,a\,\sin\left(\alpha\right)\,d\alpha=-\,a\frac{t-t^{-1}}{\,2\,i\,}d\alpha\quad\text{where}\quad t=e^{\pm i\alpha}.

Next, differentiate
t=e^{\pm i\alpha}
:

dt=\pm\,i\,e^{\pm i\alpha}\,d\alpha=\pm\,i\,t\,d\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\alpha=\frac{dt}{\,\pm\,i\,t\,}.

Putting these together:

dx=-\,a\,\sin\left(\alpha\right)\,d\alpha=-\,a\frac{t-t^{-1}}{2\,i}\cdot\frac{dt}{\,\pm\,i\,t\,}.

Observe that

\frac{t-t^{-1}}{\,t\,}=\frac{t^{2}-1}{\,t^{2}\,},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\,i\,}\cdot\frac{1}{\,\pm i\,}=\frac{1}{(-1)}\,\text{or}\,\frac{1}{(+1)},

depending on the sign. One finds (carefully tracking the
\pm
) that the net effect is

dx=a\frac{\,t^{2}-1\,}{\,2\,t^{2}\,}dt\quad(\text{with an overall sign absorbed consistently}).

Finally, also rewrite
x=a\cos\left(\alpha\right)-b
in terms of
t
:

\cos\left(\alpha\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(t+t^{-1}\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=a\,\frac{t+t^{-1}}{2}-b=\frac{\,a\,(t^{2}+1)\,}{2\,t}-b.

Likewise, from the theorems:

\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)=t,\qquad\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)=\pm\,\frac{1-t}{1+t}.

Putting all the pieces together yields

\displaystyle\int f\biggl(x,\tan\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right),\tan\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)\biggr)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int f\left(\overbrace{\frac{e^{i\alpha}+e^{-i\alpha}}{2}}^{\cos\alpha}a-b,\;\overbrace{e^{\pm i\alpha}}^{\cos\alpha\pm i\sin\alpha},\;\overbrace{\frac{1-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{1+e^{\pm i\alpha}}}^{i\tan\left(\frac{\mp\alpha}{2}\right)}\right)\overbrace{\frac{e^{-i\alpha}-e^{i\alpha}}{2i}}^{-\sin\alpha}a\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int f\left(\frac{t^{2}+1}{2t}a-b,\;t,\;\pm\frac{1-t}{1+t}\right)\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t^{2}}a\,dt.\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(3)

The sign choices
\pm
in front of
i\alpha
get matched to the domain conditions
\dfrac{x+b}{a}\geq 1
or
\leq-1
, exactly as specified by the original theorems.

Upon completing the integration with respect to
t
, the original variable
x
can be recovered by employing the substitution

t=e^{\pm i\alpha}=y\mp\sqrt{y^{2}-1}=\frac{x+b\mp\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}}{a},

ensuring that the sign matches the branch chosen during the earlier transformation.

4
Transformation for Integrals Involving
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}
and
\dfrac{\sqrt{x+b-a}}{\sqrt{x+b+a}}

Now consider an integral of the form

\int f\left(x,\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{x+b-a}}{\sqrt{x+b+a}}\right)\,dx.

As before,

x+b=a\cos\left(\alpha\right),\quad x=a\cos\left(\alpha\right)-b,\quad dx=-a\sin\left(\alpha\right)\,d\alpha.

But now
\left|\frac{x+b}{a}\right|\geq 1
, so
\sin\left(\alpha\right)
can be purely imaginary. The theorems tell us (for instance) that

\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}=\pm\,ia\,\left|\sin\left(\alpha\right)\right|=\pm\,ia\sin\left(\alpha\right)=\pm\frac{\,e^{-\,i\alpha}-e^{\,i\alpha}\,}{2}a\quad(\text{depending on branch}),

and similarly,

\frac{\sqrt{x+b-a}}{\sqrt{x+b+a}}=\frac{1-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{\,1+e^{\pm i\alpha}}=i\tan\left(\frac{\mp\alpha}{2}\right),

matching the sign conventions from Theorem 1 and 2.

Exactly as before, we write
\sin\left(\alpha\right)
,
\cos\left(\alpha\right)
in terms of
e^{i\alpha}
. We get

\sin\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{t-t^{-1}}{2\,i},\quad\cos\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{t+t^{-1}}{2}.

Then

dx=-a\sin\left(\alpha\right)\,d\alpha=\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t^{2}}a\,dt\quad\text{where}\quad t=e^{\pm i\alpha}.

Also,

x+b=a\cos\left(\alpha\right)=a\frac{t+t^{-1}}{2},\quad x=\frac{t^{2}+1}{2t}a-b.

The factor
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}
becomes
\mp\,\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t}a
, and
\frac{\sqrt{x+b-a}}{\sqrt{x+b+a}}
becomes
\pm\,\frac{1-t}{1+t}
, up to the same sign choices as in the theorems.

Putting everything together again yields

\int f\left(x,\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{x+b-a}}{\sqrt{x+b+a}}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int f\Biggl(\overbrace{\frac{e^{i\alpha}+e^{-i\alpha}}{2}}^{\cos\left(\alpha\right)}a-b,\ \overbrace{\frac{e^{\mp i\alpha}-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{2}}^{\mp i\sin\left(\alpha\right)}a,\ \overbrace{\frac{1-e^{\pm i\alpha}}{1+e^{\pm i\alpha}}}^{i\tan\left(\frac{\mp\alpha}{2}\right)}\Biggr)\overbrace{\frac{e^{-i\alpha}-e^{i\alpha}}{2i}}^{-\sin\left(\alpha\right)}a\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int f\Biggl(\frac{t^{2}+1}{2t}a-b,\ \mp\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t}a,\ \pm\frac{1-t}{1+t}\Biggr)\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t^{2}}a\,dt.\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(4)

Again, the
\pm
and
\mp
match the domain conditions spelled out in the theorems (whether
y\geq 1
,
y\leq-1
, or
-1\leq y\leq 1
in the complex plane).

After performing the integration in terms of
t
, the relationship

t=e^{\pm i\alpha}=\frac{x+b\mp\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}}{a},

can be applied to express the result in terms of the original variable
x
, in accordance with the sign choices made during the transformation.

5
Transformation for Integrals Involving
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}

Consider an integral of the form

\int f\left(x,\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}\right)\,dx.

This time one uses the hyperbolic substitution [5]:

\theta=\sinh^{-1}\left(\tfrac{x+b}{a}\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sinh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{x+b}{a},\quad\cosh\left(\theta\right)=\sqrt{1+\sinh^{2}\left(\theta\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}}{a}.

From
\sinh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{x+b}{a}
, we get

x+b=a\sinh\left(\theta\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=a\sinh\left(\theta\right)-b.

Differentiate:

dx=a\cosh\left(\theta\right)\,d\theta.

Also note

\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}=a\cosh\left(\theta\right).

Recall the standard identities [5]:

\sinh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{s-s^{-1}}{2},\quad\cosh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{s+s^{-1}}{2},\quad\text{where}\quad s=e^{\theta}.

Hence

x+b=a\sinh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{a}{2}\left(s-s^{-1}\right),\quad\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}=a\cosh\left(\theta\right)=\frac{a}{2}\left(s+s^{-1}\right).

Also

dx=a\cosh\left(\theta\right)\,d\theta=a\frac{s+s^{-1}}{2}\,d\theta.

But
d\theta=\frac{ds}{s}
. Hence

dx=a\frac{s+s^{-1}}{2}\cdot\frac{ds}{s}=\frac{s^{2}+1}{2s^{2}}a\,ds.

Putting everything into the integral:

\displaystyle\int f\left(x,\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int f\Biggl(\overbrace{\frac{e^{\theta}-e^{-\theta}}{2}}^{\sinh\left(\theta\right)}a-b,\ \overbrace{\frac{e^{\theta}+e^{-\theta}}{2}}^{\cosh\left(\theta\right)}a\Biggr)\overbrace{\frac{e^{\theta}+e^{-\theta}}{2}}^{\cosh\left(\theta\right)}a\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int f\Biggl(\frac{s^{2}-1}{2s}a-b,\ \frac{s^{2}+1}{2s}a\Biggr)\frac{s^{2}+1}{2s^{2}}a\,ds.\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(5)

where
s=e^{\theta}
and
\theta=\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right)
.

Once integration with respect to
s
is complete, one can return to the original

x
-variable by applying the substitution

s=e^{\theta}=\frac{x+b+\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}}{a},

where
\frac{x+b}{a}>0
and
a>0
.

6
Transformation for Integrals Involving
\tan\left(\frac{\psi}{2}\right)
and
\tan\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)

Let

\phi=\sec^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right),\quad\psi=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right),\quad\alpha=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right),

where
a>0
, and suppose
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[0,1]
. Let
r=e^{i\,\phi}
(with the sign choice for
e^{-i\,\phi}
if
\tfrac{x+b}{a}\in[-1,0]
). Then, by an argument analogous to the derivations of the earlier transformations (cf. formulas (3) and (4)), one obtains:

\int f\Bigl(x,\tan\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi\bigr),\tan\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha\bigr)\Bigr)\,dx=\int f\Bigl(\dfrac{2\,r}{1+r^{2}}\,a-b,r,\pm\dfrac{1+r}{1-r}\Bigr)\;\dfrac{2\,\bigl(1-r^{2}\bigr)}{\bigl(1+r^{2}\bigr)^{2}}a\,dr,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(6)

where
r=\tan\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi\bigr)=e^{i\,\phi}
. As usual, once the integral in
r
is evaluated, we back-substitute
r=\dfrac{x+b\;\mp\;\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}}{a}
(matching the sign with the chosen branch) to express the result in terms of
x
.

7
Transformation for Integrals Involving
\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}
and
\sqrt{\dfrac{a+b+x}{a-b-x}}

Under the same notations and domain conditions as in
(6)
(that is,
a>0
and
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[0,1]
, with a parallel sign adjustment if
\frac{x+b}{a}\in[-1,0]
), let

\phi=\sec^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right),\quad\psi=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x+b}{a}\right),\quad r=e^{i\,\phi}=\tan\left(\frac{1}{2}\psi\right).

Then, by a derivation fully similar to those for the previous transformation formulas, one obtains:

\int f\Bigl(x,\sqrt{\,a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}\,},\sqrt{\dfrac{a+b+x}{a-b-x}}\Bigr)\,dx

=\int f\Bigl(\dfrac{2r}{1+r^{2}}a-b,\pm\dfrac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}a,\pm\dfrac{1+r}{1-r}\Bigr)\dfrac{2\bigl(1-r^{2}\bigr)}{\bigl(1+r^{2}\bigr)^{2}}a\,dr,

where

r=\frac{a\mp\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}}{x+b}=\tan\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi\Bigr)=e^{i\,\phi}.

The integral in
r
-space may again be tackled as a rational/polynomial-like form, and then one recovers
x
by reversing
r\mapsto x
with the sign consistent for the chosen domain.

Remark
. When facing integrals whose integrand involves a factor of the form

\frac{1}{Q(x)\pm\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{1}{Q(x)\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}},

where
Q(x)
is a polynomial, we recommend applying Transformation (7). This approach systematically converts the integral into a polynomial-like/rational form. However, if the integrand contains
Q(x)\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}
(i.e., the square root appears multiplicatively in the numerator), it is simpler to factor out
i
(as in Example 2) and apply Transformation (4) instead.

8.1
Example 1.

We will apply the method detailed above to compute the following integral

\int\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\,dx.

Notice that

\sqrt{x^{2}-1}=\sqrt{(x+0)^{2}-1^{2}},

so we set
a=1
and
b=0
. For
x\geq 1
, the condition
\frac{x+b}{a}\geq 1
holds, allowing us to apply formula (4) from the USM.

Using the formula for integrals involving
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}
with
a=1
,
b=0
, and choosing the upper sign (since
x\geq 1
), we obtain

\int\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\,dx=\int\Big(-\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t}\Big)\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t^{2}}\,dt=-\int\frac{(t^{2}-1)^{2}}{4t^{3}}\,dt,

where
t=e^{\pm i\alpha}
. Expanding the integrand:

-\frac{1}{4}\int\Big(t-2t^{-1}+t^{-3}\Big)\,dt.

Integrating term by term gives

-\frac{1}{4}\Big(\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}-2\ln|t|-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-2}\Big)+C=-\frac{t^{2}}{8}+\tfrac{1}{2}\ln|t|+\frac{1}{8t^{2}}+C.

Finally, using the back-substitution
t=x-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}
appropriate for
x\geq 1
, the result simplifies to the well-known formula

\int\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\,dx=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}-\ln|x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1}|\Big)+C.

Example 2: Factoring Out i

For

a=1

e^{-\,i\,\alpha}=y-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}=x-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}.

since for
-1\leq x\leq 1

Using the relationship

Under this substitution, one obtains:

\int\frac{dx}{x^{3}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}=\frac{1}{32}\int\left(r^{-3}+2\,r^{-1}+r\right)\,dr.

\frac{1}{32}\int\left(r^{-3}+2\,r^{-1}+r\right)\,dr=\frac{1}{16}\ln\left|r\right|+\frac{1}{64}\left(r^{2}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)+C.

Example 8

Let us consider the integral

\int\sqrt{\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)}\,dx.

We begin by noting that

\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)=x^{2}-\sqrt{x^{4}-1}.

Introduce the substitution

u=x^{2},\quad\text{so that}\quad dx=\frac{du}{2\sqrt{u}}.

Then the integral becomes

\int\sqrt{x^{2}-\sqrt{x^{4}-1}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\sqrt{u-\sqrt{u^{2}-1}}}{\sqrt{u}}\,du.

Following the USM (specifically transformation formula (4)), we introduce the new variable
t
so that the integral transforms into

\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+1}}\cdot\frac{t^{2}-1}{t^{2}}\,dt,

where
t>0
and
u\geq 1
.

Splitting the integrals, we write

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\int\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+1}}\,dt-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\int\frac{1}{t\sqrt{t^{2}+1}}\,dt\right].

Using standard antiderivatives (with the second term evaluated via transformation formula (5)), we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\sqrt{t^{2}+1}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\ln\!\left|\frac{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+1}-1}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+1}+1}\right|+C\right].

Thus, after simplifying,

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\,\sqrt{t^{2}+1}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\ln\!\left|\frac{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+1}-1}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+1}+1}\right|+C.

Recalling that

t=\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr).

The antiderivative becomes

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\sec\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\ln\!\left|\frac{\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)+\sec\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)-1}{\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)+\sec\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)+1}\right|+C.

Finally, note the trigonometric identity

\frac{\tan\omega+\sec\omega-1}{\tan\omega+\sec\omega+1}=\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{\omega}{2}\Bigr).

With
\omega=\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})
, the logarithmic expression simplifies, and we obtain the compact final answer:

\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\left[\sec\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)-\ln\!\left|\tan\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}\csc^{-1}(x^{2})\Bigr)\right|\right]+C.

Bypassing integration by parts

9.1
Example 9

Let us evaluate the integral

\int e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}\,dx,\quad\text{for }|x|\leq 1,

using Transformation (4) and Theorem 3. Since
t=e^{-i\cos^{-1}(x)}
, one may write

e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}=t^{i}.

Substituting for
e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}
, we obtain

\int e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}\,dx=\int t^{i}\cdot\frac{t^{2}-1}{2t^{2}}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(t^{i}-t^{i-2}\right)\,dt.

Thus, the integral becomes

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{t^{i+1}}{i+1}-\frac{t^{i-1}}{i-1}\right]+C.

Rationalize the denominators by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the appropriate complex conjugate:

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{t^{i+1}(1-i)}{2}+\frac{t^{i-1}(1+i)}{2}\right]+C=\frac{1}{4}\,t^{i}\left[(1-i)t+(1+i)t^{-1}\right]+C.

Substituting back
t=x-i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
followed by a short algebraic manipulation shows that

(1-i)t+(1+i)t^{-1}=2\left(x-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right).

Then, the final answer becomes

\int e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}\,dx=\frac{1}{4}\,e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}\cdot 2\left(x-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+C=\frac{e^{\cos^{-1}(x)}}{2}\left(x-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+C.

Remark
. Although employing Euler’s formula to sidestep integration by parts is a familiar trick (see [3]), Example 9 does more than merely repackage that idea. In this instance, the substitution

t=e^{–i\cos^{-1}(x)}

(notably derived via Theorem 3) transforms the integrand into a straightforward power form amenable to term-by-term integration. This approach not only avoids the laborious integration by parts but also vividly illustrates how the USM consolidates techniques that are otherwise treated separately—such as the use of Euler’s formula, tangent half-angle identities, and Euler substitutions (as shown in section 11)—into a single framework.

10
Simplification of Reciprocal Differences via the Binomial–Difference Formula

In USM applications, one may encounter expressions such as

t^{n}-\frac{1}{t^{n}},

where the substitution

t=y-\sqrt{y^{2}-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{t}=y+\sqrt{y^{2}-1}

is made. It is natural then to set

v=y,\quad w=\sqrt{y^{2}-1},

so that

t=v-w\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{t}=v+w.

Thus,

t^{n}-\frac{1}{t^{n}}=(v-w)^{n}-(v+w)^{n}.

10.1
Derivation of the Binomial–Difference Formula

Using the binomial expansion we have

(v+w)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}v^{n-k}w^{k},

(v-w)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}v^{n-k}(-w)^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}v^{n-k}(-1)^{k}w^{k}.

Subtracting these yields

\displaystyle(v+w)^{n}-(v-w)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}v^{n-k}w^{k}\left[1-(-1)^{k}\right]\,.

Notice that for even
k
the factor
1-(-1)^{k}=0
, and for odd
k
it equals 2. Letting
k=2j+1
(with
j=0,\dots,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
) we obtain

(v+w)^{n}-(v-w)^{n}=2\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2j+1}v^{\,n-2j-1}w^{\,2j+1}\,.

Since

(v-w)^{n}-(v+w)^{n}=-\left[(v+w)^{n}-(v-w)^{n}\right],

it follows that

(v-w)^{n}-(v+w)^{n}=-2\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2j+1}v^{\,n-2j-1}w^{\,2j+1}\,.

Thus, with
v=y
and
w=\sqrt{y^{2}-1}
,

t^{n}-\frac{1}{t^{n}}=(v-w)^{n}-(v+w)^{n}=-2\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2j+1}\,y^{\,n-2j-1}\,\Bigl(\sqrt{y^{2}-1}\Bigr)^{2j+1}\,.

10.2
Example 10

Suppose that after applying the USM to an integral (for example,

I=\int x\sqrt{x^{2}-x}\,dx),

one obtains an antiderivative in the transformed variable
t
of the form

I=-\frac{1}{64}\left\{\frac{1}{3}\left[t^{3}-\frac{1}{t^{3}}\right]+\left[t^{2}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right]-\left[t-\frac{1}{t}\right]-4\ln|t|\right\}+C.

Now, applying the formulas above with
n=1,2,3
:

- For
n=1
:

t-\frac{1}{t}=-2\,\sqrt{y^{2}-1}\,.

- For
n=2
:

t^{2}-\frac{1}{t^{2}}=-4y\,\sqrt{y^{2}-1}.

- For
n=3
:

t^{3}-\frac{1}{t^{3}}=-2\Bigl(4y^{2}-1\Bigr)\sqrt{y^{2}-1}\,.

Substituting these compact forms into the antiderivative yields

I=-\frac{1}{64}\left\{-\frac{2}{3}\left(4y^{2}-1\right)\sqrt{y^{2}-1}-4y\,\sqrt{y^{2}-1}+2\sqrt{y^{2}-1}-4\ln|t|\right\}+C.

Factoring out

from the first three terms we have

Euler Substitutions vs. the USM

Euler substitutions (see [8-9]) for integrals involving

\sqrt{\,a^{\prime}x^{2}+b^{\prime}x+c^{\prime}\,}

include cases of which the first and second are:

First Euler Substitution (
a^{\prime}>0
):

\sqrt{a^{\prime}x^{2}+b^{\prime}x+c^{\prime}}=\pm\sqrt{a^{\prime}}+t,\quad\text{then solve for }x.

Second Euler Substitution (
c^{\prime}>0
):

\sqrt{a^{\prime}x^{2}+b^{\prime}x+c^{\prime}}=x\,t\;\pm\;\sqrt{c^{\prime}},\quad\text{then solve for }x.

Below, we briefly see how these classical forms re-derive Examples 1, 3, and 7, exactly mirroring our unified transformations but via separate case-specific formulas.

11.1
Example 1 (
\int\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\,dx
)

First Euler Substitution: With
a^{\prime}=1,b^{\prime}=0,c^{\prime}=-1
, we set
\sqrt{x^{2}-1}=-\,x+t
. Solving gives

x=\tfrac{t^{2}+1}{2t},
and a rational integrand in
t
.
A short integration and back-substitution recover the familiar result

\frac{x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,\ln\left|\,x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\,\right|+C,

matching
Example 1’s final USM answer.

11.2
Example 3
\left(\int\dfrac{1}{x\sqrt{x^{2}+x}}\,dx\right)

First Euler Substitution: With
a^{\prime}=1,b^{\prime}=1,c^{\prime}=0
, we write
\sqrt{x^{2}+x}=x+t
. Solving for
x
again yields a purely rational expression in terms of
t
. After integration, back-substitution gives

-\frac{2\sqrt{x^{2}+x}}{x}+C,

which differs from the expression in USM by only a constant.

11.3
Example 7
\left(\int\dfrac{dx}{x^{3}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}\right)

Second Euler Substitution: Since
\sqrt{4-x^{2}}=\sqrt{\,c^{\prime}+a^{\prime}x^{2}\,}
with
c^{\prime}=4>0
and
a^{\prime}=-1
, we set

\sqrt{4-x^{2}}=xt+2.

One obtains
x
in terms of
t
and a rational integrand. The final back-substitution yields

-\frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{8x^{2}}-\frac{1}{16}\ln\left|\frac{2+\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{x}\right|+C,

which coincides (up to constants) with the USM result from Example 7.

11.4
Observations and Differences

As shown:

First Euler Substitution reproduces (up to sign choices and algebraic manipulation) the transformations needed for Examples 1 and 3, which USM handles via Transformation
(4)
in a single, unified manner.

Second Euler Substitution accomplishes Example 7 in a fashion parallel to Transformation
(7)
of the USM.

Thus, Euler’s first and second substitutions naturally appear
in disguise
within USM’s framework. The
main difference
is that each Euler substitution is a
case-specific recipe
(one for
\sqrt{x^{2}\pm\ldots}
, another for
\sqrt{\ldots-x^{2}}
, etc.), while USM provides a
single, coherent method
that systematically handles inverse trig expressions, sign/branch issues, and radicals of the form

\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}
,

\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}
,

\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}
and more. Consequently,
all
cases are unified under a consistent approach—rather than memorizing separate formulas for each scenario—yet the classic Euler substitutions are plainly embedded as special cases in the USM transformations.

Admittedly, the USM also has several labeled transformations—e.g.
(3)
,
(4)
,
(5)
,
(6)
, and
(7)
—which might at first glance look like the same “case-by-case” approach as classical Euler substitutions. However, the key distinction is that
all these transformations in the USM stem from one overarching principle
(namely, rewriting
x+b
in terms of exponentials
e^{\pm i\,\alpha}
or hyperbolic functions
e^{\theta}
, combined with systematic sign/domain handling). The separate “formulas” merely highlight
subfamilies
of that principle, accommodating
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}-a^{2}}
,
\sqrt{a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}}
, or
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}
.

By contrast, Euler substitutions are typically taught as three
fundamentally different
“recipes” which one must memorize independently (one for
\sqrt{a^{\prime}x^{2}+\ldots}
if
a^{\prime}>0
, another if
c^{\prime}>0
, etc.). In practice, a student (or software) must recognize which Euler substitution to apply and confirm it satisfies the domain constraints.

The USM’s advantage, then, lies in
conceptual unification
: each step follows the same exponential (or hyperbolic) pattern, so the sign-choices and domain intervals are all systematically encoded in
\pm i\alpha
or
\theta
. Thus, once you internalize
one
central approach, it covers not only the classical
\sqrt{x^{2}\pm\cdots}
forms but also inverse trig expressions (
\sec^{-1},\csc^{-1},\sin^{-1},\ldots
), hyperbolic radicals (
\sqrt{(x+b)^{2}+a^{2}}
), and more. In short, while
USM does present multiple formulas, they are all manifestations of one unified substitution framework
—rather than separate memorized techniques.

12
Conclusion

The Unified Substitution Method (USM) represents a significant advancement in
the approach to solving integrals involving inverse trigonometric functions,
radicals, and related expressions. By synthesizing trigonometric, hyperbolic,
and complex exponential substitutions into a single cohesive framework, USM
offers several distinct advantages:

Systematic and Unified Approach.

USM consolidates various classical substitution techniques into one algorithmic
procedure. This unification eliminates the need to memorize numerous case-specific
substitutions (as happens with multiple Euler substitutions) and reduces the
cognitive load of selecting the appropriate method for each integral. The consistent
handling of domain restrictions and branch choices further enhances its robustness
and reliability.

Simplification to Rational or “Polynomish” Forms.

A key strength of USM is its ability to convert complex integrals into rational
functions or polynomial-like expressions of a new variable. For instance, certain
integrals of the form

\frac{1}{\,Q(x)\,\sqrt{\,a^{2}-(x+b)^{2}\,}}

are efficiently handled by
Transformation (7)
, which systematically
reduces them to simpler forms requiring only basic power-rule or logarithmic
integrations. Likewise, as shown in Example 4, expressions that typically call
for elaborate algebraic manipulation can become direct polynomial integrals
under the USM.

Broad Applicability and Efficiency.

In Section 9, we saw that classical Euler substitutions are actually embedded as
special cases of USM—Transformations (4) and (7) coincide with the first and
second Euler substitutions, respectively. Yet USM extends seamlessly to a wider
array of functions (including inverse trigonometric forms and radicals involving

x+b\pm a
) under a single method, rather than requiring multiple separately
memorized “recipes.”

Educational and Theoretical Value.

By emphasizing how complex exponentials and hyperbolic functions unify
trigonometric- and radical-type integrals, USM offers an insightful alternative
to memorizing long lists of standard substitutions. It clarifies sign considerations
and domain choices within one overarching technique. This not only aids
students’ conceptual understanding but also simplifies implementation in
computer algebra systems.

Although the USM may entail more intricate back‐substitutions than traditional trigonometric or hyperbolic substitutions, its complexity parallels that of Euler substitutions—an established classical technique (see [8]). This shared challenge, already normalized in historical pedagogy, is offset by the USM’s unifying framework, which systematically resolves diverse integrals under a single coherent strategy. Moreover, most integrals amenable to trigonometric or hyperbolic substitution can be tackled by Transformations (4), (5), or (7), which demand far less memorization than the extensive catalog of standard derivative/integral formulas, reduction formulas, identities, and substitution rules required for fluency in classical methods. In addition, the use of the binomial–difference formula to simplify differences of the form
t^{n}-\frac{1}{t^{n}}
further streamlines the process of obtaining compact antiderivatives, reinforcing the elegance and efficiency of the USM.

In summary
, the Unified Substitution Method offers a powerful, coherent,
and versatile tool for both education and research in integral calculus. By
encompassing Euler-style substitutions, half-angle identities, and hyperbolic
reparameterizations into one unified framework, it significantly streamlines
the integration of many functions that are otherwise approached by a patchwork
of specialized substitutions.
