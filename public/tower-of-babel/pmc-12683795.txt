Evaluating potential determinants of health system implementation of a virtual oncology financial and legal navigation intervention to address cancer-related financial toxicity
Authors: Jean Edward, Haafsah Fariduddin, Mackenzie Caldwell, Lori Eisele, Joanna Doran, Monica Bryant, John D’Orazio, Anne E. Ray, Kimberly Northrip
Published: 2025

Background

Facing rising costs of cancer care, oncology patients and caregivers are at an increased risk of experiencing financial toxicity [1]. Defined as the financial burden and stress caused by the excessive costs of cancer treatment, financial toxicity may be exacerbated by financial and socio-legal barriers, such as unemployment and income loss, unreliable and hazardous housing, and lack of suitable health insurance [2–6]. Oncology financial and legal navigation (OFLN) has emerged as a promising intervention to address financial toxicity through a multitude of ways (e.g., improving financial health literacy, helping optimize health insurance, providing assistance with out-of-pocket expenses, processing applications for disability or family medical leave) [7].

Our team has previously designed and implemented several in-person OFLN interventions that improve upon prior navigation programs. Our in-person financial navigation intervention, CC Links (Coverage and Cost-of-Care Links) [6] showed significant improvements in financial toxicity among hematologic cancer patients and caregivers, but was limited in legal navigation services. As a result, we designed and implemented FINassist (Financial and Insurance Navigation Assistance) [2], an interdisciplinary OFLN intervention which shows that place-based lawyers (via Medical Legal Partnerships) in collaboration with financial navigators and social workers can comprehensively help address financial and legal needs of cancer patients and caregivers. While CC Links and FINassist interventions demonstrated improvements in patient and caregiver outcomes, similar to previous studies [7, 8], the in-person delivery limited reach and accessibility of services. Few OFLN programs built on virtual delivery platforms currently exist and are needed to comprehensively address the pervasive impact of financial toxicity across the continuum of cancer care [8]. This includes access to self-directed resources such a financial coaching, health insurance literacy education, and other resources within and outside the health system [9].

To address these gaps, we partnered with Triage Cancer®, a national non-profit organization that provides free education and navigation services to help address financial and legal needs for cancer patients, caregivers, and healthcare staff [10]. Built upon our previous in-person OFLN interventions [2, 6], we partnered with Triage Cancer to adapt their educational resources to develop a virtual OFLN intervention that can be seamlessly integrated into our health system’s cancer care delivery workflows. The intervention consists of customized virtual navigation by trained financial and legal navigators from Triage Cancer®; this is supplemented by access to Triage Cancer’s® educational resources (such as webinars, animated videos, quick guides, checklists, etc.), agency contacts, and referrals that provide self-directed navigation resources. In leveraging a virtual platform we aim to extend the reach of OFLN services in hard-to-reach patient and caregiver populations across the cancer care continuum.

Prior to implementation of our proposed virtual OFLN intervention, it was necessary to assess the feasibility of integrating the program within existing workflows of our planned implementation site. Current implementation approaches for health system integration of OFLN services have varied across programs [11, 12]. Recent studies have demonstrated successful health system integration guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which provides a systematic approach to intervention design and implementation [13–15]. A vital component to the implementation process, formative evaluations gather feedback from key stakeholders in the planned setting via interviews, focus groups, or surveys [16]. This structured assessment is completed in order to aid in adoption, impact, and sustainability of the intervention [17]. In this study we conducted a structured formative evaluation guided by CFIR to assess facilitators and barriers to implementing a proposed virtual OFLN intervention within a pediatric and adolescent and young adult (AYA) oncology clinic. This helps us assess contextual factors of the intervention setting that may influence implementation of our proposed virtual OFLN intervention and inform necessary modifications to the implementation strategies, pathways and workflows.

Methods

Study design and conceptual framework

This study was phase 1 (see Fig. 1) of a larger program of research testing the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary efficacy of implementing an adapted virtual OFLN intervention (described above) within a pediatric and AYA oncology clinic in a large academic health system, in partnership with Triage Cancer® [10]. We used a qualitative approach guided by the CFIR [18] to assess facilitators and barriers to implementing the virtual OFLN intervention. Setting and sample

The setting for this study and the testing of the proposed intervention is a pediatric and AYA oncology clinic affiliated with a National Cancer Institute–Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center. The clinic is embedded in a large academic health system and serves a substantial rural population, providing care to approximately 250 pediatric and AYA oncology patient visits per month. The healthcare team structure within the clinic consists of oncologists, bedside nurses, nurse navigators, social workers, and a financial navigator among other supportive staff. The social work team provides direct psychosocial support (i.e. food, travel coordination, support groups, educational support) and referrals to financial and legal navigation services. The clinic has one financial navigator who assists patients and caregivers with managing costs of care (i.e. understanding health insurance, connecting to funding sources, and assisting with financial assistance applications). The clinic also has limited access to legal navigation services via a lawyer embedded in the larger academic health system. Centralized financial assistance services (specific to billing and insurance) are also provided by the larger health system.

We used purposive sampling methods to recruit a representative sample of clinic team members and patients and providers who receive services from the clinic. Inclusion criteria included: 18 years or older, English-speaking, patient, caregiver, or healthcare team member affiliated with the clinic where the proposed intervention will be implemented. We recruited participants via email, wherein we explained the purpose of the study and scheduled interviews. Participants gave verbal informed consent before the interview. The study was approved by the University of Kentucky’s Institutional Review Board (#98924).

Data collection and analysis

Interviews were conducted in person or over Zoom, lasted 60 min or less, were audio recorded, and then transcribed for analysis using NVivo, qualitative data management software. Interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide to assess financial and legal needs of patients/caregivers served in clinic, availability of OFLN resources, and to gather feedback on the proposed intervention (see Appendix).

Interview data was coded independently by two members of the research team (HF and LE) to identify and categorize recurrent themes using an inductive process. Coders sought feedback from members of the larger research team to discuss and resolve any discrepancies in their analyses and come to a consensus on emerging themes. Following thematic coding, a collaborative and iterative process was used to recode and categorize themes as implementation facilitators and barriers under the five CFIR domains: under the five CFIR domains: Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process [19].

Results

Eighteen semi-structured interviews were conducted with 9 clinic nurses, 2 nurse navigators, 1 nurse manager, 1 social worker, 1 financial navigator, 1 patient in active treatment, and 2 caregivers of patients in active treatment. We also interviewed 1 lawyer embedded in the larger health system. These participants provided a range of perspectives on current workflows and implementation considerations for the proposed virtual OFLN intervention (see Table 1 and Fig. 2). Innovation domain

In the context of our study, the innovation domain refers to the proposed virtual OFLN intervention in partnership with Triage Cancer®. Characteristics of the proposed intervention that were identified by participants as having potential to influence successful implementation pertained to its relative advantage, adaptability, and design.

Facilitators: virtual design and accessibility of centralized resources

Participants described existing financial assistance resources provided as standard of care within the larger health system as being difficult to access, unstructured and or insufficient in legal assistance. Participants also highlighted the ability of the proposed OFLN intervention to provide patients and caregivers structured, comprehensive, and individualized financial and legal navigation resources. Ongoing access to updated, self-directed educational resources on Triage Cancer’s® website was identified by participants as an effective way to empower patients and caregivers with resources to understand and advocate for their needs.

Participants stated the virtual design and delivery of the OFLN intervention was recognized as an implementation facilitator as it enabled adaptability of the program to patient and/or caregiver needs. Having access to essential resources outside the health system was seen by those interviewed as contributing to the convenience of implementing this OFLN program. Additionally, virtual access was identified by participants as an efficient way to streamline communication and support, in turn reducing the need for unnecessary travel to the clinic and ensuring timely assistance.

Barriers: limited access to broadband internet access

Participants identified patients and caregivers, particularly those residing in rural regions, as facing financial or infrastructural barriers that may prevent them from accessing reliable high-speed internet or appropriate technology to participate in the OFLN intervention.

Outer setting domain

In our study, the outer setting refers to the larger health system in which our proposed study setting (i.e. oncology clinic) is embedded. Potential implementation facilitators and barriers related to partnerships & connections, external pressure, and local attitudes were discussed by participants.

Facilitators: strong partnerships and high demand for OFLN services

Access to evidence-based resources through Triage Cancer® was identified by participants as an implementation facilitator. Several interview participants working within the clinic were familiar with and had previously accessed Triage Cancer’s® educational resources or training sessions vouched for the range and comprehensiveness of their financial and legal services, resources, and educational aids. Participants explained that the implementation of the proposed OFLN program would strengthen the clinic’s existing partnership with Triage Cancer® by changing what had been sporadic use of their resources into a structured and more collaborative partnership with consistent coordination between the two entities.

The clinic’s patient and caregiver population were described by participants as experiencing high levels of financial toxicity, subsequently resulting in a high demand for OFLN services. Patients and caregivers interviewed also described challenges with understanding their health insurance and managing costs of care, both serving as barriers to timely treatment.

Barrier: mistrust in health systems

Participants stated the lack of trust between patients/caregivers and health systems stemming from barriers to treatment accessibility and affordability are a potential implementation barrier. Those interviewed also detailed how patients often avoid returning for care after negative experiences, and this perception of an unreliable health system may cause hesitation to engage with our proposed program.

Inner setting domain

In the context of this study, the inner setting refers to the proposed study setting or the clinic where we are planning to implement the OFLN intervention. Participants identified potential implementation facilitators and barriers related to structural characteristics, relational connections, mission alignment, communications and compatibility of the inner setting.

Facilitators: staff capacity and interdisciplinary teamwork

The lack of capacity among clinic staff to address the high demand for OFLN was identified by participants as an implementation facilitator. Voiced by many participants was how the clinic’s social workers and financial navigator often get overwhelmed by requests and have limited knowledge, skills and time to address more nuanced financial and legal issues. Participants viewed the interdisciplinary, collaborative and patient-centered culture of the clinic environment serves as an implementation facilitator. It was also agreed by many participants that the goals of our proposed OFLN intervention align with the clinic’s mission and values to provide holistic care to patients and caregivers, which will support the integration of our intervention into existing workflows.

Barriers: limitations in nurse availability and delineation of related roles and responsibilities

As our proposed intervention relies on nurse referrals, participants pointed out this may serve as an implementation barrier during periods of high patient volume at the clinic as limited nurse availability could impact recruitment as they would not have adequate time to explain the intervention and consent patients for participation.

An additional barrier to implementation pointed out by participants were the clearly defined roles and responsibilities among members of the clinic staff. Nurses, social workers, and the financial navigator were described by those interviewed as focusing on their discipline-defined roles and tasks. Participants explained that this structured approach may make it challenging to integrate our proposed intervention if those outside of the psychosocial team do not feel it is their responsibility to identify patients/caregivers with financial and/or legal needs and refer them to navigation resources.

Individuals domain

The individuals domain refers to the roles and characteristics of the clinic nurses, social workers, financial navigator, nurse navigators, and patients/caregivers receiving services at the clinic. Participants described potential implementation facilitators and barriers related to innovation deliverers and innovation recipients.

Facilitators: nurses’ established rapport with patients and caregivers and high need for OFLN resources

Participants expressed that the nurses within the clinic were ideal innovation deliverers because they often spend the most time with patients/caregivers and have established trust with them. Their dedication to patient care was described by many interviewed as extending beyond their regular duties to foster personal connections, which may facilitate recruitment.

The high need for financial and legal navigation resources among patients/caregivers receiving care in the clinic was identified by participants as a pressing concern. Several of those interviewed emphasized how the complexities of accessing timely navigation services within the constraints of the healthcare system may further compound this issue.

Barriers: lack of knowledge and skills to engage in cost of care conversations

Clinic nurses were identified by participants as not being comfortable with engaging in cost of care conversations with patients/caregivers due to a lack of training on the relevant topics. Several participants further explained that this may prevent nurses from proactively identifying financial/legal issues that could be addressed through the proposed OFLN intervention, consequently impacting recruitment.

Implementation process domain

The implementation process domain refers to the activities and strategies that will be used to implement the proposed OFLN intervention. Potential facilitators and barriers to implementation fall under tailoring strategies, assessing needs and engaging.

Facilitators: recruitment, consenting and data collection completed via iPads with the help of nurses

Using iPads for recruiting, consenting, and collecting surveys was identified by participants as a streamlined approach to facilitating patient/caregiver engagement with the proposed intervention. The use of iPads was recognized by participants as a way to facilitate integration into existing clinic workflows and reduce administrative burden.

Participants highlighted that engaging nurses as the first point of contact and recruiters for our proposed intervention also allows for smooth integration of our intervention into existing patient workflows without the need for additional personnel. Additionally, it was detailed by participants that the frequency and length of interactions between nurses and patients/caregivers, especially during chemotherapy infusion visits, would further support recruitment.

Barrier: attrition due to virtual nature of proposed intervention

Participants expressed that the virtual nature of the proposed intervention could pose challenges to maintaining continuity of care. Scheduling, tracking, and ensuring consistent follow-up among patients/caregivers receiving OFLN services were all highlighted by participants as possible challenges if not captured in person. An additional aspect of this barrier as expanded upon by those interviewed lies in the varying lengths of time between recruitment, intervention delivery, and follow-up that may hinder participant engagement.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to assess contextual factors that may influence implementation of our proposed virtual OFLN intervention within a pediatric and AYA oncology clinic. We identified ten potential implementation facilitators related to high demand and need for OFLN services, adaptability of virtual resources, and efficiency of nurse-led navigation. In addition, six potential implementation barriers were identified related to nurses’ lack of knowledge and skills to engage in cost of care conversations and limitations in nurse availability and delineation of related roles and responsibilities. These findings were used to inform necessary modifications to the proposed implementation strategies and workflows to further adapt our proposed OFLN intervention and implementation processes.

Study findings indicate that the current infrastructure of the clinic is overwhelmed by the high demand for OFLN services. With the rising costs of cancer care and subsequent influx of resources needed to address financial toxicity, health systems are increasingly experiencing workload burden and understaffing issues [2, 20, 21]. Health systems also face financial burdens that preclude hiring of in-house financial and legal navigators. As identified by participants in our study, reputable third-party navigation resources, like Triage Cancer®, could help reduce burden on the healthcare team while ensuring a greater number of patients’ needs are met. Other studies have demonstrated that successful integration of third-party navigation services (i.e. Consumer Education and Training Services, Patient Advocate Foundation, and TailorMed) could help overcome system-level implementation barriers [22–24].

Rural residing patients and caregivers make up a large proportion of the clinic’s population and face barriers to care related to access, income, education and health insurance literacy [25–29]. Underserved, rural patient populations face a rising demand for health literacy resources and financial navigation programs, yet have limited availability of tailored OFLN programs [25, 30, 31]. The virtual aspect of our proposed virtual OFLN intervention can therefore benefit rural residing patients by overcoming transportation and access barriers to provide individualized coaching sessions and resources, by phone and online. Virtual programs have shown to be successful when implemented in rural settings and may be beneficial in other populations that have limited engagement with health systems [23, 24, 32]. However, reliable access to the internet for rural populations continues to be a barrier to receiving virtual interventions [33]; further policy and advocacy efforts are needed to ensure access to reliable, high-speed broadband access within these regions. Nonetheless, our findings show that the proposed implementation clinic has limited navigation services to meet growing financial and legal needs of patient/caregiver. The proposed intervention delivers OFLN services that can help meet this increased demand and improve accessibility for patients residing in rural regions.

The provision of legal navigation assistance and resources in our proposed intervention addresses a significant gap in existing financial navigation programs. Roughly 75% of cancer patients experience some type of legal need related to housing, educational accommodations, employment, disability rights, and income supports [34, 35]. Even in health systems with robust financial navigation services, unmet legal needs remain unaddressed due to a lack of patient, caregiver, and provider knowledge around legal barriers to cancer care. Addressing socio-legal needs at the point of care can help remove barriers to healthcare and increase access to preventive care and social supports [2, 36–39]. Our proposed intervention utilizes legal navigation to target this gap by assisting with legal needs such as estate planning, Medicaid appeals, and other concerns of cancer patients and caregivers.

Strong nurse-patient rapport and trust were identified as factors to help increase intervention recruitment. This relationship could also make patients comfortable with sharing sensitive information related to their financial and legal needs, especially in populations experiencing stigma related to discussing these issues with their healthcare team. Evidence indicates that nurse-led interventions can be successful in addressing sociological needs of patients and caregivers [31, 40]. However, there is a lack of nurse-led OFLN programs as described in our proposed intervention [41]. To lead such interventions, nurses need training and coaching on initiating and facilitating cost of care conversations, understanding costs and underlying legal needs associated with cancer treatment, and familiarity with relevant referral and informational resources [40, 42].

Strategies for addressing barriers to implementation

We identified several barriers to the implementation of the OFLN intervention centered around limitations in access to internet and technology, nursing staff training, and participant tracking and follow-up. In the following section, we applied CFIR-ERIC (Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change) implementation strategy matching tool to select implementation strategies to address identified implementation barriers. These proposed methods aim to strengthen the intervention by developing strategies within each barrier’s domain.

Strategy 1: provide in-person services and technological assistance

To address technologic barriers, we will focus on leveraging existing resources within the clinic and patient/caregiver time spent in clinic to engage in intervention activities. Nurse training and coaching to guide patients and caregivers through the intervention will ease communication and potential technologic challenges related to accessing Triage Cancer® resources outside of the clinic. Access to free high-speed internet and computers are offered to all patients and caregivers at the clinic. We will offer iPads to participants to complete surveys, schedule or conduct meetings with Triage Cancer® staff, and access resources on Triage Cancer’s® website. These iPads will also be used to complete study surveys during clinic visits with the assistance of healthcare and research team members to help address any technological limitations. We will also offer a paper option to all intervention participants to complete consents and surveys.

Strategy 2: support interdisciplinary communication and create tracking and follow-up systems

To ensure seamless interdisciplinary communication and workflows, we will ensure that Triage Cancer® navigators, nurses, social workers, and other members of the healthcare team are communicating via electronic health records (i.e. Epic), Microsoft Teams, and other project management software (i.e. Wrike). Timely, consistent communications among the interdisciplinary implementation team will encourage collaboration, improve workflow efficiencies and alleviate limited nurse availability around recruitment, data collection and referrals. We will also create a feedback loop using a database on Microsoft teams where healthcare and research team members can track the status of patients and caregivers enrolled in the study and to follow-up on referrals. This open line of communication will promote collaboration between each network while defining roles and responsibilities within the intervention deliverers.

We will also develop efficient tracking and follow-up systems for the implementation team to ensure intervention participant retention. We will track individuals to ensure timely completion of the intervention and follow up surveys and provide consistent reminders via email and phone calls. If participants have not scheduled their calls with Triage Cancer® or missed their appointment, the intervention team will employ a sequence of emails and phone calls to ensure the participant gets (re)scheduled. In addition, nurses will provide in-person reminders during clinic visits for participants that are unable to be reached. The incorporation of an organized system for reminders conducted by the interdisciplinary intervention team and nurses who serve as intervention champions will help encourage participation and retention.

Strategy 3: provide educational training and tools

To help address limitations in nurse knowledge and confidence around engaging in OFLN, we will provide ongoing access to training and resources to develop active engagement of nurses in the implementation process. In partnership with Triage Cancer®, we developed and evaluated a hybrid (self-directed online and in person) training for nurses related to OFLN that nurses participated in prior to intervention implementation [43]. Nurses will also have access to recruitment brochures with a list of common legal and financial concerns that can help nurses identify eligible participants. Lastly, we will also develop a semi-structured script with prompts that nurses can use during the recruitment process.

Strengths and limitations

Our study demonstrates the feasibility of applying the CFIR framework to identify facilitators and barriers to implementing OFLN interventions designed to address cancer-related financial toxicity. By using CFIR to conduct a formative evaluation, we were able to identify and address barriers to implementation a priori. We used CFIR to guide data analysis and employed rigorous qualitative data management and analysis methods with the use of two independent coders. Although all members of the clinical team that would be involved in the implementation process were interviewed, our sample size was relatively small and was limited in patient and caregiver representation. The experiences of participants in this study are unique to the study setting/clinic and themes may not be generalizable to other clinics.

Conclusions

In this study, the application of CFIR helped us to identify facilitators and barriers to implementing a virtual OFLN within our health system, which gave us the tools to make crucial modifications to the intervention strategies and workflows. As a result of this formative evaluation, we were able to successfully adapt and implement our OFLN in our pediatric and AYA oncology clinic, serving a total of 34 patients and 84 caregivers (findings to be published). While findings may not be transferable to other settings, certain key themes related to workflow, workload, and other health system pathways may be applicable to support future implementation of OFLN interventions.
