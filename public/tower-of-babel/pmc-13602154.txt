Rod + cone signal interference impairs mesopic motion discriminability in a model circuit
Authors: Adree Songco-Aguas, Fred Rieke, Gabrielle J. Gutierrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Under mesopic conditions, such as dawn or dusk, signals from both rod and cone photoreceptors contribute to perception. These parallel inputs are combined within the retina before being sent to subsequent visual areas. The integration of these kinetically distinct parallel signals poses unique challenges for human vision. Though previous behavioral studies have found that dim lighting conditions specifically impair motion perception in human subjects, the origin of this dependence is unclear. In the present study, we created a model circuit that predicts ganglion cell responses to moving stimuli by incorporating electrophysiologically derived circuit components into a Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator, a classical motion-detection model. The model circuit demonstrates that interactions between rod- and cone-derived signals negatively impact the encoding of the direction of a moving object under mesopic conditions. Furthermore, we found that motion discriminability improved if the model circuit was only sensitive to the cone-activating components of the stimuli. We conclude that rod+cone signal interference occurring at the lowest level of vision has an impact on motion direction discrimination, a higher-level task with relevance for behavior.

Introduction

In the transition between dim and bright lighting, known as mesopic lighting conditions, rods and cones are simultaneously activated. Mesopic conditions pose a unique challenge to human vision. Because mesopic vision relies on parallel streams of visual input derived from both rod and cone photoreceptors, the retinal circuitry must balance rod-mediated signals that are approaching saturation with cone-mediated signals that are barely emergent (reviewed by Buck, 2004; Buck, 2014; Grimes, Songco-Aguas, & Rieke, 2018; Stockman & Sharpe, 2006; Raphael & MacLeod, 2011). Post-photoreceptor circuitry processes the rod and cone inputs in parallel before they are combined to shape retinal outputs (Enroth-Cugell, Hertz, & Lennie, 1977; Gouras & Link, 1966). Consequently, it is not surprising that deficits in mesopic vision are a first symptom in many visual diseases (Arden & Hogg, 1985; Petzold & Plant, 2006).

Mesopic vision provides a rare opportunity to examine how parallel processing (Grimes et al., 2018; Kolb & Nelson, 1995), a common computational schema in neural circuits, influences human perception. In previous work, electrophysiological recordings were used to develop a model that predicts retinal ganglion cell responses to rod and cone inputs (Grimes, Graves, Summers, & Rieke, 2015; Grimes et al., 2018; Songco-Aguas, Grimes, & Rieke, 2023). The rod+cone retinal model captures two features of how parallel rod and cone pathways respond to contrived time-varying stimuli such as Gaussian noise and sinusoidal waves: (a) there are distinct kinetics between rod- and cone-derived signals, and (b) these parallel signals are combined prior to a shared rectifying or thresholding nonlinearity. These features are consistent with electrophysiological and human psychophysical data showing an unexpected destructive interference of rod- and cone-mediated signals (Grimes et al., 2015; MacLeod, 1972; reviewed by Stockman & Sharpe, 2006).

Models based on physiology are a useful tool for uncovering new mechanistic connections between parallel signal processing and visual perception. Here, we focus specifically on motion. Though several human behavioral studies have found that lighting conditions affect the accurate perception of motion (Billino, Bremmer, & Gegenfurtner, 2008; Grossman & Blake, 1999; Mayeur, Brémond, & Bastien, 2008; Sepulveda, Anderson, Wood, & McKendrick, 2021; Yoshimoto & Takeuchi, 2013; Yoshimoto, Okajima, & Takeuchi, 2016), the origin of this dependence is unclear. In all of these cases, the impairments primarily affect the perception of translational motion in the periphery. Compared to photopic or scotopic conditions, human observers had particular difficulty in accurately perceiving motion direction for stimuli that mimicked a walking human figure under mesopic conditions (Billino et al., 2008). Mesopic conditions also uniquely eliminated motion priming in which the perceived direction of motion of an ambiguous stimulus depends on a prior priming motion stimulus (Yoshimoto & Takeuchi, 2013; Yoshimoto et al., 2016). Here, we tested the hypothesis that differences in the kinetics of rod- and cone-derived responses in mesopic conditions produce ambiguities in motion discrimination that may contribute to these and related perceptual phenomena (Songco-Aguas et al., 2023).

There are several classical model circuits for motion detection (Adelson & Movshon, 1982; Barlow & Levick, 1965; reviewed in Borst & Egelhaaf, 1989). Fundamentally, these models compute motion direction based on the correlated spatial and temporal changes in brightness that occur when a moving object passes in front of an array of photoreceptors. The Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator consists of two input channels that represent neighboring simulated photoreceptors or groups of photoreceptors. The circuit correlates the intensity of brightness between the two input channels after the output of one is passed through a temporal low-pass filter that delays its signals relative to the other (Borst & Euler, 2011; Hassenstein & Reichardt, 1956; Reichardt, 1961). It is a relatively simple model that captures the spatiotemporal correlations underlying motion; although it was originally derived from insect optomotor behavior, it is general and abstract enough to model visual motion detection in other species (Borst & Egelhaaf, 1989; Borst & Helmstaedter, 2015; Frechette et al., 2005).

In the context of our study, the Hassenstein–Reichardt model is simply a readout of perceived motion direction of a moving stimulus given rod+cone interactions. By incorporating the rod+cone model circuit into a Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator, we can examine the impact of kinetic differences between rod- and cone-derived signals on motion processing in mesopic conditions. Specifically, we can examine how parallel rod and cone signal processing influences the ability of the retinal circuit to accurately ascertain the direction of a moving stimulus. Rod-derived signals are slower and have a stronger attenuation of low temporal frequencies than cone-derived signals (Grimes et al., 2018). Here, we demonstrated the ability of the model to discriminate motion directions across a range of motion speeds for different circuit architectures: (a) a circuit that simultaneously processes both rod- and cone-mediated motion stimuli, and (b) circuits that are exclusively responsive to either rod or cone input. We hypothesized that the perceptual deficits observed in mesopic conditions are a consequence of signal interference between rod and cone mediated signals in specific time scales. By comparing the performance differences across these circuits, we can gain insight into how the physiologically derived model retinal circuit degrades motion discrimination ability and potentially contributes to the decreased perceptual acuity observed in human subjects. By testing motion stimuli across a range of motion speeds, we examined how the known differences in signaling kinetics between rods and cones influence motion processing. Finally, we examined how the salience of the motion stimulus impacts motion direction discriminability. Altogether, these findings expand our understanding of how parallel rod+cone processes underlie the perception of stimuli that can affect human behavior.

Methods

Retinal model

The rod+cone retinal model was derived from electrophysiological recordings conducted on whole-mount preparations of isolated non-human primate retina, as previously described (Dunn, Lankheet, & Rieke, 2007; Trong & Rieke, 2008). These recordings were taken from ON parasol ganglion cells. The circuit components of the rod+cone linear–nonlinear model were derived from voltage-clamp recordings of full-field white noise (0–40 Hz bandwidth) using blue light-emitting diodes (LEDs; peak power at 460 nm) and red LEDs (peak power at 640 nm) to isolate rod and L-cone responses, respectively, as previously described (Chichilnisky, 2001; Grimes et al., 2015; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). Circuit components of the model were verified by taking the explained variance of the model from data with the same noise stimuli presented.

Filtered rod and cone signals were then summed. The summed signal was passed into a shared nonlinearity that was fit from the ganglion cell responses with a third-degree polynomial function (Figure 1B, middle panel); the output of this nonlinearity is an estimate of the excitatory synaptic input of a cell. The shape of this nonlinearity was found to be consistent between the rod-targeted and cone-targeted electrophysiological recordings, supporting the interpretation that rod and cone signals are integrated prior to this nonlinear stage, such as in the cone bipolar output synapse (Fain & Sampath, 2018; Gouras & Link, 1966; Grimes et al., 2015; Grimes et al., 2018). Indeed, this model architecture captures temporal interactions between flashed stimuli that selectively activate rods or cones (Grimes et al., 2015). Models lacking a shared nonlinear component could not capture these interactions.

Instantaneous currents below 0 amps do not result in spiking, but above that threshold the spike rate linearly increases with increasing current.

To define the shared nonlinearity between rod and cone components of the linear–nonlinear model for a single cell (Dunn, Doan, Sampath, & Rieke, 2006; Schwartz et al., 2012), the horizontal axis of the measured cone nonlinearity was scaled relative to the measured rod nonlinearity until they overlapped, and then the cone filter was scaled accordingly, as previously described (Songco-Aguas et al., 2023).

Altogether, we refer to these components of our linear–nonlinear model collectively as the rod+cone retinal circuit (Figure 1B). This model was fitted with data from five ganglion cells. The linear and nonlinear elements were fit to a respective polynomial function for each recorded retinal ganglion cell, then the parameter fits for these polynomial functions were averaged across cells. The resulting model was incorporated into the Hassenstein–Reichardt model (see following section). In some simulations, the linear–nonlinear model had only rod filters or only cone filters to enable the investigation of the distinct contributions of each component under mesopic conditions.

Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator

The architecture of the Hassenstein–Reichardt model is illustrated in Figure 1C. Signals from the input channels interact with each other via a correlator time delay (Δt). For example, the real-time response of the right input channel, rright(t), is multiplied with the time-delayed response of the left input channel, rleft(t + ∆t). By mirroring this process by multiplying the real-time response of the left input channel, rleft(t), with the time-delayed response of the right input channel, rright(t + ∆t), and then subtracting the products from one another, we remove the parts of the correlator response that result from direction-irrelevant correlations in the stimulus (Borst & Egelhaaf, 1989). The resulting polarity in the responses provides the directional tuning of the circuit. The final output of the Hassenstein–Reichardt model is calculated as rleftt+Δtrrightt-rrightt+Δtrleftt

The pulse delay, ∆s, controls the time delay between the arrival of the stimulus at one input channel and its arrival at the other input channel (Figure 1C). In other words, the pulse delay corresponds to the motion speed of the stimulus. The pulse width, w, controls the duration of the stimulus (i.e., the width of the stimulus pulse, or the overall angular size of the stimulus) (Figure 1C). Theoretically, a Hassenstein–Reichardt circuit would be most sensitive to the pulse delays that match the correlator time delay. As the pulse delay (∆s) diverges from the correlator time delay (∆t), wider and wider pulses widths (w) are needed for the circuit to register the stimulus as a moving stimulus (Figure 1D). This takes the form of a piecewise linear relationship: Δt≤Δs:wmin>-Δt+Δs;Δt>Δs:wmin>Δt-Δs

The piecewise linear relationship describes the Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator without the filter kinetics or nonlinearities of the retinal model; however, when those are included, this relationship is no longer guaranteed to be linear due to the nonlinearities inherent in the rod+cone retinal model circuit.

In our simulated experiments, we chose a default Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator time delay of 50 ms (see Figures 1C, 1D). This correlator time delay is roughly comparable to the ∼35-ms temporal separation between rod and cone filters (see Figure 1A) and falls within the range of time offsets in which rod and cone signals may interact. For pulse delays that exactly match the correlator time delay, the circuit can accurately discriminate motion direction for the largest range of pulse widths (Figure 1D). In other words, the model has optimal motion direction discrimination for moving stimuli over a wider range of stimulus sizes when the delay between two pulses, corresponding to motion velocity, matches the Hassenstein–Reichardt tuning of the correlator.

Stimulus generation and simulated experiments

For each individual trial, a pulse of light was presented to one of the linear–nonlinear model input channels. Then, to simulate spatial distance between two cells, the other input channel received the same pulse of light after a prespecified pulse delay between 0 ms and 100 ms, corresponding to stimulus velocity (larger pulse delays are slower velocities and smaller pulse delays are faster velocities). Pulses had 100% contrast over a fixed mean luminance, unless otherwise indicated. These pulses contained both rod-activating and cone-activating stimulus components, and the stimulus was scaled such that both rod and cone wings of the rod+cone retinal model were equal contributors to the spike rate output. To induce variability in the model responses, Gaussian pink noise with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 10% was scaled by the mean intensity of the stimulus and added to the pulse stimulus. Incorporating noise in this manner varies the stimulus while preserving its direction of motion, duration, and mean intensity. Noise was not correlated between rod-activating and cone-activating components of the motion stimulus and was also not correlated between stimuli presented to the left and right input channels. The true direction of motion was based on which input channel received the pulse first.

Classification and performance

Model outputs were classified as a leftward or rightward trial with a discriminant vector (Zhao, Zhang, Yang, Zhou, & Xu, 2024). The discriminant vector for a set of trials with a specific pulse delay was calculated by taking the mean response traces of the model for 500 leftward trials and 500 rightward trials, then subtracting the mean leftward response trace (non-preferred direction) from the mean rightward response trace (preferred direction). This linear discriminant analysis is effective under the conditions of our simulations in which the noise is independent of the stimulus and uncorrelated across responses. This process mirrors the Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator calculation resulting in the preferred direction of motion having a positive polarity: d=〈rpref〉-〈rnonpref〉

To automatically label motion direction for a single trial, the response trace was projected onto the discriminant vector by taking the dot product. A projection value of 0 can be interpreted as a response trace that was perfectly dissimilar from both the mean leftward trace and the mean rightward trace. Projections with a value greater than 0 were automatically labeled rightward, whereas projections with a value less than 0 were automatically labeled leftward.

For each experiment, we ran n new trials, half with leftward motion and half rightward motion. The ability of the model to discriminate motion direction (accuracy) was measured as the proportion of trials where the automatic labeling captured the true direction of motion of the stimulus. Confidence intervals for the full model output responses were computed using the Clopper–Pearson interval.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine whether the difference in performance between two circuit models was statistically significant within a single pulse delay value. Psychometric curves were fit to circuit performance data where a sufficiently good fit could be achieved. Model comparisons were performed using the likelihood ratio test and the Palamedes MATLAB toolbox (Kingdom & Prins, 2016). In all cases, we chose a conservative comparison between the rod+cone circuit and the cone-only circuit performance.

Implementation

All simulations were run in MATLAB 2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA) using a MacBook Pro with an Apple M2 chip and 24 GB of memory (Apple, Cupertino, CA). Code is publicly available on the Gutierrez lab motion-detection repository, which can be found on https://github.com/Gutierrez-lab/motion-detection.

Results

A motion discrimination circuit with rod+cone kinetics

To simulate retinal ganglion cell spike rate traces, we fit a linear–nonlinear cascade model using previously described methods (Chichilnisky, 2001; Grimes et al., 2015; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). This linear–nonlinear model circuit was fit to data collected at mesopic light levels and hence captures the unique kinetics of rod-derived and cone-derived signals under these conditions. The kinetics under mesopic conditions were characterized by a rod filter with a longer time to peak than the cone filter. Rod-derived signals were delayed by approximately 35 ms relative to cone-derived signals (Figure 1A). As a consequence, the rod component of the model was slower than the cone component in responding to the same stimulus. The rod filter is also more biphasic than the cone filter, meaning that the rod response has more of a negative response component after the initial positive response component (Grimes et al., 2018; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023).

We incorporated our rod+cone retinal models as the two input channels of a Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator (Figure 2A). The Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator detects motion using a “delay-and-compare” computation (Borst & Euler, 2011; Hassenstein & Reichardt, 1956; Reichardt, 1961). It relies on two input channels with identical response properties—in our case, two simulated retinal ganglion cells—with a fixed spatial separation between them. This spatial separation is implemented as a temporal delay, ∆t. Figure 2B shows that the mean output trace of the full model for stimuli moving in its preferred direction (rightward) has the opposite polarity as the response to stimuli moving in the non-preferred direction (leftward).

The fraction of trials in which the responses of the model were decoded as the true direction of the presented stimulus (i.e., the accuracy of the model) depended on the pulse delay (Figure 3C). As to be expected, the model performs at chance in the absence of a pulse delay (∆s = 0 ms) because the stimulus inputs are presented simultaneously to the left and right input channels; hence, for these trials, there was no actual motion in the stimulus. For pulse delays of 20 ms, the model performed with ∼85% accuracy (i.e., it incorrectly labeled the direction of motion in ∼15% of the trials). Performance plateaued for delays of 60 to 100 ms, which correspond to slow-moving stimuli, whereas smaller delays (faster motion) resulted in the circuit mislabeling a larger fraction of trials. Our previous work has demonstrated how responses to time-varying stimuli at specific temporal frequencies can be attenuated due to interference between rod and cone signals (Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). In the next section, we investigate the effect of rod+cone interference on motion direction discrimination.

Disentangling the perceptual contributions of rods and cones

Does interference between parallel rod- and cone-derived signals hinder motion discrimination? We answered this question by comparing the performance of the rod+cone circuit with the performance of (a) a circuit in which the input channels were exclusively sensitive to the rod-activating component of the presented stimuli (rod-only), and (b) a circuit in which the input channels were exclusively sensitive to the cone-activating component (cone-only). These simulations still pertain to motion discrimination in mesopic conditions because the rod and cone filters were fit to data collected in mesopic lighting. In effect, these trials are simulations of the peripheral retina in mesopic conditions with either a rod or cone knockout or equivalently for stimuli that selectively activate rods or cones. Thus, the following simulations separately examined the rod and cone contributions to the mesopic response.

Destructive interference of rod- and cone-derived signals could limit motion discrimination. Alternatively, rod and cone signals could constructively contribute to motion discrimination by averaging out the independent noise inherent to each rod and cone input pathway, thus improving the signal-to-noise ratio. If that were the case, we would expect that the rod+cone model performance would exceed that of both the rod-only and cone-only models. However, Figure 4 shows that the rod+cone model has (a) lower motion discrimination accuracy than the cone-only model, and (b) higher accuracy than the rod-only model, particularly for the shorter pulse delays. It was not surprising that the cone-only circuit outperformed the rod-only circuit, as cone-derived responses are both faster and more monophasic in their kinetics than rod-derived responses. Ultimately, a faster response time would be better at encoding faster moving stimuli. Additionally, due to the more monophasic shape of the cone filter relative to the rod filter, more of the temporal structure of a given stimulus would be preserved in the output of the circuit. Increasing the magnitude of the input noise decreased the overall performance but did not affect the relative performance among the three circuit models (not shown).

The observation that the cone-only circuit outperformed the rod+cone model circuit is in line with our initial hypothesis—namely, that rod+cone signal interactions would negatively impact motion discrimination. However, the rod+cone circuit had better performance than the rod-only circuit, which makes it difficult to determine the extent to which impaired motion discriminability may be due not only to the characteristics of rod response kinetics but also to the offset in time-to-peak response kinetics for rods and cones. We tested a circuit with two cone inputs per channel where one cone filter was shifted relative to the other cone filter by the same amount as the rod filter would have been (∼35 ms). This circuit performed as poorly as the rod-only circuit despite not having any of the broad and biphasic features of the rod response—only the offset in time to peak. Thus, the offset between the photoreceptor response kinetics, and not merely the broad and biphasic kinetics of the rod response, interferes with motion discrimination.

Our choices for the model parameter values can tune the stimulus preferences of the model. A preference for direction of motion is built into the Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator circuit during the subtraction stage (Figure 1C; additional detail in the Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator section under Methods). At the same time, the choice of correlator time delay can tune the circuit to prefer a range of pulse delays, which correspond to motion velocity. A shorter correlator time delay tunes the model to shorter pulse delays (i.e., faster stimuli), and a longer correlator time delay tunes the model for longer pulse delays (Figure 1D). With a short correlator time delay of 25 ms, the cone-only circuit performance benefits the most in the range of short-pulse delays (Figure 5, left panel). This is again because of the faster-to-peak, more monophasic shape of the cone filter compared with the rod filter. The cone filter more closely resembles an impulse response than the rod filter, thus encoding the stimulus more faithfully. Conversely, the rod-only circuit performs with the least accuracy because the biphasic shape of the rod responses across the left and right channels reduces the fidelity of the signal (i.e., making the signal more ambiguous). For long pulse delays, the rod+cone circuit outperforms the other two circuits. The diverse kinetics in the rod+cone circuit enable it to capture slow-moving stimuli despite the short 25-ms correlator time delay that tunes the circuit for fast-moving stimuli. With a long correlator time delay of 75 ms, the three model circuits have more similar performance across the range of pulse delays (Figure 5, middle panel). Across a range of correlator time delays (25 ms, 50 ms, and 75 ms), the rod+cone circuit has qualitatively consistent performance (Figure 5, right panel). Taken together, these results demonstrate that rod+cone signal interference interacts with both the directionally tuned and the velocity-tuned components of the motion correlator circuit.

Stimulus salience and motion discriminability

Which properties of the motion stimulus itself impact the separation in performance among the three different circuits? The size and relative contrast of a stimulus determine its salience. A wide stimulus pulse represents a larger, and thus more salient, stimulus than one with a narrow pulse width. Likewise, a high-contrast stimulus is more salient than a low-contrast stimulus. As shown in Figure 6, we varied the width and contrast of the motion pulse while keeping all other stimulus parameters fixed. The stimulus noise was proportional to the stimulus contrast (see Methods). We found that mesopic motion direction was more accurately classified by all three variations of the circuit (rod+cone, rod-only, and cone-only) for stimuli with broader pulse widths and greater contrast (Figure 6, top right panel). In other words, the discrimination performance of the model generally increased with stimulus salience. Performance suffered for all circuits presented with short pulse widths and low-contrast stimuli (Figure 6, bottom left panel). In comparison, the stimuli with high contrast and wide pulse width had an increased range of pulse delays in which all circuits were 100% accurate at labeling motion direction (Figure 6, top right panel).

The dashed black line indicates chance (a motion discrimination accuracy of 0.5, or 50%). For all of these conditions, there was still a separation of accuracy between the cone-only circuit relative to the rod-only and rod+cone circuits, particularly across shorter pulse delays, as was previously observed for 10-ms pulse widths at 100% contrast. In all stimulus conditions, the cone-only circuit outperformed the rod+cone circuit, and the rod-only circuit underperformed the rod+cone circuit. The salience of the stimulus did not impact the relative performance of the three circuits.

Discussion and conclusions

Unlike photopic lighting conditions where cone photoreceptors dominate the visual response and scotopic lighting where rod photoreceptors dominate the visual response, mesopic lighting activates both rods and cones (reviewed by Buck, 2004; Buck, 2014; Grimes et al., 2018). We hypothesized that destructive interference between rod- and cone-mediated signals in mesopic conditions would hinder motion perception given that it hinders the ability to perceive stationary flickering stimuli of certain frequencies (MacLeod, 1972; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). To examine this, we incorporated a rod+cone retinal circuit model trained on electrophysiology data from mesopic conditions into a directionally sensitive Hassenstein–Reichardt motion-detection circuit. We found that motion direction discriminability was decreased by the integration of rod- and cone-derived responses for a specific range of pulse delays—namely, 10 to 60 ms. When we pull apart rod+cone signal interactions by creating a circuit that is only responsive to cone inputs (i.e., essentially a rod-knockout circuit), we find that the circuit is able to discriminate motion direction in mesopic conditions with greater accuracy. Ultimately, our model demonstrates that interactions between rod- and cone-derived signals negatively impact the computation of motion direction in mesopic conditions. Less salient stimuli led to overall greater losses in motion discriminability but did not impact the performance of the rod+cone circuit relative to the cone-only and rod-only circuits.

Visual perception of motion

Our model was constructed to examine how parallel processing at the lowest level of human vision might impact a more complex behavior. We note that the rod and cone kinetic data were fit to data collected from peripheral neurons in primate retina under mesopic conditions. In many of the psychophysics experiments of motion detection in mesopic conditions, subjects are instructed to discriminate motion that is peripheral to a fixation point in their field of view (Billino et al., 2008; Sepulveda et al., 2021; Yoshimoto & Takeuchi, 2013; Yoshimoto et al., 2016). Studies show that subjects perceive the motion of a stimulus as reversing under low light conditions when a blank frame is interspersed in the stimulus, but only when the stimulus is in their periphery (Takeuchi & De Valois, 2009). This is likely due to a first-order biphasic filtering of the stimulus consistent with rod-derived responses (Snowden, Hess, & Waugh, 1995). Our model ultimately captures a known visual phenomenon (namely, mesopic perceptual impairments in peripheral vision), and it demonstrates how interaction between rod- and cone-mediated signal kinetics directly impact motion discrimination.

Specific perceptual deficits in mesopic motion processing have been reported in previous literature with implications for everyday tasks such as driving (Wood, 2020). Motion stimuli that emulate a locomoting figure among a background of incoherent dots were uniquely difficult for human observers to perceive in mesopic conditions compared with photopic or scotopic conditions (Billino et al., 2008; Sepulveda et al., 2021). Although it has been posited that rod+cone interactions occurring within the peripheral retina may underlie the phenomenon (Billino et al., 2008), there has not been a direct examination of responses at the level of the neural circuit. Our model results are in agreement with previous studies that have described perceptual deficits for motion, specifically in dim lighting conditions (Billino et al., 2008; Grossman & Blake, 1999; Mayeur et al., 2008; Sepulveda et al., 2021; Yoshimoto et al., 2013; Yoshimoto et al., 2016).

Destructive interference from integration of rod and cone signals

We found that motion processing is degraded by destructive signal interference, as evidenced by the worse performance of the rod+cone model circuit compared with its cone-only counterpart. Poor motion discriminability was to be expected from the rod-only circuit because the slow kinetics of the rod input degrade the fine temporal structure required for the accurate encoding of the time-varying stimulus (Songco-Aguas et al. 2023). Similar to the classic visual stimulus of a flickering spot (MacLeod, 1972; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023; Stockman & Sharpe, 2006), movement or change of any kind requires the stimulus to vary in time. The lag between rod-mediated responses and cone-mediated responses and subsequent destructive interference ultimately blurs the final temporal structure of the stimulus, preventing accurate stimulus encoding within a channel. Unlike a flickering spot, our moving stimulus also varied in space. Thus, the loss of fidelity in encoding within a channel propagates through the circuit, where a comparison across both channels takes place with a built-in time delay.

In our previous research, we paired electrophysiology with human psychophysics to investigate how the kinetic difference between rod- and cone-mediated signals influences visual perception in mesopic conditions (Grimes et al., 2015; Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). In one of these studies, we examined how interference between rod- and cone-mediated signaling led to the inability to perceive flickering stimuli in the peripheral retina (Songco-Aguas et al., 2023). We found evidence for destructive interference between these two signal pathways that depended on the temporal frequency of the flickering stimulus and the relative phase delay between the rod-activating and cone-activating components of the stimulus (Songco-Aguas et al., 2023).

Our motion-discrimination circuit similarly compares rod- and cone-mediated signals from two channels receiving stimulus input with a delay that represents motion across the visual field. Our interpretation for the performance drop of our model circuit in discriminability is that (a) rod+cone signal interference happens to some extent within a channel, degrading the overall output of single channels, and (b) this signal degradation is exacerbated by the motion correlator circuit in its multiplication stage. This is akin to how the linear stages of the rod+cone retinal model are responsible for signal interference, and the consequent nonlinear stages heighten the effect of this interference downstream. Specifically, the multiplication stage of the correlator is a nonlinear process that emphasizes correlations in a time-varying motion stimulus (Dror, O'Carroll, & Laughlin, 2001; Suarez & Koch, 1989). Because the rod+cone retinal model degrades the temporal structure of the stimulus, the Hassenstein–Reichardt correlator “loses” the temporal information it requires to correlate the signals between the two input channels and encode them accurately as motion.

Motion discrimination as a post-retinal computation

To investigate how interactions between parallel rod- and cone-derived signals impact the fidelity of retinal outputs (and therefore, the accuracy of motion direction discrimination), we used a classical motion correlator, the Hassenstein–Reichardt model, as a readout of motion. It was originally developed as a model of motion encoding in the insect visual system, and it relies on the correlations between two stimulus inputs to compute the presence of motion for a given stimulus (Reichardt, 1961). The classical Hassenstein–Reichardt model has been a useful model for motion perception generally, including for applications in computer vision (Basch, Cristea, Tiponut, & Slavici, 2010; Birkoben, Hansen, Ignatov, Ziegler, & Kohlstedt, 2023; Qiu et al., 2025; Yan, Todo, Kobayashi, Tang, & Li, 2022). For our purposes, it serves as a convenient readout to estimate the encoded motion information from two channels of the retinal model.

Incorporating experimentally constrained rod and cone linear kinetics and shared nonlinear processing into the Hassenstein–Reichardt model allowed us to explicitly study how signal interactions among those components impact a canonical motion computation. Although the response filters for the rods and cones in our model are physiologically realistic, the correlator time-delay and multiplication components do not have a direct physiological interpretation. Signals traveling through the parvocellular pathway are slower than those through the magnocellular pathway (Maunsell et al., 1999), allowing for a possible interpretation of the correlator time delay in the Hassenstein–Reichardt model as a consequence of this post-retinal processing feature; however, our study exclusively concerns motion discrimination, which is generally handled by the magnocellular pathway (Merigan, Byrne, & Maunsell, 1991). Nonetheless, the full model is an abstract emulation of the directionally tuned and velocity-tuned visual processing that occurs in the visual system (Maunsell & van Essen, 1983), and it allowed us to determine the accuracy with which motion information is encoded.

Several studies have observed perceptual deficits in motion processing that are specifically attributed to post-retinal processing rather than retinal processing. For example, subjects have a difficult time reporting the location of a moving object at high speeds, perhaps because of a deficit in the high-level binding of the position of the object and the cue to report the position (Linares, Holcombe, & White, 2009). Future studies may expand on our model to investigate how rod+cone interactions impact motion perception that involves additional post-retinal computations.
