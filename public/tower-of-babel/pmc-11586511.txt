Movement Awareness Therapies in Eating Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Cristina Bravo, Didier Hernández‐García, Manuel Trinidad‐Fernández, Gina Badia, Silvia Solé, José Serrano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven the rising prevalence and complexity of eating disorders, a comprehensive multidisciplinary holistic approach is necessary. This work aimed to assess the effectiveness of body and movement awareness therapies in patients with eating disorders. Search strategy was undertaken using several scientific health databases. Articles were eligible if they were randomized controlled trials analyzing the effects of movement and body awareness therapies in eating disorders. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) statement for systematic reviews was followed and the risk of bias was assessed using the “Cochrane Collaboration Tool to assess the risk of bias.” 11 articles met the inclusion criteria, including 437 patients. No high‐quality randomized controlled trials were found. Forest plot analysis of the Eating Disorder Examination scale showed a difference favors to body awareness therapies but not significantly neither homogeneous. The interventions analyzed included yoga, body awareness therapy, dance, and psychomotor therapy. Body awareness therapies provide some additional benefits for eating disorder symptoms, such as emotion regulation, self‐criticism, self‐confidence, self‐compassion, anxiety, body attitude, eating disorder symptoms, and quality of life.Keywords: awareness, body image, eating disorder, meta‐analysis, movement, physical therapy

Based on the findings of this study, these therapies are widely accessible and can be effectively integrated into various contexts, offering significant support in treating EDs.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, eating disorders (EDs) encompass abnormal behaviors related to eating that cannot be explained by another health condition, are inappropriate for the level of development of the individual, and are incongruent with cultural norms (Wei et al. 2024; World Health Organization 2024). EDs include behavioral disturbances unrelated to concerns about weight, body shape, and body awareness. In addition, they are characterized by a persistent alteration of behavior related to eating that negatively impacts the health and psychosocial capacities of those who suffer from them. They are considered psychiatric diseases with great variability in their presentation and severity and with significant nutritional repercussions, which necessitates different therapeutic approaches, making a multidisciplinary approach essential (Gómez‐Candela et al. 2018).

The various types of EDs include anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), binge‐eating disorder (BED), other specified feeding and EDs, unspecified feeding or EDs, avoidant restrictive food intake disorder, pica, and rumination disorder (American Psychiatric Association 2013). Out of all the EDs described in DSM‐5, this study has specifically focused on AN, BN, and BED.

Prevalence studies indicate wide differences by age group and sex, which are much higher in young women (AN, 0.1%–2%; BN, 0.37%–2.98%; BED, 0.62%–4.45%) (Val et al. 2022). According to DMS‐5, the 12‐month prevalence of BED among U.S. adult (aged 18 or older) females and males is 1.6% and 0.8%, respectively. In contrast, the prevalence of AN among young females is approximately 0.4%, and that of BN among young females is 1%–1.5% (American Psychiatric Association 2013). The highest prevalence is attained in women aged between 20 and 30, reaching rates of almost 6.5%. The frequency of AN increases in highly populated cities, being double in women from large cities (6.6%) compared to those from towns (3.4%) (Subdirección General de Información Sanitaria 2021).

Psychiatric comorbidities frequently found in AN, BN, and BED include anxiety disorders, depression with suicidal thoughts, compulsive behaviors, such as skin‐picking, hair‐pulling, and compulsive exercise, and impulsive behaviors, such as self‐harm, aggression, alcohol use, and drug use (Ministerio de sanidad y Consumo 2009). The most relevant clinical manifestations of people suffering from AN are leukopenia, mild anemia, hypomagnesemia, hypozincemia, hypophosphatemia, and hyperamylasemia. Laxative abuse can cause mild metabolic acidosis. Hormonal alterations of both thyroid hormones, estrogen, and testosterone, are common (American Psychiatric Association 2013). The recommended treatment for patients with ED is multidisciplinary, with psychotherapy, medical support (especially at the psychiatric level), and a nutritional approach being the main ones (Gómez‐Candela et al. 2018). However, current treatments achieve improvements in no more than 70% of patients, around 20% of whom maintain symptoms that make them very vulnerable to relapses. The remaining 25%–30% follow a chronic course, and the patients remain trapped in the pathology at great personal, social, and economic cost (Calvo Sagardoy et al. 2014).

Physiotherapy in mental health is person‐centered, aiming to improve quality of life and well‐being through a holistic approach that empowers individuals to manage essential daily activities effectively via functional movement and movement awareness (Probst and Skjærven 2018). Physiotherapy interventions appear to significantly reduce EDs and depressive symptoms in AN and BN (Minano‐Garrido, Catalan‐Matamoros, and Gómez‐Conesa 2022; Vancampfort et al. 2014). However, a comprehensive review of body and movement awareness interventions remains absent from the literature. Body and movement awareness interventions encompass balance, posture, free breathing, coordination of movement, flexibility, centering, and awareness (Skjærven 2010). This concept integrates diverse therapeutic modalities, both within the domain of physiotherapy and extending into other fields. An example of an intervention of this kind is Basic Body Awareness Therapy (BBAT), which consists of movement awareness training aimed at improving movement quality and addressing physical, mental, and relational health challenges (Catalan‐Matamoros et al. 2011). Using BBAT and cognitive behavioral exercises produces an improvement in psychopathological aspects, such as ED symptoms, as measured by the Eating Disorder Inventory (33.6 ± 32.0 in the experimental group vs. 7.3 ± 13.7 in the control group at 7 weeks; p = 0.015) and the Eating Attitudes Test‐40 (14.8 ± 21.8 in the experimental group vs. 1.2 ± 4.1 in the control group at 7 weeks; p = 0.039). Additionally, improvements were noted in body image distortion, as measured by the Body Attitudes Test (24.7 ± 26.4 in the experimental group vs. −8.3 ± 28.4 in the control group at 7 weeks; p = 0.012) (Catalan‐Matamoros et al. 2011), and in quality of life, as measured by the Eating Disorder Quality of Life (from 1.6 ± 0.6 vs. 1.8 ± 0.8 at baseline to 1.1 ± 0.8 vs. 1.0 ± 1.0 at 34 weeks; p < 0.05) (Hay et al. 2018; Rodríguez‐Davila and Soto‐González, 2017). For this reason, novel body awareness interventions are proposed as a promising approach in various mental health conditions, including EDs, and it is important to demonstrate their effectiveness.

“Movement awareness” is a term that can be defined as sensitivity to movement nuances, becoming aware of how movements are performed and experienced in relation to space, time, and energy, and identifying movement reactions in relation to internal, environmental, and relational conditions (Skjærven 2019). Eastern approaches, such as tai chi, qi gong, and yoga, are considered to be meditative movement traditions that represent another category defined by some form of movement and body positioning, focusing on breathing and a clear and calm state of mind with the goal of achieving deep states of relaxation (Langhorst et al. 2013). Furthermore, body awareness therapies refer to a group of interventions that share a common perspective focused on the internal subjective experience of the body, using the mind to affect physical functioning (Mehling et al. 2011). These include dance, the Feldenkrais method, and the Alexander technique, among others. Consequently, our review will consider movement traditions and body and movement awareness therapies that have previously been practiced within structured rehabilitation programs using valid and reliable assessment tools.

Body awareness and movement therapies are readily accessible in most of our societies, providing a cost‐effective, easy, and affordable intervention. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate their effectiveness in EDs. In this sense, previous systematic reviews on the effectiveness of physical therapy, yoga, and exercise in treating EDs have supported these approaches, demonstrating efficacy in relation to body mass index, body fat percentage, muscular fitness, and depressive and anxiety symptoms. These interventions have shown promise in reducing the drive for thinness and improving body satisfaction (COOK et al. 2016; Minano‐Garrido, Catalan‐Matamoros, and Gómez‐Conesa 2022; Ostermann et al. 2019; Vancampfort et al. 2013). However, these reviews have two fundamental differences from the one we are discussing in our research. Firstly, past reviews included only one of the multiple available therapies (e.g., yoga). Lastly, one of these systematic reviews did not include only randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies (COOK et al. 2016), and some of the studies didn't include a control group.

Thus, the purpose of this review is to assess the effectiveness of body and awareness therapies compared with care as usual and to provide a summary of movement and body awareness therapies that can be applied to people diagnosed with AN, BN, and BED.

Materials and Methods

The creation of the research question was developed based on the PICO strategy. This systematic review followed the Cochrane guidelines for the design and conduct of the review. The reporting for this review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) statement (Urrútia and Bonfill 2010). RCTs of movement awareness therapy interventions in people with EDs were also included.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

The search was carried out in the PEDro, PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases. The last search was done in January 2024. That systematic review and meta‐analysis was registered at PROSPERO with CRD42021255004. Randomized clinical trials that included the following terms were searched: EDs, AN, BN, binge‐eating, body awareness, movement therapy, movement quality, body mind therapy, yoga, tai chi, tai ji, Feldenkrais, dance, breathing, qi gong, and alexander (see Table 1 Search Strategy).

Inclusion Criteria and Study Selection

The inclusion criteria included: (a) primary studies; (b) participants were treated with an intervention related to movement awareness, focusing on balance, awareness, and breathing; (c) studies including individuals diagnosed with EDs, EDs symptoms, or difficulties in emotion regulation as outcomes; and (d) studies written in English or Spanish.

The process for selecting articles involved a peer review analysis conducted in three stages. Initially, articles were excluded based on their titles and abstracts. Next, articles were excluded after a full‐text review. Finally, the remaining articles were chosen for systematic review. The exclusion criteria over the process were: (1) not discussing EDs, (2) not describing a full movement awareness intervention, and (3) not being an RCT study.

Data Extraction

All titles and abstracts from the search results were checked using the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (D.H‐G and C.B). Initially, articles with doubts about compliance with the criteria were accepted. Afterward, the full texts of all articles were retrieved to check their acceptability so that data extraction and quality assessment could be performed later. Discrepancies were rechecked, and a consensus was reached through discussion. A third author (M.T‐F) reviewed the data to reach a consensus if needed.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias in the different articles was assessed using the “Cochrane Collaboration Tool to assess the risk of bias” (Wolfenden et al. 2020). All the risks of bias were assessed by two independent researchers (D.H‐G and C.B).

Data Synthesis

RevMan 5.4.1 was used to measure the treatment's Eating Disorder Examination (EDE) effects; the data were reported as continuous outcome data, and the mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, with between‐group differences in the mean change from baseline being recorded. The EDE (Fairburn and Cooper 1993) is a clinical interview and is the current “gold standard” for clinical diagnosis of EDs. It includes four subscales related to the cognitive features of EDs: restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern. Based on Murad et al. (2019), we also integrated a standardized analysis incorporating various scales to measure the severity of ED symptoms. To combine the data, we calculated the standardized mean difference and standard error. Due to the homogeneity expected of movement awareness interventions and the number of participants found in various studies, the fixed‐effects model was employed to calculate the true effect of interventions. Review Manager 5.4 software was used to analyze the data collection process.

Regarding measures of heterogeneity, in our study, the χ 2 test and the I 2 statistic were used (Higgins and Green 2011). The χ 2 test measures the deviation of observed effect sizes from the underlying overall effect. The authors utilize a significance threshold of p‐value < 0.05 to indicate evidence of risk of heterogeneity in intervention effects. The I 2 statistic, as defined by Higgins and Green (2011), measures of risk of heterogeneity as a percentage where a value of 0%–40% might not be an important risk, 30%–60% represents a moderate risk of heterogeneity, 50%–90% represents a substantial risk of heterogeneity, and 75%–100% represents a considerable risk of heterogeneity. The importance of the interpretation of the forest plot relies on two main factors: (a) the magnitude and direction of effects, where we anticipate mean differences < 0, favoring body awareness interventions EDE and ED symptoms; and (b) the strength of evidence regarding the risk of heterogeneity, where we expect a low risk with an I 2 < 40%. The authors selected the mean differences when combining data and used forest plots to compare results across studies.

Results

The first search identified 1992 articles (see Supporting Information). After reviewing titles and abstracts, 1296 studies were excluded due to noncompliance with the inclusion criteria. Each of the 29 selected studies underwent a thorough examination of the complete text to ensure it met the specified criteria. Finally, 11 studies were selected for inclusion in the systematic review, while seven were included in the meta‐analysis (insert Figure 1). A total of 437 patients were included in the reviewed studies. The age ranged between 14 and 72. All participants were diagnosed with EDs according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fourth or Fifth Edition (American Psychiatric Association 2013). The meta‐analysis included four articles that analyzed the EDE questionnaire and seven articles that analyzed the severity of ED symptoms.

Risk of Bias Analysis

The risk of bias in the different articles was assessed (Figures 2) and no study was labeled as low risk. The main bias was the performance bias because the participants and personnel were non‐blinded about the intervention received. Blinding participants is particularly challenging in this type of intervention, leading to an inability to fulfill this criterion. Another important bias was the detection bias. In most of them, the studies did not describe in sufficient detail if the outcome assessors were blinded. Assignment concealment was another item that presented a high number of studies with high risk due to the inherent nature of BA interventions.

Risk of bias analysis from the selected studies.

Assessment Tools

Different tools were used in the studies to calculate the evolution of the intervention group and to compare them with the control group. To evaluate the severity of the EDs, EDE‐Q was used in six articles (Boerhout et al. 2016, 2017; Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe 2020; Karlsen et al. 2018; Pacanowski et al. 2017; Ziv et al. 2023). Other tools were used, including EDI (Catalan‐Matamoros et al. 2011; Karlsen et al. 2018; McIver, O'Halloran, and McGartland 2009; Wallin, Kronovall, and Majewski 2000) Self‐Expression and Control Scale (Boerhout et al. 2016, 2017), Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale, Ways of Self‐Criticism/Aggression and Self‐Confidence Scale, the Short Form of the Self‐Compassion Scale, the Toronto Mindfulness Scale and the Short Form of Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help (Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe 2020), Emotional Avoidance Questionnaire (EDE‐Q), the Hamilton Anxiety Scale and the List of Positive and Negative Affect (Pacanowski et al. 2017), State–Trait Anxiety Inventory, the Beck Depression Inventory and measured body mass index (Carei et al. 2010; McIver, O'Halloran, and McGartland 2009; Ziv et al. 2023), and Multidimensional Body Self Relations Questionnaire, the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (Savidaki, Demirtoka, and Rodríguez‐Jiménez 2020).

Data Analysis

This systematic review and meta‐analysis is the first study on the effectiveness of movement awareness therapy in people with EDs. The studies were RCTs, in which important methodological deficiencies were found, such as a lack of a detailed description of how the randomization sequence was generated (Boerhout et al. 2016; Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe 2020; Carei et al. 2010; Pacanowski et al. 2017; Savidaki, Demirtoka, and Rodríguez‐Jiménez 2020), a lack of information about allocation concealment (Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe 2020; Carei et al. 2010; Catalan‐Matamoros et al. 2011; Pacanowski et al. 2017; Savidaki, Demirtoka, and Rodríguez‐Jiménez 2020; Ziv et al. 2023), a lack of double blinding in all articles, and a small sample size (n = 14–72) (Table 3).

Despite these shortcomings, the results of the present review suggest that movement and body awareness therapies improve symptoms of EDs. Of the 11 studies analyzed, seven observed a significant difference between groups in the severity of EDs.

Yoga was studied in the research conducted by Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe (2020), where significance was found both in the results after the intervention and in the monitoring after 1 month compared to the results at the beginning. These variables were: the number of days of binge eating (t(25) = 5.66, p = 0.001) and the frequency of binge eating (t(25) = 5.54, p = 0.001), which was measured by the EDE‐Q, the scale of difficulties in emotions regulation (t(25) = 3.67, p = 0.001), the scale of Self‐Criticism/Aggression Forms and the Self‐Confidence Scale (t(25) = 2.62, p = 0.015), and the Self‐Compassion Scale Short Form (t(25) = 4.16, p = 0.001). There were differences in time in the intervention group in the curiosity subscales of mindfulness and decentring of Toronto mindfulness scale participants (F 3,75 = 10.31, p = 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.29). In addition, Carei et al. (2010), which used the EDE, observed significant results between groups (F 2,35 = 3.26, p = 0.05, η 2 = 0.16). Furthermore, it is noteworthy that this effect was sustained 1 month after the completion of the study. Pacanowski et al. (2017) found no significant differences between groups in the EDE‐Q, and the Hamilton Anxiety Scale, but there was a significant reduction in negative emotions before meals as measured by the positive and negative affect schedule in the intervention group (Estimate = −5.3, SE = 2.0, p = 0.007). Karlsen et al. (2018) showed an improvement in the global score on the EDE after the intervention and at 6 months (F 2,16 = 5.49, p < 0.01). The subscales related to restriction and eating also improved (F 1,16 = 3.83, p ≤ 0.05), (F 1,16 = 6.40, p ≤ 0.01). McIver, O'Halloran, and McGartland (2009) used a Binge‐Eating Scale and showed a decrease of 11.6 points from the questionnaire (F 1,48 = 38.3, p = 0.001) and an improvement of IPAQ at posttest (F 1,48 = 12.8, p = 0.001). In Ziv et al. (2023), the yoga group showed statistically significant improvement in the BMD axial and anteroposterior lumbar spine (p < 0.05), BDI (p = 0.49), and EDI‐II (p = 0.02) in comparison to the control group.

BBAT was used and the EDI was applied in the study by Catalan‐Matamoros et al. (2011); there were significant differences between groups in some subscales, such as the impetus to drive thinness (Min/Max = 0/18, SD = 7.5, p = 0.003), body dissatisfaction (Min/Max = −8/23, SD = 6.8, p = 0.025), and ineffectiveness (Min/Max = 2/20, SD = 6.9, p = 0.014). In Wallin, Kronovall, and Majewski (2000), the patients were examined at the start of treatment and two‐year follow‐up; there were no significant differences between groups, but there were significant differences in the intervention group in the drive to thinness subscale (M start = 9 SD = 6.1, M 2 years = 4.6, SD = 5.6, p < 0.001). In addition, there were differences in the intervention group in Ch‐EAT (M start = 24.3, SD = 16.5, M 2 years = 12.1, SD = 14.6, p < 0.001) and in the body perception index in patients with body image alterations (M start = 127.2, SD = 33.6, M 2 years = 115.5, SD = 25.8, p = 0.09). In the study by Catalan‐Matamoros et al. (2011) there were also differences in the Body Attitude Test (Min/Max = 8/76, SD = 24.7, p = 0.012), the 36‐Item Short Form Survey in the mental health section (Min/Max = −80/−5, SD = −31.4, p = 0.002), and the Eating Attitude Test (Min/Max = 33/42, SD = 14.8, p = 0.039).

Summary Effects of Meta‐Analysis

Finally, EDE scores were used to estimate summary effects (Figure 3). Our analysis showed a positive difference in favor of body awareness, with plot values of EDE Score 0.30 points higher than the control group (WMD = −0.30 [95% CI: −0.57 to −0.02]; comparison: p = 0.04). The heterogeneity is displayed as null. Although it did not reach statistical significance, it resulted in X 2 = 2.03, df = 3 (p = 0.57), and I 2 = 0%. Due to the small number of studies included in the analysis, there seems to be a low degree of heterogeneity.

Forest plot of EDE comparison: Body awareness versus control group and Forest plot of comparison for standardized mean difference analysis of ED symptoms between body awareness versus control.

The mean difference standardized analysis of the severity of ED symptoms yielded consistent results with the EDE analysis forest plot. The analysis showed a difference but not significantly in favor of body awareness, with plot values of ED scales Std. Mean Difference − 0.22 (95% CI: −0.71 to 0.28) points higher than the control. The heterogeneity shows a considerable high risk, with statistical significance; it resulted in X 2 = 35.48, df = 9 (p < 0.0001), and I 2 = 75%. The standardized mean difference for patients receiving BA intervention reaching the 0.22 cut‐off point for improvement on the EDI, EDE‐Q, EAT‐40, BES, and Ch‐EAT scales was lower than the control group. In this analysis, three articles were disaggregated due to the analysis of ED symptoms using two instruments. Catalan‐Matamoros et al. (2011) used EAT‐40 (a) and EDI (b), Karlsen et al. (2018) used EDI (a) and EDE‐Q (b), and Wallin, Kronovall, and Majewski (2000) used Ch‐EAT (a) and EDI (b). All that data are standardized in the analysis. The studies by Brennan, Whelton, and Sharpe (2020) and Savidaki, Demirtoka, and Rodríguez‐Jiménez (2020) were not included in the meta‐analysis because the variables they examined were unrelated to the severity of ED symptoms.

Discussion

Only four types of movement awareness therapies were analyzed: psychomotor therapy to regulate anger, yoga, body awareness therapy, and dance movement therapy. Psychomotor therapy decreased internal anger but did not conclusively improve the pathology (Boerhout et al. 2016, 2017). Improvement in body awareness from conscious movement therapies may explain positive outcomes, as ED patients often lack interoceptive awareness and suppress emotions, leading to body dissatisfaction (Boerhout et al. 2017). Yoga improved emotional regulation, reduced self‐criticism, and decreased preoccupation with food, likely due to enhanced mindfulness and anatomical changes in the brain (Carei et al. 2010; McIver, O'Halloran, and McGartland 2009). Body awareness therapy positively impacted body satisfaction and attitudes toward eating, promoting both physical and mental health benefits (Catalan‐Matamoros et al. 2011). Conscious movement therapies could promote body awareness, as well as benefit the body and mental health at a neurohormonal level (Savidaki, Demirtoka, and Rodríguez‐Jiménez 2020). Another reason that would explain the effectiveness of yoga in mental illnesses is the anatomical changes that occur in yoga practitioners in the prefrontal cortex, the hippocampus, the anterior cingulate cortex, and the amygdala (Gothe et al. 2019).

Despite the heterogeneity of the different subtypes of movement awareness therapies, all of them have in common the goal of “living in the body,” that is, the integration of the mind, the body, and the vital context. They all focus on experiencing oneself from the inside, rooted in bodily balance, and through breathing, contacting one's own needs and emotions as a prerequisite for developing self‐confidence (Gyllensten et al. 2010).

In fact, there are systematic reviews that have studied the effect of yoga on EDs. The review performed by Klein and Cook‐Cottone (2013) agrees with the results of this review. Klein and Cook‐Cottone included 14 studies in their article, three of which were RCTs while the rest were cross‐sectional studies. The results suggest many potential benefits of practicing yoga for people with EDs. Most yoga interventions were associated with fewer ED risk factors and symptoms, such as body dissatisfaction, body impetus, media influence, poor interception, bulimic behaviors, binge eating, and worrying about food. They had higher levels of ED protective factors, including self‐competence, positive physical and social self‐concept, and emotion regulation. Despite these results, the authors recommend interpreting them with caution because of the variations in study quality, design, and participant characteristics, as well as variations in types and doses of yoga. The review concludes that there is a need for more high‐quality research that provides specific information on the forms of yoga practiced and the effects of doses.

Another recent review from 2019 focused on yoga in EDs (Ostermann et al. 2019), including 12 articles (e.g., eight RCTs and four uncontrolled trials). This review only found evidence of positive effects of yoga on the drive for thinness and body satisfaction. Both measures improved over time following yoga practice, whereas no differences were found between groups when comparing yoga to the control group. The article concludes that no firm recommendation can be made for or against yoga for ED patients, but it can be considered preliminary as an additional treatment in multimodal psychiatric treatment programs. Furthermore, it insists on the need for more large‐scale RCTs to conclusively judge its potential in this patient population.

This review matches the results of other systematic reviews that have studied the effectiveness of physical therapy in EDs. The 2014 review by Vancampfort et al. included eight RCTs that met all selection criteria, some of them with high methodological quality. The authors concluded that the existing bibliography was limited and that in the rest of the articles, there are significant methodological deficiencies (e.g., small sample size and lack of power analysis, double blinding, and reporting of allocation concealment). However, the review suggests that aerobic exercise and yoga may have beneficial effects on EDs and weight in AN and BN patients. Authors conducted another systematic review in 2013 on the effectiveness of physical therapy in people with BED and reached similar conclusions (Vancampfort et al. 2013).

In 2016, Cook et al. published a proposal for a guide on how to use exercise as a complement to the treatment of patients with EDs. This guideline emphasizes the great potential of therapeutic exercise in people with EDs and the need to adapt exercises to achieve the greatest health benefits, considering that they will not be suitable for all cases. This therapeutic use of exercise should be focused on medical needs, the presence of contraindications, nutritional status, attitudes about exercise, and a treatment plan before introducing exercise into any treatment program. It is important to consider the problematic use of physical activity in individuals with EDs. A systematic review conducted by Rizk et al. (2020), which included 47 articles, described how to detect this situation by evaluating factors, including intensity, frequency, duration, type, reasons, compulsiveness, and dependence on exercise in patients diagnosed with AN. Incorporating this knowledge into medical therapy would allow for a more individualized and efficient approach.

In the same way, reviews of the effectiveness of conscious movement therapies in other mental disorders, such as depression (Cramer et al. 2017; Karkou et al. 2019; Meister and Juckel 2018), anxiety (Cramer et al. 2018), schizophrenia (Sabe, Sentissi, and Kaiser 2019), and attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) have been conducted (Barranco‐Ruiz et al. 2019). The effect of therapies on stress (Pascoe, Thompson, and Ski 2017) and insomnia (Wang et al. 2019) has also been studied, as well as that on other illnesses, such as chronic low back pain (Nduwimana et al. 2020), fibromyalgia (Bravo et al. 2019), and heart disease (Li et al. 2021).

Limitations: One limitation of our study is the strategic search, which did not include combinations of MeSH terms, and only a limited number of terms were used. There is a possibility of selection bias when choosing studies for the review. Firstly, trials with a positive outcome are more likely to be published, causing an overrepresentation of these types of studies in the review. Secondly, another bias could be the language, given that only studies in English and Spanish were considered.

No high‐quality RCTs were found, as only two studies reported blinding of evaluators. This lack of blinding may lead to an overestimation of treatment effects in the reported results. In addition, the education level and the experience of the therapists who conducted the movement awareness interventions in the studies could also be biased. Also, the long‐term effect of movement awareness therapies is still unclear. Moreover, the heterogeneity between the studies and the small size of the samples limits the general conclusions and highlights the need for more research of a higher methodological quality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, psychomotor therapy could be a tool that improves anger management; yoga could be effective in reducing some symptoms of EDs, especially in emotional regulation and self‐evaluation, as well as body perception, and dance movement therapy and body awareness therapy could improve image distortion and body perception.

In summary, based on the findings of the studies evaluated and within the limitations of this study, body, and movement awareness therapies provide some additional benefits for people suffering from EDs. The beneficial outcomes are related to emotion regulation, self‐evaluation distortion, body perception, and improvements in EDE results. Future research on other movement and body awareness therapies and on long‐term outcomes may contribute to currently available evidence.
