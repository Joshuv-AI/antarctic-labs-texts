Can physician-supervised medical students expand access to infant basic life support training? A prospective quasi-experimental study on long-term knowledge retention among caregivers
Authors: Claudia Dizioli Franco Bueno, Marcelo Dias Ferreira, Davi Martins, Meyson Santos, Isabelle Christine Castro Franco, Luis Arthur Brito Rodrigues, Nathalia Sena Rocha, Walisson Ferreira Barbosa, Juliana Mattei de Araújo, Alexandre Sizilio, Antônio Pazin Filho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Basic Life Support (BLS) training is a critical component of early response to life-threatening events such as choking and cardiorespiratory arrest, particularly in infants, in whom emergencies frequently occur in the home setting and require immediate caregiver intervention.1, 2, 3, 4 Simulation-based training has become a cornerstone of BLS education, offering hands-on experience and enhancing both skill acquisition and knowledge retention. However, despite its effectiveness, the persistence of acquired knowledge over time remains limited, especially among lay populations exposed to single-session training interventions.5, 6, 7, 8

A key barrier to the broad implementation of BLS education is the limited availability of qualified instructors. In many health systems, this constraint limits dissemination of life-saving training initiatives.9 This challenge may be even more pronounced in geographically dispersed or resource-constrained settings, where simulation-based education can be limited by human resources, infrastructure, equipment, and implementation costs.10, 11, 12

Educational task-sharing – defined here as the participation of trained final-year medical students in teaching activities under direct physician supervision – has emerged as a potential strategy to increase educational capacity while preserving technical oversight.16, 17, 18, 19 This approach differs from unsupervised instructor substitution: in the present study, medical students did not teach independently, but delivered the standardized training while a supervising physician remained present and available to intervene if needed.

Previous studies have suggested that medical students can contribute to BLS education for laypersons and school populations.16, 19 However, less is known about their role in infant BLS training for parents and caregivers, particularly when long-term knowledge retention is assessed and when participants are recruited from different healthcare contexts. Caregivers of infants represent a particularly relevant target population because they are often the first responders to choking, respiratory compromise, or cardiopulmonary arrest in the home environment.

In this study, we evaluated long-term theoretical knowledge retention following infant BLS training delivered through a standardized simulation-based model using either physicians or final-year medical students under direct physician supervision. Rather than testing superiority, equivalence, or non-inferiority between instructor types, our primary objective was to assess the feasibility of this supervised model. Additionally, we explored whether healthcare setting – public versus private outpatient care, used as a proxy for socioeconomic context – was associated with knowledge retention after 12 months.

Methods

Study design and reporting guidelines

We conducted a prospective quasi-experimental study in the northern region of Brazil to assess immediate and long-term retention of BLS knowledge among caregivers of infants aged 30 days to two years. The manuscript was revised with reference to reporting guidance for healthcare simulation research and the resuscitation science adaptation of simulation-based research reporting.13, 14 The completed checklist is provided as supplementary material.

Setting, participants, and allocation

The study was conducted at the Basic Life Support Laboratory (LabSBV) of the State University of Pará (UEPA), Marabá Campus. Participants were recruited from pediatric outpatient clinics associated with both the Brazilian public healthcare system (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS) and the private healthcare sector, allowing comparison across distinct healthcare contexts as a proxy for socioeconomic variability.

We used a non-randomized, convenience sampling approach, with participants sequentially allocated to one of two groups according to instructor availability. Because allocation was not randomized, baseline characteristics were compared descriptively between groups and the potential for selection bias is addressed in the Limitations section.

The Brazilian public healthcare system provides universal, government-funded care, while the private sector includes services financed through insurance or out-of-pocket payments. This exploratory comparison was included because socioeconomic and educational factors may influence access to health information, prior exposure to emergency training, and long-term retention after community-based BLS education.

Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years old) responsible for the daily care of an infant aged 30 days to two years. Caregivers of newborns under 30 days were excluded due to differences in neonatal resuscitation protocols. Participants underwent eligibility screening to ensure physical ability to perform the training and adequate cognitive capacity to understand instructions and complete the assessments. Participants who did not complete the 12-month follow-up assessment were excluded from the final analysis, which was conducted using a complete-case approach.

Instructor groups and supervision

Participants were allocated to two groups, both receiving the same standardized BLS training protocol. In Group 1, sessions were conducted exclusively by physicians. The physician instructors were pediatricians certified in Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) by the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics, using a training structure aligned with American Heart Association principles for pediatric resuscitation education. In Group 2, training was delivered by pairs of final-year medical students under direct physician supervision, with the supervising physician present throughout the session and intervening only when necessary to ensure technical accuracy and participant safety. Before participating as instructors, medical students completed two local preparatory sessions delivered by the principal investigator.

Intervention

Training content, structure, and duration were identical across groups to ensure comparability. Sessions included 6–10 participants to facilitate individualized feedback and hands-on practice. The training followed a standardized five-phase Watch-and-Practice model: (1) a 12-min pre-test and sociodemographic questionnaire; (2) a 3-min instructional video demonstrating infant BLS techniques; (3) a 45-min hands-on session using infant manikins (Little Baby QCPR, Laerdal Medical); (4) a 10-min immediate post-test; and (5) a delayed post-test conducted at 12 months to measure long-term retention. This structure was based on watch-and-practice approaches used in resuscitation education, in which brief demonstration is followed by supervised deliberate practice and feedback.15, 16

The instructional video was produced locally by the principal investigator together with the medical students selected for the study and reviewed by PALS-certified pediatricians to ensure consistency with pediatric BLS principles aligned with American Heart Association resuscitation education. The video demonstrated the sequence of scene safety assessment, assessment of infant responsiveness, calling for help, assessment of breathing and pulse, infant cardiopulmonary resuscitation maneuvers, and foreign-body airway obstruction maneuvers.

During the 45-min hands-on session, participants practiced recognition of infant unresponsiveness and respiratory compromise; safe positioning; calling for help and activating emergency response; correct hand and finger placement for infant chest compressions; compression depth, rate, and recoil; opening the airway; rescue breaths; coordination of compressions and ventilations; recognition of choking; back blows and chest thrusts for severe foreign-body airway obstruction; and when to stop maneuvers or seek advanced support. Instructors provided immediate corrective feedback during practice.

Outcomes and assessment instrument

The primary outcome was change in theoretical BLS knowledge score across the three assessment points: baseline, immediate post-test, and 12-month follow-up. Secondary exploratory outcomes included differences in score trajectories according to instructor group and healthcare setting.

The knowledge assessment tool consisted of 19 multiple-choice questions adapted from a previously used instrument and updated according to the Brazilian Society of Cardiology resuscitation guideline.4, 16 Total scores ranged from 0 to 19 points. The instrument covered general knowledge, sequence of cardiopulmonary resuscitation procedures, method of performing components, ventilation, and chest compressions. The source guideline remains publicly available online and was last accessed on 9 July 2026; the adapted questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Appendix 1.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Given the dichotomous and multidimensional nature of the instrument, this analysis was interpreted as an exploratory measure of internal consistency rather than a definitive indicator of scale reliability. Item-level performance, item-total correlations, and Cronbach’s alpha if each item was deleted are reported in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata version 15® (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the exploratory and pragmatic nature of the study. Analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach, including participants who completed the 12-month follow-up assessment. Records of follow-up non-attendance were excluded from the complete-case analysis.

Mean scores between groups were compared using Student’s t-test, and associations between categorical variables were assessed using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test as appropriate. Linear mixed-effects models were used with participant included as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation. Time, instructor group, and healthcare setting were included as fixed effects. For clinical readability, model estimates are presented as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI). P-values are reported when appropriate.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the State University of Pará (CAAE: 53012021.3.0000.8607). All participants were volunteers and provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 59 participants were enrolled, with 29 assigned to the physician-led group and 30 to the student-supervised group. At the 12-month follow-up, 51 participants completed the final assessment, corresponding to an attrition rate of 13.6%. Follow-up was completed by 27 of 29 participants in the physician-led group and by 24 of 30 participants in the student-supervised group. In the physician-led group, two participants were lost to follow-up, one from the public sector and one from the private sector. In the student-supervised group, six participants were lost to follow-up, five from the public sector and one from the private sector. Knowledge acquisition and retention

Participants demonstrated a significant increase in theoretical knowledge immediately after training. In the complete-case analysis, the mean improvement from baseline to immediate post-test was 8.0 points (95% CI, 7.3–8.7; p < 0.001). Scores declined between the immediate post-test and the 12-month follow-up by a mean of 2.1 points (95% CI, 1.2–3.0; p < 0.001), but remained above baseline at 12 months by 5.9 points (95% CI, 4.9–7.0; p < 0.001). Participants recruited from private outpatient clinics scored higher than those from the public sector in the mixed-effects model (adjusted mean difference, 1.3 points; 95% CI, 0.5–2.1; p = 0.001).

Cronbach’s alpha for the 19-item instrument was 0.44. In the exploratory reduced-item analysis, reliability increased to 0.54. The proportion of correct responses varied widely across items and time points, demonstrating heterogeneity in item difficulty. Detailed item-level results are provided in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Discussion

In this prospective quasi-experimental study, both physician-led and student-supervised infant BLS training were associated with substantial gains in caregivers’ theoretical knowledge. Although knowledge scores declined between the immediate post-test and the 12-month follow-up, scores remained substantially above baseline after one year. No statistically significant difference was detected between instructor groups. Because the study was exploratory and not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority trial, this result should be interpreted as supporting feasibility under direct supervision rather than equivalence between approaches.

The decline in theoretical knowledge over 12 months is consistent with previous resuscitation education studies showing that BLS knowledge tends to deteriorate without reinforcement.6, 7, 8, 20 Evidence from resuscitation education suggests that spaced learning, brief refresher sessions, and self-directed learning strategies may improve retention compared with isolated single-session training, although the optimal frequency, format, and delivery mode remain context-dependent.21, 22

The absence of statistically significant differences between instructor groups suggests that, under direct supervision, final-year medical students may contribute to the delivery of infant BLS education for lay caregivers. However, this study evaluated a supervised implementation model, not an independent student-led or cost-saving model. In the student-supervised group, a physician remained present throughout the session; therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that this approach reduces personnel requirements or costs. The potential contribution of medical students may instead lie in educational capacity-building, community engagement, and development of communication, feedback, and leadership skills.17, 18, 19

Participants recruited from private clinics scored slightly higher than those from the public sector. This difference may reflect underlying structural disparities between healthcare contexts, including variations in health literacy, educational opportunities, access to information, or prior exposure to emergency care education.23 However, the magnitude and practical relevance of this difference should be interpreted cautiously because healthcare setting was only an exploratory proxy for socioeconomic context.

The moderate internal consistency of the 19-item instrument likely reflects its multidimensional structure, as the questionnaire covered different domains of infant BLS knowledge rather than a single latent construct. The wide variation in item difficulty may also have contributed to the observed Cronbach’s alpha. Therefore, the instrument should be interpreted as a pragmatic knowledge assessment tool rather than as a fully validated psychometric scale.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the non-randomized design may introduce selection bias. Second, the sample size was small and the study was not powered to detect small between-group differences or to establish equivalence or non-inferiority. Third, not all participants completed the 12-month follow-up; 51 of 59 enrolled caregivers were included in the complete-case analysis, and attrition may have introduced bias. Fourth, the outcome focused on theoretical knowledge assessed through a written questionnaire, without evaluation of psychomotor skills, which are essential in real-life emergency scenarios. Fifth, intermediate follow-up assessments were not conducted, limiting the ability to characterize the trajectory of knowledge decay over time. Finally, the study was conducted in a single geographic region using convenience sampling, which may limit generalizability.

Conclusions

Physician-led and student-supervised infant BLS training were both associated with immediate gains in caregivers’ theoretical knowledge, followed by partial decline at 12 months while remaining above baseline. Under direct physician supervision, final-year medical students may contribute to caregiver BLS education without a statistically detectable difference in knowledge scores compared with physician-led training. This exploratory finding supports feasibility, not equivalence. Planned reinforcement strategies are needed to sustain long-term theoretical knowledge retention.
