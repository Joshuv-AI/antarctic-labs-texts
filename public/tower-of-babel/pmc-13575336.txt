Evaluating the Timing of Sitz Baths After Laser Hemorrhoidoplasty: An Exploratory Study
Authors: Yu‐Tso Liao, Ho‐Hsien Chuan, Kai‐Hsing Chang, Bin‐Chi Wu, Wan‐Xin Chen, Ming‐Chi Ho, Jui‐Yun Lin, Ting‐Ju Lai, Kai‐Wen Huang, Jin‐Tung Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLaser hemorrhoidoplasty (LHP) is a minimally invasive procedure for the treatment of hemorrhoids. Early sitz baths are recommended after conventional hemorrhoidectomy; however, their effectiveness following LHP has not been evaluated.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the timing of sitz baths for postoperative recovery in patients undergoing LHP.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with grade I–IV hemorrhoids who underwent LHP between March 2023 and December 2025. Patients were grouped by sitz bath timing: operation day, postoperative day 1, or after discharge. Primary outcomes were maximum and mean visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores and change in VAS scores (ΔVAS) between operation day and postoperative day 1. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative parameters, recovery outcomes, and complications (urinary retention and bleeding).ResultsOf 310 patients, 92 began sitz baths on operation day, 146 on postoperative day 1, and 72 after discharge. Mean initiation times were 6.6 and 21.0 h for the operation‐day and postoperative‐day‐1 groups (p < 0.0001). The operation‐day group had significantly higher mean VAS pain score (1.31 ± 0.77 vs. 0.91 ± 0.57 vs. 0.93 ± 0.6, p = 0.0022) on the operation day and △VAS pain score (mean) (−0.27 ± 0.67 vs. 0.12 ± 0.63 vs. 0.14 ± 0.56, p = 0.0018) compared with other groups. Complication rates did not differ among groups. Multivariable analysis showed that delayed sitz bath was independently associated with lower mean VAS pain score on postoperative Day 1 (β = –0.374, 95% CI: –0.551 to –0.196, p < 0.001) and after discharge (β = –0.364, 95% CI: –0.572 to –0.156, p < 0.001).ConclusionsAlthough this study failed to show advantages of early sitz baths, it does not exclude potential benefits for sitz baths after LHP. Prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted.Keywords: complications, hemorrhoidectomy, Laser hemorrhoidoplasty, postoperative pain, sitz bath

Introduction

Sitz baths have long been used as standard care after anal and gynecological surgery [1, 2, 3]. They aim to relieve pain, disinfect wounds, and reduce post‐surgical complications. The mechanism is attributed to decreased internal anal sphincter tone, which relieves postoperative pain and facilitates urination [4, 5, 6]. As pain and urinary retention commonly occur immediately after hemorrhoidectomy, sitz baths are usually recommended on the day of surgery [7]. One randomized controlled trial reported that early warm sitz baths, initiated within 6 h after surgery, provided the greatest benefit [8].

Hemorrhoids are a common condition, affecting 4.4%–38.9% of the population [9]. Surgery is the definitive treatment for symptomatic hemorrhoids; however, conventional hemorrhoidectomy is associated with severe pain, complications, and delayed wound healing. To address these drawbacks, several alternatives, including energy‐based hemorrhoidectomy, stapled hemorrhoidopexy, hemorrhoidal artery ligation, and laser hemorrhoidoplasty (LHP), have been developed [10, 11, 12, 13].

LHP is a relatively recent technique that applies diode laser energy to the submucosal hemorrhoidal tissue. The single‐wavelength laser targets the congested venous plexus and connective tissue, reducing hemorrhoid volume and alleviating symptoms without the need for excision. Systematic reviews have confirmed the advantages of LHP as a minimally invasive procedure [13, 14], reporting lower postoperative pain [15, 16, 17], easier wound care [17, 18], fewer complications, and faster recovery [17, 19, 20] compared with conventional Milligan–Morgan hemorrhoidectomy.

As LHP does not require excision of grade I–III hemorrhoids, it minimizes injury to the mucosa and anal sphincter. However, an optimal postoperative protocol for LHP has not been established [21], and current care practices are extrapolated mainly from conventional hemorrhoidectomy. Given the distinct advantages of LHP, the role of sitz baths in its postoperative care should be reconsidered. We hypothesized that early sitz baths after LHP may be unnecessary, unlike after conventional hemorrhoidectomy. The aim of this study, therefore, was to evaluate the association of different timing of sitz bath and postoperative recovery in patients with hemorrhoids undergoing LHP.

Methods

Patient Selection

The requirement for informed consent from patients was waived by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital owing to the retrospective nature of this study (202512041RINC). All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Between March 2023 and December 2025, patients with symptomatic grade I‐IV hemorrhoids who underwent LHP at the National Taiwan University Hospital, Hsinchu Branch, were retrospectively included in this study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) symptomatic grade I‐IV hemorrhoids classified according to Goligher [22]; (2) failure of medical treatment; and (3) treatment with LHP. The exclusion criteria were concomitant anal pathologies (e.g., fistula or fissure), combined procedures by using ligasure (Covidien, CA, USA) or acute thrombosed hemorrhoids.

Data Collection

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records. Baseline demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, body weight, BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Class, and main presenting symptoms. Patients were categorized into three groups based on the timing of sitz bath initiation: day of operation, postoperative day 1, and after discharge. The calendar‐based grouping aimed to offer the health carers a structured and comprehensible timing frame of sitz baths as a basis for guidance. Hemorrhoid severity was assessed in the outpatient proctology clinic using the Goligher classification, where grade I was defined as internal hemorrhoids without protrusion, grade II as a prolapsed hemorrhoid complex that reduced spontaneously, grade III as a prolapsed hemorrhoid plexus requiring manual reduction and grade IV as permanently prolapsed hemorrhoids which cannot be reduced manually [22]. The ASA Physical Status Classification was assigned by anesthesiologists based on a patient's comorbidities and physical status.

Outcome Measurements

Intraoperative parameters included operative time and total laser energy delivered. Recovery outcomes included postoperative length of stay, time from recovery room exit to first sitz bath, and postoperative length of stay (LOS). Postoperative LOS was defined as time from recovery room exit to leaving hospital.

Pain intensity was measured using a visual analog scale (VAS) every 8 h during hospitalization, with 0 representing no pain and 10 representing the worst pain imaginable. The scores were summarized as maximum and mean scores on the day of surgery and postoperative Day 1. ΔVAS was defined as the within‐patient change in score between the day of operation and postoperative Day 1. Analgesic requirements were assessed by acetaminophen tablet count on the day of operation and postoperative Day 1, the patient numbers of using morphine, and Utraphen.

Postoperative complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification and Extended Clavien‑Dindo classification of surgical complications [23]. Two complications were specifically evaluated: acute urinary retention and postoperative bleeding. Postoperative bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring oral or intravenous tranexamic acid, intravenous fluid supplementation, blood transfusion, or surgical intervention. Acute urinary retention was defined as the absence of urine output for 6 h, requiring treatment with medications (e.g., tamsulosin [Harnalidge, 0.2 mg] or bethanechol [Wecoli, 10 mg]) or urinary catheterization. Post‐discharge outcomes included 14‐day emergency department reattendance and 14‐day hospital readmission.

Laser Hemorrhoidoplasty

Figure 1 illustrates the LHP procedure. Patients received bowel cleansing with a glycerin enema on the morning of surgery. Intravenous general anesthesia was induced with propofol (2.0 mg/kg), and oxygen was administered via face mask. Patients were placed in the modified lithotomy position, and a bilateral pudendal nerve block was performed using 2% xylocaine (5 mL per side; total 10 mL).

Sequential demonstration of laser hemorrhoidoplasty (a) Preoperative appearance of grade II hemorrhoids without mucosal prolapse. (b) An anal retractor shows a bulging hemorrhoidal plexus at the right posterior quadrant of the anus (arrow). (c) A puncture is made at the mucocutaneous junction (arrow), and the laser probe is inserted approximately 1.5 cm proximal to the dentate line. The red light indicates the location of the laser tip (arrowhead). (d) Immediate postoperative appearance shows slight retraction of the hemorrhoid following laser hemorrhoidoplasty.

A 1.8‐mm optical probe (CORONA, neoLaser, Caesarea, Israel) emitting at a wavelength of 1470 nm was used. An anal retractor was inserted to expose the hemorrhoidal plexus. The probe was introduced at the mucocutaneous junction and advanced into the submucosal layer, approximately 1.5 cm above the dentate line. Laser energy was delivered at 6 watts for 3 s per pulse until a total of 150–200 J was applied at each site. Ice‐bar packing was placed in the anal canal for approximately 30 s. The procedure was sequentially performed on the right posterior, right anterior, and left lateral internal hemorrhoids. At completion, a Gelfoam sterile sponge (Pfizer, New York, NY, USA) was inserted into the anal canal for hemostasis.

Postoperative Care

The sitz bath kit was purchased from a healthcare store at the institute (Figure 2a). The kit allowed patients to sit on a toilet seat with the anal wound immersed in warm water (Figure 2b). Patients were instructed preoperatively on the purpose and procedure of sitz baths. After surgery, the timing of the first sitz bath was not restricted and was determined by patient preference, typically when they decided to remove the Gelfoam® sponge due to discomfort or to defecate. After the first sitz bath, patients were advised to continue the practice four times daily. Patients who initiated sitz bath after discharge were asked to change the gauze dressing whenever the gauze dressing become wet, soiled, or saturated with fluid. All patients were followed up on first, third and fiftth weeks after discharge from the hospital. Any symptom including postoperative bleeding or urination was recorded during the follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

The study followed the recommendations put forward in the guidelines for reporting statistics for clinical research on the proper analysis, reporting, and interpretation of clinical research [24] and the SAMPL guidelines [25]. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons across the three groups defined by timing of sitz bath initiation (on operation day, postoperative Day 1, or after discharge), continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Given the relatively large sample size, ANOVA was considered appropriate because the sampling distribution of the group means can be approximated by a normal distribution according to the Central Limit Theorem, rendering the analysis robust to minor departures from normality. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

To identify factors associated with mean postoperative VAS pain score, univariable linear regression was first performed for each candidate variable independently. All variables were subsequently entered simultaneously into a multivariable linear regression model regardless of their univariable significance, to obtain mutually adjusted estimates. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of potential collinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the adjusted R 2. Covariates included in the regression model were age, sex, Goligher classification of hemorrhoids, total laser energy delivered, excision of hemorrhoids and timing of sitz bath. Subgroup analyses were performed separately for patients with hemorrhoids grade I–III and grade IV to examine whether the effect of sitz bath timing on pain outcomes differed by disease severity. All reported P‐values should be interpreted with caution in the context of multiple comparisons. Complete case analysis was used for managing missing data. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Excel 365 and SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Among them, 92 initiated sitz baths on the operation day, 146 on postoperative Day 1, and 72 after discharge. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, body weight, BMI, ASA Physical Status Class, and presenting symptoms, did not differ significantly among the three groups (Table 1). All patients in our cohort completed at least 14‐day follow‐up.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the timing of sitz bath initiation was not associated with VAS pain scores or reduce complication rates, including acute urinary retention and postoperative bleeding, following LHP. In contrast, early sitz bath increased VAS (mean) pain score on the operation day. These findings failed to show advantages of early sitz baths for patients with grade I‐IV hemorrhoids undergoing LHP, unlike the guideline recommended after conventional hemorrhoidectomy.

Sitz baths have traditionally been regarded as an important component of postoperative care after hemorrhoidectomy. However, recent evidence has called into question their necessity [26]. They remain widely used after anal and obstetric procedures [3] [27], with proposed benefits including pain relief, facilitation of urination [5], and wound cleansing. The mechanism of pain relief is thought to involve relaxation of the hypertonic anal sphincter, similar to the effects observed with local thermal stimulation. For example, Jiang et al. reported that focal thermal stimulation induces sphincter relaxation through the somatoanal reflex [4]. Sphincter contraction is recognized as a major contributor to postoperative pain, and two studies confirmed that botulinum toxin injection or topical administration to the anal sphincter reduced pain after surgery [28, 29].

The potential role of sitz baths in promoting urination after hemorrhoidectomy has also been described. Shafik et al. proposed that sitz baths trigger the thermosphincteric reflex, which relaxes the urinary sphincter and thereby facilitates micturition [6]. In this context, bladder tension may be relieved during warm sitz baths, reducing the need for urinary catheterization [5].

Despite these plausible mechanisms, the clinical benefits of sitz baths after anal surgery remain anecdotal [26, 30]. Randomized controlled trials comparing patients who did and did not undergo sitz baths are limited [8, 31, 32, 33]. Liao et al. reported that early warm sitz baths improved pain after hemorrhoidectomy [8]. However, most studies have failed to show significant analgesic benefit, although some have suggested an improvement in patient satisfaction [32, 33, 34]. Taken together, these findings indicate that sitz baths may provide more psychological reassurance than measurable reductions in pain or complications.

In addition to the ongoing controversy regarding sitz baths in the literature, we propose two possible explanations for why sitz baths did not reduce pain after LHP in our study. First, pain perception is influenced by multiple factors, including culture and ethnicity [35]. The VAS pain scores in our cohort were relatively low on both the operation day and postoperative day 1. Chen et al. reported similar findings in Taiwan, where patients undergoing LHP combined with feeding artery ligation had VAS pain scores of approximately 1–2 on postoperative day 1 [16]. Even if sitz baths provide some analgesic benefit, their effect may therefore be marginal.

Second, LHP primarily targets the internal hemorrhoidal plexus, which has limited nociceptive innervation, potentially contributing to lower subjective pain scores. Furthermore, all patients in our study routinely received oral acetaminophen for at least 7 days postoperatively. This regimen appeared sufficient, as few patients required additional analgesics such as nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs or morphine. These findings are consistent with prior studies reporting lower postoperative pain after LHP compared with Milligan–Morgan hemorrhoidectomy [36, 37, 38] or stapled hemorrhoidopexy [39] for grade II/III hemorrhoids, underscoring the minimally invasive nature of LHP.

Minimally clinically important difference is a concept to contextualize pain from patient‐centered perspective rather than only statistical significance. The threshold of 1 point reduction on a 10‐point scale indicates that a patient is experiencing a clinically meaningful improvement following hemorrhoidectomy [40]. In our study, patients of the operation‐day group experience significant reduction of pain, though the change failed to exhibit a reduction greater than 1 on the 10‐point scale. The findings may be associated with the low baseline of VAS score on the operation day for patients undergoing LHP.

Our study showed a negative ΔVAS pain score between operation day and postoperative day 1 for patients of operation‐day group, which seems likely to be explained by improved pain associated with sitz baths. However, we thought the explanation should be with caution because the VAS (max) on operation day for operation‐day group was higher than other groups, leading to a drastically improvement of pain score reflecting on ΔVAS pain score. We also acknowledged regression to the mean: the group with the highest operation‐day pain showed the largest subsequent improvement, which is expected under regression to the mean and further supports confounding by indication rather than a genuine sitz‐bath effect.

The total energy that laser delivered for hemorrhoids ablation may directly cause patients' discomfort leading to immobilization on the operative day and consequently the timing of sitz bath initiation. In our study, patients with sitz baths on operation day received lower amount of total laser energy on average, therefore may explain patients with sitz baths initiation earlier than the other patient groups. Similarly, the imbalance of laser energy may reflect different severity of hemorrhoids and biased outcomes evaluation, especially VAS pain scores among three groups. Taking these findings together, we suggest conducting a randomized controlled trial to overcome the selection bias in our future work.

Reported urinary retention rates after conventional hemorrhoidectomy range from 2.2% to 7.1% during hospitalization [16, 39, 41], while postoperative bleeding rates range from 4.1% to 24% [37, 41, 42]. Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have confirmed that LHP is associated with fewer complications than Milligan–Morgan hemorrhoidectomy [13, 19]. This study showed that patients of operation‐day group exhibited fewer urinary retention compared with other two groups. Although small sample size of the study may cause no statistical significance, we thought the effect of sitz baths should not be underestimated. Our study implied that perhaps sitz baths improved urination rather than VAS pain score and the relationship between sitz baths and urination deserved future exploration.

To guide clinical practice and improve preoperative counseling, we categorized patients according to whether they received a sitz bath initiation on the day of operation, on postoperative day 1, or after discharge. Although this classification is clinically practical, the effect of sitz baths may be more directly related to the interval between surgery and bathing. Our findings suggest that immediate sitz baths are not mandatory for patients with hemorrhoids undergoing LHP.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of certain limitations. First, it was a retrospective cohort study conducted at a single institution. Although clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records by one investigator and analyzed independently by another, selection bias cannot be excluded. For example, for the VAS pain scores of the operation day, missing data accounted for 50% of patients with sitz bath on operative day and 64.4% of patients with sitz bath on postoperative day 1. We found that VAS pain scores were not recorded mainly on patient groups with initiation of sitz bath. We postulated VAS pain scores in our cohort would be overestimated because sitz baths may temper the discomfort of patients receiving sitz baths. Unless the patients suffered from intractable pain after sitz baths, patients would not contact the nurses who were responsible to record the VAS pain score. The missing data of these improved VAS pain scores would lead to overall overestimation of max VAS pain score as reflected in our study. Prospective studies with detailed VAS pain score after sitz baths are needed to minimize this bias. Second, the relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power. This study may be underpowered to detect small between‐group differences for secondary outcomes, limiting the ability to generalize results. Larger prospective studies with expanded sample sizes are required to validate our findings. Third, the timing of sitz bath initiation was not standardized and may have been influenced by patient‐ or procedure‐related factors. For example, because total laser energy was significantly lower in the operation‐day group, indicating probably less severe hemorrhoids, the observed differences in VAS pain scores may arise from baseline disease severity instead of sitz baths timing. To overcome the selection bias caused by patient preference, we performed a regression that may control the factors such as sex, age, severity that may determine the pain sensation. Our findings should be interpreted with caution, and prospective, randomized‐controlled studies are needed to confirm causality. Fourth, this study was calendar‐based rather than physiological grouping definition. The calendar‐based grouping aimed to offer the health carers a structured and comprehensible timing frame of sitz baths. However, the grouping failed to evaluate the effects of time duration from leaving recovery room to first sitz baths on the VAS pain score and complications. To this end, we performed a regression to evaluate the continuous dose–response model showing that delayed sitz bath by every hour was independently associated with lower mean VAS pain score on operation day. To clarify the effect of sitz bath for patients after laser hemorrhoidoplasty, we will conduct a randomized‐controlled trial to evaluate whether timing of first sitz baths alleviate pain or decrease complications. Fifth, bilateral pudendal nerve block was administrated for all patients in our study before LHP. The analgesic effect of pudendal nerve block may be long‐lasting up to 24 h, obscuring the benefits effect of sitz baths. Because all groups already reported near‐zero pain, there's not enough statistical power to detect a contextual benefit that sitz baths might provide. Sixth, long‐term follow‐up on pain and postoperative complications were not available. Despite these limitations, our study provides preliminary evidence and a basis for future investigations on the role of sitz baths after LHP.

Conclusions

LHP is a minimally invasive procedure for treating grade I‐IV hemorrhoids. The method differs fundamentally from conventional hemorrhoidectomy. Our findings indicate that the timing of sitz bath initiation, whether on the day of operation, postoperative Day 1, or after discharge, did not significantly decrease postoperative pain or complication rates after LHP. These results raise important questions about the optimal timing of sitz baths in this setting. Larger prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to provide stronger evidence and guide evidence‐based postoperative care for patients undergoing LHP.
