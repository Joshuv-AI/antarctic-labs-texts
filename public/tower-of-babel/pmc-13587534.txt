Admission low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and early haematoma expansion after spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage
Authors: Gang Wang, Ruobing Bai, Wenjun Zhao, Guangyan Li, Dong Wang, Boru Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and haematoma expansion (HE) after spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) remains uncertain. This study evaluated whether admission LDL-C was independently associated with early HE and whether it improved prediction beyond the established clinical and imaging factors.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 541 consecutive adult patients with acute spontaneous ICH who had admission lipid measurements and baseline and follow-up computed tomography. LDL-C was examined as a continuous variable, quartiles and prespecified clinical thresholds. Modified Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate relative risks (RRs). Dose-response, subgroup analyses, prediction-model evaluation and sensitivity analyses were performed. Prediction models were internally validated using stratified fivefold cross-validation repeated 100 times.ResultsEarly HE occurred in 62 patients (11.5%). Median LDL-C was lower in patients with HE than in those without HE (2.17 vs. 2.45 mmol/L; p = 0.001). In the primary adjusted model, each 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C was associated with an increased HE risk (RR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.33–2.19; p < 0.001). Patients in the lowest LDL-C quartile had a higher HE risk than those in the highest quartile (RR, 4.28; 95% CI, 2.01–9.12). Restricted cubic splines suggested an inverse, approximately linear association. Adding LDL-C provided limited incremental predictive value and did not consistently improve decision-curve net benefit.ConclusionsLower admission LDL-C was independently associated with early HE after spontaneous ICH. However, its incremental predictive value beyond clinical and imaging factors was limited. Independent replication and external validation are required before clinical application.

Introduction

Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) is among the most devastating forms of stroke, with high early mortality and substantial long-term disability [1–6]. Early haematoma expansion (HE) is a central driver of neurological deterioration and unfavourable outcome and remains one of the few potentially modifiable processes after ICH onset [7]. Current guidelines and reviews identify several major predictors of HE, including onset-to-imaging time, baseline haematoma burden, antithrombotic exposure, intraventricular extension and neuroimaging markers of active or unstable bleeding [5,7]. Recent hyperacute intervention trials further underscore haematoma growth as a clinically relevant therapeutic target, although effective and broadly applicable strategies remain limited [8,9]. These established predictors primarily characterize the timing and morphology of bleeding. They may not fully capture the systemic biological vulnerability that causes some haematomas to expand while others remain stable.

This gap has prompted growing interest in blood-based biomarkers that are inexpensive, rapidly available and broadly applicable, particularly where advanced imaging is delayed or unavailable. Lipid metabolism is one candidate pathway. Cholesterol is involved in cell membrane structure, endothelial homeostasis, vascular permeability and inflammatory regulation. In ischaemic vascular disease, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is an established therapeutic target, and intensive LDL-C lowering after atherosclerotic ischaemic stroke reduces subsequent major cardiovascular events [10,11]. In haemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease, however, the interpretation of LDL-C is more complex. Lower LDL-C concentrations have been associated with increased bleeding risk in some antithrombotic-treated stroke populations and with ICH risk in observational studies, whereas randomized evidence does not show a uniform increase in ICH risk with lipid-lowering treatment [12,13]. Thus, lower LDL-C may reflect vascular fragility, systemic inflammatory-metabolic status, frailty, nutritional reserve or treatment history rather than a single causal pathway.

Prior studies evaluating LDL-C and haematoma dynamics after ICH have yielded inconsistent results. In an early prospective study, lower LDL-C was independently associated with haematoma growth, early neurological deterioration and 3-month mortality after acute ICH [14]. A large registry analysis from the Chinese Stroke Center Alliance subsequently reported that very low LDL-C was associated with a higher risk of HE or in-hospital death, although the use of a composite outcome complicates interpretation of the HE-specific association [15]. In contrast, another Chinese cohort found no independent association between serum lipid indices and haematoma growth after adjustment for clinical and imaging factors [16]. More recently, the field has become further complicated by evidence suggesting that high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), rather than LDL-C, may be more closely associated with HE, particularly in non-lobar ICH [17]. These divergent findings suggest that the lipid signal associated with HE may vary according to population characteristics, haemorrhage phenotype, outcome definition and the extent of adjustment for established predictors.

This conflicting literature leaves a clinically important question unresolved: is LDL-C still informative for early haematoma instability when HE is defined by imaging and when established clinical, radiological and lipid-related confounders are accounted for? The answer matters because LDL-C is inexpensive, routinely measured and rapidly available in most acute stroke settings. Yet its interpretation in ICH requires caution: an observational association with admission LDL-C should not be conflated with the biological or clinical effects of lipid-lowering therapy, nor should it be interpreted as evidence supporting acute lipid manipulation.

To address this uncertainty, we examined the association between admission LDL-C and imaging-defined early HE in a consecutive cohort of patients with acute spontaneous ICH. We integrated multivariable relative-risk modelling, dose-response assessment, prespecified subgroup analyses and prediction-model evaluation to determine whether LDL-C provided information beyond established clinical and imaging predictors. We hypothesized that lower admission LDL-C would be associated with higher risk of early HE after adjustment for the established clinical, imaging, and lipid-related covariates.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational cohort study included consecutive adult patients with acute spontaneous ICH admitted to The Second Hospital & Clinical Medical School, Lanzhou University, between January 2021 and January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital & Clinical Medical School, Lanzhou University (approval No. 2025 A-1073). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified data.

Spontaneous ICH was diagnosed based on clinical presentation and baseline non-contrast cranial computed tomography (CT) performed within 6 h of symptom onset. Patients were eligible if they were 18 years or older, had baseline CT-confirmed spontaneous ICH, underwent follow-up CT within 25 h for assessment of HE and had admission lipid measurements including LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides and total cholesterol. Patients were excluded if ICH was secondary to trauma, aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, tumour, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, haemorrhagic transformation of ischaemic stroke or another identified structural cause [18]. Additional exclusion criteria were unavailable symptom-onset time or CT timing, missing key laboratory or imaging data, concurrent infection or malignancy, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, a documented history of malnutrition or emergency surgery before follow-up CT that precluded assessment of HE. A flow diagram illustrating patient selection is shown in Figure 1.

Study flow diagram showing patient screening, exclusion criteria and formation of the final complete-case analytic cohort.

Clinical management followed contemporary American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines for spontaneous ICH and the institutional treatment protocol [5,19–21]. Treatment modality was categorized as conservative or surgical treatment. Conservative treatment was used for patients with stable neurological status or those considered unsuitable for surgery because of age, comorbidities or overall clinical condition. Surgical treatment included haematoma evacuation, decompressive craniectomy, and external ventricular drainage when clinically indicated. Treatment decisions were made by the treating stroke and neurosurgical teams according to neurological status, haematoma volume, mass effect, hydrocephalus, comorbidities and surgical eligibility [1,5,20,21].

Data collection and imaging assessment

Clinical and demographic data were extracted from electronic medical records. Variables included age, sex, history of hypertension or diabetes mellitus, smoking, alcohol drinking, preadmission antithrombotic use, baseline Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, onset-to-first CT time, haemorrhage location, intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), baseline haematoma volume (BHV), treatment modality (conservative vs. surgery) and admission laboratory results.

Laboratory parameters were obtained within 6 h of admission and comprised complete blood count, coagulation indices, fibrinogen and lipid profile, including LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides and total cholesterol. Lipid measurements were obtained from the first available venous blood sample collected. Because specimens were obtained during emergency assessment, fasting status was not standardized. LDL-C was measured directly using a homogeneous enzymatic assay on instrument. Information regarding preadmission statin or other lipid-lowering therapy was not consistently available. Preadmission antithrombotic use was defined as any antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy before admission and was recorded as a binary variable.

Baseline and follow-up CT scans were assessed by three neurologists, each with 3–8 years of experience in acute stroke imaging and ICH assessment. Haematoma volumes were obtained using slice-by-slice segmentation and three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction [13,22]. CT examinations were independently evaluated by two assessors, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Blinding to LDL-C values, clinical characteristics and outcomes was not formally implemented or documented. No formal inter-rater or intra-rater reliability analysis was performed.

Outcome

The primary outcome was early HE, defined as either an absolute increase in haematoma volume greater than 6 mL or a relative increase greater than 33% on follow-up CT compared with baseline CT [7,23,24]. The 25-hour follow-up window was selected to capture CT examinations performed within approximately 24 h after baseline imaging, allowing for routine clinical scheduling variation [18]. Haematoma volumes on baseline and follow-up CT were measured using 3D Slicer software by slice-by-slice segmentation followed by 3D reconstruction, as described in prior CT-based ICH volumetric studies [13,22]. Haemorrhage location was categorized as deep, lobar or infratentorial according to baseline CT [24]. Non-lobar ICH was defined as deep or infratentorial haemorrhage.

Ninety-day functional status was obtained from routine outpatient follow-up or through telephone or WeChat-based interviews. A standardized structured mRS interview, formal rater certification, duplicate scoring and central adjudication were not used. Poor functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of 3–6 [25]. Because the reliability of mRS ascertainment was not formally evaluated, functional outcome was treated as a secondary descriptive measure.

Exposure and covariates

The primary exposure was admission LDL-C level, measured in mmol/L. LDL-C was analysed as a continuous variable per 1 mmol/L lower concentration, by quartiles, and at prespecified clinical thresholds. Covariates were selected based on prior ICH literature, biological plausibility and availability before follow-up imaging. They included age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol use, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time, baseline GCS score, BHV, IVH, island sign, haemorrhage location, HDL-C and triglycerides. Total cholesterol was not included in the primary LDL-C model to reduce collinearity with LDL-C and related lipid measures.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test because most were non-normally distributed. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Analyses were conducted based on complete cases after excluding patients with missing key exposure, outcome or covariate data.

Modified Poisson regression with a log link and robust error variance was used to estimate relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for early HE [26,27]. LDL-C was coded so that the reported RR represented the change in HE risk associated with each 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C concentration. Covariates were selected a priori on clinical and methodological grounds, and no automated or outcome-driven variable-selection procedure was used. Hierarchical models were specified by sequentially increasing covariate adjustment. Model 1 included LDL-C and adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 additionally adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol drinking, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time and baseline GCS score. Model 3 additionally adjusted for BHV, IVH, island sign, haemorrhage location, HDL-C and triglycerides. Model 3 was prespecified as the primary analytic model.

LDL-C was additionally analysed in quartiles and according to clinically relevant thresholds of 1.4, 1.8, 2.0, and 2.6 mmol/L, selected on the basis of contemporary lipid management recommendations and previous studies in acute ICH [15,28]. In quartile analyses, the highest LDL-C quartile was used as the primary reference category. A test for trend was performed by entering the quartile variable as an ordinal term. For threshold analyses, patients below each threshold were compared with those at or above that threshold. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were used to examine the shape of the association between LDL-C and early HE. Three knots were placed at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the LDL-C distribution, and the cohort median LDL-C was used as the reference value. Spline models were adjusted for the same covariates as Model 3. The overall association was evaluated using a joint Wald test of all LDL-C spline terms, and nonlinearity was evaluated using a Wald test of the nonlinear spline term.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to haemorrhage location, hypertension status, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time of 3 h or less and BHV dichotomized at the cohort median of 10.32 mL. Location analyses included lobar versus non-lobar ICH, with deep ICH also examined separately. Effect modification was formally evaluated by adding a product term between continuous LDL-C and the subgroup variable to the fully adjusted model. Statistical significance within an individual subgroup was not interpreted as evidence of effect modification in the absence of a significant interaction test. All subgroup and interaction analyses were considered exploratory, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Prediction analyses compared three logistic regression models. The clinical-imaging model included age, sex, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time, baseline GCS score, log-transformed BHV, IVH, island sign and haemorrhage location. The LDL-C-enhanced model additionally included admission LDL-C. The extended lipid-clinical model further included HDL-C.

Apparent model performance was evaluated using predictions generated from models fitted in the full cohort. Internal validation was performed using stratified fivefold cross-validation repeated 100 times with a prespecified random seed of 20250723. Within each repetition, participants were randomly assigned to five mutually exclusive folds, stratified by HE status to preserve the event proportion across folds as closely as possible. Fold assignments were regenerated independently for each repetition. Each model was fitted using fourfolds and evaluated in the remaining held-out fold, such that each participant received one out-of-fold prediction in each repetition. The 100 out-of-fold predicted probabilities for each participant were averaged, and internally validated performance measures were calculated from these participant-level averaged out-of-fold predictions.

Discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and overall prediction error was quantified using the Brier score. Calibration-in-the-large was estimated from a logistic calibration model in which the coefficient of the logit-transformed predicted probability was fixed at 1; its ideal value is 0. The calibration slope was estimated from a logistic calibration model containing the logit-transformed predicted probability as the sole predictor; its ideal value is 1. A calibration slope below 1 indicates that predicted risks are too extreme. Confidence intervals for internally validated performance measures were obtained from 3,000 stratified bootstrap resamples of the participant-level outcome and averaged out-of-fold prediction pairs.

For nested apparent models, improvement in model fit was assessed using likelihood-ratio tests. Differences in apparent AUC were reported with 95% CIs obtained from 3,000 stratified bootstrap resamples, and corresponding p values were calculated using paired DeLong tests. Differences in Brier scores were evaluated using 3,000 stratified bootstrap resamples. Integrated discrimination improvement and continuous net reclassification improvement, including the event and non-event components, were calculated from apparent full-cohort predicted probabilities. Their 95% CIs and P values were obtained using 3,000 stratified bootstrap resamples. Because these reclassification measures were based on apparent predictions and may be optimistic, they were considered exploratory.

Decision-curve analysis (DCA) was performed using apparent full-cohort predicted probabilities across threshold probabilities ranging from 0.01 to 0.40. Net benefit was compared among the three prediction models and the treat-all and treat-none strategies. Because the decision curves were based on apparent rather than independently validated predictions, the analysis was interpreted as an exploratory assessment of potential clinical utility.

Sensitivity analyses included logistic regression as an alternative to modified Poisson regression; log transformation of baseline haematoma volume; exclusion of records meeting the study-defined lipid quality-control criteria; winsorization of LDL-C at the 1st and 99th percentiles; restriction to patients managed conservatively and additional adjustment for platelet count, fibrinogen and the international normalized ratio.

For the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis, the exposure was defined as LDL-C below 1.8 mmol/L. Propensity scores were estimated using age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol drinking, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time, admission GCS score, BHV, IVH, island sign and haemorrhage location. Stabilized average-treatment-effect weights were applied. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences, with an absolute standardized mean difference below 0.10 considered acceptable. A doubly adjusted weighted modified Poisson model containing the same covariates was used to estimate the weighted RR and robust 95% CI.

All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Regression modelling, RCS analyses, prediction model evaluation, IPTW analysis and figure generation were conducted using relevant R packages, including sandwich, lmtest, rms, pROC, caret, WeightIt, cobalt, ggplot2 and dcurves.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 1,167 patients admitted with acute spontaneous ICH, 926 had spontaneous ICH confirmed by clinical assessment and baseline CT within 6 h of symptom onset. Of these, 541 met all imaging, laboratory and complete-case eligibility criteria and were included in the analytic cohort. Early HE occurred in 62 patients (11.5%). The median age was 57 years (IQR, 51–66 years), and 330 patients (61.0%) were men. Haemorrhage location was deep in 404 patients (74.7%), lobar in 76 (14.0%), and infratentorial in 61 (11.3%). Compared with patients without HE, those with HE had a shorter onset-to-first CT time (median, 3.00 vs. 4.00 h; p < 0.001), a lower admission GCS score (median, 11.00 vs. 12.00; p = 0.007), a larger BHV (median, 12.73 vs. 9.93 mL; p = 0.013), more frequent preadmission antithrombotic use (11.3% vs. 3.5%; p = 0.013), and more frequent island sign (17.7% vs. 4.8%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). Poor functional outcome at 90 days was also more frequent among patients with HE (79.0% vs. 42.0%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). Median admission LDL-C was lower in patients with HE than in those without HE (2.17 [IQR, 1.57–2.63] vs. 2.45 [IQR, 1.92–3.07] mmol/L; p = 0.001) (Table 1; Figure S1).

Primary association between LDL-C and haematoma expansion

In modified Poisson regression models, lower admission LDL-C was consistently associated with a higher risk of early HE. In the unadjusted model, each 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C was associated with a 66% higher risk of HE (RR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.29–2.15; p < 0.001). This association persisted after adjustment for age and sex (Model 1: RR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.26–2.08; p < 0.001), and further adjustment for vascular risk factors, preadmission antithrombotic use, onset-to-first CT time and GCS score (Model 2: RR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.30–2.03; p < 0.001) (Table 2). In the prespecified primary model, which additionally included BHV, IVH, island sign, haemorrhage location, HDL-C and triglycerides, each 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C was associated with a 70% higher risk of early HE (Model 3: RR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.33–2.19; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Dose-response association, LDL-C quartiles, and clinical thresholds

RCS analysis using knots at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of LDL-C (1.45, 2.42 and 3.54 mmol/L) demonstrated an overall inverse association between LDL-C and early HE (P overall < 0.001), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P nonlinear = 0.38) (Figure 2B).

When LDL-C was analysed by quartiles, early HE occurred in 24 of 137 patients (17.5%) in Q1, 15 of 136 (11.0%) in Q2, 16 of 135 (11.9%) in Q3 and 7 of 133 (5.3%) in Q4 (Figure 2A). Compared with the highest LDL-C quartile (Q4) (Table 2), the lowest quartile was associated with substantially higher HE risk after full adjustment (Q1 vs. Q4: RR, 4.28; 95% CI, 2.01–9.12; p < 0.001). Adjusted risks were also higher for Q2 (RR, 2.29; 95% CI, 1.00–5.21; p = 0.049) and Q3 (RR, 3.28; 95% CI, 1.49–7.24; p = 0.003) (Table 2) compared with Q4. The trend per lower LDL-C quartile was significant (RR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.17–1.72; p < 0.001). In the reverse-reference analysis, Q4 was associated with a lower risk of HE than Q1(RR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.11–0.50; p < 0.001) (Table S1).

Clinical threshold analyses were consistent with the primary finding. Compared with patients with LDL-C concentrations at or above each threshold, the adjusted risk of HE was higher for LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L (RR, 2.09; 95% CI, 1.36–3.20; p < 0.001), < 2.0 mmol/L (RR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.08–2.53; p = 0.020) and < 2.6 mmol/L (RR, 2.10; 95% CI, 1.29–3.41; p = 0.003) (Table 3). The association for LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L was directionally similar but imprecise (RR, 1.72; 95% CI, 0.95–3.11; p = 0.073) (Table 3).

Subgroup and interaction analyses

The association between lower LDL-C and early HE was directionally consistent across most prespecified subgroups (Figure 3 and Table S2). For haemorrhage location, the adjusted RR per 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C was 1.73 (95% CI, 1.31–2.27) in non-lobar ICH and 1.52 (95% CI, 0.50–4.65) in lobar ICH, with no evidence of heterogeneity between the two groups (P for interaction = 0.351). The corresponding estimate in deep ICH was 1.71 (95% CI, 1.29–2.27) (Table S2) and was considered descriptive because deep ICH was nested within the non-lobar category.

Forest plot of adjusted RRs for early HE per 1 mmol/L lower LDL-C across subgroups. BHV was dichotomized at the cohort median of 10.32 mL.

The adjusted RR was 1.55 (95% CI, 1.12–2.15) among patients with hypertension and 1.96 (95% CI, 1.27–3.00) among those without hypertension (p for interaction = 0.38). Similarly, the estimates were 1.52 (95% CI, 1.12–2.08) for patients undergoing baseline CT within 3 h of symptom onset and 2.00 (95% CI, 1.10–3.65) for those imaged after 3 h (p for interaction = 0.71). There was also no evidence of effect modification by preadmission antithrombotic use (P for interaction = 0.651).

In contrast, heterogeneity was observed according to BHV dichotomized at the cohort median of 10.32 mL (p for interaction < 0.001). The adjusted RR was 2.89 (95% CI, 1.85–4.52) among patients with a baseline haematoma volume of 10.32 mL or less and 1.33 (95% CI, 0.99–1.78) among those with a larger haematoma. All subgroup and interaction analyses were exploratory (Figure 3 and Table S2).

Comparison with other lipid markers

In parallel lipid-marker models (Table 4), LDL-C showed the most stable association with early HE. Lower HDL-C was not associated with early HE after multivariable adjustment (RR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.50–1.69; p = 0.783). Lower total cholesterol and lower non-HDL-C were each associated with a smaller increase in HE risk (both RR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.01–1.59; p = 0.041). Ratio-based models demonstrated that a lower LDL-C/HDL-C ratio and a higher HDL-C/LDL-C ratio were also associated with early HE, whereas the triglyceride/HDL-C ratio was not (Table 4). Ratio-based findings were considered exploratory because of mathematical coupling between variables.

Prediction model performance

The clinical-imaging model yielded an apparent AUC of 0.795 (95% CI, 0.726–0.856) and an internally validated AUC of 0.758 (95% CI, 0.684–0.825) (Table S3), based on participant-level averaged out-of-fold predictions from repeated stratified fivefold cross-validation. The corresponding apparent and internally validated Brier scores were 0.083 and 0.088, respectively (Figure 4 and Table S3).

ROC curves comparing the clinical-imaging model, clinical-imaging plus LDL-C model and extended lipid-clinical model.

Adding LDL-C improved model fit (likelihood-ratio χ2, 11.599; p < 0.001) and reduced the apparent Brier score to 0.080 and the internally validated Brier score to 0.085. The apparent AUC increased to 0.808 (95% CI, 0.747–0.867), but the AUC increment was small and not statistically significant (difference, 0.013; bootstrap 95% CI, −0.007 to 0.034; paired DeLong p = 0.183) (Table S3-S4). The internally validated AUC was 0.769 (95% CI, 0.699–0.836). Calibration-in-the-large was close to 0 for both models (−0.008 and −0.007), and internally validated calibration slopes of 0.607–0.636 indicated that predicted risks remained too extreme, consistent with some degree of model overfitting (Figure S2 and Table S3-S4).

Further addition of HDL-C did not improve predictive performance. The extended lipid-clinical model had an apparent AUC of 0.808 (95% CI, 0.746–0.866), an internally validated AUC of 0.767 (95% CI, 0.697–0.833), an internally validated Brier score of 0.086 and a calibration slope of 0.624 (Figure 4 and Table S3). In exploratory reclassification analyses, the addition of LDL-C yielded an integrated discrimination improvement of 0.032 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.008–0.056; p = 0.011) and a continuous net reclassification improvement of 0.358 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.099–0.612; p = 0.008) (Table S4). The event component of the net reclassification improvement was not statistically significant (0.194; 95% CI, −0.065 to 0.452; p = 0.157), whereas the non-event component was 0.165 (95% CI, 0.073–0.253; p < 0.001) (Table S4). DCA showed no consistent net-benefit gain from adding LDL-C across threshold probabilities of 0.01–0.40 (Figure S3). Overall, adding LDL-C improved likelihood-based model fit and slightly reduced prediction error, but did not clearly improve discrimination or clinical net benefit.

Sensitivity analyses and model diagnostics

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary finding (Table S5). The association between lower LDL-C and HE remained directionally consistent when BHV was log-transformed (RR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.33–2.21; p < 0.001), after exclusion of lipid quality-control flags (RR, 1.80; 95% CI, 1.36–2.38; p < 0.001), after winsorization of LDL-C at the 1st and 99th percentiles (RR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.33–2.24; p < 0.001), among conservatively treated patients (RR, 2.13; 95% CI, 1.37–3.33; p < 0.001), and after additional adjustment for platelet count, fibrinogen and INR (RR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.20–2.05; p < 0.001) (Table S5). Logistic regression yielded a consistent association (OR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.36–2.99; p < 0.001) (Table S5).

In the IPTW analysis for LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L, all post-weighting absolute standardized mean differences were below 0.10 (Table S6). In the doubly adjusted weighted model, LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L remained associated with early HE (RR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.29–3.04; p = 0.002) (Table S5).

Additional quality-control diagnostics confirmed 62 HE events, 14 lipid quality-control flags, no missing values across the 541-patient analysis data set, and an E-value of 2.80 for the primary RR, with an E-value of 1.99 for the lower 95% CI bound (Table S7). Collinearity diagnostics did not indicate problematic multicollinearity in the primary model; and the maximum variance inflation factor was 1.45 (Table S8).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this retrospective cohort of patients with acute spontaneous ICH, lower admission LDL-C was independently associated with early HE. This association remained after adjustment for demographic characteristics, vascular risk factors, clinical severity, onset-to-first CT time, BHV, IVH, island sign, haemorrhage location, HDL-C and triglycerides. The finding was consistent across continuous, quartile-based, threshold-based and RCS analyses, and remained directionally stable in multiple sensitivity analyses.

The inverse association appeared more pronounced among patients with smaller baseline haematomas. One possible explanation is that, in patients with large baseline haematomas, mechanical tissue disruption, active bleeding, mass effect and other established determinants of expansion may predominate, reducing the relative contribution of LDL-C. Conversely, biological factors related to vascular integrity or haemostasis may be more readily detectable in patients with smaller haematomas. Nevertheless, these subgroup analyses were exploratory, several subgroups contained few HE events, multiple interactions were examined without adjustment for multiplicity, and most interaction tests, including that for lobar versus non-lobar location, were not statistically significant. The interaction with BHV should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires independent confirmation.

From a prediction perspective, adding LDL-C improved likelihood-based model fit and slightly reduced the Brier score, but the increase in AUC was small and not statistically significant. Internally validated calibration slopes were substantially below 1, indicating that the predicted risks remained too extreme and that some degree of overfitting was present. DCA did not demonstrate a consistent gain in net benefit. LDL-C should therefore be interpreted as a contextual biomarker associated with haematoma instability, rather than as a stand-alone prediction tool or a variable that is ready to guide acute treatment decisions.

Early HE remains a major determinant of deterioration and poor outcome after ICH [23,24]. Contemporary guidelines and reviews emphasize the central role of onset-to-imaging time, haematoma burden, antithrombotic exposure and imaging markers of unstable bleeding in HE risk assessment [5,7,29]. Our results suggest that admission LDL-C may add a systemic biological dimension to this established clinical-imaging framework but does not replace it.

Relation to previous studies

Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between circulating lipids and HE [14–17]. Rodriguez-Luna et al. found that lower LDL-C was associated with haematoma growth, early neurological deterioration and 3-month mortality after acute ICH [14]. A subsequent analysis from the Chinese Stroke Center Alliance similarly linked very low LDL-C with a higher risk of HE or in-hospital death [15]. In contrast, Cai et al. found no independent association between lipid indices and HE after adjustment for clinical and imaging factors [16]. More recently, Sato et al. reported an association with lower HDL-C rather than LDL-C, particularly in non-lobar ICH, suggesting that lipid-related signals may vary across populations, haemorrhage phenotypes and underlying small-vessel disease [17].

Our findings add to this literature by showing that the inverse association between LDL-C and HE persisted after adjustment for HDL-C, triglycerides, haemorrhage location, BHV and imaging markers, and was supported by dose-response and sensitivity analyses. However, the observational design cannot determine whether lower LDL-C is causally involved, reflects the acute physiological response or serves as a marker of underlying vascular, inflammatory, nutritional or treatment-related vulnerability. Prospective studies with serial lipid measurements and more complete characterization of lipid-lowering therapy, inflammation, nutritional status and small-vessel disease are needed to clarify these possibilities.

Potential biological interpretation

Several mechanisms may explain the observed association, although causality cannot be inferred from this study. Cholesterol and endothelial lipid composition contribute to membrane organization, caveolae-mediated transport and blood-brain barrier permeability [30]. Prospective population studies have also linked very low LDL-C concentrations to a higher risk of incident ICH [31]. In acute ICH, particularly in patients with hypertensive small-vessel disease, greater vascular vulnerability could plausibly impair haemostatic sealing and promote continued bleeding after vessel rupture [7,17].

Alternatively, admission LDL-C may be a marker of underlying biological vulnerability rather than a direct mediator. Acute systemic inflammation can reduce circulating total cholesterol and LDL-C concentrations, while malnutrition, frailty, comorbid illness and prior treatment may also influence lipid levels [32–35]. Associations between very low LDL-C and adverse outcomes or bleeding in selected populations may therefore reflect residual confounding or reverse causation [36]. Admission LDL-C may consequently represent an integrated marker of vascular, inflammatory, nutritional and treatment-related status rather than a single causal pathway.

This distinction is clinically important because LDL-C has different implications in ischaemic and haemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease. Intensive LDL-C lowering reduces recurrent cardiovascular events after atherosclerotic ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack [10,11], whereas the relationship between low LDL-C and haemorrhagic risk remains less certain. Although some observational data suggest increased bleeding risk at very low LDL-C concentrations [36], randomized and meta-analytic evidence does not support a uniform conclusion that lipid-lowering therapy inevitably increases ICH risk [10,12]. Serum LDL-C concentration should therefore not be conflated with the effects of lipid-lowering treatment, and our findings should not be interpreted as supporting acute lipid manipulation.

Haemorrhage location and small-vessel disease context

The association between lower LDL-C and HE was statistically significant in non-lobar and deep ICH but imprecise in lobar ICH. However, the formal interaction test did not support effect modification by haemorrhage location, and the lobar subgroup contained relatively few HE events. These findings should therefore be considered exploratory rather than evidence of a true location-specific association.

A location-dependent effect remains biologically plausible because deep and lobar ICH often reflect different small-vessel pathologies. Deep ICH is commonly associated with hypertensive arteriolosclerosis and lipohyalinosis, whereas lobar ICH is more frequently linked to cerebral amyloid angiopathy [5,37,38]. If admission LDL-C reflects vascular vulnerability, inflammatory-metabolic status or physiological reserve, its association with HE might differ across these disease contexts. Nevertheless, lipid-related associations may not be specific to LDL-C and may vary by cohort and haemorrhage phenotype [17]. Future studies integrating LDL-C, HDL-C, apolipoproteins, lipid-lowering treatment, inflammatory markers and imaging-defined small-vessel disease features are needed to clarify whether these associations reflect lipid biology, systemic vulnerability or haemorrhage subtype.

Clinical and prediction implications

The clinical appeal of LDL-C lies in its routine availability, low cost and ease of interpretation. In this study, adding admission LDL-C to a clinical-imaging model improved model fit and slightly reduced the Brier score, but the increase in AUC was small and not statistically significant. Internally validated calibration remained imperfect, and decision-curve analysis showed no consistent gain in net benefit across the evaluated threshold range. LDL-C may therefore provide complementary biological information but is not suitable as a stand-alone tool for acute management decisions.

These findings are better interpreted as biomarker evidence than as support for a clinical decision rule. Established predictors, including onset-to-imaging time, baseline haematoma volume, antithrombotic exposure, intraventricular extension and imaging markers of active or unstable bleeding, remain central to HE risk assessment [5,7,39]. Because clinical implementation requires adequate discrimination, calibration, clinical utility and external validation [40,41], LDL-C should be regarded as a contextual marker of haematoma instability rather than a replacement for established clinical and imaging predictors.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. Consecutive admissions were screened, HE was defined using established absolute and relative growth criteria and haematoma volume was measured by three-dimensional reconstruction. The analyses incorporated major clinical and imaging predictors and evaluated LDL-C continuously, by quartiles, at clinical thresholds, with restricted cubic splines, and in multiple sensitivity analyses. Prediction performance was assessed across discrimination, overall error, calibration, reclassification and decision-curve utility rather than by AUC alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-centre study, and admission LDL-C measured during acute illness may not accurately reflect habitual pre-ICH lipid exposure; residual confounding from unmeasured clinical, nutritional, inflammatory and treatment-related factors therefore remains possible. Second, CT haematoma segmentation was performed by multiple assessors, but no formal inter-rater or intra-rater reliability testing was conducted, and blinding to clinical and laboratory information was not formally implemented or documented. Third, follow-up CT timing varied within the prespecified window, and patients who underwent emergency surgery before an eligible follow-up CT were excluded. These features may have affected HE ascertainment, preferentially excluded patients with severe or rapidly progressive haemorrhage, and contributed to the relatively low observed HE rate, thereby introducing selection or spectrum bias. Fourth, 90-day modified Rankin Scale scores were obtained through routine mixed-mode follow-up without a standardized structured interview, certified assessors, duplicate scoring or central adjudication; functional outcome findings should therefore be considered descriptive. Fifth, only 62 HE events were available relative to the number of model parameters, and internally validated calibration slopes below 1 indicated some degree of overfitting. Sixth, subgroup analyses were exploratory, several strata contained few events and multiple interactions were examined without adjustment for multiplicity; the baseline haematoma volume interaction should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Finally, repeated cross-validation provided internal validation only. No independent cohort was available to replicate the association or externally validate discrimination, calibration and clinical utility, and the models should not be used for individual clinical decision-making before independent validation and, if necessary, recalibration.

Conclusions

Lower admission LDL-C was independently associated with early HE after spontaneous ICH. However, its incremental predictive value beyond established clinical and imaging variables was limited. LDL-C should therefore be considered a contextual biomarker of haematoma instability rather than a stand-alone predictor or therapeutic target. Prospective multicentre studies with independent cohorts are needed to replicate the association, externally validate and recalibrate the prediction models, and assess their transportability using standardized imaging assessment, serial lipid measurements and more comprehensive treatment, inflammatory and nutritional data before any clinical application.
