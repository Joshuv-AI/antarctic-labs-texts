Anterior part of fronto-temporal language network as the neural basis of morphologically structured word form processing: evidence from Korean morphologically complex word
Authors: Jeahong Kim, Kichun Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present fMRI study investigated which subregions of the visual word recognition network contribute to morphologically structured word form processing in Korean. Participants performed two separate lexical decision tasks in the scanner. In the Eojeol task, stimuli were three-syllable words composed of a disyllabic noun plus a case particle, whereas in the Noun task, stimuli were disyllabic (DSN) and trisyllabic (TSN) nouns. Behaviorally, reaction time was significantly longer in the Eojeol condition than in both DSN and TSN conditions, indicating greater processing demands for morphologically complex words, while accuracy did not differ across conditions. At the neural level, ROI analyses revealed that Eojeol processing elicited stronger activation than simple nouns in specific nodes of the visual word recognition related system, including the left inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis), anterior perisylvian cortex, anterior superior temporal sulcus, and anterior inferior temporal gyrus. These findings suggest that morphologically complex word recognition in Korean recruits additional resources within the visual word recognition network, highlighting the role of anterior fronto-temporal subregions in combining content and functional morphemes into unified lexical representations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12868-026-01028-z.

Introduction

Processing morphologically complex words, in which lexical stems are combined with grammatical morphemes such as case, agreement, or tense, is a key component of language comprehension because these forms provide the grammatical cues that support the assignment of syntactic roles and construct compositional meaning beyond lexical access. Unlike monomorphemic words, which primarily convey lexical semantics, morphologically complex words contain functional markers that contribute to the interpretation of structurally organized units. This process often described in terms of morpho-syntactic processing and has been associated with anterior regions of the fronto-temporal language network, including the inferior frontal gyrus and anterior temporal areas [5, 6, 8, 9, 12]. These regions widely considered to support the coordination of lexical information with higher-order linguistic representations.

Theoretical accounts converge on the view that anterior regions of the fronto-temporal language network (FTLN) play a central role in processing morphologically and grammatically structured inputs. In neurocognitive models, the left inferior frontal gyrus (LIFG) has been implicated in operations related to structured linguistic processing, including rule-based computations, selection, and control processes [5, 6, 8, 9, 22, 25, 28]. In parallel, anterior temporal regions—including the superior temporal pole, anterior superior temporal sulcus (STS), and anterior middle temporal gyrus (MTG)—have been linked to combinatorial semantic processing and the integration of lexical information [25]. Together, these regions are thought to contribute to the processing of structured linguistic representations, particularly when lexical information must be evaluated in relation to morpho-syntactic features. Accordingly, these accounts predict that anterior fronto-temporal circuits are more strongly engaged when morpho-syntactic computations are required, beyond processing driven primarily by lexical access. Nevertheless, it remains an open question whether these mechanisms, established largely in Indo-European languages, generalize to typologically different systems where morpho-syntactic information is obligatorily encoded at the word level.

Cross-linguistic diversity provides a critical testing ground for evaluating the generality of these mechanisms. Korean, an agglutinative language, encodes morpho-syntactic information in the orthographic unit known as the Eojeol [15, 16, 19, 27]. An Eojeol obligatorily combines lexical stems with case markers or inflectional endings. For example, the noun-based Eojeol 식비는 (“as for the meal cost”) unifies the stem 식비 (meal cost) with the topic particle -는. Unlike bare nouns, which can be recognized as independent lexical entries, Eojeols present lexical stems together with grammatical markers within a single orthographic unit. This structural property may place additional processing demands on the reader, as lexical and morpho-syntactic information must be jointly evaluated during recognition. At the same time, because Eojeols are presented without sentential context in many experimental paradigms, they may also differ from bare nouns in terms of familiarity or naturalness. Thus, Eojeols provide a useful test case for examining how the brain responds to morphologically structured forms, while also requiring careful consideration of alternative factors such as processing cost and contextual constraints.

Recent neuroimaging studies have begun to characterize the neural basis of Eojeol processing. Using fMRI, [13] demonstrated that morphological boundary detection in noun-based Eojeols reliably engaged the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and anterior temporal lobe (ATL), suggesting sensitivity of these regions to morphologically structured inputs. Similarly, [16] reported IFG and ATL recruitment and showed that individual differences in morphological processing performance correlated with IFG activation, underscoring the contribution of frontal mechanisms to processing morphologically complex forms. Complementing these hemodynamic findings, Kim et al., [14] employed ERP and showed that morpheme boundary effects in noun-based Eojeols were reflected in an early negativity (N150), corresponding to the ELAN (Early Left Anterior Negativity), whose neural generators have been associated with the IFG and ATL [5, 7]. In contrast, [17] adopted a broader perspective by comparing noun-based and predicate-based Eojeols, thereby targeting overall Eojeol processing rather than focusing solely on noun-based forms. This design revealed whole-brain activation patterns that were less centered on the classical fronto-temporal language network: overlapping activity was observed in the lingual gyrus and pre/postcentral gyri, with predicate-based Eojeols additionally eliciting stronger bilateral IFG responses. These findings suggest that when Eojeol processing is considered globally, morpho-syntactic complexity may engage a broader set of neural systems, including visual and sensorimotor regions, rather than being restricted to anterior language areas. However, because both conditions involved morphologically complex Eojeols only, that study could not determine whether anterior recruitment was specific to Eojeol-level morpho-syntactic structure or partly attributable to stem-related lexical processing.

The present study was designed to address this gap by directly comparing noun-based Eojeols with simple nouns in visual lexical decision tasks. Participants completed two tasks: an Eojeol task with three-syllable noun-based Eojeols (two-syllable noun plus case particle) and a Noun task with disyllabic (DSN) and trisyllabic (TSN) nouns. The tasks were administered separately, with an unrelated filler task (i.e., flanker task) inserted between them. This separated two task design was intended to reduce potential confounds, such as strategy switching between morpho-syntactic and lexical judgments or response-criterion shifts across conditions, making it more plausible that neural responses reflected each lexical condition. Behavioral accuracy and reaction times were collected alongside fMRI data. Based on prior models of morpho-syntactic computations and unification demands in the anterior FTLN, we predicted that Eojeol recognition would yield longer reaction times and stronger activation in the anterior FTLN, including the LIFG, superior temporal pole, anterior STS, and anterior temporal cortex. In this way, the present study was designed to test whether visual word recognition in Korean engages morpho-syntactic processing demands beyond those observed for simple nouns, clarifying the functional role of the anterior fronto-temporal language network. Because ROI (Region of Interest) definitions can materially influence inference, we defined ROI sets a priori using independent sources (a prior Eojeol study, meta-analytic maps, and an established reading-network study). Full details of ROI selection and extraction procedures are provided in Sect. "ROI selection and extraction.

Methods

Participants

Tweenty-three healthy right-handed volunteers participated in the experiment. Two participants were excluded due to the poor performance (i.e., accuracy less than 60%), leaving a total of 21 intact participants (females: 7; age range: 20–29; age mean: 24, age sd: 2.4). All participants were right-handed native Korean speakers with normal or corrected to normal visual acuity. None of them reported psychiatric or neurological records. Every participants provided written informed consent before the experiment. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Korea University.

Table 1 provides a summary of the experimental stimuli across the three lexical conditions. A total of 136 Korean words were selected from the Korean Word Database (2001), including 34 disyllabic noun words (DSN), 34 trisyllabic noun words (TSN), and 68 Eojeols. While the DSN and TSN conditions consisted of simple noun words, the Eojeol condition included items composed of a disyllabic noun stem combined with a one-syllable functional morpheme (case particle). In the lexical decision task with Eojeol (Eojeol task), 68 Eojeols and 68 nonwords served as the experimental stimuli. In the lexical decision task with simple nouns (Noun task), 34 disyllabic nouns and 34 trisyllabic nouns were used together with the same set of 68 nonwords employed in the Eojeol task. Thus, each task involved 136 stimuli in total.

To verify that the lexical factors were comparable across conditions, we conducted one-way ANOVAs on three lexical variables: first-syllable frequency, stem frequency, and syllable composition probability. The analyses revealed no significant differences among the three lexical conditions for first-syllable frequency (F(2, 133) = 1.32, p = .27), stem frequency (F(2, 133) = 0.43, p = .66), or mean syllable composition probability (F(2, 133) = 1.22, p = .30). Thus, the three lexical factors were statistically matched across conditions (Table 1).

Syllable composition probability was calculated as the likelihood that a particular syllable follows a given syllable within a word. In practice, this was estimated by dividing the frequency with which a specific two-syllable sequence occurred in a large corpus by the total frequency of all sequences beginning with the same first syllable. For disyllabic words, this measure corresponded to the probability of the single syllable transition within the word. For trisyllabic words and Eojeols, which contain two transitions, the average of the two probabilities was taken to represent the syllable composition probability.

To evaluate the potential risk of participants misclassifying less frequent real words as nonwords—which could skew the perceived word-to-nonword ratio and consequently alter lexical decision criteria during Korean word recognition [1, 20] (~ 15 million words; National Institute of the Korean Language, 2001). Crucially, when applying a proportional low-frequency threshold adjusted for corpus size used in this study (e.g., < 30 occurrences with ~ 15 million words, extrapolated from the < 100 threshold used in a 42-million-word corpus study by [18]), only a negligible proportion of stimuli fell into this category (DSN: 0%; TSN: 8.8%; Eojeols: 1.5%). Furthermore, item-level behavioral data revealed that almost all of these low-frequency items were successfully recognized as legitimate words by the native Korean participants (accuracy ≥ 90%, with only one exception). Because the native participants successfully distinguished these items—preventing the ratio-skewing effects often observed in L2 populations with relatively smaller lexicons—the implemented 1:1 ratio accurately reflected the participants’ perceived ratio. This ensures that ratio-driven processing demands did not confound the observed condition-specific neural activations.

Experimental design

A rapid event-related fMRI design was employed with two separate lexical decision tasks. In the Eojeol task, stimuli were three-syllable words consisting of a disyllabic noun stem followed by a one-syllable case particle. In the Noun task, stimuli were simple nouns, either disyllabic (DSN) or trisyllabic (TSN). The two tasks were administered independently and were temporally separated by an unrelated cognitive filler task to minimize potential carry-over effects.

Importantly, the noun and Eojeol conditions were not intermixed within a single lexical decision task but were instead administered as two separate task runs. Intermixing the conditions would have required participants to alternate between different processing sets—switching from morpho-syntactic heuristics for Eojeols to purely lexical judgments for nouns—thereby imposing additional control demands unrelated to the lexical contrasts of interest. Moreover, such interleaving could induce response-criterion shifts, whereby expectations derived from one condition (e.g., the presence of case markers as a cue for wordhood) biased judgments in the other condition. By separating the conditions into two independent task runs, with an unrelated filler task inserted between them, we minimized strategy switching and cross-condition interference, ensuring that the neural responses could be more clearly attributed to each lexical condition.

Although the order of tasks was fixed (Eojeol task first, Noun task second), we examined possible order effects by comparing reaction times and accuracy rates for the identical nonword stimuli that were used in both tasks. Potential order effects could have included practice effects (improved performance due to repeated exposure), fatigue effects (slower or less accurate responses in the later task), or transfer effects (strategies adopted in the first task influencing responses in the second). The results showed that the differences were minimal in magnitude and not statistically significant, suggesting that order effects were negligible (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In each trial, the target stimulus was presented at the center of the screen for 500 ms. In the Eojeol task, target stimuli consisted of Eojeol, whereas in the Noun task, disyllabic (DSN) and trisyllabic (TSN) nouns were presented. Nonword and fixation (“+”) trials were included in both tasks to provide comparable baselines. Following each target presentation, a blank screen with a jittered interval ranging from 1500 ms to 7500 ms served as the null condition. The duration of this blank interval was pseudo-randomized and inserted between task trials to reduce temporal predictability and to optimize event-related design efficiency using Optseq software (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/optseq/). All participants achieved accuracies above 80% during the practice session.

Image acquisition

Images were collected using a Simens Magnetom Trio 3T MRI scanner at the Korea University Brain Imaging Center, Seoul, South Korea. A T2*-weighted-gradient Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) sequence was used to acquire Blood Oxygenated Level Dependent (BOLD) fMRI images with the following parameters: TR = 2000ms; TE = 20ms; Flip Angle = 90°; Field of View = 240 mm; slice thickness = 3 mm, no gap 42 slices; matrix size = 80 × 80; and voxel size = 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm. High-resolution anatomical images (1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm) were acquired for each participant, with a T1-weighted, 3D MPRAGE (Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Gradient-Echo) sequence (TR = 1900ms; TE = 2.52ms; Flip Angle = 90°; Field of View = 256 mm; matrix size = 256 × 256).

fMRI data preprocessing and 1st level analysis

Functional image preprocessing and first-level modeling were conducted using SPM12 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The first three EPI volumes were discarded to allow for T1 equilibration. The remaining volumes were realigned to correct for head motion and adjusted for slice acquisition timing. Each participant’s functional images were coregistered to their T1-weighted anatomical scan. Structural images were segmented into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and the resulting deformation fields were applied to normalize the functional volumes to the MNI template (resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm voxels). Normalized images were then smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM isotropic Gaussian kernel.

At the first level, only correctly responded trials were modeled as events for the corresponding conditions (Eojeol, DSN, TSN, Fixation, Nonword); incorrect responses were excluded. Stimulus onsets were modeled with zero duration and convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function. Six motion parameters obtained from realignment were included as nuisance regressors. All findings and labels for activated brain regions were reported according to the automated anatomical atlas (AAL) (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002).

ROI selection and extraction

ROI analyses were designed to address three complementary goals: (i) to evaluate whether neural signatures previously reported for noun-based Eojeol processing replicate in the present dataset, (ii) to test a priori hypotheses about canonical lexical and syntactic components of the reading/language network using meta-analytic, task-independent evidence, and (iii) to facilitate comparability with an established reading-network parcellation widely used in prior fMRI work. Our multi-set ROI strategy, incorporating targeted benchmarking, meta-analytic validity, and network-level context, was designed to minimize reliance on any single ROI definition scheme, thereby enhancing the robustness and reproducibility of our findings. Critically, all ROI definitions were conducted entirely independently of the present dataset to avoid any circularity.

Accordingly, we defined four ROI sets based on independent sources (Fig. 1): (a) Eojeol-sensitive ROIs from an earlier whole-brain Eojeol study [17] to provide a targeted replication/benchmark of regions implicated in noun-based Eojeol processing; (b) Visual-word ROIs from Neurosynth association maps for “visual word” to capture core regions reliably linked to visual word recognition across tasks and languages; (c) Syntactic ROIs from Neurosynth association maps for “syntactic” to index regions consistently associated with morpho-syntactic computation and sentence-level structure building; and (d) Reading-network ROIs following Stevens et al., (2017) to situate our effects within a well-characterized reading network, including VWFA and the anterior-to-posterior STS axis. This multi-set strategy reduces dependence on a single ROI definition scheme while enabling converging tests of Eojeol-related effects across Eojeol-specific, meta-analytic, and canonical reading-network nodes.

Noun-based Eojeol processing ROIs were defined using coordinates reported in a recent whole-brain study [17]. That study identified left-hemisphere regions in the Precentral gyrus, middle frontal gyrus (MFG), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and bilateral lingual gyri. ROIs for these regions were constructed as 6-mm radius geodesic discs centered on the following MNI coordinates: Precentral (− 53, 27, 16), MFG (− 49, − 57, 28), mPFC (− 56, − 43, 31), left lingual (− 61, − 33, −15), and right lingual (− 61, − 33, −15).

To further investigate the neural substrates of Eojeol versus single noun processing within lexical processing nodes, we constructed additional ROIs associated with visual word processing and syntactic processing. Coordinates for these ROIs were identified using the Neurosynth association test activation maps (Yarkoni et al., 2011), based on the search terms “visual word” (117 studies) and “syntactic” (169 studies). Peaks from the visual word map included two foci in the left inferior frontal gyrus triangularis (IFG_tri: −46, 26, 14; −32, 34, 6), the middle occipital cortex (MOC: −24, − 58, 38), inferior parietal lobule (IPL: −44, − 44, 44), supplementary motor area (SMA: −2, − 14, 46), anterior inferior temporal gyrus (aITG: −42, − 44, −14), posterior inferior temporal gyrus (pITG: −44, − 14, −32), and perisylvian cortex (PSC: −52, 8, − 2). Peaks from the syntactic map included the angular gyrus (AG: −44, − 56, 26), IFG pars opercularis (IFG_oper: −50, 16, 18), precentral gyrus (− 38, 2, 48), SMA (− 10, 10, 58), and posterior STS (pSTS: −58, − 44, 6).

Additionally, to enable comparisons with a recent study of the reading network (Steven et al., 2017), we defined ROIs in the VWFA and anterior, middle, and posterior STS. These regions corresponded to peak activations for viewing words compared to pictures of objects in that study, centered on the following coordinates: aSTS (− 54, 9, − 20), mSTS (− 53, − 18, −10), pSTS (− 52, − 40, 5), and VWFA (− 45, − 57, −12).

For each participant, trial-wise estimates were obtained using a beta-series GLM implemented in SPM12 (MATLAB). Trial-specific regressors were modeled with SPM’s canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF; with temporal derivatives when applicable). For each trial, beta weights associated with the corresponding basis functions were extracted, and trial amplitude was defined as the sum across basis functions. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in Analyze format as paired header/image files, and mean signal values within each ROI were computed for every trial using spm_summarise. To ensure robust extraction, absolute file paths were reconstructed to avoid resolution errors, and trials with unmatched regressors or missing ROI files were excluded. The resulting dataset contained participant ID (21 participants), session, condition (Eojeol, DSN, TSN, Fixation, Nonword), trial index and onset, ROI identity, trial amplitude, and basis-function–specific values. These trial-wise ROI measures served as the dependent variables in subsequent mixed-effects analyses.

It should be noted that the purpose of the ROI analysis was not to characterize functional network structure, but to test whether theoretically motivated regions showed differential sensitivity to morphologically structured word forms. We acknowledge that combining coordinates from different sources may introduce some heterogeneity; however, this approach allowed us to capture functionally relevant regions consistently implicated in prior literature.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data were analyzed separately for reaction times (RT) and accuracy (ACC). RTs from correct trials only were submitted to linear mixed-effects regression models with Condition (DSN, TSN, Eojeol) as a fixed effect and Participant and Item as crossed random intercepts. Accuracy (correct = 1, incorrect = 0) was analyzed with a binomial logistic mixed-effects model with the same fixed and random-effects structure. Because the behavioral task did not include a fixation baseline, fixation was not modeled for either RT or ACC. For post-hoc analysis, we report estimated marginal means with standard errors, and FDR-adjusted pairwise z-tests. Where appropriate, standardized effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d derived from emmeans) are also provided to aid interpretability.

For ROI analyses, trial-wise beta estimates were extracted for each condition and submitted to mixed-effects models with Condition (DSN, TSN, Eojeol, Fix) as a fixed factor and Participant and Item as random intercepts. In contrast to the behavioral analyses, fixation (“+”) trials were included as an explicit neural baseline. It should be noted that lexical stimuli differed across DSN, TSN, and Eojeol by design, whereas fixation trials involved a single non-lexical symbol; therefore, random intercepts for both Subject and Target were included to account for item-specific variability despite the non-overlap of items across lexical conditions. A maximal random-effects structure (e.g., including random slopes for conditions) was not employed; rather, the random-intercepts-only structure was selected to accommodate the nesting of items within conditions and to reliably prevent non-convergence or singularity issues during model fitting.

Omnibus tests of fixed effects in linear mixed-effects models used Kenward–Roger or Satterthwaite approximations for denominator degrees of freedom, as implemented in the lmerTest and afex packages, yielding F-tests. For logistic models of accuracy, fixed effects were evaluated with Wald z-tests or χ² tests where appropriate. Post-hoc contrasts were conducted using estimated marginal means with the emmeans package; pairwise comparisons were two-sided, evaluated with asymptotic z-tests (df = ∞ by design), and controlled for the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. This approach provides conservative omnibus inference and robust pairwise contrasts, particularly appropriate given the large sample size, unequal numbers of trials across conditions, and the use of distinct stimulus sets for DSN, TSN, and Eojeol versus the single-symbol fixation baseline.

All analyses were conducted in R (lme4 for mixed-effects models, lmerTest/afex for omnibus tests and df approximation, and emmeans for marginal means and pairwise comparisons), with a two-tailed α level of 0.05.

Results

Behavioral results

Accuracy rates (ACC) and reaction times (RT) of correct trials and were collected as the participants performed the lexical decision task in the scanner. The ACC showed no significant main effect of Type [χ²(2) = 1.69, p = .430]. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed no significant differences among the conditions: the ACC rate of the Eojeol (M = 90.1%, SE = 0.9) did not differ significantly from the DSN (M = 92.3%, SE = 1.0; p = .556) or the TSN (M = 89.6%, SE = 1.1; p = .795), and there was no difference between the DSN and TSN conditions (p = .556) (Fig. 2). The RTs showed a significant main effect of Type [χ²(2) = 14.64, p < .001]. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the RTs in the Eojeol condition (M = 684 ms, SE = 5.13) were significantly longer than both the DSN (M = 639 ms, SE = 6.74; p = .004, d = 0.32) and the TSN (M = 635 ms, SE = 6.69; p = .004, d = 0.33). No significant difference was observed between the DSN and TSN conditions (p = .946, d = 0.01) (Fig. 2).

Linear mixed-effects (LME) analyses revealed significant effects of lexical condition across multiple ROIs (see Supplementary Table 1 for main effect result). In the left inferior frontal gyrus pars opercularis (IFG) and anterior part of left perisylvian cortex (PSC), activation was significantly greater for the Eojeol condition compared to both the disyllabic noun (DSN) and trisyllabic noun (TSN) conditions (p < .05), with no reliable difference between DSN and TSN. A similar pattern was observed in the left anterior superior temporal sulcus (aSTS) and left inferior temporal gyrus (ITG), where Eojeol elicited stronger activation than both noun conditions (p < .05), with significant difference between DSN and TSN (see Fig. 3; Table 2).

Together, these results indicate that morpho-syntactically structured Eojeols consistently recruited stronger engagement of the anterior part of perisylvian language network—including frontal and temporal components—than single noun words, independent of syllable length.

Linear mixed-effects (LME) analyses revealed that the activation patterns in the left pSTS, MFG, and lingual gyrus diverged from the Eojeol-dominant effects observed in the anterior part of FTLN ROIs (see Supplementary Table 1 for main effect result). In the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), no significant differences were observed among the Eojeol, disyllabic noun (DSN), and trisyllabic noun (TSN) conditions (p > .1). In addition, all lexical conditions showed stronger responses compared to fixation (p < .001). Similarly, in the left middle frontal gyrus (MFG), contrasts among the three lexical conditions were not significant (p > .5), indicating that these regions did not discriminate between Eojeol and single noun processing (see Fig. 4; Table 3).

In contrast, the left lingual gyrus exhibited a distinct pattern related to syllable length. Activation was significantly greater for DSN relative to TSN (p < .001), and Eojeol (p < .001) whereas Eojeol responses were comparable to TSN (p = .96). This suggests that the lingual gyrus was primarily sensitive to possible orthographic/phonological complexity associated with syllable length, rather than to morpho-syntactic composition. Taken together, these findings highlight a different activation profile from the Eojeol-dominant engagement seen in perisylvian regions, underscoring that pSTS and MFG were insensitive to lexical type contrasts, while the lingual gyrus reflected a syllable length effect (see Fig. 4; Table 3).

Discussion

The present study aimed to identify neural regions associated with the processing of noun-based Eojeols by directly contrasting them with simple nouns, with particular focus on whether anterior portions of the fronto-temporal language network (FTLN) are selectively engaged during Eojeol recognition. Behaviorally, reaction times were significantly longer for Eojeols than for disyllabic (DSN) and trisyllabic (TSN) nouns, consistent with the increased processing demands associated with morphologically structured forms, whereas accuracy remained comparable across conditions. Neurally, Eojeol recognition elicited stronger activation in anterior portions of the FTLN, including the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and anterior temporal cortex. In contrast, posterior and occipital regions such as the posterior temporal cortex and the lingual gyrus were engaged across conditions, consistent with domain-general orthographic and lexical processes. This pattern suggests that morphologically complex word recognition recruits anterior computations plausibly related to the morpho-syntactic unification of lexical stems with grammatical morphemes, which is likely to entail associated processing demands, while drawing on general lexical mechanisms shared with simple nouns.

This interpretation aligns with neurocognitive models that assign complementary roles to anterior FTLN subregions. The IFG, particularly pars opercularis, has been associated with structured linguistic processing and control, while the ATL supports the combinatorial semantic processing and integration of lexical information [5, 6, 8, 9, 22, 25, 28]. Evidence from prior Korean studies converges with this interpretation. Kim et al., [13] demonstrated that morphological boundary detection in noun-based Eojeols reliably engaged both the IFG and ATL, suggesting their role in processing morphologically structured inputs. Kim and Nam, [16] similarly reported IFG and ATL recruitment, with IFG activation correlating with individual differences in morphological boundary detection performance. Converging electrophysiological evidence adds further support: ([14] showed that noun-based Eojeols elicited an early negativity (N150/ELAN), whose neural generators have been localized to the IFG and ATL [5, 7]. Together, these findings substantiate the claim that frontal and anterior temporal regions form a core system to processing lexical stems with case particles.

In addition to the anterior results, the present study also revealed that the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) was robustly engaged across all three conditions—Eojeols, TSN, and DSN—with no significant differences among them. This pattern suggests that pSTS supports processes common to word recognition irrespective of morphological complexity. Rather than serving a condition-specific role, the pSTS may function as a lexical–semantic hub, mediating the mapping from orthographic or phonological forms onto meaning while supporting the integration of word-form information with semantic representations [2, 4, 11, 21, 29]. Such a domain-general role would explain its consistent activation across conditions, while processing demands associated with morphologically structured forms appear to engage anterior FTLN regions more strongly. This functional division of labor suggests that the pSTS underpins lexical access and semantic mapping, whereas anterior regions contribute the additional computations required for unifying lexical stems with grammatical morphemes in Eojeols.

Crucially, this functional division of labor between anterior and posterior language regions can be further contextualized within contemporary network-based models of language processing [5, 10]. Conversely to posterior regions such as the pSTS—which demonstrated domain-general lexical engagement across all conditions and connect to the frontal cortex via the dorsal stream (primarily through the arcuate fasciculus and superior longitudinal fasciculus)—the anterior network, including the IFG and ATL, is structurally anchored within the ventral stream. Building on this framework, our findings suggest that the morpho-syntactic unification of Eojeols is consistent with the engagement of the ventral fronto-temporal network. Several specific white matter pathways may support this communication. While the uncinate fasciculus and the extreme capsule fiber system (a key intrahemispheric tract facilitating fronto-temporal connectivity) are canonical candidates, their precise contributions to morpho-syntactic processing remain subject to ongoing investigation. Furthermore, considering the visual word recognition demands of the current paradigm, an extended network of ventral tracts—such as the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF) and the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF)—likely cooperates to facilitate these processes. Importantly, because our analyses rely on regional fMRI activations without concurrent tractography, we cannot definitively assign our functional findings to specific structural pathways. Thus, this neuroanatomical mapping should be viewed as a theoretical context rather than direct evidence of specific white matter involvement.

In line with this anterior to posterior profile, the present findings illuminate the functional characteristics of the left lingual gyrus, a region consistently implicated in visual–orthographic processing [3, 23, 24, 26]. Specifically, the current pattern provides additional context for the lingual gyrus activation reported by Kim et al., [17], who contrasted noun-based and predicate-based Eojeols to examine overall Eojeol processing. That study identified overlapping activation in the bilateral lingual gyrus and pre/postcentral gyri, with predicate-based Eojeols eliciting stronger bilateral IFG responses. While their results demonstrated that morpho-syntactic complexity modulates brain activity, the present data clarify the nature of lingual involvement: activation in the left lingual gyrus was significantly lower for disyllabic nouns than for both trisyllabic nouns and Eojeols, which did not differ from each other. This pattern suggests that lingual activity primarily reflects syllable length or orthographic load rather than morpho-syntactic processing demands, thereby enriching the interpretation of the previous findings by indicating that the occipital response is driven by domain-general visual factors rather than Eojeol-specific morpho-syntactic computations.

Taken together, these results indicate that posterior occipito-temporal regions, including the pSTS and lingual gyrus, contribute to domain-general aspects of visual word recognition such as lexical–semantic mapping and orthographic load, rather than to morpho-syntactic unification. Beyond these posterior regions, the other ROIs included in the analysis also did not show preferential activation for Eojeols (Supplementary Fig. 2–4). Although they were engaged to varying degrees across conditions, none exhibited the preferential enhancement observed in anterior FTLN regions. This pattern underscores that the IFG and ATL are particularly sensitive to morphologically structured word processing, whereas other regions primarily support more general aspects of lexical access and visual word recognition.

One limitation is that the presence of case particles in Eojeols may have contributed to anterior activations through mechanisms beyond morpho-syntactic processing. On the one hand, case particles could have been exploited as heuristic cues during lexical decision, providing partial support for judgments not only when Eojeols were compared with simple nouns (DSN, TSN) but also when contrasted with nonword controls in the separated tasks. On the other hand, certain particles also carry discourse-level or pragmatic functions, which may elicit processing demands not directly tied to morpho-syntactic structure. In addition, Eojeols may differ from simple nouns not only in their structural properties but also in familiarity or contextual naturalness, particularly when presented in isolation. While this isolated presentation might initially induce a more conservative decision criterion or task-related expectations, all participants achieved high accuracy (> 80%) during the practice session. This indicates that participants had sufficiently habituated to the task constraints and established stable response criteria prior to the fMRI runs. Nevertheless, such differences could increase processing demands and contribute to anterior FTLN activation independently of morpho-syntactic factors. Furthermore, the structural complexity of Eojeols intrinsically requires greater segmentation efforts compared to simple nouns. Therefore, the observed anterior fronto-temporal activations likely reflect an integration of morpho-syntactic computations and the domain-general cognitive load required to parse these complex word forms. In addition, theoretical accounts such as Matchin and Hickok [22] posit partly dissociable contributions of anterior FTLN subregions, assigning more syntactic and combinatorial control functions to posterior portions of the IFG (including the triangularis and opercularis) and more conceptual–semantic composition to the anterior superior temporal sulcus (aSTS). This functional differentiation suggests that the observed activation patterns likely reflect multiple interacting linguistic and cognitive processes, rather than a single unified mechanism. While our separated-run design successfully minimized major confounds—such as strategy switching and response-criterion shifts across conditions—the potential influence of these interacting processes cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, caution is needed in interpreting the role of the anterior FTLN as reflecting processes specific to morphologically structured word forms. Future studies using context-rich paradigms or more tightly controlled familiarity manipulations will be necessary to disentangle these factors more precisely.

Crucially, a significant methodological limitation of the current design is the fixed task order, in which the Eojeol task was always administered before the noun task. This fixed sequence was deliberately chosen to minimize expectation biases during the critical morpho-syntactic condition; had the noun task been presented first, the sudden appearance of case particles in the subsequent Eojeol run might have induced an artificial ‘particle-search’ strategy. While this design prioritized the naturalness of Eojeol processing, it comes with the trade-off of potential sequential biases in the subsequent noun task. Although the stable accuracy and reaction times for identical nonword stimuli indicate that basic sensorimotor execution was maintained across runs, this simple baseline cannot rule out word-specific sequential confounds. Specifically, we cannot entirely exclude the potential influence of task-set habituation, cognitive fatigue, or strategy shifts between the morphologically complex Eojeols and simple nouns. Furthermore, moving beyond localized fMRI spatial maps, incorporating recent multimodal approaches that integrate Large Language Models (LLMs), EEG, and eye-tracking could offer a more dynamic, computationally grounded framework for decoding real-time neural states during complex word processing [30, 31].

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that noun-based Eojeol recognition engages anterior FTLN regions, including the IFG and ATL, more strongly than simple noun recognition. These findings suggest that anterior fronto-temporal regions are sensitive to morphologically structured word forms and may support additional processing demands associated with evaluating lexical stems together with grammatical morphemes. By identifying both shared and condition-sensitive neural responses, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how complex words are processed in the brain. More broadly, Korean Eojeols provide a valuable cross-linguistic test case for examining how morphologically structured inputs are represented and processed during visual word recognition.
