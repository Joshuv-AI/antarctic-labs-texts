Imperfect diffusion-controlled reactions on a torus and on a pair of balls
Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We employ a general spectral approach based on the Steklov eigenbasis to describe imperfect diffusion-controlled reactions on bounded reactive targets in three dimensions. The steady-state concentration and the total diffusive flux onto the target are expressed in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the exterior Steklov problem. In particular, the eigenvalues are shown to provide the geometric lengthscales of the target that are relevant for diffusion-controlled reactions. Using toroidal and bispherical coordinates, we propose an efficient procedure for analyzing and solving numerically this spectral problem for an arbitrary torus and a pair of balls, respectively. A simple two-term approximation for the diffusive flux is established and validated. Implications of these results in the context of chemical physics and beyond are discussed.

I
Introduction

Diffusion-controlled reactions play an important role in various
natural phenomena and chemical industry

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. In his seminal work,
Smoluchowski unveiled the importance of diffusion as a mixing
mechanism for reactant molecules and established the first
mathematical framework to describe physical, chemical and biological
processes, in which the diffusive transport is the rate-limiting step

[
5
]
. While his original work was focused on the
coagulation kinetics, the proposed framework found numerous
applications in heterogeneous catalysis, recombination reactions,
etc. (see reviews

[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]

and references therein). In a nutshell, the Smoluchowski’s approach
describes multiple point-like species
A
that diffuse independently
from each other with a constant diffusion coefficient
D
and
instantly react upon hitting an immobile catalytic sphere
B
of
radius
R
:
A+B\to B
. Such an immediate reaction ensures zero
concentration of species
A
on the perfectly reactive target.
Solving the diffusion equation with a prescribed concentration
C_{A}

at infinity, Smoluchowski determined the time-dependent concentration
of species
A
, as well as their diffusive flux
J
onto the target,
which can be interpreted as the overall reaction rate. In the
steady-state regime, his famous relation reads

J=4\pi DC_{A}R,

(1)

in which the flux is proportional to the size of the target, not to
its surface area. This is a signature of diffusion-limited reactions.

The respective roles of the geometric shape of the target and of its
reactivity have been thoroughly investigated. On one hand, Collins
and Kimball introduced the notion of partial reactivity of the target
by replacing the Dirichlet boundary condition,
C_{A}=0
, by a more
general Robin boundary condition,

-D\partial_{n}C_{A}=\kappa\,C_{A}\quad\textrm{on the target},

(2)

where
\partial_{n}=(\bm{n}\cdot\nabla)
is the normal derivative
oriented outwards the bulk (i.e., inwards the target)

[
15
]
. This condition postulates that the diffusive flux
of species
A
(left-hand side) is proportional to the reactive flux
(right-hand side), and the proportionality coefficient
\kappa

characterizes the reactivity of the catalytic target
B
, ranging from

0
for inert surface, to infinity for a perfectly reactive one (in
which case it is reduced to the Smoluchowski setting). The reactivity

\kappa
is often related to the reaction constant
k_{\rm on}
and
may represent microscopic heterogeneity of the target, temporal
fluctuations of its activity, energetic or entropic barriers,
etc.
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
22
,
21
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
.
Microscopic and probabilistic interpretations of the condition
(
2
) have been discussed
[
32
]
.

For a spherical target of radius
R
, Collins and Kimball employed the
separation of variables and found an explicit form of the
time-dependent concentration, as well as the diffusive flux. In the
steady-state regime, the diffusive flux reaches a constant value:

J=\frac{4\pi DC_{A}R}{1+D/(\kappa R)}\,.

(3)

For weakly reactive targets (
\kappa\ll D/R
), the flux
J\approx 4\pi R^{2}\kappa C_{A}
is proportional to the surface area
4\pi R^{2}
of
the target and to its reactivity
\kappa
, and is almost independent
of diffusivity
D
; this is the reaction-limited regime. In the
opposite limit of highly reactive targets (
\kappa\gg D/R
), one
retrieves the Smoluchowski relation (
1
). When the shape
of the target is nearly spherical, one can employ perturbative
analysis to generalize this relation
[
33
]
. It is also
instructive to look at the ratio between the imposed initial
concentration
C_{A}
(a “voltage”) and the diffusive flux
J
(a
“current”) that represents the “resistance” (or impedance) of the
system:

Z=\frac{C_{A}}{J}=\frac{1}{4\pi RD}+\frac{1}{4\pi R^{2}\kappa}\,.

(4)

In analogy to electrostatics, this relation is often interpreted as
the sum of “resistances” of the bulk diffusion and of the surface
reaction, as two consecutive steps to the successful reaction event.
However, this intuitive interpretation relies on the simple form
(
3
), which is valid for the spherical target but fails
for arbitrary domains (see below).

On the other hand, the Smoluchowski relation (
1
) remains
valid for a perfectly reactive target of arbitrary shape if
R
is
replaced by its capacitance
R_{c}
:

J=4\pi DC_{A}R_{c}

(5)

(here we use the convention that the capacitance of a sphere of radius

R
is
R
). For several basic domains whose symmetries allow for
separation of variables, analytical computation of the capacitance
relies on curvilinear orthogonal coordinates
[
34
]
. So,
Samson and Deutch pioneered the application of bispherical coordinates
to compute the diffusive flux on a pair of perfectly reactive
spherical targets
[
35
]
. Later many authors used the
bispherical coordinates to obtain analytical solutions for
diffusion-controlled reactions on a pair of spherical targets and to
model surface reactions on axially symmetric Janus dumbbell particles

[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
.
Numerous studies were devoted to understand the impact of the spatial
arrangment of multiple perfectly reactive targets onto
diffusion-controlled reactions by using Monte Carlo simulations

[
43
,
44
,
45
]
or finite-element methods

[
46
]
, as well as semi-analytical approaches such as the
generalized method of separation of variables

[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

In this paper, we aim at clarifying the respective roles of shape and
reactivity onto the diffusive flux onto nonspherical targets. For
this purpose, we adopt a spectral approach, which was originally
developed for electrochemical settings with irregularly-shaped
electrodes
[
52
,
53
]
and then extended to
time-dependent diffusion
[
32
]
. In particular, we
explain the failure of an oversimplified picture of consecutive
diffusion and reaction resistances that was inspired by
Eq. (
4
) for a sphere, and get a general spectral
representation for the diffusive flux. We then focus on two cases, a
torus and a pair of balls in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(Fig.
1
), for which
the proposed spectral description can be efficiently computed by using
toroidal and bispherical coordinates. In particular, we derive a
simple two-term approximation for the diffusive flux. Section

II
presents our main results and their implications in the
context of chemical physics, whereas technical derivations are
relegated to Appendices
A
and
B
.

II
Main results

In this Section, we present our main theoretical results for the
diffusive flux onto a partially reactive target in three dimensions.
The target is a compact set
\Omega_{0}
whose boundary
{\partial\Omega}
is
reactive but impenetrable for the species
A
, which diffuse in the
bulk
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Omega_{0}
with a constant diffusion
coefficient
D>0
. Upon the arrival onto the catalytic surface

{\partial\Omega}
of reactivity
\kappa>0
, the species
A
may react with a
finite probability or resume its bulk diffusion. The concentration
gradient is therefore established, and we search for a steady-state
concentration of
A
, satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}\Delta C_{A}&=0&\textrm{in}~\Omega,\\
-D\partial_{n}C_{A}&=\kappa C_{A}&\textrm{on}~{\partial\Omega},\\
C_{A}(\bm{x})&\to C_{A}&\textrm{as}~|\bm{x}|\to\infty,\\
\end{array}\right.

(6)

where
C_{A}
is a prescribed concentration at infinity, and
\Delta
is
the Laplace operator. The total diffusive flux is

J=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}(-D\partial_{n}C_{A}).

(7)

We aim at determining the dependence of
J
on the reactivity
\kappa

and the shape of the boundary
{\partial\Omega}
.

II.1
General spectral expansions

To get a spectral representation for a solution of the problem
(
6
), we employ the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}
and
eigenfunctions
V_{k}
of the exterior Steklov problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}\Delta V_{k}&=0&\textrm{in}~\Omega,\\
\partial_{n}V_{k}&=\mu_{k}V_{k}&\textrm{on}~{\partial\Omega},\\
V_{k}&\to 0&\textrm{as}~|\bm{x}|\to\infty,\\
\end{array}\right.

(8)

with the conventional normalization

\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}|V_{k}|^{2}=1.

(9)

Different mathematical formulations of the exterior Steklov problem
and their equivalence are discussed in

[
54
,
55
,
56
]
(see also

[
57
,
58
,
59
]
for surveys on a much more
well-studied Steklov problem for bounded domains). Note that a
different formulation of the spectral problem (
8
) in
the context of sloshing phenomena in hydrodynamics appeared in

[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
]
.
In sharp contrast to the continuous spectrum of the Laplace operator
in unbounded domains, the spectrum of the Steklov problem
(
8
) is discrete because the boundary
{\partial\Omega}
is bounded;
the eigenvalues can be ordered in an increasing sequence,

0<\mu_{0}\leq\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty,

(10)

whereas the eigenfunctions
V_{k}
, restricted to
{\partial\Omega}
, form a complete
orthonormal basis of
L^{2}({\partial\Omega})
. This peculiar mathematical property
makes the Steklov eigenmodes particularly suitable for solving
exterior problems

[
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
]
and for describing
diffusion-controlled reactions in unbounded domains

[
69
,
70
]
. Note that restrictions of
V_{k}
onto

{\partial\Omega}
,
(V_{k})|_{{\partial\Omega}}
, are the eigenfunctions of the associated
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.

Writing
C_{A}(\bm{x})=C_{A}(1-u(\bm{x}))
and using the completeness of the
eigenfunctions
(V_{k})|_{{\partial\Omega}}
in
L^{2}({\partial\Omega})
to represent the unknown
function
u(\bm{x})
, one can deduce the following spectral representation
(see
[
32
]
for more details):

C_{A}(\bm{x})=C_{A}\biggl(1-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{V_{k}(\bm{x})}{1+\mu_{k}D/\kappa}\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}d\bm{x}\,V_{k}(\bm{x})\biggr).

(11)

It also determines the diffusive flux onto the target:

J=DC_{A}|{\partial\Omega}|\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{k}}{\mu_{k}^{-1}+D/\kappa}\,,

(12)

where
|{\partial\Omega}|
is the surface area of the target, and

F_{k}=\frac{1}{|{\partial\Omega}|}\left|\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}d\bm{x}\,V_{k}(\bm{x})\right|^{2}

(13)

are dimensionless coefficients. For a spherical target, the Steklov
spectrum is known explicitly (see, e.g.,
[
32
]
); in
particular, all coefficients
F_{k}
are zero, except for
F_{0}=1
so
that Eq. (
12
) is reduced to Eq. (
3
),
with
\mu_{0}^{-1}=R
. However, this simplification is a consequence
of the rotational symmetry of the spherical target, and it does not
hold in general. In fact, the spectral representation
(
12
) indicates that the impact of a finite reactivity

\kappa
onto the diffusive flux is in general much more sophisticated
than that for a spherical target. In particular, an intuitive
interpretation of the reaction event via Eq. (
4
) as a
sequence of the diffusion and reaction steps is not true anymore. In
fact, the first arrival of the particle onto the target is generally
followed by a sequence of failed reactions attempts and diffusive
excursions in the bulk near the target, until the successful reaction
event
[
32
]
. In this way, the geometric structure of the
target, which is represented via the spectral parameters
\mu_{k}
and

F_{k}
, is coupled to the reactivity
\kappa
.

A similar spectral representation for the electric current between two
metallic electrodes was derived in
[
52
]
. Despite an
evident similarity between two problems, the geometric setting in

[
52
]
and thus the employed Steklov problem were
different. In fact, Ref.
[
52
]
aimed at representing
microscopic irregularity of a macroscopically large electrode by
repeating periodically a given geometric pattern of the electrode
surface in both lateral directions. This is mathematically equivalent
to considering the current between an infinitely large (unbounded)
metallic surface and a source at infinity. In turn, the surface
{\partial\Omega}

of a target in our setting is bounded. Even though this distinction
may sound as a technical detail, it drastically changes the spectral
properties of the Steklov problem. For instance, the principal
eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
was zero for the periodic setting of
Ref.
[
52
]
, whereas it is strictly positive in our case.
In turn, the coefficients
F_{k}
in
[
52
]
involved
projections of
V_{k}
onto the harmonic measure density, in contrast to
our Eq. (
13
). Many other spectral properties are different
as well. At the same time, most physical interpretations from

[
52
,
53
]
remain valid in our setting.

It is instructive to inspect the limits of low and high reactivity.
As
\kappa\to 0
, one can employ the completeness relation for the
eigenfunctions
(V_{k})|_{{\partial\Omega}}
in
L^{2}({\partial\Omega})
to show that

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}F_{k}=1,

(14)

and thus to retrieve the expected reaction-limited rate:

J\approx C_{A}\kappa|{\partial\Omega}|\qquad(\kappa\to 0).

(15)

In this low-reactivity regime, the whole boundary of the target is
accessible almost uniformly by diffusion, so that
J
is proportional
to the surface area
|{\partial\Omega}|
and to the reactivity
\kappa
but is
almost independent of the diffusion coefficient
D
. Note also that
Eq. (
14
) allows one to interpret the coefficients
F_{k}

are relative weights of different Steklov eigenmodes in the spectral
expansion (
12
). In turn, the Steklov eigenvalues

\mu_{k}
determine the set of characteristic lengthscales,

\mu_{k}^{-1}
, of the target. These geometric lengthscales are then
confronted to the “reactivity length”,
D/\kappa
, which was
introduced and thoroughly discussed in

[
22
,
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
]
.

In the opposite limit of high reactivity, one recovers the generalized
Smoluchowski relation (
5
), with the capacitance

R_{c}=\frac{|\Omega|}{4\pi}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\mu_{k}F_{k}.

(16)

One sees that Eq. (
12
) describes a smooth transition
between these two limits. In particular, the relevant geometric
information of the target is fully captured via the spectral
parameters
\mu_{k}
and
F_{k}
. In order to clarify the role of these
spectral parameters onto diffusion-controlled reactions, we consider
two surfaces of revolution: a torus and a pair of spheres. The use of
toroidal and bispherical coordinates will allow us to design a very
efficient and accurate method for computing the Steklov eigenmodes.
In the next two sections, we present the main results for these
targets, whereas most technical computations are relegated to
Appendices
A
and
B
.

II.2
Torus

We consider diffusion in the exterior of a torus with the major radius

H
and the minor radius
R
:
\Omega=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~:~(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-H)^{2}+z^{2}>R^{2}\}
(Fig.
1
). In
Appendix
A
, we describe the computation of the
eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and spectral weights
F_{k}
for the
associated exterior Steklov problem. The fast convergence of our
method allows to deal with truncated matrices of small size (we use
the truncation order
{n_{\rm max}}=10
in the following examples). Note
that Eqs. (
14
,
16
) can serve for
checking the accuracy of the numerical computation. We recall that
the capacitance of the torus has an exact explicit expression

[
75
]
,

R_{c}=\frac{4}{\pi}\sqrt{H^{2}-R^{2}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(H/R)}{(1+\delta_{n,0})P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(H/R)}\,,

(17)

where
P_{\nu}(z)
and
Q_{\nu}(z)
are Legendre functions of the first
and second kind, and
\delta_{n,0}
is the Kronecker symbol (see
Appendix
A.5
for details).

presents the first three coefficients

as

. One sees that the first two
eigenmodes provide the major contribution to the diffusive flux,
whereas the contribution of the third and higher eigenmode is
negligible. This observation suggests a two-term approximation:

J^{\rm app}=DC_{A}|{\partial\Omega}|\biggl(\frac{F_{0}}{\mu_{0}^{-1}+D/\kappa}+\frac{F_{1}}{\mu_{1}^{-1}+D/\kappa}\biggr),

(18)

where

, and the spectral parameters
\mu_{k}
and

as functions of

are given in Table

(see
Appendix

for their numerical computation). Moreover,
as the torus gets thinner (i.e.,
R/H
decreases), the contribution of
the principal eigenmode rapidly approaches
1
, whereas all other
eigenmodes can be neglected. We get thus a fully explicit
approximation for a thin torus (a ring)

J\approx\frac{4\pi^{2}DC_{A}H}{\ln(8H/R)+D/(\kappa R)}\qquad(R\ll H),

(19)

where we used the asymptotic relation (
57
) for the
principal eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
. In the limit
\kappa\to\infty
, we
retrieve the generalized Smoluchowski relation
(
5
), with the known approximate expression for
the capacitance of a thin torus:
R_{c}\approx\pi H/\ln(8H/R)
. As
for a spherical target in Eq. (
4
), the “impedance”

C_{A}/J
of a thin torus can be represented as a serial connection of
“diffusion resistance”,
\ln(8H/R)/(4\pi^{2}DH)
, and “reaction
resistance”,
1/(\kappa|{\partial\Omega}|)
. Interestingly, the decrease of the
minor radius
R
has a marginal effect onto the first term (as

\ln(H/R)
), but significantly enhances the second term (as
H/R
).
In other words, even for a highly reactive thin torus, the reaction
resistance can be dominant, and an intuitive replacement of a large
but finite
\kappa
by infinity may lead to erroneous conclusions.
This observation highlights the crucial role of the reactivity, which
was often ignored in former works. Note that a similar effect of the
reactivity onto first-reaction times was discussed in

[
76
,
77
]
.

From a broader perspective, the consideration of a finite reactivity
resolves an optimal shape paradox. In fact, one may wonder what is
the “optimal way” to redistribute a given amount of catalytic
material to get the largest diffusive flux (and thus the highest
production rate)? If the catalytic germs are distributed uniformly on
a surface of the torus, one can fix the surface area,
|{\partial\Omega}|=4\pi^{2}RH
, and then search for the optimal ratio
R/H
that maximizes the
diffusive flux. When the reactivity
\kappa
is infinite, the
diffusive flux diverges as
R/H\to 0
, i.e., it can be made
arbitrarily large by taking a large but thin torus. In other words, a
fixed amount of catalytic material can yield an arbitrarily high
production rate. This mathematically correct but physically
paradoxical statement originates from the assumption of infinite
reactivity. In turn, if the reactivity is finite, the limit
R\to 0

yields the diffusive flux
C_{A}|{\partial\Omega}|\kappa
, as in the
reaction-limited regime. More generally, for any target shape, one
can rewrite the spectral expansion (
12
) as

J=C_{A}|{\partial\Omega}|\kappa\biggl(1-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{k}}{1+\mu_{k}D/\kappa}\biggr).

(20)

Since
F_{k}\geq 0
and
\mu_{k}>0
, the diffusive flux cannot exceed
its upper bound
C_{A}|{\partial\Omega}|\kappa
for any fixed surface area
|{\partial\Omega}|

and finite reactivity
\kappa
.

II.3
Pair of equal spheres

As discussed in Sec.
I
, diffusion-limited reactions on a
pair of perfectly reactive spheres were thoroughly investigated in the
past. In their pioneering work, Samson and Deutch obtained the
capacitance
[
35
]

R_{c}=2R\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{\sinh(\alpha_{0})}{\sinh(n\alpha_{0})}

(21)

that determines the diffusive flux via Eq. (
5
),
where
\alpha_{0}=\ln(H/R+\sqrt{H^{2}/R^{2}-1})
. This is the simplest
setting to analyze the effect of diffusional screening

[
22
]
, also known as diffusive interactions

[
78
]
, when two (or many) sinks compete for diffusing
particles so that the total flux is smaller than the sum of diffusive
fluxes onto each sink, if the other were absent. This effect, which
plays an important role in physics, electrochemistry, and biology

[
71
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
, e.g., in
diffusion-limited growth processes

[
79
,
80
,
81
,
82
]
, was thoroughly studied for

The advantage of the geometric model of two balls is the possibility

III
Conclusion

In summary, we revisited the classical problem of steady-state
diffusion towards a reactive target, with a special emphasis onto the
respective roles of the shape and reactivity. Employing the spectral
approach based on the exterior Steklov problem, we obtained a general
representation (
12
) of the diffusive flux onto the
target that exhibits an explicit dependence on the reactivity and
reveals the impact of the shape via the spectral parameters
\mu_{k}

and
F_{k}
– the Steklov eigenvalues and the squared projections of
the associated eigenfunctions onto a constant. This is the only
geometric information that fully determines the diffusive flux and
that can thus potentially be accessed via experimental measurements

[
53
]
. In particular,
\mu_{k}^{-1}
can be interpreted as
relevant lengthscales of the target that have to be compared to the
reaction length
D/\kappa
; in turn,
F_{k}
are the relative weights of
different eigenmodes. This spectral representation indicates that a
common view of diffusion and reaction steps as serially connected
resistances is in general wrong. In fact, the successful reaction
event is preceded by a long sequence of failed reaction attempts, so
that diffusion and reaction steps are strongly coupled.

For the cases of a torus and a pair of balls, we proposed an efficient
procedure for solving numerically the exterior Steklov problem. We
obtained that two eigenmodes provide the dominant contribution to the
diffusive flux, yielding an accurate two-term approximation
(
18
). The four parameters
H\mu_{0},H\mu_{1},F_{0},F_{1}

depend only on the aspect ratio
R/H
, and this dependence can be
found numerically or asymptotically (see Table
1
). Using
the solution of the exterior Steklov problem for prolate and oblate
spheroids
[
84
]
, we also computed the weights
F_{k}
for
such targets and checked that the two-term approximation is very
accurate as well (not shown). In addition, the principal eigenmode
yields a fully explicit and accurate approximation for the diffusive
flux onto a thin torus, as well as onto a pair of small balls. While
the second setting is rather conventional and well studied, the case
of a torus does not seem to be reported earlier.

The presented theoretical results shed light onto the intricate
coupling between geometry and reactivity in diffusion-controlled
reactions. Moreover, the knowledge of the Steklov eigenbasis allows
one to deal with more sophisticated surface reactions, far beyond the
conventional setting of a constant reactivity. On one hand, the
Steklov eigenfunctions can be used to incorporate spatially
heterogeneous reactivity via a suitable matrix representation

[
85
]
. On the other hand, one can implement an
encounter-dependent reactivity to model passivation or activation of
the reactive target via its encounters with diffusing particles

[
32
]
. Indeed, the Steklov eigenbasis is the cornerstone
of the encounter-based approach to diffusion-controlled reactions

[
86
,
87
,
88
,
89
]

and to permeation processes

[
90
,
91
,
92
,
93
]
.
Finally, one can go beyond the context of chemical physics and
describe the statistics of encounters between the diffusing particles
and the target
[
94
,
69
,
95
]
.

Appendix A
Torus

In this Appendix, we describe the main steps for computing the
eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the exterior Steklov problem for a
torus with the major radius
H
and the minor radius
R
:
\Omega=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~:~(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-H)^{2}+z^{2}>R^{2}\}

(Fig.
1
).
For this purpose, we use the toroidal coordinates

(\alpha,\theta,\phi)

[
96
]

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\\
z\\
\end{array}\right)=\frac{c}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sinh\alpha\cos\phi\\
\sinh\alpha\sin\phi\\
\sin\theta\\
\end{array}\right),

(23)

where
0\leq\alpha<+\infty
,
-\pi<\theta\leq\pi
,
0\leq\phi<2\pi
, and
c=\sqrt{H^{2}-R^{2}}
. In these coordinates, the
exterior of a torus corresponds to
0\leq\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, where

\alpha_{0}=\cosh^{-1}(H/R)=\ln\biggl(H/R+\sqrt{(H/R)^{2}-1}\biggr).

(24)

The scale factors are

h_{\alpha}=h_{\theta}=\frac{c}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta},\quad h_{\phi}=\frac{c\,\sinh\alpha}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\,.

(25)

The Laplace operator admits the partial separation of variables in
terms of associated Legendre functions
P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)

and
Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)
, as well as Fourier harmonics in

\theta
and
\phi

[
96
]
. Since

P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)
[resp.,
Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)
]
exhibits a logarithmic singularity as
\alpha\to\infty
[resp,

\alpha\to 0
], one has to choose
P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)
for
solving exterior problems and
Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha)
for
solving interior problems.

A.1
Exterior Steklov problem

For the exterior problem (
0\leq\alpha<\alpha_{0}
), we search a
solution of the Steklov problem (
8
) in the form

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\biggl[A_{m,n}^{+}\cos(n\theta)

\displaystyle+A_{m,n}^{-}\sin(n\theta)\biggr]\frac{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)}{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}e^{im\phi}.

(26)

The unknown coefficients
A_{m,n}^{\pm}
are determined by imposing
the Steklov boundary condition at the boundary
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
:

\displaystyle(\partial_{n}V)|_{{\partial\Omega}}=\frac{1}{h_{\alpha_{0}}}(\partial_{\alpha}V)|_{\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\sinh\alpha_{0}}{2c}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum\limits_{m,n}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{+}\cos(n\theta)+A_{m,n}^{-}\sin(n\theta)\bigr]e^{im\phi}

\displaystyle\quad\times\biggl\{1+2(\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta)p_{m,n}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\mu V|_{\alpha_{0}}=\mu\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum\limits_{m,n}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{+}\cos(n\theta)+A_{m,n}^{-}\sin(n\theta)\bigr]e^{im\phi},

where we used the shortcut notation
\sum\nolimits_{m,n}
for the sum
over
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, and

p_{m,n}=p_{-m,n}=\frac{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m^{\prime}}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\qquad(m=0,1,2,\ldots),

(27)

with prime denoting the derivative with respect to the argument (i.e.,

P_{\nu}^{\mu^{\prime}}(z)=\partial_{z}P_{\nu}^{m}(z)
). Using the recurrence
relation

\partial_{z}P_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\biggl(\sqrt{z^{2}-1}P_{\nu}^{\mu+1}(z)+\mu zP_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)\biggr),

(28)

we can express
p_{m,n}
as

p_{m,n}=\frac{1}{\sinh\alpha_{0}}\left(\frac{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m+1}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}+m\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{0})\right).

(29)

We rewrite then the above condition more compactly as

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{+}\cos(n^{\prime}\theta)+A_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{-}\sin(n^{\prime}\theta)\bigr]e^{im^{\prime}\phi}

\displaystyle\times\bigl\{1+2(\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta)p_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}-2R\mu\bigr\}=0,

(30)

where we used
c=R\sinh\alpha_{0}
. Multiplying this equation by

e^{-im\phi}\cos(n\theta)
(with
n>0
) and integrating over
\phi

and
\theta
, we get for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n=1,2,\ldots
:

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{+}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}\,p_{m,n})-A_{m,n-1}^{+}p_{m,n-1}

\displaystyle-A_{m,n+1}^{+}p_{m,n+1}-\delta_{n,1}A_{m,0}^{+}p_{m,0}=2R\mu A_{m,n}^{+}.

In turn, multiplication by
e^{-im\phi}
and integration over
\phi

and
\theta
yield for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle A_{m,0}^{+}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}p_{m,0})-A_{m,1}^{+}p_{m,1}=2R\mu A_{m,0}^{+}.

Denoting by
\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m}
the vector of coefficients
A_{m,n}^{+}
, the
above equations can be written in a matrix form:

\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m}\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}=\mu\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m},

(31)

with

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=\frac{1}{2R}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}\,p_{m,n})-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}p_{m,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}p_{m,n^{\prime}}-\delta_{n,1}\delta_{n^{\prime},0}p_{m,0}\biggr]\qquad(n,n^{\prime}=0,1,2,\ldots).

(32)

Similarly, multiplication of Eq. (
30
) by
e^{-im\phi}\sin(n\theta)
and integration over
\theta
and
\phi
yield

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{-}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}\,p_{m,n})-A_{m,n-1}^{-}p_{m,n-1}

\displaystyle-A_{m,n+1}^{-}p_{m,n+1}=2R\mu A_{m,n}^{-}\quad(n=1,2,\ldots),

which can also be written in a matrix form

\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m}\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}=\mu\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m},

(33)

with

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2R}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}\,p_{m,n})-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}p_{m,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}p_{m,n^{\prime}}\biggr]\qquad(n,n^{\prime}=1,2,\ldots).

(34)

One sees that the systems of linear equations determining the
coefficients
A_{m,n}^{+}
and
A_{m,n}^{-}
are decoupled and can
thus be solved separately for each
m
.

Let
\{\mu_{m,0}^{+},\mu_{m,1}^{+},\ldots\}
denote the eigenvalues
of the matrix
\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}
, enumerated in an increasing order by
n=0,1,\ldots
; the associated
left
eigenvectors are denoted as

\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}
. Similarly,
\mu_{m,n}^{-}
and
\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}
with

n=1,2,\ldots
are the
n
-th eigenvalue and the associated
left

eigenvector of the matrix
\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}
. According to
Eq. (
26
), we get two families of eigenfunctions,
enumerated by the double index
m,n
and the superscript:

\displaystyle V^{+}_{m,n}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=e^{im\phi}\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\cos(n^{\prime}\theta)\frac{P_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)}{P_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}

(35)

and

\displaystyle V^{-}_{m,n}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=e^{im\phi}\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}]_{n^{\prime}}\sin(n^{\prime}\theta)\frac{P_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)}{P_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\,.

(36)

The associated eigenvalues are
\mu_{m,n}^{+}
and
\mu_{m,n}^{-}
,
respectively. These eigenfunctions respect the reflection symmetry of
the problem (with respect to the horizontal plane):

V^{\pm}_{m,n}(\alpha,-\theta,\phi)=\pm V^{\pm}_{m,n}(\alpha,\theta,\phi).

(37)

Moreover, since
p_{-m,n}=p_{m,n}
according to
Eq. (
27
), one has
\mathbf{M}_{-m}^{\pm}=\mathbf{M}_{m}^{\pm}
so that
any eigenvalue
\mu_{m,n}^{\pm}
with
m\neq 0
is at least twice
degenerate. In particular, one can take two linearly independent
combinations of
V^{\pm}_{m,n}
and
V^{\pm}_{-m,n}
to form
real-valued eigenfunctions.

For applications, we also need to ensure the normalization of
eigenfunctions and to compute their projection onto a constant. We
get

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}|V_{m,n}^{+}|^{2}=\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,|V_{m,n}^{+}(\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi c^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{1}}^{*}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\frac{\cos(n_{1}\theta)\cos(n_{2}\theta)}{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\,.

A direct computation of this double sum may be inaccurate and
time-consuming. To avoid this step, we recall that
V_{m,n}^{+}

satisfies the Steklov boundary condition and write

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}[V_{m,n}^{+}]^{*}\frac{\partial_{n}V_{m,n}^{+}}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}=\frac{2\pi c\sinh^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{1}}^{*}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{2}}\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{\cos(n_{1}\theta)\cos(n_{2}\theta)}{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}

\displaystyle\times\biggl[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}+\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\,p_{m,n_{2}}\biggr],

where we explicitly evaluated the normal derivative. In the first
integral, one can recognize the above expression for
J_{m,n}^{+}
,
whereas the second integral is easily evaluated due to the
orthogonality of cosine functions. As a consequence, we get

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{J_{m,n}^{+}}{2R\mu_{m,n}^{+}}+\frac{2\pi^{2}c\sinh^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}\biggl(2p_{m,0}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{0}\bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}p_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}\biggr),

from which
J_{m,n}^{+}
can be easily expressed:

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{2}c^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}}{R\mu_{m,n}^{+}-\frac{1}{2}}

(38)

\displaystyle\times\biggl(2p_{m,0}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{0}\bigr|^{2}+\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}p_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}\biggr),

where we used
c=R\sinh\alpha_{0}
. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}|V_{m,n}^{-}|^{2}=\frac{2\pi^{2}c^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}}{R\mu_{m,n}^{-}-\frac{1}{2}}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}p_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}.

We also evaluate the projection of
V_{m,n}^{+}
onto a constant:

\displaystyle I_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}V_{m,n}^{+}=\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,V_{m,n}^{+}(\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=2\pi c^{2}\sinh\alpha_{0}\delta_{m,0}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\,\hat{b}_{n^{\prime}},

(39)

where

\hat{b}_{n}=\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{\cos(n\theta)}{(\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta)^{3/2}}\,.

(40)

This integral can be found from the Fourier expansion

[
75
]
:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(n\theta)}{1+\delta_{n,0}}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{\mu}(\cosh\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\pi\mu}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})(\sinh\alpha)^{\mu}}{\sqrt{8}\,(\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta)^{\mu+\frac{1}{2}}}\,,

(41)

which is valid for any
\mu\neq-1/2,-3/2,\ldots
. Setting
\mu=1
, one gets

\hat{b}_{n}=-2\sqrt{8}\frac{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{1}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{\sinh\alpha_{0}}\,.

(42)

In turn, one has
\int\nolimits_{{\partial\Omega}}V_{m,n}^{-}=0
due to the
symmetry of the eigenfunction
V_{m,n}^{-}
. As a consequence, only
the axially symmetric eigenmodes with
m=0
contribute to the
steady-state concentration and the diffusive flux. We conclude that
the diffusive flux is determined by the following spectral parameters:

\mu_{k}=\mu_{0,k}^{+},\quad F_{k}=\frac{|I_{0,k}^{+}|^{2}}{|{\partial\Omega}|J_{0,k}^{+}}\qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots),

(43)

where
|{\partial\Omega}|=4\pi^{2}HR
is the surface area of the torus, and we
included explicitly the normalization
J_{0,k}^{+}
.

A.2
Practical implementation

The crucial advantage of the proposed method is the possibility to
construct
separately
the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for each
value of
m
. For instance, it is sufficient to focus on axially
symmetric eigenmodes (
m=0
) for the analysis of the diffusive flux

J
. In the following, we choose
m
and then fix a truncation order

{n_{\rm max}}
. One needs to construct a truncated matrix
\mathbf{M}_{m}^{+}
of
size
({n_{\rm max}}+1)\times({n_{\rm max}}+1)
, and a truncated matrix
\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-}

of size
{n_{\rm max}}\times{n_{\rm max}}
. These are tridiagonal matrices,
whose elements are expressed via Eqs. (
32
,

34
) in terms of
p_{m,n}
given by Eq. (
29
).
In turn, the Legendre functions
P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(z)
with
z\geq 1

can be found either from their integral representation
[
97
]

P_{\nu}^{m}(z)=\frac{[\nu+1]_{m}}{\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\phi\,\cos(m\phi)\biggl(z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\cos\phi\biggr)^{\nu},

(44)

(with
[\nu+1]_{m}=(\nu+1)(\nu+2)\cdots(\nu+m)
), or from recurrence
relations
[
98
]
. Once the truncated matrix
\mathbf{M}_{m}^{\pm}

is constructed, its numerical diagonalization yields an approximation
for a number of eigenvalues
\mu_{m,n}^{\pm}
and the associated left
eigenvectors
\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{\pm}
, which determine the Steklov
eigenfunctions via Eqs. (
35
,
36
).
Increasing the truncation order
{n_{\rm max}}
, one can ensure the required
accuracy of the computed eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. Finally, the
coefficients
F_{k}
determining the diffusive flux are found according
to Eq. (
43
), in which
J_{0,k}^{+}
and
I_{0,k}^{+}

are evaluated from the truncated expressions (
38
,

39
), with
\hat{b}_{n}
given by Eq. (
42
).
The latter involves the Legendre function of the second kind, which
can be expressed through the Whipple’s formula

\displaystyle Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(z)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\Gamma(m-n+\tfrac{1}{2})\frac{\sqrt{\pi/2}}{(z^{2}-1)^{\frac{1}{4}}}

\displaystyle\times P_{m-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\biggl(\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^{2}-1}}\biggr).

(45)

Table
1
presents the first two eigenvalues
\mu_{0,0}^{+}

and
\mu_{0,1}^{+}
as functions of the aspect ratio
R/H
, whereas
Fig.
4
illustrates several axially
symmetric Steklov eigenfunctions for a torus. The accuracy of these
results was also verified by comparison with another computation based
on a finite-element method described in
[
70
]
.

Figure 4:

Several axially symmetric Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{0,n}^{\pm}
for
the torus with
R=0.5
and
H=1
. The cross-section in the

plane with

, that corresponds to
\phi=0
, is shown. Top

that

We note that the proposed method is not applicable in the limit
R=H
that corresponds to
\alpha_{0}=0
. According to
Eq. (
29
), the coefficient
p_{0,n}
for any fixed
n

has a finite limit,
p_{0,n}\to(n^{2}-1/4)/2
, as
z=H/R\to 1
,
where we used the asymptotic behavior of the Legendre functions:

P_{\nu}^{m}(z)\approx\frac{\Gamma(\nu+m+1)}{m!\,\Gamma(\nu-m+1)}\bigl((z-1)/2\bigr)^{\frac{m}{2}}\quad(z\to 1^{+}).

(46)

However, as the matrices
\mathbf{M}_{0}^{\pm}
are infinite-dimensional, their
construction requires the elements with large

, in which case the
asymptotic behavior is different. In fact, one can use another
asymptotic relation

P_{\nu}^{\mu}(\cosh\alpha_{0})\approx\nu^{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\sinh\alpha_{0}}}\,I_{\mu}((\nu+1/2)\alpha_{0})\quad(\nu\to\infty),

(47)

where
I_{\mu}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.
As a consequence, one gets in the leading order that
p_{m,n}\sim(n-\tfrac{1}{2})/\sinh\alpha_{0}
as

for a fixed
\alpha_{0}
.
One sees that the two limits
R\to H
and

cannot be
inter-changed, i.e., one cannot simply substitute
\alpha_{0}=0
in
the above relations. In practice, one can compute the eigenvalues for
any fixed
R<H
, but larger and larger truncation orders may be
needed as
R
gets closer to
H
.

A.3
Asymptotic behavior for a thin torus

We study the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues of the exterior
Steklov problem as
R\to 0
. In this limit, one has
z=\cosh\alpha_{0}=H/R\to\infty
and thus
\alpha_{0}
goes to infinity.

For the case
n=0
, it is convenient to use the following
representation
[
99
]

P_{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2}{1+z}}\,K\biggl(\sqrt{\frac{z-1}{z+1}}\biggr),

(48)

where
K(z)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind:

K(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\,.

(49)

Substituting the asymptotic behavior of
K(z)
near
1

into Eq. (
48
), one gets in the leading order

P_{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)\approx\frac{\sqrt{2}\ln(8z)}{\pi\,\sqrt{z}}\quad(z\to\infty).

(50)

Since

P_{\nu}^{m}(z)=(z^{2}-1)^{\frac{m}{2}}\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}P_{\nu}(z),

(51)

we also get for
m>0

P_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(z)\approx(-1)^{m}[1/2]_{m}\frac{\sqrt{2}\,[\ln(8z)-d_{m}]}{\pi\,\sqrt{z}}\quad(z\to\infty),

(52)

where
[a]_{m}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
,

d_{m}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{j-1/2}\qquad(m=1,2,\ldots),

(53)

and we also set
d_{0}=0
. Substituting this asymptotic behavior into
Eq. (
29
), we find

p_{m,0}\approx\frac{1}{z}\biggl(\frac{1}{\ln(8z)-d_{m}}-\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\qquad(z\to\infty).

(54)

In turn, for
n>0
, one has

P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(z)\simeq\frac{\Gamma(n)(2z)^{n-\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(n+\frac{1}{2}-m)}\quad(z\to\infty),

(55)

so that

p_{m,n}\approx\frac{n-\frac{1}{2}}{z}\quad(z\to\infty)

(56)

in the leading order.

Substituting these asymptotic relations to Eqs. (
32
,

34
), we see that the leading-order term of the matrix

R\,\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}
is the diagonal matrix, whose elements are
1/(\ln(8z)-d_{m}),\,1,\,2,\,\ldots
, whereas the next-order term is
O(R/H)

(with eventual logarithmic corrections). In turn, the leading-order
term of the matrix
R\,\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}
is the diagonal matrix with the
elements
1,2,\ldots
on the diagonal. As a consequence, we obtain
the following asymptotic behavior as
R\to 0
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle R\mu_{m,0}^{+}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\ln(H/R)+\ln 8-d_{m}}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle R\mu_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle\approx R\mu_{m,n}^{-}\approx n+O(R/H)\quad(n=1,2,\ldots).

(58)

This asymptotic behavior agrees with recent mathematical works, which
studied the Steklov problem for a bounded domain with a thin toroidal
hole
[
100
]
and a tubular excision from a closed manifold

[
101
]
.

(a) illustrates the high accuracy of the
asymptotic relation (
57
) for several
m
. In
addition, the panel (b) shows that the eigenvalues
\mu_{m,n}^{\pm}

with
n>0
, rescaled by
R
, approach their limits
n
linearly with

R/H
, in agreement with our predictions.

A.4
Interior problem

The computation of the Steklov spectrum for the interior of a torus
(i.e., for
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
) can be performed in the same way,
except that
P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)
is replaced by

Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)
, and the direction of the normal
derivative is the opposite. As a consequence, we obtain two families
of Steklov eigenfunctions

\displaystyle V^{+}_{m,n}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=e^{im\phi}\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\cos(n^{\prime}\theta)\frac{Q_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)}{Q_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}

(59)

and

\displaystyle V^{-}_{m,n}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=e^{im\phi}\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}]_{n^{\prime}}\sin(n^{\prime}\theta)\frac{Q_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha)}{Q_{n^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}}^{|m|}(\cosh\alpha_{0})},

(60)

where
\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}
are the left eigenvectors of the matrix

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=\frac{-1}{2R}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}q_{m,n})-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}q_{m,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}q_{m,n^{\prime}}-\delta_{n,1}\delta_{n^{\prime},0}q_{m,0}\biggr]\qquad(n,n^{\prime}=0,1,2,\ldots),

(61)

and
\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}
are the left eigenvectors of the matrix

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=\frac{-1}{2R}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}(1+2\cosh\alpha_{0}q_{m,n})-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}q_{m,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}q_{m,n^{\prime}}\biggr]\qquad(n,n^{\prime}=1,2,\ldots),

(62)

with

q_{m,n}=q_{-m,n}=\frac{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m^{\prime}}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\quad(m=0,1,2,\ldots).

(63)

Using the recurrence relation

\partial_{z}Q_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\biggl(\sqrt{z^{2}-1}Q_{\nu}^{\mu+1}(z)+\mu zQ_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)\biggr),

(64)

one gets

q_{m,n}=\frac{1}{\sinh\alpha_{0}}\left(\frac{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m+1}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}{Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}^{m}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}+m\,\mathrm{ctanh}\alpha_{0}\right).

(65)

The involved Legendre functions of the second kind can be found via
the Whipple’s formula (
45
), integral representations, or
recurrence relations
[
98
]
. Despite the general interest
in the spectral properties of the Steklov problem for the interior of
a torus, further development of this topic is beyond the scope of the
paper.

A.5
Perfectly reactive torus

For completeness, we reproduce the solution for a perfectly reactive
torus (see more details in
[
75
]
). The axial symmetry of
this problem implies
C_{A}(\bm{x})=C_{A}(1-u(\bm{x}))
, where the harmonic
function
u(\bm{x})
can be searched in the form

u=\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\cos(n\theta)\frac{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\alpha)}{P_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\alpha_{0})}\,.

(66)

The coefficients
b_{n}
are fixed by the Dirichlet boundary
condition
u|_{{\partial\Omega}}=1
:

b_{n}=\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{\pi}\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\frac{\cos(n\theta)}{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}=\frac{\epsilon_{n}\sqrt{8}}{\pi}Q_{n-\frac{1}{2}}(\cosh\alpha_{0}),

(67)

where the second equality was obtained from the Fourier expansion
(
41
) by setting
\mu=0
, and
\epsilon_{n}=1/(1+\delta_{n,0})
. As a consequence, the diffusive flux is

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=DC_{A}\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}\partial_{n}u=DC_{A}2\pi\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta h_{\theta}h_{\phi}\frac{1}{h_{\alpha}}\partial_{\alpha}u

\displaystyle=DC_{A}2\pi c\sinh\alpha_{0}^{2}\int\limits_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{1}{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\cos(n\theta)

\displaystyle\times\biggl[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}+\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\,p_{0,n}\biggr]

\displaystyle=DC_{A}2\pi c\sinh\alpha_{0}^{2}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\biggl[\frac{1}{2}\hat{b}_{n}+\frac{\pi}{\epsilon_{n}}b_{n}p_{0,n}\biggr],

where
\hat{b}_{n}
was defined by Eq. (
40
).
Substituting Eqs. (
27
,
42
,
67
)
and using the Wronskian of Legendre functions,

P_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)\partial_{z}Q_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)-Q_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)\partial_{z}P_{\nu}^{\mu}(z)=\frac{-e^{i\mu\pi}\Gamma(\nu+\mu+1)}{\Gamma(\nu-\mu+1)(z^{2}-1)}\,,

(68)

one retrieves Eq. (
17
) for the capacitance of a torus.

Appendix B
Pair of spheres

In this Appendix, we replicate the computation of Appendix

A
to a pair of spheres. For this purpose, we employ
bispherical coordinates
[
96
]

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\\
z\\
\end{array}\right)=\frac{c}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
\sinh\alpha\\
\end{array}\right),

(69)

where
-\infty<\alpha<+\infty
,
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
,
0\leq\phi<2\pi
, and
c>0
. They can be expressed in terms of
Cartesian coordinates as

\alpha=\sinh^{-1}\biggl(\frac{2cz}{Q}\biggr),\qquad\theta=\cos^{-1}\biggl(\frac{P^{2}-c^{2}}{Q}\biggr),

(70)

and
\phi=\tan^{-1}(y/x)
, with

P=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}},\quad Q=\sqrt{(P^{2}+c^{2})^{2}-(2cz)^{2}}\,,

(71)

whereas the scale factors are

h_{\alpha}=h_{\theta}=\frac{c}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta},\quad h_{\phi}=\frac{c\,\sin\theta}{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\,.

(72)

For a given
\alpha_{0}>0
, the domain
\Omega=\{\alpha>\alpha_{0}\}
is the ball of radius
R=c/\sinh(\alpha_{0})
, centered at

(0,0,H)
, with
H=c\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{0})>0
. Similarly, for

\alpha_{0}<0
, the domain
\Omega=\{\alpha<\alpha_{0}\}
is the
ball of radius
c/|\sinh(\alpha_{0})|
, centered at
(0,0,-H)
, with
H=c\,|\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{0})|
. In turn, the exterior of these two equal
balls is given by
|\alpha|<\alpha_{0}
. In the next subsection, we
focus on this setting of balls of equal radii, for which computations
are simpler. The exterior of two balls of different radii will be
discussed in Sec.
B.3
.

B.1
Exterior Steklov problem

In this subsection, we consider the exterior of two equal balls of
radii
R
, whose centers are located at
(0,0,\pm H)
. The boundary

{\partial\Omega}
of the domain is composed of two spheres, denoted as

{\partial\Omega}_{\pm}
. In the bispherical coordinates, this domain is defined
as
|\alpha|<\alpha_{0}
, where

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=R\sinh\alpha_{0},\quad\cosh\alpha_{0}=\frac{H}{R}>1,

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=\ln\bigl(H/R+\sqrt{(H/R)^{2}-1}\bigr)>0,

so that
H=c\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{0})
and
c=\sqrt{H^{2}-R^{2}}
.

Due to a partial separation of variables in the bispherical
coordinates, we search a solution of Eq. (
8
) in the
form:

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{m=-n}^{n}\biggl[A_{m,n}^{-}\frac{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha)}{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})}

\displaystyle+A_{m,n}^{+}\frac{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha)}{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})}\biggr]Y_{m,n}(\theta,\phi),

(73)

where
Y_{m,n}(\theta,\phi)
are normalized spherical harmonics:

\displaystyle Y_{m,n}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=a_{m,n}P_{n}^{m}(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},

(74a)

\displaystyle a_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(n-m)!}{(n+m)!}}\,,

(74b)

P_{n}^{m}(z)
are the associated Legendre polynomials, and

A_{m,n}^{\pm}
are unknown coefficients (we adopt the convention that

a_{m,n}\equiv 0
for any
|m|>n
).

We first impose the Steklov boundary condition on the sphere
{\partial\Omega}_{+}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V|_{{\partial\Omega}_{+}}=\frac{1}{h_{\alpha_{0}}}(\partial_{\alpha}V)|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}=\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}{2c}

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\sinh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle+(\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta)\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\mu V|_{{\partial\Omega}_{+}}=\mu\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}},

where we used the shortcut notation
\sum\nolimits_{m,n^{\prime}}
for the sum
over
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
m=-n,-n+1,\ldots,n
, and

\displaystyle C^{-}_{n}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\,\mathrm{ctanh}((n+\tfrac{1}{2})\alpha_{0}),

(75a)

\displaystyle C^{+}_{n}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\tanh((n+\tfrac{1}{2})\alpha_{0}).

(75b)

Multiplication of the above relation by
Y_{m,n}^{*}(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta
and integration over
\theta
and
\phi
yield

\displaystyle\sinh\alpha_{0}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}+A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr]+\cosh\alpha_{0}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}C^{-}_{n}+A_{m,n}^{+}C^{+}_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\biggl(A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}\biggr)[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2c\mu\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}+A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr],

(76)

where

\displaystyle[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\pi a_{m,n}a_{m,n^{\prime}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,x\,P_{n}^{m}(x)\,P_{n^{\prime}}^{m}(x).

Using the Gaunt’s formula, one can evaluate the integral, which takes
nonzero values only at
|n-n^{\prime}|=1
:

\int\limits_{-1}^{1}dx\,x\,P_{n-1}^{m}(x)\,P_{n}^{m}(x)=(-1)^{m}\frac{2(n-m)\,(n+m)!}{(4n^{2}-1)\,(n-m)!}\,,

(77)

from which

[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n,n^{\prime}}=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}\eta_{m,n}+\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}\eta_{m,n^{\prime}}\quad(n,n^{\prime}\geq|m|),

(78)

where

\eta_{m,n}=(-1)^{m}\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}-m^{2}}{4n^{2}-1}}\,.

(79)

At the boundary
{\partial\Omega}_{-}
, which corresponds to
\alpha=-\alpha_{0}
, we
get a similar boundary condition with the opposite sign

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V|_{{\partial\Omega}_{-}}=-\frac{1}{h_{\alpha_{0}}}(\partial_{\alpha}V)|_{\alpha=-\alpha_{0}}=-\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}}{2c}

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\sinh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}-A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle+(\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta)\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}-A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[-A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}},

from which we obtain another set of linear equations:

\displaystyle\sinh\alpha_{0}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}-A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr]+\cosh\alpha_{0}\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}C^{-}_{n}-A_{m,n}^{+}C^{+}_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}-A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n,n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2c\mu\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}-A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr].

(80)

Taking the sum and difference of Eqs. (
76
,

80
), we obtain two decoupled systems:

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{-}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{0}+\cosh\alpha_{0}C^{-}_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=2c\mu A_{m,n}^{-}

and

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{+}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{0}+\cosh\alpha_{0}C^{+}_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=2c\mu A_{m,n}^{+}.

Denoting by
\mathbf{A}^{\pm}_{m}
the vector of coefficients
A_{m,n}^{\pm}
,
we represent the above systems in a matrix form as

\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m}\mathbf{M}^{+}_{m}=\mu\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m},\qquad\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m}\mathbf{M}^{-}_{m}=\mu\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m},

(81)

where

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{\pm}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2c}\biggl(\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\bigl(\sinh\alpha_{0}+\cosh\alpha_{0}C^{\pm}_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle-C^{\pm}_{n^{\prime}}[\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}\biggr)\qquad(n,n^{\prime}\geq|m|).

(82)

As a consequence, the eigenpairs of the exterior Steklov problem can
be determined separately for different
m
by diagonalizing the
matrices
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}_{m}
. Denoting by
\mu_{m,n}^{\pm}
and

\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{\pm}
the eigenvalues and
left
eigenvectors of the
matrix
\mathbf{M}^{\pm}_{m}
(enumerated by
n=0,1,2,\ldots
), we realize
that
\mu_{m,n}^{\pm}
are the Steklov eigenvalues, whereas the
associated eigenfunctions are given by

\displaystyle V_{m,n}^{+}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=|m|}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\frac{\cosh((n^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})\alpha)}{\cosh((n^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})}Y_{m,n^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)

(83)

and

\displaystyle V_{m,n}^{-}(\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=|m|}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}]_{n^{\prime}}\frac{\sinh((n^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})\alpha)}{\sinh((n^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})}Y_{m,n^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi).

(84)

These eigenfunctions respect the reflection symmetry of the problem
(with respect to the horizontal plane):

V_{m,n}^{\pm}(-\alpha,\theta,\phi)=\pm V_{m,n}^{\pm}(\alpha,\theta,\phi).

(85)

For applications, we also need to ensure the normalization of
eigenfunctions and to compute their projection onto a constant. As
the boundary
{\partial\Omega}
has two components
{\partial\Omega}_{\pm}
corresponding to

\alpha_{0}
and
-\alpha_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}|V_{m,n}^{\pm}|^{2}=\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,\biggl(|V_{m,n}^{\pm}(\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)|^{2}

\displaystyle+|V_{m,n}^{\pm}(-\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)|^{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle=4\pi c^{2}\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{\pm}]_{n_{1}}^{*}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{\pm}]_{n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times a_{m,n}a_{m,n^{\prime}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}\,P_{n_{1}}^{m}(x)\,P_{n_{2}}^{m}(x).

To avoid a numerical computation of this integral and the double sum,
we employ the Steklov condition to represent
J_{m,n}^{+}
as

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}[V_{m,n}^{+}]^{*}\,\frac{\partial_{n}V_{m,n}^{+}}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}=\frac{2}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\phi}\,\biggl([V_{m,n}^{+}]^{*}\,\partial_{\alpha}V_{m,n}^{+}\biggr)\biggr|_{\alpha=\alpha_{0}}=\frac{2\pi c}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{1}}^{*}[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n_{2}}a_{m,n_{1}}a_{m,n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx\,P_{n_{1}}^{m}(x)\,P_{n_{2}}^{m}(x)}{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}}\biggl[\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}}{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}}+\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}\,C_{n_{2}}^{+}\biggr]=\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}}{2c\mu_{m,n}^{+}}J_{m,n}^{+}+\frac{c}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}},

where we used the orthogonality of the associated Legendre
polynomials. As a consequence, we get

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\mu_{m,n}^{+}-\frac{1}{2R}}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}C^{+}_{n^{\prime}}.

(86)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle J_{m,n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\mu_{m,n}^{-}-\frac{1}{2R}}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl|[\mathbf{V}_{m,n}^{-}]_{n^{\prime}}\bigr|^{2}C^{-}_{n^{\prime}}.

(87)

In addition, we evaluate

\displaystyle I_{m,n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\partial\Omega}}V_{m,n}^{+}=\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,h_{\theta}\,h_{\phi}\,\biggl(V_{m,n}^{+}(\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle+V_{m,n}^{+}(-\alpha_{0},\theta,\phi)\biggr)

\displaystyle=2c^{2}\sqrt{2\pi}\,\delta_{m,0}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}[\mathbf{V}_{0,n}^{+}]_{n^{\prime}}g_{n^{\prime}}(\cosh\alpha_{0}),

(88)

where

g_{n}(z)=\sqrt{n+1/2}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{(z-x)^{3/2}}\,P_{n}(x),

(89)

whereas
I_{m,n}^{-}=0
due to the symmetry of the eigenfunctions

V_{m,n}^{-}
. Taking the derivative with respect to
\alpha
of the
following expansion,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta}}=\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha}P_{n}(\cos\theta),

(90)

we obtain the expression for the integral:

g_{n}(\cosh\alpha_{0})=2\sqrt{2n+1}\,\frac{e^{-(n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0}}}{\sinh\alpha_{0}}\,.

(91)

We conclude that the diffusive flux is determined by

\mu_{k}=\mu_{0,k}^{+},\quad F_{k}=\frac{|I_{0,k}^{+}|^{2}}{|{\partial\Omega}|J_{0,k}^{+}}\quad(k=0,1,2,\ldots),

(92)

where
|{\partial\Omega}|=8\pi R^{2}
. The first values of
\mu_{k}
and
F_{k}
are
reported in Table
1
.
A practical implementation follows the same lines as in
Sec.
A.2
.

B.2
Perfectly reactive target

For completeness, we reproduce the steady-state concentration and the
diffusive flux onto a pair of perfectly reactive spheres from

[
35
]
. Using the expansion (
90
),
one easily gets:

\displaystyle C_{A}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=C_{A}\biggl(1-\sqrt{2(\cosh\alpha-\cos\theta)}

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0}}\frac{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha)}{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})}P_{n}(\cos\theta)\biggr).

(93)

As a consequence, the diffusive flux reads

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=2DC_{A}\,2\pi c\sqrt{2}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(n+1/2)\alpha_{0}}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx\,P_{n}(x)}{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\frac{\sinh\alpha_{0}}{2\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{0}-x}\,C_{n}^{+}\biggr)

\displaystyle=8\pi DC_{A}c\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(2n+1)\alpha_{0}}\bigl(1+\tanh((n+\tfrac{1}{2})\alpha_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle=16\pi DC_{A}c\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(2n+1)\alpha_{0}}}{1+e^{-(2n+1)\alpha_{0}}}\,,

from which the capacitance follows as

R_{c}=4R\sinh\alpha_{0}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(2n+1)\alpha_{0}}}{1+e^{-(2n+1)\alpha_{0}}}\,.

(94)

Using a geometric series representation, one recovers the expression
(
21
) by Samson and Deutch
[
35
]
. It is worth
noting that this expression differs from the conventional form of the
capacitance
C
of a pair of equal spheres that is found in textbooks
on electrostatics:

C=\frac{R}{2}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sinh(\alpha_{0})}{\sinh(n\alpha_{0})}\,.

(95)

In fact, the latter expression represents the capacitance of a
capacitor when the difference of voltage is applied to two spheres.
In turn, the expression (
21
) corresponds to the voltage
applied between the grounded pair of spheres and infinity.

B.3
Extension to spheres of different radii

The above computation can be extended to the general case of the
exterior of two balls of radii
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. Here we denote two
components of the boundary as
{\partial\Omega}_{1}
and
{\partial\Omega}_{2}
that are defined by
fixing
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, respectively. For convenience, we
assume that
0<|\alpha_{1}|<\alpha_{2}
, i.e.,
R_{2}<R_{1}
and the
second sphere
{\partial\Omega}_{2}
lies above the horizontal plane. One can
distinguish two settings:

(i) If
\alpha_{1}
is positive, one deals with a bounded domain

\Omega
between two non-concentric spheres
\Omega_{2}
and
\Omega_{1}

of radii
R_{i}=c/\sinh(\alpha_{i})
, centered at
(0,0,H_{i})
, with
H_{i}=c\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{i})>0
. The parameters
c
,
\alpha_{1}
and

\alpha_{2}
can be determined by fixing the radii
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
and
the distance between the centers
H=H_{1}-H_{2}>0
. Setting

h_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2}}{H^{2}}+1\biggr),\qquad h_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2}}{H^{2}}-1\biggr),

(96)

such that
H_{i}=h_{i}H
, we get

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=h_{i}\frac{H}{R_{i}},\quad\alpha_{i}=\ln\biggl(x_{i}+\sqrt{x_{i}^{2}-1}\biggr),

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=R_{1}\sinh\alpha_{1}=R_{2}\sinh\alpha_{2}.

(ii) In turn, if
\alpha_{1}<0
, the sphere of radius
R_{1}
lies below
the horizontal plane, with
H_{1}=c\,\mathrm{ctanh}(\alpha_{1})<0
, and one
deals with an unbounded domain
\Omega
. The parameters
c
,

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
can be determined by fixing the radii
R_{1}

and
R_{2}
and the distance between the centers
H=|H_{1}|+H_{2}
.
Setting

h_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(1+\frac{R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2}}{H^{2}}\biggr),\qquad h_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\biggl(1-\frac{R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2}}{H^{2}}\biggr),

(97)

such that
|H_{i}|=h_{i}H
, we get

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=h_{i}\frac{H}{R_{i}},\quad\alpha_{1}=-\ln\biggl(x_{1}+\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}-1}\biggr),

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=\ln\biggl(x_{2}+\sqrt{x_{2}^{2}-1}\biggr),\quad c=R_{1}\sinh|\alpha_{1}|=R_{2}\sinh\alpha_{2}.

As previously, we search a solution of Eq. (
8
) in the
form (
73
), in which
\alpha_{0}
is replaced by

\alpha_{2}
. The boundary condition on
{\partial\Omega}_{2}
remains unchanged and
yields the system (
76
) that we rewrite as

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{-}\bigl(\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{-}\bigr)+A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr(\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{+}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C_{n^{\prime}}^{-}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C_{n^{\prime}}^{+}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}=2c\mu\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}+A_{m,n}^{+}\bigr],

where

\displaystyle C_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\tanh((n+\tfrac{1}{2})\alpha_{2}),

(98a)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\,\mathrm{ctanh}((n+\tfrac{1}{2})\alpha_{2}).

(98b)

In turn, the Steklov condition on
{\partial\Omega}_{1}
becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{n}V|_{{\partial\Omega}_{1}}=-\frac{1}{h_{\alpha_{1}}}(\partial_{\alpha}V)_{|\alpha=\alpha_{1}}=-\frac{\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{1}-\cos\theta}}{2c}

\displaystyle\times\biggl[\sinh\alpha_{1}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C_{n^{\prime}}^{ss}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C_{n^{\prime}}^{cc}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle+(\cosh\alpha_{1}-\cos\theta)\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C_{n^{\prime}}^{cs}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C_{n^{\prime}}^{sc}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mu\sqrt{\cosh\alpha_{1}-\cos\theta}\sum\limits_{m,n^{\prime}}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C_{n^{\prime}}^{ss}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C_{n^{\prime}}^{cc}\bigr]Y_{m,n^{\prime}},

where

\displaystyle C_{n}^{ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{1})}{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{2})}\,,\quad C_{n}^{cc}=\frac{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{1})}{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{2})}\,,

(99a)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{sc}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\frac{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{1})}{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{2})}\,,

(99b)

\displaystyle C_{n}^{cs}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\frac{\cosh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{1})}{\sinh((n+\frac{1}{2})\alpha_{2})}\,.

(99c)

As a consequence, we get

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{-}\bigl(\sinh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{ss}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{cs}\bigr)

\displaystyle+A_{m,n}^{+}\bigl(\sinh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{cc}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{sc}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\bigl[A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{-}C_{n^{\prime}}^{cs}+A_{m,n^{\prime}}^{+}C_{n^{\prime}}^{sc}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=-2c\mu\bigl[A_{m,n}^{-}C_{n}^{ss}+A_{m,n}^{+}C_{n}^{cc}\bigr].

In this setting, two systems of linear equations are not decoupled but
can still be written in a matrix form using a
2\times 2
blocked
structure:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m}&\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m}\\
\end{array}\right)\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\mathbf{M}^{--}_{m}&\mathbf{M}^{-+}_{m}\\
\mathbf{M}^{+-}_{m}&\mathbf{M}^{++}_{m}\\
\end{array}\right)}_{=\mathbf{M}_{m}}=\mu\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{A}^{-}_{m}\\
\mathbf{A}^{+}_{m}\\
\end{array}\right),

(100)

where

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{--}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2c(C_{n}^{cc}-C_{n}^{ss})}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\biggl(C_{n}^{cc}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{-}\bigr]+C_{n}^{ss}\sinh\alpha_{1}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{cs}\biggr)-\bigl[C_{n}^{cc}C_{n^{\prime}}^{-}+C_{n^{\prime}}^{cs}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}\biggr],

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{+-}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2c(C_{n}^{cc}-C_{n}^{ss})}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\biggl(C_{n}^{cc}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{+}\bigr]+C_{n}^{cc}\sinh\alpha_{1}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{sc}\biggr)-\bigl[C_{n}^{cc}C_{n^{\prime}}^{+}+C_{n^{\prime}}^{sc}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}\biggr],

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{-+}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2c(C_{n}^{ss}-C_{n}^{cc})}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\biggl(C_{n}^{ss}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{-}\bigr]+C_{n}^{ss}\sinh\alpha_{1}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{cs}\biggr)-\bigl[C_{n}^{ss}C_{n^{\prime}}^{-}+C_{n^{\prime}}^{cs}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}\biggr],

\displaystyle[\mathbf{M}^{++}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2c(C_{n}^{ss}-C_{n}^{cc})}\biggl[\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}\biggl(C_{n}^{ss}\bigl[\sinh\alpha_{2}+\cosh\alpha_{2}C_{n}^{+}\bigr]+C_{n}^{cc}\sinh\alpha_{1}+\cosh\alpha_{1}C_{n}^{sc}\biggr)-\bigl[C_{n}^{ss}C_{n^{\prime}}^{+}+C_{n^{\prime}}^{sc}\bigr][\mathbf{P}_{m}]_{n^{\prime},n}\biggr].

A numerical diagonalization of the truncated matrix
\mathbf{M}_{m}
allows one
to approximate the Steklov eigenvalues
\mu_{m,n}
, enumerated by
n
,
whereas the left eigenvectors determine the associated eigenfunctions.
Note that the numerical computation becomes unstable in the case

\alpha_{1}>0
because
C_{n}^{cc}-C_{n}^{ss}
is very small for large

n
. An alternative form of the linear system may be needed in this
case.
