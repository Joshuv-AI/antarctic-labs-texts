Associations of early life dietary diversity and child neurodevelopment: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Tianqi Tan, Luyi Chen, Xiaonan Cai, Aifen zhou, Meng Yang, Zhongqiang Cao, Ling Yu, Ruizhen Li, Shiqiong Zhou, Han Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly-life nutrition is crucial for child neurodevelopment, but evidence on the role of dietary diversity remains limited, especially regarding its combined effects during pregnancy and infancy. This study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of maternal and infant dietary diversity score (DDS) with child neurodevelopment.MethodsThis study included 2,773 mother-infant pairs from a prospective birth cohort in Wuhan, China. Maternal DDS during pregnancy and infant DDS at 12 and 24 months of age were assessed. The Bayley Scales were used to evaluate the mental development index (MDI) and psychomotor development index (PDI) of 2-year-old children. Linear and Log-Poisson regression models were used to analyze associations, adjusting for covariates.ResultsHigher maternal DDS was associated with increased MDI scores (β = 0.61 per 1-point increase). Infant DDS at 12 and 24 months positively correlated with MDI (β = 0.52 and 0.34, respectively) and PDI (β = 0.28 and 0.26, respectively). Compared to the lowest tertile, the highest tertile of infant DDS was associated with significantly lower risks of cognitive development delay, with relative risk (95% confidence interval) of 0.32 (0.15–0.71) at 12 months and 0.67 (0.47–0.96) at 24 months (all P for trend < 0.05). Joint analysis showed cumulative benefits of sustained high DDS across early life stages. Specific foods, including, whole grains, red meat, vegetables, and fruits, were positively associated with neurodevelopment, while sweets showed negative associations.ConclusionGreater dietary diversity during pregnancy and infancy was linked to improved child neurocognitive development, emphasizing the importance of balanced nutrition across early-life stages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01328-4.Keywords: Dietary diversity, Child, Neurodevelopment, Nutrition, Cohort study

Introduction

The neurodevelopment of children plays a pivotal role in their comprehensive developmental journey [1]. It not only determines children’s cognitive, language, and motor abilities, but also has a profound impact on their future learning and social adaptability [2]. Early life, particularly the “first 1000 days of life” spanning from the fetal period to 2 years of age, is widely recognized as a critical window for the most rapid and plastic brain development in children [3, 4].

Neurodevelopment is influenced by various factors, including genetics, environment, and nutrition [5, 6]. As a key modifiable determinant, early nutrition not only supports physical growth but also provides essential substrates for neural proliferation, synaptogenesis, and myelin formation. Studies have shown that deficiencies in certain key nutrients can exert adverse effects on children’s cognitive, language, and motor abilities [7, 8]. For instance, long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids are crucial for cognitive function [9], while minerals like iron and zinc are closely related to the development of children’s cognitive and motor skills [10]. In addition, interactions among nutrients also has an impact on children’s neurodevelopment [11].

Dietary diversity usually refers to the richness and distribution balance of food types consumed by an individual, reflecting the breadth and quality of the dietary structure. Previous studies have shown that higher dietary diversity may be associated with better physical development and immune function in children [12–15]. However, current research on the longitudinal associations between early-life dietary diversity and subsequent child neurodevelopmental outcomes remains insufficient. Especially in low- and middle-income countries, problems of monotonous diets and nutritional deficiencies are more common [16]. Assessing the impact of dietary diversity on children’s development outcomes has significant public health significance.

Based on a birth cohort in China, we regularly collected dietary data from mothers and infants, and used standardized neurodevelopmental assessment tools to evaluate children’s cognitive, language, social, and motor abilities. This study adopts a prospective cohort design, aiming to systematically explore the longitudinal association between early-life dietary diversity and child neurodevelopment, and to provide a scientific basis for early nutritional interventions for children.

Methods

Study participants

Participants in this study were part of a prospective birth cohort in which pregnant women were recruited at the first antenatal examination (< 16 weeks of gestation) in the Wuhan Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital, Hubei province, central China. Details of the cohort have been published previously [17]. In brief, after signing the informed consent, participants completed questionnaires on sociodemographic characteristics, medical conditions, lifestyle, underwent anthropometry, and provided specimens at baseline. Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital (Wuhan Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital) and Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2010R009-F04). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Figure S1 shows the datasets constructed in the current study. Among 3,594 children who completed the neurodevelopment assessment at 2 years of age, we further excluded participants with implausible data and those whose dietary information was not surveyed. A total of 2,773 mother-infant pairs were included in the final analysis, of whom 758 had available data on maternal dietary intake during pregnancy, and 1,344 had available data on infant dietary intake at 12 months of age.

Dietary diversity assessment

Dietary intake information of mothers in the second trimester of pregnancy, as well as children aged 12 months and 24 months, was collected via face-to-face interviews. Participants were asked to report their food consumption frequency over the prior 4 weeks, and were permitted to use daily, weekly, or monthly as the reporting unit based on their actual intake habits to ensure reporting accuracy. All reported frequency data were uniformly converted to average weekly intake frequency before formal scoring. In this study, dietary diversity was assessed based on 14 food categories: refined grains, whole grains, tubers, vegetables, fruits, red meat, poultry meat, animal viscera, seafood, eggs, bean products, milk products, nuts, and sweets. For each food category, the standardized weekly intake frequency was scored as follows: 0 points for 0 times per week, 1 point for ≤ 3 times per week, and 2 points for > 3 times per week [18]. The distribution of scores for each food group is shown in Table S1. Accordingly, the dietary diversity score (DDS) across the 14 food categories ranged from 0 to 28 points.

Child neurodevelopment assessment

The neurocognitive development level of 2-year-old children was assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant Development of China Revision (BSID-CR) by licensed pediatric physicians at the study hospital. All evaluations were video-recorded for quality control. The BSID-CR includes two primary scales: the mental development index (MDI) to assess cognition, language, and social development, and the psychomotor development index (PDI) to assess gross and fine motor skills. Raw scores were transformed into a standardized scale with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation (SD) of 15, where lower scores indicate poorer performance. Consistent with previous studies, children with MDI or PDI scores below 80 were classified as having developmental delay [19, 20].

Covariates

Information on maternal characteristics was obtained by a questionnaire at enrollment, including age (years), annual family income (< 50,000, 50,000 ~ 100,000, > 100,000 Chinese Yuan, CNY), folic acid supplement (no, yes), and passive smoking (no, yes). Pre-pregnancy BMI (in kg/m2) was calculated based on the self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and maternal height measured at enrollment, and categorized as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 ~ 23.9 kg/m2), overweight or obesity (≥ 24.0 kg/m2). Delivery and birth information were obtained from hospital medical records, including date of birth, delivery mode (vaginal, cesarean), child’s sex (boys, girls), gestational age at delivery (weeks), birth weight (g), and birth height (cm). Gestational age at delivery was calculated based on the last menstrual period and the date of birth. Infant breastfeeding duration and complementary food intake were collected at 3, 6, and 12 months postpartum.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were presented as means and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Differences of MDI and PDI scores among groups were tested using one-way ANOVA and Student’s t-test. Missing data for categorical variables were coded as a missing indicator category for analysis.

Generalized linear models (GLM) were used to estimate the association of maternal and infant DDS with MDI and PDI scores. Log-Poisson regression models were used to evaluate the relative risk (RR) and confidence interval (CI) of DDS with cognitive development delay. Models were fit using DDS as a continuous variable or as categorical variables (tertiles). Tertiles were defined based on the distribution of DDS, with the lowest tertile (T1) as the reference. P-values for linear trend were calculated using the coding tertiles of DDS as continuous variables in the regression models. The combination of maternal and infant DDS was further divided into four groups: Low-Low, Low-High, High-Low, and High-High, with the Low-Low group as the reference. The adjusted model included maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, annual family income, passive smoking, folic acid supplement, child’s sex, birth mode, gestational age at delivery, birth weight, and birth height. We assessed multicollinearity among all covariates using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were < 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity in the adjusted regression models (Table S2).

We explored the possible nonlinear associations between DDS and child neurodevelopment (MDI and PDI scores) using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of DDS and excluding DDS above the 99th percentiles. Sensitivity analyses were conducted as follows: (1) exclusion of pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus and hypertension; (2) exclusion of children with preterm, low birth weight, and macrosomia; (3) further adjustment for infant breastfeeding duration (< 6, 6 to < 12, ≥ 12 months).

For food group-specific association analyses with child neurodevelopment, participants were grouped by weekly intake frequency of each food item. For 12 food groups, grouping was based on a cut-point of ≥ 3 times/week vs. < 3 times/week, aligned with our pre-specified DDS scoring criteria. For animal viscera and sweets, the cut-point was adjusted to ≥ 1 time/week vs. < 1 time/week, due to the extremely small number of participants with intake ≥ 3 times/week (Table S1), which would result in insufficient statistical power for the association test. All P values were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR).

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.3.2; two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 shows the distribution of baseline characteristics and child neurodevelopment scores. A total of 2,773 mother-infant pairs were included in the current analysis; the mean age of mothers was 29.8 ± 3.7 years, the pregnancy BMI was 21.1 ± 2.9 kg/m2, and 1,419 (51.2%) infants were boys. Child neurodevelopment was tested at 24.4 ± 1.1 months of age, with a mean MDI score of 111.3 ± 21.8 and a mean PDI score of 111.9 ± 17.2. Children with higher MDI scores were more likely to have younger mothers who used folic acid supplements during pregnancy, higher annual family income, vaginal delivery, and female sex (P < 0.05). Besides, higher PDI scores were observed in children whose mothers had prenatal passive smoking exposure and in those delivered vaginally (P < 0.05).

Dietary diversity score and child neurodevelopment

The median (interquartile range, IQR) of maternal DDS during pregnancy was 19 (18, 21), while infant DDS was 16 (13, 18) at 12 months and 19 (17, 21) at 24 months. Linear regression models indicated positive associations of maternal DDS with MDI scores, and of infant DDS with MDI and PDI scores (Table 2). Each one-point increase in maternal DDS was associated with an MDI increase of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.04, 1.19); corresponding increases for infant DDS at 12 and 24 months were 0.52 (0.26, 0.78) and 0.34 (0.10, 0.59) for MDI, and 0.28 (0.06, 0.50) and 0.26 (0.06, 0.45) for PDI, respectively. Compared with the lowest tertile, MDI scores increased by 4.81 (1.29, 8.32) in the highest tertile of maternal DDS; MDI scores increased by 4.53 (1.97, 7.10) and PDI scores increased by 2.24 (0.07, 4.41) in the highest tertile of infant DDS at 12 months; MDI scores increased by 3.07 (1.19, 4.95) and PDI scores increased by 2.08 (0.56, 3.60) in the highest tertile of infant DDS at 24 months (all P for trend < 0.05). A similar pattern of associations between DDS and child neurodevelopment scores was observed across all sensitivity analyses (Table S3).

The incidence of cognitive development delay (MDI < 80) was 9.5%, and psychomotor development delay (PDI < 80) was 2.7% for 2,773 children. Joint associations between maternal diet, infant diet, and child neurodevelopment

The combined associations of maternal and infant DDS with child neurodevelopment are presented in Table 3. Participants were divided into four groups based on the median DDS value at each time point. Compared with the Low-Low group (low maternal DDS during pregnancy and low infant DDS at 24 months), the MDI score was increased by 5.65 (95% CI: 1.76, 9.55) in the High-High group. Similarly, compared with infants with low DDS at both 12 and 24 months, those with sustained high DDS at both time points exhibited an increase in MDI score of 4.24 (95% CI: 1.45, 7.03) and in PDI score of 3.74 (95% CI: 1.38, 6.10).

Each food group consumption and child neurodevelopment

The consumption frequency of each food group included in the DDS is shown in Table S1. Cereals, vegetables, and fruits were the most frequently consumed foods, while animal viscera and sweets were the least frequently consumed. Figure 2 illustrates the association between food group consumption at different stages and child neurodevelopment scores. After FDR correction for multiple testing, no maternal food groups during pregnancy were significantly associated with offspring MDI or PDI (Table S5). In infants, whole grains, tubers, fruits and red meat at 12 months, as well as red meat at 24 months, were significantly positively associated with MDI; sweets intake at both 12 and 24 months was significantly inversely associated with MDI (all P FDR < 0.05). For PDI, vegetable intake at 12 months and red meat intake at 24 months showed significant positive associations (both P FDR < 0.05).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine independent and joint associations between the maternal and infant diet diversity and child neurodevelopment. Our results demonstrated that higher maternal DDS during pregnancy was associated with higher MDI scores, higher infant DDS was associated with higher MDI and PDI scores, as well as a lower risk of cognitive development delay. Joint effect analysis revealed that the positive associations were most pronounced in the group with sustained high DDS across both pregnancy and infancy. Furthermore, maternal and infant consumption of whole grains, red meat, vegetables, and fruits was positively associated with child neurodevelopmental scores, whereas sweets intake showed a negative association.

Dietary guidelines generally advocate for a diversified dietary pattern, emphasizing the balanced intake of various grains, fruits, vegetables, dairy products, and high-quality protein foods. During the early stages of life, especially from the fetal period to infancy, ensuring dietary diversity is particularly important, as it not only influences growth and development but also profoundly impacts cognitive function, immune response, and long-term health trajectories [4]. Substantial evidence indicates that dietary diversity in infancy effectively prevents deficiencies in key micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and vitamin A, thereby establishing a strong foundation for healthy development [21, 22]. Previous studies have reported that maternal dietary patterns, dietary quality, and dietary inflammatory index during pregnancy are associated with child neurodevelopment [23, 24]. This study expands these findings by employing the DDS as a comprehensive indicator to systematically evaluate the association between maternal and infant dietary patterns and child neurodevelopment. Our findings demonstrate that maintaining high DDS in both maternal pregnancy and infancy exerts a more pronounced positive effect on neurodevelopment compared to optimizing dietary diversity in a single stage. This discovery provides new insights for public health practice, underscoring the importance of sustaining dietary diversity from pregnancy through infancy as a continuous approach to supporting optimal child development via cumulative benefits across these critical windows.

For specific food groups, multiple cohort studies have confirmed that a mother’s intake of fruits and vegetables during pregnancy is associated with improved cognitive abilities in her offspring [25, 26], which is consistent with our findings regarding the benefits of vegetables and fruits. Similarly, other studies have also emphasized the importance of iron-rich foods such as red meat for the cognitive development of infants [27, 28], supporting the positive correlation we have observed. For pregnant women, particular emphasis should be placed on increasing the intake of dark-colored vegetables, whole grains, and high-quality protein. When introducing complementary foods to infants, it is important to pay attention to the diversity of food types and avoid introducing foods with added sugar too early [29]. Although the composite DDS showed consistent positive associations with neurodevelopmental outcomes, food group-specific analyses revealed heterogeneous findings. This pattern reflects that the overall DDS captures broader dietary variety and a nutrient-rich dietary pattern, while associations for individual food groups may differ. The overall beneficial effect of higher DDS is primarily driven by the cumulative contributions of nutrient-dense food groups, which provide a wide range of brain-supporting nutrients. Importantly, our findings highlight that public health and clinical guidance should promote adequate dietary diversity during pregnancy and early life, while limiting excessive sweets intake to support optimal child neurodevelopment.

The positive impact of maternal and infant dietary diversity on child neurodevelopment may involve multiple complementary biological mechanisms, all centred on the core advantages of diversified dietary patterns. Firstly, maternal dietary diversity delivers a comprehensive, synergistic supply of key nutrients for fetal brain development. Nutrients essential for neuronal proliferation and synaptic formation can be fully and balancedly covered through a diversified diet, avoiding the risk of single nutrient deficiency that impairs fetal brain development [30]. Secondly, infant dietary diversity is a core driver of early-life gut microbiota colonization and maturation, and influences child neurodevelopment through the gut-brain axis [31, 32]. Diverse food intake provides abundant dietary fiber, prebiotics and polyphenols to promote gut microbial diversity, and its fermentation product short-chain fatty acids exert neuroprotective effects and regulate synaptic plasticity [33]. Thirdly, iron and zinc in red meat are involved in myelination and synaptic plasticity [10], while excessive sugar intake may impair hippocampal development through oxidative stress [34]. Finally, the results of the joint analysis suggest that nutritional interventions during pregnancy and infancy may have cumulative benefits across early life stages, with independent favorable effects observed at both the pregnancy and infancy periods. Maternal nutritional reserves lay the foundation for the early development of infants, while the diet during infancy further consolidates this advantage [35].

This study has several significant advantages, including its prospective longitudinal design, which enables the establishment of a temporal sequence between dietary exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes. Dietary assessments at multiple time points (pregnancy, 12 months, 24 months) and the use of standardized developmental scales enhance the reliability of the results. However, several limitations must also be recognized. Firstly, dietary diversity was assessed via self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias. Secondly, although we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounding factors, we could not completely rule out residual confounding caused by unmeasured factors, such as genetic predisposition, family learning environment, and parental intelligence. For instance, mothers with higher DDS may also provide a enriched cognitive stimulation environment for their children, which may partly explain the observed association. Thirdly, we did not quantify specific nutrient intake, making it difficult to distinguish the independent contribution of active ingredients in each food group. Finally, the generalizability of our findings requires caution due to differences in dietary habits across ethnic and cultural groups. Future studies should employ randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up to establish causal relationships, while incorporating biochemical markers to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

In conclusion, this study is the first to investigate the independent and joint associations of maternal and infant dietary diversity with child neurodevelopment, and demonstrates favorable effects of higher dietary diversity at both stages. Furthermore, our findings suggest that child neurocognitive development could be enhanced in specific domains by promoting higher intake of whole grains, red meat, vegetables, and fruits, while limiting excessive sweets consumption during pregnancy and infancy.
