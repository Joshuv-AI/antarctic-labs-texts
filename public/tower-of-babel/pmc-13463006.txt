Divergent synthesis of γ-lactones and cyclopropanes via efficient [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulations of amide-sulfoxonium ylides
Authors: Yougen Xu, Haiting Wu, An Lin, Shuang Luo, Lebin Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctionalized γ-lactones and cyclopropanes, especially those bearing amide substituents, play important roles in diverse chemical and pharmacological processes, but their synthesis remains challenging. Here, we report a high-throughput protocol that employs amide-sulfoxonium ylides as versatile C1 synthons in efficient [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulation reactions with readily available alkene derivatives, delivering a variety of (hetero)amide-substituted γ-lactones and cyclopropanes in good yields. This simple approach demonstrates a broad substrate scope, high functional group compatibility, and excellent chemo- and diastereoselectivity, and can even be utilized for the late-stage diversification of complex bioactive molecules. Combined with machine learning, a yield predictive model for cyclopropane formation is established. Mechanistically, both [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulations are initiated by intermolecular Michael addition, diverging into selective 5-exo-trig and 3-exo-trig cyclization, respectively. Moreover, these synthetic transformations provide a reliable and efficient route to complex γ-lactones and cyclopropanes that are otherwise difficult to access.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

The γ-lactone structural motif is considered an attractive and important scaffold in organic synthesis and drug discovery because it exhibits diverse biological activities and forms the core structure of numerous natural products, bioactive molecules, perfumes, and food additives (Fig. 1a)1–8. Due to their importance in both medicinal chemistry and synthetic communities, the approaches for constructing the lactone skeleton have been extensively studied over the past decade. Traditionally, γ-lactone compounds are synthesized via halolactonization and intramolecular cyclization from prefunctionalized carboxylic acids9–13. However, the difficulty in synthesizing these reactants often limits its applications. Recently, transition-metal-catalyzed or photocatalytic annulation reactions of aliphatic acid derivatives have emerged as straightforward and powerful strategies for accessing complex γ-lactones. For instance, Yu et al.14,15, Jeganmohan et al.16, Maiti et al.17, Gemmeren et al.18, Glorius et al.19, Chi et al.20, and Wu et al.21 have independently achieved these transformations, such as Pd-catalyzed double γ-C(sp³)–H functionalization of aliphatic acids with iodobenzenes or allyl alcohols, β-C(sp3)–H functionalization of aliphatic carboxylic acids with allyl alcohols, C–H oxidation of carboxylic acids, photocatalytic annulation of cyclic malonyl peroxides with olefins, coupling of 1,1-dicarboxylic acids with alkenes, as well as the carbonylation of multi-substituted homoallylic alcohols with CO. Although these advances provide straightforward access to γ-lactone, the requirement for noble metals (e.g., Pd and Ag), oxidants, ligands, photocatalysts, or harsh conditions often limits their general applicability. Therefore, the development of simple and efficient methods for the synthesis of functionalized γ-lactones is highly desirable.

Alternatively, annulation of acrylic acids with alkene derivatives has been established as an efficient method for the construction of functional γ-lactones. In 2020, the Cramer group reported a CpxRh-catalyzed enantioselective [4 + 1] annulation of acryl acids with allenes, which is used for the synthesis of α,β-unsaturated γ-lactones (Fig. 1b)22. In 2025, Takemoto and co-workers developed a photocatalytic method for synthesizing γ-lactones through [3 + 2] cycloaddition between acryl acids and alkenes (Fig. 1c)23. However, challenges in the preparation of the catalysts have restricted these strategies. More recently, Pandey et al. disclosed the annulative coupling of α-substituted acrylic acids with sulfoxonium ylides, which furnishes keto γ-lactones, via O–H bond insertion and subsequent intramolecular cyclization (Fig. 1d)24. Despite significant advances in the synthesis of functionalized γ-lactones (e.g., alkenyl, alkyl, and keto-substituted) through intermolecular alkene cyclization, analogous amide-substituted derivatives have yet to be realized. Along with the γ-lactone ring, the amide moiety is also widely found in natural products and possesses a broad range of biological activities25–27. Given the importance of γ-lactone compounds and our ongoing interest in amide and sulfoxonium ylide transformations28–33, we report herein a metal-free, highly selective [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulation reaction between amide-sulfoxonium ylides and acryl acids or alkenes (Fig. 1e). Amide-sulfoxonium ylides are generally bench-stable, inexpensive, and low-toxicity reagents that can be prepared in two steps from commercially available aryl and alkyl amines via acylation and nucleophilic addition with methylide, and can be readily transformed into metal carbenes for X–H insertion and cross-coupling reactions28,29. This efficient, regioselective reaction provides straightforward access to a variety of (hetero)amide-substituted γ-lactones and cyclopropanes. Mechanistically, [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulations initiate via intermolecular Michael addition, followed by selective 5-exo-trig and 3-exo-trig cyclization, respectively. Both modes proceed via anionic/nucleophilic attack and are kinetically favored according to Baldwin’s rules34–36.

Cyclopropanes, particularly those with molecular skeletons containing an amide group, are widely found in bioactive natural products and pharmaceuticals, such as olaparib for the treatment of ovarian cancer, milnacipran as an antidepressant, and lumacaftor as a CFTR regulator (Fig. 1a)37,38. Moreover, cyclopropane molecules are also widely utilized as versatile building blocks in synthetic chemistry and as radical clocks in mechanistic studies39,40. Traditional methods for the synthesis of cyclopropanes mainly rely on [1 + 2] cyclization reactions of olefins41–56, including metal and enzyme-catalyzed reactions with diazo compounds, Simmons–Smith reaction, Kulinkovich reaction, Corey–Chaykovsky reaction, cyclopropanation of gem-dihaloalkanes, photoinduced radical cyclopropanation, and others. While significant progress has been made in constructing cyclopropane scaffolds with alcohol, amine, boron, and silicon substituents, the direct and selective synthesis of amide-substituted cyclopropanes remains largely unexplored and is generally limited in terms of drug discovery and downstream transformations.

Over the past decade, sulfoxonium ylides have emerged as important synthetic intermediates, which have been extensively employed in a variety of reactions57–68, including cyclization, X–H insertion (X = N, O, S, B, Si, Ge, and P, etc.), C–H functionalization, cross-coupling, cycloaddition, multicomponent reaction, and others. Importantly, sulfoxonium ylides have also been employed as carbene precursors in various catalytic intramolecular and intermolecular cyclopropanation reactions involving olefins69–72. Despite significant advancements, the cyclopropanation reaction between terminal alkenes and sulfoxonium ylides remains underexplored. In 2025, Baidya, Mane, and co-workers reported the first Pd(II)-catalyzed, diastereoselective cyclopropanation of alkenyl amines with sulfur ylides73. Very recently, Jin et al., Song et al., and co-workers developed a method for synthesizing 1,1-dicarbonyl-substituted cyclopropanes using water as a catalyst, which involves a [2 + 2] cycloaddition process between sulfoxonium ylides and olefins74. Unlike the work published by Jin et al., an amide-sulfoxonium ylide was first used as a C1-synthon in this study, undergoing a [1 + 2] annulation to deliver the target compounds. It is worth noting that the reaction between ester sulfoxonium ylides and Michael receptors could only give α-alkylated products in the previous report at 150 °C75, indicating that the cyclopropanation reaction of sulfoxonium ylides remains a challenge.

In this work, a wide variety of complex amide-substituted γ-lactones and cyclopropanes are facilely constructed in moderate to excellent yields with excellent chemoselectivity under mild and operationally simple conditions. This reaction proceeds via a selective intermolecular Michael addition under metal- and base-free conditions, which allows for highly efficient and regioselective [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulation. Moreover, the ease of gram-scale synthesis and versatile transformations highlight its significant potential for applications in organic synthesis. It is worth highlighting that a conducive ML-based model (XGB-MAF & RDKit) was employed for cyclopropane synthesis, achieving excellent performance in reaction yield prediction (mean absolute error in yield as 7.3% for external test).

Results and discussion

Reaction development

At the outset, we explored the intermolecular [1 + 4] annulation reaction by using amide-sulfoxonium ylide (1a) with acrylic acid (2a) as the model substrates. Extensive HTE screening revealed that amide-substituted γ-lactone product (3) was obtained in 68% yield with excellent selectivity under catalyst-free conditions in CH3CN at 30 °C in air for 24 h (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, scale-up of the reaction from 5 μmol (microscale-HTE experiment) to 0.1 mmol scale was demonstrated in a similar 65% yield. To our delight, the yield of target product (3) increased with temperature, reaching 84% at 60 °C (Table 1, entries 1–4). Control experiments identified CH3CN as the optimal solvent for this annulation reaction, whereas catalyst [Ir(cod)Cl]2 triggered side reactions that reduced efficiency and selectivity (Table 1, entries 5–13). Notably, using redistilled CH3CN and deionized H2O as solvents, the [1 + 4] annulation also proceeded smoothly, affording the target product (3) in 83% and 35% yields, respectively (Table 1, entries 11 and 12). These results demonstrate that the reaction does not require metal catalysis.

Substrate scope exploration

Under the optimal reaction conditions, the scope of substrates for synthesizing γ-lactones was evaluated. First, a broad range of amide-sulfoxonium ylides were investigated in the reaction with acrylic acid 2a (Fig. 2). Diphenylamide-sulfoxonium ylides with various electron-donating and -withdrawing functional groups on the benzene ring, such as methyl (4 and 10), methoxy (5), dimethylamino (6), fluoro (7), chloro (8), bromo (9 and 11), trifluoromethyl (12), and azo group (13), were well tolerated and furnished the desired γ-lactone products in 70–93% yields with high selectivity. Disubstituted and polycyclic arylamide-sulfoxonium ylide substrates (14 and 15) exhibited high efficiency as well and afforded the corresponding cyclization products. Substrates bearing five- or six-membered heterocycles, including acridine (16 and 17), phenothiazine (18), phenoxazine (19), carbazole (20), and quinoline (21), afforded the corresponding γ-lactone products in moderate to good yields. Various aryl amide-derived sulfoxonium ylides bearing methyl (22 and 23), isopropyl (24), benzyl (25), cyclohexyl (26), cyanomethyl (27), allyl (28), or 2-thienyl (29) substituents also underwent smooth [1 + 4] cycloaddition. Notably, N,N-dialkylamide sulfoxonium ylide (30) afforded lower yield in this reaction. This observation can be attributed to the unique electronic features of N-acylanilines (anilides), specifically amide resonance destabilization, which confers higher reactivity upon N-anilides76. In addition to tertiary amide-sulfoxonium ylides, NH-amide-sulfoxonium ylides bearing either an electron-donating substituent (31–34 and 40), a halogen (35–39), or an electron-withdrawing substituent (41), irrespective of their location at the para-, meta- or ortho- position, all gave desired products in moderate to good yields. Moreover, disubstituted and trisubstituted substrates also exhibited good efficiency and gave the corresponding γ-lactone products (42 and 43) in 44% and 67% yields, respectively. The reaction using amide-sulfoxonium ylides having naphthyl, fluorenyl, thiophenyl, benzofuryl, dibenzo[b,d]furyl, and indole groups also underwent well, affording the desired products (44–49) in 29–75% yields.

Besides amide-sulfoxonium ylides, this tandem cyclization reaction was also viable for other sulfoxonium ylides. For example, the reaction proceeded smoothly with N-heterocyclic sulfoxonium ylides (i.e., 3-methylindole and 5-chloroindole), affording the desired γ-lactone products (50 and 51) in 70% and 90% yields, respectively. The structure of product 50 was confirmed by X-ray diffraction. Moreover, the utility of the protocol was further examined on (hetero)aryl and vinyl α-keto sulfoxonium ylides, furnishing the desired keto γ-lactone products (52–55) in moderate to good yields. We then used this protocol to investigate the derivatizations of various pharmaceutical agents and other bioactive molecules, such as tryptamine, procaine, vortioxetine, and amoxapine. Under the standard conditions, these compounds smoothly underwent the [1 + 4] cycloaddition reaction to afford the corresponding γ-lactone products (56–59) in 48–88% yields. These results demonstrate the applicability of this method for the synthesis of drug-like molecules. Unfortunately, disubstituted amide or keto sulfoxonium ylides (S58–S60) failed to afford the target γ-lactone products bearing quaternary carbon were failed (Supplementary Table 3). This limitation arises because the [1 + 4] cyclization initiates via an intermolecular Michael addition that requires a proton on the ylide carbon (vide infra).

Notably, α-methyl or α-phenyl acrylic acid also were suitable substrate for the reaction, affording cyclization products (60 and 61) in 23% and 37% yields. In contrast, crotonic acid failed to yield the desired product (62), presumably due to the increased steric hindrance from the β-methyl substituents, which inhibit the key Michael addition step.

This metal-free annulation method could also be expanded to include cyclopropanation reactions involving amide-sulfoxonium ylides and acrylic ester derivatives, and the corresponding cyclopropane products (63–92) were obtained in moderate to excellent yields with high diastereoselectivities (Fig. 3). Notably, we discovered that the cyclopropanation of amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a with 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl acrylate could be efficiently achieved under a slightly modified reaction condition (in DMF at 90 °C), affording the desired substituted cyclopropane (63) in 86% yield. This [1 + 2] annulation method also features a broad scope, accommodating various amide-sulfoxonium ylides. The reaction could proceed smoothly with diarylamide-sulfoxonium ylides bearing different functional groups, such as methyl, methoxy, fluoro, chloro, bromo, and trifluoromethyl, giving the desired cyclopropane products (64–69) in generally good to high yields and high diastereoselectivities. In addition, amide-sulfoxonium ylides with various heterocycles, including acridine, phenothiazine, phenoxazine, and indole, were suitable substrates for this cyclopropanation to deliver the corresponding products (70–73) in 43–86% yields. The amide-sulfoxonium ylide containing an isopropyl (74) also worked well and gave high stereocontrol (20:1 d.r.). Delightfully, amide-sulfoxonium ylides derived from bioactive vortioxetine and amoxapine were successfully applied as cyclopropanation substrates and furnished the desired corresponding products (75 and 76) in 60% and 66% yields, respectively. Furthermore, when keto- or ester-substituted sulfoxonium ylides were employed under the same reaction conditions, the corresponding cyclopropanation products (77 and 78) were also obtained in good yields with high selectivity.

Next, we also investigated the scope of terminal alkenes in this amide-substituted cyclopropane synthesis. The alkyl-substituted acrylic esters, including ethyl, benzyl, tert-butyl, and adamantyl, which exhibited good compatibility with the reaction system (79‒83), and similarly, the phenyl-substituted acrylic ester effectively gave the desired substituted cyclopropane (84) with a 15:1 d.r. value. Notably, steric hindrance significantly influences the reaction yield and stereoselectivity (79‒83). Gratifyingly, many long-chain acrylic esters serve as suitable reaction partners to provide the corresponding cyclopropanes (85‒88), including substrates that contain an unactivated olefin, dimethylamine, primary alcohol, and ether. It was worthing noted that a bis-acrylate was subjected to the same reaction conditions, resulting in the expected product 89 was isolated in 60% yield with one acrylate group intact. Furthermore, α-Me-substituted acrylates also undergo diastereoselective cyclopropanation successfully (90 and 91). α-methylene-γ-butyrolactones (MBLs) are prevalent structural motifs in organic chemistry, frequently found in biologically active molecules and pharmaceuticals77. They served as suitable substrates in this cyclopropanation, resulting in spiroketal amides (92 and 93) in 52 and 65% yields, and the reaction maintained high chemoselectivity even in the presence of epoxide within the molecule (93). The molecular structure of 91 was confirmed by X-ray diffraction. Thus, this metal-free cyclopropanation strategy provides a convenient and practical route for the preparation of the synthetically useful amide-substituted cyclopropanes. In addition to acrylates, acrylketone (94), acrylaldehyde (95), acrylamide (96), and acrylonitrile (97) could also be used to furnish the corresponding cyclopropane products in moderate to high yields. In contrast, using styrene and aliphatic olefins as substrates failed to deliver cyclopropanation products (98 and 99), highlighting the necessity of electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., esters) on the alkene. Thus, this protocol offers a concise and selective route to diverse functionalized cyclopropanes from activated alkenes bearing ester, ketone, aldehyde, amide, and nitrile functional groups.

Machine learning model development

In the past few decades, the development of efficient synthetic methods for preparing natural products, drugs, and functional materials has often involved extensive experimentation and a trial-and-error approach. Thus, it is in great demand to develop a predictive model for a newly developed reaction, as there is always a high degree of uncertainty when dealing with unseen substrate molecules. Recently, machine learning (ML) technology has emerged as an effective tool for synthetic chemists because it has been shown good performance in yielding prediction for several classically coupling reactions78–80. This advancement in predictive analytics for results enables researchers to optimize reaction conditions, screen substrates, discovery reaction reagents, and design efficient synthetic pathways more effectively. Despite significant advances, developing ML-based models for modern reaction methods is challenging due to difficulties in collecting standard experimental data. Moreover, high-quality data, including negative data and structural diversity, play a key role in the artificial intelligence (AI) chemistry field81,82. Specifically, three key components are required to build an ML-based model: data, descriptor, and ML method.

In view of the importance of cyclopropane fragments in organic synthesis, which can be easily prepared from readily available alkenes, we conducted 648 miniaturized [1 + 2] cycloaddition reactions. Consequently, the corresponding high-throughput data were successfully collected (for details, see Supplementary Figs. 3–15). These data were divided into two datasets. One dataset with 375 reaction data, consisting of 15 amide-sulfoxonium ylides (A1‒A15) and 25 terminal alkenes (B1‒B25; Fig. 4a), was used to build a ML-based model. Another dataset with 273 reactions data, including unseen amide-sulfoxonium ylides and terminal alkenes (Fig. 4b), was used to evaluate the model for out-of-sample prediction (for details, see Supplementary Figs. 11–15).

a Substrate scope of the cyclopropanation reaction for HTE. b The development of ML-based models. c The 5-fold cross-validation result of XGB-MAF & RDKit. d Unseen amide-sulfoxonium ylides and alkenes. e External validation. f Selected examples in machine learning prediction model.

In this study, descriptors of reactants and corresponding products were used as the model input and reaction yield was defined as the model output. For example, a descriptor called molecular additive fingerprints (MAF), which derived from extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs)83. Moreover, other descriptors, such as RDKit, one-hot, and multiple fingerprint feature (MFF)84, MAF & RDKit, were also tested. Four frequently-used ML methods85–88, such as gradient boosted trees (GBT), random forest regression (RF), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGB), and support vector regression (SVR), were investigated with five types of descriptors aforementioned (Fig. 4c). The results indicated that the MAF & RDKit descriptors appear to be more effective than other descriptors for reaction prediction.

When the 375 reaction data were used for modeling, 80% of the data were used to train the regression model, and the remaining 20% were used as a validation set, and followed by a 5-fold cross-validation procedure (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Model performance was then assessed by coefficient of determination (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE). The XGB method was found to give the best performance on the validation set when MAF & RDKit were used as a descriptor (Fig. 4c, entry 19), resulting in an R2 value of 0.88, MAE of 5.9%, and RMSE of 7.9% (Fig. 4d). When employing MFF encoding as a descriptor in the XGB model, it could achieve comparable predictive performance for R2 and a slightly inferior performance for MAE and RMSE (Fig. 4c, entry 3). The model results of one hot encoding combined with the XGB ML method were good, giving an R2 value of 0.87, MAE of 6.2%, and RMSE of 8.1% (Fig. 4c, entry 11). Notably, the combination of RDKit and the SVR ML method also achieve similarly good performance, yielding an R2 value of 0.86, MAE of 6.3%, and RMSE of 8.3% (Fig. 4c, entry 8). To further demonstrate the practicability of the prediction model, we conducted an external test. Using the external dataset, which included 273 unseen reactions (as shown in Fig. 4b), the model performed quite well, with an R² value of 0.76 and MAE of 7.3%. It is worth noting that the external set included a range of unseen amide-sulfoxonium ylides (A16‒A18) and terminal alkenes (B26‒B36), representing a distinct chemical space, which resulted in a RMSE of 9.3% for the prediction model (Fig. 4e). The above results demonstrate the excellent learning ability of the XGB-MAF & RDKit model to some extent, and suggest that the model has the potential to address practical problems in synthetic chemistry. As depicted in Fig. 4f, the cyclopropanation model exhibits good performance in predicting the yields for complex substrates with various functional groups, including amine, alkyne, alcohol, γ-lactone, amide, and vortioxetine.

Synthetic applications

To further demonstrate the synthetic utility of this methodology, we carried out scale-up experiments and a series of chemical transformations. At 5 mmol scale, 3 and 63 can be produced smoothly without obvious decrease of yield (Fig. 5a). The reaction of the bis-acrylate 100 with 1a was carried out; interestingly, and furnished the corresponding amide-substituted bis-cyclopropane product 101 in 65% yield (Fig. 5b). Compound 3 could react with MeMgBr and Me2CuLi reagents to afford α,α-dimethylamine-substituted γ-lactone (102) and diol-substituted γ-lactone (103) in 50% and 74% yields, respectively89,90. Moreover, alcohol substituted 1,5-dicarbonyl compound (104) could be successfully constructed from 3 through hydrolysis and subsequently esterification. Additionally, the reaction of compound 3 with Me₂NH yielded the 1,5-diamide product (105). Meanwhile, the selective reduction of the γ-lactone moiety in compound 3 gave the corresponding amide product bearing dihydroxyl groups (106) in 81% yield. Interestingly, the amide-substituted γ-lactone (3) could also be readily transformed into either the thioamide-substituted γ-lactone (107) or the thioamide-substituted γ-thiolactone (108) via thiocarbonylation using Lawsone’s reagent (Fig. 5c)91. In addition, the amide-substituted cyclopropane 63 could be reduced by [Mn(CO)5Br] with Ph2SiH2 to deliver the corresponding tertiary amine product (109) in good yield92, and the ester was reduced with NaBH4 to yield the amide-substituted cyclopropylmethanol (110) in a 60% yield. Cyclopropanecarboxylic acid (111) can be efficiently prepared from 63 through ester hydrolysis using LiOH (Fig. 5d)93.

Mechanistic studies

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the metal-free [1 + 4] cycloaddition of amide-sulfoxonium ylides with acrylic acids, several control experiments were conducted (Fig. 5e). First, the reaction of amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a with acrylic acid 2a was conducted at room temperature, affording the corresponding γ-lactone product (3) in 52% yield. Instead, using cinnamic acid (112) instead of acrylic acid (2a) under the same conditions, no expected O–H insertion product (113) was observed, and substrate 1a was almost completely recovered. These results indicated that O–H insertion does not constitute the initial step in this tandem [1 + 4] cycloaddition reaction. Then, we conducted the experiment with ethyl acrylate (114) to replace 2a as the substrate, the Michael-addition product (115) was observed, which was confirmed by UPLC-MS (Supplementary Fig. 24). This result supported that the Michael-addition product might be a reaction intermediate, indicating that the addition process is indeed the first step in this reaction (vide infra).

In addition, to explore the mechanism of the [1 + 2] cycloaddition reaction, a series of control experiments also were carried out (Fig. 5f). Initially, a one-pot reaction of acrylic acid 2a and 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl acrylate 2b with amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a; interestingly, γ-lactone product 3 was selectively obtained in 42% yield without the formation of the cyclopropanation product, probably because intramolecular O–H insertion is easier than C–H insertion. Next, intermolecular competitive experiments demonstrated that alkene derivatives bearing more electron-deficient groups react preferentially, indicating that Michael addition is essential for the [1 + 2] annulation (Fig. 5f). Under the reaction conditions, dimethylsulfoxonium methylide reacted with benzyl acrylate (117), but no cyclopropanation product (118) was observed. However, when ethyl cinnamate (119) was used as the substrate, the corresponding [1 + 2] cycloaddition product (120) was isolated in 23% yield. These findings suggest that the classical Corey–Chaykovsky reaction exhibits higher reactivity toward internal olefins. Notably, the reaction or amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a with ethyl cinnamate (119) failed to afford the corresponding product (121) under the present conditions. This result suggests that the [1 + 2] annulation reaction may proceed via a novel pathway.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were also performed to elucidate the reaction mechanism of the metal-free [1 + 4] cycloaddition process leading to the formation of the γ-lactone product 3 (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 30). Starting from amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a with acrylic acid 2a, two possible pathways were calculated: a O–H insertion and intramolecular Michael-addition tandem process (path A, red pathway) and an intermolecular Michael-addition and O–H insertion tandem process (path B, black pathway). Path A involves an intermolecular O–H insertion reaction of 1a with 2a via proton transfer and an intermolecular SN2 proceed, which through a transition state (TS-2A) with an energy barrier of 29.6 kcal/mol, resulting in the formation of a 2-(diphenylamino)-2-oxoethyl acrylate intermediate (INT-2A). Then, intermediate INT-2A undergoes keto-enol tautomerization and intramolecular Michael addition to produce the target product (3) via a transition state TS-3A with an energy barrier of 34.4 kcal/mol. However, the computed energy barriers for the two steps are both too high, making it improbable to occur at the given reaction temperature and thus ruling out the path A process. In contrast, the energy barrier for the intermolecular Michael-addition and O–H insertion tandem process is 26.6 kcal/mol (TS-1B) and 17.7 kcal/mol (TS-2B), respectively. Notably, they are 3.0 and 16.7 kcal/mol lower than that of the O–H insertion and intramolecular Michael-addition pathway, respectively.

Free energy changes of the possible reaction pathway for the synthesis of γ-lactones.

Calculations were performed at the PWPB95-D3(BJ)/def2-QZVPP:SMD(MeCN)//M06-2X(D3)/def2-SVP:IEFPCM(MeCN) level of theory.

Based on our experimental observations and literature reports32,75, a possible mechanism for the synthesis of γ-lactone is depicted in Fig. 7a. First, the amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a reacts with acrylic acid 2a, resulting in a zwitterionic intermediate I through a Michael-addition reaction. Then, the intermediate I undergoes tautomerization to form intermediate I’. Subsequently, a proton transfer step, intermediate I’ is converted into intermediate II. Finally, intermediate II undergoes cyclization via intramolecular SN2 reaction, resulting in the formation of the desired γ-lactone product (3), with concomitant generation a molecule of DMSO. Additionally, on the basis of the above observations, a plausible reaction pathway is also proposed for this metal-free [1 + 2] annulation of amide-sulfoxonium ylides (Fig. 7b). Initially, the nucleophilic α-carbon of amide-sulfoxonium ylide 1a reacts with the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound 2b to generate a zwitterionic intermediate III, followed by tautomerization to form intermediate III’. Finally, intermediate III’ undergoes cyclization through an intramolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction, which results in the release of one molecule of DMSO and yields the cyclopropane product (63).

Conclusion

In summary, we present HTE-facilitated [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulations of amide-sulfoxonium ylides with acrylic acids and terminal alkenes, enabling divergent and highly selective access to γ-lactones and cyclopropanes. This reaction offers a flexible, streamlined, and highly efficient access to a broad range of (hetero)amide-substituted γ-lactone and cyclopropane scaffolds (97 examples), many of which are otherwise challenging to make. The strategy features mild conditions, broad substrate scope, high functional group compatibility, excellent chemo- and diastereoselectivity, and is amenable to both large-scale synthesis and product derivatization. Moreover, we have built a ML-based model, integrating XGB-MAF & RDKit, that can accurately predict reaction yields (MAE = 7.3%) across a diverse range of unseen substrates, and also successfully explored this cyclopropanation reaction space. Mechanistic studies suggest that [1 + 4] and [1 + 2] annulations initiate via intermolecular Michael addition, followed by selective 5-exo-trig and 3-exo-trig cyclization, respectively. We anticipate that this work will be widely embraced to accelerate the discovery and development of (hetero)amide-substituted γ-lactones and cyclopropanes across synthetic and pharmaceutical chemistry.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of γ-lactones

An oven-dried glass tube of 4 mL equipped with a magnetic stir bar, and was charged with amide-sulfoxonium ylide (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in CH3CN (1.0 mL); then acrylic acid (0.30 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) was added. The reaction mixture was sealed and stirred (820 r/min in IKA RCT basic) at 60 °C for 24 h under an air atmosphere. After completion, the reaction mixture was recovered to room temperature and washed with saturated solution of NaHCO3. The crude product was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 3.0 mL), and the solution was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and then concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: petroleum ether/ethyl acetate) to give the desired products.

General procedure for the synthesis of cyclopropanes

An oven-dried glass tube of 4 mL equipped with a magnetic stir bar and was charged with amide-sulfoxonium ylide (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in DMF (1.0 mL); then alkene (0.15 mmol, 1.5 equiv.) was added. The reaction mixture was sealed and stirred (820 r/min in IKA RCT basic) at 90 °C for 24 h under an air atmosphere. After completion, the reaction mixture was recovered to room temperature. The crude product was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 3.0 mL), and the organic layer was washed with water for 3 times. Finally, the organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: petroleum ether/ethyl acetate) to give the desired products.
