Cross-Reactivity of GeneXpert MTB/RIF Probe C with Nocardia Species as a Suggestive Ancillary Indicator
Authors: Yanyan Fan, Rongrong Song, Jingyi Feng, Jiankang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe slow-growing, fastidious nature of Nocardia species makes rapid molecular tools essential for diagnosis. Although the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay targets Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), the cross-reactivity of its Probe C with Nocardia has not previously been characterized.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 14,141 non-duplicate clinical samples from February 2020 to June 2023. Probe C reactivity was first assessed against Nocardia culture results. To investigate the molecular basis, we subsequently compared the rpoB target region of Probe C with the genomes of 1082 Nocardia strains from the NCBI database.ResultsProbe C showed species-specific reactivity, failing to detect many clinically prevalent species. Among culture-positive Nocardia isolates, Probe C detected 10 of 17 N. cyriacigeorgica (Ct range: 32.8–39.2), 1 of 2 N. asiatica (Ct: 39), and 2 of 8 N. abscessus (Ct range: 30.5–38.7). Notably, the most frequently isolated species, N. farcinica (59 isolates), showed no Probe C reactivity. The overall sensitivity of Probe C among all culture-confirmed nocardiosis cases was only 13.4% (13/97); even among the subset of cross-reacting species, it reached only 48.1% (13/27), highlighting its inadequacy as a standalone diagnostic test. In the full MTB-negative cohort, the Probe C-positive rate was just 0.27% (38/14,141), with 25 of those 38 signals lacking culture confirmation. Genome alignment confirmed 100% identity between the rpoB sequences of reactive Nocardia strains and the Probe C target region.ConclusionProbe C is the first MTB-targeted tool demonstrated to cross-react with Nocardia via rpoB homology, with genomic evidence ruling out other genetic sources. However, given the low overall sensitivity of Probe C among all culture-confirmed nocardiosis cases, the restricted species spectrum, and the lack of molecular confirmation for most positive signals at high Ct values, an isolated Probe C-positive result in an MTB-negative sample should be regarded as a suggestive, non-diagnostic ancillary hint that warrants Nocardia-specific confirmatory testing rather than as a diagnostic finding.

Introduction

Nocardia is an aerobic actinomycete genus distinguished by its slow growth and weak acid-fast staining, and is widely distributed in soil and aquatic environments.1 Although nocardiosis is generally considered a rare opportunistic infection, with an estimated annual incidence of up to 0.87 cases per 100,000 population, it most frequently presents as pulmonary disease—accounting for approximately 73–82% of cases—due to inhalation of the pathogen.2 While Nocardia primarily causes pulmonary and cutaneous infections among immunocompromised individuals, an increasing number of cases have also been reported in immunocompetent hosts.3 Notably, epidemiological data suggest that the incidence of nocardiosis is on the rise globally, a trend that may be attributed both to advances in diagnostic methods and to the growing population of at-risk individuals as a result of prolonged survival among immunosuppressed patients.4

The overlapping clinical presentations of nocardiosis and tuberculosis highlight the critical need for precise pathogenic differentiation. Nevertheless, current diagnostic challenges persist in nocardiosis due to its prolonged cultivation requirements and the absence of reliable diagnostic methodologies.2 Conventional diagnostic approaches for Nocardia species identification rely primarily on direct microscopic examination using Gram stain and/or modified acid-fast staining techniques, followed by culture on blood agar plates (BAP). However, these methods demonstrate limited sensitivity for Nocardia detection5 and are associated with prolonged turnaround times.6,7 These diagnostic limitations highlight the critical necessity for developing rapid, clinically accessible diagnostic modalities capable of reliably distinguishing Nocardia infections from Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM).

The GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay, a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) for tuberculosis diagnostics, employs five complementary molecular probes (designated A through E) that collectively target conserved regions of the rpoB gene to detect MTB and identify rifampicin resistance-associated mutations.8,9 This study aimed to (1) characterize the species-specific cross-reactivity profile of GeneXpert MTB/RIF Probe C with Nocardia species at both clinical and genomic levels; (2) evaluate the clinical performance metrics of Probe C reactivity, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), using Nocardia culture as the reference standard; and (3) critically assess the limitations of this cross-reactivity to provide balanced clinical guidance.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Data

Laboratory data were analyzed retrospectively to characterize the cross-reactivity between the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay and certain Nocardia species infections. In this study, 14,141 non-duplicate patient samples, including 6010 sputum, 5917 bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, 958 pleural effusion, 901 biopsy tissue, 36 cerebrospinal fluid, and 319 other samples, were sent for three detection methods: GeneXpert MTB/RIF, BAP culture, and MGIT 960 culture, between February 10, 2020, and June 7, 2023. All these samples were GeneXpert negative for MTB according to the instruction. The data were retrospectively collected from the laboratory information system and the GeneXpert MTB/RIF instrument.

Nocardia Identification

Clinical isolates suggestive of Nocardia based on colony morphology, Gram staining (Gram-positive branching bacilli), and modified acid-fast staining were definitively identified to the species level. Species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).

The GeneXpert MTB/RIF Assay

18 Nocardia species were tested with the Xpert MTB/RIF assay. Nocardia strains were inoculated onto Columbia blood agar (Thermo Scientific, UK) and incubated for 18–24 h at 35°C in a 5% CO2-enriched atmosphere. A single clone was inoculated into a sterile 0.9% NaCl solution, and adjusted to match a 0.5 McFarland standard. This was followed by a 100-fold dilution to achieve a final concentration of 106 CFU/mL, which was then tested with the Xpert MTB/RIF assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each strain was tested in a single run without technical replicates, consistent with the manufacturer’s protocol for this qualitative assay. Nocardia species included N. abscessus (CJFH-NOC155), N. anaemiae (CJFH-NOC209), N. asiatica (CJFH-NOC94), N. asteroides (CJFH-NOC521), N. beijingensis (CJFH-NOC255), N. brasiliensis (CJFH-NOC616), N. carnea (CJFH-NOC65), N. cyriacigeorgica (CJFH-NOC228), N. farcinica (CJFH-NOC618), N. otitidiscaviarum (CJFH-NOC253), N. pneumoniae (CJFH-NOC22134), N. puris (CJFH-NOC7318), N. terpenica (CJFH-NOC244), N. africana (DSM 44491), N. amamiensis (DSM 45066), N. amikacinitolerans (DSM 45539), N. brevicatena (DSM 43024), N. exalbida (DSM 44883).

Sequence Alignment

A total of 1082 genomes of Nocardia species were downloaded from the NCBI database (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/) and sequence alignment was performed against the 81‑bp rpoB gene unique to MTB. Sequence homology searches were conducted using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), and multiple sequence alignments were generated using ClustalW within the MEGA software. The deadline for sequence download was June 11, 2026. The sequence alignment results were shown in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of Probe C reactivity relative to Nocardia culture were calculated using 2×2 contingency tables, with Nocardia culture as the reference standard. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) for these proportions were computed using the Wilson method. Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Nocardia Isolation and Probe C Specificity

A total of 97 Nocardia strains were isolated from clinical samples. Probe C positivity was predominantly observed in N. cyriacigeorgica (10/17, 58.8%), N. abscessus (2/8, 25%), and N. asiatica (1/2, 50%), with Ct values inversely correlated with bacterial load (Table 2). In contrast, common species such as N. farcinica (59 isolates) and N. wallacei (3 isolates) showed no Probe C reactivity. This pronounced species specificity—whereby the most frequently encountered clinical species remain undetected—constitutes a fundamental limitation that precludes the use of Probe C as a diagnostic tool for nocardiosis.

Nocardia spp. Strains Analysis by GeneXpert MTB/RIF

We tested the cross-reactivity of Xpert MTB/RIF against a total of 18 different Nocardia species. The results showed that only 8 of them were positive for Probe C with different Ct values (Figure 2), while the remaining 10 species—comprising the majority of tested strains—showed no reactivity. Genomic Alignment of Nocardia rpoB with MTB Probe C

Among 1082 Nocardia genomes analyzed, 564 (52.1%) exhibited sequence homology to MTB Probe C. However, only 8 species (N. abscessus, N. amamiensis, N. amikacinitolerans, N. asiatica, N. brasiliensis, N. cyriacigeorgica, N. exalbida, and N. terpenica) triggered Probe C signals phenotypically (Table S1). Perfect sequence matches (100% identity) were observed in 168/168 N. cyriacigeorgica and 18/18 N. beijingensis genomes, while partial mismatches (eg, 15/18 N. abscessus, 26/27 N. brasiliensis) suggested secondary structure interference. Among the N. farcinica genomes in this dataset (n = 67), only 2 (3.0%) contained a sequence matching the Probe C target region.

Cross-Reactivity Profile of Probe C with Nocardia Species

Using culture as the reference standard, the diagnostic performance of Probe C reactivity among the 14,141 MTB-negative samples was as follows: the overall sensitivity was 13.4% (13/97; 95% CI 8.0–21.6%) among all culture-confirmed Nocardia cases; among the species capable of cross-reacting with Probe C (N. cyriacigeorgica, N. abscessus, and N. asiatica), the sensitivity was 48.1% (13/27; 95% CI 30.7–66.0%); the specificity was 99.8% (14,019/14,044; 95% CI 99.7–99.9%); the PPV was 34.2% (13/38; 95% CI 21.2–50.1%); and the NPV was 99.4% (14,019/14,103; 95% CI 99.3–99.5%). These metrics underscore that Probe C reactivity alone is neither sensitive enough to rule out nocardiosis nor specific enough to confirm it, and should not be interpreted as having diagnostic accuracy for Nocardia infection.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive systematic evaluation of the unexpected cross-reactivity between the GeneXpert MTB/RIF Probe C and certain Nocardia species. The finding that 0.27% of MTB-negative samples yielded Probe C positivity—primarily driven by N. cyriacigeorgica, N. abscessus, and N. asiatica—reveals a novel yet highly restricted molecular signature that may, in appropriate clinical contexts, serve as a suggestive ancillary hint for potential Nocardia infection.

The species-specific reactivity observed is firmly rooted in genomic homology within the rpoB gene. Our analysis confirms that 100% of N. cyriacigeorgica and 96.3% of N. brasiliensis strains harbor a sequence identical to the MTB Probe C target region. This structural mimicry explains why Probe C, which targets the rifampin resistance-determining region (RRDR), can occasionally signal in the absence of MTB. However, the converse is equally important: many Nocardia species with identical rpoB sequences (eg, N. beijingensis) did not trigger Probe C, and clinically predominant species such as N. farcinica lack the requisite homology in the vast majority of strains. Indeed, among the 67 N. farcinica genomes in our NCBI dataset, only 2 (3.0%) contained a sequence matching the Probe C target region, whereas the remaining 65 (97.0%) lacked it. The rpoB sequence of N. farcinica within the Probe C target region is therefore sufficiently divergent from that of MTB to prevent probe annealing in the vast majority of strains, meaning that its isolates do not generate a signal despite their clinical predominance, and the vast majority of nocardiosis cases will not be flagged by this incidental cross-reactivity.

The clinical presentations of pulmonary nocardiosis and tuberculosis—often featuring chronic cough, weight loss, and cavitary lesions—significantly overlap. In this context, an isolated Probe C-positive result in an MTB-negative sample is frequently dismissed as a technical artifact or background noise. Our data suggest that while this signal should not be ignored, it also must not be overinterpreted: at best, it provides a preliminary hint that may justify additional Nocardia-directed testing, but it does not constitute a screening tool given its poor sensitivity and species restriction. The strong inverse correlation between Ct values and bacterial load offers a semi-quantitative assessment in the subset of cases where cross-reactivity occurs; however, traditional culture, which requires 2–7 days for conclusive results, remains essential for definitive identification and susceptibility testing.1,10,11

A notable finding was the identification of 25 samples that were Probe C-positive but culture-negative. Without an independent molecular confirmatory assay (such as targeted Nocardia PCR or metagenomic next-generation sequencing), we cannot definitively link these 25 positive signals directly to Nocardia. We fully acknowledge this as a major limitation of our study. These unconfirmed signals represent a substantial proportion (65.8%) of all Probe C-positive results and may reflect non-specific background fluorescence, cross-reactivity with other bacterial species, or true Nocardia infection missed by culture—this ambiguity further reinforces why Probe C cannot function as a diagnostic tool and highlights the need for future prospective studies with molecular confirmation.

Some of the late Ct values (>35) observed in our study could potentially be attributed to background fluorescence rather than true biological cross-reactivity. Although our in vitro experiments demonstrated that pure Nocardia isolates at low bacterial burdens can produce delayed Probe C signals exceeding 37 cycles (Table 2), this does not establish that all late signals in clinical samples represent genuine Nocardia cross-reactivity. Alternatively, this discrepancy could reflect the superior sensitivity of nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for detecting fastidious organisms like Nocardia relative to culture, rather than indicating poor probe specificity: the fastidious growth requirements and slow replication of Nocardia often lead to false-negative cultures,12,13 and previous studies have reported higher NAAT sensitivity compared with culture for MTB.14 Nevertheless, the observed cross-reactivity rate of 48.1% (13/27) among the cross-reacting species—and only 13.4% (13/97) among all culture-confirmed cases—coupled with the complete absence of signal in the most common clinical species, N. farcinica, definitively precludes use of Probe C as a standalone diagnostic test. In the absence of independent molecular confirmation, attribution of high-Ct signals to Nocardia remains speculative, and technical replicates were not performed in our in vitro testing, so the reproducibility of high-Ct Probe C signals was not formally assessed.

The biological basis is nonetheless supported by two lines of evidence already in the manuscript: (i) pure, single-species isolates of N. cyriacigeorgica, N. abscessus, and N. asiatica reproducibly generated Probe C signals in vitro (Table 2), and (ii) genome alignment confirmed 100% identity between the rpoB sequences of the reactive species and the Probe C target region — a finding that cannot be explained by background fluorescence. We have kept both lines of evidence but now clearly state that molecular confirmation of the clinical (culture-negative) signals is required before they can be attributed to Nocardia.

Improving the recovery of Nocardia from clinical specimens remains a priority. Research on optimized culture techniques, such as the use of selective media like BCYEα+AB agar15 or innovative co-culture methods like the amoeba plate test,16 highlights principles that could be adapted to enhance Nocardia isolation.17 Applying such strategies is crucial for improving microbiological confirmation of suspected nocardiosis.

Based on these findings, we present a conceptual illustration (Figure 3) of how an isolated Probe C-positive result in an MTB-negative sample might prompt heightened clinical awareness and further Nocardia-specific testing. Importantly, however, overinterpretation of such an unvalidated signal carries clinical risks: it could cause diagnostic confusion between nocardiosis and other infections, lead to unnecessary or inappropriate antimicrobial therapy (eg, empirical sulfonamides without culture or molecular confirmation), or delay the correct diagnosis by directing attention toward a nonspecific signal. This scheme is therefore intended solely as a research-oriented illustration and is not a validated diagnostic algorithm; Probe C reactivity should never be used in isolation to guide clinical management. However, this retrospective study has several notable limitations. First, the lack of independent molecular verification for the culture-negative, Probe C-positive samples prevents definitive confirmation of Nocardia in those specific cases. Second, its single-center design and focus on pulmonary samples may limit generalizability to extrapulmonary cases. We also did not evaluate host immune status or antibiotic exposure, which may influence bacterial load and probe reactivity. Third, the small number of culture-confirmed nocardiosis cases, particularly of the cross-reacting species, limits the precision of species-specific performance estimates. Fourth, and most critically, the majority of clinically prevalent Nocardia species do not cross-react with Probe C; a negative Probe C result therefore provides no reassurance against nocardiosis and must not be used to guide clinical decision-making. In conclusion, an isolated Probe C-positive signal in an MTB-negative sample should not be routinely dismissed as an error, but neither should it be overinterpreted as evidence of Nocardia infection. Given the low overall sensitivity, the marked species restriction of the cross-reactivity, and the absence of molecular confirmation for most positive signals, this signal can at most serve as a suggestive, non-diagnostic hint that prompts Nocardia-directed confirmatory testing, and must not be used alone to guide antimicrobial therapy. Prospective multi-center studies incorporating independent molecular confirmation are needed to validate the biological basis and clinical utility of this cross-reactivity before any diagnostic application can be recommended.
