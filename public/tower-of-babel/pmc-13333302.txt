Anti-CD20 Therapies and Antinephrin Autoantibodies in IgA Nephropathy With Minimal Change Disease
Authors: Yu Liang, Zerui Yang, Xiaohan Yuan, Qian Jin, Huixian Li, Yihui Zhang, Li Jin, Songxia Quan, Linpei Zhang, Li Liu, Jiping Sun, Wanhong Lu, Xinfang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn IgA nephropathy (IgAN) with minimal change disease (MCD) (MCD-IgAN), the efficacy of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and the profile of antinephrin antibodies remain poorly understood.MethodsWe evaluated the efficacy of anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids (anti-CD20 mAbs group) compared with full-dose prolonged steroids (steroids group) and assessed the prevalence of antinephrin antibodies in a retrospective cohort of patients with MCD-IgAN.ResultsTotal remission rates of nephrotic syndrome were comparable between the anti-CD20 mAbs group and the steroids group in 45 adult patients with MCD-IgAN. Notably, the anti-CD20 mAbs group achieved a longer recurrence-free period after complete remission than the steroids group (median 30.4 vs. 17.1 months, P = 0.01). In addition, patients in the anti-CD20 mAbs group experienced significantly fewer total adverse events and rehospitalization rates. Circulating antinephrin antibodies was detected in 31.1% (19/61) of patients with MCD-IgAN, comparable to patients with MCD. Furthermore, patients with antinephrin antibodies > 150 ng/ml exhibited significantly increased hazard risk of recurrence (hazard ratio = 3.93, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.18–13.13), higher recurrence rate per person-year (incidence rate ratio: 3.16, 95% CI: 1.42–7.03) and shorter relapse-free interval than those with lower antinephrin antibodies (median 14.0 vs. 48.3 months, P = 0.02). Importantly, antinephrin antibody levels in sequential plasma samples closely tracked clinical course of disease remission and relapses during follow-up.ConclusionOur findings indicate that anti-CD20 mAbs represent an effective and safe therapeutic option for adult patients with MCD-IgAN. Circulating antinephrin antibodies were detectable and associated with a higher relapse rate, suggesting its potential utility as a biomarker of both disease activity and therapeutic target in this disease.

IgAN is the most common glomerulonephritis worldwide, exhibiting a wide spectrum of pathological and clinical patterns. A special phenotype known as MCD-IgAN, characterized by diffuse podocytopathy, nephrotic syndrome, and mild mesangial proliferation resembling MCD accounts for approximately 5% to 10% of all IgAN cases.1 The Kidney Disaese: Improving Global Outcomes 2025 Clinical Practice Guidelines for the management of IgAN recommend treating patients with MCD-IgAN with glucocorticoids, consistent with the approach for primary MCD.2 Recently, emerging clinical evidence support the efficacy of anti-CD20 mAb therapy in adult MCD,3,4 whereas the effect of anti-CD20 mAbs in patients with MCD-IgAN is rarely described in the literature.5

So far, whether MCD-IgAN is a specific diffuse podocytopathy in IgAN, or MCD with pathogenic or nonpathogenic IgA deposition remains debated.2 Our earlier study revealed distinct immunological features in patients with MCD-IgAN, including lower levels of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), reduced circulating poly-IgA complexes, less glomerular Gd-IgA1 deposition than in typical IgAN; and attenuated inflammatory responses in mesangial cells stimulated by poly-IgA complexes from patients with MCD-IgAN in vitro.6 A recent landmark study indicated autoantibodies against nephrin were detected in approximately 44% adults with MCD and 52% of children with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome,7 supporting an autoimmune etiology in these conditions. Moreover, titers of antinephrin antibodies were associated with the disease activities and relapses in certain patients, suggesting their potential utility as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.7 Another large cohort study discovered 30.3% of Chinese adult patients with MCD had circulating antinephrin IgG autoantibodies.8 The positive rates of antinephrin autoantibodies in patients with MCD varied from 19.44% to 45% in published papers.8, 9, 10 Accordingly, it was found that the relapse-free period after remission was shorter in patients with nephrotic syndrome with positive antinephrin antibodies than those with negative antibodies.8,11 Until now, 1 study with limited sample size determined that 7 out of 10 children with MCD-IgAN had antinephrin autoantibody12; another study reported renal antinephrin IgG colocalization and podocyte ultrastructure.13 Circulating antinephrin autoantibodies were not reported in larger sample size of adult patients with MCD-IgAN.

In the current study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of anti-CD20 mAbs in a large retrospective cohort of adult patients with MCD-IgAN, characterize the profiles of circulating antinephrin antibodies, and assess their clinical utility as biomarkers in the management of this disease.

Methods

Patients and Follow-Up

This was a single-center retrospective observational cohort study of patients with MCD-IgAN. A total of 83 patients diagnosed with MCD-IgAN through renal biopsy with direct light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy in the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University from May 2018 to June 2025 were enrolled. Patients with MCD-IgAN with other kidney diseases or autoimmune diseases were excluded. Among them, 45 patients had >6-month follow-up and 61 patients with baseline plasma samples at the time of renal biopsy available were included (a flow diagram for study participants in presented in Supplementary Figure S1). Clinical baseline and follow-up data including age, gender, blood pressure, serum albumin, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hematuria and 24-hour urine total protein (UTP) were collected. Pathological data of Oxford Classification scores and treatment strategies, including full-dose prolonged corticosteroids and anti-CD20 mAbs alone or combined short-course low-dose steroids were collected. The main outcomes of complete remission of clinical nephrotic syndrome were defined as UTP < 0.3 g/d. Partial remission was defined as 0.3 g/d < UTP < 3.5 g/d. Complete recurrence of nephrotic syndrome was defined as UTP > 3.5 g or urine protein-to-creatinine ratio > 3500 mg/g in patients after complete remission. Partial recurrence was defined as 0.3 g/d < UTP < 3.5 g/d after complete remission. Side effects such as infections, new onset diabetes, gastrointestinal bleeding, osteoporosis or rehospitalization after therapy were recorded. This study was conducted in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the medical ethics committee at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. XJTU1AF2025LSYY-925). Plasma samples collected at the time of renal biopsy and during follow-up were stored at −80°C before use.

Antinephrin Autoantibodies Detection

Baseline plasma antinephrin autoantibodies were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in 61 patients with MCD-IgAN. Plasma from age- and sex-matched 40 patients with IgAN with nephrotic proteinuria with segmental glomerulosclerosis lesions, 57 patients with MCD, 59 other kidney patients including patients with IgAN without nephrotic proteinuria, membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, diabetic nephropathy, antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody–associated vasculitis and 19 healthy controls were enrolled as controls. Circulating antinephrin antibodies were detected in 9 patients with specimens during the follow-up available. For ELISA method, 100 ng 50 μl/well of recombinant human nephrin protein (His-tagged NPHS1-ECD, SinoBiological 17757-H08H) was used as antigen, nonantigen wells were coated with equal bovine serum albumin at 4 °C overnight. The plate was washed 4 times and blocked with 5% milk/phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) 300μl/well for 1h at 37°C. Then serial 2-fold dilutions of sheep anti-hNPHS1 polyclonal antibody (R&D, AF4269) were used to generate standard curves and diluted plasma (1:100) with 5% milk/phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 were added to both recombinant nephrin coated and antigen-free wells; horse radish peroxidase–conjugated mouse antihuman IgG Fc mAb (abcam, ab99759) and horse radish peroxidase–conjugated donkey antisheep IgG (abcam, ab6900) were diluted 1:2000 with 5% milk/phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for the detection of samples and standards, respectively. Finally, 50 μl/well TMB substrate (BioLegend, 421101) was added with incubation for 20 minutes and stopped with 50 μl/well 0.2M H2SO4.

Plasma samples with positive and negative antinephrin antibodies measured using ELISA were validated by immunoprecipitation–Western blotting (IP-WB). For IP-WB, 3 μl human plasma sample was incubated with 100 ng recombinant human nephrin in 100 μl TBST overnight at 4 °C, 20 ng of commercial sheep anti-hNPHS1 polyclonal antibody in TBST was set as positive control. Then 10 μl of prewashed protein G magnetic beads (Cytiva, 28951379) were added and mixed at room temperature for 3 hours on rotator. After washing 5 times with TBST using rotator. The immune complexes containing recombinant human nephrin and human IgG or sheep anti-hNPHS1 polyclonal antibody were eluted with 20 μl 2×reducing loading buffer (Biotech, WB2001) by heating at 100 °C for 5 minutes. Complexes eluted from magnetic beads were used for B by using sheep anti-NPHS1 polyclonal antibody (1: 800 diluted in 5% milk/TBST) and horse radish peroxidase–conjugated donkey antisheep secondary IgG (1:2000 in 5% milk/TBST). Eluted recombinant human nephrin and sheep anti-hNPHS1 polyclonal antibody complexes was set as positive control.

Statistical Analysis

Nonnormally distributed and normally distributed quantitative parameters were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges) and mean (SDs), respectively. Categorical data were represented by frequencies or ratios. Differences in continuous variables were assessed using the nonparametric test (Mann–Whitney U test) and t test when the data were not normally distributed or normally distributed, respectively. Kaplan–Meier analysis and cox regression was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of different treatment strategies. Poisson regression was conducted to analyze annual recurrent rate per person-year. The Kappa coefficient was used to assess the agreement between antinephrin antibody detection by ELISA and by IP-WB. A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses and graphs were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 and 10.4 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and IBM SPSS 26.0 (IBM-SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY).

Therapeutic Effect Analysis During Follow-Up

Forty-five patients with MCD-IgAN given full-dose prolonged steroids or anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids therapies were followed up for a median of 29 (interquartile range:17.0–42.0) months. Patients in the anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids group or full-dose prolonged steroids group achieved comparable proteinuria in the first 3 months (0.14 [0.08–0.53] vs. 0.21 [0.06–2.91] g/24 h in the first month; 0.10 [0.08–0.20] vs. 0.06 [0.04–0.13] g/24 h in the second month; Figure 1a and Supplementary Table S1). Meanwhile, elevation of serum albumin with a trend of elevated eGFR were achieved after 2 and 3 months of all treatments (Figure 1b and c, Supplementary Table S1). The same results were shown in the percentage of proteinuria change from baseline, serum albumin and eGFR changes from baseline (Figure 1d–f). During 12 months of follow-up, all patients in the full-dose prolonged steroids group or anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids achieved complement remission. We further analyzed the remission rate at different time points. Importantly, we found that early complete remission rates of nephrotic syndrome were comparable between the 2 treatment groups (85.2% and 83.3% in the second month, 96.3% and 100% in the third month, Figure 2a). No significant differences in accumulated remission rate were shown between the 2 treatments by Kaplan-Meier curve analysis (Figure 2b). However, we found that patients with anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids treatment had longer recurrence-free time after complete remission than full-dose prolonged steroid therapy (median: 30.4 [27.4–33.3] vs. 17.1 [14.0–20.3] months, P = 0.01 of log-rank test, Figure 2c).Figure 1Patients with IgA nephropathy with minimal change disease with initial anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids or full-dose prolonged steroids treatment achieved comparable changes of proteinuria, serum albumin, and eGFR during follow-up. Side Effects of Different Treatment Strategies in the MCD-IgAN Cohort

Adverse events after initial treatment, including infections, new onset diabetes, gastrointestinal bleeding, osteoporosis, or recurrence readmission during follow-up were documented. The total adverse events rate in the anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids group was lower than that in the full-dose prolonged steroids group (11.1% vs. 40.7%, P = 0.046, Figure 3a). Infection rates and new-onset diabetes between groups were comparable (Figure 3b and c). Baseline Circulating Antinephrin Antibodies in Patients With MCD-IgAN

Circulating antinephrin IgG antibodies were detected in 61 patients with MCD-IgAN; 57 patients with MCD; 40 patients with IgAN with nephrotic-proteinuria and segmental sclerosis lesions; 59 with other kidney diseases, including membranous nephropathy, IgAN without nephrotic proteinuria, lupus nephritis, diabetic nephropathy, antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody–associated vasculitis; and 19 healthy controls. Patients with positive antinephrin antibodies were diagnosed with >100 ng/ml of titers according to the upper limit value of controls. Positive incidence of antinephrin antibodies was comparable between patients with MCD and patients with MCD-IgAN (29.8% [17/57] vs. 31.1% [19/61], P = 0.88). None of patients with IgAN with nephrotic proteinuria, other kidney disease patients, or healthy volunteers had positive antinephrin antibodies (Figure 4a and b). Antinephrin antibodies were validated by IP-WB as shown in Figure 4c, commercial antihuman nephrin antibodies and plasma from 1 patient with MCD with high antinephrin antibody were set as positive controls. The reliability of our ELISA-based method for antinephrin antibody detection was confirmed using IP-WB method in plasma from an additional 31 patients with MCD-IgAN (Supplementary Figure S2), with the Kappa coefficient demonstrating almost perfect agreement between ELISA and IP-WB (κ = 0.89, P < 0.001). Because of limited patients with positive antinephrin antibody, no significant differences in baseline serum albumin, proteinuria, serum creatinine, and eGFR levels were found between patients with MCD-IgAN with positive and negative antinephrin antibodies. Association of Antinephrin Antibodies With Clinical Outcomes During Follow-Up

Thirty-six of 61 patients with MCD-IgAN with baseline antinephrin antibodies and follow-up data available were enrolled to evaluate the association of antinephrin antibodies with clinical outcome. Baseline characteristics and treatment strategies of these patients are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Among them are 14 patients with positive antinephrin antibody at baseline. The survival analysis of remission rate was comparable between groups with positive and negative antinephrin antibodies (Figure 5a). Patients with positive antinephrin antibodies exhibited a trend toward a higher risk of disease relapse than those with negative antibodies (Supplementary Figure S4A). Furthermore, Cox regression survival analysis revealed that the highest antibody titer category (> 150 ng/ml) was associated with an increased risk of disease relapse (Supplementary Figure S4B). Patients with antinephrin antibodies > 150 ng/ml exhibited a shorter relapse-free interval than those with lower antinephrin antibodies (median: 14.0 [95% CI: 8.3–19.7] vs. 48.3 [95% CI: 40.1–56.5] months, P = 0.02, Figure 5b). Patients with antinephrin antibody > 150 ng/ml had an increased hazard risk of recurrence by 3.93 times (hazard ratio = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.18–13.13) compared with those with antinephrin antibody < 150 ng/ml by Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (Figure 5c). The recurrence rate per person-year was higher in antinephrin antibody > 150 ng/ml group than in the < 150 ng/ml group (46.8% vs. 14.8%, P = 0.01, Figure 5d). Furthermore, the annual overall incidence rate ratio of recurrence risk in the group of patients with antinephrin antibody > 150 ng/ml was 3.16 times higher than those in antibody negative group (incidence rate ratio = 3.16, 95% CI:1.42–7.03, P = 0.01; Figure 5e). Serial plasma antinephrin antibodies in 9 patients with MCD-IgAN were examined. The longitudinal disease course, treatment strategies, and levels of antinephrin antibody are shown in Table 2. Consistent with previous findings in patients with MCD, our study identified significant association between antinephrin antibodies and a longitudinally fluctuating disease course (Figure 5f). Discussion

MCD-IgAN is a special subset of IgAN with the features of clinical nephrotic syndrome and mesangial IgA deposition.2 Although corticosteroids are recommended as an effective first-line therapy for MCD-IgAN by the latest Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines,2 nephrotic syndrome relapse, steroid dependence, and intolerance are common in clinical practice. In recent years, anti-CD20 mAbs have been widely applied in the treatment of MCD, especially in cases with steroid-dependent or frequent relapses.3 Previously, we reported rapid clinical remission of 40 patients with MCD given obinutuzumab after steroid resistant or refractory disease.4 Similar results were validated by multiple other centers.14, 15, 16 However, rituximab therapy neither significantly reduced Gd-IgA1 nor improved proteinuria and kidney function in patients with IgAN after 1-year treatment in a randomized controlled trial.17 Notably, case of MCD-IgAN were underrepresented in the study. In view of the distinctive podocytopathy features in MCD-IgAN and based on our previous mechanistic findings indicating this condition as mild IgAN with superimposed MCD, we have employed anti-CD20 antibody therapy in such cases since 2018.

This study presents the first analysis of the clinical efficacy and safety of anti-CD20 mAbs in a retrospective cohort of 45 patients with MCD-IgAN, with a follow-up period > 6 months. Key findings supported that patients receiving initial anti-CD20 or combined short-course low-dose steroids achieved significantly longer recurrence-free time following complete remission than those undergoing full-dose prolonged steroids (median: 30.4 [27.4–33.3] vs. 17.1 [14.0–20.3] months, P = 0.01). Moreover, the anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroids group exhibited fewer total adverse events and lower rehospitalization rates than the full-dose prolonged steroids group. To our knowledge, this is the first cohort study to assess the efficacy and safety of initial anti-CD20 mAbs therapy in a relatively large sample of 45 patients with MCD-IgAN. Previous case serials have reported rituximab as an effective and safe alternative for patients with IgAN with superimposed podocytopathy following frequent relapse or steroid dependence.5 Our findings provide further evidence for B-cell–targeted therapies in patients with MCD-IgAN.

The pathogenesis of MCD-IgAN remained unclear. Our previous work showed significantly lower serum levels of Gd-IgA1 and antiglycan IgG autoantibodies in patients with MCD-IgAN than in those with classic IgAN. In parallel, immunofluorescence revealed markedly weaker glomerular staining for both total IgA and Gd-IgA1 in MCD-IgAN renal biopsies.6 These findings support the concept of MCD-IgAN as a dual glomerulopathy characterized by mild or latent IgAN superimposed on MCD. Recent landmark studies have identified circulating antinephrin autoantibodies in both adult and pediatric patients with active or frequently relapsing podocytopathies.7,8,10,11,18 Therefore, the efficacy of anti-CD20 mAbs in MCD could involve not only direct podocyte protection3,19 and T-cell–mediated mechanisms20,21 but also antinephrin antibody-mediated effects. Furthermore, pathogenic role of antinephrin antibodies was further confirmed by passive transfer experiments, in which patient-derived antinephrin autoantibodies induced proteinuria in wild-type rabbit.22 Collectively, these advances not only provided a potential noninvasive diagnostic alternative to renal biopsies but also pointed toward novel targeted therapeutic strategies.23

In this study, we assessed plasma antinephrin autoantibodies in adult patients with MCD-IgAN and those with MCD. The positive rate was comparable at the time of renal biopsy; 31.1% (19/61) in patients with MCD-IgAN and 29.8% (17/57) in patients with MCD, which was higher than the 19.44% reported in a previous study of MCD but lower than rates observed in certain other MCD cohorts, where positive incidence ranged from 44% in patients with general MCD to 69% in those with nephrotic syndrome not receiving immunosuppressive therapy.7,8,10,18 The comparable prevalence of antinephrin autoantibodies in patients with MCD-IgAN and those with MCD points to a stronger etiological affinity between these 2 conditions than that shared between IgAN and MCD-IgAN. Notably, antinephrin antibodies were not measurable in patients with IgAN and nephrotic-range proteinuria who had no underlying podocytopathy, suggesting its specificity in the pathogenesis of podocytopathies. Considering the present and our previous findings,6,13 we propose that the underlying pathogenic mechanism of MCD-IgAN is rooted in an autoimmune podocytopathy process with IgA deposits or mild IgAN. Recent studies reported approximately 70% (7/10) of Chinese pediatric patients with MCD-IgAN positive for antinephrin antibodies via ELISA, whereas 36% of adult patients with MCD-IgAN exhibited renal antinephrin IgG codeposition.12,13 The presence of antinephrin autoantibodies in MCD-IgAN further supports the view that the disease pathogenesis aligns more closely with MCD than with IgAN. Previous research mentioned that patients with positive antinephrin antibodies had more severe nephrotic syndrome, and a higher incidence of antinephrin antibody positivity was observed in treatment-naive patients.8 In our cohort, however, only 2 patients received initial steroid therapy, and 1 patient received anti-CD20 antibody treatment before biopsy.

Notably, consistent with the results of previous studies in the MCD population,8 our study demonstrates a strong association of antinephrin autoantibodies with the remarkably high frequency of relapses in MCD-IgAN. Furthermore, the optimal treatment for infrequent relapses remains unclear, with limited evidence supporting the efficacy of cyclophosphamide, rituximab, and calcineurin inhibitors.24 In our study, anti-CD20 mAbs or combined short-course low-dose steroid therapy acquired a longer relapse-free period in patients with MCD-IgAN. There was reasonable speculation that anti-CD20 antibody might be a suitable option for patients with MCD-IgAN with frequent relapses and positive antinephrin autoantibodies. Finally, longitudinal analysis revealed that antinephrin antibodies coincide with clinical recurrence and remission during follow-up, based on sequential detection of plasma specimens in cases. These findings indicate the potential utility of antinephrin antibodies as biomarkers of MCD-IgAN activity in the future.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the findings require further validation to confirm the superior efficacy of anti-CD20 antibody therapy in patients with MCD-IgAN. A parallel comparison cohort of adult patients with MCD is warranted to minimize the impact of confounding factors. Second, the follow-up periods were relatively short for some patients, which affected the assessment of long-term remission and could bias the relapse-free survival analysis following anti-CD20 antibody or combined short-course low-dose steroids treatment in MCD-IgAN populations. Third, the number of patients with detectable circulating antinephrin antibodies during follow-up was limited; future studies with larger sample size and longer follow-up time should incorporate serial measurements and employ multivariable Cox regression analysis to evaluate its potential as a biomarker and optimized threshold for predicting disease activity and prognosis in MCD-IgAN.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the renal efficacy and safety of anti-CD20 antibody therapy in Chinese adult patients with MCD-IgAN. Moreover, it reports the prevalence of circulating antinephrin autoantibody and revealed its potential role as a biomarker associated with both remission and relapse of nephrotic syndrome in this patient population.
