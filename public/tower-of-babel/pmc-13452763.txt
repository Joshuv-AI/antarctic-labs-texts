A cumate-inducible promoter system for tuned gene expression and magnetosome overproduction in magnetotactic bacteria
Authors: Alexandra Woller, Alexander Knöchel, Michael Rheinberger, Jürgen M. Plitzko, Dirk Schüler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetosomes are membrane-bound iron-rich organelles synthesized by magnetotactic bacteria as sensors for navigation in the Earth’s magnetic field. In the well-studied model Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense, their multistep biosynthesis is tightly controlled by > 30 specific genes clustered within a genomic magnetosome island. The lack of versatile genetic tools for strong, tunable, and orthogonal transcriptional control has impeded the analysis, engineering, and transplantation of this complex biosynthetic pathway. In addition, recent studies revealed an intricate transcriptional architecture of magnetosome gene clusters, but its significance has still remained unclear. Here, by using a bioluminescence reporter assay, we identify several novel constitutive promoters and establish the cumate-inducible repressor/promoter system CymR/Pcym as a robust and tunable regulatory module for rapid, dose-dependent transcriptional control in M. gryphiswaldense. We demonstrate its successful application for genetic tuning of magnetosome biosynthesis and inducible overproduction by controlling expression of a single essential magnetosome gene in a duplicated hybrid magnetosome gene cluster background. Additionally, we show its versatility by creating a hybrid promoter within the essential long mamAB operon, which experimentally confirms the significance of the intragenic P(mamH) promoter for the generation of long polycistronic transcripts. In summary, our data underlines the importance of balanced magnetosome gene expression. Introduction

Magnetotactic bacteria (MTB) biosynthesize membrane-bound organelles, so-called magnetosomes, which serve as compass needles for geomagnetic navigation (Murat et al. 2010; Müller et al. 2020; Wan et al. 2024; Schüler et al. 2026). Magnetosomes consist of tens of nanometer-sized crystals of a magnetic iron mineral enveloped in a proteo–lipid bilayer, i.e., magnetosome membrane (MM), which are aligned in well-ordered chains (Katzmann et al. 2011; Toro-Nahuelpan et al. 2016, 2019; Ra and Komeili 2025; Schüler et al. 2026). MTB have recently emerged as models for studying bacterial organelle formation, biomineralization, as well as magnetic nanorobots that may deliver drugs in the human body (Komeili 2012; Gwisai et al. 2022; Taoka et al. 2023; Schüler et al. 2026). As natural magnetic nanoparticles, magnetosomes exhibit extraordinary characteristics, such as strong magnetization and uniform sizes and shapes, which make them highly attractive for use in biomedical applications such as magnetic imaging, magnetic drug targeting, and hyperthermia treatment of tumors (Taukulis et al. 2015; Mannucci et al. 2018; Alphandéry 2020; Fdez-Gubieda et al. 2020; Nguyen et al. 2023; Mickoleit et al. 2024; Buchholz et al. 2026; Thieben et al. 2026).

Magnetosome formation and magnetotaxis have been mostly investigated in only a few cultured species of MTB (Byrne et al. 2010; Pfeiffer et al. 2020; McCausland et al. 2022; Herz et al. 2025; Schüler et al. 2026). Because of its genetic tractability and relatively straightforward cultivation, the well-studied alphaproteobacterium Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense (Schleifer et al. 1991; Schüler and Köhler 1992) has emerged as a powerful model as well as a promising chassis for bioproduction and synthetic biology of bacterial magnetic nanoparticles (Lohße et al. 2016; Mickoleit and Schüler 2018; Fernández-Castané et al. 2018; Rosenfeldt et al. 2021; Mickoleit et al. 2021, 2023; Zwiener et al. 2021a; Tessaro et al. 2026). In M. gryphiswaldense and related MTB, the formation of MM vesicles, the uptake of iron and biomineralization of cuboctahedral crystals of magnetite (Fe3O4), as well as the assembly and positioning of magnetosome chains along dedicated cytoskeletal filaments are controlled by a network of > 30 proteins (McCausland et al. 2022; Schüler et al. 2026). These are encoded in several compact magnetosome gene clusters (MGC), designated mam (magnetosome membrane), mms (magnetosome particle membrane-specific), and feo (magnetosome-specific Fe2+ transport system), which are part of a larger (about 137 kb) genomic region called magnetosome island (MAI) (Ullrich et al. 2005; Schübbe et al. 2006; Murat et al. 2010; Lohße et al. 2014; Silva et al. 2020; Schüler et al. 2026). Although magnetosome biomineralization only occurs under anaerobic and microaerobic conditions (Heyen and Schüler 2003; Riese et al. 2020), magnetosome genes were found to be constitutively expressed (Schübbe et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2016; Riese et al. 2022), with no unambiguous evidence for regulation by oxygen or other environmental cues (Woller et al. 2026; Schüler et al. 2026). MGC comprise several polycistronic operons, i.e., feoAB1op, mms6op, mamGFDCop, mamABop, and mamXYop, each transcribed from a primary promoter residing upstream of the first gene of each operon (Schübbe et al. 2006). In addition, multiple transcription start sites (TSS) were revealed in mms6op, mamXYop, and mamABop by transcriptomics, reporter gene fusions, and promoter knockouts (Dziuba et al. 2021; Riese et al. 2022). This transcriptional intricacy is thought to reflect the need for the numerous highly interacting magnetosome proteins to be properly expressed and targeted to the MM in balanced stoichiometries (Dziuba et al. 2021). However, the transcriptional intricacy of MGC has complicated the analysis, engineering and reconstitution of magnetosome biosynthesis. For example, M. gryphiswaldense was recently established as surrogate host for functional expression of single magnetosome genes as well as entire MGCs from foreign MTB donors (Awal et al. 2023a, 2023b), which will require the adaptation of transcriptional control compatible to the host regulatory network. Also, attempts for transplantation of magnetosome biosynthesis to other organisms so far have been limited to other alphaproteobacteria, partly owing to the poor and imbalanced transcription from native MGC promoters in foreign hosts (Kolinko et al. 2014; Dziuba et al. 2023). Therefore, the genetic “magnetization” of foreign organisms will require the understanding, engineering, simplification, and deliberate tuning of the complex transcription. In addition, attempts to control and overproduce magnetosomes so far relied on the constitutive genetic multiplication of parts or the entire MAI (Lohße et al. 2016; Dziuba et al. 2020). Thus, the ability to generate and control expression of native or engineered magnetosome pathways is also crucial for adjusting or optimizing product yields.

So far, for strong constitutive expression of own and foreign genes in M. gryphiswaldense, native magnetosome promoters were mostly used. These include PmamH, driving transcription of the mamABop (Schübbe et al. 2006; Lang et al. 2009) and PmamG, the main promoter of the mamGFDCop (Borg et al. 2014; Paulus et al. 2024), as well as PmamDC45 (Awal et al. 2023b), which is an engineered, shortened version of the PmamG promoter (Borg et al. 2014). Alternatively, the anhydrotetracycline (aTc)-inducible TetR/Ptet (Borg et al. 2014; Bertram et al. 2022; Awal et al. 2023b) and the isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranosid (IPTG)-inducible LacI/Plac system (Lang and Schüler 2008; Lang et al. 2009; Borg et al. 2014; Raschdorf et al. 2016; Marschall et al. 2017) have been used for regulated expression of native and foreign genes in M. gryphiswaldense. However, induced expression levels with these promoters were rather weak compared to native M. gryphiswaldense promoters (e.g., reached only 30% of constitutive PmamDC45-driven expression (Borg et al. 2014)).

In summary, the lack of versatile genetic tools for strong, tunable and orthogonal transcriptional control has impeded the analysis, engineering, and transplantation of this complex biosynthetic pathway. Here, we expand the regulatory toolbox for M. gryphiswaldense by characterizing a set of novel promoters using a highly sensitive bioluminescence reporter system. In addition to identifying several strong native promoters, we establish the cumate-inducible CymR/Pcym system as a robust and tunable regulatory module which enables rapid, dose-dependent transcriptional control at low inducer concentrations. In addition, we demonstrate its successful application for genetic tuning of magnetosome biosynthesis and overproduction. Together, our results provide a versatile framework for dynamic transcriptional control of magnetosome genes and may support more sophisticated genetic engineering strategies in M. gryphiswaldense and other MTB.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. If not indicated otherwise, Magnetospirillum gryphiswaldense MSR-1 (DSM No. 6361) and derivatives were routinely cultivated in flask standard medium (FSM), containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 15 mM potassium lactate, 4 mM sodium nitrate (NaNO3), 0.74 mM potassium phosphate (KH2PO4), 0.6 mM magnesium sulfate (MgSO4 · 7H2O), 50 μM iron citrate, 3 g/l soy peptone, 0.1 g/l yeast extract, in flasks containing 2% (v/v) O2 in the headspace, at 120 rpm agitation (Heyen and Schüler 2003). If applicable, FSM was supplemented with 5 µg/ml kanamycin (Km) or chloramphenicol (Cam).

Escherichia coli DH5α, WM3064, and NEB10ß strains carrying plasmids were cultivated in lysogeny broth (LB) supplemented with 25 µg/ml Km or Cam at 37 °C, with 180 rpm agitation. For cultivation of the diaminopimelic acid (DAP) auxotrophic strain WM3064, 0.1 mM dl-α,ɛ-diaminopimelic acid was added. DH5α was used for cloning, WM3064 for conjugation and NEB10ß for recombineering.

Optical densities (ODs) were determined photometrically at 565 nm for M. gryphiswaldense strains and 600 nm for E. coli. The coefficient of magnetically induced differential light scattering and quantitative monitoring of cellular magnetism (Cmag, magnetic response) was determined as reported earlier (Schüler et al. 1995).

Molecular and genetic techniques

Primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Oligonucleotides used as primers were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). The reference genome sequence of M. gryphiswaldense was GenBank accession number CP027526 (Uebe et al. 2018b). Plasmids were constructed by standard recombinant techniques as described below and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Generated constructs were verified by PCR and sequenced by Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam, Netherlands). Sequence data was analyzed with Geneious Prime 2024 (https://www.geneious.com). Whole-genome re-sequencing was performed by Novogene GmbH (Munich, Germany).

Construction of promoter fusions

To verify and measure the activity of promoters, regions of varying lengths (Suppl. Table S3) from maximal 55 to 354 bp relative to the predicted TSS were PCR amplified and cloned either by NdeI and XhoI restriction sites or overlap PCR into a suicide vector pBamII-Tn7-P-luxAE (Suppl. Table S1) upstream of the Photorhabdus luminescens luxABCDE operon, as described previously (Dziuba et al. 2021). For each promoter, two constructs were prepared with (i) the native RBS and (ii) a second optimized RBS (AGGAGA) inserted 8-bp upstream of the start codon. If necessary, the respective regulator transcribed from its own promoter was implemented in the construct upstream of the promoter of interest in reverse direction. Native promoters were cloned from the genome. Pcmt, Pcym, and cymR were synthesized by ATG:biosynthetics GmbH (Merzhausen). A promoterless control was used as a reference for background signal (Dziuba et al. 2021).

Construction of inducible gene cassettes

To create a vector backbone for inducible expression plasmids, the promoter-repressor construct cymR/Pcym with the optimized RBS (Borg et al. 2014) was cloned from pBamII-Tn7-cymR/Pcym-oRBS-luxAE with NotI and SphI restriction sites and ligated in an empty vector pBamII-Tn7, yielding plasmid pBamII-Tn7-cymR/Pcym. Genes of interest (goi), i.e., the essential magnetosome suboperons (mamIEsubop, mamLMNOsubop, and mamRBSTUsubop) or mamB translationally fused to the fluorescent reporter oegfp (a codon-optimized enhanced GFP gene (Borg et al. 2014)) via a helix linker, were ligated in linearized pBamII-Tn7-cymR/Pcym by hot fusion, yielding plasmid pBamII-Tn7-cymR/Pcym-goi. To this end, ready-to-use mixtures of 2X Hot Fusion Mix were prepared with 5 × pre-assembly buffer (containing 0.5 M Tris pH 7.5, 50 mM MgCl2, 1 mM each dNTP, 50 mM DTT, 25% PEG-8000), 0.0075 u/µl T5 exonuclease, 0.05 u/µl Phusion Hot Start DNA polymerase and water and then stored at − 20 °C. To set up a Hot Fusion reaction for single fragment assembly, 30–50 ng/µl of the linearized vector pBamII-Tn7-cymR/Pcym, 30–100 ng/µl of PCR insert (i.e., respective suboperon) and ddH2O to 20 µl were mixed, then tubes were incubated in a thermocycler for 1 h at 50 °C, then slowly ramped down to 20 °C in 5 min (0.1 °C per second), and kept at 10 °C.

Promoter fusion constructs and inducible gene cassettes were inserted into the chromosome of M. gryphiswaldense via Tn7 transposition at the attTn7 site (R. Uebe, unpublished).

Construction of the modified AMB-1 gene cassette

First, for deletion of mamBAMB-1 from pTps-MAGAMB-1 (Awal et al. 2023a) containing the AMB-1 MAI, we used Red/ET recombination, as described previously (Wang et al. 2006). For recombineering, E. coli NEB10ß harboring pTps-MAGAMB-1 and pSC101-BAD was used to prepare electrocompetent cells. NEB10ß cells were electroporated with 200–300 ng of a linear DNA fragment coding for the Cam resistance gene, including the homologous regions flanking mamBAMB-1. Thereby, mamBAMB-1 was replaced by a Cam resistance gene incorporated in reverse to eliminate the risk of side effects from the corresponding promoter, resulting in plasmid pTps-MAGAMB-1∆B (verified by PCR and whole plasmid sequencing (macrogen Europe)).

Conjugation

Plasmids were transferred by biparental conjugation with E. coli WM3064 as donor strain and M. gryphiswaldense strains, more precisely defined for each construct in the results section, as acceptor strains, generally according to the procedure described in Schultheiss and Schüler (2003). Selection for transconjugants was carried out on solid FSM with 1.5% (w/v) agar containing the respective antibiotic and screened for integration of the expression cassette by PCR.

Allelic replacement and insertion mutagenesis by homologous recombination

Homologous recombination was used for scarless insertion or replacement of genomic regions in M. gryphiswaldense by a tailored galK counterselection system as described previously (Raschdorf et al. 2014). Transformant selection was performed as described previously (Woller et al. 2026). Insertions were verified by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

Primary promoter exchange of accessory operons

To render accessory operons mamXYop, mms6op, mms36op, and mamGFDCop inducible, primary promoters PmamY, Pmms6, Pmms36, and PmamG were exchanged with Pcym. Therefore, Pcym was fused between ca. 1 kb homologous regions up- and downstream of each promoter and ligated in the pOR-15a-galK vector, digested with EcoRV, yielding pOR-15a-galK-Pcym.

Insertion of cymR outside MAI

Repressor cymR with its native promoter was cloned between two homologous regions upstream of an rRNA operon outside the MAI and ligated in the pOR-15a-galK vector, digested using EcoRV, yielding pOR-15a-galK-cymR.

Construction of hybrid promoter P(mamH)-CuO

To generate strain mamI::P(mamH)-CuO, ca. 1-kb upstream and downstream homologous regions of the intergenic region between mamH and mamI in the mamABop were fused by fusion PCR with the cumate operator sequence CuO from Pcmt in the overhang of primers. The fragment was ligated in the pOR-15a-galK vector, digested with EcoRV, yielding pOR-15a-galK-CuO. For the lux assay, P(mamH)-CuO was amplified from mamI::P(mamH)-CuO genome and cloned in pBamII-Tn7-P-luxAE as described above.

Luminescence measurements

Per promoter, two to three randomly selected transconjugants harboring vector pBamII-Tn7-P-luxAE were analyzed in three technical replicates for luminescence. Luminescence measurements were performed as described previously (Dziuba et al. 2021). If necessary, promoter activity was induced by adding the respective inducer during early exponential phase after ca. 18 h of growth and then measurements were continued. Maxima of the RLU curves (RLUmax) were used to compare promoter activities.

Induction assays

Cumate (4-isopropylbenzoic acid (C10H12O2)) was purchased from Merck/Sigma-Aldrich (CAS-Number 536-66-3) and dissolved in 100% ethanol to result in a 100 mM stock solution. Different dilutions were prepared in ethanol to give 1000-fold stocks. For “no cumate” controls, an equal volume of ethanol was added to cultures. For all experiments, pre-cultures were passaged 2-times in FSM and inoculated to OD = 0.03 in FSM supplemented with 500 µM Fe for induction assays (iron-rich FSM). If not indicated otherwise, cumate was added at the time of inoculation, and cells were incubated for 72 h at 22 °C or 24 h at 28 °C, and samples for analysis were taken after incubation, i.e. at the end of growth. All following analyses were performed after incubation. Inducers were added to a maximum concentration of 0.1 vol.%. This assay was performed for all strains that were induced with different cumate concentrations and are not described below. All induction experiments were performed in triplicates.

Time-dependent mamB induction analysis

A 28 °C overnight culture was inoculated into a fresh bottle with starting OD565 of 0.03 (t-9 h) and cultivated under mild shaking at 28 °C. Gene expression was induced by addition of 0.7 µM cumate (t0). One-milliliter samples were taken every hour to measure OD565 and Cmag and an additional 1-ml sample was immediately fixed with formaldehyde for TEM analysis and fluorescence microscopy.

Time-point analysis for magnetosome overproduction

OP1 was grown at 28 °C over 50 h. Cultures were induced with 1 µM cumate either from beginning (lag) or after 2 h (early-log), 15 h (mid-log), 24 h (late-log), and 30 h (stat), respectively. Negative control was not induced (uninduced). Samples for OD565 and Cmag measurements were taken every hour between t2 and t15, every 2 h between t24 and t32 and at t50. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) analysis were performed with samples from t50.

Genetic stability assays

The stability of the overproducer phenotype was tested with two different approaches. Two clones of OP1 were grown at 22 °C and passaged every 3 days for a total time span of 51 days (17 passages). Each clone was passaged in two replicates, constantly induced with cumate (I) and not induced (NI). OD565 and Cmag was measured each passage. After 9, 30, and 51 days, we performed separate induction assays by inducing all clones (I and NI) with 1 µM cumate and growth at 28 °C for 24 h. For each of these induction assays, in addition to OD565 and Cmag measurements, TEM and AAS analyses were performed after 24 h. After 51 days, PCR was performed to screen for pTps-MAGAMB-1∆B. As controls, we used M. gryphiswaldense WT and a mock-treated culture for each clone. In a second approach, two clones were cryo preserved at − 80 °C. After reviving, PCR of 16 CFUs per clone was performed to screen for pTps-MAGAMB-1∆B fragments, followed by an induction assay with 1 µM as described above.

Cryo-electron microscopy

Bacteria were plunge-frozen into an ethane/propane mixture cooled to liquid nitrogen temperatures using a Vitrobot Mark 4 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at blot force 6 and blotting time 4 s. Prior to application of 5 µl of sample onto R1.2/1.3 copper 200 mesh grids (Quantifoil), the grids were glow discharged for 30 s per side. Cryo-ET data was collected on a Titan Krios G3 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and a Selectris X energy filter. The samples were tilted to from − 60° to + 60° in a dose-symmetric acquisition scheme, resulting in 41 projections with a dose of 2.4 e/Å2 each (total dose 98.4 e/Å2). The projections were acquired at a nominal magnification of 64,000 ×, resulting in a pixel size of 1.89 Å. The slit width of the energy filter was set to 10 eV. Between the samples, defocus ranges between − 6 µm and − 2.5 µm were used.

Raw data was processed in AreTomo3 (version 2.1.3) (Peck et al. 2025), which integrates MotionCor2 (Zheng et al. 2017) for motion correction and AreTomo (Zheng et al. 2022) for tilt-series alignment and tomogram reconstruction using a weighted backprojection. Tomograms were reconstructed at 4 × binning. Visualization of the reconstructed tomograms was performed using IMOD (version 4.12.32) (Kremer et al. 1996).

Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS)

For intracellular iron measurements, cell samples from induction studies were incubated in 1 ml HNO3 for 3 h at 98 °C to break down the cell wall. Afterwards, the samples were filled up with ddH2O to a final volume of 3 ml and analyzed as described previously (Mickoleit et al. 2023). Measurements were performed in quintuplicates (n = 5), each as a mean of three technical replicates.

SDS-PAGE and Western blot

SDS-PAGE, Western blotting, and immunodetection were performed with whole cell samples according to protocols described previously (Dziuba et al. 2020).

Fluorescence microscopy

Bacterial cells were fixed in 0.7% (w/v) formaldehyde and transferred to 1% (w/v) agarose pads with FSM medium salts. Structured illumination fluorescent microscopy (SIM) was performed on an Eclipse Ti2-E N-SIM E fluorescence microscope (Nikon) equipped with a CFI SR Apo TIRF AC 100 × H NA1.49 Oil objective lens, a hardware based “perfect focus system,” and an Orca Flash4.0 LT Plus sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu). Fluorescence excitation with 488 nm for imaging GFP and image reconstruction and analysis were performed as reported previously (Pfeiffer et al. 2020).

Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), cells were fixed in 0.7% (w/v) formaldehyde and adsorbed onto carbon-coated copper-mesh grids (Science Services, Munich, Germany). TEM was performed on a JEM 1400 + (Jeol GmbH, Freising, Germany) with an acceleration voltage of 80 kV and micrographs were analyzed using the ImageJ Fiji package (Schindelin et al. 2012).

Statistical analyses

Error bars in barplots show mean ± standard deviation (SD). Boxplots display the minimum, maximum, and median of each data set and red points indicate mean. Magnetosome numbers and sizes are given as mean ± SD. Sample sizes were between n = 50–100 for magnetosome numbers and n = 500–1000 for magnetosome sizes.

Results

Identification and analysis of native and foreign promoters

First, we tested the strength of various native and foreign candidate promoters fused to the bioluminescent lux reporter. Among the native candidates, we selected putative promoters with strong, constitutive expression in M. gryphiswaldense (Riese et al. 2022), including those of highly transcribed housekeeping genes such as for ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins. A putative promoter sequence (hereafter termed Prrn) of an rRNA operon (rrn), comprising the 16S, 23S, and 5S rRNA, was cloned from the region 354-bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) (Riese et al. 2022) (Suppl. Table S3). Two further genes highly transcribed under all tested conditions were the monocistronic rpsU (encoding the small ribosomal subunit protein bS21) and rpmG (encoding the large ribosomal subunit protein bL33) (Riese et al. 2022) (Suppl. Fig. S1a). For putative promoters PrpsU and PrpmG, regions of 86 bp (rpmG) and 149 bp (rpsU) upstream of both genes were cloned (Suppl. Table S3). Further candidates Pporin and PcspA were cloned from the TSS to 200-bp upstream of the highly transcribed genes MSR1_31870 (encoding an outer membrane porin) and cspA (encoding a putative cold shock protein) (Suppl. Fig. S1b).

Among inducible (foreign) candidates, the well-established vanillate-, arsenite-, and taurine-inducible systems (Kaczmarczyk et al. 2014; Mostafavi et al. 2014; Dieudonné et al. 2020) failed to exhibit regulation in M. gryphiswaldense in our tests (data not shown). Therefore, we tested promoters Pcym and Pcmt from Pseudomonas putida F1 (Eaton 1996, 1997), because they are part of a promoter-repressor system that drives strong and tunable transcription in many bacteria (Choi et al. 2010; Radeck et al. 2013; Gruber et al. 2016; Dong et al. 2022), including other alphaproteobacteria related to M. gryphiswaldense (Kaczmarczyk et al. 2013).

To compare the strength of all candidate promoters, all constructs were transcriptionally fused to bacterial luminescence reporter gene cassette luxABCDE (in the following termed luxAE) because of the extremely high sensitivity of this reporter system in M. gryphiswaldense (Dieudonné et al. 2020; Dziuba et al. 2021). All constructs included both the promoter sequence and their native leader sequence up to the experimentally determined TSS (Riese et al. 2022) of the following gene, including putative ribosome binding sites (RBS) (hereafter referred to as natRBS [native RBS]). To investigate and compare maximum activity, all promoters were also tested in combination with an optimized RBS (optRBS) (Borg et al. 2014) placed downstream of the native leader sequence with an 8-nucleotide (nt) spacer to the corresponding start codon (Suppl. Fig. S2a). This architecture resembles the previously described optRBS (AGGAG followed by an 8 nt spacer) for expression in M. gryphiswaldense (Borg et al. 2014), akin to the consensus RBS sequence in M. gryphiswaldense, as determined by transcriptomic analysis (Riese et al. 2022). For Pporin, no clones could be obtained when fused to optRBS, likely due to toxic high activity (Klumpp et al. 2009). Since an RBS is naturally absent from the rRNA-encoding gene, Prrn was only tested with optRBS. The resulting promoter fusions and all following constructs in this study, if not depicted otherwise, were inserted into the M. gryphiswaldense chromosome via Tn7 transposition at the attTn7 site (R. Uebe, unpublished) (Suppl. Fig. S2a) to eliminate potential positional side effects and ensure comparability between promoters, e.g., without variation in copy number of replicative plasmids used in other induction studies (Choi et al. 2006; Kaczmarczyk et al. 2013; Dieudonné et al. 2020).

All reporter fusions exhibited significant transcriptional activity, exceeding that of a promoterless control (P-less) (Fig. 1a). Pcym expressed the highest activity, ranging from 758,000 ± 277,000 RLUmax (Pcym-natRBS) to 935,000 ± 46,000 RLUmax (Pcym-optRBS), followed by PcspA-natRBS (555,000 ± 166,000 RLUmax). Both exceeded the activity of PmamDC45, so far the strongest characterized promoter available in M. gryphiswaldense (Borg et al. 2014) (Fig. 1a). All other promoters caused weaker bioluminescence, ranging from 12,000 ± 3000 RLUmax for PrpmG-natRBS to 97,000 ± 23,000 RLUmax for PrpsU-natRBS to 277,000 ± 118,000 RLUmax for Pporin (Fig. 1a and Suppl. Table S3). In the native host P. putida F1, Pcmt and Pcym derive from the p-cumate and p-cymene degradation pathways, respectively, and both operons belong to the CymR regulon (Eaton 1997). Their cognate repressor gene cymR is located 1.8-kb upstream of the cym operon and is inducible by the plant metabolite p-cumate (4-isopropylbenzoic acid, in the following referred to as “cumate”) (Eaton 1997). Pcmt and Pcym differ in their − 10 motif, but mainly in their operator sequence (CuO), the binding site for CymR (Suppl. Fig. S2bi). CymR regulates transcription by binding to the operator site CuO in the absence of inducer. By allosteric binding of cumate to the regulatory site of the repressor, the affinity of the DNA-binding domain to the DNA is reduced by a shift in the accessible conformation (Eaton 1997; Pis Diez et al. 2022).

To render Pcmt and Pcym tunable in M. gryphiswaldense, cymR transcribed from its native promoter was inserted into the chromosome together with the Pcym-luxAE or Pcmt-luxAE fusion (Suppl. Figure 2bii and biii). Bioluminescence was analyzed at different cumate concentrations (0.1–25 µM) which did neither affect growth nor magnetosome biogenesis in WT (data not shown). Bioluminescence of mock-treated cells (0 µM cumate) harboring cymR-Pcmt-luxAE was reduced to nearly background level, i.e., 6000 ± 1500 RLUmax and 5500 ± 1200 RLUmax for natRBS and optRBS, respectively, compared to 3500 ± 2200 for the P-less control (Fig. 1b and Suppl. CymR/Pcym allows induction and tuning of magnetosome formation by controlling transcription of a single essential magnetosome gene

Next, we tested the CymR/Pcym system for induction of magnetosome synthesis. Transcriptional manipulation of accessory and essential magnetosome operons

Next, we wanted to test effects of different MGC transcription levels. The long (16.4 kb) mamABop comprises 17 genes and encodes all the essential factors for at least rudimental iron biomineralization, i.e., mamI, -E, -L, -M, -O, -Q, and -B, whereas the smaller mamXYop, mms6op, mms36op, and mamGFDCop play accessory roles in magnetosome biosynthesis and chain formation and rather regulate numbers and size of magnetosomes (Scheffel et al. 2008; Murat et al. 2010; Katzmann et al. 2010; Lohße et al. 2011; Raschdorf et al. 2013; Lohße et al. 2014; Toro-Nahuelpan et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2020; McCausland et al.; Paulus et al. 2024). To estimate the contribution of their expression level to magnetosome formation, we next attempted to make their transcription tunable by introduction of artificial regulation. As they were previously found to be transcribed as a single unit from a single primary promoter we placed these operons under the control of Pcym by replacing their native primary promoters in an M. gryphiswaldense strain that constitutively expresses cymR (see below), resulting in strains MSR::cymR::PmamY Pcym, MSR::cymR::Pmms6 Pcym, MSR::cymR::Pmms36 Pcym and MSR::cymR::PmamG Pcym, respectively. However, in the absence of cumate, MSR::cymR::PmamY Pcym and MSR::cymR::Pmms6 Pcym resembled their respective deletion phenotype while MSR::cymR::Pmms36 Pcym and MSR::cymR::PmamG Pcym cells continued to produce WT-like magnetosomes, very distinct from respective deletion phenotypes of these operons, and indicating that the transcription of these genes was not tightly repressed as expected (Suppl. Fig. S4). In the presence of different inducer concentrations, magnetosome numbers and sizes were only slightly increased in some of the strains, but not exceeding WT-level (Suppl. Fig. S4). This is in clear contrast to previous studies, where multiplication of operon copies led to a substantial increase in both magnetosome size and number (Lohße et al. 2016).

Strongest effects were previously observed upon either deletion or multiplication of the mamABop comprising 16 genes, including all essential genes for magnetosome biosynthesis (Lohße et al. 2016). It was also shown that transcription of mamABop is driven from multiple promoters (Dziuba et al. 2021; Riese et al. 2022), and this intricacy was suggested to compensate for potential instability of a single long mRNA by splitting mamABop into several separate smaller transcriptional units (TUs), and/or ensure a certain stoichiometric ratio of gene products required for the proper assembly of the magnetosome organelle (Dziuba et al. 2021).

To further test these hypotheses, we attempted to dissect and engineer transcriptional control of mamABop, which is transcribed from the multiple intra- and intergenic promoters P(mamH), P(mamE), P(mamL1), P(mamL2), P(mamO), P(mamP), and P(mamQ) (Fig. 5a) (Dziuba et al. 2021). To this end, the mamABop was divided into the three “suboperons” mamIEsubop, mamLMNOsubop, and mamRBSTUsubop (Fig. 5a), which were each transcriptionally fused to Pcym. To test for induction, constructs together with cymR transcribed from its native promoter were transferred into unmagnetic, single deletion strains each lacking a different essential mam gene comprised in the constructs, i.e., mamIEsubop in ΔmamE, mamLMNOsubop in ΔmamO, and mamRBSTUsubop in ΔmamB. After induction with different cumate concentrations, neither ΔmamE::cymR-Pcym-mamIEsubop nor ΔmamO::cymR-Pcym-mamLMNOsubop showed any difference between the uninduced and induced state (data not shown). However, induction of ΔmamB::cymR-Pcym-mamRBSTUsubop with different cumate concentrations not only complemented the non-magnetic ΔmamB phenotype, but also showed a linear-dose–response correlation of magnetic response and inducer concentration in the range of 0.1 to 0.5 µM cumate (Suppl. Fig. S5 and Suppl. Table S8), similar to single gene induction of mamB in ΔmamB-ind.

In an earlier study, promoter knockout of the primary promoter of mamABop, PmamH, still showed a magnetic phenotype although with smaller and fewer magnetosomes (Raschdorf et al. 2013). However, bioluminescence reporters later demonstrated that the intragenic P(mamH), located within the coding sequence of mamH, is the strongest of all mamABop promoters (= 10 × stronger than the primary promoter PmamH), and one of the most active promoters of all magnetosome operons comprised within the MAI (Dziuba et al. 2021), presumably driving the transcription of an unknown number of genes located downstream of P(mamH). In order to study effects of different P(mamH) activities on downstream genes, we attempted to replace it by a tunable hybrid promoter. To this end, P(mamH) was combined with the operator sites (CuO) for the transcriptional repressor CymR (Fig. 4). Specifically, the resulting hybrid promoter consisted of a modified P(mamH) sequence (Dziuba et al. 2021), in which a 35-bp region upstream of the native mamI RBS was replaced by CuO (hereafter termed P(mamH)-CuO). This construct was then fused to the bioluminescence cassette luxAE, as described above, and integrated into the M. gryphiswaldense chromosome. The hybrid promoter showed constitutive activity, exceeding the activity of the “parent” promoter P(mamH) twofold (Fig. 4ai and Suppl. Table S9). Next, we inserted cymR, transcribed from its native promoter, upstream of P(mamH)-CuO-luxAE. In the presence of CymR, bioluminescence was significantly reduced (Fig. 4ai and Suppl. Table S9), but increased linearly with increasing cumate concentrations, although remaining below native P(mamH) activity (Fig. 4aii and Suppl. Table S9). A possible explanation for the unexpectedly low activity could be the non-native genomic insertion site, which can interfere with promoter activity (Sousa et al. 1997; Goormans et al. 2020). Therefore, we next tested P(mamH)-CuO at the native genomic site within the mamABop. To this end, the same 35 bp of the P(mamH) sequence in the intergenic region between mamH and mamI in the mamABop were replaced with the CuO sequence via homologous recombination (confirmed by sequencing, Suppl. Fig. S6a), resulting in strain mamI::P(mamH)-CuO (Fig. 4bi). P(mamH) spans over the 3’ end of mamH, with the core promoter elements, i.e., − 35 and − 10 motif located within the coding sequence, and the intergenic region between mamH and mamI, driving the expression of the following genes mamI and mamE. In ΔmamI and ΔmamE mutants, magnetosome formation is entirely abolished (Yang et al. 2010; Lohße et al. 2014; Hershey et al. 2016). Therefore, restoration of magnetosome formation would be an indicator for functionality of the hybrid promoter. Therefore, it would be highly desirable to render overexpression inducible and tunable. To achieve this, we combined magnetosome gene duplication with genetic induction of the essential mamB gene. To this end, we modified the integrative plasmid pTps-MAGAMB-1 (Awal et al. 2023a), harboring the five magnetosome operons (mamABop, mamGFDCop, mms6/mms36-48op, mamXY/mag123op, and feoABop) from the closely related Paramagnetospirillum (previously Magnetospirillum) magneticum AMB-1 to minimize genetic recombination, by replacing mamBAMB-1 with a chloramphenicol resistance marker (CamR), yielding pTps-MAGAMB-1∆B (Fig. 6a). Next, OP1 was grown in iron-enriched FSM (500 µM, to avoid iron limitation at enhanced magnetosome biosynthesis) and induced at different growth stages by addition of 1 µM cumate (Fig. 6b). All cultures exhibited similar growth rates, but cells induced at the beginning reached highest ODmax = 1, compared to ODmax = 0.8 for all other cultures (Fig. 6b and Suppl. Table S11). Induction during lag, early-log, and mid-log resulted in magnetosome overproduction, i.e., a twofold increase of magnetosome number per cell, compared to WT (Fig. 6c and d, Suppl. Table S11). Magnetosome size was similar to WT, with an average of 39 nm for cells induced during lag and early-log phase (Fig. 6c and Suppl. Table S11). Cells induced during late-log or stationary phase harbored either no (stat) or significantly fewer (late-log) magnetosomes than WT (Fig. 6c and d, Suppl. Table S11). Overall, induction was most efficient if the inducer was present from the beginning of growth experiment.

Next, OP1 was cultivated with different cumate concentrations for 24 h. With 0.1 µM cumate, only few (4 ± 5) magnetosomes were formed (WT, 22 ± 7) which were aligned in loose chains (Fig. 7 and Suppl. Table S12). At 0.5, 1, and 10 µM cumate, 35 ± 17, 45 ± 18, and 48 ± 20 magnetosomes were formed, respectively (Fig. 7c and Suppl. Table S12). Highest magnetosome numbers per cell were observed with 5 µM cumate (52 ± 25 magnetosomes per cell), equivalent to a 2.7-fold increase compared to WT (Fig. 7c and Suppl. Table S12). With 0.5 and 25 µM cumate, magnetosomes were arranged in one single magnetosome chain as in WT, while the numerous magnetosomes in the presence of 1, 5, and 10 µM cumate formed two distinct chains (Fig. 7a and b, Suppl. Table S12). Magnetosomes were largest in cells induced with 25 µM cumate (36 ± 10 nm, WT, 41 ± 10 nm) (Fig. 7c and Suppl. Table S12). Although individual cells of OP1 contained up to 149 particles per cell (WT maximum, 40), on average OP1 reached only 50–80% of magnetosomes per cell than constitutive overproducer strains reported previously (75 ± 35 (Lohße et al. 2016), ca. 100 (Dziuba et al. 2020), and 65 ± 13 (Awal et al. 2023a). One reason could be the somewhat less efficient expression of foreign magnetosome genes from AMB-1 in M. gryphiswaldense, as a M. gryphiswaldense ΔmamB strain complemented with mamBAMB1 also contained slightly fewer and smaller magnetosomes than M. gryphiswaldense WT (Awal et al. 2023a). In addition, while copies of all other magnetosome genes are present from each of the donors, upon induction OP1 only contains one copy of mamBMgryph, but no mamBAMB1 copy, possibly limiting functionality. While integrating an extra inducible copy of mamBAMB1 into the M. gryphiswaldense genome (i.e., in addition to mamBMSR1) may have further enhanced magnetosome yields, the lack of an independent powerful inducible system orthogonal to CymR/Pcym (enabling decoupled expression of both mamB copies) currently prevented the experimental implementation of this idea. We next analyzed MM vesicle formation in iron-starved cells, i.e., that were grown in iron-free FSM and induced with different cumate concentrations as described above. Empty MM vesicles (eMM) were absent from the non-induced strain, but eMMs and partially filled MM vesicles became visible after induction with 0.1, 0.5, 5, and 10 µM cumate (Fig. 7d). At 0.1 µM cumate, eMMs were already WT-like in size, i.e., ranged from 28 to 71 nm (mean 46 nm) (Raschdorf et al. 2016) (Fig. 7d and Suppl. Table S12) and aligned in 2 chains, in contrast to magnetosomes formed in iron-rich FSM, which only aligned in one loose chain (Fig. 7b and d). This indicates that, although MamB is essential for MM vesicle formation (Raschdorf et al. 2016), amounts of MamB below WT-level are already sufficient to produce WT-like eMMs, but insufficient for biomineralization of WT-like magnetosome crystals. This is in line with eMMs observed in aerobically cultivated nonmagnetic WT cells, under which conditions magnetosome genes are expressed, but magnetite crystallization is prevented by high oxygen levels (Raschdorf et al. 2016; Riese et al. 2022; Tessaro et al. 2026).

In previous studies, we observed spontaneous loss or inactivation of MGC upon prolonged cultivation with permanent overexpression of constitutive overproducers M. gryphiswaldense::pTpsMAG1 and M. gryphiswaldense::pTps-MAGAMB-1 (S. Tessaro, unpublished). This was mostly caused by the numerous transposases and IS elements present within the MAI and its adjacent regions (Schübbe et al. 2003; Ullrich et al. 2005; Kolinko et al. 2011; Zwiener et al. 2021b). Therefore, to assess the long-term stability of the overproducing phenotype in OP1, and the occurrence of spontaneous mutants upon exposure to stress, cultures were grown and passaged at room temperature over 51 days, either in the presence or absence of the inducer cumate. Remarkably, the overproducing potential remained stable both in the absence and presence of the inducer for > 80 generations and after cryopreservation (for details see Suppl. Fig. S7 and S8, Suppl. Table S13 to S16).

Discussion

Characterization of novel constitutive and inducible promoters for M. gryphiswaldense

In this study, we identified and characterized five new native, constitutive promoters of various strengths, with some of them, such as PcspA, exceeding the so-far strongest native magnetosome promoter PmamDC45 used in several studies (Borg et al. 2014; Awal et al. 2023b). This collection could now be employed to adjust different constitutive expression levels in M. gryphiswaldense and related MTB, similar to approaches in other bacteria (Corynebacterium glutamicum (Yim et al. 2013) and Lactococcus cremoris (Wang et al. 2024)). However, they may not be fully orthogonal to the host regulatory network, which will be a need to address in future studies.

In addition, we characterized two cumate-inducible promoters, Pcmt and Pcym, for use in M. gryphiswaldense. Although Pcmt exhibited tighter repression than Pcym, induction of Pcym yielded a ca. 15-fold higher maximal activity and CymR/Pcym-mediated control of the essential magnetosome gene mamB resulted in complete repression in the absence of inducer, indicating a sufficiently tight repression. For comparison, the inducible E. coli system LacI/Plac (Marschall et al. 2017) and TetR/Ptet promoter (Bertram et al. 2022) used previously in M. gryphiswaldense (Scheffel et al. 2006, 2008; Scheffel and Schüler 2007; Lang and Schüler 2008; Lang et al. 2009; Raschdorf et al. 2016) are considerable weaker and not tunable, i.e., rather yield an all-or-none response (Terpe 2006; Borg et al. 2014). In addition, the inducer cumate is cheap, nontoxic, nonmetabolizable for M. gryphiswaldense and capable of traversing the cell’s outer membrane (Vermaas et al. 2022). Fold changes in luxAE expression under the control of a CymR-regulated hybrid promoter in Sphingomonas sp., in response to different cumate concentrations, were similar to those observed for luxAE expression driven by the CymR/Pcym system in M. gryphiswaldense (Kaczmarczyk et al. 2013). However, compared to the cumate concentrations in the micromolar range that were required in all other bacteria (Kaczmarczyk et al. 2013; Seo and Schmidt-Dannert 2019; Klotz et al. 2023), cumate concentrations in the nanomolar range were already sufficient for full induction of magnetosome genes under the control of CymR/Pcym in M. gryphiswaldense. Although we do not expect non-orthogonality of CymR/Pcym at the level of transcriptional cross-talk, we cannot fully exclude that the metabolic burden of overexpressing membrane integration factors, or entire magnetosome gene clusters inevitably stresses the host’s secretion (Sec) pathway, iron metabolism, and general stress responses.

Tunable transcription of magnetosome genes and operons

Previous work has indicated that proper magnetosome formation in M. gryphiswaldense depends on well-balanced protein stoichiometry (Lohße et al. 2016; Dziuba et al. 2021). For engineering and manipulation of the magnetosome biosynthetic pathway, promoters therefore should dynamically respond over a range of inducer concentrations in a dose-dependent manner (Xu et al. 2019; Li et al. 2021; Dziuba et al. 2021; Behrendt 2026). In this study, induced expression of the essential magnetosome gene mamB with CymR/Pcym was stronger and 2.5-times faster compared to induction with LacI/Plac (Raschdorf et al. 2016). Between 0.1 and 0.5 µM cumate, magnetosome biosynthesis was linearly correlated to inducer concentration, with magnetosome sizes reaching WT-level. The fact that magnetosome number per cell slightly remained below WT-level might be explained by the non-native genomic insertion site of the promoter and gene of interest, which could influence promoter activity (Sousa et al. 1997; Goormans et al. 2020). Also in future designs, expression levels of the repressor CymR should be fine-tuned by replacing its native promoter with a weaker and tunable promoter, similar to approaches in other bacteria (Choi et al. 2010; Kaczmarczyk et al. 2013).

When attempting to introduce artificial regulation to accessory operons, mamXYop, mms6op, mms36op, and mamGFDCop by replacing their primary promoters with CymR/Pcym, no significant effect on magnetosome formation was detectable. One possible reason for this might be the existence of additional, yet unidentified promoters which become activated in the absence of the native canonical promoters (Riese et al. 2022). Furthermore, artificial expression levels may interfere with the stoichiometric ratio of magnetosome proteins, as for example observed previously for the expression of magnetosome genes from replicative plasmids which resulted in spontaneous deletions and rearrangements (Lohße et al. 2016). Additionally, overexpression of integral membrane proteins, such as MamC and Mms6, in particular in excess to their cognate interacting partners and the generic secretion machinery (Sec) (Schüler et al. 2026), may cause accumulation of misfolded proteins, decreased membrane integrity, and the formation of inclusion bodies (Snoeck et al. 2024).

However, replacement of native promoters by the tunable CymR/Pcym system was partially successful for the most complex mamABop which harbors all essential magnetosome genes (Murat et al. 2010; Lohße et al. 2014). Induced expression of the “suboperon” mamRBSTUsubop restored magnetosome biosynthesis in the nonmagnetic ΔmamB parent strain. The smaller and fewer magnetosomes compared to WT might be due to that fact that this strain is merodiploidic for the regulators of magnetosome size mamR, mamS, and mamT (Lohße et al. 2014), which, again, might disturb protein stoichiometry. Our failure to induce expression of mamIEsubop and mamLMNOsubop in mutant backgrounds ΔmamE and ΔmamO might be due to the same reason, or/and the non-native genomic insertion site, possibly interfering with promoter activity (Sousa et al. 1997; Goormans et al. 2020). In conclusion, the observed transcriptional intricacy within mamABop, with 13 and 36 “sub-operons” identified under anoxic and oxic conditions, respectively (Riese et al. 2022), is difficult to mimic by introduction of a single inducible promoter.

Upon replacement of the primary mamABop promoter PmamH with CymR/Pcym, the phenotype of the uninduced strain was virtually identical to that previously described for ∆mamH in which magnetosome sizes and numbers were significantly reduced (Raschdorf et al. 2013). Furthermore, full induction essentially restored the WT-phenotype (Suppl. Fig. S9). This is consistent with the observation that a PmamH deletion mainly affected transcription of the non-essential mamH, whereas sufficient transcription of the remaining 16 genes located downstream was still maintained by intragenic promoters (Dziuba et al. 2021). In contrast, expression of downstream genes was severely impaired upon replacement of the main, intragenic mamABop promoter P(mamH) by a cumate-dependent hybrid promoter in the absence of the inducer. Since mamB is located downstream of the last intragenic promoter of mamABop, repression of P(mamH) appears to affect transcription of even the most remote “sub-operon”. Altogether, these results emphasize the importance of P(mamH) for mamABop expression and indeed support that P(mamH) substantially contributes to transcription of the whole mamABop.

Inducible magnetosome overproduction

Finally, by combining heterologous duplication of MGCs with inducible expression of an essential gene, we generated an inducible magnetosome overproducer (OP1), harboring a double hybrid set of magnetosome genes. With this approach, we achieved a twofold increase of magnetosome formation compared to wildtype. Compared to overproducer strains engineered previously by gene multiplication (Lohße et al. 2016; S. Tessaro, unpublished), which were instable and severely impaired at growth temperatures > 23°C, strain OP1 did not show any growth impairment and could be grown at 28 °C as the WT. In addition, magnetosome overproduction in OP1 remained stable over > 80 generations and upon longtime storage. Despite our expectation that metabolic burden conferred by overproduction in the induced state would decrease stability in comparison to non-induced OP strains, OP1 remained stable in both states. The overall increased stability of OP1 therefore might be partially due to reduced homologous recombination in the hybrid duplication between divergent orthologs from the two different donors.

In conclusion, we expanded the set of well-characterized constitutive and inducible promoters for M. gryphiswaldense which enabled transcriptional tuning of magnetosome genes, and was harnessed to construct a tunable, stable magnetosome overproducer. The inducible system adds a powerful part to the genetic toolbox of M. gryphiswaldense and other MTB.
