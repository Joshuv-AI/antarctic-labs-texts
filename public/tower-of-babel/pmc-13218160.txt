Incidence and risk factors of C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and syphilis among a cohort of urban Canadian gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men, 2017–2023: informing the potential impact of doxycycline prophylaxis
Authors: Gilles Lambert, Alain Fourmigue, Milada Dvorakova, Erica E M Moodie, David Moore, Nathan J Lachowsky, Daniel Grace, Trevor A Hart, Darrell H S Tan, Jody Jollimore, Annie-Claude Labbé, Claude Fortin, Mathieu Maheu-Giroux, Mark Hull, Troy Grennan, Sophie Brunelle-Newman, Terri Zhang, Allan Lal, Aki Gormezano, Joseph Cox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesDoxycycline as post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) has emerged as an efficacious strategy to reduce Chlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (N. gonorrhoeae) and syphilis (sexually transmitted infections (STIs)) among gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men (GBM). There is a need to identify prescribing criteria that maximise the number of STIs averted while minimising excessive use.DesignIn this prospective longitudinal cohort study with repeated measures and biobehavioural data collection, participants completed a questionnaire and tested for STIs at each visit.SettingCommunity-based, population-level study conducted in three large Canadian cities between February 2017 and July 2023.Participants2449 GBM were recruited through respondent-driven sampling (RDS); 1998 had ≥1 follow-up visit, contributing 7551 person-years of observation. Eligible participants were aged ≥16 years, cis- or transgender men, reported sex with another man in the past 6 months and resided in Montreal, Toronto or Vancouver.Primary and secondary outcome measuresAdjusted rate ratios (aRR) of STIs, accounting for RDS recruitment, loss to follow-up and confounding were estimated using generalised estimating equations (GEE) Poisson regression. For identified STI risk factors, the proportions of STIs averted through doxy-PEP prescription (based on the efficacy of doxy-PEP for each bacterial STI) and the number needed to treat (NNT) for 1 year to avert one STI, assuming 100% adherence, were calculated.ResultsAmong 1998 participants, the combined incidence rate of any C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and syphilis infection was 29.5 (95%CI 27.3 to 31.9) per 100 person-years. STI risk factors that had the most impact as doxy-PEP criteria were history of any of the three STIs in the past 12 months (P12M) (aRR=2.0, 95% CI 1.8 to 2.2, 36% STI averted, NNT=2.1); ≥10 male sexual partners in the past 6 months (P6M) (aRR=3.8, 95% CI 3.0 to 4.9, 41% STI averted, NNT=2.4); HIV-pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use P6M (aRR=1.7, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.0, 29% STI averted, NNT=2.5); use of any chemsex-related substance P6M (aRR=1.2, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.4, 28% STI averted, NNT=2.6); and group sex event attendance P6M (aRR=1.2, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.3, 27% STI averted, NNT=2.3). Reporting≥10 male sex partners P6M represented the most useful criterion for syphilis prevention (52% syphilis infections averted, NNT=20). Prescribing doxy-PEP to GBM having any of the following STI risk factors, namely, ≥1 bacterial STI P12M, ≥10 male sex partners P6M, or HIV-PrEP use P6M, would substantially increase the proportion of all STI diagnoses potentially averted (60%) with minimal increase of the NNT (2.7).ConclusionThis work informs on the impact of various doxy-PEP clinical prescribing criteria and demonstrates the benefit of focusing on any of the following three criteria: ≥1 bacterial STI P12M, ≥10 male sex partners P6M or HIV-PrEP use P6M.

Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis (CT), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) and syphilis, hereinafter referred to as sexually transmitted infections (STIs), may have severe health consequences1 and increase the risk of HIV infection.2 Between 2010 and 2019, the rates of reported CT, NG and infectious syphilis among men in Canada increased by 63%, 229% and 195% respectively; gay, bisexual men and other men who have sex with men (GBM) represented 41% of male infectious syphilis cases.3 Current prevalence estimates of these infections in GBM populations are obtained mainly from clinics specialising in sexual health and care of sexually transmitted and blood-borne infection (STBBI); these data may overrepresent persons who engage in at-risk sexual behaviours or have better access to health services.

Many factors increase the risks of STIs.4 5 Examining such factors using updated and context-specific data could help inform prevention strategies, such as the use of doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP). Large randomised controlled trials among GBM and transgender women have shown that doxycycline taken within 72 hours after condomless oral or anal sex reduces syphilis and chlamydia infections by approximately 75% and gonococcal infections by approximately 50%.6 7 However, prescribing doxy-PEP is complicated by concerns about potential harms of widespread use, notably antimicrobial resistance.8 Thus, prescribing criteria that maximise the impact on STI occurrence while minimising excessive use must be identified. Furthermore, doxy-PEP adherence is expected to be influenced by perceptions of STI seriousness and acquisition risk.9

The study’s objectives were to (1) estimate trends in the incidence rates of STIs between 2017 and 2023, (2) provide an updated portrait of STI risk factors, (3) evaluate the impact of doxy-PEP using hypothetical prescribing scenarios and (4) explore perceived seriousness and risk of STI among participants meeting potential doxy-PEP criteria. To do so, data from GBM who participated in a population-based biobehavioural cohort study in Montreal, Toronto, and Vancouver, Canada’s three largest cities were analysed.

Methods

Participant recruitment and data collection

Individuals eligible for participation in the Engage Cohort Study were French or English-speaking, cis- or transgender men, aged ≥16 years, who reported sex with at least one man in the 6 months prior to the study visit and who resided in Montreal, Toronto or Vancouver. Respondent-driven sampling (RDS), an adapted form of chain referral, was employed for participant recruitment.10

After the baseline study visit (2017–2019; all sites), participants returned every 6–12 months for follow-up visits (the first follow-up visit was at 12 months in Montreal and Toronto). The present analysis considers the first visit and all subsequent visits, up to February 2023. At each visit, participants completed a questionnaire using computer-assisted self-interviewing and underwent screening for STBBI. Details on how variables were defined and operationalised are found in online supplemental file 1.

Outcomes and other variables of interest

Prospective events of CT, NG and syphilis infections were ascertained. For CT and NG outcomes, laboratory-confirmed infections detected at study visits were counted, as well as self-reported diagnoses received from a doctor/nurse in the 6 months preceding the study visit. To avoid double-counting, self-reported diagnoses of CT or NG at a visit occurring <6 months after a study visit during which a positive laboratory result was obtained were excluded. Concurrent positive results for the same pathogen detected at multiple anatomical sites were considered a single infection. For syphilis, only laboratory-confirmed infections detected at study visits were considered because first infections or reinfections occurring between study visits could be ascertained based on serial serology results and treatment history. Perceived seriousness and risks of CT, NG and syphilis were measured with responses dichotomised as serious/somewhat serious versus neutral/somewhat not serious/not serious.

Based on literature and expert knowledge, potential STI risk factors were selected among the following categories: socio-demographic characteristics, sexual behaviours, contexts for finding sexual partners, substance use, mental health and STBBI prevention and history. Potential criteria for prescribing doxy-PEP were selected from the STI risk factors identified empirically within this study.

Statistical analysis

RDS recruitment often over-represents individuals who are more visible and socially connected within the target population.10 To address this selection bias, modified RDS-II weights were calculated by inverting each participant’s likelihood of inclusion in the sample, which was imputed using self-reported network size, number of participants recruited and the time taken to recruit each of them.11

To consider the COVID-19 pandemic, the study was divided into pre-pandemic (February 2017–2020), pandemic (March 2020 to August 2021) and restriction easing (September 2021 to January 2023) periods. The dates were based on the start of the study, declaration of the pandemic by the WHO12 and easing of entry requirements for non-essential travel into Canada.13 Participants with no follow-up visit during a period were considered lost to follow-up (LTFU) for that period. For each period, inverse-probability-of-censoring (IPC) weights were calculated using baseline covariates to reduce covariate imbalance between LTFU participants and those retained. RDS and IPC weights were combined and applied to all regression models.

Incidence rates of STIs and rate ratios (RR) of STIs for selected risk factors were estimated using generalised estimating equation (GEE) Poisson regression with an offset for time contribution. Robust standard errors clustered by individuals were used to account for within-subject correlation between visits. Incidence rates were calculated by city and period. RRs were assumed to be constant across cities, and the models were adjusted for city-specific incidence of STI. A multivariable GEE Poisson model estimated the association between the rate of all STIs (sum of all events) and the risk factors in the past 6 months (P6M) as well as STI history in the past 12 months (P12M) to compare rates across variables while accounting for potential socio-demographic and psychological confounders.14

The impacts of various hypothetical doxy-PEP prescribing scenarios were evaluated. In doing so, adherence of 100% was assumed; that is, at each visit, participants who met the specified criteria were considered to have taken doxy-PEP within 72 hours of every episode of condomless oral or anal sex until their subsequent visit. Across all study periods, the total number of STI events among participants using doxy-PEP were reduced by doxy-PEP clinical trial efficacy estimates. This enabled consideration of the varying efficacies of doxy-PEP for each bacterial STI. Specifically, 74%–88% for CT, 55%–57% for NG and 77%–87% for syphilis (online supplemental file 2). Finally, for each criterion, the number needed to treat (NNT) with doxy-PEP for 1 year to avert one STI diagnosis was calculated by dividing the number of participants on doxy-PEP over time (expressed in PY) by the number of STI diagnoses averted among all participants.

Sample proportions of perceived seriousness and perceived risk of acquisition of each STI were calculated. Associations between selected doxy-PEP criteria and each of these two perceptions were estimated using a series of multivariate GEE Poisson regressions adjusted for potential confounders.

Analyses were performed using R v4.1.2 (R Core Team, 2021).

Patient and public involvement

The Engage study collaborated with community engagement committees (CECs) in each respective city throughout the duration of the project. CECs consisted of members of the local gay, bisexual and queer communities. CEC members were consulted regularly for feedback and input on all stages of the Engage study: questionnaire development, participant recruitment, study visits, data analysis, results publication and dissemination, as well as knowledge transfer and exchange.

Results

Overall, 11 569 visits were recorded (2449 at baseline and 9120 during follow-up). The median time between two consecutive visits was 7.5 (IQR 6.1–12.1) months.

Participant characteristics at baseline

Among participants recruited at baseline, 37% were ≤29 years old, 53% identified as French or English Canadian, 15% had immigrated to Canada in the last 5 years, 49% completed a university-level education, 82% identified as gay, 1.2% identified as trans men and 55% were single without a current main partner. Lifetime and P12M self-reported prevalence of an STI were 55% and 26%, respectively. Laboratory-confirmed HIV infection was detected among 18% of participants (online supplemental file 3).

Incidence rates of sexually transmitted infections

During the total time of observation, 1998 participants had ≥1 follow-up visit, corresponding to 7551 PY (mean, 3.8 PY; median, 4.2 PY). The total number of STI events detected or self-reported at follow-up was 2317. For CT, 332 infections were detected, and 705 were self-reported; for NG, 308 infections were detected, and 749 were self-reported; and for syphilis, 223 infections were ascertained. Overall, 8.4% and 40.9% of detected CT and NG infections, respectively, were detected at the pharynx only. Between 2017 and 2023, CT infection incidence ranged from 12.0 to 15.7|100 PY by city, and NG infection incidence was comparable at 11.6–14.8|100 PY by city. Syphilis incidence was the lowest, ranging from 2.3 to 4.5 infections per 100 PY by city (table 1). Compared with Montreal, the overall STI rate was similar in Toronto (RR=1.1; 95% CI 0.9 to 1.4) and higher in Vancouver (RR=1.2; 95% CI 1.0 to 1.4). Pooled across cities, the overall STI rate was 29.5|100 PY (27.3–31.9). Compared with the pre-pandemic period, it was lower during the pandemic (RR=0.5; 95% CI 0.4 to 0.6) and restriction easing (RR=0.7; 95% CI 0.6 to 0.8) periods. However, the overall STI rate was higher during the restriction easing period compared to the pandemic period (RR=1.3; 95% CI 1.1 to 1.7).

Factors associated with sexually transmitted infections

Univariable regression analyses (online supplemental file 4) showed that most risk factors for CT, NG and syphilis were shared and yielded similar RR. For example, the RRs for HIV-pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) users were 4.3 (95% CI 3.6 to 5.2), 3.7 (95% CI 3.1 to 4.4) and 4.1 (95% CI 2.8 to 6.0) for CT, NG and syphilis, respectively. However, the following factors showed a higher RR for syphilis than for CT or NG: living with HIV infection, use of crystal methamphetamine, use of gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), having sex with a partner living with HIV infection, providing or receiving transactional sex and injection drug use. The following factors were associated with all STI events in univariable regression analyses, but were not retained in the multivariable regression model: not having a main partner, sextoy sharing, giving or receiving rimming, transactional sex, attendance at a bathhouse/sex club, alcohol use disorder, hazardous cannabis use, cocaine/crack use, harassment-discrimination score ≥2, childhood sexual abuse, lifetime sexual coercion and arrival in Canada <5 years ago from a country other than the USA or Western Europe.

Multivariable regression analysis showed that the rate of all STIs was most strongly associated with having multiple male sex partners in the P6M and a history of ≥1 STI in the P12M (aRR=2.0, 95% CI 1.8 to 2.2). The regression model was adjusted for several potential confounders, including age, education level, sexual orientation, financial strain, annual income, city, recent arrival in Canada (<5 years) and the COVID-19 pandemic period. Other statistically significant factors included living with HIV and the following factors P6M: PrEP use, condomless anal sex, use of a chemsex-related substance, injection drug use, attendance at group sex events, daily use of an internet application to connect with men and a sexual compulsivity score ≥2.4 (table 2).

Prescribing doxy-PEP to 100% of GBM (no criterion applied) would avert: 81% of CT diagnoses (NNT=9.0), 55% of NG diagnoses (NNT=12.9), 81% of syphilis diagnoses (NNT=41.9) and 69.5% of all three STI diagnoses (NNT=4.7). As criteria become more restrictive, the estimated proportions of people prescribed doxy-PEP and the estimated proportions of STI diagnoses averted are more limited. Whichever criterion is used, the NNT estimate is much higher for a case of syphilis averted (range, 15 to 33) than for a case of CT or NG averted (range, 3 to 9).

The scenario using the criterion ‘history of ≥1 STI P12M’ would result in 23% of participants’ PY on doxy-PEP use, 36% of all STI diagnoses being averted and an NNT of 2.1. The criteria ‘CT history’ and ‘NG history’ would have similar effects on the proportions of all STIs averted (24%), with NNTs of 1.8, which represents a seven-fold higher impact compared with ‘syphilis history’ (4% of STIs averted with NNT of 2.5). Reporting multiple male sex partners P6M is the other major single criterion. Not surprisingly, ‘reporting≥10 male sex partners’ (41% of STIs averted with NNT of 2.4) would avert a slightly lower proportion of STIs than ‘reporting≥5 male sex partners’ but would require treating less individuals. ‘Reporting ≥10 male sex partners’ specifically represented the most balanced criterion for syphilis prevention (52% of syphilis cases averted with NNT of 20).

Applying any of the following single criteria, ‘HIV-PrEP use’, ‘use of a chemsex-related substance’ and ‘attendance at a group sex event’ would result in somewhat similar proportions of all STI diagnoses averted (27% to 29%), with NNT varying from 2.5 to 2.7. Using these same criteria specifically for the group of participants not having a ‘history of ≥1 STI P12M’ would also lead to a similar proportion of STI diagnoses averted (25%–28%) with NNT varying from 2.3 to 2.6. Furthermore, applying the criterion ‘reporting ≥10 male sex partners P6M’ within the group of persons without STI history would lead to 38% of STI diagnoses averted with an NNT of 2.5.

Living with HIV infection would lead to half of the impact of the criterion ‘HIV-PrEP use’ on the proportion of all STIs averted (17%), with an NNT of 3.6. However, it would result in a greater impact when considering the proportion of syphilis diagnoses averted (31%) with an NNT of 21.

Considering either criterion, ‘history of STI P12M’ or ‘reporting ≥10 male sex partners P6M’ would greatly increase the proportion of diagnoses averted (56%) without substantially affecting the NNT (2.5), as would considering either ‘history of STI P12M’ or ‘HIV-PrEP use’ (53%, 2.5). Prescribing doxy-PEP to GBM having any of the following STI risk factors: ≥1 bacterial STI P12M or ≥10 male sex partners P6M or HIV-PrEP use P6M would further increase the proportion of STI diagnoses potentially averted (60%) with minimal increase of the NNT (2.7).

Perceived seriousness and risk of sexually transmitted infections

The proportion of participants who perceived an STI as ‘somewhat serious’ or ‘serious’ was similar for NG and CT (45% vs 44%) but higher for syphilis: 55% (aPR=1.3; 95% CI 1.2 to 1.4). This proportion was significantly lower among participants reporting any of the potential doxy-PEP prescribing criteria (table 4). The proportion of participants who perceived that they were ‘somewhat likely’ or ‘likely’ to acquire the STI within the next 12 months was similar for NG and CT (18.0% versus 18.0%), but lower for syphilis at 11.9% (aPR=0.7; 95% CI 0.6 to 0.8). Perceived likelihood of STI acquisition was significantly higher among those reporting any of the potential doxy-PEP prescribing criteria (table 5).

Discussion

Almost one third (30%) of sexually active GBM living in Canada’s three largest cities is expected to acquire at least one STI each year. The incidence (2017–2023) rates of NG and CT were similar, whereas the rate of syphilis first/reinfection was six times lower. The incidence of STIs decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic period compared with that during the pre-pandemic period (a decrease ranging from 42% to 59% across cities), consistent with the reduction in sexual partners reported during the pandemic.15 After the easing of pandemic restrictions started, STI incidence trended towards a return to pre-pandemic levels, also observed in surveillance data in many high-income countries.16 17 Due to a general lack of published population-based incidence estimates accounting for the COVID-19 pandemic, it is challenging to further contextualise observed trends.

In this study, a past STI diagnosis doubled the risk of a new STI within the next 12 months (RR=2.0, 95% CI 1.8 to 2.2). Moreover, reporting multiple male sex partners was the strongest predictor of STI acquisition, with a clear dose–response effect. Beyond partner count, various contexts also appear to contribute to STI risk. Group sex increases opportunities for multiple sexual acts within a short timeframe and is linked to both reduced condom use and partners with higher risk behaviours.18 Chemsex, which overlaps with multiple psychosocial vulnerabilities (including sexual compulsivity), may entrench individuals in sexual networks with higher STI prevalence and interfere with protective behaviours.1921 Daily use of geosocial networking apps is also associated with increased STI risk, likely due to integration into sexualised subcultures.22 HIV-PrEP users were significantly associated with higher rates of STI than non-HIV-PrEP users/individuals not living with HIV infection. This finding aligns with previous reports of HIV-PrEP users having an increased incidence of STIs after initiating HIV-PrEP.23 In part, this may reflect more frequent and systematic STI screening among HIV-PrEP users. In this study, although HIV-PrEP use has steadily expanded, reaching 32% in 2023, its strong association with STI risk behaviours and concomitant higher STI incidence has not decreased.

Evidence for doxy-PEP efficacy has been specifically documented among GBM living with HIV and HIV-PrEP users, two groups who regularly engage with healthcare services, facilitating adoption of doxy-PEP. Traeger et al24 examined the impact of doxy-PEP prescribing strategies and showed that prescribing doxy-PEP based on recent STI history as the main prescribing criterion, prevents more infections per person treated than strategies based only on HIV-PrEP use or HIV status.24 Using similar hypothetical scenarios, we observed the same pattern in our data. Current guidance2528 recommends offering doxy-PEP to GBM with a history of STI P12M. Of note, the French guidelines specify at least two episodes of bacterial STI P12M, which appears rather restrictive based on our findings. The CDC extends the doxy-PEP offer to GBM and transgender women without documented STI history who present other high-risk characteristics, though these are not precisely defined. The Australian consensus statement frames doxy-PEP primarily as a strategy for syphilis prevention.27 Indeed, treating asymptomatic CT infections in men may be of limited benefit.29 It recommends doxy-PEP for GBM with a recent syphilis diagnosis or at least two other recent bacterial STIs. In our data, this recommendation would avert 17.0% of syphilis cases. The Australian consensus statement also recommends doxy-PEP for GBM anticipating a period of increased STI risk, such as attending sex events or engaging with multiple casual sexual partners while travelling.27 Indeed, using a behavioural criterion such as reporting ≥10 male sex partners P6M (which is one of the specific STI risk factors proposed in the German/Austrian doxy-PEP guidelines) appeared to be the most useful criterion for syphilis prevention in this study. Our results support considering a limited number of criteria to yield the best balance between effectiveness in averted infections and potential harms of unnecessary doxycycline use (box 1). Specifically, offering doxy-PEP only to individuals with either a STI diagnosis P12M or those reporting ≥10 male sex partners P6M or using HIV-PrEP would avert 60% of STIs while treating 2.7 GBM for 1 year to avert one STI.

Real-world adherence data for doxy-PEP use are limited. In this analysis, we simulated scenarios using 100% adherence across STI risk factor criteria. While this approach was helpful for comparisons, it is likely idealistic. Indeed, three randomised controlled clinical trials have reported a global self-reported adherence rate of approximately 80%30 among men who have sex with men living with HIV infection or using HIV-PrEP. Furthermore, we found that GBM who meet the doxy-PEP criteria tend to perceive themselves at higher risk for STI (which may support their willingness to use the intervention).31 However, they also tend to perceive STIs as less serious, which could limit real-world adherence (slightly more than half of them do not see syphilis as a serious condition). Therefore, prescribing doxy-PEP should be based on informed discussions about both the benefits and potential harms of use. In addition, our finding regarding perceived likelihood of having STI among participants without identified doxy-PEP prescribing criteria could contribute to unnecessary antibiotic use among individuals at lower STI risk.

Finally, the estimated impacts of doxy-PEP in this study do not account for the additional benefits accrued for GBM through reduced overall STI prevalence among their sexual partners. Neither do they include the benefits of decreased STI-related specific morbidity among cisgender women (10% of participants reported sex with a cisgender woman at least once during the entire cohort).

Strengths and limitations

Adjustment for recruitment method, loss to follow-up and within-subject correlation from repeated measures was applied using RDS-II weights, IPC weights and GEE regression, respectively, which helped limit related biases. Another strength of this study is that it accounts for both CT and NG infections detected during the study visit and self-reported diagnoses. Indeed, a large proportion of Engage Study participants (79%) reported STI testing at least once outside of the study, meaning that some infections might not have been captured by the study itself. This is particularly relevant for CT and NG, which are not detectable a few weeks after treatment. Since the question on self-reported diagnoses referred to a 6-month period, and given that CT and NG tests detect nucleic acids for a maximum of 4 weeks post-treatment, the likelihood of double-counting a single infection was relatively low. In our data, CT was both detected and self-reported during the same study visit only 75 times (7.6%); a similar result was observed for NG. In addition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of the multivariable regression model of all STI events using only laboratory-detected events, finding similar estimates for risk factors in the main analysis.

There are also limitations. First, because information on the number of condomless sexual acts was not collected, it was not possible to estimate the number of doxy-PEP doses needed to avert an infection. Second, the number of infections averted and therefore the NNTs are contingent on the STI rates and levels of doxycycline-resistant NG in a given community, perhaps limiting generalisability of these findings. Third, current guidance recommends doxy-PEP for transmen who have sex with men.32 In our data, the small group size of transmen (1.2% of baseline sample) and a negligible number of STI events and limited follow-up precluded subgroup-specific estimates of STI incidence and doxy-PEP impact. Therefore, the inclusion of transmen is not expected to have meaningfully affected our findings. Finally, participation in the cohort may have influenced prevention behaviours leading to an underestimation of the true STI incidence in the community.

Conclusion

Population-based data are essential to appreciate the true burden of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis among GBM. Prevention interventions must address a broad range of STI risk factors. Our findings contribute to efforts to understand the impact of various doxy-PEP prescribing criteria, highlighting the benefit of focusing on the following three criteria: bacterial STI history, numerous sexual partners or HIV-PrEP use. Future research may consider evaluating the implementation of doxy-PEP across various clinical indications, including an understanding of population-level impacts on STI occurrence and antimicrobial resistance.
