The Study in Outpatient Medicine using Nudges to Improve Sleep (SOMNUS): study protocol for an 18-month factorial, cluster-randomized trial to increase guideline-concordant treatment of insomnia in primary care
Authors: Jonathan N Cloughesy, Jeffrey A Linder, Tara K Knight, Stephen D Persell, Mark D Sullivan, Daniella Meeker, Tiffany Brown, Ji Young Lee, Emily P Stewart, Craig R Fox, Noah J Goldstein, Ria Grace Sumitro, Michael V Vitiello, Wendy J Mack, Jason N Doctor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines support cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. However, the most widely prescribed treatments are pharmacological sedatives referred to as “Z-drugs,” which are not recommended as first-line or for long-term use. The Study in Outpatient Medicine Using Nudges to Improve Sleep (SOMNUS) is an 18-month, factorial, cluster-randomized trial designed to evaluate the effectiveness of behavioral interventions that nudge primary care clinicians to provide guideline-concordant treatment for patients with insomnia by facilitating CBT-I use and discouraging Z-drug orders.MethodsThe SOMNUS trial takes place at Northwestern Medicine, an academic health system located in and around Chicago, IL, USA, and will enroll 444 primary care clinicians working in 64 outpatient clinics. Using a 2 × 2 factorial design, clinicians will be clinic-cluster-randomized to receive one of two nudges embedded in the electronic health record (EHR), their combination, or neither. The first nudge sets the default dispense quantity for new Z-drug orders to 10 pills with no refills. The second nudge is a just-in-time prompt with a one-click option to align orders with guidelines and requires clinicians to enter a justification if prompt recommendations are not followed. The primary outcome is the change in encounter-level Z-drug pill count from the 18 months before through the 18 months after the intervention period begins. Secondary outcomes will assess CBT-I referrals, monthly Z-drug pill count, Z-drug treatment duration, and benzodiazepine pill count.DiscussionThe SOMNUS trial will use EHR-integrated behavioral nudges that aim to increase guideline-concordant treatment of insomnia in primary care. Results can inform scalable approaches to improve insomnia treatment.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06640023. Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Chronic insomnia is a prevalent and costly disorder, affecting 5–15% of adults with total costs exceeding US $100 billion per year [1, 2]. Randomized controlled trials have found cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) to be a safe and effective treatment for insomnia across all age groups in the short and long term [1, 3–7], leading professional organizations worldwide to recommend CBT-I as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia [8–13]. CBT-I is a multicomponent treatment that integrates behavioral modification, including time-in-bed restriction and stimulus control, with cognitive restructuring, relaxation training, and sleep hygiene education [3]. Despite first-line recommendations, patients rarely receive CBT-I referrals in clinical practice [14].

Z-drugs, including zolpidem, eszopiclone, and zaleplon, are the most widely prescribed class of drugs for insomnia in the USA, with approximately 30 million outpatient prescriptions dispensed in 2018 [15]. Contrary to guidelines, Z-drugs are usually prescribed as the first-line treatment for insomnia. In addition, while guidelines recommend against prescribing Z-drugs for more than 5 weeks, Z-drug use frequently exceeds this duration [8, 16]. Z-drug use is associated with short- and long-term adverse events [17, 18], including complex sleep behaviors like sleepwalking [15, 19], next-day cognitive impairment [20, 21], heightened risk of falls and fractures [22–24], and physical dependence [17]. Accordingly, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends treatment remains as short as possible [17], and the American Geriatrics Society has issued a strong recommendation to avoid prescribing Z-drugs to adults aged 65 years and older [25]. However, prescription sleep medication use is highest among older adults [26, 27].

Interventions using behavioral economics principles may discourage Z-drug prescribing and facilitate referrals to CBT-I. Behavioral interventions that make subtle changes to the clinical decision-making environment, often called “nudges,” can help clinicians provide guideline-concordant treatment while retaining clinicians’ autonomy over their decisions for patients [28]. While most prior efforts to curb Z-drug prescribing presume a knowledge deficit and have focused on clinician education or patient counseling [29, 30],1 the SOMNUS trial uses insights from behavioral science to increase guideline-concordant clinician decision-making.

The two nudges tested in this study are as follows: (1) A manipulation of the auto-populated pill quantity for new Z-drug orders (Z-drug Quantity Default) and (2) a just-in-time prompt to provide guideline-concordant care or justify the rationale for not doing so (Redirection + Accountable Justification). In prior research, modified quantity defaults and justification alerts have resulted in moderate-to-large reductions in inappropriate prescribing of opioids and antibiotics [33–35]. However, they have not yet been tested to reduce Z-drug prescribing, nor in combination with one another despite the potential for synergistic effects.

Explanation for the choice of comparators {9b}

Nudges will be compared to a no-intervention control group. The control group receives access to insomnia management guideline education prior to the trial but does not receive any electronic health record (EHR) nudges during the intervention period. Clinician education is a widely used strategy in primary care settings to encourage appropriate prescribing practices, including sedative–hypnotics, and therefore represents an appropriate comparator to EHR nudges [30].

Objectives {10}

The primary objective of SOMNUS is to determine whether clinician-facing nudges embedded in the EHR in combination with insomnia management guideline education are superior to only providing guideline education to reduce the quantity of Z-drug pills prescribed at patient encounters. Secondary objectives include determining if EHR nudges affect the quantity of CBT-I referral orders, monthly Z-drug pill count, duration of Z-drug treatment episodes, and benzodiazepine pills prescribed.

Methods: participant and public involvement and trial design

Participant and public involvement {11}

A pilot study involving six primary care clinicians at Northwestern Medicine provided an opportunity to modify the interventions based on clinician feedback. No additional participant or public involvement occurred.

Trial design {12}

SOMNUS is a cluster-randomized superiority trial within a large academic healthcare system. Clinicians will be randomized at the clinic level in a 2 × 2 factorial design and a 1:1:1:1 allocation to receive insomnia management guideline education alone, the addition of one of two EHR nudges, or their combination (Fig. 1).

Trial design

To improve balance on the primary dependent variable, clinics will be stratified into low and high prescribing clinics by a median split of the proportion of encounters in which a Z-drug was prescribed between October 15, 2023, and October 14, 2024. Clinicians will be attributed to the clinic in which they saw patients during the plurality of in-person and telehealth encounters between April 23, 2024, and October 22, 2024, and will receive the intervention they were assigned to regardless of the clinic in which they see patients on a given day. The trial will take place over an 18-month intervention period with a 12-month post-intervention follow-up period. The trial is prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06640023). This protocol follows the SPIRIT 2025 reporting guidelines [36, 37].

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Trial setting {13}

The trial will take place at outpatient primary care clinics that are part of Northwestern Medicine, a large academic health system located in and around Chicago, IL, USA. The trial will include 444 primary care clinicians working in 64 outpatient clinics. All clinics use a single instance of the Epic® electronic health record (EHR) system.

Eligibility criteria for participants {14a}

Northwestern Medicine primary care clinicians that treat adult patients in outpatient internal medicine, family medicine, and geriatric clinics will be enrolled in the study. Eligible clinicians include physicians, nurse practitioners, and physician assistants. Six clinicians who participated in a pilot to test the EHR alerts as well as two clinician-investigators involved with the design and conduct of the trial will be excluded. In addition, because CBT-I is contraindicated for those with bipolar disorder, encounters with patients who have been diagnosed with bipolar disorder (ICD-10 diagnosis code F31.X) within 1 year prior to the encounter or patients who have bipolar disorder on their active problem list during the encounter will not receive the Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention. All encounters with patients meeting these criteria will be excluded from study analyses.

Eligibility criteria for sites and those delivering interventions {14b}

Northwestern Medicine clinics were eligible for inclusion if they provide internal medicine, family medicine, or geriatric medicine in outpatient settings.

Who will take informed consent? {32a}

A waiver of informed consent for clinician enrollment and review of electronic health record data collected during the course of routine care delivery was obtained from the University of Southern California Institutional Review Board (IRB no. UP-23–00317). This waiver was requested to improve the external validity of the trial by reducing threats to generalizability that could result from allowing clinicians to choose to enroll [38]. The IRB determined the research involves no more than minimal risk to participants, could not practicably be carried out without the waiver, and does not adversely affect the rights and welfare of subjects, as interventions are integrated into standard clinical workflows and do not compromise decision-making autonomy. The University of Southern California IRB is the trial’s single reviewing IRB and approved the full study protocol.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {32b}

There are no additional consent provisions for use of trial data in ancillary studies.

Interventions and comparator

Interventions and comparator description {15a}

Guideline education will be delivered within an optional online module that is linked in an email delivered to all enrolled clinicians 2 weeks prior to the start of the intervention period (Supplementary Material 1). The email alerts clinicians to the existence and purpose of the SOMNUS trial. The module begins with an approximately 20-min baseline survey for which clinicians must provide informed consent. The survey is followed by information about the purpose of the study and insomnia treatment guidelines, such as that CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment for insomnia. The online module also includes information about Z-drug prescribing safety, a notice that a CBT-I referral has been created for the trial and a copy of the referral, and a description of the EHR modifications the clinician will experience as part of the trial. Clinicians receive a US $150 Amazon e-gift card for completion of the survey and education module. The online module, including the baseline survey and guideline education components, can be viewed in Supplementary Material 1. The CBT-I referral that was created for this trial is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Participants in each of the three intervention arms will receive access to guideline education in the same manner described above, along with the Z-drug Quantity Default intervention, the Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention, or both during the 18-month intervention period. Clinicians assigned to a No Intervention control will only receive guideline education.

Z-drug quantity default Intervention

Clinicians assigned to the Z-drug Quantity Default arm will receive pre-trial access to guideline education and a modified default pill quantity for orders of zolpidem, zolpidem CR, zaleplon, and eszopiclone oral tablets. The dispense quantity default will be set to 10 pills with 0 refill at all dosage levels, which reduces the preexisting defaults by approximately 90% (Supplementary Material 3). The pill quantity defaults will be modified in Z-drug orders on a clinician’s preference list, a personalized list of frequently used orders that are queried when searching for a medication. When entering an order, clinicians can proceed with the order template as is (i.e., retaining the dosage, pill quantity, and refill number) or can modify the order details before signing. Clinicians can modify the default pill quantities in their preference list and create a new default pill quantity for any drug at any time.

The 10-pill default quantity for Z-drug orders in the preference list is only used when clinicians initiate a new Z-drug order. When clinicians reorder a Z-drug prescription that had been previously ordered for a patient, the quantity displayed is duplicated from the prior order.

Although clinicians have the freedom to alter the pill quantity in any order, defaults exert strong pulls on clinical decision-makers through a variety of mechanisms. First, clinicians may opt to reduce cognitive effort during a patient visit and choose the default option to avoid such effort [39]. Second, a default may become the reference option against which clinicians compare alternatives, making moving away from the default unappealing [40]. Third, defaults imply endorsement, leading clinicians to infer that the preselected option has been chosen because it is considered the recommended action [41]. Further, for continuous outcomes like pill count, the default may anchor judgments about appropriate pill count that affect pill count selection [42]. Setting defaults to a low but clinically defensible quantity could potentially keep adjusted pill counts closer to guideline recommendations, as compared to higher default pill count [34, 43]. In time-constrained environments such as a primary care visit, clinicians are especially likely to rely on heuristics, making the default option especially influential. In healthcare, defaults have increased vaccinations, increased cancer and infectious disease screening, increased generic drug prescribing, decreased opioid prescribing, and decreased unnecessary imaging [44, 45].

Z-drug pill quantity defaults in the EHR commonly exceed recommended treatment durations set by clinical practice guideline organizations. For example, prior to the intervention period, Northwestern Medicine’s pill quantity default for zolpidem, the most widely prescribed Z-drug, is 90 pills with one refill (> 25-week supply), far surpassing the American College of Physicians’ guideline-recommended maximum treatment duration of 4–5 weeks [8]. The Z-drug quantity defaults used in Northwestern Medicine’s default preference list from the pre-intervention period are provided in Supplementary Material 3, along with the modified pill quantity defaults for clinicians assigned to receive the Z-drug Quantity Default intervention.

Redirection + accountable justification intervention

In addition to pre-trial guideline education, clinicians assigned to the Redirection + Accountable Justification arm receive an EHR prompt whenever a Z-drug order is added to the pending order set at eligible encounters, prior to signing. Two different types of prompts are possible at an encounter.

For encounters with patients who have been prescribed 180 days or fewer of any Z-drug in the past 365 days at Northwestern Medicine, an EHR prompt states that “z-drugs are not first-line for insomnia and need explicit justification at signing” and “CBT-I…is now first line treatment for insomnia.” The prompt provides a pre-selected, one-click option to remove the Z-drug and add a CBT-I referral to the pending order. If a CBT-I referral has previously been ordered for the patient in the prior 180 days, the prompt does not recommend that a CBT-I referral be ordered.

For encounters with patients who have been prescribed more than 180 days of Z-drugs in the past 365 days, the prompt will recommend the clinician adds a CBT-I referral to the order (“CBT-I…is now first-line treatment for insomnia… Absence of CBT-I referral requires justification in the chart at signing.”), but it will not prompt the clinician to remove the Z-drug order.2 The prompt provides a pre-selected, one-click option to add a CBT-I referral to the pending order. If a CBT-I referral has previously been ordered for the patient in the prior 180 days, no prompt appears.

In either case, if the clinician does not accept the prompt’s recommendations and proceeds to sign the order, they will be prompted to provide a clinical justification for their decision. If the justification prompt is dismissed, the clinician is alerted that “No justification given for Z-drug prescribing” or “No justification given for absence of CBT-I referral” will be recorded in the patient’s chart under a section titled “High-Risk Prescribing.” During refill encounters, such as when a Z-drug refill is requested by the patient or a pharmacy, the Z-drug order may be added to a pending order by someone other than the enrolled clinician. In this case, the prompt to remove the Z-drug and/or add a CBT-I referral does not appear. However, the justification prompt will still display at order signing when the enrolled clinician signs the order.

The Redirection + Accountable Justification prompt overtly states that a given action is or is not recommended and sets the default action to accept the recommended options. In doing so, the default option reinforces the strength of the recommendation, and the prompt creates an opportunity for pause, allowing clinicians to easily change their choice [46]. By also notifying clinicians that they will need to provide justification in the patient’s chart if they do not follow the recommended pathway, the prompt elicits reputational concerns and motivates behavior change to preserve one’s clinical reputation when there is no valid justification [47–50]. Prior work has successfully applied justifications to change clinician decision-making, including by reducing inappropriate antibiotic prescribing [33]. Screenshots of the Redirection + Accountable Justification prompts can be viewed in Supplementary Material 3.

Combined intervention

Clinicians assigned to the combined intervention arm will receive the interventions contained within the Z-drug Quantity Default arm and the Redirection + Accountable Justification arm.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {15b}

Clinicians may request to discontinue the Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention by notifying the Northwestern study team. Study team contact information and instructions on how to submit feedback are included in the online education module distributed to all enrolled clinicians. In addition, clinicians assigned to receive the Z-drug Quantity Default intervention are able to independently change the default pill count for Z-drug order templates in their preference list. Otherwise, the interventions will not be discontinued or modified during the 18-month intervention period as they do not limit clinicians’ freedom in ordering Z-drugs, the amount of Z-drugs, or otherwise providing care for their patients, except as requested by the Data and Safety Monitoring Board in the event it is deemed necessary for patient safety.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions/comparator {15c}

The Redirection + Accountable Justification prompts are delivered using a computerized algorithm for eligible encounters, ensuring intervention adherence. However, in the spirit of a pragmatic trial, clinicians assigned to the Z-drug Quantity Default will not be restricted from modifying the pill quantity defaults in their preference list. The Z-drug pill quantity defaults in each clinician’s preference list will be reviewed at the end of the 18-month intervention period to assess how many clinicians modified the Z-drug Quantity Default intervention.

Concomitant interventions {15d}

For the first 5 months of the intervention period, SOMNUS will overlap with another clinic–cluster–randomized trial at Northwestern Medicine examining the effect of two behavioral interventions embedded in the EHR on clinician prescribing behavior in the setting of high-risk polypharmacy. The concomitant intervention aims to nudge clinicians to deprescribe medications for adults ages 65 and older who take five or more total medications and have been prescribed three or more medications concurrently known to raise the risk of falls including Z-drugs or have evidence of high fall risk and take one or more drugs known to raise the risk of falls. Both interventions are randomized at the clinic level. Therefore, prior to randomization, a clinician’s chance of being assigned to either study’s intervention arms is independent of the other. A screenshot of a sample alert when interventions from both trials fire simultaneously is provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Ancillary and posttrial interventions {34}

Following trial completion, clinical leadership at Northwestern Medicine will determine whether to reinstate the EHR nudges based upon the results of the trial. There will be no restrictions on posttrial use of the trial interventions at Northwestern Medicine.

Outcomes {16}

The primary outcome is the change in the mean encounter-level Z-drug pill count at eligible encounters from 18 months prior to the intervention period to 18 months after the intervention period begins. Z-drug pill counts will be transformed to be dose equivalent, such that one 10-mg pill is considered equivalent to two 5-mg pills. Lowering prescribed pill counts can reduce treatment duration and subsequent adverse events, as dependence occurs rapidly [51].

Secondary outcomes include the number of patients who are referred to CBT-I, the mean Z-drug pill count per clinician month, the proportion of encounters in which patients are prescribed more than 35 Z-drug pills for use within a 5-week period, and the mean dose-equivalent encounter-level benzodiazepine pill count. A CBT-I referral was created in the Northwestern EHR for this trial (Supplementary Material 2), and this outcome will be measured from the start of the intervention period to 18 months after the intervention period begins. As with the primary outcome, the Z-drug pill count per clinician month, Z-drug treatment episode duration, and benzodiazepine prescription outcomes will be measured from 18 months prior to the intervention period to 18 months after the intervention period begins. The encounter-level Z-drug pill count and CBT-I referral outcomes will also be evaluated separately for subgroups of patients with greater than 180 days and 180 or fewer days of Z-drugs prescribed in the last year. All primary and secondary outcomes will be re-evaluated after a 12-month follow-up period, a total of 30 months after the intervention starts.

Harms {17}

Harms, including hospitalization or emergency department visits due to Z-drug withdrawal, new and refill benzodiazepine prescriptions, and sleep-related clinician-reported adverse events, will be monitored in interim analyses conducted every 6 months for the Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB). While the expectedness of adverse events is low, each identified hospitalization or emergency department visit due to Z-drug withdrawal will be investigated. For these cases, a manual physician chart review will be performed by trial investigator J. A. L. to examine the expectedness of the event, the likelihood that the safety event was study related, and the severity of the event. If additional information is required, study personnel will conduct an interview with the clinicians who treated the patient experiencing the adverse event. A case report form will be created and presented in a de-identified fashion to the trial principal investigator, J. N. D. Details about the reporting of adverse events and unanticipated problems to the DSMB, USC IRB, and NHLBI can be found in the Data Safety Monitoring Plan in Supplementary Material 5.

Participant timeline {18}

Eligible participants received an email on November 4, 2024, informing clinicians that they will be enrolled in a research study to reduce unnecessary Z-drug prescribing. The email also contained a link to the baseline survey and education module (Supplementary Material 1). Three reminder emails were sent to clinicians who did not complete the survey and education module unless they opted out of emails. The survey and education module remained accessible to clinicians until November 22, 2024. At the end of the 18-month intervention period, enrolled clinicians will be emailed a second, optional survey.

Due to a technical challenge, the Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention was activated on November 15, 2024, and the Z-drug Quantity Default intervention was activated on December 20, 2024. The period between November 15 and December 20 will be excluded from study analyses, and the intervention period is considered to begin on December 20, 2024. Both interventions will remain activated during the 18-month intervention period. After the 18-month intervention period is complete on June 20, 2026, the Redirection + Accountable Justification prompts will be deactivated during a 12-month follow-up period; however, the Z-drug Quantity Default will not be returned to pre-intervention values after the intervention period because Northwestern Medicine may be reluctant to return to higher pill defaults that are discordant with guideline recommendations.

Measurement of Z-drug and benzodiazepine orders will occur from the 18 months prior to the intervention period until the end of the follow-up period, while measurement of CBT-I referral orders will begin at the start of the intervention period, because the CBT-I referral did not exist prior to the trial (upple Fig. 2).

Timeline of study activities

Sample size {19}

The sample includes 444 clinicians practicing across 64 clinics at Northwestern Medicine. We assumed an intraclass cluster coefficient of 0.05 and 0.09 for clinic and clinician respectively [33]. Using Northwestern Medicine Z-drug prescribing data from November 15, 2023, to November 15, 2024, we assumed a monthly clinician average of 4.8 Z-drug prescriptions with an average of 43 pills per prescription over the 36-month observation period. Using simulated data generated 1000 times and assuming that prescribed Z-drug pill quantities follow a Poisson distribution, we have 80% (95% CI: 77.57%, 82.63%) power to detect a significant pre- to post-intervention mean reduction of 1.58 pills for both Redirection + Accountable Justification and Z-drug Quantity Default study arms with alpha set to 0.05. As a result, at our assumed parameters, a sample size of 64 clinics with 444 clinicians will provide sufficient power to detect clinically meaningful effects on the primary outcome.

Recruitment {20}

All eligible primary care clinicians at Northwestern Medicine will be automatically enrolled in the study. A waiver of consent was granted for this minimal risk trial to avoid bias and lack of generalizability resulting from the self-selection of clinicians into a behavioral study. Clinicians will not receive any financial incentives for participating in the trial. However, clinicians could receive a US $150 Amazon e-gift card for completion of the baseline survey and education module. Clinicians were required to provide consent in order to participate in the baseline survey and education module.

Assignment of interventions: randomization

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

Randomization and assignment to study arm were conducted by J. N D. using the random package in R, a computerized random number generator that returns true random numbers from random.org [43].

Sequence generation: type of randomization {21b}

Prior to randomization, clinics were stratified into low- and high-prescribing groups using a median split of the proportion of encounters in which a Z-drug was prescribed between October 15, 2023, and October 14, 2024. Within each stratum, clinics were allocated to one of the four study arms using permuted block randomization with a block size of 32 and a fixed allocation quota of eight clinics per arm.

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

Arm assignment was not shared with enrolled clinicians until the education module was distributed. The education module described the EHR changes that would occur, but there was no explicit description of how the intervention differed for other clinicians.

Implementation {23}

After randomization was completed by J. N. D., allocation was shared with Northwestern Medicine’s Epic EHR analyst team. The Epic analyst team programmed the Redirection + Accountable Justification and Z-drug Quantity Default interventions so that each enrolled clinician received the interventions associated with their clinic’s assigned study arm.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

Clinicians will not be blinded to intervention assignment as the intervention requires identifiable changes to the clinician’s EHR. Trial investigators, except for the trial data analyst (J. Y. L.), are blinded to clinic and clinician assignment during the conduct of the trial; however, blinding will be lifted after trial completion during data analysis. The trial data analyst and the Epic analyst team, responsible for programming the EHR interventions, will remain unblinded throughout the trial.

How blinding will be achieved {24b}

Clinic identifiers are masked by the trial data analyst (J. Y. L.) before providing trial investigators with clinics for randomization. During interim data analyses for the DSMB, the trial data analyst will mask allocation before sharing DSMB reports.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

The trial data analyst will share unmasked summary reports with the DSMB at their request.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {25a}

Data for all primary and secondary outcomes, including orders for Z-drugs, CBT-I referrals, and benzodiazepines, will be captured by the EHR and stored in the Northwestern Medicine Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW). Additional exploratory outcomes are collected from the clinician survey administered at baseline and at the end of the 18-month intervention period. Survey data will be collected on Qualtrics. The survey assesses details of the clinician’s specialty, years of practice, attitudes and experiences toward quality improvement, familiarity with clinical guidelines, EHR satisfaction, and job satisfaction, as well as validated scales assessing perspective taking and empathy, psychological reactance, and resistance to persuasion [52–54]. The questions included in the baseline survey can be viewed in Supplementary Material 1.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {25b}

Outcomes related to clinician prescribing behavior will be recorded by the EHR throughout the course of the trial and will be retained in the trial dataset including data from clinicians who request to discontinue prompts or modify their Z-drug pill quantity default. Clinicians with concerns about the study interventions will be given an opportunity to review them with a study investigator and to have the prompts discontinued by request. Numbers and proportions of clinicians discontinuing prompts and preference list Z-drug default modification will be reported for each study arm.

Data management {26}

The EHR will record encounter-level, patient-level, and clinician-level data for each qualifying encounter. Electronic data capture by the EHR is considered reliable and valid. EHR-captured data will be securely stored in the Northwestern Medicine EDW, and a complete study dataset will be created by trial data analysts after the completion of the trial.

Confidentiality {33}

To prevent unauthorized access or accidental loss of data, all data will remain at Northwestern Medicine for data analysis. After analyses are completed, patient identifiers will be destroyed, and a limited dataset will be stored indefinitely at Northwestern Medicine.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

We evaluate main effects of the Z-drug Quantity Default and Redirection + Accountable Justification arms in our statistical model as well as their interaction in the combined arm. Hierarchical regression models with a knot at the intervention start date will model trajectories of the primary outcome starting 18 months prior to the intervention period and ending 18 months after the intervention period begins. The model for the primary outcome consists of a mixed-effects two-part zero-inflated Poisson model; analysis will occur at the encounter level. To evaluate the presence of a Z-drug order, a logit distribution will be fit to model the likelihood of an encounter consisting of a Z-drug order (vs. no Z-drug order). Among encounters with Z-drug orders, a Poisson distribution will be used to estimate the mean prescribed pill count. Randomized intervention arm will be the primary independent variable in both the logit and Poisson pieces of the analytic model using binary indicator terms for the Z-drug Quantity Default and Redirection + Accountable Justification arms; a covariate for post- vs. pre-intervention period will estimate the post-intervention change in outcome. The model includes covariates that control for the randomization stratification covariate (low- vs. high-prescribing clinic) and the patient’s prescribed Z-drug pill count in the year prior to the start of the intervention period. Interaction terms of arm by pre-/post-period will test if the post-intervention change differs among intervention arms. All models will include random intercept effects for the primary care clinic and clinician to account for nonindependence of observations within clusters and a potential imbalance in time-invariant clinic and clinician characteristics. All analyses will use two-tailed tests and an alpha of 0.05; exponentiated coefficients of prescribed pill counts will be reported with 95% confidence intervals, using the No Intervention arm as the comparator. A correction for multiple hypothesis testing will not be applied.

Secondary outcomes will be evaluated using a similar methodology. Two-part models with a logit and Poisson distribution will be fit for the benzodiazepine pill count outcome, a logit distribution will be used to assess the proportion of encounters in which patients are prescribed more than 35 Z-drug pills for use within a 5-week period, and a Poisson distribution will be used to assess the number of patients receiving a CBT-I referral and the mean Z-drug pill count per clinician month.

A follow-up analysis will be conducted on all outcome measures 12 months after the intervention period has completed to measure persistence of effects after the Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention is turned off. The follow-up analysis will compare the Redirection + Accountable Justification arm with the No Intervention control arm starting 18 months prior to the intervention period and ending 30 months after the intervention period begins (at the end of the follow-up period).

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

All analyses will be performed in an “intention-to-treat” fashion, such that all enrolled clinicians, including protocol non-adherers, will be included and analyzed as randomized regardless of their intervention exposure.

How missing data will be handled in the analysis {27c}

We do not anticipate any missing data of primary and secondary outcomes due to robust electronic data capture in the EHR. As a result, imputation will not be performed.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {27d}

Subgroup analyses will be conducted among short-term and long-term Z-drug users for the primary Z-drug pill count outcome and the CBT-I referral outcome. Consistent with existing research, short-term users are considered patients who have been prescribed 180 or fewer days of Z-drugs in the 365 days prior to the relevant encounter, while long-term users are considered patients who have been prescribed more than 180 days of Z-drugs in the 365 days prior to the relevant encounter. These definitions align with Redirection + Accountable Justification intervention, in which clinicians are prompted to remove Z-drug orders for short-term users but not for long-term users due to the risk of withdrawal symptoms. Additional exploratory subgroup analyses will also examine the effect of the interventions on primary and secondary outcomes among patients ≥ 75 years of age and patients who have been prescribed an opioid.

Interim analyses {28}

Interim analyses will be conducted by the trial data analyst (JYL) and shared with the DSMB in blinded summary reports for ongoing review in 6-month intervals during the intervention period. The interim analyses will consist of safety measures, including hospitalization or emergency department visits due to Z-drug withdrawal, new benzodiazepine prescriptions, and clinician-reported adverse events. Interim analyses will only be used for safety monitoring and reporting of ongoing trial conduct and will not be used to assess intervention effectiveness. Following each summary report, the DSMB will make recommendations to the trial investigators and USC IRB as to whether the study is approved to continue as planned, any requests for additional data, or if changes to the trial protocol or statistical analysis plan will be necessary. If necessary, the trial investigators will then submit revisions to the DSMB, USC IRB, and the NHLBI.

Protocol and statistical analysis plan {5}

The full trial protocol, clinician-encounter level EHR data, and statistical code needed to reproduce the analysis can be shared with researchers as part of a HIPAA-compliant limited dataset under a signed data use agreement for 1 year following trial completion. The complete statistical analysis plan can be viewed in Supplementary Material 4.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {3d}

Discussion

The guideline recommendations for managing insomnia are consistent and clear: CBT-I, not pharmacological therapy, should be the first-line treatment for insomnia [8–13, 55–57]. Moreover, if prescribed, Z-drugs should not be used for longer than 5 weeks [8, 17]. Yet, in practice, Z-drugs remain the preferred initial treatment option for many clinicians [58], and treatment durations commonly exceed what is recommended as safe and effective [8]. These practices expose patients to unnecessary risks of falling, confusion, parasomnias, and dependence, necessitating new solutions that align the management of insomnia with best practice.

This cluster-randomized trial will evaluate the effectiveness of two behavioral interventions embedded in the EHR that are designed to nudge primary care clinicians to refer patients to CBT-I and reduce Z-drug prescriptions. The behavioral interventions, consisting of a modified pill quantity default and a just-in-time alert that prompts guideline concordant care or requires a clinical justification, exert influence over clinicians’ decision-making using principles of behavioral economics and social psychology, including reputational concerns, implicit recommendations, and the anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic [42, 46, 50]. While the EHR nudges tested in this trial have independently proven effective at reducing inappropriate antibiotic and opioid prescribing [33–35], they have not yet been examined in the context of insomnia treatment, nor have their effects been evaluated in combination with each other.

The causes of guideline discordant insomnia treatment are multi-faceted, and this intervention does not intend to address them all. System-level barriers, including the limited availability of CBT-I providers and duration of primary care visits, create notable barriers to CBT-I access and encourage “quick fixes,” including pharmacological treatments [14]. Although digital and self-guided CBT-I resources are available and effective [59, 60], they can require more self-discipline, lack some CBT-I components, and may be inaccessible to older adults who prefer traditional treatment modalities. Patient-level barriers, which include lack of knowledge of non-pharmaceutical options, reluctance to engage with CBT-I, and trivialization of insomnia symptoms, can also result in, respectively, demand for Z-drugs, non-compliance with nonpharmacological therapies, and underdiagnosis or undertreatment [14]. The interventions being evaluated in this study are therefore one piece of the puzzle in improving the effectiveness and safety of insomnia management.

Conclusion

The SOMNUS trial represents an innovative approach to improving insomnia management by using behavioral insights to align clinical practices with established treatment guidelines. By embedding EHR nudges to reduce Z-drug prescriptions and encourage CBT-I referrals, the study intends to address inappropriate prescribing that exposes patients to unnecessary risks. The trial’s outcomes have the potential to influence policy and practice on a broader scale, paving the way for guideline-concordant insomnia therapy that prioritizes treatment effectiveness and patient safety.
