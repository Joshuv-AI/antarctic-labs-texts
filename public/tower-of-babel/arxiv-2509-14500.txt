The whys and hows of conditioning of DG plane wave Trefftz methods: a single element. Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Joseph Coyle, Nilima Nigam

Abstract
Plane-wave Trefftz methods (PWB) for the Helmholtz equation offer significant advantages over standard discretization approaches whose implementation employs more general polynomial basis functions. A disadvantage of these methods is the poor conditioning of the system matrices. In the present paper, we carefully examine the conditioning of the plane-wave discontinuous Galerkin method with reference to a single element. The properties of the mass and stiffness matrices depend on the size and geometry of the element. We study the mass and system matrices arising from a PWB on a single disk-shaped element. We then examine some preconditioning strategies, and present results showing their behaviour with three different criteria: conditioning, the behaviour of GMRES residuals, and impact on the L 2 L^{2} -error.

Introduction

Finite element approaches based on Trefftz methods – where the approximation basis is built from solutions of the differential operator being studied – are well-established. They offer many advantages including flexibility in terms of tesselating domains, their theoretical convergence rates and potential for dimension reduction. Unfortunately, it has also been observed that the Trefftz methods for the Helmholtz operator lead to severely ill-conditioned matrices. The theoretically-predicted rates of convergence may not be observed in practice. It is our goal in this paper to study these issues within the context of a
single
spatial element.

We recall from
[

It is well-documented, see for example

Our goal in this paper is to provide an analysis of the conditioning of these local matrices relative to the mesh size

To motivate this, we observe that if we employ a plane-wave basis, then we do not have the same notion of a

However, we observe that the

of the physical element can be quite general. Consequently, we begin our presentation with a simple shape for

the disk of radius

As we shall see going forward, the conditioning also depends in a slightly less intuitive way on the geometry of the element.
The related analysis leads to a natural progression first to regular, then cyclic, and finally general polygons. We provide some numerical experiments which highlight the theoretical results, and then suggest some preconditioning strategies.

Notation and background

We first set notation, and review some results which will be used frequently. Throughout we consider
p
plane wave directions of unit length given by

\mathbf{d}_{m}:=\langle\cos(\theta_{m}),\sin(\theta_{m})\rangle;\qquad\theta_{m}:=\frac{2\pi}{p}m,\quad m=0,...,p-1

(13)

and a point
\mathbf{x}(t)
in the
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
plane is represented as

\mathbf{x}(t)=|\mathbf{x}(t)|\left\langle\cos(t),\sin(t)\right\rangle.

(14)

Noting that

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{d}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}(t)|(\cos(t)\cos(\theta_{m})+\sin(t)\sin(\theta_{m}))=|\mathbf{x}(t)|\cos(\theta_{m}-t),

we can then write

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot(\mathbf{d}_{m}-\mathbf{d}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2|\mathbf{x}(t)|\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{m}-\theta_{\ell}}{2}\right)\sin\left(t-\frac{\theta_{m}+\theta_{\ell}}{2}\right).

(15)

Defining

a(n):=2\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}\right)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad b(n,t):=\sin\left(t-\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}\right),

(16)

leads to

\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot(\mathbf{d}_{m}-\mathbf{d}_{\ell})=|\mathbf{x}(t)|a(m-\ell)b(m+\ell,t).

(17)

Using properties of the sine function, we get

\displaystyle a(m-\ell)=-a(\ell-m),

\displaystyle\quad a(x\pm p)=-a(x),

(18)

and

\displaystyle b(x\pm p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-b(x,t).

(19)

We will also take advantage of the fact that if
{\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}}=\langle\cos(t),\sin(t)\rangle
is a unit vector then

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi_{m}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}}=i\kappa|\mathbf{x}|\varphi_{m}(\mathbf{d}_{m}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}})=i\kappa h\varphi_{m}\cos(\theta_{m}-t).

(20)

2.1
Identities involving Bessel functions

The Bessel function of the first kind and of order
n
,
J_{n}(x)
, will appear frequently in the subsequent sections. We record some important facts which can be found in, for example,
[
14
,
21
]
, or readily derived:

•

The Fourier expansion of the function
e^{ix\sin(t)}
can be expressed via the Jacobi-Anger expansion:

e^{ix\sin(t)}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(x)e^{int}.

Consequently, the Bessel functions of the first kind
J_{n}(t)
are the Fourier coefficients of a periodic and analytic function, and must decay rapidly with
n
.

For integer
n
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}},
the Jacobi-Anger expansion provides an identity we use frequently:

\displaystyle J_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{ix\sin(t)-int}\,dt=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(x\sin(t)-nt)\,dt.

(21)

•

The Bessel functions satisfy, for integer
n
,
J_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}J_{-n}(x)
as well as
J_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}J_{n}(-x)
.

•

We shall encounter integrals of the form

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{iA\sin(t-\beta_{1})}\cos(qt-\beta_{2})dt

where
A,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
are fixed and
q
is an integer. Simple manipulations yield

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{iA\sin(t-\beta_{1})}\cos(qt-\beta_{2})dt=\begin{cases}2\pi J_{q}(A)\cos(q\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}),&q\,\,\mbox{even}\\
&\\
2\pi iJ_{q}(A)\sin(q\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}),&q\,\,\mbox{odd}\end{cases}.

(22)

We also recall that for nonnegative
n
,

J_{n}(z)\approx\frac{1}{\Gamma(n+1)}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{n},\quad\text{when}\,\,0<z\ll\sqrt{n+1}.

(23)

Finally, we shall need the following identity in Section 3.

Lemma 1
.

Let
B>0
be constant. For
M,L\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

I_{1}(M,L):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2M}\left(B\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right)\,e^{-iLx}\,dx=\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2L}\left(B\sin(t)\right)\,e^{-i2Mt}\,dt.

(24)

Proof.

We easily see that
I_{1}(M,L)
is real, by exploiting the even behaviour of
J_{2M}
. Next, for fixed integers
M,L
, we calculate, using (
21
)

\displaystyle I_{1}(2M,L)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2M}\left(B\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right)\,e^{iLx}\,dx=2\int_{0}^{\pi}J_{2M}(B\sin(s))\,e^{i2Ls}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2M}(B\sin(s))\,e^{i2Ls}\,ds=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{iB\sin(s)\sin(t)-i2Mt-2Ls}\,dt\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2L}\left(B\sin(t)\right)\,e^{-i2Mt}\,dt.

That is,
I_{1}
is the (scaled)
2M
Fourier (cosine) coefficient of
J_{2L}(B\sin(t)).

2.2
Properties of circulant and Toeplitz matrices

Throughout, if
\mathtt{A}
is an
n\times n
matrix with
n
eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(\mathtt{A}),i=0,1,...,n-1
, we denote

\Lambda(\mathtt{A}):=\mathtt{diag}[\lambda_{0}(\mathtt{A}),\lambda_{1}(\mathtt{A}),..,\lambda_{n-1}(\mathtt{A})].

(25)

If the context is clear, we will suppress the argument in
\lambda_{i}(\tt A)
.

We enumerate well-known properties of Toeplitz and circulant matrices, see for example,
[
9
]
. Consider the
n\times n
matrices of form

\mathtt{T}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}t_{0}&t_{1}&t_{2}&\cdots&t_{n-1}\\
t_{-1}&t_{0}&t_{1}&\cdots&t_{n-2}\\
t_{-2}&t_{-1}&t_{0}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&t_{1}\\
t_{-(n-1)}&t_{-(n-2)}&\cdots&t_{-1}&t_{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}\hskip-2.8903pt,\mathtt{C}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
c_{n-2}&c_{n-1}&c_{0}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}&c_{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

The matrix
\mathtt{T}_{n}
with constant diagonals is a Toeplitz matrix, and
\mathtt{C}_{n}
is a special form of the Toeplitz matrix in which each row is a right cyclic shift of the row above. Circulant matrices are uniquely specified by their first row and oftentimes denoted by
\mathtt{C}_{n}=\mathtt{circ}[c_{0},c_{1},...,c_{n-1}].

Multiplication by a circulant matrix is equivalent to a circular convolution. It should be noted that an
n\times n
matrix
\mathtt{C}
is circulant if and only if

[\mathtt{C}]_{m,\ell}=[\mathtt{C}]_{i,j}\mbox{ when }i-j=(m-\ell)\mod{n}.

(26)

The eigenvalues of a circulant matrix
\mathtt{C}={\rm circ}[c_{0},c_{1},...,c_{n-1}]
are given by

\lambda_{q}(\mathtt{C})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{j}e^{-ij\theta_{q}},\qquad q=0,1,...,n-1,\qquad\theta_{q}:=\frac{2\pi}{n}q,

(27)

and the associated eigenvectors are

v_{q}=[1,e^{-i\theta_{q}},...,e^{-i(n-1)\theta_{q}}]^{\sf T},\qquad q=0,1,...,n-1.

(28)

We denote by
\mathtt{U}
the
n\times n
unitary matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors
v_{q}
above:

\mathtt{U}:=[v_{0}|v_{1}|...|v_{n-1}].

(29)

If
c_{j}=f\left(\theta_{j}\right)
for a continuous periodic
generating function

f:[0,2\pi]\rightarrow[0,2\pi]
then

\displaystyle\lambda_{q}(\mathtt{C})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f(\theta_{j})e^{-iq\theta_{j}}\approx\frac{n}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x)e^{-iqx}\,dx,\qquad|q|\leq n-1.

(30)

The sum above can be interpreted in terms of a quadrature rule, in which case the integral term defines the Fourier transform of
f
up to
n-1
. The quadrature is exact if
f\in{\rm span}\left\{e^{ijx}:\,|j|\leq p-1\right\}.

Several of the matrices we discuss in this paper are not only circulant, but also real. We refer elsewhere (e.g.,
[
20
]
) for a detailed exposition of the properties of such matrices, and record some facts we use.
The
n\times n
matrix
{\tt C}
is real circulant if and only if

\lambda_{0}(\mathtt{C})\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{q}(\mathtt{C})=\bar{\lambda}_{q-n}(\mathtt{C}),\quad q=1,2,...,n-1.

(31)

If the
p\times p
matrix
\mathtt{C}
is real, circulant and symmetric, then all the eigenvalues are real. Moreover, from (
31
) we see that several eigenvalues have multiplicity. In fact, we see
[
20
]

•

If
n
is odd, then
\lambda_{0}(\mathtt{C})
has odd multiplicity, and all other eigenvalues have even multiplicity

•

If
n
is even, either
\mathtt{C}
has precisely two eigenvalues of odd multiplicity, or none. All others eigenvalues have even multiplicity.

Any circulant matrix admits the decomposition

\mathtt{C}:=\mathtt{U}^{*}\mathtt{\Lambda}\mathtt{U}

(32)

where
\mathtt{U}
is the unitary matrix defined in (
29
), and

\mathtt{\Lambda}=\mathtt{diag}[\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n-1}]
.
Application of circulant matrices is therefore fast (via the FFT). Matrix inversion is also fast,

\mathtt{C}^{-1}=\mathtt{U}^{*}{\mathtt{\Lambda}^{-1}}\mathtt{U}.

(33)

3
The behaviour of the PWB on a disk

To develop some understanding of the theoretical issues around the conditioning of the mass, cross, and stiffness matrices on general polygons, we first examine the situation of a single element
K
which is a disk of radius
h
centered at the origin. As mentioned in the Introduction, the PWB behaves differently from traditional polynomial basis methods, in that affine-mapped physical elements do not map the PWB to another one. Nonetheless, the size of the physical element plays an important role in the conditioning of the PWB. Motivated by these facts, we first examine the behaviour of the PWB on an element
K
which is a
disk.
This allows us to obtain a quantitative description of the impact of size as well as wave number on an isotropic physical element. These results can then be used to provide insight into the impact of both the mesh size and the number of plane wave basis functions on the conditioning of a more general-shaped physical element. A similar analysis was conducted in Section 2 of
[
17
]
, with a focus on the mass matrix on the disk.

Let
K
denote a disk centered at the origin with radius
h
, and consider a PWB centered at
\mathbf{x}_{K}=(0,0)
with equally-spaced planewave directions. If
\mathbf{x}\in\partial K
then
|\mathbf{x}|=h
and employing (
17
) we obtain

\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{d}_{m}-\mathbf{d}_{\ell})=ha(\ell-m)b(m+\ell,t).

(34)

We first arrive at closed-form expressions of the matrix entries (the generating functions) in terms of Bessel functions

Theorem 2
.

Let
K
be a disk of radius
h
centered at the origin. For
p
evenly spaced plane waves
the general entries of the mass, cross, and stiffness matrices are, for
\ell=1,...,p,

\displaystyle[\mathtt{M}^{disk}]_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi hJ_{0}(\kappa ha(m-\ell)),

(35)

\displaystyle[\mathtt{S}^{disk}]_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\kappa h^{2}a(m-\ell)J_{1}(\kappa ha(m-\ell)),

(36)

and

\displaystyle[\mathtt{D}^{disk}]_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi\kappa^{2}h^{3}J_{0}(\kappa ha(m-\ell))\cos\left(\frac{2(m-\ell)}{p}\pi\right)

(37)

\displaystyle+\,\pi\kappa^{2}h^{3}J_{2}(\kappa ha(m-\ell)),

respectively.

These expressions will lead to the generating functions of these matrices.

Proof.

To establish (
35
), we employ (
34
) and the defnintion of
b
given in (
16
) to get

\displaystyle[\mathtt{M}^{disk}]_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\iota\kappa h(\mathbf{d}_{m}-\mathbf{d}_{\ell})\cdot\mathbf{x}}hdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\iota\kappa ha(m-\ell)b(m+\ell,t)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\iota\kappa ha(m-\ell)\sin\left(t-\frac{\pi(m+\ell)}{p}\right)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int_{-\frac{\pi(m+\ell)}{p}}^{2\pi-\frac{\pi(m+\ell)}{p}}e^{\iota\kappa ha(m-\ell)\sin(t)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi hJ_{0}(\kappa ha(m-\ell)),

where we have used the integral representation in (
21
). Similarly, for the cross matrix we use (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}p\kappa^{2}h^{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{2L+2}(2\kappa h\sin(t))+J_{2L-2}(2\kappa h\sin(t))\,\,dt,

leading to

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}(\mathtt{S}^{disk})\approx\frac{1}{4}p\kappa^{2}h^{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}2J_{2L}(2\kappa h\sin(t))\cos(2t)+J_{2L+2}(2\kappa h\sin(t))+J_{2L-2}(2\kappa h\sin(t))\,ds,

for
\,\,L=1,...,\left\lfloor\frac{p-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Finally if
p
is even

\displaystyle\lambda_{p/2}(\mathtt{S}^{disk})\approx\frac{1}{4}p\kappa^{2}h^{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}2J_{p}(2\kappa h\sin(t))\cos(2t)+[J_{p+2}(2\kappa\sin(t))+J_{p-2}(2\kappa h\sin(t))]\,dt,

proving the theorem.

One could use recurrence relations to rewrite the expressions in terms of derivatives of Bessel functions.

As the number of plane waves
p
grows, the spectral condition number of the stiffness matrix will grow as

{\tt Cond}(\mathtt{D}^{disk})=C(\lambda_{p/2})^{-1}\approx C(p+2)!(\kappa h)^{-2-p}.

PWB on polygons

The mass and systems matrices on the disk are circulant, and therefore their properties could be described analytically. We next study the behaviour of the mass matrix on cyclic polygons, and notice that they are close to Toeplitz. This suggests the use of circulant preconditioners. In this section we identify two simple-to-construct circulant matrices which are close to the mass matrix on a polygon.

5
Preconditioners and condition numbers

In previous sections, we established that the mass, stiffness and cross matrices
\mathtt{M}^{K},\mathtt{S}^{K},\mathtt{D}^{K}
for the plane-wave basis are
necessarily
ill-conditioned as the size of the system increases. To address this, one typically constructs a preconditioner for the system matrix
\mathtt{SyS}^{K}
. The
choice
of preconditioner is dictated by the source of ill-conditioning, as well as the iterative method one will use to solve the linear system. A good preconditioner is one which is efficient to implement and improves accuracy.

We document in Figure
8
the dependance of the condition number of
SyS
on both element size
h
and wavenumber
\kappa
. This is consistent with the behaviour of the condition number of the system matrix on the entire mesh in
Figure 1
(c), where the conditioning rapidly deteriorates with the number of plane-waves.

Figure 8:
Dependance of condition numbers of the full system matrix, on element size h and frequency
\kappa
. As the number of plane waves increases, the conditioning deteriorates. Computations on an equilateral triangle with vertices on disk of radius
h
, equispaced PWB (left).

In the concrete case of the PWB, we present several choices of preconditioners. Each of these is based on the properties of the PWB, either on the polygonal element
K,
or a disk of similar diameter. Consequently, each of these preconditioners (apart from the regularized ones) will
also
be poorly conditioned. Nonetheless, as we shall see, some dramatically improve the conditioning of the system matrix, while others have good properties when used as part of a direct solve or iterative strategy such as GMRES.

In previous work on a mesh (instead of a single element)
[
6
]
, a modified Gram Schmidt method is used to construct an orthogonal change of basis, leading to a better conditioned system matrix. Concretely, if
\mathtt{M}
denotes the mass matrix on the whole mesh, then

\mathtt{M}={\tt Q}\tilde{\mathtt{M}}{\tt Q}^{*},
where
{\tt Q}
is obtained from a QR decomposition of
(\mathtt{M}+\mathtt{M}^{*})
. The matrix
\tilde{\mathtt{M}}
is then somewhat better conditioned, and to solve
{\tt Mx=f}
we instead solve

{\tt\tilde{M}y}={\tt Q}^{*}{\tt f},{\tt x=Qy}.

Another related approach would be to start with the spectral decomposition of the mass matrix on a single element:

{\tt\mathtt{M}^{K}=X\Lambda({\mathtt{M}^{K}})X^{*}},

and to use
\Lambda({\mathtt{M}^{K}})^{-1}
as a preconditioner to solve

\Lambda({\mathtt{M}^{K}})^{-1}\mathtt{SyS}^{K}{\tt x}=\Lambda(\mathtt{M}^{K})^{-1}{\tt f}.

While the columns of
{\tt X}
are orthogonal (eigenvectors), this approach has two problems. First, we would need to compute the eigendecomposition of the matrix
\mathtt{M}^{K}
- this preconditioner would not be efficient in practice. Second, the
\Lambda(\mathtt{M}^{K})
is severely ill-conditioned as
p
increases.

A compromise based on the observations of the previous sections, is to exploit the ‘nearly Toeplitz’ nature of the mass matrix as the number of plane-waves
p
increases. In the previous section, we noted that the mass, cross, and stiffness matrices associated with polygonal elements were neither Toeplitz nor circulant. Nonetheless, as the number of sides increases, these matrices tended towards a Toeplitz behaviour. It has been well-documented
[
5
,
20
,
19
,
9
]
that circulant preconditioners are very effective for Toeplitz matrices. An important criterion for the selection of a preconditioner is its ease of application. To this end, we focus on circulant preconditioners in three categories:

1.

Disk-based.
These strategies are based on the observation that on the disk of radius
h
, the same as the radius of the circumcircle of polygonal element
K
, matrices
\mathtt{M}^{disk},\mathtt{S}^{disk},
and
\mathtt{D}^{disk}
are circulant, Theorem
3
. On a polygon, the matrices are
close
to circulant, though note the eigenvectors of
\mathtt{SyS}
will not be the same as those on the disk.
For this class of preconditioners, we can use the exact expressions for the mass matrix
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
or the system matrix
\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}
on a disk of radius
h
, derived in Section 3; their eigenvalues are efficiently computed using a DFT of their first row. In other words, the eigenvalue decompositions

\mathtt{M}^{disk}=\mathtt{U}\Lambda({\mathtt{M}^{disk}})\mathtt{U}^{*},\qquad\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}=\mathtt{U}\Lambda({\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}})\mathtt{U}^{*}

can be computed very efficiently, and preconditioners based on these can be applied fast.

2.

Polygon-based.
In this approach, we directly work with the mass and system matrices
\mathtt{M}^{K},\mathtt{SyS}^{K}
on the physical element
K
itself. We know (from Section 4) that these matrices are neither Toeplitz nor circulant. Nonetheless, we can
build
circulant matrices from these matrices from their first rows, as in Section 4. Once again, the application of such preconditioners will be fast.

We denote by
\mathtt{C}^{K}_{R}
the circulant matrix constructed by using the first row of
\mathtt{M}^{K}
respectively. Recall also that
\mathtt{C}^{K}_{best}
is a circulant matrix generated from the average value of the diagonals of the mass matrix
\mathtt{M}^{K}
, (see (
52
))

\mathtt{C}_{best}^{K}:=\text{circ}[c_{0},c_{1},...c_{p-1}],\quad c_{i-j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{p-q=i-j(modn)}[\mathtt{M}^{K}]_{pq}.

(62)

3.

Regularization-based.

As we have seen previously, the system matrix
\mathtt{SyS}
becomes severely ill-conditioned with many eigenvalues of extremely small magnitude. A regularization - explicitly setting eigenvalues smaller than a threshold to 0 - could be a viable preconditioning strategy for such matrices. Such ideas have been fruitfully applied to the solution of ill-posed Toeplitz problems,
[
5
,
8
]
.
However, to avoid an eigendecomposition of
\mathtt{M}^{K}
, we use instead the (easily computed) eigenvalues of
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
, corresponding to an element with similar size as
K
.

In order to solve
{\tt Ax}={\tt f}
, suppose
{\tt A}
is close to a circulant matrix
{\tt Q}=\mathtt{U}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{\delta}\end{bmatrix}\mathtt{U}^{*}
where
\Lambda_{1}
is a diagonal matrix with entries of magnitude
\geq\delta>0
and
\Lambda_{\delta}
is diagonal with entries smaller than this regularization parameter
\delta
. In this case, one can define either

{\tt Q}^{regularized}:=\mathtt{U}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\mathtt{U}^{*}
and use its inverse as a preconditioner, or one can directly use the singular matrix
{\tt Q}^{\dagger}:=\mathtt{U}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&{\tt Z}\end{bmatrix}\mathtt{U}^{*}
where
{\tt Z}
is a zero matrix.

In the concrete case of the Trefftz methods, we choose
{\tt Q}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{disk})^{1/2}
, whose eigenvalues are readily computed.

To compare the performance of these preconditioners, we shall fix the element shape
K=
an equilateral triangle,
h=0.1,
the radius of its circumcircle, and
\kappa=0.2\pi
, unless otherwise specified. The condition numbers of the preconditioned system will be compared.
The idea we intend to exploit is that as the number of plane-waves becomes large, the deviation of the mass matrix
\mathtt{M}^{reg}
from the circulant matrix
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
becomes small, as in Figures
5
and
6
. We investigate the behaviour of several preconditioners for the matrix
{\tt SyS}
defined in (
12
), with the eventual goal of solving
{\tt SySx}={\tt f}
.

We anticipate square-root type preconditioners
\mathtt{P}
which approximate
\mathtt{SyS}^{-1/2}
will outperform preconditioners which approximate
\mathtt{SyS}^{-1}
while solving linear systems, since they will be better conditioned; the singular values of
\mathtt{SyS}
include both very small and very large values as the number of plane waves increases.

To test this, we solve
\mathtt{SyS}{\tt x=f}
using the polygon-based circulant matrix
\kappa^{2}\mathtt{C}^{K}_{{\tt R},\mathtt{M}}
as a preconditioner. That is, to solve
{\tt SyS}^{K}{\tt x}={\tt f}
, we solve instead

(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{disk})^{-1}{\tt SyS^{K}{x}}=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{disk})^{-1}{\tt{f}}
(left preconditioning). We compare the results with using the square root of this matrix,
(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{disk})^{1/2}
, instead. From Figure
9
we clearly see that this preconditioner leads to well-conditioned system matrices in comparison with the ‘square-root’ preconditioners
\mathtt{P}_{1},\mathtt{P}_{3},\mathtt{P}_{5}
which approximate
({\mathtt{M}^{K}})^{-1/2}
. However, the
L^{2}
-errors in solving a point-source scattering PDE are substantially worse using this preconditioner.

Based on these results, in the remainder we focus our attention on 7 preconditioning matrices
\mathtt{P}_{1}-\mathtt{P}_{7}
of square-root type, and their performance as left, two-sided, or right preconditioners.
We group these into three broad categories as before.

•

Disk-based preconditioners.

Consider a disk with the same center and radius as the circumcircle of the element
K
. We use the (square root of)
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
or
\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}
as preconditioners. Concretely, define

\mathtt{P}_{1}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{disk})^{-1/2}=\kappa\mathtt{U}\Lambda({\mathtt{M}^{disk}})^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*},\qquad\mathtt{P}_{2}:=\mathtt{U}(\Lambda({\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}}))^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*}

(63)

•

Polygon-based preconditioners.

We can generate circulant matrices from the first row of either
\kappa^{2}\mathtt{M}^{K}
or
\mathtt{SyS}^{K}
, and use these as preconditioners to define

\mathtt{P}_{3}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{C}^{K}_{{\tt R},\mathtt{M}})^{-1/2}=\mathtt{U}(\kappa^{2}\Lambda({\mathtt{C}^{K}_{{\tt R},\mathtt{M}}}))^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*},\qquad\mathtt{P}_{4}:=(\mathtt{C}^{K}_{{\tt R},\mathtt{SyS}})^{-1/2}.

(64)

A Hermitian circulant matrix can be generated by an averaging process from
\mathtt{M}^{K}
in (
52
),

\mathtt{P}_{5}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{C}^{K}_{best})^{-1/2}=\mathtt{U}(\kappa^{2}\Lambda\left(\mathtt{C}^{K}_{best}\right)^{-1/2})\mathtt{U}^{*}.

(65)

•

Regularization-based preconditioners.

A simple regularized preconditioner can be constructed as

\mathtt{P}_{6}:=\kappa\mathtt{U}\,{\tt diag}(\mu_{1},...,\mu_{\delta},1,1,...,1)^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*},\qquad\mu_{i}:=\lambda_{i}(\mathtt{M}^{disk})

The eigenvalues of
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
are easy to compute. In
\mathtt{P}_{6}
we retain the
\mu_{i}
such that
{\mu_{i}}>\delta
in magnitude, and set the others to 1. The matrix
\mathtt{P}_{6}
has smaller norm than that of
\mathtt{P}_{1}
. The tolerance
\delta>0
is defined by the user. This idea is motivated by
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
on preconditioning Toeplitz matrices, who recommend the use of (right) regularization-based preconditioners.

Another form of regularization is the use of a singular preconditioner, motivated by the work of
[
8
]
who studied the behaviour of such preconditioners with GMRES while solving non-self-adjoint ill-posed problems.
We define

\mathtt{P}_{7}:=\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathtt{U}\,{\tt diag}(1/\mu_{1},...,1/\mu_{tol},0,0,...0)^{1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*}.

(66)

Here, again
\mu_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the mass matrix on the disk
\mathtt{M}^{disk}
so that
{\mu_{i}}\geq\delta
in magnitude.

In Figure
10
, we present results on the system condition numbers based on these choices, for
\kappa h=0.01\pi.
As can be seen, the best reduction on condition number is provided by the use of the left-right preconditioners. The preconditioners
\mathtt{P}_{i}
we have chosen are of square-root type, and therefore
\mathtt{P}_{i}\mathtt{SyS}\mathtt{P}_{i}\approx{\tt I}
.

The regularized preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{6},\mathtt{P}_{7}
offer a reduction in condition number which depends on the tolerance
\delta
picked. These preconditioners should be used with care.

6
Preconditioners and
L^{2}
errors

One deploys preconditioners
\mathtt{P}
while using an iterative solver such as GMRES to solve the problem
\mathtt{SyS}^{K}{\tt x}={\tt f}
. One could also use a direct approach (such as
Matlab
’s backslash ) on the preconditioned system. We examine the action of the matrices
\mathtt{P}_{i}
as
left preconditioners
,
two-sided preconditioner
or
right preconditioners
while solving the system
\mathtt{SyS}^{K}{\tt x}={\tt f}
. That is, we solve for
{\tt x}
as

{\mathtt{P}}\mathtt{SyS}^{K}{\tt x}={\tt P}{\tt f},\qquad\mathtt{P}{\tt SyS^{K}}\mathtt{P}{\tt y}=\mathtt{P}{\tt f},\,\,{\tt x}=\mathtt{P}{\tt y},\,\,\text{or}\,\,\qquad{\tt SyS^{K}}\mathtt{P}{\tt y}={\tt f},\,\,{\tt x}=\mathtt{P}{\tt y}.

For convenience we remind the reader of the PDE of interest: find
u\in H^{1}(K)
such that

\displaystyle\Delta u+\kappa^{2}u=0\,\mbox{ in }K,\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath{$n$}}}}+i\kappa u=g_{K}\,\,\mbox{ on }\partial K.

(67)

The associated system matrix on a single element,
\Omega=K
is
\mathtt{SyS}^{K}=(k^{2}\mathtt{M}^{K}+ik(\mathtt{S}^{K}-\bar{\mathtt{S}}^{K})+\mathtt{D}^{K})
.
In order to test ideas, we use
g_{K}
for which the solution
u
is known. We
generate the related
{\tt{f}}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
and solve

{\tt\mathtt{SyS}^{K}\,{x}={f},\hskip 10.4999ptx}=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{p}]

(68)

We can then compare the computed pde solutions
u_{p}:=\sum_{m=1}^{p}x_{m}\phi_{m}
of (
67
) with the exact solution. The measure we track is the relative
L^{2}
-error of the computed solution
u_{p}
as a function of the number of PWB :

E(p):=\left(\int_{K}|u-u_{p}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}/\left(\int_{K}|u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(69)

This allow us to directly study the impact of using the preconditioners
\mathtt{P}_{1}-\mathtt{P}_{7}
with
Matlab
’s GMRES preconditioner. We set
\mathtt{M}_{i}:=\mathtt{P}_{i}^{-1}
for
i=1,...,6
(these inverses are easily computed thanks to their structure), and the function call is then
gmres(
\mathtt{SyS}
, f, restart, tol, maxit,
\mathtt{M}_{i}
)
for left preconditioning. The preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{7}
is
not
invertible, and therefore the call to
Matlab
would be
gmres(
\mathtt{P}_{7}\mathtt{SyS}
, f, restart, tol, maxit)

We address the following questions in this section.

1.

Which preconditioners
\mathtt{P}_{1},...,\mathtt{P}_{7}
work best with a direct solver such as
Matlab
’s backslash?

2.

Which preconditioners work best with GMRES?

3.

How sensitive is our preconditioning strategy to the size of the domain
K
and the wavenumber
\kappa
?

4.

Is there a universally-recommended preconditioning strategy?

6.6
Discussion

Based on the preceding analysis and examples, we summarize:

•

A preconditioner which reduces the Trefftz system condition number may
not
yield better results in terms of the computed pde solution. This is because the preconditioners may themselves be very poorly conditioned: if
\mathtt{P}\mathtt{M}\approx\mathtt{I}
then the conditioning of matrix-vector multiplication
\mathtt{P}\tt{f}
will be poor. For instance, in Figure
10
preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{4}
produces very well-conditioned systems matrices. However, in Figure
11
we see that its performance both as part of a direct solver and for GMRES is not as good as others.

•

For element which are close to cyclic polygons, using evenly-spaced plane-wave directions with center at the element centroid yields accurate results.

•

For physical elements
K
with an extreme aspect ratio, using evenly-spaced plane-wave directions with center at the element centroid provided the best results.

•

Amongst the preconditioners considered, in most experiments
P_{2}:=\mathtt{U}(\Lambda({\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}}))^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*}
, the ’best circulant preconditioner’
\mathtt{P}_{5}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{C}^{K}_{best})^{-1/2}
and the singular preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{7}
improved GMRES accuracy.

•

The preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{5}
is Hermitian, at least for equally-spaced planewaves. The singular preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{7}
is also Hermitian. Neither, therefore, introduce any artificial damping in the system.

•

Left preconditioning as part of a GMRES solve yields more accurate results compared to right preconditioning.

•

In most examples considered,
Matlab
’s direct solver (backslash) on the unpreconditioned system is optimal. The benefits of preconditioning are seen with the iterative solver.

These observations lead us to conjecture that using the circulant matrices
\mathtt{P}_{2},\mathtt{P}_{5},
or the singular matrix
\mathtt{P}_{7}
– all easy to build and efficient to apply– as part of a left block-preconditioning strategy for iterative systems could improve accuracy for plane-wave Trefftz methods on meshes. We explore this in forthcoming work.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we investigate the behaviour of the plane-wave basis on a
single
element. We provide computational evidence that as the number of plane waves increases, the system matrix becomes nearly Toeplitz. The system matrix is exactly circulant for a disk, on which the spectrum can be characterized easily. Prompted by these observations, we propose several circulant preconditioners which are easy to build and apply. These preconditioners can reduce the condition numbers of the system matrix, and some improve the
L^{2}
-errors in the computed solution as part of a direct strategy or GMRES.

Amongst the preconditioners considered, in most experiments

P_{2}:=\mathtt{U}(\Lambda({\mathtt{SyS}^{disk}}))^{-1/2}\mathtt{U}^{*}
, the ‘best circulant preconditioner’
\mathtt{P}_{5}:=(\kappa^{2}\mathtt{C}^{K}_{best})^{-1/2}
and the singular preconditioner
\mathtt{P}_{7}
improved GMRES accuracy when used as left preconditioners. In forthcoming work, we explore the performance of these preconditioners as part of a block preconditioning strategy on a mesh.

As a final comment, we observe that the goal of reducing the condition number would lead to
different
recommendations than the goal of reducing the
L^{2}
-error.
