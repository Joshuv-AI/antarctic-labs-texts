ACBP/DBI Neutralization Stimulates Anticancer Immune Responses by Upregulating Lamin A in T Cells
Authors: Sijing Li, Carlos López-Otín, Maria Chiara Maiuri, Isabelle Martins, Guido Kroemer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcyl CoA binding protein (ACBP, encoded by the gene diazepam binding inhibitor, DBI) is a tissue stress hormone involved in pathological aging that mediates immunosuppression. The antibody-mediated neutralization of ACBP/DBI enhances anticancer immune responses in the context of chemoimmunotherapy. ACBP/DBI inhibition also upregulates the expression of the anti-aging gene Lmna coding for Lamin A in tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes. This study was designed to explore the role of Lmna in the anticancer effects of ACBP/DBI neutralization.MethodsWe generated mice lacking Lmna expression in T lymphocytes due to a conditional knockout. Mice bearing orthotopic (cutaneous) MCA205 fibrosarcomas were treated with an anti-ACBP/DBI monoclonal antibody in the context of chemoimmuntherapy (oxaliplatin combined with a PD-1 blocking antibody), and tumor growth was monitored.ResultsAs compared to immunocompetent control mice, in which ACBP/DBI neutralization plus chemoimmunotherapy was highly efficient in reducing MCA205 growth, mice lacking ACBP/DBI in T cells largely failed to respond to the therapeutic intervention. This result was undistinguishably observed in both female and male mice.DiscussionAltogether, these results indicate that Lmna expression by T lymphocytes is required for the anticancer effects of the combination of chemoimmunotherapy and ACBP/DBI neutralization. We conclude that lamin A, a factor required for optimal T cell activation, is causally involved in the immunostimulatory effects of ACBP/DBI neutralization.Keywords: endozepine, Hutchinson-Gilford progeroid syndrome, immune checkpoint inhibitor, immunotherapy, progeria

Introduction

Acyl CoA binding protein (ACBP), which is encoded by the gene diazepam binding inhibitor (DBI), is a phylogenetically ancient protein that is expressed in the cytosol of multiple cell types including all major myeloid subsets and epithelial cells under the control of multiple transcription factors, many of which are stress-activated.1–3 The preponderant isoform of ACBP/DBI is a small (88 amino acid residues) leaderless peptide that is actively secreted through a non-conventional autophagy-dependent “exophagic” secretory pathway or is passively released upon loss of plasma membrane integrity during cell death.4–6 Hence, ACBP/DBI protein is detectable in plasma. Notably, its levels increase with age and body mass index, both of which represent major risk factors for cancer.7,8 Extracellular ACBP/DBI protein acts on gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) type A receptors (GABAAR) expressed by numerous cell types outside of the central nervous system to inhibit autophagy and to stimulate inflammation, fibrogenesis and cellular senescence, thereby contributing to pathological aging.6,8,9 Indeed, inhibition of ACBP/DBI by neutralizing monoclonal antibodies delays or prevents the manifestation of organ damage induced by doxorubicin, cisplatin or tetracarbon chloride (that cause premature aging of the heart, kidney and liver, respectively) as well as the phenotypic alterations accompanying systemic aging in a mouse model of Hutchinson-Gilford progeroid syndrome.8 For this purpose, we generated several monoclonal antibodies neutralizing ACBP/DBI.6,10

In specific cancer types, ACBP/DBI is upregulated, likely contributing to tumor cell metabolism and optimal fatty acid oxidation, as documented for glioblastoma, as well as for bone marrow metastases of various carcinoma types.11,12 Beyond this cell-autonomous pro-tumorigenic function of ACBP/DBI, there is evidence that extracellular ACBP/DBI can contribute to carcinogenesis as well. Thus, in the UK biobank, a diagnosis of any cancer type is accompanied by an increase in plasma ACBP/DBI concentrations.13 More intriguingly, apparently healthy individuals with supranormal plasma ACBP/DBI levels are at high risk of developing cancer.14 Mechanistically, this may be explained by immunosuppressive effects of ACBP/DBI. Indeed, in mouse models of carcinogenesis, neutralization of ACBP/DBI by antibodies slows tumor progression, but only in immunocompetent, not in immunodeficient animals.14 In mice bearing various cancers, including breast, liver, lung carcinomas and fibrosarcomas, neutralization of ACBP/DBI reduces tumor growth, in particular in combination with PD-1 blockade, chemotherapy, or chemoimmunotherapy (oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy plus PD-1 blockade).14–16

Mechanistic studies revealed that ACBP/DBI neutralization enhances the capacity of dendritic cells to present tumor antigens17 and favors the phagocytic capacity of macrophages as well,18 suggesting direct effects on myeloid cells. In addition, ACBP/DBI neutralization enhances the infiltration of cancers by non-exhausted CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTL), but reduces the local presence of regulator T cells (Tregs), hence improving the CTL/Treg ratio, which is a proxy for effective immunosurveillance.14,16 Intriguingly, single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) experiments revealed that T lymphocytes infiltrating mouse cancers exhibit an upregulation of the gene coding for lamin A (Lmna) in response to ACBP/DBI neutralization.14 Although these results do not demonstrate that ACBP/DBI directly regulates Lmna expression in T cells (an effect that might be mediated indirectly via effects on myeloid cells including dendritic cells), it is noteworthy that all CD4+ and CD8+ subsets upregulate Lmna in response to ACBP/DBI neutralization.14 Since lamin A is mutated in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome,19,20 as well as in other age-associated diseases (such as atypical progeroid syndrome,21 dilated cardiomyopathy with conduction system disease,22,23 congenital muscular dystrophy24 Emery-Dreifuss molecular dystrophy,25,26 limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type 1B, familial partial lipodystrophy,27 Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 2B1,28 and mandibuloacral dysplasia29,30) we were intrigued by this finding.

Our hypothesis was that the upregulation of Lmna induced by ACBP/DBI neutralization in tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes contributes to the stimulation of anticancer immune responses. To test this, we generated a T cell–specific LMNA knockout model and evaluated the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy combined with ACBP/DBI neutralization in a mouse tumor setting.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Design

This study was designed to investigate the contribution of T cell Lmna to the combination of chemoimmunotherapy plus ACBP/DBI neutralization (which is particularly efficient) rather than chemoimmunotherapy and ACBP/DBI neutralization alone (which are less efficient). For this, we ablated Lmna expression specifically in T cells by a conditional knockout and investigated the effect of this genetic manipulation on the efficacy capacity of therapeutic interventions to control tumor growth and to extend the survival of tumor-bearing mice.

Generation of Genetically Manipulated Mice

C57Bl/6J Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/− mice (T cell–specific Lmna deletion) and Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre−/− littermate controls31 were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions. Efficient deletion of Lmna in T cells in the Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/− mice has been demonstrated previously.31 Both female and male mice (8–12 weeks old) were used. Animals were housed five per cage on a 12h light/dark cycle, with water and food ad libitum, and allowed at least one week of acclimation before experimentation.

Tumor Growth Experiments

Mice were shaved and injected subcutaneously with 0.3 × 106 MCA205 cells under light isoflurane anesthesia (2% induction for ≤ 5 min). Tumors became measurable ~7 days later and were quantified using an electronic caliper. Tumor volume was calculated as: (length × width2)/2. Mice were randomized into treatment groups to ensure comparable age, body weight distribution, and tumor burden, using the RandoMice software. When tumors reached approximately 50 mm3 (defined as day 0), treatments were initiated. On D0, mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of oxaliplatin (10 mg/kg; CT) or vehicle (Veh). This was followed by i.p. administration of anti-ACBP/DBI (αACBP clone 7A; Fred Hutch Antibody Technology, Seattle, WA, USA; 5 mg/kg; Iso1 as an IgG2 isotype control) and anti-PD-1 (αPD-1 clone 29F.1.A12, BioXCell; 200 µg/mouse; Iso2 as and IgG1 isotype control) according to the schedule depicted in Figure 1A “CTR” denotes the Veh + Iso1 + Iso2 control condition. Female and male mice underwent the same treatment protocol. The final recorded tumor volume for each mouse was included in the growth curves until the last mouse in each group reached its humane endpoint and was euthanized. Animals were excluded only upon meeting pre-defined humane endpoints (eg, ulceration, tumor volume exceeding the approved limit, >20% body-weight loss, or severe clinical distress) or when technical failure precluded valid assessment (unsuccessful injection). No additional data-point exclusion or outlier filtering was performed. Group sizes varied due to breeding yield and pre-defined exclusion criteria. Tumor measurements and endpoint assessments were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation (treatment and genotype). Tumor size was monitored every 2–3 days until predefined humane endpoints. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following the institutional and ethics regulations concerning the use of animals for research purposes. Statistical Analyses

Tumor growth was evaluated separately for female and male mice, and subsequently analyzed as a pooled cohort. The computer program used for the statistical analyses of tumor growth curves is TumGrowth (freely available online at https://kroemerlab.shinyapps.io/TumGrowth/). Tumor growth was analyzed using the Welch’s one-way ANOVA followed by Games–Howell post hoc test. Data are shown as mean ± SEM. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and differences between groups were assessed using the Log rank test. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1) using the survival package (version 3.3-1) for Kaplan–Meier estimation and Log rank testing, and the survminer package (version 0.4.9) for visualization. Survival curves were generated with ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) and were displayed with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General Design of the Study

C57Bl/6J mice lacking Lmna expression in T cells (genotype: Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/−) or control mice retaining Lmna expression in T cells (genotype: Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre−/−) were injected subcutaneously with syngeneic MCA205 fibrosarcoma cells. Eight days later, when tumors measured 25 to 50 mm3, the animals received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of oxaliplatin or vehicle. In addition, they received i.p. injections of anti-ACBP/DBI and anti-PD-1 antibodies or vehicle control injections according to the treatment schedule in Figure 1A. Tumor volumes were monitored throughout the experiment.

Effect of the Lmna Knockout on Female Mice

In vehicle treated female MCA205 recipients, tumors exponentially grew irrespective of the genotype (Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre−/− or Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/−) (Figure 1A, B and Supplementary Figure 1) causing the death of all animals at or before day 25 post-treatment (Figure 1C). However, in female MCA205 carriers treated with the full treatment schedule (oxaliplatin, anti-PD-1 plus anti-ACBP antibodies), therapeutic outcome was strongly influenced by the genotype. Thus, in Lmna-sufficient recipients, all tumors shrunk and became undetectable around days 19 to 22 post-treatment, but relapsed in 3 out of 9 mice (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure 1), causing their death around 57 days post-treatment, while permitting protracted survival of 6 out of 9 mice (Figure 1C). In Lmna-deficient females, none of the tumors showed a protracted involution post-treatment, but instead steadily progressed (Figure 1B) and killed all mice by 57 days post-treatment (Figure 1C). Thus, T cell-specific Lmna deletion strongly compromised the control of MCA205 fibrosarcomas by the combination treatment in female mice.

Effect of the Lmna Knockout on Male Mice

Very similar results were obtained in male mice. Again, the genotype (Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre−/− or Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/−) had no influence on the progression and lethal outcome of fibrosarcomas. Moreover, relative tumor control and long-term survival induced by the combination therapy (oxaliplatin + anti-PD-1 + anti-ACBP/DBI) was observed in 5 out of 9 male of Lmna-sufficient control mice, but not in any of the Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/− mice lacking Lmna in T lymphocytes (Figure 2A, B and Supplementary Figure 2), arguing against any kind of sexual dimorphism in treatment responsiveness.

Pooled Analysis of Female and Male Mice

Pooled analysis of Lmna-dependent therapeutic responses in both males and females (Figure 3A and B) yielded similar results as separate analyses of each sex, though with a corresponding diminution of the p-values distinguishing the two genotypes with respect to animal survival (p=8.99e-06 for the pooled analysis of both sexes versus p=0.0047 in females and 0.0057 in males only) (Figures 1C, 2B and 3B).

In conclusion, these findings suggest that Lmna expression by T cells is essential for the long-term control of tumors by chemoimmunotherapy stimulated by ACBP/DBI blockade.

Discussion

The present work suggests that immunostimulation by ACBP/DBI neutralization, which leads to an increase in Lmna expression by tumor-infiltrating T cells indeed requires Lmna to be present in such lymphocytes for this effect to manifest.14 Indeed, as shown here, mice in which T cells lack Lmna are refractory to chemoimmunotherapy invigorated by ACBP/DBI neutralization when compared to control animals expressing Lmna in T lymphocytes.

The implication of Lmna in immune responses has previously been demonstrated outside of the realm of cancer immunity. For example, LMNA expression is low in resting naïve T lymphocytes from both humans and mice, but increases upon T cell activation, to which LMNA protein contributes.32 Thus, LMNA facilitates the accelerated formation of immune synapses by favoring the interaction between the nucleoskeleton and the cytoskeleton, thereby promoting F-actin polymerization.32 Mice bearing Lmna-deficient T cells do not exhibit defects in thymopoiesis and in the distribution of major T cell populations in secondary lymphoid organs at baseline.32 However, mice lacking Lmna in immune cells after bone marrow transplantation of Lmna-deficient hematopoietic stem cells into irradiated wild type recipients exhibit reduced contact hypersensitivity responses against the hapten oxazolone.32 In addition, Rag1−/− mice reconstituted with Lmna−/− CD4+CD25− T cells exhibit deficient Th1 (but not Th2) cell responses in vivo, thereby compromising the clearance of vaccinia virus or Leishmania major.33 Such Lmna−/− CD4+CD25− T cells also exhibit a skew towards enhanced regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, which compromises their capacity to induce inflammatory bowel disease upon their adoptive transfer into Rag1−/− mice.34 In this context, it appears intriguing that ACBP/DBI neutralization induced alterations in the tumor microenvironment include not only an increase in non-exhausted, activated cytotoxic T cells but also a reduction of Treg.14 In patients with breast cancer, neoadjuvant chemotherapy with three immunogenic anticancer drugs (epirubicin, cyclophosphamide and docetaxel)35 causes an increase in the frequency of LMNA-expressing CD8+ T cells,36 suggesting that LMNA plays a role in anticancer immunity in patients as well.

LMNA appears to play an immunostimulatory role in other immune cell types as well. Thus, a dendritic cell (DC) subpopulation expressing high levels of LMNA plays a pathogenic role in genetically determined (heterozygous TREX1 mutant) chilblain lupus, a familial interferonopathy affecting infants.37 Indeed, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells from Lmnaflox/flox::LysM-Cre+/− mice (which lack Lmna specifically in myeloid cells) exhibit reduced capacity to form immunological synapses with CD4+ T cells, as well as reduced responses to Toll-like receptor stimulation.38 Although dendritic cells (and in particular conventional dendritic cells type 1 or cDC1) play a primordial role in anticancer immunity,39,40 the experiments shown here involved the specific ablation of Lmna expression in T cells (in the Lmnaflox/flox::dLck-Cre+/− which causes a selective knockout in the T lymphoid lineage).41 Hence, the suppression of Lmna expression in T cells alone suffices to abolish the curative effects of anti-ACBP/DBI-enhanced chemoimmunotherapy.

The target cells of ACBP/DBI in the immune system have not been elucidated. While ACBP/DBI is relatively low-abundant in lymphoid cells including T and B cells, myeloid cells including macrophages and dendritic cells tend to express high levels of ACBP/DBI.3 However, information on the critical gamma-amino butyric acid (GABA) type A receptor (GABAAR) subunit gamma2 (GABRG2) that allows for binding of ACBP/DBI in different immune cell subtypes is scarce. Both mouse and human T cells have been shown to express GABRG2,42,43 but myeloid cells can express GABRG2 as well.44,45 Benzodiazepines, which act on GABRG2, inhibit the proliferation of both human and mouse T cells,46 but may also act on macrophages47 and potentially other myeloid cells including DCs as well.44,48–50 Notably, distinct DC subpopulations can up- or downregulate LMNA expression by T cells in vitro,34 suggesting that ACBP/DBI might modulate LMNA expression in T cells indirectly through effects on DCs. Hence, further experimentation is required to understand which immune cell types directly respond to ACBP/DBI and its neutralization.

In sum, the present work demonstrates a potential mechanism through which ACBP/DBI neutralization enhances the outcome of cancer chemoimmunotherapy. This mechanism involves the upregulation of LMNA in T cells via pathways that warrant additional mechanistical investigation.

Limitations of This Study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we assessed the impact of Lmna knockout in T cells exclusively in tumor-bearing mice treated with a combination of chemoimmunotherapy and ACBP/DBI neutralization, rather than in mice receiving chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or ACBP/DBI-blocking antibodies as monotherapies. Consequently, the specific contribution of Lmna to each therapeutic modality could not be evaluated independently. Second, Lmna knockout was induced at baseline, prior to tumor inoculation and administration of the combination treatment, rather than during or after these interventions. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that baseline alterations in immune system function contributed to the observed effects. Third, as discussed above, the principal immune cell population responding to ACBP/DBI and its neutralization remains unidentified. In particular, it remains unclear whether ACBP/DBI exerts its immunostimulatory effects exclusively through myeloid cells or whether it may also directly modulate T lymphocyte function.
