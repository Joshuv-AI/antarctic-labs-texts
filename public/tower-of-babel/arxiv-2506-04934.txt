On the geometry of synthetic null hypersurfaces
Fabio Cavalletti, Davide Manini, Andrea Mondino

Abstract
. This paper develops a synthetic framework for the geometric and analytic study of null (lightlike) hypersurfaces in non-smooth spacetimes. Drawing from optimal transport and recent advances in Lorentzian geometry and causality theory, we define a synthetic null hypersurface as a triple ( H , G , 𝔪 ) (H,G,\mathfrak{m}) : H H is a closed achronal set in a topological causal space, G G is a gauge function encoding affine parametrizations along null generators, and 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} is a Radon measure serving as a synthetic analog of the rigged measure. This generalizes classical differential geometric structures to potentially singular spacetimes. A central object is the synthetic null energy condition ( 𝖭𝖢 e ​ ( N ) \mathsf{NC}^{e}(N) ), defined via the concavity of an entropy power functional along optimal transport, with parametrization given by the gauge G G . This condition is invariant under changes of gauge and measure within natural equivalence classes. It agrees with the classical Null Energy Condition in the smooth setting and it applies to low-regularity spacetimes. A key property of 𝖭𝖢 e ​ ( N ) \mathsf{NC}^{e}(N) is the stability under convergence of synthetic null hypersurfaces, inspired by measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. As a first application, we obtain a synthetic version of Hawking’s area theorem. Moreover, we extend the celebrated Penrose’s singularity theorem to continuous spacetimes and prove the existence of trapped regions in the general setting of topological causal spaces satisfying the synthetic 𝖭𝖢 e ​ ( N ) \mathsf{NC}^{e}(N) .

1.
Introduction

Null (or lightlike) hypersurfaces are fundamental objects in
Lorentzian geometry and mathematical relativity, playing a central
role in the causal structure of spacetimes and in the formulation of
physically significant results such as Hawking’s
area
theorem
[
29
]
and Penrose’s singularity
theorem
[
58
]
. Traditionally, the analysis of such
hypersurfaces—defined as co-dimension one submanifolds whose normal
vector field is null—relies on the smooth differential-geometric
setting. However, the increasing interest in low-regularity
spacetimes, including those with continuous metrics or metric-measure
structures, necessitates the development of a robust synthetic
framework capable of encoding the geometric and causal features of
null hypersurfaces beyond smooth regimes.

The goal of this paper is to introduce and develop a synthetic theory of null hypersurfaces and the Null Energy Condition (NEC) in non-smooth spacetimes, within the general setting of topological causal spaces
[
34
,
35
]
. The main outcomes are:

•

The
well-posedness
and
covariance/invariance
of
the synthetic notions;

•

The
compatibility
of the synthetic constructions of the present paper with the classical smooth notions;

•

The
stability
of the synthetic NEC under a suitable
extension of the measured Gromov–Hausdorff
convergence to synthetic null hypersurfaces;

•

Extensions
of certain classical results,
namely
Hawking’s area theorem and Penrose’s singularity theorem to settings
of extremely low regularity (the former to topological causal spaces, the latter to continuous spacetimes).

1.1.
Motivations

Null hypersurfaces

Often, the null hypersurfaces appearing in applications (e.g., as Cauchy horizons) lack smoothness. This motivates the development of a theory for null hypersurfaces under minimal regularity assumptions.
Recall that a hypersurface
H\subset(M,g)
, where
(M,g)

is a Lorentzian manifold, is said to be
null
if the
restriction of the Lorentzian metric
g
to
H
is degenerate i.e., it has a one-dimensional kernel at every point.
Equivalently, the normal vector field to
H
is also tangent to
H
. Null hypersurfaces naturally arise in general relativity: they encode the propagation of light-like signals and gravitational radiation, they serve as geometric models for event horizons, Cauchy horizons, and null infinity.
A deeper understanding of their geometry may shed new light on fundamental problems in general relativity, for instance see the recent progress on the classification of Cauchy horizons (see for instance
[
50
,
7
,
27
]
).
Furthermore, null hypersurfaces feature prominently in theoretical frameworks such as holography and the AdS/CFT correspondence, where boundary structures often possess null character.

Null Energy Condition

The
Null Energy Condition (NEC)
, in its geometric form, requires that the Ricci tensor satisfies

(1.1)

\Ric(v,v)\geq 0,\quad\text{for all null\;
(i.e., light-like)
vectors $v$.}

This condition encapsulates the classical expectation that the
effective energy density measured along lightlike directions is
nonnegative.
The NEC arises as a central assumption in several foundational
results in Lorentzian geometry and general relativity. In the
classical (non-quantum) regime, it is expected to hold for all
physically reasonable matter models (cf.
[
10
, Ch. 4.6]
).
Its mathematical significance is underscored by the following applications:

(1)

Black Hole Area Theorem
: The NEC underpins the proof of Hawking’s area theorem
[
29
]
, which asserts the non-decreasing nature of the event horizon area of a black hole in classical general relativity. This result serves as a geometric analog of the second law of thermodynamics.

(2)

Penrose’s Singularity Theorem
: The NEC is a key
hypothesis in Penrose’s singularity theorem
[
58
]
,
which guarantees geodesic incompleteness under conditions of
gravitational collapse and the existence of trapped
surfaces. This theorem represents a foundational result in the
mathematical theory of black holes, recognized by the
2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Singular spacetimes

As already anticipated in the foundational work of Lichnerowicz in the 1950s
[
38
]
, many physically realistic models predict spacetimes with matter fields that yield energy-momentum tensors of low regularity. Through the Einstein equations, this leads to Lorentzian metrics of regularity below
C^{2}
. Notable examples include matched spacetimes modeling stellar interiors and exteriors
[
42
]
, self-gravitating compressible fluids
[
6
]
, and shock wave solutions. Certain models require even lower regularity, such as impulsive gravitational waves (see Penrose’s original construction
[
59
]
, and the more recent
[
25
, Ch. 20]
), and cyclic cosmologies
[
37
]
.

Ultimately, a long-term motivation for considering Lorentzian geometries with weak regularity stems from the expectation that at quantum gravitational scales—such as near black-hole singularities or the origin of the universe—the classical manifold model of spacetime may break down. In these regimes, spacetime may exhibit extreme irregularity, potentially eluding approximation by smooth structures.

In the setting of Lorentzian metrics of low regularity, curvature is
typically defined in the sense of distributions, leveraging the smooth
background manifold. This approach has been developed, for instance,
by Geroch and Traschen
[
23
]
, allowing the definition of
distributional curvature tensors for
W^{1,2}_{\text{loc}}

Lorentzian metrics satisfying an appropriate non-degeneracy
condition—automatically satisfied, e.g., for
C^{1}
-metrics (cf.
[
24
]
).

A key objective in this paper is to address curvature—not only in the sense of generalized tensors on a smooth underlying manifold, but in settings where
the spacetime itself is singular
, and neither the metric tensor nor the ambient differentiable structure is assumed to be smooth.

Why extending singularity theorems to non-smooth spacetimes

The singularity theorems constitute a cornerstone of Lorentzian geometry and general relativity, rigorously demonstrating that spacetime singularities—understood as causal geodesic incompleteness—arise generically under physically reasonable conditions. Classic results such as those of Penrose
[
58
]
and Hawking
[
28
]
establish incompleteness in scenarios involving complete gravitational collapse or cosmological expansion, respectively. These foundational theorems, developed in the late 1960s, were originally formulated under the assumption that the spacetime metric
g
is smooth.

However, already in their seminal monograph
[
30
]
, Hawking and Ellis raised concerns about the regularity assumptions inherent in these theorems. In particular, they emphasized that a lack of low-regularity generalizations would limit the physical robustness of the conclusions: if singularity theorems cease to hold for metrics below the
C^{2}
-threshold, then spacetime incompleteness—hence singularity in the standard sense (cf.
[
19
, p. 10]
,
[
30
, Sec. 8.1]
)—could potentially be avoided by a mere drop in regularity. For instance, a
C^{1,1}
-metric would imply, via the Einstein equations, at most a finite discontinuity in the matter fields, which is hardly pathological from a physical standpoint.

While regularity issues surrounding the singularity theorems have been noted for decades (cf.
[
63
, Sec. 6.2]
), substantial progress has only recently been achieved. On the one hand, significant developments in Lorentzian causality theory have extended the framework to encompass continuous metrics
[
18
,
62
]
, and even more abstract settings
[
35
,
47
]
. On the other hand, advances in analytic techniques—especially convolution-based regularization—have led to rigorous proofs of Penrose’s singularity theorem for
C^{1}
metrics
[
24
]
and of Hawking’s singularity theorem for
C^{0,1}
metrics
[
9
]
.

The present work pushes this frontier decisively further by establishing a version of Penrose’s singularity theorem for continuous metrics, thereby encompassing a broad spectrum of physically relevant, non-smooth spacetimes.

Let us mention that Hawking’s singularity theorem was extended by the first and third author
[
15
]
to the setting of Lorentzian length-spaces satisfying timelike Ricci lower bounds in a synthetic sense; see also the more recent extension by Braun–McCann
[
5
]
to synthetic variable lower bounds on the timelike Ricci. However, as detailed below, the null setting of Penrose’s singularity theorem poses considerable additional challenges, that are addressed in the present work.

1.2.
Previous contributions on NEC and optimal trasport

A first synthetic characterization of the NEC for smooth spacetimes was obtained by McCann
[
44
]
, as a limiting procedure of timelike Ricci lower bounds. This approach builds on top of the optimal transport characterization of timelike Ricci lower bounds obtained for smooth spacetimes in
[
43
,
51
]
and the synthetic
\mathsf{TCD}(K,N)
condition studied in
[
15
]
(see also
[
4
,
16
,
5
,
3
]
for more recent developments) in the framework of Lorentzian length spaces introduced by Kunzinger–Sämann
[
35
]
. The advantage of the characterization of the NEC obtained in
[
44
]
is that it extends to non-smooth Lorentzian length spaces; the drawback, as already remarked in
[
44
]
, is the lack of stability under convergence. Moreover, it remains an open question to draw applications from such an overture.

A distinct approach was later proposed by Ketterer
[
32
]
, who first studied optimal transport inside null hypersurfaces and considered displacement convexity of entropy for
singular
measures supported on co-dimension two spacelike submanifolds embedded in null hypersurfaces.

After
[
32
]
, the authors
[
12
]
established
that (
1.1
) can be equivalently reformulated in terms
of entropy convexity for
diffused
probability measures
supported on null hypersurfaces.
More precisely,
[
12
]
provided a systematic study, in smooth spacetimes, of the interplay between Ricci curvature in null directions, rigged measures on null hypersurfaces, and optimal transport of probability measures on null hypersurfaces which are absolutely continuous with respect to a rigged measure.

1.3.
A novel theory for synthetic null
hypersurfaces

In the present work, we use the smooth characterization of the NEC in terms of optimal transport provided in
[
12
]
as a starting point to develop a synthetic theory of null hypersurfaces and of the Null Energy Condition in non-smooth spacetimes.

Synthetic null hypersurfaces

The building blocks of the novel synthetic framework for null hypersurfaces are:

•

a closed achronal set
H
within a topological causal space
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathcal{T})
,

•

a gauge function
G:H^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
encoding affine parametrization along causal curves (where
H^{\circ}\subset H
denotes the subset of intermediate points of the null generators of
H
, see Definition
3.3
),

•

a reference measure
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(H)
satisfying
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{s}(H))=0
, where
\mathfrak{s}(H)
denotes the static (non-causally connected) part of
H
.

The triple
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
defines what we call a
synthetic null hypersurface
. This notion is shown to be compatible with classical null hypersurfaces in smooth Lorentzian manifolds, as well as with achronal boundaries arising as
\partial I^{+}(A)
for a compact, achronal, space-like,
C^{2}
-submanifold
A\subset M
of codimension
k\geq 2
(see Section
3.2
).

The lack of a variational characterization for

null
geodesics (contrary to
timelike
geodesics) poses serious challenges in developing a synthetic theory of null hypersurfaces and of the Null Energy Condition in a non-smooth setting.

Indeed, in smooth spacetimes, null geodesics are uniquely determined
(up to affine reparametrizations) by the solution of the geodesic
equation.
In contrast, in the non-smooth setting, causal curves may admit wild
reparametrizations—such
as those involving non absolutely continuous functions like the Vitali function—that preserve causal behavior without reflecting genuine geodesicity. To overcome this, we define
G
-causal curves
as those for which the composition with the gauge function
G
is affine, a choice that restores the synthetic analog of affine parametrization and allows for a meaningful definition of null geodesics.

A similar ambiguity appears for the reference measure: in the smooth
setting, the rigged measure
\mathfrak{m}_{L}

concentrated
on a null hypersurface is not unique and it depends on the choice of a
null geodesic vector field
L
, whose definition requires the differential
structure of the manifold.
From a more synthetic point of view, different rigged measures can be obtained via multiplication by a transverse
function. The synthetic counterpart of the rigged measure
\mathfrak{m}_{L}
is given by the measure
\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(H)
which is part of the data defining a synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
.
In order to show well-posedness of the theory, we establish its covariance under multiplication of the reference measure
\mathfrak{m}
by transverse
functions (see
Section

4
).

Synthetic Null Energy Condition

The cornerstone of our theory is the synthetic Null Energy Condition, denoted by
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
, where
N>0
plays the role of a synthetic upper bound on the dimension of the spacetime. This condition is formulated as the concavity of the functional

(1.2)

\U_{N-1}(\mu|\mathfrak{m}):=\exp\left(-\frac{\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m})}{N-1}\right),

where
\operatorname{Ent}(\mu|\mathfrak{m})
denotes the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy of
\mu
relative to
\mathfrak{m}
.
The functional
\U_{N}
is well-known
in information theory as the
Shannon entropy power
(see, e.g.,
[
21
]
; for instance, the entropy
power on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the functional
\U_{N/2}
),
and it was studied in connection to Ricci curvature bounds in
[
22
]
.

The synthetic
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition requires that for any pair of probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
admitting a causal coupling, there exists a
G
-causal dynamical plan
\nu\in\operatorname{OptGeo}_{G}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
such that
\U_{N-1}(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m})
is concave in
t
, where
\mu_{t}:=(e_{t})_{\sharp}\nu
is the Wasserstein geodesic connecting
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
via the law
\nu
.

This condition is inspired by the convexity-based characterizations of lower Ricci bounds for Riemannian manifolds obtained in
[
43
,
56
,
20
,
69
]
which led to the celebrated
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
spaces of Lott–Sturm–Villani
[
66
,
67
,
40
]
.

The synthetic framework developed in the present work
exhibits important stability and compatibility properties. Notably, we
show that the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition is independent of the specific
choice of gauge and reference measure within appropriate equivalence
classes, see
Theorem

4.6
,
thereby extending to the
synthetic setting a property previously established in the smooth realm
(see
[
12
]
, in particular Prop. 7.1.).
Furthermore, under additional geometric assumptions such as null
non-branching or local compactness, we prove that the sets of initial
and final points
\mathfrak{a}(H)
,
\mathfrak{b}(H)
of the null
generators of
H
are negligible with respect to
\mathfrak{m}
, a
property crucial for the applications, see
Theorem

5.13
and

Proposition

3.16
, respectively.

In addition to foundational results, we demonstrate that the synthetic
theory recovers the classical NEC in smooth
settings. Theorems
3.17

and
3.18
establish that if the classical
NEC (
1.1
) holds, then the associated synthetic null
hypersurface satisfies the synthetic
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition.
This compatibility is shown both in the presence of global
cross-sections and for achronal boundaries of the form
\partial I^{+}(A)
, even in the absence of smooth global coordinates.

In positive signature, a key property of the Lott–Sturm–Villani

\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
condition is the stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff
convergence of metric measure spaces
[
66
,
67
,
40
]
(see also
[
68
]
).
In
Section

9
we establish two stability theorems for the Null Energy Condition under a null counterpart of the measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence for synthetic null hypersurfaces, see
Theorem

9.2
and
Theorem

9.5
for the precise statements.

Optimal transport along synthetic null hypersurfaces

In

.
It has been later revisited with tools of

More recently, it has been developed

1.4.
Applications

A synthetic version of the Hawking’s Area Theorem

Hawking proved the area theorem in 1971
[
29
]
. In its original formulation, it states that in a smooth spacetime satisfying the Null Energy Condition, the area of a smooth black hole horizon can never decrease.

In classical general relativity, this result played a fundamental role in the development of black hole thermodynamics. The area of a black hole horizon is interpreted as a measure of its entropy, and according to the second law of thermodynamics, entropy cannot decrease over time.

The theorem was later revisited by Chruściel, Delay, Galloway, and Howard
[
17
]
, as well as Minguzzi
[
45
]
, who extended it by relaxing the regularity assumptions on the null hypersurface while maintaining the assumption of a smooth ambient spacetime.

More recently, Ketterer
[
32
]
and—using a different approach more closely aligned with the present work—the authors
[
12
]
, extended Hawking’s Area Theorem to smooth weighted spacetimes satisfying the Null Energy Condition, employing tools from optimal transport theory.

Theorem

7.3
generalizes Hawking’s area theorem to the non-smooth setting of a synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
contained in a topological causal space, satisfying the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition.

Penrose’s Singularity Theorem for continuous spacetimes

In
Section

8
, we propose a way to extend Penrose’s singularity
theorem to the setting of a continuous spacetime (i.e., when the
Lorentzian metric is continuous).
Recall that Penrose’s singularity theorem
[
58
]
states that,
if a spacetime satisfying the Null Energy Condition admits a non-compact Cauchy
hypersurface and it contains a trapped surface, then it is null
geodesic incomplete; i.e., there exists a null geodesic
\gamma
(in
the sense that it solves the geodesics-ODE:

\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}
) whose maximal domain of
definition is strictly contained in
\mathbb{R}
.

Besides the proof, a first challenge in generalizing such a result for
spacetimes of low regularity
(i.e., when the Lorentzian metric is of regularity lower than
C^{2}
)
is to make sense of the very statement.
Penrose’s singularity theorem was extended by
Kunzinger–Steinbauer–Vickers
[
36
]
to
C^{1,1}
spacetimes
and by Graf
[
24
]
to
C^{1}
spacetimes (see
also
[
1
,
46
,
41
]
for
extensions to Finsler spacetimes).
In such a regularity, the Null Energy Condition is understood in distributional sense
and all the other ingredients in the statement (i.e., existence of a
compact Cauchy hypersurface, null geodesic completeness, and existence
of a trapped surface) can be phrased, with proper care, as in the smooth
setting; the proof is then performed via a clever approximation
argument by smooth Lorentian metrics.

In the setting of
C^{0}

space-times
(and, more generally, of closed cone structures),
Minguzzi
[
47
, Th. 2.67]
was able to extend part of the proof
of Penrose’s incompleteness theorem.
Namely, he proved that there are no future trapped sets (recall the classical terminology that a compact achronal set
S
is future trapped if
\partial I^{+}(S)
is compact)
in a spacetime admitting a stable non-compact Cauchy surface (see
also
[
39
, Th. 4.9]
, where
C^{0}
space-times are considered and
the stability assumption is dropped).
Having in mind the proof of Penrose’s singularity theorem,
a natural question is then to find conditions ensuring the
existence of a trapped set in low-regularity
spacetimes.
This is where, the NEC, the geodesic
completeness, and the future trapped set play a key role.

When the spacetime is merely continuous, the Null Energy Condition cannot be phrased in a distributional sense (this would require the Lorentzian metric
g
to be in the Geroch–Traschen class, i.e.,
g\in L^{\infty}_{loc}\cap W^{1,2}_{loc},\,g^{-1}\in L^{\infty}_{loc}
). We will thus employ the synthetic
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition discussed above. Also the notions of trapped surface and of geodesic completeness present challenges, as both involve the Christoffel symbols and thus first derivatives of the Lorentzian metric. Thus, ignoring for the moment the technical difficulties involved in the proof, a basic question is how to even phrase the statement of Penrose’s singularity theorem for a
C^{0}
-spacetime.
We propose an
answer, building on the synthetic optimal transport tools developed in the present work.

•

A synthetic notion of null completeness
.
In the smooth setting, it was pointed out that the incomplete
geodesic produced by Penrose’s singularity theorem is
achronal.
Thus, negating the existence of such a geodesic means to
require that all maximally-defined geodesics
are either complete or they cease to be achronal
(or, equivalently, they cease to be maximizing).
In
Section

8.1
we will take this observation as definition
of weak null completeness (see
Definition

8.1
).
A null-geodesically complete manifold
trivially satisfies
such a property;
Proposition

8.2
exhibits an example of a
weakly null complete Lorentzian manifold that is not null complete,
justifying the terminology.
The advantage of the weak null completeness is that it can be rephrased in terms of gauges. Indeed,
Proposition

8.3
shows that for a strongly causal Lorentzian manifold
(M,g)
, the weak null completeness is equivalent to the existence of a (natural)
proper
gauge. Thus, the properness of the gauge shall be seen as a synthetic counterpart of the weak null completeness.

•

A synthetic notion of future convergence
.
The classical definition of future convergence
for an achronal 2-codimensional submanifold
S

states that the mean
curvature of
S
on
both (incoming and outgoing)
sides of
\partial I^{+}(S)
to be strictly negative.
This notion is meaningful in the
smooth setting.

Definition

8.4
provides a synthetic formulation,
called
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future convergence
.
The building blocks of such a definition are the causal relation, the gauge
G
,
and the measure
\mathfrak{m}
.
The underlying idea is that the mean curvature is the first variation of area, and the area is in turn a first variation of volume.

With the above notions, we prove:

•

A Penrose’s theorem in a synthetic setting
,
Theorem

8.5
. Roughly, it states that if an achronal set
S
is
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging and
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
is a synthetic null
hypersurface satisfying the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition for some
N>2
, then
H
is compact; i.e.,
S
is future trapped.

•

A Penrose’s singularity theorem in
C^{0}
-spacetimes
,

Corollary

8.8
. Roughly, it states that if
(M,g)
is a continuous spacetime admitting a non-compact Cauchy surface and containing a compact achronal set
S
which is
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging and such that
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
satisfies the

\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition, for some
N>2
, then
(M,g)
is not weakly null
complete.

We shall write
x<y
when
x\leq y,x\neq y
. We say that
x
and
y
are
timelike
(resp.

) related if
x\ll y
(resp.
x\leq y
). Let
A\subset X
be an arbitrary subset of
X
. We define the
chronological
(resp.

) future of

\displaystyle I^{+}(A)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in X\,:\,\exists x\in A,\,x\ll y\}

\displaystyle J^{+}(A)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in X\,:\,\exists x\in A,\,x\leq y\}

respectively. Analogously, we define

(resp.

chronological
(resp.

. In case

(resp.
J^{\pm}(x)
) instead of

(resp.

).

Definition 2.3

(Topological causal space)
.

We say that
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
is a

, provided:

•

(X,\ll,\leq)
is a causal space;

•

\mathfrak{T}
is a Polish
and proper topology on

•

In the product
X\times X
, the relation
\leq

the relation

Recall that a Polish topology is

Throughout the paper,

will denote an arbitrary interval.

Definition 2.4
.

[Causal/timelike curves]
A non-constant curve
\gamma:I\to X
is called (future-directed)

(resp.

) if
\gamma
is continuous and if for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in I
, with

\gamma_{t_{1}}\leq\gamma_{t_{2}}
(resp.
\gamma_{t_{1}}\ll\gamma_{t_{2}}
). We say that
\gamma

\gamma(I)
are related with respect to
\ll
.

We recall the following

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal space.
We say that a set

is weakly convex, if for all

, such that

, there
exists a causal curve
\gamma
contained in

connecting

to

.

Remark 2.6
.

We point out that
Definition

2.4
is more general than the
one given in the seminal paper
[
35
]
by Kunziger and Sämann,
for we do not require that a causal curve is (locally) Lipschitz.
In that paper, the authors proved that if
(M,g)
is a strongly
causal Lorentzian manifold (i.e., any point admits arbitrarily small neighborhoods which are weakly convex) with
g
continuous, then a causal Lipschitz curve is casual in the classical
sense (i.e., its tangent vector is causal almost everywhere).

Next Lemma recovers Kunziger’s and Sämann’s result, by proving, in
the same setting, that any causal curve admits a Lipschitz
reparametrization.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(M,g)
be a strongly causal Lorentzian manifold with
g
continuous.
Let
\gamma:I\to M
be a causal curve.
Then there exists a reparametrization of
\gamma
which is locally
Lipschitz-continuous.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
\gamma
is injective
and that
0\in I
.
Since the statement is local, it suffices to show that
\gamma

admits a Lipschitz reparametrization in a neighborhood of
0
.
Let
U
a neighborhood of
\gamma_{0}
.
We may assume that
U
is coordinated, i.e., that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Up to further restricting
U
, we can also assume that
g|_{U}
is
close to the standard Minkowski product, in the sense that, for all

x\in U
, the causal cone in
T_{x}U=\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
g_{x}
is contained in the
causal cone of
-2{\mathrm{d}}x_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}{\mathrm{d}}x_{i}^{2}
,
i.e., if
X
is a
g
-causal vector, then

(2.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|X_{i}|^{2}\leq 4|X_{n}|^{2}.

By strong causality, we can assume
U
to be
weakly convex.
Let
[a,b]
, such that
\gamma([a,b])\subset U
,
and let
[\alpha,\beta]=\gamma_{n}([a,b])
.

We claim that, for all
a\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq b
, it holds that

(2.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\gamma^{i}_{t_{1}}-\gamma^{i}_{t_{2}}|^{2}\leq 4|\gamma^{n}_{t_{2}}-\gamma^{n}_{t_{2}}|^{2}.

Indeed, fix
t_{1}\leq t_{2}

and let
\delta:[0,1]\to U
be a causal (in the classical, i.e., differential sense) curve connecting

\gamma_{t_{1}}
to
\gamma_{t_{2}}
.
A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\gamma^{i}_{t_{1}}-\gamma^{i}_{t_{2}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\delta^{i}_{0}-\delta^{i}_{1}|^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\delta}^{i}\right|^{2}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|\dot{\delta}^{i}\right|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\delta}^{n}|=2(\delta^{n}_{1}-\delta^{n}_{0})=2|\gamma^{n}_{t}-\gamma^{n}_{r}|.

It follows that, for all
\tau\in[\alpha,\beta]
, there exists a
unique
t\in[a,b]
, such that
\gamma_{n}(t)=\tau
(if it were not
unique, then (
2.2
) would contradict the injectivity of

\gamma
).
We can therefore define the reparametrization
\eta_{\tau}=\gamma_{t}
,
so that
\tau=\gamma^{n}_{t}
.
This reparametrization is Lipschitz-continuous, because

|\eta^{n}_{\tau_{1}}-\eta^{n}_{\tau_{2}}|=|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|
, and

(2.3)

|\eta^{i}_{\tau_{1}}-\eta^{i}_{\tau_{2}}|=|\gamma^{i}_{t_{1}}-\gamma^{i}_{t_{2}}|\leq 2|\gamma^{n}_{t_{1}}-\gamma^{n}_{t_{2}}|=2|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|.\qed

3.
Null synthetic setting

3.1.
Synthetic definitions

In the general setting of topological causal spaces, the role of smooth
null-hypersurfaces will be played by
closed achronal sets where the causal relation does not trivialise in a sense that will be clarified later.

Among geometrically meaningful closed achronal sets, one can find, e.g.,
achronal boundaries and Cauchy horizons.
We recall below the definition of achronal boundary
that is classical in the smooth literature, see for instance
[
60
,
30
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Achronal boundary)
.

Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal space.
A set
H\subset X
is an
achronal boundary
if
there exists a set
S\subset X

such that
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
.

The next lemma shows that achronal boundaries are actually achronal, so the terminology is consistent.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal space.
Then for any subset
S\subset X
,
\partial I^{+}(S)
is achronal.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary that there exist
x,y\in\partial I^{+}(S)
,
such that
x\in I^{-}(y)
.
Since
I^{-}(y)
is open, there exists an open neighborhood
U\subset I^{-}(y)
of
x
.
Since
x\in\partial I^{+}(S)
, then there exists

w\in U\cap I^{+}(S)
.
It follows that
w\ll y
and thus, by transitivity of
\ll
, we get that
y\in I^{+}(S)
, a contradiction.
∎

It is worth underlining that
every maximal achronal set is an achronal boundary, see for instance
[
48
, Theorem 2.97]
. Since
achronal sets are subsets of their own achronal boundary, they
provides the natural framework
for a synthetic treatment of null geometry that will take into account both the local and the global picture.

We next define
the maximal and minimal elements of a closed achronal set
H
with respect to
the pre-order
\leq
, denoted below as
\mathfrak{b}(H)
and
\mathfrak{a}(H)
respectively. Such objects are well-known to be useful in the study of achronal boundaries within smooth space-times, see e.g.
[
30
, Section 6.3]
, and in the analysis of the
L^{1}
optimal transport problem within metric spaces.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a closed achronal set

H
, the set of
final
elements of
H
is given by

\mathfrak{b}(H):=\{m\in H\colon\ \nexists x\in H,\ m\leq x,\ x\neq m\},

and the set of
initial
elements of
H
is given by

\mathfrak{a}(H):=\{m\in H\colon\ \nexists x\in H,\ x\leq m,\ x\neq m\}.

Then the achronal set without end-points is defined by

{H}^{\circ}:=H\setminus\left(\mathfrak{a}(H)\cup\mathfrak{b}(H)\right).

Finally, the set of
static
elements of
H
is given by

\mathfrak{s}(H):=\mathfrak{a}(H)\cap\mathfrak{b}(H).

In the sequel, whenever no confusion arises, we shall write

\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}(H)
, and
\mathfrak{b}
and

\mathfrak{s}
with the analogous meaning.

We briefly discuss
the measurability of
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

In a topological causal space
X
,
the sets
\mathfrak{a}(H),\mathfrak{b}(H),\mathfrak{s}(H)
,
and
H^{\circ}
are Borel measurable.

Proof.

Since the topology on
X

is induced by a proper metric
and
J^{+}
is closed,
denoting by
\Delta=\{(x,x)\colon x\in X\}
,
the set
J^{+}\setminus\Delta\cap H\times H
is
\sigma
-compact,
i.e., is countable union of compact sets.
As projections of
\sigma
-compact set is
\sigma
-compact,
writing

\mathfrak{a}=H\setminus P_{2}\left((J^{+}\setminus\Delta)\cap H\times H\right),

we obtain that
\mathfrak{a}
is Borel measurable and the same conclusion is valid for
\mathfrak{b}
proving the claims.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If
\gamma:I\to H
is any causal curve then

\gamma_{I}\cap\mathfrak{a}
contains at most a single element:
suppose by contradiction there are two of them, then one has to be in the causal future of the other giving a contradiction. The same is true for
\gamma_{I}\cap\mathfrak{b}
.

Hence
\gamma^{-1}(\mathfrak{a})
and

\gamma^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})

are two closed intervals in
I

on each of which
\gamma
is constant.
Therefore, without loss of generality we can assume that if
\gamma

is a non-constant causal curve and if
I=[0,1]
, then
\gamma_{(0,1)}\subset{H}^{\circ}
.

We introduce the notion of gauge
function.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set and let
G:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Borel map.
We say that
G
is a
gauge
function for
H
, if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

for any injective causal curve
\gamma
in

{H}^{\circ}
,

G\circ\gamma
is strictly increasing and continuous;

(2)

for any causal curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to{H}
,
such that
\gamma_{(0,1)}\subset{H}^{\circ}
, then

\sup_{t\in(0,1)}G(\gamma_{t})
and

\inf_{t\in(0,1)}G(\gamma_{t})
are both finite.

Finally a gauge function G is called
proper

if the pre-image of every compact set is precompact.

Assumption (1) implies that
for any causal (possibly non injective) curve
\gamma
in
{H}^{\circ}
,

G\circ\gamma
is non-decreasing
and continuous.
This fact follows by reparametrizing
\gamma
.
Assumption (2) above is needed in order to avoid possible
blow-ups of the gauge, as the following example illustrates.
The concept of properness of a gauge will be used in
Section

8
to suitably reformulate the null completeness property.

In the smooth setting, a causal curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to H
solves the geodesics equation if and only
if
G\circ\gamma
is affine
for a natural gauge
G
(see
Remark

3.10
below).
We take this last observation as the definition of a
causal null geodesic in the null synthetic setting.

Definition 3.7

(
G
-Causal curves)
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set and let

G:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
be a gauge.
We say that a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to H
is
G
-causal
, if and only
if it is causal,
\gamma_{(0,1)}\subset{H}^{\circ}
,
and
G\circ\gamma|_{(0,1)}
is an affine function.

The set of
G
-causal curves
will be denoted by
\Caus_{G}\subset C([0,1];H)
.

Sometimes, with a slight abuse of notation, we will consider

G
-causal curves whose domain is not the interval
[0,1]
.

We are now in position to give the definition of synthetic null hypersurface.
We denote by
\mathcal{M}^{+}(H)

the set of non-negative Radon measures on
H
.

Definition 3.8

(Synthetic Null Hypersurface)
.

Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal space.
We say that the triple
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
is a
synthetic null hypersurface
,
if

•

H
is
a closed achronal set,

•

G:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
is a gauge for
H
,

•

\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(H)
does not give postive mass to the static elements of
H
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{s})=0
.

Remark 3.9
.

The request of
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{s})=0
is necessary to avoid examples of closed achronal sets which do not have the geometry of a null hypersurface. For instance, if
H
is an acasual set (e.g., a spacelike hypersurface), then the set of static points will coincide with
H
itself and therefore
the only admissible measure
to consider over
H
in order to form a synthetic null hypersurface is
\mathfrak{m}\equiv 0
.

3.2.
Compatibility with the smooth setting

We next discuss the compatibility of the above synthetic constructions with the smooth
theory, by associating to a classical null hypersurface a synthetic
one.
This is the content of the following remarks.

Remark 3.10

(Compatibility with the smooth setting: the global case)
.

We proceed by first discussing the global case, i.e., when a global smooth cross-section is available.
Let
H
be a causal and achronal, smooth null hypersurface, admitting

S\subset H
a global smooth cross-section for
H
.
Using the cross-section
S
, we can build a global null geodesic vector field

L
over
H
and the corresponding flow map

\Psi_{L}:S\times\mathbb{R}\to H
.
A natural gauge function
G_{L,S}:H\to\mathbb{R}

is then defined as the function such that
z=\Psi_{L}(p,G_{L,S}(z))
, for some
(unique)
p\in S
.
To check that
G_{L,S}
is indeed a gauge function, observe that
if
\gamma
is a causal curve in
H
, then it lies in the generator of
H
passing through
\gamma_{0}
and,
in particular, it must be of the form

\gamma_{t}=\Psi(\gamma_{0},s(t))
,
for some non-decreasing function
s
.
Therefore
G_{L,S}(\gamma_{t})=G_{L,S}(\gamma_{0})+s(t)
, which is
non-decreasing.
If
\gamma
is also injective, then
s
must be strictly increasing.
Moreover, we point out that
a causal curve is
G_{L,S}
-causal if and only
if it is a null geodesic in
H
.
Finally, as reference measure over
H
, we choose the rigged measure
\vol_{L}
(see
[
12
]
).
A synthetic null hypersurface associated to
H
is
(H,G_{L,S},\vol_{L})
.

Remark 3.11

(Compatibility with the smooth setting: the local case)
.

In several situations of interest (e.g., in the presence of recurring geodesics like the case of compact Cauchy horizons
[
50
,
7
,
27
]
) a global cross-section for
H
does not exist.
In these cases, it is possible to proceed via a local argument as follows.

Let
(M,g)
be a Lorentzian manifold and
U\subset M
is any open set. For
x,y\in U
, we set by definition
x\ll_{U}y
, provided that there exists a
C^{1}
curve

\gamma:[0,1]\to U
, connecting
x
to
y
, such that
\dot{\gamma}

is a future-directed timelike vector.
Notice that
\ll_{U}
is (in general) strictly contained in
\ll\cap\;(U\times U)
;
in a similar way we define
\leq_{U}
and
denote by
\mathcal{T}_{U}
the topology on
U
induced by
\mathcal{T}
.
We will write
X_{U}
as a shorthand notation for the
topological causal space
(U,\leq_{U},\ll_{U},\mathcal{T}_{U})
;
in particular
X_{U}
is the topological causal space
induced by the Lorentzian manifold
(U,g|_{U})
.

Now if
H
is a smooth null hypersurface,
then for any
x\in H
, we can take a coordinated neighborhood
U
for
x
.
Up to restricing
U
, we have that
H\cap U
is causal, achronal and
closed and that
a cross-section
S
is given by the intersection of
H
and the

0
-level set of the time coordinate.
We can thus apply the construction described in
Remark

3.10
.

Remark 3.12

(Compatibility with the smooth setting: achronal boundaries)
.

Let
(M,g)
to be a strongly causal,
causally closed,

C^{2}
Lorentzian manifold.
Let
A\subset M
be a compact,
achronal, space-like,

C^{2}
sub-manifold of codimension

k\geq 2
. Then

H:=\partial I^{+}(A)

is a locally-Lipschitz topological hypersurface
(see, e.g.,
[
60
, Lemma 3.17]
and
[
30
, Proposition 6.3.1]
).
If
A
is a singleton,
H
is a future cone; if the codimension of
A
is
2
, then
H
is the central object in the proof of Penrose’s singularity theorem.
To include achronal boundaries of this type in our setting,
we proceed as follows.

Under the above assumptions, it is a well-known fact that

(3.1)

H=\partial I^{+}(A)=J^{+}(A)\setminus I^{+}(A).

Indeed, the inclusion “
\subset
” is a consequence of the the causal closedness of
(M,g)
and the compactness of
A
. Conversely, the inclusion “
\supset
” follows by the
C^{2}
-regularity of
g
, for a proof see
[
47
, Prop. 2.13]
.

Firstly, we claim that
\mathfrak{a}\subset A
.
Indeed, if
x\in\mathfrak{a}\subset J^{+}(A)
, there exists

y\in A\cap J^{-}(x)
; it holds that
y\notin I^{+}(A)
, otherwise the push-up
property would imply that
x\in I^{+}(A)
, a contradiction.
Hence
y\in H
, therefore
y=x
, otherwise
x
would not be an
initial point (i.e., minimal for
\leq
).

We next claim that
J^{+}(A)=J^{+}(\mathfrak{a})
.
Indeed, fix
x\in J^{+}(A)
.
By compactness, there exists
y
, a minimal element (w.r.t.
\leq
)
of
A\cap J^{-}(y)\subset J^{+}(A)
;
y\in H
, otherwise,

y\in I^{+}(A)
, a contradiction with its minimality.
Let
z\in H\cap J^{-}(y)
; minimality of
y
implies that
z=y
,
therefore
y\in\mathfrak{a}
, thus
x\in J^{+}(\mathfrak{a})
.

As a consequence,
I^{+}(A)=I^{+}(\mathfrak{a})
and

(3.2)

H=J^{+}(A)\setminus I^{+}(A)=J^{+}(\mathfrak{a})\setminus I^{+}(\mathfrak{a}).

Let
M^{-}:H\setminus\mathfrak{b}\to A
be the map defined by

\displaystyle M^{-}(x):=\inf J^{-}(x)\cap A,

where the infimum is taken w.r.t. the causal order
\leq
.
Since
x
is not a final point, then
J^{-}(x)\cap A
is totally
ordered (i.e., we can always compare elements), hence there is at
most one minimal point.
Compactness of
A
guarantees that the infimum is attained.
Checking the measurability of
M^{-}
is standard.
Notice that
M^{-}
takes values in
\mathfrak{a}
.

We claim that the set
A\setminus\mathfrak{a}
is compact.
Indeed, let
x_{n}
be a sequence in
A\setminus\mathfrak{a}
; by
the compactness of
A
, up to a subsequence,
x_{n}\to x\in A
.
Let
y_{n}\in A
, be such that
y_{n}<x_{n}
; up to a subsequence

y_{n}\to y\in A
.
Causal closedness yields
y\leq x
.
If
y\neq x
, then
y<x
, proving that
x\in A\setminus\mathfrak{a}
.
If on the contrary
x=y
, then, since
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
converge to
the same point
x
and are causally related, then
T_{x}A
is not
space-like, a contradiction. Thus,
A\setminus\mathfrak{a}
is compact.

It follows that
\mathfrak{a}
is open in
A
, thus a
sub-manifold. Notice also that
\mathfrak{a}
is acausal.

Consider
\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
, the normal bundle to

\mathfrak{a}
and endow it with the restriction of the ambient
Lorentzian metric
g
; notice that each fiber of

\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
is a
k
-dimensional Minkowski space-time.
Let
W\in\mathfrak{X}(TM)
be a vector field inducing the time
orientation; since
A\subset M
is a smooth spacelike hypersurface, we can assume
W|_{A}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{N}A)
.
Let

(3.3)

\hat{H}:=\{v\in\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}\colon g(v,v)=0\text{ and }g(v,W)<0\}.

We claim that

H\setminus\mathfrak{a}\subset\exp(\hat{H})
.
Indeed, Equation (
3.2
) implies
that if
x\in H\setminus\mathfrak{a}
, there exists a null curve

\gamma
connecting
x
with
\mathfrak{a}
; by

Proposition

5.8
, we can assume that
\gamma
is a
geodesic.
It is immediate to see that
\dot{\gamma}_{0}
a future-directed null
vector and that it is orthogonal to
\mathfrak{a}
, thus

\dot{\gamma}_{0}\in\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
.

We now define a gauge for
H
:
for
x\in{H}^{\circ}
,
x=\exp(v)
for some (unique)

v\in\hat{H}\cap\mathscr{N}_{M^{-}(x)}\mathfrak{a}
; define

(3.4)

G(x):=-g(v,W).

Notice that
G
can be continuously extended to
0
on the set

\mathfrak{a}
.

Next, we endow the manifold
\hat{H}\subset\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
with a tangent vector
field
\hat{L}\in\mathfrak{X}(T\hat{H})
.
In order to do so, notice that at each point
v\in\mathscr{N}_{x}\mathfrak{a}
, the tangent space splits as the direct sum (in the sense of linear algebra)

T_{v}\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}\cong\mathscr{N}_{x}\mathfrak{a}\oplus T_{x}\mathfrak{a}=T_{x}M.

We denote by
\Sigma:T\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}\to TM|_{\mathfrak{a}}
,

\Sigma_{v}:T_{v}\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}\to T_{x}M
, such a morphism of fiber
bundles.
Let
\hat{g}:=\Sigma^{*}g
be the pull back on
T\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
of the Lorentzian metric
g
;
since on each fiber
\Sigma_{v}
is an isomorphism, we can also
define
\hat{W}\in\mathfrak{X}(T\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a})
as

\hat{W}_{v}:=(\Sigma_{v})^{-1}(W)
, giving a time-orientation to

\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
.
Observing that
\hat{H}
is a smooth null hypersurface in the smooth Lorentzian manifold
(\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a},\hat{g})
, we can set

\hat{L}
as the null vector field

\hat{L}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{N}\hat{H})
, such that

\hat{g}(\hat{L},\hat{W})=-1
.

Denote by
U\subset\hat{H}
the open set where the exponential map is
defined and its differential has maximal rank.
For any
v\in U
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{v}\subset U
, such
that
\exp|_{U_{v}}
is a diffeomorphism on its image.
In particular

(3.5)

H_{v}:=\exp(U_{v})

is a smooth null hypersurface.
We endow
H_{v}
with the null geodesic vector field

L^{v}:={\mathrm{d}}\exp|_{U_{v}}[\hat{L}]
and consider the rigged volume

\vol_{L^{v}}
.
By pulling back the rigged measure
\vol_{L^{v}}
associated to
L^{v}
, we obtain the measure

(3.6)

\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{v}:=(\exp|_{U_{v}})^{-1}_{\sharp}\vol_{L^{v}}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(U_{v}).

Given
v_{1},v_{2}\in U
, it is immediate to see that the
measures
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{v_{1}}
and
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{v_{2}}
coincide on

U_{v_{1}}\cap U_{v_{2}}
.
We can therefore glue these local measures to define a global measure

\hat{\mathfrak{m}}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(U)
.
Let

B:=\{v\in U\colon\exists\lambda>1,\;\exp(\lambda v)\notin I^{+}(A)\}.

On one hand, it clearly holds that
\exp(B)\subset{H}^{\circ}
.
On the other hand, if
x\in{H}^{\circ}
, then
x
is not a focal point
for the geodesic connecting
\mathfrak{a}
to
x

(see
[
48
, Th. 6.16 (a)]
); thus it is not a
conjugate point, meaning that the differential of the exponential map
has maximal rank at
\exp^{-1}(x)
, and therefore
\exp^{-1}(x)\in B
.
Thus

\exp(B)={H}^{\circ},

and we can endow
{H}^{\circ}
with
\mathfrak{m}:=\exp_{\sharp}(\hat{\mathfrak{m}}\llcorner_{B})
.
A synthetic null hypersurface associated to
H
is then

(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
.

3.3.
Optimal transport and the synthetic Null Energy Condition
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)

A concept analogous to the one of optimal dynamical plan can be introduced in the
synthetic setting by means of the gauge function and the
G
-causal curves
as follows.
Firstly, given any two probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
, we introduce

\OptCaus^{H}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,1];H))\colon(e_{0})_{\sharp}\nu=\mu_{0},(e_{1})_{\sharp}\nu=\mu_{1},\ \nu(\Caus)=1\},

where
\Caus\subset C([0,1];H)
is the closed subset of causal curves and

e_{i}:C([0,1];H)\to H
are the evalutation map at
t=i
, with
i=0,1
.
We also recall the definition
of the set of causal couplings

\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X)\colon(e_{i})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu_{i},\ i=0,1\}.

In particular,

\OptCaus^{H}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\neq\emptyset
only if
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\neq\emptyset
.
Notice that since
H
is closed, as topological space
H
is Polish and therefore
C([0,1];H)
is Polish with the topology of the uniform convergence.
In particular, every element of

\mathcal{P}(C([0,1];H))
is inner regular with respect to compact sets of

C([0,1];H)
.

Definition 3.13
.

Let
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
be a synthetic null hypersurface and let
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
be
two probability measures.
Any
\nu\in\OptCaus^{H}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

is said to be
G
-causal
if
and only if
\nu(\Caus_{G})=1
. The set of such
G
-causal plans
\nu
is denoted by
\OptGeo^{G}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
.

Given a synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
,
for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({H})
, the Boltzmann–Shannon
entropy
\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m})
of
\mu
with respect to
\mathfrak{m}
is defined by

\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m}):=\int_{X}\rho\log(\rho)\,\mathfrak{m}\in[-\infty,+\infty),

if
\mu=\rho\,\mathfrak{m}
and
(\rho\log(\rho))_{+}
is
\mathfrak{m}
-integrable;
otherwise we set
\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m})=+\infty
.
We denote by

\Dom(\Ent(\cdot|\mathfrak{m})):=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X):\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m})\neq+\infty\}.

Let us
consider the following dimensional variant of the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy: given
N>0
,
for
\mu\in\Dom(\Ent(\cdot|\mathfrak{m}))
, define

(3.7)

\U_{N}(\mu|\mathfrak{m}):=\exp\left(-\frac{\Ent(\mu|\mathfrak{m})}{N}\right),

and set
\U_{N}(\mu|\mathfrak{m})=0
otherwise.
Such a functional
\U_{N}
is well-known
in information theory as the Shannon entropy power (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
; for instance, the entropy
power on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the functional
\U_{N/2}
),
and it was studied in connection to Ricci curvature bounds in
[
22
]
.

Definition 3.14

(
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
for synthetic null hypersurfaces)
.

Let
N>0
. Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal
space.

The synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
satisfies the Null Energy Condition
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
if and only if the following holds:
for all measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H)

such that

\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\neq\emptyset
,
there exists
\nu\in\OptGeo^{G}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

such that

(3.8)

\U_{N-1}(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m})\geq(1-t)\U_{N-1}(\mu_{0}|\mathfrak{m})+t\U_{N-1}(\mu_{1}|\mathfrak{m}),\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,1],

where
\mu_{t}:=(e_{t})_{\sharp}\nu
.

We now collect two geometric consequences of
Definition

3.14
.
The first one is contained in the following

Remark 3.15

(
\mathsf{NC}^{e}
implies weak convexity)
.

Note that if both

\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
are not contained in
\Dom(\Ent(\cdot|\mathfrak{m}))
, then (
3.8
) is trivially satisfied.
On the other hand, if either
\mu_{0}
or
\mu_{1}
belongs to

\Dom(\Ent(\cdot|\mathfrak{m}))
, then (
3.8
) implies that,
for all
t\in(0,1)
,

\mu_{t}

is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathfrak{m}
.

Notice also that if
x\leq y
in
H
, then taking
\mu_{0}=\delta_{x}
,

\mu_{1}=\delta_{y}
,
\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\{\delta_{(x,y)}\}
.

Definition

3.14
implies that there exists
\nu\in\OptGeo^{G}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
,
producing a
G
-causal curve from
x
to
y
.
In other words,
H
is weakly convex.

We now prove that the sets of initial and
final points can be neglected provided
the set of
G
-causal curves satisfy the following compactness property:
for
\mathfrak{m}
-a.e.
x\in H
there exists a neighborhood
U
, such that
\mathfrak{m}(U)>0
and
the set

\{\gamma\in\Caus_{G}\colon\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in K\},

is compact, where
K\subset U
is any compact set.
A synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
satisfying such assumption
will be said
locally compact
.

This assumption is independent from the non-branching condition,
that is usually assumed in the literature when that initial and final points have measure zero.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
be a
synthetic null hypersurface
satisfying the Null Energy Condition
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
, for some
N>0
.
Assume
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
to be locally compact.
Then

\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{b})=0.

Proof.

It will suffice to prove that
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{a}\setminus\mathfrak{b})=0
:
by assumption
\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{s})=0
(see

Definition

3.8
)
and therefore it will follow that

\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{a})=0
.
The remaining part is symmetric.

Suppose by contradiction

(3.9)

\mathfrak{m}(\mathfrak{a}\setminus\mathfrak{b})>0.

Define the function
A:{H}^{\circ}\times{H}^{\circ}\cap J\to\mathbb{R}
as

A(x,y)=G(y)-G(x)\geq 0
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and consider the set

(3.10)

W_{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,z,w)\in\mathfrak{a}\setminus\mathfrak{b}\times{H}^{\circ}\times{H}^{\circ}\colon(x,z),(z,w)\in J,A(z,w)>\varepsilon\}.

The set
W_{\varepsilon}
is Borel.
It holds that

(3.11)

\mathfrak{a}\setminus\mathfrak{b}=\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}P_{1}(W_{\varepsilon}).

The (contradiction) assumption gives
\mathfrak{m}(P_{1}(W_{\varepsilon}))>0
, for some
\varepsilon>0
.
By inner regularity of
\mathfrak{m}
, there exists a compact set
K\subset P_{1}(W_{\varepsilon})
with
\mathfrak{m}(K)>0
.
We define the following analytic set:

\Lambda:=P_{1,3}(W_{\varepsilon}).

By Von Neumann’s selection Theorem (see for instance
[
65
, Th. 5.5.2]
),
we deduce that
\Lambda
contains the graph of a measurable map
T
.
Define the absolutely continuous measure

\mu_{0}:=\mathfrak{m}\llcorner_{K}/\mathfrak{m}(K)\in\mathcal{P}(H)
and

\mu_{1}:=T_{\sharp}\mu_{0}

so that

({\rm Id},T)_{\sharp}\mu_{0}\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
.

Up to neglecting a set of small
\mu_{0}
-measure, we can also assume that

T
is continuous and therefore also
\mu_{1}

can be assumed with compact support:
hence we have deduced the existence of a compact set
K\subset H
such that

\mu_{0}(K)=\mu_{1}(K)=1
.

Definition

3.14
gives then a measure

\nu\in\OptGeo^{G}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
such that
the function
t\mapsto\U_{N}((e_{t})_{\sharp}\nu)
is concave.
By
Remark

3.15

(e_{t})_{\sharp}\nu\ll\mathfrak{m}
.

Fix any
x\in K
and an open neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
\mu_{0}(U)>0
;
then we deduce the existence of
\bar{t}>0
such that

\nu(\{\gamma\in\Caus_{G}\colon\gamma_{0}\in U,\ \gamma_{t}\in U\})>0.

Indeed by continuity of causal curves,

\nu\otimes\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\left\{(\gamma,t)\in\Caus_{G}\times(0,1)\colon\gamma_{0}\in U,\gamma_{t}\in U\right\}\right)>0,

then Fubini’s Theorem implies the claim.
Then, since
\nu
is inner regular,
there exists a compact set
\bar{C}
such that

\bar{C}\subset\{\gamma\in\Caus_{G}\colon\gamma_{0}\in U,\gamma_{\bar{t}}\in U\},\quad\text{with }\quad\nu(\bar{C})>0.

Denote by
\bar{\nu}
the renormalized restriction of
\nu
to
\bar{C}
and
define the probability measures

\bar{\mu}_{0}:=(e_{0})_{\sharp}\bar{\nu},\qquad\bar{\mu}_{1}:=(e_{\bar{t}})_{\sharp}\bar{\nu}.

By construction, they are causally related
and both concentrated on a compact set inside
U
.
Since
U
was arbitrary, we can take as
U
the one additional
verifying the local compactness hypothesis and deduce the existence of a measure

\eta\in\OptGeo^{G}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1})
such that
the function
t\mapsto\U_{N}((e_{t})_{\sharp}\bar{\nu})
is concave and
\eta(C)=1
,
for some compact set
C
.

For each
t\in[0,1]
, denote now by
C_{t}:=e_{t}(C)
. Notice that each

C_{t}
is a compact set,
C_{t}
converges to
C_{0}
in Hausdroff topology
as
t\to 0
and

C_{0}\subset\mathfrak{a}\setminus\mathfrak{b}
.
Finally, by the entropy inequality (
3.8
) and a double Jensen’s inequality we have

\mathfrak{m}(C_{t})^{\frac{1}{N-1}}\geq\U_{N-1}(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m})\geq(1-t)\U_{N-1}(\mu_{0}|\mathfrak{m})=(1-t)\mathfrak{m}(C_{0})^{\frac{1}{N-1}}.

Then we reach a contradiction as follows.
Since
C_{0}
is a subset of initial points, necessarily
C_{t}\cap C_{0}=\emptyset
;
by Hausdorff convergence for each
\epsilon>0

for
t
sufficiently close to
0
we have

\mathfrak{m}((C_{0})^{\epsilon})\geq\mathfrak{m}(C_{0}\cup C_{t})=\mathfrak{m}(C_{0})+\mathfrak{m}(C_{t})\geq(1+(1-t)^{N})\mathfrak{m}(C_{0}).

Hence letting
t\to 0
and then
\epsilon\to 0

we reached a contradiction and proved the claim.
∎

),

.

(Compatibility of
\mathsf{NC}^{e}

.

.

be a

,

, for any normal vector

, if

We now complement
Theorem

3.17
by showing that the classical
NEC is also compatible with the synthetic
\mathsf{NC}^{e}
along achronal boundaries,
without any further regularity assumption. Recall that achronal
boundaries fall within the class of synthetic null hypersurfaces, see
Remark

3.12
.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Lorentzian manifold of
dimension
n
.
Assume
M
to be causally closed and strongly causal.
Let
A\subset M
be a
C^{2}
achronal, space-like sub-manifold of
codimension
k\geq 2
.
Let
H:=\partial I^{+}(A)
.
Assume that the classical NEC is valid on
H
, i.e.,

\Ric(w,w)\geq 0
, for any
light-like vector
w\in TH
at the differentiability points of
H
.

Then the synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
, as defined in

Remark

3.12
, satisfies
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(n)
.

Proof.

The claim is a consequence of
Theorem

6.8
and the constructions performed in
Remark

3.12
, to which we refer for the notation. For the sake of brevity, we outline the argument, omitting some straightforward technical details.

Recall the definition (
3.3
) of
\hat{H}
as a
C^{2}
-submanifold of
TM
endowed with the measure
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}
locally given by (
3.6
). By its very definition,
\hat{H}
is a ruled submanifold, generated by the future pointing light-like vectors
v\in\mathscr{N}\mathfrak{a}
. It is convenient to parametrize such generators on a suitable set
Q
of indices, that can be identified with a suitable measurable subset of
\hat{H}
.
We disintegrate the measure
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}
on
\hat{H}
along such generators of
\hat{H}
, as
follows

\hat{\mathfrak{m}}=\int_{Q}\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha),

where
\mathfrak{q}
is a probability measure on
Q
, and each measure
\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}
is concentrated on the future pointing half-line generated by
v_{\alpha},\;\alpha\in Q
.
Since the exponential map does not mix such generators, we can also
disintegrate the measure
\mathfrak{m}
, as
follows

(3.12)

\mathfrak{m}=\int_{Q}\exp_{\sharp}(\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}\llcorner_{B})\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha).

Fix a vector
v\in\hat{H}
. Consider the
C^{2}
null hypersurface
H_{v}
defined in (
3.5
)
and its rigged measure
\vol_{L^{v}}
. Since
H_{v}
is
C^{2}
, then

\Ric(w,w)\geq 0
for all light-like
w\in TH_{v}
in the
differentiability points of
H
. Moreover
H
is a locally-Lipschitz sub-manifold, then a.e. differentiable. It follows that

\Ric(w,w)\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }w\in TH_{v}|_{H}.

By disintegrating the rigged measure
\vol_{L^{v}}
along the above family of generators, we get

\vol_{L^{v}}=\int_{Q}\vol_{L^{v},\alpha}\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha).

For
\mathfrak{q}
-a.e.
\alpha\in Q
, the conditional measure
\vol_{L^{v},\alpha}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the
1-dimensional Lebesgue measure on the corresponding generator, with density
a_{L^{v},\alpha}
.
By
[
12
, Theorem 5.11]
, such densities satisfy

(3.13)

(a_{L^{v},\alpha})^{\prime\prime}(t)+\frac{((a_{L^{v},\alpha})^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{n-2}\leq 0,\quad t=-g(W,(\exp|_{U_{v}})^{-1}(x)),\quad\forall x\in H_{v}\cap H.

Since locally the measure
\mathfrak{m}
coincides with
\vol_{L^{v}}
, the uniqueness of the disintegration implies that the conditional measure
\exp_{\sharp}(\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}\llcorner_{B})
has density
a_{L^{v},\alpha}
, for
\mathfrak{q}
-a.e.
\alpha\in Q
.
For
v_{1},v_{2}\in\hat{H}
, one can check that

a_{L^{v_{1}},\alpha}(t)=a_{L^{v_{2}},\alpha}(t),\qquad t=-g(W,u),\qquad u\in U_{v_{1}}\cap U_{v_{2}}.

Therefore, the disintegrated measures can be glued, finding that
\exp_{\sharp}(\hat{\mathfrak{m}}_{\alpha}\llcorner_{B})
is absolutely continuous, w.r.t. the Lebesgue
measure, with density
a_{\alpha}
satisfying the differential inequality (
3.13
). The conclusion now follows from
Theorem

6.8
.
∎

4.
Equivalence relations for measures and gauges

In the smooth setting, the measure
\mathfrak{m}_{L}
and
the gauge
G_{L,S}
depend on the choice of the
vector field
L
.
Therefore, a priori
Definition

3.14
might
depend on these choices as well. One of the goals of our previous work
[
12
]
was to show the independence of the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}
condition from such a choice.
The goal of this section is to show such independence also in this
more general setting.

In this section, we consider a given topological causal space

(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
and
all the synthetic null hypersurfaces will be subsets of
X
.

Over
{H}^{\circ}

we can define two equivalence relations, one for measures
and one for gauges.
Firstly we recall the concept of transverse function that in
the smooth setting can be given by asking
f
to be constant along
the generators of the null hypersurface.
Since this characterization can be stated without referring to
the smooth structure, it is natural to consider it also in our framework.

Definition 4.1

(Transverse function)
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set.
A function
f:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
is transverse
if, for any causal curve
\gamma
in
{H}^{\circ}
,
f\circ\gamma
is constant.

In the smooth setting, if
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{N}H)
are two null
geodesic vector fields, then
L_{1}=\varphi L_{2}
for some transverse
function
\varphi
and
the relation between the two rigged measures is
\vol_{L_{1}}=\frac{1}{\varphi}\vol_{L_{2}}
.
Accordingly, we identify measures which are equivalent up to multiplication by a transverse function.

Definition 4.2

(Equivalence relation for measures)
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set
and
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\mathfrak{m}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(H)
be two measures.
We set
\mathfrak{m}_{1}\sim\mathfrak{m}_{2}

if and only if

\mathfrak{m}_{1}\llcorner_{H\setminus{H}^{\circ}}=\mathfrak{m}_{2}\llcorner_{H\setminus{H}^{\circ}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{m}_{1}\llcorner_{{H}^{\circ}}=f\mathfrak{m}_{2}\llcorner_{{H}^{\circ}},

for some
strictly positive transverse function
f
.

Continuing the analogy with the smooth setting,
the definition of

G_{L,S}
is depending both on
L
and
S

but the only relevant aspect of the theory is the set
G
-causal curves.
We therefore introduce an equivalence relation also between gauges.

Definition 4.3

(Equivalence relation for gauges)
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set and
let
G_{1},G_{2}:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
be two gauges.
We set

G_{1}\sim G_{2}
if and only if
\Caus_{G_{1}}=\Caus_{G_{2}}
(see

Definition

3.7
).

Next we prove that
the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition
is well defined for equivalence classes of gauges and reference measures, i.e., the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition
does not
depend on the choice of the measure and the gauge inside the respective
equivalence classes.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set, let
\nu\in\OptCaus^{H}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
for a
pair of probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
which are
concentrated on
{H}^{\circ}
.

Set
\mu_{t}:=(e_{t})_{\sharp}\nu
. Then, for every transverse function
f:{H}^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

(4.1)

\int_{H}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}=\int_{H}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{s},\qquad\text{ for all }t,s\in[0,1].

If moreover
f
is non-negative and the integrals
above are equal to
1
,
defining
\tilde{\mu}_{t}:=f\mu_{t}
,
then

\tilde{\nu}:=(f\circ e_{0})\,\nu\in\OptCaus^{H}(\tilde{\mu}_{0},\tilde{\mu}_{1})

and
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=(e_{t})_{\sharp}\tilde{\nu}
.

Proof.

Since

f
is transverse, then
f\circ\gamma
is constant for all

\gamma\in\Caus_{H}
, such that
\gamma_{[0,1]}\subset{H}^{\circ}
,
therefore, since
\nu(\Caus_{H})=1
,
we can compute

\int_{H}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}-\int_{H}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{s}=\int_{C([0,1];H)}(f\circ e_{t}-f\circ e_{s})\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu=0.

For the second part, a direct computation gives

\tilde{\mu}_{t}=f\mu_{t}=f(e_{t})_{\sharp}(\nu)=(e_{t})_{\sharp}(f\circ e_{t}\nu)=(e_{t})_{\sharp}(f\circ e_{0}\nu)=(e_{t})_{\sharp}(\tilde{\nu}).\qed

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
H
be a closed achronal set,
\nu\in\OptCaus^{H}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
for a pair of probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
concentrated on
{H}^{\circ}
and
let
\mathfrak{m}_{1},\mathfrak{m}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({H})
such that
\mathfrak{m}_{1}\sim\mathfrak{m}_{2}
.

Then the function
t\mapsto\Ent(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{1})-\Ent(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{2})
is constant.

Proof.

Let
f
be any transverse function such that

\mathfrak{m}_{1}=f\mathfrak{m}_{2}
and

\rho_{k,t}
be the density of
\mu_{t}

\displaystyle\Ent(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{H}\log(\rho_{2,t})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}=\int_{H}\log(\rho_{1,t})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}+\int_{H}\log f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\Ent(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{1})+\int_{H}\log f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{eq:integral-transverse}}}}{{=}}\Ent(\mu_{t}|\mathfrak{m}_{1})+\int_{H}\log f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{0}.\qed

The next result follows straightforwardly from the propositions above.

This section investigates more in details the geometry of synthetic null hypersurfaces, starting with relations between gauges and causal curves.

In this section we will often assume the closed achronal set to be weakly convex; this assumption is justified by

Proposition 5.1

Let

5.1.
Null non-branching and first properties

Assuming that branching phenomena do not occur within
H
permits to deduce much more on the structure of the achronal set.
We start giving the definition of null non-branching.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
H\subset X
be a closed achronal set. We say that
H
is
forward null non-branching
if the following holds.
For every pair of injective
causal curves
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}:[0,1]\to H
, such that
\gamma^{1}_{0}=\gamma^{2}_{0}
and
\gamma^{1}_{1/2}=\gamma^{2}_{1/2}
,
then
either
\gamma^{1}_{[0,1]}\subset\gamma^{2}_{[0,1]}
or

\gamma^{2}_{[0,1]}\subset\gamma^{1}_{[0,1]}
.

We say that
H
is
backward null non-branching
if
H
is
forward null non-branching in the causally reversed space.

We say that
H
is
null non-branching
if it is both forward
and backward null non-branching.

The next two propositions guarantees that the null non-branching condition
is available for a broad class of Lorentzian manifolds.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
(M,g)
be
a strongly causal Lipschitz Lorentzian manifold.
Let
S
be an achronal set and
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
its achronal boundary.
Assume
{H}^{\circ}
to be
C^{1,1}_{loc}
.
Then
H
is null non-branching.

Proof.

Since
{H}^{\circ}
is
C^{1,1}
, there exists a
Lipschitz-continuous, null vector field

L\in\mathfrak{X}(T{H}^{\circ})
.
First, we claim that if
\gamma:I\to{H}^{\circ}
is a causal
curve, then there exists a reparametrization
\eta:J\to{H}^{\circ}
of
\gamma
, such
that
\eta
is
C^{1}
and
\dot{\eta}=L
.
Indeed, by
Lemma

2.7
we can assume

\gamma
to be Lipschitz-continuous.
Then in the differentiability points,
\dot{\eta}\in T{H}^{\circ}

and
\dot{\eta}
is a future-directed causal vector.
It thus holds that
\dot{\eta}_{r}
is parallel to
L_{\eta_{r}}
.
Therefore, we can define the
L^{\infty}
function

\varphi:I\to(0,\infty)
by the property

\dot{\gamma}_{r}=\varphi(r)L_{\gamma_{r}}.

If we define

T(s):=\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\varphi(r)\,{\mathrm{d}}r\bigg)^{-1}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\eta_{s}:=\gamma_{T(s)},

it is then immediate to see that
\eta
satisfies the desired
property.
Consider now
a pair of injective causal curves
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}:[0,1]\to U
, such that
\gamma^{1}_{0}=\gamma^{2}_{0}
and

\gamma^{1}_{1/2}=\gamma^{2}_{1/2}
.
By the first part, we know that there exist

\eta^{i}:J^{i}\to{H}^{\circ}
, which are reparametrizations of

\gamma^{i}|_{(0,1)}
, satisfying
\dot{\eta}^{i}=L
, for
i=0,1
.
We can assume
0\in J^{i}
and
\eta^{i}_{0}=\gamma^{i}_{1/2}
.
By continuity, they can be extended to the closure
\overline{J^{i}}
.
Since these two curves satisfy the same ODE, then they coincide in
the intersection
\overline{J^{1}}\cap\overline{J^{2}}
. The thesis follows.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
(M,g)
be
a strongly causal
C^{2}
Lorentzian manifold.
Let
\gamma:I\to M
be a null curve (i.e., a causal curve such that its

Then
\gamma
is the reparametrization of a geodesic.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.7
, we may assume
\gamma
to
be locally-Lipschitz and that

.
Since the statement is local, it suffices to show that
\gamma

can be reparametrized into a geodesic in a neighborhood of

Let

.
Let

be the light-cone in

, emanating from

\hat{H}

be the light-cone in the Minkowski spacetime. Denote by
\hat{L}
the radial vector field along
\hat{H}
.
Since

is
C^{2}
,
the exponential is a map of class

, and therefore a
diffeomorphism between a neighborhood of

and a
neighborhood of

.
As a consequence,

is a

sub-manifold of

is a null geodesic vector field along

We define the gauge

is the Euclidean norm in

is of class
C^{1}
.
By strong causality,
\gamma

Since by assumption

is causal, it follows that

is

Denoting

, a.e..
As a consequence there exists
\hat{\eta}

\hat{\eta}_{t}=\hat{L}_{\hat{\eta}_{t}}

Since

is a geodesic vector field, then

is a

We conclude that

.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
(M,g)
be
a strongly causal

Lorentzian manifold.

Then every closed achronal set is null non-branching.

Proof.

Let

\gamma_{s}^{0}=\gamma_{s}^{1}

\gamma^{0}_{0}=\gamma^{1}_{0}

5.2.
Ray decomposition of achronal sets and disintegration

We fix the following definition: the set
\mathcal{R}:=(J\cup J^{-1})\cap(H\times H)
will be called the
transport relation
.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
H
be a null non-branching, weakly convex closed achronal set.
Then
\mathcal{R}
is an equivalence relation on
{H}^{\circ}
.

Proof.

The symmetric and reflexive properties are trivially verified. We are therefore only left with checking the transitive property.

Suppose then
(x,y),(y,z)\in\mathcal{R}
.
Without any loss of generality we can assume that all these points are
different and that
x\leq y
and
z\leq y
(the other cases are either trivial or equivalent to this one).
Assume on the contrary that neither
x\leq z
, nor
z\leq x
.
Let
p\in J^{+}(y)\cap H
, such that
p\neq y
.
Let
\gamma
and
\delta
be two curves connecting
x
and
z

(respectively) to
p
, passing through

.
Up to reparametrizing we can assume that
\gamma_{1/2}=\delta_{1/2}=y
, a
contradition with the backward non-branching-ness.
∎

If
\alpha\in{H}^{\circ}
, we will denote by

the equivalence class containing

(5.1)

{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}:=\{x\in{H}^{\circ}:(x,\alpha)\in\mathcal{R}\}.

We will denote by

(5.2)

{H}_{\alpha}:=\{x\in H:(x,\alpha)\in\mathcal{R}\}.

The set

is closed, because
\mathcal{R}
is closed.
We will denote by
\overline{{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}}
the topological

in

.
By definition of closure
\overline{{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}}\subset H_{\alpha}

Remark 5.16
.

As a consequence of non-branching, each of the sets

and

contains at most one element.
Therefore,
\overline{{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}}=H_{\alpha}
.
Indeed, by contradiction assume

\{x\}=H_{\alpha}\cap\mathfrak{a}\setminus\overline{{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}}
.
Since

G
-causal curve
\gamma
connecting

to
\alpha
.
Since
\gamma_{t}\in{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}
,

can be approximated by points in

.

Since the causal relation
\mathcal{R}
is induced by
\leq
, we obtain the following consequence of

Proposition

5.15
.

Let

be a null non-branching, weakly convex closed achronal set,

is the graph of a

.
We need to see that

is an interval, i.e., that

or

\gamma
a causal curve connecting

to

By continuity of

contains all the elements between

and

Therefore,

is the graph of a function

It holds that
\eta_{s}\leq\eta_{t}

and deduce that

is

-causal.
Finally, for any point

We now extend

Notice that in

The next proposition characterizes the equivalence of gauges as an
affine transformation with transverse coefficients.

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
H
be a null non-branching, weakly convex,
closed achronal set,
admitting two gauges

and

.
Then
G_{1}\sim G_{2}
, if and only if
G_{1}=f+hG_{2}
, for some
transverse functions

, with

.

Proof.

The “if” part is trivial, so we consider the other implication.
Fix

two equivalent gauges, and let

Since

\Psi_{G_{2}}(z,\,\cdot\,)

-causal, hence
G_{1}
-causal,
therefore
G_{1}\circ\Psi_{G_{2}}(z,\,\cdot\,)
is affine;
it follows that the derivative in the equation above exists
and

is transverse.
Since

then
\Psi_{G_{1}}(z,h(z)\,t)=\Psi_{G_{2}}(z,t)

We can thus compute

\displaystyle\big((G_{1}-hG_{2})\circ\Psi_{G_{1}}(z,t)\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1-h(z)\bigg(G_{2}\circ\Psi_{G_{2}}\bigg(z,\frac{t}{h(z)}\bigg)\bigg)^{\prime}=0,

∎

The next proposition guarantees the existence of a
\mathcal{A}
-measurable cross-section for the

\mathcal{R}
.
Here
\mathcal{A}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated

Proposition 5.19

(Measurable selection)
.

Let
H
be a null non-branching, causal, weakly convex closed achronal set.
Then there exists a
\mathcal{A}
-measurable quotient map
\mathfrak{Q}:{H}^{\circ}\to{H}^{\circ}
for the equivalence relation

\mathcal{R}
, i.e.,
for all

(x,\mathfrak{Q}(x))\in\mathcal{R}

\mathfrak{Q}(x)=\mathfrak{Q}(y)\iff(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}(x).

Proof.

We construct a cross-section
Q\subset{H}^{\circ}
, as follows.
Let
(U_{n})_{n}
be a countable base for the topology of
X
, such that
U_{n}

are precompact.
Consider the compact sets
C_{n}:=\overline{U_{n}\cap{H}^{\circ}}\subset H
.
Define

(5.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{n}:=P_{1}((J^{-1}\setminus\Delta)\cap(C_{n}\times C_{n}))=\{x\in C_{n}\colon\exists y\in C_{n},x<y\},\end{aligned}

The sets

are
\sigma
-compact, as they are projections of
intersections of
\sigma
-compact sets (
\Delta
is the diagonal,
whose complementary is open, thus
\sigma
-compact,
by the properness of the topology).
Define

(5.6)

\displaystyle L_{n}:=C_{n}\cap{H}^{\circ}\setminus A_{n}=\{x\in C_{n}\cap{H}^{\circ}\colon\nexists y\in C_{n},x<y\},

\displaystyle B_{n}:=P_{1}(\mathcal{R}\cap({H}^{\circ}\times L_{n}))\cap{H}^{\circ}=\{x\in{H}^{\circ}\colon\text{the set }{H}^{\circ}_{x}\cap L_{n}\text{ is non-empty}\},

Finally, define

(5.7)

E_{n}:=B_{n}\setminus\bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}B_{1}\bigg),\qquad Q_{n}:=L_{n}\cap E_{n},

which belong to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by analytic sets.
Define

(5.8)

Next, we show that the set

is indeed a cross-sections.

Step 1.
For all
x\in{H}^{\circ}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

contains at most one element.
Fix
x\in{H}^{\circ}\cap\mathcal{R}(U)
.
Assume on the contrary that there exists

,

.
Then either
v\leq w
or
w\leq v
.
If

, then

, because (
5.6
) would be contradicted by

.
Analogously,

leads to a contradiction.

Step 2.
For all
x\in{H}^{\circ}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

L_{n}\cap{H}^{\circ}_{x}

contain one element.

Fix
x\in{H}^{\circ}
.
Then there exist two points
y,z\in{H}^{\circ}_{x}
,
such that
y<x<z
.
Since
H
is causal,
x\notin J^{-}(y)\cup J^{+}(z)
,
therefore there exist
n
and
W
, such that
U_{n}

is a neighborhood of
x
, and
W
a neighborhood of

J^{-}(y)\cup J^{+}(z)
, such that

.
Thus,

.
Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to{H}^{\circ}
be a causal curve connecting

to

Let
\bar{t}:=\sup\{t:\gamma_{t}\in C_{n}\}<1
and let

We claim that

, such
that

If

We notice that

is neighborhood of

.

An immediate consequence of the last step is that for all

there exists

, such that

.
Therefore, it holds that

, for the

.
As a consequence, for all

Step 3.
Construction and measurability of the quotient map
\mathfrak{Q}
.

Define the map
\mathfrak{Q}
as
\mathfrak{Q}(x)=\alpha
, if

and

In other words, the graph of the map

is

We next show that the map

We adopt the convention that

Fix an open set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{-1}(W)

\displaystyle=P_{1}(\mathcal{R}\cap{H}^{\circ}\times(Q\cap W))\overset{\eqref{eq:def:Qpm}}{=}P_{1}\bigg(\mathcal{R}\cap{H}^{\circ}\times\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(L_{n}\cap W\cap E_{n}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{1}(\mathcal{R}\cap{H}^{\circ}\times(L_{n}\cap W\cap E_{n}))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:stable-set}}{=}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{1}(\mathcal{R}\cap{H}^{\circ}\times(L_{n}\cap W)\cap E_{n}\times E_{n})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{1}(\mathcal{R}\cap{H}^{\circ}\times(L_{n}\cap W))\cap E_{n},

-algebra generated by analytic sets;

Theorem 5.20
.

Let

6.1.
Existence and uniqueness of a monotone light-like Optimal Transport

Definition 6.1

(Monotone set)
.

Given
(Y,\leq)
a partially ordered set,
A\subset Y\times Y
is said to be
monotone

if

x_{1}\leq x_{2},\ x_{1}\neq x_{2}\Longrightarrow y_{1}\leq y_{2},\quad\text{for all }(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in A.

Definition 6.2

(Monotone plans)
.

Given a topological causal space
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
and two
probability measures
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, a coupling

\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is called
monotone
if
it is concentrated on a Borel set
A\subset X\times X
that is
monotone in the sense of
Definition

6.1
in
(X,\leq)
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
H
be a null non-branching, weakly convex,
closed achronal set admitting
a gauge function
G
.
Let
A\subset J\cap({H}^{\circ}\times{H}^{\circ})
.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

The set
A
is monotone in
(H,\leq)
.

(2)

for all
\alpha\in Q
, there exists a monotone set

B_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, such that

(6.1)

A\cap({H}^{\circ}_{\alpha}\times{H}^{\circ}_{\alpha})\subset\Psi_{G}(\alpha,\,\cdot\,)\otimes\Psi_{G}(\alpha,\,\cdot\,)(B_{\alpha})

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2).
Take as

B_{\alpha}:=(\Psi_{G}(\alpha,\,\cdot\,)\otimes\Psi_{G}(\alpha,\,\cdot\,))^{-1}(A)
.
The fact that
B_{\alpha}
is monotone is trivial.

(2)
\implies
(1).
Fix
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in A
.
If
x_{1}<x_{2}
, then all these four points belong to the
same class
H_{\alpha}
.
Then
x_{i}=\Psi_{G}(\alpha,t_{i})
and
s_{i}=\Psi_{G}(\alpha,s_{i})
, for some

(t_{i},s_{i})\in B_{\alpha}
,
i=1,2
.
The assumption
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
gives
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
and the
monotonicity of
B_{\alpha}
gives
s_{1}\leq s_{2}
, concluding the
proof.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
H
be a non-branching, weakly convex,

.
Let

\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}({H}^{\circ})

\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\neq\emptyset

\pi\in\Pi_{\leq}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

Proof.

Let

Step 1.
Existence.

\mathfrak{q}:=\mathfrak{Q}_{\sharp}(\mu_{1})=\mathfrak{Q}^{1}_{\sharp}(\bar{\pi}).

(6.4)

\bar{\pi}=\int_{Q}\bar{\pi}_{\alpha}\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha),

\bar{\pi}_{\alpha}

the fibers of
\mathfrak{Q}^{1}

Since
\pi(J)=1
, then the measure
\bar{\pi}_{\alpha}
are concentrated

.

Synthetic Hawking’s Area Theorem

In this section, as an application of the theory
developed above, we provide a new formulation of Hawking’s Area Theorem, where the assumption that the Ricci
curvature is non-negative on null vectors is replaced by the

\mathsf{NC}^{e}
condition.

Firstly, we give a suitable
definition for the area of a cross-section that is compatible with the smooth one. It will be convenient to consider also its local version;
for this purpose, recall the notation

\mathcal{R}(A)=(J^{-}(A)\cup J^{+}(A))\cap H,

for any subset

of the null non-branching synthetic null hypersurface
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})

Definition 7.1
.

Let
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
be a null non-branching synthetic null hypersurface.
Let

be a set.
We define the (future)
\varepsilon
-enlargement of

(7.1)

The (future) Minkowski content of

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{G}^{+}(S):=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(S^{+}_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}.

Let

with respect to

The definition below shall be read as a synthetic counterpart
of the smooth notion of a
null hypersurface being future geodesically complete.

Definition 7.2
.

Let

be a
closed achronal set admitting a gauge function

We say that

We are now in position to state and prove Hawking’s area theorem.

Let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}((S_{2})_{\varepsilon}^{+})

8.
Penrose’s singularity theorem for synthetic and continuous spacetimes

In this section, we propose a way to extend Penrose’s singularity
theorem to the setting of a continuous spacetime (i.e., when the
Lorentzian metric is continuous).
Recall that Penrose’s singularity theorem
[
58
]
states that if a spacetime satisfyng the Null Energy Condition admits a non-compact Cauchy hypersurface and it contains a trapped surface, then it is null geodesic incomplete; i.e., there exists a null geodesic
\gamma
(in the sense that it solves the geodesics-ODE:
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}
) whose maximal domain of definition is strictly contained in
\mathbb{R}
.
Besides the proof, a first challenge in generalizing such a result for a continuous spacetime is to make sense of the very statement. We first start with the concept of null completeness.

8.1.
Weak null completeness

In the smooth setting, it is known to experts that the incomplete geodesic yielded by Penrose’s singularity theorem is achronal (see
[
58
,
60
]
,
[
2
, Sec. 12.4]
,
[
47
, Th. 2.67]
).
Negating the existence of such a geodesic therefore amounts to requiring that every maximally extended geodesic is either complete or fails to remain achronal (or, equivalently, fails to be maximizing).
We adopt this condition as the definition of
weak null completeness
, as it admits an equivalent synthetic characterization, which we establish below.

Definition 8.1

(Weak null completeness)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a strongly causal Lorentzian manifold.
We say that
(M,g)
is
weakly future null complete
if for any
maximally-defined (on the right) null geodesic (in the differential
sense)
\gamma:[0,b)\to M
, either
b=\infty
, or

\gamma_{0}\ll\gamma_{t}
, for some
t\in[0,b)
. The notion of
weakly past null complete
is analogous, by considering maximally defined (on the left) null geodesics. We say that
(M,g)
is
weakly null complete
if it is both weakly future and past null complete.

It is clear that a null complete manifold is weakly null complete as the case of
b<\infty
never occur (and therefore being weakly null incomplete will be a stronger property of being null incomplete).
On the other hand, we now exhibit an example of a weakly null complete Lorentzian manifold that is not null complete justifying the terminology.

In particular, we now provide a sufficient condition for a
warped product to be weakly null complete but not
null complete. This amounts to show that all the incomplete inextendible null geodesics are not globally maximizing. The geometric idea is, roughly, of an upside down cone-like shape where all the causal geodesic end in the tip and all the null geodesics move in spiral, more and more densely as they approach the tip.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
(F,g_{F})
be a compact Riemaniann manifold and consider the
warped product
M:=(-\infty,0)\times_{f}F
, endowed with the metric

(8.1)

g={\mathrm{d}}t^{2}-f^{2}(t)\,g_{F}.

Assume that
f(t)\leq O(\sqrt{-t})
as
t\to 0^{-}
.

Then
M
is a weakly null complete, but
both null and time-like incomplete. More precisely, every future directed geodesic (either timelike or null) is future incomplete, and every future directed null geodesic is not globally maximizing.

Proof.

In this proof, we denote by
\gamma:[0,b)\to M
an arbitrary maximally-defined
future-directed geodesic in
M
, with
\gamma_{s}=(t_{s},x_{s})
.
We will write
(\dot{t},\dot{x})=\dot{\gamma}:=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\gamma
and
\nabla_{\dot{x}_{s}}\dot{x}_{s}
for the covariant derivative in
(F,g_{F})
of
\dot{x}
with respect to itself, i.e., the acceleration of the curve
s\mapsto x_{s}\in F
. Also, we denote the derivative of the warping function
f:(-\infty,0)\to\mathbb{R}
by
f^{\prime}:=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}f
.
The equations governing the evolution of
\gamma

are
[
55
, Prop. 7.38, p. 208]

(8.2)

\displaystyle\ddot{t}_{s}=-g_{F}(\dot{x}_{s},\dot{x}_{s})\,f(t_{s})\,f^{\prime}(t_{s}),

(8.3)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\dot{x}_{s}}\dot{x}_{s}=-2\,\frac{f^{\prime}(t_{s})}{f(t_{s})}\,\dot{t}_{s}\,\dot{x}_{s}=-2\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\log(f\circ t_{s})}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\,\dot{x}_{s}.

Notice that
|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}=|\dot{t}_{s}|^{2}-f^{2}(t_{s})g_{F}(\dot{x}_{s},\dot{x}_{s})
is constant in
s
, since
\gamma
is a geodesic. We can rewrite (
8.2
) as

(8.4)

\ddot{t}_{s}=-(|\dot{t}_{s}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2})\,\frac{f^{\prime}(t_{s})}{f(t_{s})}.

If
\dot{x}_{s}\equiv 0
then, from (
8.2
), we get that
t
is affine and thus
b
must be
finite.

If instead
\dot{x}_{s}\not\equiv 0
, then

\frac{\dot{t}_{s}\,\ddot{t}_{s}}{|\dot{t}_{s}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\log(|\dot{t}_{s}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2})}{{\mathrm{d}}s}=-\frac{f^{\prime}(t_{s})\,\dot{t}_{s}}{f(t_{s})}=-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\log(f(t_{s}))}{{\mathrm{d}}s}.

By integrating, we deduce that

\frac{|\dot{t}_{s_{2}}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}}{|\dot{t}_{s_{1}}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}}=\frac{f^{2}(t_{s_{1}})}{f^{2}(t_{s_{2}})},\qquad\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,b),s_{1}\leq s_{2},

therefore

(8.5)

\dot{t}_{s_{2}}=\sqrt{|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}+(|\dot{t}_{s_{1}}|^{2}-|\dot{\gamma}|^{2})\frac{f^{2}(t_{s_{1}})}{f^{2}(t_{s_{2}})}}.

We can now take the limit on the r.h.s. as
s_{2}\to b^{-}
, deducing
that
\lim_{s\to b^{-}}\dot{t}_{s}=\infty
, therefore
b
must be
finite.
This proves future incompleteness.

A consequence of the limits
\lim_{s\to b^{-}}t_{s}=0

and

\lim_{s\to b^{-}}\dot{t}_{s}=\infty

is that

\displaystyle t_{s}=\int_{b}^{s}\dot{t}_{w}\,{\mathrm{d}}w\geq s-b,\qquad\text{ for $s$ large enough,}

therefore

(8.6)

f(t_{s})\leq O(\sqrt{b-s}).

We now show that any null geodesic starting at a given
point
p=(t_{0},x_{0})
will definitely enter in
I^{+}(p)
.
We claim that for any
z\in M
,

(8.7)

J^{+}(p)\cup(-\infty,0)\times\{z\}\neq\emptyset.

Suppose on the contrary that there exists
z
contradicting (
8.7
).
Let
L:=(\exp^{g_{F}}_{x_{0}})^{-1}(z)
and

\dot{t}_{0}:=f(t)\sqrt{g_{F}(L,L)}
, and consider the null geodesic

\gamma
starting from
p
, with initial velocity

\dot{\gamma}_{0}=(\dot{t}_{0},L)
.
We will obtain a contradiction by showing that the projection of
\gamma
on
F
passes through
z
.
Equation (
8.3
) states that the geodesic
acceleration of
x
is parallel to its velocity, thus
x
is a
reparametrization of a geodesic
y
in
F
, i.e.,
x_{s}=y_{r(s)}
.
Since
F
is compact, then Hopf–Rinow theorem guarantees it is complete. Then
y_{r}
is defined for all positive
r

and
y
accumulates at some point in
F
.
A simple computation gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\dot{x}_{s}}\dot{x}_{s}=\nabla_{\dot{r}(s)\dot{y}_{r(s)}}(\dot{r}(s)\dot{y}_{r(s)})=\ddot{r}(s)\dot{y}_{r(s)}=\frac{\ddot{r}(s)}{\dot{r}(s)}\dot{x}_{s},

which, combined with (
8.3
), yields

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\log\dot{r}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}=\frac{\ddot{r}}{\dot{r}}=-2\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\log(f\circ t)}{{\mathrm{d}}s},

or, equivalently:

\dot{r}(s)=\frac{C}{f^{2}(t_{s})},

for some constant
C
. Integrating, we get that

r(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{C}{f^{2}(t_{w})}\,{\mathrm{d}}w.

Plugging the expansion (
8.6
) in the equation
above, we deduce that
r(s)\to+\infty
, as
s\to b^{-}
.
This means that
x
runs on the whole trajectory of
y
and therefore the curve

x
passes through the point
z
.

Having proved (
8.7
), we deduce that

\partial I^{+}(p)
intersect each line
(-\infty,0)\times\{z\}
in
only one point, for each fixed
z\in F
.
Moreover, it is well-known
(see
[
55
, Cor. 14.27, p. 415]
) that
\partial I^{+}(p)

is a
C^{0}
manifold, thus
\partial I^{+}(p)
can be seen as a
graph hypersurface over
F
(in particular, it is a Cauchy hypersurface).
Since
F
is compact and
\partial I^{+}(p)
is a graph
surface, then there exists
t_{1}\in(t_{0},0)
such that

\partial I^{+}(p)\subset(t_{0},t_{1})\times F.

It follows that any maximal null geodesic
starting from

will definitely enter
I^{+}(p)
, thus failing to be maximizing.
∎

The weak null completeness condition can be rephrased using gauges.
We recall that if
M
is a smooth Lorentzian manifold
and

a compact acausal submanifold,
a

is
given by pushing via the exponential a fiber-wise affine function

(see

In particular

can be extended to

Recall also that

is called

if the pre-image of every compact set is precompact.

We will now show that if

is equivalent to the existence of natural proper gauges, with the latter having the great advantage of being formulated without any need of an underlying manifold structure.

Thus,
the properness of the gauge shall be seen as a synthetic
counterpart of the weak null completeness
.
The statement of the next proposition is given for weak future null completeness;
one can state a similar theorem for past completeness.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
(M,g)

,
for some

(2)

Conversely, if

,
then for every

either

\gamma

(3)

Proof.

Proof of
1
.
We recall from

that

(8.8)

{H}^{\circ}\subset\exp(\{v\in\mathscr{N}A\colon g(v,v)=0\text{ and }g(v,W)<0\}),

3.12

We check that

Regarding closedness, let

It follows that the curve

.

.

Proof of
2
.
Fix
\gamma:[0,b)\to M

Up to a reparametrization, we can assume

Suppose by contradiction that

.

.

Proof of
3

\gamma:[0,b)\to M
a future inextendable null

Consider

the future light-cone emanating from

.
Applying part

8.2.

Having thoroughly discussed the completeness issue, a possible way to state
the desired result on the existence of trapped sets is the following:
if
S
is a compact
achronal surface, such that
(\partial I^{+}(S),G,\mathfrak{m})
satisfies the

\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
with a proper gauge
and
S
is
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging, then
\partial I^{+}(H)
is compact.

The
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
has already been defined. It remains to discuss the
“
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging” condition.
This is a delicate point, firstly because of the non-smooth synthetic setting, secondly because
of the presence of initial points of
H
inside
S
, where the parametrization given by the
gauge is not available.

We next define the “
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging” condition, followed by a discussion of its compatibility with the smooth notions.

Definition 8.4
.

Let
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
be a synthetic null
hypersurface satisfying the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition,
with
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
, for some achronal set
S
.

We say that
S
is
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging
,
if there exists
\theta<0
, such that

(8.9)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(S_{\varepsilon}^{+}\cap\mathcal{R}(A))-\varepsilon\,\mathfrak{m}^{+}_{G}(S;A)}{\varepsilon^{2}/2}\leq\theta\,\mathfrak{m}^{+}_{G}(S;A),

for all
A\subset{H}^{\circ}
.

Recall that the classical future convergence requires the mean curvature of

S
on both (incoming and outgoing) sides of
\partial I^{+}(S)
to be strictly negative.
If
S
is smooth and compact, this is equivalent to assume that its mean
curvature (in both the incoming and outgoing directions) is bounded above by some
\theta<0
.
Equation (
8.9
) is then an integral
characterization of this curvature bound. The basic idea is that the mean curvature is given by first variation of area, and area is in turn given as first variation of volume. Notice indeed that (
8.9
) corresponds to a second order Taylor expansion of the volume (cf.
[
15
, Remark 5.4]
, for a similar construction in the timelike framework).

A careful reader may wonder about the necessity of the set
A\subset{H}^{\circ}
in Definition
8.4
and whether one can simplify the definition by stating
everything just in terms of
S
. The reason for including
A\subset{H}^{\circ}
in Definition
8.4
is that, in general, there is more than one generator for
H
leaving at
each point of
S
(in the classical setting, exactly two generators) and it is desirable to have a criterion to select exaclty one.
Restricting the analysis to
\mathcal{R}(A)
, for any
A\subset{H}^{\circ}
, allows to overcome this issue.

In the statement of the synthetic Penrose’s theorem,
we will assume the gauge to satisfy the additional condition

(8.10)

\inf_{x\in H_{\alpha}}G(x)=0,\qquad\text{ for $\mathfrak{q}$-a.e.\ }\alpha\in Q,

where
Q
is the quotient set of
Theorem

5.20
. In the smooth
setting, one can always find a gauge satisfying (
8.10
), as discussed in
Remark

3.12
.

Moreover we recall the classical terminology that a compact achronal set
S
is
future trapped
if
\partial I^{+}(S)
is compact.

Theorem 8.5

(Penrose’s theorem in a synthetic setting)
.

Let
(H,G,\mathfrak{m})
be a null non-branching, synthetic null
hypersurface satisfying the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition for some
N>2
,
where
H=\partial I^{+}(S)
for some compact achronal set
S
, and
\supp\mathfrak{m}=H
.
Assume moreover (
8.10
).

If
S
is
(G,\mathfrak{m})
-future converging,
then
\left\|G\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathfrak{m})}<\infty
.
In particular, if the gauge
G
is proper, then
H
is compact; i.e.,
S
is future trapped.

Proof.

We divide the proof in two parts.

Step 1.

By the Localization
Theorem

6.8
, there exists a disintegration of
\mathfrak{m}
,
given by the measures
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
satisfying the
\mathsf{CD}(0,N-1)
condition.
Let
h_{\alpha}:[0,b_{\alpha})\to[0,\infty)
be the density of
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}
.
For the class of synthetic null hypersurfaces
fulfilling (
8.10
)
the relative Minkowski content can be more easily described.
In particular, it will be convenient to look for a class of sets
A\subset{H}^{\circ}
for which the following identity is valid:

(8.11)

\int_{B}h_{\alpha}(0)\,\mathfrak{q}(d\alpha)=\mathfrak{m}^{+}_{G}(S;A),

where
B=\mathcal{R}(A)\cap Q
.
To establish (
8.11
), notice that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(S^{+}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{R}(A))}{\varepsilon}=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{B}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}h_{\alpha}(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

Since
t\mapsto h_{\alpha}(t)
is continuous, we can consider the
real-valued measurable map

Q\ni\alpha\mapsto\|h_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}
.
For each
\delta>0
there exists a compact set
K\subset Q

such that
\mathfrak{q}(K)>1-\delta
and, for all
\alpha\in K
,
it holds that

\|h_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq C
,
for some positive constant.
A direct application of dominated convergence theorem implies that for all
A\subset\mathcal{R}(K)\cap{H}^{\circ}
,
the identity in
Equation

8.11
is valid.

In the next step, we will use
Equation

8.11
in order to obtain an upper bound on
b_{\alpha}
independent on
\delta>0
; this will imply that
\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
.

Step 2.

The concavity of the function
t\mapsto h_{\alpha}(t)^{\frac{1}{N-2}}
implies that

(8.12)

h_{\alpha}(t)\geq h_{\alpha}(0)\bigg(\frac{b_{\alpha}-t}{b_{\alpha}}\bigg)^{N-2},\qquad\forall t\in[0,b_{\alpha}].

If
N\geq 3
, Bernoulli’s inequality
(i.e.,
x^{N-2}\geq 1+(N-2)(x-1)
, for all
x\geq 0
) yields

(8.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{b_{\alpha}-t}{b_{\alpha}}\bigg)^{N-2}-1\bigg)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\geq\frac{2-N}{b_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}t\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{2-N}{2b_{\alpha}},\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0.

If
2<N<3
, the trivial inequality
x^{N-2}\geq x
, holding for all
x\in[0,1]
, implies that

(8.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{b_{\alpha}-t}{b_{\alpha}}\bigg)^{N-2}-1\bigg)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\geq{b_{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}t\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2b_{\alpha}},\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0.

In the rest of the proof, we will assume that
N\geq 3
and we will use (
8.13
); in case
2<N<3
, the argument is completely analogous, using (
8.14
).

Let
\theta<0
be given by
Definition

8.4
.
Consider any
\delta>0

and the let
K\subset Q
be
given by the step 1 of the proof.
Let

A\subset\mathcal{R}(K)\cap{H}^{\circ}
be any measurable set where (
8.11
) holds, with
B=\mathcal{R}(A)\cap Q
.
Then (
8.9
) can written as

\displaystyle\theta\int_{B}h_{\alpha}(0)\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:mean-curvature}}{\geq}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}(h_{\alpha}(t)-h_{\alpha}(0))\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:mcp-penrose}}{\geq}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{B}h_{\alpha}(0)\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{b_{\alpha}-t}{b_{\alpha}}\bigg)^{N-2}-1\bigg)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:expansion-convexity}}{\geq}\int_{B}\,h_{\alpha}(0)\frac{2-N}{b_{\alpha}}\,\mathfrak{q}({\mathrm{d}}\alpha).

The arbitrariness of
A\subset\mathcal{R}(K)\cap{H}^{\circ}
implies the
arbitrariness of
B\subset K
, and thus

(8.15)

b_{\alpha}\leq-\frac{N-2}{\theta},\qquad\text{ for $\mathfrak{q}$-a.e.\ }\alpha\in K.

Since the estimate is independent of
\delta>0
, taking the limit as
\delta\to 0
yields that the inequality in (
8.15
) is valid for
\mathfrak{q}
-a.e.
\alpha\in Q
.
Combining this with (
8.10
), we deduce
that

G(x)\in\left[0,\frac{2-N}{\theta}\right],\quad\text{for $\mathfrak{m}$-a.e.\ $x\in{H}^{\circ}$}.

The properness of
G
implies that
G^{-1}([0,\frac{2-N}{\theta}])
is precompact.
Therefore
\mathfrak{m}
is concentrated on a precompact set, yielding that

H=\supp\mathfrak{m}
is compact.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

Inspecting the proof of
Theorem

8.5
, one can check that the assumption (
8.10
) can be replaced by the following weaker (but slightly more technical) condition:
the function

\alpha\mapsto\mathcal{G}(\alpha):=\inf_{x\in H_{\alpha}}G(x)
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\mathfrak{q})
.
In this case, the proof gives that

G\leq\frac{2-N}{\theta}+\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathfrak{q})}
,
\mathfrak{m}
-a.e.. The same argument as above yields that, if
G
is proper, then
H
is compact.

Remark 8.7
.

In the spirit of
Proposition

7.5
, we discuss how

8.5
behaves under a different choice of gauge and
reference measure.
Namely, let

and let

be a gauge for

and
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
be a measure,

.
Assume that

)

G_{1}=f+hG_{2},\quad\mathfrak{m}_{1}=\frac{1}{h}\mathfrak{m}_{2},

for some transverse functions

(8.16)

-C\leq f\leq C,\quad 1/C\leq h\leq C,

for some constant

Let us discuss the invariance of the assumptions of

•

The assumption that the reference measure has full support is trivially preserved (i.e.,

•

Eq. (

•

Regarding the future converging assumption, following the argument of

7.5
one can prove that

is

-future converging if and only if it is

(G_{2},\mathfrak{m}_{2})
-future converging.
In this case, the constant

is replaced by

\theta/C
.

Notice that also the thesis of

7.5
is covariant, i.e., properness of the

on its equivalence class; indeed,

G_{2}^{-1}([-a,a])\subset G_{1}^{-1}([-C(a+1),C(a+1)])
.

Finally, let us point out that the bound (
8.16
) is necessary, otherwise both the hypothesis and
the thesis of
Proposition

7.5
would cease to be invariant.

9.
Stability of the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)
condition

In this section we state and prove two stability theorems for the
Null Energy Condition. The first one is a null counterpart of Sturm’s stability of the
\mathsf{CD}
condition
[
66
,
67
]
.

We recall that, in a metric space
(X,\mathsf{d})
given a sequence of
closed sets
B_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
the Kuratowski-lim-sup
of
B_{n}
is

(9.1)

\text{K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=\{x\in X\colon\exists\text{ a subsequence }x_{n_{k}}\in B_{n_{k}}\text{ such that }x_{n_{k}}\to x\}.

We recall the definition of push-up property.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
(X,\leq,\ll)
be a causal space.
We say that it has the
push-up property
, if
\forall x,y,z\in X

(9.2)

x\leq y\ll z\text{ or }x\ll y\leq z\qquad\implies\qquad x\ll z.

The push-up property is satisfied in
locally-Lipschitz Lorentzian manifolds
[
18
]

and, more generally, in Lorentzian pre-length spaces
[
35
]
(see also
[
39
]
for the case of
C^{0}
-Lorentzian metrics).

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
(X,\ll,\leq,\mathfrak{T})
be a topological causal space
satisfying the push-up property and let
N>1
.
Let
(H_{n},G_{n},\mathfrak{m}_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, be a sequence of
synthetic null hypersurfaces, with
\mathfrak{m}_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{n})
.
Assume the following.

(1)

There exist monotone
(recall
Definition

6.2
)
transport plans
\Lambda_{n}\in\Pi(\mathfrak{m}_{\infty},\mathfrak{m}_{n})
,
such that

(9.3)

\Lambda_{n}\rightharpoonup(\id,\id)_{\sharp}\mathfrak{m}_{\infty},\qquad\text{ in duality with continuous and bounded functions}.

(2)

For every compact set
K\subset X
, the set of curves

(9.4)

\{\gamma\in C([0,1];X)\colon\gamma\in C_{G_{n}}\text{ for some
$n\in\mathbb{N}$ and }\gamma_{i}\in K,\,i=0,1\}\text{ is precompact.}

(3)

Denoted by

the set of
G_{n}
-causal curves, it
holds that

(9.5)

\text{\rm K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{G_{n}}\subset C_{G_{\infty}}.

If the synthetic null hypersurfaces
(H_{n},G_{n},\mathfrak{m}_{n})
satisfy the
\mathsf{NC}^{e}(N)

condition, for all

(H_{\infty},G_{\infty},\mathfrak{m}_{\infty})

Proof.

Let
\mu_{i}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{\infty})

\mathfrak{m}_{\infty}

Step 1
. Definition of the approximations
\mu_{i}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{n})
,

We disintegrate

Notice that, by monotonicity of

(P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,x}\otimes(P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,y}(J)=1.

We push the measures

and
\hat{\pi}_{\infty}
, as follows

(9.8)

\mu_{i}^{n}:=\int_{H_{\infty}}(P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,x}\,\mu_{i}^{\infty}({\mathrm{d}}x),\qquad\hat{\pi}_{n}:=\int_{H_{\infty}\times H_{\infty}}((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,x})\otimes((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,y})\,\hat{\pi}_{\infty}({\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y),

where

is the projection on the second variable.
We now check that

\displaystyle(P_{1})_{\sharp}\hat{\pi}_{n}

\displaystyle=(P_{1})_{\sharp}\int_{H_{\infty}\times H_{\infty}}((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,x})\otimes((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,y})\,\hat{\pi}_{\infty}({\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{n}(J)

\displaystyle=\int_{H_{\infty}\times H_{\infty}}((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,x})\otimes((P_{2})_{\sharp}\Lambda_{n,y})(J)\,\hat{\pi}_{\infty}({\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y)

,

),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{{H}^{\circ}}\int_{X}\varphi(y)\,(P_{2})_{\sharp}(\Lambda_{n,x})({\mathrm{d}}y)\mu_{i}^{\infty}({\mathrm{d}}x)

The next two technical lemmas (included for the
reader’s convenience) recall two elementary facts linking the
Kuratowski convergence of sets and the weak convergence of probability
measures.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a metric space.
Let
(C_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of subsets of
X
.
Assume that
\text{\rm K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{n}\subset C
, for
some subset
C\subset X
.

Then, for any compact subset
K\subset X
, the following holds: for every

\varepsilon>0
there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(9.14)

C_{n}\cap K\subset C^{\varepsilon},\quad\text{for all }n>k.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, up
to passing to a unrelabeled subsequence, there exists a sequence

x_{n}\in C_{n}\cap K
, such that
\dist(x_{n},C)>\varepsilon_{0}
.
Up to a further subsequence, using the compactness of

K
, we have that
x_{n}\to x
, for some
x\in K
.
Therefore, by definition of Kuratowski
convergence (
9.1
), we infer that

x\in\text{\rm K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{n}\subset C
. This contradicts that

\dist(x,C)\geq\varepsilon_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a Polish space.
Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of probability
measures, weakly converging to
\mu
.
Let
(C_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of subsets of
X
.
Then

(9.15)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C_{n})\leq\mu\Big(\text{\rm K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{n}\Big).

Proof.

Define
C:=\text{\rm K-}\limsup_{n\to\infty}C_{n}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
By tightness, there exists a compact set
K\subset X
, such that

\mu_{n}(K)\geq 1-\varepsilon
, for all
n
.
From
Lemma

9.3
, we deduce that
C_{n}\cap K\subset C^{\varepsilon}
, for all

n
large enough.
It follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C_{n}\cap K)+\varepsilon\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\overline{C^{\varepsilon}})+\varepsilon\leq\mu(C^{2\varepsilon})+\varepsilon,

having used the upper semicontinuity of measures of a closed set
under weak convergence.
By arbitrariness of

is closed, we conclude.
∎
