Intracellular trafficking and nuclear compartmentalization of AAV gene delivery
Authors: Tarun E. Hutchinson, Rajat Emanuel Singh, Jennifer Yu Oliver, Ana Davis, Mark Potter, Jane Hsi-Bell, Mario Mietzsch, Antonette Bennett, Robert McKenna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) capsids are widely used as therapeutic gene delivery vectors. The cellular trafficking pathway utilized by AAVs to transport the therapeutic gene to the nucleus and within the different nuclear compartments is still not clearly defined. This study maps the timeline and spatial trajectory of rAAV2-GFP (capsid packages a transgene that encodes GFP), AAV2-EMPTIES, and AAV2 variants during trafficking, over a 72-h time course using cellular cytoplasmic and nuclear markers by confocal microscopy and image analysis in fixed HeLa cells. This comparative spatiotemporal movement of rAAV2-GFP versus the AAV2-EMPTIES or AAV2 variants was shown to differ in cytoplasmic accumulation, late endosome association, transportation via actin and microtubule filaments, perinuclear accumulation, nuclear penetration, euchromatin, and heterochromatin regions of nucleus. The results demonstrate that rAAV2-GFP capsids were internalized into the cell within 2 hours; they utilized both actin and tubulin for transportation, penetrated the nucleus, and expressed the packaged GFP transgene. This is different from the trafficking profile exhibited by the AAV2-EMPTIES and AAV2 variants that were inhibited from entering the nucleus (except for AAV2-E563A). These data provide detailed insights into the trafficking of AAV both in the cytoplasm and the nucleus where it delivers its packaged transgene.

Introduction

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) capsids are the most widely utilized vectors for gene therapy applications, with ten biologics approved to date by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and/or the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of genetic disorders such as spinal muscular dystrophy, hemophilia, and retinal disorder.1,2,3,4 In addition, there are currently over 150 ongoing clinical trials in the USA alone listed (www.clinicaltrials.gov). The AAVs belong to the Dependoparvovirus genus of the Parvoviridae family. They require a helper virus to complete a replicative cycle. The rAAVs are composed of a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) transgene expression cassette packaged into the T = 1 icosahedral capsid. The capsids are ∼26 nm in diameter and are assembled from 60 copies of three overlapping VPs, VP1 (∼80 kDa), VP2 (∼65 kDa), and VP3 (∼60 kDa). The VPs (VP1:VP2:VP3) are incorporated into the capsid at an approximate population ratio of 1:1:10.5 The VPs share a common C-terminus but vary in the length of their N-termini. VP1 has an N-terminal extension of ∼137 amino acids from VP2 and is called VP1 unique (VP1u) region. VP1 and VP2 are extended at their common N-termini of VP3 with a shared ∼65 amino acid extension, referred to as the VP1/VP2 common region.6 These N-terminal regions of VP1 and VP2 contain important components required for AAV infectivity; these include nuclear localization signals (NLSs), a calcium-binding and a phospholipase A2 (PLA2) domain in VP1u.7 Hence, the capsid VPs not only play a role in the protection of the therapeutic gene, but they are also important for the targeting of specific tissue, trafficking the cellular milieu, and traversing the nucleus to release the gene for transcription and replication (Figure 1).Figure 1Model of AAV cellular traffickingAAV infection is initiated by the binding of the primary receptor on the host cell. Model of AAV cellular trafficking

AAV infection is initiated by the binding of the primary receptor on the host cell. The cell membrane undergoes a conformational change in the presence of clathrin-associated proteins to form the early endosome (pH 6.0). The early endosome is acidified and forms the late endosome (pH 5.5) and the lysosome (pH 4.0). AAV escapes the late endosome and travels to the perinuclear space by either/both actin and microtubule networks. The AAV particle then interacts with the nuclear pore and enters and travels to the nucleolus where genome ejection and replication occur.

AAV cellular trafficking is initiated by the interaction of the virus capsid with the primary receptor displayed on the target host cell. AAVs predominantly utilize glycans as the primary receptors and proteins as their secondary receptors, for example integrin and laminin.8,9,10 Once the capsid binds to the cellular receptors, three pathways have been proposed to facilitate its endocytosis into the cellular milieu: (1) the clathrin-independent carriers (caveolae) and GPI-enriched endocytic compartment (CLIC/GEEC) and AAVR, (2) macropinocytosis, and (3) clathrin-mediated endocytosis and the trans-Golgi network (TGN) pathway.11,12 It is not clear whether all pathways are used equally or if there is a predominant pathway that is utilized for the trafficking of the AAV vector from the cell exterior to the cytoplasm. The most widely accepted pathway is the clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the AAV, followed by the acidification of the early endosome (pH 6) and the late endosome (pH 5.5). The low endosomal pH of the late endosome facilitates the externalization of VP1u, which contains a PLA2 domain that has been proposed to cleave the endosome and allow the virus to escape into the cellular matrix.13,14 Recently, it has also been suggested that the trafficking of the virus occurs via the TGN,15 which hosts both GPR108 and AAVR.13,14 Both the pH and Ca2+of the TGN would be optimal for the activity of the PLA2 of VP1u and would facilitate the escape of the AAV2 from the TGN to the cytoplasm. This is followed by the interaction of the AAV with the microtubule network at the perinuclear region, specifically the microtubule organization center (MTOC). This interaction appears to inhibit AAV transduction. It was also proposed that the RhoA-ROCK-actin pathway may play a role in the transport of AAV2 to the nucleus.16

It is widely accepted that intact AAV capsids enter the nucleus through the nuclear pore by its interaction with importin-β.17 The transgene is released in the nucleoplasm to facilitate second strand synthesis.18 The chromatin in the nucleus exists either in a euchromatin (active) or a heterochromatin (condensed, inactive) state. The heterochromatin is localized around the nuclear membrane and nucleolus periphery. The exact location of the euchromatin is not clearly defined, and although AAV vectors have been detected in the nucleolus, its colocalization with either heterochromatin or euchromatin has not been previously shown. The vector genome will persist as an episome; however, random insertion into the host genome can occur, specifically near sites identified as transcriptionally active regions, promoters, and sites of DNA damage.19,20,21

There are significant gaps in our knowledge of the cellular machinery, including how microtubules and/or actin filaments dynamics play a role in capsid transport, how the capsid is transferred from the endosome toward the perinuclear region, and how capsid enters the nucleoplasm through the nuclear pore, where the genome is released to facilitate transcription and ultimately translate the genome or transgene (Figure 1). Dissecting the molecular pathways involved in these trafficking events may provide information that can be utilized to overcome some of the current limitations in gene therapy applications, such as the high dose of rAAV vector required for therapeutic efficacy, which is complicated by the production and separation of the genome containing particles and the empty particles, the immune response, and the detailed knowledge of the trafficking of the virus within the cytoplasm and nucleus. Hence, the goal of the study is to dissect the molecular pathways involved in the transport of AAV2 vectors from infection to genome expression utilizing confocal microscopy. Comparative analysis of the various capsids including rAAV2-GFP (GFP transgene package, wild-type [WT] AAV2), AAV2-EMPTIES (a capsid void of a transgene), AAV2-VP3 (a capsids consisting only of VP3, with no VP1 or VP2 capsid proteins), and AAV2-Y704A and AAV2-E563A (as functionally deficient capsid variants) is used to explore critical steps during trafficking.

Results

Characterization of rAAV WT and variant vectors

In this study, various AAV2 capsids were used, including rAAV2 WT capsids that package a transgene that expresses GFP (rAAV2-GFP), as well as empty capsids (AAV2-EMPTIES). These capsids were used as a direct comparison, to determine if the packaged genome influences the time point and location of the vector during cellular trafficking. The AAV2-VP3 and AAV2-VP2/3 variants were selected to probe the effect of the absence/presence of VP1u and the VP1/2 common region on trafficking.22 The AAV2-HD/AN capsid is a variant with an amino acid substitution in the PLA2 domain in VP1u 23 that was added to the list of variants tested, to confirm its importance for cellular trafficking.14 The AAV2-E563A capsid is a variant that impedes an identified protease site,24,25 and the AAV2-Y704A capsid variant has been shown to be important for second strand synthesis and transcription.25 Hence, a total of seven constructs were selected to probe cellular trafficking by immunofluorescent. All the capsids—rAAV2-GFP, AAV2-EMPTIES, AAV2-VP3, AAV2-VP2/3, AAV2-HD/AN, AAV2-Y704A, and AAV2-E563A—were purified by AVB affinity chromatography. The VP content and sample purity were verified by SDS PAGE and the capsid integrity determined by negative stain EM, for a representative group (rAAV2-GFP, AAV2-EMPTIES, AAV2-VP3, and AAV2-HD/AN) (Figure 2). The rAAV2-GFP and variant constructs (except AAV2-VP3 and AAV2-VP2/3) had the expected VP1:VP2:VP3 ratio of 1:1:10 ratio, and all the constructs assembled T = 1 icosahedral capsids with a diameter of ∼25 nm. AAV2 EMPTIES accumulate in the cytoplasm during cellular trafficking

In immunofluorescence assays, it was observed that the AAV2-EMPTIES and rAAV2-GFP penetrate the cells with the peak intensity of AAV2-EMPTIES detected in the cytoplasm at 24-h post-infection, whereas rAAV2-GFP is dispersed in the cytoplasm within 2-h post-infection (Figures 3A, 3Bi–iv, and 3C). In the first 2 h, AAV2-EMPTIES accumulated near the cell membrane and co-localized with actin filaments, based on the superposition of AAV2 A20 signal (red) and actin signal (green), producing a yellow signal in the cytoplasm (Figures 3Aiii), which is different from the yellow signal observed in the nucleolus, which is located in the nucleus. Within the next 12 h, AAV2-EMPTIES no longer accumulate near the cell membrane but appear more dispersed in the cytoplasm (Figures 3Av–viii and 3C). At 48 h and 72 h post-infection, AAV2-EMPTIES appear to accumulate at the perinuclear junction with negligible quantities in the nucleus (Figures 3Aix–xii and 3E). The same trafficking phenotype was observed in the A549 cell line (Figure S1), which confirms the accumulation of AAV2-EMPTIES close to the cell membrane at the 2-h time point of the virus infection. AAV2 genome affects cellular trafficking pathway

AAV capsids interact with multiple cellular organelles while trafficking to the nucleus to deliver the genome. One of the first of these interactions is the tight junction protein (TJP1), also known as ZO-1 (Zonula Occludens-1). These proteins are involved in maintaining cell-to-cell barriers, regulating paracellular permeability for molecules between cells, and establishing a link between the transmembrane proteins and the actin cytoskeleton.28,29,30 It has been previously shown that AAV9 caused a modest increase in transgene delivery in a mouse CNS when mannitol was added pre-treatment. Mannitol is known to loosen tight junctions between endothelial cells.31 Furthermore, a study comparing AAV2 and AAV9 penetration of the blood-brain barrier revealed that AAV2 potentially disrupts tight junctions of the blood-brain barrier, while AAV9 had no effect on tight junction protein expression or localization.32,33 Therefore, to determine if AAV2 trafficking was affected by the presence of TJP1, immunofluorescence of rAAV2-GFP was compared to the AAV2-EMPTIES in HeLa cells at TJP1 sites. AAV2-EMPTIES colocalized with the actin cytoskeleton and presumably with TJP1 (Figures 4Ai–iii and 4Bi–v) at 2 h post-infection, while rAAV2-GFP did not colocalize with TJP1 at the same time point (Figures 4Aiv–vi and 4Bvii–x). Similar data were obtained for cell-to-cell barrier functions with only actin staining (Figure 4Bv and x) for reproducibility. AAV2 utilizes heparan sulfate proteoglycan as its primary receptor and multiple secondary receptors to facilitate recognition and binding of the target tissue, which have been extensively reviewed.34,35,36,37,38,39 The next step in AAV cellular trafficking is the endocytosis and acidification of the virus capsid to externalize VP1u. From this study, the AAV2-EMPTIES and rAAV2-GFP stained independently of caveolin-1 (Figure 4Ci–x) but co-stained with clathrin (Figure 4Di–x), during the trafficking process. AAV2 interacts with both actin and microtubules to traffic to the nucleus

Previously, AAV2 capsids have been shown to traffic the cytoplasm via the microtubular network in a unidirectional manner and toward the perinuclear region.42 Both the microtubules and actin filaments form skeletal support and represent the structures used by several viruses to traffic the cell.43,44,45 Although, it is known that both the microtubular network and actin filaments are required for AAV viral trafficking, nocodazole16,46 and cytochalasin D,47,48 known inhibitors of microtubule network and actin filaments, respectively, were used to investigate their effects on AAV2 transport within cells. From this study, both the rAAV2-GFP and AAV2-EMPTIES were observed to colocalize with actin and the microtubule network 2 h post-infection (Figure 5A). In the presence of cytochalasin D, both the actin and microtubule networks appear to destabilize and lose their filamentous architecture, whereas when compared to the addition of nocodazole only the microtubular filaments appear to be destabilized (Figure 5B). Therefore, the AAV2-EMPTIES transport is significantly inhibited by both cytochalasin D and nocodazole to ∼40% and ∼30% of the control (without treatment) in the cytoplasm, respectively, and less than 10% of the control in the nucleus, which was already shown to be negligible (Figure 5Ci and ii). Similarly, transport of rAAV2-GFP in the cytoplasm was significantly reduced to ∼30% and 15% of the control in the presence of cytochalasin D and nocodazole, respectively, with negligible transduction in the nucleus for both treatments (Figure 5Ciii and iv). AAV2 trafficking mutants inhibited at varying steps in the virus life cycle

As shown previously and in this study, the trafficking of AAV2 in cells is initiated by receptor-mediated endocytosis.11,40,41,49,48,50,51 The known primary receptor of AAV2 is heparin sulfate proteoglycan, and secondary receptors include AAVR and integrin.9,10,13,34,37,39,52,53 Structural studies have further identified the capsid residues involved in these interactions, and these have been confirmed by amino acid substitutions.36,54,55,56 Another critical step in the trafficking pathway of AAV is the exposure of capsids to the acidification of the late endosome, which facilitates the externalization of VP1u.26,48,57,58,59,60,61 It is thought that the externalization of VP1u allows the phospholipase domain to become surface accessible and cleave the phospholipids of the late endosome and permit capsid escape.23,57,62,63,64,65 The most updated model of AAV trafficking implies that the phospholipase domain of VP1u is not required for endosomal escape. The pH of the late endosome is pH (5.5–4.0), and the Ca2+ concentration is suboptimal for the PLA2 activity. The TGN has been proposed to be the site of PLA2 activity, as the Ca2+ concentration and physiological pH provide the optimal environment for the PLA2 activity.66 The AAV2-HD/AN capsid with amino acid substitutions within the phospholipid domain of VP1u has no enzymatic activity, whereas the AAV2-EMPTIES have been shown not to externalize VP1u, and AAV2-VP3 and AAV2-VP3 capsid variants do not contain VP1u and were all shown to be defective for trafficking to the nucleus (Figures 6A and 6B). Additionally, it has been proposed that the AAV capsids require acidification to effectively traffic to the nucleus. The two AAV2 variants (AAV2-Y704A and AAV2-E563A) that contain amino acid substitutions in VP3 at a region of the capsid that have been shown to undergo multiple conformational changes at low pH were also screened for their effect on AAV2 trafficking. AAV2 localizes with euchromatin and the nucleoli in the nucleus

The nucleolus is a membrane-less structure found in the nucleus and is associated with AAV nuclear trafficking.67 The nucleolus plays an important role in cellular transcription and the cell-cycle pathway.67,68,69,70 Single-cell analysis using immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization reveals the colocalization of AAV2 capsid and genome in the nucleoli during viral infection. To visualize AAV2 capsids in the nucleus and determine whether they are associated with actively transcribing euchromatin DNA or inactive heterochromatin DNA, rAAV2-GFP capsids were labeled with the A20 antibody.71,72 The nucleoli was labeled with fibrillarin, and both euchromatin and heterochromatin were visualized by binary imaging. Discussion

The comparative immunofluorescence analysis between the trafficking of rAAV2-GFP and AAV2-EMPTIES in HeLa cells illustrate the trajectory required for successful AAV gene transfer, replication, and expression. The data presented provide a baseline time course to probe AAV2 trafficking within the cell; this is also consistent with previously reported timeline for AAV infection.72,73,74 The FACs and immunofluorescence illustrate for the first time that AAV2-packaged transgene affects the cellular trafficking pathway and facilitates uninhibited transport to the nucleus when compared to AAV2-EMPTIES (Figure 3). The accumulation of the AAV2-EMPTIES at the cellular membrane and its co-localization with TJP1 serve to illustrate that the presence of the transgene potentially inhibits the co-localization of the AAV2 capsid with TJP1 and allows the transport of the AAV2-GFP across the tight junctions. As stated before, these proteins are involved in maintaining cell-to-cell barriers, regulating paracellular permeability for molecules between cells, and establish a link between the transmembrane proteins and the actin cytoskeleton.28,29,30 This hypothesis would need to be verified by an assay to probe AAV2-GFP and AAV2-EMPTIES interaction with TJP1 or other tight junction proteins.

The binding of AAV2 to its attachment factors on the cell surface that is not associated with any tight junction protein has been extensively studied.9,10,34,75 The primary attachment factor AAV2 is heparin sulfate proteoglycan, and the AAV2-FULLS and AAV2-EMPTIES can both be purified by heparin column chromatography; therefore, heparin binding does not explain the difference in the membrane association between AAV2-EMPTIES and AAV2-GFP post-infection. The next step in the trafficking pathway is the internalization of the virus capsid. Three pathways have been proposed for this step: (1) the clathrin-independent carriers (caveolae) and GPI-enriched endocytic (CLIC/GEEC) and AAVR, (2) macropinocytosis, and (3) clathrin-mediated endocytosis and the TGN pathway.11,12 Both AAV2-EMPTIES and rAAV2-GFP stained independently of caveolin-1, while colocalizing with clathrin, during the trafficking process. This observation confirms previous data that support clathrin-mediated endocytosis of AAV during cellular trafficking.11,40,41 Acidification of the virus to facilitate VP1u externalization has been shown to occur in the endosome, and endosomal escape mediated by the phospholipase domain has recently been proposed to occur by the interaction with AAVR, followed by transport to the TGN, which houses GPR108 and contains the optimal pH and Ca2+ concentration for the PLA2 activity and consequently escapes into the cytoplasm, on route to the nucleus.13,15,76

The panel of AAV2 variants, which consists of either the absence of VP1u (AAV2-VP2/3) or a mutation in the PLA2 domain (AAV2-HD/AN), accumulated in the perinuclear region and did not enter the nucleus (Figure 6). This is consistent with the known role of VP1u with regard to the escape of the endosome and NLS.7 The two variants, AAV2-Y704A25 and AAV2-E563A,61,77 which have been previously shown to undergo multiple conformational changes at low pH, were also screened for their effect on AAV2 trafficking. AAV2-Y704 was defective for nuclear entry, and AAV2-E563A had a similar phenotype as AAV2-GFP and was detected in the nucleus 24 h post-infection. Y704A was previously characterized as defective for infectivity due to significant reduction in the second strand synthesis of the transgene. This was determined by cellular fractionation and quantitation of the transgene at different time points in the nucleus compared to the cytoplasm. We should also add that similar to our data, the immunofluorescence data also showed that no virus was detected in the nucleus.24 Amino acid substitution analysis of residues D561–E565 to alanines was shown to be defective for receptor binding and infectivity.78 Additionally, the AAV2-E563A capsid has been previously characterized and shown to be defective for infectivity77; however, the trafficking phenotype of AAV2-E563A capsid is still not known.

Furthermore, the transport of AAVs has been shown to be influenced by the cytoskeletal network of the cell, which includes microtubules and actin filaments. Immunofluorescence analysis of AAV2 trafficking was performed in the absence and presence of nocodazole16,46 and cytochalasin D,47,79 which disrupt microtubules and actin filaments, respectively (Figure 5). This observation supports the conclusion that both actin filament and microtubular networks affect the overall morphology of the cell and is important for the transport of AAV2 capsids (both fulls and empties) through the cytoplasm and to the nucleus. The mechanism of interaction of AAVs with either actin filament or the microtubular network is not known but offers an intriguing area of interest, especially based on the accumulation of AAV2-EMPTIES and several of the AAV variants at the MTOC.

The release of the transgene in the nucleus of the cell and its transport to the nucleolus are important steps in AAV transgene replication and transcription. The data presented illustrate that intact rAAV2-GFP capsids accumulate in the nucleolus 24 h post-infection. Approximately 68% of the AAV2-GFP capsids were observed in the nucleus; these capsids based on the A20 stain did not dissociate but remained intact (Figures 3Gvi and 7), and 30% of them were associated with the nucleoli and euchromatin (Figures 7A–7D). These data are consistent with previous data pointing to the nucleolus as the site of capsid accumulation.80 It is not clear if the genome is ejected (loss of packaged genome) at this site, but it does confirm nucleoli accumulation of assembled AAV2 capsids at a site where the DNA is actively transcribing.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provided a spatiotemporal view of AAV trafficking, from receptor binding and endocytosis to cytoplasmic transport via microtubules and/or actin, overcoming tight junction linkers at the plasma membrane and pH-related barriers in the endosome, nuclear targeting, and euchromatin association in the nucleus. Ultimately achieving efficient gene expression is crucial for advancing the field of gene therapy. Future research efforts that further elucidate the intricate cellular mechanisms governing these processes will be pivotal in overcoming the current limitations of AAV-based gene therapy, thus enabling safer and more effective treatment options for a wide range of genetic disorders.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

HEK293 cells were maintained as adherent in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum and 100 units of penicillin/mL and 100 μg of streptomycin (Caisson Laboratories, Smithfield, UT) at 37°C in 5% CO2. HeLa (ATCC CCL-2) and A549 (ATCC CCL-185.1) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained according to ATCC-recommended protocols. Briefly, cryopreserved cells were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath for approximately 2 min and centrifuged at 125 × g for 10 min, and the cryoprotective medium was immediately discarded under aseptic conditions. Cells were resuspended in complete growth medium consisting of DMEM, supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B). Resuspended cells were transferred to T-75 flasks and cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 until confluency, typically within 2–3 days.

Cells at full confluency were detached by rinsing the monolayer with 0.25% (w/v) trypsin–0.53 mM EDTA solution for 5–15 min at room temperature. Cell detachment and dispersion were monitored using an inverted microscope. Dispersed cells were either split at a 1:5 ratio or counted and reseeded at defined densities to achieve confluency in appropriate culture vessels: 35-mm glass-bottom dishes for immunofluorescence microscopy (1 × 106 cells per dish), T-75 flasks (6 × 106 cells per flask), or 6-well plates (1 × 106 cells per well). Cell confluency was monitored, after which cells were infected with or without AAV2 WT or mutant virus-like particles (AAV2-EMPTIES, AAV2-Y704A, AAV2-VP3, AAV2-VP2/3, AAV2-HD/AN, and AAV2-E563A) at an MOI of 5 × 104 vector genomes (vg) per cell. Infections were performed by incubating cells with virus for 30 min at 4°C to allow binding. Cells were either immediately washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (0-min time point) or shifted to 37°C for an additional 10 min, followed by washing with complete medium and further incubation at 37°C in a CO2 incubator for the indicated time points (as specified in each figure). Cells were then washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. For the inhibition studies, the cells were pretreated with either cytochalasin D (1 μM; actin inhibitor) or nocodazole (30 μg/mL; microtubule inhibitor) for 1 h at 37°C prior to infection. Following pretreatment, cells were infected with AAV2 WT or AAV2 variants either in fresh medium without inhibitors or in the continued presence of inhibitors and incubated for 2 h at 37°C.

Site-directed mutagenesis

The AAV2-VP3 and AAV2-VP2/3-only plasmids, described previously,81 containing AAV2 rep and AAV2 cap with a mutated VP1 and VP2 start codon served as the template for site-directed mutagenesis PCR for the production of AAV2-VP3 only and a mutated VP1 start codon for the generation of AAV2-VP2/3. PCR products were incubated at 37°C overnight with DpnI restriction enzyme (NEB, Ipswich, MA) to degrade the methylated template plasmid. The reactions were then transformed into DH5α competent cells (NEB, Ipswich, MA), which were cultured on LB-ampicillin selective media and further amplified to isolate the plasmid. Clones were submitted for Sanger sequencing (Genewiz-Azenta, South Plainfield, NJ) to verify the introduced mutation.

Recombinant AAV production and purification

rAAV2-GFP, AAV2-VP2/3, and AAV2-VP3-only capsids were produced by triple transfection of HEK293 cells, utilizing pHelper82 (Stratagene) and pXRAAV plasmid and the gfp producing transgene pTRUF11. AAV2-EMPTIES were generated without the addition of the transgene. The transfected cells were harvested 72 h post-transfection, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 100 mM Na2HPO4, and 2 mM KH2PO4), and pelleted and resuspended in PBS with 1 mM MgCl2 and 2.5 mM KCl. The resuspended cells were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles (−80°C to 37°C) and subsequently incubated with 125 units/mL benzonase for 1 h at 37°C before centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min to pellet the cellular debris. The supernatants were purified by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation as previously described.83 The iodixanol fraction of the gradient was subsequently purified by AVB Sepharose affinity chromatography and concentrated as previously described.84 The sample was analyzed by SDS-PAGE to confirm its purity and by negative stain EM to verify capsid integrity.

Flow cytometric analysis

Flow cytometry was used to confirm the AAV2-UF11 gene delivery and the kinetics of GFP gene expression. Cells were sub-cultured in 6-well plates and infected at various time points between 2 and 24 h with AAV2-GFP and AAV2-EMPTIES at an MOI of 105. Infected cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS and 2% FCS); 100 μL trypsin (0.25% (w/v) trypsin 0.53 mM EDTA) was added to each well and the detached cells resuspended in 1 mL FACS buffer. The cells were immediately analyzed for GFP expression after gating on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Microscopy

Immunofluorescence microscopy

The immunofluorescence microscopy was performed to determine the AAV2 intracellular trafficking and localization in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments at certain post-infection time point in HeLa cells. The cells were grown, infected with rAAV2-GFP, AAV2-EMPTIES, and AAV2 variants for specific time points in 35 mm glass bottom dishes (MatTek Corporation) as mentioned above. The quantity of AAVs used for the immunofluorescence analysis was based on the physical particle concentration, with 1 mg/mL equivalent to ∼1.45 × 1014 capsids/mL. After specific time points post-infection, the media was discarded, and the cells were stained by the procedure adopted from a previously described method.18 It was then washed with 1mL cold PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 4°C, washed three times with 1mL cold PBS, permeabilized with 0.2% cold Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at 4°C, and washed again three times with 1 mL PBS. The non-specific sites were blocked by incubating with 5% normal goat serum (NGS) in PBS for 1 h at 4°C. After blocking, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated overnight in the dark at 4°C in combination with mouse monoclonal anti-A20 (1:500 dilution), rabbit monoclonal anti-fibrillarin (1:400 dilution), and rabbit polyclonal anti-TJP1 (1:500 dilution) antibodies. The cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with an appropriate combination of corresponding secondary antibody according to manufacturer’s instruction for 2 h at room temperature. The cells were washed with PBS and counter-stained for actin cytoskeleton by ActinGreen 488 (1 drop/mL) and NucBlue (1 drop/mL or 1 μgm.mL−1), respectively. Cells were washed with PBS, mounted with either ProLong Glass Antifade (2D images) or VectaMount (z stack analysis), and covered with round glass coverslip.

The labeled cells were observed and analyzed at laser wavelengths of 405 nm (NucBlue), 488 nm (ActinGreen), 543 nm (A-20 stained with Alexa Fluor 546), and 633 nm (for either α-tubulin, fibrillarin, or TJP1 proteins stained with Alexa Fluor 647) by using smart setup configuration in Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Jena). Under the same laser exposure, the images (single plane or z stack) were captured by Plan Apochromat 63X/1.40 oil DIC M27 objectives, keeping all the microscope settings and software (ZEN 2010 software packages) identical for all treatments and controls. The images were enhanced by increasing/decreasing same amount (%) of brightness and contrast from all exposures in each set.

Image quantification and statistical analysis

The confocal microscopy images were obtained at multiple time points by using Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.40 oil DIC M27 objective. These were used to quantify AAV2 Puncta (stained for A20) and total number of nuclei (stained by Hoechst) in an image. The microscopy images (set of five) for each time point and treatments were processed by Fiji ImageJ software program, as the images were split for A20 and nuclei-stained channels. The threshold for brightness and contrast was set by selecting “Triangle” threshold for all the images. The threshold images for both the channels were converted into binary images. The nuclei binary image was outlined for nuclei and A20 binary image processed for A20 puncta count by point selection using appropriate controls for all the given parameters. The outline image of nuclei was overlaid on the point-selected (A20 puncta count) image to form composite image. The total number of A20 puncta was counted (intracellular and intranuclear) for all the cells in an image, across all treatments, time points, and controls. The numbers were used for statistical analysis and graphical representation. The data are shown as mean ± SEM. The statistical difference in puncta counts between immunofluorescence images (AAV2GFP vs. AAV2 EMPTIES) for each time point was determined by two tailed Student’s unpaired t test. The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and is represented by ∗. The data were collected for at least three biological replicas. Control images were also collected before the addition of rAAV vectors or treatment (Figure S2).

Comprehensive z stack analysis of rAAV2-GFP infection was obtained by the acquisition of images of specific regions of the nucleus of HeLa Cells. These images were acquired and separated into individual channels. Binary images were generated for each channel, and compartment-specific masks were created to localize viral particles within defined cellular compartments. The binary viral signal was then overlaid with the compartment masks to refine and confirm accurate localization. The image shown is representative of multiple images processed using the same workflow.
