Heterogeneity in the association between cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline: a pooled analysis of five prospective cohort studies from 24 countries
Authors: Juanjuan Li, Lili Cui, Yuan Xu, Yuyan Ding, Junhao Huo, Dongmei Niu, Hong Chen, Bo Kou, Jiangping Li, Liqun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe assessed the association between cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cognitive decline in adults aged 50 years or older using pooled data from five multinational cohorts, quantified heterogeneity, and explored its potential sources.METHODSIndividual‐level data from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), Health and Retirement Study, Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe, English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, and Mexican Health and Aging Study (83,159 adults ≥50 years) were pooled. Linear mixed models were used to estimate CVD–cognitive decline associations, followed by random‐effects meta‐analysis. Heterogeneity was explored via descriptive stratification analyses, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses. In supplementary analyses, we fitted time‐varying CVD models, mutually adjusted models for stroke and heart disease, and separate time‐varying models for stroke and heart disease as sensitivity analyses.RESULTSSubstantial heterogeneity across cohorts in the association between CVD and cognitive decline (I 2 = 97.5%, p < 0.001) was observed. The stratification analyses revealed that CHARLS differed from the other four cohorts across multiple characteristics (region, sex, body mass index, and education). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings, although the association reversed in CHARLS when analyses were restricted to participants with CVD not taking medication. Additionally, heart disease correlated with accelerated cognitive decline across four Western cohorts, with heart disease‐to‐stroke ratios between 1.8:1 and 5.2:1, while stroke alone showed no significant link in any cohort.DISCUSSIONThe association between CVD and cognitive decline varies markedly across populations, with opposite directions observed between Chinese and Western cohorts. This heterogeneity may be partially explained by variations in the composition of heart disease and the use of medication across five cohorts.

BACKGROUND

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, particularly among middle‐aged and older adults. 1 According to a 2019 study, CVD accounted for over 17 million deaths annually, representing 29% of total global mortality. 2 In China, CVD contributed to approximately 4.58 million deaths in the same year, accounting for 43% of all deaths. 3 With accelerating population aging, the prevalence and burden of CVD are projected to rise further, 4 , 5 , 6 underscoring the need to understand its broader health consequences, including its association with cognitive function. 7

The relationship between CVD and cognitive decline is biologically plausible. 8 Conditions such as heart failure and severe coronary artery disease can impair cardiac output, leading to chronic or intermittent cerebral hypoperfusion. 9 This may trigger oxidative stress, neuronal damage, and synaptic dysfunction, ultimately contributing to cognitive impairment. 10 Additional mechanisms include inflammatory responses, cerebral microbleeds, microembolism, and adverse drug events. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Moreover, CVD shares multiple modifiable risk factors with cognitive decline, 15 collectively termed “Life's Essential 8” by the American Heart Association, including hypertension, hyperglycemia, obesity, and physical inactivity. 16

Emerging evidence indicates that the association between CVD and cognitive decline may vary across different populations. This variability is likely attributable to differences in CVD risk factor profiles, 17 genetic predispositions, 18 environmental influences, 19 and access to healthcare. 20 For instance, the Health and Aging Brain Study has demonstrated that cardiometabolic health significantly contributes to racial and ethnic disparities in cognitive aging, with interventions targeting diabetes and hypertension expected to produce the most substantial cognitive improvements among non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic populations. 17 Additionally, a comparative study of Koreans and UK populations revealed ethnic disparities in the combined effects of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype and vascular risk factors on accelerated brain aging. 18 This suggests that the interaction between genetic susceptibility and vascular burden may not be consistent across different ancestral groups. Furthermore, the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis found that subclinical cardiovascular disorders were differentially associated with cognitive decline and impairment across racial and ethnic groups. 19 These findings lead us to hypothesize that the relationship between CVD and cognitive decline may vary across populations with distinct ancestral and geographical backgrounds.

Despite growing evidence, existing studies have notable limitations. Most were conducted in single countries or high‐income regions, limiting generalizability across diverse socioeconomic and ethnic contexts. Many rely on cross‐sectional designs, which cannot establish temporal sequencing or long‐term cognitive trajectories. Furthermore, there is limited large‐scale prospective evidence exploring factors that may moderate the association between CVD and cognition, and no consensus exists on whether this relationship varies systematically across populations.

To address these gaps, we analyzed individual‐level data from five large, nationally representative longitudinal cohort studies – China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) (China), Health and Retirement Study (HRS) (USA), Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) (Europe), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) (UK), and Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) (Mexico) – which encompass diverse cultural, socioeconomic, and health‐system contexts. Using linear mixed‐effects models to account for repeated measurements and individual‐level variation, we aimed to systematically evaluate the association between CVD and longitudinal cognitive trajectories, with a particular focus on quantifying heterogeneity across cohorts and identifying potential sources of variation.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We searched PubMed and Web of Science to identify studies examining the association of CVD and cognitive decline. Relatively few population‐based investigations quantified the association between CVD and cognitive decline, and harmonized, cross‐cohort evidence spanning Western and non‐Western populations remains limited. Interpretation: Our findings reveal substantial cross‐cohort heterogeneity in the CVD–cognition relationship, with a protective effect in CHARLS but harmful effects in HRS and SHARE. This heterogeneity may be partially explained by variations in the composition of heart disease and the use of medication across five cohorts. Future directions: (1) Determine whether CHARLS findings are due to selection bias, unmeasured confounding, or real population differences; (2) examine how country‐level CVD treatment and medication coverage affect cognitive outcomes; (3) determine whether harmonized cognitive tests can distinguish measurement errors from actual population differences; and (4) identify which cognitive domains consistently link to CVD across various populations and healthcare contexts.

METHODS

Ethics

All cohorts received ethical approval from their respective Institutional Review Boards: CHARLS (Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, IRB00001052‐11015), HRS (University of Michigan), SHARE (Max Planck Society), ELSA (National Research Ethics Service South Central‐Berkshire), and MHAS (University of Texas Medical Branch, Mexican National Institute of Public Health, and Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography). All participants provided written informed consent. No additional ethical approval was required for this study. The authors were not involved in primary data collection.

Study design and population

We analyzed individual‐level data from five prospective longitudinal cohort studies of aging: CHARLS (China), HRS (USA), SHARE (Europe), ELSA (UK), and MHAS (Mexico). These cohorts provide harmonized measures of cognitive function, CVD, and key covariates. The Program on Global Aging, Health and Policy partnered with the data producers of these surveys to distribute internationally comparable data to the research community, thereby facilitating cross‐country analyses on aging.

We included participants aged 50 years or older at baseline from the following waves: CHARLS waves 1 to 4 (2011 to 2018), HRS waves 11 to 14 (2012 to 2018), SHARE waves 4 to 7 (2011 to 2017), ELSA waves 7 to 9 (2014 to 2019), and MHAS waves 3 to 6 (2012 to 2021). Participants with missing CVD status or incomplete baseline cognitive assessments were excluded. Thus, participants with cognitive impairment or dementia were included at baseline if they had complete cognitive assessments. The final analytical sample comprised 83,159 individuals. Measures

Assessment of CVD

CVD was defined consistently across five cohorts based on self‐reported physician diagnosis, including (1) heart disease (e.g., myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, and congestive heart failure); (2) stroke; or (3) current use of prescribed medication for any of these conditions. 21 , 22 Current use of prescribed medication for any of these conditions refers to participants who reported taking prescribed medication for heart disease or stroke. In all cohorts, participants were asked about medication use only after reporting a physician diagnosis. This definition was validated in previous studies; for example, in ELSA, the agreement between self‐reported coronary heart disease and medical records was 77.5%. 14 The definition is therefore acceptable for large‐scale epidemiological studies.

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed across three domains: memory, numeracy, and orientation. 23

Memory was measured using immediate and delayed recall tests based on the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test. Participants recalled a 10‐word list immediately and after a delay. Scores from both trials were summed, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 20.

Numeracy was assessed using the serial sevens test (subtracting 7 from 100 up to five times) in CHARLS, HRS, SHARE, and ELSA (score range 0 to 5) and the animal naming fluency test in MHAS (score range 0 to 66).

Orientation was evaluated by asking participants to name the month, date, day of the week, and year (score range 0 to 4).

Domain‐specific scores were summed to generate a global cognitive score (range 0 to 29 for cohorts using serial sevens, or 0 to 90 for MHAS). 24 To enable cross‐cohort comparability, we standardized cognitive scores into Z‐scores using baseline means and standard deviations. One Z‐score unit represents one standard deviation from the baseline mean.

Covariates

All covariates were assessed at baseline and selected based on previous literature. 25 , 26 Sociodemographic characteristics included age (years), sex (male vs female), and educational level (categorized as below high school, high school, or above high school according to the International Standard Classification of Education, ISCED 1997). Lifestyle factors included alcohol consumption (yes or no) and current smoking status (yes or no). Health‐related factors comprised body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, and depressive symptoms. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, self‐reported physician diagnosis, or the use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (47.5 mmol/mol), self‐reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or current use of glucose‐lowering drugs.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using cohort‐specific instruments: the Euro‐Depression Scale (Euro‐D) in SHARE (EuroD‐12, threshold ≥ 4) 27 ; the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D) in CHARLS (CESD‐10, threshold ≥ 10), HRS (CESD‐8, threshold ≥ 3), ELSA (CESD‐8, threshold ≥ 3), and MHAS (CESD‐9, threshold ≥ 5). 28 , 29 Detailed descriptions of covariate definitions and questionnaire items are provided in Tables S2 and S3.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by CVD status, using means (standard deviation [SD]) or medians (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups were performed using t‐tests, Mann‐Whitney U tests, chi‐squared tests, or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate.

To assess the association between CVD and cognitive trajectories, we used linear mixed‐effects models (LMM) with random intercepts to account for within‐individual correlations across repeated measurements. The models included CVD status (CVD vs non‐CVD), follow‐up time (years since baseline), their interaction, baseline age, and the covariates listed earlier. The interaction term coefficient represents the difference in the annual rate of cognitive decline between the CVD and non‐CVD groups, with non‐CVD as the reference. LMM inherently accommodates missing data under the missing‐at‐random assumption. 30 , 31 For missing covariate data (proportions reported in Table 1), we used multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputed datasets, including all covariates and the outcome in the imputation model. Results were pooled using Rubin's rules. In supplementary analyses, we enhanced the primary models in two ways: by creating a time‐varying CVD model to reflect changes in participants’ CVD status over time and by fitting a mutually adjusted model with time‐varying stroke and heart disease to assess the independent effects on cognitive decline. Both supplementary models employed the same LMM framework as the primary analysis.

Pre‐specified subgroup analyses were conducted by sex, age (≥65 years), education (high school or above), hypertension, and diabetes. Effect modification was tested using two‐way and three‐way interaction terms with CVD status and time. Sensitivity analyses included (1) being restricted to participants with at least two follow‐up assessments; (2) treating time as a categorical variable; (3) excluding participants with prevalent CVD at baseline to examine incident CVD; (4) excluding participants with baseline cognitive impairment; (5) excluding participants with cognitive impairment within 2 years; (6) excluding participants with cognitive impairment within 5 years; and (7) being restricted to participants with CVD not taking medication. To further assess the potential impact of differential loss to follow‐up, we tested two‐way interaction terms (CVD × Age and CVD × Baseline cognition) and repeated the primary analyses using a complete‐case dataset restricted to participants who completed all scheduled follow‐up waves. Additionally, to test whether the effects in the mutually adjusted model were sensitive to its specification, we fitted separate time‐varying models for stroke and heart disease.

We pooled cohort‐specific estimates using random‐effects meta‐analysis, with heterogeneity quantified by the I 2 statistic. To examine heterogeneity, we stratified cohorts by baseline characteristics (e.g., attrition rate, region, BMI, and education) using the natural distribution of the dataset. For each stratum, we averaged cohort‐specific βs and derived 95% CIs from their standard errors (SEs), with non‐overlapping CIs suggesting subgroup differences. Subgroup analyses by sample size (large vs small), statistical significance (significant vs non‐significant), and region (Europe vs other) were also conducted. The regional classification was further refined by performing region‐stratified analyses in the pooled cohort.

Robustness was assessed using leave‐one‐out analyses and five alternative estimators for heterogeneity variance (DerSimonian‐Laird, restricted maximum likelihood, Paule‐Mandel, Hunter‐Schmidt, and Sidik‐Jonkman).

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1), with a two‐tailed significance level of 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of participants

The mean age, sex distribution, and CVD status of the cohorts were assessed. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics (Table S4 shows the prevalence of baseline cognitive impairment [Z‐score < −1.5 SD] for each cohort, both overall and by CVD status). A total of 83,159 participants were included across five cohorts: 9303 from CHARLS, 18,578 from HRS, 33,469 from SHARE, 8188 from ELSA, and 13,621 from MHAS. In each cohort, participants with CVD were older than those without CVD (CHARLS: 63.2 ± 8.0 vs 61.0 ± 7.7 years; HRS: 72.0 ± 10.6 vs 65.7 ± 10.1 years; SHARE: 69.3 ± 9.8 vs 63.1 ± 9.5 years; ELSA: 71.1 ± 9.6 vs 66.1 ± 9.1 years; MHAS: 69.0 ± 9.3 vs 64.6 ± 9.4 years). The proportion of male participants differed between CVD and non‐CVD groups. Baseline CVD prevalence varied from 7.51% to 28.65% across the cohorts. The proportion of participants excluded due to missing baseline data varied considerably across cohorts, ranging from 9.6% in HRS to 63.6% in CHARLS (Table S5).

Standardized global cognitive function scores were lower in the CVD groups than in the non‐CVD groups in four of five cohorts, with the exception of CHARLS. Specifically, in HRS, the CVD group scored −0.1 (SD = 1.0) versus 0.0 (SD = 1.0) for the non‐CVD group; in SHARE, −0.3 (SD = 1.2) versus 0.1 (SD = 0.9); in ELSA, −0.2 (SD = 1.0) versus 0.1 (SD = 1.0); in MHAS, −0.2 (SD = 1.0) versus 0.0 (SD = 1.0). In CHARLS, no difference was observed, with both groups scoring 0.0 (SD = 1.0). Baseline characteristics of excluded participants versus the analytic sample, by cohort, are presented in Table S5.

Table S6 summarizes follow‐up duration and the number of assessments for each cohort, overall and by CVD status. Median follow‐up duration ranged from 4 years (ELSA) to 9 years (MHAS), with a median of three to four assessments completed per participant. Participant loss to follow‐up varied from 17.2% in ELSA to 39.2% in SHARE, with higher attrition rates among those with CVD across all cohorts (Table S6). Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses conducted using complete‐case data produced results that were consistent with the primary findings (Tables S7 and S8).

Cognitive trajectory changes in CVD versus non‐CVD groups

LMM analysis (Table 2) revealed cohort‐dependent interactions between time and CVD status. In CHARLS, a significant positive interaction (β = 0.035, 95% CI: 0.013 to 0.056, p = 0.001) indicated a slower rate of cognitive decline among participants with CVD, a pattern consistent with the trajectory plot shown in Figure 2A. In contrast, both HRS (β = −0.025, 95% CI: −0.031 to −0.018, p < 0.001) and SHARE (β = −0.097, 95% CI: −0.110 to −0.084, p < 0.001) showed significant negative interactions, indicating accelerated cognitive decline in the CVD groups, as illustrated in Figures 2B,C, respectively. For ELSA and MHAS, the Time × CVD interactions were not significant (p > 0.05), and trajectory plots revealed no meaningful differences in cognitive change between the two groups (Figures 2D,E). Exploratory analyses of specific cognitive domains (memory, numeracy, and orientation) revealed that the direction and significance of associations varied across cohorts and domains (Table S9 and Figures S1–S5). In CHARLS, CVD was associated with slower decline in memory and orientation (both p < 0.05), whereas in HRS, CVD was associated with faster decline across all three domains (all p < 0.001). In SHARE, a significant negative association was observed only for memory (p < 0.001). In MHAS, a significant negative association was also observed for orientation (p = 0.018). No consistent associations were observed in ELSA.

Supplementary analyses

In supplementary analyses, time‐varying CVD models showed a positive association in CHARLS (β = 0.037, p < 0.001), a negative association in HRS (β = −0.057, p < 0.001), SHARE (β = −0.086, p < 0.001), and MHAS (β = −0.014, p = 0.006), with a non‐significant association in ELSA (p = 0.091) (Table S10). In mutually adjusted models including both stroke and heart disease simultaneously as time‐varying exposures (Table S11), heart disease was significantly associated with faster cognitive decline in HRS (β = −0.019, p = 0.001), SHARE (β = −0.034, p < 0.001), ELSA (β = −0.026, p = 0.042), and MHAS (β = −0.013, p = 0.036), but not in CHARLS (p = 0.102). Stroke showed no significant association in any cohort (all p > 0.05). High heart‐to‐stroke ratios were observed in CHARLS (4.2:1) and ELSA (5.2:1), compared with HRS (2.8:1), SHARE (2.9:1), and MHAS (1.8:1) (Table S12). Separate models without mutual adjustment yielded consistent results (Table S13): Heart disease remained significantly associated with faster cognitive decline in HRS (β = −0.019, p = 0.001), SHARE (β = −0.039, p = 0.001), ELSA (β = −0.029, p = 0.028), and MHAS (β = −0.014, p = 0.019), but not in CHARLS (p = 0.237). Stroke was not significant in any cohort (all p > 0.05).

Meta‐analysis of the five cohorts

A meta‐analysis of five cohorts was performed using the Hartung‐Knapp random‐effects model. The analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across studies (I 2 = 97.5%). The overall pooled β coefficient was −0.0207 (95% CI: −0.08 to 0.04, p = 0.3887), indicating no significant overall effect. The individual study weights were 19.4% for CHARLS, 20.3% for HRS, 20.0% for SHARE, 20.0% for ELSA, and 20.3% for MHAS, as illustrated in Figure 3.

Descriptive stratification analyses

Descriptive stratification analyses revealed notable subgroup differences for region, sex, BMI, education, drinking, and depression, with non‐overlapping 95% confidence intervals across strata. In contrast, stratifications by attrition rate, age, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension did not show clear subgroup differences (Table S15). To further examine whether BMI and education modified the CVD–cognitive function association at the individual level, we performed three‐way interaction analyses in each cohort (Table S16). These analyses showed that the three‐way interactions were statistically significant only in SHARE, for both BMI (β = 0.003, p = 0.018) and education (high vs low: β = −0.040, p = 0.021).

Subgroup analyses for meta‐analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted across the five cohorts to evaluate differences in effect size by geographic region, study significance, and sample size (Table S17). A significant negative effect was observed only in the subgroup of non‐significant studies (k = 2, β = −0.0072, p = 0.038); no other subgroups showed statistically significant associations (all p > 0.05). Consistent trends across subgroups were presented in Figure 4. Due to the limited number of cohorts (k = 5), the subgroup comparison was exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Region‐stratified analyses revealed significant negative associations in all European subregions and North America, a positive estimate in East Asia, and a marginally non‐significant association in Latin America (Table S18).

Sensitivity analyses for meta‐analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave‐one‐out approach and multiple random‐effects models (Table S19). In the leave‐one‐out analysis, only the exclusion of CHARLS resulted in a significant pooled effect (β = −0.034, 95% CI: −0.065 to −0.003, p = 0.031), and the exclusion of any other cohort yielded non‐significant effects (all p > 0.05). Heterogeneity (I 2) across these leave‐one‐out models ranged from 91.80% to 98.10%. In the random‐effects model, the pooled effect estimate remained non‐significant (β = −0.021, 95% CI: −0.063 to 0.021, p > 0.05), and I 2 values ranged from 96.21% to 98.80%.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed cognitive trajectories in middle‐aged and older adults with and without CVD across five cohorts: CHARLS, HRS, SHARE, ELSA, and MHAS. Although the pooled effect from the meta‐analysis showed no overall association between CVD and cognitive decline, substantial heterogeneity was observed across cohorts. Specifically, in CHARLS, non‐CVD individuals exhibited faster cognitive decline, whereas in HRS and SHARE, CVD patients showed accelerated cognitive decline. In ELSA and MHAS, no between‐group differences were found. Consistent with our disaggregated analyses, heart disease emerged as the primary driver of the CVD–cognition association, with heart disease‐to‐stroke ratios ranging from 1.8:1 to 5.2:1 across cohorts. This association was consistently observed in HRS, SHARE, ELSA, and MHAS, but not in CHARLS. These findings indicate that the relationship between CVD and cognitive decline varies across populations, underscoring the need for personalized cognitive monitoring and targeted public health strategies for middle‐aged and older adults.

The marked heterogeneity across cohorts suggests that the effect of CVD on cognitive decline is population‐dependent and warrants further investigation to identify population‐specific contributing factors. 32 , 33 , 34 In CHARLS, the finding of faster cognitive decline among non‐CVD individuals may be attributable to screening bias and the healthy user effect. First, CVD patients often receive regular medical monitoring and interventions that could mitigate cognitive decline. 35 , 36 In addition, CVD patients in China tend to have higher socioeconomic status, which facilitates access to high‐quality medical services and regular screenings, further reinforcing their healthcare advantage. 37 , 38 Consistent with this, our supplementary analysis of CHARLS data revealed that participants with CVD had notably higher household incomes (median: 17,686 vs 13,000 Chinese Yuan, p = 0.004) and composite socio‐economic status scores (p = 0.004) than those without CVD (Table S20). Second, selection bias arising from socioeconomic disparities may also play a role. In the Chinese middle‐aged and elderly populations, a CVD diagnosis reflects not only health status but also socioeconomic factors, including healthcare access and health literacy. 39 , 40 Third, the “non‐CVD” label may conceal many high‐risk individuals who, due to limited socioeconomic resources, are not screened. Consequently, their cognitive decline is neither identified nor intervened upon, leading to a faster decline trajectory. 41 Moreover, CHARLS showed a high heart disease‐to‐stroke ratio of 4.2:1, with a positive estimate for the association between heart disease and cognitive decline. Despite not being statistically significant, likely due to smaller sample size and reduced power after disaggregation, the positive direction aligns with the positive association observed in the primary analysis for CHARLS, suggesting that variations in the composition of heart disease may explain part of the observed heterogeneity.

In contrast, the HRS and SHARE cohorts showed accelerated cognitive decline in CVD patients, consistent with findings from European and American cohort studies. 42 , 43 Research suggests that CVD accelerates cognitive decline through mechanisms such as cerebral small vessel disease, reduced cerebral perfusion, and inflammation. 44 , 45 Furthermore, European and American cohorts benefit from better‐established chronic disease registries and standardized cognitive assessments, which reduce bias and facilitate the detection of CVD effects on cognition. 46 In ELSA and MHAS, the absence of significant differences may be explained by shorter follow‐up durations, less sensitive cognitive assessment tools, heterogeneous definitions of CVD, or relatively small sample sizes of CVD patients, all of which reduced statistical power. 47 Notably, in MHAS, the time‐varying CVD model showed a significant negative association, unlike the non‐significant baseline‐fixed model. This indicates that using baseline‐fixed exposure variables might lead to an underestimation of the true impact of CVD on cognitive outcomes. Additionally, supplementary analyses of CVD subtypes revealed that the association was consistently driven by heart disease across four Western cohorts, while stroke had no significant independent effect. This aligns with research indicating that heart disease may lead to cognitive decline through systemic hypoperfusion, chronic inflammation, and cerebral small vessel disease, resulting in neuronal injury. 13 , 14 To investigate ancestry's role in the observed heterogeneity, we performed race‐stratified analyses in HRS and ELSA, the only cohorts with race/ethnicity data (Table S21). The results suggest that race/ethnicity may be associated with the heterogeneity in the CVD–cognition link. These observations indicate that the association between CVD and cognitive decline varies according to study design, population characteristics, and assessment methods. 48

To further examine population differences and confirm the robustness of results across cohorts, we conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent patterns of association between CVD and cognitive decline across various subgroups. Notably, the diabetes subgroup in SHARE exhibited a stronger effect, affirming that the overall findings are not skewed by any single demographic factor. Sensitivity analyses showed that in CHARLS, the association reversed from positive to negative when focus was placed on participants with unmedicated CVD. This suggests the primary positive effect may be associated with the use of medication. Other cohorts maintained a stable negative association, emphasizing the need to consider medication effects in CVD–cognition studies, especially in populations with high medication coverage. Excluding participants with cognitive impairment at baseline, within 2 years, or within 5 years addressed reverse causation concerns, with consistent results across all cohorts indicating that pre‐existing cognitive decline does not explain our findings. To further investigate reverse causation, we analyzed cohorts with available diagnosis age data, finding significant heterogeneity in reverse causation evidence across cohorts and CVD subtypes (Table S22). Furthermore, separate time‐varying models for stroke and heart disease yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the mutually adjusted model findings. Overall, these analyses support our conclusion that heart disease is linked to accelerated cognitive decline and highlight the use of medication as a key factor in cross‐cohort heterogeneity.

We conducted descriptive stratification analyses to investigate sources of heterogeneity. Results revealed that CHARLS varied from other cohorts in several characteristics, indicating that heterogeneity might have stemmed from compositional differences. Additional individual‐level interaction analyses showed that BMI and education were associated with the CVD–cognition relationship only in SHARE, pointing to cohort‐specific effect modification. Overall, the heterogeneity may be linked to both compositional differences and cohort‐specific effect modification.

Subgroup analyses further revealed population‐specific patterns. When stratifying by region or sample size, we observed no statistically significant associations. However, stratification by outcome significance showed that the pooled effect was non‐significant among cohorts with significant individual associations, whereas the pooled effect became significant among cohorts with non‐significant individual associations. This suggests that combining the ELSA and MHAS datasets, both individually non‐significant, may yield a small but significant negative association due to the increased sample size. This finding confirms that sample size may be associated with heterogeneity and indicates that small sample studies may produce negative results owing to insufficient statistical power. Region‐stratified analyses showed significant differences across geographic areas, highlighting the need to refine regional classifications to address cross‐cohort variability. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the conclusions. In the leave‐one‐out analysis, excluding any single cohort did not significantly alter the pooled effect or heterogeneity, with the exception of CHARLS. Excluding CHARLS altered the pooled effect estimate, indicating that CHARLS exerts some influence but does not alter the core conclusion. Results remained consistent across different random‐effects models, demonstrating that model selection did not affect the study's findings.

This study has several important clinical implications for cognitive health in CVD patients. In four Western cohorts, heart disease was linked to faster cognitive decline, while stroke had no significant independent effect. This suggests that regular cognitive assessments during follow‐up visits are important for patients with heart disease. Early interventions, including optimizing blood pressure and cholesterol treatments, managing blood sugar, and using cardiac rehabilitation to enhance heart function, may help reduce cognitive decline. 13 Conversely, in East Asian populations, non‐CVD individuals show faster cognitive decline, possibly due to limited healthcare access and lower health literacy, which result in unrecognized cognitive risks. Strengthening cognitive screening and health education is crucial to reducing disparities in cognitive health. When we combined the ELSA and MHAS datasets, the result shifted from non‐significant to significant, highlighting the importance of adequate sample sizes in studies to accurately assess the impact of CVD on cognitive function.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CVD status mainly relied on self‐reported physician diagnoses in most cohorts, so we recognized the potential for exposure misclassification, which could weaken the observed associations. Second, inconsistent data on CVD onset precluded pre‐ and post‐diagnosis trajectory analyses across five cohorts, which may have introduced survivor bias. Nonetheless, our sensitivity analysis excluding baseline CVD cases yielded consistent results. Third, detailed cause‐specific reasons for loss to follow‐up were not consistently available across all harmonized cohorts, precluding a systematic analysis of attrition mechanisms. Fourth, while we used pre‐harmonized datasets for cross‐cohort comparability, harmonization cannot completely remove cross‐cultural differences in cognitive test results, self‐reported health, and healthcare access. These differences may cause residual confounding and should be considered in cross‐cohort comparisons. Fifth, the absence of comprehensive clinical data regarding CVD treatment and severity across cohorts may be associated with the observed cross‐national heterogeneity. Sixth, the datasets lacked data on transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), preventing their inclusion in our disease classification. This might have led to incomplete coverage of CVD and affected the comprehensiveness of our findings. Seventh, although five cohorts are observational in nature and we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to minimize the potential impact of reverse causation, the possibility of residual reverse causality cannot be completely ruled out. Eighth, we note that while our primary analysis treats CVD as a unified entity, the true association is likely concentrated in heart disease, and therefore, our composite findings should be interpreted with caution.

In summary, this study revealed substantial variability in the association between CVD and cognitive decline among middle‐aged and older adults, influenced by population background. Small studies frequently yield false‐negative results, whereas larger studies or meta‐analyses are better equipped to detect true effects. These findings underscore the need for population‐specific, targeted interventions to effectively mitigate cognitive decline in diverse populations.
