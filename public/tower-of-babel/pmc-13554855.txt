Racing the Clock in Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Amir Shabtay, Mony Shuvy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: acute myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock, M-TEER, papillary muscle rupture

Papillary muscle rupture (PMR) is a rare but catastrophic mechanical complication of myocardial infarction, typically occurring within the first week after infarction and associated with mortality rates approaching 80% when left untreated.1 PMR causes abrupt loss of leaflet coaptation, resulting in acute severe mitral regurgitation (MR), a marked increase in left atrial pressure, and a precipitous decline in forward cardiac output, rapidly leading to pulmonary edema, end-organ hypoperfusion, and cardiogenic shock. Persistent severe MR perpetuates this vicious cycle, providing a strong physiological rationale for urgent correction of the underlying mechanical lesion. Emergency mitral valve intervention remains the standard of care. However, perioperative mortality in this population is close to 40%, and more than half of patients are considered ineligible for surgery because of refractory shock or prohibitive comorbidities.2

Mitral transcatheter edge-to edge repair (M-TEER) has become an established therapy for selected patients with MR. Its favorable safety profile and ability to provide immediate hemodynamic improvement have expanded its use beyond guideline-supported indications into a growing number of emerging clinical scenarios.3, 4, 5 One such scenario is acute ischemic MR complicating acute myocardial infarction. In this setting, the substantial risks associated with emergency surgery, including the physiological burden of cardiopulmonary bypass, make M-TEER an attractive alternative. M-TEER may serve either as a bridge to definitive surgical treatment in patients who are initially too unstable to undergo surgery or as definitive therapy in carefully selected cases.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Yue et al6 report a case of ultra-early M-TEER. Their patient presented with cardiogenic shock due to posteromedial PMR and was stabilized using dual mechanical circulatory support (MCS) with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and an intra-aortic balloon pump. Within hours of diagnosis, the patient underwent successful TEER, leading to rapid hemodynamic recovery, successful venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation weaning by postoperative day 2, and survival to hospital discharge following staged complete coronary revascularization. The real novelty of this report lies in the exceptionally short interval between diagnosis and intervention, raising the hypothesis that early correction may be an important determinant of outcomes.

This case adds to the emerging evidence supporting TEER as a potential treatment option in patients with acute MR. Broader data from cardiogenic shock populations suggest potential benefit of TEER, with a patient-level pooled analysis demonstrating improved short- and intermediate-term survival in patients unsuitable for surgery, and the transcatheter valve therapy Registry reporting successful MR reduction in 85.6% of 3,797 patients, which was associated with lower 1-year mortality and heart failure hospitalization.7 Specific evidence in PMR remains limited but encouraging. In the updated IREMMI (International Registry of MitraClip in Acute Mitral Regurgitation following Acute Myocardial Infarction) following Acute Myocardial Infarction registry, 23 patients with PMR underwent TEER, achieving an 87% procedural success rate, significant reductions in MR severity and left atrial pressure, and survival to discharge in 70% of patients.8 However, nearly one-quarter ultimately required surgery, highlighting the role of TEER as a rescue or bridging strategy within a multidisciplinary approach. The ongoing CAPITAL MINOS (Transcatheter Mitral Valve Repair for Inotrope Dependent Cardiogenic Shock) trial will provide the first randomized evidence for M-TEER in patients with cardiogenic shock and significant MR.9

Given the need for rapid restoration of valve function in patients deteriorating with cardiogenic shock, a new paradigm may be considered.10 Rather than viewing M-TEER and surgery as competing strategies, both should be integrated within a coordinated Shock and Heart Team framework. Following rapid multidisciplinary assessment and hemodynamic stabilization with MCS when required, an initial M-TEER strategy may be considered in patients at prohibitive or very high surgical risk. If successful, transcatheter repair may serve as definitive therapy or as a bridge to recovery, allowing subsequent reassessment of the need for surgical intervention. Conversely, inadequate MR reduction should prompt immediate transition to rescue surgery. This strategy requires close collaboration between structural heart and cardiac surgical teams, functioning as a single integrated unit from the outset (Figure 1).

Despite the enthusiasm generated by this strategy, significant technical caveats remain. Patients with acute PMR typically have a normal-sized left atrium, making device navigation and leaflet grasping more challenging. Furthermore, in complete PMR, the extensively flail and hypermobile leaflet is difficult to capture, often requiring multiple devices to achieve acceptable coaptation.8 There is also a unique mechanical concern: leaflet grasping transmits traction to an already disrupted, ischemic papillary muscle-chordal apparatus. A theoretical risk exists that device implantation could extend a partial rupture or precipitate a complete avulsion, causing further deterioration. This reality underscores the need for highly experienced operators and immediate surgical backup.

In conclusion, the report by Yue et al expands the boundaries of what is possible by demonstrating that M-TEER is logistically and technically feasible during the hyperacute phase of cardiogenic shock. However, widespread implementation of an early strategy demands advanced imaging, MCS expertise, and immediate surgical rescue capabilities that are not universally available. Publication bias also likely favors the reporting of these highly successful outcomes. For community hospitals lacking on-site cardiac surgery or structural expertise, implementing this strategy requires major organizational changes in regional care pathways. One such approach is the unique model demonstrated by Bruoha et al,11 where a dedicated structural team from a tertiary center travels directly to an outside hospital to perform urgent M-TEER on-site for a patient with PMR. Whether via this type of mobile specialized team or through rapid transfer networks after hemodynamic stabilization to a surgical center, a coordinated multidisciplinary infrastructure remains the indispensable foundation of success.

Moving forward, the primary question is whether hyperacute restoration of mitral valve competence can alter the trajectory of cardiogenic shock and improve survival in this critically ill population. Answering this will require prospective, multicenter registries with time-to-intervention tracking and, ultimately, randomized clinical trials.
