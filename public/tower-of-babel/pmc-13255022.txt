Transforming pharmacy practice through contemporary leadership models: Behaviouraldrivers of workplace motivation, innovation and professional performance
Authors: Yousef Saeed Alqarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global pharmacy workforce is witnessing a transition from focusing on medication dispensing to patient-centered clinical care. This transition demands a similar change in the workforce, especially in leadership. However, leadership in pharmacy relies significantly on role-based management instead of evidence-based behavioural models. This narrative review synthesized contemporary behaviour focused leadership models - transformational, authentic, servant and distributed leadership, and their effects on pharmacy workforce motivation, innovation and professional performance. A narrative literature review was conducted by integrating interdisciplinary evidence extracted from pharmacy, healthcare and leadership literature. Two models are suggested for empirical testing in future studies to replace existing valid frameworks.A narrative literature review was conducted by searching published literature from 2000 to the present in MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science and Scopus. It's no longer sufficient for pharmacy leadership to continue to engage in a transaction - based management approach. Contemporary leadership behaviour has exhibited a tendency to fulfil pharmacy workers basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness), as defined by self-determination theory (SDT). Evidence suggests that contemporary leadership behaviours are associated with reduced burnout, increased resilience, and enhanced climates of innovation. The pharmacy profession should view leadership as more than a soft skill or a management position and instead frame it as a primary behavioural competency. Two conceptual heuristic models are offered here to further guide empirical research, education and leadership training, given the current lack in evidence.Keywords: Pharmacy leadership, Transformational leadership, Self determination theory, Workforce motivation, Professional burnout, Patient safety, Practice innovation

Introduction

Global healthcare faces many challenges including aging population and the constant fight against chronic diseases with complex treatments. Pharmacy practice has evolved from a place to dispense medications to providing patient-centered care.1, 2 Modern pharmacists are expected to provide multiple healthcare services, such as advanced clinical care, manage medications for patient care teams, enhance public health and lead chronic disease clinics.

The modern pharmacist needs to improve their skills beyond medication dispensing and basic clinical skills. While clinical knowledge is essential, effective leadership is the main driver for success and maintaining a developed role.3 Leadership isn't reserved for senior executives, but it's essential to the success of an organization, the well-being of its workers, and professional performance. Leadership, at all levels, allows the provision of advanced pharmaceutical services, ensuring high safety standards, and the modernization of pharmacy, from the staff pharmacist to the chief pharmacy officer.4

Although the broadened role of the modern pharmacist highlights the need for proactive leaders, there is a significant gap between this need and the function of most management positions. Role-based and hierarchical models haven't proven adequate at motivating a skilled and independent workforce, especially in high stress environments.5 Transformational leadership is thoroughly discussed in management studies literature. However, the application of these studies in pharmacy practice is limited.6

The core issue discussed in this review was the poor practical application of behavioural leadership theories at an advanced level to guide pharmacy education, management and policy. Contemporary leadership models, which are dependent on behavioural factors including motivation, empowerment, and relationships, offer the optimal path to transform pharmacy practice. By exploring leadership models including Transformational, Authentic and Servant leadership, we aim to close the gap between theoretical leadership science and its practical applications.7 A narrative review approach was selected due to the theoretical and collaborative nature of leadership research, which covers pharmacy, healthcare systems and organizational psychology. This approach allows integrating diverse evidence sources and supports theory development, particularly in fields where empirical evidence related to pharmacy is insufficient. Despite the increased recognition of leadership as an essential skill in pharmacy, existing literature is still fragmented, with limited integration of behavioural leadership theories in pharmacy practice models. This has created a gap between theoretical leadership and practical application in pharmacy settings.

Unlike previous pharmacy leadership reviews that primarily describe leadership styles independently, this review integrates contemporary behavioural leadership models with Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to explain the psychological mechanisms through which leadership behaviours may influence workforce motivation, innovation, burnout, and professional performance in pharmacy settings. In addition, this review proposes two conceptual multilevel heuristic models specific to pharmacy leadership practice.

Methods

This study used a narrative literature review methodology to synthesize interdisciplinary evidence related to leadership in pharmacy practice. MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for English-language publications from 2000 to 2025. Search terms included combinations of “pharmacy leadership,” “transformational leadership,” “servant leadership,” “authentic leadership,” “distributed leadership,” “employee motivation,” “burnout,” “psychological safety,” and “healthcare innovation.”

Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to leadership behaviours, workforce outcomes, professional performance, innovation, burnout, resilience, and organizational culture in pharmacy or broader healthcare settings. Full-text articles were subsequently reviewed for conceptual relevance and contribution to leadership theory integration.

Inclusion criteria included studies discussing behavioural leadership models, leadership-related workforce outcomes, healthcare leadership, organizational psychology, and pharmacy workforce development. Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conceptual leadership frameworks, and healthcare-based empirical studies. Non-English publications, editorials without conceptual relevance, and articles unrelated to healthcare or workforce leadership were excluded.

Due to the narrative and theory-building nature of the review, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed. However, thematic consistency, conceptual relevance, methodological clarity, and applicability to healthcare or pharmacy practice were considered during synthesis. A thematic synthesis approach was used to identify recurring behavioural patterns, psychological mechanisms, and organizational outcomes that informed the proposed conceptual models.

The evolution of leadership theory

In the 20thcentury leadership theories transformed from observing the personal features of great men to understanding the leadership behaviours and the situations in which those behaviours are efficient. The transition from identifying inherent personality traits (bravery, intelligence, dominance) to observing behaviours that can be taught (communication skills, vision, empathy) became essential, especially in a context such as pharmacy, where personality is fixed, but behaviour can be changed.10, 11

Limitations of role based management

Historically, the pharmacy profession depended on role-based leadership, which included choosing a leader through formal authority, position or assigned tasks. Despite its importance for the stability of pharmacy operations, it fails to identify a leaders' ability to exert influence to inspire professionals.8 Because pharmacists are educated professionals, behavioural leadership is likely to resonate than a hierarchy-based leadership.9

The full range leadership model

The full range leadership model (FRLM) distinguishes between transactional and transformational leadership, providing the foundation for modern pharmacy leadership.10 Transactional leadership is defined as a leadership where the leader rewards his followers for achieving the desired goals. Rewards include recognition or salaries. This includes both “Contingent Reward” and “Management by Exception” (corrective action). Transactional leadership remains an essential part of routine pharmacy operations, especially regarding critical safety tasks which must be performed in alliance with operational procedures, but it rarely provokes innovation or worker performance exceeding expectations.11 Transformational Leadership (TFL) is regarded as an effective leadership that bridges this gap and promotes an evolving environment. TFL is the most optimal leadership style to promote better outcomes, it promotes employees to transition from focusing on self-interest to group interest. It's implemented by four behaviours: Idealized influence, Inspirational Motivation, Intellectual stimulation and Individualized Consideration.12 Multiple analytic studies have proven the validity of TFL's influence on worker performance.13

Intellectual stimulation holds great value for pharmacists as it allows their roles to surpass medication dispensing to clinical problem-solving skills which validates pharmacists as decision makers. Experimental field studies have proven that TFL influences followers' development and performance, this has been tested and TFL was proven to enhance service delivery.14 The four behavioural dimensions of Transformational Leadership are illustrated in Fig. 1. Despite these limitations, transactional leadership is essential in pharmacy environments where safety requires strict and accurate alliance with protocols.

Ethical and relational models

Models that complement the FRLM are models that focus on moral and relational dimensions of influence.

Authentic leadership emphasizes integrity and genuineness. Authentic leaders adhere to personal morals over external pressures emphasize the desire to share their true self in relationships, known as “relational transparency”.15 Theoretical development emphasizes the benefits of including perspectives related to balance processing and moral perspectives. In the pharmacy field, authentic leadership holds great value, as patient safety requires public trust, which depends on integrity and transparency. Without these qualities in leadership, a culture of reporting mistakes without fear can't exist.16

In contrast, Servant Leadership proposes an inverted power hierarchy. A leaders' main motivation is to serve others, watch them grow and strengthen the community at the expense of their personal aspirations. This model is consistent with the morals of patient centered care. Multiple studies indicate that what distinguishes servant leadership is their primary focus on stewardship.17 Pharmacy leaders who adopt servant leadership create a work environment where employees feel supported, which consequently improves their performance and increases their empathy in patient care.18

Ethical leadership models have proven their influence in moral courage and voice behaviours among healthcare providers. Empirical studies have shown that authentic and servant leadership enhance ethical environment by legitimizing upward communication, increasing pharmacists' perceived ease of reporting near misses or unsafe order errors and decreasing their reluctance to question ethically improper prescribing.19 These leadership styles limit the level of moral pressure in this profession by aligning the ethical practices of the organization with their professional values, which allows pharmacists to maintain their moral agency and preserve public trust in the medication dispensed.20 While these leadership styles are each unique, the three models are complementary, as ethical leadership focuses on the adherence to norms, authentic leadership focuses on the importance of self-awareness and internal morals and Servant Leadership is centered on the interests of stakeholders, along with the growth of the followers.21

Systemic models: Shared and distributed leadership

In an environment where interprofessional collaboration in pharmacy practice is increasing, the “heroic solo leader” model must be abandoned. Shared Leadership explains this concept; it explains that leadership should be situated within a team instead of the individual.22 Team members move to leadership in accordance with the task. Likewise, Distributed Leadership suggests that leadership responsibilities are allocated between team members based on expertise.23

Such models are necessary in complex hospital settings. A pharmacist leads the medication reconciliation efforts, a nurse is responsible for a patients' physical exam, and a physician is responsible for making a diagnosis. So, leadership is distributed based on expertise, and pharmacists can lead in their domains without a formal title.24 This outlook is of increasing significance in as leadership research focuses more on complex and multilayered systems.

While most evidence is extracted from general leadership and healthcare literature, pharmacy-specific literature remains limited, highlighting the need for pharmacy-specific research.

The education practice gap

Despite the existence of these models, there is a gap between leadership education and pharmacy practice.

Curricular management

Many countries require graduate students to complete a program in leadership skills. However, leadership is often confused with management. Students are often taught operational skills, including budgeting, inventory and workflow analysis instead of personal skills like influence and vision setting.25 Leadership is often acknowledged in curricula, but their leadership competencies are merely a collection of soft skills (communication or professionalism, etc.) under the leadership umbrella that aren't described in terms of teachable skills and behaviours. We must increase the relevance and scope of leadership development within and beyond the university curriculum and practical applications.26

However, if curricula doesn't include the leadership behaviours and skills mentioned in these models, then professional organizations, through programs such as the International Pharmaceutical Federation's Leadership Development Programme, provide progressive leadership pathways that include the personal skills that curricula doesn't cover.27 Global needs assessment perceive leadership development as the highest priority for the profession, but most pharmacy education only focuses on pharmacy science and clinical skills at the expense of leadership.28, 29

The shock of practice

A major cause of shock of practice that new pharmacists experience was the disparity between educational content and daily practice. After graduating with the hopes of achieving advanced clinical roles, they are shocked with the transactional nature of a high volume dispensing and management process.30 Additionally, pharmacists in the beginning of their career don't feel safe to practice leadership. The climate isn't supportive so they are reluctant to implement changes or suggest new ideas.31 Teachers personal aspirations for their students and the transactional and operational nature of the workplace are likely to cause new pharmacists to be disappointed toward the profession and their perceived ability to influence the work place. Redefining leadership as a professional identity instead of a positional role is essential to overcome this gap. By integrating leadership into professional identity helps students will tend to view themselves as agents of change.32 The role of leadership in professional identity is essential, as it affects motivations' behavioural drivers, which are needed to manage the current challenges of modern pharmacy practice.

These systemic issues need to be addressed by developing professional pathways and implementing evidence based competency frameworks for professional development at all career stages.33, 34 These structural initiatives cannot fully compensate for the psychological barriers that prevent pharmacists from embracing leadership identity, pharmacists often lack the confidence to see themselves as leaders, even when they're in management roles. Evidence also shows that these barriers to becoming leaders are noncalculative and largely affective, meaning they can be easily overcome with training and appropriate management styles.35 Therefore, training in mentorship and leadership skills can help close the gap between aspirational professional ideals and actual practice, strengthening the professional self - identity needed to sustain leadership engagement.36, 37 Similar findings suggest the implementation of formal mentorship programs and integration of leadership training at the beginning of professional training would bridge this divide and enhance the necessary professional identity for long term leadership commitment.37, 38

Behavioural drivers of motivation: A psychological perspective

Understanding how behavioural leadership impacts the work environment requires looking into the psychological mechanisms that motivate these behaviours.39 Autonomy can be defined as free will and support of ones' actions. While competence refers to the effectiveness experienced in task mastery and relatedness refers to feeling connected and belonging to other people. In accordance with organizational psychology and healthcare literature and adapted to pharmacy practice, self-determination theory (SDT) suggests that three psychological needs must be met to generate high quality, autonomous motivation.

Leadership as a needs satisfier

Modern leadership behaviour fulfils those needs. Transformational Leadership, especially Individualized Consideration, supports competence, which means that leaders guide employees and encourage them to grow. TFL enhances employee empowerment and influences the relationship between leadership and job satisfaction.40 Servant and Authentic Leadership encourage relatedness through fostering an environment of trust, transparency and care. Distributed Leadership encourages autonomy by giving pharmacists autonomy over their work methods.41

Fulfilling these needs enhances pharmacists' job satisfaction. Job satisfaction reflects a mix of environmental, psychological and physiological aspects to employee feelings toward a job.28 In contrast, the lack of these basic needs by using controlling and unsupportive management behaviours is likely to lead to burnout or loss of motivation which hinders the stability of work in the pharmacy profession.9 Autonomous motivation involves volitional action and endorsed action by the person, a style associated with higher levels of performance, wellbeing and resilience among healthcare professionals.42

Impact on engagement and retention

Evidence strongly supports the satisfaction of these needs leading to job satisfaction and pharmacist retention. Due to the global shortage of pharmacists, leadership is a driving factor in retaining employees.43 Supporting relatedness and autonomy in leadership reduces employees Intention to Leave (ITL) and strengthens their Intention to Stay (ITS). Furthermore, these behaviours drive psychological empowerment, which is a cognitive state where professionals perceive their work to be meaningful and their ability to leave a mark.44 The leader who sets a clear vision enhances the value of routine tasks, while distribution of authority strengthens the sense of impact. This leads to Organizational Identification, where professionals perceive themselves as representatives of their profession, and can result in greater volitional effort and loyalty.45

According to Self Determination theory, when these basic psychological needs are satisfied, individuals are intrinsically motivated,22, 45 and this internal motivation causes them to participate more and feel better, as their genuine interest in their work pushes them more than any external factor.46 A work environment that supports basic needs promotes motivation and worker function in healthcare, as well as providing organizational benefits, especially when basic psychological needs are met, such as autonomy, competence and relatedness.42 Additionally, it promotes lifelong learning, and has a positive influence on quality of healthcare and patient safety.42, 47 In contrast, when basic psychological needs are hindered by a controlling or unsupportive style of management, pharmacists reported controlled motivation or amotivation, a situation in which their ITL and wellbeing diminished.42, 48

Burnout, resilience and safety

The evidence linking leadership to burnout and safety outcomes comes from observational and cross-sectional studies, which limits cause and effect analysis. The pharmacy profession is experiencing a burn out epidemic represented by emotional exhaustion, cynicism (depersonalization) and reduced professional efficacy. Leadership behaviours are important contributors to the work environment.49 Especially, controlling or unsupportive management practices which can contribute to increasing emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, leading to high turnover rates and negative patient outcomes.50

The transactional trap vs the transformational buffer

The passive Transactional Leadership style or management by exception leadership, which requires the leaders to only intervene when mistakes are made, creates a climate where employees are anxious and monitored, increasing employee cynicism and exhaustion. On the other hand, the positive behaviours associated with Transformational Leadership can act as a buffer, helping employees tolerate job demands. Specifically, TFL enhances resilience, defined not as a skill, but as the adaptive capacity of people that makes them able to persist and adapt despite experiencing difficulties or adversity, impacted by the environment.51

This adaptive capacity may help buffer the burden of job demands and allows professionals to deal with stressors in an efficient manner and maintain performance with the increasing demands, while controlling environments where the worker has less autonomy and relatedness will minimize the capacity of their workers.52 Workers in such environments are at risk of burnout, defined as emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and low personal accomplishment, leads to difficulty in maintaining employees in the pharmacy profession and compromises quality of patient care.53

Psychological safety and patient safety

A crucial method by which leaders connect to safety is Psychological Safety, the shared belief that the team is safe to take personal risks. Servant and Authentic leaders adopt this environment by admitting their mistakes and regarding failure as a learning opportunity.54 This outcome has positive effects on patient safety, because burnout is associated with mistakes. By limiting burnout and promoting a safe environment, leaders encourage pharmacists to report near misses and safety concerns. According to studies on mistake management, a culture of safety means a culture of leadership, and leadership behaviours may impact the rate and transparency of error reporting.55 Therefore, a transformational leadership contributes to achieving better patient safety outcomes and team performance than a controlling leader who focuses on productivity and performance more than the well-being of his employees which leads to worse outcomes and more mistakes.

Leading innovation in pharmacy

Innovation failure in pharmacy, whether in new clinical services or new technologies, is rarely linked to lack of technical skills, it's rather mostly associated with lack of leadership, which should provide a clear vision, psychological security and empowerment necessary to challenge norms and encourage experimentation.56

Transactional leadership ensures stability and efficient daily operations, while transformational leadership are more capable to deal with innovation in the field of their expertise, and enabling their team to integrate innovative practices that advance healthcare. This integration demands that team members adopt new competencies, including leading an adaptive change process and building teamwork, which are essential competencies to overcome the complexities of providing modern healthcare.52 However, empirical evidence specific to pharmacy driven innovation is limited, and findings are often extracted from broader literature on healthcare leadership.

Behaviours that catalyse innovation

Intellectual stimulation is the primary source of innovation. Leaders must protect the time needed for teams to solve problems, and to ask: “How can we do better?” Instead of “How can we do this faster?” Intellectual stimulation promotes creative performance of the team, a trait associated with transformational leadership.57 Visionary Leadership a “why” that drives change. The transition from medication dispensing to providing cognitive services is a substantial change in the pharmacy profession, but enables the visionary leader to provide a sense of purpose to motivate the workforce to tolerate the transitions temporary inefficiencies and risks.58

Furthermore, Distributed Leadership can achieve innovation from scratch. By enabling frontline pharmacists to act as “clinical champions” for certain projects such as an opioid stewardship program and others, the organization could get rid of the bureaucratic routine that hinders innovation and implement the needed changes that respond to local patient needs. Qualitative research among pharmacists working in hospital pharmacies has identified many behaviours that drive innovation between teams.59 Including benefiting from available departmental resources, aligning new roles with hospital goals, growing a culture that allows risk taking and creative problem solving to achieve team goals, entrepreneurial behaviours and managing complex change processes to enable ongoing innovation.52 Leaders must also have the capacity to work in dynamic variable environments and apply innovation changes during staff and regulation variations.60

Conceptual models for pharmacy leadership

There is limited empirical evidence specific to pharmacy - led innovation and findings typically come from broader healthcare leadership studies. These models were developed using thematic synthesis from the literature, incorporating leadership behaviours, psychological mechanisms (Self Determination Theory) and observed workforce outcomes. The following models are therefore conceptual and heuristic and are meant to be explored by future research than confirmed.

The behavioural leadership motivation performance model

This model offers a causal sequence linking leadership to outcomes.

Input (Leadership Behaviours): Leaders adopt specific behaviours, categorized in three groups: Transforming & Inspiring (Vision, Stimulation), Supporting & Developing (Coaching, Empathy) and Ethical & Exchange (Transparency, Clarity of Standards).

Proximal mechanisms (Psychological needs): These behaviours satisfy the workforce's need for Autonomy, Competence and Relatedness, consistent with the SDT, which increases Intrinsic Motivation and Trust.

Intermediate outcomes (capacity): High motivation increases engagement in work, Resilience and Psychological safety.

Distal outcomes (Performance): These conditions eventually lead to achieving organizational outcomes: High professional performance, enhanced patient safety, innovation in providing services and maintaining workforce.

The multilevel pharmacy leadership practice model

Leadership requirements differ according to the level of analysis.

Individual level: Focus is on training and guidance (transformational/ servant) to build competence and efficacy.

Team level: Focus is on Shared Leadership and Relational Transparency to build team trust, promote role transparency and strengthen interprofessional collaboration.

Organizational level: Focus is on the Idealized Influence and Inspirational Motivation to bring the pharmacy department into alignment with the broader health system's goals and culture.

System level: Focus is on Advocacy Leadership, to advocate for policy changes, payment models and legislation that are consistent with the progressive nature of pharmacy practice.

Evidence on system-level leadership supports the incorporation of vertical (hierarchical) and horizontal (collaborative) leadership processes across healthcare organizations. Empirical evidence suggests that disparities in leadership behaviours at all levels can hinder successful implementation of strategy, despite the existence of effective leadership at each individual level. In pharmacy practice, having empowerment and cooperation at the frontline level, effective team communication and a clear organizational vision is critical if pharmacies are to expand advanced services. A multilevel leadership model would maintain consistency in behavioural cues that support the autonomy, psychological safety and innovation of the professional workforce at all levels.60

The application of these models is limited in environments that lack resources or acute care environments where transactional leadership is essential due to the nature of the operational requirements.

Practical implications and conclusion

Implications for management and policy

Adopting a behaviour-based leadership model necessitates changes to the way pharmacy leaders are educated and evaluated.

From traits to behaviours: Instead of using personality tests, pharmacy leadership development should use evaluations based on behaviour like situational judgment tests and behavioural simulation scenarios.13 Pharmacists should be taught to lead and follow certain behaviours, like holding a “blameless post mortem” after a safety incident.

360-degree feedback: Assessing leaders should include feedback from peers and juniors, with focus on the leaders influence on motivation and psychological safety, rather than sole focus on finances.

Structural empowerment: Managers should be willing to give up authority and power. Pharmacy policies and procedures should establish and support Distributed Leadership through recognition programs and rewards for clinical staff that excel in leadership roles and clinical areas without formal titles.61

Additionally, every level of the pharmacy business should include succession planning and continuous professional development to guarantee an adequate pipeline of leaders who can manage and address these challenges.49 This need is amplified due to the complex nature of modern healthcare issues, which are described as complex issues, difficult to solve with simple solutions and require team collaboration instead of command and control approaches. Instead solutions lie in adaptation and inclusivity which promotes interprofessional collaboration and innovation to face evolving health challenges.42, 46 Combining nursing, pharmacy and health administration expertise within integrated leadership structures can improve patient care and organizational efficiency.16, 45

Future research

Future work should transcend cross-sectional surveys. Longitudinal studies are needed to effectively follow the influence of leadership interventions on maintaining the workforce over periods of time. Furthermore, strong intervention studies, like randomized trials of leadership training programs, are essential to determine the relationship between leadership behaviours and objective patient outcomes, like the number of mistakes made.62 Special consideration should be given to digital transformation and the role of transformational leadership in supporting the adoption of technology.

This review has several limitations. As a narrative review, it may be subject to selection bias and was not conducted using formal systematic review protocols. The literature included in this review was multidisciplinary and drawn from general leadership and broader healthcare research; therefore, some findings may not be directly applicable to pharmacy practice settings. In addition, many of the included studies report associative relationships rather than direct causal effects between leadership behaviours and workforce or patient outcomes. Differences across healthcare systems, workforce structures, and pharmacy practice environments may also affect the generalizability and transferability of the proposed theoretical models and leadership observations.

Conclusion

This transition of pharmacy from a product oriented economy to a patient centred profession requires modern leadership competencies. Transformational, Authentic, and Servant Leadership models are not idealistic methods but groups of predictable actions, if applied by current leaders they have proven to satisfy employee psychological needs, such as Autonomy, Competence and Relatedness.

By satisfying these needs, burnout may be reduced, resilience is promoted, as well as fostering an innovation environment needed to face complex healthcare challenges of the 21st century. The models previously mentioned are presented as conceptual methods that enable educators and practitioners to incorporate their core behaviours into daily activities of pharmacy practice. Finally, pharmacist's professional future is dependent on leadership skills, a commitment to behaviour based leadership is a commitment to the future well-being of pharmacy and the patients it serves.
