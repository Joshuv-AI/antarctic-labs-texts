Guided filtering and smoothing for infinite-dimensional diffusions Thanks: ∗ Corresponding author: Thorben Pieper-Sethmacher, thorbenpieper@tudelft.nl .
Thorben Pieper-Sethmacher 1,∗, Daniele Avitabile 2,3, Frank van der Meulen 2

Abstract
. We consider the filtering and smoothing problems for an infinite-dimensional diffusion process X X , observed through a finite-dimensional representation at discrete points in time. At the heart of our proposed methodology lies the construction of a path measure, termed the guided distribution of X X , that is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of X X , conditioned on the observations. We show that this distribution can be incorporated as a potent proposal measure for both sequential Monte Carlo as well as Markov Chain Monte Carlo schemes to tackle the filtering and smoothing problems respectively. In the offline setting, we extend our approach to incorporate parameter estimation of unknown model parameters. The proposed methodology is numerically illustrated in a case study for the stochastic Amari equation.



1.
Introduction

Consider an
infinite-dimensional diffusion
equation of the form

(1)

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\left[AX_{t}+F(t,X_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\geq 0,\\
\quad X_{0}&\sim\mu_{0}.\end{cases}

Here,
A
denotes the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a Hilbert space
H
, whereas
F
is a Lipschitz continuous non-linearity and
Q
a symmetric, positive operator of trace class on
H
. The process
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a cylindrical Wiener process on
H
, defined on a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
and
\mu_{0}
denotes a Borel measure on
H
.
Infinite-dimensional diffusions like that of
Equation

1
arise, for example, in the context of semilinear stochastic partial differential equations or stochastic field equations.

Throughout this work, we assume that
Equation

1
admits a unique mild solution
X
satisfying

(2)

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=S_{t}X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}S_{t-s}F(s,X_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}S_{t-s}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

The process
X
is an
H
-valued, predictable Markov process with transition kernel
(\mu_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
defined by
\mu_{s,t}(x,B):=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in B\mid X_{s}=x)
for all Borel sets
B\subset H
and
x\in H
.

Suppose at discrete observation times
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{n}=T
, we are given
finite-dimensional observations

y_{i}
through realisations
Y_{i}=y_{i}
of

(3)

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid X_{t_{i}}

\displaystyle\sim k_{i}(X_{t_{i}},\cdot),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Here,
k_{i}(X_{t_{i}},\cdot)
denotes a conditional density on
\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
, where
m_{i}
is the dimension of the observation at time
t_{i}
.
For the sake of convenience, we assume from now on that

(4)

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid X_{t_{i}}\sim f(\cdot;LX_{t_{i}},\Sigma),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
L:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a bounded linear operator, termed the
observation operator
,
\Sigma
is a positive-definite matrix and
f(\cdot;Lx,\Sigma)
is the Gaussian density with mean
Lx
and covariance
\Sigma
.
However, our methods generalise to non-Gaussian observation densities and we will discuss how to adapt to such cases towards the end of the paper.

Based on the described setup, we address three estimation problems.
Firstly, we are concerned with
state estimation
of the unobserved, latent path
X
.
Here, we consider both the
online
problem of estimating the
filtering
distribution

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{i}}\in B\mid Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

and as well as the
offline
problem of estimating the
smoothing
distribution

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in B\mid Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}),\quad t\in[0,T],

for any Borel set
B\subset H
.
Moreover, if
Equation

1
is parametrised by some unknown model parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we are interested in
parameter estimation
of
\theta
in the offline setting.

1.1.
Approach

Our approach relies heavily on the exponential changes of measure studied in
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2025a
]
.
Consider for now the case that
n=1
, i.e. we observe one realisation
y
of the random variable
Y\mid X_{T}\sim k(X_{T},\cdot)
. In this simplified case, estimating the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=y
solves both the filtering as well as the smoothing problem.

It is well-known that this conditional distribution can be derived by a change of measure known as
Doob’s h-transform
as follows. Defining for any fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the likelihood function

\displaystyle h(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}k(z,y)\mu_{t,T}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z),\quad t\in[0,T],x\in H,

one shows that the process
(h(t,X_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a non-negative
\mathbb{P}
-martingale and hence defines a unique change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{h}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(T,X_{T})}{C^{h}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}},

with
C^{h}:=\mathbb{E}[h(0,X_{0})]
acting as a normalising constant. Under the new measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
, the law
\mathcal{L}^{h}(X)
of
X
is exactly the conditional distribution of
X
given
Y=y
in the sense that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{h}[\varphi(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)\mid Y=y]

for any bounded and measurable function
\varphi
.
Moreover, under certain regularity conditions on
h
, it was shown in
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2025a
]
that the change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
is of Girsanov type. The process
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{h}
is then a mild solution to the SPDE

(7)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{h}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[AX^{h}_{t}+F(t,X^{h}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,X^{h}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{h}_{t},

where
W^{h}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{h}
-cylindrical Wiener process.

Equation

7
differs from the original dynamics in
Equation

1
only by an additional drift term involving the
score

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,x)
of the likelihood function
x\mapsto h(t,x)
that steers the process
X^{h}
into regions of high probability given the observation
Y=y
.
With slight abuse of notation, we write
X^{h}
for the process
X
defined on the stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P}^{h})
and call it the
conditioned process
. Note that the existence of
\mathbb{P}^{h}
and
X^{h}
does not rely on the existence of a mild solution to Equation (
7
).

In most cases, the transition kernel
\mu
of
X
, and hence the function
h
, is intractable. This renders directly sampling the conditioned process infeasible, even if conditions for the well-posedness of Equation (
7
) are satisfied.
A natural approach to overcome this problem is to substitute the intractable function
h
by a tractable function
g
that ‘approximates’
h
in some sense.
Besides being tractable, the function
g
should

(i)

be informed by the observed data in a way that resembles the original function
h
,

(ii)

be such that there exists a mild solution
X^{g}
to the equation

(8)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{g}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[AX^{g}_{t}+F(t,X^{g}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,X^{g}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(iii)

be such that the laws of
X^{h}
and
X^{g}
are absolutely continuous in path space with a tractable Radon-Nikodym (R.N.) derivative
\Phi
.

One can then obtain
weighted samples
of the conditioned process
X^{h}
by sampling the process
X^{g}
and associating with each sample the weight
\Phi(X^{g})
.
Since
X^{g}
is ‘guided’ by the observation
Y=y
, we refer to it as the
guided process
and its law as the
guided distribution
.

In our approach, we define
g
by substituting the transition kernel
\mu
in the definition of
h
with a tractable transition kernel
\nu
of an auxiliary process
Z
. If
Z
is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we show that the function
g
defines a change of measure on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
by

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{g}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g(T,X_{T})}{C^{g}}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\dfrac{\mathcal{K}g}{g}(s,X_{s})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}

such that
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{g}
satisfies Equation (
8
) with respect to a
\mathbb{P}^{g}
-cylindrical Wiener process
W^{g}
.
Here,
\mathcal{K}
denotes the infinitesimal generator of the original, unconditioned process
X
and
C^{g}
is another normalising constant.

The function
x\mapsto g(t,x)
is to be interpreted as the likelihood function of
Z_{t}=x
under the simplified dynamics of
Z
and therefore satisfies condition (i). Moreover, condition (ii) is ensured by the Gaussian nature of
g(t,x)
implying a global Lipschitz condition of the score function
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,x)
.
Lastly, by construction of
\mathbb{P}^{h}
and
\mathbb{P}^{g}
, the laws of
X^{g}
and
X^{h}
are absolutely continuous in path space, hence satisfying the third condition imposed on
g
.
In
[
Pieper-Sethmacher et al., 2025b
]
it was shown that this absolute continuity persists if the observation kernel degenerates to a Dirac measure in
Lx
, i.e. if
Y\mid X_{T}\sim\delta_{Lx}(\cdot)
. In other words, the guided distribution remains a valid importance sampling distribution for
X^{h}
even under highly informative observations.

Our aim in this paper is to apply this basic idea of constructing the conditioned process
X^{h}
as well as the guided process
X^{g}
by a change of measure to derive solutions to both the filtering as well as the smoothing problem.

For the filtering problem, we propose the law of
X^{g}
as a natural candidate for the proposal distribution in a

particle filter
(or
sequential Monte Carlo
) approach. The particle filter is a well-established algorithm that approximates the filtering distribution in (
5
) by a set of samples (‘particles’)
X_{t_{i}}^{(j)}
with associated weights
w_{i}^{(j)}
. At each iteration
i
, the particles are evolved forward following a proposal distribution
X_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}^{(j)}\sim\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X_{t_{i}}^{(j)})
with the associated weights following the recursive update

\displaystyle w_{i+1}^{(j)}\propto w_{i}^{(j)}\,k(X^{(j)}_{t_{i+1}},y_{i+1})\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\left(X^{(j)}_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\in\cdot\mid X_{t_{i}}^{(j)}\right)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X_{t_{i}}^{(j)})}\left(X^{(j)}_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\right).

It is well-known that the locally optimal proposal distribution
\mathbb{Q}
is given by the conditional distribution of
X^{(j)}_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i}},Y_{i+1}=y_{i+1}
(cf. 10.3,
[
Chopin et al., 2020
]
).
This corresponds to the
h
-transform introduced above with the intractable function

\displaystyle h_{i}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}k(z,y_{i+1})\mu_{t,t_{i+1}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z),\quad t\in(t_{i},t_{i+1}],x\in H.

A natural candidate for a substitute proposal distribution is therefore the guided distribution corresponding to the observation
y_{i+1}
.

On the other hand, the smoothing problem can be solved after extending the change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
to include the complete set of observations
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
by defining the function

\displaystyle h(t,x):=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\left(\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}k(x_{t_{j+1}},y_{j+1})\,\mu_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}(x_{t_{j}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{j+1}})\right)\mu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}].

In Section
2
we show that
h
defines a change measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
under which the law of
X^{h}
is exactly the smoothing distribution (
6
) of
X
.
Substituting again the Markov kernel
\mu
with the kernel
\nu
of an auxiliary process
Z
leads to a tractable change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{g}
and a corresponding guided process
X^{g}
that satisfies yet another SPDE, while maintaining absolute continuity of the laws of
X^{h}
and
X^{g}
with a tractable Radon-Nikodym derivative
\Phi
.

For certain choices of
Z
, we show that
g
can be directly computed on the basis of the recursion known as the
backwards information filter

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}g(t,x)&=\int g(t_{i},x_{t_{i}})\,\nu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],\\
g(t_{i},x)&=k(x,y_{j})\,g^{+}(t_{i},x),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\end{split}

with
g^{+}(t_{i},x):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\downarrow t_{i}\\
t\neq t_{i}\end{subarray}}g(t,x)
.
This enables drawing weighted samples of
X^{h}
which can then be passed onto general Markov chain Monte Carlo or importance sampling schemes to sample from the smoothing distribution of
X
.

1.2.
Outline

In Section
2
we derive the two changes of measure that define the smoothing and guided distribution of
X
. In particular, we present two ways of efficiently computing the score
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g
needed to sample from the guiding distribution and evaluate the corresponding weights.
The implementation of the guided distribution into a particle filtering scheme is discussed in Section
3
. Section
4
derives a Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm that targets the smoothing distribution of
X
and posterior of model parameters
\theta
.
Performance of the proposed methodology is illustrated in Section
5
through an example based on the stochastic Amari equation.

1.3.
Frequently used notation

We denote by
H
a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle x,y\rangle
and norm
|x|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}
.
The process
X
denotes the mild solution to Equation (
1
), defined on the stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, with Markov transition kernel
\mu=(\mu_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
. If
F
is independent of
X
and instead
F(t,X_{t})=a_{t}
for some predictable,
H
-valued process
(a_{t})_{t}
, we write
Z
in place of
X
for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process that satisfies (
1
) and
\nu=(\nu_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
for its transition kernel.

The kernel
k_{i}(x,\cdot)
defines a conditional density on
\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
for any
x\in H
. For most of the paper, we assume
k_{i}(x,\cdot)=k(x,\cdot)
to be a Gaussian density on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with mean
Lx
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
, where
L:H\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a bounded linear observation operator.
By
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
we denote the observations of
X_{t_{i}}
with density
k_{i}(X_{t_{i}},\cdot)
at observation time
t_{i}
, with
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots Y_{n})
defined as the joint vector of all observations. We refer to individual components of one observation vector
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
as measurements.
For realisations of
Y_{i}
, we write
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
. We shall also write
y_{i}^{+}=(y_{i},\ldots,y_{n})
for the vector of all non-past observations at time
t_{i}
. Moreover,
m_{i}^{+}
denotes the dimension of
y_{i}^{+}
, i.e.
m_{i}^{+}=\sum_{j=i}^{n}m_{j}
. For a function
f
,
f^{+}(t):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\downarrow t\end{subarray}}f(s)
is defined as the right-sided limit of
f
at
t
whenever existent.

By
C([0,T];H)
we denote the space of continuous,
H
-valued functions endowed with the supremum norm and its Borel algebra.
For the laws of
X
and
Z
on the Borel algebra of
C([0,T];H)
we write
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}(Z)
respectively.
With slight abuse of notation, we shall also write
X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
for the process
(X_{t})_{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
and
\mathcal{L}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]})
for its law on
C([t_{i-1},t_{i}];H)
.

The realised observations
y
together with the transition kernels
\mu
and
\nu
define the functions
h
and
g
.
These define the changes of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
and
\mathbb{P}^{g}
under which the law of
X
is, respectively, the smoothing and guided distribution given the observations
y
. We denote these by
\mathcal{L}^{h}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)
.

2.
Changes of measure

The aim of this section is to derive the two changes of measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
and
\mathbb{P}^{g}
that respectively define the intractable smoothing distribution
\mathcal{L}^{h}(X)
and the tractable guided distribution
\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)
of
X
. In particular, the construction will show that

(i)

\mathcal{L}^{h}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)
are absolutely continuous with Radon-Nikodym derivative

\displaystyle\Phi(X):=\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{h}(X)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)}(X)=\frac{C^{g}}{C^{h}}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\langle F(s,X_{s}),G(s,X_{s})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),

where
C^{g}
and
C^{h}
are normalising constants and
G(t,x)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,x)
is a tractable function,

(ii)

the distribution
\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)
can be sampled by numerically solving the SPDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{g}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[AX^{g}_{t}+F(t,X^{g}_{t})+QG(t,X^{g}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{g}_{t}.

Moreover, we derive two ways of efficiently computing
G
needed to sample from
X^{g}
and evaluate the corresponding weights.
The proofs of the results in this section can be found in Appendix
A
.

2.1.
The smoothing distribution

We start by constructing the smoothing distribution of
X
given
Y_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=y_{n}
. We do so by defining a measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
such that

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{h}\left[\varphi(X)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)\mid Y_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=y_{n}]

for all bounded and measurable functionals
\varphi:C([0,T],H)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Note that for
n=1
, this includes the locally optimal proposal distribution in the guided particle filter as introduced in
Section

1.1
.

To this end, define the function
h:[0,T]\times H\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

(11)

\displaystyle h(t,x):=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\left(\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}k(x_{t_{j+1}},y_{j+1})\,\mu_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}(x_{t_{j}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{j+1}})\right)\mu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with
h(0,x):=h^{+}(0,x)
.
For the sake of notational clarity, we drop the dependence of
h
on the observations
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
.
However, it is worth pointing out that on any time interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
,
h(t,x)
has a natural interpretation as the likelihood function of
X_{t}=x
given all non-past observations
y_{i}^{+}=(y_{i},\ldots,y_{n})
, i.e. in Bayesian notation

\displaystyle h(t,x_{t})=p(y_{i}^{+}\mid x_{t}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}].

A direct consequence of the definition of
h
is that it satisfies the following recursive relation known as the
backwards information filter
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The function
h(t,x)
defined in (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle h(t,x)=\int h(t_{i},x_{t_{i}})\,\mu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}].

In particular,
h
is continuous on any interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i})
and left continuous at any
t_{i},i=1,\dots,n
.
Moreover, at the observation times
t_{i}
, it holds that

\displaystyle h(t_{i},x)=k(x,y_{j})\int h(t_{i+1},x_{t_{i+1}})\,\mu_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}})=k(x,y_{j})\,h^{+}(t_{i},x).

With the choice of
h
established, define now the process
(E^{h}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
by

(12)

\displaystyle E^{h}_{t}:=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)h(t,X_{t}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with
E^{h}_{0}:=h(0,X_{0})/C^{h}
and
C^{h}:=\mathbb{E}[h(0,X_{0})]
acting as a normalising constant.

Remark 2.2
.

At any time
t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
, the product
\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(x_{t_{j}},y_{j})
appearing on the right-hand side of (
12
) is the likelihood of previous states of
X
at observation times given past observations, whereas
h(t,x_{t})
is the likelihood of
X_{t}=x_{t}
given all non-past observations.
Hence, in total, the term

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(x_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)h(t,x_{t},y),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],

is the likelihood of
(X_{t_{1}}=x_{t_{1}},\ldots,X_{t_{i-1}}=x_{t_{i-1}},X_{t}=x_{t})
given all observations
y_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The process
(E^{h}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined in (
12
) is a non-negative
\mathbb{P}
-martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Given its martingale property and noting that
E^{h}_{0}=1
, the process
E^{h}
defines a measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
by

(13)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{h}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}

\displaystyle=E^{h}_{T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}.

The following theorem establishes that this is indeed the change of measure under which the law of
X
is the smoothing distribution.

Theorem 2.4
.

(i)

The measure
\mathbb{P}^{h}
satisfies (
10
).

(ii)

In addition, if
h
is Fréchet differentiable in
x
such that
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}h\in C_{m}((t_{i-1},t_{i});H)
for all
i=1\ldots n
, then the process
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{h}
is a mild solution to the SPDE

(14)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{h}_{t}&=\left[AX^{h}_{t}+F(t,X^{h}_{t})+Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log h(t,X^{h}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{h}_{t},\\
X^{h}_{0}&\sim\mu_{0}^{h},\end{cases}

where
W^{h}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{h}
-cylindrical Wiener process and
\mu_{0}^{h}(B):=\mathbb{P}^{h}(X_{0}\in B)
.

Remark 2.5
.

The assumption in Theorem
2.4
(ii) is in general hard to verify. However, to sample from the law of
X
under
\mathbb{P}^{h}
, the differential form of
X^{h}
in
Equation

14
is not necessary.
Instead, weighted samples of the smoothing distribution are obtained by sampling

2.2.
The guided distribution

To construct the guided distribution, we follow similar steps as in the previous section, replacing
\mu
in the definition of
h
with a tractable transition kernel
\nu
of an auxiliary process
Z
.

For this, let
Z
be the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, defined as the unique mild solution to the SPDE

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z_{t}&=\left[AZ_{t}+a_{t}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\geq 0,\\
Z_{0}&\sim\nu_{0}.\end{cases}

The
H
-valued process
(a_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is assumed to be predictable with integrable trajectories and
\nu_{0}
denotes a Gaussian measure on
H
.
Let
(\nu_{s,t})_{s\leq t}
be the transition kernel of
Z
defined by
\nu_{s,t}(x,B):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}\in B\mid Z_{s}=x)
for any
0\leq s\leq t
,
x\in H
and Borel set
B\subset H
. It is well-known that
\nu_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
is a Gaussian measure on
H
with mean
S_{t-s}x+\int_{s}^{t}S_{t-u}a_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
and covariance operator

(16)

\displaystyle Q_{t-s}:=\int_{0}^{t-s}S_{u}QS_{u}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

Following the definition of
h
in Equation (
11
), we define the mapping
g
by

(17)

\displaystyle g(t,x):=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\left(\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}k(x_{t_{j+1}},y_{j+1})\,\nu_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}(x_{t_{j}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{j+1}})\right)\nu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with the convention that
g(0,x):=g^{+}(0,x)
.

The function
g(t,x)
on
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
has a natural interpretation as the likelihood of
Z_{t}=x
given the vector
(y_{i},\dots,y_{n})
of non-past observations
y_{i}\sim k(Z_{t_{i}},\cdot)
under the simplified dynamics of
Equation

15
.
Moreover, just like the
h

g
satisfies the recursion of the backwards information filter

(18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}g(t,x)&=\int g(t_{i},x_{t_{i}})\,\nu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],\\
g(t_{i},x)&=k(x,y_{i})\,g^{+}(t_{i},x),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\end{split}

with
g^{+}(t_{i},x)=\int g(t_{i+1},x_{t_{i+1}})\,\nu_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}})
.

Contrary to the generally intractable
h
, the Gaussian nature of
\nu_{s,t}(x,\cdot)
and
k(x,\cdot)
enables us to compute the function
g
using the recursion in (
18

Theorem 2.6
.

On any interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
,
i=1,\dots,n,
let
L_{t}\in L(H,\mathbb{R}^{m^{+}_{i}})
be given by

(19)

\displaystyle L_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}LS_{t_{i}-t}\\
LS_{t_{i+1}-t}\\
\vdots\\
LS_{t_{n}-t}\end{bmatrix}

and let
R_{t}\in L(\mathbb{R}^{m^{+}_{i}})
and
\alpha_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{+}_{i}}
be defined by the backwards recursions

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{t}&:=\begin{cases}\Sigma+LQ_{t_{n}-t}L^{*},&i=n,\\
\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma&0\\
0&R^{+}_{t_{i}}\end{bmatrix}+L_{t_{i}}Q_{t_{i}-t}L^{*}_{t_{i}},&\text{else},\end{cases}\\
\alpha_{t}&:=\begin{cases}L\left(\int_{t}^{t_{n}}S_{t_{n}-s}a_{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),&i=n,\\
\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\alpha^{+}_{t_{i}}\end{bmatrix}+L_{t_{i}}\left(\int_{t}^{t_{i}}S_{t_{i}-s}a_{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),&\text{else}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Then, the function
g(t,x)
is given by

(21)

\displaystyle g(t,x)=f(y_{i}^{+};L_{t}x+\alpha_{t},R_{t}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}].

Following Theorem
2.6
, the function
g
can be expressed on any interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
as the Gaussian density in Equation (
21
). This lets us define a tractable change of measure
\mathbb{P}^{g}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
as follows.
Denote by
G
the score function of the likelihood
x\mapsto g(t,x)
, i.e.

(22)

\displaystyle G(t,x):=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g(t,x)=L_{t}^{*}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{i}^{+}-L_{t}x-\alpha_{t}),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],x\in H.

Moreover, define the process
(E^{g}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
by setting

(23)

\displaystyle E^{g}_{t}:=\frac{1}{C^{g}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)g(t,X_{t})\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\langle F(s,X_{s}),G(s,X_{s})\rangle\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),\quad t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with
E^{g}_{0}:=g(0,X_{0})/C^{g}
and
C^{g}:=\mathbb{E}[g(0,X_{0})]
acting as a normalizing constant.

The theorem presented below shows that
E^{g}
is a martingale that defines a measure
\mathbb{P}^{g}
on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
under which the law of
X
is exactly the guided distribution as introduced in
Section

1.1

Theorem 2.7
.

(i)

The process
E^{g}
defined in (
23

\mathbb{P}
-martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

(ii)

Under the measure
\mathbb{P}^{g}
defined on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
by
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{g}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}=E^{g}_{T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}}
, the process

is the unique mild solution to the SPDE

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{g}_{t}&=\left[AX^{g}_{t}+F(t,X^{g}_{t})+QG(t,X^{g}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{g}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],\\

From the construction of

Remark 2.8
.

Drawing samples from
\mathcal{L}^{g}(X)
as well as evaluating the weights
\Phi(X)
requires computation of the function
G
defined in Equation (
22
).
In practical applications, this is carried out on a gridded domain in both space and time.

In that case, the infinite-dimensional operators
A
,
S_{t}
and
Q
are typically approximated by
M{\times}M
-dimensional matrices on the given spatial - or spectral - grid. Likewise, the observation operator
L
is represented by a
m{\times}M
-dimensional matrix.
Evaluating
G
based on Theorem
2.6
on a temporal grid of
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
with
N
grid points then requires the following operations:

1.

The operators
L_{t}
are given in closed form and are a simple concatenation of linear compositions of
L
and the semigroup
S
. For each observation time
t_{i}
, the matrix multiplication
L_{t}=L_{t_{i}}S_{t_{i}-t}
needs to be carried out for
N
grid points of
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
. This results in
O(Nm_{i}^{+}M^{2})
operations.

2.

To compute
R_{t}
, the matrix approximations of
Q_{t_{i}-t}=\int_{0}^{t_{i}-t}S_{s}QS_{s}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
need to evaluated. If this integral cannot be solved directly, it needs to be numerically integrated with computational complexity of
O(NM^{3}).
Additionally, the matrix multiplications
L_{t_{i}}Q_{t_{i}-t}L^{*}_{t_{i}}
will need to be carried out, leading to an additional total cost of
O(Nm_{i}^{+}M^{2})
.
Moreover, evaluation of
G
will require the matrix inversion of
R_{t}
. Due to the block matrix structure of
R_{t}
, this is of cost
O((n-i+1)m^{3})=O(m_{i}^{+}m^{2})
for each inversion.

3.

Lastly, to compute
\alpha_{t}
, we need to numerically backwards integrate the term
\int_{t}^{t_{i}}S_{t_{i}-s}a_{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
. This is of complexity
O(NM^{2})
after discretisation, on top of the cost
O(Nm^{+}_{i}M)
of evaluating
L_{t_{i}}\int_{t}^{t_{i}}S_{t_{i}-s}a_{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
.

Hence, in total, the cost of the operations required to compute
G
using Theorem
2.6
on a spatio-temporal grid with
(N,M)
grid points is of complexity

\displaystyle O(N\max\{M^{3},m^{+}_{i}M^{2},m_{i}^{+}m^{2}\}).

In principle, the spatial grid size
M
chosen by the user is fixed but can be arbitrarily large.
However, with growing number of observations
n
, the dimension
m^{+}_{i}=(n-i+1)m
quickly grows larger than
M
, leading to a computational costs of complexity
O(Nm^{+}_{i}M^{2})
.
In that case, it is beneficial to parametrise
g
in a different manner as we will explore in the next section.

3.
Guided particle filtering

In this section, we show how a special case of the guided distribution can be used as proposal distribution in a particle filtering scheme.
For the convenience of the reader, we give a brief outline of the particle filter (PF).
We refer to
[
Doucet et al., 2001
]
or
[
Chopin et al., 2020
]
for book-length treatments of this field.

The particle filter uses a set of particles
\{X^{(j)}_{t_{i}}:j=1,\ldots,J\}
with associated weights
\{w^{(j)}_{i}:j=1,\ldots,J\}
to approximate the filtering distribution (
5
) at any observation time
t_{i}
by a Monte Carlo approximation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_{t_{i}})\mid Y_{1}=y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=y_{i}]

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j}w^{(j)}_{t_{i}}\varphi(X^{(j)}_{t_{i}})

for any bounded and measurable function
\varphi:H\to\mathbb{R}
.

The particles and their weights are sequentially updated with incoming observations as per the following recursion.
Given an estimate
\{(X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}},w^{(j)}_{i-1}):j=1,\ldots,J\}
of the filtering distribution at time
t_{i-1}
and given a Markov kernel
\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid x)
from
H
to
C([t_{i-1},t_{i}];H)
, the particles evolve forward to the next observation time
t_{i}
according to

\displaystyle X^{(j)}_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\sim\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}}),\quad j=1,\ldots J.

The weights are then updated (and normalised) via

\displaystyle w_{i}^{(j)}\propto w_{i-1}^{(j)}\,k(X^{(j)}_{t_{i}},y_{i})\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})}\left(X^{(j)}_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\right),\quad\text{s.t. }\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{i}^{(j)}=1.

Here,
\mathcal{L}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})
denotes the law of
X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
satisfying (
1
) with initial state
X_{t_{i-1}}=X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}}
.

Typically, to prevent
weight degeneracy
, i.e. one or few weights dominating the others, a resampling step of the particles based on the normalized weights is introduced. This can be done every fixed number of steps or in an adaptive number based on maintaining the
effective sample size

ESS_{i}:=1/\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}(w_{i}^{(j)})^{2}\right)
above a predefined threshold.

Input:

Observations
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, number of particles
J
, threshold
J_{0}

Output:

Particles and weights
\Bigl\{\left(X_{t_{i}}^{(j)},w_{i}^{(j)}\right):j=1\ldots J\Bigr\}
for each
i=1,\dots,n

Initialise
\{X_{0}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}\sim\mu_{0}
and set
w_{0}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{J}
;

for

i=1

to

n

do

1. Evolve particles:

for

j=1

to

J

do

(i) Sample guided proposal:

X^{(j)}_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\sim\mathcal{L}^{g_{i}}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}});

(ii) Compute unnormalized weight:

\tilde{w}_{i}^{(j)}=w_{i-1}^{(j)}\,g_{i}(t_{i-1},X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})\exp\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\langle F(s,X^{(j)}_{s}),G_{i}(s,X^{(j)}_{s})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right);

2. Normalize Weights:

w_{i}^{(j)}=\frac{\tilde{w}_{i}^{(j)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\tilde{w}_{i}^{(k)}};

Compute effective sample size:

\text{ESS}=1/\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(w_{i}^{(j)}\right)^{2}
;

3. Resample:

if

ESS

<J_{0}

then

Resample
\{X_{t_{i}}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}
according to weights
w_{i}^{(j)}
;

Set
w_{i}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{J}
for all
j
;

Algorithm 1

Guided Particle Filter

The proposal kernel
\mathbb{Q}
is a parameter of the particle filter that is to be chosen by the user.
If
\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})=\mathcal{L}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})
, the algorithm is known as the
bootstrap particle filter (BPF)
. Since proposals in the BPF are ‘uninformed’ by the observations, it is known to typically struggle with highly degenerate weights.

On the other hand, the locally optimal proposal kernel is given by the conditional distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})=\mathbb{P}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\in\cdot\mid X_{t_{i-1}}=X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}},Y_{i}=y_{i}).

The proposal
\mathbb{Q}
minimises the variance of the weights
w_{i}^{(j)}
, see for example
[
Chopin et al., 2020
]
, Theorem 10.1 for details.
However, as noted in the introduction and previous section, this proposal kernel is typically intractable.

We propose instead to steer the particles on the interval
[t_{i-1},t_{i}]
into regions of high probability given
Y_{i}=y_{i}
by the guided distribution introduced in Section
2.2
.
For this, define

\displaystyle g_{i}(t,x):=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\nu_{t,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}}),\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}],

and let
\mathbb{P}^{g_{i}}
be the change of measure on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
as defined in Theorem
2.7
.
We adopt as proposal kernel the guided distribution

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\cdot\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})=\mathcal{L}^{g_{i}}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}}),

i.e. the law of
X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
under
\mathbb{P}^{g_{i}}
initiated at
X_{t_{i-1}}=X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}}
.
Particles are then evolved forward in time by simulating the guided process

(33)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{g_{i}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[AX^{g_{i}}_{t}+F(t,X^{g_{i}}_{t})+QG_{i}(t,X^{g_{i}}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{g_{i}}_{t},\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with respective initial conditions
X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}},j=1,\ldots J
and
G_{i}(t,x)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{D}_{x}\log g_{i}(t,x)
. Moreover, Theorem
2.7
implies that the update step in the corresponding weights is given by

(34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}w_{i}^{(j)}&\propto w_{i-1}^{(j)}\,k(X^{(j)}_{t_{i}},y_{i})\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{g_{i}}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\mid X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})}\left(X^{(j)}_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}\right)\\
&=w_{i-1}^{(j)}\,k(X^{(j)}_{t_{i}},y_{i})\,\dfrac{g_{i}(t_{i-1},X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})}{g_{i}(t_{i},X^{(j)}_{t_{i}})}\exp\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\langle F(s,X^{(j)}_{s}),G_{i}(s,X^{(j)}_{s})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\\
&=w_{i-1}^{(j)}\,g_{i}(t_{i-1},X^{(j)}_{t_{i-1}})\exp\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\langle F(s,X^{(j)}_{s}),G_{i}(s,X^{(j)}_{s})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).\end{split}

is summarised in Algorithm

Remark 3.1
.

L_{t}=LS_{t_{i}-t}
,
\alpha_{t}=L\left(\int_{t}^{t_{i}}S_{t_{i}-s}a_{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)
and
R_{t}=\Sigma+LQ_{t_{i}-t}L^{*}
, whereas
G_{i}
is the score

\displaystyle G_{i}(t,x)=L_{t}^{*}R_{t}^{-1}\left(y_{i}-L_{t}x-\alpha_{t}\right),\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}].

3.1.
Tempering and moving steps

Even though the proposal kernel introduced in Equation (
32
) defines a suitable, likelihood-informed importance sampling distribution, the guided particle filter might still face weight degeneracy due to the high dimensionality of the state space.
Since this is a common issue in particle-based inference, many solutions to overcome it have been proposed and studied in the literature. For the purpose of our numerical experiments in Section
5
, we introduce two such solutions -
tempering
and
moving
(or
particle rejuvenation
) steps - into the guided particle filter. This follows the suggestions made in
[
Llopis et al., 2018
]
, where the filtering problem was considered in a similar setup to ours.
The following brief exposition is based on that work.

Denoting by
\Pi_{i}
the conditioned law
\Pi_{i}:=\mathcal{L}(X_{[0,t_{i}]}\mid Y_{1}\dots Y_{i})
, an application of the Bayes theorem and the change of measure
(
32
)
gives the recursion

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{i}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\Pi_{i-1}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{g_{i}})}(X_{[0,t_{i}]})&\propto g_{i}(t_{i-1},X_{t_{i-1}})\exp\left(\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\langle F(s,X_{s}),G_{i}(s,X_{s})\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\\
&=:\Lambda_{i}(X_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}).\end{split}

Suppose that, at observation time
t_{i}
, we have an evenly weighted set of samples of the distribution
\Pi_{i-1}
.
The filtering step at
t_{i}
then corresponds to targeting the measure
\Pi_{i}
by an importance sampling scheme with proposal distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{i}:=\Pi_{i-1}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{g_{i}}
. Samples from this proposal are drawn by forward propagating the given particles of
\Pi_{i-1}
following the dynamics in (
33
) with IS weights given by
\Lambda_{i}
in (
35
).

Now fix the observation time
t_{i}
and drop the subscript
i
to simplify notation.
In state spaces of high dimension and/or the situation of highly informative observations, the proposal distribution
\mathbb{Q}
might not be a sufficiently good approximation for the target
\Pi
.

Tempering aims to overcome the distance between
\Pi
and
\mathbb{Q}
by introducing a sequence of intermediate distributions
\Pi^{l}
between
\Pi
and
\mathbb{Q}
defined by

(36)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi^{l}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}(X)\propto\Lambda(X)^{\psi_{l}}

for a set of inverse temperatures
0=\psi_{0}<\ldots<\psi_{L}=1
.
Notably, we have absolute continuity of
\Pi^{l+1}
with respect to
\Pi^{l}
with R.N. derivative proportional to
\Lambda(X)^{\psi_{l+1}-\psi_{l}}
.
Instead of targeting
\Pi
directly by
\mathbb{Q}
, one progressively steps through this sequence of artificial distributions, starting from
\mathbb{Q}
to target the flattened
\Pi^{1}
and ending up at targeting
\Pi
with proposals drawn from
\Pi^{L-1}
.

Crucially, the parameters
\{\psi_{l}\}_{l}
can be chosen adaptively as follows.
Given a set of evenly weighted particles of
\Pi^{l}
at current inverse temperature
\psi_{l}
, the parameter
\psi_{l+1}
is set by

\displaystyle\psi_{l+1}:=\inf\{\psi\in(\psi_{l},1]:\ESS_{l,l+1}\leq\alpha J\}

with the definition of
\inf\O:=1
.
The hyperparameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
is pre-specified by the user and
\ESS_{l,l+1}
denotes the essential sample size of the weights
\Lambda(X)^{\psi_{l+1}-\psi_{l}}
between
\Lambda^{l+1}
and
\Lambda^{l}
.

After
\Pi^{l+1}
has been determined, particles are resampled based on the weights
\Lambda(X)^{\psi_{l+1}-\psi_{l}}
.
Subsequently, to increase particle diversity, each particle is moved by a few steps of an MCMC scheme that targets
\Pi^{l+1}
.
This can be done by proposing independent samples from
\mathbb{Q}
and accepting/rejecting the proposals in a Metropolis-Hastings step based on the likelihood in Equation (
36
).
Alternatively, proposals can be localised based on the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson scheme, see Section
4
for details.
The complete

procedure is summarised in Algorithm

It is to be carried out at each observation time
t_{i}
in place of Steps

and

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{h}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(X)=E^{h}_{T}=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}(X_{t_{i}},y_{i})\right)

on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
.
For the guided distribution of
X
, the Markov kernel
k_{i}
needs to remain Gaussian in order to preserve the tractable expressions for
g(t,x)
obtained in Section
2.2
. It is therefore defined under the change of measure

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{g}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(X)=E^{g}_{T}=\frac{1}{C^{g}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}(X_{t_{i}},y_{i})\right)\Psi(X).

In total, the R.N. derivative of the smoothing distribution with respect to the guided distribution then takes on the form

\displaystyle\Phi(X)=\frac{C^{g}}{C^{h}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{l_{i}(X_{t_{i}},y_{i})}{k_{i}(X_{t_{i}},y_{i})}\right)\Psi(X).

5.1.
Stochastic Amari equation

In the following section, we evaluate our proposed methodology for filtering, smoothing, and parameter estimation in the context of a partially observed stochastic Amari model.
For this, let
H=L^{2}(D)
for some bounded spatial domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For ease of exposition, we assume the domain
D
to be rectangular and impose periodic boundary conditions on it.
Consider then the mild solution
X
to the stochastic Amari equation

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\left[-X_{t}+F(X_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\geq 0,\\
\quad X_{0}&=x_{0},\end{split}

with non-linearity
F:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
defined by

(44)

\displaystyle F(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{D}k_{F}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})f(X_{t}(\xi^{\prime}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}.

The process
X_{t}
models a stochastic neural field on
D
, with
X_{t}(\xi)
representing the average neural activity at location
\xi\in D
and time
t\geq 0
.

The kernel
k_{F}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})
captures neural connectivity between locations
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
, whereas
f
is an activation function, typically a sigmoid-like function or similar.
In our experiments, we consider

(45)

\displaystyle\begin{split}k_{F}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})&=\frac{A}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-(|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|-\delta)^{2}\right)-\frac{A}{\sqrt{\pi}B}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|-\delta}{B}\right)^{2}\right)\\
f(x)&=\frac{1}{1+\exp\big(-\eta\,x+\zeta\big)}-\frac{1}{1+\exp(\zeta)},\end{split}

for a set of parameters
A>0,B>0,\eta>0,\zeta>0
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
.

For the mild solution
X
to exist, one may consider any trace-class operator
Q
on
L^{2}(D)
. We let
Q
be a diagonal operator defined by its eigenvalues

(46)

\displaystyle q_{l}=\sigma_{0}^{2}(\rho_{0}^{-2}+(2\pi l)^{2})^{-(d/2+\eta_{0})}

with respect to the Fourier basis functions
(e_{l})_{l=1}^{\infty}
on
L^{2}(D)
. The operator
Q
is then the Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator defined by the Matérn kernel on
D
, see for example
[
Borovitskiy et al., 2020
]
, Theorem 5.

Observation and guiding scheme

We consider observations
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of the latent process
X
at observation times
(t_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
defined as in Eq. (
4

L:L^{2}(D)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
we set as

(47)

\displaystyle Lx=\left(\int_{D}x(\xi)\omega_{j}(\xi)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\right)_{j=1}^{m}=\left(\langle x,\omega_{j}\rangle\right)_{j=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

for a set of positive, square-integrable functions
\omega_{j}\in L^{2}(D),j=1,\ldots m
.
The adjoint operator
L^{*}
then takes the form

\displaystyle L^{*}y=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}\omega_{j}\in L^{2}(D),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Specifically, we let
\omega_{j}=\tfrac{1}{|D_{j}|}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{j}}
for a series of mutually disjoint subsets
D_{j}\subset D
,
j=1\ldots m
. The operator
L
then represents observing localised spatial averages of
X_{t_{i}}(\xi)
over the domains
D_{j}
at observation time
t_{i}
.

Method

We apply Algorithm
5
to the given dataset with
N=15\,000
MCMC iterations and pCN step size
\beta=0.1
.
As priors we choose

\displaystyle\eta\sim\Unif([0,15]),\,\zeta\sim\Unif([0,3]),\,A\sim\Unif([0,8]),\,\delta\sim\Unif([0,1]).

The parameters
\{\eta,\zeta,A,\delta\}
are updated individually in a Gibbs sampling manner. Proposals for each parameter are chosen via an adaptive scaling Metropolis scheme (see e.g.
[
Andrieu and Thoms, 2008
]
) as follows.
At iteration
j
, given a step size
S^{\eta}_{j}
, say we propose
\eta_{j+1}
via

\displaystyle\eta_{j+1}=\eta_{j}+\exp(S^{\eta}_{j})V

with
V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Setting
\theta_{j+1}=[\eta_{j+1},\zeta_{j},A_{j},\delta_{j}]
, we accept/reject
\theta_{j+1}
with Metropolis acceptance probability

\displaystyle M_{j+1}=\min\left(1,\dfrac{\pi(\theta_{j+1})g_{\theta_{j+1}}(0,x_{0})\Psi_{\theta_{j+1}}(\Gamma_{\theta_{j+1}}(x_{0},V))}{\pi(\theta_{j})g_{\theta_{j}}(0,x_{0})\Psi_{\theta_{j}}(\Gamma_{\theta_{j}}(x_{0},V))}\right).

Subsequently, the step size
S^{\eta}_{j}
is updated via

\displaystyle S^{\eta}_{j+1}=S^{\eta}_{j}+r_{j}(M_{j+1}-\alpha^{*})

where
r_{j}
is a scaling function and
\alpha^{*}
a target acceptance rate.
The remaining parameters
\zeta_{j},A_{j},\delta_{j}
are updated equivalently in the standard Gibbs sampling fashion, each with their own adaptive step sizes
S^{\zeta}_{j},S^{A}_{j},S^{\eta}_{j}
.
In our implementation, we set
r_{j}=j^{-2/3}
and
\alpha^{*}=0.234
. The step sizes are initiated at
S_{0}=1
for all parameters.
To stress-test Algorithm
5
, we run the experiment twice, initialising each parameter at either end of its prior interval.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

We first show that the function
h
defined in (
11
) is space-time harmonic on any time interval
(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
, i.e.

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(t,X_{t})\mid X_{s}=x]

\displaystyle=h(s,x)

for any
x\in H
and
s,t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
such that
s<t.

Indeed, from plugging in (
11
) and using the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(t,X_{t})\mid X_{s}=x]

\displaystyle=\int h(t,x_{t})\mu_{s,t}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t})

\displaystyle=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\left(\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}k(x_{t_{j+1}},y_{j+1})\,\mu_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}(x_{t_{j}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{j+1}})\right)\mu_{s,t}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t})\mu_{t,t_{i}}(x_{t},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}})

\displaystyle=\int k(x_{t_{i}},y_{i})\left(\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}k(x_{t_{j+1}},y_{j+1})\,\mu_{t_{j},t_{j+1}}(x_{t_{j}},\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{j+1}})\right)\mu_{s,t_{i}}(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t_{i}})

\displaystyle=h(s,x).

This gives (
51
).
To show that
E^{h}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale, first consider the case that
t_{i-1}<s<t\leq t_{i}
.
Then, by the Markov property of
X
, the definition of
E^{h}
and the preceding display we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E^{h}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)h(t,X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\mathbb{E}\left[h(t,X_{t})\mid X_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})h(s,X_{s})=E^{h}_{s}.

On the other hand, if
t_{i-2}<s\leq t_{i-1}<t\leq t_{i}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E^{h}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)h(t,X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C^{h}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}k(X_{t_{j}},y_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[k(X_{t_{i-1}},y_{i-1})h(t,X_{t})\mid X_{s}\right]

\displaystyle G_{i}(t,x)=L_{t}^{*}R_{t}^{-1}\left(y_{i}-L_{t}x\right),\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}],

with
L_{t}=LS_{t_{i}-t}
and
R_{t}=\Sigma+LQ_{t_{i}-t}L^{*}
.
Let us define, with slight abuse of notation,
\Delta_{i}:=t_{i}-t
.

Given
Ax=-x
, it holds that
(S_{t})_{t}
is the semigroup given by
S_{\Delta_{i}}=\exp(-\Delta_{i})
.
Hence, following the definition of
Q_{t}
given in (
16
), we have

\displaystyle Q_{\Delta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Delta_{i}}\exp(-2s)Q\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1-\exp(-2\Delta_{i})}{2}Q.

Plugging this into the expression of
G_{i}
above gives the first claim of Proposition
5.1
.
The matrix representation of
LQL^{*}
in the second claim can be deduced from the fact that, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\displaystyle(LQL^{*})y

\displaystyle=LQ\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\omega_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=L\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}q_{l}\langle\omega_{i},e_{l}\rangle e_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}q_{l}\langle\omega_{i},e_{l}\rangle\langle\omega_{j},e_{l}\rangle\right)_{j=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

∎
