Adult-onset ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum across diverse neurological disorders: a video-oculographic case series
Authors: Pavol Skacik, Stefan Sivak, Egon Kurca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOcular flutter and opsoclonus are rare ocular motor disorders most often associated with paraneoplastic or autoimmune conditions, although their full aetiological spectrum in adults remains incompletely defined. Because the distinction between ocular flutter and opsoclonus may be challenging using routine video-oculographic tracings alone, the broader term flutter–opsoclonus spectrum is used in this study.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of adult patients evaluated between September 2024 and February 2026 at a tertiary neurology centre, in whom high-frequency saccadic oscillations consistent with the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum were documented using video-oculographic recordings alongside comprehensive neurological assessment.ResultsFive patients with distinct underlying disorders were identified, comprising autoimmune, neurodegenerative, prion-related, and demyelinating aetiologies. These included anti–glutamic acid decarboxylase stiff-person spectrum disorder, relapsing anti–NMDA receptor encephalitis with dual antibody positivity, multisystem atrophy of the cerebellar subtype, sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, and advanced progressive multiple sclerosis. Flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities frequently co-occurred with additional ocular motor findings, including saccadic intrusions, various forms of nystagmus, and deficits of smooth pursuit and saccades.ConclusionsThis series expands the recognised aetiological spectrum of adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities and highlights their diagnostic value as clinical signs of potentially serious neurological disease. Objective eye-movement recording facilitates detection and should be considered in patients presenting with unexplained cerebellar syndromes, encephalopathies, or complex movement disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05047-5.

Introduction

Ocular flutter and opsoclonus are rare, closely related ocular motor disorders characterised by bursts of involuntary, high-frequency saccadic oscillations without an intersaccadic interval [1]. Ocular flutter is typically confined to the horizontal plane, whereas opsoclonus is classically multidirectional and chaotic. In routine video-oculographic recordings, however, this distinction may be difficult to establish with certainty, particularly in the presence of superimposed nystagmus. Therefore, where applicable, we use the broader descriptive term flutter–opsoclonus spectrum in the present study to refer to high-frequency saccadic oscillations without intersaccadic intervals when the available recordings did not allow confident differentiation between ocular flutter and definite multidirectional opsoclonus.

These abnormalities may occur with additional neurological features—most commonly myoclonus and ataxia—constituting opsoclonus–myoclonus (–ataxia) syndrome (OMAS) [2]. Reported causes include paraneoplastic, para-infectious, autoimmune, and toxic–metabolic conditions [3]. Among antineuronal antibodies, anti-Ri (ANNA-2) is most frequently described in adult paraneoplastic opsoclonus, particularly in association with small-cell lung carcinoma [4], although a broader antibody spectrum has been reported [3, 5].

Proposed mechanisms underlying these saccadic oscillations include dysfunction of saccadic burst neurons within pontine and mesencephalic reticular formations (brainstem hypothesis) and impaired Purkinje cell-mediated inhibition from the cerebellar vermis and fastigial nuclei (cerebellar hypothesis), likely reflecting a network-level disturbance involving cerebellar–brainstem circuits [6, 7].

Evaluation of adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities requires systematic investigation, including brain MRI, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, testing for infectious, paraneoplastic, and autoimmune antibodies, and age-appropriate malignancy screening such as thoracoabdominopelvic CT (TAP-CT) or whole-body PET [8]. Management targets the underlying cause and often involves immunotherapy—corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasma exchange, or rituximab—with outcomes varying across reports, in addition to symptomatic treatment [1, 9, 10].

To our knowledge, the conditions represented in this series have only rarely been associated with flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities. This case series broadens the recognised clinical and aetiological spectrum of adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum and supports systematic diagnostic evaluation, particularly for potentially treatable causes. Objective eye-movement recordings further enable comparison across diverse neurological aetiologies.

Patients and methods

We conducted a retrospective observational case series of adult patients evaluated at the Department of Neurology, University Hospital Martin, Slovakia, between September 2024 and February 2026, in whom flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities were identified during detailed ocular motor assessment.

Patients were identified through a review of neuro-otological and neuro-ophthalmological examination records and videonystagmography databases. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, documented high-frequency saccadic oscillations consistent with the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum on video-oculographic recordings, and a confirmed neurological diagnosis established according to accepted clinical and laboratory criteria. Patients with isolated or inconsistent findings insufficient to support a diagnosis within the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum were excluded.

Eye movements were recorded using an infrared videonystagmography system, Synapsys VNG Ulmer (Synapsys/Inventis), equipped with a monocular wired VNS4X infrared camera and Goggle Flex mask. The camera has a resolution of 744 × 480 pixels, a field of view of 100° × 55° horizontally and vertically, respectively, and a sampling rate of 25 Hz. The system includes a 7-m cable and mechanical occluder and is powered by a 5-V DC supply. Detailed ocular motor testing, including assessment of smooth pursuit and saccades, was additionally performed using a binocular infrared recording system (Table 1).

Recordings included assessment of spontaneous gaze and fixation, as well as vestibular testing when clinically indicated. All patients underwent comprehensive neurological examination, with particular attention to cerebellar and vestibular signs. For descriptive purposes, opsoclonus was defined as spontaneous, arrhythmic, conjugate, multidirectional saccadic oscillations without intersaccadic intervals. However, because the available video-oculographic tracings did not allow reliable confirmation of multidirectional oscillations in all cases, particularly in the presence of superimposed nystagmus, the observed abnormalities were interpreted more broadly as belonging to the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum.

All patients underwent standardized etiological evaluation, including brain magnetic resonance imaging, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, serum neuronal antibody testing, infectious screening, and malignancy screening when clinically indicated.

Results

Ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum in anti-GAD stiff-person spectrum disorder

A 44-year-old woman presented with a four-month history of progressive stimulus-sensitive lower-limb spasms and rigidity causing severe gait impairment. Neurological examination demonstrated generalised hypertonia, brisk reflexes, and pyramidal signs. Electromyography showed continuous motor unit activity, and serum anti–glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (anti-GAD) were markedly elevated, while neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and paraneoplastic testing were unremarkable.

Oculomotor assessment revealed impaired smooth pursuit, saccadic dysfunction, and frequent high-frequency saccadic intrusions consistent with the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum; video-oculography additionally demonstrated spontaneous horizontal, gaze-evoked, and rebound nystagmus. These findings supported a diagnosis of anti-GAD–associated stiff-person spectrum disorder with flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities. Treatment with plasma exchange was followed by a marked reduction in these saccadic oscillations (Fig. 1, Additional file 1).

Ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum during relapse of anti-NMDA/anti-GAD encephalitis with dual antibody positivity

A 28-year-old patient with a previous diagnosis of anti–NMDA receptor encephalitis was admitted with a third relapse characterised by cognitive impairment and focal aware seizures. Laboratory testing during relapse demonstrated elevated anti–NMDA receptor antibody levels with additional anti-GAD positivity. In the context of recurrent neuropsychiatric symptoms and seizures, supportive antibody findings, and negative evaluation for alternative infectious, structural, and metabolic causes, these findings were considered consistent with autoimmune recurrence.

During neurological intensive care monitoring, the patient developed new gait instability accompanied by limb dysmetria, ataxia, bradyteleokinesis, a positive Romberg test, and impaired tandem gait. Ocular motor examination revealed rapid saccadic oscillations without intersaccadic intervals, occurring in both horizontal and vertical traces, consistent with the ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum. Additional findings included saccadic dysmetria, impaired smooth pursuit, spontaneous downbeat nystagmus, and perverted head-shaking nystagmus.

Brain MRI demonstrated FLAIR hyperintense signal abnormalities in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 2). The patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulins and corticosteroids, followed by rituximab, after which the clinical picture improved, and follow-up VNG showed resolution of these saccadic oscillations.

Prominent opsoclonus in multisystem atrophy, cerebellar subtype

A 55-year-old man presented with a one-year history of progressive dysarthria and gait instability. Neurological examination demonstrated appendicular and gait ataxia, hypokinetic-rigid features, and urinary retention suggestive of autonomic dysfunction. Extensive investigations, including paraneoplastic and neuronal antibody panels, infectious screening, and thoracoabdominopelvic CT, were unrevealing. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed cerebellar atrophy with characteristic features including the hot cross bun sign and signal changes in the middle cerebellar peduncles, findings considered consistent with multisystem atrophy of the cerebellar subtype according to diagnostic criteria [11]. Oculoar motor evaluation using videonystagmography demonstrated opsoclonus as the predominant ocular motor abnormality, along with dysmetric saccades and impaired smooth pursuit (Fig. 3, Additional file 2).

Ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum preceding rapid deterioration in sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease

A 63-year-old man presented with rapidly progressive neurological decline characterised by spastic dysarthria, asymmetric cerebellar ataxia, hyperreflexia, and pyramidal signs. Cerebrospinal fluid testing was positive for 14-3-3 protein, and real-time quaking-induced conversion assay detected pathological prion protein, while PRNP gene analysis showed no pathogenic variants. Malignancy and inflammatory evaluations were negative. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated diffusion restriction and FLAIR hyperintensities involving the left cerebral hemisphere and basal ganglia.

Treatment with high-dose corticosteroids was not associated with clinical improvement. Ocular motor testing revealed high-frequency saccadic oscillations consistent with the ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum, accompanied by spontaneous vertical upbeat nystagmus on videonystagmography (Fig. 4; Additional file 3). In the context of rapidly progressive neurological deterioration, the overall findings were considered consistent with sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, and the patient died within weeks.

Ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum in advanced secondary progressive multiple sclerosis

A 40-year-old patient with multiple sclerosis diagnosed in 2020 presented with advanced disease despite several disease-modifying therapies, ultimately requiring mitoxantrone. The diagnosis had previously been established on the basis of typical clinical features, characteristic magnetic resonance imaging findings, and positive cerebrospinal fluid oligoclonal bands. At evaluation, the patient was severely disabled, with an Expanded Disability Status Scale score of 8.0 [12], and was bed- or wheelchair-bound.

Neurological examination demonstrated marked ocular motor dysfunction, including severely impaired smooth pursuit, pronounced saccadic dysmetria, and spontaneous saccadic oscillations. Eye-movement recordings showed irregular high-frequency saccadic intrusions without intersaccadic intervals, involving both horizontal and vertical traces and consistent with the ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum. Superimposed pendular nystagmus was also present in the vertical trace.

Repeat magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated extensive demyelinating changes compatible with advanced multiple sclerosis, with no evidence of alternative structural, infectious, toxic, or paraneoplastic causes. In this clinical context, the findings were considered consistent with ocular flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities occurring in association with advanced progressive multiple sclerosis, suggesting that this uncommon manifestation may reflect involvement of brainstem–cerebellar networks and may warrant recognition to avoid unnecessary diagnostic investigations (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This case series highlights the heterogeneity of aetiologies associated with adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities detected on videonystagmography. The observed saccadic oscillations varied across cases in their occurrence, modulation by visual fixation, and association with other fixation instabilities, including superimposed nystagmus, gaze-evoked and rebound nystagmus, and impaired saccadic and smooth pursuit function. Across cases, these abnormalities were frequently overlooked or misinterpreted at the bedside but were reliably identified using videonystagmography, underscoring the diagnostic value of objective eye-movement recordings in complex or progressive neurological syndromes.

From an aetiological standpoint, this series broadens the recognised spectrum of adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities. In addition to classical immune-mediated causes, we observed such abnormalities in association with anti–GAD stiff-person spectrum disorder, combined anti-NMDA/anti-GAD encephalitis, prion disease, multisystem atrophy, and advanced progressive multiple sclerosis—conditions only rarely reported in this context.

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis typically presents with neuropsychiatric symptoms, seizures, and complex movement disorders, often with a relapsing course [13]. Although cerebellar involvement is not usually prominent, emerging evidence suggests that it may occur, including isolated cerebellar presentations [14], consistent with experimental data demonstrating NMDA receptor expression within cerebellar networks affecting granule cell layers [14, 15]. In our patient with dual anti-NMDA and anti-GAD antibodies—an association previously reported [16]—the relative contribution of each antibody to the cerebellar and ocular motor phenotype remains uncertain. Anti-GAD antibodies are well established in autoimmune cerebellar ataxia, stiff-person spectrum disorders, and autoimmune encephalitis [17], and their coexistence may reflect complex immune-mediated network dysfunction requiring further investigation.

Disturbances of eye-movement control are well recognised in multisystem atrophy and may serve as useful clinical indicators when differentiating it from other parkinsonian disorders [18, 19]. Prior studies have primarily emphasised fixation instability, square-wave jerks, and various forms of spontaneous and provoked nystagmus [20]. Our observation suggests that flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities may represent an uncommon manifestation within the neurodegenerative spectrum of multisystem atrophy, extending the range of ocular motor findings associated with this disorder. Ocular motor abnormalities have also been described in prion diseases, including sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease [21], further illustrating the diversity of ocular motor presentations in this condition.

Ocular motor abnormalities are well recognised in multiple sclerosis, with a wide variety of manifestations reported. Available literature indicates that saccadic oscillations within the flutter–opsoclonus spectrum may occur during acute disease phases as well as in chronic progressive stages, although they appear to represent a relatively uncommon presentation compared with smooth pursuit abnormalities, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, spontaneous nystagmus, and related findings [22, 23].

Importantly, flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities in this cohort occurred both in potentially treatable disorders and in neurodegenerative conditions lacking disease-modifying therapies. In immune-mediated cases, immunotherapy was temporally associated with clinical improvement, including reduction or resolution of these saccadic oscillations [24]. These observations highlight the clinical importance of prompt aetiological evaluation, as detection of flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities should prompt systematic investigation for potentially treatable causes [25].

Our findings also suggest that these abnormalities may be under-recognised in routine neurological practice. In patients with progressive encephalopathies, ataxias, and complex movement disorders, ocular motor abnormalities may be overlooked despite their diagnostic and localising value, supporting the use of systematic ocular motor assessment in complex neurological syndromes [26]. Videonystagmography appears to be a practical tool for detecting high-frequency saccadic oscillations and related ocular motor abnormalities that might otherwise remain unnoticed. Given their association with serious and occasionally treatable neurological disease, flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities may represent clinically meaningful signs warranting comprehensive diagnostic evaluation (Fig. 6).

By systematically documenting flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities across diverse neurological conditions using objective eye-movement recordings, this series provides comparative clinical observations that may assist clinicians in recognising these rare signs and considering a broader differential diagnosis when they are encountered.

Conclusion

In summary, this series expands the recognised clinical and aetiological spectrum of adult-onset flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities and provides detailed videonystagmographic characterisation across diverse neurological disorders, together with a proposed diagnostic algorithm for patients presenting with these findings. These observations support increased clinical awareness and underscore the need for prospective studies using standardised eye-movement recordings in patients with encephalopathies, progressive ataxias, and complex movement disorders.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and small sample size limit generalisability and preclude estimation of the frequency of flutter–opsoclonus spectrum abnormalities in the disorders described. The sampling rate and methodological limitations of the VNG system may have reduced precision in characterising small-amplitude or high-frequency saccadic oscillations and other abnormalities of visual fixation. Moreover, in several cases, concomitant nystagmus complicated interpretation of the tracings and limited confident differentiation between ocular flutter and multidirectional opsoclonus. Representative videos were available only for selected patients. Finally, because of the heterogeneous aetiologies, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than evidence of causal disease-specific mechanisms.
