Advances in adjunctive root coverage techniques: a narrative review of laser therapy, hyaluronic acid, and nanomaterials
Authors: Helmi M. A. Khatib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gingival recession (GR) is a prevalent condition characterized by the apical migration of the gingival margin, leading to root exposure and aesthetic and functional challenges. While conventional surgical techniques like coronally advanced flaps and connective tissue grafts are considered the gold standard for root coverage, these methods are limited by patient morbidity and inconsistent long-term outcomes. Adjunctive strategies such as laser therapy, hyaluronic acid (HA), and nanomaterials have been introduced to improve clinical results. This narrative review explores the rationale and clinical relevance of these adjunctive therapies in root coverage procedures. This narrative review (2017–2025) critically examines laser therapy, hyaluronic acid, and nanomaterials as adjuncts in surgical root coverage. Human studies were prioritized, with preclinical data included for nanomaterials. Biological mechanisms, clinical efficacy, and limitations were analyzed to provide a comparative perspective and highlight future research directions in periodontal regeneration. The literature indicates that while these therapies offer distinct benefits, their clinical effectiveness remains inconclusive. Laser therapy aids tissue healing, HA offers regenerative effects, and nanomaterials facilitate bioactive delivery. However, clinical evidence on long-term efficacy, especially in achieving consistent root coverage, remains limited. Adjunctive therapies such as laser therapy, HA, and nanomaterials show promise in improving outcomes for gingival recession treatment. However, further high-quality clinical trials are needed to validate their efficacy, safety, and long-term results.

Introduction

The oral cavity plays an essential role in vital physiological functions, including mastication and speech, while also contributing significantly to facial aesthetics. The periodontium—which includes the alveolar bone, periodontal ligament (PDL), cementum, and gingiva—is primarily responsible for maintaining tooth stability, distributing occlusal forces, and serving as a barrier against microbial invasion. However, various pathological and mechanical factors—including dental caries and periodontal disease—can compromise the integrity of the dentition and supporting structures, with gingival recession being one of the most prevalent and clinically significant manifestations.

Gingival recession (GR), defined as the apical migration of the gingival margin relative to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ), resulting in root surface exposure, is a common clinical finding, particularly among older adults. In a cross-sectional study conducted in Białystok among individuals aged 65–74 years, approximately 79% of subjects exhibited gingival recession on at least one tooth surface (Sulewska et al. 2017). GR presents multiple clinical challenges, including dentin hypersensitivity, increased risk of root caries, compromised aesthetics, and potential deterioration of periodontal support, its etiology is multifactorial, encompassing anatomical factors (e.g., thin periodontal biotype, alveolar dehiscence), traumatic events (e.g., aggressive toothbrushing, occlusal trauma), and inflammatory periodontal diseases (Mostafa and Fatima 2022; Niemczyk et al. 2024).

Various surgical techniques have been developed to treat GR, with coronally advanced flaps (CAF), subepithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTG), and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) being considered the current gold standard for root coverage (Chambrone et al. 2022; Mostafa and Fatima 2022). These approaches have shown efficacy in reducing recession depth and improving clinical attachment. However, these techniques are associated with certain limitations, such as increased patient morbidity, surgical complexity, and inconsistent long-term outcomes (Chambrone et al. 2022).

In response to these limitations, various adjunctive strategies have been introduced to enhance the clinical predictability of root coverage procedures, reduce invasiveness, and promote true periodontal regeneration. Among these, lasers, hyaluronic acid (HA), and nanomaterials have attracted increasing attention due to their potential to modulate wound healing and improve regenerative outcomes.

This narrative review seeks to fill a critical gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive, integrated analysis of three emerging adjunctive therapies—laser therapy, hyaluronic acid, and nanomaterials—in periodontal plastic surgery. Unlike previous reviews that often focus on a single modality or only on clinical outcomes, this work simultaneously integrates three core dimensions: fundamental biological mechanisms, current clinical evidence (including its inconsistencies), and translational barriers such as protocol standardization and cost-effectiveness. By offering this holistic and comparative perspective, the review provides clinicians and researchers with a consolidated framework to understand the potential, limitations, and future directions of these strategies, while highlighting the need for high-quality studies to validate their clinical utility.

Review design and objective

This study was designed as a comprehensive narrative review. The primary aim was to critically synthesize the current literature on the efficacy, underlying mechanisms, and clinical applications of three adjunctive therapies—laser therapy, hyaluronic acid (HA), and nanomaterials—in surgical root coverage procedures. The review focused on evaluating biological mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and comparative effectiveness of these adjuncts in periodontal plastic surgery.

Laser therapy in periodontal plastic surgery

Laser therapy has emerged as a valuable adjunct in periodontal plastic surgery, offering minimally invasive alternatives for root coverage (RC) and soft tissue regeneration. Its clinical utility spans a wide range of applications—from root surface biomodification to modulation of wound healing—aiming to enhance treatment outcomes and patient comfort.

Mechanisms of Action

Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT):

Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT):

Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT):

Recent evidence suggests that LLLT promotes bone regeneration through the coupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Its effects are mediated by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which activate hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), leading to the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β). These molecular pathways stimulate type H vessel formation, enhance osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, and facilitate extracellular matrix and collagen fiber deposition. Collectively, these mechanisms accelerate vascularized bone formation and provide promising insights into bone tissue engineering applications (Bai et al. 2021).

2.Erbium Lasers (Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG):

2.Erbium Lasers (Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG):

Erbium Lasers (Er:YAG and Er,Cr:YSGG):

Erbium lasers operate at mid-infrared wavelengths, allowing efficient ablation of hard and soft tissues with minimal thermal damage. They are particularly effective in removing the smear layer and detoxifying root surfaces, exposing a biologically active dentin matrix conducive to fibrin clot formation and graft integration. Additionally, erbium lasers can facilitate atraumatic harvesting of de-epithelialized gingival grafts (DGG), improving intraoperative visibility and hemostasis. However, these advantages have not consistently translated into superior clinical outcomes in terms of root coverage (Poormoradi et al. 2018).

3.Diode Lasers:

3.Diode Lasers:

Diode Lasers:

Diode lasers, particularly at the 980 nm wavelength in continuous-wave mode, exert their effects primarily through photothermal interaction with soft tissues, leading to precise incisions and efficient hemostasis. In periodontal plastic surgery, this has been explored for vestibular releasing incisions, where the thermal effect may reduce muscle reattachment and minimize flap tension during healing (Kalakonda et al. 2016). Consequently, this mechanism may contribute to improved flap stability and more predictable wound closure.

Limitations and controversies

Low-Level Laser Therapy offers several biological and clinical advantages in periodontal plastic surgery. It enhances mitochondrial activity and ATP synthesis, leading to faster cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration. Clinically, LLLT has been shown to reduce postoperative inflammation, pain, and edema, while accelerating wound healing and re-epithelialization. It also promotes angiogenesis and collagen synthesis, resulting in improved flap stability and better esthetic outcomes. When used adjunctively with procedures such as coronally advanced flap (CAF), LLLT increases clinical attachment gain, enhances root coverage predictability, and improves the quality of the regenerated gingival tissue. (Amitha et al. 2022). Despite these positive reports and the theoretical benefits of laser-assisted approaches—such as enhanced hemostasis, reduced postoperative discomfort, and accelerated wound healing—broader systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials have yielded inconsistent results. Several studies failed to confirm significant improvements in CRC or long-term outcomes compared to conventional techniques alone) (Santamaria et al. 2017, Poormoradi et al. 2018, Yan et al. 2018; Eltayeb et al. 2021).

Notably, randomized controlled clinical trials comparing CAF with and without adjunctive laser therapy have shown conflicting results, with some reporting improved patient-centered outcomes but no significant differences in root coverage percentages. This inconsistency underscores the urgent need for additional high-quality, multicenter clinical trials to clarify the clinical value of lasers in root coverage procedures.

Hyaluronic acid in periodontal therapy

Hyaluronic acid (HA) has gained attention as a biological adjunct in periodontal plastic surgery due to its favorable biocompatibility, viscoelasticity, and wound-healing properties. It has been applied alongside various root coverage techniques—such as coronally advanced flaps (CAF) and modified coronally advanced tunnel (MCAT)—with the goal of improving healing quality, gingival thickness, and esthetic outcomes.

Mechanisms of action

Anti-inflammatory and Healing Modulation:

Anti-inflammatory and Healing Modulation:

Anti-inflammatory and Healing Modulation:

HA reduces local inflammation by downregulating proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) and modulating immune cell activity. This creates a more favorable environment for early healing and reduces postoperative discomfort (Mohammad et al. 2023).

2.Tissue Regeneration and Matrix Stability:

2.Tissue Regeneration and Matrix Stability:

Tissue Regeneration and Matrix Stability:

HA enhances fibroblast proliferation, supports collagen synthesis, and promotes extracellular matrix organization. Its hygroscopic nature allows it to maintain a hydrated matrix, facilitating soft tissue remodeling and graft integration (Asparuhova et al. 2019).

3.Cellular Signaling and Angiogenesis:

3.Cellular Signaling and Angiogenesis:

Cellular Signaling and Angiogenesis:

High-molecular-weight HA interacts with surface receptors (e.g., CD44) on fibroblasts and keratinocytes, supporting angiogenesis and epithelial repair. This promotes faster soft tissue closure and greater tissue thickness (Al-Rekabi et al. 2019).

Limitations and controversies

Recent clinical investigations have highlighted the potential of hyaluronic acid (HA) as an adjunct in the management of gingival recession and periodontitis. A systematic review by Mehta et al. (2022) analyzed randomized controlled clinical trials and concluded that adjunctive HA application in root coverage procedures yielded superior outcomes in terms of complete root coverage, mean root coverage, clinical attachment gain, and keratinized tissue width compared with controls. Similarly, a randomized controlled clinical trial by Olszewska-Czyz et al. (2021) demonstrated significant improvements in clinical attachment gain and reductions in gingival inflammation with HA in non-surgical periodontal therapy, without reporting adverse effects.

Nevertheless, despite these favorable biological and anti-inflammatory properties, clinical findings remain inconsistent. While some randomized controlled trials reported enhanced mean root coverage and gingival thickness with HA, others failed to demonstrate significant improvements in complete root coverage (CRC) or attachment gain compared with conventional approaches (Skierska et al. 2024; Talebi Ardakani et al. 2025). Variability in study design, HA formulations, concentrations, and application protocols, coupled with the small number of available trials, complicates direct comparisons and limits the strength of current evidence. Moreover, most studies are constrained by small sample sizes and short follow-up periods (< 12 months), restricting conclusions on long-term stability. These inconsistencies highlight the urgent need for standardized application protocols and well-designed, large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with extended follow-up to determine the true therapeutic value of hyaluronic acid (HA) in periodontal regeneration (Inchingolo et al. 2025).

Nanomaterials in periodontal and root coverage therapies

Nanomaterials have gained increasing attention as adjuncts in periodontal and root coverage procedures, owing to their exceptional physicochemical characteristics—particularly their high surface area-to-volume ratio, tunable porosity, and ability to mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM). These properties enable improved delivery of bioactive molecules, promote cell adhesion, and enhance wound healing, thereby supporting both hard and soft tissue regeneration, offering a promising platform for advancing regenerative periodontal therapies (Xu et al. 2023; Zong et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2024).

Classification and functional properties

Natural Polymers:

Natural Polymers:

Natural Polymers:

Natural biopolymers such as chitosan, bacterial cellulose, and gelatin exhibit favorable biocompatibility and biodegradability. Chitosan, in particular, has shown the ability to stimulate gingival fibroblast proliferation, enhance osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs), and promote mineralized tissue formation, making it a strong candidate for scaffold-based periodontal applications (Shen et al. 2018; Varoni et al. 2018).

2.Synthetic Polymers:

2.Synthetic Polymers:

Synthetic Polymers:

Synthetic polymers including polylactic acid (PLA) and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) offer greater mechanical integrity and processability. Their degradation profiles can be controlled to match the rate of tissue regeneration, and they have demonstrated support for stem cell proliferation and osteogenesis in periodontal defect models (Shen et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2018).

3.Inorganic Nanomaterials:

3.Inorganic Nanomaterials:

Inorganic Nanomaterials:

Inorganic components such as nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) and bioactive glass (BG) enhance osteogenic activity and neovascularization. Magnesium-doped nHA scaffolds have shown increased bioactivity and bone regeneration in preclinical studies. BG nanoparticles support osteoblast proliferation and bone formation, although their efficacy in regenerating cementum and periodontal ligament (PDL) remains limited (Wang et al. 2021a, b; Shi et al. 2024).

Advanced fabrication techniques

Emerging fabrication methods allow for better structural and functional customization of nanomaterial-based scaffolds. Electrospinning permits fine control over fiber diameter and porosity, facilitating cell migration and nutrient diffusion (Zhuang et al. 2019; Lan et al. 2021). Computer-aided design (CAD) and three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies further enable the creation of multiphasic scaffolds tailored to specific anatomical and biological requirements (Wang et al. 2021a, b).

Immunomodulatory and regenerative mechanisms

Nanomaterials can actively modulate the local immune response to favor regenerative outcomes. Many nanoparticle systems polarize macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, increasing TGF-β and IL-10 secretion while downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 (Mahon et al. 2020). Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), particularly at 45 nm, have shown promising potential in periodontal regeneration by modulating both inflammation and cell differentiation. They promote macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhance the expression of reparative factors such as BMP-2. Animal studies further confirm their ability to promote new bone, cementum, and periodontal attachment formation while limiting tissue destruction in periodontitis models (Ni et al. 2019).

Antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory applications

The antimicrobial properties of nanomaterials contribute to improved infection control, especially on exposed root surfaces prone to bacterial colonization. Their integration into antibacterial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has introduced new possibilities for periodontitis management (Afrasiabi et al. 2021). Also, zinc oxide-based hydrogels have demonstrated efficacy in reducing the load of periodontopathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, while simultaneously promoting clinical healing (Mou et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2024).

Limitations and controversies

Despite the promising regenerative potential of nanomaterials in periodontal therapy, significant concerns regarding their biocompatibility and long-term safety present a major translational hurdle. The primary apprehensions stem from their potential to induce toxicity, which is intrinsically linked to their physicochemical properties such as size, shape, and surface charge. A critical issue is their unpredictable biodistribution; nanoparticles can disseminate from the application site and accumulate in secondary organs like the liver and spleen, potentially provoking persistent inflammatory responses or oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Furthermore, the clearance kinetics of many engineered nanomaterials remain a challenge, as their resistance to enzymatic breakdown can lead to long-term retention in the body. These unresolved safety questions are compounded by formidable regulatory challenges, including the lack of nano-specific, standardized long-term toxicity testing protocols (Havelikar et al. 2024). Therefore, while nanomaterials offer a revolutionary platform for bioactive delivery and tissue engineering in root coverage procedures, a thorough and systematic evaluation of their safety profile is an indispensable prerequisite for their routine clinical adoption in periodontology. Moreover, controlling key parameters such as particle size, morphology, and surface charge remains technically demanding but essential for ensuring consistent biological behavior and predictable degradation (Chen and Huang 2022, Xu et al. 2023, Zong, Bronckaers et al. 2023, Shi et al. 2024).

Conclusion

The management of gingival recession remains a critical component of periodontal plastic surgery. Conventional surgical techniques, such as coronally advanced flaps (CAF) with a connective tissue graft (SCTG), continue to represent the gold standard, providing predictable and durable outcomes. While the exploration of adjunctive technologies—including laser therapy, hyaluronic acid, and nanomaterials—aims to address limitations like patient morbidity and technique sensitivity, their current role must be carefully qualified.

The mechanisms of these adjuncts are distinct: lasers offer precise soft tissue management, hyaluronic acid exhibits anti-inflammatory and matrix-stabilizing properties, and nanomaterials provide innovative drug-delivery platforms. Nevertheless, the translation of these mechanistic advantages into consistent and superior clinical outcomes remains unproven. The current body of evidence is characterized by significant heterogeneity in protocols, limited sample sizes, and a scarcity of long-term comparative studies.

In brief, it should be emphasized that although laser and HA have demonstrated encouraging clinical outcomes, their adjunctive use in root coverage procedures is still under evaluation and cannot yet replace established gold-standard approaches. Their potential is promising, but clinical application should continue within well-controlled and standardized research frameworks. The next essential step involves conducting high-quality, multicenter randomized controlled clinical trials to confirm their efficacy, safety, and long-term stability. Only through such rigorous, evidence-based validation can these innovative techniques be reliably integrated into routine clinical practice, advancing periodontal regenerative therapy toward greater precision and predictability.
