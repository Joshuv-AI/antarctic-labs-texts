Efficient Adaptive Data Analysis over Dense Distributions
Joon Suk Huh

Abstract
Modern data workflows are inherently adaptive, repeatedly querying the same dataset to refine and validate sequential decisions, but such adaptivity can lead to overfitting and invalid statistical inference. Adaptive Data Analysis (ADA) mechanisms address this challenge; however, there is a fundamental tension between computational efficiency and sample complexity. For T T rounds of adaptive analysis, computationally efficient algorithms typically incur suboptimal 𝒪 ⁡ ( T ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T}) sample complexity, whereas statistically optimal 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}(\log T) algorithms are computationally intractable under standard cryptographic assumptions. In this work, we shed light on this trade-off by identifying a natural class of data distributions under which both computational efficiency and optimal sample complexity are achievable. We propose a computationally efficient ADA mechanism that attains optimal 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}(\log T) sample complexity when the data distribution is dense with respect to a known prior. This setting includes, in particular, feature–label data distributions arising in distribution-specific learning. As a consequence, our mechanism also yields a sample-efficient (i.e., 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) \mathcal{O}(\log T) samples) statistical query oracle in the distribution-specific setting. Moreover, although our algorithm is not based on differential privacy, it satisfies a relaxed privacy notion known as Predicate Singling Out (PSO) security ( Cohen and Nissim 2020 ) . Our results thus reveal an inherent connection between adaptive data analysis and privacy beyond differential privacy.

1
Introduction

The inherent adaptivity prevalent in modern data science workflows, while undeniably powerful for discovery and iterative refinement, introduces significant challenges to statistical validity. The repeated interaction with and reuse of the same dataset for both hypothesis generation and subsequent validation can lead to
overfitting
or the generation of
false discoveries
(often colloquially termed “p-hacking” or attributed to “researcher degrees of freedom”). Such issues result in findings that may appear statistically significant within the analyzed sample but fail to generalize reliably to the underlying population from which the data were drawn. This breakdown of classical statistical guarantees contributes directly to the reproducibility crisis observed across various empirical scientific fields
(
Ioannidis 2005
;
Gelman and Loken 2014
)
. The core problem, often termed “double-dipping”, arises when the same data is utilized first to inform the analysis strategy and then again to validate the resulting conclusions, thereby coupling the randomness in the inferential target with the randomness in the data used for inference.

Adaptive data analysis (ADA)
(
Dwork et al. 2015c
)
is a modern algorithmic framework aimed at enabling statistically valid inference in adaptive settings, using as few samples as possible. At its core is the design of an intermediary, called an ADA mechanism, that mediates between the data and the analyst, providing answers to adaptively chosen queries while ensuring statistical validity. Since the foundational work of
Hardt and Ullman 2014
;
Dwork et al. 2015c
, there has been extensive progress in developing algorithmic techniques for preserving validity in adaptive settings
(
Dwork et al. 2015a
;
Dwork et al. 2015b
;
Cummings et al. 2016
;
Russo and Zou 2016
;
Rogers et al. 2016
;
Bassily et al. 2016
;
Smith 2017
;
Feldman and Steinke 2017
;
Feldman and Steinke 2018
;
De Stefani and Upfal 2019
;
Zrnic and Hardt 2019
;
Jung et al. 2020
;
Fish et al. 2020
;
Rogers et al. 2020
;
Kontorovich et al. 2022
;
Dagan and Kur 2022
;
Marchant and Rubinstein 2024
;
Blanc 2025
)
, primarily based on Differential Privacy (DP)
(
Dwork et al. 2014
)
and/or algorithmic stability
(
Bassily et al. 2016
;
Smith 2017
;
Shenfeld and Ligett 2019
;
Shenfeld and Ligett 2023
)
.

In the standard setting, an analyst asks linear queries
q(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and seeks to estimate expectations of the form
\expec_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(x)]
. When the queries are fully adaptive, existing computationally efficient approaches achieve
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
sample complexity, where
T
is the number of query rounds. This rate is tight for
general
data distributions: an
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
lower bound holds for any
computationally efficient
ADA mechanism under standard cryptographic assumptions, such as the existence of one-way functions
(
Steinke and Ullman 2015
;
Steinke and Ullman 2016
;
Bun et al. 2017
;
Ullman et al. 2018
;
Nissim et al. 2023
;
Dinur et al. 2023
)
.

In contrast, data sketching-based methods such as Private Multiplicative Weights (PMW), which maintain an internal sketch of the dataset
(
Hardt and Rothblum 2010
)
, can achieve
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
sample complexity, but are computationally intractable, as maintaining such a sketch often requires time exponential in the data dimension
n
.

Hence, there is a general tension between computational efficiency and sample efficiency in adaptive data analysis. However, existing hardness results that rule out efficient
o(\sqrt{T})
sample complexity assume that the mechanism must work for
arbitrary
data distributions. This observation raises a natural question:
Is there a natural class of data distributions
\mathcal{D}
that admits both
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
sample complexity and computational efficiency?

We answer this question in the affirmative. Specifically, we show that for any sufficiently well-spread (“dense”) distribution with respect to a known prior (which may be uniform), there exists an ADA mechanism that is both computationally efficient and sample efficient, with sample complexity growing only logarithmically in the number of queries. Such distributions arise naturally in distribution-specific learning
(
Kharitonov 1993
;
Balcan and Long 2013
;
Shamir 2018
;
Blanc et al. 2025
)
, including distributions over pairs
(x,f(x))
where
x
is drawn from a known marginal distribution over features and
f
is an unknown Boolean function.

1.1
Main Results

Efficient ADA Mechanism for Dense Distributions.

We first present a computationally efficient ADA mechanism for dense data distributions. Density is defined with respect to a known reference (prior) distribution. Specifically, we assume that both the mechanism and the analyst are given a prior distribution
\mathcal{D}_{g}
generated by a known, efficiently computable function
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
over a finite domain
\mathcal{I}
. We then consider an
unknown
target distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is
\lambda
-dense in
\mathcal{D}_{g}
, meaning that
\Pr_{i\sim\mathcal{D}}[I=i]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\Pr_{i\sim\mathcal{D}_{g}}[I=i]
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Intuitively, this condition implies that one could sample from
\mathcal{D}
using
\mathcal{D}_{g}
via rejection sampling with acceptance probability at least
\lambda
. However, this form of “rejection samplability” does not imply that the analyst can perform data analysis over the unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
, since rejection sampling requires explicit knowledge of the probability mass function of
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 1

(Restatement of Theorem
5
,
6
)
.

Let
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
be an efficiently computable function. For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{I}
that is
\lambda
-dense with respect to the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{g}
generated by
g
, there exists an ADA mechanism that answers
\expec_{k\sim\mathcal{D}}[q_{t}(k)]
to within
\epsilon
accuracy for any sequence of
T
adaptively chosen queries
(q_{t})_{t\in[T]}
, where each
q_{t}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, using only
M\in\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}\epsilon^{-4}\cdot\log T)
i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{D}
, and running in time
\mathsf{poly}(n,\epsilon^{-1},\lambda^{-1},T)
.

Notably, the sample complexity is independent of the ambient data dimension
n
, matching the
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
sample complexity achievable in the non-adaptive setting via Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound.

Sample-efficient Distribution-Specific Statistical Query Oracle.

Why, then, do we care about dense target distributions? A natural example arises in canonical machine learning settings. Consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over pairs
(X,Y)
, where
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{h}
for some
known
marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h}
over a feature space
\mathcal{X}
generated by
h:\{0,1\}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, and
Y=f(X)\in\{-1,1\}
for an unknown labeling function
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
. In this case, we can choose a prior distribution
\mathcal{D}_{g}
generated by
g(r)\coloneq(h(r_{1:n-1}),(r_{n}-1)/2)
for an
n
-bit seed
r
, and one can verify that
\mathcal{D}
is
(1/2)
-dense in
\mathcal{D}_{g}
. Here, the distribution
\mathcal{D}
is unknown to the analyst due to the unknown labeling function
f
, while the marginal distribution over features,
\mathcal{D}|_{X}
, is known and efficiently samplable via
h
.

This setting corresponds to the classical notion of distribution-specific learning, and any mechanism that answers (linear) queries with respect to such a distribution
\mathcal{D}
implements a distribution-specific Statistical Query (SQ) oracle
(
Kearns 1998
)
. Consequently, our mechanism naturally yields a sample-efficient distribution-specific SQ oracle, as formalized in Theorem
7
.

Privacy Guarantee.

Many existing ADA mechanisms are built on differential privacy (DP) and thus often inherit formal DP guarantees. In contrast, our mechanism is not based on DP and does not provide DP guarantees. Nevertheless, perhaps surprisingly, it still offers a meaningful privacy guarantee: it satisfies
Predicate Singling Out
(PSO) security
(
Cohen and Nissim 2020
)
, a notion designed to capture the “singling out” risk addressed by the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). While PSO security is strictly weaker than DP, it rules out a broad class of privacy attacks and remains relevant in settings where DP may be unnecessarily conservative. In our case, PSO security (Theorem
9
) follows from an argument that builds on a key lemma from our sample complexity analysis and, notably, requires no explicit noise addition.

1.2
Technical Overview

Unlike many computationally efficient ADA mechanisms that achieve
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
sample complexity, our mechanism does not rely on differential privacy or algorithmic stability arguments. Instead, it builds on an iterative data sketching strategy inspired by the Private Multiplicative Weights (PMW) algorithm
(
Hardt and Rothblum 2010
)
. The goal is to maintain an internal sketch of the dataset
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
such that linear statistical queries evaluated on the sketch closely approximate their values on
S
. While PMW achieves this by maintaining a distribution over the data universe and updating it via multiplicative weights, this approach incurs computational cost proportional to the domain size
2^{n}
, which is intractable.

We avoid this intractability by using (unconstrained) Online Gradient Descent (OGD)
(
Zinkevich 2003
)
over a unit ball and sketching
S
within this ball. Specifically, our algorithm can be viewed as a lazy version of OGD, where an update is performed only when the approximation error exceeds a prescribed threshold. This lazy update rule, together with the standard regret bound for OGD, implies that the number of such updates, and hence the number of vectors stored in the sketch, is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}\epsilon^{-2})
. Crucially, the intractability of PMW is avoided because answers can be reconstructed efficiently from the sketch via randomized inner-product estimation, as the sketch vectors lie within the unit ball of
\ell^{2}
geometry. Such sketch vectors can faithfully represent
\mathcal{D}
since
\mathcal{D}
is
\lambda
-dense in
\mathcal{D}_{g}
for some known sampling function
g
, which allows
\mathcal{D}
to be represented as a
2^{n}
-dimensional vector in the seed space of
g
.

The bounded number of updates (mistakes) of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}\epsilon^{-2})
in constructing the internal sketch of the dataset implies the existence of a succinct transcript of the mechanism’s update behavior, of size
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}\epsilon^{-2}\cdot\log T)
bits. As this transcript can simulate the mechanism’s exact behavior, it can be used to reconstruct all queries asked by
\mathcal{A}
, using only the internal randomness of the mechanism and the analyst, and without access to the sample
S
. This transcript plays a central role in ensuring statistical validity. Since it determines the entire query sequence, we can apply standard concentration arguments, namely Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound, over the small space of possible transcripts. This yields uniform convergence, guaranteeing that
|q_{t}(S)-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
for all
t\in[T]
with high probability, with sample size
|S|\in\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}\epsilon^{-4}\log T)
.

1.3
Related Work

Many algorithmic frameworks for adaptive data analysis (ADA) are based on differential privacy or algorithmic stability, as studied in
(
Bassily et al. 2016
;
Smith 2017
;
Feldman and Steinke 2018
;
Shenfeld and Ligett 2019
;
Jung et al. 2020
;
Blanc 2025
)
. These approaches provide strong generalization guarantees under adaptivity and run efficiently, but typically incur a sample complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
.

Closer to our approach, the idea of answering queries by reconstructing (or sketching) a dataset was introduced in
Blum et al. 2013
, which used the exponential mechanism
(
McSherry and Talwar 2007
)
both to reconstruct the dataset and to privately answer
non-adaptive
queries. This was extended in the seminal work of
Hardt and Rothblum 2010
, which proposed the Private Multiplicative Weights (PMW) algorithm, based on the multiplicative weights framework
(
Freund and Schapire 1999
;
Arora et al. 2012
)
, to handle adaptive queries while satisfying differential privacy. Notably, while PMW is designed as a differentially private algorithm, it also serves as an ADA mechanism with
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
sample complexity and later extended beyond linear queries
(
Ullman 2015
)
. However, it is computationally intractable in general, as it requires sampling from Gibbs distributions over exponentially large domains like
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Organization

In §

, we introduce the adaptive data analysis framework and define (pseudo)dense data distributions. In §

2.1
Problem Setting: Adaptive Data Analysis.

An adaptive data analysis (ADA) mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is an algorithm that holds a sufficient amount of i.i.d. data
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}
drawn from an
unknown
distribution
\mathcal{D}
over a finite domain
\mathcal{I}
, and provides answers
a_{t}
to a sequence of adaptively chosen Boolean queries
(q_{t})_{t\in[T]}
, where each
q_{t}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, selected by an analyst (or adversary)
\mathcal{A}
. The goal is to ensure that these answers are
\epsilon
-accurate; that is,
|a_{t}-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})|\leq\epsilon
for all
t\in[T]
.

In the non-adaptive setting, where all queries are chosen in advance, it suffices for
\mathcal{M}
to hold
|S|\in\mathcal{O}(\log T)
samples, by standard concentration arguments (e.g., Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound). Our goal is to retain this
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
sample complexity even when queries are chosen adaptively, under certain assumptions on the data distribution, while ensuring that the ADA mechanism remains
computationally efficient
; that is, it runs in time
\mathsf{poly}(\epsilon^{-1},\lambda^{-1},\log N,|S|,T)
.

We formalize adaptive data analysis as a game between the ADA mechanism
\mathcal{M}
and the analyst (or adversary)
\mathcal{A}
, referred to as the
Adaptive Query Game
, depicted in Game
2
. Note that allowing
\mathcal{A}
to choose the distribution
\mathcal{D}
in Game
2
only strengthens the
\mathcal{A}
; thus, our result also applies to the more natural setting in which
\mathcal{D}
is
unknown
to both the analyst
\mathcal{A}
and the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
.

Let
\Pr_{\mathsf{AQ}_{N,M,T}[\mathcal{A}\rightleftharpoons\mathcal{M}]}
denote the probability distribution over all random variables (such as the
a_{t}
s and
q_{t}
s) that are realized during the execution of the game
\mathsf{AQ}_{N,M,T}[\mathcal{A}\rightleftharpoons\mathcal{M}]
. We then define accuracy of
\mathcal{M}
as follows:

Definition 2

(Accuracy)
.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is said to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-accurate with respect to
\mathcal{A}
with sample complexity
M_{0}
if, for all
M\geq M_{0}
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathsf{AQ}_{N,M,T}[\mathcal{A}\rightleftharpoons\mathcal{M}]}\Big[\forall t\in[T],\ \left|a_{t}-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})\right|\leq\epsilon\Big]\geq 1-\delta,

where the probability is taken over the internal randomness of both
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{A}
.

Dense Data Distribution.

We assume that both the analyst and the mechanism have access to a function
g:[N]\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
for some finite domain
\mathcal{I}
, and hence can sample from the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{g}
, where
K\sim\mathcal{D}_{g}
is given by
I\coloneq g(X)
with
X\sim U_{[N]}
. We further assume that
g
is efficiently computable, namely in
\mathsf{poly}(\log N)
time. We say that a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{I}
is
(\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-dense
if
\Pr_{i\sim\mathcal{D}}[I=i]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\Pr_{i\sim\mathcal{D}_{g}}[I=i]
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Our algorithm, in fact, applies to a slightly broader class of distributions that includes not only truly dense distributions but also
pseudodense
ones, namely distributions that are indistinguishable from dense distributions from the perspective of the analyst. We formalize this notion below.

Definition 3

(Pseudodensity)
.

For any adversary
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{\mathcal{I}}
denote the set of all queries that
\mathcal{A}
may output at any point, on any input, and under any internal randomness. We say that a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{I}
is
(\epsilon,\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-
pseudodense
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
if there exists a
(\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-dense distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
over
\mathcal{I}
such that
|q(\mathcal{D})-q(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon
for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\mathcal{A}}
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
almost surely selects a distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is
(\epsilon,\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-pseudodense with respect to itself, we say that
\mathcal{A}
is an
(\epsilon,\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-
pseudodense challenger
.

Working in the Seed Space Without Loss of Generality.

Since the prior generator
g
is known, it is fixed throughout the AQA game. We can therefore absorb
g
into the analyst’s queries by composing them with
g
. Concretely, we replace each query
q_{t}:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
with the pullback query
q_{t}\circ g:[N]\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, so that the analyst effectively asks
q_{t}(g(\cdot))
over the seed space
[N]=\{0,1\}^{n}
. Under this viewpoint, we may work directly over the seed space and identify
\mathcal{I}=[N]
without loss of generality.
From this point onward, we adopt this convention throughout the remainder of the paper.
Accordingly, we focus on
(\epsilon,\lambda,U_{[N]})
-pseudodense distributions and challengers.

2.2
Application: Implementing Distribution-Specific Statistical Query Oracle

The above setting also captures a standard distribution specific learning scenario. Consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h,f}
over labeled examples
(X,Y)
, where
X
is drawn from a
known
marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h}
over a finite feature space
\mathcal{X}
, generated by an efficient sampler
h
, and
Y\coloneq f(X)\in\{-1,1\}
for an unknown labeling function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{-1,1\}
. Define a prior distribution
\mathcal{D}_{g}
by letting
I\sim\mathcal{D}_{g}
be given as
I\coloneq g(R)\coloneq(h(R_{1:n-1}),R_{n})
for a uniformly random seed
R\sim U_{[N]}
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{D}_{g}
samples a feature vector
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{h}
together with an independent uniform label bit.

Under this construction, the true labeled distribution
\mathcal{D}
is
(1/2)
-dense with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{g}
. Consequently, an ADA mechanism designed for
(\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{g})
-dense distributions can be interpreted as providing a
distribution-specific
statistical query interface for the labeled distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h,f}
.

Definition 4

(Distribution-specific SQ oracle
(
Kearns 1998
)
)
.

Fix a known distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h}
over a feature space
\mathcal{X}
and an unknown labeling function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\{-1,1\}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{h,f}
denote the induced distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,1\}
obtained by sampling
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{h}
and setting
Y\coloneq f(X)
. A distribution-specific statistical query oracle, denoted
\mathsf{DSQ}_{h}(f,\epsilon)
, takes as input a query function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{-1,1\}\to[-1,1]
and returns a value
v
satisfying

\displaystyle\left|v-\phi(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})\right|\leq\epsilon.

An algorithm that interacts with the unknown
f
only through calls to
\mathsf{DSQ}_{h}(f,\epsilon)
is called a distribution-specific SQ algorithm (with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{h}
).

From the discussion above, it is straightforward that an efficient ADA mechanism yields an implementation of
\mathsf{DSQ}_{h}(f,\epsilon)
using only
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
samples, up to a minor mismatch in the query range. In the standard DSQ model, a query
\phi
may take values in
[-1,1]
, whereas in our setting the queries are Boolean-valued in
\{-1,1\}
. This mismatch can be handled by a standard randomization argument: for any fixed
\phi:X\times\{-1,1\}\rightarrow[-1,1]
, one can sample i.i.d. Boolean queries
(q^{\phi}_{i})_{i\in[B]}
, where each
q^{\phi}_{i}:X\times\{-1,1\}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
satisfies
\expec[q^{\phi}_{i}(x,y)|(x,y)]=\phi(x,y)
. Then
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}q^{\phi}_{i}(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})
is an unbiased estimator of
\phi(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})
, and by Hoeffding’s inequality it concentrates around
\phi(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})
at rate
\mathcal{O}(B^{-1/2})
. Choosing
B
sufficiently large, we obtain
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}q^{\phi}_{i}(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})\approx\phi(\mathcal{D}_{h,f})
, which reduces
[-1,1]
-valued queries to Boolean queries in our framework.

Sample Complexity Analysis

In this section, we analyze the sample complexity of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}
as stated in Theorem
6
.

Analysis Overview.

Our analysis consists of two parts.

1.

We establish the following uniform concentration guarantee: with high probability, it holds that for all
t\in[T]
,
|q_{t}(S)-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, provided that the sample size
|S|
exceeds the threshold specified in Theorem
6
.

2.

Then, we show that the answers
a_{t}
output by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}
are close to
q_{t}(S)
, i.e., for all
t\in[T]
,
|a_{t}-q_{t}(S)|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
with high probability.

To establish the first part,
|q_{t}(S)-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
for all
t
, we outline the following approach. We argue that the sequence of queries
(q_{t})_{t\in[T]}
issued during the execution of
\mathsf{AQ}_{N,M,T}[\mathcal{A}\rightleftharpoons\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}]
can be reconstructed from a transcript of the internal update trajectory of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}
(specifically, the decisions made in Lines 7–11 of Alg.
2.2
)
without
access to the underlying sample set
S
. When this transcript admits a
succinct
representation, using only
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
bits, standard arguments based on Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound can then be used to obtain the uniform concentration bound
|q_{t}(S)-q_{t}(\mathcal{D})|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
with high probability, using
|S|\in\mathcal{O}(\log T)
samples.

The second part,
|a_{t}-q_{t}(S)|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, can be understood as a consequence of the fact that a mistake budget of
|\mathcal{G}|\leq 128(\lambda\epsilon)^{-2}
is sufficient, which will be justified via the regret bound of OGD. Therefore, the condition in Line 7 of Alg.
2.2
is equivalent to the condition
|\hat{u}_{t}-\lambda q_{t}(S)|>\lambda\epsilon/2
. Hence, if this condition is not triggered (Lines 12–14), the output
\hat{u}_{t}/\lambda
is
\epsilon/2
-close to
q_{t}(S)
.

5
Privacy Guarantee

In this section, we establish the privacy-preserving properties of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}
. Although
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{AQA}\textup{-}\mathsf{OGD}}
does not satisfy stronger guarantees such as differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2014
)
, we show that it enjoys a form of meaningful privacy protection known as Predicate Singling Out (PSO) security
(
Cohen and Nissim 2020
)
. This notion was introduced as a formal counterpart to the concept of “singling out” as defined in the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Predicate Singling Out (PSO).

The main privacy leakage mode we consider is Predicate Singling Out (PSO), which formalizes the GDPR’s notion of identifying an individual. Specifically, given a dataset
S\subseteq[N]
, a PSO adversary attempts to produce a predicate

that

a single entry in

be a dataset of size

over
[N]\simeq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}

be a predicate. We say that
p

isolates
a row in
S
if there exists a
unique

i\in S
such that
p(i)=1
, or equivalently, if
p(S)=1/M
. We denote this event by
\mathsf{iso}(p,S)
.
