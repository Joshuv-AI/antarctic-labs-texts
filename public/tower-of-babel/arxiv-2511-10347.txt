Functional limit theorems for elephant random walks on general periodic structures
Shuhei Shibata

Abstract
This paper investigates functional limit theorems for the Elephant Random Walk (ERW) on general periodic structures, extending the Bertenghi’s results on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} . Our results reveal new structure-dependent quantities that do not appear in the classical setting ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , highlighting how the underlying structure affects the asymptotic behavior of the walk.



1
Introduction

The asymptotic behavior of random walks with long-range memory has been extensively studied in recent years. In particular, Elephant Random Walk (ERW) has attracted considerable attention. The ERW model was introduced by Schütz and Trimper
[
23
]
in 2004 to study memory effects in a one-dimensional discrete-time random walk with a complete memory of its past, and it exhibits a phase transition from diffusive to superdiffusive behavior.

One natural generalization of the one-dimensional ERW is to consider higher-dimensional settings, particularly random walks on the standard lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. A number of works have addressed this extension (cf.
[
3
,
4
,
7
,
16
,
14
,
13
,
6
,
11
,
21
]
).
Bercu and Laulin
[
3
]
investigated the asymptotic behavior of the Multi-dimensional Elephant Random Walk (MERW) via a martingale approach. Later, Bertenghi
[
7
]
established functional limit theorems for MERW by employing a Pólya-type urn analysis, following the approach initiated by Baur and Bertoin
[
2
]
. This connection between the (M)ERW and Pólya urns enables the derivation of functional limit theorems for the (M)ERW within the general framework developed by Janson
[
17
]
.

In addition to studying the asymptotic behavior of a single walker, another natural extension is to investigate the interaction of multiple ERWs, particularly the collision problem—that is, whether two independent elephant random walks on the same lattice meet infinitely often or only finitely many times. Roy, Takei and Tanemura
[
22
]
studied the case where both ERWs on
\mathbb{Z}
have the same memory parameter. Later, Shibata and Shirai
[
24
]
extended their results to the case of different memory parameters and also obtained asymptotic results for the distance between them. Although this paper does not address the collision problem, we mention it here to emphasize the diversity of recent developments in the study of ERW.

Most of the works mentioned above focus on the ERWs on the standard lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
In this paper, we extend this framework to more general structures, including
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, as well as the
triangular lattice
, the
hexagonal lattice
and the
brick wall
, see Fig.
1
. Inspired by the work of Bertenghi
[
7
]
, who established functional limit theorems for the ERW on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we derive the corresponding asymptotic results for the ERW on more general state spaces such as these lattices, using a Pólya-type urn approach. Our results reveal new underlying structure-dependent quantities which are not observed in the classical
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
case, demonstrating that the underlying structure significantly influences the asymptotic behavior of the walk.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
and Section
3
, we provide the mathematical definition of the state spaces we consider and the ERW on them. In Section
4

5

6

2
Settings

To set the stage for our results, we first introduce the mathematical framework considered in this paper.

Given two finite sets of distinct vectors
\mathcal{U}:=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{W}:=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m^{\prime}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for fixed integers
m,m^{\prime}\geq 2
. We call the elements of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}

step vectors
since, in Section
3
, we will define a random walk whose steps are drawn from these sets. We will consider two settings depending on whether the step sets coincide, namely the case
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
.

•

Case
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
:
In this case,
we consider the semigroup generated by them, that is,

\Gamma=\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}u_{i};\ k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.1)

Here, we assume that this semigroup
\Gamma
is a
lattice
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., it is a discrete additive subgroup that spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
. For this case,
\Gamma
is analyzed as a single-colored vertex set, as in the triangular lattice on the left in Fig.
1
, owing to the structural indistinguishability of all vertices.

•

Case
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
:

In this case, we first define the semigroup

\Gamma_{0}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}l_{j}w_{j};\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}l_{j},\ k_{i},l_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}.

Here, we assume that this semigroup is a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We then consider the following state space
\Gamma
that can be expressed as the disjoint union of
\Gamma_{0}
and the translated set
\Gamma_{0}+\mathcal{U}
:

\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}\sqcup(\Gamma_{0}+\mathcal{U})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.2)

We define the vertex classes by
Z_{\mathcal{U}}:=\Gamma_{0}
and
Z_{\mathcal{W}}:=\Gamma_{0}+\mathcal{U}
. Under these settings, we enforce the
Alternating Rule
:

–

For any
z\in Z_{\mathcal{U}}
and
i=1,\dots,m
,
z+u_{i}\in Z_{\mathcal{W}}
.

–

For any
z\in Z_{\mathcal{W}}
and
j=1,\dots,m^{\prime}
,
z+w_{j}\in Z_{\mathcal{U}}
.

This rule ensures that the walk defined in Section
3
always remains within
\Gamma
.
\Gamma
is therefore a bipartite graph with vertices classified according to the decomposition in (
2.2
), and is analyzed as a two-colored vertex set, as in the hexagonal lattice and brick wall shown in the center and right panels of Fig.
1
.

Remark 2.1
.

When
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\dots,\pm e_{d}\}
where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Gamma
coincides with the standard integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Note that there are also many other ways to choose the vectors that generate
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Various well-known structures, such as the triangular lattice, the hexagonal lattice and the brick wall, see Fig.
1
, can be realized as
\Gamma
, by choosing suitable families of step vectors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
. For clarity, in Section
6
, we list the representative choices of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
for several typical examples.

Remark 2.2
.

We introduce the
\Gamma
given by (
2.2
) to represent a setting in which each vertex is assigned a specific type (or color), as in the hexagonal lattice.
Although the following example does not satisfy the decomposition of (
2.2
), one could construct an ERW model and a corresponding setting that covers it:

\mathcal{U}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},(1,1)^{\top}\},\quad\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2}\}.

We also note that the trivial case
m=1
can be analyzed within the same framework, but it is excluded from our study.

3
Definition of the ERW on
\Gamma

Next, we introduce the mathematical definitions of the ERW
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
\Gamma
.

For a given dimension
d\geq 1
and the sets
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
introduced in Section
2
, let
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be random sequences taking values in
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
, respectively. First, we discuss the case
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
. We consider an ERW that alternately moves between two vertex classes
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
and
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
by making a step chosen from
\mathcal{U}
when at
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
, and a step chosen from
\mathcal{W}
when at
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
. At time
n=0
, the elephant is started from the origin in
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
. Let
1\leq i_{0}\leq m
and
1\leq j_{0}\leq m^{\prime}
be fixed. At time
n=1
, for the sake of definiteness, the elephant takes a deterministic step in the direction of
u_{i_{0}}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{1}=u_{i_{0}}
. By the Alternating Rule, it then moves from the origin to a vertex in
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
. At time
n=2
, the elephant takes a deterministic step in the direction of
w_{j_{0}}
, i.e.,
\tau_{1}=w_{j_{0}}
. By the Alternating Rule, it then moves from the vertex in
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
to a vertex in
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
. Subsequently, the elephant proceeds by alternately visiting the two vertex classes
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
and
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
,
and generates an alternating sequence of step choices,
\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2},\sigma_{3},\tau_{3},\dots
.

For each
n=1,2,\dots
, given the past steps
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}
and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n}
, we define the
(n+1)
-th step of the two processes by

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_{U_{n}})=p,

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma)=\frac{1-p}{m-1}\quad\text{for all $\sigma\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{\sigma_{U_{n}}\}$},

and

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n+1}=\tau_{W_{n}})=p,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n+1}=\tau)=\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}\quad\text{for all $\tau\in\mathcal{W}\setminus\{\tau_{W_{n}}\}$},

where
p\in(0,1)
is called the
memory parameter
, and the random variables
U_{n}
and
W_{n}
are uniformly distributed on
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. We assume that all four collections of random variables,
U_{n}
and
W_{n}
and the process
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the process
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, are mutually independent.

The sequences
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
generate the ERW
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
\Gamma
given by (
2.2
) with
S_{0}=\mathbf{0}
defined by

S_{2n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma_{i}+\tau_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2n-1}=S_{2(n-1)}+\sigma_{n}\quad\text{for $n=1,2,\dots$},

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{0}:=(0,\dots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We call this process
Type-II ERW
. It is important to note that the ERW moves alternately between
Z_{\mathcal{U}}
and
Z_{\mathcal{W}}
with taking values alternately from
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. This ensures that over any
2n
steps, the number of movements drawn from the set
\mathcal{U}
is precisely
n
, and similarly, the number of movements drawn from
\mathcal{W}
is precisely
n
. This deterministic balance in step counts plays a key role in our analysis.

Next, we discuss the case
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
. We note that since the process depends on the entire past trajectory, we describe the definition separately from the case
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
.

The ERW
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
\Gamma
given by (
2.1
) with
S_{0}=\mathbf{0}
is defined by

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\quad\text{for $n=1,2,\dots$}.

(3.2)

We call this process
Type-I ERW
. When
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\dots,\pm e_{d}\}
, i.e.,
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we recover the MERW introduced by Bercu and Laulin
[
3
]
and Bertenghi
[
7
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

The essential difference between Type-I ERW and Type-II ERW does not lie in the distinction between
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
, but rather in how past steps are chosen: For Type-II ERW, only steps chosen from odd or even times are used, whereas for Type-I ERW, the choice depends on the entire past.
For example, when considering
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2}\}
, our current formulation defines the ERW using (
3.2
), but one could also consider an ERW based on (
3.1
) and then this yields a different stochastic model. However, in Section
5
, we show that the limiting distributions of Type-II ERW under
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
and Type-I ERW coincide, except for the superdiffusive case where the initial step affects the asymptotic behavior of the walk, see Theorem
5.8
.

4
Connection to the Pólya-type urns

In this section, we explain the relation between the ERW on
\Gamma
and Pólya-type urn models. The urn model used for the ERW belongs to the results provided by Janson
[
17
]
for the more general framework of Pólya urns.

First, we discuss the case
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
. Consider two discrete-time urns. One urn contains balls of
m
distinct colors, and the other contains balls of
m^{\prime}
distinct colors. At any time
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the compositions of the two urns are described by
m
-dimensional random vector
X_{n}=(X_{n}^{1},X_{n}^{2},\dots,X_{n}^{m})^{\top}\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{m}
and
m^{\prime}
-dimensional random vector
Y_{n}=(Y_{n}^{1},Y_{n}^{2},\dots,Y_{n}^{m^{\prime}})^{\top}\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\})^{m^{\prime}}
, where each component
X_{n}^{i}
and
Y_{n}^{j}
denote the number of balls of color
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,m^{\prime}\}
at time
n
, respectively. Let
X_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
Y_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, that is there are no balls in the two urns at time zero. Let
1\leq i_{0}\leq m
and
1\leq j_{0}\leq m^{\prime}
be as defined in Section
3
. At time
n=1
, we restrict ourselves to the initial deterministic configuration
X_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{i_{0}}
and
Y_{1}=\mathbf{e}^{{}^{\prime}}_{j_{0}}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{k}
and
\mathbf{e}^{{}^{\prime}}_{k}
denote the
k
-th standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}
, respectively. For each urn process at any time
n\geq 2
, we draw one ball uniformly at random from the urn, observe its color, return it to the same urn, and then, with probability
p
, add one additional ball of the same color to the same urn. With the remaining probability
1-p
, we instead add one ball among the
m-1
remaining colors to the same urn, each with probability
(1-p)/(m-1)
. We then update
X_{n}=(X_{n}^{1},X_{n}^{2},\dots,X_{n}^{m})^{\top}
accordingly, so that
X_{n}
describes the composition of the urn after the
(n-1)
-th drawing. The process
Y_{n}
is defined similarly to
X_{n}
, and the two processes are assumed to be independent.

The connection with the ERW model is the following: If
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is Type-II ERW, then we have

\displaystyle\{S_{2n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{n}^{i}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}Y_{n}^{j}w_{j}\}_{n=0}^{\infty},

(4.1)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. This means that the extent to which the ERW moves in the directions
u_{i}
and
w_{j}
at time
n
is governed by the numbers of balls
X_{n}^{i}
and
Y_{n}^{j}
, respectively.

In the case
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
, the connection with Type-I ERW is given by

\displaystyle\{S_{2n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{2n}^{i}u_{i}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}.

(4.2)

Remark 4.1
.

We note that in general, even when
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
, (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) cannot be combined, as sampling from the entire urn process
X^{i}_{2n}
(whole past) and sampling only from one of the two urn processes
X^{i}_{n}
and
Y^{j}_{n}
(odd or even times) produce distinct stochastic processes, see also Remark
3.1
.

As demonstrated in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), to understand the long-time behavior of the ERW, it suffices to study the asymptotic behavior of the corresponding urn process. The key quantities governing the asymptotics of the urn process are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the so-called
replacement matrix
. In our case, the replacement matrices associated with the processes
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are given by
the
m\times m
matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}p&\frac{1-p}{m-1}&\cdots&\frac{1-p}{m-1}\\
\frac{1-p}{m-1}&p&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1-p}{m-1}&\cdots&\cdots&p\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1-p}{m-1}J_{m}+\frac{mp-1}{m-1}I_{m},

(4.3)

and the
m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}
matrix

A^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}p&\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}&\cdots&\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}\\
\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}&p&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}&\cdots&\cdots&p\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1-p}{m^{\prime}-1}J_{m^{\prime}}+\frac{m^{\prime}p-1}{m^{\prime}-1}I_{m^{\prime}},

(4.4)

where
J_{m}
denotes
m\times m
all-ones matrix and
I_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix. The matrix
A
has the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=1
of multiplicity one and
\lambda_{2}=(mp-1)/(m-1)
of multiplicity
m-1
. The right eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is
v_{1}=\frac{1}{m}(1,\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The same holds for
J_{m^{\prime}},I_{m^{\prime}},\lambda_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},\lambda_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}
and
v_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}
substituting
m
with
m^{\prime}
.

It is well-known (cf.
[
1
,
10
,
17
,
18
]
) that the asymptotic behavior of the urn process depends on the position of
\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}
relative to
1/2
. In our settings, for each process, the critical values associated with the memory parameter
p
are

p_{c}^{m}=\frac{m+1}{2m}

(4.5)

by solving
\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}=1/2
, and

p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}=\frac{m^{\prime}+1}{2m^{\prime}}

(4.6)

by solving
\lambda_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}/\lambda_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}=1/2
.
We observe that when
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\dots,\pm e_{d}\}
, i.e.,
m=2d
, then
p_{c}^{m}=(2d+1)/(4d)
, which coincides with the critical value derived by Bercu and Laulin
[
3
]
and Bertenghi
[
7
]
. Moreover, for
d=1
, we recover
p_{c}^{m}=3/4
, corresponding to the phase transition originally observed by Schütz and Trimper
[
23
]
.

As shown in (
4.1
), when
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathcal{W}
, we need to consider two Pólya urn processes within a single ERW. In Section
5
, we derive asymptotic results for ERW by distinguishing cases according to the relative magnitudes of
p_{c}^{m}
and
p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
.

In addition, defining
a=(mp-1)/(m-1)
, we note that the following equivalence is obtained:

a<\frac{1}{2}\Leftrightarrow p<p_{c}^{m},\quad a=\frac{1}{2}\Leftrightarrow p=p_{c}^{m},\quad a>\frac{1}{2}\Leftrightarrow p>p_{c}^{m}.

(4.7)

This relation also holds for
p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
and
a^{\prime}
, by taking
a^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}p-1)/(m^{\prime}-1)
.

Remark 4.2
.

Obviously,
we can generalize the case where
\Gamma
is generated by the sets of vectors
\{u_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{m_{1}},\dots,\{u_{i,l}\}_{i=1}^{m_{l}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
l\geq 3
. The ERW
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is defined on the multipartite graph
\Gamma
partitioned into
l
vertex classes,
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{l}
. The walk is enforced to move cyclically between vertex classes: A step taken from
Z_{i}
must lead to a vertex in
Z_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,l-1
, and a step taken from
Z_{l}
must lead back to a vertex in
Z_{1}
. Under this procedure, we can establish the same class of results for the ERW as those presented in Section
5
by employing similar analytical techniques. However, considering the
kagome lattice
, see Fig.
2
, corresponding to
l=3
, the walk on such a multipartite graph moves randomly in general, and unlike the above cyclical setting or the strictly bipartite case
l=2
considered in this paper, the number of visits to each vertex class is not deterministically balanced and a different type of analysis is required.

5
Main result

In this section, we derive a strong law of large numbers and functional limit theorems for the ERW on
\Gamma
in all regimes. For our purpose, it is most convenient to adapt the general results of Janson
[
17
]
and to translate them into the setting of the ERW by using the connection established in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) together with the continuous mapping theorem.

It should be noted that the results presented below extend those results found in Bertenghi
[
7
]
. We also show, in relation to Remark
3.1
and Remark
4.1
, that the limiting distributions of Type-II ERW under
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
and Type-I ERW coincide, except for the superdiffusive regime.

5.1
A LLN-type convergence result

Our first result concerns a strong law of large numbers. The following property holds regardless of the regime, in other words, we eliminate the dependency on the numbers
m
and
m^{\prime}
of edges emanating from each vertex.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be Type-I or Type-II ERW. Then, for all
p\in(0,1)
, we have the almost sure convergence

\frac{S_{n}}{n}\to\frac{1}{2}(\bar{u}+\bar{w})\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

where
\bar{u}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}
and
\bar{w}=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}w_{j}
.

Proof.

First, we consider Type-II ERW. By Theorem
3.21
in Janson
[
17
]
, we have the almost sure convergence

\frac{X_{n}}{n}\to\lambda_{1}v_{1}=\frac{1}{m}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}

and

\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\to\lambda_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}v_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}
are the largest eigenvalue of the replacement matrix
A
and
A^{\prime}
as given in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), and
v_{1}
and
v_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}
are its corresponding (right) eigenvector, respectively. Hence, the claim follows by (
4.1
) and the continuous mapping theorem.

Similarly, for Type-I ERW, we obtain the almost sure convergence
S_{n}/n\to\bar{u}
as
n\to\infty
from (
4.2
).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

If
p=1
, which is not covered by the law of large numbers of Theorem
5.1
, the
ERW is trivial since by definition of the process one then has
\sigma_{n}=\sigma_{1}
and
\tau_{n}=\tau_{1}
for all
n\geq 1
. Hence,

S_{2n}/n=u_{i_{0}}+w_{j_{0}}
and
S_{n}/n=u_{i_{0}}
hold true for Type-II and Type-I ERW, respectively.

5.2
Functional-type convergence results

Next, we discuss the functional-type limit theorems. Their convergence results concern the distributional convergence of the ERW on
\Gamma
in the Skorokhod space
D([0,\infty))

of right-continuous functions with left-hand limits. In what follows, we assume without loss of generality that
p_{c}^{m}\leq p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
, as the opposite case can be treated similarly. We classify the possible situations into three cases and we derive the corresponding asymptotic results: (I)
0<p<p_{c}^{m}\leq p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
(Theorem
5.3
), (II)
0<p_{c}^{m}=p=p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
(Theorem
5.5
),
(III)
0<p_{c}^{m}=p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}<p
(Theorem
5.8
).
The remaining parameter regimes

0<p_{c}^{m}=p<p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
,

0<p_{c}^{m}<p\leq p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
,
and

0<p_{c}^{m}<p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}<p

are discussed in

and

.

First, we provide the functional central limit theorem in the diffusive regime.

Theorem 5.3
.

(
diffusive regime
) Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be Type-I or Type-II ERW and
0<p<p_{c}^{m}\leq p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
. Then, we have the distributional convergence in
D[0,\infty)
,

\left\{\frac{S_{\lfloor 2nt\rfloor}-nt(\bar{u}+\bar{w})}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}\Rightarrow\left\{W_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

where
\left\{W_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Gaussian process with
W_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, and for
0<s\leq t
, the covariance structure is specified by

\mathbb{E}W_{s}W_{t}^{\top}=C_{a}s\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{a}\Sigma(\mathcal{U})+C_{a^{\prime}}s\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{a^{\prime}}\Sigma(\mathcal{W}),

where
a
is given in (
4.7
),
C_{a}=1/(1-2a)
,

\Sigma(\mathcal{U})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(u_{i}-\bar{u}\right)\left(u_{i}-\bar{u}\right)^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma(\mathcal{W})=\frac{1}{m^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}\left(w_{j}-\bar{w}\right)\left(w_{j}-\bar{w}\right)^{\top}.

Remark 5.4
.

In Bertenghi case i.e.,
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}=\{\pm e_{1},\pm e_{2},\dots,\pm e_{d}\}
, we have
\bar{u}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\Sigma(\mathcal{U})
deduces to
I_{d}/d
where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Therefore, the covariance coincides with the form given by Theorem
4.2
in
[
7
]
. Furthermore, in this case, the coordinates of the limiting process
\{W_{t}=(W^{1}_{t},\dots,W^{d}_{t})^{\top}\}_{t\geq 0}
can be expressed as independent
noise reinforced Brownian motion
(cf.
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
).

In general,
\Sigma(\mathcal{U})
and
\Sigma(\mathcal{W})
are typically non-diagonal, indicating that the diffusion along different coordinate axes is correlated. In this case,
W_{t}
can be represented by the following Wiener integral:

W_{t}=\Sigma(\mathcal{U})^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{t}{v}\right)^{a}dB_{v}+\Sigma(\mathcal{W})^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{t}{v}\right)^{a^{\prime}}dB^{{}^{\prime}}_{v},

where
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{B^{{}^{\prime}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are independent standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motions. Moreover, as shown by Lamperti
[
20
]
, the limiting process
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
appearing in such a functional limit theorem is known to be a
self-similar process
.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

First, we consider Type-II ERW. We apply to Theorem
3.31
(i) in Janson
[
17
]
, which establishes
that
\{n^{-1/2}(X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-nt\lambda_{1}v_{1})\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{n^{-1/2}(Y_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-nt\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}v^{{}^{\prime}}_{1})\}_{t\geq 0}
converge in distribution in
D([0,\infty))
towards a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued Gaussian processes
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
-valued Gaussian processes
\{V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{t\geq 0}
with
V_{0}=V_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
, respectively. We note that
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{t\geq 0}
are independent, and the covariance structure of
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is closer
specified under Remark
5.7
Display
(5.6)
in Janson
[
17
]
. By technical calculation for its covariance (see, Appendix A in Bertenghi
[
7
]
for details), we obtain for
0<s\leq t
,

\mathbb{E}[V_{s}V_{t}^{\top}]=\frac{c_{a,s,t}}{m}\left(I_{m}-\frac{J_{m}}{m}\right)

(5.1)

and

\mathbb{E}[V_{s}^{{}^{\prime}}(V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\top}]=\frac{c_{a^{\prime},s,t}}{m^{\prime}}\left(I_{m^{\prime}}-\frac{J_{m^{{}^{\prime}}}}{m^{\prime}}\right),

(5.2)

where

c_{a,s,t}=C_{a}s\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{a}.

(5.3)

By (
4.1
), we deduce that
\{n^{-1/2}(S_{\lfloor 2nt\rfloor}-(nt\bar{u}+nt\bar{w})\}_{t\geq 0}
converges in distribution in
D([0,\infty))
towards a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued process, denoted by

\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and specified by

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}V^{i}_{t}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}(V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}})^{j}w_{j}

(5.4)

almost surely, where
V^{i}_{t}
and
(V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}})^{j}
denote the
i
-th component of
V_{t}
for
i=1,\dots,m
and the
j
-th component of
V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}}
for
j=1,\dots,m^{\prime}
, respectively. As
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a linear combination of the components of
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{t\geq 0}
, it follows that
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Gaussian process started from zero, and thus the law of
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is completely determined by its covariance structure.

Since we have

\mathbb{E}[V_{s}^{i}V_{t}^{j}]=\frac{c_{a,s,t}}{m}\left(\delta_{i,j}-\frac{1}{m}\right),

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta function, it follows from the independence of
\{V_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}}\}_{t\geq 0}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{s}W_{t}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[V_{s}^{i}V_{t}^{k}]u_{i}u_{k}^{\top}+\sum_{j=1}^{m^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[(V_{s}^{{}^{\prime}})^{j}(V_{t}^{{}^{\prime}})^{l}]w_{j}w_{l}^{\top}

\displaystyle=c_{a,s,t}\Sigma(\mathcal{U})+c_{a^{\prime},s,t}\Sigma(\mathcal{W}).

Similarly, for Type-I ERW, it follows from (
4.2
) and the continuity and self-similarity of
V_{t}
that
\{n^{-1/2}(S_{\lfloor 2nt\rfloor}-2nt\bar{u})\}_{t\geq 0}
converges in distribution in
D([0,\infty))
towards a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Gaussian process
\{\tilde{W_{t}}\}_{t\geq 0}
, and the covariance structure is specified by
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{W}_{s}\tilde{W_{t}}^{\top}]=2c_{a,s,t}\Sigma(\mathcal{U})
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

\mathbb{R}^{d}

(5.5)

Remark 5.7
.

In the case
0<p_{c}^{m}=p<p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
, under the scaling by
n^{-t/2}(\log n)^{-1/2}
, we observe that the asymptotic behavior of
X
dominates that of
Y
. Hence, for Type-II ERW, we obtain a centered continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{E}W_{s}W_{t}^{\top}=s\Sigma(\mathcal{U}).

Finally, we consider the limit theorems related to the superdiffusive regime. In contrast to the previous two regimes, the Type-II ERW under
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
and the Type-I ERW exhibit different limiting distributions.

Theorem 5.8
.

(
superdiffusive regime
) Let
0<p_{c}^{m}=p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}<p
and
a
be given by (
4.7
). Then, the following almost sure functional convergence holds: For Type-II ERW
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, we have

\left\{\frac{S_{\lfloor 2nt\rfloor}-nt(\bar{u}+\bar{w})}{n^{a}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}\Rightarrow\left\{t^{a}L\right\}_{t\geq 0}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

where
L
is some
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable different from zero.

For Type-I ERW, we have

\left\{\frac{S_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-nt\bar{u}}{n^{a}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}\Rightarrow\left\{t^{a}\tilde{L}\right\}_{t\geq 0}\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

where
\tilde{L}
is some
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable different from zero.

Proof.

First, we consider Type-II ERW. We note that in the notation of Theorem
3.24
in Janson
[
17
]
, we have
\Lambda^{\prime}_{III}=\{\lambda_{2}=a\}
. We are
therefore in the setting of the last part of the cited theorem and get that
\{n^{-a}(X_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-nt\lambda_{1}v_{1})\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{n^{-a}(Y_{\lfloor nt\rfloor}-nt\lambda_{1}v_{1})\}_{t\geq 0}
converge almost surely to
\{t^{a}\hat{W}_{1}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{t^{a}\hat{W}_{2}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where
\hat{W}_{1}=(\hat{W}_{1}^{1},\dots,\hat{W}_{1}^{m})^{\top}
and
\hat{W}_{2}=(\hat{W}_{2}^{1},\dots,\hat{W}_{2}^{m})^{\top}
are some independent nonzero random vectors lying in the eigenspace
E_{\lambda_{2}}
of the replacement matrix
A
as displayed in (
4.3
).

Hence, by (
4.1
), we obtain the nonzero
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vector, which is constructed as the sum of independent random vectors,

L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat{W}_{1}^{i}u_{i}+\hat{W}_{2}^{i}w_{i})

(5.8)

almost surely.

Similarly, for Type-I ERW, we obtain the nonzero
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vector

\tilde{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{W}_{3}^{i}u_{i}

(5.9)

from (
4.2
) almost surely, where
\hat{W}_{3}=(\hat{W}_{3}^{1},\dots,\hat{W}_{3}^{m})^{\top}
is some nonzero random vector lying in the eigenspace
E_{\lambda_{2}}
of the replacement matrix
A
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

We refer to the case
0<p_{c}^{m}<p\leq p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}
and
0<p_{c}^{m}<p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}<p
. We first assume
0<p_{c}^{m}<p<p_{c}^{m^{\prime}}

X
which scales as

dominates the term associated with
Y
which scales as

that depends only on the process
X

Y
dominates the term associated with
X

4.7

L
that depends only on the process
Y
.
