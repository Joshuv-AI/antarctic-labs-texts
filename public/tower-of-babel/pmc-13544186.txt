Outcomes of adolescents and young adults with AML treated on pediatric vs adult protocols∗
Authors: Baher Krayem, Jeetayu Biswas, Andriy Derkach, Michael Rafizadeh, Jenna Ciervo, Christopher Famulare, Omar Abdel-Wahab, Neerav Shukla, Tanya Trippett, Christopher Forlenza, Kavitha Ramaswamy, Martin Tallman, Mark Geyer, Maria Luisa Sulis, Eytan M. Stein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•AYA patients treated with pediatric-style and adult regimens demonstrate similar complete remission rates and depth of response in AML.•Adult protocols show comparable or superior overall survival despite older age and unfavorable risk, challenging cytotoxic intensification.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy affecting individuals across all age groups.1 Adolescents and young adults (AYA), defined as patients aged 15 to 39 years by the National Cancer Institute, represent a distinct clinical population in whom AML accounts for ∼40% of leukemias.2,3 In population-based registries, 5-year overall survival (OS) among AYA patients in the United States has improved substantially, from 18.6% during 1975 to 1984, to 56.3% during 2003 to 2010. Nevertheless, AYA patients continue to experience worse outcomes than younger children (aged <15 years), and the biological and therapeutic factors underlying this disparity remain incompletely defined.4

This prognostic lag persists in the era of contemporary, risk-adapted therapy.5, 6, 7 The NOPHO-AML 2004 study demonstrated a trend toward inferior 5-year OS among patients aged 10 to 17 years compared with younger children (64% vs 76%).8 A landmark meta-analysis by Woods et al comparing 517 AYA patients treated on pediatric Children’s Oncology Group (COG) vs adult Cancer and Leukemia Group B and Southwest Oncology Group trials further demonstrated that even modest age differences influence prognosis, with older AYA patients experiencing worse event-free survival and OS driven largely by increased toxicity and treatment-related mortality.9 These findings establish AYA patients as a distinct, higher-risk population in whom age itself remains a critical prognostic variable.

Age-related outcome differences may be mediated, in part, by a distinct molecular landscape that evolves across even modest age intervals. Patients aged 16 to 21 years more frequently harbor FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3–internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD), nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1), or CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha, basic leucine zipper domain mutations and a normal karyotype, whereas lysine methyltransferase 2A (KMT2A) or nucleoporin 98 (NUP98) rearrangements and core-binding factor fusions predominate in younger children.10, 11, 12, 13 Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 mutations, rare in childhood AML, begin to emerge in adolescence, frequently co-occurring with NPM1 mutations.14 This shifting mutational landscape provides a biological rationale for considering age-adapted therapeutic approaches.

Standard treatment diverges substantially between adult and pediatric services. In adult practice, induction consists of 1 cycle of cytarabine combined with daunorubicin (7+3), followed by risk-adapted consolidation and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) in first complete remission (CR1) for patients at intermediate and adverse risk per European LeukemiaNet 2022 (ELN 2022) guidelines.15 Pediatric regimens instead use 2 cycles of cytarabine, daunorubicin, and etoposide (ADE)-based induction, reflecting a strategy of upfront cytotoxic intensification.16,17 Consolidation is guided by minimal residual disease (MRD) assessment and cytogenetic risk, with HSCT in CR1 reserved for MRD-positive patients and those at high risk; patients not at high risk proceed to additional intensive chemotherapy cycles. High-risk designation in pediatric AML is determined according to the International Expert Panel consensus criteria, which define unfavorable disease by the presence of FLT3-ITD at high allelic ratio, monosomy 7, monosomy 5/del5q, MECOM rearrangements [MDS1 and EVI1 complex locus; inv(3)/t(3;3)], DEK::NUP214 fusion, or MRD positivity after induction. These criteria were recently updated to incorporate newly identified high-risk entities, including NUP98 rearrangements, CBFA2T3::GLIS2, ETV6 fusions, and additional high-risk KMT2A fusion partners, reflecting advances in molecular characterization of pediatric AML over the past decade.18,19 These divergent frameworks create a clinically meaningful management gap precisely within the AYA age range.

Pediatric-style intensification, however, appears to carry an incremental toxicity burden in older adolescents. In COG AAML0531, treatment-related mortality was concentrated among patients aged ≥11 years, with deaths attributable primarily to infectious complications during intensification.20 Patients aged 10 to 17 years experienced significantly higher rates of grade 3/4 septic shock and greater cumulative morbidity than younger children,8,21 suggesting that adolescent physiology may limit tolerance to the degree of cytotoxic intensification well-tolerated in younger patients.8,21

The optimal therapeutic framework for AYA AML, therefore, remains unsettled. Comparative studies have yielded conflicting evidence, and whether pediatric-style intensification confers a meaningful survival advantage over adult regimens in this population has not been established.22,23

In this single-center retrospective study, we hypothesize that AYA patients treated on adult protocols achieve comparable remission depth, MRD clearance, and survival outcomes relative to those treated on pediatric regimens, without the incremental toxicity associated with intensified induction. For the purpose of this study, we define AYA as patients aged 14 to 29 years, reflecting the institutional service boundary at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) rather than a population-based epidemiologic definition.

Methods

Patient selection

A retrospective single-center analysis was performed using all sequentially diagnosed patients with newly diagnosed AML aged 14 to 29 years, who received treatment from February 2010 to August 2025 at MSKCC, New York, NY. Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the treating service and corresponding chemotherapy protocols (pediatrics [Peds] or adults [Adults]). At MSKCC, newly diagnosed patients aged ≤21 years are treated by the pediatric service and those aged ≥22 years are treated by the adult service. Exceptionally, patients aged >21 years with therapy-related AML arising in the context of previous treatment for a pediatric solid tumor or lymphoma have been managed by the pediatric service at the discretion of the treating team. Patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia were excluded.

Data collection and analysis

Patients diagnosed with AML who underwent induction chemotherapy at MSKCC were identified and stratified by age at diagnosis. Electronic health records were reviewed to extract baseline demographics, clinical and laboratory parameters, treatment regimens, and longitudinal outcome data, including MRD assessments.

Disease risk was stratified using 2 distinct frameworks to allow for age-appropriate comparison: the 2022 ELN recommendations for adults, and the International Pediatric AML Expert Panel consensus criteria for pediatric patients.18 Comprehensive cytogenetic and molecular profiling was performed as clinically indicated, with results integrated into the respective risk classification schemata to define favorable, intermediate, and adverse risk groups.

Cytogenetic analysis

Cytogenetic data were retrieved from clinical reports generated by the MSKCC Clinical Cytogenetics Laboratory. Analysis comprised conventional G-banding karyotyping performed on bone marrow aspirate samples and fluorescence in situ hybridization targeting recurrent AML-associated abnormalities, including RUNX1::RUNX1T1, CBFB::MYH11, KMT2A, and 3q26 (MECOM/EVI1) rearrangements, as well as deletions involving chromosomes 5, 7, and 17.

Molecular profiling

Molecular profiling methodologies evolved throughout the study period: single-gene assays for FLT3-ITD and NPM1 were used from 2010 to 2013; clinical next-generation sequencing via RainDance Technologies was used between 2013 and 2016; and the MSK-IMPACT (Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets) heme panel has served as the primary molecular diagnostic tool since 2016. Detected variants were interpreted in accordance with current clinical guidelines and integrated with cytogenetic findings to establish final risk stratification.

Treatment protocols

Pediatric patients were treated according to COG protocols, including AAML0531, AAML1031, AAML1421, and AAML1831.20,24, 25, 26 Most patients underwent 2 cycles of induction chemotherapy (double induction). Standard induction consisted of the ADE regimen: cytarabine (100 mg/m2 every 12 hours for 10 days), daunorubicin (50 mg/m2 daily for 3 days), and etoposide (100 mg/m2 daily for 5 days). Depending on the specific protocol and molecular profile, this backbone was supplemented with gemtuzumab ozogamicin, bortezomib, or FLT3 inhibitors (midostaurin, gilteritinib). Postremission management involved risk-adapted consolidation with high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC)-based chemotherapy or HSCT, guided by cytomolecular risk and MRD status assessed after induction therapy.

Adult patients were treated with standard induction chemotherapy consisting of the 7+3 regimen (cytarabine 100-200 mg/m2 continuous infusion for 7 days, and daunorubicin 60-90 mg/m2 daily for 3 days). Patients achieving only partial remission upon bone marrow evaluation at day 14 underwent reinduction. Consolidation HiDAC or HSCT was determined by ELN risk stratification. Patients with induction failure proceeded to salvage chemotherapy protocols.

The requirement for informed consent was waived, given the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Response assessment

Response to therapy was assessed by bone marrow examination after induction, typically after 2 cycles of intensive chemotherapy, with remission defined according to ELN 2022 criteria. MRD was evaluated primarily by multiparametric flow cytometry. Flow cytometric strategies differed by service: pediatric-service patients were assessed using a centralized difference-from-normal approach for all subtypes, whereas adult-service patients were assessed internally using a combined leukemia-associated immunophenotype and difference-from-normal approach. The sensitivity of the adult assay evolved during the study period, transitioning from a 3-tube panel with a sensitivity of 0.1% (2013-2021) to a single 28-color tube with a sensitivity of 0.01% (2021 onward). In addition, for adult patients with molecularly trackable markers such as NPM1 mutations or core-binding factor (CBF)-AML fusions, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction was used for MRD monitoring in accordance with ELN guidelines; patients without evaluable data at the time of response assessment were categorized as “MRD not available.”

Transplantation and follow-up

HSCT was offered according to institutional guidelines. For adult patients, HSCT was generally recommended for those with intermediate- or adverse-risk disease, MRD positivity after induction, or other high-risk clinical features. In the pediatric service, transplantation was reserved for patients classified as high risk based on cytogenetic and molecular findings or MRD positivity after induction.

Relapse and survival data were obtained through chart review and verified against institutional registries. Follow-up was measured from the date of diagnosis to the date of last contact or death.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons between Peds and Adults treatment groups were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of CR to relapse, death, or last follow-up. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons between groups were made using the log-rank test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess associations between clinical and molecular variables and OS. Variables with P values of < .10 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate Cox regression models to identify independent prognostic factors. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for all Cox regression analyses.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P values of < .05 were considered statistically significant.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the MSKCC Institutional Review Board (protocol number 18-258). The requirement for informed consent was waived given the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Treatment regimen

Detailed treatment specifications by protocol and service are provided in Table 1. Among Adults (n = 44), 43 patients (98%) received 7+3–based induction. Of these, 30 (68%) were treated with standard 7+3 alone, whereas 13 (30%) received 7+3 combined with targeted agents, including midostaurin (n = 4), crenolanib (n = 2), quizartinib (n = 1), and ivosidenib (n = 2). Gemtuzumab ozogamicin was added in 4 patients (9%), and a single patient received MEC (mitoxantrone, etoposide, cytarabine) as initial induction.

In the Peds cohort (n = 37), induction therapy was largely protocol-driven within COG trials (AAML0531, AAML1031, AAML1831). ADE-based chemotherapy (cytarabine, daunorubicin, etoposide) was the predominant regimen, administered to 24 patients (65%). Standard 7+3 was used in 2 patients (5%), 10+3 regimens with extended cytarabine exposure in 3 (8%), high-dose cytarabine–based induction in 4 (11%), CPX-351 in 3 (8%), and hypomethylating agent plus venetoclax in 1 patient (3%). Targeted and antibody-based therapies were commonly incorporated: FLT3 inhibitors were given to 10 patients (27%; midostaurin in 8, and gilteritinib in 2) and gemtuzumab ozogamicin to 7 patients (19%).

Double induction was administered to 35 of 37 pediatric patients (95%), compared with only 3 of 44 Adults (7%), underscoring the contrast between multicycle, protocol-based pediatric strategies and the predominantly single-cycle 7+3 approach used in adult practice. After initial 7+3 induction in the Adults cohort, postinduction management varied based on response. HiDAC-based chemotherapy was administered to 33 patients (75%) overall, 27 (61%) as consolidation after adequate response, and 6 (14%) as salvage therapy for insufficient response. Two additional patients (4%) received FLAG-IDA (fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte-colony stimulating factor, and idarubicin) as salvage, and 1 (2%) received azacitidine monotherapy. Five patients (11%) proceeded directly to allogeneic HSCT after a single induction cycle without further intervening chemotherapy.

Response and MRD outcomes

After 2 cycles of intensive chemotherapy, composite CR (CRc; CR + CR with incomplete hematologic recovery [CRi]) rates were high and comparable between the Adults and Peds cohorts (79% vs 78%; P = .9; Table 2). However, the response pattern differed between groups: patients in the Adults cohort more frequently achieved CR (70% vs 40%), whereas those in the Peds cohort more often attained CRi (38% vs 9%). Transplantation

Among the 81 evaluable patients, 54 (67%) underwent HSCT. In the Peds cohort (n = 37), 22 patients (59%) proceeded to HSCT, including 18 (49%) who received HSCT in CR1. In the Adults cohort (n = 44), 32 patients (73%) received HSCT, with 24 (55%) in CR1. Thus, nearly 2-thirds of AYA patients overall underwent allogeneic transplantation, with no statistically significant differences between Peds and Adults cohorts in either overall transplant use or CR1 transplant rates (Table 1). Among the 54 patients who underwent allogeneic SCT, adult-protocol patients demonstrated a 5-year OS of 78.6% (95% CI, 64.7-95.6) with a median OS not reached, compared with a 5-year OS of 43.6% (95% CI, 24.4-77.7) and a median OS of 59.9 months (95% CI, 50 to not reached) in the pediatric cohort (P = .13). Among the 42 patients who received HSCT in CR1, Adults reported a 5-year OS of 80.6% (95% CI, 65-99.8), with median OS not reached, vs a 5-year OS of 40.4.1% (95% CI, 19.6-83.3) and a median OS of 59.9 months in pediatric patients. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .13), likely reflecting the modest sample size of the transplanted subgroup, the trend toward superior posttransplant survival in adult-protocol patients is consistent with the overall cohort findings and suggests that the observed survival advantage is not solely attributable to differences in transplant-use rates.

Among patients proceeding to HSCT in CR1, the median number of intensive chemotherapy cycles administered before transplant was 2 in the Adults cohort and 3 in the Peds cohort, reflecting the additional consolidation cycle routinely administered in pediatric protocols before transplantation.

Relapse and survival

Overall cohort

Despite the comparable early disease-control metrics detailed earlier, long-term survival outcomes diverged notably between the 2 treatment groups (Figure 2). Median OS was not reached in either group, but 5-year OS was markedly higher among the Adults cohort (77.9% vs 56.7%). Three-year RFS was comparable between groups (26% in the Adults cohort vs 28% in the Peds cohort), with median RFS not reached in either cohort. In univariate Cox regression analysis (supplemental Table 1), several factors were significantly associated with OS. Unfavorable risk classification by both ELN 2022 criteria (adverse risk: HR, 15.9; 95% CI, 2.04-124; P = .008; intermediate risk: HR, 8.71; 95% CI, 1.09-69.7; P = .041) and pediatric risk stratification (unfavorable risk: HR, 15.8; 95% CI, 2.00-125; P = .009; intermediate risk: HR, 9.50; 95% CI, 1.21-74.3; P = .032) were each significantly associated with inferior OS. Notably, treatment protocol (Adults vs Peds) was not significantly associated with survival in univariate analysis. In multivariate analysis adjusting for age and genomic risk (supplemental Table 2), ELN 2022 risk classification (adverse risk: HR, 18.5; 95% CI, 2.13-161; P = .002; intermediate risk: HR, 8.84; 95% CI, 1.06-74.0) retained independent prognostic significance, whereas treatment protocol remained nonsignificant (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.19-1.88; P = .4).

Intermediate-adverse risk subgroup

Twenty-six (59%) and 29 (78%) patients in the Adults and Peds cohorts, respectively, were considered to fall within intermediate or adverse risk subgroups by ELN 2022. The pattern of similar early response with divergent survival was maintained. CRc rates remained comparable between Adults and Peds cohorts (65% vs 69%). As in the overall cohort, adult-service patients more frequently achieved CR with full hematologic recovery (58% vs 34.5%), whereas those in the Peds cohort had a higher proportion achieving CRi (7% vs 34.5%). MRD negativity among responders was achieved in 71% of Adults cohort patients compared with 60% of Peds cohort patients (nonstatistically significant), and nonresponse rates were similar (35% vs 31%). The survival advantage observed in the overall cohort persisted in this higher-risk subgroup, with Adults cohort patient achieving a 5-year OS of 62.7%, with median survival not reached, compared with 47.9% 5-year survival and a median survival of 59.9 months in the Peds cohort (Table 2; Figure 2). These findings demonstrate that even among patients with intermediate or adverse molecular and cytogenetic features, adult-based treatment approaches using early HSCT for this higher-risk population yielded a trend toward superior long-term outcomes despite comparable depth of initial remission.

Discussion

The treatment of AYA patients with AML represents a unique clinical and biological challenge. Positioned at the interface between pediatric and adult hematology, this age group historically experiences inferior survival outcomes compared with younger children, despite similar treatment intent and access to supportive care. In this single-center retrospective cohort of 81 AYA patients with AML, treatment on pediatric vs adult services yielded remarkably similar early disease-control metrics but strikingly divergent long-term survival outcomes. This dissociation between depth of remission and OS challenges fundamental assumptions about the role of induction intensification in this population and provides important insights into optimal therapeutic strategies for AYA AML.

Across the entire cohort, CRc was achieved in 79% of patients, with MRD negativity documented in 63% and 55% of responders in Adults and Peds cohorts, respectively. Notably, within the intermediate and adverse-risk strata, in which equipoise between treatment paradigms would be most expected, CR/CRi rates (65% vs 69%), MRD clearance (71% vs 60%), and rates of allogeneic transplantation in CR1 (55% vs 49%) were virtually identical between Adults and Peds cohorts. Despite these comparable markers of initial disease control, OS consistently and substantially favored patients treated on adult protocols using early transplant and less consolidation chemotherapy. Five-year OS was 77.9% in Adults cohort patients compared with 56.7% in Peds cohort patients for the entire cohort, with this survival advantage persisting in the intermediate-adverse risk subgroup (62.7% vs 47.9%). Critically, this survival benefit emerged despite adult service–treated patients were significantly older (median age, 24 vs 19 years) and harbored similar biological risk features.

This profound dissociation between remission quality and long-term survival suggests that outcome in AYA AML is not primarily determined by the ability of frontline therapy to induce deep remission with more intensive therapies, but rather by the complex interplay of treatment-related toxicity, postremission management including HSCT, and salvage strategies across the entire treatment continuum. Pediatric service–treated patients were exposed to substantially higher cumulative anthracycline doses, a direct consequence of the multicycle induction strategy used in pediatric protocols rather than dose escalation per se, with 95% receiving double induction compared with 7% of Adults cohort patients. In Adults, a single cycle of 7+3 delivered daunorubicin or a daunorubicin equivalent dose of 60 to 90 mg/m2 × 3 days, ∼180 to 270 mg/m2 per patient. The ADE regimen administered to pediatric-service patients includes daunorubicin 50 mg/m2 per day × 3 days per cycle, and with double induction, the cumulative daunorubicin exposure becomes 300 mg/m2, ∼1.1 to 1.7 times the anthracycline burden of a single adult induction cycle. These data suggest that the intensified approach subjects AYA patients to greater cumulative cytotoxic exposure during induction (of both anthracycline and etoposide). Adult service–treated patients predominantly received a single cycle of 7+3–based induction (93%), followed by risk-adapted consolidation (as detailed in “Methods”), with more use of HSCT for patients with ELN 2022 intermediate- or adverse-risk disease. Among 26 Adults cohort patients meeting ELN 2022 intermediate- or adverse-risk criteria, 17 (65%) proceeded to allogeneic HSCT in CR1. A directly comparable analysis in the Peds cohort is limited by the use of a distinct risk classification schema; among 12 Peds cohort patients classified as having unfavorable-risk disease by pediatric criteria, 4 proceeded to HSCT in CR1.

Our findings align with, and extend, a growing body of evidence questioning the paradigm that escalating cytotoxic intensity improves outcomes in older AYAs. Most comparative data for this age group derives from pediatric cooperative trials enrolling patients up to age 25 to 29 years, whereas AYA patients remain markedly underrepresented in adult AML studies. Within pediatric trial series, progressive dose escalation and cycle proliferation have failed to translate into meaningful survival gains. A recent real-world analysis comparing daunorubicin-cytarabine with ADE as first induction in pediatric AML demonstrated virtually identical 2-year event-free survival and OS, even when restricted to contemporary cohorts receiving gemtuzumab ozogamicin, despite substantially greater treatment intensity in the ADE arm.27 In the current COG frontline trial AAML1831, etoposide was removed from induction therapy based on previous adult AML data and concerns regarding secondary malignancies. Comparative analyses with historical phase 3 COG trials AAML0531 and AAML1031 demonstrated that omission of etoposide did not adversely affect end-of-induction MRD negativity, relapse risk, disease-free survival, or OS across risk groups.28 Collectively, these data indicate that etoposide does not confer a clinically meaningful benefit when added to daunorubicin-cytarabine–based induction in pediatric AML. Similarly, an AML-BFM study of 119 pediatric patients with therapy-related AML reported superior outcomes when allogeneic transplantation was performed after 2 rather than 4 induction cycles, suggesting that earlier consolidation with transplant may be preferable to extended chemotherapy exposure in high-risk patients.29 These observations, combined with well-documented increases in treatment-related toxicity among adolescents compared with younger children, including significantly higher rates of grade 3/4 septic shock and long-term morbidity, collectively challenge the assumption that “more intensive is better” for the AYA population.8,21

From a therapeutic standpoint, these data suggest that AYA patients may benefit from precision-based, risk-adapted treatment strategies that prioritize molecular risk stratification, MRD-directed decision-making, judicious integration of targeted agents, and timely allogeneic transplantation, with careful consideration of cumulative chemotherapy exposure. Adult treatment frameworks, which emphasize flexibility in treatment intensity and systematically incorporate transplantation for intermediate and adverse-risk disease, achieved equivalent depth of remission with a trend toward superior survival in this cohort. Notably, the paradigm of treatment deescalation is also gaining traction in the adult AML literature, with lower-intensity venetoclax-based regimens having demonstrated activity even in younger, fit patients with specific high-risk disease features, further challenging the assumption that cytotoxic intensification is universally beneficial across all risk subgroups.30 These findings suggest that AYAs may represent a metabolically and physiologically distinct population whose tolerance for intensive chemotherapy differs from younger children, whereas their disease biology increasingly resembles that of adults. The observation that both pediatric and adult approaches achieved similar remission rates underscores the efficacy of both treatment philosophies, whereas differences in survival outcomes may reflect varying tolerability of cumulative treatment intensity in this specific age group.

Several important limitations of this study merit acknowledgment. Treatment allocation was not randomized and may have been influenced by referral patterns, institutional practices, clinician preferences, or psychosocial considerations that cannot be fully captured in retrospective analysis. The modest sample size, while substantial for an AYA AML series at a single center, limits the precision of subgroup analyses and the statistical power to detect modest effect sizes between treatment arms. Temporal changes in supportive care quality, transplantation techniques, conditioning regimens, and availability of targeted agents over the 15-year study period may confound direct comparisons between protocols. In addition, detailed toxicity data, including treatment-related mortality, specific organ toxicities, and quality of life outcomes, were not systematically captured in this retrospective analysis, limiting our ability to definitively attribute survival differences to treatment-related complications. The single-center design, although providing uniformity in diagnostic and treatment infrastructure, may limit generalizability to other institutions with different patient populations or practice patterns.

Nonetheless, several strengths of this study provide confidence in the robustness of our findings. The use of uniform diagnostic infrastructure, consistent application of both pediatric and ELN 2022 risk classification schemas, detailed multiparametric flow cytometry-based MRD assessment, and comprehensive long-term follow-up within a single tertiary care center provide a coherent and internally consistent view of how 2 fundamentally different therapeutic philosophies perform in contemporary AYA practice. The comparative analysis of a well-defined intermediate-adverse risk subgroup, in which clinical equipoise is most relevant, strengthens the generalizability of our conclusions beyond patients with favorable-risk disease who would be expected to do well regardless of treatment intensity.

Collectively, these findings fundamentally challenge the prevailing assumption that pediatric-intensity induction chemotherapy should be the default therapeutic strategy for AYA AML. Instead, our data support an emerging paradigm in which treatment intensity is thoughtfully calibrated to biological risk profile and MRD response dynamics rather than age or arbitrary protocol assignment. The results suggest that adult-based, risk-adapted treatment regimens can achieve at least equivalent initial disease control with at least comparable OS in this vulnerable population, providing a compelling rationale for the development of harmonized, AYA-focused treatment protocols that deliberately integrate the most effective elements of both pediatric and adult therapeutic approaches while avoiding the potential toxicity of excessive treatment intensification.

Future directions

Future study should deliberately integrate pediatric and adult cooperative groups to develop harmonized, AYA-specific AML protocols. Prospective, multicenter trials should prioritize MRD-directed, toxicity-adjusted strategies rather than testing fixed “pediatric” or “adult” templates against each other. Increasingly, dedicated AYA programs embedded within adult services recognize that the needs of patients in their teens and twenties differ substantially from those of both younger children and older adults; analogous joint models are less common on the pediatric side, in which care typically stops at 18 years. A more immediate and realistic starting point is the development of AYA-specific AML trials through collaboration between pediatric and adult cooperative groups. Such intergroup efforts have already demonstrated success in Hodgkin lymphoma and sarcoma, and represent a proven model that is notably lacking in AML and acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Prioritizing AYA representation in cooperative group trial design, rather than defaulting to either pediatric or adult protocol templates, offers the most actionable path toward harmonized, evidence-based treatment for this underserved population.
