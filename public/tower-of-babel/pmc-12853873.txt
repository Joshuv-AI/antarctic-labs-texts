Football cooperative: a pragmatic controlled trial of a community-based physical activity social intervention for men when replicated using a Social Return on Investment (SROI) framework protocol paper
Authors: Chief Ibrahim, Paula Carroll, Tom Egan, Michael Harrison, Aisling McGrath, Laura Finnegan, Noel Richardson, Peter Krustrup, Steve Daly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcross the western world, men tend to have lower life expectancy compared to women and have higher mortality rates for most of the leading causes of deaths at all ages. While biological factors such as genetics and hormones may seem to contribute to men’s heightened vulnerability to certain diseases and health conditions, these elements alone do not fully explain the significant disparities observed in health outcomes between men and women. To address this challenge, the Football Cooperative (FC) initiative provides social recreational football games for men aimed at reducing social isolation and improving their overall health and wellbeing. Since 2021, the FC initiative has been the subject of a feasibility study for scale up and an implementation science study to develop an implementation strategy to replicate across multiple sites.MethodsAdopting a pragmatically controlled design within a social return on investment (SROI) framework, this study proposes to assess the efficacy of the FC initiative when replicated from two to twelve sites to (a) determine the SROI of FC games when replicated (b) the biopsychosocial health impacts the FC games (c) the game play demands of the FC games and (d) the reach of FC games at scale. To comprehensively address the aims, a mixed methods approach will be employed whereby each step of the seven steps of the SROI framework will be treated as separate studies; steps 1, 2 and 3 will adopt a qualitative approach (interviews and focus group discussions) to develop Theories of Change (TOC) for each stakeholder group, while steps 4, 5 and 6 will adopt quantitative approaches (objective assessments and surveys over time). Step 7 will detail the processes involved in the calculation of the final SROI figure.ConclusionsThe findings from this study will inform fidelity of delivery and equitable benefit for participants when the FC initiative is scaled up nationally and internationally. The use of SROI strengthens the case for investment while providing insights into how Evidence Based Practices (EBPs) can be adapted and scaled in other settings.Trial registrationThis study has been registered with the ‘International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number’ registry [ISRCTN14888262] as of the 2nd of July 2025.

Introduction

Globally, concerns about men’s health have come under increased public health scrutiny; across the western world, men have a lower life expectancy than women and have higher death rates for most of the leading causes of death and at all ages [1]. While men may be more vulnerable to certain diseases and illnesses than females [2] their susceptibility due to genetic or hormonal factors does not account for all of the differences in health outcomes observed [3]. The fact that men display fewer positive health behaviours and more risk-taking behaviours than their female counterparts has been well established [3]. Modifiable health behaviours such as diet, exercise, substance use, use of social support and safety practices have been identified as the most important contributors to health [4]. Therefore, men’s health outcomes would be significantly improved by reducing health risk behaviours and improving health promoting behaviours [5].

In Ireland, there is a marked decline in moderate/vigorous physical activity (PA) levels among men aged 15–24 to 65–74 years (70%−39%) whereby men aged 35 to 44 (78%) showed the most interest in becoming more active. Also, overweight (25%−48%) and obesity (9%−25%) levels tend to increase sharply with the drop off in PA among men from 15 to 24 to 65–74 years [6]. Male obesity in Ireland has tripled from 1990 to 2024 (7.8% to 21.7%) [7] with just 36% of men in Ireland currently of ‘normal’ weight [6].

Another key concern impacting the lives of men in Ireland is loneliness, which is a risk factor for morbidity and mortality of many of the leading causes of death [8, 9]. There is a strong relationship between social isolation and loneliness with all-cause mortality in men, noting that the relative risk of premature mortality is particularly higher in younger adults (aged 25–49 years) [10]. Compounding their experience are gender norms or ‘masculine beliefs’ that are associated with men’s reluctance to disclose what is socially stigmatised and/or influences their health risk appraisal when they need help or become ill [11]. Men are also known to delay help-seeking for mental and emotional health issues [12]. Further evidence suggests that men struggle to acknowledge their symptoms or illness to others and even to themselves, and avoid seeking help because of stigma [11, 13] and to conform to a socially prescribed male role of being strong [14, 15]. Consequently, both the obesity epidemic and the emerging ‘epidemic’ of loneliness [16–18] are now on international public health agendas and warrant urgent attention [19, 20].

This excess burden of ill-health has underpinned the emergence of a dialogue around men’s health across academic, policy and media contexts [21–23] in recent years. Ireland was the first country in the world to publish a National Men’s Health Policy [24] which was followed by the first report on the state of men’s health in Europe [25] and the World Health Organisations (WHO) European men’s health strategy [1]. It is reported that ‘men’s health disadvantage is an issue of inequity and not biological inevitability’ [25]. The report further explains that there are few initiatives tailored to address men’s needs either in formats appealing to men or situated in locations that men are more likely to access. Notably, Ireland’s men’s health policies call for the development of gender sensitive health promotion initiatives for men [26] and stress the importance of PA as a hook in the development of these initiatives whilst the WHO men’s health strategy identifies recreational and sports settings as part of ‘gender-specific’ health promotion approaches to engaging men [1].

Why recreational football?

Recreational football serves as an effective, broad-spectrum non-pharmaceutical intervention for managing lifestyle-related conditions like hypertension and metabolic syndrome [27–29]. Diverse movements and activities involved in recreational football (high-speed runs, sprints, turns, jumps and tackles) combined with moderate to high intensity levels of engagement in multiple aspects of physical fitness including metabolic, cardiovascular and musculoskeletal fitness stimulate adaptive responses across several physiological systems in the body [30]. Recreational football has positive impacts on cardiorespiratory fitness, blood pressure, bone density, blood sugar regulation, resting heart rate, postural control and body fat mass [29, 31–33]. Recreational football holds the potential for enhancing aerobic fitness and for both preventing and treating Non Communicable Diseases (NCDs) as it can tackle low motivation which is a major factor contributing to physical inactivity [29, 34–36].

Football-based interventions have been implemented across diverse settings and target groups, for instance, to improve well-being and reduce social isolation for young people with severe mental illness [37] as a health education initiative for children [38, 39] and to promote weight loss and healthy lifestyle habits among men [40]. Football is considered a valuable tool for reaching men who are often hesitant or unwilling to use conventional health services [41]. Football is thought to provide a safe space for emotional expression, enabling men to share feelings that are often socially frowned upon such as sentimentality, fear, pain, hurt, doubt and a desire for care and support [42]. Men ‘do’ emotion through actions such as engaging in football [43]. This emotional connection that many men share with football seems to make them feel more comfortable when engaging in football-based health initiatives. The emphasis on involvement, encouragement and building relationships within football-based initiatives can serve as an effective model for health promotion efforts [44].

Football, with an estimated 4 billion global fans, stands as the world’s most popular sport. However, it takes on unique local forms and holds diverse roles and meanings in different contexts [45]. It is evident that football and football settings play a significant role in improving men’s health [46, 47] a factor that inspired the initiation of the Football Cooperative (FC) initiative in Ireland.

Football cooperative

The FC initiative is a social enterprise (SE) established in 2017 for men, by men and uses recreational football as the vehicle to bring men together to improve their overall health and achieve social gains for local communities. FC games are currently at two sites in Ireland and are (a) accessible to participants (b) flexible regarding attendance (played 2–3 nights/week) (c) affordable to ensure inclusion for all (€5 per game) and (d) offered to all levels of fitness and football capacity. Recreational football differs from Association Football in terms of the pitch used and the number of players participating in a game. Games are played on an AstroTurf pitch with floodlights (to ensure year-round activity) for 60–90 min depending on pitch availability. Games create a team environment (5–11 a-side depending upon attendance). Team selection is crucial to ensuring both the game and social experience is maximised. The Game Coordinator will tactfully select the teams for the game with the objective to find a balance while ensuring the composition of teams is unique from one game to another. The values upon which the SE is driven are cultivation of community, inclusivity, sportsmanship and life-long learning.

Evidence based planning for replication

In order to address a public health issue at a population level, the ‘knowledge to practice’ gap must be traversed. A conceptual framework on how to traverse this trajectory (see Fig. 1) involves identifying the problem (Stage 1), developing potential solutions (Stage 2), testing solutions in a controlled or ‘real world’ setting (Stage 3), replicating effective solutions across multiples sites (Stage 4) and thereafter disseminating effective solutions for wider use at scale (Stage 5) [48]. However, it is notoriously difficult to achieve this in reality; less than half of evidence-based practices (EPBs) traverse this trajectory [49] and those that do, take an average of 17 years to do so [49–51].

Notably, the Scottish Football Fans in Training (FFIT) weight loss programme for men was effective in both reducing weight and improving health outcomes for men, and in scaling up from 11 to over 40 professional football clubs across the UK by 2019 with 30 more joining from 2020. Furthermore, the program has also effectively ‘scaled out’ into other jurisdictions (Europe - EuroFITT) and to other sports such as football in Australia (AussieFITT), rugby in England (Move like a Pro) and New Zealand (RU-FIT NZ) and ice-hockey in Canada (Hockey-FIT)) [47].

Similar to the FFIT trajectory the FC initiative has a vision to scale FC games nationally (2 sites to 60 sites by December 2029) and internationally (3 European countries in 2028). To achieve this, the FC initiative partnered with academia to build an evidence base to underpin its evolution to scale and the ‘FC Project’ was conceived. In June 2021, an advisory board (AB) to the overall FC project was established whose role continues to be to inform the strategic direction of the FC project. It meets biannually, and at the time of writing, it has met on 9 occasions (next meeting December 2025). Since its establishment, the AB has evolved to include 46 individuals across 32 organisations (at the time of writing) that represent government departments, national and international football organisations, sporting organisations, national and regional health organisations, a social enterprise organisation, an all-island men’s health organisation and a volunteer organisation. The research team, representing academics from Ireland and Denmark across Departments of Sports and Exercise and Faculties of Health Sciences and Business also sit on the AB.

The investigation of the effectiveness of the FC initiative at a single site via Social Return on Investment (SROI) [52], and health impact frameworks (Study 1) is complete [53] along with an assessment of game play demands across both sites (Study 3) [54]. The translational formative evaluation with a view to developing an implementation strategy to replicate the FC initiative (Study 2) will be finalised in December 2025.

The key prerequisite for scaling up a population health intervention is that it is effective, reaches those who need it, is adopted by those stakeholders who are in a position to support it, aligns with strategic context and is acceptable and feasible to the community [55]. From Study 1, it is evident that the FC initiative is effective; for every €1 invested a social return of €17.60 is achieved [52, 56] and improves health outcomes up to 12 M post-baseline [53]. The improvement in health outcomes observed may be due to the game play demands experienced; during FC games, men run on average 6.8Km and spend 50% of their time with a heart rate (HR) of ≥ 80% of HRmax [54]. Furthermore, the FC initiative appeals to men with multiple modifiable cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors therefore reaches those men who are in need of intervention [57]. The replication of the FC initiative is also supported by the Football Association of Ireland (FAI) and the Local Sports Partnership (LSP) network1, a factor that will ensure is sustainable delivery. The FC initiative also aligns with the National Men’s Health Action Plan [26] that calls for a plan for scaling up EBPs for men’s health initiatives (Task 1.3), building capacity to engage men via a gender specific approach (Theme 2) and ensuring that research continues to underpin the development of men’s health practice (Theme 3). Consequently, the AB has recommended replicating FC games in multiple sites in Ireland with a view to plan for national roll out. Currently, a plan is underway with industry partners to replicate the FC initiative over a three-year period in line with evidence from Study 2. UEFA are a key partner in this project and are committed to translating the Irish model across European FAs.

Materials & methods

Data collected to achieve Aim 1 will further be analysed to address Aims 2, 3 and 4. Data analysis for Aims 2, 3 and 4 will be presented after the seven step SROI methodology is presented.

Aim 1: SROI of the FC initiative when replicated

SROI is well recognised for its inclusion of social impact and a strong engagement with stakeholders. This section begins with the theoretical framework of the eight SROI principles that will guide the seven SROI methodological steps (see Fig. 2 below) whereby each step will be considered as a separate study.

The SROI evaluative framework can be summarised as an economic valuation of an intervention/service/programme that places stakeholders at the centre of its analysis [59]. It is used to measure, report and understand the economic, social and/or environmental impact of a given intervention or programme using financial proxies. The SROI methodology is guided by eight key principles that underpin each step of the process (as highlighted in Fig. 2 above). These principles provide ethos and guidance for using and reporting on this methodology. The eight SROI principles from Social Value UK [58] are presented in Table 1 below along with commentary on how they will be upheld in this study.

Step 1: develop stakeholder map and select participants for research

Stakeholders are defined as individuals, groups of people or organizations who might be impacted by, or have an influence on the activity being analysed, experiencing either positive or negative changes as a consequence Social Value UK [60],. To align with the first principle of SROI ‘Involve Stakeholders’ a comprehensive stakeholder mapping process will be done in order to decide which stakeholders to be included in the study.

Research design

The Banke-Thomas et al., [59] stakeholder categorization (beneficiaries, implementers, promoters and funders) will guide the mapping process. Beneficiary stakeholders will be the direct/indirect ‘users’ of the FC initiative; Implementer stakeholders will include those who support operational activities of the FC initiative; Promoter stakeholders will include those who provide support and a conducive environment for the FC initiative to take place; Funder’s stakeholders will include those who will finance the FC initiative’s replication across multiple sites.

Notably, four stakeholder groups were identified in Study 1 namely Participants (Ps), Significant Others (SOs), Community Partners (CPs) and Volunteer Coordinators (VCs). It is envisaged that similar stakeholder groups may be identified in this study. CP stakeholders in this study may extend to the Football Association of Ireland (FAI) Clubs, LSP staff and others in the region who will be involved in the promotion of the FC initiative. Stakeholders who have provided funding for replication will also be considered in the mapping process. Potential stakeholders will be mapped across stakeholder categories as per Table 2 below. Based upon the findings of Study 1, potential stakeholders that will experience material outcomes along with their inclusion criteria for the study are listed below.

Beneficiaries

Ps: This stakeholder group will be directly involved in the FC games and therefore, it is expected that as per Study 1, they will experience material outcomes from participation. Ps will be eligible for inclusion if they; a) ≥ 18 years, b) a member of the FC initiative at one of the new sites, c) have completed the physical activity readiness questionnaire (PAR-Q), d) provide written consent and e) can read and write in English. Answering ‘yes’ to any item on the PAR-Q will not warrant inevitable exclusion from the study. It should be noted that as this study is based in a ‘real world’ setting, these men will participate in the FC initiative regardless of the PAR-Q result and whether they are part of the evaluation or not. Therefore, as per study 1 men will be advised to discuss any issues arising from PAR-Q with their own GP. Also, in keeping with good practice all PAR-Qs will be given to the VCs so that they can be mindful of the particular needs of men when picking teams.

SOs: As per Study 1, it is expected that in addition to observing attributable outcomes to the FC players in relation to their participation in FC games, SOs [spouses, partners or close family members] would also directly/indirectly experience material outcomes from their partners/family members participation in the FC initiative. SOs are eligible for inclusion if they (a) are at least 18 years old, (b) are a spouse, partner or close family member of an FC player, (c) can read and write in English and c) provide written consent.

Implementers

VCs: It has been well established that volunteering has an impact on well-being [61] and therefore, as per Study 1, it is likely that this stakeholder group will be considered for inclusion in the study. This stakeholder group will organise and administer the FC games at new sites, deal with issues arising on/off the field, manage payments, rate participants, and foster and safeguard the FC values and ethos. Members of the VC stakeholder group are eligible for inclusion if they (a) are at least 18 years old, (b) can read and write in English and (c) provide written consent.

The FC operational lead: This will be a new role in the FC SE and will incorporate administrative, logistical and functional demands such as the registration and induction of new players, training, mentoring and supporting VCs, creating social events, fostering and safeguarding the FC values and ethos. This stakeholder will be eligible for inclusion if they (a) are at least 18 years old, (b) can read and write in English and (c) provide written consent.

Promoters

CPs: This stakeholder group will be expected to support the initiative through player recruitment, the provision of access to Astroturf pitches for the games and the management of pitch rental. Some CPs may also act as ‘delivery partners’ and in those cases, they will be considered as part of the ‘Implementer’ stakeholder group. CP stakeholders will be eligible for inclusion if they a) are at least 18 years old, b) can read and write in English and c) provided written consent.

Funders

Fs: This stakeholder group will include FAI and Health Services Executive (HSE) and others who will have provided funding for replication of the FC initiative. Fs stakeholders will be eligible for inclusion if they (a) are at least 18 years old, (b) can read and write in English and (c) provided written consent.

Data Collection

Stakeholders for each category will be identified through consultation with the FC Founder, other delivery partners and the literature. Thereafter a workshop style focus groups will be held with delivery partners in order to (a) draft the logic model over 12 months [inputs, outputs, activities] for the FC initiative and (b) define inclusion and exclusion criteria for study stakeholders. Data will be gathered on how potential stakeholders will impact or be impacted by the FC initiative so as to determine the materiality of their outcomes as per Principle 4. Based upon the findings from this workshop, a rationale for inclusion/exclusion of each stakeholder group will be co-developed between the research team, FC initiative and the delivery partners.

Step 2: consult stakeholders to inform outcome measures

This step aligns with the second SROI Principle ‘Understanding what changes.’ To understand the outcomes, each stakeholder group will be consulted and a Theory of Change (ToC) which is a comprehensive description and illustration that not only defines outcomes that are expected to happen in a particular context but also describes how and why those outcomes are expected [62] will be drafted.

Research design

A qualitative approach will be adopted to consult with stakeholders.

Recruitment

All stakeholders, except for SOs, will be contacted by the FC initiative gatekeeper and invited to participate in the study.

Ps, VCs, CPs, Fs

Invitations seeking expression of interest (EOI) to take part in the study will be sent from the FC initiative gatekeeper to all stakeholders via email/FC App two weeks prior to data collection. Thereafter, a reminder will be sent five days prior to data collection via email/FC App. The researchers’ [CI] email will be included in the invitations so that those who will be interested can contact the researcher directly. Those who express their interest will make direct contact with the researcher [CI] who will then send on the study information sheet and gain consent. Thereafter, data collection will be scheduled via email for times and dates convenient to the stakeholders.

SOs

This stakeholder group will be recruited via purposive sampling. All Ps who participate in this step of the research will be asked to invite their SOs to participate in the study. Furthermore, at the end of interviews with Ps, a reminder invitation for the SOs stakeholders will be given. Ps will share emails of SOs who express an interest to participate in the study with the researcher [CI] who will send them study information sheet and gain consent. Thereafter, arrangements will be made for data collection at the time of their convenience.

Data collection

Data will be collected via interviews and focus groups depending upon the availability and preference of stakeholders. Data collection will focus on motivation to get and stay involved in the FC initiative [Ps, VCs, CPs] and the impact of participation on them personally and their family [Ps, VCs, and SOs] and their organisation [CPs, Fs]. Data will be transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Transcripts will be analysed deductively using predefined codes namely (a) the environmental factors that influenced their experience [inputs and activities] (b) participant characteristics [outputs] (c) the impact of the programme on them and (d) the outcomes they experienced as a result of this experience. A ToC will be drafted for each stakeholder group and discussed with the wider research team [CI, SD, PC, TE, LF, AMcG] and refined following review of the data and examples in literature.

Step 3: validate theories of change for each stakeholder group

The draft ToC for each stakeholder group must be validated by its members. This step adheres to the second [Understanding What Changes], fourth [Including Only Material Items], seventh [Verifying the Results] and eighth [Be Responsive] SROI principles.

Research design

A qualitative approach will be used to validate ToCs with each stakeholder group.

Recruitment

Stakeholders will be recruited in a similar manner as Step 2 above for a workshop style focus group.

Data collection, analysis and validation

The final ToC for each stakeholder group will be validated prior to Step 4 for each stakeholder group via a two-step verification process that will include (a) a workshop style focus group and (b) a validation of the final draft via email.

During the focus groups, the draft ToC for each stakeholder group will be shown to the particular stakeholder group and they will be facilitated to discuss (a) the environment created by the FC initiative and how it appeals to them (b) what do they have in common (Ps and SOs only) (c) how they work together and/or with the FC initiative (VCs and CPs only), (d) what impact they expect from their direct/indirect involvement in the FC initiative and (e) outcomes gained from their direct/indirect involvement in the FC initiative both positive and negative. Each draft ToC will be revised in response to the workshop discussions.

Thereafter, members of the research team, will independently, deductively analyse the focus groups via review of the recordings and the edited draft ToC. All ToCs will be discussed at a team meeting to achieve consensus on the final drafts. The final ToC will then be sent to each member of the focus group of each stakeholder group for validation. If no additional changes are suggested by the stakeholders, the ToCs will be adopted for use in Step 4.

Step 4: measure outcomes for each stakeholder group

After defining the outcomes for each stakeholder group in the ToCs, the next step in the SROI analysis is to measure the respective outcomes. The research designs, recruitment strategies and data collection methods for each stakeholder group are discussed below.

Ps: research design

A non-randomised pragmatic controlled experimental multi-site pretest-posttest design up to 12 months post baseline will be adopted to measure the outcomes for the participant stakeholder group.

Recruitment

Ps will be recruited for the Intervention Group (IG) across seven newly established sites; similar to Step 2 above, the FC gatekeeper will invite Ps at registration via the FC App to be involved in the study. Those who are interested in taking part will be asked to contact the researcher directly [CI] who will then send on the study information sheet. Likewise, the FC SE and its partners will establish ‘Comparison in Waiting’ sites (n = 4; they will be offered the initiative after 12 months (12 M)) and these Ps will make up the Comparison in waiting group (CG).

Sample size estimates and study participant numbers

As per Study 1, it is probable that physical, mental and social health outcomes will be identified by Ps in their ToC. Also, given the data regarding CVD risk from Study 1 [53], systolic blood pressure and waist circumference have been identified as the primary outcome measures with sample size estimates based on standard deviation and test-retest correlation data from previous studies. The study targets a 4-mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure and 3 cm reduction in waist circumference in the IG relative to the CG. With 80% power to detect significant differences with p = 0.05, it is estimated that 140 and 80 participants will be required to detect significant intervention effects in systolic blood pressure and waist circumference respectively. Allowing for a variance inflation factor of 1.7 to account for the Cluster Randomised Trial design and a 20% loss to follow up, circa 300 participants will be needed to detect changes in systolic blood pressure.

This step aligns with the third [Value the things that matter], seventh [Verify the result] and fifth [Do not overclaim] SROI principles whereby the outcomes for each stakeholder group will be valued at 12 M.

Research design

Two approaches will be adopted to determine values for outcomes. For the Ps stakeholder group where possible health care fiscal proxies that (a) integrate per diem standard rates and average costing e.g., cost of healthcare visits or (b) account for reduced health risk will be sourced from the Department of Health in Ireland to value outcomes. Where such fiscal proxies do not exist for Ireland, a contingency valuation on the outcomes experienced by stakeholders i.e., willingness to pay (WTP) approach will be adopted via a self-reported cross-sectional survey. Only the WTP approach will be adopted for all other stakeholder groups.

Data collection

The 12 M survey will be administered to all the stakeholders as per Step 4 above. Each stakeholder group will be asked (a) whether they experienced the outcome and (b) to place a financial value (from €0- €10,000) on each of the outcomes they experienced from their direct or indirect involvement in the FC initiative. They will be asked to consider this value either as a direct benefit from having this outcome or as an opportunity cost [an estimate of how much they would be willing to pay to avoid not achieving this outcome.

Research design

Discussion

This paper seeks to present the protocols used in this study to assess the efficacy of the FC initiative when replicated from two to twelve sites with a view of supporting others seeking to close the knowledge-practice gap for a similar intervention.

Specifically, this paper details the SROI methodology that will be used in this study to express in financial terms the social, ecological and economic value of the FC initiative in the face of the investment expenditure that will be been incurred [68]. Such economic evaluations provide decision-makers with information to systematically analyse and compare the economic efficiency of various EBPs in order to guide resource allocation and policy decisions. The choice of methodology depends on the nature of the project, the types of outcomes being evaluated, and the available data. Traditionally, methodologies such as the Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA), which considers not just the cost but also the savings over time from the intervention [69], Cost-Utility Analysis (CUA), which is similar to CEA but compares at least two choices (of intervention or example) concerning both outcomes, measured in utility and costs [70] and Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA), which can be described as an analytic and systematic process of evaluating the costs and benefits of programmes/projects often for society’s benefit or social in nature [71], have been used to inform decision-makers. More recently, research has suggested that SROI may offer advantages over these traditional methodologies, particularly if the intervention being considered is of specific local importance or relevance i.e., it does not apply across the whole population [72]. Involving stakeholders (including those beyond direct beneficiaries) and in particular allowing them to decide what is measured, material and to verify same [73], ensures the evaluation provides evidence of success from diverse perspectives and this is a key strength of SROI [74].

Advantages of SROI evaluation include (a) its focus on the long-term impact (b) its monetisation of social effects which allows a fuller analysis of both expenditures as well as the results (c) its accounting of unintended, as well as the negative effects of social projects (d) its ability to evaluate already implemented projects, as well as forecasting the return from planned innovations [75]. However, SROI is not without its limitations such as (a) lack of data, (b) the resource intensiveness of the process, (c) difficulties in capturing social value in a single number, (d) challenges of assigning appropriate fiscal proxies, (e) challenges of proving cause and effect, (f) challenges of calculating deadweight all of which can lead to (g) uncertainty of the accuracy of the final ratio [76]. To mitigate for these limitations, conservatism should be applied when assigning and discounting value.

SROI has grown in popularity and globally, it has been adopted in various ways; Europe utilises it for policy decisions [77], Australia and Singapore integrate it into business practices [78] and in the UK SROI projections are required for government contracts [79]. However, despite its popularity, there is a dearth of academic literature detailing the protocols used in SROI evaluations. It is important that such protocols are widely shared to elevate the quality of SROI evaluations by educating others how to overcome the limitations and build upon the strengths of this important methodology. This paper seeks to address this knowledge gap in the literature.

Scaling up EBPs is essential to achieving population-level impact, yet this process is often hindered by a lack of contextualised, real-world evidence to guide decision-making [80]. Interventions that show promise at pilot stage frequently fail to reach scale due to gaps in implementation knowledge, insufficient economic justification, or poor alignment with local systems [81]. Therefore, it is not enough for an intervention to demonstrate efficacy; it must also show that it can be implemented effectively, equitably, and sustainably in diverse settings [82]. Generating robust, multi-dimensional evidence, including social and economic returns, is critical to making a persuasive case for scaling, especially when public resources are limited [83].

As EBPs move from pilot stage to replication and scale up, economic evaluation becomes essential to ensure that limited resources are allocated to interventions that are not only effective, but also offer meaningful value for money [84]. SROI offers a compelling means of demonstrating broader social, health and community level benefits often overlooked in traditional cost effectiveness metrics. By capturing the full spectrum of benefits produced by the FC initiative, credibility and strategic appeal of scale-up proposals may be enhanced, which may be critical in garnering the support of funders and policy makers to invest in scaled up implementation [85]. As such, this protocol aims to contribute to the growing field of implementation science by demonstrating how SROI can be used not only to justify initial investment, but also to support the expansion of EBPs like the FC initiative.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the field of SROI and can support others to demonstrate effectiveness to ensure EBPs achieve scale. The use of SROI not only strengthens the case for investment but also provides insights into how EBPs initiatives can be adapted and scaled in other settings [86]. Sharing the protocols used in this evaluation could create a standardised SROI practice within public health evaluation thereby enhancing their credibility and usefulness in informing scale-up initiatives.
