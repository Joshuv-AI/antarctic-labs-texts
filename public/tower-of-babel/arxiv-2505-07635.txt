Interpreting Graph Inference with Skyline Explanations
Dazhuo Qiu, Haolai Che, Arijit Khan, Yinghui Wu

Abstract
Inference queries have been routinely issued to graph machine learning models such as graph neural networks ( 𝖦𝖭𝖭𝗌 \mathsf{GNNs} ) for various network analytical tasks. Nevertheless, 𝖦𝖭𝖭 \mathsf{GNN} outputs are often hard to interpret comprehensively. Existing methods typically conform to individual pre-defined explainability measures (such as fidelity), which often leads to biased, “one-sided” interpretations. This paper introduces skyline explanation , a new paradigm that interprets 𝖦𝖭𝖭 \mathsf{GNN} outputs by simultaneously optimizing multiple explainability measures of users’ interests. (1) We propose skyline explanations as a Pareto set of explanatory subgraphs that dominate others over multiple explanatory measures. We formulate skyline explanation as a multi-criteria optimization problem, and establish its hardness results. (2) We design efficient algorithms with an onion-peeling approach, which strategically prioritizes nodes and removes unpromising edges to incrementally assemble skyline explanations. (3) We also develop an algorithm to diversify the skyline explanations to enrich the comprehensive interpretation. (4) We introduce efficient parallel algorithms with load-balancing strategies to scale skyline explanation for large-scale 𝖦𝖭𝖭 \mathsf{GNN} -based inference. Using real-world and synthetic graphs, we experimentally verify our algorithms’ effectiveness and scalability.

Contributions
.
We summarize our contributions as follows:

(1)
Formulation of Skyline Explanations.

We introduce a class of skyline explanatory queries (
\mathsf{SXQ}
)
that returns a skyline set of explanatory subgraphs optimizing multiple explainability measures. We formalize subgraph dominance and prove the computational hardness of query evaluation problem.

(2)
Algorithmic Solutions.

We develop efficient algorithms for evaluating
\mathsf{SXQs}
: an onion-peeling method with
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation, a diversification strategy to capture structural and embedding-level variety, and parallel algorithms that scale well to
e.g.,
billion-edge graphs.

(3)
Extensive Empirical Evaluation.

Experiments on diverse real-world graphs show that our method generates more comprehensive explanations and scales effectively. For example, on
\mathsf{Cora}
(citation network), it improves the Integrated Preference score of state-of-the-art explainers by
2.8\times
, on and achieves an
8.2\times
speedup over
\mathsf{GExp}
on the million-edge
\mathsf{OGBN\_arxiv}
(citation network).

GNNs have demonstrated themselves as powerful tools in performing various graph learning tasks.
Recent studies proposed multiple variants of the GNNs, such as graph convolution networks (GCNs)

, graph attention networks (GATs)

Explanation of GNNs
.
Several
\mathsf{GNN}

(1) Learning-based methods aim to
learn substructures of underlying graphs that
contribute to the output of a
\mathsf{GNN}
.
GNNExplainer
[
6
]
identifies subgraphs with node features that maximize the influence on the prediction by learning continuous soft masks for both adjacency matrix and feature matrix.
CF-GNNExplainer
[
7
]

learns the counterfactual subgraphs, which
lead to significant changes of the

PGExplainer
[
10
]
parameterizes the learning process of mask matrix using a multi-layer perceptron.
GraphMask
[
32
]
learns
to mask the edges through each layer of
\mathsf{GNNs}
that
leads to the most sensitive changes to their output.
(2) Learning-based
\mathsf{GNN}
explainers require prior
knowledge of model parameters and incur learning overhead for large graphs. Post-hoc approaches
perform post-processing to directly compute subgraphs that optimize
a fixed criteria. For example,
[
12
]
utilizes Monte-Carlo tree search to compute subgraphs that optimize a game-theory-inspired Shapley value.

[
33
]
follows a similar approach, yet optimizes HN values, a topology-aware variant of Shapley value.
SAME
[
34
]
extends structure-aware Shapley explanations by using expansion-based Monte Carlo tree search to extract multi-grained connected substructures.
These methods are constrained to
pre-defined criterion, and cannot easily adapt to customizable,
multiple explanability criteria.
Beyond post-hoc explainers, GSAT
[
35
]
uses stochastic attention within the information bottleneck framework to filter irrelevant components, while PGIB
[
36
]
combines prototype learning with the bottleneck to highlight prototypical subgraphs.

Closer to our work are
\mathsf{GNN}
explainers that
optimize pre-defined, multiple criteria.
CF
2

[
8
]

optimizes a linear function of factual and counterfactual measures and learns feature and edge masks as explanations. RoboGExp
[
37
]
generates factual, counterfactual, and
robust subgraphs, which remain unchanged
upon a bounded number of edge modifications.
GEAR
[
9
]
learns
\mathsf{GNN}
explainers by adjusting the gradients of multiple objectives geometrically during optimization.

\mathsf{MOExp}

[
11
]
solves a bi-objective optimization problem to find Pareto optimal set over “simulatability” (factual) and “counterfactual relevance”.
Despite these methods generating explanations
over multiple criteria, the overall
goal remains pre-defined – and not configurable
as needed. In addition,
\mathsf{MOExp}
does not control the size of explanations, which may result in large number of explanations that are hard to inspect.

Skyline Query
.
A skyline query is a multi-criteria database query that retrieves a set of
non-dominated
records from a dataset, based on a given set of attributes
[
41
,
42
]
. A record dominates another if it is at least as good in all dimensions and strictly better in at least one.
For example, when searching for hotels, a skyline query would return those that are not worse than any other option in both price and distance to the city center, while being strictly better in at least one of them.
The result forms the
Pareto frontier
of the dataset, representing the most interesting or optimal choices without requiring users to specify explicit weighting functions across multiple criteria. Skyline queries are widely used in decision making, recommender systems, and multi-objective optimization tasks.

III
Skyline Explanations

As aforementioned in Example
I
,
explanations that optimize a single explainability measure may not be
comprehensive for users’ interpretation
preference. Meanwhile, a single explanation that optimizes multiple
measures may not exist, as two measures
may naturally “conflict”. Thus, we
pursue high-quality explanations for
\mathsf{SXQ}
in terms of
multi-objective optimality measures.
We next introduce our skyline explanation structure and the corresponding generation problem.

III-A

Skyline Explanatory Query

We start with a class of
explanatory queries.
A
Skyline explanatory query
, denoted as
\mathsf{SXQ}
,
has a form

{\mathsf{SXQ}}(G,{\mathcal{M}},v_{t},\Phi)
,
where (1)
G
=
(V,E)
is an input graph,
(2)
{\mathcal{M}}
is a
\mathsf{GNN}
;
(3)
v_{t}\subseteq V_{T}
is a designated test node of interest,
with an output
{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G)
to be explained;
and (4)
\Phi
, the
measurement space
, refers to a set of normalized explainability
measures to be
maximized
, each has a range
(0,1]
.
For a measure to be better minimized (
e.g.,

{\mathsf{conc}}
,
{\mathsf{fdl}}^{-}
in Table
I
), one can readily convert it to an inverse counterpart.
To characterize the
query semantic, we
specify explanatory subgraphs and
their dominance relation.

Explanatory Subgraphs
.
Given a node of interest

,
a subgraph
G_{\zeta}
of
G
is an

subgraph

the output
{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G)

a counterfactual

G_{\zeta}
satisfies

of the two conditions:

\circ

{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G)
=
{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G_{\zeta})
;

\circ

{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G)\neq

{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G\setminus G_{\zeta})

The interpretable space
\zeta

w.r.t.
a skyline explanatory query
{\mathsf{SXQ}}(G,{\mathcal{M}},v_{t},\Phi)

contains all the explanatory subgraphs
w.r.t.
output
{\mathcal{M}}(v_{t},G)
.

Dominance
. Given a measurement space
\Phi

\zeta
, we say that

by another

, denoted as
G_{\zeta}\prec G^{\prime}_{\zeta}

if

\circ

\phi(G_{\zeta})\leq\phi(G^{\prime}_{\zeta})
; and

\circ

there exists a measure

A

(2)

IV-A

Optimization.
To further reduce verification cost,

\mathsf{ASX}
-
\mathsf{OP}
uses two optimization strategies,
as outlined below.

Edge Prioritization
.

\mathsf{ASX}
-
\mathsf{OP}
adopts an edge prioritization
heuristic to favor
promising candidates
with small loss of

{\sf DS}
and that are more likely to
be a skyline explanation (
\mathsf{ASX}
-
\mathsf{OP}
, line
9
). It ranks each transaction
t
=
(s,s^{\prime})

in
\zeta
based on a loss estimation of
{\sf DS}

by estimating
\Phi(s^{\prime})
.
The cost vector of each transaction is aggregated to an average weight based on each dimension,
i.e.,

w(t)
=
\frac{1}{|\Phi|}

\sum_{i=1}^{|\Phi|}(\phi_{i}(s)-\hat{\phi_{i}(s^{\prime})})
. The candidates from spawned states with the smallest loss of

{\sf DS}
are preferred. This helps
early convergence of high-quality explanations,
and also promotes early detection of
non-dominating candidates (see “early pruning”).

Early Pruning
.

\mathsf{ASX}
-
\mathsf{OP}
also exploits a
monotonicity property
of
measures
\phi\in\Phi
to early determine the non-
\epsilon
-dominance
of an explanatory subgraph.
Given
\zeta^{\prime}
and
a measure
\phi\in\Phi
,
we say
\phi
is
monotonic

w.r.t.
a path
\rho
in
\zeta^{\prime}
,
if for a state
s
with candidate
G_{s}
and
another state
s^{\prime}
with a subgraph
G_{s^{\prime}}
of

on the same path

,

{\phi_{l}(G_{s})}\geq\frac{\phi_{u}(G_{s^{\prime}})}{1+\epsilon}
,
where
\phi_{l}(G_{s})
(resp.
\phi_{u}(G_{s^{\prime}})

of
\phi(G_{s})
(resp. upper bound estimation of

\phi(G_{s^{\prime}})
),
i.e.,

\phi(G_{s^{\prime}})\leq\phi_{u}(G_{s^{\prime}})\leq\phi_{u}

By definition, for any

Diversified Skyline Explanations

Skyline explanation may still contain explanatory subgraphs having highly “similar”
or overlapped nodes. This may lead to
redundant and
biased explanations.
In practice, diversified explanations often clarify the model output
more comprehensively
[
49
,
50
]
.
We next investigate a diversified scheme for

{\mathsf{SXQ}}^{k}
evaluation, in terms of coverage
and the difference between node representations.

Diversification of Explanatory Query Evaluation
.
Given a query
{\mathsf{SXQ}}^{k}
=
(G,{\mathcal{M}},v_{t},k,\Phi)
, the diversified
evaluation of
{\mathsf{DivEVAL}}({\mathsf{SXQ}}^{k})
, is to
find a
(\zeta^{\prime},\epsilon)
-explanation
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
, s.t.

{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}^{*}=\argmax_{{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq\zeta,|{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}|\leq k}{\sf DivS}({\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon})

(4)

where
{\sf DivS}({\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon})
is a
diversification
function
defined on
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
, to quantify
its overall diversity,
and is defined as

{{\sf DivS}({\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon})=\alpha\cdot{\sf NCS}({\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon})+(1-\alpha)\cdot\sum_{G_{s},G_{s^{\prime}}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}}{\sf CD}(G_{s},G_{s^{\prime}})}

(5)

here
{\sf NCS}
, a
node coverage
measure, aggregates
the node coverage of explanatory subgraphs in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
:

{\sf NCS}({\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon})=\frac{|\bigcup_{G_{s}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}}V_{G_{s}}|}{|V_{G^{L}}|};

(6)

and
{\sf CD}
, an
accumulated difference
measure, aggregates the node difference
between two subgraphs in terms of Cosine distances of their embeddings:

{\sf CD}(G_{s},G_{s^{\prime}})=1-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{G_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{G_{s^{\prime}}}}{||\mathbf{x}_{G_{s}}||_{2}\cdot||\mathbf{x}_{G_{s^{\prime}}}||_{2}}

(7)

where
\mathbf{x}_{G_{s}}
is the embedding of
G_{s}
obtained by node embedding
learning such as Node2Vec
[
51
]
. The two
terms are balanced by a constant
\alpha
.

Parallel Skyline Explanatory Querying

We next scale our approximate algorithms to query workloads.
It is desirable to parallelize the evaluation of a batch of queries
involving a set of targeted nodes
V_{T}
to multiple threads.
For example, a server may receive
a set of
\mathsf{SXQ}
queries requesting fast explanations over a set
V_{T}
of newly
detected illicit IP addresses from multiple analysts.

A random distribution of queries across threads may lead to redundant verification or “stranglers”, due to the redundant computation of
overlapped neighbors of the nodes in
V_{T}
.
We thus introduce a parallel
algorithm with a workload clustering strategy to maximize the parallelism.

Parallel Algorithm
. The parallel algorithm, denoted as
\mathsf{ParaSX}
, works with
m
threads to evaluate a query set
Q
with
n

{\mathsf{SXQ}}
queries,
involving a test node set
V_{T}

(pseudocode given in Appendix). (1)
Inter-Batch parallelization
: It first partitions
Q
into
N
small batches to be evaluated in parallel, guided by a clustering strategy
with a goal that for any pair of target nodes
v_{t}
and
v_{t}^{\prime}
from two different batches,
their
L
-hop neighbors have minimized overlap (see “Workload Clustering”).
(2)
Batch Assignment
.

\mathsf{ParaSX}
then solves a makespan minimization problem to assign the
N
batches to

m
threads, with a dynamic scheduling strategy that prioritizes the processing of
queries with “influencing” output nodes that have highest common
L
-hop neighbors for those in the same thread. This is to maximize the shared computation for edge peeling and verification.
(3)
Inter-query Pipelining
:
Each local thread then invokes
\mathsf{ASX}
-
\mathsf{OP}
(
v_{t}

k

follows its local ranks of highly influential nodes, and automatically
skips processing a node, if its information

L

measures the proportion of human-crafted ground-truth nodes present in the explanatory subgraph.
For computational simplicity, we transformed each minimization measure (
{\mathsf{conc}}
,
{\mathsf{fdl}}^{-}
) into its inverse counterpart, so that all measures are expressed as
maximization
objectives.
As most
\mathsf{GNN}
explainers
are not designed to generate explanations for
multiple explainability measures,
for a fair comparison,
we employ three quality indicators (
QIs
)

[

62

63

64

These quality indicators are widely-used to measure how good each result
is in a multi-objective manner.
Consider a set of
n

\mathsf{GNN}
explainers, where each explainer
A
reports a
set of explanatory subgraphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
.

(1)
QI-1
: Integrated Preference Function (IPF) (Higher is better)
[
65
]
.
IPF score unifies and compares the quality of non-dominated set solutions with a weighted linear sum function.
We define a normalized IPF of an explanation
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
from each
\mathsf{GNN}
explainer
A
with a normalized
single-objective score:

{\mathsf{nIPF}}({\mathcal{G}}_{A})=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{G}}_{A}|\cdot|\Phi|}\sum_{G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{A}}\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi(G)

(8)

(2)
QI-2
: Inverted Generational Distance (IGD)
(Lower is better)

[
66
,
61
]
,
a most commonly used distance-based QI. It
measures the distance from each solution
to a reference set that contains
top data points with
theoretically achievable “ideal” values. We introduce IGD
for explanations as follows.
(a) We define a universal
space
{\mathcal{G}}
=
\bigcup_{i\in[1,n]}{\mathcal{G}}_{i}

from all the participating
\mathsf{GNN}
explainers,
and for each

\phi\in\Phi
,
induces a reference set

{\mathcal{G}}_{\phi}^{k}\in{\mathcal{G}}
with
explanatory subgraphs
having the top-
k
values in
\phi
.
(b) The normalized IGD of an
explanation
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
from an explainer
A

is defined as:

{\mathsf{nIGD}}({\mathcal{G}}_{A})=\frac{1}{k\cdot|\Phi|}\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}\sum_{G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\phi}^{k}}\min_{G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{A}}d(\phi(G),\phi(G^{\prime}))

(9)

d(\cdot)
is Euclidean distance function following
[
61
,
62
]
.

(3)
QI-3
: Maximum Spread (MS) (Higher is better)
[
61
]
.
\mathsf{MS}

is a widely-adopted spread indicator that
quantifies the range of the minimum and maximum values a solution can achieve in
each objective. For a fair comparison,
we introduce a normalized
\mathsf{MS}
score
using reference sets in QI-2.
For each measure
\phi\in\Phi
,
and an explanation
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
,
its normalized MS score on
\phi
is computed as:

{\mathsf{nMS}}({\mathcal{G}}_{A})^{\phi}=\frac{\phi(G^{A*}_{\phi})}{\phi(G_{\phi}^{*})}

(10)

where
G_{\phi}^{*}
is the explanatory subgraph with the best score on
\phi

in universal set
{\mathcal{G}}
, and
G^{A*}_{\phi}

is
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
’s counterpart on
\phi
.

(4)
Efficiency
: We report the total
time of explainers. For learning-based methods, we include learning costs.

VII
Conclusion

We proposed a class of skyline explanations that optimize multiple explainability measures for interpreting GNN predictions. We showed the hardness of explanatory query processing problem. To address this, we developed sequential, parallel, and diversified algorithms with provable Pareto-optimality guarantees. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our methods are practical for large-scale graphs and GNN-based classification, producing more comprehensive explanations than state-of-the-art GNN explainers.
