Universal cervical length screening and vaginal progesterone: Distinguishing strategy effectiveness from treatment efficacy
Authors: Mira Zethelius, Lina Bergman, Ann‐Catrin Ekelund, Cecilie Hongslo Vala, Bo Jacobsson, Pihla Kuusela, Ann Liljegren, Björn Lindkvist, Max Petzold, Petteri Sjögren, Ulla‐Britt Wennerholm, Tove Wikström
Published: 2026

Sir,

We are writing in response to the letter from Boujenah and Rozenberg, 1 commenting on our recent systematic review. 2 We thank the authors for their interest in the subject.

We agree that the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) 3 , 4 were not designed to assess the efficacy of vaginal progesterone in women with a short cervix. Rather, they evaluated our research question, whether a universal cervical length screening strategy followed by vaginal progesterone to women with a short cervix improves population‐level outcomes compared with no screening.

We acknowledge that the two screening RCTs had limited statistical power, which was reflected in our GRADE assessment, demonstrating a very low level of evidence. Importantly, we did not conclude that vaginal progesterone is ineffective. Our conclusion was rather that it remains uncertain whether a strategy of universal cervical length screening followed by progesterone treatment reduces preterm birth or improves neonatal outcomes, and that adequately powered randomized trials or well‐designed stepped wedge trials with adequate sample size are needed.

The effectiveness of a screening program depends not only on the efficacy of the subsequent treatment, but also on, for example, the screening threshold (cervical length), the prevalence of screen‐positive women and adherence to treatment, all contributing to the overall effect at a population level. Consequently, the clinical effectiveness of a screening strategy must be evaluated in trials specifically designed to assess the screening program as a whole.

Our findings therefore do not contradict the possibility that vaginal progesterone is beneficial in selected women with a short cervix. Rather, the available evidence does not currently demonstrate with sufficient certainty that universal cervical length screening in a general population followed by progesterone treatment to those with a short cervix improves outcomes compared with no screening. Our conclusion concerns the evidence supporting implementation of a screening strategy, not the efficacy of progesterone itself. Also, Boujenah and Rozenberg characterize cervical length screening as a low‐cost intervention. We believe this claim is insufficiently supported since the implementation of universal screening would require substantial additional personnel and ultrasound resources; why further trials are needed to establish the cost‐effectiveness of the implementation of a universal cervical length screening program as a whole.

The central criticism thus conflates two distinct research questions: whether vaginal progesterone is efficacious in women with a short cervix, and whether a policy of universal cervical length screening followed by selective progesterone treatment improves outcomes at the population level. The first question was addressed in a previous systematic review, 5 showing a reduction in preterm birth when women with a short cervix were treated with progesterone. The second question remains unanswered as current evidence fails to show an effect but is based on underpowered randomized trials.
