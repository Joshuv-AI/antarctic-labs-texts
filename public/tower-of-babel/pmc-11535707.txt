Click and learn: a longitudinal interprofessional case-based sepsis education curriculum
Authors: Samita M Heslin, Asem Qadeer, AnnMarie E Kotarba, Sahar Ahmad, Eric J Morley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a global healthcare challenge and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality. In the USA, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has integrated the Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle (SEP-1) into their Core Quality Measures, which has been linked to lower mortality rates. However, SEP-1’s multiple bundle elements present compliance challenges without comprehensive education and a collaborative approach involving nurses and providers (attending physicians, resident physicians, nurse practitioners and physician assistants).MethodsWe developed a virtual longitudinal, case-based curriculum using Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development and evaluated its effectiveness using the Kirkpatrick model. The curriculum was distributed hospital-wide over a 32-month period.ResultsA total of 3616 responses were received for the Sepsis Case-Based Curriculum modules, with 47% from nurses and 53% from providers. Introduction

With over 30 million cases each year,1 sepsis is a worldwide healthcare problem. The global mortality rate of sepsis is 25%–30%,2 3 making it also a leading cause of morbidity and mortality. Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from severe infection,4 has been researched for decades. To assist with the management and treatment of sepsis, an international consensus group created the Surviving Sepsis Campaign with evidence-based guidelines, including early administration of antibiotics and goal-oriented resuscitation.5

In the USA, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), the largest single payor has integrated the Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle (SEP-1) into its Core Quality Measures since 2015. Hospitals are required to report SEP-1 compliance data for full CMS reimbursement.6 Additionally, beginning in fiscal year 2026, the CMS-SEP1 measure will be part of the Hospital Value-Based Purchasing Programme, tying hospital reimbursement with SEP-1 compliance. Compliance with SEP-1 has been associated with lower 30-day mortality and length of stay.7

The SEP-1 bundle encompasses several key elements, including the timely administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, ordering of blood cultures and measurement of lactate levels within 3 hours of meeting severe sepsis criteria. Patients meeting severe sepsis criteria with two episodes of hypotension or a lactate level of 4 or greater require fluid resuscitation, and vasopressors are indicated for fluid-refractory hypotension. Furthermore, SEP-1 has multiple documentation requirements.8

Despite its importance, SEP-1 compliance remains a challenge, with less than half of US hospitals achieving full compliance in 2019.9 This highlights the ongoing need for education, resources and quality improvement initiatives to improve sepsis management and outcomes.

Meeting the SEP-1 bundle elements requires a collaborative approach involving both nurses and providers (attending physicians, resident physicians, nurse practitioners and physician assistants). Their active participation is crucial for detecting sepsis, ordering and drawing blood cultures and lactate levels, prescribing and administering antibiotics and managing fluids or vasopressors as necessary.10 Therefore, both nurses and providers play essential roles in ensuring the efficient, timely and effective care of sepsis, and it is imperative that they understand the SEP-1 bundle elements.

Given the complexity of SEP-1 and the large number of clinical staff in each hospital, there is a clear need for a comprehensive, ongoing education programme with regular updates. Previous studies on sepsis education have used various methods, including didactic lectures, fact sheets, grand rounds, algorithms, tutorials and laminated cards.11 However, there is no consensus on the most effective educational approach. Furthermore, to our knowledge, there are no published articles on hospital-wide interprofessional education regarding the SEP-1 criteria.

Objectives

In this study, our goal was to create and assess the effectiveness of a virtual sepsis and SEP-1 education programme consisting of 16 case-based modules. This curriculum was designed for widespread implementation across a large hospital, targeting both nurses and providers. The development process followed Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development, and its effectiveness was evaluated using the Kirkpatrick model.

Methods

Setting and participants

This curriculum was implemented at a 624-bed suburban, academic and tertiary care hospital. The curriculum was designed by the Education Subgroup of the Sepsis Committee, which consisted of key stakeholders including nursing and provider staff and leadership. The curriculum consisted of 16 case-based modules, each of which was electronically distributed to all nurses and providers in the hospital from January 2021 to August 2023.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Development of the curriculum

The curriculum was developed using Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development.12

First, a literature review was conducted to identify challenges in meeting SEP-1 measures and assess general needs. Second, a targeted needs assessment was performed by reviewing 100 random sepsis cases per month to identify commonly missed SEP-1 bundle elements.

In the third step, findings from the needs assessments were reviewed iteratively to determine four main objectives: education about diagnosing sepsis, timely administration of antibiotics and blood cultures, ordering of lactates, and fluid resuscitation orders and documentation.

For the fourth step, the educational curriculum was designed, consisting of a series of virtual case-based modules based on the most commonly missed SEP-1 bundle elements at the hospital. Case-based education, known for its effectiveness, was used, with cases developed in collaboration with the sepsis education subcommittee, incorporating both nursing and provider perspectives.

Each case was developed by the interdisciplinary sepsis education subcommittee and incorporated both nursing and provider perspectives. The cases were based on real scenarios, requiring participants to diagnose the patient with sepsis and answer a question about meeting the SEP-1 measures. After answering, participants received the correct answer along with a detailed explanation. An infographic or mnemonic was also included to provide a visual explanation (figure 1). A new case-based module was distributed every 2 months, resulting in 16 modules (figure 2).

Example of case-based learning module in the curriculum. Each module consists of a clinical case scenario and question related to the SEP-1 measure. Each module also incorporates a detailed answer explanation with infographics. SEP-1, Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle.

Topics covered in the 16 case-based modules.

In the fifth step, the curriculum was implemented by incorporating the case-based modules into the Qualtrics (Seattle, Washington, USA) survey tool and disseminating modules hospital-wide through various channels, including hospital leadership, official communication emails, newsletters and peer-to-peer distribution by nursing and provider staff.

Lastly, in the sixth step, the effectiveness of the curriculum was evaluated by attaching a survey to the case-based modules, following the Kirkpatrick Model for evaluation.13

Kirkpatrick level 1 (reaction) was assessed by soliciting participant feedback through an open-ended question about the aspects of the learning activity they found most meaningful. For level 2 (learning), participants were asked to rate on a Likert scale whether their knowledge of sepsis had increased as a result of the activity. To evaluate level 3 (behaviour), participants were asked to rate on a Likert scale their likelihood of incorporating what they learnt into their practice. Finally, for level 4 (results), 100 random sepsis charts were evaluated each month to assess bundle compliance.

Data analysis

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research. Participants’ demographic characteristics, including their role (nurse or provider), the service they worked in, and if they worked in an intensive care unit were studied. The proportions of nurses and providers who correctly answered the case-based module were also evaluated. Additionally, the responses to the survey questions that were attached to each module were analysed.

This survey included three Likert scale questions about how confident the participant was in their answer choice, whether the participant’s knowledge about sepsis had increased as a result of this activity, and whether the participant was likely to use something learnt in this activity in their own practice. The Likert scale results were reported as frequencies and medians with IQR.

Two optional open-ended questions regarding participants’ perceptions of the activity’s most meaningful aspects and suggestions for improvements were analysed qualitatively to identify themes. Responses were coded line by line to assign descriptive labels (codes) to text segments representing key ideas or concepts, which were then grouped into categories based on similarities. A participant’s response may fall into multiple categories. Data within each category were analysed for patterns and trends. Findings were reported by summarising the main themes, supported by quotes or examples.

Results

A total of 3616 responses were received for the Sepsis Case-Based Curriculum’s modules. The average number of responses per module was 226. The number of responses per module was consistent throughout the study period. Of the responses, 47% were from nurses, and 53% were from healthcare providers. Among the provider responses, 33% were from those working in intensive care settings (ICU or step-down units), compared with 32% of nurses in the same settings. Regarding specialty, 52% of provider responses came from medicine and medical subspecialties, while 48% were from nurses. In contrast, 18% of providers were from surgery and surgical subspecialties, compared with 26% of nurses. Additionally, 14% of provider responses were from emergency medicine, compared with 18% of nurse responses (figure 3).

Demographics of the participants of the sepsis case-based curriculum’s modules.

In total, the responses from nurses were 56% correct and the responses from providers were 51% correct. When asked how confident they were about the answer, the median nurse answer was 4 (IQR 1) and the median provider answer was 4 (1) on a 5-point Likert scale (1=not at all confident, 2=not very confident, 3=neither, 4=fairly confident and 5=very confident). When asked whether they were likely to use something learnt in this activity in their own practice, the median nurse response was 5 (1) and the median provider response was 5 (2) on a 5-point Likert scale (1=disagree, 2=somewhat disagree, 3=neutral, 4=somewhat agree, 5=agree). Similarly, when asked if their knowledge of sepsis has increased as a result of this activity, the median nurse reply was 5 (1) and the median provider reply was 4 (1) on a 5-point Likert scale (1=disagree, 2=somewhat disagree, 3=neutral, 4=somewhat agree, 5=agree) (figure 4).

Survey respondents were given the option to respond to two free-response questions. The first question was ‘What did you find meaningful in this activity?’ Out of the 3616 respondents, 328 (9.1%) provided answers to this question. The second question was ‘What would you like to change in this learning activity?’ Out of the 3616 respondents, 293 (8.1%) provided answers to this question. Discussion

Sepsis is a global healthcare concern and has a significant morbidity and mortality. Delayed recognition of sepsis can be life-threatening, with each hour delay associated with an increase in mortality.14 The SEP-1 bundle, part of the CMS Core Quality Measures, is focused on the early identification and intervention of sepsis. However, the SEP-1 bundle is an all-or-none measure and needs 100% achievement of its multiple elements to be complaint. Education and a team-based effort among nurses and providers are crucial for successful compliance of the SEP-1 bundle elements.

We created a longitudinal case-based education curriculum on sepsis and SEP-1 at our institution. Since nurses and providers are both crucial to the early recognition and treatment of sepsis, our education was distributed hospital-wide on a large scale. There were a total of 3616 responses among 16 cases disseminated from January 2021 to August 2023. We created our curriculum using Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development and evaluated it using the Kirkpatrick model.

A comparable number of responses were received from nurses and providers, with a similar distribution among medicine, surgery and emergency medicine specialties, as well as from intensive care settings. The results indicate that both groups answered the questions correctly slightly over half the time and expressed varying levels of confidence in their answers. This suggests that while participants felt fairly confident, there are opportunities for further learning and refinement of their understanding of sepsis treatment and the SEP-1 measure.

The majority of respondents agreed that they were likely to use something learnt in this activity in their own practice and that their knowledge of sepsis has increased as a result of this activity. When asked what they found meaningful in this learning activity, the most common themes that emerged included the review of the CMS SEP-1 criteria, increased awareness of the Sepsis Action Plans, and the activity was a good refresher. When asked what they would change in this learning activity, the most common themes that emerged included no suggested changes, requests for more frequent cases and preferences for additional questions.

Additionally, during the time period of this study, our hospital’s SEP-1 bundle compliance improved from 71% (Q1 2021) to 80% (Q3 2023). To our knowledge, this is one of the first interdisciplinary, longitudinal, hospital-wide, case-based SEP-1 education curriculum.

Understanding the various bundle elements of SEP-1 is critical. Early recognition and treatment of sepsis offer benefits, including improved mortality. Additionally, SEP-1 compliance is currently a CMS Core Quality Measure and will additionally be tied to hospital reimbursement in 2026. Successfully, completing the SEP-1 bundle elements is an interdisciplinary effort involving both providers and nurses. The hospital is a busy environment with diverse care providers, including new and seasoned staff requiring refresher training, and practitioners have limited time for additional educational activities. Thus, a longitudinal, engaging, comprehensive yet concise educational curriculum that relates to real-life scenarios is essential.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include that the education was sent hospital-wide and could result in selection and response bias. Self-ratings and self-reported measures could also result in response and response-shift bias. The responses to case-based questions and the survey are completely anonymous, so we are unable to know how many respondents completed the full series of 16 cases. Similarly, we also do not know how many new learners responded to each case. Additionally, this study was conducted over 2 years and may need a longer time period to gauge change and the sustainment of the change. Although the improvement in the hospital SEP-1 bundle compliance may be confounded by other initiatives, we believe that this longitudinal case-based curriculum was a crucial part of the multimodal strategies that resulted in the SEP-1 bundle compliance improvement at our hospital. There is a need for future studies to look at implementing similar large-scale interdisciplinary case-based SEP-1 and sepsis curricula in other settings and locations.

Conclusion

Sepsis presents a significant global burden in terms of morbidity and mortality. The CMS SEP-1 bundle serves as a pivotal Core Quality Measure aimed at enhancing the early identification and treatment of sepsis. However, SEP-1’s multiple bundle elements pose challenges in achieving compliance without comprehensive education and a collaborative approach involving nurses and providers. To address this, we developed a longitudinal, case-based curriculum using Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development and evaluated its effectiveness using the Kirkpatrick model. Over a 2-year period, we disseminated this curriculum on a large scale in a virtual format to nurses and providers. Participant feedback indicated a high likelihood of incorporating the curriculum’s teachings into their practice, with reported increases in their knowledge of sepsis. Participants also noted that the curriculum improved their understanding of sepsis and the SEP-1 measures, enabling them to apply these concepts to real-life scenarios. Notably, our hospital’s overall SEP-1 bundle compliance increased by 9 percentage points during the study period. Based on our findings, we recommend further studies to explore the development and implementation of similar interdisciplinary curricula in other healthcare settings and regions. Such initiatives could significantly contribute to improving sepsis management and patient outcomes on a broader scale.
