Spatially explicit prediction of Nepal’s forest biomass stocks, a data-driven bioregionalisation and machine learning approach
Authors: Shiva Khanal, Rachael H Nolan, Belinda E Medlyn, Matthias M Boer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract BackgroundEstimation of forest biomass stocks in vast and heterogeneous mountain ranges is critical in the context of climate change mitigation and remains challenging because of limited field observations and unknown relationships between variation in forest biomass and environmental heterogeneity. We addressed this challenge by using forest inventory plot observations and a novel spatial modelling approach. In the first step of our approach, we employ a rigorous clustering process to identify a homogeneous group of locations based on tree species and topoclimatic variables and predict potential forest aboveground biomass (AGB). Subsequently, in the second step, we incorporate finer-scale variables, including proxies of forest structure, disturbance likelihood, and elevation zones, to model deviations from the predicted potential AGB. ResultsOur method significantly improves forest AGB estimation in heterogeneous mountain landscapes, achieving a 25% reduction in prediction error compared to the best-performing existing model. The final forest AGB map, generated at 30 m resolution, reveals distinct spatial patterns, with the Central Himalayas emerging as a key carbon reservoir, harbouring forest patches exceeding 1000 t ha-1. Aggregation of these predictions yielded a total forest AGB of 1982 Mt. In addition, we produced a 250 m resolution potential forest AGB map with associated prediction standard error. ConclusionThe spatially explicit estimates of actual and potential forest biomass presented is important step towards elucidation of spatial distribution patterns of forest carbon pools and environmental controls. It also provides support for critical initiatives, including climate change mitigation strategies, monitoring forest landscape restoration, and combatting forest degradation challenges. The proposed approach, integrating both broad-scale environmental controls and fine-scale deviations, offers a robust method that is potentially applicable other mountainous regions and contributes for tracking changes in forest carbon over time, essential for REDD+ initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-025-00367-4.Keywords: Central Himalayas, Forest aboveground biomass, Spatial prediction, Nepal, Potential forest biomass

Background

The accurate prediction of spatial variation in forest carbon stocks across a country is crucial for various reporting requirements, such as REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) initiatives [1]. Global-scale estimates of forest carbon vary significantly [2, 3] and cannot meet the accuracy requirements at the national level [4, 5]. To comprehensively account for forest carbon and its dynamics, it is essential to estimate both potential and actual biomass stocks and consider the impact of disturbances [6]. Broadly, the spatial distribution of forest carbon stocks is a function of several factors, including climate [7, 8], topography [9, 10], species composition [11, 12] and disturbance/management regime [13, 14]. Improving forest carbon estimates necessitates accounting for key controls related to forest structure, climate, and disturbance. However, the challenge in quantifying forest carbon stocks and their spatial variability over large mountainous regions, such as the Central Himalayas, firstly results from a lack of field observations of forest biomass and secondly from the pronounced environmental heterogeneity that causes variation in forest site quality so that we can’t easily extrapolate forest biomass observations from field sites to the rest of the region.

The estimation of aboveground biomass (AGB) in forested areas often relies on variables such as stem diameter, wood density, total height, and forest type [15]. While the importance of wood density may vary across studies [16–18], employing region-specific or species-specific wood density values has been demonstrated to reduce uncertainty in biomass estimates, particularly in tropical regions [19], and this may apply to other forest biomes as well. Inclusion of species information is crucial as there is a significant relationship between variations in forest carbon storage and species composition and abundance, from the tropical lowlands [16] to the alpine zones of the Himalayas [20]. Neglecting species information may increase uncertainty in biomass estimates [21], but this can be partially mitigated by incorporating information on forest types that represent species assemblages [15]. However, forest type classification, based on species composition and abundance, becomes challenging in forests with substantial variations in species and dominance. In such cases, a probability-based classification approach that considers both tree species occurrence and environmental controls can provide a robust solution [22]. Biogeographical classification of regions into clusters based on species distribution, analogous to ecoregion stratification, offers an option for capturing varying mean carbon densities [23–25].

Traditionally, clustering forest inventory plots into distinct forest types involved separate clustering and spatial prediction steps [26, 27]. However, an alternative approach called Regions of Common Profile (RCP) simultaneously models combinations of species groupings and environmental conditions that resemble species assemblages [28]. The membership of each location to an RCP is determined by grouping locations based on species observed as a function of environmental gradients [29]. This approach assumes that the species composition at a given location with particular environmental characteristics depends on the mean expectation of species present at that location, and the regionalisation represented by each RCP has a roughly constant probability of observing the same set of species. By capturing the environmental gradients that shape broad-scale vegetation distribution, this method provides probabilistic memberships to vegetation assemblages over large geographical areas [30].

Environmental factors, such as climate and topography, exert long-term influences on forest structures over broad geographical areas. Climate, in particular, is often represented using coarse-resolution spatial datasets. These environmental controls have implications for the potential carbon storage across different locations. However, disturbances can impede the realization of the potential carbon stocks. Therefore, potential AGB can be predicted as a function of broad environmental gradients [18], but further insights into more dynamically changing limiting factors are necessary to understand local deviations from the potential [13, 31]. Forest disturbances such as avalanches or landslides, which typically manifest themselves at fine spatial scales, cause local forest carbon stocks to be reduced below their potential, leading to alterations in forest structure and carbon stocks. The resulting fine-scale variation in forest structure and forest carbon density is not captured by or cannot be predicted by broad climatic gradient. For instance, human-induced forest disturbances, such as tree removal, primarily occur in small spatial extents near human population centers and accessible locations. Consequently, modeling the spatial distribution of forest stands with lower AGB than expected based on the broad climatic gradients necessitates additional higher-resolution datasets that account for local constraints. Therefore, we hypothesize that datasets with relatively fine spatial resolutions (30–500 m) and global coverage, such as digital elevation models and derived terrain attributes [32], tree cover [33], canopy height [34], and burnt area [35], could be used to implicitly model fine-scale variation in the likelihood of disturbance and other terrain-driven environmental controls on forest biomass, thereby improving the accuracy of forest carbon assessment.

Building on previous findings that underscore the role of broad-scale climatic gradients and fine-scale topographic indicators in shaping forest AGB patterns across Nepal [36–38], this study advances a spatial modelling framework to predict both potential and actual forest carbon stocks at high spatial resolution. We hypothesize that integrating multi-scale predictors specifically, variables operating at two spatial scales: (i) broad climatic gradients and (ii) local topoclimatic and disturbance proxies can substantially improve the accuracy of forest AGB estimation. To test this hypothesis, we applied a RCP-based method to model the potential forest AGB (AGBpot) at a coarse scale. This approach employed probability-based classification to identify locations according to species presence and their associated potential forest AGB distributions. We then quantified the deviation from the predicted potential AGB (AGB) by incorporating variables representing proxies for disturbance probability. The specific objectives of this study were to: (a) develop a spatially explicit model of AGBpot using species distribution and climatic variables; (b) quantify AGB as an indicator of disturbance and forest degradation; (c) evaluate the predictive performance of the proposed method relative to existing approaches using national forest inventory data and (d) generate a high-resolution forest AGB map for Nepal to identify carbon-dense regions and inform conservation priorities.

Materials and methods

Field datasets

Field data were sourced from the National Forest Inventory (NFI) of Nepal. The georeferenced plot-level aboveground biomass (AGB) estimates dataset is available at the Figshare repository: https://figshare.com/s/6c185764f6989c293441. Detailed descriptions of the sample plot design, forest attribute measurement protocols along with the estimation of plot level forest biomass pools are provided by Khanal and Boer [37]. Additionally, we used the tree species records at the plot level from the national forest assessment database of Nepal.

Given the observed role of climatic energy and water availability on the plot-level variation in forest biomass [38], we used mean and variability of annual air temperature and precipitation as proxy covariates. Implementation of RCP modelling

To predict the probability of particular species assemblages in relation to climatic conditions, we used a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model, implemented in the ‘regimix’ function in the R package RCPmod [29]. The MoE model integrates mixture models and generalized linear models (GLMs), where mixture models yield model parameters as a function of covariates, and GLMs provide a statistical structure [44]. This model groups forest inventory plots into Region of Common Profile (RCP) based on similarities in species presence-absence and environmental covariates. This approach allows the identification of bioregions by delineating areas where the probability of observing specific species compositions remains relatively constant. In our implementation, the RCP model specified the binary species matrix on the left-hand side and climatic covariates on the right-hand side, modelling RCP membership probabilities via multinomial logistic regression. The species submodel was set to NULL, indicating that no additional covariates were included for species occurrence within RCPs; species probabilities were therefore modelled solely through species-specific, RCP-specific intercepts. We used a Bernoulli distribution for the species data, corresponding to a binomial GLM with a logit link function, appropriate for binary presence-absence responses. This specification assumes that variation in species occurrence within each RCP is fully captured by the RCP structure itself.

In this study, the MoE model within RCP modelled site-specific species presence/absence as a function of environmental covariates. To address potential instability in RCP model fitting arising from species recorded in only a few plots, we limited our analysis to species recorded in a minimum of three plots, resulting in a reduction of the number of species from 471 to 242. The fitted MoE object was bootstrapped 500 times using default Bayesian Bootstrap sampling, implemented through the ‘regiboot’ function in RCPmod [29]. This step provided bootstrap estimates of the model parameters. Subsequently, we predicted the RCP membership probabilities, standard error, and confidence intervals for the complete study area (grids at 250 m resolution) using the ‘predict.regimix’ function on the fitted model and a bootstrapped parameter object. The optimal number of RCPs was determined as the number that minimized the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) for the combination of environmental variables.

The forest inventory plots followed a cluster design, with each cluster comprising four to six field plots situated at distances of 150 m or 300 m between plots, depending on their location being in the lowlands or mountainous regions, respectively [37]. For validation purposes, we randomly selected validation plots, ensuring that no two forest inventory plots fell within a single 250 m grid cell in the covariates space. The final dataset consisted of observations from 1620 plots for training and 389 for validation, and these were utilized throughout the study, including the second step for predicting local deviation from potential forest AGB (AGB) and evaluating accuracy. Subsequently, we conducted a comparison between the RCP-based forest clustering and existing forest type classifications to quantify improvements in characterizing forest AGB distribution. Simple linear models were employed to compare the variance of plot-level forest AGB explained by different forest type classification datasets.The first data set includes 16 classes representing major species and their associations [45]. The second data set, potential vegetation, consists of 45 classes of forest types based on climate potential [46]. The third data set, physiographic regions, is categorized into five regions based on elevation and topography [47].

Prediction of potential forest AGB (AGB )

The upper limit or potential of forest aboveground biomass (AGBpot) for each Region of Common Profile (RCP) cluster was estimated by interpreting the 95th percentile AGB observed in the inventory plots belonging to each RCP. Weighted estimation based on repeated sampling of plots above the 95th percentile AGB for each RCP, along with their corresponding membership probabilities, was employed to derive AGBpot. Using 100 bootstrapped repeated random sampling, we derived 100 realisations of AGBpot and confidence intervals over the entire study area. The significance of mean AGB differences among plots belonging to different RCP clusters was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Deviations between potential and actual forest AGB (AGB).

The plot-level AGB was modelled as a function of a set of predictors representing proxies for disturbance intensity in forest, in particular those related to human land use and terrain-driven mass movements (Table 2). These proxies were selected to capture the disturbance probabilities across the entire country at high spatial resolution, aiming to enhance the prediction accuracy of AGB. For example, one of the proxies is a cost surface that provides an estimation of the travel time to reach a location from the road network while considering the impact of slope gradient, which contributes to increased travel time. Although other obstacles, such as water bodies, may also have an effect, we assumed that the slope gradient primarily constrains land use and other human activities that affect forest biomass in mountainous regions, such as the Himalayas. The elevational gradient indirectly affects the level of disturbance, as population density declines with increasing elevation, making accessibility more challenging and resulting in lower levels of human disturbance in high-elevation forest. Consequently, elevational belts were utilized to capture some of these variations in the absence of reliable gridded data on human population density and land use.

In addition to these disturbance-related predictors, we included high-resolution estimates of percentage tree cover and forest height to account for fine-scale structural variation in actual AGB that may not be fully represented by potential AGB derived from RCPs. While RCP modelling incorporates species composition as a function of environmental features, it does not directly capture stand-level structural attributes that strongly influence biomass, particularly in forests at different successional stages or with varying disturbance histories. Forest height and canopy cover are widely recognized indicators of structural maturity, crown closure, and disturbance recovery [48, 49] and thus provide complementary information to the other covariates in explaining deviations between actual and potential AGB. The spatial distribution of the covariates across Nepal is shown in Supplementary Figure S4.

To model and predict plot-level AGB, we fitted a random forest (RF) model using the randomForest [55] and caret [56] packages in R. The RF model was chosen due to its higher prediction accuracy [57] and efficiency [58] when using high-resolution predictors. Model training employed 10-fold cross-validation to estimate out-of-sample performance and a fixed random seed for reproducibility. The number of trees was set to 500 (default in randomForest), and the number of variables randomly selected at each split was optimised during training via cross-validation. All available predictors were included in the model without prior feature selection. Variable importance was computed using the mean decrease in Gini index, providing a relative ranking of predictors in terms of their contribution to model accuracy. The model was trained using a subset of field plot observations, and the AGB was spatially predicted for the entire study area, i.e. national territory of Nepal. The model’s performance was validated against the previously set-aside field data. To compute the mean and standard deviation of spatially explicit AGBact, 100 sets of predicted AGBpot rasters (produced by 100 bootstraps) and a predicted AGB raster were used.

Considering the highly heterogeneous landscapes of the Central Himalayas, it is important to evaluate whether the training data, which represents a subset of field plot observations, can effectively capture the variability within the larger prediction space covering the study area. To address this, the AOA (Area of Applicability) concept [59] was employed in modelling of AGB. The ‘aoa’ function in the R package CAST [60] was used to evaluate the AOA. The dissimilarity index (DI) was calculated for each prediction location by comparing its Euclidean distance to the nearest training data point with the average distance between all training points. The DI for the predictor space was compared to a threshold value derived from cross-validated training data to determine whether a location is within the AOA.

Prediction of total aboveground forest carbon stocks

We computed the total national aboveground forest carbon stock in Nepal by summing predicted AGBact for all individual grid cells. The standard prediction errors for all grid cells were aggregated to express them as a Relative Percentage Error. The total air-dried forest AGB was converted to carbon using the air-dried to oven-dried ratio (0.91) [45] and the biomass-carbon ratio (0.47) recommended by the IPCC [61].

Benchmarking against existing approaches

We evaluated the performance of our modeling approach for predicting forest AGB by comparing it to two alternatives: a RF-based model and existing global AGB products. First, we compared our RCP-based approach to a RF-based model trained on all predictor variables. Both models were trained on the 1620 plot training set and evaluated on the 389 plot independent validation set, with RMSE computed from predictions on the latter. This allowed us to directly assess the benefit of the RCP-based approach versus using all covariates simultaneously. Second, we compared our predicted forest AGB to four global AGB datasets derived from observations, remote sensing, and regional estimations [62–65]. These existing datasets have spatial resolutions ranging from 1 km to 100 m. To ensure a fair comparison, we aggregated our higher-resolution predictions to match the grid sizes of the global datasets using a mean function. We used our aggregated AGB estimates for 389 test locations against each global dataset. We also aimed to identify any regional biases in our predictions relative to existing global products. Results of these benchmarking analyses are provided in Section Benchmarking with existing global AGB data products, where we compare RCP-based estimates with four global datasets and assess their accuracy and spatial deviations.

Results

Map of AGBpot and AGB

We estimated the potential aboveground biomass (AGBpot) and the standard error (SE) of prediction for each 250 m grid cell within Nepal’s forested area by integrating membership probabilities, dominant class assignments, and observed AGB values across Regions of Common Profile (RCPs). This process involved repeated sampling of inventory plots with observed aboveground biomass (AGBact) values above the 95th percentile for each RCP, weighting these values by their membership probabilities for each RCP (Fig. 2A). The estimation utilized three key components: (i) membership probabilities assigned to five RCP classes (Supplementary Figure S9A), (ii) the dominant RCP class for each grid cell (Supplementary Figure S9B), and (iii) the 95th percentile value of observed AGB in forest inventory plots within each RCP class (Supplementary Figure S9C). The optimal number of RCP classes was determined through 500 bootstrapped model fittings, selecting five classes based on the minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Supplementary Figure S1). The resulting distribution of AGBpot exhibited variation across the RCPs, with forests in the northern regions demonstrating a higher AGBpot compared to the lower-elevation forests in the south. The spatial distribution of RCPs aligned with the elevational gradient in the Central Himalayas, and significant differences in forest AGB were observed among most RCP classes.

Additionally, we also modelled the deviation (AGB) between the predicted potential aboveground biomass (AGBpot) and the observed or actual plot-level aboveground biomass (AGBact) by incorporating a set of indicators of the disturbance levels related to human population density, land use and terrain-driven mass movements (e.g. avalanches, landslides). The deviation, AGB, was found to be relatively large for high-elevation forests (Fig. 2C). Particularly, the high-elevation forest stands in the northern regions, including stands with the highest forest AGB according to the national forest inventory, exhibited substantial deviations (e.g. >700 t ha−1). In contrast, the lowest deviations (<200 t ha−1) were predominantly observed in the lower elevation forests within the study area. The variable importance plot generated by the Random Forest model for predicting AGB (Supplementary Figure S8 indicated that elevation zones, percent tree cover, and canopy height are the most influential predictors, while other proxies of forest disturbances, such as forest fragmentation classes, demonstrated low importance. The results exhibited a reasonable agreement between observed and predicted AGB (Fig. 2D). Notably, inventory plots situated in higher elevation zones (>2000 masl), primarily represented by RCP 3, displayed relatively high forest AGB and were predicted to have a larger AGB. The scatterplot comparison between observations and predictions indicated that AGB was underestimated for low AGB plots and overestimated for high AGB plots. For the model predicting AGB, the input data used for model fitting effectively captured the environmental variability of the majority of Nepal’s forested area (Supplementary Figure S7). However, a small fraction of the total area (%), primarily distributed along the upper edges of the high elevation regions (as indicated by AOA=0 and red colours in Supplementary Figure S7, corresponds to forests where the prediction of AGB is not reliable. This pattern was expected because a relatively high proportion of the forest inventory plots in the high mountains were inaccessible and could not be sampled by field crews, causing those environments to be undersampled.

We compared the performance of the RCP-based model with that of a RF-based model of AGBact using the same independent validation data described above. RMSE was computed from predictions on this validation set, ensuring consistency with the earlier-described spatially independent split. The RF-based model yielded a larger prediction error (RMSE = 132 t ha−1) compared to the average error obtained from the RCP-based model (RMSE = 106 t ha−1). The RF-based model performed relatively poorly for plots located in the high elevation regions of Nepal, where the AGB was larger than low elevation regions.

The total forest AGB for Nepal estimated using our approach was 1982 Mt, which is equivalent to 848 Mt C (SE = 2.54%) using the air-dried oven-dried ratio (0.91) and biomass-carbon ratio (0.47) recommended by the IPCC [61]. To evaluate the advantage of using RCP-based clustering in characterizing forest types and biomass distribution, we compared the variance in forest AGB explained by existing forest classification datasets. Our results indicate that the RCP-based clustering method better explains spatial variations in forest AGB than other approaches (Supplementary Table S1). This highlights the advantage of using probability-based class information, as the RCP-based classification captures forest AGB variability more effectively than existing forest type classifications.

Benchmarking with existing global AGB data products

Comparison of predicted actual forest AGB with existing global AGB data products showed that the root mean square error (RMSE) for the four global estimates ranged from 172 to 290 t ha−1, which was up to two and a half times larger compared to the RCP-based approach used in this study (Fig. 4). The largest deviation between the predictions of this study and existing global datasets was predominantly for plots belonging to RCP 3, which represent high-elevation forests (>2000 masl). The high forest AGB plots (>500 t ha−1) in these high-elevation forest stands were not represented by the global data, as the maxima of the global datasets were much lower across all existing global datasets. A spatial overlay of observed georeferenced plot-level forest AGB also showed similar deviations with global datasets (Supplementary Figure S10). Thus, this comparison highlights the need for representation of high carbon stocks of high-elevation forests in the Himalayas in global-scale assessments. The range of predicted AGB was smaller in this comparison, as the predicted forest AGB grid cells for the test locations were aggregated to a coarser scale using the average function.

Discussion

This study advances previous research by providing spatially explicit predictions of forest carbon stocks for the entire forest estate of Nepal, incorporating both broad-scale environmental gradients and fine-scale topographic variations. Earlier findings highlighted the roles of allometry, forest structure, and terrain in mediating forest carbon distribution and identified the occurrence of extreme carbon stock stands in high-elevation forests as a key feature of Nepal’s forest ecosystems [36, 37]. By leveraging recently developed spatial modelling tools, this work extends beyond plot-level observations to spatially represent forest carbon distribution, addressing prior recommendations to integrate large-scale and terrain-driven factors for improved predictions.

Our results revealed the spatial distribution of AGBpot and AGBact across the heterogeneous mountainous landscape in the Central Himalayas. Our approach of combining a data-driven bioregionalisation with machine learning achieved higher accuracy than existing methods based on a RF-based prediction. RCP modeling identifies areas with similar species assemblage based on environmental variables, improving predictions by capturing spatial variability in environmental conditions that influence forest AGB. Our findings revealed previously unidentified locations of high AGB forests in the Central Himalayas, highlighting the importance of considering species composition for accurate biomass prediction.

Our approach facilitated a spatially explicit estimation of AGBpot. Several approaches have been used to estimate upper bounds of forest biomass distribution as AGBpot. Our approach effectively addressed several challenges encountered by previous methods for predicting AGBpot. For example, relying on weighted overlays of diverse spatial layers related to climatic indices, precipitation, soil, and topography [66], often encounter difficulties in predictor weight allocation. Moreover, they tend to overlook key factors affecting variation in forest biomass, such as variation in tree species composition. Another common approach defines AGBpot for an ecoregion as the 95th percentile value of available forest AGB observations within that region [6]. However, this method assumes strict membership of a location to a single ecoregion, potentially resulting in high variability within each ecoregion in environments characterized by significant heterogeneity. In environments with pronounced heterogeneity, fuzzy logic-based techniques, including probabilistic classifications and class memberships, prove to be more suitable for characterizing complex ecological classes based on multivariate information [67]. For instance, the estimation of potential forest carbon based on remotely sensed tree cover in protected areas [68] could be limited due to the fact that forests within protected areas can also experience disturbances [69] and may not have reached their maximum potential for forest carbon storage. Additionally, the representativeness of ecosystem types covered by existing protected area systems may be limited. Likewise, using ecoregion-specific stand age is effective due to its influence on forest carbon [70]. However, stand age data for natural and secondary forests is often unavailable. Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs) offer an alternative, but their data requirements are often unmet in many study areas, and utilizing generic parameters can lead to unreliable predictions [71]. Our study leverages available forest inventory data and gridded environmental predictors by employing Region of Common Profile (RCP) modeling. This approach models the probability of a location belonging to fuzzy ecoregion types based on tree species assemblages and bioclimatic gradients. RCP modeling provided a practical solution for estimating potential forest AGB in our study area due to its data availability and ability to capture both species composition and environmental variability.

The predicted geographical distribution of the RCPs exhibited a strong topographic effect, with membership probabilities correlated to the main elevation zones in Nepal, which runs east–west, as depicted in Supplementary Figure S9. This aligns with the widely used physiographic classification system [72] and reflects the strong influence of topography on ecological processes in this mountainous region. Notably, high-elevation areas were dominated by a single RCP (RCP 3), likely due to the unique topoclimate and vegetation distribution, characterized by limited growing seasons and extensive snow cover. This is further supported by partial effect plots (Supplementary Figure S6) showing a negative relationship between GDD and membership probability for RCP 3, while RCP 4, representing the subtropical region of the Central Himalayas, exhibited an inverse relationship with GDD. Further subdividing the study area into 10 RCP classes resulted in minimal changes to the distribution (Supplementary Figure S3). Both classifications (5 and 10 RCPs) consistently identified high-elevation regions as a distinct class, with lower elevations further differentiated by additional classes. Notably, the 5-class RCP classification explained more variance in forest AGB compared to non-RCP alternatives.

The predicted spatial distribution of actual forest biomass (AGBact) revealed that high-elevation forests in the Central Himalayas have stands with higher AGB compared to low-elevation forests, consistent with previous studies [73–76]. For instance, forests located above 2000 masl in the Central Himalayas have recorded AGB values ranging from 500 to 600 t ha−1 [76, 77], with certain species like Quercus floribunda (Moru oak) reaching over 700 t ha−1 at an elevation of 2200 masl [76]. The Mixed Oak forest within the 1800 and 2500 masl range has AGB values as high as 787 t ha−1 [75]. Moreover, our findings of high forest AGB in the high elevation zones of the Central Himalayas align with observations of high forest carbon levels in the Eastern Himalayas [78, 79] and the Western Himalayas [80–82]. Plot-based estimates have indeed confirmed that some high-elevation forests in Nepal’s Central Himalayas rank among the most carbon-dense forests in the entire Himalayan region, with AGB exceeding 1000 t ha−1 [37]. The Himalayas harbour substantial carbon storage potential owing to elevated soil carbon stocks [83], relatively tall trees [34], and high tree cover [68]. This observation aligns with findings from studies emphasizing an increase in carbon stocks with elevation [84], with high forest AGB exceeding 700 t ha−1 throughout the Himalayan range [75].

We observed a considerable deviation between observed AGBact and predicted AGBpot for high-elevation forests. This deviation, AGB can be attributed to the pronounced variability in forest AGB within this region, influenced by local disturbances such as avalanches [85], landslides [86], and terrain conditions that either facilitate or hinder forest growth [87]. Elevation emerged as the most influential factor affecting the deviation between AGBact and predicted AGBpot, serving as a proxy for population density and accessibility. It effectively distinguishes regions characterized by high population density and dense road networks in the subtropical plains from mountainous regions with low population density and limited road access. Forest AGB is typically lower in smaller patches and near forest edges due to higher disturbance intensity [88, 89]. However, some small forest patches may naturally have low biomass and occur in sparsely populated areas, away from human settlements, where disturbance is minimal. This may explain the low importance of fragmentation classes in predicting AGB.

The aggregation of predicted forest AGB resulted in a total forest AGB value of 1982 Mt for Nepal’s forest estate, equivalent to 848 Mt C (SE = 2.54%). In contrast, Nepal’s national report estimated the total AGB of trees with a diameter at breast height (DBH) cm to be 1165.95 Mt by multiplying the average AGB density with the total areas of forests and other wooded land classes in Nepal [45]. Notably, the plot-level forest AGB density used in this study includes trees with a DBH cm. Therefore, our estimate is expected to be higher than the estimate presented in the national report due to the difference in the DBH threshold.

The validation of AGBact predictions against independent validation plots demonstrated the effectiveness of our RCP-based modelling approach, yielding a relatively low prediction error. Validating large-scale potential biomass estimates is inherently challenging due to the integration of diverse datasets and the limited availability of independent validation data [90]. In our case, the availability of nationally representative and consistent forest inventory datasets enabled the allocation of independent test datasets, strengthening the robustness of our validation. Our model exhibited exhibited reasonable accuracy, effectively capturing the variability in forest carbon distribution across the study area.

The benchmarking assessment against widely used global forest AGB products revealed a substantial underestimation of biomass for Nepal in these datasets. The magnitude of underestimation was particularly pronounced in mid- to high-elevation forests, where global models appear to struggle with representing complex vertical structure, steep terrain, and diverse species assemblages. This discrepancy likely arises from differences in input remote sensing data (e.g., coarse-resolution), global allometric models that may not be well calibrated for Himalayan forest types, and the smoothing effects of global-scale model fitting.

The observed bias between existing global-scale AGB predictions and national-scale predictions is consistent with previous reports of high error rates when comparing global biomass estimates to local plot-based measurements [4, 5], especially in heterogeneous mountain environments. This finding underscores the risks of directly applying global biomass datasets for national-level reporting, REDD+ monitoring, or climate policy in such regions without regional recalibration. Our results highlight the added value of regionally optimised modelling approaches that integrate detailed forest inventory data and high-resolution covariates to better represent fine-scale biomass variability.

The high-elevation forests in the Central Himalayas showcase stands with exceptionally high above-ground biomass (AGB) values, albeit accompanied by relatively large prediction uncertainties (Figs. 2 and 3). It is important to note that these carbon-dense stands are limited in extent, representing only a small proportion of the vast high mountain region and are likely confined to distinct topoclimatic refugia. The relatively high levels of prediction uncertainty for high-elevation forests indicate that the model used to estimate AGBpot and AGB did not fully capture the forest attributes, including high AGB and its drivers, in the high mountain region. This uncertainty may arise from the input predictors, which are subsets of global datasets and may, therefore, entail unknown or higher uncertainties in the Himalayas due to the lack of field observations for model calibration. For example, the gridded global climate data often fails to capture the topography-induced heterogeneity of climatic conditions in high mountain areas [91], while input datasets derived from space-borne remote sensing may exhibit relatively large uncertainties in rugged mountain regions [92].

Improving the accuracy of AGB predictions in the second step could benefit from directly incorporating reliable observations of forest structure, rather than relying on proxies derived from subsets of globally predicted data. This approach would help reduce uncertainty related to the limitations of these proxies. Additionally, the sampling scheme employed for the national forest inventory introduces another potential source of uncertainty in AGB predictions, particularly in high mountains (>3000 masl). In these areas, only a fraction of the regular sampling locations were accessible and measured, likely biasing the data towards dense, intact primary forests with high biomass density. This undersampling issue is evident in the AOA analysis, where locations in high elevations fall outside the prediction range due to the lack of representative data. While comprehensively covering high mountain areas with forest inventory plots might be impractical, the substantial variability in these regions necessitates exploring alternative approaches. The unique combination of topoclimatic conditions, species composition, and disturbance regimes in high mountain ecoregions suggests the need for a separate model specifically tailored to predict their forest carbon distribution. For example, human disturbance plays a dominant role in lower-elevation forests, while climate, topography, and natural disturbances are more influential factors at higher elevations [36]. Considering the limitations of the large-area model presented here in capturing the complexities of forest structure and biomass across diverse geographical zones, from subtropical regions to the high Himalayas, a dedicated investigation into the specific drivers and spatial distribution of forest carbon in high-elevation forests is highly warranted. Our approach reveals the remarkable extent and potential of aboveground biomass within the Central Himalayan forests.

Conclusions

With the aim to achieve the most accurate estimate of forest carbon stocks in Nepal, we present spatially explicit predictions of actual forest aboveground biomass in fine spatial resolution and aggregated these predictions to estimate Nepal’s total aboveground forest carbon stock. Our approach integrates both the broad-scale environmental controls of potential forest AGB and the fine-scale deviation of the actual forest AGB from the climatic potential. The reasonable prediction accuracy observed with independent validation data confirmed the significance of including predictors operating on varying scales for improved characterisation of forest AGB distribution. As this approach provided an improved estimate of Nepal’s forest carbon stocks, it demonstrates potential as a robust methodology for estimating forest carbon stocks in other mountainous regions. The approach would also allow tracking changes in forest carbon over time by using repeatedly measured sample plots, a key requirement for REDD+ purposes.
