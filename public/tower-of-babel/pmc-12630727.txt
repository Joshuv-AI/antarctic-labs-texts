Huge transverse Maxwell stress in ferroelectric fluids and prototyping of new ferroelectric motors
Authors: Tatsuhiro Tsukamoto, Suzushi Nishimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrostatic actuators typically rely on Maxwell stress, whereas the use of transverse electrostatic force (TEF) has been overlooked because of its weakness. The discovery of polar nematic liquid crystals in 2017 changed this, enabling lower operating voltages and high output power. We here explore TEF in a ferroelectric fluid and demonstrate a ferroelectric motor based on a new driving principle. Using ferroelectric nematic liquid crystals, we show that TEF can elevate the fluid between electrodes with a gap of 2.5 mm up to more than 80 mm at only 28 V mm−1, corresponding to a stress greater than 1000 N m−2. Polarization analysis also revealed a continuous paraelectric-to-ferroelectric transition. Unlike electromagnetic motors, ferroelectric motors require no metal rotors or magnets, resulting in a lower weight and simplified device structure and eliminating the need for rare-earth materials. These results suggest that ferroelectric fluids can enhance electrostatic actuator performance and practicability.

Introduction

Electrostatic devices have long been used in actuators such as microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) because their configuration is more straightforward than that of electromagnetic devices1,2. Recently, electrostatic actuators based on dielectric elastomers and fluids have become attractive because of their potential applications in soft robotics, such as artificial muscles3–8. The common principle of these actuators is the use of Maxwell stress, which is generated when a voltage is applied between a pair of electrodes facing each other. Maxwell stress was originally described as a tensor that simultaneously generates not only an attractive force acting parallel to the electric field but also a repulsive force acting perpendicularly (Fig. 1a); however, electrostatic devices that use this repulsive force have rarely been reported. Dielectric elastomer actuators reported thus far have used deformation, where compliant electrodes compress elastomers by an electrostatic attractive force and are consequently expanded perpendicular to the electric field3,9,10. Here, the repulsive force is not considered. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, compared with the attractive force, which is proportional to the electrode area, the repulsive force is only proportional to one side of the electrode; consequently, especially when the interelectrode distance D is short, the actual generated repulsive force is inevitably weaker than the attractive force even though the magnitude of each stress is equal. Even for an electrostatic attractive force, the relative dielectric constant for ordinary dielectrics is only 1–10; thus, a voltage greater than 1000 V is required to attain a practical force. Therefore, a weaker repulsive force would not be worth considering. However, the discovery of polar nematic liquid crystals by two independent research groups at approximately the same time in 2017 changed this situation11,12. Subsequent studies have revealed that these liquid crystals have ferroelectric and antiferroelectric phases13–18 and relative dielectric constants greater than 10,00012,19–22. Another essential feature of these materials is that they are fluids, unlike inorganic ferroelectric materials, which are rigid and difficult to deform. Exploiting these unique features, researchers have already demonstrated a double-helical-coil electrode (DHCE) actuator with a driving voltage that is substantially reduced by a factor of 1000 compared to conventional devices23. For electrostatic attractive stress, including the above-mentioned actuator, the acting force is parallel to the electric field between the electrodes; thus, to enable extraction of the generated force, at least one of the electrodes must be free to move. However, because the electrostatic repulsive stress acts perpendicular to the electric field, both electrodes can be fixed, which reduces the risk of short circuits when the device is driven and further simplifies the device structure.

In the present study, we report visualizing and quantitatively analyzing the transverse electrostatic force (TEF) in a ferroelectric fluid using a eutectic liquid crystal mixture, DIO/DIO-CN, that exhibits a ferroelectric nematic phase at temperatures from 22 to 52 °C (Fig. 1b). To illustrate the practicability of the TEF, we also demonstrate a new motor design that does not require metal rotors or magnets and is entirely different from conventional electrostatic devices that rely on electrostatic attractive force, including DHCE devices23.

Experimental setup

An experimental setup was designed to analyze the TEF accurately through gravimetric measurements in which a ferroelectric fluid was elevated. We fabricated a jig using a 3D printer; the experimental arrangement is shown in Fig. 2a. The apparatus consists of a reservoir filled with DIO/DIO-CN and stainless steel rulers fixed vertically in the fluid to enable height measurement; the rulers also function as electrodes. When a voltage is applied, the TEF causes the ferroelectric fluid to rise between the electrodes until the upward TEF balances the weight of the elevated fluid, as illustrated in the inset in Fig. 2a. A video camera records the entire process. The rulers are only 0.5 mm thick to reduce potential fluid turbulence that could affect the experimental results. To ensure a uniform electric field distribution, we exposed only the thin sections of the electrodes while masking the other electrode surfaces with polyimide tape. Visualization of the TEF

A direct-current (DC) voltage was applied to the device to visualize and quantify the TEF. When the voltage was increased in 10 V intervals from 0 to 150 V for D = 10 mm, the fluid height gradually increased: 0 mm at 0 V, 1.0 mm at 50 V, 14.6 mm at 100 V, and 26.5 mm at 150 V (Fig. 2b–e, respectively, Supplementary Video 1). For D = 2.5 mm under an applied voltage of 80 V, the fluid height reached 100 mm (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Video 2). To our knowledge, this represents the first observation of a TEF-induced macroscopic rising motion of a dielectric material on the scale of several tens of millimeters. Neither silicone oil nor the nematic liquid crystal 4-cyano-4′-pentylbiphenyl (5CB) showed any rise under the same conditions, indicating that the TEF is negligible for conventional dielectrics and non-ferroelectric liquid crystals (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Video 3). These observations confirm that such a significant elevation is unique to ferroelectric fluids.

Electric field dependence of TEF

Experiments for D = 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mm revealed that the fluid height increased with decreasing D and was proportional to the applied voltage (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Videos 2, 4–6).

To further analyze the TEF, we investigated the transverse electrostatic stress, defined as the TEF per unit area. Polarization dynamics

We conducted polarization-reversal current measurements to confirm that the source of the TEF was polarization, as given in Eq. (1). We investigated the polarization by measuring the current response for DIO/DIO-CN under an applied triangular AC voltage ( ± 1, ±2, …±30 V mm−1, 100 Hz) for D = 1.0 mm. The current slightly increased at ±2 V mm−1, with a tiny hysteresis (Fig. 3a, purple). At ±8 V mm−1, a current peak characteristic of polarization switching was observed (Fig. 3a, yellow). At ±22 V mm−1, complete polarization-reversal currents were observed (Fig. 3a, pink). By calculating the polarization, P, from these currents, we observed the polarization’s growth. At ±2 V mm−1, a hysteresis curve with tiny spontaneous and remnant polarizations was observed, indicating weak ferroelectricity (Fig. 3b, purple). At ±8 V mm−1, the spontaneous and remnant polarizations exceeded those at ±2 V mm−1 (Fig. 3c, yellow). At ±22 V mm−1, an entire hysteresis curve was observed, reflecting the high ferroelectricity (Fig. 3c, pink). From these results, we investigated how polarization changes with the electric field. First, a steep linear increase in polarization is observed at low electric field (Fig. 3c, highlighted in purple), then the slope decreases at medium electric field (Fig. 3c, highlighted in yellow) and saturation at ~6 µC cm−2 occurs at high electric field (Fig. 3c, highlighted in pink), which has an analogy to the electric field-induced increase of second harmonic generation (SHG) intensity in DIO12. Numerous recent studies have investigated the mechanism of ferroelectric phase manifestation in rod-shaped nematic liquid crystals. Based on them, one of the plausible interpretations is that the ferroelectric order does not appear suddenly upon phase transition to a ferroelectric phase but rather polar order gradually forms in a preceding phase15 and becomes polar cybotactic clusters afterwards24,31,32. In addition, the development of the ferroelectric phase is known to be induced not only by temperature but also by an electric field33–35. As the number of clusters increases in response to external stimuli such as an electric field, their size increases, and coalescence occurs12. Based on these findings, it is reasonable to expect the formation and growth of polar cybotactic clusters in DIO/DIO-CN.

Let us take a closer look at the growth process of ferroelectricity in our experimental system. As shown in Fig. 3a, the polarization-reversal current peaks shift to higher voltages under higher electric fields at fixed frequency (higher sweep rates, or dE/dt). In addition, the standard deviations of polarization values in Fig. 3c are large under low-to-medium electric fields, especially in 4–6 V mm−1. These results indicate the presence of unstable ferroelectric order in the early stage of growth, which is highly sensitive to heat and sweep rate, presumably due to the high fluidity of the material. Although fundamental polarization mechanisms and/or physical states are different between ferroelectric nematic materials and inorganic and organohalogen-based polymeric (relaxor) ferroelectrics, the observed polarization sensitivity against thermal and sweep rate conditions in this study is analogous to that of polar nanoregions and diffusive phase transitions found in relaxor materials36,37.

These prior reports and experimental findings suggest that DIO/DIO-CN forms polar cybotactic clusters and exhibits relaxor-like behavior in the early stage of polar order development in our experimental system. As the number and size of clusters increase under higher electric fields, the properties approach those of a perfect ferroelectric.

Validation of the Maxwell stress for TEF

On the basis of the polarization data in Fig. 3c, we estimated the TEF per unit area, σxx, by applying the Maxwell stress tensor in Eq. (1). Parameter σxx shows an increase through a transitional dependence on E2 to E, with ~850 N m−2 at 28 V mm−1 (Fig. 3d). The estimated stress transition and magnitude agree well with the observed stress, σ⊥, in Fig. 2h. We identify the unprecedented TEF per unit area as the component of the Maxwell stress tensor perpendicular to the electric field. This highlights that the polarization P accumulated at the interface between the dielectrics and the electrodes is essential for producing a TEF.

Demonstration of the scalability and stability of TEF

Thus far, we have shown that a huge transverse Maxwell stress of ~1000 N m−2 can be attained at an electric field of 28 V mm−1 using a ferroelectric fluid. However, even though the stress was considerable, the experimentally generated force was weak—on the scale of a few tens of milligrams. To demonstrate that we can achieve a stronger TEF and drive practical devices, we attempted to directly measure the TEF generated in an experimental system scaled up by a factor of 100. Although the aforementioned experiments were conducted in air, a TEF also occurs in resins because their dielectric constant is only several times that of air. After confirming that the TEF could raise a resin piece of 5 mg (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Video 7), we constructed the experimental setup shown in Fig. 4b (details shown in Supplementary Figs. 5–9). Thin aluminum parallel-plate electrodes with D = 2.5 mm were inserted into a 3D-printed reservoir (Fig. 4b). A resin mover with a base area of 100 mm2 was clamped to a tensile machine. The depth of the ferroelectric fluid was set to 6 mm, and the mover was immersed to a depth of 1 mm below the fluid surface such that the TEF acted only on its bottom. When DC 60 V was applied, the resultant TEF reached 73 mN (Fig. 4c) and the corresponding stress of 730 N m−2 was equivalent to that obtained on a small scale (Figs. 2h and 3d), confirming the high scalability and practicability of the TEF. In addition, the force remained stable, exhibiting no apparent fluctuation for at least 25 s after reaching a constant value within 5 s of the voltage being applied (Fig. 4c). This demonstrates the high spatiotemporal stability of the polarization event in a volume as large as 750 mm3 (= 50 mm length × 2.5 mm width × 6 mm depth), irrespective of the high fluidity of the material. Novel design of ferroelectric motors

When considering device applications of the TEF, its most important advantage over an electrostatic attractive force is the ability to fix the electrodes. One application in which this advantage can be best utilized is in motors.

Consider parallel-plate electrodes placed in a ferroelectric fluid. If a resin mover is positioned between the electrodes, the TEF acts on the interface between the mover and the fluid. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, if the width of the mover is shorter than that of the electrodes, the mover will receive a TEF from both ends, and the TEF will cancel out; thus, the mover will not move. If the width of the mover is increased, the TEF acts on only one side of the mover; thus, the mover moves to the opposite end of the electrode and then stops. Three pairs of electrodes as the stator and two resin movers as the rotor are required for continuous movement. When the first electrode pair (“U” in Fig. 5b) and resin mover (rotor) complete the aforementioned operation (1–2 in Fig. 5b), if the electrode arrangement and driving voltage waveform are chosen such that the third electrode pair (“W” in Fig. 5b) and resin mover (rotor) start operating (2–3 in Fig. 5b), continuous operation is repeated for as many of these configurations as there are. If the electrodes are arranged in a straight line, they will operate as a linear actuator; if they are arranged in a circle, they will operate as a motor. As a proof-of-concept design, Fig. 5c, d show a prototype ferroelectric motor consisting of a 3-phase 24-pole stator with an 11.25° electrode angle and a 3.75° space angle between stators and a single-phase 16-pole rotor with a 12.5° electrode angle and a 10° space angle. Supplementary Video 8 shows this prototype being driven with a 3-phase square wave with an amplitude of 0–60 V and a duty ratio of 33%. The smooth rotation can be seen as increasing the driving frequency. Most electrostatic motors studied thus far required a high applied electric field of several 10–100 MV m−1 39–41. By contrast, the ferroelectric motor was confirmed to rotate with an electric field as weak as 0.03 MV m−1 (= 60 V/2 mm), which means that a driving voltage as low as one-thousandth could be achieved.

The most important advantage of this ferroelectric motor over ordinary electromagnetic motors is that it does not require a voltage to be applied to the rotor, which means that the rotor can be made of resin instead of metal, making it lightweight and reducing inertia. In addition, because the rotor does not need to be energized, mechanisms such as carbon brushes and slip rings are unnecessary. Furthermore, because no magnetic field is used, no permanent magnets or electromagnets are needed, making the entire motor lighter. The lack of a magnetic field also means that rare-earth metals that increase the magnetic force, such as neodymium and dysprosium, are not needed, nor is copper for the electromagnetic coils.

Electrostatic force has thus far only been used in limited applications, such as MEMS, because it is ten thousand times weaker than the electromagnetic force. However, now that researchers have found that ferroelectric fluids can be used to generate forces 1000 times stronger than those generated by conventional dielectric devices, the ferroelectric motor will be added to the list of devices capable of converting electrical energy into kinetic energy. Using ferroelectrics to enhance the electrostatic force is a natural counterpart to using ferromagnetic materials such as iron, nickel, and cobalt for electromagnetic force. The demand for motors for electric vehicles and other applications is increasing, along with the trend toward a decarbonized society. In conjunction with this trend, the issue of economic security over mineral resources such as rare-earth metals is becoming a matter of concern. We hope that ferroelectric motors, which are made of elements that exist ubiquitously on Earth, will be one of the solutions to this common problem for humanity.

Methods

Materials

DIO and DIO-CN were synthesized via the esterification of carboxylic acid and phenol components23. The carboxylic acid and phenol components, as well as other necessary reagents and solvents, were purchased from Sakai Kogyo, Tokyo Chemical Industry, and Kanto Chemical. Unless otherwise noted, all chemicals were obtained from commercial suppliers and were used without prior purification.

Preparation of DIO/DIO-CN

To a 20 mL vial was added an equivalent amount of DIO and DIO-CN. The sample was dissolved in chloroform. The solution was sonicated for 1 min until it became homogeneous. Then, the solvent was removed by a rotary evaporator. The sample was further dried in vacuo, affording the 1/1 mixture of DIO and DIO-CN.

3D printing and metalworking

Jigs and motor parts were designed using 3D-CAD software (Fusion 360, Autodesk) and slicer software (CHITUBOX basic and CHITUBOX Pro, ChiTuBox). Jigs and motor parts were fabricated using 3D printers (Mars 3 and Mars 5 Ultra, ELEGOO) and 3D-printing resins (TR300 by Phrozen, RESINMAGIC 3D by AS). After the 3D-printing process, jigs and parts were cleaned three times by ultrasonic rinsing using 2-propanol, dried under blown air, and then post-cured in a UV curing chamber (EeezCure 180, XYZprinting).

Only the CAD data for electrodes for the ferroelectric motors were transferred to a machining center and a wire discharge machining machine to prepare metal parts of SUS 303. (Processing was performed by a party outside the research team).

Experimental setup for TEF measurements

Plastic jigs were fabricated as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. They feature a cubic DIO/DIO-CN reservoir with a volume of 1000 mm3 (10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm). In addition, each jig has grooves of 0.55 mm thickness of various lengths (1.0, 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mm) designed to fix stainless steel rulers that served both as electrodes and as height references. The jigs also have a thin slit to hold a thermocouple (180 µm in thickness, ST-51SC-100-C, RKC Instrument) connected to a temperature controller (TJA-550K, AS ONE), enabling direct temperature monitoring of the ferroelectric fluid. The rulers (15 mm × 0.5 mm × 175 mm, S-15, Lion Office Products), with a thickness of only 0.5 mm, mitigate potential turbulence of the fluid, which could affect the experimental outcomes. To ensure the uniformity of the electric field distribution between the electrodes, only the thin sections of the electrodes were exposed; the remaining surfaces were masked with polyimide tape (50 µm thick, Teraoka). The top parts of the electrodes were further secured via 3D-printed double-hole slits to maintain parallel alignment (Supplementary Fig. 2). The rulers and thermocouple were inserted into the jig (Supplementary Fig. 3). DIO/DIO-CN preheated (75 °C) in an inert oven (DN410I, Yamato Scientific) was dropped into the reservoir using a pipette. The assembly was maintained at 75 °C in the inert oven for approximately 15 min before being transferred into a preheated (60 °C) heating cabinet (VS-470WH 25 L, Versos). The thermocouple was connected to the device to measure the DIO/DIO-CN temperature. The cabinet temperature was slowly reduced manually until both the cabinet and the DIO/DIO-CN stabilized between 46 and 48 °C. During the temperature control, the rulers were connected to a function generator (WF1943B, NF) and a power amplifier (HSA4051, NF) for voltage application. A video camera (iPhone 12 Pro, Apple) was mounted onto a tripod to record the experiments.

General procedure for TEF measurements

After the experiment recording started, a DC voltage was applied to the device and was manually increased at a certain voltage interval. The height of the liquid surface at each applied voltage was measured from the corresponding video image. An example of a screenshot of the recorded experiment is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. When the ferroelectric fluid stopped its elevation at each applied voltage, the height was read at the lowest position of the liquid bridge (approximately in the middle of the electrodes) using the rulers as height references. The voltage intervals were as follows: 2 V for an interelectrode distance of 1.0 mm, 5–10 V for 2.5 and 5.0 mm, and 10 V for 10 mm. The recorded videos are shown in Supplementary Videos 2 and 4–6. The height of the silicone oil and 5CB was measured at 80 V at an interelectrode distance of 2.5 mm; the corresponding videos are shown in Supplementary Video 3. Note that the zero-point height is 1 cm in reference to the ruler. A series of experiments was carried out five times, and the mean and standard deviation were calculated.

Experimental setup for current and polarization measurements

For the simultaneous measurement of current and polarization with TEF, we added a current amplifier to the experimental setup for the aforementioned TEF measurements. Here, we inserted a 1:1000 shunt circuit consisting of 1 Ω and 1000 Ω parallel resistors because the minimum gain of the current amplifier was 1000-fold. Once the current from the sample was attenuated to 1/1000, it was input to a programmable current amplifier (CA5351, NF) with a minimum gain of 1000-fold, allowing gain adjustment suitable for the magnitude of the measured current, and then recorded by a digital oscilloscope (DS-5414A, Iwatsu Electric).

General procedure for current and polarization measurements

An alternating voltage (triangular wave, ±1, ±2,… ±32 V, 100 Hz) was applied to the samples. Each dataset of the applied voltages and corresponding currents was recorded at a sampling rate of 20 kHz after 128 synchronous additions to reduce noise.

Experimental setup for measurements of the scalability and stability of TEF

Two jigs were fabricated using the aforementioned 3D printer. The upper jig is a resin piece with a base area of 100 mm2 (2 mm × 50 mm), which is approximately 100 times larger than the base area in the previous experiment using ruler electrodes shown in Figs. 2 and 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 5). The lower jig was equipped with a DIO/DIO-CN reservoir 52 mm in length, 3.25 mm in width, and 10 mm in depth (Supplementary Fig. 6). The jig also had cylindrical holes for heaters. A thin aluminum plate (HA0323, Hikari) was cut to prepare plate electrodes of 52 mm (plus 4 mm for fixing) in length, 0.3 mm in thickness, and 6 mm in height, with one of the sides masked with polyimide tape (Supplementary Fig. 7). One of the edges had an extra aluminum part equipped with tin-coated copper wires. These plate electrodes were fixed in grooves in the reservoir to remain parallel at an interelectrode distance of 2.5 mm, completing the assembly of the lower jig (Supplementary Fig. 8). Thermocouples for regulating the heating temperature were inserted into a thin slit in the jig and fixed by polyimide tape. The tip of each thermocouple was designed to be only 1 mm apart from the closest edge of the cylindrical hole. To the reservoir, DIO/DIO-CN maintained at 75 °C was added to entirely immerse the electrodes and was kept at the same temperature for approximately 15 min. The reservoir was then clamped to the lower part of the tensile testing machine with a 50 N load cell (EZ-SX, Shimadzu). Cylindrical heaters (100 V 30 W, HLW1032, Hakko Electric) were inserted into cylindrical holes in the reservoir, where a heat-conductive grease (13.2 W m−1 K−1, SMZ-01R, Shinwa Sangyo) was used for sufficient thermal coupling to the device. The heating temperature was set to 45 °C to reduce the DIO/DIO-CN temperature gradually. Concomitantly, the 3D-printed resin piece shown in Supplementary Fig. 5 was clamped to the tensile testing machine and kept 1 mm deep from the top of the electrodes in the reservoir. Between the resin piece and reservoir, a thermocouple was inserted to enable direct monitoring of the DIO/DIO-CN temperature. An overview of the experimental setup and a block diagram for the experiment are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9.

General procedure for measurements of the scalability and stability of TEF

The stroke of the upper clamp was set to its minimum level (stroke mode: 1.0 mm s−1 until reaching 0.1 mm, followed by holding mode) to avoid affecting the experiment before the temperatures of both the heaters and DIO/DIO-CN were stabilized at 45–46 °C. The force measurement was started at a sampling rate of 200 Hz when the slight upright displacement of the clamp occurred instantly, which caused a force fluctuation at approximately t = 0. After a waiting period of approximately 6 s to confirm the stability of the force baseline at 0 N in the absence of an applied voltage, DC 60 V was applied to the electrodes. The resultant force was measured for approximately 30 s before the voltage was turned off. The experiments were repeated five times to confirm their reproducibility.

Data processing in measurements of the scalability and stability of TEF

The five-point moving average before and after the raw data was evaluated to reduce background noise in the TEF measurement. The results of five force measurements under the aforementioned conditions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, where the raw data and the moving averages are shown in gray and red, respectively. The average force—an average of each raw force on average during 26.0–35.995 s (2000 data points)—was 0.073 N. The standard deviation of the average force was 0.00066. The coefficient of variation—the standard deviation divided by the average force—was 0.0090.

Experimental setup for a ferroelectric motor

Preheated DIO/DIO-CN (75 °C) in an inert oven (DN410I, Yamato Scientific) was injected into the ferroelectric motor using a pipette. The motor with DIO/DIO-CN was kept at 75 °C in the oven for approximately 15 min before being moved to the heating cabinet. After the motor was moved to the cabinet, the temperature was slowly reduced until it stabilized at 46–48 °C. The pulse waveform signals were generated by a 3ch function generator (FY8300S-20M, FeelTech), amplified by a 3ch piezo driver (HJPZ-0.3× 3, Matsusada Precision) with a gain of 30, and then applied to the motor under test. A digital oscilloscope (DS-5414A, Iwatsu Electric) was also used to observe the signals.
