Unraveling the mechanism of “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” of fermented sour pork via multi-omics analysis: insights from microbial community analysis, flavoromics, untargeted metabolomics and targeted metabolomics
Authors: Shijie Liu, Qian Ding, Lijun Zhao, Miaoyun Li, Yanxia Liu, Dong Liang, Yangyang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the dynamic changes of FSP in different fermentation processes were systematically explored by using multi-omics technology. Esters, alcohols and terpenoids provided fruity, wine-like, and herbal aromas for FSP, which were the main components of pleasant flavors. Fermentation was associated with the deep degradation of proteins, lipids and carbohydrates. With the extension of fermentation time, the enrichment of amino acids, peptides and fatty acid derivatives was the main contributing substance to the “tasting delicious” of FSP, and FAA in 60d FSP increased by 8.82-fold. Notably, the flavor substances of (+/−)-4-Terpineol, Phenylethyl Alcohol, 1-Octen-3-One and 1-Hepten-3-One exhibit concentration-dependent aroma characteristics and may contribute to bad flavor. At the same time, the gradual formation of Butane-1,4-diamine and lipid oxidation products may contribute to the formation of the characteristic volatile “smelling slightly off” flavor of FSP. It provided theoretical basis for the flavor regulation and industrial production of FSP.Keywords: FSP, Bacterial community succession, Volatile compounds, Non-volatile compounds, HS-SPME/GC–MS, Smelling slightly off but tasting delicious

Introduction

Fermented foods are widely appreciated for their distinctive flavors and nutritional benefits. In the fermentation process, microorganisms and endogenous enzymes catalyze complex metabolic reactions involving carbohydrates, lipids and protein, forming nutrients and complex flavor compounds (Liu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). These compounds have special smell and taste characteristics, including sourness, alcoholic notes, umami, and occasionally off-odors. Notably, many traditional fermented foods possess, to varying degrees, a characteristic “off-odor.” Fermented products with pronounced pungent or unpleasant odors are commonly found worldwide, such as fish sauce in Southeast Asia, natto in Japan, Hongeohoe in Korea and blue cheese in France (Estévez et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; X. Wang et al., 2025). Similarly, there are a large number of traditional fermented foods with the characteristics of “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” in China, such as Hainan fish tea, Fujian fish sauce, Anhui stinkfish and Guangxi fermented sour bamboo shoots (Cui et al., 2024; Du et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021). Although the smell is a little unpleasant, many traditional naturally fermented foods are popular among some consumers. Without this distinctive “off-odor,” these products would lose their unique flavor “soul” (Hu et al., 2023).

Fermented sour pork (FSP) is a traditional fermented meat product widely consumed in southwestern China, particularly in Guizhou, Hunan and Jiangxi provinces, and is well known for its characteristic “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” (Lan et al., 2026). Typically, FSP is prepared by fermented streaky pork with rice flour and seasonings in sealed earthenware jars at ambient temperature for approximately 30–60 d. Similar to other naturally fermented meat products, such as fermented ham, fermented fish, and air-dried cured meats, the flavor of FSP is shaped by the interactions among diverse microbial communities (Guo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). However, due to the prolonged and complex fermentation process of FSP, the microbial succession, as well as the formation of volatile and non-volatile flavor compounds, remain insufficiently understood. Therefore, in order to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” of FSP, it is necessary to study the changes of metabolites and microbial flora involved in the formation of FSP flavor (Zang et al., 2025).

In recent years, multi-omics approaches have emerged as powerful tools for elucidating the mechanisms of flavor formation and comprehensively characterizing the associated metabolic pathways in fermented foods (Shi et al., 2025). In the field of flavor research, techniques such as electronic nose, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), gas chromatography-olfactometry (GC-O) and headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME/GC–MS) can precisely identify the key aromatic compounds that affect the flavor characteristics in fermented foods, providing direct technical support for flavor profile analysis (Hao et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2026; Mehren et al., 2026). High-throughput sequencing techniques (such as 16S/18S/ITS amplicon sequencing) can precisely characterize the microbial community structure in the fermentation system, while metabolomics techniques can achieve qualitative and quantitative analysis of various metabolites during the fermentation process (Fabrice et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026). Zhang et al. (2025) revealed the formation and change of the flavor of grass carp with fermented distiller's grains through the methods of omics and bioinformatics based on the dynamic correlations between microbial groups and metabolites. Niu et al. (2026) constructed a multi-omics framework using UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap-MS/MS and HS-SPME-GC × GC-TOF-MS to elucidate the dynamic evolution of key flavor compounds during the roasting of Yunnan Arabica coffee. Therefore, the multi-omics method is of irreplaceable significance for clarifying the flavor profile characteristics and revealing its flavor formation mechanisms of FSP. Nevertheless, at present, there is still a lack of research on the formation of “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” flavor for fermented meat products by integrating microbial community analysis, flavoromics, untargeted metabolomics and targeted metabolomics.

Based on previous observations, we proposed two working hypotheses. First, the characteristic “slightly off” aroma may arise from the accumulation of specific odor-active volatile compounds whose sensory perception exhibits concentration-dependent effects during fermentation. Second, the desirable eating quality of fermented sour pork may result from the simultaneous accumulation of non-volatile taste-active metabolites (such as amino acids, organic acids and nucleotides), which partially compensate for or mask unpleasant odor perception through cross-modal odor–taste interactions. These hypotheses suggest that both volatile and non-volatile metabolic pathways, together with microbial succession, cooperatively determine the unique sensory property of fermented sour pork.

To test these hypotheses, FSP with different fermentation stages (0d, 30d and 60 d) made in the laboratory was taken as the research object. By integrating the insights of physical and chemical indexes, microbial community analysis, flavoromics, untargeted metabolomics and targeted metabolomics, the dynamic evolution of complex flavor profile and microbial community in FSP fermentation process was clarified. This work provides novel insights into the molecular basis underlying the “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” phenomenon in fermented sour pork, offers a theoretical foundation for flavor regulation and promotes the industrial development of FSP. Materials and methods

Materials and agents

Streaky pork, rice, white wine and other ingredients were purchased from a local Shuanghui supermarket (Zhengzhou, China).

A centrifugal column-based bacterial DNA extraction kit was purchased from Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Agarose was obtained from Biowest (France), and the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit was purchased from Omega Bio-tek (USA). Chromatographic pure methanol, chromatographic pure acetonitrile, chromatographic pure formic acid, chromatographic pure water, chromatographic pure propanol and PCR kits were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lactic acid, oxalic acid, succinic acid, and malic acid (chromatographic grade) were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. All other chemicals were of analytical grade and obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of FSP

FSP was prepared according to a previously reported method with slight modifications (Lan et al., 2026). Streaky pork was cut into pieces and mixed thoroughly with salt, rice flour and white wine. The mixture was then packed into fermentation jars and fermented at 25 °C for 60 d. Samples were collected at 0d, 30 d and 60 d, and all samples were stored at −80 °C prior to further analysis. Three independent fermentation batches were prepared for each treatment, and each batch was regarded as one biological replicate. All analyses were performed using these biological replicates.

Determination of physicochemical properties

Mixed 5 g FSP with 40 mL of 0.06 mol/L KH2PO4 solution (pH 2.6), homogenized for 3 min, and ultrasonically extracted for 8 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 10000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and diluted to 50 mL. 1 mL aliquot was filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous membrane and analyzed by HPLC. Chromatographic conditions were C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm), mobile phase A was methanol, mobile phase B was 0.1% phosphoric acid and A: B = 2:98. Column temperature was 30 °C, flow rate was 0.8 mL/min, injection volume was 20 μL and detection wavelength was 214 nm. Organic acids were quantified using the external standard method. The pH was determined according to previously published methods. Total acidity (TA) and TVB-N were determined according to Chinese standards GB 12456–2021 and GB 5009.228–2016, respectively (Guan et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026).

Microbial community analysis

Total microbial DNA was extracted from FSP at different fermentation stages using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) with barcode sequences. PCR products were purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit (China Yuhua) and quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Amplicon sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) (Gómez et al., 2026).

Volatile omics analysis

Volatile flavor compounds in FSP were analyzed using HS-SPME/GC–MS. 2 g of FSP sample was transferred to a 15 mL SPME vial, supplemented with 2 g NaCl, 6 mL deionized water, and 2-octanol (25 mg/L) as the internal standard. The sealed vial was equilibrated at 55 °C in a magnetically stirred water bath. Volatiles were adsorbed onto an SPME fiber for 40 min and thermally desorbed at 250 °C for 5 min in the GC inlet. GC separation was conducted on a DB-5MS capillary column with an inlet temperature of 250 °C and helium carrier gas flowing at 1.0 mL/min. The temperature ramp started at 40 °C, rose to 180 °C at 5 °C/min, and finally reached 220 °C at 5 °C/min. For MS detection, electron ionization (70 eV) was adopted with a 230 °C ion source; mass spectra were acquired over the m/z range of 33–450 amu. Volatile compounds were tentatively identified by comparing their mass spectra with the NIST11 spectral library together with retention index (RI) values calculated using a series of n-alkanes under the same chromatographic conditions. Volatile compounds were semi-quantified using a single internal standard, and the concentrations were expressed as ng/g equivalent of the internal standard(Ji et al., 2026).

Extraction and analysis of non-volatile metabolites

Placed 50 mg of each sample into a 2 mL centrifuge tube containing grinding beads. Metabolites were extracted with 400 μL methanol–water (4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine as an internal standard. Samples were homogenized for 6 min at −10 °C (50 Hz) and ultrasonically extracted for 30 min at 5 °C (40 kHz). After incubation at −20 °C for 30 min, samples were centrifuged at 13000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to vials for analysis. Chromatographic conditions: samples (3 μL) were separated on an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of 95% water and 5% acetonitrile (with 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B consisted of 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, and 5% water (with 0.1% formic acid). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, and the column temperature was 40 °C. Mass spectrometry conditions: data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes, with a scan range of 70–1050 m/z. Sheath gas flow rate was 50 psi, auxiliary gas flow rate was 13 psi and auxiliary gas heater temperature was 425 °C. Spray voltage was set at +3500 V (positive mode) and − 3500 V (negative mode), and the capillary temperature was 325 °C. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching the accurate mass, isotope distribution and MS/MS fragmentation spectra against public metabolite databases. According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI), all metabolite identifications reported in this study should be regarded as putatively annotated compounds (MSI Level 2) unless confirmed with authentic reference standards (Baek et al., 2025).

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Approximately 20 mg sample from each fermentation stage was mixed with 141 μL water, 100 μL of 0.15% DOC solution, and 4 μL of internal standard solution (100 μg/mL; Lys-d4, Try-d5, Gln-d4). The mixture was ultrasonicated at 5 °C (40 kHz) for 10 min, followed by the addition of 5 μL of 10 M trichloroacetic acid. After freezing for 10 min, the samples were centrifuged at 14000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. A 25 μL aliquot of supernatant was diluted with 375 μL water and filtered through a 0.2 μm PTFE membrane. LC-ESI-MS/MS (UHPLC-Qtrap) was used for qualitative and quantitative detection of the target substance in the sample. The specific parameters were as follows: chromatographic conditions: advanceBio MS Spent Media column (2.1 × 50 mm, 2.7 μm), column temperature 40 °C, injection volume 1 μL. The mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate (95% water), and the mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate (95% acetonitrile). Mass spectrometry conditions: SCIEX QTRAP 6500+, operated in both positive and negative ion modes; CUR, 35; CAD, medium; IS, +5500/−4500 V; TEM, 550 °C; GS1, 50; GS2, 50 (Horie et al., 2020). Results and discussion

Physical and chemical index analysis

The dynamic changes in physical and chemical indexes of FSP at three fermentation stages were determined and analyzed, the results were presented in Table 1. The pH value decreased continuously with the progression of fermentation and the lower pH environment could not only effectively inhibit the growth and reproduction of spoilage microorganisms and improve the microbial safety of products. Although the TVB-N value of FSP increased with the extension of fermentation time, it remained far below the safety limit (TVB-N ≤ 15 mg/100 g) at 60 d of fermentation, ensuring the edible safety of the product (Liu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). The total acid content increased continuously and remarkably during fermentation. Lactic acid was the predominant organic acid throughout the fermentation process, with its content rising rapidly from 4.81 ± 0.23 mg/g at 0 d to 25.42 ± 0.68 mg/g at 60 d. By contrast, the contents of malic acid, succinic acid and oxalic acid remained at relatively low levels throughout the whole period. To sum up, during the fermentation process, the pH value of FSP decreased continuously, the TVB-N value was in a safe range and a large number of organic acids mainly lactic acid accumulated, which provided an important theoretical basis for the quality control of FSP.

Bacterial community succession

A Venn diagram was used to assess the shared and unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) in FSP. A total of 25 OTUs were common across all three fermentation stages, while the numbers of unique OTUs at 0d, 30 d and 60 d were 656, 595 and 9, respectively, indicating a progressive reduction in microbial diversity during fermentation. PCoA based on Bray–Curtis distance and Jaccard similarity were shown in Fig. 2(B) and (C)，the FSP sample points in the three fermentation stages were relatively scattered, suggesting significant differences in bacterial community structures over time. The diversity and relative abundance of bacteria at the phylum level were shown in Fig. 2(D), the abundance of FSP in three fermentation stages were quite different, and the abundance of Firmicutes is the highest, especially in 60d FSP samples, where it became the most dominant phylum (99.49%). The diversity and relative abundance of bacteria at the genus level were shown in Fig.2(E), the dominant genera in 0d FSP were Pandoraea, Serratia, Lactobacillus and Methylobacillus. In 30 d FSP, the number of Lactobacillus increased to 77.28% and new dominant genera such as Lactococcus (3.16%) appeared. When the fermentation reached 60 d, Lactobacillus (63.30%) and Pediococcus (33.68%) became the dominant genera in FSP. Thus, Lactobacillus and Pediococcus were identified as the core dominant genera throughout FSP fermentation. According to the results of high-throughput sequencing, the bacterial community composition was screened at the taxonomic level and 12 species were obtained, as shown in Table S1. In the 30 d FSP, Latilactobacillus sakei was dominant species, with a relative abundance of 73.86%. The dominant species in 60 d FSP evolved intoLactiplantibacillus plantarum and Lentilactobacillus garvieae, with relative abundance of 32.53% and 46.79%, respectively. Lactic acid bacteria were dominant in the late stage of FSP fermentation, mainly Lactobacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus. In order to find the species with significant differences in abundance among different groups, we conducted LEfSe analysis on the microorganisms of FSP samples in three fermentation stages and only showed the different microorganisms that met the significance threshold of linear discriminant analysis greater than 4.0. At the same time, we used the histogram of linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to analyze LEfSe information, as shown in Fig. S1. The results showed that the main microbial types in the fermentation process of FSP were Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus sakei, Pediococcus pentosaceus, Lactobacillus graminis, Serratia liquefaciens and Klebsiella aerogenes.

Analysis of volatile substances

Because white wine was incorporated before fermentation, the volatile profile of the 0 d samples represents both the intrinsic meat volatiles and ingredient-derived aroma compounds. Therefore, the interpretation of fermentation effects was based primarily on the differences between fermented samples and the corresponding 0 d control rather than on the mere presence of individual volatile compounds. The statistical chart of volatile substances classification of FSP in three fermentation stages was shown in Fig. 3(A), the major categories include esters (22.52%), organic heterocyclic compounds (13.89%), terpenoids (10.90%), ketones (10.25%), alcohols (8.24%) and hydrocarbons (7.92%). In Fig. 3(B), the relative contents of various volatile substances have changed significantly with the progress of fermentation, and ester substances have increased significantly in the middle and late stage of fermentation (30–60 d). Most of these substances have fruit fragrance and flower fragrance, and they are the main contributors to the pleasant aroma of FSP. Sulfur-containing/nitrogen-containing compounds and organic heterocyclic substances gradually accumulate with the extension of fermentation time. Most of these substances have pungent odor, and their content changes directly mediate the formation of the “smelling slightly off” characteristics of FSP.

The predicted odor characteristics radar plot in Fig. 3(C) intuitively illustrated the differences in flavor profiles across fermentation stages, odor descriptors assigned to the identified volatile compounds were obtained from published odor databases (‌NIST mass spectrometry library). These descriptors were used only to assist the interpretation of volatile profiles and represent predicted odor characteristics rather than results from sensory evaluation. 0 d FSP exhibited a relatively simple profile dominated by “fatty” and “earthy” notes. The flavor profiles of 30 d FSP were mainly “sweet”, “citrus” and “Herbal” notes. The flavor profile of 60 d is mainly “sweet”, “fruity”, “minty” and “musty”. Although this study provides comprehensive chemical evidence supporting the characteristic flavor of fermented sour pork, formal sensory validation using trained descriptive panels or consumer acceptance tests was not performed. Therefore, the sensory interpretation of “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” should be regarded as being inferred from integrated flavoromics and metabolomics evidence rather than directly confirmed by human sensory evaluation. Future studies incorporating quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) and consumer testing will further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. Compared with the 0 d FSP, the different volatile substances in the 30 d and 60 d FSP were mainly up-regulated and the amount and difference range of up-regulated substances increase significantly with the fermentation time. The OPLS-DA scores and OPLS-DA permutation test chart were shown in Fig. 3(F)-(G) and Fig.S2, FSP in different fermentation stages was completely separated in two-dimensional space, with high aggregation within groups and significant differences between groups. The key flavor markers of FSP in three fermentation stages were presented in Fig. 4(H) and (I). In 30 d FSP, VIP analysis identified ethanol, (±)-linalool, (Z, Z)-limonene, and phenylethyl alcohol as core differential volatile compounds. Among them, ethanol exhibited the highest VIP value and was significantly upregulated during fermentation, contributing a mild alcoholic aroma (Hu et al., 2024). Meanwhile, terpenoids such as linalool, limonene, and eucalyptol were also significantly up-regulated. These substances usually have floral, fruity and herbal aromas, which are important components of the fresh and pleasant aroma of sour meat and significantly enhance the flavor layering of FSP (R. Wang et al., 2025). As fermentation progressed to 60 d, the types and VIP value distribution of key volatile substances had obviously evolved, and ethanol, acetic acid, esters and some fatty acid derivatives became the dominant markers. It is worth noting that the VIP value of ethanol remained high, and the VIP values of acetic acid and other organic acids increased significantly (Lan et al., 2025). These substances not only contributed to the sour taste characteristics of FSP, but also were the key precursors for the synthesis of esters, further enhancing the fruit and flower fragrance of FSP.

The correlation analysis between sensory flavor characteristics and flavor substances of 30d FSP vs 0d FSP based on the first-class classification of HMDB were shown in Fig.4(A). Ethanol had a strong correlation with the flavor attributes of “sweet”, “strong”, “alcoholic”, “wine” and “medicine”, and it was the core contribution to the early wine flavor characteristics. Ethyl acetate and terpenoids (such as (+/−)-Linalool and (+/−)-Limonene) were mainly associated with pleasant flavor attributes such as “fruity”, “orange” and “lemon”. Benzaldehyde and (+/−)-4-Terpineol enriched the flavor level of FSP through the association with “citrus” and “woody”. The correlation analysis between sensory flavor characteristics and flavor substances of 60d FSP vs 0d FSP based on the first-class classification of HMDB were shown in Fig. 4(B). The correlation strength between alcohol, ethyl acetate and “wine”, “fruity” and “sweet” was further enhanced, which became the core support for the pleasant flavor of FSP. At the same time, the correlation between (+/−)-4-Terpineol and phenylethyl alcohol and bad flavor attributes such as “musty”, “earth” and “medicinal” was significantly enhanced. Ethanol, esters, terpenoids and other substances formed the pleasant flavor skeleton of FSP through the association with the flavors of fruit and wine, while the association of high concentrations of (+/−)-4-Terpineol and phenylethyl alcohol with the flavors of mildew and earthy smell may produce unpleasant odor. The dynamic balance of the two substances together may shape the “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” flavor characteristics of FSP. Although ROAV analysis indicated that (+/−)-4-Terpineol, phenylethyl alcohol, 1-octen-3-one and 1-hepten-3-one were important contributors to the aroma profile, the present study did not determine their absolute concentrations or experimentally verify concentration-dependent sensory transitions. Therefore, the future studies combining absolute quantification, aroma recombination and omission experiments will be required to validate this mechanism.

Fig. 4(C)-(D) were ROAV line charts and stacked area charts, respectively, which intuitively showed the changing rules of core flavor active substances in different fermentation stages. Table S2 presented the OAV and ACI results of volatile compounds during different fermentation stages. The results showed that ROAV values of (+/−)-Linalool, Ethyl 2-Methylbutyrate, Ethyl Isovalerate and Ethyl Acetate increased significantly with the fermentation process, and became the key active components driving flavor change. (+/−)-Linalool, as an important terpenoid aroma substance, was significantly enriched in the fermentation process, with its fresh floral and citrus fruit aroma characteristics (Zhao et al., 2026). Ethyl 2-Methylbutyrate had a strong fresh fruit aroma, accompanied by a slight fermented wine aroma, mainly the sweet aroma of ripe strawberries, pineapples and apples, which was fresh and penetrating. These substances could effectively improve the fishy smell of raw meat and enhance the layering and pleasure of flavor (Rocío et al., 2018). 1-Octen-3-One showed a trend of increasing at first and then decreasing with the fermentation time. The high concentration of 1-Octen-3-One in 30d FSP increased the characteristics of metallic flavor and earthy flavor, while the low concentration of 1-Octen-3-One in 60d FSP showed the characteristic mushroom flavor and meat flavor (Han et al., 2026). 1-Hepten-3-One also releases earthy and metallic odor, but it kept decreasing with the fermentation time (Li et al., 2025). The bubble diagram of KEGG pathway topology analysis were shown in Fig. 4(E) and Fig. 5(F), highlighting the key metabolic pathways potentially involved in flavor substance formation during different fermentation stages. When FSP was fermented for 30 d, toluene degradation, caprolactam degradation, aminobenzoate degradation and pyruvate metabolism were the key pathways, suggesting that the metabolism of aromatic compounds and sugar was significantly remodeled in the early stage of fermentation. When FSP was fermented for 60 d, toluene degradation, ethylbenzene degradation, biosynthesis of diverse alkaloids, limonene degradation and monoterpenoid biosynthesis became the core pathways, reflecting the enhanced metabolism of flavor precursors such as terpenoids and alkaloids in the later stage of fermentation.

Analysis of non-volatile substances

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

The dynamic changes of non-volatile substances in FSP during fermentation were analyzed by untargeted metabolomics. PLS-DA scores were shown in Fig. 5(A) and 6(B). The samples in the three stages were completely separated in two-dimensional space and the samples in the group were highly aggregated, with good stability and prediction ability. Furthermore, the common and specific characteristics of metabolites in different fermentation stages were analyzed by Venn diagram, and the results were shown in Fig. 5(C). A total of 1935 kinds of differential metabolites were detected in the three stages, and there were 30 kinds (1.51%), 139 kinds (7.09%) and 285 kinds (14.59%) of specific metabolites were detected in the 0 d, 30 d and 60 d stages, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(D), the annotation results of three-level classification based on HMDB database showed that non-volatile metabolites in FSP cover multiple categories, among which amino acids, peptides and analogues account for up to 24.18%, followed by not Available, 11.68%, fatty acids and conjugates (4.37%) and carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates (4.07%), as well as a variety of secondary metabolites such as esters, flavonoids, terpenoids and phenylpropionic acids, which indicated that the degradation and transformation of protein, lipids and carbohydrates during fermentation were metabolomics changes. As shown in Fig. 5(E), the results of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis show that the differential metabolites were mainly enriched in the metabolic pathway, among which amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism were the most abundant. It was further confirmed that the core metabolic activities such as protein hydrolysis, lipid oxidation and glycolysis were dominant in the fermentation process, and the dynamic changes of these pathways were directly related to the process of FSP flavor substance generation, flavor substance accumulation and quality improvement.

With the prolongation of fermentation time, the number of up-regulated metabolites increased from 543 to 603, indicating that the synthesis and accumulation of metabolites dominated the metabolic variation during fermentation, which was directly associated with the enrichment of flavor substances in the middle and late fermentation stages. Fig. 6(C) and 6(D) visually analyzed the dynamic change characteristics of the core differential metabolites of 30 d FSP and 60 d FSP compared with 0 d FSP by radar chart. Compared with the 0 d control group, most of the differential metabolites of 30 d FSP showed a significant upward trend, especially amino acids and peptide metabolites (such as Asp-Tyr-Leu, Valylglutamic acid, Arg-Leu-Phe), which were not only the direct contributors to the umami and mellow taste of FSP, but also the important precursors of subsequent flavor substances. Alpha-(1,2-Dihydroxyethyl)-1,2,3,4-tetrahydro-7-hydroxy-9-methoxy-3,4-dioxocyclopenta[c][1]benzopyran-6-acetaldehyde was a coumarin derivative, which mainly exerts antioxidant and flavor-modulating effects in FSP. Meanwhile, the up-regulation of lipid metabolism-related products (such as 3-oxodecanoic acid and 1-Hydroxyacorenone) indicated the initiation of early lipid oxidation, laying a material foundation for the subsequent generation of volatile aldehydes and ketones. With the development of fermentation to 60 d, the categories and up-regulation amplitudes of amino acid metabolites (such as Asp-Tyr-Leu, Prolylphenylalanine, Leu -Asp-Phe, Tryptophyl-Proline, Ile-His-Leu, Arg-Leu-Phe, Gly-Ile-Phe and Thr-Val-Val) were significantly enhanced, indicating that protein hydrolysis reached its peak, and the formation of a large number of free amino acids and small peptides directly enhanced the “tasting delicious” characteristics of FSP. At the same time, the continuous up-regulation of lipid metabolites (such as 1-Hydroxyacorenone may contribute to the further accumulation of fat oxidation products, which could generate aldehyde and acid volatile components through subsequent reactions, enriching the flavor level of sour meat. It is worth noting that the abundance of amine compounds (such as Butane-1,4-diamine) increased significantly during fermentation. This increase was may be related to the development of the characteristic “smelling slightly off” aroma as fermentation progressed (Liang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). Although butane-1,1-diamine accumulated significantly during fermentation, the present study did not directly evaluate its aroma activity using GC–O, AEDA or aroma recombination approaches. Therefore, its contribution to the characteristic “slightly off” odor should be regarded as a potential association inferred from metabolomic evidence rather than direct sensory validation.

The clustering heatmaps of differential metabolites at different fermentation stages are displayed in Fig. 6(E) and 6(F), clearly presenting the expression patterns of differential metabolites. All the differential metabolites could be divided into several expression modules, in which amino acids were up-regulated in the whole fermentation process and the enrichment degree was the highest in the 60 d stage, which was consistent with the accumulation law of free amino acids produced by protein hydrolysis. These amino acids not only directly endow FSP with umami, sweet and bitter taste attributes, but also participate in decarboxylation and deamination reactions to produce volatile amines and aldehydes, which were closely related to the formation of the “smelling slightly off but tasting delicious” flavor of FSP. The VIP value plots of different metabolites of FSP in different fermentation stages were shown in Fig. 6(G) and 6(H). The differential metabolites with high VIP value in 30 d FSP mainly included lipid metabolites (such as 4-Hydroxybutyric acid), glycosides and some primary metabolites, reflecting the start of basic metabolic activities such as fat oxidation and carbohydrate degradation in the early stage of fermentation. With the development of fermentation to 60 d, the core differential metabolites with high VIP value gradually shifted to amino acids and amines, such as Tryptophyl-Threonine, Gamma-Glutamylleucine, Methyllysine, etc., which indicated that protein hydrolysis and amino acid metabolism became the core factors driving the metabolomic differences in the middle and late stage of fermentation. As shown in Fig.S3 of KEGG topology analysis, tyrosine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism served as the core perturbed pathways throughout the entire fermentation process of FSP. This indicated that amino acid catabolism and lipid oxidation/hydrolysis were the pivotal metabolic processes driving flavor formation in FSP.

The content of free amino acids (FAA) and TAV of FSP at different fermentation stages was shown in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, the total free amino acid (TAA) content increased significantly throughout fermentation (p < 0.05), rising from 289.33 mg/g at 0 d to 2551.96 mg/g at 60 d (8.82-fold), indicating extensive protein degradation during fermentation. However, changes in amino acid concentration alone cannot accurately reflect their sensory contribution. Among the umami amino acids, glutamic acid (Glu) exhibited the highest TAV throughout fermentation, increasing dramatically from 0.73 at 0 d to 11.31 at 60 d. Aspartic acid (Asp) also exceeded the taste threshold after fermentation (TAV = 1.16). These results indicate that Glu, together with Asp, became the dominant contributors to the characteristic umami taste of fermented sour meat. In particular, the remarkably high TAV of Glu suggests that it plays a pivotal role in improving taste quality after prolonged fermentation. Sweet amino acids also accumulated substantially during fermentation. Alanine (Ala) showed the greatest sensory contribution among the sweet amino acids, with its TAV increasing from 0.63 to 3.23, indicating that Ala became an important contributor to sweetness after 60 d of fermentation. Although glycine (Gly) increased markedly in concentration, its TAV remained below 1, suggesting only a limited direct contribution to sweetness. Bitter amino acids generally accumulated during fermentation, and several exceeded their taste thresholds at 60 d. Methionine (Met) exhibited the highest TAV (5.31), followed by histidine (His, 4.89), lysine (Lys, 3.18), isoleucine (Ile, 2.01), arginine (Arg, 1.55), leucine (Leu, 1.42) and valine (Val, 1.22). Although several bitter amino acids reached TAV values greater than 1, the final product did not exhibit excessive bitterness, suggesting that their sensory perception was likely moderated by the synergistic enhancement of umami compounds, sweetness, organic acids, and other taste-active metabolites. Overall, The differential accumulation of various amino acids jointly constructed the complex taste system of FSP characterized by “umami (Glu/Asp)-sweet (Ala/Gly)-slight bitterness (Met/His/Lys)”, rather than a simple superposition of single taste attributes.

Conclusion

Based on the joint analysis of untargeted metabolomics and targeted metabolomics, this study systematically expounded the characteristic substances and formation rules of the flavor of FSP. During fermentation, lactic acid accumulated continuously while TVB-N remained within safe limits. Esters, alcohols and terpenoids (such as linalool, ethyl 2-methylbutyrate, ethyl isovalerate) contributed fruity, wine and herbal notes, forming the core desirable flavor profile. Free amino acids in 60 d fermented products increased by 8.82-fold, forming a compound flavor of “umami (Glu/Asp)-sweet (Ala/Gly)-slight bitterness (Met/His/Lys)”. Minor off-notes were primarily derived from (+/−)-4-terpineol, phenylethyl alcohol, 1-octen-3-one and 1-hepten-3-one, which have been reported to exhibit concentration-dependent sensory characteristics in previous studies. Butane-1,4-diamine and lipid oxidation products further intensified the typical subtle off-flavor of sour meat. This work preliminarily elucidates the molecular basis of FSP flavor formation via multi-omics strategies and delivers critical theoretical guidance for precise flavor modulation and standardized industrial manufacturing of FSP. In the future, studies involving several independent fermentation batches will be conducted to further verify the repeatability of the observed microbial and metabolic changes, and this mechanism needs to be verified by combining absolute quantification, aroma recombination and omission experiments.
