Living on behalf of a lost only child: a unique grief coping strategy for China's shidu parents
Authors: Yudi Zhang, Wenyi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Bereaved parents face substantial mental health risks. In addition to seeking help from mental health professionals, bereaved parents often adopt their own grief coping strategies. Among these, some Chinese parents who have lost their only child (shidu parents) adopt a unique, culturally shaped coping strategy known as ‘living on behalf of the child.’Methods: A purposive sampling method was used in this study to invite shidu parents who were living on behalf of the child to participate in interviews. In total, 10 interviewees were included in the analysis; specifically, 8 participated by telephone, and 2 participated face-to-face. A thematic analysis was performed to code the data and extract themes.Results: For China's shidu parents, living on behalf of the child refers to doing what the child was doing when the child was still alive, doing things that the child wanted to do but had not been able to do, fulfilling the child's unmet responsibilities, maintaining relationships with the child's friends, and experiencing life from the child's perspective. This approach supports shidu parents by helping them regain a sense of purpose in life, improve their emotional stability, and increase their level of interpersonal support; however, it may also increase their level of life stress.Conclusion: As a culturally unique grief coping strategy, living on behalf of the child is practiced among shidu parents as they adjust part of their identity from being parents to being children. This strategy facilitates meaning reconstruction among shidu parents. The effects of this strategy on shidu parents are mainly positive. However, some negative effects can also occur, which makes finding an appropriate balance necessary.KEYWORDS: Living on behalf of a lost only child, grief coping strategies, bereaved parents, parents who have lost an only child, shidu parents, meaning reconstruction, Chinese culture

Introduction

In the United States, approximately 60,000 people under the age of 25 years die each year (Agha et al., 2025). In China, the one-child policy that spanned from 1980 to 2016 created a large population of parents who have lost their only child (shidu parents). Demographers estimated that in 2020, China had 2.888 million shidu mothers aged 50 years and above, and this number is expected to continue to increase (Liu & Wang, 2024). The death of a child initiates a grief process for the parents and puts their mental health at great risk. Many studies have shown that parents who have lost a child face substantial mental health risks. For example, the likelihood of these parents being hospitalised for mental illness increases significantly (Li et al., 2005), and they have a higher risk of developing mental illness than those who have lost other relatives (spouses, parents, or siblings) do (Guldin et al., 2017). These negative effects can persist for a prolonged period of time (Rogers et al., 2008). Several surveys on shidu parents in China have also shown that they have a higher risk of depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder, and prolonged grief disorder (Ye et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2022; Zhang & Jia, 2019).

Faced with the erosion of self-worth imposed by the grieving process, some bereaved parents seek professional psychological help, but many attempt to cope on their own for a number of reasons, for example, because ‘discussing the pain of losing a child is too distressing’ and ‘finding accessible professional support is a struggle’ (Lichtenthal et al., 2015). Some coping strategies, such as engaging in physical activity (Williams et al., 2021), seeking positive interpersonal support (Zhou et al., 2022), channelling energy into work (Schoonover et al., 2022), and self-healing by helping others (Huo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026), are universal and adopted by bereaved parents across cultural backgrounds. Additionally, bereaved parents implement some unique, culturally specific coping strategies. For example, Buddhist parents who have lost a child can achieve a quiet and peaceful state by chanting Buddhist scriptures, whereas Christians find psychological support through prayer (He & Wang, 2017). Most bereaved parents in Sweden regard nature as an important resource, which reflects the cultural characteristic of a high attachment to nature among northern European societies (Ahmadi & Zandi, 2021). In Ghana, parenting is viewed as a collective effort, and parents who have lost a child may seek support from the community (e.g. companionship and conversations) (Asare et al., 2025). These unique grief coping methods help bereaved parents draw strength and resources from their culture and can effectively assist them in managing their grief. In fact, we discovered a unique cultural approach to coping with grief, namely, ‘living on behalf of the child,’ which means that shidu parents assume the child's role themselves and continue the child's life at an imaginary level. To our knowledge, this grief coping strategy has not been discussed in the literature, which has resulted in a research gap.

Living on behalf of the child reflects both a sustaining anchor for shidu parents to continue living and their continuing bonds with the deceased child, which closely aligns with meaning reconstruction theory. This theory posits that meaning reconstruction among bereaved individuals comprises three dimensions: sense-making, benefit-finding, and identity change. Adaptive continuing bonds with the deceased child and the practice of living on behalf of the child may correspond to benefit-finding and identity change, respectively (Gillies & Neimeyer, 2006; Neimeyer, 2001). This study explores how living on behalf of the child, as a culturally distinctive grief coping strategy among China’s shidu parents, can effectively and practically facilitate a reconstruction of their meaning in life.

To explore this grief coping strategy among Chinese shidu parents, this study attempts to answer the following two questions: (1) How is this strategy expressed by China's shidu parents who adopt this strategy? (2) What are the effects of practicing this strategy, including both potential positive and negative effects, on China's shidu parents? This study interprets the findings within the framework of meaning reconstruction theory and further examines how Chinese culture has shaped this unique strategy for coping with grief. An in-depth interview method was implemented in this study, as it enables thick descriptions of phenomena and helps researchers understand the rich, contextualised details of this coping strategy (Polit & Beck, 2010; Ponterotto, 2006). In this way, this method is suitable for addressing the questions posed in this study. This research not only enhances the understanding of culturally adaptive grief but also offers practical implications for providing aid to shidu parents in China.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to invite shidu parents who practiced living on behalf of the child to participate in interviews. The inclusion criteria for the parents in this study were as follows: (1) their only child had died and (2) they had applied the mindset of living on behalf of the child, which significantly influenced their life choices and lifestyles. The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) less than one year had passed since their child’s death and (2) no quiet, undisturbed space for conducting telephone interviews was available, or face-to-face interviews could not be conducted because of time or distance. From November 2024 to February 2025, we recruited participants through the following two channels: (1) recruitment posters for participants were posted on a WeChat public account dedicated to the support of shidu parents, whose followers were largely shidu parents, and (2) shidu parents with whom the researchers had interacted over several years were contacted. In the end, 10 people were invited to participate in the interviews (8 via telephone and 2 in person). Additional invitations for new participants were terminated after data saturation, i.e. the information provided by the 9th and 10th interviewees did not generate any new codes.

Among the 10 participants, 3 were male and 7 were female, and their age ranged from 56–75 years, with an average age of 65.2 years. Their duration of shidu was between 2 and 25 years, with an average of 12.2 years; 8 people had lost male children, while 2 had lost female children. Data collection

All of the interviews were conducted by the second author (WZ). For the 8 participants who participated in telephone interviews, the interviewer arranged the appointments and reminded the participants that they should be in a quiet environment that was free of disruption during the interview. For the 2 in-person interviews, the interviewer travelled to the participants’ locations (Tianjin and Shanghai). The researchers prepared an interview outline in advance and used it as an important reference during the interviews. The main questions in the outline were the following: (1) What does living on behalf of the child mean to you? (2) Please describe how you came to adopt the idea of living on behalf of the child. (3) Which of your life choices and lifestyle patterns reflect living on behalf of the child? (4) What role do you think living on behalf of the child plays in your grieving process? (5) What challenges and difficulties have you encountered in life while living on behalf of the child? (6) Have your concepts or behaviours regarding living on behalf of the child changed over time? (7) What are your expectations or plans for the future?

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Science and Technology Beijing (No. 2022-1-106). All of the interviewees signed an informed consent form, which included the identities and contact information of the researchers, the purpose of the study, the participation format (interviews), and the potential risks (primarily the possibility of experiencing negative emotions when discussing the experience of loss). The consent form also emphasised that participation was voluntary and that participant privacy would be protected (through participant anonymity and confidential data storage). During the interview, if the interviewer detected that the interviewee was experiencing a change in mood, then the interviewer would ask the interviewee whether a break was needed. In all of the interviews conducted for this study, no serious negative emotions were triggered.

Data analysis

In this study, the thematic analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006, 2013) was used for data analysis. The specific steps were as follows. (1) Becoming familiar with the data: An immersed reading of the interview transcripts was performed to become familiar with the data as a whole, and the initial ideas were recorded. (2) Generating initial codes: A section-by-section reading of the interview data was conducted, and the smallest meaningful units related to the topic of living on behalf of the child were identified. (3) Searching for themes: The relevant codes were then classified into candidate themes, and each candidate theme was named, whereby 2 candidate themes emerged, i.e. the concrete manifestations of living on behalf of the child and their impact on shidu parents. (4) Reviewing the themes: The clarity of the theme framework, the degree of overlap among the subthemes, and the overall fit of all themes and subthemes with the topic of living on behalf of the child were examined in this study. In this step, we found an overlap between two subthemes, namely, ‘fulfilling the child's unmet responsibilities’ and ‘contributing to society on behalf of the child.’ In China’s collectivist culture, contributing to society is widely regarded as an individual’s responsibility; therefore, these two subthemes were combined into the theme ‘fulfilling the child's unmet responsibilities.’ (5) Defining and naming the themes: After the first two steps were completed, two themes and nine subthemes were generated. (6) Producing the report: The results of the data analysis were presented in a logical manner, and representative sentences were extracted from the interview data and embedded into the description of the results.

The following measurements were taken to ensure the trustworthiness of the study. (1) Since accurate transcription is essential for maintaining data analysis quality (Steinke, 2004), all of the transcripts used in this study were carefully reviewed to ensure their accuracy. (2) Self-reflection was maintained throughout the data analysis. Specifically, the possible influence of researchers’ biases and assumptions on data analysis was considered, and an open attitude toward alternative approaches to the formation of codes, subthemes, and themes was maintained. (3) NVivo12 (QSR, 2020) was used to analyze the data, which made the entire process of data analysis transparent and traceable. (4) Peer debriefing was used to invite academic peers who were engaged in grief research to review the research process and results and provide constructive feedback.

Results

Concrete manifestations of living on behalf of the child

The practice of living on behalf of the child by shidu parents is reflected in five main aspects, namely, doing what the child was doing when the child was still alive, doing things that the child wanted to do but had not been able to do, fulfilling the child's unmet responsibilities, maintaining relationships with the child's friends, and experiencing life from the child's perspective.

Doing what the child was doing when the child was still alive

This aspect includes activities in which these children invested much time and effort during their lifetime. For example, S10's child enjoyed participating in volunteer activities when he was alive. After his death, S10 became actively involved in various volunteer initiatives. She said,

My son used to volunteer a lot back then. Why did he volunteer? What exactly is volunteering? I didn’t know. I kept criticizing him: ‘Just focus on your studies! Why bother volunteering?’ Later (after he passed away), I thought about the things that he hadn't finished, and that’s when I thought about volunteering.

S8's child participated in disability advocacy during his lifetime. After the child’s passing, S8 assumed the mission of disability advocacy from the child. He said, ‘My son had a stronger sense of social responsibility than we do, and he has helped many people with disabilities … Now, we are continuing the work our child left unfinished.’ When helping various disabled people, S8 often thought that if his son was still alive, he would certainly be helping these people.

Doing things that the child wanted to do but had not been able to do

These activities represent the child's wishes during their lifetime, which remained unfulfilled when the child passed away. Shidu parents strive to fulfil these dreams. Before his passing, S3's child mentioned wanting to go to Inner Mongolia (an autonomous region of China) and to plant trees and contribute to combating desertification in China. To realise her child's wishes, S3 founded a nonprofit organisation that was mainly aimed at afforestation. She said,

For me, although my child is no longer here, what he wanted to do is still here. The desire to go to Inner Mongolia to plant trees has given me a new purpose in life. I quit my job and started an organization called ‘Greenlife.’

S1's child was an entrepreneur. Before passing away, his desires were to grow and strengthen his company and to buy a nice house. After her child passed away, S1 considered adopting the completion of these tasks as her motivation to live. She said, ‘I’m doing my best to fulfill everything my son left unfinished … I bought a house … The market's not great right now, but once it picks up a bit, I will get his company back on its feet … Because he had these unfulfilled dreams, that is what keeps me going no matter how tough things get.’

Fulfilling the child's unmet responsibilities

This aspect includes the tasks that the children were expected to undertake according to social norms or were encouraged to perform based on social morality. Shidu parents actively assume these responsibilities, including their family obligations, moral duties toward others, and contributions to society, on behalf of their deceased children. S5 repaid the debts owed by her child and took on the responsibility of caring for her granddaughter by tending to her daily needs like a parent and planning for her marriage dowry. Before her death, S6’s daughter accepted donations from others because she could not afford medical treatment. S6 believed that although her child had passed away, she wanted to live her own life well so that the donors did not believe that they had made donations in vain. This became her way of repaying those who had helped her on behalf of her child. S6 said, ‘The child's illness was very costly, so the company leadership organised a fundraising drive for us. In total, they raised more than 100,000 yuan. We must live well, and that is how we repay everyone's kindness.’ After S2 recovered from the intense concern of losing her child, she began to help other shidu parents by providing emotional support and offering care when needed. S2 explained that her helpful behaviour contributes to society on behalf of her child. She said, ‘I’ve raised my child to adulthood, and if he hadn't died, then he would have contributed to our society. Although I don’t possess his extraordinary abilities, I, too, will contribute what little I can.’

Maintaining relationships with the child’s friends

Some shidu parents choose to cope with the loss of their children by continuing interactions with their children's friends. Owing to the generation gap, this kind of interaction does not occur generally in daily life but instead requires an opportunity, such as when the child's friends need help or during holiday activities. S8 explained that the real disappearance of a person is not death but rather being forgotten, and her association with her child's friends made her feel as if her child's spirit had not completely disappeared. S4 helped the friends of her deceased child who encountered difficulties. She said,

My child's classmates come to me if they have a problem, and I will help them to the best of my ability. Through interacting with his classmates and his close friends, it feels as though my child's life is being continued.

S9 and her husband were invited to a dinner party with their child's friends during the Chinese New Year.

When Chinese New Year comes, they (the child's friends) invite us to have dinner together. Now we gather with them on behalf of my son … Everyone is so warm and welcoming. Whether it’s men or women, I love asking each of them for a hug. It’s such a special feeling, and I can’t quite put into words that sense of fulfillment.

Experiencing life from the child's perspective

Some shidu parents choose to experience a rich and varied life on behalf of their children and imagine that their children have also gained these life experiences, including the experiences of travelling, working, and watching their grandchildren grow. These shidu parents expand the relatively short life of their children at an imaginary level. When performing various daily activities, S7 would put his son's photos in the bag that he carried with him, and when he was on a business trip or travelling, he would carry his son's knapsack when going out so that he could feel as if his son saw everything that he had seen. The experience is like a continuation of his son's life. S7 said, ‘Although my son's life is over, I want to continue his life, so I take him out with me.’ S1 mentioned that she had watched her grandson grow up on behalf of her child and vividly described her own eyes as her child's eyes. She said, ‘I will watch his son grow up for him (my son), use my eyes for his eyes, and use my personal experience to represent my son's personal experience.’

Impacts of living on behalf of the child on shidu parents

This study reveals that the lives of shidu parents who choose to live on behalf of the child are affected both positively and negatively. The positive aspects include regaining a sense of purpose in life, improving emotional states, and increasing the level of interpersonal support. The negative aspects involve amplifying their level of life stress.

Positive impact: Regaining a sense of purpose in life

In the initial period after the death of a child, shidu parents often experience intense grief. They may feel that the world is dark and that life has lost its direction. After enduring this acute phase of grief, some shidu parents regained their sense of purpose by living on behalf of their child. S4 devoted much time and energy to charity activities. She believes that her child was a kind person, and she wanted to continue her child’s goodness. She described her journey of transformation as follows:

When my child was alive, I worked hard to earn money like everyone else. After he passed away, I realized I could not keep living just to work and make money like before. I had to do something to give my life meaning, and my child is my greatest motivation … Deep in my heart, I feel that by doing these things, I'm carrying on his kindness, doing what he would have done, and continuing his way of being in the world.

One important aspect of S1's choice to live on behalf of her child is seeing her grandson grow up. S1 said, ‘When my grandson graduates from university, I will die without regret. There will be another twelve years. At that time, I will be 86 years old, so I must be strong and live a good life.’ S3 has dedicated herself to fulfilling her child's wish to plant trees and create forests. She believes that what she is doing can elevate her child's spirit. She said, ‘If we succeed, people will know I planted these trees in memory of my son. They will surely remember his good deeds. Although my child is gone, his spirit lives on.’ S3 sometimes interprets natural phenomena as reactions of her child. She said,

Once, a heavy snowfall occurred, and I regarded the flying snow as my child's excited reaction. Looking at the tall and straight branches of the small trees (planted by her organization), I seem to see once again the youthful figure of my child.

Positive impact: improving one's emotional state

For the shidu parents, the concept of living on behalf of the child is like a beacon that illuminates their path forward. When asked whether her mood had improved after she began to practice living on behalf of her child, S5 answered affirmatively. ‘Yes. I am in a much better mood than before. I have never sung before. Now I often hum a song to myself.’ S1 specifically described that when grief over the loss of her child is triggered by certain circumstances, she is able to adjust her mindset more quickly.

I can’t keep living in sorrow and grief, because after all, I must live on behalf of my son to fulfill the dreams he left unfinished. Now I shift my state of mind much faster (referring to the grief over losing her son). Last year, it would take a day or two to move past the sadness, but now, it doesn’t. It might take only twenty minutes to shift because I’ve come to terms with it. I can’t follow my son into death. He's still counting on me to fulfill all the dreams he left behind.

S3 feels that living on behalf of her child gives her an outlet for her emotions:

Human emotions must have an outlet. If we keep dwelling on the pain, our suffering may not be reduced. After all, there is no way to make up for the pain of losing a child. We cannot solve problems with tears alone; instead, we must think more deeply about how to fulfill the things our children wished to accomplish in life. After doing these things my child wanted to do, I felt a great sense of release and relief.

Positive impact: increasing the level of interpersonal support

As previously mentioned, one way for shidu parents to live on behalf of the child is to continue to interact with their children's friends, and this approach can certainly increase the level of interpersonal support for shidu parents. There are also other ways of living on behalf of the child that may increase the level of interpersonal support received. After the death of his child, S8 dedicated himself to the charitable work that his child had pursued during his lifetime and helped many people. Some of the people whom his child assisted maintain long-term contact with S8. S8 said,

We helped a girl during our charitable activities. Although we do not visit her every day, she's always on our minds. We collect or buy clothes for her, and this is a bond that runs deeper than blood. That child is very warm toward us, too. When she achieves good grades, she immediately sends us a WeChat message to share the good news … Over the past decade or so, she's kept in touch with us all along.

Beyond the individuals they assist, shidu parents may also form deep friendships with the volunteers who offer help alongside them. S8's wife was once sick and hospitalised, and many volunteers from charitable activities came to visit. S3 followed her child’s last wish to vigorously promote afforestation and encouraged the participation of many volunteers. She has very good relationships with these volunteers. She said,

They (the volunteers) have been truly kind to me. A few days ago, I took a short vacation in City S, and volunteers from all around came to visit me … These gestures transcend systems and rules, encouraging me to keep doing this work well.

Negative impact: an increase in life stress

This study finds that although living on behalf of the child offers many benefits for shidu parents, it can also cause certain stress, such as adverse health effects and economic pressures. S3 is 75 years old. Although many of her contemporaries are taken care of at home and entertain every day, S3 is still striving to realise her child's wish to plant trees. Sometimes, her body cannot tolerate it. She said, ‘Ever since I started to plant trees on behalf of my child, it has hurt my body greatly … I have fallen a lot while doing charitable activities, and I have suffered many injuries … I sacrificed my own health to do this.’ S3 founded a nonprofit organisation called Greenlife and paid a considerable portion of the operating expenses out of her own pocket. After the interview, S3 gave the interviewer a souvenir from the community service. She said that the souvenir is used by the organisation to reward volunteers. Since the organisation’s funds could not cover these expenses, she personally covered the costs out of her own pocket. S5 participates in taking care of her granddaughter on behalf of her child. Although S5's granddaughter lives with her father and stepmother, she often stays with S5 for a period of time. Although S5 is not wealthy, she uses her own money to pay for some of her granddaughter's food, clothing and other expenses. She said, ‘When my granddaughter needs money, I scrape together a little to give her … That is the responsibility I should fulfill.’

Discussion

Living on behalf of the child and meaning reconstruction among shidu parents

Meaning reconstruction theory conceptualises bereavement-related meaning reconstruction as comprising three dimensions: sense-making, benefit-finding, and identity change (Gillies & Neimeyer, 2006). This study shows that in the process of living on behalf of the child among shidu parents, adaptive continuing bonds with the deceased child have benefits for the parents, and living from the perspective of the child reflects identity change. Therefore, living on behalf of the child is a process of meaning reconstruction among shidu parents.

First, adaptive continuing bonds with the deceased child help shidu parents engage in benefit-finding. According to Gillies and Neimeyer (2006), benefit-finding involves maintaining a healthy bond with the deceased, integrating the deceased into present and future life, and drawing strength to continue living. Living on behalf of the child reproduces a way of continuing bonds with the deceased. In Eastern cultures, maintaining continuing bonds with the deceased is not regarded as a sign of maladaptive adjustment but is widely accepted as a normal and beneficial form of coping with grief (Klass et al., 1996; Klass & Steffen, 2018). A quantitative study by Field and Filanosky (2009) indicated that internalised continuing bonds, in which mental representations of the deceased serve as a secure base, are positively linked to personal growth among bereaved individuals. This study revealed that this type of grief coping strategy can help shidu parents in China regain a sense of purpose in life, improve their emotional states, and increase their levels of interpersonal support. However, this study also demonstrates that this strategy can cause adverse health effects and economic pressure for shidu parents. Therefore, the appropriate use of this strategy is particularly important for the physical and mental health of shidu parents. Moreover, a qualitative study found that ‘living for self’ represents a positive change for some Chinese shidu parents (Xu et al., 2024). Shidu parents must strike a balance between living on behalf of their child and living for themselves. While they live on behalf of their child, shidu parents must also recognise their own needs and engage in self-care.

Second, living from the perspective of the child by shidu parents represents a form of identity change. The core essence of living on behalf of the child is extending the child's life at the imaginary level by playing the role of the child and regarding this activity as the key meaning of life. Living on behalf of the child is different from ‘living for the child,’ as living for the child occurs when shidu parents practice their own role (as parents). For example, previous studies have reported that the factors that support the survival of bereaved parents include the ability to continue honouring the child, the ability to keep communicating with the child in one's imagination, and the ability to give the child in heaven peace of mind (He et al., 2017; Superdock et al., 2024). With these coping strategies, shidu parents fully retain their identity as the child's parents and live their own lives. However, living on behalf of the child means adopting the child’s role. When shidu parents adopt this grief coping strategy, some of their identity features change from those of parents to those of children. Although they are actually parents, they also take on aspects of the child's identity simultaneously such that they, in some way, live the child's life.

The role of Chinese culture

The various manifestations of shidu parents who adopt living on behalf of the child profoundly reflect the connotations of Chinese culture. First, doing what the child was doing when the child was still alive and doing things that the child wanted to do but had not been able to do both serve to fulfil the child’s wishes. China's familism culture bestows parents with the responsibility of always guarding their children, and this responsibility does not end with the death of their children (Yang, 2004). Shidu parents regard the things that their children cherished during their lifetimes (including those things that are done and those that have not yet been started) as their unfinished missions in life. Parents continue to perform these actions on behalf of their children so that the meaning of their children's lives can continue. Second, in contrast to Western culture, China's collectivist culture does not emphasise the liberation of human nature but rather the responsibility of the individual to others and society (Qi & Peng, 2025). Shidu parents take care of their children’s unfinished business (e.g. caring for grandchildren, repaying their debts, and showing gratitude) to avoid any moral deficiencies on the part of their children or as a means of uplifting the moral value of their children's lives (e.g. contributing to society on behalf of the children). Third, Chinese culture emphasises relationships with friends. Confucianism lists friends as one of the ‘five basic human relations’ (Li, 2021; Xu, 2024), and in folk tradition, ‘Friends are like brothers.’ Therefore, Shidu parents continue to interact with their children's friends on behalf of their children, thereby reflecting the importance that they attach to relationships with friends, which allows shidu parents to feel the continuity of the child's spirit through this type of interaction. Fourth, in the hearts of shidu parents, the life of a child who did not live to a natural end remains incomplete. By continuing to experience life on behalf of their child, they extend the trajectory of their child's life, which aligns with the Chinese pursuit of wholeness/completeness (Cheng, 2013). Overall, the different manifestations of living on behalf of the child reflect not only the regret that shidu parents feel because they ‘cannot be with the child to the end’ but also their efforts to compensate for this regret. This unique approach profoundly embodies the connotation of ‘parent–child oneness’ in Chinese culture, and the strength drawn from culture enables them to continue their efforts.

Implications for practice

This study finds that the impacts of living on behalf of the child on Chinese shidu parents are predominantly positive and include regaining a sense of purpose in life, improving emotional well-being, and enhancing interpersonal support. Although this practice among shidu parents may increase their life stress, this negative effect generally occurs within a manageable range. As a culturally adaptive grief coping strategy, living on behalf of the child facilitates post-bereavement adjustment among shidu parents through both benefit-finding and identity change. Therefore, when providing psychological support to shidu parents, social workers and other professionals in China can adopt the perspective of living on behalf of the child to help bereaved parents rebuild confidence in life. For example, social workers can engage shidu parents in exploring whether they are willing to do what the child used to do, accomplish what the child intended to do but had not completed, fulfil the child’s unmet responsibilities, maintain relationships with the child’s friends, and experience life from the perspective of the child. Among these five forms of living on behalf of the child, the latter two are generally less likely to generate life stress and may therefore have greater adaptive value. Moreover, professionals need to help shidu parents find an appropriate balance between living on behalf of the child and living for themselves.

Limitations and future directions

This study reveals a grief coping strategy with unique cultural attributes that is adopted by some shidu parents in China, namely, living on behalf of the child, and as such, fills an existing gap in grief research. However, this study has several limitations. First, this study recruited only shidu parents who adopted the grief coping strategy of living on behalf of the child, resulting in a highly homogeneous sample that may introduce selection bias; moreover, no shidu parents who chose not to adopt this coping strategy were included as controls. Second, living on behalf of the child represents the fulfilment of their children’s life roles by shidu parents. However, certain life roles become feasible only at specific ages. For instance, parents might choose to act on behalf of their child only when the child is old enough and demonstrates a significant and particular interest. Therefore, can shidu parents whose children died when they were very young deal with their grief by living on behalf of their child? If so, how do its manifestations differ from those of shidu parents whose children died at an older age? These issues are not addressed in this study. Third, among the 10 participants in this study, 8 were interviewed by telephone and 2 were interviewed face-to-face. Given the emotionally sensitive nature of the topic, the non-face-to-face interviews may have affected the participants’ expression, the interviewer’s ability to capture nonverbal cues, and rapport building, which may have resulted in reduced data richness and consequently, limited the depth of the study.

Future research could further examine the distinctiveness of the living on behalf of the child coping strategy across different cultural contexts and family structures. For example, among the participants in this study, the number of parents who lost a son (8) was four times that of those who lost a daughter (2), which raises the question of whether this pattern is related to the preference for sons over daughters in the Confucian culture. Another question is whether living on behalf of the child is exclusive to shidu families or whether it also appears among bereaved parents who still have other surviving children. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to address these questions. In addition, as living on behalf of the child facilitates meaning reconstruction among shidu parents, future quantitative research could employ instruments such as meaning reconstruction scales and prolonged grief scales to further assess the adaptiveness of this grief coping strategy.

Conclusion

This study shows that living on behalf of the child is a unique grief coping strategy that is adopted by some shidu parents in China and that this strategy has profound cultural implications. When shidu parents practice living on behalf of their child, part of their identity shifts from that of being parents to that of being children. The effects of living on behalf of the child on the shidu parents are mainly positive, but there are also negative effects; therefore, identifying an appropriate state of balance is necessary.
