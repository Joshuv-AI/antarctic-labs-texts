Diagnostic performance of the vertebral bone quality score for osteoporosis is minimally influenced by lipid parameters in lumbar fusion patients: a retrospective study

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD), deterioration of trabecular bone structure, and impaired bone strength [1]. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score has emerged as a useful tool for evaluating osteoporosis, predicting fracture risk, and estimating postoperative complication risk following spine surgery [2–4]. Studies indicate that the VBQ score can be influenced by diverse physiological and technical variables, including ethnicity, age, sex, body mass index (BMI), lipid parameters, dietary patterns, and MRI-related variables [5,6]. As these influencing factors remain incompletely elucidated, variability in VBQ scores persists across populations and imaging systems [7,8]. Therefore, identifying factors that affect VBQ score is essential for its broad clinical implementation.

Several studies investigating the clinical application of VBQ have raised concerns that lipid parameters and hyperlipidemia (HLP) may influence VBQ measurements [3,4,7,9]. The proposed mechanism is that HLP promotes ectopic lipid accumulation within the bone marrow, increasing VBQ scores (indicative of lower BMD) and potentially leading to false-positive osteoporosis diagnoses [7]. Consistent with this hypothesis, Aynaszyan et al. [5] reported that HLP and elevated lipid levels may result in artificially elevated VBQ values, potentially reducing the accuracy of osteoporosis detection. Similarly, Xue et al. [10] demonstrated higher VBQ scores in patients with HLP, further supporting the premise that HLP may artificially elevate VBQ readings. However, although these findings suggest that lipid parameters and HLP may affect the diagnostic accuracy of VBQ for osteoporosis assessment, their impact has not been fully elucidated.

Because magnetic field strength (1.5T or 3.0T) significantly influences VBQ score [9,11], a novel calibrated VBQ score (C-VBQ) has been developed to improve consistency across MRI magnetic field strengths [12]. By incorporating calibration to both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and subcutaneous fat, C-VBQ has demonstrated greater generalizability across different magnetic fields. Nevertheless, further research is needed to explore whether the lipid parameters affect the diagnostic accuracy of VBQ and C-VBQ for evaluating osteoporosis. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the influence of lipid parameters and HLP on the values and diagnostic performances of VBQ and C-VBQ in patients undergoing lumbar fusion surgery.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the ethical review board of Tianjin Hospital (MER-2024178) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study is reported in accordance with the STARD (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) guidelines [13].

Patient inclusion and exclusion

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients aged >50 years who underwent surgery for degenerative lumbar disorders at our institution between August 2022 and August 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of lumbar dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), quantitative computed tomography (QCT), and MRI performed within a 1-month interval; and (2) laboratory testing results obtained within 1 month of lumbar MRI. Exclusion criteria were: (1) previous fractures involving the L1–L4 vertebrae; (2) prior instrumentation at L1–L4; (3) a history of spinal metastasis or infection; (4) previous treatment with teriparatide; (5) inadequate image quality for VBQ scoring; and (6) refusal to participate. Collected data included age, sex, BMI, laboratory parameters, DXA T-scores, QCT measurements, and MRI findings.

DXA examinations and QCT measurements

DXA scans were performed using a GE Lunar iDXA system (enCORE software ver. 16.0; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Lumbar computed tomography (CT) images were acquired using a GE 64-slice CT scanner (CT660, GE Healthcare; tube voltage: 120 kV) and analyzed on a dedicated workstation (Mindways QCT Pro, ver. 6.1; Mindways Software, Austin, TX, USA) to determine volumetric BMD (vBMD) [14]. Based on the World Health Organization DXA criteria, osteoporosis was defined as a T-score ≤−2.5 at the hip, femoral neck, or lumbar spine [1,15]. In this study, only lumbar spine T-scores were considered, and osteoporosis was defined as a T-score ≤ −2.5. According to the American College of Radiology QCT classification using the average vBMD at L1/L2, osteoporosis was defined as an average L1–L2 vBMD <80 mg/cm3 [9].

Magnetic resonance examinations and VBQ measurements

Lumbar MRI scans were performed using three scanners: one 1.5T system (HDXT ECHOSPEED 16 CHANNEL; General Electric Company, Cincinnati, OH, USA) and two 3.0T systems (GE Discovery MR750 3.0T [General Electric Company]; Philips Ingenia 3.0T Cx [Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands]). Imaging parameters, including repetition time (TR) and echo time (TE), were set as follows: for the 1.5T scanner, TR=601.2±149.9 ms, TE=9.4±1.8 ms; for the 3.0T scanners, TR=671.6±92.9 ms, TE=13.3±2.1 ms. A uniform slice thickness of 5 mm was used for all examinations. The resulting VBQ scores were categorized as VBQ 1.5T and VBQ 3.0T, according to magnetic field strength.

The VBQ scoring method was first described by Ehresman et al. [16] in 2019. It is based on the principle that signal intensity (SI) on non-contrast T1-weighted lumbar MRI primarily reflects fat infiltration within the trabecular bone. As shown in Fig. 1A, SI measurements were obtained from the trabecular bone of the L1–L4 vertebral bodies on midsagittal images. To standardize measurements, the SI of the CSF adjacent to the L3 vertebra was used as a reference. For each region of interest (ROI), the area, mean SI, and standard deviation (SD) were recorded. Elliptical ROIs measuring 140–160 mm2 were placed within the L1–L4 vertebral bodies, while a smaller ROI of 20–40 mm2 was used for the ventral CSF at L3. To address the substantial influence of magnetic field strength (1.5T or 3.0T) on VBQ measurements, a calibrated VBQ score (denoted as C-VBQ) was developed to reduce scanner-related variability [12]. The C-VBQ-adjusted metric standardizes magnetic resonance (MR) signal intensities relative to both CSF and subcutaneous fat, providing a refined measure of fat infiltration within vertebral bone. In this method, the CSF signal is assigned a score of 0, whereas subcutaneous fat (after subtracting the background CSF SI) is assigned a score of 10. As demonstrated in Fig. 1B, an elliptical ROI of 40–60 mm2 was placed within the subcutaneous fat to determine its SI. The elliptical ROI was positioned within the region of highest MR SI at the lumbar or thoracolumbar level, avoiding the fascial layer, and with its long axis oriented parallel to the skin. The eccentricity of the ellipse was adjusted based on the thickness of the subcutaneous fat. All other measurement procedures remained consistent with the standard VBQ protocol. MR image analyses were performed using Vue PACS software ver. 12.2.1.0104 (Philips Electronics, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The first two authors independently measured all VBQ measurements while blinded to DXA and QCT results. Interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC for VBQ measurements was 0.805 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.758–0.845), indicating good reliability, while the ICC for C-VBQ measurements was 0.841 (95% CI, 0.799–0.877), indicating good-to-excellent reliability. The mean VBQ values obtained by the two reviewers were used for the final analysis.

Lipid parameters and HLP

The lipid parameters evaluated in this study included triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (CHOL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), and the LDLC/HDLC ratio. The normal reference ranges were defined as follows: TG <2.3 mmol/L, CHOL <6.2 mmol/L, LDLC <4.1 mmol/L, HDLC ≥1.0 mmol/L, and LDLC/HDLC ratio <2.0. HLP was defined by the presence of any of the following: TG ≥2.3 mmol/L, CHOL ≥6.2 mmol/L, LDLC ≥4.1 mmol/L, or HDLC <1.0 mmol/L.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Interobserver reliability was assessed using the ICC based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement. For continuous variables following a normal distribution, group comparisons were made using Student t-test. Comparisons across more than two groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlations between variables were quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) calculated to evaluate diagnostic accuracy. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), while histograms were generated with SigmaPlot ver. 11.0 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation of VBQ scores with clinical variables

We examined correlations between laboratory parameters and VBQ scores across three groups: VBQ 1.5T (n=194), VBQ 3.0T (n=100), and C-VBQ (n=294), each stratified into low and high VBQ subgroups. As presented in Table 1, significant inter-subgroup differences were observed for age, sex, BMI, T-score, QCT, glucose, glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (AST), and uric acid levels, but not for any lipid parameter. However, HDLC exhibited a trend toward significance in the VBQ 1.5T group (1.11±0.26 vs. 1.23±0.52, p=0.055). Further analysis demonstrated significant associations of VBQ scores with BMI, T-score, and QCT across quintiles, but not with age (Supplement 2).

Correlation of lipid parameters with clinical variables

Correlations between lipid parameters (including TG, CHOL, LDLC, HDLC, and LDLC/HDLC) and clinical variables are presented in Supplement 3. Significant associations were observed between certain lipid parameters and sex, BMI, T-score, and QCT. Among the VBQ metrics, only HDLC demonstrated significant correlations (VBQ 1.5T: r=0.215, p=0.003; C-VBQ: r=0.211, p=0.001). As depicted in Fig. 2, patients were stratified into quintiles by age, BMI, T-score, and QCT. Significant differences across T-score quintiles were observed for CHOL (p=0.002) and HDLC (p=0.019), but not across age, BMI, or QCT quintiles.

Influence of HLP and HDLC on the diagnostic effect of VBQ scores

HLP was present in 167 of 294 patients (56.8%). ROC analysis determined its impact on diagnostic performance (Table 2). All VBQ scores exhibited substantial diagnostic utility for osteoporosis under both T-score and QCT criteria. After stratifying by HLP status, VBQ scores remained significant in all subgroups except VBQ 3.0T in patients without HLP (n=42, p=0.114). AUC values were comparable between patients with and without HLP (Supplement 5). Given the specific association between HDLC and VBQ scores, ROC analysis was further stratified by HDLC level, with comparable AUC values observed across groups (Table 2). Overall, lipid parameters and HLP had minimal impact on the diagnostic performance of VBQ scores.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the influence of lipid parameters on VBQ scores in patients undergoing lumbar fusion surgery. In this retrospective cohort of 294 patients, only HDLC demonstrated a positive correlation with VBQ scores among all lipid parameters examined. ROC curve analysis further revealed that lipid parameters and HLP exerted minimal influence on the diagnostic performance of the VBQ scores.

MRI is an essential component of preoperative evaluation for spine surgery. As an established opportunistic screening metric, the VBQ score evaluates BMD by quantifying bone marrow fat infiltration [17,18]. Its correlation with BMD is well-established, and the VBQ score has demonstrated utility in predicting specific osteoporosis-related postoperative complications [3,6,16,19]. The use of CSF as an internal reference standard for VBQ mitigates variations attributable to scanner models [17,19]. However, recent investigations indicate that magnetic field strength (1.5T or 3.0T) significantly influences the VBQ score [9,11,20]. Because the upper signal limit on T1-weighted sequences is notably influenced by field strength, a modified calibration method incorporating subcutaneous fat as an upper reference was proposed [12]. The resulting C-VBQ score demonstrates consistency across 1.5T and 3.0T scanners, thereby minimizing the confounding effect of the magnetic field strength on the VBQ measurements [12].

Beyond magnetic field strength, several physiological and technical factors may influence the clinical utility of the VBQ score. Previous studies have reported no significant effect of factors such as sex, steroid use, or smoking on VBQ measurements [5]. One study examining the effect of age found that while VBQ was effective for assessing osteoporosis in patients younger than 70 years, its efficacy diminished in those aged >70 years [21]. This finding may reflect a ceiling effect of age-related bone marrow conversion. Elevated BMI or obesity may promote ectopic adipocyte accumulation within bone marrow cavities, potentially increasing the VBQ score (indicative of lower BMD). Furthermore, as obesity is closely associated with HLP, this association may further increase VBQ scores and contribute to false-positive predictions [7]. Conversely, Zhao et al. [8] reported a significant non-linear relationship between BMI and VBQ, with better diagnostic performance noted in patients with a BMI ≥23.8 kg/m2. Collectively, these studies highlight the multifactorial nature of VBQ score determinants and the need for a more comprehensive understanding of their effects.

Increasing epidemiological evidence emphasizes the link between lipid metabolism and osteoporosis [5]. Fatty infiltration into the bone is associated with impaired bone strength and quality, contributing to bone density loss [2]. Moreover, adipocyte proliferation within the bone marrow microenvironment promotes the release of proinflammatory and immunoregulatory mediators [8], which accelerates osteoclast production and activation while simultaneously impairing osteoblast differentiation, thereby favoring bone resorption [8]. HLP is broadly categorized into hypertriglyceridemia and hypercholesterolemia. Although there is no consensus on the effects of HLP on BMD, hypercholesterolemia and elevated HDLC levels may negatively impact bone density [5,22].

HLP is closely linked to bone marrow adiposity, raising concern that serum lipid parameters may confound the VBQ score. Among the lipid parameters evaluated, only HDLC demonstrated a weak positive correlation with the VBQ scores, whereas the overall effects of lipid parameters on VBQ were minimal. The selective correlation between HDLC and VBQ scores, in contrast to other lipid parameters, warrants mechanistic consideration. Unlike TG and LDLC, which are primarily affected by dietary intake and hepatic synthesis, HDLC possesses unique biological functions, including reverse cholesterol transport, anti-inflammatory properties, and immunomodulation. However, within the bone marrow milieu, elevated HDLC levels may paradoxically promote a pro-inflammatory state that enhances osteoclast activity and activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby affecting bone formation and promoting marrow fat infiltration [22–24]. Consistent with this hypothesis, several clinical studies have reported an inverse relationship between HDLC levels and BMD. A meta-analysis by Zhao et al. [25] included 12 studies that found elevated HDLC levels in patients with osteoporosis. Saoji et al. [26] demonstrated a significant correlation between lipid metabolism and osteoporosis, identifying HDLC and TG as key predictors. The genome-wide study by Zhang et al. [27] identified HDLC levels, but not TG levels, as a causal risk factor for reduced lumbar spine BMD.

HLP has also been reported to influence VBQ score and contribute to discrepancies between VBQ and DXA measurements [5,19]. VBQ primarily reflects bone quality indirectly through assessment of fat infiltration in cancellous bone [17]. Since vertebral bodies are highly vascular, patients with HLP may exhibit increased adipose tissue content within vertebral vessels, potentially leading to spuriously elevated VBQ scores [8,10]. Supporting this hypothesis, Xue et al. [10] reported that patients with HLP demonstrated higher VBQ scores than those without. Aynaszyan et al. [5] revealed that HLP was the only variable significantly associated with elevated VBQ scores in their healthy bone cohort, with VBQ values overlapping those typically observed in osteopenia or osteoporosis. Their subsequent sub-analysis specifically linked elevated HDLC levels to the higher VBQ scores [5].

In the present study, among all the lipid parameters assessed, only HDLC demonstrated a positive correlation with VBQ scores, with minimal impact on diagnostic performance. Pearson correlation analysis confirmed a weak linear positive correlation between HDLC and VBQ scores, without evidence of exponential or other non-linear associations in trend tests across lipid quintiles. Patients in the high VBQ 1.5T group exhibited a trend toward higher HDLC levels (1.11±0.26 vs. 1.23±0.52, p=0.055). Weak but significant correlations were observed between HDLC and VBQ 1.5T (r=0.215, p=0.003) and C-VBQ (r=0.211, p=0.001). When stratified by lipid status, only HDLC subgroups exhibited a significant difference in C-VBQ (2.95±0.88 vs. 2.73±0.78, p=0.046). After adjustment for T-score and quintile stratification, HDLC remained significantly associated with VBQ 3.0T (p=0.037) and C-VBQ (p=0.003). ROC analysis demonstrated comparable AUCs across HDLC groups.

In this study, the prevalence of osteoporosis differed markedly between DXA T-score criteria (51 patients, 17.3%) and QCT criteria (126 patients, 42.9%). The use of age- and sex-adjusted Caucasian reference thresholds may overestimate osteoporosis in East Asian populations. Emerging data suggests that adjusted cutoffs, such as a T-score ≤ −3.7 and a QCT threshold of approximately 50 mg/cm3, may be more appropriate for this population [28,29]. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationships among T-scores, QCT, and VBQ in East Asian cohorts.

Our results demonstrated a weak positive association between HDLC levels and VBQ scores. Although higher HDLC levels were associated with slightly elevated VBQ scores, this effect was not considered sufficient to meaningfully limit the clinical utility of VBQ scores. VBQ remained a significant osteoporosis predictor in surgical patients, although with lower diagnostic performance than T-score and QCT [2]. Nevertheless, caution may be warranted when interpreting VBQ scores to assess osteoporosis in patients with abnormal HDLC levels. Future efforts should focus on standardizing VBQ measurement, addressing its existing limitations, and defining its complementary role alongside other diagnostic modalities to improve osteoporosis detection and diagnostic precision. The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms for holistic bone quality assessment in spinal surgery patients represents a promising direction for future research [30].

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and a single-center design limited the scope of statistical analysis. Second, because osteoporosis screening takes precedence over osteopenia assessment in spine surgery patients, the impact of lipid parameters on osteopenia was not evaluated. Third, as the research was conducted within a lumbar surgery cohort from a single institution, selection bias may be present, and the findings may not be generalizable to the broader population or to patients without advanced lumbar degenerative disease. External validation in diverse, multi-institutional cohorts and non-surgical populations is required before broad application of these findings. Fourth, clinical indicators of osteoporosis, such as fragility fractures, were not incorporated into the current analysis, which may have underestimated osteoporosis prevalence. Fifth, the DXA T-score assessment in this study was limited not only by the exclusion of hip or femoral neck Tscores, which often demonstrate discordance with lumbar DXA and could confound comparisons with lumbar QCT and VBQ, as well as by the use of diagnostic thresholds derived from Caucasian reference data that may not be optimal for East Asian populations. Sixth, the mechanistic link between HDLC and VBQ scores remains speculative, as this study was designed to assess clinical associations rather than causality. Future basic science studies are needed to validate the proposed pathways before definitive diagrams or mechanistic models can be generated. Finally, the influence of potential confounders, such as smoking status, diabetes, liver disease, metabolic syndrome, hematologic disorders, therapies altering bone marrow composition, additional laboratory parameters, lipidlowering medications (e.g., statins which influence both serum lipid profiles and bone metabolism), and MRI acquisition parameters like spinecho sequences and TR/TE relaxation times, merits further investigation. Due to the retrospective design, these variables were not systematically available in the present analysis. Prospective studies with comprehensive data collection are warranted to confirm the independence of the observed associations.

Conclusions

In this retrospective study, among all lipid parameters, only HDLC demonstrated a positive correlation with VBQ scores. ROC analysis revealed that lipid parameters and HLP exerted minimal influence on VBQ diagnostic performance. Although a weak correlation was observed between HDLC and VBQ scores, it did not translate into clinically meaningful effects on diagnostic performance. Overall, lipid parameters and HLP do not substantially affect the diagnostic utility of VBQ for osteoporosis assessment.
