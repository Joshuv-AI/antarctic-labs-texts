Measurable one-ended spanning trees
Matt Bowen, António Girão, Héctor Jardón-Sánchez, Grigory Terlov

Abstract
. We show that a one-ended, locally finite, measurable graph on a standard probability space admits a measurable one-ended spanning subtree if and only if it is measure-hyperfinite. This answers a question posed by Bowen, Poulin, and Zomback and extends recent results of Timár and Conley, Gaboriau, Marks, and Tucker-Drob.



1.  Introduction and main results

In this paper, we are interested in measurable combinatorics, the study of properties and applications of graphs defined on measure spaces. Our main objects of interest are measurable graphs. A Borel graph
\mathcal{G}=(X,E)
is a graph whose vertex set
X
is a standard Borel space and the edge set
E
is a Borel subset of
X^{2}
. Such a graph is called measurable if
X
is equipped with a Borel probability measure
\nu
(e.g.
[0,1]
with the Lebesgue measure). Background on this subject can be found in
[
KM16
,
Pik21
]
. One of the central structural notions in this field is hyperfiniteness: a measurable graph
\mathcal{G}
on
(X,\nu)
is called (measure-)
hyperfinite
(or
\nu
-hyperfinite for short) if it can be written as an increasing union of measurable graphs whose connected components are finite
\nu
-a.e.

While the compactness theorem from first-order logic implies that many classical results from finite graph theory immediately generalize to infinite graphs, these extensions rely on the axiom of choice and typically only yield non-measurable results.
In contrast, if one is only interested in measurable or Borel solutions to combinatorial problems, then the situation is quite different, even in the very constrained setting of acyclic graphs.
For example, Marks
[
Mar16
]
has constructed, for all
d\geqslant 2
, a
d
-regular acyclic Borel graph with no Borel
d
-coloring, showing that the Borel version of Brooks’ theorem need not hold.
This was later extended to give examples even for hyperfinite graphs
[
CJM+23
]
.
Similarly, Kun has constructed for all
d\geqslant 2
a
d
-regular acyclic measurable graph with no measurable perfect matching, showing that Hall’s theorem need not hold in the measure setting
[
Kun21
]
.

Although these examples are acyclic, they are leafless and hence belong to a quite special subclass of trees. The following definition constitutes a principal obstruction to well-behaved measurable combinatorics.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a connected locally finite graph
G
is
\mathbf{\kappa}
-ended
if
\kappa
is the supremum number of infinite connected components that can be obtained by removing a finite set of vertices from
G.
We say that a locally finite measurable graph
\mathcal{G}
on
(X,\nu)
is
\kappa
-ended
if for
\nu
-a.e.
x\in X
its
\mathcal{G}
-connected components are
\kappa
-ended, and we say that
\mathcal{G}
admits a measurable
\kappa
-ended spanning tree
if there is a measurable subgraph
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
which is acyclic, one-ended, and has the same connected components as
\mathcal{G}

\nu
-a.e.

Notice that
d
-regular trees for
d\geqslant 2
(the aforementioned examples, in particular) must have at least two ends. On the other hand, a locally finite tree is one-ended if and only if its vertex set can be exhausted by iteratively removing leaves. This property enables measurable variants of many algorithms from finite combinatorics that do not hold for arbitrary measurable graphs. Indeed, while the above examples show that the measurable versions of Brooks’ theorem and Hall’s theorem fail in general, they are known to hold for measurable graphs that admit measurable one-ended spanning trees, as was shown by Conley, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CMTD16
]
and Bowen, Kun, and Sabok
[
BKS21
]
, respectively. In addition, measurable one-ended spanning trees have found several recent applications, including measurable solutions to Tarski’s circle squaring problem and the construction of measurable
k
-factors and integral flows
[
BKS21
,
BKS25
]
, Borel balanced orientations
[
BPZ24
]
, and factors of Euclidean spaces and Poisson point processes
[
Tim04
,
Tim21
,
Tim23
]
.

With these applications in mind, it is natural to ask when a locally finite measurable graph
\mathcal{G}
admits a measurable one-ended spanning tree. Classically, Halin’s theorem
[
Hal64
]
implies that an infinite locally finite graph admits a one-ended spanning tree if and only if the graph itself is one-ended.
In the measurable setting, the situation is more subtle. The existence of a measurable one-ended spanning tree of a measurable graph
\mathcal{G}
on
(X,\nu)
implies that
\mathcal{G}
is (
\nu
-)hyperfinite, so at the very least hyperfiniteness is a necessary condition. If in addition
\mathcal{G}
is probability measure preserving (pmp), a result of Adams
[
Ada90
]
shows that any measurable spanning tree of a hyperfinite graph has at most two ends. This was later sharpened by Timár
[
Tim19
]
and by Conley, Gaboriau, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CGMTD21
]
, who proved that under the same hypotheses, the tree can in fact be chosen to be one-ended whenever
\mathcal{G}
is one-ended.

Outside the pmp setting, hyperfinite graphs need not have at most two ends anymore. Indeed, there exist hyperfinite Borel graphs with infinitely many ends (e.g. see
[
CTT22
, Example 1.2]
).
We note that by
[
Mil04
, Proposition 2.1]
any locally countable measurable graph on
(X,\nu)
is measure class preserving (mcp) on a
\nu
-conull set.
A natural extension of Adams’ result for such graphs was recently obtained in
[
TTD25
]
, where the classical notion of ends is replaced by a weighted analogue, known as nonvanishing ends.
To our knowledge, the only result for spanning trees in the mcp generality stated directly in terms of classical ends is due to Bowen, Poulin, and Zomback
[
BPZ24
]
, who showed that Schreier graphs of free Borel actions of one-ended amenable groups admit measurable one-ended spanning trees and asked if it is possible to do this in general for locally finite hyperfinite mcp graphs.

Our main result shows that hyperfiniteness is, in fact, the only obstruction to finding measurable one-ended spanning trees, thereby giving a positive answer to
[
BPZ24
, Question 1]
.

Theorem 1.2.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite, one-ended, mcp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\nu)
. Then
\mathcal{G}
is hyperfinite if and only if it admits a measurable one-ended spanning tree a.e.

We highlight that Theorem
1.2
enables many extensions of results that rely on
[
Tim19
,
CGMTD21
]
to the mcp setting.
Similarly to the recent rise of activity in the mcp setting in measured combinatorics and measured group theory
[
Tse22
,
TTD25
,
BPZ24
,
CTT22
,
Pou24
,
BCR24
]
, there has been an increasing amount of interest in percolation beyond the unimodular setting
[
Hut20
,
CTT22
,
HP24
,
TT25
,
BCR+25
]
. In the latter setting, our main result gives the following generalization of
[
Tim19
, Corollary 1.2]
.

Corollary 1.3.

For a connected, locally finite, one-ended graph
G
, let
\Gamma
be a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
that acts quasi-transitively on
G
. Then
\Gamma
is amenable if and only if there is a
\Gamma

with one end a.s.
Moreover, such a spanning tree can be constructed as a factor of i.i.d.

We note we phrased Corollary

[

is stated in terms of amenability of the graph (i.e. the Cheeger constant is zero). For unimodular quasi-transitive graphs these notions of amenability are equivalent by the Soardi–Woess–Salvatori theorem
[
SW90
,
Sal92
]
. One can also replace the assumption of amenability on

by an equivalent hypothesis of weighted-amenability of

[

]
and further considered in
[

]

3.  Proof of Theorem
1.2

Throughout this section
\mathcal{G}
denotes a locally finite mcp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\nu)
unless otherwise stated. As pointed out in the preliminaries, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mu(\mathcal{G})<\infty
, where
\mu
denotes the edge measure associated to
\nu
.

We proceed to sketch an outline of the proof. Our basic strategy towards constructing a measurable one-ended spanning tree is obtaining a nested sequence of
substantial
measurable subgraphs
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{G}_{n})\to 0
. For a precise statement of this approach, see Proposition
3.3
below the following definition of substantial.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an mcp graph on a standard probability space
(X,\nu)
. A measurable subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is
substantial
if a.e.
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
-component is either one-ended or is an isolated vertex and a.e. one-ended
\mathcal{G}
-component contains exactly one one-ended
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
-component. Given such as subgraph, we let
O(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
denote the measurable set of vertices in one-ended components and
I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
be the union of isolated vertices.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an acyclic one-ended Borel graph on a standard Borel space
X
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
L^{>n}\subset X
consisting of those
x\in X
at distance at least
n
from a leaf, i.e. a degree one vertex, is Borel. The induced subgraph
\mathcal{T}[L^{>n}]
is substantial. Each
\mathcal{T}
-connected component consists of a unique
\mathcal{T}[L^{>n}]
-connected component together with a union of finite trees, each attached by a single edge.

The above example motivates the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a one-ended, locally finite, hyperfinite, mcp graph with
\mu(\mathcal{G})
finite. Then
\mathcal{G}
has a measurable one-ended spanning tree if and only if
there exists a nested sequence of substantial measurable subgraphs
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{G}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of Proposition
3.3
can be found in Section
3.1
. In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we are only left with the task of finding some
\varepsilon
such that, whenever we are given a substantial subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{G}
we can find a further substantial subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime})\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
. Roughly speaking, we must ensure that we can always delete “sufficiently many edges” from a locally finite one-ended hyperfinite mcp graph while preserving the end structure.

By hyperfiniteness, the Borel graph
\mathcal{G}
admits a measurable spanning tree
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{G}
. Our first edge-deletion result is the following and is proven in Section
3.2
.

Proposition 3.4.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a one-ended, locally finite, hyperfinite, mcp graph with measurable spanning tree
\mathcal{T}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
one-ended with
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
, such that
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, and
\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})\leqslant\mu(\mathcal{T})+\varepsilon
.

The above proposition implies that, without loss of generality, we may assume that a measurable spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}
constitutes most of the edge-mass of the graph. Therefore, we focus our attention now in deleting edges from measurable spanning trees
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{G}
.

The analysis of this scenario depends on whether or not edges belong to the core of
\mathcal{T}
, defined as follows.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a Borel graph and
\mathcal{T}
a Borel spanning tree. Then, the
core
of
\mathcal{T}
(relative to
\mathcal{G}
) is defined as the Borel subset

\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{G})=\{e\in\mathcal{T}:e\textnormal{ is infinitely many fundamental cycles relative to }\mathcal{T}\}.

In Proposition
3.11
, we observe that the tree
\mathcal{T}
is one-ended precisely when the core is empty. Perhaps it is then counterintuitive at first that our next step is to ensure that the whole tree belongs to the core. As we will discuss in the next paragraphs, this ensures strong connectivity properties which allow us to progress. In Section
3.3
we prove the following.

Proposition 3.6.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a one-ended, locally finite, hyperfinite, mcp graph with measurable spanning tree
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{T})<\infty
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a Borel subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{G})
with
\mu(\mathcal{S})>(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{G}))
such that
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{S}
is substantial.

We can then assume moreover that most of the mass of a measurable spanning tree belongs to the core. In Lemma
3.19
we show that
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T(G)}}
induces a subgraph of
\mathcal{T}
with finite connected components. Therefore, upon contracting these, we may assume, up to a small error, that the whole tree belongs to the core. This ensures sufficient connectivity to apply the tree packing theorem of Nash-Williams, as stated in Theorem
2.1
. In Section
3.4
we will prove the following.

Proposition 3.7.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite, hyperfinite, one-ended mcp graph on a finite measure space
(X,\nu)
with
\mu(\mathcal{G})<\infty
. Assume
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is a measurable spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)=\mathcal{T}
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a substantial Borel subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
with
\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})<\mu(\mathcal{G})-\mu(\mathcal{T})/3+\varepsilon.

While the following subsections are devoted to proving the above statements, we now present the proof of our main result from them.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a measurable spanning tree for
\mathcal{G}
. By Proposition
3.3
it is enough to construct a nested sequence of substantial subgraphs with vanishing edge-measure. We do this showing that there exists
0<C<1
such that every locally finite, hyperfinite, one-ended mcp graph
\mathcal{G}
with
\mu(\mathcal{G})<\infty
admits a substantial subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{G}
with
\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})\leqslant C\mu(\mathcal{G})
.

Suppose
\varepsilon>0
to be chosen later.
If
\mu(\mathcal{T})<(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{G})
, then by Proposition
3.4
there exists
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
as desired. Therefore, we may assume that
\mu(\mathcal{T})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{G})
.

Again, we may assume that
\mu(\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(G))\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{T})
, or otherwise the proof would be concluded by Proposition
3.6
. Contracting the edges in the finite connected components of the core – this is at most
\varepsilon\mu(\mathcal{T})
of the mass – we may assume that
\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)=\mathcal{T}
. Indeed, Lemma

shows that all connected components of the core are finite, unles they already are the whole tree. Finally, in this case, by Proposition

\mu(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})<\mu(\mathcal{G})-\mu(\mathcal{T})/3+\varepsilon\mu(\mathcal{T})+\varepsilon\mu(\mathcal{G})\leqslant\frac{1}{3}(2+5\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{G}).

Choosing
\varepsilon<1/5
completes the proof.
∎

3.1.  Decreasing sequence of substantial subgraphs

A fundamental step in our proof of Theorem
1.2
is to show that existence of a measurable one-ended spanning tree is equivalent to the existence of a nested sequence of
substantial
measurable subgraphs
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{G}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{G}_{n})\to 0
. This is precisely Proposition
3.3
, which this section is devoted to prove.

In the next lemma we show that, in an analogous fashion to the situation described in example
3.2
, whenever
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
is substantial, we can attach the isolated vertices to
O(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
as part of a forest of finite trees.

Lemma 3.8.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a locally finite, one-ended, hyperfinite, mcp graph on a finite measure space
(X,\nu)
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
a substantial measurable subgraph. Then, for any fber
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{R}|_{\mathcal{G}[I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})]}
and

\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{G}

i)

|[x]_{\mathcal{T}}\cap O(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})|=1
for
\nu
-a.e.

, and

ii)

[x]_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq[x]_{\mathcal{T}}
for at least
(1-\delta)\nu(I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime}))
-many
x\in X
.

Proof.

By hyperfiniteness of
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{R}|_{\mathcal{G}[I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})]}
, has
\mathcal{G}
-connected equivalence classes, each of whose intersection with

\nu\left(x\in I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime}):\exists y\in O(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})\ \text{such that}\ (y,x)\in\mathcal{E}\right)\geq(1-\delta)\nu(I(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})).

We construct
\mathcal{T}
as the union of three distinct trees
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{i}

\mathcal{T}_{\infty}

\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{E}

admits a Borel selection of exactly one end in each of its components. For an edge
(x,y)
in such a tree
\mathcal{T}
let
\max_{\mathcal{T}}\{x,y\}
be the point that is further from the selected end. We now extend the relative weight function
\mathbf{w}
, as in Section
2.2
, to the edges of
\mathcal{T}
by setting
\mathbf{w}^{y}(x,y):=\mathbf{w}^{y}(\max_{\mathcal{T}}\{x,y\})
.

By Lemma
3.20
and
[
Mil09
, Theorem E]
we can assume that
\mathcal{T}
has infinitely many ends. In particular, there are vertices with
\mathcal{T}
-degree at least
3
. Call maximal
\mathcal{T}
-paths all of whose vertices have
\mathcal{T}
-degree
2
a
segment
. Call an edge

redundant
if it goes between two vertices on the same segment. Note that segments may be of infinite length (in which case it also has to be
\mathbf{w}
-finite) but cannot be bi-infinite, as otherwise they would be a
2
-ended spanning tree of their component contradicting to the fact that
\mathcal{T}
has infinitely many ends.

Lemma 3.21.

Removing the redundant edges from
\mathcal{G}
produces a substantial Borel subgraph.

Proof.

The end-vertices of a redundant edge are connected by a segment in
\mathcal{T}
. Since no edge in
\mathcal{T}
is redundant, removing redundant edges preserve connectivity. It thus suffices to show that the Borel graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
obtained by removing the redundant edges from
\mathcal{G}
is one-ended.

To this end consider a connected component
G
of
\mathcal{G}
, containing the corresponding connected components
G^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
T
of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Let
K
be a finite subset of edges of
G^{\prime}
and
u,v\in V(G)
vertices in infinite connected components of
G^{\prime}\backslash K
. In particular both
u
and
v
lie in infinite connected components of
G\backslash K
, so by one-endedness there exists a path
P\subset G\backslash K
from
u
to
v
. Any redundant edge in
P
may be substituted by a path in some segment of
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that
P\subset G^{\prime}\backslash K
. It follows that
G^{\prime}\backslash K
has a unique infinite connected component. Since
K
was arbitrary, we deduce that
G^{\prime}
is one-ended, concluding the proof.
∎

Observe that an edge in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{T}
is not redundant if and only if the unique fundamental cycle it induces contains a vertex with
\mathcal{T}
-degree at least three. Removing redundant edges, we may assume that every
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{T}
-edge induces such a fundamental cycle.

Consider any path that consists of vertices with
\mathcal{T}
-degree
2
that are not adjacent to any of the edges in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.
Such paths necessarily have finite length, or we would have contradicted the fact that
\mathcal{G}
is one-ended;
indeed if there was such an infinite path it would constitute an isolated ray in
\mathcal{G}
, hence contradicting one-endedness assumption on
\mathcal{G}
. We can safely contract this path into an edge whose weight is set to be equal to the total

-weight of the edges in the path. Equivalently, one can collapse the vertices in this path with the vertex in its outer boundary point that is furthest the from the selected end in
\mathcal{T}
yielding a single vertex whose weight is set to be equal to the total
\mathbf{w}
-weight of the path and the
\mathbf{w}
-weight of the end point.
In particular, after doing this contraction we can assume that all vertices in non-trivial
\mathcal{G}
-components have

-degree at least

or else have
\mathcal{T}
-degree
2
and at least one edge in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.

Recapping, we can assume that every edge in
\mathcal{T}
is in infinitely many fundamental cycles and that every vertex in a non-trivial
\mathcal{G}

-degree at least

or has
\mathcal{T}
-degree

and at least one edge that induces a fundamental cycle meeting a vertex of

-degree at least

\mathcal{T}
has no degree

vertices since we mar assume that
\mathrm{Core}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{T}
by Remark

.

Lemma 3.22.

Every non-trivial
\mathcal{G}
component is
3
-edge-connected.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction suppose that there are two edges
e,f
whose removal cuts a
\mathcal{G}
-connected component
G
into multiple connected components. Since
G
is one-ended, exactly one of the connected components of
G\backslash\{e,f\}
is infinite. Since
\mathcal{T}
has degree at least 2, every vertex of
G
is the first vertex of three edge-disjoint rays. Therefore, any connected component of
G\backslash\{e,f\}
containing a vertex of
\mathcal{T}
-degree at least 3 must be infinite. Since there is exactly one infinite connected component of
G\backslash\{e,f\}
, it follows that all vertices with
\mathcal{T}
-degree at least 3 belong to the same

connected component.

In particular, we may assume that both
e
and
f
are on a
\mathcal{T}
-degree two path and isolate a finite subpath. But every vertex on this subpath has an edge whose fundamental cycle meets a
\mathcal{T}
-degree

vertex, a contradiction.
∎

A graph
\mathcal{G}
is
locally

3
-
edge connected
if for every
\{x,y\}\in E(G),
there are
3
edge-disjoint paths from

to

Lemma 3.23.

A graph is locally
3
-edge-connected if and only if each of its connected components is
3
-edge-connected.

Proof.

The if direction is Menger’s theorem. For the only if direction, look at a potential edge cut of size
2
and consider the third path between one of the cut edges.
∎

We are finally ready for our last edge deletion procedure.

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

Let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a witness to hyperfiniteness of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{T}
be the spanning subtree of
\mathcal{G}
. In light of Proposition
3.20
we may assume that
\mathcal{T}
admits a Borel selection of a unique end in a.e.
\mathcal{T}
-component (in case of selection of 2 ends, Proposition
3.20
yields a stronger conclusion). As above, extend the relative weight function
\mathbf{w}
to the edges of
\mathcal{T}
and set it to be equal to
0
for the edges in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
3.22

\mathcal{G}
is
3
-edge-connected and by Lemma
3.23
it is locally
3
-edge-connected. Thus there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that

\mu\left((y,x)\in\mathcal{G}:\exists\ 3\ \text{edge-disjoint paths in $\mathcal{G}_{N}$ between}\ x\ \text{and}\ y\right)>(1-\varepsilon)\mu(\mathcal{G}).

In each
\mathcal{G}_{N}
-connected component contract edges whose end-vertices are not contained in
3
-edge disjoint paths. This results in a Borel family of finite locally
3
-edge-connected graphs. By Lemma
3.23
these graphs are
3
-edge-connected and account for
1-\varepsilon
fraction of the total edge measure in
\mathcal{G}.

Duplicate each edge (giving each new edge the same
\mathbf{w}
-weight as it used to have), producing a
6
-edge-connected multigraph. By the Nash-Williams theorem
[
NW61
]
there are three edge-disjoint spanning trees of this multigraph. We claim that one of them corresponds to a spanning tree of the unduplicated graph that has at most
2/3
of the total
\mathbf{w}
-weight.

To see this, note that each edge in the original graph appears at most twice in the union of edges from the three spanning trees. In particular, the total
\mathbf{w}
-weight of the spanning trees is at most twice of the original weight of the graph, and hence one of the trees has at most
2/3
of the total
\mathbf{w}
-weight.

Finally, delete edges in each
\mathcal{G}_{n}
component that are not in the spanning tree we have just produced. Note that the resulting subgraph is still one-ended by Proposition

.
∎

4.  Implications for percolation theory

We briefly recall the background for percolation theory and refer the reader to
[
LP16
,
Pet17
]
for further details. Percolation theory studies random subgraphs of a given countable graph
G
. To be precise, a bond
percolation process

\mathbf{P}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is a probability measure on
2^{E}
. We say that percolation is
\Gamma
-invariant, for a subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}(G)
, if its law is invariant under the action of
\Gamma
on
G
. Percolation theory on quasi-transitive graphs dates back to the 1990s
[
BS96
,
HPS99
,
BLPS99
]
and is largely divided into two further settings: the unimodular and nonunimodular. One way to define this notion for a locally finite connected graph is to call such a graph
G

unimodular
if its automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
is unimodular (meaning that the left-invariant Haar measure is also right-invariant); otherwise,
G
is
nonunimodular
. We note that for most percolation results in the nonunimodular setting, it is enough to assume the existence of a closed nonunimodular subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}(G)
; hence, such results are often of interest even in the unimodular setting, e.g.
[
Hut20
]
.

The assumption of unimodularity is in many ways parallel to the assumption of probability measure preservation in measured combinatorics and group theory. In fact, the connection between these fields goes beyond a similarity in themes and techniques: results in one often enable advances in the other
[
Gab05
,
GL09
,
CTT22
,
TT25
,
BCR+25
]
. The main bridge between the two theories is the cluster graphing construction introduced by Gaboriau
[
Gab05
, Section 2]
, see
[
CTT22
, Section 5]
for the presentation tailored to the nonunimodular/mcp settings.

In the mcp setting, the relative weight function on a Borel graph is induced by the Radon–Nikodym cocycle of its connectedness relation with respect to the underlying measure. For a quasi-transitive graph, it is induced instead by the Haar measure defined on the group of automorphisms. Let
\Gamma
be a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
that acts quasi-transitively on
G
. The relative weight function (i.e. a cocycle)
\mathbf{w}_{\Gamma}:V^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is given by the modular function
\mathbf{w}^{y}_{\Gamma}(x)=m(\Gamma_{x})/m(\Gamma_{y})
, where
\Gamma_{v}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma v=v\}
is the stabilizer of
v\in V
and
m
is the Haar measure on
\Gamma
. It also has a more combinatorial representation

(4.1)

\mathbf{w}^{y}_{\Gamma}(x):=\frac{m(\Gamma_{x})}{m(\Gamma_{y})}=\frac{\left|\Gamma_{x}y\right|}{\left|\Gamma_{y}x\right|}.

Note that
\Gamma
is unimodular if and only if
\mathbf{w}_{\Gamma}^{x}(y)\equiv 1
for all
x,y\in V
from the same
\Gamma
-orbit.

The notions of amenability and hyperfiniteness are of classical interest in percolation theory, for formal definitions see
[
BS96
,
BLPS99
,
AL07
,
TT25
]
. The equivalences among them were shown in
[
BLPS99
]
and are analogous to the corresponding results in measured group theory
[
CFW81
,
Kai97
]
.

We are now ready to present the implication (or a translation) of Theorem
1.2
to this setting.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

The proof follows immediately from the cluster graphing construction; indeed,
[
BLPS99
, Theorem 3.9]
, and
[
TT25
, Theorem 1.4 and Lemma 4.2]
, which translate amenability of
\Gamma
to hyperfiniteness of the corresponding cluster graphing, where Theorem
1.2
applies.
Given a one-ended spanning tree of the cluster graphing, one can derive the same for
G
easily via the family of component-wise isomorphisms between the connected components of the cluster graphing and
G
.

Alternatively, one can follow our argument in proof of Theorem
1.2
almost verbatim to derive the corollary. Indeed, by
[
BLPS99
, Theorem 5.1]
the graph
G
admits a hyperfinite exhaustion. Repeating the steps in our proofs yields a
\Gamma
-invariant random spanning tree that is one-ended almost surely.

Since the proof of
[
BLPS99
, Theorem 5.1]
actually constructs the hyperfinite exhaustion as a factor of i.i.d. and the rest of our proof is deterministic, the resulting one-ended random spanning tree is also a factor of i.i.d.
∎
