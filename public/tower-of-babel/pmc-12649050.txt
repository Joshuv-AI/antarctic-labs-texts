Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells overexpressing leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) promote arteriogenesis and functional recovery in a mouse model of critical hindlimb ischemia
Authors: Girlaine Café Santos, Carine Machado Azevedo, Tiago Oliveira Ribeiro, Beatriz dos Santos Costa, Ivanilson Pimenta Santos, Mila Oliveira Nascimento, Rosane Borges Dias, Daniela Nascimento Silva, Rosalia Mendez-Otero, Vitor Fortuna, Milena Botelho Pereira Soares
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritical limb ischemia (CLI) is the most severe manifestation of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), for which there are still no effective therapeutic options. Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) can secrete bioactive molecules capable of promoting tissue regeneration and neovascularization. The therapeutic potential of MSCs can be enhanced by genetic modification to overexpress molecules of interest. Here, we produced an umbilical cord-derived MSC line overexpressing leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), MSC_LIF, and evaluated its pro-angiogenic/arteriogenic potential.MethodsMSCs were transduced using a second-generation lentiviral system to express LIF. Transgene overexpression was confirmed by qRT-PCR and ELISA. The transcriptional and secretory profile of MSC_LIF was accessed by qRT-PCR and angiogenic protein array. The pro-angiogenic/arteriogenic potential of MSC_LIF was tested by counting microvessel sprouting in the in vitro 3D aortic ring model, in addition to measuring hemoglobin with Drabkin’s reagent and quantifying CD31+ and αSMA+ vessels by fluorescence microscopy in the in vivo Matrigel plug assay. In the critical hind limb ischemia model developed in C57Bl/6 mice, ischemic score and exercise test were evaluated as functional parameters. Blood perfusion was quantified using a fluorescent vascular probe. Blood vessel distribution was assessed by quantification of CD31 and αSMA markers, and tissue damage was assessed by histological staining and qRT-PCR.ResultsGene expression analysis demonstrated that MSC_LIF upregulates strategic genes in the neovascularization process and the secretion of angiogenic proteins. MSC_LIF increased the number of microvessel sprouting in aortic rings and promoted enhanced migration of endogenous vasculature into the Matrigel plugs. Intramuscular administration of MSC_LIF was able to restore blood flow in the ischemic paw due to the formation of CD31+ blood vessels and αSMA+ arterioles. The treatment promoted protection against tissue damage and modulation of the ischemic environment through the expression of genes related to angiogenesis/arteriogenesis (such as Vegf, Mmp2 and Mmp9), inflammation (such as Il10) and myoprotection (Myog, Myod and Pax7).ConclusionLIF overexpression is a promising strategy to increase the potential of MSC in the treatment of CLI and for tissue repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04762-z.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells, LIF, Arteriogenesis, Critical hindlimb ischemia

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a chronic obstructive vascular disease characterized by reduced blood flow in the lower limbs [1]. The most severe clinical manifestation of PAD is critical limb ischemia (CLI), whose main symptoms include pain at rest, ulcers and gangrene, leading to an increased risk of cardiovascular events, amputations and death [2, 3].

Therapeutic approaches available for CLI involve the use of drugs to manage symptoms and prevent cardiovascular events or attempts to save the limb through surgical or endovascular revascularization [4]. However, 40% of patients with CLI undergo amputation within 6 months of diagnosis and the mortality rate is 25% in the first year [5]. Advanced therapies, such as cell or gene therapy, have been investigated to increase survival and improve limb perfusion in CLI patients [6].

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) are a population of cells obtained from multiple autologous or allogeneic sources and have been widely used in biological therapies for CLI due to their angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties [7–10]. The therapeutic properties of MSCs can be improved through genetic manipulation to enhance the secretion of molecules with biological activity [11].

Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) is a cytokine belonging to the Interleukin-6 (IL-6) superfamily capable of regulating several biological processes such as cell survival, proliferation and differentiation, protection/regeneration of multiple tissues, and hematopoiesis [12, 13].

More recently, we identified that LIF secretion by MSCs was related to an increase in the angiogenic potential of these cells [14], however, this effect was not investigated in a model of ischemic disease. Here, umbilical cord-derived MSCs genetically engineered to overexpress LIF were investigated for their potential to promote artery formation and functional recovery in a mouse model of critical hindlimb ischemia.

Materials and methods

Isolation and culture of human umbilical cord-derived MSC

The human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells used in this study were aproved from the ethics committee of the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (CAAE: 22734213.0.3001.5275).

Basically, MSCs were isolated from umbilical cords provided with consent after cesarean section of healthy participants at 37 weeks of gestation. The umbilical cord was disinfected with 70% ethanol in deionized water and washed in PBS solution for subsequent transport to the site of cell isolation in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) high glucose, supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Blood vessels were removed for segmentation of Wharton’s jelly by enzymatic digestion with collagenase type II (Sigma-Aldrich - Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The cell suspension was diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich - Merck) and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min to concentrate the cells. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM culture medium supplemented with 15% FBS and transferred to culture flasks. Wharton’s jelly MSC culture was maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was completely replaced every 3 days, until the monolayer reached 90% confluence, when MSCs were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) for expansion or use in subsequent experiments.

Lentivirus production and MSC genetic modification

The production of lentiviruses and transgenesis of human cells carried out in this work were assessed and approved by the Internal Biosafety Commission – CIBio of SENAI CIMATEC (CQB 561/21, approval number 002/2021). Second-generation lentiviral vectors were produced as described by Santos et al.[14]. Briefly, the second-generation lentiviral system carrying the coding sequence of the human LIF gene (LIF) was produced in HEK 293FT cells by transient co-transfection with a plasmid packaging psPAX2 (cat. no. 12260; Addgene, Massachusetts, USA), an envelope protein expressing plasmid pMD2.G (cat. no. 12259; Addgene) and the expression vector with puromycin resistance gene pEGIP (cat. no. 26777; Addgene) containing LIF or gfp as gene inserts, in a proportion of 2:1:3, respectively, using the calcium phosphate method [15]. HEK 293FT cell supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection, and suspended lentiviral particles were concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 50,000 × g for 90 min at 4 °C. The viral titers obtained were estimated as previously described [14].

MSCs were transduced by incubation with non-replicative lentiviral particles carrying the gene of interest or mock infection control particles. MSCs derived from umbilical cord at the fourth passage (P4) were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 48 h in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C, until reaching 80% confluence. MSC transduction was performed by incubating the cells with stocks of the LIF or mock lentiviruses at 1 MOI for 24 h. After this period, the culture medium was changed and the cells were cultured for 48 h before adding 2 µg/ml of Puromycin Dihydrochloride (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) to select properly transduced cells.

Lentiviral transduction of MSCs was confirmed by analysis of LIF gene expression by real-time quantitative PCR and by quantification of LIF protein in culture supernatants by ELISA.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from wild-type MSC and MSC overexpressing LIF (MSC_LIF) cell lines using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantification of RNA concentration was performed using a NanoDrop™ Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 260 nm, and samples with a ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 obtained by the A260/A280 ratio were considered of high quality for cDNA synthesis using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) after treatment with DNAse I (Thermo Scientific™). For gene expression analyses, target gene primers purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies™ (Additional file 1) and the fluorescent dye SYBR™ Green Universal Master Mix (Applied Biosystems™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used. Amplification reactions were performed in triplicate using an Applied Biosystems™ 7500 Real-Time PCR Systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under standard thermal cycling conditions, and the mean cycle threshold (Ct) values ​​were used to calculate the gene expression. the GAPDH gene was determined as a normalizer according to previous work [14].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay ELISA

Cell culture supernatants derived from MSC and MSC_LIF were collected after 24, 48 and 72 h of culture, centrifuged at 1,500 rpm to remove cellular debris and stored at -20 °C until use. LIF protein concentrations were quantified using Human LIF Quantikine® ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry analysis

The identification of surface markers characteristic of MSCs was performed using the BD Stemflow™ hMSC Analysis Kit (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA). MSC and MSC_LIF were detached from the culture flasks using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific), washed with 1x PBS, stained with Trypan Blue Solution 0.4% and counted in Countess® II FL Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, the cells were resuspended in 0.9% saline, distributed into flow cytometry tubes (1 × 106 cells per tube) and incubated for 30 min with positive antibodies: CD105 PerCP-Cy™5.5, CD73 APC, CD90 FITC, CD44PE; negative antibodies: CD45, CD34, CD11b, CD19 and HLA-DR, all PE; or isotype control antibodies, diluted at a ratio of 1:100. After the incubation period, the tubes were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, cells were washed twice with 1x PBS and resuspended in 1 ml of 0.9% saline solution. Immunophenotyping analyses were performed using an LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and at least 50,000 events were collected and analyzed.

MSC trilineage differentiation assay

For the trilineage differentiation assay, 1 × 104 MSC and MSC_LIF cells were seeded in 24-well plates and maintained with DMEM medium supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After the cells reached 50–60% confluence, the DMEM medium was replaced with the specific differentiation medium.

For adipogenesis induction, cells were cultured with 500 µl of the StemPro Adipogenesis Differentiation Kit (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific), with medium change every two days for 14 days, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and staining with Oil Red O dye (Sigma-Aldrich - Merck) to highlight fat-filled vacuoles present in the cytoplasm. For osteogenesis induction, cells were cultured with 500 µl of the StemPro Osteogenesis Differentiation Kit (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 14 days, with half of the medium changed every two days. To demonstrate the deposition of a calcium-rich matrix after differentiation, cultures were fixed in 4% PFA and stained with 2% Alizarin Red S solution (Sigma-Aldrich - Merck). For chondrogenic differentiation, cells were cultured for 21 days with 500 µl of StemPro Chondrogenesis Differentiation Kit (Gibco™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific). Glycosaminoglycan synthesis was observed by staining with Alcian Blue 8 GX solution (Sigma-Aldrich - Merck) after fixation with 4% PFA. Supplemented DMEM medium was used as a negative control for differentiation and the assays were performed in triplicate, with three independent repetitions. Images were captured using an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope with an Olympys EP50 digital camera.

Preparation of conditioned medium

To obtain conditioned media, MSC and MSC_LIF cells were cultivated in 75 cm2 culture flasks with 10 ml of EBMTM-2 medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) without the addition of factors and supplements for 72 h. The culture supernatants were then collected in tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to remove cellular debris.

For concentration, 2 ml aliquots of the conditioned medium were placed in glass vials, frozen in a freezer at -80 °C for 1 h and placed in a FreeZone 1 L Benchtop lyophilizer (Labconco, Missouri, USA) at -50 °C and a pressure of 0.050 mbar for 24 h. Total protein dosage was determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Scientific™) and the lyophilisates were reconstituted in 1x PBS at a concentration of 200 µg/ml of total proteins.

Screening of angiogenic proteins

The determination of angiogenic proteins present in the conditioned medium of MSC and MSC_LIF was performed from immunoassay using Proteome Profiler Human Angiogenesis Array Kit (R&D Systems), following the manufacturer’s specifications.

Briefly, 1 ml of the reconstituted conditioned medium was mixed with a cocktail of biotinylated detection antibodies and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The mixture was then transferred to the membrane labeled with capture antibodies for angiogenic proteins and incubated overnight at 8 °C. After this period, the marked membrane was incubated with Streptavidin-HRP for 30 min at room temperature and subjected to the revealing reagent for another 1 min. The reveal of the positive spots was carried out using an ImageQuant LAS-4000 photodocumentation camera (GE HealthCare, Illinois, USA) and quantification of the results was performed by determining the average signal (pixel density) of the pair of duplicate spots representing each protein using ImageJ software version 2.9.0 (Fiji).

Fibroblast colony-forming unit (CFU-F) assay

To quantify the clonogenic profile of MSCs, 1 × 104 MSC or MSC_LIF cells were plated in 75 cm2 culture flasks and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The culture medium was replaced every 3 days and the cells were maintained for 15 days. To detect CFU-Fs, the cells were stained with 0.01% crystal violet solution (Labsynth®, São Paulo, Brazil). Clusters with more than 50 aggregated cells were counted as colonies using an Olympus AX70 bright field microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 10x magnification.

Soft agar colony formation (SACF) assay

The soft agar tumorigenicity assay was performed in 12-well plates. 1 ml of a mixture of 2x DMEM and 1% agarose solution in a 1:1 ratio was added to each well to form the base layer (0.5% agar). The plate was kept at room temperature for 10 min to solidify the bottom layer. After this period, another 1 ml of a mixture of 2x DMEM containing 2.4 × 104 MSC or MSC_LIF cells and 0.7% agarose solution in a 1:1 ratio was added to the wells to form the top layer (0.35% agar). MRC5 and HeLa cells (2.4 × 104 cells/well) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After solidification of the upper layer, 1 ml of 1x DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin was added to each well and the cultures were maintained in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 15 days.

Sprouting aortic ring assay

All procedures performed on animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Instituto Gonçalo Moniz, FIOCRUZ (CEUA, IGM-019/2023). This work was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. The animals were kept in the IGM facility with food and water available ad libitum and 12-hour photoperiod. The mice were isogenic, specific pathogen free (SPF), immunologically competent and healthy.

The microvessel sprouting assay from mouse aortic rings was performed as previously described [16]. Male C57Bl/6 mice (n = 4) aged 4–8 weeks were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) solution and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The thoracic cavities were then opened to dissect the entire length of the artery, before insertion of the heart and lung at the anterior end, and before branching into the iliac arteries at the posterior end. Vessels were cleaned of blood and fat covering using an Olympus SZX12 stereomicroscope, cut into rings of ~ 1.0 mm thickness, and transferred to tubes containing 5 ml of unsupplemented DMEM overnight at 8 °C. Each aortic ring was randomly distributed into wells of a 48-well plate, embedded in 250 µl of fibrin gel, and incubated with MSC-conditioned medium or MSC_LIF, which was replaced every 2 days. EGMTM-2/ BulletKit™ medium supplemented with serum and growth factors (Lonza) was used as a positive control (+ CTL), and EBMTM-2basal medium (Lonza) was used as a negative control (- CTL). During 14 days, microvessel sprouting was monitored and recorded using the Leica DMi6000B (Wetzlar, Germany) inverted phase contrast microscope. The assay was performed in triplicates and the experiment was repeated three times. The number of microvessel sprouts was quantified by direct counting and the area density occupied by the sprouts was measured using the ImageJ software version 2.9.0 (Fiji).

Matrigel® plug assay

The in vivo Matrigel® (Corning® - Merck) plug implantation experiments were performed based on previous experiments [17]. Male C57Bl/6 mice (n = 4), aged 6–8 weeks, were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) solution and then subcutaneously injected, into the pelvic region, with 500 µl of a mixture of Matrigel® basement membrane matrix (phenol red-free, reduced growth factors) and conditioned medium derived from MSC and MSC_LIF in a 9:1 ratio. Each animal received two implants, totaling 8 plugs per group. 0.9% saline was used as negative control (- CTL) during the assay. After 10 days, the mice were anesthetized, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the implants were retrieved for further analysis. The experiments using animals were repeated twice.

To quantify the hemoglobin (Hb) present inside the plugs, they were homogenized in distilled water, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatants were incubated with Drabkin’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich - Merck) for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance of the mixture was measured in a SpectraMax® M2 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, California, USA) at 540 nm.

Blood vessel quantification was performed using indirect immunofluorescence of sections of the paraffin-embedded plugs. Tissues were stained with goat anti-mouse CD31 antibody (R&D Systems, diluted 1:100 in 1% BSA Triton-X 0.3% solution) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor™ 568-conjugated chicken anti-goat antibody (Invitrogen - Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:1000 in 1x PBS), and Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated rat anti-mouse αSMA antibody (eBioscience - Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:400 in 1x PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. The expression of CD31 and αSMA in the blood vessels was recorded by Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope and the quantification of the mean length/diameter of blood vessels positive for these markers was analyzed using ImagePro Plus software version 7.0 (Media Cybernetics, Maryland, USA).

Critical hindlimb ischemia model

The critical hindlimb ischemia model was induced in C57/Bl6 mice, equally divided into males and females, aged between 4 and 8 weeks (n = 10). The ischemic lesion was surgically obtained by occluding the proximal and distal portions of the femoral artery and subsequently removing the intermediate section of the vessel (Additional file 3 A), as described by Niiyama et al. [18]. Briefly, the animals were anesthetized with a solution of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) intraperitoneally and after sedation, the left paw was cleaned with 70% alcohol and the hair from the thigh region was removed. The left paw was again cleaned by alternating spraying with 70% alcohol and 0.1% iodine solution three times. The animals were placed in dorsal recumbency on a sterile surgical field under a Olympus SZX12 stereomicroscope and then an incision of approximately 2 cm in length was made in the skin, from the knee towards the medial thigh, in order to reveal the femoral artery, which was carefully separated from the femoral vein and nerve and occluded at the proximal and distal ends with a 7 − 0 silk suture using double knots. The arterial segment between the knots was cut and removed, followed by closure of the incision with 5 − 0 nylon suture. Suture asepsis was performed by spraying 0.1% iodine solution daily for up to 5 days. Immediately after surgical procedures, animals were randomly selected and treated with 1 × 106 MSC or MSC_LIF cells suspended in 50 µl of 0.9% saline solution intramuscularly through a BD Ultra-fine II 30G syringe (Becton Dickinson, New Jersey, USA). As control, 0.9% saline solution was used. The SHAM group corresponds to the animals that received the fake surgical procedure, while the NAIVE group indicates the non-induced (right) paw of each animal.

After induction of ischemic injury, animals’ health was monitored daily by tracking their weight, food and water intake, and general activity assessment. Furthermore, the animals were monitored weekly to evaluate parameters such as locomotor performance and blood perfusion (Additional file 3-B). After 21 days, the mice were anesthetized with ketamine and xylazine solution, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the gastrocnemius muscles were dissected and processed for histopathological analysis, immunofluorescence for vascular markers, and gene expression analysis. Tissue analyses were performed by blinded investigators.

Vascular labeling and bioimaging

Blood perfusion in the ischemic paw was measured by intravenous injection of the IVISense Vascular 750 probe (Revvity - PerkimElmer, Massachusetts, USA) at a concentration of 2 nmol/100 µl (n = 3). Twenty-four hours after injection of the fluorescent marker, the mice were subjected to inhalational anesthesia with 2% isoflurane and placed in dorsal decubitus on the AMI HTX (Spectral Instruments Imaging©, Arizona, USA) in vivo spectral analyzer for image acquisition at a wavelength of 745 nm. Blood perfusion was inferred by quantifying the fluorescence intensity in the region of interest (ROI) and subsequently calculating the signal difference between the naive (right) and ischemic (left) paws. Bioimage acquisition was performed 2 days before and repeated 24 h, 7 days, 14 days and 21 days after surgical procedures. Animals were included in the study if they had undergone successful femoral artery occlusion, defined by a decrease of at least 60% in blood perfusion in the induced paw compared to the naive paw 24 h after surgeries. No animals were excluded from the analysis.

Assessment of ischemic score

The ischemic score was determined as previously described [19]. Briefly, a score from 1 to 8 was assigned according to the level of ischemic involvement in the affected paw, including: cyanosis, necrosis of the toes, foot, or leg, or amputations. The interpretation of the scale is described in Fig. 6D. Assessments of the ischemic paw were performed before injury induction and 7, 14, and 21 days after injury and treatments.

Treadmill exercise test

Locomotor performance was assessed using a physical effort test on a LE8700 treadmill (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain), which induces stimulation with electric shock, allowing continuous exercise until muscle failure. The initial speed of the treadmill was set at 6 cm/s, with an increase of 6 cm/s every five minutes until the animal reached exhaustion and remained on the electrified grid for more than 5 s without responding to the electrical stimuli (10 mA). The total distance covered was calculated from the sum of the distance covered multiplied by the time obtained in each stage.

Histopathological analysis

After euthanasia, the gastrocnemius (GST) muscles were isolated, washed with 1x PBS and fixed by submersion in a 10% formalin solution for 24 h. After this period, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and processed for histopathological analysis. Histological sections of the muscle groups were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate tissue structures and picrosirius red/fast green to evaluate fibrosis.

Brightfield images were captured by an Olympus VS-110 slide scanning microscope. To determine tissue fibrosis, 10 fields per animal were quantified using the ImagePro Plus software version 7.0 (Media Cybernetics).

Immunofluorescence analysis

To perform immunofluorescent analyses, fresh muscle tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek® O.C.T. compound (Sakura Finetek USA, California, USA), rapidly cryopreserved in isopentane cooled by liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. The cryopreserved tissues were cut in a cryostat at -19 °C, the sections were fixed in 4% PFA solution and washed 3 times in 1x PBS for 5 min. The nonspecific sites were blocked by incubation with the CAS-Block™ histochemical reagent (Invitrogen™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min.

To assess the presence of CD31-positive blood vessels, sections were stained with goat anti-mouse CD31 antibody (R&D Systems, diluted 1:100 in 1% Triton-X 0.3% BSA solution) overnight at 4 °C and subsequently incubated with chicken anti-goat antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor™ 568 (Invitrogen - Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:1000 in 1x PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. To assess the presence of arterioles, sections were stained with rat anti-mouse αSMA antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor™ 488 (eBioscience - Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:400 in 1x PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Muscle tissue was labeled with Phalloidin conjugated with Alexa Fluor™ 633 (Invitrogen™ - Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:400 in 1x PBS) for 1 h at room temperature.

Images were recorded using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope and the percentage of positive area for CD31 and αSMA markers was quantified using ImagePro Plus version 7.0 software (Media Cybernetics).

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated according to literature reports (KADAM; BHALERAO, 2010), to provide at least 80% power, a Type I error probability (α) = 0.05 and a conservative effect size of 0.25.

The Shapiro-Wilk and D’Agostino-Pearson tests were used to determine whether the data sets belonged to a Gaussian distribution. Parametric data were analyzed using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test for multiple comparison. Nonparametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s multiple comparison post-hoc test.

The results of the experiments were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and the analyses were performed using GraphPad software version 5.0 (Prism, California, USA). Values ​​of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

LIF overexpression increases cell replication and expression of stemness markers in MSC

Genetic modification of umbilical cord-derived MSC cells was performed with lentiviral vectors carrying LIF or gfp control vector (MOCK) (Fig. 1A). Transduction was well tolerated by MSC, and selection with the antibiotic puromycin led to complete killing of the non-transduced control (Fig. 1C), whereas properly transduced cells survived and proliferated (Fig. 1D–E).

qRT-PCR analysis showed that MSC_LIF cells had high transgene expression compared to MSC_MOCK and wild-type MSC (Fig. 1F), and LIF protein production was found in the supernatants of MSC_LIF cells collected after 24, 48, and 72 h of culture (Fig. 1G).

The expression levels of surface markers characteristic of mesenchymal cells were assessed by flow cytometry, and both MSC and MSC_LIF lines showed high expression of the mesenchymal markers CD90, CD73, CD105 and CD44, while exhibiting low expression of the hematopoietic markers CD45, CD11b, DC19 and HLA-DR, in addition to the endothelial precursor CD34 (Fig. 1H). The trilineage differentiation assay was performed and confirmed the maintenance of the multipotency potential of the transduced cells, since MSC_LIF differentiated into all three mesodermal lineages (adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic), similarly to wild-type MSCs (Fig. 1I).

The doubling potential of MSC_LIF was investigated by the CFU-F assay, which demonstrated an increased ability of MSC_LIF to form colonies with more than 50 cells compared to MSC (Additional file 2 A, C). The Population doubling (PD) assay also demonstrated an increase in the cumulative number of cells over 15 days of culture (Population doubling number - PDN) and a reduction in doubling time (Population doubling time - PDT) for the MSC_LIF cell line compared to the wild-type cell line (Additional file 2 D, E). Gene expression analysis revealed that MSC_LIF significantly increased the expression of the cell proliferation and stemness-related genes SOX2, NANOG, OCT4, and KLF4 compared to wild-type MSC (Additional file 2 F).

The soft agar colony formation assay demonstrated that, unlike the HeLa tumor cell line, MSC and MSC_LIF did not show the ability to grow without an anchoring surface (similar to the control cell line MRC5). In addition, the expression of apoptosis regulatory genes BAX (proapoptotic) and BCL2 (anti-apoptotic) and the tumor suppressor gene P53 demonstrated that MSC_LIF, like the wild-type MSC, did not present tumorigenic characteristics Additional file 2G–K).

LIF overexpression increases the expression of pro-angiogenic markers and proteins in MSC

Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR of the investigated cell lines showed that LIF overexpression in MSCs upregulated the transcription of angiogenesis-related genes VEGF, MCP1, IL-8, and ANG, in addition to genes expressed in pericytes, such as NG2, TAGLN, and COL4A1 (Fig. 2A, B).

Secretome analysis using an angiogenic protein array demonstrated that MSC_LIF was able to secrete 11 different proteins involved in angiogenesis, and of these proteins, artemin, EGF, HGF, PIGF, serpin F1, and VEGF were significantly higher than in MSCs (Fig. 2C, D).

Some genes upregulated during collateral arterial vessel formation, such as eNOS, iNOS, and matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9, were significantly more expressed in MSC_LIF than in MSC. A significant increase in the expression of the matrix metalloproteinase inhibitor TIMP2, but not TIMP1, was also found in MSC_LIF cells (Fig. 3).

MSC_LIF induces microvessel sprouting formation in vitro

To investigate the pro-angiogenic effects of MSC_LIF on arterial tissue, a sprouting assay was performed on mouse aortic rings co-cultured with MSC and MSC_LIF. Microvessel growth from aortic explants was observed after 72 h of culture with MSC, MSC_LIF and the endothelial growth medium EGM-2 (+ CTL), but not for the wells incubated with the basal endothelial medium EBM-2 (- CTL). After 14 days, the total number of microvessels was significantly higher in the explants co-cultured with MSC_LIF compared to MSC and - CTL (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, the percentage of the culture well area occupied by vascular sprouts (microvessel density) was significantly higher for the MSC_LIF group compared to MSC and - CTL, while + CTL showed significantly higher density than all other groups (Fig. 4C).

MSC_LIF restores blood flow, reduces ischemia and improves functional performance in a model of critical hindlimb ischemia (CHI)

The ability of MSC and MSC_LIF to induce the formation of collateral arteries was investigated in an in vivo model of CHI (Additional file 3). The administration of MSC_LIF gradually increased blood flow in the ischemic paw to levels closer to the SHAM group, although without statistical significance, while treatments with MSC and vehicle (saline solution) maintained tissue perfusion statistically lower than the SAHM group throughout the experimental time. Blood flow was determined indirectly by quantifying the fluorescence emitted by a vascular probe injected intravenously (Fig. 6A, C).

The improvement of blood flow was accompanied by greater salvage of the ischemic paw (Fig. 7B), improvement in the ischemic score (Fig. 6D, E) and in locomotor performance, in addition to a gradual increase in body weight, indicative of health of the experimental specimens, after treatment with MSC_LIF compared to the other treatments (Fig. 6F, G). MSC administration was not able to significantly increase blood flow, but conferred an improvement in the ischemia score, body weight gain, and functional assessment in relation to the SALINE group.

Discussion

Critical limb ischemia (CLI) is a highly debilitating and progressive condition that affects 237 million people worldwide [20]. Therapies to restore lost vasculature are one of the main therapeutic approaches currently being investigated to reverse the harmful effects of tissue ischemia due to CLI [21]. Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) have known pro-angiogenic therapeutic properties, such as secretion of growth factors, cytokines and other biomolecules, and they also serve as excellent gene therapy vehicles [22, 23].

In this work, we developed a line of human umbilical cord-derived MSCs genetically modified to overexpress LIF. LIF overexpression did not affect the immunophenotype and multipotency profile characteristic of MSCs, while at the same time conferring changes in the cell population duplication behavior relative to wild-type cells.

LIF is a pleiotropic glycoprotein named for its ability to inhibit the proliferation of myeloid leukemic cells, but in contrast, it exhibits proliferative activity in bone marrow progenitor cells, being essential for hematopoietic recomposition [12, 24]. In MSCs, LIF has previously been described as capable of maintaining the self-renewal profile and increasing MSC proliferation through the regulation of gene expression [25, 26].

Here, the observed increase in proliferation was related to changes in the transcriptional profile of the transgenic line, such as increased expression of SOX2, NANOG, OCT4 and KLF4, genes related to stemness and cell proliferation [27]. Despite the evident increase in cell duplication, genetic transduction did not result in the loss of the need for anchorage to support cell growth or genetic alterations in the mechanisms that regulate apoptosis or tumor suppression, indicating the absence of malignant transformation in MSC_LIF.

MSC_LIF also showed increased expression of genes identified as markers of MSC pro-angiogenic potential [28] and genes expressed in perivascular cells [29], results consistent with our previous study, which expressed LIF in mouse MSC [14]. In the present study, we observed that, in addition to pro-angiogenic genes, MSC_LIF is also capable of secreting several proteins involved in angiogenesis, as well as key proteins in this process, such as VEGF, EGF and HGF [30].

Previous studies have already indicated that LIF plays a role in the regulation of VEGF in different cell types. Jahanbin et al. (2020) demonstrated that choriocarcinoma cells (JEG-3) undergo upregulation of VEGF expression after 72 h of exposure to LIF [31], while Huang et al. (2019) identified a significant increase in VEGF secretion by endometrial stromal cells (ESC) when cultured in the presence of LIF [32]. In addition, MSCs derived from human limbal stroma demonstrated increased expression of the perivascular markers PDGFR-B and ACTA2 when cultured and expanded in the presence of LIF and b-FGF [33]. In contrast, there is little evidence to support LIF-mediated EGF expression. Some embryonic implantation studies have established a possible synergistic role between LIF and EGF [34, 35]. Song and collaborators identified that LIF produced by both endometrium and blastocyst increased EGF expression [36], while Cheng et al. established that EGF may assume a compensatory role in the absence of LIF during embryonic development [34]. These effects can be explained by a possible cross-talk between EGF cellular signaling with IL-6 family members, such as LIF and IL-6 itself, via activation of the JAK/STAT3 pathway, which induced increased expression of these cytokines as well as the chains of their receptor complexes IL-6R α, LIFR α and GP130 [37]. Despite the aforementioned evidence, the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of LIF on the expression of angiogenic growth factors, such as VEGF and EGF, in MSCs are not well understood and still require further investigation.

However, angiogenesis does not support perfusion compensation in the face of arterial occlusion. Thus, vessel formation by collateral arterial growth is related to vascular restoration of tissue after PAD [38]. Here, we demonstrated that MSC_LIF also has higher expression of markers related to arteriogenesis, such as eNOS, iNOS, and the matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9. A previous study evaluating the composition of the decidual extracellular matrix during trophoblast implantation demonstrated that LIF was responsible for increasing the expression of MMP-2 and 9, while reducing their tissue inhibitors (TIMPs) [39].

The effects of LIF on collateral artery formation have not been determined, but studies have shown that arterial tissue responds to LIF treatment in different ways. Rolfe et al. identified that LIF administration was responsible for inhibiting the occurrence or progression of atherosclerotic lesions in rabbit carotid arteries [40]. Furthermore, LIF expression levels were 11-fold higher in arterial segments of Ossabaw miniature pigs affected by in-stent neointimal hyperplasia, indicating that LIF upregulation may be a compensatory mechanism for this pathological condition [41].

To evaluate the arteriogenic effect of MSC_LIF, we performed a three-dimensional culture model of mouse large vessel (aortic) explants. Our results demonstrated that co-culture of MSC_LIF stimulated microvessel sprouting and migration, indicating direct activity of the cells in arterial tissues. In addition, in vivo assays with Matrigel plugs stimulated with conditioned medium derived from MSC_LIF showed a greater presence of blood vessels, evidenced by both Hb measurement and CD31 staining, similar to that previously described [14]. Moreover, the presence of αSMA+ arterioles inside the plugs was demonstrated in this study.

To confirm the arteriogenic effects of MSC and MSC_LIF, we used an experimental model of critical hindlimb ischemia in mice. The CHI model performed was efficient in reproducing the most severe effects of critical limb ischemia, such as necrosis and loss of the affected limb [3], symptoms observed in the experimental groups treated with the vehicle. In this model, blood flow was gradually improved after intramuscular injection of MSC_LIF and the return of perfusion was related to the improvement of the ischemic score [19] and the locomotor capacity of the animals in a treadmill walking test. Furthermore, MSC_LIF infusion increased blood vessel density in muscle tissue, an effect observed in the GST muscle, distal to the site of ischemia. Treatment with recombinant LIF or cells transfected to express the cytokine promoted beneficial effects in different models of ischemic diseases, such as myocardial infarction and cerebral ischemia, however such events were related to tissue protection, but not to the formation of new blood vessels [42–44].

Our results demonstrated that not only CD31+ capillaries, but also αSMA+ arterioles were found in greater proportion in tissues stimulated with MSC_LIF, indicating the arteriogenic potential of the treatment. Our results suggest that the effect generated by MSC_LIF treatment was primarily due to a paracrine effect, since the conditioned medium derived from MSC_LIF exhibited pro-angiogenic and arteriogenic activity, as observed by the Matrigel plug assay. Moreover, a preclinical study of human MSCs injected intramuscularly in the CLI model demonstrated that the transplanted cells were able to persist in the tissues up to three weeks, displaying high expression of von Willebrand factor (vWF) [45]. Further studies are needed to investigate the persistence and fate of the MSC_LIF cells in the CLI model.

Some studies have reported that the GST muscle is the most affected by ischemic insult during CHI [46, 47]. Here, we performed an analysis of this tissue to determine whether treatment with MSC_LIF would be able to confer myoprotection. Revascularization caused by MSC_LIF provided a reduction in muscle atrophy, evidenced by the decrease in GST muscle weight in the MSC and SALINE groups. In addition, histopathological analysis showed greater preservation of typical muscle tissue structures in the MSC_LIF group, similar to the SHAM and NAIVE groups, while SALINE presented important tissue alterations, such as atrophy, chronic inflammation and fibrosis. Treatment with both MSC and MSC_LIF was effective in reducing the expression of inflammatory markers in the tissue, however only MSC_LIF conferred a potent upregulation of the anti-inflammatory marker Il10, reinforcing the role of LIF in the immunomodulatory ability exhibited by MSC [48]. The reduction in tissue damage observed after MSC_LIF treatment was accompanied by other protective/regenerative effects, such as increased gene expression of the enzyme Sod2 and the muscle regeneration genes Myod, Myog, and Pax7, indicating that LIF therapy conferred resistance to oxidative stress and supported muscle regeneration.

Finally, the expression of angiogenic/arteriogenic factors was significantly upregulated in muscle tissues treated with MSC_LIF, reinforcing the ability of these cells to modulate the ischemic environment toward the formation of new blood vessels. The analysis of Hif1a gene expression indicated a greater capacity for resolving tissue ischemia by MSC_LIF compared to the other treatments, and the high expression of Stat3 in the MSC_LIF group may be related to the intense activation of the LIF signaling pathway throughout the experimental period.

Taken together, these data suggest that the transcriptional and secretory profile of MSC and, consequently, the therapeutic efficacy of this cell can be modulated by LIF overexpression, confirming not only the findings observed in our previous work [14], but also expanding the therapeutic potential of MSC_LIF in a model of ischemic disease. However, many questions still remain unanswered, such as the molecular and signaling mechanisms underlying the increased angiogenic and arteriogenic potential of MSC_LIF compared to control MSC, as well as the graft retention period and the mechanism of recruitment/induction of new vessels in vivo.

Conclusion

In this study, it was proposed that overexpression of LIF in MSC could have arteriogenic potential, the most important biological effect in CLI therapies. Here, we found that MSC_LIF increased the transcriptional levels of pro-angiogenic and arteriogenic biomarkers, as well as the secretion of key angiogenic regulatory molecules, promoting the restoration of vasculature and protection of skeletal muscle, which resulted in greater salvage of the ischemic limb and improvements in locomotor function.
