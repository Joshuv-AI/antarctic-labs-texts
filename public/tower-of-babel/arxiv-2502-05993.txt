1.
Introduction

Given a power series
F(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}f_{i}q^{i}

or simply a sequence of numbers
F=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}}
, by the
Hankel determinants of
F
we mean the doubly indexed family

(1.1)

\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}(F):=\left|\begin{matrix}{f}_{\ell}&{f}_{\ell+1}&\cdots&{f}_{\ell+j-1}\\[4.0pt]
{f}_{\ell+1}&{f}_{\ell+2}&\cdots&{f}_{\ell+j}\\[4.0pt]
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
{f}_{\ell+j-1}&{f}_{\ell+j}&\cdots&{f}_{\ell+2j-2}\end{matrix}\right|

where
\ell,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, with the convention
\Delta^{(\ell)}_{0}(F):=1
. The determinant
\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}(F)
is thus of size
j\times j
, and we call the number
\ell
the
shift
of the determinant, since it is clear that

\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}\bigl((f_{i})_{i\geqslant 0})\bigr)=\Delta_{j}^{(0)}\bigl((f_{i+\ell})_{i\geqslant 0})\bigr).

Similarly, if
F(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f_{i}q^{i}
is a power series, we have

(1.2)

\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}(F)=\Delta_{j}^{(0)}(F^{(\ell)})

where

F^{(\ell)}(q):=\frac{1}{q^{\ell}}\left(F(q)-\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}f_{i}q^{i}\right)=\frac{1}{q^{\ell}}\left(\sum_{i=\ell}^{+\infty}f_{i}q^{i}\right).

When
\ell=0
, we will often use the shorter notation
\Delta_{j}(F)
instead of
\Delta^{(0)}_{j}(F)
and we speak of
ordinary Hankel determinants
of
F
. On the other hand we will write simply
\Delta^{(\ell)}(F)
to denote the entire sequence
(\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}(F))_{j\geqslant 0}
.

In this article we would like to present the remarkable properties of Hankel determinants of a family of power series

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\qquad\text{(golden ratio)},

\Delta_{j+2n(n+1)}^{(\ell)}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{j}^{(\ell)}\quad\text{for all }j\geqslant 0.

This result reflects the periodicity of the
H
-fraction expansion (
1.20
) but also reveals some symmetric patterns inside it; see
Section

3.3
.
Explicit formulas for the Hankel determinants
\Delta_{j}^{(\ell)}
will be given in
Theorem

5.1
for the case
\ell=0
, together with a few examples for small values of
n
; cases
\ell=1,\ldots,n+1
will then follow from
Theorem

D
below.

Now we describe the dynamics of the Hankel sequences:

Theorem C

(Gale-Robinson recurrence)
.

Sequences
\Delta^{(\ell)}
with
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n+1\}
all satisfy the same following three-term Gale-Robinson recurrence:

\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j+2n+2}\,\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j}=\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j+2n+1}\,\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j+1}-\bigl(\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j+n+1}\bigr)^{2}\quad\text{for all }j\geqslant 0.

Recall that a
three-term Gale-Robinson sequence
is a sequence
(S_{j})
defined by a recurrence of the form

(1.21)

S_{j}S_{j+k}=\alpha S_{j+a}S_{j+k-a}+\beta S_{j+b}S_{j+k-b},

where
k\geqslant 4
,
1\leqslant a<b\leqslant k/2
and
\alpha,\beta
are some integer constants. Similarly, a
four-term Gale-Robinson sequence
is defined by allowing a third term
\gamma S_{j+c}S_{j+k-c}
in the right-hand side of (
1.21
); see
[
Gal91
]
. One can see at once that Somos-4 sequences and Somos-6 sequences (definition (
1.16
)) are special instances of three-term Gale-Robinson sequences and four-term Gale-Robinson sequences, respectively. Two such examples are thus provided by the Hankel determinants
\Delta^{(\ell)}(\Phi_{n})
(
\ell=0,1,\ldots,n+1
), for
n=1
(remind (
1.15
)) and
n=2
(see
[
OP25
]
§1.4).

While Somos sequences arose from elliptic function theory and can be studied from this point of view (see e.g.
[
Hon07
,
HS08
]
), some of their properties are best understood with S. Fomin & A. Zelevinsky’s theory of cluster algebras, and in particular the
Laurent phenomenon

[
FZ02
]
which implies that all (three or four-term) Gale-Robinson sequences with positive integer coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and initial data
S_{0}=S_{1}=\cdots=S_{k-1}=1
have the property of
integrality
, i.e. all terms of such a sequence are integers. This approach gave a unifying proof of the integrality of Somos-
k
sequences for
k=4,5,6,7
, which was already known to several authors.

Another main property of Somos-Gale-Robinson sequences is that they exhibit solutions to discrete dynamical systems which are integrable in the sense of Liouville-Arnold
[
FH14
,
HLK20
]
. In this way, Hankel determinants of
q
-deformed metallic numbers provide examples of
periodic

\{-1,0,1\}
-solutions of the corresponding discrete integrable systems; see the detailed example of
\Phi_{1}(q)
in
[
OP25
]
, §1.4.
Lastly, let us add that many examples of Somos sequences are produced by Hankel determinants, see
[
CHX15
,
Hon21
]
and the references therein.

Let us come back to the presentation of our results. As we have seen,
Theorem

C
points out special relations existing between consecutive Hankel determinants inside the same
\ell
-shifted sequence. Incidentally, we have also discovered an explicit formula connecting the members of two consecutive shifted sequences, which implies in particular that all shifted sequences are completely determined by the ordinary Hankel determinant sequence
\Delta^{(0)}=\Delta(\Phi_{n})
:

Theorem D

(Contiguity relations)
.

Pairs of consecutive shifted sequences
(\Delta^{(\ell+1)},\Delta^{(\ell)})
with
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
are interconnected by the following formula:

(1.22)

\Delta^{(\ell+1)}_{j}=(-1)^{j+\frac{n(n+2\ell-1)}{2}}\Delta^{(\ell)}_{j+n+1}\quad\text{for all }j\geqslant 0.

Note that Theorems
B
,
C
and

were proved by V. Ovsienko and the second author in

for

for any

The reader has certainly noticed that all our statements deal with shifted sequences

for a parameter

such that

. As far as concerns the case

Conjecture E

The case

2.
Background material

2.1.
S. Morier-Genoud and V. Ovsienko’s
q
-numbers

Our article is devoted to the continuous fraction expansion and Hankel determinant properties of the
q
-deformation
of particular real numbers. Let us define precisely what kind of quantization is meant here and give a brief introduction to the subject.

Quantization of integers is a very classical topic which goes back to Euler and Gauss: any integer
n\geqslant 0
can be deformed as a polynomial

(2.1)

[n]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{n-1}.

Equivalently, one can define
[n]_{q}
as the unique solution of the recurrence

(2.2)

[n+1]_{q}=q[n]_{q}+1,\qquad[0]_{q}=0.

First form of definition (
2.1
) can also be used to define
q
-deformation of negative integers
n<0
:

[n]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}=-q^{-1}-q^{-2}-\cdots-q^{n}

and this gives a polynomial in
q^{-1}
.
A considerable amount of related objects have been based on definition (
2.1
), e.g.
q
-factorials,
q
-binomials,
q
-hypergeometric functions,
q
-calculus, etc. used in combinatorics, number theory, fractals, operator theory, mathematical physics… But until recently we missed a really satisfactory extension to more general numbers (reals, or even just rationals). For instance, for a rational
m/n
, the naïve ideas

\left[\frac{m}{n}\right]_{q}:=\frac{[m]_{q}}{[n]_{q}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\left[\frac{m}{n}\right]_{q}:=\frac{1-q^{m/n}}{1-q}

both lead to the same notion (up to a rescaling) which lacks crucial properties, such as verifying (
2.2
) or being a rational function of
q
; it is then difficult to have nice enumerative or geometric interpretations.

In 2018, S. Morier-Genoud and V. Ovsienko began a beautiful construction of

q
-analogues for rational, real and even complex numbers (see the seminal papers
[
MGO20
,
MGO22
,
Ovs21
]
) which in a very few years has shown to be successful in the mathematical community, thanks to connections with many topics: enumerative combinatorics, cluster algebras, Markov-Hurwitz approximation theory, braid groups, combinatorics of posets, Calabi-Yau triangulated categories, Lie algebras of differential operators, supersymmetry and supergeometry… see e.g.
[
MSS21
,
BBL23
,
CO23
,
KOR23
,
MO24
,
MGOV24
,
Tho24
,
KMR+25
,
Jou25
]
.

Several equivalent models are available for Morier-Genoud & Ovsienko’s
q
-real numbers. One of them involves continued fractions and is the best suited for our purposes.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and consider its regular continued fraction expansion (finite if and only if
x\in\mathbb{Q}
)

x\;=\;a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{2}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{3}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

with
a_{i}
integers, positive for
i\geqslant 1
. The
q
-deformation
or
q
-analogue
of
x
is the following algebraic continued fraction:

(2.3)

[x]_{q}:=[a_{0}]_{q}+\frac{q^{a_{0}}}{[a_{1}]_{q^{-1}}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{q^{-a_{1}}}{[a_{2}]_{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{q^{a_{2}}}{[a_{3}]_{q^{-1}}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{q^{-a_{3}}}{[a_{4}]_{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

where
[n]_{q}
stands for the
q
-integer as in (
2.1
), and
[n]_{q^{-1}}=q^{1-n}[n]_{q}
is the same expression with reciprocal parameter.
Note that, when infinite, the right-hand side in (
2.3
) always converges in the sense of formal Laurent series.

Let us indicate some important characteristics of these
q
-numbers; we cite the original references but most of the properties are explained also in the short survey
[
Ovs23
]
.

1)

[
MGO20
]
For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
[x]_{q}
coincides with the classical definition (
2.1
) of Euler and Gauss.

2)

[
MGO22
]
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the Laurent series
[x]_{q}
has integer coefficients. More precisely the quantification map
[\,\cdot\,]_{q}
has the following images

[\,\cdot\,]_{q}:\quad\mathbb{Q}_{\geqslant 0}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}(q),\quad\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}(q),\quad\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\to\mathbb{Z}[[q]],\quad\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}(\!(q)\!).

3)

[
MGO22
,
LMG21
]
We have, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

[x+1]_{q}=q[x]_{q}+1,\qquad\left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{q}=-\frac{1}{q[x]_{q}}.

These two crucial properties can be interpreted as commutation of the quantification map
[\,\cdot\,]_{q}
with the action on
\mathbb{Z}(\!(q)\!)\cup\{\infty\}
of a group of linear-fractional transformations which is isomorphic to the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover,
[\,\cdot\,]_{q}
is the unique
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant quantification which fixes
0
; see
[
OP25
]
, §2.2. Such an invariance is at the heart of the construction and plays a crucial role in the proof of numerous properties of these
q
-numbers. Also, it can be understood as invariance with respect to the Burau representation of
the braid group
B_{3}

[
BBL23
,
MGOV24
]
;

4)

The radius of convergence of the Taylor series at
0
of the
q
-golden number
\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right]_{q}=\Phi_{1}(q)
equals
\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
, and it is conjectured that the radius of convergence of the Taylor series at
0
of the
q
-deformation of any positive real number is always
\geqslant\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}

[
LMGOV24
]
. This statement has already been verified for all

-metallic numbers
\Phi_{n}(q)
(see
[
Ren22
,

]
) and for a large class of

[

]
, §4.4).
For general rational or real numbers, progress has been made recently, see
[
EGMS24
,
Eti25
]
.

5)

[
LMG21
]
When
x=\frac{r\pm\sqrt{p}}{s}
is a quadratic irrational number (
p,r,s
integers,
p>0
),
[x]_{q}
is itself of the form

(2.4)

[x]_{q}=\frac{R(q)\pm\sqrt{P(q)}}{S(q)}

with
P,R,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
and

a palindrome.

Examples illustrating formula (

-deformation

of the metallic numbers

,

from which follows at once a formula of type (

\Phi_{n}(q)=\frac{1}{2q}\left(q[n]_{q}+(q^{n}+1)(q-1)+\sqrt{\Bigl(q[n]_{q}+(q^{n}+1)(q-1)\Bigr)^{2}+4q}\right).

See
[

]
or Proposition 2.5 in

for details. Another important property we will use is that

admits the following Taylor series expansion about zero:

(2.6)

\Phi_{n}(q)=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}+q^{2n}+\sum_{i=2n+1}^{+\infty}\kappa_{i}q^{i},

where coefficients

2.2.
H
-fractions and their Hankel determinants

In this subsection we recall some material from
[
Han16
]
. Fix a field
\mathbb{F}
, an indeterminate
q
, and let
\delta
be a positive integer. A
super
\delta
-fraction
is a continued fraction of the form

(2.7)

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}\,q^{k_{0}}}{1+qu_{1}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{1}\,q^{k_{0}+k_{1}+\delta}}{1+qu_{2}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{2}\,q^{k_{1}+k_{2}+\delta}}{1+qu_{3}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{3}\,q^{k_{2}+k_{3}+\delta}}{1+qu_{4}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
,
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{F}[q]
are polynomials such that
\deg(u_{i})\leqslant k_{i-1}+\delta-2
.

One of the main interests of this notion is that, not only (
2.7
) gives always a power series, but also, conversely, for a given positive integer
\delta
, any power series admits one and only one super
\delta
-fraction expansion (finite if and only if
F
is rational). We will need to recall the proof of this result at the beginning of
Section

3.1
.

Example 2.2
.

According to Theorem 3.2 in
[
OP25
]
, for any
n\geqslant 1
the
q
-deformed metallic number
\Phi_{n}
admits the following
1
-periodic expression as a super
3
-fraction:

\Phi_{n}(q)=[n]_{q}+\frac{q^{2n}}{\langle n\rangle_{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\left(\frac{q^{2n+1}}{\langle n\rangle_{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\right)^{*}

where
\langle n\rangle_{q}
was defined in (
1.19
). To be accurate, it is the shifted function

\Phi_{n}^{(n+1)}(q)=\frac{\Phi_{n}(q)-[n]_{q}}{q^{n+1}}

(see definition (
1.2
)) which matches exactly definition (
2.7
) of a super
3
-fraction, with
k_{j}\equiv n-1
for
j\geqslant 0
,
v_{0}=1
and
v_{j}\equiv-1
for
j\geqslant 1
.

Super
\delta
-fractions with
\delta=2
are of special importance, they are called
Hankel continued fractions
, or more simply
H
-fractions
. Firstly, they generalize the classical notion of regular
C
-fraction and regular
J
-fraction (when all
k_{i}
are zero and
\delta=1
,
\delta=2
, respectively) and offer the possibility to compute the Hankel determinants of
F
with the following striking formula (see Theorem 2.1 of
[
Han16
]
): if we define

(2.8)

s_{p}:=p+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}k_{i}\quad\text{if }p\geqslant 1,\quad s_{0}:=0,\quad\mathcal{S}:=\{s_{p},\ p\geqslant 0\}

and

(2.9)

\varepsilon_{p}:=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\frac{k_{i}\left(k_{i}+1\right)}{2}\quad\text{if }p\geqslant 1\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{0}:=0,

then

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{s_{p}}(F)=(-1)^{\varepsilon_{p}}v_{0}^{s_{p}}v_{1}^{s_{p}-s_{1}}v_{2}^{s_{p}-s_{2}}\cdots v_{p-1}^{s_{p}-s_{p-1}}&if $p\geqslant 1$,\\
\Delta_{j}\left(F\right)=0&if $j\notin\mathcal{S}$.\end{cases}

(Recall that, by convention above,
\Delta_{s_{0}}(F)=\Delta_{0}(F)=1
.) In this way, classical formulas known for
C
-fractions and
J
-fractions (see e.g. Theorems 29 and 30 in
[
Kra05
]
) are generalized. Let us mention that similar formulas to (
2.10
) had been studied earlier in several references (mostly independently); see
[
Han20
]
, the historical remark after Theorem 2.2.

Besides formula (
2.10
), a second reason for which we think that Hankel continued fractions have a particular interest is their strong connection with a classical continued fraction expansion in the field
\mathbb{F}(\!(q)\!)
of Laurent series. Let us elaborate, since this relation was not yet discovered in the paper
[
Han16
]
.

E. Artin has shown
[
Art24
]
that any Laurent series in
q

f(q)=f_{-n}q^{-n}+\cdots+f_{-1}q^{-1}+f_{0}+f_{1}q+f_{2}q^{2}+\cdots

can be expanded, in a unique manner, as a continued fraction of the form

(2.11)

f(q)=a_{0}(1/q)+\frac{1}{a_{1}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{2}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{3}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

where
a_{0}
is a polynomial and, for any
j\geqslant 1
,
a_{j}
is a non constant polynomial. Artin’s expansion is traditionnaly called the
regular continued fraction expansion
of
f
, because of the familiarity with the classical notion of regular continued fraction expansion for real numbers
3
3

3

Usually, Artin’s theorem is stated in the field
\mathbb{F}(\!(1/q)\!)
of Laurent series in the variable
1/q
but we adapt it to our context.
.

It turns out that regular continued fractions and Hankel continued fractions are closely and simply related when we restrict to power series. Take such a function

f(q)=f_{1}q+f_{2}q^{2}+f_{3}q^{3}+\cdots

with no constant term

and write its regular expansion (
2.11
):

(2.12)

f(q)=\frac{1}{a_{1}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{2}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{a_{3}(1/q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

Fix
j\geqslant 1
. Since
a_{j}
is a non constant polynomial, there exist two natural numbers
m_{j}
and
n_{j}
, with
1\leqslant n_{j}\leqslant m_{j}
, and a family of scalars
(c_{j,r})_{r=n_{j}}^{m_{j}}
, with
c_{j,n_{j}}\not=0
and
c_{j,m_{j}}\not=0
, such that

a_{j}(1/q)=\frac{c_{j,n_{j}}}{q^{n_{j}}}+\frac{c_{j,n_{j}+1}}{q^{n_{j}+1}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{j,m_{j}}}{q^{m_{j}}}.

We then write

a_{j}(1/q)=\bigl(1+u_{j}(q)q\bigr)\frac{c_{j,m_{j}}}{q^{m_{j}}}

where

u_{j}(q)=\frac{c_{j,n_{j}}}{c_{j,m_{j}}}q^{m_{j}-n_{j}-1}+\frac{c_{j,n_{j}+1}}{c_{j,m_{j}}}{q^{m_{j}-n_{j}-2}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{j,m_{j}-1}}{c_{j,m_{j}}}

is a polynomial in
q
satisfying

(2.13)

\deg(u_{j})=m_{j}-n_{j}-1\leqslant m_{j}-1.

But then (
2.12
) reads

\displaystyle f(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+u_{1}(q)q)\frac{c_{1,m_{1}}}{q^{m_{1}}}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{(1+u_{2}(q)q)\frac{c_{2,m_{2}}}{q^{m_{2}}}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{(1+u_{3}(q)q)\frac{c_{3,m_{3}}}{q^{m_{3}}}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1,m_{1}}^{-1}{q^{m_{1}}}}{1+u_{1}(q)q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{c_{1,m_{1}}^{-1}c_{2,m_{2}}^{-1}{q^{m_{1}+m_{2}}}}{1+u_{2}(q)q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{c_{2,m_{2}}^{-1}c_{3,m_{3}}^{-1}{q^{m_{2}+m_{3}}}}{1+u_{3}(q)q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

This expression is exactly of the form

f(q)=q\,F(q)

where

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}\,q^{k_{0}}}{1+qu_{1}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{1}\,q^{k_{0}+k_{1}+2}}{1+qu_{2}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{2}\,q^{k_{1}+k_{2}+2}}{1+qu_{3}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

and

(2.14)

\begin{cases}v_{0}=c_{1,m_{1}}^{-1}&\\
v_{j}=-\left(c_{j,m_{j}}c_{j+1,m_{j+1}}\right)^{-1}&for $j\geqslant 1$\\
k_{j}=m_{j+1}-1&for $j\geqslant 0$.\end{cases}

Since
\deg(u_{j})\leqslant k_{j-1}
by (
2.13
), the expression of
F(q)
above is a
H
-fraction expansion (take (
2.7
) with
\delta=2
) for the parameters (
2.14
).

In conclusion, Artin’s regular continued fraction expansion for the power series without constant term
f(q)
yields a Hankel continued fraction expansion for the power series
F(q)=f(q)/q
. And conversely, the Hankel continued fraction expansion for any power series
g(q)
yields the regular continued fraction expansion for
(g(q)-g(0))/q
. We thus have a correspondance between the two kinds of expansion and it is easy to switch from one model to the other, thanks to the conversion formulas (
2.14
).

As an example, consider a regular
J
-fraction expansion

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}}{1+u_{1}q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{1}\,q^{2}}{1+u_{2}q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{2}\,q^{2}}{1+u_{3}q}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

where
u_{i},v_{i}
are scalars and
v_{i}
are nonzero. It is nothing but a
H
-fraction with all
k_{j}=0
, and if

f(q)=f_{0}+qF(q),

then
f(q)
admits a regular continued fraction (
2.12
) with all
a_{j}
polynomials of degree
1
and determined by the parameters of the
J
-fraction as follows:

f(q)=f_{0}+\frac{1}{c_{1}+\frac{d_{1}}{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{c_{2}+\frac{d_{2}}{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{1}{c_{3}+\frac{d_{3}}{q}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

with

\begin{cases}d_{j}\neq 0&for $j\geqslant 0$\\
v_{0}=d_{1}^{-1}&\\
v_{j}=-(d_{j}d_{j+1})^{-1}&for $j\geqslant 1$\\
u_{j}=c_{j}/d_{j}&for $j\geqslant 0$.\end{cases}

Such a transformation for regular
J
-fractions may be considered as folklore and not necessarily connected to Artin’s expansion; it appears e.g. in
[
Wal48
]
, §42.

3.
The Hankel continued fraction expansion for
\Phi_{n}

We already recalled the examples of
H
-fractions (
1.17
) and (
1.18
) that were obtained for
\Phi_{1}(q)
and
\Phi_{2}(q)
in
[
OP25
]
. In this section we prove that the
H
-fraction presentation for
\Phi_{n}
stated in
Theorem

A
is actually valid for all
n\geqslant 3
. It is a quite difficult problem, and we will solve it by applying a variant of an algorithm which was introduced in
[
Han16
]
. It was originally designed to treat quadratic power series in coefficients in a finite field, but actually works perfectly for any field
\mathbb{F}
and in particular in our situation
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
.

3.1.
An algorithm for
H
-fractions

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field,
q
an indeterminate. Here we present an algorithm which is tailored to produce explicit
H
-fractions for a certain class of power series
F\in\mathbb{F}[[q]]
, including our particular function
\Phi_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[[q]]
.

To begin with, let us explain how any power series
F\in\mathbb{F}[[q]]
can we written, in a unique way, as a
H
-fraction, i.e. a super
\delta
-fraction (
2.7
) with
\delta=2
(actually the same argument works for any
\delta
, see the proof of Theorem 2.1 in
[
Han16
]
).

First, one roughly expands the power series
F(q)
as

F(q)=v_{0}q^{k_{0}}+O(q^{k_{0}+1})

where
v_{0}\neq 0
and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
, so that
F(q)/(v_{0}q^{k_{0}})=1+O(q)
. Hence one can write

(3.1)

\frac{v_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{F(q)}=D_{0}(q)-q^{k_{0}+2}F_{1}(q)

with
D_{0}
a polynomial such that
D_{0}(0)=1
and
\deg(D_{0})\leqslant k_{0}+1
, and
F_{1}(q)
a power series. Notice that the condition on the degree makes
D_{0}
and
F_{1}
uniquely defined. Equivalently we have

(3.2)

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)-q^{k_{0}+2}F_{1}(q)}

and repeating the process with
F_{1}
will give similar objects
v_{1},k_{1},D_{1},F_{2}
such that

F_{1}(q)=\frac{v_{1}q^{k_{1}}}{D_{1}(q)-q^{k_{1}+2}F_{2}(q)}

hence

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{1}q^{k_{0}+k_{1}+2}}{D_{1}(q)-q^{k_{1}+2}F_{2}(q)}.

Going on with this process inductively will lead to the Hankel continued fraction

(3.3)

F(q)=\frac{v_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{1}q^{k_{0}+k_{1}+2}}{D_{1}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{-v_{2}q^{k_{1}+k_{2}+2}}{D_{2}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathord{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\cdots$}}

Thus we have proved that any power series
F(q)
can be developed (uniquely) as a
H
-fraction, and next problem is to compute explicitly data sequences
(v_{i})
,
(k_{i})
and
(D_{i})
from
F
. We do not know the answer in general, but when the power series is
quadratic
in the sense that there exists three polynomials
A,B,C\in\mathbb{F}[q]
such that

(3.4)

A+BF+CF^{2}=0

(together with some conditions on
A,B,C
that will be described soon) an algorithm will help us to do that.
Of course, it will only be usable in case of a finite number of steps. This situation can happen when sequences of data are finite by nature (e.g. when the field is finite; see the original applications in
[
Han16
]
) or when the super fraction is going to be
periodic
(or
ultimately periodic
), which will be our case when considering
F=\Phi_{n}
, although the field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
is infinite.

Let us elaborate. Suppose from now on that
F
satisfies the quadratic equation (
3.4
) together with the following conditions

(3.5)

A\neq 0,\quad B(0)=1,\quad C\neq 0,\quad C(0)=0.

(Recall from (
2.5
) that this is the case for
F=\Phi_{n}
.) Then the quadratic equation (
3.4
) with conditions (
3.5
) has a unique solution

F=\frac{-B+\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2C}.

According to (
3.1
) we need to know the first term of the expansion of
F(q)
about
q=0
. Because

F(q)=\frac{-A(q)}{B(q)+C(q)F(q)}

with conditions (
3.5
), we see that this amounts to know the lower degree term of
A(q)
. Writing the nonzero polynomial
A
as

(3.6)

A(q)=a_{k}q^{k}+O(q^{k+1})

with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
a_{k}\not=0
, the left-hand side of (
3.1
) reads

\displaystyle\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}}{F(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2a_{k}q^{k}C}{-B+\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2a_{k}q^{k}C(\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}+B)}{-4AC}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}C}{A}\times\frac{-B-\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2C}.

(From now on, for polynomials we will often write
P
instead of
P(q)
, for easier reading.) Let

\Gamma(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}q^{j}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4q}}{2q}

be the generating function of the Catalan numbers (
1.3
). We calculate

\Gamma\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)=\frac{B}{A}\times\frac{B-\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2C}

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{-B-\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{B}{C}-\frac{-B+\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{B}{C}+\frac{A}{B}\>\Gamma\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{B}{C}+\frac{A}{B}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)^{j}.

Inserting this equality in (
3.7
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}}{F(q)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}C}{A}\biggl(-\frac{B}{C}+\frac{A}{B}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)^{j}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}B}{A}\biggl(-1+\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)^{j+1}\biggr).

Now, as explained in (
3.1
) there exists a unique polynomial
D
with
D(0)=1
and
\deg(D)\leqslant k+1
, and a unique power series
R_{1}
such that

\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}}{F(q)}=D-q^{k+2}R_{1}(q)

hence

(3.8)

\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}B}{A}\biggl(-1+\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)^{j+1}\biggr)=D(q)-q^{k+2}R_{1}(q).

Recall that we want to compute explicitly the polynomial
D
since it will produce the denominator in the first step (
3.2
) of the
H
-fraction expansion of
F
. To do so, remind from (
3.5
) that we have
B=1+O(q)
and
C=O(q)
. Together with (
3.6
) this implies

\frac{AC}{B^{2}}=\frac{[a_{k}q^{k}+O(q^{k+1})]\cdot O(q)}{[1+O(q)]^{2}}=a_{k}O(q^{k+1})+O(q^{k+2})

so that
\left(\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)^{j+1}=O(q^{k+2})
for all
j\geqslant 1
. In other words
the series in the left-hand side of (
3.8
) can contribute to
D
only with its first term, and there exists a power series
R_{2}
such that

\frac{-a_{k}q^{k}B}{A}\left(-1+\frac{AC}{B^{2}}\right)=D-q^{k+2}R_{2}(q).

Writing
C(q)=c_{1}q+c_{2}q^{2}+\cdots+c_{d}q^{d}
and remembering
B=1+O(q)
, we conclude that
D
is uniquely determined by the following conditions:

(3.9)

\deg(D)\leqslant k+1\quad\text{and}\quad a_{k}q^{k}\frac{B}{A}-a_{k}c_{1}q^{k+1}=D-q^{k+2}R_{3}(q)

for some power series
R_{3}
. Property that
D(0)=1
will be automatic, by (
3.1
).

All of this leads us to formulate the following definition, already introduced in
[
Han16
]
, but refined here in the special case
\delta=2
, i.e. when super fractions are
H
-fractions.

Algorithm 3.1
.

[Algorithm
NextABC
for
H
-fractions]

Prototype:

(A^{*},B^{*},C^{*},k,a_{k},D)={\tt NextABC}(A,B,C)
.

Input:

three polynomials
A,B,C\in\mathbb{F}[q]
satisfying (
3.5
).

Output:

a positive integer
k
,

a nonzero scalar
a_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
,

a polynomial
D\in\mathbb{F}[q]
of degree
\leqslant k+1
such that
D(0)=1
,

three polynomials
A^{*},B^{*},C^{*}\in\mathbb{F}[q]
satisfying (
3.5
).

Step 1:

Define
k,a_{k}
by (
3.6
).

Step 2:

Define
D
by (
3.9
).

Step 3:

Set

\displaystyle{A^{*}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{q^{2k+2}}\left(\frac{-D^{2}A}{a_{k}}+BDq^{k}-Ca_{k}q^{2k}\right)

\displaystyle{B^{*}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2AD}{a_{k}q^{k}}-B

\displaystyle{C^{*}}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{Aq^{2}}{a_{k}}.

In this algorithm, all objects are well (and uniquely) defined by the previous discussion.
It remains only to justify that
A^{*},B^{*},C^{*}
are indeed polynomials and that they also satisfy (
3.5
); but this comes from Lemma 3.2 in
[
Han16
]
.

Now, let us check that our algorithm
NextABC
works as expected:

\bullet

Start with a quadratic power series
F
as in (
3.4
), with the associated triple of polynomials
(A,B,C)
satisfying (
3.5
).

\bullet

Set
(A_{1},B_{1},C_{1},k_{0},a_{0},D_{0}):={\tt NextABC}(A,B,C)
. Then we have

F(q)=\frac{-a_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)-q^{k_{0}+2}F_{1}(q)}

as in (
3.2
). The main point now is that the power series
F_{1}
is itself quadratic, more precisely it satisfies the equation

A_{1}+B_{1}F_{1}+C_{1}F_{1}^{2}=0.

This fact is also a statement taken from Lemma 3.2 in
[
Han16
]
.

\bullet

Thus the algorithm can be applied a second time and we set
(A_{2},B_{2},C_{2},k_{1},a_{1},D_{1}):={\tt NextABC}(A_{1},B_{1},C_{1})
. As in the beginning of this subsection we have

F_{1}(q)=\frac{-a_{1}q^{k_{1}}}{D_{1}(q)-q^{k_{1}+2}F_{2}(q)}\qquad\text{hence}\qquad F(q)=\frac{-a_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\frac{a_{1}q^{k_{0}+k_{1}+2}}{D_{1}(q)-q^{k_{1}+2}F_{2}(q)}

with
F_{2}
still quadratic:
A_{2}+B_{2}F_{2}+C_{2}F_{2}^{2}=0
. Going on through this process will define
(k_{j},a_{j},D_{j})
for any
j\geqslant 0
and will lead to the aimed
H
-fraction (
3.3
).

\bullet

Suppose that there exists integers
0<p<p^{\prime}
such that
(A_{p^{\prime}},B_{p^{\prime}},C_{p^{\prime}})=(A_{p},B_{p},C_{p})
. Then the algorithm will repeat the same sequences indefinitely and the
H
-fraction is ultimately periodic:

(3.10)

F(q)=\frac{-a_{0}q^{k_{0}}}{D_{0}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{367007}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{262148}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}}\displaylimits_{j=1}^{p-1}\frac{a_{j}q^{k_{j-1}+k_{j}+2}}{D_{j}(q)}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\left(\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{367007}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{262148}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}}\displaylimits_{j=p}^{p^{\prime}-1}\frac{a_{j}q^{k_{j-1}+k_{j}+2}}{D_{j}(q)}\right)^{*}.

This is precisely what is going to happen when
F=\Phi_{n}
.

Of course, in theory it is not necessary to define
D
by (
3.9
) rather than by (
3.1
) to get the
H
-fraction; the original algorithm in
[
Han16
]
, constructed to produce super
\delta
-fractions for any
\delta
, does not do that. But in our case
\delta=2
, it will be much more easier to find the polynomials
D_{j}
by looking at simple expressions involving only polynomials
A_{j},B_{j},C_{j}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Actually,
Algorithm

3.1
can be simplified. Indeed, because of definition of
C^{*}
in Step 3 we see that
we will always have
C=O(q^{2})
, except maybe at first implementation of the algorithm
. In other words
c_{1}=0
and definition (
3.9
) of the polynomial
D
in Step 2 of
Algorithm

3.1
becomes simpler.

Actually, we will encounter further simplifications when applying
Algorithm

3.1
to
F=\Phi_{n}
: the coefficients
a_{k}
will always be
\pm 1
, as we shall see soon.

3.2.
Proof of
Theorem

A

Fix an integer
n\geqslant 3
. In this subsection we will apply
Algorithm

3.1
to
F=\Phi_{n}
and prove
Theorem

A
. As explained after the statement of
Algorithm

3.1
, our task is to exhibit a family of sextuplets associated with
F=\Phi_{n}
:

(3.11)

\mathcal{Q}_{j}:=(A_{j},B_{j},C_{j},k_{j},a_{j},D_{j}),\quad j\geqslant 0,

by applying repeatedly the algorithm, until periodicity is observed and stops the process. Then the
H
-fraction will have the form (
3.10
).

In fact, it turns out that we will need to define two different families of objects to achieve our goal. We start with the first one.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{(m)}\bigr)_{m\geqslant 1}
denote the sequence of sextuplets

\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{(m)}=\bigl(A_{1}^{(m)},B_{1}^{(m)},C_{1}^{(m)},k_{1}^{(m)},a_{k_{1}}^{(m)},D_{1}^{(m)}\bigr)

constituted of polynomials
A_{1}^{(m)},B_{1}^{(m)},C_{1}^{(m)},D_{1}^{(m)}
, integers
k_{1}^{(m)}
and numbers
a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}\in\{\pm 1\}
defined as follows:

1)

If
m=1
:

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(q^{n}+1\right)}-2\,q}{q-1},

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=q,

\displaystyle k_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle D_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=1-q.

2)

If
m=3j+1
with
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
:

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1\right)}+q^{j+1}}{{\left(q-1\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(q^{n}+1\right)}-2\,q^{j+1}}{q-1},

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-q^{j+1},

\displaystyle k_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle D_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=1-q.

3)

If
m=3j+2
with
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
:

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle={\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}q^{n-j-2},

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(2\,q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1\right)}}{q-1},

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\left(q^{4}-q^{3}+q^{2}\right)}{\left(q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1\right)}+q^{j+3}}{{\left(q-1\right)}^{2}},

\displaystyle k_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=n-2-j,

\displaystyle a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle D_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{n-j}-1}{q-1}=[n-j]_{q}.

4)

If
m=3j+3
with
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
:

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=q^{j},

\displaystyle B_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)[n]_{q},

\displaystyle C_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}q^{n-j},

\displaystyle k_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=j,

\displaystyle a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle D_{1}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=[j+2]_{q}-q.

Using (
2.5
) we see that definition of
\bigl(A_{1}^{(1)},B_{1}^{(1)},C_{1}^{(1)}\bigr)
is such that

A_{1}^{(1)}+B_{1}^{(1)}\Phi_{n}+C_{1}^{(1)}\Phi_{n}^{2}=0,

i.e. this first triple corresponds to the initialization of the process described in
Section

3.1
. Now we prove that our algorithm
NextABC
produces successively all the sequences defined above.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
1\leqslant m\leqslant 3n-3
. Then

{\tt NextABC}\bigl(A_{1}^{(m)},B_{1}^{(m)},C_{1}^{(m)}\bigr)=\bigl(A_{1}^{(m+1)},B_{1}^{(m+1)},C_{1}^{(m+1)},k_{1}^{(m)},a_{k_{1}}^{(m)},D_{1}^{(m)}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
m
be an integer such that
1\leqslant m\leqslant 3n-3
. To make our calculations easier to read, we just write
A
,
B
,
C
,
k
,
a_{k}
,
D
instead of
A_{1}^{(m)}
,
B_{1}^{(m)}
,
C_{1}^{(m)}
,
k_{1}^{(m)}
,
a_{k_{1}}^{(m)}
,
D_{1}^{(m)}
respectively. Let us apply the algorithm
NextABC
for each case introduced in

.

1)

Case

.

Step 1:

Since
A=-1
, (
3.6
) gives
k=0
and
a_{k}=-1
.

Step 2:

Since
C=q
, we have
c_{1}=1
and (
3.9
) says that the polynomial

is defined by

q-\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(q^{n}+1\right)}-2\,q}{q-1}=1-q^{n+1}-\frac{q^{n}-q}{q-1}=D+O(q^{2})

and that
\deg(D)\leqslant 1
. We see immediately that

.

Step 3:

Applying the definitions, we easily check that
A^{*}=A^{(2)}
,
B^{*}=B^{(2)}
,
C^{*}=C^{(2)}
.

2)

Case
m=3j+1
with
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
.

Step 1:

Here,

A=-\frac{{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}{\left(q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1\right)}+q^{j+1}}{{\left(q-1\right)}^{2}}=1+O(q)

so that
k=0
and
a_{k}=1
.

Step 2:

Since
C=-q^{j+1}
, we have
c_{1}=0
and by (
3.9
),

D
is the unique polynomial of degree
\leqslant 1
such that

\frac{B}{A}=D+O(q^{2}).

But

\frac{B}{A}=\frac{{(\left(q^{2}-q+1)(q^{n}+1)-2\,q^{j+1}\right)}{(q-1)}}{{(q^{2}-q+1)}{(q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1)}+q^{j+1}}=\frac{-1+2q+O(q^{2})}{-1+q+O(q^{2})}

because
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
, hence
D=1-q
.

Step 3:

We obtain easily
A^{*}=A^{(m+1)},\ B^{*}=B^{(m+1)},\ C^{*}=C^{(m+1)}
.

3)

Case
m=3j+2
with
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
.

Step 1:

Since

A={\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}q^{n-j-2}=q^{n-j-2}+O(q^{n-j-1})

we have
k=n-j-2
and
a_{k}=1
.

Step 2:

Since

C=\frac{{\left(q^{4}-q^{3}+q^{2}\right)}{\left(q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1\right)}+q^{j+3}}{{\left(q-1\right)}^{2}},

we have
c_{1}=0
. By (
3.9
),
D
must satisfy
\deg(D)\leqslant n-j-1
and

\frac{2\,q^{n-j}-q^{n}-1}{q-1}=D+O(q^{n-j}).

Splitting the left-hand side as
\frac{q^{n-j}-q^{n}}{q-1}+\frac{q^{n-j}-1}{q-1}
, we deduce that

D=\frac{q^{n-j}-1}{q-1}=[n-j]_{q}.

Step 3:

With the definitions, we check that

A^{*}=A^{(m+1)},\ B^{*}=B^{(m+1)},\ C^{*}=C^{(m+1)}
.

4)

Case
m=3j+3
with
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-2
.

Step 1:

In this case
A=q^{j}
, so that

k=j
and
a_{k}=1
.

Step 2:

Since

C=-{\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)}q^{n-j}
,
we have
c_{1}=0
. By (
3.9
),
D
is a polynomial of degree
\leqslant j+1
which satisfies

\left(q^{2}-q+1\right)[n]_{q}=D+O(q^{j+2}),

that is to say

1+q^{2}+q^{3}+\cdots+q^{n-1}+q^{n+1}=D+O(q^{j+2}).

Condition
j+1\leqslant n-1
implies that

D=1+q^{2}+q^{3}+\cdots+q^{j+1}=-q+[j+2]_{q}.

Step 3:

As in the previous cases, we easily find that

A^{*}=A^{(m+1)},\ B^{*}=B^{(m+1)},\ C^{*}=C^{(m+1)}
,
where
m+1=3j+1
with
1\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
.

Thus the lemma is proved.
∎

A remark on symmetries

Formula (
1.20
) reveals three types of symmetries
inside
the period of the continued fraction. Let
(\alpha_{i})
and
(\beta_{i})
denote the sequences of numerators and denominators of the continued fraction (
1.20
), respectively, i.e. write

\Phi_{n}(q)=\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{367007}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{262148}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}}=\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\beta_{0}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\left(\mathop{\vphantom{\sum}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{524296}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{367007}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{}{262148}{$\mathcal{K}$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{6n-4}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\,\mathrel{\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$+$}}\,\right)^{*}.

1)

Symmetries inside the whole period (and, actually, a bit larger block):

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{6n-1-i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,6n-2.

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=\beta_{6n-2-i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,6n-3.

2)

Symmetries inside the first block
U_{n}(q)
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{3n-2-i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,3n-3.

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ where }\chi_{i}=\begin{cases}0&if $i\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$\\

-1&if $i\equiv-1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$.\end{cases}

Symmetries of type (1) and (2) imply also similar symmetries inside the block

.

3)

‘Pseudo half-period’ for numerators inside the whole period:

\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i+3n+1},\quad i=1,\ldots,3n-3.

These identities reflect that the sequence of numerators in block
W_{n}(q)
is essentially the same as in block
U_{n}(q)
.

Proposition 4.6

We have

\Delta_{s_{p+6n-4}}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{p}}

First of all, note that, since parameters

). Therefore (
5.4
) is equivalent to (
5.6
) for
0\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-1
, and obviously it suffices to check the equality when
3n\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-1
.

To do so, let us compare
\Delta_{s_{q}}
and
\Delta_{s_{6n-1-q}}
by using (
4.12
). Actually we must treat the case
q=6n-1
apart, since (
4.12
) does not make sense for
\Delta_{s_{0}}
. But we can check directly relation (
5.6
) by using Proposition
4.6
and formula (
5.1
):

\Delta_{s_{6n-1}}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{3}}=(-1)^{n}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Delta_{s_{0}}.

Now, let us continue our calculations in the generic case
3n\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-2
and use (
4.12
) for the Hankel determinants:

\frac{\Delta_{s_{q}}}{\Delta_{s_{6n-1-q}}}=\frac{(-1)^{\varepsilon_{q}}\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}\,\bigl(v_{1}^{s_{1}}v_{2}^{s_{2}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}\bigr)}{(-1)^{\varepsilon_{6n-1-q}}\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}\bigr)^{s_{6n-1-q}}\,\bigl(v_{1}^{s_{1}}v_{2}^{s_{2}}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}^{s_{6n-2-q}}\bigr)}.

Under our assumption we have
6n-1-q\leqslant 3n-1\leqslant q-1
. Thus some products are canceling and we further simplify this expression by using successively (
4.8
), the fact that
v_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
and (
4.6
):

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{s_{q}}}{\Delta_{s_{6n-1-q}}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varepsilon_{6n-1}}\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}\bigr)^{s_{q}-s_{6n-1-q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varepsilon_{6n-1}}\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}\bigr)^{s_{q}+s_{6n-1-q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varepsilon_{6n-1}}\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}\bigr)^{s_{6n-1}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}\bigr).

Recall from (
4.9
) that all
v_{j}
’s involved in this formula are equal to
-1
except
v_{0}=v_{3n-2}=v_{3n+1}=1
. Hence

\bigl(v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{6n-2-q}\bigr)^{s_{6n-1}}=(-1)^{(6n-2-q)s_{6n-1}}=(-1)^{qs_{6n-1}}

because
6n-2-q<3n-2
.
Moreover, by (
4.8
) we have

(-1)^{\varepsilon_{6n-1}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}}=(-1)^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2}}=(-1)^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}k}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}.

Thus we have proved so far that

(5.7)

\frac{\Delta_{s_{q}}}{\Delta_{s_{6n-1-q}}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{qs_{6n-1}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}\bigr).

To proceed we must distinguish between several cases.

\bullet
Suppose first that
q\geqslant 3n+2
. On the one hand,

\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}=(-1)^{(2q-6n-1)s_{q}}=(-1)^{s_{q}}

because in this product all terms but two equal
-1
.
On the other hand,

v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}=(-1)^{\alpha_{q}}

with

\displaystyle\alpha_{q}

\displaystyle:=s_{6n-1-q}+\cdots+s_{q-1}-s_{3n-2}-s_{3n+1}

\displaystyle=\bigl(s_{6n-1-q}+\cdots+s_{3n-3}+s_{3n-1}+s_{3n}+s_{3n+2}+\cdots+s_{q-1}+s_{q}\bigr)-s_{q}.

The reason to write last expression is that, inside the parenthesis, all terms are pairing to form sums of the type
s_{k}+s_{6n-1-k}
which all equal
s_{6n-1}
by (
4.6
). There are
q-3n
such pairings, so that

v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}=(-1)^{(q-3n)s_{6n-1}}(-1)^{s_{q}}.

Finally we obtain that

\frac{\Delta_{s_{q}}}{\Delta_{s_{6n-1-q}}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{qs_{6n-1}}(-1)^{s_{q}}(-1)^{(q-3n)s_{6n-1}}(-1)^{s_{q}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{ns_{6n-1}}

with
s_{6n-1}=(n+1)(2n+1)
, hence (
5.6
).

\bullet
Suppose that
q=3n+1
. Then

\bigl(v_{6n-1-q}\cdots v_{q-1}\bigr)^{s_{q}}=\bigl(v_{3n-2}v_{3n-1}v_{3n}\bigr)^{s_{3n+1}}=1

and

v_{6n-1-q}^{s_{6n-1-q}}\cdots v_{q-1}^{s_{q-1}}=v_{3n-2}^{s_{3n-2}}v_{3n-1}^{s_{3n-1}}v_{3n}^{s_{3n}}=(-1)^{s_{3n-1}+s_{3n}}=(-1)^{s_{6n-1}}.

Thus (
5.7
) yields

\frac{\Delta_{s_{3n+1}}}{\Delta_{s_{3n-2}}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{(3n+1)s_{6n-1}}(-1)^{s_{6n-1}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{ns_{6n-1}}

with
s_{6n-1}=(n+1)(2n+1)
, hence (
5.6
) when
q=3n+1
.

\bullet
When
q=3n
, the proof is similar to the preceding case.

(C) Proof of Part (1) for
3n\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-5
.
We now apply symmetry (
5.4
) to the formulas of Part (1) already proved for
0\leqslant q\leqslant 3n-1
to deduce the remaining formulas, when
3n\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-5
.

\bullet
Suppose that
q=3p
with
n+1\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
(hence
3n+3\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-6
). By (
5.4
),

\Delta_{s_{3p}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Delta_{s_{3(2n-p-1)+2}}

with
0\leqslant 2n-p-1\leqslant n-2
. Hence, by (
5.3
) for Case (A) we have

\Delta_{s_{3p}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{(2n-p)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{p\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}=(-1)^{(p+1)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{n},

as required.

\bullet
Suppose that
q=3p+1
with
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
(hence
3n+1\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-5
). By (
5.4
),

\Delta_{s_{3p+1}}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Delta_{s_{3(2n-p-1)+1}}

with
1\leqslant 2n-p-1\leqslant n-2
. Hence, by (
5.2
) for Case (A),

\displaystyle\Delta_{s_{3p+1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{(2n-p-1)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(2n-p-1)(2n-p)}{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{(p+1)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{-n+\frac{p(p+1)}{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{p(p+1)}{2}}

which is again (
5.2
).

\bullet
Suppose that
q=3p+2
with
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-3
(hence
3n+2\leqslant q\leqslant 6n-7
). This case is similar to the previous one and we skip details.

\bullet
The only remaining case is
q=3n
, which follows immediately from (
5.4
) and from the value of
\Delta_{s_{3n-1}}
given by the second row in (
5.3
).
∎

6.
The Gale-Robinson relations

Assume as usual that
n\geqslant 3
. For any integer
j\geqslant 0
and any
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n+1\}
, set

\Gamma_{j}^{(\ell)}:=\Delta_{j}^{(\ell)}\,\Delta_{j+2n+2}^{(\ell)}-\Delta_{j+1}^{(\ell)}\,\Delta_{j+2n+1}^{(\ell)}+\bigl(\Delta_{j+n+1}^{(\ell)}\bigr)^{2}.

We want to prove the Gale-Robinson recurrence stated in
Theorem

C
, which reads

(6.1)

\Gamma_{j}^{(\ell)}=0\qquad\text{for all $j\geqslant 0$ and all $\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n+1\}$}.

Assuming the validity of
Theorem

D
(to be proved in
Section

7
) it is easily checked that

\Gamma_{j}^{(\ell+1)}=\Gamma_{j}^{(\ell)},

so that it suffices to prove (
6.1
) in the case
\ell=0
, and this will be our purpose in this section.

For simplicity we put
\Gamma_{j}:=\Gamma_{j}^{(0)}
.
Because of the (anti-)periodicity property shown in
Theorem

B
, it remains to show that

(6.2)

\Gamma_{j}=0\quad\text{for }0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n(n+1)-1,

where

\Gamma_{j}:=\Delta_{j}\,\Delta_{j+2n+2}-\Delta_{j+1}\,\Delta_{j+2n+1}+\bigl(\Delta_{j+n+1}\bigr)^{2}.

By
Lemma

4.3
we know which Hankel determinants are nonzero and, by the previous theorem, we know the explicit values of these nonzero determinants, so we just have to check that
\Gamma_{j}
vanishes in any case. Adding and multiplying five Hankel determinants
\Delta_{i}
which can be either
-1
,
0
or
1
is certainly a triviality but the difficulty comes from the fact that we have to handle them simultaneously, and there is no clue of how their values are connected in general. In fact, we will distinguish four cases, looking at pairs of successive determinants
(\Delta_{j},\Delta_{j+1})
and discussing on the nullity of one of them, in order to simplify the expression of
\Gamma_{j}
when possible. And, because (
6.2
) must be checked for
j\leqslant 2n(n+1)-1
we will have to discuss on Hankel determinants
\Delta_{i}
with index

i=j+2n+2\leqslant 2n(n+1)-1+2n+2=2n(n+2)+1,

i.e. we need the description of the intersection
\mathcal{S}\cap\{0,1,\ldots,2n(n+2)+1\}
. One the one hand, Lemma
4.3
implies the characterization:

(6.3)

j\in\mathcal{S}\text{ and }0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n(n+1)\iff j\text{ satisfies one of the conditions (i)-(v)}

where

(i)

j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n+1)
and
0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n(n+1)

(ii)

j\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n+1)
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant n^{2}

(iii)

j\equiv n\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n+1)
and
n+(n+1)^{2}\leqslant j\leqslant n+(2n-1)(n+1)

(iv)

j\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
and
n\leqslant j\leqslant(n-1)n

(v)

j\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)
and
1+(n+4)n\leqslant j\leqslant 1+(2n+1)n
.

On the other hand, the
j\in\mathcal{S}
such that
2n(n+1)\leqslant j\leqslant 2n(n+2)+1
can be found by Lemma
4.5
: they are such that
j-2n(n+1)\in\mathcal{S}
and characterization (
6.3
) can be applied to these translated numbers.

Example 6.1
.

When
n=5
, according to (
6.3
) we must determine the
j\in\mathcal{S}
such that
0\leqslant j\leqslant 60
. By the results of
Lemma

4.3
there are
27
of them, equally divided into three subsets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\{s_{3p},\ 0\leqslant p\leqslant 8\}=\{0,6,12,18,24,36,46,51,56\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{s_{3p+1},\ 0\leqslant p\leqslant 8\}=\{1,7,13,19,25,41,47,53,59\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{s_{3p+2},\ 0\leqslant p\leqslant 8\}=\{5,10,15,20,30,42,48,54,60\},

and symmetries read as follows:

s_{j+16}=s_{j}+41\quad\text{for }0\leqslant j\leqslant 13,\qquad s_{j}+s_{29-j}=66\quad\text{for }0\leqslant j\leqslant 29.

One can visualize the set
\mathcal{S}\cap\{0,1,\ldots,60\}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}
and all the symmetries on
fig.

6.1
.

Figure 6.1.
The set
\mathcal{S}\cap\{0,1,\ldots,2n(n+1)\}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}
when
n=5
.

Now, let us begin the proof of the Gale-Robinson recurrences (
6.2
). Computations are tedious, but easy and similar in the first three cases we will look at. Therefore, full details will be provided in the first and fourth cases only.

Case 1:
\Delta_{j}\neq 0
and
\Delta_{j+1}\neq 0
.
According to the previous discussion this situation corresponds to the following five possibilities:

(a)

j=n=s_{2}
,

(b)

j=p(n+1)=s_{3p}
for
0\leqslant p\leqslant n-1
,

(c)

j=n^{2}-n-1=s_{3n-5}
,

(d)

j=n^{2}+4n+1=s_{3n+3}
,

(e)

j=n+(p+1)(n+1)=s_{3p+1}
for
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
,

and we go for a case-by-case examination.

(a)

If
j=n=s_{2}
then

j+1=n+1=s_{3}
,
j+n+1=2(n+1)=s_{6}
,
j+2n+2=3(n+1)=s_{9}
and
j+2n+1=3n+2\notin\mathcal{S}
, so that,

\Gamma_{j}=\Delta_{s_{2}}\Delta_{s_{9}}+\Delta_{s_{6}}^{2}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{3n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{3}+1=0.

(b)

Suppose that
j=p(n+1)=s_{3p}
for
0\leqslant p\leqslant n-1
.

\bullet

We look first at the generic situation
0\leqslant p\leqslant n-3
. Then
j+1=p(n+1)+1=s_{3p+1}
,
j+n+1=(p+1)(n+1)=s_{3(p+1)}
,
j+2n+2=(p+2)(n+1)=s_{3(p+2)}
and
j+2n+1=n+(p+1)(n+1)\notin\mathcal{S}
. Thus

\Gamma_{j}=\Delta_{s_{3p}}\Delta_{s_{3(p+2)}}+\Delta_{s_{3(p+1)}}^{2}=(-1)^{(p+2)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(p+2)(p+1)}{2}}(-1)^{p\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{p(p-1)}{2}}+1=0.

\bullet

If
j=(n-2)(n+1)=s_{3(n-2)}
, then we have
j+1=s_{3(n-2)+1}
,
j+n+1=s_{3(n-1)}
,
j+2n+2=s_{3(n-1)+2}
and
j+2n+1=n+(p+1)(n+1)\notin\mathcal{S}
. Thus

\Gamma_{j}=\Delta_{s_{3(n-2)}}\Delta_{s_{3(n-1)+2)}}+\Delta_{s_{3(n-1)}}^{2}=(-1)^{(n-2)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(n-2)(n-3)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)^{2}}{2}}+1=0.

\bullet

If
j=(n-1)(n+1)=s_{3n-3}
, then
j+1=s_{3n-2}
,
j+n+1=s_{3n-1}
,
j+2n+2=s_{3n}
and
j+2n+1=n^{2}+2n\notin\mathcal{S}
. Hence

\Gamma_{j}=\Delta_{s_{3n-3}}\Delta_{s_{3n}}+\Delta_{s_{3n-1}}^{2}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)^{2}}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{2}}+1=0.

(c)

When
j=n^{2}-n-1=s_{3n-5}
we have
j+1=s_{3n-4}
,
j+n+1=s_{3n-2}
,
j+2n+1=s_{3n-1}
and
j+2n+2=n^{2}+n+1\notin\mathcal{S}
, so that

\Gamma_{j}=-\Delta_{s_{3n-4}}\Delta_{s_{3n-1}}-\Delta_{s_{3n-1}}^{2}=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)^{2}}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)^{2}}{2}}-1=0.

(d)

If
j=n^{2}+4n+1=s_{3n+3}
, then
j+1=s_{3n+4}
but
j+n+1
,
j+2n+1
and
j+2n+2
are not in
\mathcal{S}
and give zero determinants. Trivially,
\Gamma_{j}=0
as well.

(e)

Assume that
j=n+(p+1)(n+1)=s_{3p+1}
for
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
.

\bullet

We suppose first that
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-4
. In this case,
j+2n+2\leqslant n+(2n-1)(n+1)<2n(n+1)
so that all indices remain inside the first (anti-)period: we compute as before
j+1=s_{3p+2}
,
j+n+1=s_{3(p+1)+1}
,
j+2n+2=s_{3(p+2)+1}
and observe that
j+2n+1=2n+(p+2)(n+1)\notin\mathcal{S}
(look at congruences modulo
n
and
n+1
). Thus

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{s_{3p+1}}\Delta_{s_{3(p+2)+2)}}+\Delta_{s_{3(p+1)+1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{p\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{p(p+1)}{2}}(-1)^{(p+2)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(p+2)(p+3)}{2}}+1

\displaystyle=0.

\bullet

Suppose that
p=2n-3
. Then
j+1=s_{3(2n-3)+2}
,
j+n+1=s_{3(2n-2)+1}
,
j+2n+1=n+2n(n+1)
and
j+2n+2=n+2n(n+1)
. This time, the last two indices exceed the (anti-)period length
2n(n+1)
, so we use Lemma
4.5
to see that
j+2n+1\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+2=s_{2}+2n(n+1)
. For the latter, the corresponding determinant is calculated with property (
4.10
):

\Delta_{n+2n(n+1)}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{n}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{2}}.

This yields

\Gamma_{j}=\Delta_{s_{3(2n-3)+2}}(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{2}}+1=(-1)^{(2n-3)\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{(2n-3)(2n-2)}{2}}(-1)^{n}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}+1=0.

\bullet

Suppose now that
p=2n-2
. Then
j+1=s_{3(2n-2)+2}
and all other indices exceed the (anti-)period length:
j+n+1=n+2n(n+1)
,
j+2n+1=2n+2n(n+1)
and
j+2n+2=2n+1+2n(n+1)
. Using Lemma
4.5
and (
4.10
) we get:

\displaystyle\Delta_{n+2n(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{n}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{2}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{2n+2n(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{2n}=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{5}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{2n+1+2n(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\Delta_{2n+1}=0.

Finally, we find again that

\Gamma_{j}=-\Delta_{s_{3(2n-2)+2}}(-1)^{n}\Delta_{s_{5}}+1=-(-1)^{n}(-1)^{n}(-1)^{n(n-1)}+1=0.

and this completes the proof in Case 1.

Case 2:
\Delta_{j}\neq 0
and
\Delta_{j+1}=0
.
Since verifications are similar to Case 1 we will only provide the possible values of the indices
j,j+1,j+n+1,j+2n+1,j+2n+2
; then, using Theorem
5.1
it is easy to check that one always has
\Gamma_{j}=0
.

Let us describe the situation. According to (
6.3
) one has seven possibilities for
j
which are listed below. In each case we indicate the corresponding values only for indices
j+n+1
and
j+2n+2
: the ones for
j+1
and
j+2n+1
are useless since by hypothesis
\Delta_{j+1}\Delta_{j+2n+1}=0
.

(a)

j=1+p(n+1)=s_{3p+1}
for
0\leqslant p\leqslant n-3
. Then
j+n+1=s_{3(p+1)+1}
and
j+2n+2=s_{3(p+2)+1}
.

(b)

j=(p+1)n=s_{3p+2}
for
1\leqslant p\leqslant n-2
. Then
j+n+1=(p+2)n+1\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+2=(p+3)n+2\notin\mathcal{S}
.

(c)

j=n^{2}=s_{3(n-1)+1}
. Then
j+n+1=n^{2}+n+1\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+2=n^{2}+2n+2\notin\mathcal{S}
.

(d)

j=n(n+1)=s_{3(n-1)+2}
. Then
j+n+1=s_{3n}
and
j+2n+2s_{3n+2}
.

(e)

j=(n+1)^{2}=s_{3n}
. Then
j+n+1=s_{3n+2}
and
j+2n+2=s_{3(n+1)+2}
.

(f)

j=(p+2)(n+1)=s_{3p+2}
for
n\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-3
. Then
j+n+1=s_{3(p+1)+2}
and
j+2n+2=s_{3(p+2)+2}
.

(g)

j=1+(p+3)n=s_{3p}
for
n+2\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
. Then
j+n+1=2+(p+4)n\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+2=3+(p+5)n\notin\mathcal{S}
.

N.B. For subcases (f) and (g), one must use Lemma
4.5
and property (
4.10
) when indices are
\geqslant 2n(n+1)
. Subcase (g) can only occur when
n\geqslant 4
.

Case 3:
\Delta_{j}=0
and
\Delta_{j+1}\neq 0
.
Again, the situation is very similar to the first ones. With (
6.3
) we see that this case corresponds to seven possibilities for
j+1
. Since
\Delta_{j}\Delta_{j+2n+2}
always vanishes by hypothesis, we give only the corresponding values of
j+n+1
and
j+2n+1
.

(a)

j+1=(p+1)n=s_{3p+2}
for
0\leqslant p\leqslant n-3
. Then
j+n+1=s_{3(p+1)+2}
, and
j+2n+1=s_{3(p+2)+2}
except if
p=n-3
in which case
j+2n+1=n^{2}=s_{3(n-1)+1}
.

(b)

j+1=p(n+1)=s_{3p}
for
2\leqslant p\leqslant n-1
. Then
j+n+1=(p+1)n+p\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+1=(p+2)n+p\notin\mathcal{S}
.

(c)

j+1=n(n+1)=s_{3(n-1)+2}
. Then
j+n+1=n(n+2)\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+1=n(n+3)\notin\mathcal{S}
.

(d)

j+1=(n+1)^{2}=s_{3n}
. Then
j+n+1=(n+1)^{2}+n=s_{3n+1}
and
j+2n+1=1+n(n+4)=s_{3(n+1)}
.

(e)

j+1=(n+1)^{2}+n=s_{3n+1}
. Then
j+n+1=1+n(n+4)=s_{3(n+1)}
and
j+2n+1=1+n(n+5)=s_{3(n+2)}
.

(f)

j+1=1+(p+3)n=s_{3p}
for
n+1\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
. Then
j+n+1=1+(p+4)n=s_{3(p+1)}
and
j+2n+1=1+(p+5)n=s_{3(p+2)}
.

(g)

j+1=n+(p+1)(n+1)=s_{3p+1}
for
n+2\leqslant p\leqslant 2n-2
. Then
j+n+1=2n+(p+1)(n+1)\notin\mathcal{S}
and
j+2n+1=3n+(p+1)(n+1)\notin\mathcal{S}
.

N.B. For subcases (f) and (g), one must use Lemma
4.5
and property (
4.10
) when indices are
\geqslant 2n(n+1)
. Subcase (g) can only occur when
n\geqslant 4
.

Case 4:
\Delta_{j}=0=\Delta_{j+1}
.
In this case, the Gale-Robinson recurrence (
6.2
) will be true if and only if
\Delta_{j+n+1}
always vanishes. Assume on the contrary that
\Delta_{j+n+1}\neq 0
, i.e.
j+n+1\in\mathcal{S}
. We shall use again (
6.3
) and distinguish four subcases.

(a)

Suppose that
j+n+1\leqslant s_{3n}=(n+1)^{2}
, i.e.
j\leqslant n(n+1)=s_{3n-1}
. According to (
6.3
), we must have

j+n+1\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n+1)\quad\text{or}\quad j+n+1\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n+1)\quad\text{or}\quad j+n+1\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)

which amounts to say that
j
fullfills (i) or (ii) in (
6.3
) (first two cases) or that
j+1
satisfies (iv) in (
6.3
) (last case). This implies in return that
j\in\mathcal{S}
or
j+1\in\mathcal{S}
, i.e.
\Delta_{j}\neq 0
or
\Delta_{j+1}\neq 0
, which is a contradiction.

(b)

Suppose that
j+n+1
is one of the following three numbers:

s_{3n+1}=n+(n+1)^{2},\quad s_{3n+2}=(n+2)(n+1),\quad s_{3n+3}=1+(n+4)n.

In the first case, we have
j+1=(n+1)^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
by (
6.3
), hence
\Delta_{j+1}\neq 0
, contradiction. In the second case, we have
j=(n+1)^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\Delta_{j}\neq 0
, also a contradiction. In the third case we have
j+1=n+(n+1)^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\Delta_{j+1}\neq 0
, still a contradiction.

(c)

Suppose now that
n+(n+1)(n+2)=s_{3n+4}\leqslant j+n+1\leqslant s_{6n-4}=2n(n+1)
. Then
j+n+1
satisfies conditions (i) or (iii) or (v) of (
6.3
), which implies that either
j
satisfies (i) or
j+1
satisfies (i) or
j+1
satisfies (v). This leads in all cases to
\Delta_{j}=0
or
\Delta_{j+1}=0
, a contradiction.

(d)

To finish the proof, suppose that
j+n+1\geqslant s_{6n-3}=2n^{2}+2n+1
(see (
4.14
)). Since
j\leqslant 2n(n+1)-1
one also has
j+n+1\leqslant(2n+1)(n+1)-1=2n^{2}+3n=s_{6n-2}
by (
4.15
). In other words,
j+n+1\in\{s_{6n-2},s_{6n-3}\}=\{2n^{2}+2n+1,2n^{2}+3n\}
. Therefore, either
j=2n^{2}+n+1=s_{6n-6}
or
j=2n^{2}+2n-1=s_{6n-5}
. In both cases
\Delta_{j}\neq 0
, contradiction.

We thus have completely established
Theorem

C
in case
\ell=0
.

The contiguity relations (

of two consecutive shifts

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that

F(q)=f_{0}+f_{1}q+f_{2}q^{2}+\cdots

is a quadratic power series satisfying the equation

(7.1)

A+BF+CF^{2}=0

where
A,B,C
are polynomials (with
C\neq 0
). Then the shifted series

F^{(1)}(q)=f_{1}+f_{2}q+\cdots=\frac{F(q)-f_{0}}{q}

is also quadratic, satisfying the equation

A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}F^{(1)}+C^{\prime}\bigl(F^{(1)}\bigr)^{2}=0

with
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
polynomials given by

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}(A+f_{0}B+f_{0}^{2}C),

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle=B+2f_{0}C,

\displaystyle C^{\prime}

\displaystyle=qC.

Proof.

Inserting
F=qF^{(1)}+f_{0}
in equation (
7.1
) gives immediately

(7.2)

A+f_{0}B+f_{0}^{2}C+q(B+2f_{0}C)F^{(1)}+q^{2}C\bigl(F^{(1)}\bigr)^{2}=0.

Now, the constant term in the left-hand side of (
7.1
) is
A(0)+B(0)F(0)+C(0)F(0)^{2}
, and this is actually also the constant term of the polynomial
A+f_{0}B+f_{0}^{2}C
. Thus it equals zero, and the whole left-hand side of (
7.2
) can be divided by
q
.
∎

We use this lemma to derive the following generalization of (
2.5
) which gives the quadratic equations satisfied by the shifts of
\Phi_{n}

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,n+1\}

where

are polynomials with integer coefficients given as follows: for

\displaystyle A^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{\ell+1}-(q^{2}-q+1)(q^{n}-q^{n-\ell}+1)}{(q-1)^{2}},

\displaystyle B^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2q^{\ell+1}-(q^{2}-q+1)(q^{n}+1)}{q-1},

\displaystyle C^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=q^{\ell+1},

and for

\displaystyle A^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=-q^{n-1},

\displaystyle B^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(q^{2}-q+1)+(q^{2}-3q+1)q^{n}}{q-1},

\displaystyle C^{(n+1)}

We proceed by induction on

\bullet

A simple calculation ensures that these formulas for

\displaystyle f(n,\ell,j)

\displaystyle=j+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}+j+\ell+1+\frac{(n-\ell-2)(n-\ell-1)}{2}+j+n

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+3)}{2}+1+\frac{(n-\ell-2)(n-\ell-1)}{2}+3j+n.

But

\displaystyle(n-\ell-2)(n-\ell-1)

\displaystyle=n(n-\ell-2)-\ell(n-\ell-2)-(n-\ell-2)

\displaystyle=n(n-2\ell-3)+\ell(\ell+3)+2,

hence

\displaystyle f(n,\ell,j)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+3)}{2}+1+\frac{n(n-2\ell-3)}{2}+\frac{\ell(\ell+3)}{2}+1+3j+n

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{n(n-2\ell-3)}{2}+j+n\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{n(n+2\ell-1)}{2}+j\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2),

and this last formula implies the contiguity relation (
1.22
) when
\ell=0,1,\ldots,n-2
.

\bullet

Assume now that
\ell=n-1
. By
Corollary

7.5
and (
1.19
) we have

\Phi_{n}^{(n-1)}(q)=\frac{1}{1-q^{2}G_{3}(q)}

with

G_{3}(q)=\frac{q^{n-1}}{1+q^{2}+q^{3}+\cdots+q^{n-1}+2q^{n}-q^{n+1}\,\Phi_{n}^{(n)}(q)}.

Applying
Lemma

7.6
we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}(\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}\Delta_{j}(-\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\Delta_{j+n}(G_{1})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j}(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\Delta_{j+n+1}(\Phi_{n}^{(n-1)}).

It is easily seen that this is exactly (
1.22
) for
\ell=n-1
.

\bullet

Lastly, assume that
\ell=n
. By
Corollary

7.5
and (
1.19
) we have

\Phi_{n}^{(n)}(q)=\frac{q^{n}}{1+q^{2}+q^{3}+\cdots+q^{n-1}+2q^{n}-q^{n+1}-q^{n+2}\,\bigl(-\Phi_{n}^{(n+1)}(q)\bigr)}.

Again, applying
Lemma

7.6
yields

\Delta_{j}(\Phi_{n}^{(n+1)})=(-1)^{j}\Delta_{j}(-\Phi_{n}^{(n+1)})\\
=(-1)^{j}(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Delta_{j+n+1}(\Phi_{n}^{(n)})

which is the same as (
1.22
) when
\ell=n
.

This finishes our proof of
Theorem

D
in all cases.
∎

7.3.
The shifted case, modulo
p

We end our article by proving
Theorem

F
. Fix a prime number
p
and let
\ell\geqslant 0
. Since
\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}(q)
is a power series with integer coefficients, according to Theorem 1.1 of
[
Han16
]
, the statement of
Theorem

F
reduces to prove that
\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}
satisfies a quadratic functional equation

A^{(\ell)}+B^{(\ell)}\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}+C^{(\ell)}\bigl(\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}\bigr)^{2}=0

with three polynomials
A^{(\ell)},B^{(\ell)},C^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
such that

B^{(\ell)}(0)=1,\quad C^{(\ell)}\neq 0,\quad C^{(\ell)}(0)=0.

Let us actually prove that such an equation holds with

(7.7)

B^{(\ell)}(0)=1,\quad C^{(\ell)}=q^{\ell+1}.

We proceed by induction on
\ell\geqslant 0
.
By (
2.5
), it is clear that conditions (
7.7
) are fulfilled for
\ell=0
. Now, assume that they are true for some
\ell\geqslant 0
. By
Lemma

7.1
we know that
\Phi_{n}^{(\ell+1)}
satisfies the quadratic equation

A^{(\ell+1)}+B^{(\ell+1)}\Phi_{n}^{(\ell+1)}+C^{(\ell+1)}\bigl(\Phi_{n}^{(\ell+1)}\bigr)^{2}=0

with
A^{(\ell+1)},B^{(\ell+1)},C^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
satisfying

B^{(\ell+1)}=B^{(\ell)}+2\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}(0)C^{(\ell)},\quad C^{(\ell+1)}=qC^{(\ell)}.

By induction hypothesis, this gives

C^{(\ell+1)}=q^{\ell+2},\quad B^{(\ell+1)}(0)=B^{(\ell)}(0)+2\Phi_{n}^{(\ell)}(0)C^{(\ell)}(0)=1,

as required.
