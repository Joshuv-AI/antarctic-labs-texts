Cost-effectiveness analysis of different types of human papillomavirus vaccination to prevent cervical cancer in Iran
Authors: Negin Bashari, Majid Davari, Ali Akbarisari, Elahe Khorasani, Rajabali Daroudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer (CC) remains a major public health concern, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where prevention and screening access are limited. Iran has a relatively low incidence of CC (age-standardized incidence rate: 2.2 per 100,000 women), but a high mortality-to-incidence ratio (44%), which suggests diagnoses occur at later stages. Despite the availability of three prophylactic HPV vaccines (bivalent, quadrivalent, and nine-valent), their cost-effectiveness in Iran is still uncertain. This study evaluates the economic and health impacts of HPV vaccination strategies in Iran.MethodsA static Markov cohort model simulated the lifetime effects of HPV vaccination on a hypothetical cohort of 100,000 twelve-year-old girls. The model incorporated Iranian epidemiological data, vaccine efficacy, and direct medical costs (vaccination, screening, and treatment). Outcomes included cases of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), CC, genital warts (G W), quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). Sensitivity analyses assessed robustness.ResultsThe nine-valent vaccine demonstrated the highest effectiveness, reducing CC cases by 98.4% and deaths by 99%. It was followed by the bivalent vaccine, which resulted in a 95.7% reduction in CC, and the quadrivalent vaccine, which achieved a 90.4% reduction. However, none of these vaccines is cost-effective at current prices. The ICERs are significantly above Iran’s cost-effectiveness threshold of $4,761.90 per QALY (1 times the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita). Specifically, the bivalent vaccine has an ICER of $33,179/QALY, the quadrivalent $45,088/QALY, and the nine-valent $50,067/QALY. Threshold analysis showed that vaccine prices would need to decrease by 82–85% to be considered cost-effective. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed that vaccination strategies remained not cost-effective across parameter uncertainties.ConclusionsAlthough HPV vaccines provide health benefits, they are not cost-effective in Iran at current prices due to high costs and low CC rates. Substantial price reductions are needed to support nationwide HPV vaccination efforts.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) remains a significant public health issue, ranking as the fourth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer death among women worldwide [1]. Persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) is the leading cause of CC and genital warts (G W). The burden of cervical cancer is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where over 85% of cases and about 84% of new cases occur [1–3]. This unequal burden underscores disparities in access to effective screening and prevention services.

Although detailed national data on CC incidence and mortality in Iran are limited [4, 5], estimates from the HPV Information Centre suggest about 917 new cases in 2018, with an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of 2.2 per 100,000 women, and 467 related deaths [6]. While Iran’s overall CC incidence is below global averages, its mortality-to-incidence ratio exceeds 44%, indicating delayed diagnoses and poor prognosis [5]. Additionally, the incidence seems to be increasing in some regions of the country, particularly in the central provinces [7].

A recent meta-analysis reports that the prevalence of HPV among Iranian women is 9% in those with normal cytology, rising to 81% in women with CC [8]. High-risk HPV (hrHPV) types, especially HPV-16 and HPV-18, dominate in precancerous and cancerous lesions, with HPV-16 appearing in over 50% of CC [8]. Conversely, low-risk HPV (lrHPV) types such as HPV-6 and HPV-11 are more common in women with normal cytology or benign conditions like GW [4, 9, 10], though they are found in nearly 10% of CC cases [8]. This variation highlights the significant presence of both hrHPV and lrHPV in Iran. However, CC prevention remains inadequate, as there is no organized national screening program in place. Screening mainly occurs on an opportunistic basis, with low participation rates, especially in rural areas, and primarily relies on the Papanicolaou test (Pap smear) without incorporating HPV DNA testing [11].

In response to the global burden of HPV-related diseases, three prophylactic vaccines have been developed: a bivalent HPV vaccine (Cervarix®; GlaxoSmithKline, Rixensart, Belgium), a quadrivalent HPV vaccine (Gardasil®; Merck & Co., Inc., Whitehouse Station, NJ, USA), and a nine-valent HPV vaccine (Gardasil 9®, Merck & Co., Inc., Whitehouse Station, NJ, USA). All three vaccines target HPV-16 and HPV-18, the most common oncogenic types, which are responsible for more than 70% of cervical CC cases worldwide [12]. In addition, the quadrivalent and nine-valent vaccines also protect against HPV-6 and HPV-11, which account for around 90% of GW [13]. The 9-valent vaccine also protects against five additional high-risk HPV types (HPV31, HPV33, HPV45, HPV52, and HPV58) that, along with HPV-16/18, account for approximately 90% of CC cases [12]. Although some cross-protection has been observed with the bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines, their efficacy against non-vaccine types remains limited [14].

Considering the importance of evidence-based policy-making, conducting a cost-effectiveness analysis is crucial before implementing national vaccination programs. This study aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of different HPV vaccination strategies for preventing CC in Iran, offering insights into their potential economic and health impacts.

Methods

Model structure

The cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted using a static Markov cohort model with annual cycles. This model allows for the assessment of the lifetime effects of HPV vaccines on cases, deaths, costs, life years, and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) related to CC and GW [15]. The model was adapted to the Iranian setting by incorporating country-specific data on epidemiology and costs [16].

The model structure comprises a series of health states that simulate the natural progression of HPV infection. The cohort is assumed to be HPV-naive [No-HPV] at the start, and with each cycle, there is a chance of remaining uninfected or becoming infected and moving to different health states. These states include hrHPV, lrHPV, premalignant conditions, CC, and GW. The model also accounts for the detection and treatment of pre-malignant lesions through screening (Fig. 1).

The model structure, which includes a series of health states, replicates the natural history of HPV infection. Subjects move between health states each cycle based on fixed annual transition probabilities. The cohort entering the model is assumed to be No-HPV, and with each cycle, there is a chance of remaining uninfected or becoming infected, transitioning to either the hrHPV or lrHPV state. Participants in hrHPV may stay in this state, revert to No-HPV, or progress through pre-malignant states, known as Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN) states [CIN1, CIN2/3, and persistent CIN2/3], before developing CC and facing CC death.

If pre-malignant lesions or CC are detected, participants transition to corresponding states (e.g., detected CIN or detected cancer), which are linked to higher chances of regression, treatment success, or cure. Since CC screening in Iran is mainly opportunistic, detection is based on fixed detection probabilities instead of screening coverage or frequency parameters.

CCs are categorized into four prognostic stages, numbered 1 to 4. Each advancing stage is associated with a worse prognosis, lower treatment success rates, and increased recurrence and relapse risks. Cases of CC can be treated and cured [Cancer Cured], or may result in death from cancer [Cancer Deaths]. Subjects infected with lrHPV may develop GWs or low-risk CIN1 [lrCIN1], which might be detected and treated or may spontaneously clear (returning to [No-HPV]). Each year, individuals face age-specific risks of dying from other causes (Fig. 1).

Input data

Demographics

The study population for the cost-effectiveness analysis consisted of a hypothetical cohort of 100,000 twelve-year-old girls. This age group was chosen based on recommendations from the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) [17] and confirmation by local experts. The model was run for 80 years in 1-year cycles to cover the total lifetime of the cohort. Age-specific mortality rates for the general population of Iran were obtained from the World Health Organization (WHO) mortality database [18].

Transition probabilities and epidemiological data

In each cycle of the model, transition probabilities determine how the cohort transitions between different health states. Since there were no relevant studies on health states transition probabilities in Iran, international studies were used as a proxy (Table 1). A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results.

Detection probabilities for CIN and cervical cancer were incorporated in the model as transition probabilities from undetected to detected health states. These transitions reflect the effect of screening and diagnostic follow-up in the Iranian healthcare setting, where cervical cancer screening is opportunistic rather than organized. Given the absence of a population-based screening program, detection was not modeled based on coverage or screening intervals, but rather through fixed detection probabilities. For CIN lesions, the probabilities were derived from a retrospective cytology study conducted in Iran [33]. For cervical cancer, stage-specific detection probabilities were obtained from an Iranian cost-effectiveness modeling study [26] (Table 1).

To accurately represent the Iranian population, local data on the prevalence of HPV types in precancerous lesions and CC were used [4]. For CIN detection, we used data from a retrospective cytology study conducted in Iran [26]. For CC, stage-specific detection probabilities were derived from an Iranian cost-effectiveness modeling study.

The incidence rate of hrHPV infection was estimated using calibration methods based on stage- and age-specific rates of CC in Iran [30]. The mortality rate of the general population of Iran was obtained from the life table published by the WHO [28]. To estimate CC mortality by stage, survival data from a retrospective observational study conducted in Iran and other countries were used [32]. Based on these estimates and data from the Global Cancer Observatory (GCO) [30] Stage-specific CC mortality rates for Iran were calculated.

Cost estimation

The cost estimation for the cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted from the payer’s perspective, including direct medical costs. These costs encompassed vaccination, screening, diagnostics, and treatment of HPV-related diseases. All costs were measured based on 2019 national medical tariffs and converted to United States dollars (USD) using the exchange rate set by the Central Bank of Iran.

The price of each dose of the quadrivalent HPV vaccine was obtained from the Iran Food and Drug Administration (IFDA). For the bivalent and nine-valent HPV vaccines, which are not currently available in Iran, the prices were estimated based on the prices in Greece, the reference country for drug pricing in Iran. The assumed price per dose was $41.32 for the bivalent HPV vaccine, $88.45 for the quadrivalent HPV vaccine, and $75.71 for the nine-valent HPV vaccine.

The costs of diagnosing and treating precancerous lesions and GW were estimated based on the clinical care pathways outlined in the National Guideline for the Management of Sexually Transmitted Infections in Iran [19] and a CC screening model developed in Iran [26]. Unit costs were calculated using the 2019 national medical tariffs of Iran, considering a weighted average of public and private sector tariffs, reflecting direct medical expenses such as diagnostic procedures, treatments, and follow-up care. Stage-specific treatment costs for cervical cancer were obtained from a national cost–benefit analysis based on retrospective hospital data and validated by an expert panel [20], representing local data from Iranian hospitals (Table 1).

Utilities

Utility values were obtained from published international studies due to the lack of Iran-specific data. Values for GW were derived from patient-reported outcomes using the EQ-5D (EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire) in a multi-center observational study conducted in the United Kingdom (UK) [21] and utility weights for CC and precancerous lesions were sourced from a health economic evaluation of HPV vaccination conducted in Singapore [22]. These studies were selected based on the use of validated instruments and high methodological quality. To address potential uncertainty, one-way sensitivity analyses were conducted on these parameters.

Vaccine effectiveness

To determine the effectiveness of the HPV vaccine, various factors were taken into consideration. The overall vaccine effectiveness for each type of HPV lesion was calculated based on the proportion of cases caused by different HPV subtypes and the vaccine efficacy in an HPV-naive population. Vaccine efficacy against CIN, CC, and GW lesions was taken from clinical trials [23, 24]. Other studies were used to support and discuss our findings effectively [25, 27, 29].

Cross-protection against 10 types of the most common oncogenic HPV subtypes was also taken into account. This cross-protection, which includes HPV-31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59, was based on the findings of relevant clinical trials [31, 34].

It was assumed that vaccine efficacy provides lifelong protection for all three vaccines. This assumption enabled a more realistic estimation of the long-term impact of the HPV vaccine on cervical lesions. Data on HPV-type distribution in CIN and cervical cancer were obtained from a study conducted in Iran [4]. While data on GW were gathered from another study conducted in Iran [35], along with expert opinions (Table 2). To estimate the maximum effect of HPV vaccination on cervical lesions, a 100% vaccine coverage rate was assumed. This hypothetical scenario facilitated a deeper understanding of the potential impact of widespread vaccination. However, it is essential to note that achieving 100% vaccine coverage is challenging in practice.

The results of the research suggest that the two-dose vaccination schedule offers comparable levels and duration of protection in terms of immunological efficacy [37, 41]. Therefore, a two-dose vaccination schedule was used in the base-case analysis.

Based on these assumptions and data sources, the following vaccine effectiveness values were used in the base-case analysis. Specifically, effectiveness estimates for GW were 0% for the bivalent vaccine, 74.2% for the quadrivalent vaccine, and 74.2% for the nine-valent vaccine. For CIN1, effectiveness was 55.7% for the bivalent, 60.2% for the quadrivalent, and 69.1% for the nine-valent vaccine. For CIN2/3, it was 73.7% for the bivalent, 62.8% for the quadrivalent, and 71.9% for the nine-valent vaccine. Finally, for CC, the assumed effectiveness was 79.6% for the bivalent, 71.1% for the quadrivalent, and 78.1% for the nine-valent vaccine.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The model was programmed using TreeAge Pro and estimated the number of CIN lesions, CCs, CC deaths, GWs averted, QALYs gained, and total costs for the different types of HPV vaccines over the lifetime of the cohort. Incremental costs, outcomes, and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) were also calculated to compare various vaccination schedules, using the no vaccination strategy as the base-case scenario.

In the base-case analysis, both costs and utilities were discounted at an annual rate of 5%, consistent with the IFDA executive manual [38] and standard practice in health economic evaluations conducted in Iran [42].

According to the WHO threshold recommendation, an intervention is considered highly cost-effective if its ICER is less than one-time gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, could be cost-effective if less than three times GDP per capita, and not cost-effective if it exceeds three times GDP per capita [39]. Although this threshold has been commonly used as a criterion for cost-effectiveness [36], some studies have suggested that lower thresholds may be more appropriate, particularly in LMICs, with some recommending one-time GDP per capita to reflect opportunity costs in these settings better [38, 43, 44]. Nonetheless, we used a one-time GDP per capita of 4,761.90 USD for Iran in 2019, based on the World Bank data [40], as the cost-effectiveness threshold in this study.

Sensitivity analysis

Both one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis and multivariate probabilistic sensitivity analysis were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. The main parameters were varied, and distributions were assigned to transition probabilities, vaccine effectiveness, and the proportion of outcomes (GW, CIN, and cancer) attributed to each HPV type, along with costs, utilities, and the discount rate of costs. Normal distribution was used when confidence intervals were available; otherwise, a uniform distribution ranging from 25% below to 25% above the base case value was applied (Table 3). A total of 100,000 samples were generated from the assigned distribution.

Incremental costs, QALYs gained, and cost-effectiveness

We evaluated the cost-effectiveness of three HPV vaccination strategies (bivalent, quadrivalent, and nine-valent) compared to no vaccination. The model adopted a lifetime horizon and applied a 5% annual discount rate to both costs and utilities. We considered both 2-dose and 3-dose vaccination schedules, with the no vaccination strategy as the base-case scenario.

Compared to the base-case scenario, the two-dose bivalent vaccine had the lowest ICER of $33.179 per QALY gained. In the base case scenario, the ICERs for the quadrivalent and nine-valent vaccines were 45.088 and 50.067 US dollars per QALY gained, respectively. However, when the analysis considered a 3-dose scheme, the ICERs increased further (50.264, 69.384, and 76.778 US dollars per QALY gained, respectively). Therefore, the highest ICER was observed for the three-dose nine-valent vaccine, compared to the base case scenario. A summary of the mean costs, QALYs gained, and ICERs is shown in Table 5. All these values exceeded the cost-effectiveness threshold of one-time GDP per capita in Iran (USD $4,761.90); therefore, none of the vaccination strategies were considered cost-effective in Iran.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis

A one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis was performed using the Net Monetary Benefit (NMB) framework to evaluate the robustness of the results. The study indicated that the no-vaccination strategy remained the most cost-effective option across all variations in the model’s input parameters within the explored ranges, confirming the stability of the findings under uncertainty. Among the key variables examined, the utility value associated with GW had the greatest impact on the NMB, followed by the transition probability from persistent CIN2/3 to CC. Additionally, changing the discount rate applied to costs, from 3% to 7%, had a significant influence on the results. Although adjustments to the discount rate affected the magnitude of the NMB, none of the HPV vaccination strategies became cost-effective within these parameter ranges. Discussion

This study assessed the cost-effectiveness of three HPV vaccines (bivalent, quadrivalent, and nine-valent) for preventing CC in Iran using a lifetime Markov model. Our results show that none of the vaccination strategies met the cost-effectiveness threshold (1 times the GDP per capita, $4,761.90/QALY), mainly due to high vaccine costs and Iran’s relatively low CC incidence. The nine-valent vaccine had the highest clinical efficacy, reducing CC cases by 98.4% and deaths by 99%, but its ICER ($50,067/QALY, 11 times Iran’s GDP per capita) greatly exceeded Iran’s threshold. Likewise, the bivalent and quadrivalent vaccines, while more affordable, remained economically unviable at current prices. This lack of cost-effectiveness is even more evident in scenarios involving three-dose vaccines (Table 5).

The nine-valent vaccine provides broader protection against oncogenic HPV types, aligning with global studies [49], but its high cost mirrors challenges observed in other LMICs [50–53]. The quadrivalent vaccine provides a 73% reduction in GW, mirroring its established efficacy [54]. Still, its limited impact on CC prevention in low-incidence settings reduces cost-effectiveness.

Threshold analysis revealed that vaccine prices must drop by 82–85% to align with Iran’s GDP-based threshold (Table 6), consistent with findings from similar LMICs [43, 52]. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the findings across various parameter changes; vaccination was not cost-effective at current prices (Figs. 3 and 4).

Our findings align with previous Iranian studies, which showed that the HPV vaccine is not cost-effective in Iran [45, 55]. However, caution should be exercised when comparing ICERs directly due to differences in vaccine prices, cost inputs, and model structures. Similarly, a systematic review of the cost-effectiveness of HPV vaccination in LMICs showed that it was cost-effective in all LMICs except Iran, where the high vaccine cost was a significant barrier [46].

In contrast, Jit et al. evaluated the cost-effectiveness of bivalent HPV vaccination in 179 countries using the Papillomavirus Rapid Interface for Modelling and Economics (PRIME) model. They found it to be highly cost-effective in Iran. However, their model relied on international data sources and was not calibrated with Iran-specific inputs. The authors also recognized poor data quality and a lack of reliable country-specific information for many countries in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, including Iran. Additionally, the vaccine prices used in their analysis were standardized across middle-income countries, but in practice, the vaccine cost in Iran is substantially higher than the benchmark used in their study [47].

Several international studies, including those from Venezuela [48], Taiwan [56], China [57], Philippines [58], Singapore [59] and Canada [60], have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of HPV vaccination using Markov models; however, direct comparisons with Iran are limited due to significant differences in epidemiological and economic contexts. Regarding economic capacity, the GDP per capita in Singapore, Taiwan, China, and Canada ranged from approximately 2 to over 13 times that of Iran (4,761 USD in 2019) [40].

Iran’s low CC incidence (ASIR: 2.2 per 100,000) reduces the absolute benefit of vaccination compared to high-incidence countries [61]. This contrasts with studies from high-burden regions where vaccination is cost-effective even at higher prices [23]. Opportunistic screening in Iran [26] further dilutes vaccination’s incremental benefit, as undetected cases inflate long-term costs.

In deterministic sensitivity analyses, adjusting the incidence of oncogenic and lrHPV types by ± 20% had minimal impact on the overall cost-effectiveness rankings. Even under high-incidence assumptions, no vaccination strategies surpassed the cost-effectiveness threshold, indicating the robustness of the base-case findings. Similarly, reducing the discount rate for costs from 5% to 3%, as illustrated in the tornado diagram, did not change the preferred strategy away from none when assessed through the NMB framework.

This study offers several strengths. It is Iran’s first economic evaluation comparing all three HPV vaccine types with a calibrated static Markov model based on national data. It assesses both CC and GW outcomes for two- and three-dose strategies, providing a thorough overview of possible health and economic effects.

However, the model had some limitations. As a static framework, it did not account for herd immunity or transmission dynamics. Other potentially relevant strategies, such as catch-up vaccination or vaccinating boys, were not included. Due to limited local data availability, HPV incidence and GW prevalence were estimated based on expert opinions and regional extrapolations. While emerging evidence suggests that single-dose HPV vaccination has potential, at the time of this analysis, no robust RCT data were available to assess its long-term effectiveness, especially since even two-dose strategies were not cost-effective. Therefore, single-dose vaccination was not included in the current analysis. Additionally, the study did not account for indirect costs or certain logistical costs associated with vaccine delivery.

Conclusions

While HPV vaccination is a key part of preventing CC worldwide, our analysis reveals significant economic obstacles to implementing it in Iran. The nine-valent vaccine, although more effective, is not cost-effective at current prices, and even the cheaper bivalent vaccine exceeds Iran’s willingness-to-pay limit. Therefore, launching a national HPV vaccination program is not economically justified under current conditions.
