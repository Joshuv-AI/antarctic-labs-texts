Mediastinal Pancreatic Pseudocyst With Esophageal Compression Presenting as Acute Chest Pain
Authors: Bibek Saha, Savan Kothadia, Kirsten Losclazo, Sarah Dwyer Holland, Kena P. Vyas, Banreet Dhindsa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute pancreatitis is a major cause of inpatient morbidity and mortality. Pancreatitis can be complicated by the formation of pseudocysts, which develop after 4 weeks in the peripancreatic space. Most pseudocysts resolve spontaneously but indications for drainage include superimposed infection or organ compression. We present a rare case of a young male cocaine user who presented with acute chest pain found to have esophageal compression from a large mediastinal pseudocyst. The etiology of the chest pain was misdiagnosed on multiple occasions and attributed to a cardiopulmonary or musculoskeletal source, or esophagitis. He underwent endoscopic ultrasound-guided cystogastrostomy with symptom resolution.

INTRODUCTION

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a major cause of inpatient morbidity and mortality, where it encompasses more than an estimated 300,000 US emergency department visits annually.1 The most common etiologies include cholelithiasis (21%–33%), alcohol use (16%–27%), and hypertriglyceridemia (2%–5%).1,2 While AP is typically treated supportively with early nutritional intake, intravenous fluids and pain control, more severe disease, as well as complications, including shock, infection, coagulopathy, acute respiratory distress syndrome, compartment syndrome, and renal failure may require a higher level of care and interdisciplinary management.1,3

In addition, AP can be complicated by acute pancreatic fluid collection (developing <4 weeks) and pseudocyst/walled off necrosis (developing >3–4 weeks) formation.1 Most pancreatic pseudocysts resolve spontaneously, but indications for drainage include superimposed infection and clinical suspicion of obstruction of the biliary tract, stomach, or intestines.4,5 We present a rare case of a young cocaine and alcohol user who presented with acute chest pain and was found to have esophageal compression from a large mediastinal pseudocyst.

CASE REPORT

A 34-year-old man with a history of recurrent episodes of AP (∼9 admissions in past 4 years) due to alcohol use disorder complicated by splenic vein thrombosis with gastric varices (∼1 year prior), multifocal pseudocysts (first present 2 years prior), and chronic pancreatitis, cocaine abuse, and tobacco use (9 pack years) presented to the emergency department for acute chest pain, dyspnea, nausea, emesis, and decreased oral intake 1 day after discharge from an outside hospital (OSH).

Specifically, he had 2 hospitalizations at OSH within 4 weeks of presentation for atypical chest and epigastric pain after ingesting alcohol and cocaine. Workup, including computed tomography (CT), was negative for pulmonary embolus, suggestive of pneumonia, showed a markedly thickened esophagus suspicious for esophagitis, and revealed a pseudocyst likely tracking across the hiatus into the posterior mediastinum. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed Los Angeles grade B esophagitis without report of esophageal compression. Overall, the etiology was thought to be musculoskeletal vs inhalational injury from cocaine, or esophagitis, and he was discharged on omeprazole 20 mg daily. Before this, the patient had a long-standing history at OSH of multiple admissions for AP or an associated complication, and either left against medical advice or was lost to follow-up as an outpatient.

At our center, he reported intermittent chest pain exacerbated by movement. He endorsed mild abdominal pain, nausea, emesis, and chills but denied fever, dysphagia, and odynophagia.

On physical examination, he was tachycardic (104 bpm), and the remaining exam was unremarkable. Laboratory test results revealed a white blood cell count of 13.9 (ref. 4.2–10 × 109/L) and macrocytic anemia where hemoglobin was 12.2 (ref. 13.4–16 g/dL). Liver chemistries were normal, and lipase was 166 (ref. 13–55 IU/L). D-Dimer was elevated to 4,009 (ref 0–230 ng/mL). He tested negative for ethanol and cocaine. Troponin was normal but electrocardiogram (EKG) revealed borderline T-wave abnormalities in inferior leads. CT was negative for cholelithiasis but revealed portal vein thrombosis, multiple pseudocysts, and enlargement of the multiloculated pseudocyst (4.5 × 3.4 cm) within the pancreatic body that extended along the distal esophagus with diffuse esophageal wall thickening and left atrium compression. The largest paraesophageal component of the pseudocyst measured 3.8 × 2.0 cm (Figure 1). Overall, his symptoms were thought to be due to mass effect from the mediastinal pseudocyst rather than acute and/or chronic pancreatitis.

He underwent EGD-endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), which revealed moderate to severe extrinsic compression of the middle and distal esophagus by the pseudocyst (4.9 × 4.7 cm; Figure 1). A cystogastrostomy was performed with placement of a 15 × 10 mm lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) into the gastric cardia 2 cm below the gastroesophageal junction and drainage of purulent material was observed. His chest pain resolved, he was treated with antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam followed by amoxicillin-clavulanic acid for a 7-day course), he declined alcohol cessation assistance, and he was discharged. On outpatient CT 3 weeks later, the paraesophageal pseudocyst had resolved; however, there was worsening, enlarging, and new intra-abdominal pseudocysts with mass effect on the left kidney. The patient underwent repeat EGD-EUS where the initial LAMS was removed, and a new cystogastrostomy was performed with placement of the LAMS at the gastric body. The patient did not follow up in clinic but did undergo repeat EGD 4 months later, where he was asymptomatic. EGD revealed a normal esophagus and the region of the cystogastrostomy had healthy-appearing tissue. The LAMS was removed, and the patient currently awaits outpatient follow-up and imaging.

DISCUSSION

Pseudocysts are common, and are formed when there is disruption of the main pancreatic duct in the setting of pancreatitis, increased ductal pressure or trauma leading to the release of pancreatic enzymes into the retroperitoneum and associated injury.6,7 While pseudocysts typically involve the pancreas and surrounding organs in the peripancreatic space, they can uncommonly erode into other regions including the kidneys, liver, scrotum and mediastinum.6,8 Mediastinal pseudocysts are extremely rare, can have an atypical presentation and is thought to occur when the retroperitoneal fluid tracks into the posterior mediastinum most commonly via the esophageal or aortic hiatus.6,9

We reported an exceptionally rare case of mediastinal pseudocyst causing chest pain initially thought to be due to a cardiopulmonary (acute coronary syndrome or coronary vasospasm due to cocaine use, pulmonary embolus, pneumonia) or musculoskeletal etiology, or esophagitis but found to be secondary to esophageal compression, mediastinal inflammation with cardiac compression possibly contributing. A comprehensive literature search of Medline and Embase of published manuscripts in English revealed only 8 similar cases (Table 1).6,8–14 Incorporating our case, nearly all (7/9) patients were men and the majority were in their third or fourth decades of life. While alcohol is the major cause of pancreatitis, paradoxically pseudocysts in general are more common in women.15 However, our findings suggest that mediastinal pseudocysts with esophageal compression and chest pain may be more prevalent in males, perhaps explained by the higher rate of recurrent and chronic pancreatitis in males.16,17 However, as demonstrated by the 9 cases, mediastinal pseudocysts with esophageal compression and chest pain can occur irrespective of the chronicity or frequency of pancreatitis. Atypical chest pain and dysphagia/odynophagia appear to be the most common symptoms; however, our case was unique given the lack of dysphagia/odynophagia. This absence of dysphagia/odynophagia, combined with cocaine use as well as the atypical presentation of mediastinal pseudocysts as compared with abdominal pseudocysts likely explains the misdiagnosis at prior hospitalizations. The presence of dyspnea and nonspecific EKG findings also made the diagnosis challenging; however, this may have been better explained by the cardiac compression (Table 1).

Currently, management of pancreatic pseudocysts includes percutaneous drainage, laparoscopic approaches, and endoscopic modalities (Table 1).18 Overall, guidelines advocate endoscopic drainage as the first-line intervention.18,19 The major endoscopic modalities include endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography-mediated transpapillary drainage, which involves dilating and stenting the pancreatic duct, and EUS-guided cystogastrostomy, which involves a transmural drainage approach. The latter is the favored endoscopic approach, as the former was found not to provide any additional clinical benefit to transmural drainage except in the context of pancreatic duct disruption, where the combination may reduce the recurrence rate of pancreatic fluid collections.18,20 Our case was unique as it involved EUS-guided cystogastrostomy. Indeed, placement of an LAMS may be challenging in such cases given the risk of esophageal deployment; extra caution should be taken to ensure stent deployment below the gastroesophageal junction.

In conclusion, we present a rare case of mediastinal pseudocyst as a cause of acute chest pain secondary to esophageal compression. Physicians should have high clinical suspicion for this entity when a patient with alcohol use disorder, or history of acute or chronic pancreatitis and few cardiac risk factors presents with atypical chest pain and dysphagia/odynophagia. Earlier recognition may facilitate prompt and appropriate management, improving outcomes and preventing readmissions.
