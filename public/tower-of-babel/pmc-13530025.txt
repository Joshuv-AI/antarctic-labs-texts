Comment on: “Fusion Construct Length is Associated With Increased Risk of Delayed Reoperation Following Cervical and Lumbar Fusion”
Authors: Sharath Raj, Vijay Goni
Published: 2026

We read with interest the study by Rahmani et al, 1 which used the PearlDiver Mariner database to characterize delayed reoperation after cervical and lumbar fusion at 2, 5, and 10 years. Two aspects of the analysis warrant comment. First, the 1-year landmark design censors patients without distinguishing death from other reasons for loss of follow-up, and the Cox and Kaplan-Meier methods used here treat death as noninformative censoring. Over a 10-year horizon in a cohort with meaningful rates of obesity, osteoporosis, and opioid use, death is a genuine competing event: a patient who dies cannot subsequently undergo reoperation, yet standard survival methods can still credit that patient with reoperation-free time, 2 and a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model, or at least a sensitivity analysis, would clarify how much of the widening cervical-lumbar gap by 10 years reflects true reoperation risk rather than differential competing mortality. Second, because PearlDiver’s Cox implementation does not support user-defined referent categories, the hazard ratios for single-, two-, and three-or-more-level fusion were each estimated against the full cohort rather than against a genuine single-level referent. The manuscript describes this only as limiting direct relative risk estimation, but the effect runs deeper: each level’s hazard ratio is generated against a composite reference that includes the exposed group itself, which can inflate the apparent gradient across levels, including the headline near-tenfold hazard for three-or-more-level cervical fusion. A matched-cohort design with a genuine single-level comparator, as used in other PearlDiver-based reoperation studies, 3 would let readers judge the true incremental risk of adding fusion levels.

A third point concerns the 10-year reimbursement analysis, which reports a standardized mean difference of 0.073 (95% CI, 0.059-0.086) alongside a nonsignificant P value of .626, even though smaller standardized differences reached P<.001 at 90 days, 2 years, and 5 years. 4 A confidence interval that excludes the null paired with a substantially larger P value at the same time point is unusual, and clarifying whether this reflects a reporting error, a change in statistical test, or a genuine loss of power in the smaller 10-year cohort would help readers interpret the long-term cost comparison. We would welcome the authors’ response on these points, which bear directly on how confidently construct length can be used for surgical counseling and risk stratification.
