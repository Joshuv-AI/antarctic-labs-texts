Zecheng Gan, Xuanzhao Gao, Yuqing Li

Abstract
Simulating the dynamics of charged particles in quasi-two-dimensional (quasi-2D) nanoconfined systems presents a significant computational challenge due to the long-range nature of electrostatic interactions and the geometric anisotropy. To address this, we introduce a novel quasi-Ewald splitting strategy tailored for particle-based simulations in such geometry. Our splitting strategy seamlessly integrates a collection of advanced numerical techniques, including optimal quadrature rules [L. N. Trefethen, S ​ I ​ A ​ M ​ R ​ e ​ v . SIAM~Rev. 64(1)(2022), pp.132-150], fast pairwise kernel summation methods [S. Jiang and L. Greengard, C ​ o ​ m ​ m ​ u ​ n . C ​ o ​ m ​ p ​ u ​ t . P ​ h ​ y ​ s . Commun.~Comput.~Phys. 31(1)(2022), pp.1-26], and the random batch method with importance sampling in k k -space [S. Jin, L. Li, Z. Xu et al., S ​ I ​ A ​ M ​ J . S ​ c ​ i . C ​ o ​ m ​ p ​ u ​ t . SIAM~J.~Sci.~Comput. 43(4)(2021), pp.B937-B960]. The resulting algorithm achieves an 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ) \mathcal{O}(N) overall computational complexity, where N N denotes the total number of confined particles. Simulations of several prototype systems validate the accuracy and efficiency of our method. Furthermore, we present numerical observations specifically related to nanoconfined charged many-body systems, highlighting phenomena such as dielectric boundary effects, anisotropic diffusion, and the structure of the electrical double layer (EDL) under conditions of charge asymmetry.

Typically, such systems possess a nano-sized longitudinal thickness in the

Among quasi-2D systems, charged many-body systems under dielectric nanoconfinement are of particular interest to us, such as graphene

A key observation in these systems is the

Despite significant advancements in simulation techniques over the past few decades, achieving accurate and efficient representations of the dielectric confinement effect remains a formidable challenge

Moreover, the intrinsic multi-scale nature of quasi-2D systems, combined with the long-range interactions inherent to electrostatic forces, presents profound difficulties for both theoretical modeling and numerical simulation approaches.

Linear-scaling algorithms have been extensively developed for the efficient simulation of isotropic, non-confined
N
-particle systems under periodic or free-space boundary conditions.
These approaches primarily follow three strategies: (1) Fourier spectral methods, (2) tree-based methods, and (3) random batch sampling methods.

Random batch sampling methods, such as the random batch Ewald (RBE)

,
41

42

While these methods are robust for isotropic Coulomb systems, quasi-2D systems with confined geometries introduce unique challenges due to reduced symmetry, necessitating either significant modifications to existing algorithms or the development of new methodologies.

Various methods have been developed to reduce the

For systems with a homogeneous dielectric background, early contributions include the Lekner summation-based MMM2D method

More recent developments include the Ewald3DC method

However, the requirement of a homogeneous dielectric background limits their general applicability.
To address sharp dielectric interfaces, one approach involves solving for the induced charge density or surface potential through various optimization techniques
[

,

,
36

37
,

Alternatively, the Image Charge Method (ICM) can be integrated with established electrostatic solvers

In this work, we propose an

To overcome the difficulties caused by the geometry of quasi-2D systems, we propose a tailored quasi-Ewald splitting strategy, which divides the original Coulomb kernel into short-range real space and long-range Fourier space components.
Through the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, the Green’s function for quasi-2D nanoconfined electrostatics is derived,
one thus arrives at an analytic and fast convergent lattice summation formula for this class of systems.
We further develop a set of efficient and accurate methods to numerically evaluate these components.
We apply analytic reduction combined with a tailored Gauss quadrature rule

Notably, QEM is

Consequently, this approach is capable of addressing challenges posed by extremely thin systems with polarizable boundaries, which are challenging to address with existing approaches.

In this section, we introduce the key model concepts and mathematical notations that will be used throughout this paper.

Physically, these systems model charged many-body interactions with dielectric confinement effects

Next, we describe molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of quasi-2D charged systems in a canonical (NVT) ensemble.
Consider
N
charged particles inside the simulation box with positions
\bm{r}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
, carrying charges
q_{i}
(
i=1,2,\ldots,N
), and satisfying the overall-charge-neutral condition (if not, the electrostatic interaction will be ill-defined under doubly periodic boundary conditions
[
20
]
):

\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}=0\;.

(2)

Then MD simulation of the
N
charged particles in a canonical ensemble essentially requires numerically integrating the following
6N
-dimensional Newton’s equations with Langevin thermostat
[
19
]
,

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{i}&=\bm{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}t\;,\\
m_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{i}&=\bm{F}_{i}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}_{i}\;,\end{split}

(3)

where
\bm{F}_{i}
represents the force exerted on the
i
-th particle, and
\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}_{i}=-\gamma\bm{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{i}
represents the coupling with the environment (heat bath), where
\bm{W}_{i}
are i.i.d. Wiener processes,
\gamma
the reciprocal characteristic time associated with the thermostat, and
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}
.
It is understood that for Langevin dynamics, the damping term
-\gamma\bm{v}_{i}\mathrm{d}t
and thermal fluctuation term
\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{i}
are balanced by the fluctuation-dissipation relationship, thus approximating a NVT ensemble.
Finally, the force
\bm{F}_{i}
is computed via
\bm{F}_{i}:=-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}_{i}}U
, where
U
is the potential energy of the system.
Specifically, for electrostatic energy of quasi-2D systems, the potential energy
U
reads,

U=\frac{1}{2}{\sum_{\bm{m}}}^{\prime}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}G(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{\bm{m}})\;,

(4)

where
\bm{m}=(m_{x},m_{y},0)
with
(m_{x},m_{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\bm{L}_{\bm{m}}:=(m_{x}L_{x},m_{y}L_{y},0)
denotes the quasi-2D lattice vectors, and the lattice sum

{\sum\limits_{\bm{m}}}^{\prime}
is defined such that the singular contribution in the bare Coulomb interaction for
i=j,~\bm{m}=\bm{0}
is excluded.
Finally, we define
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})
as the Green’s function in a quasi‑2D geometry, governed by Poisson’s equation subject to dielectric interface conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left[\epsilon(\bm{r}){\nabla}_{\bm{r}}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})\right]=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}),&\text{for}~\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;,\\
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\cap\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{u}}\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\cap\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}\;,\\
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})\to 0,&\text{as}~{r}\to\infty\;,\end{array}\right.

(5)

where
r:=\left\lVert\bm{r}\right\rVert=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}
, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
represents the Euclidean norm.

The ICM can be applied to analytically solve the above set of equations, which expresses the Green’s function as an infinite series of image charges, constructed through an iterative process of image-charge reflections
[
35
,
61
]
.
Note that after each reflection, the magnitude of image charges is weakened by a dimensionless factor
\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}
(or
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
), measuring the dielectric contrasts for the upper and lower dielectric interfaces, defined as

\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}:=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}},~~\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}:=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}}\;.

(6)

Since the dielectric constants are all positive, consequently,
{\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}
satisfy that
\left\lvert\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\right\rvert<1
and
\left\lvert\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\right\rvert<1
,
hence the following infinite series ICM representation of the Green’s function
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})
is guaranteed to be convergent:

G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}}\left[\frac{1}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\right\rVert}+\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma_{l}^{+}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{+}^{\prime(l)}\right\rVert}+\frac{\gamma_{l}^{-}}{\left\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{-}^{\prime(l)}\right\rVert}\right)\right]\;,

(7)

where the factors for the
l
-th level image charges read
\gamma_{l}^{+}=\gamma_{d}^{\lceil l/2\rceil}\gamma_{u}^{\lfloor l/2\rfloor}
and
\gamma_{l}^{-}=\gamma_{d}^{\lfloor l/2\rfloor}\gamma_{u}^{\lceil l/2\rceil}
. Here the superscript
+(-)
indicates the location of image charges in
\Omega_{\textrm{u}}\left(\Omega_{\textrm{d}}\right)
. The notation
\lceil x\rceil(\lfloor x\rfloor)
represents the “ceil” (“floor”) function, and the position of the
l
th-level images are given by,

\bm{r}_{\pm}^{\prime(l)}=\left(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},(-1)^{l}z^{\prime}\pm lL_{z}\right)\;.

(8)

The above ICM representation of the Green’s function provides theoretical benchmark for systems considered here. However, it is important to note that due to the long-range nature of electrostatic interactions, Eq. (
7
) is not well suited for large-scale MD simulations of quasi-2D charged systems due to its high computational complexity, especially for strongly-confined systems (i.e., when
L_{z}\ll L_{x,y}
).

Finally, we revisit the idea of the Ewald splitting technique for efficient calculations of the electrostatic interactions in a quasi-2D geometry.
For quasi-2D systems
without
dielectric interface conditions, the so-called Ewald2D method
[
71
,
33
,
14
]
has been developed, which decomposes the interaction energy
U
as

U=U_{s}+U_{l}\;,

(9)

where the short-range and long-range components are given by

\begin{split}U_{s}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}{\sum_{\bm{m}}}^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{\alpha}\left\lVert\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L_{m}}\right\rVert)}{\left\lVert\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L_{m}}\right\rVert}-\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{{\pi}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}^{2}\;,\\
U_{l}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}{\sum_{\bm{m}}}\frac{\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{\alpha}\left\lVert\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L_{m}}\right\rVert)}{\left\lVert\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L_{m}}\right\rVert}\;,\end{split}

(10)

respectively. Note that
\mathrm{erf}(x)
is the error function

\mathrm{erf}(x):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\;,

(11)

and
\mathrm{erfc}(x):=1-\mathrm{erf}(x)
,
\alpha>0
is the splitting parameter that can be adjusted to maximize performance in MD simulations
[
19
]
.
The computational efficiency can be enhanced via Ewald decomposition due to the fact that, (a)
U_{s}
decays fast in real space and is short-ranged, so it can be efficiently evaluated via truncation; and (b)
U_{l}
is still long-ranged, but the interaction becomes smooth even when
\bm{r}_{i}\to\bm{r}_{j}
and
\bm{m}=\bm{0}
, so that it converges spectrally in Fourier space.
Consequently, based on
[
20
, Lemma 2.4]
, the potential
U_{l}
can be transformed into the following summation form and evaluated in the
k
-space,

U_{l}=U_{l}^{\bm{0}}+\sum_{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}U_{l}^{\bm{k}}\;,

(12)

where,

U_{l}^{\bm{k}}:=\frac{\pi}{2L_{x}L_{y}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}}{k}\left[\xi^{+}(k,z_{ij})+\xi^{-}(k,z_{ij})\right],

(13)

with
\bm{\rho}_{ij}:=(x_{i}-x_{j},y_{i}-y_{j})
,
z_{ij}:=\left\lVert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rVert
,
k:=\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert
, and

\xi^{\pm}(k,z_{ij}):=e^{{\pm kz_{ij}}}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{k}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\pm\sqrt{\alpha}z_{ij}\right)\;,

(14)

U_{l}^{\bm{0}}:=-\frac{\pi}{L_{x}L_{y}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}\left[{z_{ij}}\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{\alpha}{z_{ij}})+\frac{e^{-\alpha z_{ij}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\alpha\pi}}\right]\;.

(15)

While the Ewald2D summation accelerates numerical computations for the interaction energy
U
, it has several significant drawbacks: (a) even with an optimal choice of
\alpha
, computing
U
for an
N
-particle system requires a computational complexity of
O\left(N^{2}\right)
, which is still very expensive for large particle numbers; (b) the functions
\xi^{\pm}\left(k,z_{ij}\right)
are ill-conditioned, as they grow exponentially with

kz_{{ij}}
, resulting in catastrophic error cancellation in numerical computations; and (c) the Ewald2D formula is applicable only to homogeneous systems without dielectric interface conditions.
These challenges underscore the need to develop novel numerical methods that can simultaneously handle long-range interactions, quasi-2D geometry, and dielectric interface conditions, while ensuring both numerical stability and computational efficiency.

\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
,

\displaystyle\hat{G}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}}{2k\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\sum_{p=1}^{4}\Gamma_{p}e^{-ka_{p}(z;z_{0})}\;,

(22)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1:4}

\displaystyle:=\left[1,\gamma_{\mathrm{d}},\gamma_{\mathrm{u}},\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\right]\;,

\displaystyle a_{1:4}(z;z_{0})

\displaystyle:=\left[\left\lvert z-z_{0}\right\rvert,z+z_{0},2L_{z}-z-z_{0},2L_{z}-\left\lvert z-z_{0}\right\rvert\right]\;.

Remark 4
.

(a) The condition
\lvert\gamma_{u}\gamma_{d}\rvert<1
ensures the uniqueness of the quasi-2D Green’s function;
(b) All the components in
a_{1:4}(z;z_{0})
have positive values; (c) The overall charge neutrality condition Eq. (
2
) is needed so that for
k=0
, the infinite boundary condition can be met for
N
-particle systems.

Based on the quasi-Ewald splitting we introduced in Eqs. (
16
)-(
17
) for the delta source, that is,
\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{0})=\sigma_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r}_{0})+\sigma_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r}_{0})\;,

we shall find the corresponding decomposition for the Green’s function as

G(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})=G_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})+G_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})\;,

(23)

where
G_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})
and
G_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})
are the electrostatic potentials generated by the charge densities
\sigma_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r}_{0})
and
\sigma_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r}_{0})
, respectively.
These can be solved analogously by using Prop.
2
; the resulting expressions are summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 5
.

The Green’s function given in Theorem
3
admits a quasi‑Ewald splitting, namely,

G(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})=G_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})+G_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})\;,

(24)

where for any
\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{s}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=\hat{G}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\;,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{l}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=\hat{G}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\;,

and for
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{s}(\bm{0},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=0\;,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{l}(\bm{0},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=\hat{G}(\bm{0},z;z_{0})\;.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward: one can solve for
\hat{G}_{s}
and
\hat{G}_{l}
by replacing the Dirac delta source with
\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{l}
, then using Prop.
2
and following the same steps as was done in the proof of Thm.
3
.
We omit the details.

Finally, one can recover
G_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})
and
G_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})
in real space by using the inverse quasi-2D Fourier transform,

\displaystyle G_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{G}_{s}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{G}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\;,

\displaystyle G_{l}(\bm{r};\bm{r_{0}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{G}_{l}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{G}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\;.

Thus the total electrostatic interaction energy
U
can be expressed as

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=U_{s}+U_{l}\;,

where

U_{s}:=\frac{1}{2}{\sum_{\bm{m}}}^{\prime}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}G_{s}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{m})\;,

(25)

and

U_{l}:=\frac{1}{2}{\sum_{\bm{m}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}G_{l}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{m})\;.

(26)

In the following sections, we will present a collection of numerical techniques that enable the evaluation of Eqs. (
25
)-(
26
) in particle-based simulations with an optimal computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(N)
.

4
Optimal quadrature rules for short-range interactions

We begin by deriving an optimal quadrature rule for the evaluation of the short-range interaction energy
U_{s}
.
As discussed earlier,
G_{s}
is the potential field generated by
\sigma_{s}
, which represents a source point screened by a flattened Gaussian density surrounding it.
Due to this fact, the leading order of
G_{s}
in the far field behaves as a quadrupole, i.e., the potential decays as
1/r^{3}
, which is a short-range interaction in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Consequently, the infinite lattice summation in Eq. (
25
) can be truncated to a finite sum in real space with an inter-particle cutoff distance
r_{c}
,

U_{s}\approx U_{s,*}={\sum_{\left\lVert\bm{\rho}_{ij,m}\right\rVert\leq r_{c}}}^{\prime}q_{i}q_{j}G_{s}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{m})\;,

(27)

where
\bm{\rho}_{ij,m}:=\bm{\rho}_{i}-\bm{\rho}_{j}+(L_{x}m_{x},L_{y}m_{y})
, indicating that among all possible pairs of particles, only the terms with
\left\lVert\bm{\rho}_{ij,m}\right\rVert\leq r_{c}
need to be computed. As such, neighbor-list algorithms can be applied to reduce the cost to
\mathcal{O}(N)

[
19
]
if
r_{c}
is a constant that does not vary with the system size.

The main difficulty in computing Eq. (
27
) lies in efficient and accurate quadrature rules for evaluating
G_{s}
, which reads,

\displaystyle G_{s}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}}{k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\left(\sum_{p=1}^{4}\Gamma_{p}e^{-ka_{p}(z_{i};z_{j})}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\;,

where
\bm{\rho}_{ij}:=\bm{\rho}_{i}-\bm{\rho}_{j}
. Due to the cylindrical symmetry,
G_{s}
can be further reduced to the following 1D
inverse Hankel transform
format,

\displaystyle G_{s}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j})

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}}{k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\left(\sum_{p=1}^{4}\Gamma_{p}e^{-ka_{p}(z_{i};z_{j})}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\;,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-ka_{p}(z_{i};z_{j})}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho_{ij}k)\mathrm{d}k\;,

where
\rho_{ij}:=\left\lVert\bm{\rho}_{ij}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\bm{\rho}_{i}-\bm{\rho}_{j}\right\rVert
, and
\mathcal{J}_{0}(\cdot)
is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind, whose expression reads
\mathcal{J}_{0}(r):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}r\sin\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta\;.

Consider Eq. (
28
), without loss of generality, we develop optimal quadrature rules for evaluating the following integral

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-ak}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k\;,

(29)

where
a>0
and
\rho>0
are constants.
First, we split Eq. (
29
) into the summation of two terms,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-ak}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\right)\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k}_{\text{Term}~\mathrm{I}}-\underbrace{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k}_{\text{Term}~\mathrm{II}}\;.

Clearly, Term II is numerically more tractable, as its integrand contains the factor
e^{\frac{-k^{2}}{4\alpha}}
, which decays rapidly with increasing

k
.
For term I, we further decompose it into two terms,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k}_{\text{Term}~\mathrm{I(a)}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-2kL_{z}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k}_{\text{Term}~\mathrm{I(b)}}\;.

Notice that
\left\{e^{-ax},\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}\right\}
is a Hankel transform pair, namely,
\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ax}\mathcal{J}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}
, thus
Term
\mathrm{I(a)}
can be obtained analytically,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\;.

(30)

Although
\mathcal{J}_{0}(r)
is slowly decaying and oscillatory as
r\to+\infty
, both Term
\mathrm{I(b)}
and Term
\mathrm{II}
contain rapidly decaying terms
e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}
and
e^{-kL_{z}}
, allowing efficient calculations using optimal quadrature rules proposed by Trefethen et al. in
[
75
]
.
For a given truncation parameter
M>0
, the infinite integral can be first approximated by truncation,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}F(k)\mathrm{d}k\approx\int_{0}^{M}F(k)\mathrm{d}k\;,

and then the integral on
[0,M]
can be efficiently evaluated using Gauss-Legendre quadrature.
We further provide truncation error estimates to determine
M
in practice, which are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

We denote the truncation errors of Term
\mathrm{I(b)}
and Term
\mathrm{II}
respectively by

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{I}_{b}(M)

\displaystyle:=\int_{M}^{+\infty}\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-2kL_{z}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k\;,

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{II}(M)

\displaystyle:=\int_{M}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}e^{-ak}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho k)\mathrm{d}k\;,

whose estimates read

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta\mathrm{I}_{b}(M)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lvert\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\right\rvert}{1-\max\{0,\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho L_{z}}}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{2L_{z}M})\;,

(31)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta\mathrm{II}(M)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}{1-\max\{0,\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}\}}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{M}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)\;.

(32)

Proof.

The proof relies on the properties of the special function
\mathcal{J}_{0}(r)

[
48
,
70
]
, details are omitted here and can be found in Appendix. Note that
\mathrm{erfc}(\cdot)
decays exponentially, ensuring fast convergence in
M
.

\mathcal{E}
in the evaluation of

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{J}_{0}(50x)e^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

using (a). Gauss–Laguerre (half interval), and (b). Gauss–Hermite (full interval) and truncated

,

; and

. And

6
.

We validate the proposed quadrature rule by testing its convergence on two benchmark integrals involving the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho x)e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x
and
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{J}_{0}(\rho x)e^{-x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x
. Furthermore, we compare our results with standard Gauss-Laguerre and Gauss-Hermite quadratures, which are specifically tailored for integrals of these forms.
First, fixing
\rho=50
, the absolute error
\mathcal{E}
versus the number of quadrature points
n
is presented in Fig.

. Our proposed truncated Gauss–Legendre rule converges significantly faster than Gauss–Laguerre and Gauss–Hermite quadrature, consistent with the findings in
[

]
.
Moreover, for varying
\rho\in[0,10]
, we set the absolute error
\mathcal{E}=10^{-4}
,
10^{-8}
and
10^{-12}
(by choosing a suitable
M
according to Thm.
6
), respectively. We examine the minimal number of quadrature points
n
required for the proposed quadrature rule to achieve the desired accuracy.
As shown in Fig.

, only a few tens of points suffice for both integrals, demonstrating the efficiency of the method.

5
Fast algorithms for long-range interactions

Now we present efficient methods to calculate the long-range interaction energy
U_{l}
.
First, based on the Poisson summation formula, we have

\sum_{\bm{m}}G_{l}(\bm{r}_{i};\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{m})=\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{y}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}}\hat{G}_{l}(\bm{k},z_{i};z_{j})e^{\textrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}\;,

(33)

where

\mathcal{K}^{2}:=\left\{\bm{k}\in\frac{2\pi}{L_{x}}\mathbb{Z}\times\frac{2\pi}{L_{y}}\mathbb{Z}\right\}\;.

Substituting
\hat{G}_{l}(\bm{k},z_{i};z_{j})
derived in Cor.
5
, we obtain the following formula for
U_{l}
,

\displaystyle U_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{m}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}G_{l}(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{L}_{m})

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}\Bigg\{{\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert}+

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}}{k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{4}\Gamma_{p}e^{-ka_{p}(z_{i};z_{j})}\right)e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\Bigg\}\;.

To reduce the computational cost for Eq. (
34
) to
\mathcal{O}(N)
, we integrate the random batch sampling method with an efficient pairwise summation technique.
The details of our approach will be discussed in the remainder of this section.

5.1
Part one:
\mathcal{O}(N)
pairwise summation method

First, for simplicity, we define
auxiliary functions
h_{1:4}(\bm{k})
as

\displaystyle h_{1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\;,

\displaystyle h_{2}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\;,

\displaystyle h_{3}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\;,

\displaystyle h_{4}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}k\left(1-\gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mathrm{d}}e^{-2kL_{z}}\right)}\;,

and another set of auxiliary functions
S_{0:4}(\bm{k})
as

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}L_{x}L_{y}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}{\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert}\;,

\displaystyle S_{1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert}\;,

\displaystyle S_{2}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k(z_{i}+z_{j})}\;,

\displaystyle S_{3}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{k(z_{i}+z_{j})}\;,

\displaystyle S_{4}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{k\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert}\;.

Consequently, the long-range interaction energy
U_{l}
can be written as,

U_{l}=S_{0}+\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{4}S_{p}(\bm{k})h_{p}(\bm{k})\right)\;.

(35)

Evaluating
S_{0:4}(\bm{k})
by direct summation would incur an
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
computational cost.
However, the auxiliary functions
S_{2}(\bm{k})
and
S_{3}(\bm{k})
are
multiplicatively separable
; that is, they can be expressed as

\displaystyle S_{2}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k(z_{i}+z_{j})}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i}}e^{-kz_{i}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{j}}e^{-kz_{j}}\right)\;,

\displaystyle S_{3}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{k(z_{i}+z_{j})}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i}}e^{kz_{i}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{j}}e^{kz_{j}}\right)\;,

which reduces their computational complexity to
\mathcal{O}(N)
.
In contrast,
S_{0}
,
S_{1}(\bm{k})
and
S_{4}(\bm{k})
are non-separable due to the presence of the absolute value term
\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert
, obstructing direct factorization.
Consequently, to eliminate the absolute value operation, we first sort all
N
particles according to their positions along the
z
-axis, ensuring the order:

0<z_{1}<z_{2}<\cdots<z_{N-1}<z_{N}<L_{z}\;.

Since for all
i
,
z_{i}\in(0,L_{z})
is uniformly bounded within the simulation box, strategies such as the bucket sorting
[
11
]
can be applied, and the computational cost can be of order
\mathcal{O}(N)
. Especially considering actual particle-based simulations, only local corrections are needed for successive time steps.
Now, once all
N
particles are sorted,
S_{0}
can be written as

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}{q_{j}(z_{i}-z_{j})}\right)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\left(z_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}q_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}z_{j}q_{j}\right)\;.

To efficiently compute this sum, we further introduce two auxiliary sequences
\left\{T_{1}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\left\{T_{2}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, both of which can be iteratively updated as follows:

\displaystyle T_{1}(1)=q_{1},~~T_{1}(i+1)=T_{1}(i)+q_{i+1}\;,

\displaystyle T_{2}(1)=z_{1}q_{1},~~T_{2}(i+1)=T_{2}(i)+z_{i+1}q_{i+1}\;.

Finally, using these auxiliary sequences,
S_{0}
can be efficiently calculated as

S_{0}=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\left(z_{i}T_{1}(i)-T_{2}(i)\right)\;,

which requires only
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations.

A similar strategy
[
38
]
can be applied for the evaluation of
S_{1}(\bm{k})
and
S_{4}(\bm{k})
.
For
S_{1}(\bm{k})
, after sorting, we have that

\displaystyle S_{1}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k\left\lvert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k(z_{i}-z_{j})}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i}^{N}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}e^{-k(z_{j}-z_{i})}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i}}e^{-kz_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}q_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{j}}e^{kz_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i}}e^{kz_{i}}\sum_{j=i}^{N}q_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{j}}e^{-kz_{j}}\;.

We introduce another two auxiliary sequences
\left\{T_{3}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\left\{T_{4}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and it is noteworthy that
\left\{T_{3}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
is constructed through forward iteration, whereas
\left\{T_{4}(i)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
is generated through backward iteration:

\displaystyle T_{3}(1)=q_{1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{1}}e^{kz_{1}}\;,

\displaystyle T_{3}(i+1)=T_{3}(i)+q_{i+1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i+1}}e^{kz_{i+1}}\;,

\displaystyle T_{4}(N)=q_{N}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{N}}e^{-kz_{N}}\;,

\displaystyle T_{4}(i-1)=T_{4}(i)+q_{i-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i-1}}e^{-kz_{i-1}}\;,

and

S_{1}(\bm{k})
can be efficiently calculated as

S_{1}(\bm{k})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{i}}\left[e^{-kz_{i}}T_{3}(i)+e^{kz_{i}}T_{4}(i)\right]\;.

We observe that the same recurrence scheme can be applied to the calculation of
S_{4}(\bm{k})
, and the details are omitted for brevity.

In summary, for any given
\bm{k}
in Eq. (
35
), it now only takes
\mathcal{O}(N)
cost to evaluate
S_{0}
and
\sum_{p=1}^{4}S_{p}(\bm{k})h_{p}(\bm{k})
.
We proceed to introduce the random batch method to efficiently perform the summation over
\bm{k}
.

5.2
Part two: random batch importance sampling in
k
-space

In this section, we introduce the random batch method
[
43
,
40
]
for efficient summation over
\bm{k}
, so that the overall computational complexity for
U_{l}
can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(N)
.
The idea of random mini-batch sampling originates from optimization and machine learning, such as the well-known stochastic gradient descent (SGD) method. S. Jin et al. first proposed the random batch method (RBM) for interacting particle systems
[
40
]
.
This approach was subsequently extended to sampling in
k
-space using importance sampling for variance reduction.
The resulting random batch Ewald method (RBE)
[
43
,
56
,
55
,
54
,
57
,
59
,
58
,
22
]
has since been successfully applied to various ensembles and systems, demonstrating remarkable superscalability in large-scale simulations.

Here, to further accelerate the computation for the long-range interaction energy
U_{l}
, we first define
\phi(\bm{k})
as,

\phi(\bm{k}):=\sum_{p=1}^{4}S_{p}(\bm{k})h_{p}(\bm{k})\;,

(36)

then Eq. (
35
) becomes

U_{l}=S_{0}+\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\phi(\bm{k})e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\;.

(37)

We define

U_{l}^{\bm{0}}:=S_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}:=\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\phi(\bm{k})e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}\;,

(38)

then
U_{l}
can be written as
U_{l}=U_{l}^{\bm{0}}+U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\;.

Since

U_{l}^{\bm{0}}

can be computed with
\mathcal{O}(N)
cost, we focus on the efficient evaluation of
U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}
via random batch method.

By understanding the lattice summation over
\bm{k}
as calculating an expectation value under the probability measure
\mathcal{P}(\bm{k})\propto e^{\frac{-k^{2}}{4\alpha}}
defined on the lattice
\mathcal{K}^{2}
, we can build a stochastic approximation for
U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}
via importance sampling,

U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\approx U_{l,\ast}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}:=\frac{H}{P}\sum_{\eta=1}^{P}\phi(\bm{k}_{\eta})=\frac{H}{P}\sum_{\eta=1}^{P}\sum_{q=1}^{4}S_{q}(\bm{k}_{\eta})h_{q}(\bm{k}_{\eta})\;,

(39)

where
\{\bm{k}_{\eta}\}_{\eta=1}^{P}
are a mini-batch of frequencies sampled from
\mathcal{P}(\bm{k})
using the Metropolis algorithm
[
67
]
, where
P
is the batch size,
and

H:=\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}e^{-\frac{k^{2}}{4\alpha}}

the normalization constant for
\mathcal{P}
.
Then the corresponding stochastic estimator of forces in Fourier space can also be obtained as

\bm{F}_{l,i}\approx\bm{F}_{l,i}^{*}=-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}_{i}}U_{l}^{\bm{0}}-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}_{i}}U_{l,\ast}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\;.

(40)

In what follows, we present a theoretical analysis of the random batch approximation, emphasizing its convergence rate. We demonstrate that the batch size
P
is independent of the particle number
N
, which ensures that the summation over
\bm{k}
does not affect the overall linear scaling.
Let us start with considering the fluctuations, i.e., the stochastic error introduced by the importance sampling at each time step. The fluctuations of the force acting on the
i
th particle due to random batch approximation, denoted as
\bm{\chi}_{i}
, are defined as follows

\bm{\chi}_{i}:=\bm{F}_{l,i}^{*}-\bm{F}_{l,i}={\nabla}_{\bm{r}_{i}}U_{l,\ast}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}_{i}}U_{l}^{\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}\;.

(41)

The unbiasedness of random batch approximation can be obtained straightforwardly, and is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

\bm{F}_{l,i}^{*}
is an unbiased estimator, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\bm{\chi}_{i}=\bm{0}
, and its variance can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\bm{\chi}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{H}{P}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}}e^{-\frac{\left\lVert\bm{k}\right\rVert^{2}}{4\alpha}}\Bigg|\Bigg|\nabla_{\bm{r}_{i}}\phi(\bm{k})-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{2},\bm{k}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0}}\nabla_{\bm{r}_{i}}\phi(\bm{k}^{\prime})e^{-\frac{\left\lVert\bm{k}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}}{4\alpha}}\Bigg|\Bigg|^{2}\;.

Furthermore, under the Debye-H
\ddot{\text{u}}
ckel (DH) approximation
[
49
]

Lemma 8
.

Under the assumption of the DH theory, given function of the form

\mathscr{G}(\bm{r}_{i})=\sum_{j\neq i}q_{i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}f(z_{ij}),

(42)

where

is bounded by a constant

\mathscr{G}(\bm{r}_{i})\leq q_{i}C_{f}\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}\;,

where

Finally, we introduce the following Theorem

for the boundedness and convergence in the fluctuations originated from the random batch approximation.

Theorem 9

Next, we demonstrate that such a random batch approximation for forces can capture the finite time dynamics of the Langevin thermostat, i.e., Eq. (
3

Specifically, consider a numerical integration of Eq. (
3

\Delta t
the discretized time step,
\bm{r}_{i}
,
m_{i}
and
\bm{p}_{i}
the position, mass, and momentum of the
i
th particle, respectively. We have the following strong convergence result.

Theorem 10
.

(Strong convergence)
Let
(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{v}_{i})
be the solutions to

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{i}=\bm{v}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t\;,\\
&m_{i}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}_{i}=\left[\bm{F}_{i}-\gamma\bm{v}_{i}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{i}\;,\end{split}

where
\{\bm{W}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. Wiener processes. Let
(\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{i})
be the solutions to

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{i}=\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t\;,\\
&m_{i}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{i}=\left[\bm{F}_{i}+\bm{\chi}_{i}-\gamma\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}}\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{i}\;,\end{split}

with the same initial values as
(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{v}_{i})
. Suppose that the masses
m_{i}
’s are bounded uniformly from above and below.
If the forces
\bm{F}_{i}
are bounded and Lipschitz and
\mathbb{E}\bm{\chi}_{i}=0
, then for any
T>0
, there exists
C(T)>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\left\lVert\bm{r}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{v}_{i}-\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\right\rVert^{2})\right]\leq C(T)\sqrt{\Lambda\Delta t}\;,

where
\Lambda
is an upper bound for
\max_{i}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\bm{\chi}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}
and is independent of
N
.

Similar proofs can be found in
[
52
,
41
,
83
]
, and thus, we omit the proof here.
It is important to note, however, that in practical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, both electrostatic and Lennard-Jones interactions can violate the required Lipschitz continuity and boundedness conditions as
r\to 0
.
Consequently, rigorously justifying convergence with singular interactions is challenging and remains an open question. Nonetheless, we contend that Thm.
10
should still hold in practice for two reasons: (1) the Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential, which is employed in MD simulations to represent the finite size of particles, provides strong short-range repulsion, preventing particles from approaching the singularity; and (2) the substantial variance reduction achieved through the importance sampling technique mitigates stability issues.
Numerical results presented in the next section will further validate the effectiveness of the random batch method in capturing finite-time structure and dynamic properties, consistent with the conclusions of Thm.
10
.

7
Conclusion

In summary, we have developed the quasi-Ewald method (QEM) for fast computation of quasi-2D nanoconfined electrostatics.
Our method is validated to be efficient and accurate, and can be used for investigating various dielectric confinement effects in quasi-2D charged many-body systems. In the future, we will consider extending the QEM to quasi-2D systems confined by metamaterials, where the dielectric contrast
\gamma
can be extended onto the whole complex plane (except countable infinite poles)
[
87
]
.
Notably, ICM will diverge in such scenarios, but we expect QEM would still work since it does not rely on ICM.
Furthermore, we plan to apply the QEM to real applications, such as simulation studies of bio-membranes and battery-electrolyte systems, where confinement effects
can significantly influence their properties. We finally note that the variance bound proven in Thm.
9
is based on the DH theory, which becomes inaccurate for strongly-correlated ionic systems
[
17
,
3
]
; obtaining a tight variance bound for such challenging scenarios is reserved for our future investigation.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-{\nabla}_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left[\epsilon(\bm{r}){\nabla}_{\bm{r}}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})\right]=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}),&\text{for}~\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;,\\
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\cap\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{u}}\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{-}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\partial_{z}G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})|_{+},&\text{on}~\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\cap\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}\;,\\
G(\bm{r};\bm{r}^{\prime})\to 0,&\text{as}~{r}\to\infty\;,\end{array}\right.

then for
\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})}{{\partial z}^{2}}-k^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})&=-\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{0}}\delta(z-z_{0})}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}},~z\in[0,L_{z}],\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})}{{\partial z}^{2}}-k^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})&=0,~z>L_{z},\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})}{{\partial z}^{2}}-k^{2}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})&=0,~z<0,\end{split}

with the boundary and interface conditions satisfying

\begin{split}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})&=\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})\;,\\
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})&=\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})&=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})\;,\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})&=\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})\;,\\
\lim_{z\to\infty}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})&=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})=0\;.\end{split}

Clearly,
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})
and
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})
should take the following forms to satisfy the infinite boundary conditions

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{u}}(z_{0})e^{-kz},~z>L_{z}\;,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{d}}(z_{0})e^{kz},~~~~z<0\;.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=-k\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0}),~z>L_{z}\;,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})

\displaystyle=k\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0}),~z<0\;.

Then via Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, we obtain a new set of boundary value problems for
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})
defined on the bounded domain
[0,L_{z}]
only:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})=k\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})=k\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},0;z_{0})\;,

(46)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{c}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})=-k\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})=-k\epsilon_{\mathrm{u}}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},L_{z};z_{0})\;.

(47)

Finally, since
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})
takes the form

\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{k},z;z_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{aligned} &Ae^{kz}+Be^{-kz}\;,\end{aligned}&z_{0}<z<L_{z},\\
\begin{aligned} &Ee^{kz}+Fe^{-kz}\;,\end{aligned}&0<z<z_{0},\end{array}\right.\;

by the continuity and jump condition of
\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\partial_{z}\hat{G}_{\mathrm{c}}
at
z=z_{0}
, respectively; as well as the new BCs at
z=0
and
z=L_{z}
(Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
)),
one can solve for the unknown coefficients
A
,
B
,
E
,
F
.
Analogously, one can solve for the case
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
and we omit this for brevity.

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma 5.2

Under the DH approximation, one is able to estimate functions associated with the
i
-th particle in the form:

\mathcal{G}(\bm{r}_{i})=\sum_{j\neq i}q_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{ij}}f(z_{ij}),

(48)

where
|f(z_{ij})|
is bounded by a constant
C_{f}
independent of
z_{ij}
. The DH theory considers the simplest model of an electrolyte solution confined to the simulation cell, where all
N
ions are idealized as hard spheres of diameter
r_{a}
carrying charge
\pm q
at their centers. The charge neutrality condition requires that
N_{+}=N_{-}=N/2
. Let us fix one ion of charge
+q
at the origin
r=0
and consider the distribution of the other ions around it.

In the region
0<r\leq r_{a}
, the electrostatic potential
\phi(\bm{r})
satisfies the Laplace equation
-\Delta\phi(\bm{r})=0
. For
r\geq r_{a}
, the charge density of each species is described by the Boltzmann distribution
\rho_{\pm}(\bm{r})=\pm qe^{\mp\beta q\phi(\bm{r})}\rho_{r}/2
with number density
\rho_{r}=N/V
. In this region, the electrostatic potential satisfies the linearized Poisson-Boltzmann equation
[
49
]
:

-\Delta\phi(\bm{r})=2\pi\left[q\rho_{r}e^{-\beta q\phi(\bm{r})}-q\rho_{r}e^{+\beta q\phi(\bm{r})}\right]\approx-4\pi\beta q^{2}\rho_{r}\phi(\bm{r}),

(49)

and its solution is given by

\phi(\bm{r})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{q}{4\pi r}-\dfrac{q\kappa}{4\pi(1+\kappa a)},&r<r_{a},\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{qe^{\kappa a}e^{-\kappa r}}{4\pi r(1+\kappa a)},&r\geq r_{a},\end{cases}

(50)

where
\kappa=\sqrt{\beta q^{2}\rho}
denotes the inverse of Debye length
\lambda_{\text{D}}
. By this definition, the net charge density for
r>r_{a}
is
\rho_{>}(\bm{r})=-\kappa^{2}\phi(\bm{r})
. Let us fix
\bm{r}_{i}
at the origin. Given these considerations, for
r\geq r_{a}
, one obtains the following estimate:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(\bm{r}_{i})|

\displaystyle\approx\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash B(\bm{r}_{i};r_{a})}\rho_{>}(\bm{r})e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\rho}}f(z)d\bm{r}\right|

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q_{i}C_{f}e^{\kappa a}}{4\pi(1+\kappa a)}\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r}4\pi r^{2}dr

\displaystyle=q_{i}C_{f}\lambda_{\text{D}}^{2}\;,

where
B(\bm{r}_{i};r_{a})
stands for the ball of radius
r_{a}
centered at
\bm{r}_{i}.

It is noteworthy that upper bound Eq. (
51
) is derived under the continuum approximation. In the presence of surface charges, the charge distribution along the
z
-direction may lack spatial uniformity. However, due to the confinement of particle distribution between two parallel plates, the integral in Eq. (
51
) along the
z
-direction remains bounded. An upper bound in the form of
|\mathcal{G}(\bm{r}_{i})|\leq C_{s}C_{f}q_{i}
can still be expected, where
C_{s}
is a constant related to the thermodynamic properties of the system. The above proof can also be found in Appendix F of Ref.
[
21
]
, which is some of the authors’ earlier work for quasi-2D Coulomb systems but without considering the sharp dielectric interfaces.
