Genome-wide identification of the crustacyanin gene family in Neocaridina denticulata sinensis based on comparative genomics and localization analysis
Authors: Dandan Feng, Hongrui Li, Yijie Liu, Shangpeng Wang, Yingwen Li, Yangcan Gao, Yuying Sun, Jiquan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCrustacyanins (CRCNs) are a pigment-protein widely distributed in crustaceans, and their main function is to bind to astaxanthin to achieve regulation of crustacean body color through protein-ligand interactions. The CRCN gene family forms a highly homologous gene cluster through duplication and subsequent mutations. Current research primarily focuses on characterizing specific subunits of the CRCN gene family in particular species, whereas systematic studies on the subunits of the CRCN gene family across multiple species at the genome level are still lacking.ResultsA total of 24, 44, 51, 17, 4, and 2 CRCN genes were identified in Neocaridina denticulata sinensis, Penaeus chinensis, Macrobrachium rosenbergii, Procambarus clarkii, Eriocheir sinensis, and Portunus trituberculatus, respectively. Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that the CRCN gene family is distributed in gene clusters on specific chromosomes in shrimp. Ka/Ks analysis showed that the tandem duplicated gene pairs of CRCN are under purifying selection. Background

Crustacyanins (CRCNs) are a class of carotenoid-binding proteins widely present in crustaceans and have received widespread attention for their unique role in color regulation [1, 2]. The CRCN gene family comprises a group of highly homologous genes that endow crustaceans with diverse color-changing abilities and other biological functions through different selection pressures and adaptive changes during gene replication or mutation [3, 4]. However, the number of members in the CRCN gene family varies among species, with some species having multiple CRCN gene family members while others have only one or two [4, 5].

The three-dimensional structure of the CRCN proteins is typically composed of multiple subunits, which form stable protein complexes through non-covalent interactions [6–8]. The spatial conformation and amino acid arrangement of the CRCN complex form a highly specialized binding cavity that can stably encapsulate astaxanthin molecules [9]. When the CRCN complex combines with astaxanthin, it gives crustaceans a blue or green appearance [10]. Heating or inducing a conformational change causes the complex to dissociate, releasing the astaxanthin and giving the shrimp or crab a reddish body color [7, 11]. The multi-subunit structure of CRCN determines a significant redshift when bound to astaxanthin and directly affects the molecular mechanism of color regulation [8]. The discovery not only deepens the understanding of crustacean pigment biology but also has important implications for the fundamentals of protein-carotenoid interactions.

The interaction between CRCN and astaxanthin enables these animals to have color adaptability in specific environments, allowing them to gain survival advantages in ecological environments [4, 12]. In addition, the sequence variation and structural adjustment of genes in different species may be the adaptive features formed by these animals in long-term evolution to improve their competitive survival advantage [13]. Therefore, CRCN complexes are not only involved in color regulation but may also play important roles in other life processes.

In recent years, through crystallography [14], spectroscopy [15], and other biophysical methods [16], researchers have gradually revealed the complex and fine interaction mechanism between CRCN and astaxanthin. With the development of genomics and molecular biology techniques, research on the CRCN gene family has gradually expanded from initial gene discovery to systematic exploration of gene function [17, 18], expression patterns [16], immune response [19], and so on. The comparative genomics analysis is a comprehensive approach that can reveal similarities and differences between species by comparing the genomes of different species and studying individual genomes [20]. However, there is a lack of detailed reports on the genome-wide identification and evolutionary analysis of the CRCN gene family in crustaceans.

This study identified and compared the CRCN gene family at the genomic level in six crustacean species, including Neocaridina denticulata sinensis, Penaeus chinensis, Macrobrachium rosenbergii, Procambarus clarkii, Eriocheir sinensis, and Portunus trituberculatus. We identified the CRCN gene family from six crustacean species, including the construction of gene family trees for each subunit, analysis of physicochemical properties, gene structure, conserved motifs, chromosomal localization, and homology analysis. Then, we analyzed the selection pressure on tandemly duplicated gene pairs and calculated the divergence times between these tandem gene pairs. Due to the excellent growth characteristics of N. denticulata sinensis, this shrimp has become a good experimental model for studying Decapoda [21, 22]. In addition, the immunohistochemistry (IHC) study is a major source of data on protein expression and localization [23]. Therefore, we selected a representative CRCN gene (NdCRCN-6) from N. denticulata sinensis for recombinant expression, obtaining a CRCN-specific binding antibody. The protein localization results showed that CRCN was expressed in both epidermal and muscle cells of N. denticulata sinensis, but its expression level in epidermal cells was significantly higher than that in muscle cells. Based on the whole gene data of six crustacean species, this study delves into the function of the CRCN gene family and enhances the richness of CRCN functional studies.

Methods

Experimental animals

N. denticulata sinensis was procured from an aquaculture center in Anxin County, Baoding, Hebei Province. The shrimp, 1.8 ± 0.2 cm in length, were acclimated in a recycling system (29 cm×18 cm×15 cm, transparent water tank) at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C for two weeks. The shrimp were fed a commercial diet (Sera Shrimp Natural, Kunshan) twice daily. To generate polyclonal antibodies, 7-week-old female BALB/c mice (body weight: 20–25 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and raised under specific pathogen-free conditions. All experimental animals were raised under the same conditions and complied with the ethical standards of animal welfare.

Identification of the CRCN gene families

The genomic data of N. denticulata sinensis were obtained from our laboratory (unpublished). Given the important role of crustaceans in aquaculture and fisheries, as well as the challenges faced by genome data assembly quality, this study selected five representative crustaceans for comparative analysis, including P. chinensis, M. rosenbergii, Procambarus clarkii, E. sinensis, and P. trituberculatus. The CRCN gene sequences of the five crustaceans mentioned above were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clone/, accessed 16 September 2024) (GenBank accession numbers listed in S1 File and genome projects in S2 File). The complete genome protein sequences of N. denticulata sinensis were compared against the CRCN protein sequences of the five other crustacean species using the BLASTP tool (v2.6.0). Based on the BLASTP results, all candidate proteins potentially containing the Lipocalin domain were further validated by submitting them to the Pfam (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/, accessed 17 September 2024) and NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/?term=, accessed 17 September 2024). This step was used to confirm the final members of the CRCN gene family. The CRCN genes of N. denticulata sinensis (NdCRCNs) were renamed based on their chromosomal positions in the genome. The molecular weight, instability index, and theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of the NdCRCN proteins were calculated using TBtools-II software [24]. The subcellular localization of the proteins was predicted using the WoLF PSORT online tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/, accessed 18 September 2024). Finally, the CRCN genes of all species were mapped onto chromosomes using TBtools-II to visualize their genomic distributions.

Phylogenetic analysis of CRCN gene families

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of the CRCN gene family in N. denticulata sinensis, P. chinensis, M. rosenbergii, P. clarkii, E. sinensis, and P. trituberculatus, multiple sequence alignment of all CRCN protein sequences was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA [25]. The alignment results were subsequently trimmed to remove low-quality regions. A phylogenetic tree was then inferred using the maximum-likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess node support [26].

Structure and conserved-motif analysis of the NdCRCN gene family

To extract the coding sequences (CDS) and UTR locations of NdCRCN genes, we used in-house Perl scripts. A phylogenetic tree of the NdCRCN proteins was constructed using the NJ method. The conserved motifs were identified using the MEME Suite online tool (https://meme-suite.org/, accessed 18 September 2024), and the structural domains of NdCRCN proteins were analyzed using the NCBI CD-Search tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi, accessed 20 September 2024). Finally, an integrated visualized diagram was drawn using TBtools-II software to display the phylogenetic tree, structural domains, gene structures, and conserved motifs analysis results of the NdCRCN gene family.

Syntenic analysis of homologous gene pairs in the CRCN gene families

To explore the homologous relationships of CRCN genes among N. denticulata sinensis, P. chinensis, M. rosenbergii, P. clarkii, E. sinensis, and P. trituberculatus, the longest amino acid sequences from these six species were first extracted. Then, gene families were identified using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4) with Diamond as the alignment tool. Single-copy genes from the OrthoFinder results were aligned end-to-end using MAFFT (v7.427). These alignments were then subjected to phylogenetic analysis using RAxML (v8.2.12), employing the PROTGAMMAJTT model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to construct the maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree. Subsequently, divergence time trees were constructed using MCMCTREE (v4.9 h) based on the species divergence times. Using these divergence timings, BLAST was employed to compare CRCN proteins and identify homologous gene pairs across species. Finally, the results of the syntenic analysis were visualized using JCVI software [27], with the positions of homologous gene pairs annotated on the syntenic maps.

Analysis of tandem duplication, selective pressure, and divergence time of the CRCN gene families

To analyze the tandemly duplicated gene pairs of the CRCN gene family in N. denticulata sinensis, P. chinensis, P. clarkii, and M. rosenbergii, the MCScanX tool was used to identify tandemly duplicated genes from whole-genome protein sequences [28]. The Perl script was employed to extract tandem duplication gene pairs from the MCScanX results, specifically filtering for gene pairs belonging to the CRCN gene family. Tandem duplication was defined according to the following criteria: (a) both genes in the pair belong to the target gene family, and (b) the gene pairs were defined by MCScanX as being adjacent or physically close on the same chromosome. The identified tandemly duplicated gene pairs were subjected to nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignments using the ParaAT tool [29], generating AXT format alignment files. These files were further processed using the KaKs_Calculator tool with the Yang-Nielsen (YN) algorithm to calculate synonymous substitution rates (Ks) and nonsynonymous substitution rates (Ka) [30]. To investigate the evolutionary history of the CRCN gene family, the divergence times of tandemly duplicated gene pairs were calculated based on the Ks values obtained from the KaKs_Calculator. The formula T = Ks/(2×r) was used to estimate the divergence time (T), where r represents the substitution rate. Based on previous studies on Alpheus snapping shrimp [31], the substitution rate was set to 2.64 × 10− 9 per site per year. This analysis provided insights into the evolutionary trajectories of tandemly duplicated CRCN genes, revealing their divergence times and potential adaptive significance in crustaceans.

Construction of the expression profile of NdCRCN genes in different tissues

To investigate the expression patterns of the NdCRCN gene family in N. denticulata sinensis across different tissues, we constructed expression profiles using transcriptomic data from five tissues, including the ovary, muscle, gill, eyestalk, and epidermis. These transcriptomic data were generated in our laboratory and have not been published. This analysis aimed to reveal the potential tissue-specific functions of the NdCRCN genes. Firstly, raw transcriptomic data from the five tissues were processed using fastp (v0.23.4) for quality control, removing low-quality reads and adapter sequences to ensure high-quality data. Then, the clean reads were aligned to the N. denticulata sinensis reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), generating alignment files for downstream analysis. Subsequently, gene expression levels were quantified using HTSeq (v2.0.2), which calculated the standardized expression levels of each gene in fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) (S3 File). To ensure comparability across tissue samples, the expression data were further normalized. Using the normalized FPKM data, the expression patterns of NdCRCN genes were visualized across the five tissues by constructing a circular heatmap with TBtools-II.

NdCRCNs mRNA expression in epidermis by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted from the epidermis using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (TIANGEN, China). The quality of total RNA was detected by using agarose gel electrophoresis. Immediately, the total RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed with HiScript II® Q Select RT SuperMIX for qPCR Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing) following the manufacturer’s instructions into cDNA for qPCR template. 18 S rRNA was used as an internal reference gene, and the full-length sequence was obtained from GenBank (GenBank accession number: OP185352). The reaction system of qPCR was 10 µL volume, including 5 µL 2 x Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal® Technology, Wuhan), 0.2 µL NdCRCNs-F/R (10 mM) (S4 File), and 0.5 µL cDNA. The qPCR was performed according to the procedure of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 15 s (Bioer LineGene 9600, Hangzhou). To ensure that the amplification product was a single expansion, melting curve analysis was performed from 60 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.4 °C/s. All reactions were performed in triplicate, including an NTC (no template control). To investigate gene expression levels, the expression of CRCN genes was measured using the Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method [32].

Protein expression and antibody preparation

The CRCN subunit sequences exhibited high homology with other CRCN sequences from the same species, with nucleotide similarity ranging from 72.1% to 99.8% and protein similarity ranging from 68.2% to 99.3% [4]. Given the high sequence similarity among NdCRCNs and the highest expression of this gene family in the epidermis, NdCRCN-6 was selected as a representative candidate gene for further investigation. Based on our previously constructed vector pCT7-CHISP6H [33], a pair of primers pCT7-CHISP6H-NdCRCN-6-F/R (S4 File) was designed for PCR amplification. Then, pCT7-CHISP6H-NdCRCN-6 was transformed into BL21-Star (DE3) pLysS competent cells (WEIDI, Shanghai) and then cultivated in 1,000 mL LB liquid medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C until the cell concentration reached OD600 nm of 0.6–0.8. The bacteria were induced to express for 6 h by adding isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) with a final concentration of 200 µg/mL. During these 6 h, sampling was taken for 15% (w/v) SDS-PAGE analysis and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue R 250 (Solarbio, Beijing). The collective bacterial sludge was suspended in PBS and broken via an ultrasonic cell crusher (SCIENTZ, Ningbo). To obtain single protein products, the broken supernatant was purified by Ni2+-NTA (Cytiva, Danaher). To obtain polyclonal antibodies against recombinant NdCRCN (rNdCRCN), the purified protein was mixed with complete Freund’s adjuvant (F5881, Sigma-AIdrich) at a volume ratio of 1:1 and then injected subcutaneously into mice. Two weeks after immunization, the antigenic protein was mixed with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (F5506, Sigma-AIdrich) at a volume ratio of 1:1 and then injected into the abdominal cavity of the mice for four consecutive immunizations.

Western blotting analysis

To evaluate the specificity of antibodies, the total protein of the epidermis was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Samples were electrophoresed according to a method previously reported by Yuan et al. [34]. Then, the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, Merck) with a pore size of 0.45 μm. After blocking with blocking buffer (10 mM TBS containing 7% skim milk and 1‰ Tween 20), the membrane was incubated with a mouse antibody against rNdCRCN (1:1,000 dilution in blocking buffer) at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was treated with HRP-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (AS003, Abclonal) in a 1:5,000 dilution in a blocking buffer at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the binding characteristics were determined by an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (MaxiLuminTM-WB, Biokits).

IHC methodology

The fresh tissues were soaked in 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, AR1069) and were fixed for 24 h. Following fixation, the tissue specimens underwent dehydration via a graduated ethanol series and were subsequently embedded in paraffin. Each 5 μm thick section was cut and affixed to adhesion microscope slides (CITOTEST, Nantong). The IHC protocol for tissue sections was conducted as described in previous literature with slight modifications [35]. In brief, tissue sections were incubated with 3% H2O2 solution at room temperature for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase. To expose the antigenic epitopes, the tissue sections were treated with a citric acid solution at 95 °C for 20 min. After blocking with blocking buffer (10 mM PBS containing 10% goat serum and 1‰ Tween 20), the tissue sections were incubated with a mouse antibody against rNdCRCN (1:1,000 dilution in blocking buffer) at 4 °C overnight. Then, the tissue sections were treated with HRP-labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (AS003, Abclonal) in a 1:2,000 dilution in a blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, signals were developed using the DAB Horseradish Peroxidase Color Development Kit (P0203, Beyotime).

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 19 software was used to detect significant differences, and the statistical data were expressed as the mean ± SD of three samples. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple tests were used for data analysis in gene expression and relative activity. The difference was deemed significant at P < 0.05. The data for this study were processed by GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0.2, San Diego, USA).

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of the NdCRCN gene family members

In N. denticulata sinensis, 24 members of the NdCRCN gene family were identified and named NdCRCN-1 to NdCRCN-24 according to their chromosomal positions in the genome (Gene and protein sequences were shown in S5 File). The length of NdCRCN proteins ranged from 184 to 397 amino acids, with molecular weights varying between 20,776.63 Da and 45,755.76 Da (Table 1). The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) of these proteins ranged from 4.73 to 7.73, with most NdCRCN proteins exhibiting slightly acidic characteristics. The instability index of NdCRCN proteins ranged from 28.29 to 55.29, indicating that some proteins were highly stable while others exhibited lower stability. The aliphatic index ranged from 62.97 to 93.46, indicating that NdCRCN proteins possessed varying degrees of thermal stability. In addition, the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) ranged from − 0.586 to −0.024, demonstrating that all NdCRCN proteins were hydrophilic. For subcellular localization, most NdCRCN proteins were predicted to be extracellular. However, NdCRCN-22 was predicted to be localized to the plasma membrane (Table 1).

Chromosomal localization of CRCN gene family members

The chromosomal localization analysis revealed that the CRCN gene family in N. denticulata sinensis was primarily distributed on chromosome 23, forming a clear gene cluster. Additional NdCRCN genes were mapped to chromosomes 10, 18, and 24 (Fig. 1a). Four CRCN genes have been identified in E. sinensis, which were distributed on three different chromosomes (Fig. 1b). In M. rosenbergii, the main CRCN genes were localized in two gene clusters on a single chromosome (Fig. 1c). In P. chinensis, CRCN genes were mainly clustered on chromosomes of NC_061831.1 (Fig. 1d). In P. clarkii, a total of 17 CRCN subunit genes were identified, which are distributed across four chromosomes. The majority of these genes are located on chromosome NC_059632.1, forming a distinct gene cluster (Fig. 1e). For P. trituberculatus, CRCN genes were distributed across two chromosomes, with CRCN genes present on NC_059304.1 and NC_059285.1 (Fig. 1f). The chromosomal localization results showed that the CRCN genes tended to cluster on specific chromosomes in shrimp species.

Phylogenetic analysis of the CRCN gene family members

A phylogenetic tree was constructed to explore the evolutionary relationships of the CRCN gene family, including 24 NdCRCN, 44 PchCRCN, 51 MrCRCN, 17 PclCRCN, 2 PtCRCN, and 4 EsCRCN proteins (GenBank accession numbers were shown in S1 File). All CRCN protein sequences were categorized into six groups. Among them, Group 2 contained the largest number of members. Groups 2 and 3 consisted entirely of the CRCN-A2 subunits, whereas Groups 4–6 were composed exclusively of the CRCN-C1 subunits. In contrast, Group 1 contained both CRCN-A2 and CRCN-A1 subunits. NdCRCN proteins were distributed across Groups 1, 2, 3, and 6 (Fig. 2).

Motif and gene structure analysis of CRCN family members

By analyzing the annotation files of the NdCRCN gene family, a total of 24 NdCRCN genes were identified, and their gene structures were analyzed (Fig. 3). The results showed that the number of exons in these genes ranged from 3 to 8, whereas the number of introns varied from 2 to 6. A notable variation was observed in intron lengths, with the longest intron measuring 44,096 bp (NdCRCN-1) and the shortest measuring 195 bp (NdCRCN-13). In addition, significant differences were observed in the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) among NdCRCN genes. For instance, the 5′ UTR and 3′ UTR of NdCRCN-1 possessed 374 bp and 822 bp, respectively, whereas those of NdCRCN-13 were only 60 bp and 79 bp. Intron lengths also varied substantially, with the longest measuring 44,096 bp in NdCRCN-1 and the shortest 195 bp in NdCRCN-13. The lengths of the conserved motifs varied from 8 amino acids (motif 6) to 46 amino acids (motif 2). The NdCRCN gene family was distributed across Groups 1, 2, 3, and 6, with Group 6 containing the CRCN-C1 subunits and Groups 2 and 3 comprising the CRCN-A2 subunits. Group 1 comprised a smaller number of genes compared to other groups, including both the CRCN-A2 subunits (NdCRCN-1, NdCRCN-21, NdCRCN-24) and the CRCN-A1 subunits (NdCRCN-22, NdCRCN-23).

Tandem duplication, selective pressures, and divergence history of the CRCN gene families

Tandem duplication analysis revealed 7, 18, 5, and 9 gene pairs in N. denticulata sinensis, M. rosenbergii, P. chinensis, and P. clarkii, respectively. In all species, the Ka/Ks ratios of the tandem duplicated gene pairs were consistently less than 1 (Fig. 6a-d), indicating that purifying selection was the main force maintaining the functional stability of the CRCN gene family. The Ka/Ks values in N. denticulata sinensis ranged from 0.0464 to 0.1891, from 0 to 0.3153 in M. rosenbergii, from 0 to 0.4381 in P. chinensis, and from 0.1673 to 0.254 in P. clarkii (S6 File). Divergence time estimation revealed a wide range of duplication events across the four species (Fig. 6e). In N. denticulata sinensis, duplication events were concentrated between 100 and 700 Mya, with the earliest event occurring at 676 Mya. In M. rosenbergii, duplication events spanned from 4 to 700 Mya. In P. chinensis, the duplication events occurred between 3 and 689 Mya. In P. clarkii, the duplication events ranged from 21 to 669 Mya (S6 File).

Expression profiles of NdCRCN genes in different tissues

The circular heatmap revealed different expression patterns of the NdCRCN gene family members in different tissues. Consistent with previous research results [8], NdCRCNs were mainly expressed in the epidermis, with NdCRCN-6 possessing the highest expression level. Furthermore, NdCRCNs were also expressed in muscle, ovarian, and gill tissues. However, the expression profiles of some members of the NdCRCN gene family were generally low in various tissues. (Fig. 7a). The qPCR results (Fig. 7b) showed that the expression of NdCRCN genes in the epidermis was consistent with the above heatmap results, further verifying the reliability of the above results (Fig. 7a). There was a highly expressed gene (NdCRCN-6) among the 24 NdCRCN genes in the epidermis. There were also some non-expressed NdCRCN genes (NdCRCN-1, 10, 12, 13, 24) in the epidermis (Fig. 7b). However, non-expressed NdCRCN genes in the epidermis showed slight expression in ovaries or muscles from the heatmap results (Fig. 7a).

Discussion

In N. denticulata sinensis, M. rosenbergii, P. chinensis, and P. clarkii, 7, 18, 5, and 9 tandemly duplicated gene pairs were identified, respectively. The Ka/Ks ratio is a common metric for quantifying the selective pressure on genes at the sequence level across different species [36]. Ka/Ks analysis showed that all gene pairs across species had Ka/Ks ratios less than 1 (Fig. 6a-d), indicating that the CRCN gene family has been evolving under purifying selection. However, selection pressures varied among species. In N. denticulata sinensis, the Ka/Ks values ranged from 0.0464 to 0.1891, with most gene pairs showing values below 0.1, suggesting greater conservation of the CRCN gene family in this species. In contrast, P. chinensis exhibited higher Ka/Ks values, ranging from 0 to 0.4381, indicating greater potential for functional evolution. Previous studies have demonstrated that the number of CRCN genes rapidly increased in P. monodon, but these genes retained their original function, particularly in color regulation [4].

Divergence time analysis revealed varying duplication events across species. In N. denticulata sinensis, duplication events occurred mainly between 100 and 700 Mya, with the earliest event at approximately 676 Mya. In M. rosenbergii, duplication events ranged from 4 to 700 Mya, while in P. chinensis, events occurred between 3 and 689 Mya. In P. clarkii, duplication events ranged from 21 to 669 Mya (Fig. 6e). Most CRCN gene duplication events took place before the divergence of the common ancestor of the selected species (around 331 Mya) (Fig. 4). These results suggest that the CRCN gene family has undergone multiple rounds of expansion and evolutionary divergence, with purifying selection playing a significant role in its evolution, while some genes may have potential for neofunctionalization. For example, a study on Exopalaemon carinicauda finds that the CRCN gene family is not only involved in color formation but also plays an important role in resistance to heavy metal stress [19].

The observation that members of the NdCRCN gene family exhibit varied expression levels across different tissues implies a possible functional divergence within the family. The diversity and family extension of CRCN genes may reflect the molecular evolutionary processes of crustaceans in adapting to different ecological environments, predation, and reproductive strategies. For instance, an investigation into the crustacean genomic system revealed that the expansion of immune-related gene families—including those encoding antimicrobial peptides and lysozymes—significantly augmented the resistance of crustaceans to pathogenic infections [37]. In addition, research has demonstrated that the DDE transposase 4 gene in E. sinensis exhibits significant tandem duplication, and it is hypothesized that this gene expansion may be associated with enhanced tolerance of the species [38].

The similarity observed among the antigenic epitopes indicates that the corresponding antigen proteins are highly conserved in both structure and sequence, which may consequently result in cross-reactivity [39]. Considering that the CRCN antibody derived from N. denticulata sinensis elicited antigen-antibody cross-reactivity in P. chinensis, M. rosenbergii, and P. clarkii (Fig. 9b), these findings suggest that the CRCN proteins in these species exhibit a high degree of structural and sequence conservation. By contrast, no cross-reactivity was detected in the P. trituberculatus and E. sinensis, indicating significant structural and sequence differences in the CRCN protein between shrimp and crab. Multiple paralogous genes within the gene family serve redundant functions, thereby enhancing the survival ability of organisms under environmental stress, gene mutations, or gene loss [40]. In addition, the latest research indicates that there is a functional compensation effect among members of the NdCRCN gene family, suggesting that members of this family may have functional redundancy [41]. Functional redundancy ensures that even if some genes mutate or lose function, other similar genes can still maintain normal physiological function [42]. Evolutionary analysis suggests that these homologous genes likely originated through gene duplication (Fig. 6). In terms of ecological adaptation, shrimp usually face significant environmental pressures, including predation threats, reproductive needs, and adaptation requirements to color changes in the environment [43, 44]. The shrimp color regulation system necessitates an increased number of CRCN gene copies to manage complex environmental fluctuations, potentially driving the expansion of the CRCN gene family and the formation of prominent gene clusters (Fig. 1). In contrast, crabs are relatively less sensitive to color changes and may rely more on external environmental factors such as food sources or more stable color characteristics [45, 46]. Consequently, crabs require fewer CRCN gene copies than shrimp, and the formation of gene clusters is not essential for efficient gene expression.

Although members of the CRCN gene family are highly similar in sequence, they exhibit different expression patterns in different tissues. Furthermore, there are significant differences in the expression of multiple subunits of the CRCN gene family in the same tissue. For E. carinicauda, CRCN-C1 subunit genes are mainly expressed in the eyestalks, CRCN-C2 subunit genes are highly expressed in the ventral nerve cord, while CRCN-A1 and CRCN-A2 subunit genes are mainly expressed in subcutaneous adipose tissue [19]. CRCN subunits show specific expression patterns in different tissues, suggesting that this spatial specificity enables tissues to regulate protein function according to physiological needs. In addition, different subunits of the same protein complex exhibit structural and functional differences, thus playing specific biological roles in different tissues. For instance, hemocyanin and cryptocyanin are highly similar in amino acid sequence, but significant differences in key structural domains endow them with different biological functions [47]. Hemocyanin contains complete copper binding sites and can reversibly bind with oxygen, which is responsible for transporting oxygen in the hemolymph. However, cryptocyanin lacks key copper-binding sites and cannot bind to oxygen, mainly serving as a storage protein [48]. The structural differences between hemocyanin and cryptocyanin protein subunits enable different subunits within the same protein family to perform specific functions in different tissues [49]. On the one hand, hemocyanin ensures an adequate oxygen supply for the organism, while on the other hand, cryptocyanin provides the necessary protein and energy reserves required during molting and development [47]. Gene families are usually arranged in clusters in the genome, which not only makes gene replication more efficient but also ensures that they maintain certain functional connections during evolution [50]. The organization of gene clusters helps to coordinate gene expression in epigenetics, which is crucial for some physiological processes [51]. Therefore, the differences in sequence among members of the CRCN gene family may be aimed at more flexibly regulating the physiological activities of crustaceans.

Astaxanthin is an essential component in the life activities of shrimp due to its significant antioxidant properties [52]. However, shrimp themselves cannot synthesize astaxanthin and must rely on food for intake [53]. CRCN belongs to the lipocalin superfamily and can bind hydrophobic molecules such as astaxanthin [54]. Immunological assays analysis showed that NdCRCN was expressed in the muscle tissues of N. denticulata sinensis (Figs. 8a and 9b). Therefore, we infer that NdCRCN in muscles may be involved in the transport of exogenous astaxanthin. Furthermore, the higher signal intensity of CRCN observed in the epidermis, relative to that in muscle tissue, suggests that its predominant epidermal expression may be linked to astaxanthin binding, thereby modulating body coloration. Differences in the spatial distribution of proteins encoded by the same molecule are manifested not only during post-transcriptional regulation but also as an adaptive strategy shaped by long-term selective pressures during biological evolution [55]. By finely localizing mRNA, cells can ensure the production of specific proteins in different parts of the cell, thereby forming internal functional regions [56]. A portion of mRNA may undergo splicing or editing in the nucleus or near the nucleus region, while another portion is rapidly transported to the extracellular periphery for translation [57]. This division of labor enables protein functions to diversify or have different regulatory patterns in time and space, allowing cells to choose appropriate gene expression patterns and face various environmental stimuli to achieve more complex biological functions. However, due to the high sequence similarity among CRCN subunits, current methodologies are insufficient to delineate the distinct functions of each subunit in crustacean physiology. Therefore, future research should focus on elucidating the molecular roles of individual CRCN subunits.

Conclusions

In summary, this study conducted a genome-wide identification of the CRCN gene family in N. denticulata sinensis and five other crustacean species, providing an in-depth analysis of the evolutionary dynamics of the CRCN gene family in crustaceans. The research results showed that members of this gene family are highly conserved and form multiple gene clusters through gene replication. CRCN genes were widely distributed in the double-stranded structure of DNA. In addition, the number of CRCN gene clusters in shrimp was significantly more than that in crabs. Given the high similarity among CRCN genes, a CRCN gene (NdCRCN-6) from N. denticulata sinensis was selected for prokaryotic expression, and then an antigen protein was obtained. The obtained NdCRCN-specific binding antibody caused cross-reactivity in P. chinensis, M. rosenbergii, and P. clarkii. The IHC results showed the expression of CRCN in both the epidermal and muscle cells of N. denticulata sinensis. Notably, a spatial segregation between NdCRCN mRNA and its protein was observed within the epidermal tissue. This study systematically analyzed the information on multiple crustacean CRCN gene families at the genomic level for the first time, providing important references for further research on the CRCN gene family.
