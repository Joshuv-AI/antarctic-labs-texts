Artificial intelligence in advanced liver disease: From risk stratification to decision
Authors: Chia-Fu Liu, Chien-Lin Chang, Ching-Chih Chang, Shao-Jung Hsu, Hui-Chun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The diagnosis and management of liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension have been challenging for hundreds of years, placing a tremendous burden on medical and social resources. In routine practice, clinicians usually depend on various documented scoring and stratification systems to establish the diagnosis and make decisions. These systems have traditionally been constructed through manual review of retrospective data, image interpretation, and statistical analyses. However, the development of artificial intelligence (AI) has allowed for the analysis of large and varied datasets, and consequently the ability to identify novel factors and test the predictive power of different combinations of factors that would not be feasible using traditional means. Considering the promise of using AI for research in this notorious disease entity, this review article discusses recent advances in AI technology regarding various clinical aspects of liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Topics covered include the diagnosis of cirrhosis and clinically significant portal hypertension, detection and prediction of major cirrhosis-related complications (gastroesophageal varices, hepatic encephalopathy, infection, acute kidney injury, hepatorenal syndrome, and hepatocellular carcinoma), assessing the prognosis, and evaluating the treatment response. The potential benefits, drawbacks, limitations, and future perspectives of AI are also discussed.

1. END-STAGE LIVER DISEASE: A CHALLENGING DISEASE ENTITY

Acute and chronic liver insults can activate a cascade of compensatory repair mechanisms. However, tissue repair is usually suboptimal and leads to fibrogenesis rather than liver regeneration. Failure to treat or control the attributable factors can result in the development of advanced liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and portal hypertension. Consequently, the prognosis of cirrhotic patients can be negatively impacted by complications such as gastroesophageal variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), and hepatorenal syndrome (HRS). In 2021, cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases caused 1.43 million deaths globally.1 Furthermore, it is estimated that cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases will cost the world economy $2.649 trillion (2.502-2.827) in 2021–2050.2

Considering the dismal prognosis of patients with liver cirrhosis, early identification, diagnosis, outcome prediction, and treatment recommendations are urgently needed. However, heterogeneous etiologies, clinical course, treatment choices, and multiorgan complications pose tremendous challenges to currently applied modalities and scoring systems. The standard clinical workup of cirrhotic patients includes taking their history, performing a physical examination, and arranging laboratory, imaging, and other assessments, including liver biopsy and molecular tests. However, the relative value of these assessments in terms of their influence on the clinical course and outcome differs, and their relationships are not usually even linear. Furthermore, interobserver and intraobserver variabilities are also possible. These features may account for the suboptimal performance of currently used diagnostic tools and scoring systems,3 and have prompted researchers to explore the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in this distinct disease entity.

2. THE POTENTIAL OF APPLYING AI IN MODERN MEDICINE

AI is a general description that encompasses classical machine learning (ML) and deep learning techniques.3 AI-based algorithms have been proven to be able to deal with extremely diverse information in various medical fields, such as ML of various features of lesions to enhance the diagnostic yields of image modalities including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and endoscopy, extrapolating adaptable models from a huge amount of data, high-throughput screening of blood and tissue samples, providing recommendations on personalized treatment strategies, and outcome prediction. By learning from preexisting data and applying the inferred model to new data, AI is good at mitigating the difficulties in managing very large amounts of various types of data. It can also contribute to reducing the cost associated with traditional research methods.

Recent studies have also indicated that AI-based analysis is potentially more precise than traditional statistical methods when examining the association and significance of a presumed prognostic factor, or when the prognostic impact of a variable is influenced by other factors in an interacting, multidimensional nonlinear way.4 Such situations are encountered in various complicated pathological entities, including liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension.

3. FREQUENTLY APPLIED AI TECHNIQUES IN CLINICAL FIELDS

Classical ML techniques rely on and are also limited by features defined by human researchers. Through the development of algorithms, hardware, and the availability of huge datasets, deep learning approaches can now manage far more parameters than classical methods, making deep learning models more flexible and feasible regarding the processing and evaluation of complex datasets. In the medical field, one of the most commonly used deep learning methods is the artificial neural network (ANN).5 ANNs process data in a way that mimics the human brain through an input layer, an output layer, and one or more hidden layers. The input layer is constructed using preexisting data, and the output layer is constructed of outcomes. After training, an ANN is capable of assigning weights to various inputs, and differences between the ANN output and true output can be reduced to a minimum. Overall, after training, an ANN can make predictions on new patterns or datasets.6–8 In addition, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a class of ANNs, are frequently applied to analyze and identify image patterns and cellular and histopathological features.9 The schematic architectures of an ANN and a CNN are shown in Fig. 1.

Of note, transformer-based models have become the dominant architecture in modern medical AI applications because they can process large amounts of heterogeneous data and capture long-range relationships more effectively than traditional ML or neural networks.10 Such transformers now underpin most large language models (LLMs), vision-language models, and multimodal foundation models used in healthcare. Natural language processing (NLP) is the broader field of AI focused on enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language. In this field, LLMs are a powerful subset that facilitate advanced text analysis.11 The use of LLMs, such as in supporting medical record-taking, has already been implemented by several health systems in the United States, and they are anticipated to improve health care efficiency, quality, and patient outcomes.12 An exciting advance is the development of vision-language foundation models that combine imaging interpretation with language processing. Less manually labeled data are required because the models learn from massive image-text datasets. Therefore, these systems are able to interpret various kinds of images, generate radiology reports, answer image-based clinical questions, retrieve similar cases, and assist radiologists in workflow management.13 Multimodal foundation models that integrate imaging, pathology, clinical records, laboratory data, and genomics are widely considered to be the most transformative direction because they mirror real-world clinical reasoning and may eventually support precision medicine.6

Generative AI has also expanded beyond text generation to synthetic medical image generation, drug discovery, medical report generation, clinical trial design, and research automation. Recent advances include diffusion models and multimodal generative systems capable of producing realistic medical images and improving image reconstruction quality. These approaches may help address data scarcity and accelerate biomedical research.7 Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is also gaining attention. XAI refers to AI systems that provide understandable reasons for their predictions or recommendations, allowing clinicians to understand why a model reached a particular conclusion rather than treating the model as a “black box.” In clinical settings, explainability is particularly important because clinicians, patients, regulators, and healthcare systems require information transparency to perform shared decision-making. This highlights the potential of XAI because many current deep learning models can accurately predict outcomes but provide little insight into their reasoning.8

4. THE FEASIBILITY OF USING AI IN LIVER CIRRHOSIS AND PORTAL HYPERTENSION

Although many advances have been made in the past decades, cirrhosis and portal hypertension remain an overwhelming global health issue, characterized by remarkable morbidity and mortality rates that have adverse impacts on patient well-being and medical resources. The development of cirrhosis is influenced by multiple factors, including etiologies, lifestyle, and genetic predisposition, which makes prevention, diagnosis, and treatment even more challenging.

AI applications are essentially not guided by human-defined features; rather, they learn from preexisting information and examples. AI applications automatically mine data from huge, multidimensional datasets, leading to a performance potentially superior to traditional methods. Considering the distinct nature of AI and its ability to manage a wide range of interacting and complex factors, its application in liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension is promising.

5. THE PERFORMANCE OF AI IN LIVER CIRRHOSIS AND PORTAL HYPERTENSION

5.1. Diagnosis and severity stratification

Deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) are one of the most commonly applied deep learning methods for cirrhosis. They can process image data by mimicking the actions of the human visual system, automatically extracting image characteristics, and providing outcome prediction based on the analysis. In 2018, Yasaka et al.14 developed a DCNN model that could analyze gadoxetic acid-enhanced hepatobiliary phase magnetic resonance (MR) images. They found that the DCNN could accurately differentiate between different stages of liver fibrosis, thereby providing a new method to assess liver cirrhosis noninvasively. In 2020, Liu et al.15 developed another model using DCNN to automatically detect clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) in patients with cirrhosis. The researchers collected 10 014 liver images and 899 spleen images from cirrhotic patients who underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or MR analysis within 14 days of hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) measurements, since HVPG remains the gold standard to assess the presence and severity of portal hypertension. The model demonstrated an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.940 in the MR cohort, showing the potential of a DCNN for the noninvasive, rapid, and accurate detection of CSPH. Another study developed an automated HVPG quantitative estimation model based on CT images, which achieved an AUROC higher than 0.80 and outperformed other noninvasive tools. In addition, the model was validated using internal and external test datasets for various HVPG stages (≥10, ≥12, ≥16, and ≥20 mmHg, respectively), showing the ability to grade HVPG noninvasively with the aid of AI.16 Furthermore, since changes in the hepatic vascular system are pivotal pathophysiologic features of portal hypertension, another study developed a model by enrolling consecutive patients with liver cirrhosis who underwent CT or MR studies and HVPG measurements. A vascular geometric model was constructed from vascular characteristics extracted from CT and MR images of the portal vein, hepatic vein, aorta, and inferior vena cava, respectively, and the results showed that it performed well in identifying CSPH in both the internal and external test sets.17

Based on standard laboratory parameters, another study developed ML models trained on three (3P: platelet count [PLT], bilirubin, international normalized ratio) or five (5P: plus cholinesterase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, and international normalized ratio was replaced by activated partial thromboplastin time) parameters in patients with compensated cirrhosis. The results showed that training on the merged cohort optimized model performance for CSPH (area under the curves [AUCs] for 3P and 5P: 0.775 and 0.789, respectively) and severe portal hypertension (0.737 and 0.828, respectively).18

Histology remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of liver cirrhosis. To examine the performance of AI in this regard, a previous study developed an ML model to identify fibrosis patterns associated with HVPG, and the resultant ML-HVPG score was validated. The results showed that the ML-HVPG score was significantly correlated with HVPG and that it could differentiate between patients with normal (0-5 mmHg) and elevated (5.5-9.5 mmHg) HVPG and CSPH. The discrimination ability of the ML-HVPG score for CSPH was further improved with the addition of an ML parameter for nodularity, enhanced liver fibrosis, platelets, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and bilirubin.19

6. DETECTION AND OUTCOME PREDICTION OF MAJOR CIRRHOSIS-RELATED COMPLICATIONS

6.1. Gastroesophageal varices

Chen et al.20 established a real-time DCNN system, termed ENDOANGEL, to diagnose gastroesophageal varices and predict the risk of variceal rupture. It was trained with 8566 endoscopic images of gastroesophageal varices and 6152 images of normal esophagus/stomach. The authors found that the ENDOANGEL system exhibited superior accuracy in detecting esophageal varices (EVs) and gastric varices compared with endoscopists. The system also outperformed endoscopists in identifying red color signs of varices. Another study identified 12 factors independently affecting the occurrence of variceal bleeding in univariate analysis and used them to develop an ANN model. The model had an AUROC of 0.959, which was significantly higher than values from the North Italian Endoscopic Club (NIEC) (0.669) and revised North Italian Endoscopic Club (Rev-NIEC) indices (0.725; both p < 0.001). Decision curve analysis revealed improved net benefits of the ANN model compared with the NIEC and Rev-NIEC indices.21 Bayani et al.22 used the ensemble learning method to select the most potent predictors of EV grade based solely on routine laboratory and clinical data and found that the Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) model predicted all targets correctly with 100% precision. The most significant variables, according to the CatBoost model, were Child-Pugh score, white blood cell count, vitamin K, and international normalized ratio. The authors concluded that the ensemble learning method allowed for the prediction of EV risk in a routine clinical setting with the potential to reduce the need for esophagogastroduodenoscopy and the cost of long-term follow-up. A recent international cohort study combined shear wave elastography-derived liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and spleen stiffness measurement (SSM) values and components of the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score to develop logistic regression (LR)-FIB-4plus and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)-FIB-4plus models, respectively. The XGBoost-FIB-4plus score demonstrated superior predictive performance for high-risk varices and outperformed the individual parameters (LSM, SSM, PLT, and FIB-4).23 Regarding the outcomes and prognosis, a recent study developed an AI-based acute variceal bleeding (AVB) model for cirrhotic patients with AVB. The authors found that the model could predict treatment outcomes, stratify the risk of treatment failure, aid in clinical decisions, and optimize personalized treatment strategies.24

6.2. Hepatic encephalopathy

Saboo et al.25 applied four ML techniques (random forest [RF], support vector machine [SVM], LR, and gradient boosting) and compared AUROC values for stool, saliva, or both, and found that stool microbiota composition was significantly more informative in distinguishing cirrhosis from HE compared with saliva. Furthermore, a retrospective cohort study identified independent risk factors for ML to predict 28-day mortality in patients with HE and found that the ANN model had the highest AUROC (0.837). Calibration analysis revealed that the model was well-calibrated, suggesting that it could better predict 28-day mortality in patients with HE. The performance of the ANN model was also superior to existing scores, including the Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) and MELD-Sodium (MELD-Na) scores.26

Regarding the performance of NLP in predicting HE, a recently published study analyzed the ability of NLP to predict HE.27 The relevant terms were selected based on guideline review and physician evaluation, and the individual contribution of terms toward HE diagnosis was assessed with ML cross-validation. The results revealed that five signs, symptoms, and treatment decisions with simple phrases extracted from inpatient charts using NLP successfully defined HE across three cohorts.

Although minimal HE has certain clinical significance, it is even more difficult to diagnose due to the subtle symptoms and a lack of efficient and accurate diagnostic tools. Chen et al28 enrolled 65 patients with liver cirrhosis and covert HE (29 with minimal HE and 36 without HE) and generated mean diffusivity and fractional anisotropy maps from white matter diffusion tensor imaging. They used Bayesian ML to determine white matter regions that characterized group differences and found that two white matter regions consistently differentiated minimal HE from no HE. Furthermore, the mean diffusivity and fractional anisotropy values in white matter regions were predictive of survival, indicating that using AI to identify image clues may contribute to the early detection and timely treatment of minimal HE.

6.3. Infection

Hu et al.29 used the CatBoost algorithm to establish a practical and explainable prediction model for the risk of SBP in cirrhotic patients with ascites. The analysis identified six variables: total protein, C-reactive protein, prothrombin activity, cholinesterase, lymphocyte ratio, and apolipoprotein A1 that were closely related to the occurrence of SBP. A recent study using the Veterans Health Administration Corporate Data enrolled cirrhotic patients who underwent paracentesis between 2009 and 2019. XGBoost models were trained, tested, and validated, and the final model was found to be able to exclude SBP with a high negative predictive value, suggesting that it could indicate those unlikely to have SBP and thus avoid unnecessary paracentesis.30

6.4. Acute kidney injury and hepatorenal syndrome

In a study investigating predictors of the outcomes of acute kidney injury (AKI) in cirrhotic patients, Mousa et al.31 applied an RF model and identified serum albumin as the most significant predictor with an importance score of 0.350, followed by neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (0.280) and chronic kidney disease (0.170). In another study, the XGB model was found to have better performance than the LR model, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, MELD score, MELD-Na score, and Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II score in predicting AKI among cirrhosis patients, and it may allow for early diagnosis and treatment.32 Regarding the performance of AI in predicting HRS, Yao et al.33 retrieved information on treatment, comorbidities, laboratory findings, and demographics of cirrhotic patients from the electronic intensive care unit (eICU) and Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) databases. Variables were screened using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, XGBoost, and RF, and eight critical variables associated with HRS were identified. The final prediction model showed excellent discrimination for HRS.

6.5. Hepatocellular carcinoma

A number of AI models for the prediction of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have been developed in patients with various stages of liver fibrosis. Nevertheless, relatively few models have been developed to predict the risk of HCC in cirrhotic patients. Between 2002 and 2011, a cohort of 34 932 patients with cirrhosis was enrolled from a national liver transplantation waiting list database in the United States. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was applied to develop and validate a risk prediction model for HCC. Six independent risk factors, including age, diabetes, race, etiology, sex, and severity of liver dysfunction, were used to develop the ADRESS-HCC risk model. This model was considered useful in predicting the 1-year risk of HCC among cirrhotic patients.34 Sharma et al.35 developed the Toronto HCC Risk Index using multivariable Cox regression. The 10-year cumulative incidence rates of HCC in cirrhotic patients were 3%, 10%, and 32% in the low-risk (<120 points), medium-risk (120-240 points), and high-risk (>240 points) groups, respectively. The values remained consistent during internal and external validation.

7. ASSESSMENT OF PROGNOSIS

Considering the poor prognosis and shortage of liver donors, an accurate prediction of the prognosis in patients with liver cirrhosis is essential to establish an efficient treatment strategy, decide the appropriate timing of referral for liver transplantation, and accurately allocate livers to the most critical patients. The MELD score36 was originally proposed to assess the risk of death in patients with end-stage liver disease undergoing insertion of a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic stent shunt, and it is routinely applied to estimate the severity of liver damage and select the most critically ill patients for transplantation. However, it does not capture the full complexity of individual patient characteristics. Traditionally, outcome prediction scores have been developed using conventional statistical methods, mostly derived from multivariate regression analysis. However, biological processes are inherently complex and nonlinear, and involve many interacting variables that are poorly captured by linear models. Under such conditions, AI-based models may offer advantages over traditional statistical approaches by better accommodating this complexity.3 Furthermore, the progression of liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension-related complications is diverse and can be unpredictable, so precise outcome prediction and personalized decision-making are crucial for improving the prognosis of this disease entity. In this regard, AI is considered promising due to its ability to manage, link, interpret, and reason with large amounts of data and image features.

Cucchetti et al.37 analyzed data from 251 cirrhotic patients listed for liver transplantation. They trained an ANN model to predict 3-month survival on 188 of the patients, tested it on the remaining 63 patients (internal validation group), and finally on 137 patients listed for liver transplantation at King’s College Hospital, London, United Kingdom (external cohort). They found that the ANN model performed significantly better than the MELD score in both the internal and external validation groups, and concluded that the ANN model was able to assess the risk of mortality in cirrhotic patients more accurately than the MELD score and could thus better prioritize liver transplant candidates, consequently reducing mortality on the waiting list.37 To predict short-term mortality in cirrhotic patients with acute decompensation, Salkić et al.38 developed and validated two ANN-based models for predicting 28-day and 90-day mortality in patients admitted due to acute decompensated liver cirrhosis. The AUROC of the ANN model to predict 28-day mortality was 0.811, higher than the values for both the MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 scores. Moreover, the AUROCs of the ANN model, MELD-Na, and MELD 3.0 scores to predict 90-day mortality were 0.839, 0.682, and 0.703, respectively, showing the superiority of the ANN model in predicting mortality. A recent study applied ML in a prospective global cohort of cirrhotic patients to evaluate whether data obtained on the day of admission could predict inpatient mortality.39 RF was found to be the ML method with the highest AUROC, and it remained better than LR regardless of country income level.

Regarding the application of AI in metabolomics, a recent study enrolled 64 005 UK Biobank participants with metabolomic profiles. An ML framework was applied to analyze metabolomic profiles derived from 168 circulating metabolites, and the resulting nomogram was compared against conventional risk scores. The metabolomic state model accurately predicted the 10-year risk of liver cirrhosis complications in the training cohort (time-dependent AUROC, 0.84) and was superior to the FIB-4 index. Furthermore, the nomogram integrating metabolomic state, AST, PLT, waist/hip ratio, and smoking status yielded a time-dependent AUROC of 0.930 at 3 years, 0.889 at 5 years, and 0.861 at 10 years in the validation cohort. The study concluded that this metabolomic state–integrated nomogram could enable personalized risk stratification of liver cirrhosis-related complications over time.40

8. EVALUATION OF THE TREATMENT RESPONSE

The combination of high-throughput proteomics and ML has substantially contributed to the efficiency and accuracy of protein extraction and analysis.41 Based on this progress, Richards et al.42 reported that a noninvasive candidate protein signature composed of 12 protein markers could predict HVPG reduction in patients with hepatitis C-related liver cirrhosis after a sustained virologic response. This model yielded an AUROC of 0.86, sensitivity of 83%, specificity of 83%, and accuracy of 83%. The authors concluded that protein marker profiling combined with ML algorithms could provide a noninvasive alternative for potentially risky HVPG measurements. In another study, Hsieh et al.43 combined multiple ML methods to train a prediction model for cirrhosis complications using 14 features. They found that SVM and RF methods could predict jaundice and multiple complications in patients receiving the antiviral agents lamivudine and entecavir, respectively.

Even though AI holds great promise for the identification, treatment, and outcome prediction of liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension, a number of issues must be addressed before broad implementation is possible. First, limitations in AI research should be considered, and attention should be paid when applying the findings. For example, retrospective studies may involve selection bias. In addition, the number of clinical indicators analyzed may not be exhaustive, and additional relevant variables may exist but not be identified, potentially impacting the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the prediction model. Differences in basic patient characteristics between the training and validation cohorts, especially from different centers, may further lead to variability in the data and influence the accuracy of ML and the performance of the model. An insufficient number of data points may also cause overfitting of the training set and affect the representativeness of the study. In addition, if the clinical and laboratory data are only recorded once, the absence of follow-up data may affect the predictive ability of the model over time. The enrolled cohorts may also not be fully representative of all patients. In different contexts, gastroenterologists may use different sets of data, thereby raising concerns over the generalizability of prediction models.

The major limitations and challenges currently still lie in clinical application. This involves integration into clinical workflows, regulatory and ethical concerns regarding patient data and decision-making, model interpretability, and physician trust.

9. PERSPECTIVES

Although much progress has been made, further research on personalized, time-dependent risk stratification and prognostication, along with strategies for continuously monitored care, is required. AI models trained on longitudinal patient data may provide early warnings days or weeks before clinical deterioration becomes apparent. This could enable the development of preventive interventions, personalized follow-up schedules, and a reduction in hospital admissions. Furthermore, as patients with cirrhosis are highly heterogeneous, future AI platforms may help determine the possibility that a patient will benefit from treatment such as beta-blockers, endoscopic therapy, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, and/or novel antifibrotic drugs. Hopefully, rather than relying on the average data of certain populations, AI will enable individualized treatment recommendations based on a patient’s unique biological and clinical profile. Furthermore, in this era of multiomics, the ability of AI to manage complicated and multidimensional data may also enable it to integrate genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and microbiome information with clinical data. This could lead to the discovery of new therapeutic targets, a better understanding of cirrhosis progression, and personalized prognostic models.

Over the next decades, the most likely trends are from isolated prediction models toward integrated support systems that continuously analyze imaging, laboratory, physiologic, and molecular data. The most transformative advances are expected to come from combining multimodal data into unified predictive platforms capable of managing cirrhosis and portal hypertension as dynamic, continuously evolving diseases.
