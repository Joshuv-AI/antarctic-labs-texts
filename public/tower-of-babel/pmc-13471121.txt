Lateral Meniscus Oblique Radial Tear All‐Inside Repair
Authors: Patrick M. Ryan, Steve Goodin, Garrett R. Jackson, Steven F. DeFroda, Clayton W. Nuelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLateral meniscus oblique radial tears, particularly high‐grade types 3 and 4, are common in acute anterior cruciate ligament injuries and contribute to significant knee instability and meniscal extrusion if left unrepaired. Surgical repair is necessary to restore normal knee kinematics and meniscal function. This article describes a reproducible all‐inside surgical technique for repairing a type 3 lateral meniscus oblique radial tear.

This presentation highlights the procedures used to conduct a lateral meniscus oblique radial tear all‐inside repair. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70224.

Lateral meniscus oblique radial tears (LMORTs) are a common posterior horn injury often seen with acute anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears. 1 , 2 These tears have been reported in up to 12% of ACL injuries and are the most common type of associated lateral meniscus pathology. 2 , 3 Four types of LMORTs have been described, and of these, types 3 and 4 LMORTs have been found to increase anterior tibial translation, rotatory instability, and meniscal extrusion when compared with the intact state. 3 , 4 , 5 Importantly, these alterations in knee kinematics persist even after ACL reconstruction if the LMORT is not repaired. 5 , 6 , 7 In contrast, meniscal repair has been shown to restore normal laxity and reduce extrusion, whereas partial meniscectomy fails to correct instability and may further compromise joint loading.

Furthermore, clinical studies have shown that surgical fixation of type 3 and 4 LMORTs can provide significant improvements in patient‐reported outcomes scores. 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 Additionally, in a recent study of 97 patients, Jeon et al. 2 found that 80% of patients showed complete healing on second‐look arthroscopy at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up. These findings reinforce that repair of high‐grade LMORTs is necessary to improve knee stability, protect the ACL reconstruction, and preserve meniscal function.

In this technique, we describe an all‐inside repair strategy for an LMORT lesion using 2 side‐to‐side suture tapes. This construct provides a straightforward, reproducible method to restore hoop tension across the meniscus and secure the tear, while minimizing the risk of propagation and secondary degeneration.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Preparation and Positioning

The patient is placed in the supine position on the operating table. After induction of general anesthesia, a thorough examination under anesthesia is conducted to evaluate knee stability and range of motion. A nonsterile tourniquet is placed on the operative thigh but not inflated. The patient's extremity is then prepared and draped using a knee arthroscopy setup per surgeon's preference.

LMORT Repair

A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is performed, starting with the establishment of the anterolateral portal. A 30° arthroscope is used for the entirety of the procedure. Then, the anteromedial portal is established with a spinal needle using an outside‐in technique. A probe is introduced for diagnostic arthroscopy. Intra‐articular findings, including other meniscal tears and chondral injuries, are addressed as necessary. In the lateral compartment, an LMORT type 3 tear is then identified (Figure 1). It involves approximately 50% of the root (Figure 2). Given this finding, the decision is then made to proceed with all‐inside repair of the tear to stabilize the meniscal root and restore the hoop tension across the meniscus. While still viewing from the lateral portal, a 3.2 mm Knee Scorpion Suture Passer (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is introduced from the anteromedial portal and brought across the knee into the lateral compartment. The suture passer is then everted such that the suture will be passed from superior to inferior. The first limb of the suture tape is passed through the peripheral aspect of the intact posterior meniscus and capsule with care taken to rotate the suture passer to provide ample room and avoid damaging the articular cartilage of the plateau (Figure 3). The other limb of the suture tape is then passed through the anterior flap of the meniscus tear. This results in an inverted suture crossing the tear. A modified Tuckahoe arthroscopic sliding knot is then tied and slid down to reduce the tear. While maintaining tension on the post suture limb, 3 alternating half hitches are then placed to provide stability of the knot. The suture is then cut flush with minimal tails. This same procedure is then repeated in the more anterior aspect of the tear, first passing in the posterior aspect of the meniscus in inverted fashion followed by the other limb across the anterior flap in a similar inverted fashion (Figure 4). This is then tied with a modified Tuckahoe arthroscopic sliding knot and 3 subsequent half hitches with the knot on the inferior aspect of the meniscus. The suture is again cut flush. This provides 2 suture tapes across the tear, achieving excellent stability with the knots placed on the inferior aspect of the meniscus in a low‐profile manner (Figure 5). DISCUSSION

LMORTs have been reported in 6% to 15% of acute ACL injuries and are one of the most prevalent lateral meniscus injuries in this setting. 1 , 2 , 3 A classification of 4 types of LMORTs by Krych et al. has only recently been described in the literature, whereas Jeon et al. further subclassified type 4 tears into 4a and 4b, where in 4b, the tear extends into the meniscocapsular junction. 2 , 3 LMORT 3 and 4 were found to have increased anterior laxity with anterior drawer and pivot shift, meniscal extrusion, and higher axial load transmitted directly to the tibial surface in cadaveric studies. 5 , 10 Increased anterior laxity is present even after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and is greater for type 4 than type 3 LMORTs. 5

There are multiple treatment options to consider for these tears. 9 Historically, LMORTs have been treated conservatively or have been solely debrided; more recently, this has become less favorable. 7 , 11 Partial meniscectomy has been found to result in greater laxity and increased extrusion, which may place greater strain and contribute to ACL graft failure, whereas repair has been shown to restore normal baseline stability. 5 , 12 For these reasons, partial meniscectomy should likely only be considered for some type 1 tears at risk of propagating, while repair of LMORT type 2 to 4 is recommended. 7 Type 2 tears can be considered root variant tears, and transtibial repair has been recommended. 7 , 9 Repair with all‐inside spanning suture and even an inside‐out approach can be used to treat LMORT types 3 and 4. The all‐inside suture spanning technique described here may be best for addressing LMORT type 3 and type 4 tears but could also be considered for type 2 tears, or even a single spanning suture for larger type 1 tears, depending on surgeon preference.

This technique is easy to reproduce and has multiple advantages. Compared with inside‐out techniques, all‐inside repairs have been shown to have faster operative times and postoperative recovery, while avoiding risks such as extra‐articular knots, nerve injury or irritation, and lateral geniculate artery injury. 13 , 14 , 15 Additionally, compared with inside‐out as well as capsular‐based all‐inside repairs, this repair avoids incorporating the capsule, which avoids risk to surrounding soft tissue and neurovascular structures, as well as avoiding the risk of over constraining the lateral meniscus. 16 It also avoids the use of all‐inside devices that may cause irritation, synovitis, or dislodge into the joint. 17 The self‐capturing suture passer also improves the passing angle for suture through the meniscus and decreases the risk of skiving compared with other instruments such as suture hooks and also decreases additional steps such as the need for using shuttling sutures. The ability to place the knot on the undersurface of the meniscus avoids knot prominence and possible subsequent chondral abrasion; this is not proven in literature and has only been reported in case reports. 18 The use of suture tape also has increased surface area with increased resistance to tissue cut through. 9

Despite the notable advantages, there are some disadvantages to this technique. The suture passer jaw may be bulky, which may carry a risk of iatrogenic cartilage injury when attempting to pass the suture in the tight posterior aspect of the lateral compartment. The posterior root may be especially challenging to access in an ACL‐intact knee. Additionally, care must be taken when piercing the meniscus with the suture passer, as the needle may pose an injury risk to the articular cartilage (Tables 1 and 2). Compared with knotless constructs, this technique relies on the ability to tie knots, which is more technically demanding and poses a risk of inadequate tension if the knot stack loses tension when applied.
