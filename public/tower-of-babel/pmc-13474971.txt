Repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST imaging at 5 T: phantom validation and clinical evaluation in patients with brain tumors
Authors: Tong Chen, Ling Wang, Tianjun Wang, Chao Zheng, Zhiwei Qin, Wu Cai, Guangzheng Li, Chunhong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the repeatability and reproducibility of amide proton transfer (APT) and glutamate chemical exchange saturation transfer (GluCEST) imaging at 5 T and to investigate their clinical value in patients with brain tumors.MethodsPhantom experiments under varying pH conditions and bovine serum albumin (BSA) concentrations were performed to assess the within-session repeatability, between-session and between-day reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), mean absolute difference, coefficient of repeatability (CoR) and coefficient of variation (CV). In vivo APT and GluCEST imaging was performed in 96 patients with brain tumors. Intra- and inter-observer agreements of APTmean and GluCESTmean measurements were evaluated using ICCs and Bland–Altman analysis across tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain tissue regions. Quantitative tumor-region APT and GluCEST metrics (APT/GluCESTmean, APT/GluCEST difference, and APT/GluCEST change ratio) were compared among major tumor subtypes using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance for differentiating high-grade gliomas from low-grade gliomas.ResultsIn phantom studies, both APT and GluCEST demonstrated robust repeatability and reproducibility with high ICCs (> 0.96) and low mean absolute difference, CoR and CV. Both APT and GluCEST-weighted signals showed nonlinear pH-dependent changes and increased with higher BSA concentrations. In patients, intra- and inter-observer agreements were excellent for APTmean and GluCESTmean in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain regions (ICCs > 0.93). Tumor and peritumoral edema regions showed higher APT and GluCEST values than normal brain tissue (p < 0.05). After multiple-comparison correction, high-grade gliomas showed significantly higher APTmean, APT difference, GluCESTmean, GluCEST difference and GluCEST change ratio than low-grade gliomas. GluCEST-derived metrics showed favorable performance for glioma grading, with GluCEST change ratio achieving the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.846, followed by GluCEST difference (AUC = 0.838) and GluCESTmean (AUC = 0.836).ConclusionAPT and GluCEST imaging at 5 T showed excellent repeatability and reproducibility in phantom experiments and robust measurement agreement in patients with brain tumors. APT and GluCEST measurements demonstrated subtype-related differences, and GluCEST-derived metrics showed favorable performance for differentiating high-grade from low-grade gliomas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02486-x.

Background

Chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a powerful, non-invasive technique for probing molecular information in vivo. By exploiting the proton exchange between solute molecules and bulk water, CEST enables the indirect detection and quantification of specific metabolites and proteins [1]. Compared to conventional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), CEST MRI offers significantly higher spatial resolution and sensitivity by detecting exchangeable protons, along with the ability to switch contrast on and off through selective saturation, thereby better addressing the clinical needs for detailed molecular imaging [2]. CEST contrast agents fall into two main categories: endogenous agents, which are molecules naturally occurring within cells, and exogenous agents, which are synthetically introduced and can possess highly complex chemical structures [3]. Both amide proton transfer (APT) imaging and glutamate CEST (GluCEST) MRI are endogenous CEST-MRI techniques: APT detects mobile proteins and peptides by saturating their amide protons at + 3.5 ppm (offset from water at 0 ppm, corresponding to approximately 8.25 ppm absolute), whereas GluCEST specifically images glutamate by targeting its amine protons at + 3.0 ppm (approximately 7.75 ppm absolute) [4, 5].

Emerging research supports multiple clinical applications for APT imaging in neuro-oncology [6–8]. Tumors characterized by uncontrolled proliferation and elevated mobile protein content consistently show hyperintense APT signals relative to contralateral brain tissue. These increased APT signals correlate positively with high cellularity, as confirmed by histological validation [9]. For instance, APT signal intensity correlates positively with glioma grade and enhances diagnostic accuracy, even in non-enhancing tumors [10, 11]. Furthermore, the technique holds promise in differentiating tumor recurrence from treatment-related changes, positioning it as a potential imaging biomarker for therapeutic response assessment [12]. Glutamate, a key neurotransmitter regulating cognition, memory, and learning, plays a critical role in brain function. Dysregulated glutamate metabolism is closely linked to the pathogenesis and progression of various neurological disorders and brain tumors [13, 14]. In gliomas, elevated glutamate levels promote tumor proliferation and invasion, adversely affecting patient prognosis [15]. Given glutamate’s dual role in tumor metabolism—serving as both an energy source and as a biosynthetic precursor in rapidly proliferating cells—GluCEST MRI presents a promising tool for cancer imaging [5, 16].

The detection of CEST effects depends on multiple factors, including the chemical exchange rate, resonance frequency offset relative to water, pH, temperature, and magnetic field strength [17]. Although high-field MRI (5 T) improves spectral resolution for metabolite differentiation, it also introduces challenges such as B₀ and B₁ field inhomogeneities and motion artifacts, which must be corrected to ensure data reliability [18]. For clinical translation—particularly in cancer imaging with techniques such as GluCEST—high reproducibility between healthy and tumor tissues is essential. Quantitative assessment of this reproducibility, along with measurements consistency across diagnosticians, is critical for establishing robust, non-invasive imaging biomarkers. To adhere to best practices for establishing quantitative imaging biomarkers, we followed recommendations from the Quantitative Imaging Biomarkers Alliance (QIBA) [19], which emphasize rigorous assessment of repeatability, reproducibility, and standardization across platforms and readers. Addressing these technical and methodological challenges is key to realizing the full clinical potential of CEST MRI.

Therefore, this study evaluated the repeatability and reproducibility of 5 T CEST MRI using an in vitro model to quantify simulated protein and pH levels, alongside a parallel clinical investigation in patients with brain tumors to evaluate the clinical value.

Methods

This prospective study was approved by the institutional review board (No. 2025193) in our hospital.

Phantom studies

Two CEST phantoms were prepared for quantitative measurements: protein phantoms with varying bovine serum albumin (BSA) concentrations and pH values. To assess concentration-dependent CEST signal, BSA was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 6.8 at six concentrations (6% to 16% in 2% increments), covering the range of physiological protein tissue concentrations in brain tissue. To evaluate pH-dependent CEST signal, BSA was dissolved in PBS at a fixed concentration of 10% with eight pH values (5.2 to 8.0 in 0.4 increments), adjusted using HCl and NaOH. Each BSA solution was placed into 15 mL plastic tubes. All tubes were secured in a tube rack and placed in a foam box filled with rice to minimize air-related artifacts during signal acquisition. Phantoms were heated to 37 °C ± 1 °C prior to scanning.

Patients studies

This prospective study enrolled patients with suspected brain tumors who underwent 5 T MRI at our hospital between January 2025 to December 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) suspicion of brain tumors based on preoperative MR imaging; (3) no adjuvant treatment or prior surgery; and (4) surgical resection after undergoing 5 T MRI and have a definitive histopathological diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included: (1) contraindications to MRI or inability to complete the MRI protocol; (2) presence of significant image artifacts or signal loss compromising diagnostic quality; (3) refusal of surgical resection/biopsy or absence of histopathological diagnosis.

Protocol of scan-rescan test

To assess the repeatability and reproducibility, phantom experiments followed a scan–rescan design (Fig. 1). Each phantom underwent three imaging sessions: two sessions with repositioning in between (between‑session reproducibility), and a third session conducted 7–14 days later (between‑day reproducibility). Each session comprised two consecutive APT and GluCEST acquisitions (within‑session repeatability). Brain tumor patients underwent a single scan session, which included conventional structural sequences: T1‑weighted imaging (T1WI), T2‑weighted imaging (T2WI), diffuse‑weighted imaging (DWI), fluid‑attenuated inversion recovery sequences (FLAIR) and contrast‑enhanced T1‑weighted imaging (CE-T1WI). Both APT and GluCEST imaging were performed prior to contrast agent administration.

APT and GluCEST image acquisition and post-processing

All MRI examinations were performed on a 5 T MRI scanner (uMR Jupiter, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) using a 48-channel head-neck coil. Detailed acquisition parameters for APT, GluCEST, and conventional sequences are provided in Table 1. Both APT and GluCEST sequences were performed using a fat-suppressed single-shot fast spin echo (SSH-FSE) sequence with a continuous-wave rectangular radiofrequency (RF) saturation pulse. For APT imaging, a root‑mean‑square B₁ amplitude (B1rms) was 2 µT with a saturation duration of 2000 ms; For GluCEST, the B1rms was 3 µT with a saturation duration of 1000 ms. APT images were acquired at frequency offsets of ± 3.9, ± 3.5, and ± 3.1 ppm, with the ± 3.5 ppm offset acquired twice. GluCEST images were acquired at ± 3.0, ± 3.4, and ± 2.6 ppm, with the ± 3.0 ppm offset acquired twice to improve signal‑to‑noise ratio. In patients with brain tumors, CEST image was targeted to the slice showing the maximum tumor cross-sectional area.

Image analysis

The imaging data were processed using uOmnispace.MR R002 (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China), which automatically generated APT and GluCEST maps for subsequent analysis. For phantom scanning, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined fixed-size circles positioned at the center of each tube’s cross-section. Each ROI was manually placed with a minimum area of 35 mm². For brain tumor patients, two neuroradiologists independently delineated ROIs on solid tumor tissue, peritumoral edema, and contralateral normal‑appearing brain tissue (CNBT) using T2WI, T2-FLAIR and CE-T1WI images as anatomical references. Solid tumor ROI was defined as the viable solid component of the lesion. For enhancing tumors, ROIs were placed on the contrast-enhancing solid portion on CE-T1WI. For non-enhancing tumors, ROIs were placed on the non-enhancing solid component showing mild hyperintensity on T2WI/T2-FLAIR, with tumor-related features such as tissue expansion, mass effect, and growth beyond normal anatomical boundaries [23, 24]. Cystic, necrotic, hemorrhagic, and large vascular components were excluded. Peritumoral edema was defined as T2WI/T2-FLAIR hyperintensity in white matter. Contralateral normal-appearing brain tissue ROIs were selected according to the location of the primary lesion, including supratentorial white matter, deep gray matter structures, the pons, and the cerebellar hemisphere. Each ROI was manually placed with a minimum area of 50 mm². To standardize the measured APT and GluCEST values, APT/GluCEST difference and APT/GluCEST change ratio were calculated. The APT/GluCEST difference was calculated as the tumor value minus the corresponding normal brain tissue value (tumor ROI − CNBT), and the change ratio was calculated as the difference divided by the corresponding normal brain tissue value(difference/CNBT). These ROIs were then co‑registered and mapped onto the corresponding CEST maps to extract APT and GluCEST values. To assess intra‑observer agreement, one neuroradiologist repeated the ROI delineation for all three tissue compartments after a one‑month interval.

Statistics

Repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements in phantom studies, as well as intra- and inter-observer agreement in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain regions, were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way random-effects model and absolute agreement. Absolute repeatability and reproducibility metrics were calculated for mean and each pH level and BSA concentration. The measurement difference was defined as di=X1i−X2i, where X1i and X2i were the two paired measurements. The mean difference was calculated as the average of di, and the mean absolute difference was calculated as the average of /di/. The standard deviation (SD) of repeated measurements was calculated from the paired measurements within each condition, and the mean SD was obtained by averaging these values across conditions. The coefficient of repeatability (CoR) was calculated as 1.96×SDdifference1.96 when derived from the paired differences. The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated as CV (%) = SDrepeated/ Meanrepeated× 100. Lower values of mean absolute difference, CoR, and CV indicate better repeatability and reproducibility. Bland-Altman analysis was performed to assess measurement bias and limits of agreement. ICCs are reported with 95% confidence intervals and interpreted as follows: 0.00–0.39, poor; 0.40–0.59, fair; 0.60–0.74, good; and 0.75–1.00, excellent.

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare imaging metrics among the five most common brain tumor types, including APTmean and GluCESTmean values, as well as the the APT/GluCEST difference and APT/GluCEST change ratio. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were subsequently performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. For each imaging metric, 10 pairwise comparisons were performed among the five tumor subtypes. By multiplying the uncorrected P values by 10, the Bonferroni corrected P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Repeatability and reproducibility in phantom study

Across both pH and BSA phantom experiments, APT and GluCEST-weighted signal measurements demonstrated excellent repeatability and reproducibility, with ICC values exceeding 0.96 under all conditions (Table 2). Additional absolute repeatability/reproducibility analyses were performed at pH and BSA level, as shown in Table 3. Across the pH phantom, both APT and GluCEST weighted signal demonstrated low absolute variability, with mean CVs of 1.612%–2.145% for APT and 2.289%–2.377% for GluCEST weighted signal. In the BSA phantom, APT measurements remained stable, with mean CVs of 2.014%–2.335%. GluCEST weighted signal showed slightly greater variability, particularly for within-session repeatability, but the mean CVs remained below 5% across all comparisons. Detailed level-specific repeatability metrics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. These results indicate excellent measurement stability across different experimental conditions and time intervals.

Both APT and GluCEST-weighted signals showed clear pH-dependent changes in the phantom experiment (Fig. 2A). With decreasing pH from near-physiological to mildly acidic conditions, the signal intensities of both APT and GluCEST-weighted signal increased. In contrast, at higher pH levels, signal intensities were relatively lower. Across the tested pH range, GluCEST-weighted signal exhibited larger variations than APT, indicating greater sensitivity to changes in pH. In addition, increasing BSA concentration led to a progressive enhancement of both APT and GluCEST-weighted signals, demonstrating a concentration-dependent effect (Fig. 2B).

Clinical characteristics of brain tumor patients

96 patients with brain tumors underwent APT and GluCEST scanning. The mean age of the patients was 55 ± 16 years, including 40 men (41.7%) and 56 women (58.3%). Among the 96 patients, 68 (70.8%) had malignant tumors and 28 (29.2%) had benign tumors. Malignant tumors mainly included high-grade gliomas (30/96, 31.3%), low-grade gliomas (13/96, 13.5%), lymphomas (13/96, 13.5%), metastatic tumors (10/96, 10.4%), and medulloblastomas (2/96, 2.1%). Benign tumors were predominantly meningiomas (20/96, 20.8%).

Consistency in brain tumor patients

The intra- and inter-observer agreement of APT and GluCEST measurements in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal tissue regions are summarized in Table 4. For intra-observer agreement, ICCs ranged from 0.934 to 0.955 for APTmean and from 0.966 to 0.973 for GluCESTmean. For inter-observer agreement, ICCs ranged from 0.932 to 0.961 for APTmean and from 0.952 to 0.966 for GluCESTmean. Bland-Altman plots demonstrated good agreement among the three repeated measurements of both APT and Glu values in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain tissue regions (Fig. 3).

APT and GluCEST characteristics of normal brain regions

The mean of APT and GluCEST measurements across different anatomical locations in normal brain are summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1. APTmean values in normal brain tissue showed a narrow distribution and remained close to zero, with most values ranging between approximately − 0.5% and 1.0%. GluCESTmean values were mainly distributed within the range of 5.2% to 7.0% across different measurement locations. Mild regional variations were observed across different anatomical locations. The cerebellum and brainstem demonstrated relatively higher GluCEST values compared with supratentorial regions, whereas the frontal and parietal lobes showed lower GluCEST levels. APTmean values remained largely consistent across brain regions, with slightly elevated values observed in the cerebellum.

APT and GluCEST characteristics of brain tumors and peritumoral edema

Comparison of APT and GluCEST values among different pathological subtypes in tumor and peritumoral edema regions were shown in Table 5. The APT and GluCEST values measured in all tumor regions and peritumoral edematous regions were significantly higher than those in normal brain tissue(p < 0.05). Significant differences in APT and GluCEST values among the five most common tumor subtypes were observed (APT: H = 13.64, P = 0.009; Glu: H = 14.35, P = 0.006). The Violin plots demonstrated distinct distributions of APT and GluCEST values across the five most common tumor subtypes in the tumor region (Fig. 4). In uncorrected pairwise comparisons, high-grade gliomas showed significantly higher APT values than low-grade gliomas (P = 0.001) and meningiomas (P = 0.006). For GluCEST, significant differences were observed between high-grade and low-grade gliomas (P < 0.001), high-grade gliomas and meningiomas (P = 0.031), and between lymphoma and low-grade gliomas (P = 0.027). After Bonferroni correction, significant differences were observed only between high-grade and low-grade gliomas for both APT (corrected P = 0.011) and GluCEST (corrected P = 0.003), while no other pairwise comparisons were statistically significant. For peritumoral edema regions, no statistically significant differences in APT or GluCEST values were observed among different tumor subtypes after multiple-comparison correction (Kruskal-Wallis test, all P > 0.05). However, this analysis was limited by markedly unbalanced sample sizes across subtypes, therefore, the nonsignificant results may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than a true absence of biological differences.

ROC analysis for differentiating high-grade from low-grade gliomas

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of APT- and GluCEST-derived metrics for differentiating high-grade from low-grade gliomas. Overall, GluCEST-derived metrics showed higher diagnostic performance than APT-derived metrics (Table 6; Fig. 7). GluCEST change ratio achieved the highest AUC of 0.846 (95% CI: 0.697–0.996), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.708, sensitivity of 86.7%, and specificity of 76.9%. GluCEST difference and GluCESTmean also demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.838 and 0.836, respectively. Among APT-derived metrics, APTmean and APT difference showed moderate diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.779 and 0.764, respectively, whereas APT change ratio showed limited discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.559.

Discussion

The APT and GluCEST techniques have demonstrated increasing potential as non-invasive molecular MRI approaches for probing endogenous metabolite distributions within the brain. High-field MRI offers intrinsic advantages for CEST imaging by improving spectral resolution and signal sensitivity. The increased chemical shift dispersion facilitates more reliable separation of CEST signals, while the enhanced signal-to-noise ratio improves detectability. Furthermore, prolonged water T1 at higher field strength increases saturation transfer efficiency, leading to stronger CEST contrast without altering exchange kinetics [25]. With the recent emergence of 5 T MRI systems, it is essential to specifically evaluate the repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements at this field strength before reliable clinical application. In this study, we performed a systematic repeatability and reproducibility evaluation of APT and GluCEST measurements using a rigorously designed phantom model. Complementarily, we extended this validation framework to a clinical setting by assessing the inter- and intra-observer measurement agreement of APT and GluCEST signals in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain regions in a cohort of brain tumor patients in 5 T MRI systems.

Phantom experiments demonstrated high repeatability and reproducibility for both APT and GluCEST-weighted signal, with the low mean absolute difference, CoR and CV, indicating minimal measurement variability. Previous studies have demonstrated that 3 T APT imaging exhibits good reproducibility in both patients with brain tumors and healthy volunteers [26, 27]. In addition, Zhou et al. [28]. reported high consistency of 5 T GluCEST imaging in healthy subjects. Despite these encouraging findings from in vivo studies, repeatability and reproducibility validation using well-controlled in vitro phantom models remains scarce. In particular, few studies have systematically assessed the repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements while simultaneously investigating their signal responses to changes in pH and protein concentration, two key factors known to influence chemical exchange saturation transfer effects [29]. The phantom study confirmed that both APT and GluCEST signals were associated with pH and protein concentration, consistent with prior reports [30]. Higher BSA concentrations produced stronger APT effects, consistent with increased availability of exchangeable protons contributing to saturation transfer [31]. Although GluCEST signals also increased with rising BSA concentration, the observed signal changes at 3.0 ppm cannot be interpreted as glutamate‑specific because BSA does not contain physiologically relevant glutamate. Nevertheless, Zhou et al. [32] demonstrated a linear correlation between GluCEST signal and glutamate concentration (R² = 0.97) and confirmed that glutamate is the predominant contributor to the 3.0 ppm signal. Both APT and GluCEST signals also exhibited nonlinear pH-dependent behavior, reflecting the sensitivity of proton exchange kinetics to the tissue microenvironment [30]. At 5 T field strength, the signal intensities increased from acidic conditions toward the mildly acidic range and reached relatively higher values around pH 6.4. With further increases in pH, both signals gradually decreased, particularly at pH 7.6–8.0. GluCEST values were consistently higher than APT values and exhibited larger signal variations across the tested pH range, suggesting greater pH sensitivity. Mildly acidic conditions may adjust the amide and amine proton exchange rates to a range that is more favorable for saturation transfer under the applied RF saturation scheme, thereby increasing detectable CEST contrast. This interpretation is supported by Cai et al. [20], who also reported a nonlinear pH dependence of GluCEST, with the signal increasing from pH 3 to pH 6 and then decreasing from pH 6 to pH 8 under their experimental saturation parameters. Ying et al. [33] also reported a significant negative correlation between GluCEST signal intensity and pH (ρ ≥ 0.893), further indicating that GluCEST signals systematically increase in more acidic environments.

In brain tumor patients, excellent inter- and intra-observer agreements were observed for APT and GluCEST measurements in tumor, peritumoral edema, and normal brain regions. Comparative analysis across different pathological subtypes revealed that, after multiple-comparison Bonferroni correction, statistically significant differences were only detected between high-grade and low-grade gliomas in tumor regions. This finding is consistent with previous studies, indicating that APT and GluCEST exhibit good discriminative ability for differentiating gliomas with different pathological grades [11, 28]. APT and GluCEST did not exhibit significant performance in distinguishing gliomas from other malignant tumors, possibly because CEST-derived metrics are influenced by common biological factors such as cellularity, protein concentration, and tissue acidity, which are shared across multiple malignant brain tumors. Zhou et al. [28] reported mean GluCEST values of 0.89 ± 0.56 for low-grade gliomas, 6.41 ± 1.23 for high-grade gliomas, and 3.48 ± 0.55 for cerebellopontine angle meningiomas, all of which were lower than the values observed in our study. This discrepancy may be related to differences in sample size and the anatomical distribution of meningiomas between the two studies. Although no statistically significant differences were detected among peritumoral edema across tumor subtypes, possibly owing to limited sample size, both APT and GluCEST values in edema regions were consistently elevated compared with normal brain tissue. This observation indicates that APT and GluCEST are capable of detecting tumor-associated microenvironmental changes in peritumoral tissues, rather than merely characterizing tumor cores. Consistent with our findings, Sugawara et al. [34] demonstrated that APT values were significantly higher in tumor cores than in peritumoral edema, and higher in peritumoral edema than in ipsilateral and contralateral normal white matter. Besides, they also found significantly higher APT signals in glioblastomas versus brain metastases, both in the tumor core and peritumoral edema. However, a significant difference in the relative APT increase was observed only in the tumor core, not in the edema regions. In contrast to our study, which evaluated the overall and pairwise discriminative capacity of APT/GluCEST differences and GluCEST change ratio across five tumor types, we found that APT and GluCEST difference and GluCEST change ratio maintained statistically significant differences between high-grade and low-grade gliomas after Bonferroni correction. The ROC analysis further demonstrated the value of GluCEST-derived metrics for glioma grading. GluCEST change ratio showed the highest diagnostic performance for differentiating high-grade from low-grade gliomas, with an AUC of 0.846, followed by GluCEST difference and GluCESTmean, with AUCs of 0.838 and 0.836, respectively. In comparison, APT-derived metrics showed relatively lower performance, with AUCs of 0.779 for APTmean, 0.764 for APT difference, and 0.559 for APT change ratio. These results suggest that GluCEST-derived metrics may better capture the biological differences between high- and low-grade gliomas and may serve as useful quantitative biomarkers for glioma grading.

Previous studies have primarily explored APT imaging at 3 T and GluCEST imaging at ultra-high field strengths, particularly 7 T, limiting the clinical translation of combined CEST approaches [35]. In this study, we demonstrated the in vitro repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements under controlled pH and protein concentration conditions at 5 T, and further confirmed their feasibility in patients with brain tumors. These findings indicate that 5 T represents a promising intermediate field strength for simultaneous APT and GluCEST imaging, bridging the gap between conventional clinical scanners and ultra-high-field systems. Collectively, our results support the potential clinical applicability of multiparametric CEST imaging at 5 T for tumor characterization. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively limited, particularly for several tumor subtypes and repeated-measurement analyses. According to quantitative imaging recommendations such as QIBA, larger cohorts and multiple repeated acquisitions may be required for rigorous precision qualification and formal conformance testing. Second, although the phantom experiments demonstrated robust measurement stability, they cannot fully reflect the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment. In addition, because the BSA did not contain glutamate, the GluCEST-weighted data only support the technical reproducibility and environmental sensitivity of the CEST pipeline. Therefore, further studies using glutamate-containing phantoms are needed to better characterize the biochemical specificity of GluCEST measurements. Third, the association between glioma molecular biomarkers and CEST metrics was not analyzed, which limits the biological interpretation of our findings. Finally, the current 5 T CEST protocol was limited to single-slice acquisition with relatively thick slices, which may not fully capture the spatial heterogeneity of the entire tumor. Partial-volume effects and slice-selection bias may therefore influence quantitative measurements. Future development of volumetric three-dimensional CEST imaging at 5 T may enable more comprehensive whole-tumor characterization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the excellent repeatability and reproducibility of APT and GluCEST measurements at 5 T in phantom experiment models and robust measurement agreement in patients with brain tumors. Both APT and GluCEST techniques showed potential for quantitative brain tumor characterization, while GluCEST-derived metrics may provide complementary information for differentiating high- from low-grade gliomas, pending validation in larger, molecularly characterized cohorts. These findings support the clinical translation of GluCEST at 5 T as a valuable tool for noninvasive tumor assessment.
