Clinical management of complications after chronic spinal cord injury: a review of recent advances
Authors: Xingyu Zhao, Yaobin Wang, Yuhua Yang, Weikang Zhang, Lei Zhang, Shaocheng Liu, Zhenlei Liu, Kai Wang, Hao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe neurological disorder resulting from trauma or disease, characterized by persistent motor, sensory, and autonomic deficits below the level of the lesion. As the condition becomes long-standing, the management of multisystem complications – particularly those affecting motor, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and urinary functions – emerges as a major clinical challenge. These complications not only impair quality of life but also contribute to increased rates of rehospitalization and impose significant economic burdens on healthcare systems.DiscussionAlthough no curative therapy is currently available, treatment strategies have evolved into a comprehensive, multilayered framework incorporating rehabilitation, pharmacological interventions, neuromodulation techniques, and surgical procedures. Nevertheless, existing management approaches remain predominantly reactive and symptom-oriented, with notable limitations in long-term effectiveness, accessibility, and sustained patient engagement. This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding the pathophysiology of chronic SCI complications and critically evaluates current therapeutic limitations. A fundamental paradigm shift is urgently required – one that moves beyond traditional reactive models toward proactive, patient-centered care grounded in multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized treatment plans.ConclusionGiven the numerous challenges and the limitations of the current management strategies, further research is urgently needed for the management of chronic spinal cord injury complications. Future progress is expected to be driven by innovations in neural regeneration and precision medicine, aiming to transform clinical management from passive symptom mitigation to active functional restoration, thereby improving long-term outcomes for individuals living with chronic SCI.

Introduction

Chronic spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe neurological disorder arising from diverse etiologies, resulting in profound and persistent impairments in motor and sensory function below the level of injury, as well as a wide array of complex, interrelated long-term complications – including chronic pain, pressure injuries, and multi-system organ dysfunction. It poses a significant and escalating global public health challenge. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study, over 20 million individuals worldwide are currently living with SCI, and the disease burden has risen markedly: between 1990 and 2019, prevalence, incidence, and years lived with disability (YLDs) increased by 81.5%, 52.7%, and 65.4%, respectively[1]. These findings provide compelling evidence that chronic SCI represents not only a practical but also a progressively worsening health management challenge, demanding urgent attention from healthcare systems and policymakers.

Confronting this challenge, management paradigms vary significantly across countries. In most developed nations, patients typically receive timely and effective treatment within systematic inpatient rehabilitation and community-based continuity of care systems. In contrast, most developing countries face severe realities including insufficient medical resources, uneven regional distribution, and an underdeveloped integrated multidisciplinary management system for the chronic phase of the disease. Current management models often focus on limited restoration of motor function, failing to fundamentally address lifelong complex issues such as neurogenic bladder, neurogenic bowel, chronic pain, pressure injuries, osteoporosis, and mental health concerns. The management of these complications transcends the capacity of any single discipline, urgently necessitating a shift from traditional reactive approaches towards a new paradigm centered on patient-focused, multidisciplinary collaboration, and individualized management. Effective synergy among rehabilitation physicians, therapists, nurses, psychologists, and patients is crucial, dedicated to both disease treatment and prevention (Figure 1).

Comprehensive and multi-level management framework for chronic spinal cord injury.

This review systematically outlines current management strategies for chronic SCI and its complications, and critically evaluates the limitations and potential optimization of existing treatments across multiple domains. The aim is to provide a comprehensive synthesis of recent advances and offer clinical recommendations for advancing the management of chronic SCI towards a more systematic and proactive direction.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature pertaining to the clinical management of complications after chronic spinal cord injury was selected based on a hierarchy of evidence. The highest priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials and the next types of studies are observational studies, cohort studies, and case-control studies. High-quality case series were also eligible for inclusion. We excluded publications such as narrative reviews, expert opinions, conference abstracts, theses, animal studies, and editorials. Only studies published in English were considered. To capture both established principles and recent advances, the search was restricted to publications from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2025. Within this window, studies published from 2019 onward were prioritized in the synthesis to specifically highlight recent advances. No restrictions were applied based on country of origin.

Information sources

Following the establishment of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we independently conducted a comprehensive systematic search across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library. Studies were systematically screened based on their titles, abstracts, and subsequently, full-text articles. Furthermore, the reference lists of all identified relevant studies were manually screened to ensure no eligible publications were overlooked.

Search strategy

The search strategy for this study was developed based on the PubMed guidelines. To minimize the risk of omitting relevant literature, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as ‘Spinal Cord Injuries’, ‘Chronic Disease’, ‘Disease Management’were employed to define specific search topics. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine MeSH terms with other relevant keywords, thereby optimizing the search scope and enhancing the precision of the results. Furthermore, this strategy was adapted to meet the specific syntax and requirements of the other three databases included in the search. The complete search strategies, including all MeSH terms, keywords, Boolean operators, and database-specific syntax for each of the four databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library), are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Selection process

The study selection process was conducted independently by X.Z. (a board-certified neurosurgeon with 6 years of clinical experience in spinal cord injury management and formal training in systematic review methodology) in accordance with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and screened by title and abstract. After X.Z. completed title/abstract screening, H.W. independently re-screened the excluded records to verify that no relevant studies had been erroneously omitted. After that, during the process of full – text screening, any article for which X.Z. was uncertain about eligibility was automatically referred to H.W. for final adjudication. This approach balances feasibility with methodological rigor, reducing bias when selecting the literature.

Results

Study selection

Following a comprehensive and systematic search across four electronic medical databases, 4591 articles were initially identified. After excluding duplicate records, articles underwent relevance screening based on titles, abstracts, and full texts. Ultimately, 105 articles met inclusion criteria and were included in the review. Autonomic nervous system complications

Autonomic nervous system complications arise from the disruption of information pathways between the brain and the autonomic nervous system below the level of injury, representing critical factors affecting both acute life-threatening events and long-term health risks in patients.

Autonomic dysreflexia

Autonomic dysreflexia (AD) is a potentially life-threatening syndrome occurring in patients with SCI at or above the T6 level, characterized by paroxysmal hypertension, headache, facial flushing, diaphoresis, and bradycardia [2].

For individuals with chronic SCI, AD remains a persistent risk. When an acute episode occurs, emergency management is the first priority to save lives. Immediately place the patient in an upright sitting position with legs dependent to reduce venous return and lower blood pressure; quickly loosen any constrictive clothing, belts, binders, compression stockings, etc., to remove potential noxious stimuli [2]; and promptly perform a systematic check for the most common triggers, such as bladder distension and bowel impaction.

If initial measures are ineffective or the situation is critical, pharmacological intervention is necessary. If blood pressure remains persistently elevated or symptoms persist after positional changes and trigger removal, rapid-acting, short-duration antihypertensive agents should be administered immediately. Sublingual nitroglycerin is the preferred choice due to its rapid onset [3]; nifedipine capsules, bitten and held sublingually, are also commonly used, though caution is warranted against precipitous blood pressure drops [2].

Long-term prevention centers on implementing regular bladder and bowel management regimens to eliminate the primary triggers. This includes scheduled intermittent catheterization and regular bowel evacuations. Educating patients, family members, and caregivers to accurately recognize the early symptoms of AD and master initial management steps is also key to preventing its severe consequences.

Cardiovascular system complications

Following SCI, the cardiovascular system, deprived of supraspinal regulation, exhibits various forms of circulatory dysfunction, the management of which must span the entire chronic phase.

Orthostatic hypotension

Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is defined as a decrease in systolic BP ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg within 3 min of upright posture [4]. Non-pharmacological first-line treatments include tilt-table training, abdominal binders, compression stockings, and increased fluid/sodium intake [4]; if insufficient, midodrine or fludrocortisone may be used [5]. Recent research in athletes with SCI shows OH affects about 33.3% and impairs performance, and the Valsalva maneuver can assess autonomic dysfunction, with prolonged pressure recovery time indicating impaired cardiovascular control [6]. Furthermore, cardiovascular-specific epidural stimulation (CV-scES) significantly improved mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure in chronic cervical SCI [7], and home blood pressure monitoring protocols have been refined to guide midodrine dosing [5].

Cardiac arrhythmias

Due to loss of cardiac sympathetic innervation and relative vagal predominance, bradycardia is a potentially life-threatening issue for individuals with high-level injuries. Severe symptomatic bradycardia may require temporary or permanent cardiac pacemaker implantation. For persistent resting tachycardia, potential underlying triggers should be thoroughly investigated and treated. If tachycardia persists despite addressing triggers, β-blockers (e.g. propranolol, atenolol) or ivabradine may be considered for heart rate control.

Deep vein thrombosis

Although the risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) peaks in the acute phase, patients with chronic SCI remain at a perpetually elevated baseline risk due to persistent immobility. Long-term management focuses on non-pharmacological strategies such as maintaining regular limb movements and using pneumatic compression devices during prolonged sitting. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for DVT in chronic patients presenting with new-onset limb swelling. Once DVT is confirmed, therapeutic anticoagulation should follow standard protocols [8] to prevent thrombus propagation and pulmonary embolism. Massage or vigorous manipulation of the affected limb should be avoided to prevent dislodgement. However, some studies indicate that compared to unfractionated heparin (UFH), LMWH shows no significant advantage in efficacy and risk prevention, nor in thromboprophylaxis efficacy based on the level of SCI [9]. Inferior vena cava filter placement is reserved for patients with absolute contraindications to anticoagulation or those who experience recurrent thrombosis despite adequate anticoagulation.

Thermoregulatory dysfunction

Due to impaired sympathetic pathways controlling sweating and cutaneous vasodilation/constriction, patients lose the ability to finely regulate body temperature in response to environmental changes, manifesting as a poikilothermic state where body temperature fluctuates with ambient temperature. This not only causes subjective discomfort but can also exacerbate spasticity, trigger AD, and lead to other serious consequences [2].

Measures for hyperthermia include maintaining a cool ambient temperature, enhancing air circulation, using personal cooling devices like cool towels, and sponging the skin with cool water [2]. The core measures for hypothermia are effective insulation and prevention of heat loss, achieved by raising the room temperature, adding clothing, using blankets, and consuming warm fluids. Particular attention should be paid to protecting the head, neck, and extremities[expert opinion].

Respiratory system complications

Among SCI patients, respiratory system complications are the most significant cause of both acute and chronic mortality [10,11]. Their management spans the entire disease course, sustaining life and progressively improving pulmonary function and quality of life.

Ventilatory support and assisted cough

For chronic SCI patients who remain dependent on ventilatory support, meticulous long-term management is essential. This includes ongoing monitoring for respiratory insufficiency, optimization of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) settings for comfort and efficacy, and rigorous tracheostomy care to prevent complications such as stenosis or infection. Secretion management via manually assisted coughing or mechanical insufflation-exsufflation devices remains a cornerstone of daily routine to prevent atelectasis and pneumonia. A retrospective cohort study of 94 patients with CSCI (AIS A-C) demonstrated that prophylactic intermittent non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (iNPPV) initiated within 72 h of admission was associated with significantly lower incidence of pneumonia (29.0% vs 56.5%, p < 0.001) compared to usual care [12].

Manually assisted coughing is an effective secretion clearance technique. Synchronized with the patient’s own cough effort, the caregiver applies specific chest or abdominal thrusts during expiration to facilitate secretion expulsion. High-frequency chest wall oscillation (HFCWO) devices generate subtle, rapid oscillations to mobilize secretions toward the central airways. These can often be self-administered, enhancing convenience and adherence.

Respiratory muscle training

Respiratory muscle training (RMT) is an effective strategy to improve pulmonary function in chronic SCI. A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that RMT significantly improved total lung capacity, forced vital capacity, FEV1, and maximum voluntary ventilation [13]. Locomotor step training with body weight support improved maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures and sEMG activity, indicating spinal neural plasticity [11]; combining RMT with the abdominal draw-in maneuver may yield greater benefits [14]. A novel respiratory motor control assessment tool using sEMG vector analysis has shown high specificity and sensitivity [15]. Notably, respiratory training also improved cardiovascular autonomic reactivity, reporting resolution of orthostatic hypotension in 7 of 11 patients [16]. For chronic respiratory insufficiency due to high cervical SCI, diaphragmatic pacing system implantation offers liberation from mechanical ventilation [17].

Surgical management

For patients with chronic respiratory insufficiency due to high cervical spinal cord injury, diaphragmatic pacing system (DPS) implantation offers the possibility of liberation from mechanical ventilation [17]. Thoracoplasty may be considered in patients with severe restrictive ventilatory defects to improve respiration by enhancing chest wall compliance. Tracheopexy addresses tracheomalacia, improving ventilation by stabilizing the airway.

Prevention

Optimal primary care for individuals with chronic SCI includes appropriate surveillance for SCI-specific respiratory disease and key preventive care measures. Clinical recommendations emphasize the importance of promoting influenza immunization, implementing respiratory muscle training, and instituting aggressive secretion management strategies when needed. Regular respiratory function assessments, including spirometry and respiratory muscle strength testing, allow for early identification of decline and timely adjustment of treatment plans [18].

Motor system complications

Motor dysfunction and spasticity

Motor dysfunction and spasticity are the most frequent manifestations of chronic SCI, the management of which should be tailored to individual needs [19], forming a comprehensive intervention system with multidimensional goals of improving function, alleviating symptoms, and enhancing quality of life.

Rehabilitation exercise (muscle strength, balance, coordination) is foundational [19]. Osteopathic manipulative treatment alleviates spasticity and improves quality of life [20]. Functional electrical stimulation (FES) cycling reduces lower limb spasticity [21] and improves upper limb independence [22]; transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) modulates pre- and postsynaptic inhibition to control spasticity [23,24], and transcranial magnetic stimulation improves walking speed [22]. Robotic-assisted gait training provides high-intensity, standardized patterns, ameliorating spasticity and walking capacity [25].

Pharmacological therapy is a primary systemic approach for managing spasticity. Baclofen, a GABAB receptor agonist, is the most effective medication for spasticity and can be administered orally or intrathecally depending on the location and severity of spasticity [26,27], with enhanced efficacy when combined with physical therapy [28]. Tizanidine exerts its muscle relaxant effects via central α2-adrenergic receptors. Gabapentin and benzodiazepines are also commonly used. For focal spasticity, botulinum toxin type A injections offer a highly targeted solution [29]. By precisely injecting into overactive target muscles, it blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, significantly reducing spasticity in specific muscle groups. Furthermore, Sativex® (nabiximols, a cannabinoid oromucosal spray) effectively reduces spasticity in patients unresponsive to conventional therapies [30]. Riluzole has entered clinical trials as a means to reduce spasticity in chronic SCI patients, demonstrating safety and efficacy [31].

When conservative treatments fail, surgical interventions become the last resort. Epidural spinal cord stimulation has been shown to produce varying degrees of improvement in spasticity for the majority of SCI patients [32,33]. Selective dorsal rhizotomy, by sectioning specific sensory nerve roots, can permanently reduce muscle tone. Although its irreversibility carries risks of sensory loss, further weakness, or bladder dysfunction, preliminary studies have demonstrated it to be a safe and effective treatment [34,35].

Muscle atrophy and weakness

The pathophysiological basis of muscle atrophy and weakness lies in the loss of upper motor neuron drive to spinal anterior horn cells and the disconnection between lower motor neurons and muscle units. Its management requires a multi-level, individualized, comprehensive treatment principle aimed at delaying disease progression, preserving existing function, and enhancing patient quality of life.

The focus of physical and exercise therapy interventions is on early initiation and adherence to long-term, relatively high-intensity training. For muscles with preserved innervation, resistance training is crucial to maintain and enhance their function. For muscles completely denervated, Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) not only induces periodic contractions in passive modes to delay atrophy and improve local blood flow but also, in modalities like cyclical FES cycling or FES-assisted walking, prompts functional muscle contractions, thereby improving muscle mass and volume, such as isometric peak torque of the quadriceps and leg volume [36]. Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation can also effectively restore upper limb motor function [37] and walking distance [38] in patients with long-term SCI. However, some studies indicate that the effect of strength training combined with electrical stimulation for over 8 weeks on very weak muscle strength in SCI patients is minimal [39]. Exoskeleton-assisted walking has been shown to improve motor capacity and quality of life in individuals with chronic SCI [40–44], but careful patient selection and risk assessment are essential. A 2024 case report documented bilateral tibial fractures in a patient with T10 complete SCI during an initial exoskeleton training session; subsequent DEXA scan revealed underlying osteoporosis, highlighting the critical importance of preoperative bone health assessment [45]. A 2022 systematic review of 41 studies (including 166 tetraplegic patients) reported minor adverse events affecting the cutaneous, cardiovascular, or musculoskeletal systems in 17 patients, with occurrence of adverse events significantly higher in paraplegic compared to tetraplegic populations (p = 0.001). Only one major adverse event involved a tetraplegic patient. The authors emphasized that good patient selection and preparation are essential to minimize adverse events [46]. While a 2025 randomized controlled trial demonstrated that exoskeleton use was associated with fewer adverse events compared to conventional knee-ankle-foot orthoses (17 vs. 31 events) in carefully selected patients with complete paraplegia (T4-T12) [47], these findings underscore that exoskeleton safety depends on rigorous screening – including DEXA scanning for osteoporosis assessment and evaluation for joint contractures – prior to implementation [45–47].

Nutritional support is an indispensable component of long-term management. Ensuring adequate intake of high-quality protein is a physiological prerequisite supporting exercise therapy. Concurrently, supplementation with nutrients such as Vitamin D, creatine, and β-hydroxy β-methylbutyrate (HMB) serves as beneficial adjunctive strategies, although their definitive efficacy, optimal dosing, and long-term benefits in the specific SCI population require larger-scale validation. Exercise and nutritional management should be implemented following assessment of individual circumstances and accompanied by effective follow-up to avoid excess [48].

In the realm of pharmacological treatment, androgens (e.g. testosterone) and selective androgen receptor modulators have been investigated for increasing muscle mass and strength, but their potential side effects, including endocrine disturbances and cardiovascular risks, warrant caution. Some studies suggest that ursolic acid and β2-adrenergic agonists may be more effective than androgens in mitigating muscle atrophy [49].

Surgical interventions, such as tendon transfer procedures, utilize functional muscle groups to compensate for lost key functions. Their success depends on thorough preoperative assessment and systematic postoperative rehabilitation. The laparoscopic implantation of a neuroprosthesis (LION procedure) with an implantable pulse generator and low-frequency pelvic nerve stimulation represents a promising new treatment method for SCI patients, showing significant improvement in walking capacity [50].

Skeletal complications

The motor system following chronic SCI faces the dual challenge of osteoporosis and heterotopic ossification, conditions requiring distinct yet concurrent management strategies.

Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is characterized by rapid and severe bone loss [51], primarily affecting the pelvis and lower limbs, significantly increasing the risk of pathological fractures in patients.

Although early standing using standing frames and weight-bearing training are widely advocated and offer health benefits, they often fail to prevent the precipitous bone loss occurring initially post-injury. In nutritional therapy, adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation are essential. Pharmacological treatment primarily aims to reduce bone resorption or promote bone formation. Bisphosphonates (e.g. alendronate, zoledronic acid, risedronate, ibandronate) are currently the preferred option; early intravenous administration has been proven effective in slowing lower extremity bone loss, with particularly favorable effects on hip and lumbar spine osteoporosis [52,53]. For patients with severe osteoporosis and high fracture risk, recombinant human parathyroid hormone (e.g. teriparatide), the anti-RANKL human monoclonal antibody (denosumab), and the anti-sclerostin human monoclonal antibody (romosozumab) offer more aggressive anabolic treatment options. However, experience with their use in the SCI population remains relatively limited, and costs are high [54]. Additionally, 830-nanometer photobiomodulation laser therapy has proven effective in improving bone mineral density at the proximal femur and mid-forearm in chronic SCI patients [55]. Extracorporeal nanoscale vibrations have been demonstrated to promote the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells toward an osteoblast-like phenotype while enhancing osteogenesis and inhibiting osteoclastogenesis. Enhanced doses of nanovibration stimulation may also help mitigate osteoporosis in less severe cases [56].

Early screening and prevention of osteoporosis are clinically imperative [57]. Regular monitoring of bone mineral density, combined with assessment of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, serum calcium, and phosphorus levels, should become routine in chronic phase management.

Heterotopic ossification

Heterotopic ossification (HO) involves the pathological formation of bone in soft tissues around joints, most commonly the hips and knees, leading to limited joint range of motion, local swelling, and pain.

Gentle range of motion exercises help maintain joint mobility and prevent contractures. However, forceful stretching may exacerbate local inflammation and injury, potentially stimulating HO progression. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, e.g. indomethacin) are effective for early prevention, acting by inhibiting cyclooxygenase. Studies have confirmed lower incidence rates of HO in patients using NSAIDs [58]. If HO leads to severe joint functional limitation, surgical excision becomes the only definitive treatment. To prevent recurrence, immediate postoperative administration of NSAIDs is an effective adjunct.

Sensory disorders

Neuropathic pain

Neuropathic pain arises from pathological changes within the central or peripheral somatosensory pathways, often described by patients as burning, electric shock-like, or stabbing sensations. Beyond direct pathway damage, a growing body of evidence indicates that deficits in mental body representations (MBRs) – the brain’s internal models of the body’s position, dimensions, and awareness – play a critical role in the occurrence and maintenance of neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury (SCI) [59,60]. Following SCI, the brain no longer receives accurate sensory input from denervated body regions, leading to fragmented or distorted body representations that manifest as impaired body awareness and altered visuospatial body maps. These MBR deficits are thought to contribute to chronic pain through maladaptive neuroplasticity in brain regions integral to both body representation and pain perception, including the insular and parietal opercular cortices [59,60].

Emerging therapeutic approaches targeting MBR restoration have shown promising results. A 2023 pilot study found that a 12-week Qigong intervention designed to enhance body awareness significantly reduced neuropathic pain intensity in adults with chronic SCI (Cohen’s d = 1.75), with pain relief maintained at 1-year follow-up [61]. A 2025 systematic review further demonstrated that virtual reality-based interventions aimed at recalibrating body representation through multisensory integration significantly reduce pain intensity and improve embodiment across neurological conditions including SCI [62]. Recognizing the clinical need for targeted assessment, the SCI-BodyMap scale is currently being developed and validated to measure MBR deficits in this population [59]. These findings suggest that interventions targeting the restoration of accurate body representations may offer novel therapeutic pathways for neuropathic pain after SCI [60–62].

Rehabilitation strategies based on neuroplasticity represent a new direction in management. Intermittent theta-burst stimulation appears optimal for pain relief [63]. Non-invasive neuromodulation techniques like repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS), by modulating cortical excitability, can influence pain signaling at the central level [64]. A 2022 systematic review and network meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (n = 507) found that non-invasive brain stimulation significantly reduced neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury compared to the control group (SMD = −0.84, 95% CI −1.27 to −0.40, p = 0.00). The effect varied by modality: rTMS demonstrated significant pain reduction (SMD = −0.92, 95% CI −1.56 to −0.28, p = 0.01), while tDCS did not (SMD = −0.70, 95% CI −1.45 to 0.04, p = 0.06). Network meta-analysis indicated rTMS had a higher probability of being optimal (p = 0.62 vs. 0.38 for tDCS) [65]. Virtual Reality (VR) therapy effectively distracts patients’ attention from pain through immersive multi-sensory environments, with efficacy validated in clinical trials [66,67].

Pharmacological management follows individualization and combination therapy. Gabapentin and pregabalin are first-line agents; a 2025 meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (682 patients) found that gabapentinoids reduced average daily pain by 1.75 points (0–10 scale; 95% CI −3.23 to −0.28; p = 0.02) and increased the proportion achieving ≥50% pain relief (RR 2.19; 95% CI 1.47–3.25; p < 0.001), though dizziness (RR 2.83), somnolence (RR 3.77), and blurred vision (RR 8.55) were common [68]. Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline) primarily act by enhancing descending inhibitory pathways [69]. Intrathecal baclofen infusion effectively reduces neuropathic pain [70]; bumetanide has shown significant average pain reduction [71]; NFX-88 (2.10 g/day) demonstrates a high safety profile and best pain relief effect in a phase IIa trial [72]. The 8% capsaicin patch improved symptoms and activity, with 35% and 29% pain reduction at weeks 2 and 4, respectively [73]. Clinically, it is essential to balance each patient’s specific situation with the benefits and drawbacks of each medication to determine the ideal treatment for chronic SCI-related pain [74].

For medication-refractory pain, spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is an advanced intervention; a 2025 meta-analysis reported significant pain reduction (mean difference −4.73; 95% CI −4.76 to − meta-ap < 0.00001) compared to conventional management [75], although nearly half of patients do not obtain long-term relief [76].

Paresthesia

Paresthesia includes non-painful abnormal sensations like numbness or tingling, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life and psychological state. Promoting neuroplastic changes in the sensory cortex through mirror therapy, graded tactile stimulation, and sensory substitution devices can be beneficial. Cognitive-behavioral therapy also helps patients modify psychological factors related to abnormal sensations, effectively reducing the distress caused by symptoms.

Sensory loss

Sensory loss is the root cause of secondary injuries like pressure injuries and burns. Implementing regular, comprehensive skin inspections and enhancing environmental safety are essential. Using protective orthoses on vulnerable areas is also necessary. Smart wearable devices equipped with distributed temperature and pressure sensors can provide real-time monitoring and issue alerts upon detecting hazardous pressure or temperature levels. Furthermore, a single intrathecal infusion of Wharton’s Jelly-derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (WJ-MSCs) in patients with chronic complete SCI has been shown to improve sensory function in areas adjacent to the injury level [77].

Pressure injuries

Pressure injuries (PIs), also known as pressure ulcers or bedsores, are localized damage to the skin and/or underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence, resulting from prolonged pressure or pressure in combination with shear. In the SCI population, PIs are a frequent and serious complication, causing not only persistent physical suffering and psychological distress but also substantially increasing the risk of systemic infection and even death [78]. Furthermore, the long-term management and treatment of PIs impose a heavy burden on patients’ families and significant socioeconomic costs, making them a pressing public health concern [79].

Conservative management

For chronic SCI patients, the fundamental goal of conservative treatment is to reverse the pathological state of the wound through precise interventions and restart/maintain the physiological healing process [78,79]. Debridement is the primary step, removing non-viable tissue and biofilm. Modern wound dressings are selected to maintain a optimal wound environment; choices include alginate dressings or antimicrobial dressings targeted against biofilms. For complex, hard-to-heal wounds, Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT) represents a major technological advance in conservative care, achieving multiple synergistic effects including efficient exudate removal, edema reduction, and stimulation of granulation tissue formation [79].

Surgical management

When conservative measures fail, surgical intervention becomes the definitive solution for functional restoration. Flap techniques (e.g. myocutaneous, fasciocutaneous) have significantly improved early mobility and long-term quality of life for patients, although studies indicate that the average hospital stay for patients undergoing flap reconstruction was prolonged by 71 days [80]. Protective colostomy can prevent local complications following surgery, improve quality of life, and reduce long-term hospitalization and medical costs [81]. Stem cell therapy may also represent a promising option for patients with refractory ulcers [82].

Prevention

In PI management, prevention is the most critical practice embodying patient-centered, humane care. For chronic SCI patients, prevention is a systematic health engineering project requiring the participation of the healthcare system, caregivers, and patients themselves.

This system starts with accurate risk assessment. Clinically used scales like the Braden Scale or Norton Scale provide a foundational tool for risk stratification. Guided by risk assessment, key preventive measures include: daily comprehensive skin inspection; regular, scheduled positional changes; and keeping the head of the bed elevated at the lowest acceptable angle to reduce shear forces [79]. Concurrently, appropriate support surfaces are the technical guarantee for pressure relief and redistribution. Air-fluidized beds offer superior pressure relief. Small cohort studies suggest that off-loading cushions may outperform air cushions in pressure relief [83]. Air-based cushions demonstrate better interface pressure reduction compared to gel and foam cushions [84]. Emerging technologies such as interface pressure mapping offer real-time visual feedback on seated pressure distribution. A 2022 study of a mobile pressure mapping application in six wheelchair users with SCI found that real-time visual pressure feedback may be more useful than traditional timer-based reminders for promoting weight shift behaviors. While adherence to reminders was low, the live pressure map was used for both morning wheelchair setup and regular spot checks throughout the day, suggesting that this technology holds promise for enhancing pressure injury prevention in community-dwelling individuals with SCI [85]. Furthermore, telemedicine has proven a feasible method, reducing PI incidence and severity while accelerating patient recovery [86]. Chronic SCI patients often suffer from malnutrition due to metabolic changes and inadequate intake, directly impairing tissue repair capacity. Therefore, nutritional assessment should be performed first. Providing sufficient key nutrients like protein, Vitamin C, and zinc, aiming for a positive nitrogen balance, strengthens tissue resilience and consolidates the overall preventive effect (Figure 3).

Genitourinary system complications

SCI-related genitourinary disorders can significantly impact patients’ psychological health and social quality of life while increasing the risk of other long-term complications. The modern management philosophy has evolved from simple organ protection to a holistic care model centered on the patient’s overall health and social participation.

Neurogenic bladder

When SCI disrupts supraspinal and sacral micturition centers, it results in neurogenic bladder with either detrusor overactivity or areflexia [10]. Urodynamic evaluation is essential to guide treatment. Conservative management typically involves behavioral interventions, such as moderating caffeine intake, timed voiding, and pelvic floor muscle training to improve incontinence [87]. Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) remains the preferred method for bladder emptying, reducing infection and stones, while indwelling catheters should be minimized [88,89]. Beyond conservative management, electrical stimulation therapy has been shown to increase bladder capacity and reduce residual urine volume [90,91]. Pharmacologically, intra-detrusor onabotulinumtoxinA (Botox®) injections significantly improve incontinence (84.3% of patients experience reduced episodes) [92]. Anticholinergics (e.g. solifenacin) and the β3-adrenergic agonist mirabegron inhibit detrusor overactivity, while α-blockers (e.g. tamsulosin) reduce bladder outlet resistance. Stem cell therapy has demonstrated safety and efficacy in early studies, though meta-analyses indicate the degree of improvement may not be significant [93,94].

Urinary Tract Infections and renal failure

Prevention of Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) relies on adequate fluid intake, strict aseptic technique during catheterization (if applicable), and avoiding unnecessary antibiotic use. Treatment should be strictly based on established indications. Management should be guided by urine culture and sensitivity results, using targeted antibiotics for an adequate duration. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or fluoroquinolones are first-line choices for susceptible organisms in patients without contraindications [95]. Gentamicin bladder instillations have also been shown to reduce UTI incidence by 83% [96]. Bladder management strategies should be re-evaluated post-infection.

Prevention of renal failure centers on maintaining low intravesical pressures. Regular monitoring of renal function and upper urinary tract morphology is crucial for the early detection and management of vesicoureteral reflux and hydronephrosis, key to preventing progressive renal deterioration.

Reproductive system dysfunction

In managing male sexual dysfunction, Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (PDE5is; sildenafil, tadalafil) are commonly used oral medications for erectile dysfunction, proven effective and safe. When oral medications are ineffective or contraindicated, intracavernosal injections of vasoactive drugs, vacuum erection devices, and penile prosthesis implantation [97] offer various options. For men unable to ejaculate naturally but desiring fertility, semen can be retrieved via penile vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation, followed by assisted reproductive technologies like intrauterine insemination (IUI) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) to achieve parenthood [98]. Management of female sexual dysfunction focuses on the use of water-based lubricants, vibratory stimulation of sensitive areas, professional psychosexual counseling, and management of bladder and bowel issues.

Bowel dysfunction

Neurogenic bowel dysfunction (NBD) is one of the most common complications following SCI. Through dietary modifications, medication, and stimulation techniques, patient quality of life can be improved and further deterioration prevented [99].

General management

A detailed history and physical examination are used to classify NBD as either upper motor neuron (reflexic) or lower motor neuron (areflexic) type, which determines the core direction of subsequent management strategies. Studies report that high fiber intake can sometimes exacerbate NBD symptoms, while low fiber intake may contribute to dysbiosis [100]. Ensuring adequate daily fluid intake (2000–2500 ml) and dietary fiber (around 15 g) is generally recommended [101]. Scheduled defecation is typically arranged at fixed times daily or every other day, aiming for complete evacuation.

Assisted defecation techniques

Rectal stimulation, via digital rectal examination, insertion of suppositories, or mini-enemas, can trigger the sacral defecation reflex, promoting peristalsis in the descending colon and rectum to achieve effective reflex emptying. Electrical rectal stimulation has also enabled patients to achieve complete defecation successfully [102]. For upper motor neuron type bowel, instructing patients to perform Valsalva maneuvers combined with clockwise abdominal massage can help propel stool. For lower motor neuron type bowel, lacking reflexic defecation, caregivers often need to perform deep suprapubic pressure or manual digital evacuation. Transanal irrigation systems work by regularly infusing a controlled volume of warm water into the colon to stimulate and cleanse it. This method significantly reduces the frequency of both fecal incontinence and constipation [103,104].

Pharmacological management

Oral medications include osmotic agents like polyethylene glycol and lactulose, which retain water in the bowel lumen; prokinetic agents like prucalopride, which enhance colonic motility; and secretagogues like lubiprostone and linaclotide, which increase intestinal fluid secretion to facilitate defecation. Botulinum toxin A injection into the anal sphincter is a safe treatment that can reduce sphincter tone and anal canal pressure during defecation, improving symptoms of difficult evacuation [105].

Surgical management

Electrical stimulation combined with conventional treatment can increase defecation frequency and stool output [106]. When other methods fail and the condition severely impacts social life, a colostomy can effectively improve bowel management and enhance patient autonomy [107].

Metabolic and endocrine alterations

Chronic SCI triggers multiple metabolic and endocrine changes. Male hypogonadism is a common complication; assessment should include morning serum total testosterone with LH/FSH to distinguish primary from secondary hypogonadism. Testosterone replacement may be considered after careful risk–benefit evaluation, although SCI-specific guidelines remain limited. Insulin resistance and dyslipidemia can emerge early post-injury; a low-carbohydrate/high-protein (LC/HP) diet has been shown to improve lipid profiles [108]. Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent due to limited sun exposure and altered metabolism. Routine vitamin D supplementation is recommended, with regular monitoring of serum 25-OH vitamin D levels. While current management focuses on replacement and supplementation, these strategies remain symptomatic and do not address the underlying autonomic dysregulation. Future interventions should explore combining neuromodulation with metabolic therapies to restore functional metabolism rather than simply compensating for failure.

Discussion

Management of autonomic dysfunction in chronic SCI has become a staged process, ranging from emergency to long-term management, yet all of them still remain symptomatic and fail to address the pathology caused by the loss of autonomic circuitry. Acute symptom relief pharmacotherapy has been found effective in treating chronic SCI; nonetheless, they are associated with risk of hemodynamic disturbances and lack of definitive permanent outcomes. Future studies need focus on neural regeneration approaches to restore injured autonomic pathways and developing a unified, multisystem control infrastructure that is able to emulate individual autonomic function and shift the focus of care away from relief to recovery and maintenance of systemic physiological control.

At the pulmonary disease level, an important issue is the lack of integration of formalised respiratory rehabilitation as part of community and long-term care. Future work in this area needs to focus on the standardisation and distribution of new cutting edge respiratory neuromodulation methods, the use of biomaterials for diaphragmatic repair and regeneration, and intelligent remote sensing of the condition.Wearable technologies to track oxygen saturation and respiratory rate, along with cloud analytics, support anticipatory care. Technological developments paired with holistic care models potentially offers a substantial avenue for long-term respiratory functioning, health and quality of life outcomes in SCI.

Clinical management of the motor symptom is evolved into a comprehensive model including rehabilitation, medication, neuromodulation and surgery, yet all these strategies combined still fall short of being able to cause actual neural repair. Also, the long term efficacy of most interventions is not well backed by robust follow up studies and more expensive technologies increase health disparities.Neural regenerative breakthroughs including the stem cell and gene therapies and precision medicine approach based on multi-omics data will likely drive further fundamental development and re-set the paradigm of treatment objectives towards rebuilding rather than compensatory repair of neural function.

For sensory disorders, particularly neuropathic pain, current management encompasses a diverse range of interventions from pharmacotherapy to neuromodulation. A key limitation, however, is the inability to repair damaged sensory pathways. Pharmacological responses show high inter-individual variability, and the optimal parameters and long-term sustainability of neuromodulation require further clarification. Future sensory rehabilitation should emphasize pathway repair via strategies such as stem cell therapy and biomaterial scaffolds. At the same time, refining precise neuromodulation protocols, potentially guided by brain-computer interfaces for personalized intervention, may help transcend the limitations of current symptomatic management and achieve genuine sensory restoration.

The rational and structured process of PI management based on PIs’ risk assessment, PI conserving care, surgical wound repair is well defined and nutrition and posture maintenance are essential tools. However, the present PI strategies are all dependent on caregivers’ based exogenous mechanism to manage care and not restoring patients’ endogenous protective sensation and tissue recovery.Solely relieving pressure through pressure relieving devices does nothing but transfer the stress without addressing intrinsic tissue vulnerability and reconstructive surgery does nothing more than patch a damaged region of the body, which leads to significant recurrence. Prevention of PI should change from passive to active in the near future by considering implanted biosensors that actively monitor tissue perfusion.Enhancement of tissue repair through angiogenesis-promoting treatments and tissue engineering are two important aspect for truly prevention and functional reconstruction.

Management of urinary system complications was defined by an integrated care of the neurogenic bladder process, focused on CIC, anticholinergics, and botulism injections, and pathways for rehabilitation of sexual function, with a clear lack of integration of bladder, sexual, and bowel management separated into different processes (often used in sequence), and the continuing omission of treatment of the fundamental issue of altered control of neural mechanisms.There are promising strategies like stem cell therapy still under active research. In future work, it is advisable to combine neural regeneration with accurate neuromodulation (such as epidural and sacral nerve stimulation) to restore volitional control with sensory feedback. The ultimate goal of the functional restoration would be the achievement of an artificial bionic organs able to sense and to empty in autonomous way.

Improving the feedback of a well targeted neuro-stimulation to bowel through the adequate combination of delivery systems and output neurons, implementing stem cell applications for enteric nervous system repair and implementing the use of wearable devices with machine learning algorithms to help predict the time of bowel movement and thus, personalize the care.It is crucial to provide a long term, multispecialty follow up program that would incorporate intestinal, urological and cutaneous management in one comprehensive care program for the continuous improvement of patient’s quality of life (Figure 4).

Application targets of neural regulation technology in the management of chronic SCI.

The management of the metabolic and endocrine changes associated with SCI currently focusses on treatment of specific problems, namely hypogonadism, insulin resistance and vitamin D deficiency, via replacement and supplementation strategies; however, these remain essentially symptomatic and fail to address the etiology that is related to autonomic dysregulation and abnormal central control of the neuro-endocrine-metabolic network.Prospective metabolic interventions might explore how the degree and extent of neural damage relate to downstream endocrine-metabolic responses, and how neuromodulatory tools might be combined with metabolic interventions to enable restoration of the functional metabolism rather than simply overtaking compensatory failures.

Despite the numerous advancements in research related to nerve regeneration over the decades, no therapy has yet been able to restore the lost neural connections in chronic SCI. There may be the following reasons for this. Firstly, the glial scar is a biochemically active structure that actively inhibits axonal growth through chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans[109]. Even if axons can grow, the regenerating axons must span a relatively long distance, which is far beyond what can be achieved in animal models. In addition, long-term denervation leads to the gradual atrophy of anterior horn cells and motor end-plates, making the target muscles unable to receive re-innervation. Secondly, there is still a significant gap between animal research and human trials. The main reasons may include species differences in the anatomical structure of the corticospinal tract, the heterogeneity of injury models, and mismatched outcome indicators (rodent motor scores vs. human functional scales). Moreover, cell transplantation has the problem of low survival rate. Biomaterial scaffolds cannot replicate the three-dimensional structure required for functional connections. Crucially, electrical stimulation achieves movement through neuromodulation of the remaining neural circuits, which is a compensatory strategy rather than axonal regeneration. Additionally, most trials are small-scale, highly heterogeneous, and there is a lack of consensus on the minimum clinically important difference in regeneration outcomes, effective neurophysiological measurement methods for restoring connectivity, or the adaptive design of combination therapies. Given these obstacles, feasible future progress will come from combination approaches (neuromodulation + rehabilitation + drug therapy) and early intervention to prevent chronic atrophy, gradually achieving functional improvement while continuously promoting basic regeneration research as a long-term goal.

Currently, treatment of chronic spinal cord injury is supported by intricate systems that address deficits in autonomic, respiratory, motor, sensory, and other domains, and although these interventions succeed at ameliorating symptoms and maintaining viability, they are constrained by the lack of treatment potential for restoring damaged neurons and circuits. Ultimately, the paradigm of care moving forward will lie on the ground of a paradigm change, that is, from external symptomatic restorations to internal therapeutic cure and, driven by the synergy of regenerative medicine, precision neuromodulation, and smart technologies, at attempting to restore broken connections in an effort at recreating the physiological control of the body, thus overcoming a life long compensation with a path to functional recovery.

Conclusion

Given the numerous challenges and the limitations of the current management strategies, further research is urgently needed for the management of chronic spinal cord injury complications. However, a complete regenerative cure remains a long-term aspiration rather than an imminent reality, as multiple biological, translational, and trial design barriers have yet to be overcome. Near- to mid-term progress will most likely come from optimizing multidisciplinary combination protocols that integrate neuromodulation, task-specific rehabilitation, and targeted pharmacotherapy – leveraging residual neural circuitry to achieve incremental functional gains. While neural regeneration continues to be a worthy goal, the most immediate impact on patient outcomes will arise from moving beyond passive symptom management toward active, technology-assisted restoration within the constraints of current evidence.
