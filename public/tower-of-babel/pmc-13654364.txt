Editorial for the Special Issue on Cholesterol Crystal Embolization☆
Authors: George S Abela
Published: 2026

The presence of Cholesterol Crystals (CCs) has been reported as clefts by light microscopy for centuries as a feature of advanced atherosclerotic plaques. Clefts are seen as empty spaces where crystals used to reside but were dissolved and etched out by tissue processing using lipid solvents, mainly ethanol. Some have referred to CCs as inert and noncontributory to plaque activity [1] and others have even referred to them as a postmortem artifact. However, more recent studies have elucidated a more active role of CCs. In fact, the articles presented in this Special Issue on Cholesterol Crystal Emboli tell a different story. Although CC embolism from ruptured aortic plaques or following mechanical trauma of plaques during catheterization procedures has been reported, those were mainly to the lower extremities causing CCs embolization syndrome (CES), but other aspects of CCs had not been fully recognized [2], [3].

The CES is mainly described in the peripheral circulation and is associated with systemic and local inflammation triggered by CCs. In fact, the clinical features of this condition are skin mottling (levido reticularis) and ‘blue toes’ that is often associated with a morbid outcome [4]. Also, crystal emboli to the renal arteries have been associated with renal injury with failure that may be irreversible. The mechanisms involved with this process are related to both arteriolar obstructions causing ischemia as well as inflammation that can often mimic systemic arteriopathy [4], [5]. However, looking forward it has become evident that CCs are not idle by standers in atherosclerotic plaque but rather very active elements.

To understand the role of CCs in greater depth would require starting where they originate in atherosclerotic plaques [6]. Unlike previously reported that CCs are a marker of an advanced atherosclerotic plaque, CCs actually start to form very early in atherosclerosis and have been found within a few weeks after initiating a high cholesterol diet [7]. Furthermore, CCs initiate an inflammatory process early in plaque formation via the NLRP3 inflammasome like uric acid crystals in gout [7], [8]. As CCs form and grow, they occupy a greater volume within the plaque and expand leading to mechanical injury of the fibrous cap that isolates the plaque from the circulating blood [9]. Hence, as expansion of CCs continues within the plaque they can rupture and tear the fibrous cap leading to a plaque rupture with subsequent thrombosis causing a clinical event such as a myocardial infarction or stroke. Evidence for this process is demonstrated by expanding CCs in vitro cutting through fibrous tissue and by a physicochemical study demonstrating the ability of CCs to tear through fibrous tissue [10], [11]. Moreover, postmortem studies performed on patients who died with an acute myocardial infarction had evidence of CCs perforating the fibrous caps at the culprit arterial site while those patients who died of other conditions and had atherosclerotic plaques did not demonstrate the same findings [12]. Also, aspirates of obstructive materials in culprit coronary arteries of patients with acute myocardial infarction had extensive amounts of CCs embedded within the thrombotic material [13]. Finally, optical coherence tomography (OCT) studies in human patients undergoing interventional coronary procedures for myocardial infarction had evidence of CCs in the arterial wall at the site of interest [14]. What has made it possible to appreciate the role of CCs was an innovative method of tissue preparation that avoids use of ethanol thus preserving the CCs making their role and extensiveness visible by scanning electron microscopy. However, dehydrating the tissue in a vacuum or in air instead of ethanol allows CC preservation and this technique has been accepted in microscopic technology reports [15]. Furthermore, using fluorescence confocal microscopy, fresh atherosclerotic plaques without any processing had similar findings of CCs perforating the plaque cap.

Release of CCs into the circulation is not a benign process since CCs traveling down the artery can injure the endothelium of the arterial wall leading to vasoreactive response further worsening ischemia [16]. Once CCs are embedded within the target organ tissue, they trigger an aggressive immune response with invasion by macrophages into that space [17]. The reactive inflammation can then lead to further tissue injury beyond the ischemia. As mentioned above, in the peripheral circulation to the extremities, this leads to livedo reticularis and gangrene. In the kidney arteries CCs emboli can lead to renal failure [18]. If plaque rupture occurs in the carotid arteries, this can lead to central retinal artery occlusion and blindness [5], [19]. CCs emboli to the brain can lead to cognitive impairment and localized stroke [20], [21]. In the heart, emboli of CCs into the distal myocardium can trigger an inflammation with a reduction in myocardial blush by angiography suggestive of small arteriolar obstruction [6]. In diabetic retinopathy CCs contribute to the hemorrhage and injury leading to vision loss [19]. Importantly, presence of CCs may not all be related to emboli but rather local formation of CCs in an organ tissue as noted in the brain matrix [21] (Central Figure).

Plaque rupture releases cholesterol crystals that can enter the circulation and embolize to occlude the capillary bed and trigger local end-organ inflammation. The combination of ischemia and inflammation can cause myocardial injury, induce strokes and cognitive dysfunction [5], renal failure [18], diabetic retinopathy, livedo reticularis of the skin and peripheral vascular disease with limb ischemia. Management of CCs emboli has been controversial and specifically related to anti-coagulation or thrombolysis where some case reports mention worsening of CSE. This was believed to be related to leaving the ruptured plaque exposed with continued release of crystal emboli to the distal circulation [5]. Current recommendations includes use of statins and aspirin to subdue the ongoing plaque rupture process and inflammatory injury. Previous work had demonstrated that both aspirin and statins can dissolve CCs, hence their anti-inflammatory effect [22]. In an elegant study by Komatsu et al., they demonstrated CCs emboli in humans with special angioscopic technique, non-obstructive general angioscopy or NOGA showed elution of CCs released from spontaneously ruptured abdominal aortic ruptured plaques in patients [23]. Some of those CCs were also captured during the study confirming their presence. In their current publication they propose a method of how CCs trigger inflammation [24].

In summary, our understanding of the role of CCs has evolved from an inert particle reported as empty clefts by light microscopy to a very dynamic particle that enhances plaque growth and rupture and then travels to end organs to induce both ischemic and inflammatory injury. The attached articles in this Special Issue highlight these effects of CCs and the Central Figure converges these features together with a ruptured plaque at its core.
