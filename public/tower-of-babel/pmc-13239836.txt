Recent Advances in Thalassemia Research: A Comprehensive Assessment From Diagnostic Technologies to Clinical Treatment

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThalassemia is a common hereditary blood disorder characterized by impaired globin synthesis, leading to anemia and a variety of related complications. With the rapid advances in genomics and molecular biology, remarkable progress has been made in this field in recent years, especially in the in‐depth exploration of genetic mechanisms, the innovation of diagnostic techniques and the continuous update of therapeutic strategies. It is worth noting that with the emergence of new treatment strategies such as gene therapy and stem cell transplantation, offer new hope for patients. Despite significant progress, further research is needed to address the limitations of existing treatment methods and optimize long‐term management for patients.MethodsThis review introduces the epidemiology, pathogenic mechanisms, and related complications of thalassemia.ResultsIt also highlights innovations in the diagnosis of thalassemia (such as TGS and Genomics technologies) and treatment advancements (such as CRISPR/Cas9, HSCT, and Luspatercept) and their limitations, and explores why transformative tools have not been fully utilized in regions with high prevalence of thalassemia.ConclusionsUnlike existing reviews that focus solely on either diagnostic or therapeutic aspects, this article integrates both perspectives with an emphasis on clinical accessibility. It aims to provide sustainable and equitable management guidance for clinicians, researchers, and policymakers involved in thalassemia care.Keywords: gene therapy, genetic mechanisms, globin production disorder, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, thalassemia

Introduction

Thalassemia is a hemolytic anemia caused by genetic mutations or deletions that disrupt erythrocyte globin synthesis. It is mainly classified into alpha thalassemia (α‐thalassemia) and beta thalassemia (β‐thalassemia) [1] and is currently among the five most common birth defects worldwide [2]. The disease is more prevalent in the Mediterranean region, Southeast Asia and Africa. In China, it is particularly common in areas south of the Yangtze River, especially in Guangxi, Guangdong, Yunnan, Sichuan, and Chongqing [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Thalassemia imposes a significant economic burden on affected individuals and their families. Diagnostic advances have evolved from hematological tests and basic genomic technologies to modern approaches, particularly third‐generation sequencing (TGS), which improves the detection of rare genotypes and diagnostic accuracy [8]. Conventional therapeutic strategies remain limited, mainly including regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy to alleviate anemia and prevent iron overload. These treatments rarely achieve a cure and carry significant side effects [9, 10].

Emerging curative options, such as gene therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and gene editing technology, have brought new hope to patients [11, 12]. For example, modifying patients' hematopoietic stem cells using gene editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9 could lead to durable recovery of blood production and symptom alleviation in patients [13, 14]. Based on the epidemiology and pathogenic mechanisms of thalassemia, this review evaluates the implementation bottlenecks of diagnostic innovations (such as TGS, genomic technologies, etc.) and treatment progress (such as CRISPR/Cas9, HSCT, Luspatercept, etc.). It further analyzes the systemic barriers that hinder transformative tools from benefiting patients in high‐incidence areas, providing integrated references for clinical decision‐making, research directions, and health policies.

The Epidemiology of Thalassemia

Studies have reported that the global prevalence of thalassemia is between 1.5%–20% (with α‐thalassemia at 5%–20%, β‐thalassemia at around 1.6%) [10]. The highest prevalence is observed mainly in tropical and subtropical areas, especially in African, Asian, and Mediterranean countries [15]. The global epidemiology of thalassemia exhibits marked geographic heterogeneity, driven by evolutionary selection pressure from malaria, population movements, and medical resources. This has given rise to three distinct epidemiological patterns:

First, regions where malaria was historically prevalent have higher rates of thalassemia carriers [15]. Studies suggest that the genetic mutations associated with thalassemia may be an adaptive response to the selective pressure of malaria [15]. In Asia, taking China as an example, the average carrier rates of α‐thalassemia and β‐thalassemia are 16.77% and 4.7% [16], respectively. The prevalence varies greatly between regions. For example, carrier rates of α‐thalassemia and β‐thalassemia in Hainan Province were 45.04% and 5.11% [16], respectively. The carrying rates of α‐thalassemia and β‐thalassemia in Guangzhou were 12.7% and 4.2% [16], respectively. The epidemiology of thalassemia in Hainan and Guangzhou—once high‐incidence areas for malaria—may be influenced by the adaptive response to selective pressure from malaria. Additionally, the highly developed economy of Guangzhou has made population movement a key factor affecting the prevalence of thalassemia.

Secondly, immigration and consanguineous marriage are also important factors influencing the epidemiology of thalassemia. In Europe, taking Greece and Spain as examples, the combined prevalence of α‐thalassemia and β‐thalassemia in Greece is 27.2 per 100,000 in Greece versus 0.2 per 100,000 in Spain, the prevalence of α‐thalassemia is 0.03 per 100,000 in Spain [17]. In Southeast Asia, taking Thailand and Malaysia as examples, the carrier rate of α‐thalassemia are 30%–40% and 4.5%, respectively [18]. Immigration is an important factor affecting the epidemiology of thalassemia in Europe [19] and Thailand and Malaysia [20] in recent years. Pakistan has a significantly high incidence of consanguineous marriage, reaching 73% [21]. In this region, the prevalence rate of thalassemia among the consanguineous marriage population is over 60% [22], which significantly increases the risk of thalassemia transmission.

In addition, medical infrastructure plays a crucial role in the burden of disease. In Africa, the carrier rate of α‐thalassemia can reach 20%–30% in some specific populations [23]. This situation is tied to the genetic background and environmental factors of the region [24], along with a weak healthcare system and a lack of sufficient genetic testing and screening capabilities, which means many potential thalassemia patients miss out on timely diagnosis and treatment. Furthermore, a lack of public health education and awareness has resulted in a poor understanding of the prevalence and severity of the disease [24].

These patterns underscore the need for region‐specific screening paradigms, including on prenatal programs in endemic areas, ethnicity‐based approaches in migrant‐receiving countries, and more flexible criteria in regions with high population mobility. In addition, it is also necessary to strengthen public health and awareness education, increasing the public's understanding of the severity of diseases.

Molecular Mechanism and Genotype–Phenotype Correlations

The Genotype–Phenotype Spectrum and Clinical Management of α‐Thalassemia

Silent and Mild α‐Thalassemia

Thalassemia is characterized by reduced or absent synthesis of one or more globin peptide chains due to globin gene defects. The α/non‐α chain ratio in hemoglobin is unbalanced, resulting in reduced HbA (α2β2) production and reduced hemoglobin synthesis. At the same time, the relatively excess unpaired globulin chains abnormally precipitate and attach to the erythrocyte membrane, resulting in oxidative damage of the membrane and shortened lifespan of red blood cells (RBC); both factors contribute to the development of anemia [10, 25]. α‐thalassemia exhibits a significant genotype–phenotype gradient, with normal individuals having four functional α‐globin genes (genotype αα/αα). When one gene is deleted or inactivated (e.g., ‐α3.7/αα or ‐α4.2/αα), individuals are silent carriers, typically asymptomatic with normal or minimally abnormal hematological indicesl [26]. The clinical management of such carriers involves genetic counseling, as their hematological changes may be confused with iron deficiency anemia, requiring differentiation through iron metabolism indicators and genetic testing [27]. When two α genes are deleted, whether in cis (e.g., ‐‐SEA/αα) or trans (e.g., ‐α3.7/‐α4.2), it presents as mild α‐thalassemia, characterized by microcytic hypochromic anemia, with hemoglobin (Hb) levels usually mildly decreased (> 90 g/L) [26]. Patients are generally asymptomatic or have mild symptoms [28], but anemia may exacerbate under physiological stress such as pregnancy, requiring individualized assessment. Spouse screening is therefore essential to assess the risk of offspring developing severe α‐thalassemia (e.g., Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis syndrome), which is a core component of genetic counseling and prenatal prevention [29].

HbH Disease: Genotype Heterogeneity and Clinical Manifestation Variability

When three α genes are deleted (e.g., ‐‐SEA/‐α3.7), it leads to a relative excess of β‐globin chains and the formation of unstable Hemoglobin H (HbH), clinically manifested as HbH disease, with anemia severity ranging from mild to moderate (Hb 70–100 g/L). Some patients may require intermittent blood transfusions [29]. Individualized management strategies should be based on genotype and phenotype severity. Most patients are of the intermediate type and do not require regular blood transfusions, but regular monitoring of Hb and ferritin levels is necessary to assess anemia and iron overload [30]. For patients with significant splenomegaly or symptoms of hypersplenism (such as leukopenia and thrombocytopenia), splenectomy may be considered to improve blood cell counts and reduce transfusion needs [31].

Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis Syndrome: Prenatal Diagnosis and Genetic Interruption

When all four α genes are deleted (e.g., ‐‐SEA/‐‐SEA), it leads to the lethal Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis Syndrome, clinically manifested as generalized fetal edema, hepatosplenomegaly, cardiomegaly, and placentomegaly. This condition often results in intrauterine fetal demise or early postnatal death [28]. Clinical decision‐making for this condition thus centers on prevention: identifying high‐risk couples through population carrier screening (i.e., both partners are α0‐thalassemia carriers, such as ‐‐SEA/αα). For confirmed high‐risk couples, fetal cells can be obtained for genetic analysis through chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis. For fetuses diagnosed with Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis Syndrome, couples may choose to terminate the pregnancy based on circumstances [32], which is currently the main means of preventing this lethal syndrome. This series of processes from screening, genetic counseling to prenatal diagnosis and intervention constitutes an effective primary prevention strategy, which is crucial for reducing the disease's birth rate [22].

Genotype–Phenotype Spectrum and Clinical Management of β‐Thalassemia

The clinical manifestation spectrum of β‐thalassemia is extremely broad, ranging from asymptomatic carriers to life‐threatening severe anemia. This heterogeneity mainly arises from the diversity of mutations in the β‐globin gene (HBB) and their interactions with other genetic modifiers. Genotype is the primary determinant of phenotype severity; mutations can be classified into β0 and β+ types based on the extent of impairment in β‐chain synthesis [33]. When these severe mutations exist in homozygous or compound heterozygous forms, such as β0 homozygotes (e.g., CD41‐42/CD41‐42) or β0/β+ compound heterozygotes (e.g., CD41‐42/IVS‐II‐654), they disrupt β‐chain synthesis, usually leading to severe β‐thalassemia. Patients often present with progressively worsening anemia within 6–12 months after birth and must rely on regular blood transfusions to maintain Hb levels (usually required to be > 90–100 g/L) and life, along with regular iron chelation therapy [33]. Certain β+ mutation combinations may retain residual β‐globin synthesis capability, such as some β+/β+ homozygotes (e.g., IVS‐II‐654/IVS‐II‐654) or β+/β0 compound heterozygotes (e.g., IVS‐I‐6/CD39) [33], which may lead to intermediate thalassemia, with Hb levels typically between 70 and 100 g/L [34]. Transfusion needs are not constant, but in stress states such as infection, pregnancy, or accelerated growth, there may be temporary “transfusion demand periods”. Despite fewer transfusion needs, chronic hemolysis and increased intestinal iron absorption due to ineffective erythropoiesis can still lead to progressive iron overload [35]. Therefore, regular monitoring of iron load (through serum ferritin and MRI assessment), systematic screening and management of these unique complications are essential to improve patients' long‐term quality of life and prevent severe complications. A deep understanding of the genotype–phenotype association is the cornerstone for achieving precise clinical stratification and treatment decision‐making.

In China, more than 98% of α‐thalassemia mutations are accounted for by the ‐‐SEA/αα, ‐α3.7/αα, ‐α4.2/αα, αCS/αα, αQS/αα, αWS/αα, while more than 90% of β ‐thalassemia mutations include CD41‐42, IVS‐II‐654, ‐28, CD71‐72, CD17, βE, ‐29, and CD43 [4, 36]. In the Southeast Asian region, α‐thalassemia mutations such as ‐‐SEA/αα are more common [37]. Whereas β‐thalassemia mutations such as CD41‐42 are particularly prevalent [38]. Hereditary Patterns and Clinical Decision‐Making

The genetic pattern of thalassemia is primarily autosomal recessive, meaning that a person must inherit one mutated gene from each parent in order to exhibit symptoms of the disease. Because of this genetic pattern, thalassemia appears to be a distinct familial cluster in certain populations [15]. This clustering not only affects the health of individuals, but also poses a challenge to public health policies and screening strategies, especially the importance of genetic testing and genetic counseling in high‐risk populations [35]. For high‐risk couples who have given birth to children with severe thalassemia or both are carriers of the same type of thalassemia, clinical decision‐making focuses on prenatal intervention. Clinicians need to comprehensively analyzing the genotypes of both partners to provide accurate genetic targets for subsequent prenatal diagnosis [39].

Clinical Manifestations of Thalassemia

Clinical Features of Anemia

Based on clinical characteristics, thalassemia is classified into two main types: non⁃transfusion dependent thalassemia (NTDT) and transfusion dependent thalassemia (TDT) [40]. The core pathophysiological differences between TDT and NTDT stem from the severity of the imbalance of α/β globin chains. This imbalance further leads to distinct differences in ineffective erythropoiesis and hemolysis [41]. Patients with NTDT generally have mild symptoms and usually do not require or only occasionally require blood transfusion, with most able to survive for extended periods; In contrast, patients with TDT have more severe symptoms and require frequent transfusions [42]. Therefore, deepening from “subtype management” to “personalized management” is crucial for fully understanding and addressing the differences among patients.

Related Complications and Treatment Strategies

Due to varying degrees of imbalance in globin chains, patients with TDT and NTDT present significantly different complications and management strategies. Their key complications and management strategies are heterogeneous as follows:

Cardiac and Liver Diseases

Iron overload in TDT patients is mainly caused by long‐term RBC transfusions. When iron storage capacity is saturated, iron deposits in solid organs such as the heart and endocrine glands [43]. Myocardial disease and heart failure caused by cardiac iron overload are major threats to the survival of TDT patients [44, 45]. For TDT patients, in addition to serum ferritin testing, regular assessment of cardiac and liver iron deposition through cardiac and liver MRI T2 imaging is necessary to prevent fatal cardiac complications [46]. In contrast, iron overload in NTDT patients arises from increased intestinal absorption, with the absorbed iron more likely to deposit directly in liver parenchymal cells [47]. Therefore, the risk of liver‐related diseases (such as liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma) is particularly prominent in NTDT patients [48], necessitating closer monitoring of liver iron concentration and fibrosis degree, such as through MRI R2 (for quantifying liver iron concentration) and elastography, while being highly vigilant for the occurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma [46].

Thrombosis and Pulmonary Hypertension

NTDT patients present a hypercoagulable state due to chronic hemolysis and splenic dysfunction [49], with the risk of thrombosis and pulmonary hypertension significantly higher than that of TDT [49]. Given the high thrombotic risk, all NTDT patients should undergo thrombotic risk assessment, and anticoagulant or antiplatelet prophylaxis should be considered in high‐risk situations (such as post‐splenectomy, pregnancy, and surgery). Additionally, regular echocardiographic screening for pulmonary hypertension should be included in the routine follow‐up plan for NTDT patients [50]. Pulmonary hypertension in TDT patients often stems from iron overload‐related heart failure, with the pathophysiological core being myocardial fibrosis caused by myocardial iron deposition and dysfunction in contraction and relaxation [51]. Monitoring should focus more on assessing heart failure caused by iron overload, and active iron chelation therapy is fundamental for preventing and improving the disease.

Bone Diseases

TDT is characterized by craniofacial deformities, cortical thinning of long bones, and osteoporosis due to marrow expansion [52, 53], with a significantly higher prevalence than that in NTDT patients [54]. The core of TDT bone disease management lies in early, adequate, and regular transfusion protocols (maintaining Hb levels at 90–100 g/L) and appropriate chelation therapy, which are crucial for preventing skeletal deformities and endocrine complications (such as gonadal dysfunction, thyroid dysfunction, and parathyroid dysfunction), fundamentally suppressing abnormal marrow proliferation [55]. Early diagnosis and orthodontic treatment help prevent and correct craniofacial deformities [56]. Additionally, treatment for thalassemia‐related osteoporosis includes adequate intake of calcium and vitamin D, sufficient iron chelation, hormone replacement, and primarily inhibiting osteoclast function through bisphosphonates [57]. NTDT patients [52] face dual challenges of osteoporosis and extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH) masses (which can cause spinal cord compression and dysfunction of adjacent organs), with management focusing on regular imaging monitoring, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to assess the progression of EMH masses. For symptomatic or progressive lesions, consideration should be given to radiotherapy or the use of hydroxyurea and other medications to suppress extramedullary hematopoiesis [58]. Notably, the application of new drugs such as erythropoiesis‐stimulating agents (e.g., roxadustat) in NTDT is being explored, but their safety in EMH patients, especially regarding the risk of mass enlargement or new mass formation, still requires cautious evaluation [58]. NTDT patients also need active management of coexisting osteoporosis, similar to TDT patients [58].

Endocrine and Quality of Life

TDT patients experience a high incidence of endocrine disorders such as growth retardation, impaired glucose tolerance, and gonadal and thyroid dysfunction. This is mainly due to early severe iron overload in these patients. Strict and regular iron chelation therapy and timely endocrine replacement therapy starting from childhood (usually at 2–3 years old) [43] are therefore necessary. These interventions can delay or prevent endocrine damage in TDT patients. Endocrine complications in NTDT appear later, but chronic fatigue, facial changes, and decreased exercise tolerance significantly impair their quality of life [59]. While monitoring iron overload progression in NTDT patients, clinicians should strengthen systematic assessment of patients' quality of life and provide psychological support. Endocrine screening should be included in their long‐term follow‐up plan to achieve comprehensive management of physical and mental health [60].

In summary, clinicians must develop targeted monitoring strategies and treatment plans based on a profound understanding of the differences in the complication spectrum between TDT and NTDT patients. This is the key to achieving precise and individualized clinical management for thalassemia patients.

Diagnostic Methods for Thalassemia

Laboratory Screening Techniques

The diagnosis of thalassemia relies primarily on laboratory testing techniques that provide important information about blood composition and RBC characteristics. Common screening techniques include complete blood count (CBC), hemoglobin electrophoresis, and determination of iron metabolism markers [1]. A CBC can help identify the presence of anemia and assess parameters such as erythrocyte size and morphology, with MCV < 80 fL and/or MCH < 27 pg. often used as important thresholds to initiate further specific testing [61]. Simultaneously, serum ferritin levels are tested; if ferritin levels are normal or even elevated alongside low MCV and MCH strongly suggest thalassemia, necessitating progression to the next stage of the diagnostic process [61]. Subsequently, hemoglobin electrophoresis is then used to separate different types of hemoglobin to initially determine if a specific type of thalassemia such as α‐thalassemia or β‐thalassemia is present. An elevated HbA2 level (usually > 3.5%) is a key laboratory indicator for diagnosing β‐thalassemia heterozygotes, while a significant increase in HbF is commonly seen in β‐thalassemia homozygotes or certain deletion‐type α‐thalassemia (such as Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis syndrome) [62]. However, factors like patient age (e.g., newborns), iron deficiency (which may underestimate HbA2), or HbA2 variants can interfere with electrophoresis, requiring molecular testing for confirmation.

Advances in Molecular Diagnosis

Genetic testing is the gold standard for thalassemia diagnosis, and molecular diagnostic techniques have made remarkable progress in recent years. Mutations associated with thalassemia can be quickly identified through gene sequencing technology. The main clinical detection techniques for the deletion of thalassemia genes are Gap‐polymerase chain reaction (Gap‐PCR), Southern blot hybridization, Multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA), and Real‐time PCR. The main detection techniques for mutant thalassemia were PCR‐reverse dot blot (PCR‐RDB), High‐resolution melting analysis (HRM), and Sanger sequencing. Techniques such as PCR probe melting curve analysis (PMCA), and Next generation sequencing (NGS) [63, 64, 65] can detect both deletion and mutant thalassemia simultaneously. Clinically, first‐line tools (Gap‐PCR, Real‐time PCR, PCR‐RDB, and PMCA) screen common variants; rare/complex variants require second‐line methods (MLPA, Sanger sequencing), although accurately identifying complex structural variants remains challenging [66]. NGS has a wide detection range, but still has limitations, and needs to be used in combination with other technologies [67, 68]. With the development of molecular biology and the concept of precision medicine, the emergence of TGS technology has the advantages of high throughput and ultra‐long reading length with accuracy rates as high as 99.8%. This is especially important reference value in complex genomic regions [69, 70] and has broad application prospect in detecting thalassemia genes fields [71]. Genomics Technologies

Genomic technologies play an important role in the screening of thalassemia. Although traditional screening methods are effective, they have some limitations when it comes to accuracy and efficiency. In recent years, new genomic technologies such as microfluidic chip technology have significantly improved thalassemia screening efficiency. For instance, using microfluidic plasma electrophoresis lets us quickly and accurately detect neonatal α‐thalassemia, with high sensitivity and specificity [73]. The total analysis time was reduced from 1110 min per batch (Immobilized pH gradient isoelectric focusing, IPG IEF) and 46 min per batch (liquid chromatography, LC) to 36 min per batch. It achieved 100% sensitivity and 98% specificity in α‐thalassemia screening, providing an efficient, sensitive, and specific tool for newborn screening of α‐thalassemia. Genomic screening methods can also do non‐invasive tests during pregnancy by looking at free DNA in the mother's blood, avoiding the risks and discomfort of traditional methods [72]. Currently, this technology is mainly applied to the inference of fetal genotypes in high‐risk couples (i.e., both partners are carriers of thalassemia). By using the relative mutation dosage (RMD) analysis method, the proportion of mutant and wild‐type alleles in maternal peripheral blood is statistically determined through the sequence probability ratio test (SPRT), enabling noninvasive inference of fetal genotype and thus assessment of disease risk [74]. Additionally, the relative haplotype dosage (RHDO) analysis method can be employed, which uses next‐generation sequencing of cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) from maternal peripheral blood. Haplotypes are constructed using adjacent single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) sites on the same chromosome and undergo quantitative analysis [75], with a success rate of 98.16% and 100% accuracy. Noninvasive prenatal testing for monogenic diseases (NIPT‐M) is valuable for pregnant women avoiding invasive tests [75]. However, clinicians must clearly inform the examinees that the accuracy of these methods are not 100%, and its detection performance is also limited by factors such as fetal DNA concentration and maternal background mutations.

Emerging Treatment Strategies

Current Status and Challenges of Gene Therapy

Gene therapy is a cutting‐edge therapeutic strategy that has shown great potential in the treatment of thalassemia in recent years. It addresses the root cause by repairing or replacing faulty genes. The development of gene editing technologies with zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), transcriptional activator‐like effector nucleases (TALENs), and CRISPR/Cas9 has made precisely targeting and editing genomic DNA possible [76].

The CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology provides a revolutionary strategy for the treatment of thalassemia. Its core principle is the precise cutting of the genome by the Cas9 nuclease guided by specific sgRNA, followed by gene modification through the cell's own DNA repair mechanism [77]. The current application strategies mainly focus on two directions: first, the precise repair of point mutations in the β‐globin gene (HBB), aiming to introduce the correct DNA template through the homology‐directed repair (HDR) mechanism to directly correct common pathogenic mutations such as CD41/42. Research has successfully utilized CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated HDR for repair in patient‐derived hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs), and the repaired cells showed an increased proportion of HBB‐expressing cells in in vitro erythroid differentiation, confirming their long‐term hematopoietic reconstitution ability in mouse transplantation models [78]. However, the efficiency of HDR in non‐dividing cells such as hematopoietic stem cells is low, and there is a risk of introducing unintended genomic changes, which constitutes the current main technical bottleneck [79]. Secondly, reactivating fetal hemoglobin (HbF) expression has become the fastest advancing clinical direction, such as the CTX001 trial, which utilizes non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ) to disrupt the BCL11A binding site in the promoter region of the HBG1/HBG2 genes or directly disrupt the BCL11A gene itself, thereby relieving the transcriptional repression of the γ‐globin gene and inducing HbF synthesis to compensate for the lack of β‐globin [80]. This functional compensation strategy does not directly repair the mutated gene but can effectively improve the anemia phenotype.

Despite the enormous potential of gene editing technologies represented by CRISPR/Cas9 in the treatment of Thalassemia, the path to widespread clinical application still faces many challenges [77]. The first challenge lies in balancing editing efficiency and long‐term efficacy; treatment relies on the reinfusion of edited autologous hematopoietic stem cells after in vitro editing, requiring a sufficient number of long‐term reconstituting stem cells (LT‐HSCs) to be effectively edited to ensure the patient can persistently produce functionally normal RBC. Under the current process, how to maximize editing efficiency while maintaining the viability, proliferative potential, and homing ability of stem cells remains an unresolved technical bottleneck. Secondly, off‐target effects and genomic instability are core issues concerning treatment safety [81]. CRISPR/Cas9 may produce unintended cuts across the entire genome, leading to oncogenic mutations or other functional abnormalities. Although the development of high‐fidelity Cas9 variants, optimization of sgRNA design, and the adoption of whole‐genome off‐target detection technologies (such as CIRCLE‐seq) can significantly reduce risks, completely eliminating off‐target effects remains a challenge, and their potential long‐term risks require decades of follow‐up assessment. Furthermore, immunogenicity and delivery systems are another major barrier to technology translation. The body may generate pre‐existing or treatment‐induced immune responses against the bacterial‐derived Cas9 protein, affecting treatment efficacy or causing adverse reactions [77]. Currently, viral vectors (such as Lentivirus) carry the risk of insertional mutations, while nonviral delivery systems (such as Electroporation and nanoparticles) face issues of low delivery efficiency and cytotoxicity [77]. Finally, accessibility and ethical considerations are social challenges that gene therapy cannot avoid. Existing gene therapy products (such as Zynteglo) are extremely expensive, priced at over one million dollars, severely limiting their accessibility globally, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries where thalassemia is prevalent [82].

Next steps will target efficient, low‐toxicity in vivo delivery systems and long‐term safety tracking. Cost reduction via innovation and policy will also be critical to making gene therapy universally accessible.

Application and Prospect of HSCT

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is currently the only available curative therapy for patients with β‐thalassemia major [83]. The expansion of donor sources is key to addressing the bottleneck of transplantation. Traditionally, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matched sibling donors are the preferred choice, but their availability is limited. With the development of transplantation techniques, non‐related fully matched donors, umbilical cord blood, and haploidentical (related haploidentical) transplantation have become important alternatives [83], and the indications for HSCT are continuously expanding. For example, a study from Turkey showed that the overall survival rate 5 years after HSCT can reach 92.3%, with a thalassemia‐free survival rate of 82.1%. The study also suggested that for patients with matched donors, transplantation before the age of 7 could yield better outcomes [84]. In addition, the successful application of unrelated donor transplantation, including matched unrelated donors and haploidentical donor transplantation, has provided more patients lacking matched related donors with opportunities for cure [85], and the exploration of haploidentical transplantation has made it possible for nearly all β‐thalassemia major patients to have transplantation opportunities [85]. Despite the continuous advancement of HSCT technology, however, the clinical application of HSCT still faces many limitations and complexities. For example, research have shown that patients undergoing systemic HSCT commonly experience complications such as acute and chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), infections, pulmonary complications, and hepatic veno‐occlusive disease (VOD). GVHD is one of the most challenging complications, and its occurrence involves an excessive response of donor T cells to host alloantigens [86]. The onset of acute GVHD begins with tissue damage caused by conditioning, where released inflammation cytokines activate host antigen‐presenting cells, thereby strongly stimulating donor T cell proliferation. Activated donor T cells recognize host major or minor histocompatibility antigens, triggering a cytokine storm and targeting tissues such as the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract, leading to characteristic tissue damage. Chronic GVHD involves a more complex immune dysregulation, including the activation of autoreactive T cells and B cells, as well as the fibrotic process. GVHD is a major treatment‐related cause of death in patients with β‐thalassemia undergoing HSCT [85]. Infections are another major risk; for example, in severely immunosuppressed patients, reactivation of human herpesvirus 6 (HHV‐6) can lead to severe neurological complications such as encephalitis [87]. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is also relatively common after transplantation and is associated with reduced survival rates. Studies have reported that the overall survival was 97.4% in patients without CMV reactivation, while in those with CMV reactivation, it was 80.3% after HSCT [88]. Additionally, transplant‐related organ toxicities are widespread, such as hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS/VOD) [85] and pulmonary complications (e.g., a high incidence of pulmonary embolism reaching 88%) [89].

While HSCT can offer a chance for a cure, its related complications must not be overlooked. Therefore, physicians must carefully assess the patient's overall condition and create a personalized treatment plan before performing HSCT. This is essential to minimize complications and optimize treatment outcomes.

Next efforts will prioritize refining GVHD risk prediction models and developing novel immune tolerance regimens. Optimizing conditioning regimens to reduce organ toxicity and pairing infection control strategies with expanded haploidentical transplantation will also be key. These steps aim to shift HSCT from a high‐risk curative option to a safe, accessible standard therapy—opening cure opportunities for patients without fully matched donors.

Recent Advances in Drug Therapy

In the medical treatment of thalassemia, there have also been some notable advances in recent years. Targeting the core pathophysiological aspects of thalassemia (ineffective hematopoiesis and iron overload), breakthroughs have been achieved in the development of novel targeted drugs. Among them, the drug Luspatercept, which targets transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) superfamily ligands, has demonstrated clear clinical value. As a ligand trap, Luspatercept improves late‐stage erythrocyte maturation disorders by binding and inhibiting ligands such as GDF11, thereby reducing ineffective hematopoiesis [90]. The key study supporting the approval of Luspatercept is the BELIEVE Phase III clinical trial, which focused on adult patients with β‐thalassemia requiring regular blood transfusions. The results showed that 21.4% of patients receiving Luspatercept treatment reached the primary endpoint (a reduction in transfusion burden of ≥ 33% within 13–24 weeks), while only 4.5% of the placebo group achieved this [91]. Additionally, serum ferritin and transferrin saturation levels in the treatment group showed a decreasing trend, indicating a potentially positive impact on iron metabolism [92]. However, its limitations include a response rate that is not 100%, and limited efficacy data for non‐transfusion‐dependent patients, with unclear effects of long‐term use on iron overload [93].

Another class of promising drugs is the pyridoxal metabolism regulator Mitapivat, which is an oral pyruvate kinase‐R (PKR) activator. Its mechanism of action is to activate erythrocyte pyruvate kinase, increasing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and reducing 2,3‐diphosphoglycerate (2,3‐DPG) levels. Preclinical studies have shown that it can improve ineffective hematopoiesis, iron overload, and anemia in Hbbth3/+ mouse models [94]. A Phase II study targeting non‐transfusion‐dependent α and β‐thalassemia demonstrated that Mitapivat has the potential to increase hemoglobin levels and acceptable safety [95]. Its mechanism of action is independent of globin chain synthesis, providing new treatment options for patients with different genotypes [93]. Furthermore, regulating iron metabolism has also become a new strategy to improve ineffective hematopoiesis. For example, the oral ferroportin inhibitor VIT‐2763 regulates abnormal erythropoiesis by limiting the availability of iron [96]. In Hbbth3/+ mouse models, VIT‐2763 used alone or in combination with the iron chelating agent Deferasirox improved anemia and ineffective hematopoiesis without affecting the chelation efficacy of Deferasirox [97].

Subsequent research should focus on optimizing Luspatercept's response‐predictive biological indicators, which can help accurately select beneficiary populations in clinical practice. Expanding Mitapivat trials to transfusion‐dependent patients and larger Phase III cohorts, plus exploring iron metabolism regulator‐iron chelator combinations. These multi‐target interventions aim to comprehensively address ineffective hematopoiesis and deliver personalized therapies for thalassemia patients across genotypes and clinical phenotypes.

Directions for Future Research

Discovery of Novel Biomarkers

In the thalassemia study, novel biomarkers have proven critical for early diagnosis and individualized treatment. They can not only help identify the early stages of the disease, but also monitor disease progression and response to treatment. Studies have shown that specific gene expression patterns and metabolite levels may be associated with thalassemia severity, which provides the basis for the discovery of novel biomarkers. For example, expression levels of miRNA have been found to be strongly correlated with erythropoiesis and iron metabolism, and these miRNA may serve as potential biomarkers for assessing a patient's disease state and predicting therapeutic outcomes [98].

miR‐15a/16‐1 targets the MYB gene, a transcription factor that typically inhibits γ‐globulin synthesis. Studies have demonstrated that elevated miR‐15a/16‐1 levels significantly reduce MYB expression, thereby relieving the inhibition on γ‐globulin synthesis [99]. This mechanism is of great significance for alleviating β‐thalassemia symptoms, as increased γ‐globulin can compensate for deficient β‐chains and improve anemia in patients. However, the clinical application of miR‐15a/16‐1 as a biomarker is limited by the lack of standardized detection methods and the need for prospective validation in diverse patient cohorts [100]. miR‐486‐3p participates in HbF induction by directly targeting BCL11A, a master repressor of γ‐globulin expression [101]. This miRNA shows significant upregulation in patients with β‐thalassemia and correlates positively with HbF levels [102]. Notably, miR‐486‐3p regulates the extra‐long isoform of BCL11A through binding to its 3′UTR, representing a fine‐tuning mechanism for HbF modulation [102]. Despite these promising findings, most studies have been conducted in small cohorts with heterogeneous methodologies, limiting the reproducibility and generalizability of results [100]. miR‐144 presents a paradoxical role in thalassemia pathophysiology. Unlike other miRNAs that directly target HbF repressors, miR‐144 primarily regulates oxidative stress tolerance by targeting NRF2 [103]. High expression of miR‐144 in thalassemia leads to decreased NRF2 activity and reduced antioxidant capacity, exacerbating erythrocyte oxidative damage [103]. This dual function—potential involvement in HbF regulation through indirect mechanisms while primarily affecting red cell survival through oxidative stress pathways—complicates its utility as a standalone biomarker. Furthermore, the biphasic expression pattern observed in different thalassemia subtypes necessitates careful interpretation in clinical contexts [104]. miR‐214 participates in the control of oxidative stress by regulating the ATF4 signaling pathway, affecting erythropoiesis and homeostasis. The biphasic expression of miR‐214 in β‐and α‐thalassemia suggests context‐dependent roles that require further elucidation before clinical application [104]. miR‐19b‐3p regulates SOX6 through a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network involving hsa‐circRNA‐100466, thereby influencing HbF expression. This intricate regulatory axis highlights the complexity of miRNA‐mediated HbF modulation and underscores the limitations of single‐miRNA biomarker approaches [105].

Despite the mechanistic insights gained from preclinical studies, several critical barriers impede the clinical translation of miRNA biomarkers in thalassemia: First, methodological standardization is lacking: the lack of consensus regarding sample collection (serum vs. plasma vs. extracellular vesicles), RNA isolation protocols, and normalization strategies introduces significant technical variation that limits reproducibility across studies [100]. Second, preanalytical variables are not well controlled: factors such as age, sex, hemolysis status, and iron chelation therapy can substantially influence circulating miRNA levels, yet these confounders are often inadequately controlled in current studies [106]. Third, clinical validation is insufficient: most miRNA biomarker studies employ small, single‐center cohorts without independent validation sets, raising concerns about overfitting and generalizability [100].

Exploration of Individualized Medicine

Personalized medicine has great potential in thalassemia treatment. In addition to considering a patient's genetic background, it also integrates comprehensive information such as lifestyle and environmental factors, thereby providing more tailored treatment plans. In thalassemia management, personalized medicine enables more precise targeting of specific genetic mutations, such as using gene editing technology. CRISPR‐Cas9 therapy (CTX001) targeting BCL11A has achieved transfusion independence in excess of 90% of β‐thalassemia patients, representing a functional cure through HbF reactivation [80]. However, this represents a standardized approach rather than patient‐specific mutation correction, which remains investigational. Studies have shown that Prime Editing can accurately modify the genome without causing double‐strand breaks, making gene repair safer when treating genetic diseases and steering clear of unnecessary mutation risks [107], yet it faces clinical barriers including low editing efficiency and lack of human trial data. This technological advancement suggests that truly individualized treatment regimens may be achievable for each patient in the future, significantly enhancing the feasibility of personalized medicine. Therefore, advancing research and clinical translation of these technologies will be crucial for improving thalassemia treatment outcomes. Integrating gene editing technology with personalized medicine is expected to provide more effective treatment options for patients with thalassemia, thereby improving their quality of life and reducing socioeconomic burdens.

Despite those prospects, personalized medicine and gene editing still run into a bunch of challenges in practical application, including ethical concerns, technical feasibility (off‐target effects, genotoxicity risks) [81], and treatment costs (a price of USD 2.8 million per patient) [82]. Next efforts must prioritize cost reduction and equitable access to realize true individualized therapy.

Establishment of International Cooperation and Research Networks

International cooperation is essential for thalassemia research and management. By establishing an international research network, researchers are able to share data, resources, and best practices to accelerate research progress and clinical application. For example, research institutions in multiple countries can collaborate to conduct large clinical trials to verify the effectiveness and safety of new treatments. In addition, international collaboration can facilitate knowledge exchange between different countries and drive the introduction of new technologies and methods that can improve the management of thalassemia worldwide. Future collaborative networks will facilitate data sharing and speed up the translation of research into clinical care.

Conclusions

As a common hereditary blood disease, thalassemia has made remarkable progress in the understanding of its genetic mechanisms and treatment strategies. Traditional treatments, mainly including regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy, aim at controlling anemia and managing iron levels. However, these treatments often come with a range of complications, such as iron overload, which can harm vital organs like the heart and liver and ultimately impact patients' quality of life. Emerging curative options, HSCT and gene therapy, have transformed the therapeutic landscape, yet each faces distinct barriers to clinical implementation.

Although HSCT can cure thalassemia, it is limited by the availability of HLA‐matched donors and complications including GVHD, infections, and organ toxicity. Future research should integrate donor source expansion with personalized complication prophylaxis to lower treatment‐related mortality, thereby advancing HSCT from a high‐risk curative intervention to a safe, widely accessible standard therapy.

Gene therapy has shown unprecedented efficacy, such as: CRISPR‐Cas9 therapy (CTX001) targeting BCL11A has achieved transfusion independence in over 90% of β‐thalassemia patients, representing a functional cure through HbF reactivation, but clinical translation still faces many challenges, such as: (1) safety risks: off‐target effects and genomic instability may lead to carcinogenesis; (2) cost barriers: the cost of each treatment is approximately $2.8 million, with limited accessibility in disease‐endemic areas; (3) technical bottlenecks: ex vivo editing needs to balance efficiency with stem cell viability.

Next priorities should prioritize two core directions: optimizing the safety and efficacy of therapeutic regimens, and exploring novel biomarkers (such as miR‐15a/16‐1, miR‐486‐3p, etc.) and therapeutic targets. Interdisciplinary collaboration should also be further strengthened, uniting expertise in genetics, clinical medicine and basic research to address the key challenges in thalassemia treatment. Moreover, the integration of basic scientific research and clinical practice should be advanced to develop more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies, which will ultimately improve the quality of life and clinical prognosis for patients with thalassemia.
