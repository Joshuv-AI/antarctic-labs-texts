Three lectures on tropical algebra
Jeffrey Giansiracusa, Kevin Kühn, Stefano Mereta, Eduardo Vital

Abstract
. This document is a slightly expanded version of a series of talks given by J. Giansiracusa at the workshop ‘Geometry over semirings’ at Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona in July 2025. In the first lecture we introduce tropical polynomials, ideals, congruences, and how the connection with tropical geometry is made via congruences of bend relations. Tropical geometry and matroid theory are telling us that we should focus attention on a narrow slice of the world of tropical algebra, and this leads to the theory of tropical ideals (as developed by Maclagan and Rincón) and an abundance of interesting open questions. In the second lecture we examine the relationship between Berkovich analytification and tropicalization from the perspective of bend relations, giving a refinement of Payne’s influential limit theorem. In the third lecture we set aside geometry and focus on tropicalization via bend relations as a construction in commutative and non-commutative algebra. Constructions such as symmetric algebras, exterior algebras, matrix algebras, and Clifford algebras can be tropicalized. In the case of exterior algebras, the resulting tropical notion beautifully completes the picture of the Plücker embedding and gives a new perspective on the tropical Plücker relations. For matrix algebras and Clifford algebras, Morita theory becomes an interesting topic.



Introduction

This document is a slightly expanded version of a series of talks given by J. Giansiracusa at the workshop ‘Geometry over semirings’ at Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona in July 2025.

First lecture

The tropical semiring
(\mathbb{R}\cup\infty,\mathrm{min},+)
is an interesting place to do algebra, and it is intimately connected to tropical geometry.
In this talk, I will introduce tropical polynomials, ideals, congruences, and how the connection with tropical geometry is made via congruences of bend relations.
Tropical geometry and matroid theory are telling us that we should focus attention on a narrow slice of the world of tropical algebra.
This leads to the theory of tropical ideals (as developed by Maclagan and Rincón) and an abundance of interesting open questions.
I will try to summarize what we know and what we don’t yet know about tropical ideals and sketch the outlines of the associated notion of tropical schemes that we are beginning to see.

Second lecture

In 2008 Payne proved that the Berkovich analytification of an affine variety is homeomorphic to the category-theoretic limit of all of its tropicalizations. We will explore this phenomenon from the perspective of tropical algebra, bend relations, and universal objects in category theory. We also present our perspective on the linear version of this story.

Tropicalizing a scheme
X
requires a choice of an embedding into a toric variety. The limit of all such embeddings exists as a mild generalisation of a toric embedding, and it can be explicitly described. The tropicalization determined by this embedding has a universal property: it maps to all other tropicalizations. The Berkovich analytification also has this property, and the two are in fact homeomorphic.

If
X=\mathrm{Spec}\>A
, then the Berkovich analytification is the space of valuations on
A
. If one admits valuations taking values in idempotent semirings that are not necessarily totally ordered, then the category of valuations on
A
has an initial object, and the target of this universal valuation is precisely the algebra corresponding to the universal tropicalization.

Third lecture

In this talk we will set aside geometry and focus on tropicalization via bend relations as a construction in commutative and non-commutative algebra.
By starting at the level of tensor algebras, constructions such as symmetric algebras, exterior algebras, matrix algebras, and Clifford algebras can be tropicalized.
In the case of exterior algebras, the resulting tropical notion beautifully completes the picture of the Plücker embedding and gives a new perspective on the tropical Plücker relations.
For matrix algebras and Clifford algebras, Morita theory becomes an interesting topic. I will present some facts and some questions.

1.2.
The tropical semiring

A semiring
S
is a ring where an element may not have an additive inverse. More precisely, a
semiring
is a triple
(S,+,\cdot)
, where
S
is a nonempty set, and
+,\cdot:S\times S\to S
are operations called
addition
and
multiplication
, respectively. We write
r+s
for
+(r,s)
and
rs
for
\cdot(r,s)
. The addition and multiplication satisfy the following four axioms:

(SR1)

The pair
(S,+)
is a commutative monoid with identity
0
;

(SR2)

The pair
(S,\cdot)
is a monoid with identity
1\neq 0
;

(SR3)

For all
r,s,t\in S

r(s+t)=rs+rt\quad\text{ and }\quad(r+s)t=rt+st;

(SR4)

For all
s\in S
, one has
0s=s0=0
.

As for rings, given a semiring
(S,+,\cdot)
, to be short with notation, we write
S
to represent the semiring
(S,+,\cdot)
.

Example 1.2.1
.

As first basic examples we point out that:

•

Any ring
(R,+,\cdot)
is a semiring where every element has an additive inverse;

•

The set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
with usual addition and multiplication is an example of a semiring which is not a ring;

•

The set
\{0,\infty\}
with addition given by the minimum, and multiplication by the classical addition is a semiring.

1.3.
Quotients of semirings

A
congruence

\sim
on a semiring
S
is an equivalence relation on
S
such that the semiring operations of
S
descend to well-defined operations on the quotient
S/\hskip-3.0pt\sim
. It is straightforward to show that an equivalence relation is a congruence if and only if it satisfies the following two conditions (for any
a,b,r,s\in S
):

(CS1)

If
a\sim b
and
r\sim s
, then
a+r\sim b+s
;

(CS2)

If
a\sim b
and
r\sim s
, then
ar\sim bs
.

In other words,
\sim
is a congruence on
S
if and only if

\{(r,s)\in S\times S\mid r\sim s\}

is a subsemiring of
S\times S
. A congruence
\sim
on
S
is
proper
if there exist
a,b\in S
with
a\nsim b
, that is, if
\{(r,s)\in S\times S\mid r\sim s\}
is a proper subsemiring of
S\times S
. As for many algebraic structures, the quotient
S/\hskip-3.0pt\sim
by a proper congruence is naturally endowed with the structure of a semiring. Namely, let
[s]
be the class of
s\in S
in the quotient
S/\hskip-3.0pt\sim
. The addition and multiplication in
S/\hskip-3.0pt\sim
are defined by

[r]+[s]\coloneqq[r+s]\quad\text{ and }\quad[r][s]\coloneqq[rs],

respectively. When the congruence is not proper, then the quotient is a single point and we have violated the part of semiring axiom
(SR2)
requiring
0\neq 1
.

Given a morphism
\varphi:S\to S^{\prime}
of semirings, we define its
kernel congruence
by

\ker(\varphi)\coloneqq\{(r,s)\in S\times S\mid\varphi(r)=\varphi(s)\}.

The image of
\varphi
, defined as
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{\varphi(s)\mid s\in S\}
, is a subsemiring of
S^{\prime}
. Replacing ideals by congruences, analogues of the classical isomorphism theorems for rings hold for semirings; see
[
12
, Prop. 2.4.4]
for details.

The
congruence generated
by a set of pairs
\{(r_{i},s_{i})\}_{i\in\Lambda}
, denoted by
\langle r_{i}\sim s_{i}\rangle_{i\in\Lambda}
, is the smallest congruence such that
r_{i}\sim s_{i}
for all
i\in\Lambda
.
It can be described as the intersection of all congruences containing the relations
r_{i}\sim s_{i}
; this uses the easy-to-check fact that an intersection of congruences is again a congruence.
Alternatively, the congruence generated by a set of pairs can be constructed by first taking the subsemiring of
S\times S
generated by the pairs
(r_{i},s_{i})
, and then taking the symmetrisation and transitive closure by adding
(a,c)
whenever
(a,b)
and
(b,c)
are present.

One might wonder why we need to talk about congruences when we all learn about quotients in algebra in terms of ideals. The answer is that ideals and congruences are entirely equivalent descriptions of quotients of rings, but for semirings there are many more congruences than ideals, and so there are quotients that are not specified by ideals. We now examine this in more detail.

An ideal
I\subset S
determines a congruence generated by the relations
\{f\sim 0\}_{f\in I}
. This gives a map

\{\text{Ideals in $S$}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{I}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\{\text{Congruences on $S$}\}.

Going in the opposite direction, a congruence
\mathcal{C}
determines an ideal
\{f\>\>|f\sim_{\mathcal{C}}0\}
, and so there is a map

\{\text{Congruences on $S$}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{K}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\{\text{Ideals in $S$}\}.

When
S
is a ring, any relation
r\sim s
implies and is implied by the relation
r-s\sim 0
since we can subtract or add
s
on both sides. Hence the above pairs of maps are bijections inverse to one another. In contrast, on a semiring where we do not have additive inverses, the correspondence above can fail. The composition
\mathcal{K}\circ\mathcal{I}
is always the identity (so
\mathcal{I}
is injective and
\mathcal{K}
is surjective), but the reverse composition need not be the identity.

Example 1.3.1
.

On
\mathbb{T}[x,y]
, consider the congruence generated by the relation
x\sim y
. The quotient map
\mathbb{T}[x,y]\to\mathbb{T}[x,y]/(x\sim y)
does not identify any elements with
\infty=0_{\mathbb{T}}
, Hence
\mathcal{K}
sends both this congruence and the trivial congruence to the trivial ideal
(0_{\mathbb{T}})
.

1.4.
Tropical polynomials

Let
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
be the semiring of tropical polynomials in
n
variables, and
f\in\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. We write

f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}

where
c_{\alpha}=\infty
for all but finitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and
X^{\alpha}
denotes the tropical monomial
X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.
Note that, in analogy with the classical case, where
1X^{\alpha}
is written only as
X^{\alpha}
, in the tropical notation
X^{\alpha}=0\odot X^{\alpha}
. A polynomial
f
is an algebraic expression, and it represents a function
\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
given by sending

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\left\{c_{\alpha}+\langle\alpha,x\rangle\right\}

i.e., we are evaluating
f
at the point
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
using the arithmetic operations of
\mathbb{T}
.

Analogously to the classical case, the
degree
of the tropical polynomial
f
is defined as
\deg(f)\coloneqq\max\{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}\mid c_{\alpha}\neq\infty\}
. We say that
f
is
homogeneous
(of degree
d
) if each monomial
c_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}
of
f
with
c_{\alpha}\neq\infty
has degree
d
.
For any pair
f
and
g
of tropical polynomials, we clearly have
\deg(f\odot g)=\deg(f)\odot\deg(g)
. Thus, as in the world of rings, the semiring of tropical polynomials is graded by degree, which is to say that
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
can be written as the (direct) sum

\oplus_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]_{d}

where the
d
piece consists of the set of all homogeneous tropical polynomials of degree
d
. Note that the
\mathbb{T}
-module
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]_{d}
is free of rank
\binom{n+d-1}{d}
.

1.5.
The vanishing locus of a tropical polynomial

We are now ready to revisit the three perspectives on tropical varieties from the beginning of this lecture.

First we consider the tropical world’s version of the zero set of a polynomial. The function

\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
defined by a tropical polynomial attains the value
\infty=0_{\mathbb{T}}
either nowhere or only at the point
(\infty,\ldots,\infty)
if it has no constant term; moreover, a tropical Laurent polynomial defines a function
(\mathbb{T}\smallsetminus\{0\})^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}
that never attains the value
\infty
. Thus the preimage of
\infty
is not the appropriate definition of the variety it determines.

Instead, the widely accepted definition of the variety of a tropical polynomial is given by the set of points satisfying usual min-attained-at-least-twice condition. The following argument for the appropriateness of this definition is due to Mikhalkin. Given
f\in\mathbb{T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\dots,X_{n}^{\pm}]
, we consider its associated function
f\colon\thinspace\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Its graph has the following three properties:

(1)

It is piecewise linear;

(2)

It has slopes in
\mathbb{Z}
;

(3)

It is convex downward.

Functions satisfying these three conditions are said to be
(tropically) regular
.

.

1.6.
Tropical varieties are solutions to equations

We are now ready to come to the fundamental question of this lecture. We saw that, in passing from the classical to the tropical world, the definition of a variety as zero locus should be replaced with the definition as tropical vanishing locus. Our second perspective for classical varieties was as the solution set to equations
f=0
.

Given a tropical polynomial
f
, what is the system of equations whose solution set is the tropical hypersurface
V^{\textup{trop}}(f)
?

Note that a system of equations in a semiring
S
is the same thing as a congruence on
S
.

The answer to the above question is not unique! This should not be surprising to anyone who has studied scheme theory and is familiar with non-reduced structures; distinct schemes over a field
K
can have the same
K
-valued points. For instance, the affine schemes
\Spec(K[X]/\langle X\rangle)
and
\Spec(K[X]/\langle X^{2}\rangle)
both have a single
K
point but they are most definitely not isomorphic as their structure sheaves are different.

There is a
maximal choice
. Given a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can consider the set of equations
f=g
for all pairs of tropical polynomials satisfying
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in X
. When
X
is a sufficiently nice subset (a large class that includes polyhedral complexes such as tropical varieties), the solution set for this set of equations recovers
X
. This maximal choice loses information and corresponds to taking the reduced scheme structure, which in the affine case means passing from an ideal to its radical.

There are many other possible choices, but we would like one that depends in some canonical way on the tropical polynomials
f
.

Let
f=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}c_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}
be a Laurent polynomial in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\dots,X_{n}^{\pm}]
. The
support
of
f
, denoted by
\textup{Supp}(f)
, is the set of
\alpha
such that
c_{\alpha}\neq\infty
. That is,

\textup{Supp}(f)\coloneqq\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid c_{\alpha}\neq\infty\}.

Given an exponent vector
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we define
f_{\widehat{\beta}}
as the (Laurent) polynomial obtained by deleting the
\beta
-monomial
c_{\beta}\odot X^{\beta}
from
f
. In symbols,

f_{\widehat{\beta}}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}.

Observe that if
\beta\notin\textup{Supp}(f)
, then
f_{\widehat{\beta}}=f
.

Definition 1.6.1
.

Let
f=\bigoplus_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}
be a Laurent polynomial.
The
bend relations
of
f
are the equations

\left\{f\sim f_{\widehat{\beta}}\mid\beta\in\textup{Supp}(f)\right\},

and the
bend congruence
of
f
denoted
\mathcal{B}(f)
, is the congruence generated by the bend relations of
f
.

Example 1.6.2
.

Let
f(X,Y)=X\oplus Y\oplus 0
. The bend relations of
f
are

\{X\oplus Y\oplus 0\sim X\oplus Y,\;X\oplus Y\oplus 0\sim X\oplus 0,\;X\oplus Y\oplus 0\sim Y\oplus 0\}.

Proposition 1.6.3
.

The solution set to the bend relations of a tropical polynomial (or tropical Laurent polynomial)
f
is precisely the tropical hypersurface
V^{\textup{trop}}(f)
.

Proof.

Consider a point
p\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
. The value of
f
at
p
is the minimum of the value of each linear term at
p
. The valued of
f_{\widehat{\beta}}
at
p
is the minimum of all the terms other than the
\beta
term. These two are equal if and only if the minimum is not attained solely by the
\beta
term. We thus have the equality
f(p)=f_{\widehat{\beta}}(p)
for all
\beta
if and only if the minimum is not attained solely by any single term, which is to say that
f
tropically vanishes at
p
.
∎

The above proposition begins to justify our assertion that the bend relations of
f
are the appropriate answer to the question of what equations define a tropical variety.

Adapting our third perspective on varieties to the tropical setting now proceeds easily: the set of solutions to the bend relations of
f
is precisely the set of
\mathbb{T}
-algebra homomorphisms
\mathbb{T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{n}^{\pm}]\to\mathbb{T}
that descend to the quotient by the congruence
\mathcal{B}(f)
.

Note that
\mathcal{B}(f)
determines
f
up to multiplication by a scalar.
Indeed, let
f
be a homogeneous tropical polynomial of degree
d
, that is
f
is in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]_{d}\cong\mathbb{T}^{N}
, where

N=\binom{n+d-1}{d}
. Now, note that

\Hom_{\mathbb{T}\text{-mod}}\hskip-3.0pt\Big(\hskip-2.0pt\big(\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]/\mathcal{B}(f)\big)_{d},\mathbb{T}\Big)=\big\{w\in\mathbb{T}^{N}\mid w\perp f\big\}=f^{\perp},

where
\langle w,f\rangle\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\odot f_{i}
, and by definition

f^{\perp}\coloneqq\big\{w\in\mathbb{T}^{N}\mid\min_{i}\{w_{i}+f_{i}\}\text{ is attained at least twice}\big\}.

Finally, a standard fact from tropical linear algebra gives us that
(f^{\perp})^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}(f)
.

1.7.
Tropicalization

There are many different constructions called tropicalization to be found in the literature. In these lectures we will focus only on the following ones:

(1)

Tropicalizing polynomials by valuating their coefficients;

(2)

Tropicalizing varieties by either valuating the coordinates of their points or by tropicalizing their defining polynomials;

(3)

Tropicalizing linear spaces as a special case of varieties.

A
valuation
on a field
K
is a map
\nu\colon\thinspace K\to\mathbb{T}
such that, for all
a,b\in K
it satisfies the following three axioms:

(V1)

\nu^{-1}(\infty)=\{0\}
;

(V2)

\nu(ab)=\nu(a)\odot\nu(b)
;

(V3)

\nu(a+b)\geq\nu(a)\oplus\nu(b)
.

Let
\nu
be a valuation. By Axiom
(V2)
, it follows that
\nu(a)=\nu(1a)
is equal to
\nu(1)\odot\nu(a)=\nu(1)+\nu(a)
for all
a\in K
. This implies that
\nu(1)=1_{\mathbb{T}}=0
. Moreover,
0=\nu(1)=\nu\big((-1)^{2}\big)=2\nu(-1)
, i.e.
\nu(-1)=1_{\mathbb{T}}=0
. Therefore
\nu(-a)=\nu(a)
for each
a\in K
. For an element
a\in K^{*}
, we have
0=\nu(aa^{-1})=\nu(a)+\nu(a^{-1})
, i.e.
\nu(a^{-1})=-\nu(a)
.
Furthermore, if
\nu
is a valuation, then for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
the map
\lambda\nu
(ordinary multiplication) is also a valuation. The
trivial valuation
is the map
\mu\colon\thinspace K\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
defined as
\mu(t)=1_{\mathbb{T}}=0
if
t\in K^{*}
and
\mu(0)=0_{\mathbb{T}}=\infty
. For the readers interested in learning more about valuations, we refer to
[
25
]
.

Example 1.7.1

(
p
-adic valuation)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
be prime. The
p
-adic valuation
on the rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
is the valuation
\nu_{p}\colon\thinspace\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{T}
defined by:

\nu_{p}(r)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\ell,&\text{if }r=p^{\ell}\displaystyle\frac{a}{b}\quad\text{ and }\quad p\nmid ab,\\[-11.0pt]
\\
\infty,&\textup{ if }r=0,\end{cases}

where
a
and
b
are integers. For example, if
p=3
, then
\nu_{3}(5/6)=-1
and
\nu_{3}(27/7)=3
.

Example 1.7.2

(Puiseux series)
.

Let
K\{\hskip-3.0pt\{t\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
be the field of
Puiseux series
with coefficients in the field
K
. An element
s(t)
of
K\{\hskip-3.0pt\{t\}\hskip-3.0pt\}
is a formal power series

s(t)=s_{1}t^{k_{1}/n}+s_{2}t^{k_{2}/n}+s_{3}t^{k_{3}/n}+\cdots,

where each
s_{i}\in K
,
n
is a non-null natural number, and
k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots
are integers. We define a valuation
\tau\colon\thinspace K\{\hskip-3.0pt\{t\}\hskip-3.0pt\}\to\mathbb{T}
by
\tau\big(s(t)\big)=\min\{k_{i}/n\mid s_{i}\neq 0\}
if
s(t)
is in
K\{\hskip-3.0pt\{t\}\hskip-3.0pt\}^{*}
and
\tau(0)=\infty
.

Let

\nu:K\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
be a valuation, and
F=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}
a polynomial in
K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
. We apply the valuation
\nu
on the coefficients of
F
to obtain a tropical polynomial
\textup{trop}(F)
in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, i.e.

\textup{trop}(F)\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\alpha}\nu(c_{\alpha})\odot X^{\alpha}.

If
J\subseteq K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
is an ideal, then its
tropicalization
is defined as

\textup{trop}(J)\coloneqq\langle\textup{trop}(g)\mid g\in J\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}].

If the ideal
J
is homogeneous, then
\textup{trop}(J)
is a homogeneous ideal as well. Note that, to obtain the tropicalization of an ideal, we need to tropicalize all polynomials
g\in J
, not just a generating set. For instance, if
J
is finitely generated, say
J=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}\rangle
, then
\langle\textup{trop}(g_{1}),\dots,\textup{trop}(g_{\ell})\rangle\subseteq\textup{trop}(J)
, but a proper inclusion can occur.

Example 1.7.3
.

Consider
g=X+1\in K[X]
. Then
\textup{trop}(g)=X\oplus 0
. Since cancellations cannot happen over
\mathbb{T}
, given any
h\in\langle\textup{trop}(g)\rangle
, if the leading monomial term of
h
is proportional to
X^{n}
then
X^{n-1}
must also be present. However,
(X-1)g=X^{2}-1
, and so the tropicalization of the ideal
\langle g\rangle
contains
X^{2}\oplus 0
, which doesn’t satisfy the above condition and hence is not in
\langle\textup{trop}(g)\rangle
.

Consider an ideal
J\subseteq K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
over an algebraically closed field
K
equipped with a valuation. For simplicity, let us assume that the valuation is surjective to
\mathbb{T}
(this can always be arranged by passing to a valued field extension). The ideal
J
defines a variety
V(J)\subset K^{n}
. The tropicalization of the set
V(J)
can be defined as

\textup{trop}\big(V(J)\big)\coloneqq\left\{\big(v(p_{1}),\ldots,v(p_{n})\big)\in T^{n}\>\>|\>\>(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in V(J)\right\}.

However, thanks to the Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry, this tropicalization can alternatively be described as the intersection of all tropical hypersurfaces associated with the tropicalizations of polynomials in
J
:

\textup{trop}\big(V(J)\big)=\bigcap_{f\in\textup{trop}(J)}V^{\textup{trop}}(f)\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{n}.

We have seen that a tropical hypersurface
V^{\textup{trop}}(f)
can be described as the solution set to the system of equations given by the bend relations of
f
, and so
\textup{trop}\big(V(J)\big)
is the solution set to the bend relations of
\textup{trop}(J)
. Following Grothendieck’s insight in developing scheme theory, a system of equations contains more information than its solution set over a particular coefficient (semi)field, and so we should consider the systems of equations as geometric objects in their own right. This motivates a definition:

Definition 1.7.4
.

The
scheme-theoretic tropicalization
of an affine scheme
\Spec K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/J
embedded in
\mathbb{A}_{K}^{n}
is the object whose algebra of regular functions is

\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/\mathcal{B}\big(\textup{trop}(J)\big).

Ideals in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1}\ldots,X_{n}]
of the form
\operatorname{trop}(J)
have some nice properties that can be abstracted to the notion of
tropical ideals
. To explain this, we first need a short digression to recall some basic facts about tropical linear spaces.

1.8.
Tropical linear spaces

Let
E
be a (finite) set, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
\binom{E}{r}\coloneqq\{V\in 2^{E}\mid\#V=r\}
. A
valuated matroid
on the ground set
E
and rank
r
is a pair
M=(E,\mu)
where
\mu:\binom{E}{r}\to\mathbb{T}
is a non-trivial map, i.e.
\mu(B)\neq\infty
for some
B\in\binom{E}{r}
, satisfying the following axiom:

(VM)

For every
B,C\in\binom{E}{r}
, and
b\in B\setminus C
there exist
c\in C\setminus B
such that

\mu(B)+\mu(C)\geq\mu\big((B\cup\{c\})\setminus\{b\}\big)+\mu\big((C\cup\{b\})\setminus\{c\}\big).

Dress and Wenzel were the first to define and work with valuated matroids in their seminal work
[
8
]
.

The
valuated basis exchange axiom

(VM)
is equivalent to the
Plücker relations
; see, for instance
[
2
]
and
[
19
]
.
A set
B\in\binom{E}{r}
is called a
basis
of the valuated matroid
M
if
\mu(B)\neq 0_{\mathbb{T}}
. Let
N=(E,\mathcal{B})
be a (classical) matroid with bases
\mathcal{B}
and rank
r
. Define
\mu_{N}:\binom{E}{r}\to\mathbb{T}
as
\mu_{N}(B)=1_{\mathbb{T}}
if
B\in\mathcal{B}
, and
\mu_{N}(B)=0_{\mathbb{T}}
otherwise. Then,
M_{N}=(E,\mu)
is a valuated matroid.
In this sense, a valuated matroid is a generalization of a matroid. Note that if
(E,\mu)
is a valuated matroid, then
(E,a\mu)
is also a valuated matroid, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Furthermore, for any real number
b
, it follows that
(E,\mu+b)
is a valuated matroid as well.

Example 1.8.1

(Uniform matroid)
.

Let
E=[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and let
d\leq n
be a natural number. Define
\mu:\binom{E}{d}\to\mathbb{T}
as a constant function, for example, say
\mu(B)=1_{\mathbb{T}}
for each
B\in\binom{E}{d}
. It is immediate to see that
\mu
satisfies axiom
(VM)
. Thus, the pair
(E,\mu)
is a valuated matroid. For a more specific and geometric description, see
[
10
, Example. 2.1]
.

A linear space
V\subset K^{n}
of dimension
d
is determined by its Plücker coordinate vector
P\in K^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
. Applying the valuation to each component of
P
yields a vector
\textup{trop}(P)\in\mathbb{T}^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
that can be shown to satisfy Axiom
(VM)
. Valuated matroids arising in this way are said to be
realisable
over the valued field
K
.

The
Dressian

\mathit{Dr}(n,d)\subset\mathbb{P}\mathbb{T}^{\binom{[n]}{[d]}}
is the space of all valuated matroids up to tropical scaling. For any valued field
K
, there is the subspace of all valuated matroids that are realizable over
K
, and this is called the
tropical Grassmannian

\mathit{Gr}^{\operatorname{trop}}(n,d)
. See, for instance,
[
27
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
18
]
.

A valuated matroid
p\in\mathbb{T}^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
determines a submodule
L(p)\subset\mathbb{T}^{n}
, called its associated
tropical linear space
, as follows. For each subset
A\in\binom{[n]}{d-1}
we have the vector
\alpha_{A}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
given by

(\alpha_{A})_{i}=p_{A\cup\{i\}};

this is called the
valuated co-circuit vector
associated with
A
. The tropical linear space
L(p)
is the
\mathbb{T}
-submodule of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
spanned by the set of all valuated co-circuit vectors. It can be shown out that
L(p)
determines
p
up to tropical multiplication by a scalar. Hence we can talk about realizable and non-realizable tropical linear spaces just as we do for valuated matroids. One can also construct
L(p)
as the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces associated with the
valuated circuit vectors

\beta_{B}=\bigoplus_{i\in B}p_{B\smallsetminus\{i\}}\odot X_{i}\in\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]

for each
B\in\mathbb{T}^{\binom{[n]}{d+1}}
.

A foundational fact about linear spaces and their tropicalizations is that, if we start with a linear space
V\subset K^{n}
, then the tropicalization of
V
as a variety coincides with the tropical linear space
L\big(\operatorname{trop}(P)\big)
determined by the tropicalization of
P
; see

[
23
, Prop. 4.4.4]
. This can be thought of as a linear version of the Fundamental Theorem.
For further details on tropical linear spaces and valuated matroids, we refer the reader to
[
26
]
,
[
23
, § 5.5]
, and
[
11
]
.

1.9.
Tropical ideals

We are now ready to return to the task of examining and generalizing the ideals of
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
of the form
\operatorname{trop}(J)
.

Given an ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, and
d\geq 0
we denote by
I_{\leq d}
the subset of
I
consisting of polynomials with degree at most
d
.

Definition 1.9.1
.

A
tropical ideal

I\subseteq\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
is an ideal such that
I_{\leq d}
is a tropical linear space for each
d\geq 0
.

Equivalently, a tropical ideal
I
is an ideal that satisfies the
vector elimination axiom
of a valuated matroid: For any
f=\bigoplus_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}
and
g=\bigoplus_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}
in
I_{\leq d}
such that
f_{\beta}=g_{\beta}\neq\infty
, there exist a polynomial

h=\bigoplus_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}\odot X^{\alpha}\in I_{\leq d}\quad\text{ with }\quad\begin{cases}h_{\beta}=\infty\\
h_{\alpha}\geq f_{\alpha}\oplus g_{\alpha}\textup{ for all }\alpha,\textup{ with equality whenever }f_{\alpha}\neq g_{\alpha}\end{cases}

(see
[
20
, Def. 1.1]
).

Given an ideal
J\subseteq K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, its tropicalization
\textup{trop}(J)
is a tropical ideal; see
[
20
, pg. 641]
. On the other hand, there exist
non-realizable
tropical ideals, i.e., tropical ideals that are not of the form
\textup{trop}(J)
; see
[
20
, Example 2.8]
and
[
9
, Theorem A]
.

Tropical ideals have many interesting properties that make them either very nice to work with or frustrating, depending on your angle. Here we present a few of them.

General ideals in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
might not be finitely generated, and the class of all ideals does not satisfy the ascending chain condition.
For tropical ideals, the question of finite generation is even worse: they are almost never finitely generated as ideals. The tropicalization of the ideal
\langle X-1\rangle
demonstrates this, where for each
d
we see that
X^{d}\oplus 0
is present but is not in the ideal generated by elements in strictly lower degrees. Worse still, a tropical ideal need not be determined by any of its truncations to a finite degree, as shown in
[
20
, Example 3.10]
. This poses an important set of question:

Question 1.9.2
.

How can we construct non-realizable tropical ideals using a finite amount of information?

Question 1.9.3
.

How can we work with tropical ideals (beyond the realizable case) in computer algebra systems?

On the other hand, tropical ideals
satisfy the ascending chain condition; i.e., there is no infinite ascending chain of tropical ideals

J_{1}\subsetneq J_{2}\subsetneq J_{3}\subsetneq\cdots

in
\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
; see
[
20
, Thm. 3.11]
, where this was proven by developing a Gröbner theory for tropical ideals.

Given a tropical ideal
I
, it has a Hilbert function defined using ranks of valuated matroids (or dimensions of tropical linear spaces) in place of dimensions of linear spaces), and it follows by an argument using the ACC that the Hilbert function eventually agrees with an integer-coefficient polynomial function
[
20
, Thm. 3.8]
. Moreover, tropicalization of homogeneous ideals preserves the Hilbert function; i.e., the Hilbert functions of an ideal
J\subset K[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
and the tropical ideal
\operatorname{trop}(J)
are identical.

For a tropical ideal
I
, the associated tropical variety
V^{\textup{trop}}(I)
is the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces
V^{\textup{trop}}(f)
for
f\in I
. A striking theorem (
[
22
, Thm. 6.6]
) says that
V^{\textup{trop}}(I)
is a balanced weighted polyhedral complex.

One might be tempted to declare that the right definition of ‘tropical variety’ is anything coming from a tropical ideal. However, the results of
[
7
]
should at least give one pause. They show that there exist tropical linear spaces
L\subset\mathbb{T}^{n}
that are not the variety of any tropical ideal. Indeed, let
V_{8}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2,3}
be the Vámos matroid and a uniform matroid respectively. Then the direct sum
V_{8}\oplus\mathcal{U}_{2,3}
gives us such an example.

The Vámos matroid is non-realizable, and hence so is the sum
V_{8}\oplus\mathcal{U}_{2,3}
. Hence one can ask the following.

Question 1.9.4
.

Is there a tropical ideal
I
such that
V^{\textup{trop}}(I)
is a non-realizable tropical linear space?

A tropical ideal
I
and its associated bend congruence
\mathcal{B}(I)
are equivalent packagings of the same information, and the set of homomorphisms

\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/\mathcal{B}(I)\to\mathbb{T}

is precisely the tropical variety
V^{\textup{trop}}(I)
. Hence we should consider
the algebra

\mathbb{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/\mathcal{B}(I)

as the algebra of functions of the tropical
scheme
. With an appropriate notion of
\Spec
for
\mathbb{T}
-algebras, we can consider these to be the local affine models for constructing general non-affine
tropical schemes
. A fascinating challenge that we will not discuss here is:

Question 1.9.5
.

What is the appropriate notion of gluing these affine pieces together?

2.
Second lecture

In this lecture we will explore the relationship between the scheme-theoretic perspective on tropicalization via bend relations and Berkovich’s analytic spaces.

2.1.
The story for linear spaces

Let
K
be a field,
v\colon\thinspace K\to\mathbb{T}
valuation, and
|a|\coloneqq e^{-v(a)}
the corresponding norm. For an
n
-dimensional
K
-vector space
V
, a
K
-semivaluation
on
V
is a map
w\colon\thinspace V\to\mathbb{T}
such that
w(a+b)\geq\min\{w(a),w(b)\}
and
w(\lambda a)=v(\lambda)\odot w(a)
.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

Let
a,b\in V
with
w(a)\neq w(b)
. Then
w(a+b)=\min\{w(a),w(b)\}
.

In particular, we observe that for any triple
a,b,c
satisfying
a+b+c=0
we have that the minimum of
w(a),w(b)
, and
w(c)
is attained at least twice. Thus,
K
-semivaluations are solutions to certain bend relations. In
[
4
, § 7]
it was shown, that these are the points of a certain tropicalization. In the following, we will sketch this idea.

Given a vector space
V
, let us consider the rather larger vector space
\widehat{V}
with basis given by the underlying set of
V
. I.e.,
\widehat{V}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{a\in V}K
, and we write the basis as
\{x_{a}\mid a\in V\}
. There is a canonical projection map

\pi\colon\thinspace\widehat{V}\to V

defined by sending
x_{a}\mapsto a
, and hence there is a linear isomorphism
V\cong\widehat{V}/\ker(\pi)
.
We will be tropicalizing this presentation of
V
. This might appear counter to the way we usually tropicalize subspaces of a fixed space (a torus of affine space). However, we will take the scheme-theoretic dual perspective here. The large vector space
\widehat{V}
is the space of linear functions on an ambient space (analogous to the polynomial algebra
K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
), and
V
is the space of linear functions on a sub-object
Z
(analogous to
K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/J
); the map
\pi
identifies functions that agree on
Z
. The tropicalization of this geometric picture is given by the
\mathbb{T}
-module

\textup{trop}_{\pi}(V)\coloneqq\left(\bigoplus_{a\in V}\mathbb{T}\right)/\mathcal{B}\big(\operatorname{trop}(\ker(\pi))\big).

Understanding the structure of the tropicalization clearly requires a description of the kernel of
\pi
.

Proposition 2.1.2
.

The kernel of
\pi
is spanned by the elements:

•

x_{a}+x_{b}+x_{c}
for
a+b+c=0
, and

•

x_{\lambda a}-v(\lambda)x_{a}
for
\lambda\in K,a\in V
.

The kernel of
\pi
is a linear subspace of
\widehat{V}
(albeit infinite dimensional if
K
is an infinite field), and so the tropicalization of
\ker(\pi)
is a tropical linear space in
\widehat{V}
(corresponding to a valuated matroid on ground set
V
). In general, tropicalizing a spanning set for a linear space

does not yield a generating set for the tropical linear space
\textup{trop}(L)
, and the bend relations of a spanning set need not generate the bend congruence of
\textup{trop}(L)
. However, in the case of the spanning set Proposition
2.1.2
, this is true.

Proposition 2.1.3
.

The congruence
\mathpzc{B}\big(\textup{trop}(\ker(\pi))\big)
is generated by:

•

\mathpzc{B}(x_{a}\oplus x_{b}\oplus x_{c})
for
a+b+c=0
, and

•

x_{\lambda a}\sim v(\lambda)x_{a}
for
\lambda\in K,a\in V
.

Proof.

The argument is by elementary algebraic manipulations analogous to
[
14
, Prop. 3.4.3]

∎

Corollary 2.1.4
.

The points of
\textup{trop}(V)
, i.e., morphisms
\textup{trop}(V)\to\mathbb{T}
, are exactly the
K
-semivaluations on
V
. Hence, the space of semivaluations on
V
is a tropical linear space.

Proof.

A
\mathbb{T}
-valued point

sends each vector
x_{a}
to a tropical number

. The relations
x_{\lambda a}\sim v(\lambda)\odot x_{a}
encodes the condition that

is compatible with the valuation

on
K
, and the relations
\mathpzc{B}(x_{a}\oplus x_{b}\oplus x_{c})
precisely encode the condition that

satisfies the ultra-metric triangle inequality.
∎

We note that the valuated matroid whose associated tropical linear space is the space of semivaluations has ground set

, which is infinite in general. In
[

it was shown, that this tropical linear space is the limit of all finite tropicalizations of

Consider a finite set

\mathbb{T}
-module

\textup{trop}_{q}(V)\coloneqq\mathbb{T}^{E}/\mathcal{B}\big(\operatorname{trop}(\ker(q))\big),

and the
\mathbb{T}

\textup{trop}(Z)\subset\mathbb{T}^{E}
. These tropicalizations are functorial in the sense that a diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{E_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{E_{2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{1}}

Theorem 2.1.5
.

The colimit of all tropicalizations of finite presentations

\pi\colon\thinspace\widehat{V}\to V
.

Proof.

Given a presentation

It is clearly surjective since for any

Third lecture

In this lecture we will begin to explore what happens when we try to tropicalize objects defined by quotients of algebras of non-commutative polynomials. This is mostly a report on work that was done by Beth Zhou and Luca Chadwick when they were undergraduates at Durham.

3.1.
Non-commutative tropicalization

Recall that given a cancellative monoid
M
, the process of tropicalization associates to the monoid algebra
K[M]
the tropical monoid algebra
\mathbb{T}[M]
. Given an ideal
I\subseteq K[M]
, valuating the coefficients yields a tropical ideal
\operatorname{trop}(I)\subset\mathbb{T}[M]
(this requires that
M
is cancellative). The quotient algebra
K[M]/I
is associated on the tropical side with the quotient
\mathbb{T}[M]/\mathpzc{B}\big(\operatorname{trop}(I)\big)
.

Here is an important observation: the monoid
M
does not need to be a commutative! In the extreme non-commutative case, we can consider the free associative monoid of words on
n
letters
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, where the product is given by concatenation of words and the unit is the empty word. This free monoid is denoted
F_{n}
. The associated monoid algebra
K[F_{n}]
is traditionally written as
K\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
; this is algebra of noncommutative polynomials over
K
.
Notice that

K\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{\infty}(K^{n})^{\otimes d};

i.e., the noncommutative polynomial algebra is the same as the tensor algebra
T(K^{n})
.

Given a
K
-algebra
A
, a choice of generating set is equivalent to a surjective homomorphism
\pi\colon\thinspace K\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle\twoheadrightarrow A
. The kernel of
\pi
is a two-sided ideal of
K\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
, and its tropicalization
\operatorname{trop}(\ker(\pi))
is a two-sided ideal of the tropical noncommutative polynomial algebra
\mathbb{T}\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
and a tropical linear space — i.e., it is a 2-sided tropical ideal. Thus, in this non-commutative setting we can define the tropicalization of
A
with respect to
\pi
as
\mathbb{T}\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle/\mathpzc{B}\big(\operatorname{trop}(\ker(\pi))\big)
, in complete analogy with what we did in the commutative setting.

.

Fix a valued field

. Then:

(2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K\langle x_{i,j}\mid i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\rangle}

3.2.
The classical Plücker embedding story

One of the many very useful things that exterior algebras do is provide a framework for describing the Plücker embedding of the Grassmannian

\mathit{Gr}(n,d)
into projective space

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge^{d}K^{n}\right)
. We first recall how this works, before moving on to the tropical analogue of this story in the next section.

Given a
d
-dimensional subspace
Q\subseteq K^{n}
, we can decompose
K^{n}
as
Q\oplus Q^{\perp}
and by projecting we obtain a surjection
K^{n}\rightarrow Q
, thus a surjection

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bigwedge}^{d}K^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bigwedge}^{d}Q.}

As
Q
has dimension
d
, the target is 1-dimensional and we obtain a point
p_{Q}\in\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{d}K^{n})
. Here
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{d}K^{n})
denotes to the space of
1
-dimensional quotients of
\bigwedge^{d}K^{n}
(rather than 1-dimensional subspaces).

One can recover
Q
from the point
p_{Q}
as follows. Consider the map

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\wedge p_{Q}\colon\thinspace K^{n}={\bigwedge}^{1}K^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\bigwedge}^{d+1}K^{n};}

it can be shown that
\ker(-\wedge p_{Q})=Q^{\perp}
, and so
\ker(-\wedge p_{Q})^{\perp}=Q
.

In general, given a vector
p\in\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{d}K^{n})
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a subspace
Q
such that
p=p_{Q}
;

(2)

p
satisfies the Plücker relations;

(3)

\textup{dim}\big(\ker(-\wedge p)\big)=n-d
;

(4)

\textup{rank}(-\wedge p)=d
.

3.3.
The tropical Plücker embedding story

Since
\bigwedge^{d}_{\operatorname{trop}}\mathbb{T}^{n}
is a free module with basis given by the size
d
subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, one can think of valuated matroids (in their tropical Plücker vector formulation) of rank
d
as living here.

Given a valuated matroid
p
, the corresponding tropical linear space
L_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{n}
is

\displaystyle L_{p}:

\displaystyle=\textup{span}(\{\text{valuated cocircuit vectors of $p$}\})

\displaystyle=\{\text{valuated circuit vectors of $p$}\}^{\perp}

i.e., the valuated circuits of
p
are the linear equations defining
L_{p}
; each valuated circuit in fact is a linear form
\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
, and points of
L_{p}
are those points
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
at which each of the linear forms above tropically vanishes. In the classical story, subspaces and quotients are interchageable thanks to orthogonal projection. In the tropical world, subspaces and quotients have somewhat different characters.

In other words, using a notation recalling that of the classical case, define

Q_{p}\coloneqq\mathbb{T}^{n}/\mathpzc{B}(\{\text{circuits of $p$}\})=\mathbb{T}^{n}/\mathpzc{B}(L_{p}^{\perp}),

where
L_{p}^{\perp}
is the tropical linear space dual to
L_{p}
. Note that
L_{p}=Q_{p}^{\vee}
.

Conjecture 3.3.1
.

L_{p}^{\vee}=Q_{p}
.

Notice that the definition of
Q_{p}
makes sense even for vectors
p\in\bigwedge^{d}_{\operatorname{trop}}\mathbb{T}^{n}
that do not satisfy the tropical Plücker relations since we can still define the circuit vectors via the recipe

\bigoplus_{i}p_{A\smallsetminus\{i\}}\odot x_{i}

for
A\subset[n]
of size
d+1
.

Proposition

tells us how tropicalize the exteior algebra of a quotient

by giving a recipe in terms of the tropicalization of

. This strongly suggests a natural generalization of tropical exterior algebra to non-realizable quotient modules.

.

.

(3)

If
p
satisfies the tropical Plücker relations, then the associated tropical linear space
L_{p}
is equal to the set

\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\mid\text{each component of $-\wedge p$ tropically vanishes at $x$}\}.

(4)

The isomorphism
\bigwedge_{\operatorname{trop}}^{d}\mathbb{T}^{n}\cong\bigwedge_{\operatorname{trop}}^{n-d}\mathbb{T}^{n}
implements duality of (valuated) matroids.

Remark 3.3.4
.

Notice that in general one can define the circuits of any vector
p\in\bigwedge^{d}_{\operatorname{trop}}\mathbb{T}^{n}
as the components of the map as in part
(2)
of
Theorem 3.3.3
above, even when
p
does not satisfy the tropical Plücker relations.

3.4.
Clifford algebras

Let
V
be a vector space over
K
, and
q\colon\thinspace V\rightarrow K
a quadratic form.

Definition 3.4.1
.

The Clifford algebra
Cl(V,q)
is the
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-graded algebra

Cl(V,q)\coloneqq T(V)/\big(u\otimes u=q(u)\big),

where
T(V)
denotes the tensor algebra on
V
.
Over
\mathbb{C}
all non-degenerate quadratic forms are equivalent, so for
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
q=\textup{I}_{n}\in\textup{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
, we denote
Cl(\mathbb{C}^{n},q)
as
C_{n}
.

By explicit computations one can easily show the following results.

Proposition 3.4.2
.

The following isomorphisms hold:

(1)

C_{0}\cong\mathbb{C}
;

(2)

C_{2}\cong\textup{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
;

(3)

C_{m}\otimes C_{n}\cong C_{n+m}
;

(4)

C_{n}
is Morita equivalent to
C_{n+2}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 3.4.3
.

Bott periodicity is a deep and foundational theorem in homotopy theory. It says that the space
\mathbb{Z}\times BU
representing complex
K
-theory is a 2-
periodic
infinite loop space. This means that if we apply the based loop space functor to it twice then we get the original space back. One of the classic proofs of Bott Periodicity
[
1
]
begins with the periodicity of complex Clifford algebras.

Definition 3.4.4
.

Let
C_{n}^{\operatorname{trop}}
be the tropicalization of the presentation
T(\mathbb{C}^{n})\twoheadrightarrow C_{n}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 3.4.5
.

For tropicalized Clifford algebras, we have:

(1)

C_{m}^{\operatorname{trop}}\otimes C_{n}^{\operatorname{trop}}\cong C_{m+n}^{\operatorname{trop}}
.

(2)

C_{2}^{\operatorname{trop}}
is not isomorphic to
\textup{Mat}_{2\times 2}(\mathbb{T})
. The following isomorphism instead holds:

C_{2}^{\operatorname{trop}}\cong\mathbb{T}[x,y]/\{x^{2}=0,y^{2}=0\}

i.e.,
C_{2}^{\operatorname{trop}}
is isomorphic to the tropicalization of
\textup{Mat}_{2\times 2}(K)
with respect to a different presentation.

Question 3.4.6
.

Is
C_{n+2}^{\operatorname{trop}}
Morita equivalent to
C_{n}^{\operatorname{trop}}
?

A motivation for the above question comes from a notorious conjecture of MacPherson. He asked if the space of oriented matroids is homotopy equivalent to the real Grassmannian. A somewhat weaker question is:

Question 3.4.7
.

Do the spaces of oriented matroids exhibit a form of Bott periodicity?
