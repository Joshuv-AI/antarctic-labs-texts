Multiple-Timescale Solutions to the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) Epidemic Model Equations in the Case of High Basic Reproduction Number
Oleg B. Shiryaev

Abstract
A class of multiple-timescale asymptotic solutions to the equations of the susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model is presented for the case of high basic reproduction number, with the inverse of the latter employed as the expansion parameter. High values of the basic reproduction number, a coefficient defined as the ratio of the infection and recovery rates built into the SIR model equations, are associated with escalating epidemics. Combinations of fast and slow timescales in the suggested multiple-timescale solutions prove adequate to reflect the acknowledged epidemic paradigm, which is characterized by the concatenation of a sharp outbreak with a subsequent protracted plateau. Explicit solutions for the numbers of the infected, susceptible, and recovered compartments of the SIR model are derived via the asymptotic treatment, and the epidemic peak timing and magnitude are assessed on this basis. The asymptotic results agree seamlessly with numerical simulations based on the SIR model.



I
Introduction

The objective of the present inquiry is to develop explicit asymptotic solutions to the equations of the susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) epidemic model
in the case of high basic reproduction number.
Originally enacted in
OriginalSIRPaper
,
SIR model continues to serve the purpose of laconic description
of epidemic processes. In its framework, the population covered by infectious disease is described as a conglomerate of dynamically evolving compartments
of the susceptible, infected, and permanently immune recovered. Coefficients
governing the transitions between the compartments - the infection
and recovery rates - tend to remain steady over considerable periods
of time. The ratio between the two quantities is identified
in the context of the SIR model as the basic reproduction number,
its high values signaling a steep rise of the epidemic (in contrast, the epidemic fades if the basic reproduction
rate is less than unity). Estimates of the basic reproduction number vary widely in practice, but consensus converges on the order
of several as applied to the past COVID epidemic
YingLiu
.

The SIR model equations are routinely solved numerically. Analytical, readily usable solutions for high reproduction numbers are suggested in what follows. Such solutions should facilitate the application of the SIR and related models in epidemiological studies and generally clarify significant mathematical aspects of the epidemic dynamics.
The SIR model is reformulated below in terms of time
normalized to the inverse of the infection rate, with the resulting
equations involving only one coefficient, a
dimensionless small parameter which coincides with the inverse of the basic reproduction rate. As the next step, solutions to the problem are generated in the form of multiple-timescale asymptotic series (the technique is exemplified e.g. in
Nayfeh
; theory pertinent specifically to the type of problem explored is spelled out in
Tikhonov
). The asymptotic expressions combine fast and slow terms, the former corresponding to the epidemic peak and the latter
- to a protracted plateau. Thereby, the character of the multiple-timescale
solutions constructed via the asymptotic algorithm
evokes the well-known pattern of the COVID epidemic.

It should be noted that multiple upgrades of the SIR model are available.
In particular, enhancements are achieved by adding compartments to
the original epidemic model. Reader can find an extensive review on the subject in
HethcoteReview
. At the same
time, the potential for improving prediction accuracy by the incorporation of
additional compartments into the epidemic model is called into
question in a number of studies. According to
Roda
, the SIR
model outperforms the more extensive SEIR (susceptible, exposed, infected,
recovered) one in dealing with the dataset acquired
in Wuhan under the containment regime. An
argument in favor of the SIR
model over the SEIR one specifically with regard to COVID is that,
according to prevalent evidence,
individuals are capable of spreading the disease over the entire time of staying infected,
including the latency period
ChongYouEstradaRef19
. As another update of the basic model, SIR simulations with time-dependent
transition rates are carried out
WangpingEstradaRef18
. The
SIR and related models are also generalized to be implemented on networks
which emerge due to various venues of population mobility
WenjungMeiChongYouEstradaRef16
.
A thorough survey of the available epidemic models, along with
broader coverage of the topic of epidemic-related simulations, are given in
EstradaReview
.

While the approaches outlined above grow in sophistication, the SIR
model remains a fairly accurate instrument in succinct
modeling of the epidemic dynamic
ChongYouEstradaRef19
;
DArienzoEstradaRef17
;
WangpingEstradaRef18
.
An implicit analytical solution to the SIR model equations is presented in
Harko
.
The asymptotic solutions to the SIR equations established in the following have explicit form, agree closely
with numerical results, and are suitable for
direct application in simulations or, alternatively, for inferring the SIR model transition rates from
empirical data via regression.

II
SIR Model and Normalization

The SIR model comprises the following equations

\frac{di}{dt}=\beta\,s\,i-\gamma\,i,\quad\frac{ds}{dt}=-\beta\,s\,i,\quad\frac{dr}{dt}=\gamma\,u,

(1)

where
i
,
s
, and
r
are the time-dependent sizes of the infected,
susceptible, and recovered fractions of the population, with
\beta

and
\gamma
standing for the infection and recovery rates.

Eqs. (
1
)
are bundled together by the obvious conservation law
i(t)+s(t)+r(t)=N
,
where
N
denotes the total population. The necessary
initial values
i(0)
and
s(0)
are assumed to be empirically available.

The model can be cast in normalized form by introducing a dimensionless
variable representing time rescaled by the inverse of the infection rate and along with the related functions depending on it, so that

\tau=\beta\,t,\>i(t)=u(\tau),\>s(t)=\sigma(\tau),\>r(t)=\rho(\tau).

(2)

Eqs. (
1
) translate into

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(\tau)-\sigma(\tau)\,u(\tau)+\epsilon\,u(\tau)=0

,

(3)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(\tau)+\sigma(\tau)\,u(\tau)=0

,

(4)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}(\tau)-\epsilon\,u(\tau)=0

,

(5)

\displaystyle\epsilon=\gamma/\beta

,

(6)

the latter defining a dimensionless coefficient which may be identified as the inverse of the basic reproduction number. The quantity

\epsilon
becomes a small parameter in the case where the basic
reproduction number is high so that a spike in the number of infected
is underway. Asymptotics of the solutions to
Eqs. (
3
)–(
5
)
in this small parameter are developed below.

Adding up the first and second of the above equations leads to a linear
equation

\sigma^{\prime}(\tau)+u^{\prime}(\tau)+\epsilon\,u(\tau)=0

(7)

to replace Eq. (
3
). The set comprising
Eqs. (
4
) and (
7
)
is treated here as an equivalent of the SIR model embodied in
Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
).

The conservation law for the functions introduced is

u(\tau)+\sigma(\tau)+\varrho(\tau)=N.

(8)

The above equation effectively ensures the decoupling of
Eq. (
5
)
from the original set, considering that
\varrho(\tau)
can be found directly
from it.

III
Multiple-Timescale Asymptotic Method

III.1
Asymptotic Solutions to SIR Equations

In line with the general asymptotic approach spelled out e.g. in
Nayfeh
,
solutions to
Eqs. (
3
)–(
5
)
are developed in the form of multiple-timescale asymptotic
series

\displaystyle u(\tau)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)+\epsilon\left(u_{1}(\tau)+\varLambda u_{1}(\psi)\right)+\ldots\,,

\displaystyle\sigma(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)+\epsilon\left(\sigma_{1}(\tau)+\varLambda\sigma_{1}(\psi)\right)+\ldots\,,

\displaystyle\rho(\tau)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda\rho_{0}(\psi)+\epsilon\left(\rho_{1}(\tau)+\varLambda\rho_{1}(\psi)\right)+\ldots\,,

where terms simultaneously depend on two variables,
\tau
and

\psi=\epsilon\,\tau.

(9)

The variables
\tau
and
\psi
, related via a scaling parameter, may be conventionally described
as fast and slow times. The above series are substituted into
Eqs. (
4
) and (
7
),
and the equations following from Eq. (
4
)
have the form

m=m_{0}+\epsilon\,m_{1}+\ldots,\quad n=n_{0}+\epsilon\,n_{1}+\ldots,

(10)

where the ellipses denote subsequent orders in
\epsilon
and

\begin{split}m_{0}&=u_{0}(\tau)\,\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)+\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+u_{0}(\tau)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)\,\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)+\\
&+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\tau),\quad n_{0}=u_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)\,.\end{split}

(11)

Prior to splitting the equations into asymptotic orders in
\epsilon
,
those should be segregated into groups of terms depending on either

\tau
or
\psi
. To this end, it should be taken into account that

\begin{split}u_{0}(\tau)\,\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)&+\sigma_{0}(\tau)\,\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)=\varLambda\sigma_{0}(0)\,u_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda u_{0}(0)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\\
&+\epsilon\,\tau\,\left(\varLambda u_{0}^{\prime}(0)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(0)\,u_{0}(\tau)\right)+\ldots\,.\end{split}

Therefore, the first two terms in Eq. (
11
)
actually contribute to both the zeroth and the first orders in
\epsilon
.
As a further step, the equations thus recompiled are split into orders
in the small parameter. The resulting set of asymptotic equations is

\displaystyle\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)\,\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)

\displaystyle=0,

(12)

\displaystyle\varLambda\sigma_{0}(0)\,u_{0}(\tau)+u_{0}(\tau)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda u_{0}(0)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)

\displaystyle=0,

(13)

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\varLambda u_{0}^{\prime}(\psi)+\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)+\varLambda\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\psi)

\displaystyle=0.

(15)

Eq. (
12
) implies that

\varLambda\sigma_{0}(\psi)=0,

(16)

and Eq. (
14
) integrates to

u_{0}(\tau)=-\sigma_{0}(\tau)
,
the integration constant being set to zero in the above as this can
be achieved by manipulating the initial conditions.
Eq. (
13
)
becomes

\varLambda u(0)\,\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(\tau)-\sigma_{0}(\tau)^{2}=0

and yields the solution

\sigma_{0}(\tau)=\frac{\varLambda u_{0}(0)\,\sigma_{0}(0)}{\sigma_{0}(0)+\left(\varLambda u_{0}(0)-\sigma_{0}(0)\right)\exp\left(\varLambda u_{0}(0)\,\tau)\right)}

(17)

Taking into account Eq. (
16
), one
finds
from Eq. (
15
)
that
\varLambda u_{0}^{\prime}(\psi)+\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)=0,
and,
therefore, that

\varLambda u_{0}(\psi)=\varLambda u(0)\exp(-\psi).

(18)

Overall, the solution to
Eqs. (
3
)–(
5
)
to the lowest order in the small parameter
\epsilon
are

\displaystyle\sigma(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\ldots\,,

(19)

\displaystyle u(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{0}(\tau)+\varLambda u(0)\exp(-\psi)+\ldots\,,

(20)

where

\sigma_{0}(\tau)

is given by Eq. (
17
).
Accordingly,

\rho(\tau)=N-\varLambda u(0)\exp(-\psi)

due to the conservation law given by Eq. (
8
).
The latter demonstrates that, to the lowest order, the recovered compartment
remains exempt from the fast dependence on time over the epidemic process.

It should be noted in the context of
Eqs. (
17
)–(
20
) that the initial values
\sigma_{0}(0)

and
\varLambda u(0)
are to be related to the initial conditions for the
original problem
(
3
)–(
5
)
via the relations
\varLambda u(0)-\sigma_{0}(0)=i(0)
and
\sigma_{0}(0)=s(0)
.

Overall, the asymptotic solutions to the normalized set of
Eqs. (
3
)–(
5
)
to the lowest order in
\epsilon
translate into explicit
formulae for the dependence of the compartments of the susceptible,
infected, and recovered on time under the conditions of an epidemic with
high reproduction number. To the lowest order in
\epsilon
,
the sizes of the compartments vs time
are found to be

\displaystyle u(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{s(0)\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)}{s(0)+i(0)\exp\left(\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)\tau\right)}+\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)\exp(-\psi)+\ldots\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\sigma(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{s(0)\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)}{s(0)+i(0)\exp\left(\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)\tau\right)}+\ldots\,,

(22)

\displaystyle r(\tau)

\displaystyle=N-\left(i(0)+s(0)\right)\exp(-\psi)+\ldots\,.

(23)

Notably, the susceptible compartment is described by a fast-evolving function as the lowest-order solution for it depends only on
\tau
.
In contrast, the recovered compartment depends only on
\psi
, thus varying on the slow timescale.
At the same time, the solution for the infected compartment exhibits multiple-timescale behavior, depending on both
\tau
and
\psi
.
Its first term is responsible for the intense rise of the epidemic over the initial phase, and the second - for the subsequent lasting plateau. As shown below, the peak of the epidemic is shaped by the interplay of the two terms.

III.2
Magnitude and Timing of the Epidemic Peak

The extremum condition for the infected compartment obtained from
Eqs. (
17
)–(
20
)
is

\frac{s(0)i(0)\left(s(0)+i(0)\right)\exp\left(\left(s(0)+i(0)\right)\tau_{\max}\right)}{\left(s(0)+i(0)\exp\left(\left(s(0)+i(0)\right)\tau_{\max}\right)\right)^{2}}=\epsilon,

the timing of the peak based on the above condition being

\tau_{\max}=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{s(0)^{2}+s(0)\,i(0)}{\epsilon\,i(0)}\right)}{s(0)+i(0)}-\frac{2\,\epsilon}{\left(s(0)+i(0)\right)^{2}}+\ldots\,.

(24)

The peak approximately expressed to the lowest order is
found to be

u_{\max}=u\left(\tau_{\max}\right)=s(0)+i(0)-\epsilon\left(\ln\left(\frac{s(0)\left(s(0)+i(0)\right)}{\epsilon\,i(0)}\right)+1\right)+\ldots\,.

(25)

IV
Conclusions

Multiple-timescale asymptotic solutions to the equations of the SIR epidemic model are established in the present study for the case of high basic reproduction number, with the inverse of the latter playing the role of a small parameter for the expansion. Explicit expressions for the compartments of susceptible, infected, and recovered vs time are derived.

The classical SIR theory continues to be widely employed in the modeling of the epidemic dynamics.
Within the SIR perspective, the affected population is split into three dynamically interconnected compartments - the susceptible who are vulnerable to the disease, the currently infected, and the recovered who are assumed to retain immunity. The basic reproduction number, which may be defined in the context of the SIR model as the ratio of the transmission and recovery rates
(both built into the model), plays the role of a switch between the epidemic scenarios as its values less than unity imply a muted epidemic regime, while its high magnitudes are indicative of intense unfolding of the process.

The suggested approximate solutions to the SIR model equations are developed in the form of series with terms simultaneously depending on the fast and slow variables equal to time normalized to the inverse of either the transmission or the recovery rate. The duality reflects the fact that the solutions represent a concatenation of a fast-evolving outbreak which peaks early in the course of the epidemic and a protracted plateau. The solutions describing the susceptible, infected, and recovered compartments are resolved as compact, directly usable expressions. The asymptotic results agree thoroughly with the numerical solutions for the SIR model.

The explicit approximate solutions to the SIR model equations are used above to calculate analytically the timing and height of the epidemic peak vs the initial conditions for the three-compartment system.
Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) can be applied to forecast the parameters of the workload to which the healthcare system will be exposed when the epidemic culminates.
It should be noted as well that the relatively compact analytical solutions obtained above for the numbers of the susceptible, infected, and recovered may serve to simplify inferring the rates of transitions between the compartments from empirical data, the rates being instrumental in predictive simulations of epidemic processes.
