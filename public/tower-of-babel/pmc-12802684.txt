Giant Coronary Artery Aneurysm as a Rare Cause of Premature Ventricular Contractions
Authors: Yuko Matsui, Satoshi Higuchi, Kensuke Kikuchi, Shonosuke Watanabe, Kunihiro Kani, Shun Hasegawa, Daigo Yagishita, Morio Shoda, Hiroshi Niinami, Junichi Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) rarely cause arrhythmias.Case SummaryA 64-year-old woman presented with palpitations. Electrocardiography revealed frequent monomorphic premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), with a rightward inferior axis and early transition in the precordial leads. Computed tomography identified a giant CAA in the proximal left anterior descending artery, adjacent to the left coronary cusp and the right ventricular outflow tract, with a fistulous connection to the pulmonary artery. Initial endocardial catheter ablation at the right ventricular outflow tract and left coronary cusp was ineffective. Surgical intervention including CAA resection, fistula closure, and epicardial cryoablation resulted in complete elimination of the PVCs.DiscussionGiant CAAs, although rare, may serve as arrhythmogenic substrates for PVCs. Surgical intervention may be required for successful elimination of arrhythmias when catheter ablation fails.Take-Home MessagesThis case demonstrates that giant CAAs may serve as arrhythmogenic substrates for PVCs. When catheter ablation fails, surgical management may provide a solution.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old woman had been experiencing frequent palpitations and skipped beats for approximately 1 year. Flecainide was initially prescribed for the palpitations, with minimal improvement. Her blood pressure and pulse rate were within normal limits, and physical examination revealed no significant findings. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (Figure 1A) demonstrated sinus rhythm without significant ST-segment changes. Frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) were noted, with a QRS duration of 157 ms, a rightward inferior axis, early transition, and a maximum deflection index1 of 0.44, all consistent with an epicardial left ventricular outflow tract origin. Past Medical History

The patient had a history of colonic diverticulitis and an incidental finding of a coronary artery aneurysm (CAA) with a maximum short diameter of 16 mm on a computed tomography (CT) scan performed 7 years ago. She chose to undergo watchful observation without further intervention. Her family history was unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient's symptoms, including palpitations and a sensation of skipped beats, were suspected to be caused by PVCs. However, given her history of CAAs, a thorough evaluation of the aneurysms was deemed necessary.

Investigations

CT imaging showed no significant coronary artery stenosis but revealed a CAA with a maximum short diameter of 23 mm and calcification in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD), adjacent to the left coronary cusp (LCC) and the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), along with a fistula connecting to the pulmonary artery (PA) (Figure 2A). Angiographic findings were consistent with the CT findings, confirming a large CAA originating from the proximal LAD and a fistula connecting to the PA (Figure 2B). Injection of contrast agent into the left coronary artery triggered frequent PVC bigeminy (Figure 1B). Oxygen saturation levels were measured sequentially in the right atrium, right ventricle, main PA, left PA, and aorta. The calculated Qp/Qs ratio was 1.14. Laboratory results showed a mildly elevated B-type natriuretic peptide level of 26 pg/mL (normal: <18.4 pg/mL), with no electrolyte imbalances or thyroid dysfunction. Management (Medical/Interventions)

Endocardial catheter ablation using electroanatomic mapping (CARTO 3, Biosense Webster) was initially performed to target the PVCs. Intracardiac echocardiography (CARTO-Sound, Biosense Webster) confirmed the presence of the CAA adjacent to the LCC and the RVOT (Figure 3A). The earliest activation site of the PVC, recorded by bipolar electrodes, was identified at the LCC, with an activation 34 ms ahead of the QRS onset. Two months after the catheter ablation, the patient underwent surgical coronary aneurysmectomy and cryoablation. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established with arterial return via the ascending aorta and venous drainage through both the superior and inferior vena cava. Myocardial protection was achieved using antegrade coronary perfusion. Intraoperative findings revealed a CAA adjacent to the RVOT, consistent with preoperative imaging (Figure 4A). The opening of the fistula between the main PA and the CAA was identified (black arrows, Figures 4B and 4C) and was subsequently sutured and ligated. The aneurysm originating from the LAD was carefully dissected, and its anterior wall was incised. The fistulous opening from the LAD (black arrow, Figure 4D) was identified, sutured, and securely sealed. Finally, cryoablation was applied to the base of the aneurysm using the CryoICE system (AtriCure) (Figure 4E). Surgical pathology showed no significant atherosclerosis within the CAA but revealed myxoid degeneration of the media, adventitial fibrosis, and mild lymphocytic infiltration.Figure 4Intraoperative Findings(A) Intraoperative view showing a CAA adjacent to the RVOT. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful, with satisfactory blood flow in the LAD confirmed on both CT and coronary angiography (Figures 5A and 5B). Remarkably, the PVCs with a rightward inferior axis were completely resolved after surgery, and no recurrence was observed 1 month postoperatively.Figure 5Postprocedure Imaging(A) Computed tomography imaging after surgical coronary aneurysmectomy. Discussion

Giant CAAs as a cause of arrhythmias are rare, yet our case highlights the relationship between the location of a CAA and the origin of PVCs demonstrated using electroanatomic three-dimensional mapping. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing CAAs as a potential, albeit uncommon, cause of PVCs.

A CAA is defined as a dilation exceeding 1.5 times the diameter of the adjacent segment, with a reported prevalence of 0.2% to 4.9%.2, 3, 4 Giant CAAs, which are defined as those measuring ≥20 mm or 4 times the diameter of the adjacent segment, are rare, with an incidence of 0.02%.5,6 Although CAAs are often asymptomatic, they can lead to severe complications such as thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and rupture, which may result in life-threatening conditions such as cardiac tamponade. Reports of arrhythmias associated with CAAs are limited, with some cases describing giant CAAs compressing the right atrium, causing atrial arrhythmias,7,8 while others report ventricular arrhythmias arising from RVOT obstruction.9,10 The underlying mechanisms of arrhythmias in these cases are thought to include mechanical compression from the epicardial side, or myocardial ischemia due to a steal phenomenon caused by the CAA or coronary artery fistula.

In our case, electroanatomic three-dimensional mapping identified the earliest activation site of the PVCs at the contact site of the CAA, suggesting that mechanical stimulation of the myocardium by the aneurysm played a pivotal role in arrhythmogenesis. Additionally, the exacerbation of PVCs after coronary contrast injection suggests that the steal phenomenon may have contributed to the arrhythmia. A previous case described ventricular tachycardia that was suppressed by endocardial catheter ablation at the RVOT, where low-voltage areas were found adjacent to a CAA and coronary artery fistula. That patient, with a long-standing CAA, likely developed the low-voltage area because of chronic compression by the aneurysm. In contrast, our case did not show significant low-voltage areas at the site of contact with the CAA, which may be attributed to the aneurysm's size and the disease duration, suggesting that electrical remodeling may be more pronounced with larger or more long-standing aneurysms.

Management strategies for CAAs are variable, including conservative medical therapy, catheter-based interventions, and surgical resection.11 However, there are no universally accepted treatment guidelines. In the current case, surgical intervention was deemed necessary given the increase in CAA size from 16 to 23 mm over 7 years, the presence of a coexisting fistula, and the likely contribution of both mechanical compression and the steal phenomenon to the arrhythmia. A limitation in this report is that it is uncertain which procedure was more effective for eliminating the PVCs during the surgical intervention, coronary aneurysmectomy or cryoablation. However, it is technically simple to perform cryoablation in addition to the resection of the CAA during the operation, and considering the possible cause of the PVC, the strategy of adding surgical ablation may have increased the effectiveness.

Conclusions

A giant CAA can be a rare but potential cause of ventricular arrhythmia. This case suggests that surgical resection of a CAA may be a viable treatment option for arrhythmias arising from regions adjacent to the aneurysm, especially in patients who do not respond to conventional catheter ablation.
