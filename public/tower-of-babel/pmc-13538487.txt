Reference quality of OpenEvidence across five medical specialties
Authors: Tamir Lotan, Mahmud Omar, Yiftach Barash, Jonathan B. Kruskal, Muneeb Ahmed, Olga R. Brook, Eyal Klang, Alon Gorenshtein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OpenEvidence (OE) is a retrieval-augmented clinical AI tool. We evaluated the references OE returned to 150 standardized prompts across five specialties, analyzing all 4979 citations. No reference was confirmed as fabricated; three abstracts had attribution errors. Most references were recent and high-impact, though journal concentration varied by specialty. This study assessed reference existence and characteristics, not whether references support OE’s clinical claims - a benchmark for evaluating clinical AI tools.

OpenEvidence (OE) is a RAG-based clinical AI tool used in clinical medical information retrieval1,2. It draws on licensed content from the JAMA Network, Cochrane, NCCN, and other specialty sources. General-purpose LLMs are known to generate non-existent references at high rates; estimates suggest 30-69% of LLM-generated biomedical references are fabricated3,4. A separate audit of 2.5 million published papers found fabrication rates rising 12-fold between 2023 and early 20265. It is important to distinguish two forms of fabrication: references invented by the AI itself, which do not exist, and fabricated references embedded within genuinely published papers. OE’s retrieval architecture addresses the former by drawing only from real, indexed sources, but it does not protect against the latter, since a retrieved paper may itself contain fabricated citations within its own reference list. Prior studies have evaluated OE’s response accuracy6 and clinical usefulness1,2. A recent independent benchmark found that frontier general-purpose LLMs outperformed OE and other clinical AI tools on medical knowledge and clinician alignment, but like earlier evaluations did not assess the quality of the references these tools retrieve7. To date, no study has characterized the quality, composition, or reliability of the references OE returns.

Clinicians using OE in daily practice may rely on AI-generated responses as is, given their clinical time constraints and the fact that these responses tend to be well referenced. Without evaluation, it is unclear what types of sources populate these reference lists, how current they are, or whether source quality differs across specialties. Using the same 150 standardized, published, and validated prompts developed by Omar et al.8 for their evaluation of GPT-4 and Google Gemini Pro, we analyzed 4979 OE-generated references across five specialties. Our analysis evaluates whether references exist and characterizes their quality and source profile; we do not assess whether the retrieved references support the clinical claims OE makes within its responses.

Methods

Study design and prompts

This was a cross-sectional observational study requiring no patient data or institutional review board approval. All 150 prompts, spanning five specialties (Oncology, Cardiology, Rheumatology, Psychiatry, Infectious Diseases; 30 prompts each), were copied verbatim from Omar et al. and pasted directly into the OE search box. The inline reference list from each response was then extracted for analysis.

Data collection

All prompts were submitted to OE by a single investigator (T.L.) between March and April 2026, using an OE account registered under a medical-student profile. OE may personalize responses by user profile; our prompts were therefore entered under a single profile. For each prompt, T.L. submitted the query, reviewed the OE response, and extracted every inline citation into a standardized classification spreadsheet (title, first author, journal, publication year, source type, journal tier).

A small minority of responses returned malformed reference lists (placeholder or scaffold text, or content cut off mid-sentence). When this occurred, the prompt was resubmitted, and only responses returning a complete reference list were analyzed; the malformed response was discarded.

Reference classification

References were classified by source type (PubMed-indexed, Cochrane, guideline, regulatory, conference abstract, or supplementary/other) and journal tier. Flagship journals were defined a priori as journals with broad international reach and highest citation impact across all biomedical disciplines, specifically the New England Journal of Medicine, The Lancet, JAMA, BMJ, Nature, Science, Cell, and their named sub-journals. All other journals were assigned to first quartile (Q1), second quartile (Q2), or third quartile (Q3) per SCImago Journal Rank 2024 designations (not the Journal Citation Reports; https://www.scimagojr.com, accessed May 2026).

Reference verification

Reference verification was performed independently by two reviewers (T.L. and A.G.); any inconsistency was adjudicated by a senior reviewer (E.K.). Verification was conducted in two stages. Stage 1 (manual): each PubMed-indexed reference was searched in PubMed by title and confirmed by matching journal, first author, and publication year; non-PubMed sources were verified by direct access to the originating organization’s website or database (e.g., Cochrane Library, FDA Orange Book, NCCN website, CDC Yellow Book, DSM-5-TR). Stage 2 (automated): all unique PubMed-indexed references additionally underwent automated NCBI E-utilities API lookup by title and first author. Returned PubMed Identifiers were used to retrieve full publication records via the esummary endpoint, and metadata was programmatically compared against the spreadsheet. Discrepancies were flagged and manually reviewed; year differences of ±1 and surname variants involving diacritics were not counted as errors.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in Python 3.12 using the pandas library. We report descriptive statistics only: counts and percentages of total and unique references by source type and journal tier; median, mean, and proportions above thresholds for publication year; and journal concentration metrics (single-journal and top-3 concentration per specialty). As this is a descriptive characterization study, no inferential statistical tests were performed.

During manuscript preparation, Claude (Anthropic) was used to assist with writing and editing. All content was reviewed and edited by the authors, who take full responsibility for the published work.

No reference was confirmed as fabricated. Three American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) meeting abstracts had author attribution errors (0.06%); all were confirmed to exist with correct titles and years. No predatory journals were identified.

Among 3,787 unique references, 25.9% came from flagship journals and 47.3% from Q1. When repeated citations are included, flagship papers accounted for 31.5% of all 4979 references, compared with 25.9% of unique references. Tier profiles differed across specialties: Cardiology showed the highest Q1 proportion (61.0%), while flagship representation ranged from 17.3% (Rheumatology) to 33.3% (Infectious Diseases).

Source composition varies by specialty

Guideline, regulatory, and supplementary sources comprised 5.7% of all references (n = 286), spanning Cochrane reviews (n = 93, 1.9%), Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulatory documents (n = 67, 1.3%), American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, government health agency documents (VA/DoD, DHHS/NIH), Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 5th edition (DSM-5-TR), and American Psychiatric Association (APA) resources, among others. These sources appeared where clinically expected. Based on direct observation during data collection, the OE interface does not label source type in the generated text; all citations appear as identical numbered references.

The median publication year of unique references was 2022 (mean 2020.5); 69.8% were from 2020 or later. Journal concentration varied substantially by specialty. Journal of the American College of Cardiology (JACC) accounted for 21.1% of unique Cardiology references (148/702), the highest single-journal concentration in the dataset, compared with 5.6% for the leading journal in Oncology. This likely reflects the ACC/AHA’s central role in cardiovascular guideline publication.

Implications for clinical use

This study found that no references were confirmed as fabricated across 4979 OE-generated references. A recent audit of 2.5 million biomedical papers found that reference fabrication has increased 12-fold since 2023, now affecting one in every 277 published papers5. Our finding extends prior work by Omar et al., who documented substantial fabrication in GPT-4 and Gemini Pro using the same prompt set8, and is consistent with Gorenshtein et al., who found that specialized medical retrieval agents select predominantly top-tier journals9. The higher proportion of flagship journals among repeated citations than among unique references (31.5% vs 25.9%) suggests OE’s retrieval weights high-impact foundational sources, which may be appropriate for well-established clinical topics. A recent independent evaluation found that frontier general-purpose LLMs outperformed OE and other clinical AI tools on medical knowledge, clinician alignment, and real-world clinical queries7. That evaluation did not assess citation quality, which the authors identified as an unmeasured dimension relevant to real-world deployment. Our findings address precisely this gap: whatever the relative performance of OE on knowledge benchmarks, its retrieval of verifiable, recent, high-impact references represents a distinct and measurable strength, particularly given the high fabrication rates of general-purpose LLMs.

Beyond fabrication, the source ecosystem is more heterogeneous than the interface suggests. Guideline, regulatory, and supplementary sources sit alongside peer-reviewed trials without distinction, and journal concentration varies substantially by specialty. Cardiology’s JACC concentration (21.1%) likely reflects the field’s guideline-driven evidence structure rather than retrieval bias. These findings support confidence in the existence and bibliographic accuracy of OE-generated references, while source composition still requires scrutiny.

Journal concentration patterns cannot be attributed to retrieval algorithms alone. OE has publicly announced licensing partnerships with the JAMA Network, NCCN, Cochrane, and selected specialty societies; the full set of journals in OE’s corpus and the terms of inclusion are not publicly disclosed. The 21.1% Cardiology concentration in JACC may reflect the central role of ACC/AHA guidance, corpus-level access, licensing or partnership effects, or a combination of these factors. Conversely, the relative under-representation of certain specialty flagship journals—for example, Clinical Infectious Diseases, the IDSA guideline-publishing journal, does not appear among the top-three Infectious Diseases journals in our dataset—is consistent with the possibility that licensing access shapes which sources OE can retrieve. We are unable to distinguish these factors with publicly available information.

Several limitations should be considered. First and most importantly, this study evaluates reference existence and characteristics only; whether retrieved references support the clinical claims OE makes was not assessed, and the clinical utility of OE’s evidence base is therefore not directly addressed here. Second, all prompts were submitted by a single investigator with an OE account registered as a medical student; OE may personalize responses by profile, and our findings may not generalize to other clinician profiles. Third, journal tier reflects citation impact and brand recognition rather than methodological quality per se; higher-impact journals have been shown to have higher retraction rates outside predatory sources10 and tier is an imperfect proxy for the trustworthiness of an individual paper. The tier classification also cannot identify highly cited or practice-changing work published outside the flagship and Q1 categories. Fourth, structured prompts may generate different citation patterns than the shorter free-text queries typical in clinical use. Fifth, the analysis reflects OE as of March-April 2026; the tool updates regularly. Only five specialties and one platform were studied. Finally, confirming that a reference exists and is correctly attributed does not verify the integrity of that reference’s own content; a real, indexed paper retrieved by OE may itself contain fabricated or erroneous citations within its reference list, a layer of evidence quality our analysis does not capture.

Within the scope of reference existence and quality characteristics - and acknowledging that this study did not evaluate whether OE’s references support the clinical claims it makes - we found the following: OE retrieves real and predominantly recent, high-impact references, in contrast to the fabrication rates documented in general-purpose LLMs. Source composition and journal concentration differ by specialty and are not labeled in the current interface. These findings provide a reference quality benchmark for OE and a replicable framework for evaluating other clinical AI tools. As these tools evolve, so must the scrutiny we apply to them.
