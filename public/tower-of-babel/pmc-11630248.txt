Momentum-resolved EELS and CL study on 1D-plasmonic crystal prepared by FIB method
Authors: Akira Yasuhara, Masateru Shibata, Wakaba Yamamoto, Izzah Machfuudzoh, Sotatsu Yanagimoto, Takumi Sannomiya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigate a one-dimensional plasmonic crystal using momentum-resolved electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) and cathodoluminescence (CL) techniques, which are complementary in terms of available optical information. The plasmonic crystal sample is fabricated from large aluminum grains through the focused ion beam method. This approach allows curving nanostructures with high crystallinity, providing platforms for detailed analysis of plasmonic nanostructures using both EELS and CL. The momentum-resolved EELS visualizes dispersion curves outside the light cone, confirming the existence of the surface plasmon polaritons and local modes, while the momentum-resolved CL mapping analysis identified these surface plasmon polaritons and local modes. Such synergetic approach of two electron-beam techniques offers full insights into both radiative and non-radiative optical properties in plasmonic or photonic structures.

Introduction

Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) and cathodoluminescence (CL) represent two primary electron microscopy techniques capable of probing the optical properties of materials and devices at spatial resolutions below the diffraction limit of light [1–3]. EELS provides insights into excitation, while CL furnishes information about emission processes [4,5]. Consequently, EELS and CL serve as complementary tools for investigating optical properties, each offering access to the excitation and emission processes, respectively [6–8]. In addition to the conventional spectral measurements, both methods support momentum-resolved measurements, enabling the analysis of the in-plane momentum of the generated particles (e.g. photons) or quasi-particles [9–11]. This in-plane momentum analysis becomes crucial for systems with surface propagating waves, such as surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs), which find widespread applications [12–15]. Notably, periodic structures supporting SPPs, known as plasmonic crystals (PlCs), including metamaterials, exhibit intriguing optical properties like cavities, waveguides and topological features analogous to atomic crystals by introducing defects or by ‘doping’ [16–19]. When investigating PlCs using an electron beam, the CL approach delivers high spatial resolution for excitation with excellent momentum resolution of emission within the light line [20]. On the other hand, EELS allows access to the dispersion outside the light line, albeit with a trade-off between spatial resolution and momentum-resolved measurement [21]. While there have been a number of momentum-resolved CL studies on PlCs, corresponding EELS approaches are scarce probably due to the requirement of the electron beam transmission through the sample material [22].

As the plasmonic materials, Al, Au, Ag and Cu nanostructures have garnered extensive attention due to their superior optical characteristics, offering enticing applications based on unique catalytic, electric and optical properties [15,23–25]. Despite their intrinsically high mobility of free electrons, the optical properties of these plasmonic materials are hindered by poor crystallinity resulting in poor propagation of SPPs [26]. This deterioration arises from electron scattering at grain orientation, boundaries or other atomic defects [27,28]. Additionally, challenges are also in the fabrication precision due to variations in grain size and crystalline orientation, impacting etching rates and mechanical stiffness. The quest for larger crystals, preferably single crystals, is driven by the desire to fully exploit plasmonic properties and achieve optimal structures. However, attaining such structures is nontrivial, given that metallic nanostructures for plasmonic devices are typically produced through thin film deposition processes, which usually produce polycrystalline films consisting of nanometer scale grains. This constraint emphasizes the necessity to navigate the intricacies of producing larger, well-defined structures to fully unleash the capabilities of plasmonic materials.

In this work, we utilize momentum-resolved EELS and CL techniques to investigate one-dimensional (1D) aluminum PlCs, allowing analyzing both inside and outside the light line. Through this comprehensive study of combined EELS and CL analysis, we not only clarify the optical property of 1D PlC but also review the advantages of both techniques. For the plasmonic material, we chose Al since it has high penetration for the electron for EELS measurement still having moderate SPP propagation [29]. We have achieved a high-crystallinity PlC by employing a focused ion beam (FIB) method and fabricating a 1D PlC structure out of bulk Al.

Experimental methods

Sample fabrication

The fabrication of 1D PlC on a free-standing Al membrane for the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation was performed by FIB (JIB-PS500i, JEOL Ltd, Japan) supporting a drift compensation function, which enables precise processing. First, a 2-µm-thick Al block was meticulously extracted from bulk Al. Large crystals were chosen via observation by secondary ion microscopy in the FIB instrument. The lamella was subsequently positioned on a Mo grid using the in situ probe pickup method. Then, a 200-nm-thin Al lamella was manufactured by a focused Ga-ion beam. To achieve a precisely defined 1D crystal structure with a depth of 50 nm, fine control over the dose rate of the Ga-ion beam was executed. On the top side, a periodic structure featuring terraces and trenches, each ∼500 nm wide, was sculpted. Additionally, electron-induced carbon deposition was performed on the flat back side of the sample within the FIB instrument. This step was undertaken to eliminate the influence of SPPs on the back side without the periodic structure [30].

Momentum-resolved EELS measurement

TEM and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) measurements were conducted utilizing a probe-forming spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (JEM-ARM200F, JEOL Ltd, Japan) equipped with a cold-type field emission gun (Fig. 1a). The momentum-resolved EELS analysis was performed employing a GIF Continuum ER (Gatan, Inc., USA). An energy-filtered TEM (EFTEM) mode was used to acquire the cross-section of the momentum plane at a given energy, where diffraction patterns from the 1D crystal were recorded using the energy-selecting slit while changing the loss energy. By recording images at different energies, an EFTEM data cube is obtained. For the momentum-resolved EELS spectroscopy, a dedicated slit on the diffraction plane was introduced to select the momentum-dispersion vector. Given the significantly smaller momentum of SPPs compared to electrons, leading to a larger period for diffraction, the momentum-resolved EELS measurement was conducted with an exceptionally large camera length setting, contrasting standard electron diffraction for atomic lattices [6,31,32]. Figure 2 provides a comparison between standard and long-camera-length electron optical settings. In the long-camera-length configuration for the SPP momentum measurement (Fig. 2b), intermediate and projector lenses were utilized to magnify the diffraction and adjust the azimuth angle of the diffraction pattern on the detector plane, where the EELS slit is positioned (also depicted in Fig. 1a). While the presented EELS data in the next sections were performed in this long-camera-length setting, conventional short-camera-length EELS results are summarized in the Supplementary Data for comparison (Supplementary Figure S1). All the EELS measurements were performed at 200 kV acceleration voltage.

Four-dimensional CL system

The CL measurement system was constructed on the JEM-2100F (JEOL Ltd, Japan) with a probe-forming spherical aberration corrector, operated at 80 kV acceleration voltage. This system is composed of a parabolic mirror at the specimen position in the TEM to collimate the emitted light guided out the TEM vacuum column (Fig. 1b) [33]. All the other light optics elements, such as lenses, polarizer, angle selective mask, and spectrometer, are located outside the TEM column under the atmospheric pressure. The angle-dispersed CL spectra are collected to acquire a CL photon map while the electron beam scanned over the specimen. Thus, four-dimensional CL dataset, consisting of momentum-(angle-) and energy-dispersed 2D data at each electron beam position, is obtained after scanning the electron beam over the sample [20,34].

At the intersection of the dispersion curves, enhanced intensities are observed. There look characteristic horizontal lines along the momentum axis, which horizontally connect the intersection points of the dispersion curve. These energies are indicated by arrowheads in Fig. 5b at ∼2.6, 5.0 and 6.5 eV. Such momentum-independent bands indicate the presence of localized surface plasmon modes. For 1D structures, the resonance condition of the local mode can be approximated as a half the mode wavelength matching the structure length. Because in the current 1D PlC the width of the local structure (e.g. trench) is just the half of the period, the resonance energy of the local mode and the intersection of the dispersion line coincide (cf. at the gamma point or zero in-plane momentum). In addition, for 1D PlCs with the same terrace and trench widths, no band gap formation is expected at the gamma point [17]. We discuss further details to identify the modes in the next sections.

Mapping by EELS

The local surface plasmon modes can be visualized by EELS mapping without momentum resolution since the momentum of the local modes spreads out as discussed in the previous section. In this section, we perform EELS mapping to identify the local modes on the 1D PlC. Figure 6a shows the high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image of the mapping area. Since the high-angle annular dark-field signal is stronger at the thicker areas, the bright contrast regions in Fig. 6a indicate the terraces, whereas the dark contrast regions are trenches. Figures 6(b–e) display the EELS mapping results at representative energies, which are displayed in the upper part on each figure. The line profiles across the horizontal space are also shown in Fig. 6f for comparison. In the Fig. 6g, this line profile across the periodic structure is displayed spectroscopically with the energy axis dispersed in the vertical axis. At the energy of ∼1.5 eV, one can find a profile with stronger intensities at the edge of the structure, implying the local mode polarized on the terrace which is expected to have the antinode at the edge. However, the modes at the terrace positions are not clearly visualized due to the weak signal originating from the thickness. At ∼2.6 eV, a single antinode is visible at the center of the trench. This local mode in the trench is expected to have the nodes at the edges. At ∼5.0 eV, two antinodes are found inside the trench, indicating the second-order standing wave. These two standing wave type modes well correspond to the momentum-independent horizontal lines in the dispersion relation in Fig. 5b. The third order mode, which should appear ∼6.5 eV, is not anymore clearly resolved. At the higher energies >7.0 eV, surface plasmon modes are concentrated showing stronger intensities in Fig. 6g.

Dispersion analysis by CL

To conduct a comprehensive analysis of SPP and local modes within the fabricated PlC, we further investigate the PlC using the momentum-resolved CL technique. Although limited within the light cone, the CL approach, utilizing the electron beam for spatial resolution and photon detection for momentum, achieves both nanoscopic spatial resolution and momentum resolution simultaneously. This stands in contrast to EELS, which faces limitations in achieving both high spatial and momentum resolutions due to the Fourier limit of the electron (or the uncertainty principle). Through a four-dimensional CL mapping approach, dispersion plots at all electron beam positions become available after a single measurement, facilitating the analysis of the existing modes and the dispersion [19]. The results of the CL dispersion analysis are presented in Fig. 7. It is noted that the STEM images in the insets are all BF images with opposite contrasts for the terrace and trench to DF images in the previous sections. Examining the electron beam position-integrated dispersion curves for the emissions toward the top (Fig. 7b) and bottom (Fig. 7c) directions, dispersion lines are apparent solely in the topward emission. Conversely, only transition radiation proximate to the light line is observable in the downward emission, given the absence of a periodic structure on the bottom side. In addition to the dispersion line depicted in Fig. 7b, a discernible local mode ∼2.6 eV, extending the intensity in the momentum direction, is also evident.

The observed dispersion relations of SPP exhibit variations contingent upon the electron beam position, as depicted in Fig. 7d–g. As we discussed in the EELS section, no clear band gap is discerned in this particular structure [17]. It is evident that the local mode, showing strong intensity ∼2.6 eV, is situated in the trench rather than the terrace, imparting a curvature to the dispersion line off the theoretical one that considers only periodicity without local structures. We note that due to damping in aluminum, the SPP dispersion lines are broader than SPPs on silver [35]. Additionally, another discernible local mode appears to exist ∼1.5 eV, particularly pronounced at the edges, as illustrated in Fig. 7f and g, which is consistent to the EELS mapping results of Fig. 6.

Mapping by CL

The existing modes can be further analyzed through momentum- and energy-selected CL mapping. In Fig. 8, CL mapping results at representative energies and momenta for SPP and local modes are presented. For Position 1, located at the upper branch of the dispersion line around the gamma point (as indicated in Fig. 8a), intensity maxima are observed on the terrace and trench centers in the corresponding CL map. This spatial intensity distribution with the dispersion result signifies the standing wave of the propagating SPPs having antinodes on both the terrace and trench, as illustrated in the inset in Fig. 8b. For Position 2 at the lower branch of the dispersion line, as seen in Fig. 8a, intense signals are localized at the edge of the terrace. This aligns with the expected pattern of the standing wave of the propagating SPPs with antinodes at the edges, as indicated in the inset in Fig. 8b for Position 2. Notably, two distinct local modes are visible at Positions 3 and 4. At Position 3, the local mode on the terrace edge is observed, while at Position 4, the LSP mode in the trench is distinctly visible. These observations are consistent with the EELS analysis discussed previously. It is also noted that the in-plane-polarized local mode (corresponding to Position 3) has upward radiation while the mode localized inside the trench (corresponding to Position 4) has only sideward radiation as this mode is polarized vertically.

Conclusions

We conducted a comprehensive investigation of a 1D PlC using momentum-resolved EELS and CL techniques, leveraging their complementary nature. The PlC sample, fabricated from large Al grains through the FIB method, provided a platform for detailed analysis by EELS and CL. Momentum-resolved EELS allowed for the clear visualization of dispersion curves extending beyond the light cone, thereby confirming the presence of the SPP and local modes. EELS mapping enabled a spatial analysis of the local modes, revealing the spatial features of the existing modes up to higher orders. Momentum-resolved CL analysis was subsequently employed to further explore these modes. Through photon-momentum-resolved mapping and a comparative analysis with EELS results, both SPP and local modes were successfully identified.

While the EELS approach enabled us to observe the dispersion relation outside the light line, which is “invisible” by CL, nanoscale spatial mapping is possible only by integrating all the angular (in-plane momentum) information because of the reciprocal relation. In contrast, the presented momentum-resolved CL mapping has clearly shown the mode distributions at the given points on the dispersion pattern, resolving both the space (by electron) and the momentum (by photon). Thus, the combination of these two complementary electron-beam techniques facilitated a profound understanding of the optical properties inherent to the plasmonic or photonic structures. Although we have demonstrated such analysis on a rather simple system of a PlC, combinatorial approach becomes more powerful, e.g. when an emitter material is involved in PlC. In such cases, by combined EELS and CL, excitation and emission processes can be separately observed in multiple dimensions (space, energy and momentum) [8,32,36]. This holistic approach holds promise for advancing our understanding of complex optical phenomena in various materials.
