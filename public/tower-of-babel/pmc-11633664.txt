Setting standards in residential aged care: identifying achievable benchmarks of care for long-term aged care services
Authors: Johannes Schwabe, Gillian E Caughey, Robert Jorissen, Tracy Comans, Len Gray, Johanna Westbrook, Jeffrey Braithwaite, Peter Hibbert, Steven Wesselingh, Janet K Sluggett, Nasir Wabe, Maria C Inacio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBenchmark is an important aspect of quality measurement and evaluation of long-term care services (LTCS) performance. In this study, we aimed to estimate achievable benchmarks of care (ABC©) for 12 quality indicators used to monitor the quality of care in Australian LTCS and to identify LTCS characteristics associated with attaining the estimated ABC.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using integrated population-based datasets from long-term care, health care, and social welfare sectors within the Registry of Senior Australians (ROSA) National Historical Cohort. All LTCS residents in 2019 were included. Twelve risk-adjusted quality indicators were examined. ABC were defined as the performance level of top-ranked LTCS, including those sequentially from rank 1 onward, until the combined number of residents included at least 10% of all residents nationally. Indicator-specific ABC for 2019 were estimated using Bayesian-adjusted performance fraction ranking. Logistic regressions estimated LTCS characteristics associated with ABC attainment.Results2746 LTCS and 244 419 residents (≥65 years) between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2019 were included. The cohort was mostly female (65%), with a median age of 86 years, and 56% had dementia. The ABC provide performance targets based on the observed levels of top-performing LTCS. The ABC for premature mortality (0.007%), weight loss hospitalizations (0.1%), pressure injuries (0.2%), delirium and dementia hospitalizations (0.2%), and medication-related adverse events (0.4%) were lower than 1% and attained by 17–59% of LTCS. The ABC for fractures (1.3%), falls (3.9%), and emergency department presentations (5.1%) were between 1 and 5% and attained by 7–11% of LTCS. The ABC for antipsychotic use (10.5%), chronic opioid use (12.6%), high sedative load exposure (26.8%), and antibiotic use (47.8%) were between 10 and 50% and met by 6–7% of LTCS. Smaller LTCS and government-owned LTCS were more likely to achieve the ABC compared to medium, larger, private, and not-for-profit LTCS.ConclusionThis is the first national estimation of ABC for Australian LTCS, identifying real-world examples of LTCS with relatively better national performance. The ABC are realistic goals for LTCS improvement efforts. They can be leveraged as national standards in quality monitoring reports and incentive programs. Smaller and government LTCS were generally more likely to attain ABC.Keywords: quality improvement, performance measurement, nursing homes, residential aged care, adverse events, emergency department

Introduction

As of 2022, an estimated 142 million older people require long-term care worldwide [1]. In Australia, approximately 250 000 people need care in long-term care services (LTCS) annually. Providing high-quality care for this growing population while balancing challenges such as individuals’ high disease and frailty burden, providers’ limited resources, and policymakers’ regulatory issues is a global challenge. While there is a general international consensus on how quality care should be defined, measured, and monitored, the approaches vary.

In 2020, 11 countries had population-level quality monitoring programs. These included seven European countries (Denmark, Finland, Germany, Iceland, Netherlands, Sweden, and United Kingdom), two North American countries (Canada and United States), and two Australasian countries (Australia and New Zealand) [2]. These programs provide information on care quality domains to providers, regulators, and the public, including physical and psychosocial function, health, social well-being, safety, and quality of life [2]. In Australia, three programs exist: the Victorian Public Sector Residential Aged Care Services (PSRACS) [3] quality indicators, limited to one state’s public services; the National Mandatory Quality Indicator Program (NMQIP) [4], covering all national services; and the academic-, consumer-, clinician-, and provider-led Registry of Senior Australians (ROSA) Outcome Monitoring System (OMS) [5], reporting on five indicators nationally and seven additional indicators for four states, representing 87% of the population.

Quality monitoring programs include identifying outliers and setting benchmarks to promote quality improvement in specific sectors (e.g., hospitals, LTCS, surgeons). Benchmarking approaches vary internationally. Benchmarks are often derived from measures of central tendencies or based on expert opinion [6]. These methods provide valuable information but may also suffer from bias, fail to capture nuances, and may be unrealistic for the populations they represent. This limits what benchmarking intends to do—drive and maximize improvement from data monitoring and evaluation. Only the Australian Victorian PSRACS and an Icelandic program offer benchmarks internationally [2]. The PSRACS reference ranges were developed based on literature review, data analysis, and expert consensus [7], while the Icelandic ranges were solely based on expert consensus [8].

One proposed data-driven approach to identifying realistic benchmarks is the Achievable Benchmarks of Care (ABC©) methodology [9, 10]. The ABC have been applied in quality improvement interventions across various health care settings but never to LTCS settings. The ABC summarize the performance of top-ranked LTCS, offering real-world examples of LTCS that excel [10]. This methodology is a promising starting point for benchmarking in LTCS settings, especially without other strategies.

In the present study, we apply the ABC method to a national population-based quality monitoring program for LTCS in Australia. The aims of the study were to (1) estimate ABC for 12 quality indicators used to monitor quality of care in Australian LTCS and (2) to identify LTCS characteristics associated with attaining the estimated ABC.

Methods

Study design, data source, and setting

The cross-sectional study investigated LTCS in the ROSA National Historical Cohort of 2019 [11, 12]. ROSA integrates national and state-based health, aged care, and social welfare information for ∼3.5 million Australians who have accessed aged care services between 2002 and 2020 and has been previously described [11, 12]. The datasets used in this study included national aged care eligibility assessments for LTC services (performed at home or in an institution), respite or transition care services; national LTCS service records; national entry into LTCS need assessments; national pharmaceutical dispensing records (Pharmacy Benefits Scheme); national subsidized health service records (Medicare Benefits Schedule); state-based hospitalization and emergency department (ED) presentations for four out of seven Australian states and territories (87% of the national cohort); and the National Death Index (NDI).

Cohort

The overall cohort included older (≥65 years old) permanent residents of Australian LTCS between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2019 who did not identify as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander and spent at least 1 day in care in 2019 (N = 244 419 individuals in 2746 services). In specific analysis, subsets of this cohort were used as specified in Supplementary Table S1.

Quality indicators

The 12 risk-adjusted quality indicators from the ROSA OMS were employed (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1) [5]. These indicators have been previously described and employed in several studies nationally [5, 13, 14]. The indicators were purposefully developed for use cases such as quality monitoring, benchmarking, and national evaluation of unwarranted variation in care practices and outcomes. Five medication and mortality indicators are available nationally: exposure to high sedative load, antipsychotic use, chronic opioid use, antibiotic use, and premature mortality. Seven indicators based on hospitalization data, including hospitalizations with or for falls, fractures, medication-related adverse events, weight loss and malnutrition, delirium and/or dementia, and pressure injuries, as well as emergency department presentations are available for four states (New South Wales, Queensland, South Australia, and Victoria). For each indicator, the data source, numerator, denominator, additional inclusion and exclusion criteria, and covariates for risk-adjustment were determined (Supplementary Table S1). To account for resident profiles, indicators were, at a minimum, risk-adjusted for age, sex, and number of health conditions and, where relevant, for additional covariates. The probability of an outcome (expected rate) was estimated using logistic regressions including the model-specific covariates.

Service characteristics

The following service characteristics were ascertained from LTCS records and examined: size (number of residents during the year studied), ownership type (government, not-for-profit, and private), service location (state), and geographical remoteness in the categories of the Accessibility/Remoteness Index of Australia (ARIA: major cities, inner regional, outer regional, remote, and very remote). To account for the potential interactions between provider types and geographical remoteness (202 of the total 236 government LTCS are outside of major cities, and 776 of the 837 private LTCS are in major cities), provider types were analysed by remoteness.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the individuals and LTCS examined. The ROSA OMS quality indicators were estimated as specified above [5]. An indicator-specific ABC was defined as the performance level in the subset of top-ranking LTCS, including those sequentially from rank 1 onward, until the number of included residents accounted for at least 10% of the cohort population [10]. Specifically, for each indicator, LTCS were ranked on their Bayesian adjusted performance fractions (APF) [10]. The APF addresses the problem of LTCS with few residents and was calculated as follows: APF = (x + 1)/(d + 2), where x is the number of residents with the indicator-specific outcome (e.g. fall) and d is the number of indicator-eligible residents (for an indicator with no exclusions, d is the number of LTCS residents). The size-weighted average performance was calculated in the subset of top-performing LTCS based on actual performance (and not APF). The ABC is visualized in an example histogram (Supplementary Figure S1).

To identify service characteristics associated with the likelihood of attaining ABC, logistic regression models were employed to estimate odds ratios (OR) and confidence intervals (CI), visualized with forest plots and a heatmap. Service size was split into six categories (1–29, 30–59, 60–89, 90–119, 120–149, and 150+). Reference categories were selected based on their frequency, representing the most common categories: New South Wales (state), 60–89 residents (LTCS size), and not-for-profit LTCS in major cities (ownership type/geographical remoteness). There was minimal missing data (n = 11/2746 or 0.4% of all LTCS had missing service remoteness data), and a complete case analysis was conducted. Due to the large number of tests, the false discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Cohort

2746 LTCS and 244 419 individuals were included in the analysis of five medication and a mortality indicator. 2390 LTCS and 213 446 individuals were included in the analysis of seven hospitalization-related indicators. Overall, the cohort was 65.3% female (n = 159 563), with a median age of 86 years (IQR = 80–91), 56.6% (n = 138 286) had five or more health conditions, and 55.3% (n = 135 071) were living with dementia (Supplementary Table S2). Of the 2746 LTCS included, 32.4% (n = 889) were in NSW, 56.9% (n = 1562) had fewer than 90 residents, and 57.3% (n = 1573) were not-for-profit services (Tables 2 and 3).

Achievable benchmarks of care

The estimated mean performances and ABC for the 12 indicators examined are shown in Table 2 (hospitalization-related indicators) and Table 3 (medication and mortality indicators). The ABC in premature mortality (ABC = 0.007%), weight loss and/or malnutrition-related hospitalizations (ABC = 0.1%), pressure injury-related hospitalizations (ABC = 0.2%), delirium and/or dementia hospitalizations (ABC = 0.2%), and medication-related adverse events (ABC = 0.4%) were estimated to be lower than 1%. The proportions of LTCS that attained these ABC ranged from 17% (396/2390) in medication-related adverse events to 59% (1615/2746) in premature mortality.

The ABC in fractures (ABC = 1.3%), falls (ABC = 3.9%), and ED presentations (ABC = 5.1%) were estimated to be between 1 and 10%. The proportions of LTCS that attained these ABC ranged from 7% (177/2390) in falls to 11% (264/2382) in ED presentations.

The ABC for antipsychotic use (ABC = 10.5%), opioid use (ABC = 12.6%), high sedative load (ABC = 26.8%), and antibiotic use (ABC = 47.8%) were estimated to be between 10 and 50%. The proportions of LTCS that attained these ABC ranged from 6% (163/2745) in high sedative load exposure to 7% (195/2745) in antipsychotic use.

Characteristics of ABC-attaining LTCS

Across all indicators (Tables 4 and 5, Supplementary Figures S2–S14), compared to medium-sized LTCS (60–89 residents), smaller LTCS (1–29 and 30–59 residents) were more likely to attain the ABC, with ORs ranging from 1.9 (30–59 residents, premature mortality, 95% CI: 1.5, 2.4) to 18.1 (1–29 residents, ED presentations, 95% CI: 3.9, 321.0). Compared to medium LTCS, larger LTCS were less likely to achieve the ABC in fractures, pressure injuries, weight loss and malnutrition-related hospitalizations, antibiotic use, chronic opioid use, high sedative load, and premature mortality, with ORs ranging from 0.04 (150+ residents, medication-related adverse events, 95% CI: <0.1, 0.1) to 0.5 (120–149 residents, weight loss and/or malnutrition hospitalisations, 95% CI: 0.3, 0.7).

Compared to not-for-profit LTCS in major cities, government LTCS in major cities were more likely to achieve the ABC in falls (OR = 4.0, 95% CI: 1.3, 10.8), and private LTCS in major cities were less likely to achieve the ABC in pressure injuries (OR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.5, 1.0) (Tables 4 and 5, Supplementary Figure S2–S14).

Compared to not-for-profit LTCS in major cities, LTCS outside of major cities were more likely to achieve the ABC in six hospitalization-related and one medication indicators but less likely to achieve the ABC in three medication indicators (Tables 4 and 5, Supplementary Figure S2–S14). Specifically, LTCS outside of major cities were more likely to achieve the ABC in weight loss and malnutrition-related hospitalizations, ED presentations, medication-related adverse events, delirium and/or dementia-related hospitalizations, falls, pressure injuries, and antibiotic use, with ORs ranging from 1.4 (not-for-profit, delirium and/or dementia-related hospitalisations, 95% CI: 1.1, 1.9) to 7.2 (government, ED presentations, 95% CI: 4.7, 11.2) but less likely to achieve the ABC in premature mortality, chronic opioid use, and high sedative load, with ORs ranging from 0.3 (government, chronic opioid use, 95% CI: 0.1, 0.6) to 0.7 (private, premature mortality, 95% CI: 0.5, 1.0).

The results on associations of service location (state) and benchmark attainment are reported in writing in the Supplementary Results and in tabular and visual form in Tables 4 and 5 and Supplementary Figures S2–S14.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

Data-driven ABC were estimated for 12 quality indicators, revealing substantial variation in the quality of care among 2746 LTCS nationally in 2019. The ABC ranged from less than 1% to 75% of the national indicator performance averages. These considerable differences between the performance standards set by the ABC and the average LTCS performances highlight the substantial quality improvement potential in many LTCS, leading to meaningfully improved outcomes for older people in care and significant cost reductions for the health and care systems.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study identifying national and data-driven ABC in a comprehensive set of quality indicators based on a population-level quality indicator program for LTCS. Our findings are generalizable to the Australian LTCS setting and valuable for providers and regulators, given the absence of information regarding best practices. This study additionally employed established risk-adjusted indicators used nationally by the Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety as the definition of quality of care and regularly reported to South Australian aged care providers [2, 15–17]. That means that the resulting ABC are less affected by resident profile bias, which could be introduced if top-performing LTCS had unusually healthy residents.

Our study has several limitations due to its design, data sources, and methodology. The causal mechanisms underlying the associations of service characteristics with benchmark attainment are unidentifiable with our observational study. The hospitalization-related indicators only estimate the prevalence of events leading to or diagnosed during hospitalization. The true prevalence of events would be higher because not all require hospitalizations. These indicators can be affected by regional hospitalization rates and inconsistent hospital diagnosing [5]. The identified benchmarks are based on 2019 data, prior to the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, Australia’s Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (2019–2021), and subsequent aged care reforms. Integrating aged care and health care data is time-intensive process, typically taking 2–3 years for linkage across multiple data custodians and integrating authorities before analysis can begin. While our access to more recent data has improved considerably, with complete data linkage achieved in 2023, the inherent delays in data availability must be acknowledged, highlighting the need for national improvements in data availability. The dosage of sedatives was not considered in the high sedative load indicator. While the identified ABC offer generally contemporary benchmarks for the aged care sector, they should be recalculated and refined periodically to ensure it captures major shifts within the sector. The changes in the estimates of measures employed in this study have been relatively minor from year to year, and significant population changes would be necessary to drastically affect the ABCs [18]. We had no data on LTCS’ workforce profiles, likely impacting performance. In some indicators, the resident profiles of benchmark-attaining LTCS deviate slightly from the overall cohort (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). In fractures, antibiotic use, opioids use, and high sedative load, residents of benchmark-attaining LTCS are slightly more male and younger. In antipsychotics use and delirium and dementia hospitalizations, there are fewer residents with dementia. We cannot completely rule out residual confounding in our analysis. The ABC based on low-prevalence indicators are limited in identifying top-performing LTCS because the APF method causes the ABC in low-prevalence indicators to be higher than benchmarks based on high-prevalence indicators.

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

The identified ABC provide promising targets for LTCS in Australia, where certain LTCS are delivering high-quality care and can serve as national models for best practices. Compared to the lower thresholds of the expert opinion reference ranges from the Icelandic quality monitoring program [8], the ABC are slightly more conservative across comparable indicators, such as falls (ABC = 3.9% and Iceland = 6.1%) and antipsychotic use (ABC = 10.5% and Iceland = 13.5%). While this may be partly due to differences in the technical specifications of the indicators and data sources, it highlights the excellent level of performance of the top-ranking LTCS.

Implications for policy, practice, and research

The ABC can assist providers and decision-makers in interpreting quality indicator performance at the service, state, or national level, filling a gap that has consistently been identified in the quality monitoring literature [2]. However, it is important to recognize that the event occurrences underlying the quality indicators are not fully under LTCS control. For example, one factor potentially influencing quality indicator performance but not fully controllable by LTCS is the variation in general practitioners seen by residents (e.g. differing in their readiness to prescribe medications) [19]. Therefore, the ABC should be understood as quality level achievable under the right circumstances.

To attain the ABC in five indicators with low prevalence but high potential harm, LTCS had to prevent these events completely, including premature mortality, and hospitalizations for or with weight loss and/or malnutrition, medication-related adverse events, delirium and/or dementia, and pressure injuries. Many LTCS attained these no-event benchmarks, ranging from 17 to 59%. Occurrences of these events are likely related to inappropriate medication use, medication mismanagement, or resident neglect resulting from insufficient care plans or staffing [2]. Demonstrating that many LTCS did not record these events sends an important message: any occurrence should trigger a careful review of why the event occurred and which care practices might prevent future occurrences. The correspondence of the no-event benchmarks with the lower bounds of the PSRAC reference ranges further underlines their attainability [20]. However, since one event is enough to fail a no-event benchmark in a year and because event occurrence is not entirely under LTCS control, the reliability of a single no-event benchmark in distinguishing excellent from non-excellent LTCS may be limited when only 1 year of reporting is considered. Failing to attain one of the no-event benchmarks once over several years does not necessarily indicate non-excellence. However, consistently falling short across several years does. We thus recommend assessing the no-event benchmarks over multiple years and indicators.

The ABC for falls, fractures, and ED presentations demonstrate that top-performing LTCS have events representing a quarter of the national averages. Lower rates can reduce hospitalization rates and care costs, as falls in older people are the leading cause of hospitalized injury (41%) and injury-related deaths (37%)[21], costing $790 million in Australia in 2021 [22]. Hip fractures, common after falls, are associated with decreased mobility and increased mortality [23]. Falls and fractures are preventable by managing extrinsic (lighting, flooring, and staffing) and intrinsic (mobility, cognition, and frailty) factors and medication use [24, 25]. Benchmark-attaining LTCS likely exemplify effective fall management.

When looking at the medication ABC, the benchmark-setting LTCS perform at levels of 50–75% of the national averages. The medications underlying the chronic opioid use, antipsychotics use, and high sedative load indicators have all been targeted by national and international guidelines and warnings on overprescribing and overuse [26–28]. The ABC can complement and support these national and international efforts for safe and efficient medication management.

Smaller LTCS were more likely to attain the ABC than larger LTCS. One reason could be more personalized, integrated, and continuous care in smaller LTCS, making it easier to address residents’ care needs. Given the consistent evidence of better care in smaller facilities [29], more investment and investigation into smaller facility models are needed to counteract the prevailing trend of increasingly larger facilities [30].

Non-metropolitan LTCS are more likely to attain hospitalization-related ABC than metropolitan LTCS but are less likely to attain two medication-related and the mortality quality ABC. This corroborates previous research [31] and may be due to limited hospital access in remote Australia [32] or closer integration of LTCS and hospital services outside major cities. Consequently, hospitalization-related ABC might seem too conservative for major cities. However, the association could also result from unrestricted hospital access in major cities, and many metropolitan LTCS attain the hospitalization-related ABC, demonstrating their achievability in cities. Thus, the hospitalization-related ABC apply unrestricted in major cities.

Conclusions

In this first nation-wide estimation of ABC for LTCS, we identified real-world examples of LTCS with better national performance, particularly among smaller and government-owned LTCS. These estimated benchmarks are realistic starting goals for LTCS improvement efforts. Providers can use the ABC to identify specific areas needing enhancement, set measurable targets, and implement targeted interventions to improve care quality. The identification of LTCS characteristics associated with ABC attainment should help identify LTCS with better care models and could guide intervention design. The ABC can also be leveraged to establish national performance standards by incorporating them into existing quality monitoring programs such as the ROSA Outcome Monitoring System. Furthermore, policymakers could use the ABC to develop incentives programs, such as public recognition for facilities that attain the ABC. In sum, the ABC will help to identify, disseminate, and promote best practices and successful care models, driving sector-wide improvements and better outcomes for residents.
