Introduction

The role of real-world evidence in evaluating clinical interventions is growing, supported by increasingly mature causal inference methodologies [1, 2]. In observational settings, however, the validity of effect estimates still hinges on how completely and appropriately confounding variables are identified and controlled. Robust confounder lists are particularly challenging to construct in modern high-dimensional environments, where exposures, outcomes and confounders are drawn from heterogeneous electronic health records and large registries [3, 4]. To help formalize clinical questions, the evidence-based practice community widely employs the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework for structural decomposition [5], which now underpins both interventional and observational study design [6] and offers a standardized entry point for systematic scoping of candidate confounders for adjustment [7].

Traditionally, candidate confounders for adjustment are selected through a manual, expert-driven process. Clinical researchers design study protocols, synthesize prior evidence, build directed acyclic graphs (DAG) or other causal models, and compile variable lists using labor-intensive literature reviews [8–10]. This classic process is time-consuming, subjective, and highly dependent on domain expertise, creating significant barriers for early-career investigators and for teams in resource-limited settings. Although early natural language processing (NLP) tools and literature-based discovery systems showed promise for automating part of this process, their performance has been constrained by shallow representations and limited capture of clinical context [11].

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) have offered new options for automating causal evidence discovery and confounder identification for adjustment [12, 13]. LLM-based agent frameworks for causal inference and systems such as the Evidence Triangulator can synthesize findings across heterogeneous study designs [12, 17], while SemMedDB- and literature-based discovery pipelines mine computable knowledge directly from biomedical text [16]. Yet these approaches introduce practical challenges for typical clinical researchers: many rely on full-text access, cloud-hosted APIs, or manually curated knowledge graphs, raising concerns about patient privacy, copyright, and reproducibility, and making local deployment difficult [14, 15]. Moreover, SemMedDB-based pipelines remain restricted to explicit subject-predicate-object triples and often struggle to recover high-value confounders that are only implicitly described at the abstract level [16]. Consequently, to our knowledge, no existing system simultaneously offers abstract-only operation, local deployment, traceable confounder suggestions, and large-scale, model-agnostic evaluation.

To address this gap, we developed a heuristic research support tool that scopes candidate confounders for adjustment for observational studies from PICO-based queries. At its core, our tool implements a three-stage RAG workflow running entirely on a locally deployed LLM. Given a user-specific PICO query, it operates over a locally mirrored PubMed abstract corpus, combining PICO-parsed lexical retrieval, embedding-based re-ranking, PICO-level screening and LLM-based extraction with heuristic scoring to return a ranked, literature-linked list of candidate confounders for adjustment. In this study, we (i) construct a 1,000-query benchmark spanning 20 clinical specialties, (ii) evaluate the pipeline along four dimensions—internal consistency, output volume, efficiency and clinical acceptance—and (iii) compare it with a pure SemMedDB baseline that operates on the SemMedDB knowledge graph. Our findings suggest that an abstract-only, locally deployed RAG architecture can, in an exploratory capacity, meaningfully augment expert judgment and support the design of high-quality observational studies. The systematic novelty of our approach lies in the integration of PICO-level semantic gating with a heuristic weighting architecture. Unlike generic RAG systems, our workflow functions as a cascading evidence filter that ensures suggested variables are not only textually similar but also contextually aligned with the specific clinical architecture of the research question.

In this manuscript, we provide a working definition for the system’s primary outputs, termed “candidate confounders for adjustment”: these are pre-exposure variables surfaced from the literature that are plausibly associated with both the intervention and the outcome, and thus warrant systematic consideration for inclusion in adjustment sets during the study design phase. By focusing on this “scoping” role, the system aims to identify a comprehensive set of potential covariates for expert review, rather than to independently adjudicate their definitive causal roles.

Methods

Study design

In this study, we developed and evaluated a PICO-driven evidence synthesis pipeline to automate the scoping of candidate confounders for adjustment for observational research. The pipeline operates on a locally mirrored PubMed database containing titles, abstracts, journal metadata and standard indexing metadata current to July 2025 [19], acquired for non-commercial academic use. All literature processing and model inference are performed within the institutional network.

To assess clinical utility and performance, we collaborated with experts from five institutions: (1) the Department of Gynecology, Liuzhou Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital; (2) Ian Frazer Centre for Children’s Immunotherapy Research, The University of Queensland; (3) the Department of Liver Transplantation, The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University; (4) the Department of Obstetrics, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital; and (5) Chest Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Each team reviewed outputs for a curated set of 1,000 predefined PICO queries spanning 20 medical specialities and provided structured feedback on clinical relevance and research value. The full list of PICO queries is provided in Supplementary Materials (Appendix A).

The pipeline was deployed on a localized, all-in-one server. The Qwen2.5-14B model served as the core LLM, hosted behind a local OpenAI-compatible HTTP API. A web-based prototype of the pipeline is available for non-commercial academic use [32]. All experiments were conducted on a server featuring an AMD Ryzen™ AI Max + 395 CPU, 128 GB of DDR5 RAM, a 2 TB solid-state drive, and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

For comparative analyses, we also implemented a pure SemMedDB baseline pipeline that identifies candidate confounders by intersecting population-intervention-outcome risk predicates in the SemMedDB knowledge graph, without any LLM-based retrieval. Its design and results are presented in the Supplementary Analyses section.

Workflow configuration

We have implemented a three-stage architecture for PICO-based scoping of candidate confounder for adjustment (Fig. 1).

In Stage 1 (corpus construction), the user’s PICO query is decomposed into Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome components. Each component is mapped to controlled-vocabulary headings and synonyms using a locally stored Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) thesaurus, and these terms are combined with free-text keywords to search a locally mirrored PubMed abstract database. This stage initializes the evidence corpus by retrieving a candidate batch of abstracts for each query, with pre-specified lower and upper bounds on the number of records per query to balance recall and noise.

In Stage 2 (literature refinement and PICO-level filtering), both the structured PICO representation of the query and the candidate abstracts are encoded into dense sentence embeddings, and cosine similarity is used to re-rank the evidence corpus. The top-ranked subset is passed to an LLM-based module that identifies and records the PICO elements actually studied in each paper; abstracts whose reported populations, interventions, comparisons or outcomes are inconsistent with the original query are filtered out. In this way, the corpus is progressively focused on studies whose clinical context and mechanistic relationships are most relevant to the research question.

In Stage 3 (candidate confounder extraction and evidence synthesis), a hybrid extraction framework combines multi-strategy algorithms with LLM-enhanced semantic analysis to identify candidate confounders for adjustment through PICO element comparison. For each retained abstract, the pipeline extracts candidate confounders that may be related to both the exposure and outcome domains and associates them with PMIDs and sentence-level evidence. The extraction process maintains transparency by linking each candidate confounder back to specific evidence snippets within the source abstracts; a concrete example of this mapping from raw text to extracted variables is provided in Supplementary Materials (Appendix F). Evidence synthesis then applies a pre-specified heuristic weighting scheme that combines citation frequency, journal impact factor (Clarivate Analytics Journal Citation Reports, 2025 [31]), temporal recency and cross-study support into an aggregate score, followed by deduplication, grouping into clinically meaningful categories and basic quality assessment before returning a ranked list of candidate confounders for adjustment to the user.

Fig. 1Three-stage PICO-based pipeline for scoping candidate confounders for adjustment. Stage 1 (corpus construction) builds a query-specific abstract corpus from the local PubMed mirror using PICO elements expanded with controlled-vocabulary terms and related keywords. Stage 2 (literature refinement and PICO-level filtering) semantically re-ranks and screens this corpus so that only studies whose reported PICO profiles match the original query are retained. Stage 3 (confounder extraction and evidence synthesis) applies a hybrid rule-based and LLM-guided extractor together with a heuristic weighting scheme (citation frequency 40%, journal impact factor 30%, temporal recency 20% and cross-study support 10%) to generate a deduplicated, categorized and ranked list of literature-linked candidate confounders for adjustment, along with secondary suggestions for alternative outcomes and control group designs. All prompts used in the workflow are listed in Supplementary Materials (Appendix E). In all experiments, pipeline settings were held constant, and repeated runs were executed under identical settings to quantify output variability. The selection of these specific weights follows a “mock research” logic, designed to simulate the internal heuristic used by clinical experts to prioritize evidence quality and reliability. This scheme was finalized through iterative testing to balance processing speed, identified candidate confounders, and the mitigation of LLM hallucinations. The robustness of the weighting scheme was validated through a perturbation analysis on 50 randomly sampled pilot queries; by jointly varying the weights (sum of absolute deviations ≤ 10%) over five random iterations per query, the system maintained a 90.1% mean overlap in the identified confounder sets (up to top 5), demonstrating high compositional stability. While these parameters are user-adjustable, we maintained this fixed configuration to ensure a stable and reproducible performance baseline across all 1,000 evaluated PICO queries

PICO query development and classification

All 1,000 PICO queries in this study, sourced from targeted literature searches and systematically validated by domain experts for its clinical plausibility, PICO completeness, and applicability to real-world cohorts. All queries were evenly distributed across 20 specialties (50 per specialty), with the full listing provided in Supplementary Materials (Appendix A).

Results

The workflow successfully executed on 962 (96.2%) of 1000 PICO queries and the remaining queries were excluded from subsequent analyses. Detailed outputs are provided in Supplementary Materials (Appendix B).

For multi-round internal consistency, the average candidate confounder list consistency was 94.6%±8.7% and the average PMID set consistency was 79.4%±23.5%. Across three repeated runs, 71.2% of queries achieved a PMID set overlap of more than 80%.

The pipeline suggested a median of 6 candidate confounders (IQR 8; mean 7.74 ± 6.87) for adjustment and retrieved a median of 33 unique PMIDs (IQR 7; mean 28.77 ± 12.2). A hierarchical breakdown of these candidates indicated that comorbidities were the most frequent category identified (30.35%), while socioeconomic factors were the least frequent (0.39%).

The median processing time per query was 44.5 s (IQR 31.72), with a mean of 59.84 ± 64.11 s per query. This time varied across medical domains, with the shortest median processing time observed in Rheumatology (32.54 s; IQR: 42.17) and the longest in Oncology (52.58 s; IQR: 15.12).

An expert panel review of all pipeline outputs yielded an overall acceptance rate of 87.12% and an ICC(2,1) of 0.63. All detailed results are summarized in Fig. 3.

Additional research

Beyond the primary investigation, we conducted exploratory studies to assess the workflow’s broader utility and generalizability.

Additional metrics

Beyond adjustment confounder scoping, we leveraged the workflow’s secondary outputs—specifically the identified alternative outcomes and control group designs—to augment and refine existing PICO frameworks for clinical studies. Across all executed queries, the workflow identified a total of 144,379 alternative outcomes and 3,475 distinct control group designs. This corresponds to a median of 154 (IQR 85; mean 150.08 ± 67.26) alternative outcomes and 4 (IQR 1; mean 3.61 ± 0.49) control group designs, providing researchers with a broad set of exploratory options for outcome selection and control group design (Fig. 3 (D)). These secondary outputs are intended for hypothesis generation and require expert review before being used in protocol development.

Comparative analysis

SemMedDB has long been used globally as a structured knowledge resource for capturing subject–predicate–object relations from biomedical literature [18, 20]. However, its recall and precision remain limited because SemRep [30], the underlying NLP system, often misses implicit relations and may introduce extraction noise. As a result, SemMedDB is useful for broad hypothesis generation but insufficient for applications demanding high-fidelity causal signal extraction, particularly in confounder identification for adjustment [16, 21, 22].

To contextualize the performance of our LLM-guided pipeline, we implemented a pure SemMedDB baseline that restricts all reasoning to the SemMedDB knowledge graph. Given user-specified PICO elements, it only retains semantic triples whose predicates signal risk (ASSOCIATED_WITH, PREDISPOSES, CAUSES, AFFECTS, COEXISTS_WITH, INTERACTS_WITH, COMPLICATES and STIMULATES, INHIBITS). It then successively intersects entities linked to the population, intervention, and outcome through these predicates, and reports the surviving entities as candidate confounders for adjustment. However, it omits all LLM-based retrieval, synthesis, and outcome/control group generation. The baseline runs on an NIH-maintained SemMedDB/PubMed abstract snapshot up to 2024, whereas our pipeline queries a locally mirrored PubMed corpus (current to July 2025); this temporal mismatch should be considered when interpreting comparative results.

In comparative experiments, the SemMedDB baseline remained computationally lightweight (median 3.63 s; IQR: 6.28; mean 4.23 ± 3.26 s per query) versus our PICO workflow’s processing time (median 44.5 s; IQR: 31.72; mean 59.84 ± 64.11 s per query), but this computational efficiency came at the cost of output quality: while it matched our pipeline’s median of 6 candidate confounders, it exhibited a narrower retrieval range (IQR 6; mean 6.39 ± 4.8) and left 134 PICO queries with zero outputs (n = 866 expert evaluable queries). In contrast, our pipeline demonstrated greater depth for complex queries (median 6; IQR 8; mean 7.74 ± 6.87) and was more robust, resulting in only 80 zero-output cases among the 962 technically successful runs (n = 882 for expert evaluable queries) despite its more stringent filtering for clinically interpretable entities. The baseline retrieved a significantly higher volume of literature, with a median of 46 unique PMIDs (IQR 59; mean 52.72 ± 41.31 per query), compared to a median of 33 (IQR 7; mean 28.77 ± 12.2 per query) in the PICO pipeline, because its triples often bundled mechanistic or non-human relations, which is reflected in its category profile: 72.78% of variables were labeled Unspecified and only 16.03% as Comorbidity, compared with our pipeline’s balanced mix (Comorbidity 30.35%, Demographic 26.30%, Tumor Burden 9.54%, Laboratory 8.97%, etc.). These contrasts highlight the trade-off: a graph-only SemMedDB pass can screen literature quickly, but embedding the same knowledge source inside an iterative, LLM-guided RAG pipeline yields richer, more structured, and clinically ready candidate confounder sets even if processing times are higher.

Beyond characteristic differences, we further evaluated the clinical utility of the SemMedDB baseline using the same clinician acceptance framework applied to our PICO pipeline. The results indicated that the PICO pipeline offers substantial improvements in practical utility. Specifically, our pipeline achieved an acceptance rate (defined as the proportion of all individual expert scores ≥ 4) of 87.12%, whereas the SemMedDB baseline only reached 74.2%. Statistical analysis revealed that the experts significantly preferred the suggestions from the PICO pipeline (Mann-Whitney U test, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.68). Furthermore, the ICC was notably higher for our pipeline (0.63) compared to the baseline (0.44), suggesting that the baseline results were not only lower in quality but also more prone to expert disagreement. Details for this clinical utility comparative analysis and the granular distribution of expert disagreement are presented in Supplementary Materials (Appendix G).

Discussion

A validated abstract-only, local RAG pipeline is feasible and practically useful

We developed and validated a locally deployed, LLM-based tool that systematizes scoping of candidate confounders for adjustment for observational studies. By running entirely on locally mirrored PubMed abstracts, our pipeline maintained high internal consistency across multiple independent runs, received favorable ratings from the clinician panel, and executed efficiently with an average one-minute processing time per query. This observed variability in output consistency is primarily attributed to the inherent stochasticity of the locally deployed LLM during semantic filtering and extraction. For clinical users, this suggests the tool is best utilized as a comprehensive inspiration engine, where minor fluctuations in the supporting literature pool do not typically alter the core list of suggested candidate confounders, but rather provide a broader range of evidence for expert audit.

To examine whether the pipeline surfaces adjustment confounders consistent with published practice, we conducted an exemplar concordance analysis across five PICO queries spanning urology, cardiology, psychiatry, neurology, and endocrinology. For each exemplar, we compared the pipeline’s ranked candidate confounders for adjustment with confounders adjusted for in representative randomized and observational studies (Detailed in Table 2).

In exemplar concordance analyses (Table 2), many pipeline-suggested candidates fell into the same adjustment domains as confounders used in published randomized and observational studies. These concordances do not imply that the pipeline can replace expert causal modeling or distinguish confounders from mediators/colliders; rather, they suggest that an abstract-only, locally deployed RAG workflow can provide a practical starting point for literature-backed confounder scoping when full-text access or cloud-based inference is constrained. The approach is best viewed as an adjunct to, not a substitute for, domain expertise and explicit causal reasoning.

The structure of published evidence strongly shapes which candidate confounders are recoverable from abstracts

Across the 20 specialties, convergence speed and the likelihood of empty outputs followed how uniformly each field reports patient context. For OBGYN queries—where abstracts typically detail standardized treatment pathways like reproductive surgery or endocrine management—the process was notably efficient. The median processing time was 40.69 s (IQR 33.35; mean 38.68 ± 25.82 s per query), with only 6 zero-output cases identified out of 50. In contrast, neurologic and psychiatric topics—where disease presentations and abstract reporting vary widely—had the highest fractions of zero-output cases (20% and 14%, respectively). Domains with the longest average processing times included ophthalmology, otolaryngology, orthopedics and oncology, all exceeding 80 s per run on average, reflecting how heterogeneous conditions can leave abstracts with little structured information for the pipeline to surface.

The distribution of candidate confounder categories echoed the same pattern. Among 7,450 candidate entries, 30.35% were comorbidities and 26.30% were demographics, whereas lifestyle and socioeconomic factors together accounted for less than 5%. Because our pipeline can only exploit information that is encoded in abstracts, it preferentially recovers variables that are systematically collected and explicitly reported—such as demographics and major comorbidities—while more nuanced clinical details, granular social determinants of health (SDoH) and site-level factors are often omitted or buried in narrative text, making them difficult to normalize and therefore underrepresented in the output.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our knowledge base was restricted to PubMed abstracts and associated indexing metadata; while extensive, this abstract-only, representation excludes information that appears only in full texts, registries or electronic health records, and any omissions or biases in abstract reporting or indexing are inherited by the pipeline. Second, although our 1,000 expert-curated PICO queries span 20 major clinical specialties, they mainly reflect relatively simple treatment questions centred on one primary exposure and outcome; a subset of queries did include multiple outcomes (e.g., outcome1/outcome2/outcome3), but preliminary experiments suggested that performance was less reliable and we did not systematically optimize or evaluate the workflow for multi-endpoint or more complex design structures, such as time-varying interventions, multi-arm comparisons or prognostic modelling. Third, the SemMedDB baseline is limited by the coverage and structure of the SemMedDB snapshot (up to 2024) and by reliance on explicit semantic predications; because our PubMed mirror is current to July 2025, this temporal mismatch is an additional limitation of the comparative analyses. Finally, this work represents an exploratory system primarily focused on the scoping phase of clinical study design rather than downstream causal validation. While our evaluation confirmed internal properties—such as consistency, output volume, and clinician acceptance—and demonstrated concordance with selected published adjustment sets, it did not directly quantify the impact on bias reduction or the calibration of treatment effect estimates in real-world clinical datasets. As this is a preliminary investigation, the system is intended to augment expert scoping and should be followed by rigorous causal modeling (e.g., DAG development) to ensure the appropriateness of the final adjustment set.

Conclusion

In this study, we presented and evaluated an abstract-only, locally deployed RAG workflow for systematically scoping candidate confounders for adjustment from PICO-based clinical questions. Operating entirely on a locally mirrored PubMed abstract corpus, the pipeline achieved high internal consistency, produced clinically interpretable candidate confounder suggestions that aligned well with exemplar randomized and observational models across 20 specialties, and was judged acceptable and useful by a multi-institutional panel of clinicians. In an exploratory capacity, these findings suggest that such a workflow can meaningfully augment expert judgment in the early stages of observational study design, particularly in settings where access to full texts, large engineering teams, or commercial cloud services is limited.

Future work should extend this framework with full-text analysis to offset biases inherent to abstracts and to recover more nuanced clinical and SDoH variables. It will also be important to evaluate the downstream impact of pipeline-derived candidate confounder sets on effect estimates in real-world analyses and to systematically explore alternative LLM backbones and prompting strategies. More broadly, the continued development of secure, model-agnostic and locally deployable AI tools of this kind may help support more rigorous, transparent and reproducible clinical research across a broader range of healthcare environments.
