PAGE: an R package for network detection of multivariate error-prone gene expression data with the availability of auxiliary information
Authors: Li-Pang Chen, Wan-Yi Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGene expression data in bioinformatics studies often contain multivariate or high-dimensional variables. One key research problem is to uncover the network structure among gene expression variables, which can help identify pathway-level disruptions associated with diseases and support the development of targeted therapies. With the increasing availability of auxiliary variables (known as covariates), it is desirable to incorporate them to enhance network detection of the main variables (known as responses). The main challenge lies in accurately selecting informative covariates and recovering the network structure among responses, especially when using linear or nonlinear models to characterize the relationships between multivariate responses and covariates. Another challenge is the presence of measurement error in gene expression data, which may result from limitations in measurement precision or human recording errors.ResultsTo address these challenges and provide a reliable, publicly accessible analytical tool, we develop an R package named PAGE. This package includes three core functions that support measurement error correction, variable selection, and network estimation under both linear and nonlinear modeling frameworks. The application of PAGE is demonstrated using a yeast cell cycle dataset.ConclusionBased on the analysis and demonstration, we find that the R package PAGE is valid for dealing with the complex network structure. In addition, throughout the simulation studies, we find that the correction of measurement error is crucial, and the R package PAGE is useful to handle error-prone data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06477-1.

Introduction

Motivating data

The analysis of gene expression data is a fundamental task in bioinformatics, providing crucial insights into cellular mechanisms and disease pathogenesis. One major challenge in such analyses is the multivariate or high-dimensional gene expression profiles. In addition to the primarily interested variables, auxiliary multi-source data are available due to the data collection.

The motivating data in our study is the yeast cell cycle dataset initially analyzed by Chun and Keleş [12], available in the R package spls. This dataset integrates two high-throughput sources. The first is an mRNA expression dataset collected by Spellman et al. [32], measuring expression levels of 542 genes at 18 time points over a 119-minute period, synchronized using α-factor, labeled by “alpha0”, “alpha7”, …, “alpha119”. The second is a ChIP-chip dataset from Lee et al. [26], providing binding profiles for 106 transcription factors (TFs). Consequently, it indicates that the dimensions of mRNA expressions and TFs are 18 and 106, respectively, and the sample size is given by 542. However, as commented by Shedden and Cooper [31], the synchronization method, which is used to collect the mRNA variables, may introduce artificial cycle patterns that are unrelated to the true cell cycle, yielding the systematic measurement error. On the other hand, TFs in ChIP-chip data could be mismeasured due to some various factors, including non-specific antibody binding (e.g., Worsley Hunt and Wasserman [39]), indirect interactions (e.g., Farnham [14]), and a lack of understanding of TFs binding specificities. In other words, different from most existing literature pretending that variables are precisely measured, this fact relaxes such an implicit assumption and reveals that the collected mRNAs and TFs are measured with error.

Literature review

The objective of this study is to explore the underlying graphical structure of mRNA expressions, which captures pairwise dependencies among mRNAs. Understanding these networks enables the identification of co-regulated gene modules, key transcriptional regulators, and pathway-level disruptions associated with diseases, thereby facilitating biomarker discovery and the development of targeted therapies (e.g., Cai et al. [3]; Chen and Tsao [10]). As noted in Chun and Keleş [12], the TFs can be used to characterize mRNAs using a regression model. With the accommodation of TFs, one may need to identify informative TFs with respect to mRNAs. The other challenge is to deal with measurement error in mRNAs and TFs.

In the existing literature, numerous statistical approaches have been proposed for network detection by assuming that all variables are free of measurement error and ignoring the accommodation of auxiliary variables, such as the graphical lasso (glasso, Friedman et al. [17]), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _1$$\end{document}-penalized precision matrix estimation (clime, Cai et al. [5]), and a variety of other methods reviewed in Chen [9]. In the presence of measurement error, several correction strategies have been explored, such as Chen and Yi [11], Chen and Tsao [10], and Wainwright [35, 209].

With auxiliary variables treated as covariates, multivariate linear regression models can be used to characterize the relationship between mRNAs and TFs. With the absence of measurement error, Lee and Liu [27] and Wang [37] extended the Gaussian graphical lasso and conditional likelihood methods to simultaneously detect the network structure of responses and select informative covariates, respectively. With the involvement of measurement error, a recent paper Cui and Yi [13] introduced methods for correcting measurement error in multivariate covariates but kept the response precisely measured. Moreover, this approach does not take the detection of network structure of responses into account.

We can see that all existing R packages do not take the auxiliary variables into account. Contributions

The methodological landscape reviewed above reveals a gap: There is no unified approach that simultaneously addresses both measurement error in multivariate responses and covariates, detects network structures, and selects informative covariates. On the other hand, most existing methods focus on linear relationship between the responses and covariates, which could be restrictive and unrealistic in applications. Moreover, existing methods lack user-friendly implementations for practical applications.

To bridge this gap, we develop a new R package PAGE, known as Predictor-Assisted Graphical models under Error-in-variables. As summarized in Table 1, the R package PAGE is valid for detecting the network structure of the error-prone mRNAs and selecting informative mismeasured TFs for multivariate linear or nonlinear regression models. More specifically, the R package PAGE provides several strategies for variable selection and network detection in the presence of measurement error. For linear models, it offers two estimation procedures based on corrected likelihood functions that jointly select relevant covariates and estimate the conditional dependency structure among responses. For nonlinear models, PAGE employs random forest regression combined with regression calibration to adjust for measurement error, followed by an extension of the graphical lasso to recover the underlying gene network. In general, these tools are designed to be broadly applicable and computationally efficient and contain flexible arguments for the implementation, facilitating reproducible research in gene expression analysis and beyond.

Simulation results and interpretations

In this section, we implement the functions in the R package PAGE to analyze the simulated data. To see the impact of measurement error, we consider the following four settings:

EE: Both responses and covariates are subject to measurement error and without correction.

EC: Ignorance of correcting measurement error in responses but correction of measurement error in covariates.

CE: Ignorance of correcting measurement error in covariates but correction of measurement error in responses.

CC: The proposed methods with correction of measurement error in responses and covariates.

In particular, we note that the ‘EE” settings adopted to the functions Joint_Gaussian and Cond_Gaussian refer to the estimation methods proposed by Lee and Liu [27] and Wang [37], respectively.

Analysis of yeast cell cycle data

In this section, we apply the R package PAGE to analyze the yeast cell cycle data. The corresponding demonstrations of the R functions are included in Appendix D.2 of the supplementary materials.

Background descriptions and setup

In this section, we apply the estimation methods in the R package PAGE to analyze our motivating data: yeast cell cycle dataset, which is available in the R package spls. This dataset in the R package spls contains two multivariate variables. The first variable is the cell cycle gene expression collected by Spellman et al. [32] from an experiment synchronized using α-factor. It contains expression levels for 542 genes, with measurements taken every 7 min over a 119-minute period, resulting in 18 mRNA expression observations recorded by “alpha0”, “alpha7”, ⋯, “alpha119”. The second variable is the chromatin immunoprecipitation on chip (ChIP-chip) conducted by Lee et al. [26], which records the binding profiles of 106 transcription factors (TFs). Following the application in Chun and Keleş [12], our interest is to characterize the relationship between mRNA expression and TFs, then we treat them as responses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Y}$$\end{document} and covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {X}$$\end{document} with p = 106, m = 18, and n = 542, respectively.

In applications, as commented by Shedden and Cooper [31], the synchronization method, which is used to collect the mRNA variables, may introduce artificial cycle patterns that are unrelated to the true cell cycle, yielding the systematic measurement error. On the other hand, TFs in ChIP-chip data could be mismeasured due to some various factors, including non-specific antibody binding (e.g., Worsley Hunt and Wasserman [39]), indirect interactions (e.g., Farnham [14]), and a lack of understanding of TFs binding specificities. Consequently, measurement errors in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Y}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {X}$$\end{document} are ubiquitous and should be taken into account. Therefore, following the notation in Sect. 2.2 we denote \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {W}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}$$\end{document} as observed and error-prone cell cycle gene expressions and transcription factors, respectively. Moreover, we adopt the classical measurement error models (3) and (7) to characterize \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {W}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}$$\end{document}, respectively.

The goals of the data analysis include (i) correcting for measurement errors in both mRNAs and TFs, (ii) identifying the important TFs associated with mRNAs, and (iii) detecting the network structure of mRNAs. We aim to adopt three estimation methods in the R package PAGE and see the impacts of the estimation methods.

To implement the proposed methods for the correction of measurement error, we require knowledge of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\eta }}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\delta }}$$\end{document}. However, they are unknown and cannot be estimated because of the unavailability of auxiliary information. To address this issue, we conduct sensitivity analyses and specify \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\eta }}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\delta }}$$\end{document}.

According to Bryant et al. [1], the technical variation in high-quality microarray experiments can be relatively small. Consequently, we specify \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\eta }}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma }_{\boldsymbol{\delta }}$$\end{document} as diagonal matrices with common entries \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _\eta$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _\delta$$\end{document} specified as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\sigma _\eta ,\sigma _\delta ) = (0.003, 0.1)$$\end{document}, (0.005, 0.15), and (0.007, 0.2), reflecting minor, moderate, and severe effects, respectively. [19]; Forsberg and Ljungdahl [16]; Gasch and Werner-Washburne [18]), those selected TFs, including “MIG1_YPD”, “MSN4_YPD”, and “STP2_YPD”, primarily influence mRNA expression indirectly through their roles in environmental sensing and metabolic regulation. That is, rather than directly controlling the periodic expression of core cell cycle genes, these factors respond to external stimuli, such as glucose availability, stress conditions, and amino acid levels, and modulate the transcription of target genes accordingly.

On the other hand, due to the high-dimension of TFs (p=106), the functions in the R package GUEST need longer computational time since they deal with variable selection and modeling the relationship between the mRNAs and the TFs.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a new R package PAGE, whose main goal is to estimate the network structures of multivariate error-prone variables with the availability of auxiliary variables. Our R package is valid for characterizing the responses and covariates by linear and nonlinear models, and provides several novel estimation approaches. All functions in this R package provide flexible arguments for users to handle various scenarios. The application to the yeast cell cycle data also provides insights from a bioinformatics perspective, including the network structure of mRNAs and the impact on transcription factors when modeling their relationships.

The current development of this package primarily focuses on completed responses without missingness. In the future work, it is useful to extend our R package to accommodate detection of network structure with other features, such as incomplete responses caused by the censoring effects, which is also a challenge in multivariate survival analysis.
