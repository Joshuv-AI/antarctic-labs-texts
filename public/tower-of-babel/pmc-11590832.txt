Online registry of neonatal necrotising enterocolitis in Shenzhen: protocol for a multicentre, prospective, open, observational cohort study
Authors: Xin Guo, Jinxing Feng, Xiaoli Zhao, Erya Ying, Dong Liu, Huiying Tu, Yuqin Yan, Haiyun Huang, Xiaodong Li, Xianhong Chen, Shujuan Zeng, Yuehua Lin, Kanghua Zhou, Lian Zhang, Shihua Yuan, Qianshen Zhang, Fang Lin, Lei Dou, Yannan Chai, Yunxiang Cai, Rongtian Liu, Lichun Bai, Tumin Chen, Zhihua Chen, Zhangxing Wang, Yuefeng Li, Xuelei Gong, Xiaoyi Fang, Airun Zhang, Guanming Li, Zhuyu Liao, Cheng Chen, Jun Chen, Yanping Guo, Ying Liu, Hong Tang, Hanni Lin, Dan Dan Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNecrotising enterocolitis (NEC) of the intestine of preterm infants leads to the risk of abdominal surgery, short bowel syndrome, neurodevelopmental disorders and death. Although the risks of NEC and its complications have been recognised in many countries, few countries have established NEC-specific registries to continuously monitor its aetiology and prognosis. In China, the understanding of risk factors and prognosis of NEC is incomplete, characterised by a lack of evidence from prospective and multicentre studies. Therefore, we designed a multicentre, prospective, open observational cohort study with the aim of investigating the risk factors and prognosis of NEC in a real-world setting in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, by constructing an online registry of children with NEC and a bank of biospecimens.MethodsThis is a prospective, multicentre, open observational cohort study. From June 2024 to June 2028, more than 600 patients with NEC from 15 tertiary hospitals in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, will be enrolled in the study. By constructing an online registry for NEC, clinical data will be collected during the prenatal and hospitalisation periods. Prospectively, biospecimens will be collected during the period of suspected NEC, at the time of confirmed NEC, and at the time of confirmed severe NEC, and filed in the online registry system. Follow-up data will include postdischarge healthcare needs, growth patterns measures, eye or vision examinations, cranial MRI findings, brainstem auditory evoked potentials or automated auditory brainstem responses, and the Chinese Griffith Developmental Scale at corrected age 18–24 months. Follow-up results were likewise recorded in an online registry system. Hospitalisation outcomes, including severe NEC, somatic growth and survival status, will be collected at discharge. Follow-up outcomes will include loss to visit, survival status, somatic growth measures and severe neurodevelopmental deficits at corrected age 18–24 months. This study will enhance our overall understanding of the risk factors and outcomes of NEC, ultimately helping to reduce the incidence of neonatal NEC and its poor prognosis.Ethics and disseminationOur programme has received approval from the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research Projects of the Longgang District Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital in Shenzhen City (ethics approval number: LGFYKYXMLL-2024-47-01). We anticipate presenting our findings at various national conferences and submitting them to peer-reviewed paediatrics journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400085043.Keywords: NEONATOLOGY, SURGERY, Gastrointestinal imaging, Observational Study

Introduction

Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) is a prevalent gastrointestinal condition predominantly affecting premature infants, with an incidence rate of approximately 11.9 per 10 000 live births and about 10 per 1000 preterm deliveries.1 We conducted a survey of 25 neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) in Shenzhen during 2022. Our survey found that the prevalence of NEC among infants with gestational age (GA) less than 32 weeks was 2.63%.2 Premature infants who develop NEC are at increased risk for abdominal surgery, short-gut syndrome, neurodevelopmental impairment (NDI) and death.3 4 NEC and its serious complications have been reported in most parts of the world such as Asia,1 Africa,5 Latin America,6 Europe,7,9 and the USA.10 Preterm infants with NEC have a high mortality rate of about 20.9%, which has attracted a lot of attention from researchers.1 Preterm birth, low birth weight, small for GA babies and blood transfusions may increase the risk of neonatal NEC, whereas breastfeeding and probiotic use may decrease the risk of neonatal NEC.11 High lactate levels, congenital heart disease or patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) are associated with the severity or mortality of NEC in preterm infants.12 Quality improvement initiatives such as increased breastfeeding and conservative feeding during blood transfusions and treatment of PDA can reduce the incidence of NEC.13 Therefore, the identification of risk factors is critical to reduce the incidence of NEC.

Although many countries have recognised the dangers of NEC and its complications, few have constructed NEC-specific registries to continuously monitor its aetiology and prognosis. In 2017, the NEC Society in the USA established a multicentre collaborative NEC registration system and biosample bank, aiming to offer an all-encompassing platform for NEC research to share biological samples and clinical data.14 With advancements in technology and heightened awareness, nations are hastening to create comprehensive registries for newborn cohorts, using these databases to investigate the incidence and risk factors of NEC.10 15 16 However, the limitation of these databases is that they were not originally designed with NEC as the primary research focus. This inevitably necessitates the use of retrospective data mining, which can impact the reliability and validity of the research findings.

In China, the absence of a formal registry system hinders comprehensive studies on the aetiology and outcomes of NEC. China also lacks multicentre studies for NEC. A 2017 multicentre retrospective study conducted in China examined 188 patients with NEC and identified predictive factors for intestinal stenosis associated with this condition.17 However, the risk factors included in this study were not comprehensive and did not involve other important outcomes of NEC. Many studies have focused on NEC risk factors and prediction models, but most are single-centre, retrospective studies and the variables included are not comprehensive enough.18,22

From the aforementioned, it is evident that in the real-world context of China, the comprehension of the risk factors and consequences of NEC is not exhaustive, characterised by a paucity of evidence from prospective and multicentric research. Therefore, we designed a multicentre, prospective, open observational cohort study aimed at investigating the risk factors associated with NEC and their outcomes in a real-world setting in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province. This study will provide biomarkers and predictive tools that can be used to reduce the incidence of severe NEC and its complications. Furthermore, we have implemented an online registration system for neonatal NEC and established a biological sample repository to facilitate the achievement of these objectives.

Objective

The study’s primary objectives entail: (1) determining the incidence rate of severe NEC among children diagnosed with NEC; (2) evaluating growth patterns of NEC survivors; and (3) assessing the neurodevelopmental progress of children with NEC at corrected ages between 18 and 24 months.

The study’s secondary objectives include (1) investigating both short-term and long-term complications of NEC; (2) analysing risk factors for severe NEC, and subsequently developing a predictive model for the condition; (3) comparing the postdischarge growth patterns of children affected by surgical NEC compared with those treated for medical NEC; and (4) comparing the neurodevelopmental progress at corrected ages of 18 or 24 months among those with and without the diagnoses of surgical NEC.

Methods

Study design

This investigation is a multicentre, prospective, open, observational cohort study, scheduled to be conducted from 1 June 2024 to 1 June 2028. Based on the data from 2023, approximately 110 000 live births occurred in Shenzhen. This study aims to enrol over 600 children diagnosed with NEC from the NICUs of 15 tertiary hospitals (figure 1 illustrates the flowchart of the study design). All infants enrolled will undergo follow-up for a maximum duration of 24 months post discharge. The study protocol has received approval from the ethics committees affiliated with all participating centres. The ‘Clinical guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (2020)’, as promulgated by the Evidence-Based Medicine Group of the Neonatologist Society under the Chinese Medical Doctor Association, will guide patient management at all centres.23 The Longgang District Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City spearheaded the design and initiation of this study. It also serves as the coordinating centre and is the affiliate institution of the initiator (CC), coordinator and principal investigator (PI; XG). To enhance the generalisability of findings, an additional 14 centres within Shenzhen were invited to participate. Each centre averages about 10 cases of NEC annually and possesses extensive experience in managing the condition. Some are equipped to perform surgical interventions for NEC, allowing for internal transfers of surgical patients, which facilitates continuous data tracking. Each centre has designated three neonatologists to serve as the data collection recorder, data verifier and sample collection technician, respectively. Given the open nature of the study, we extend a warm invitation to qualified centres to join during the recruitment phase. Our research team conducts regular seminars aimed at summarising project advancements, upholding quality standards, and recruiting additional research centres. On the integration of new centres, a thorough evaluation is conducted to verify their compliance with the study’s eligibility criteria. To facilitate seamless project execution, we provide comprehensive support to these new entities, focusing on equipping research coordinator with an in-depth understanding of the study’s goals, methodology, adherence to the research protocol, quality assurance measures and efficient data-sharing practices. This programme references the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (see online supplemental material 1 for details).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the general public were not involved in the design of this study. We will establish a community advisory board, which includes parents of newborns and expectant parents. During the implementation phase of the study, we will seek input from community advisory board members regarding the research design. Should they provide reasonable suggestions, the research team will convene meetings to deliberate on their adoption and potential revisions. Participation is entirely voluntary. The results will be disseminated to parents and the public through academic publications, public health education and neonatal conferences.

Informed consent

This study aims to collect clinical data and biological samples from mothers and newborns. The relevant details will be thoroughly outlined in the informed consent form, ensuring full disclosure to parents to obtain their written consent. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Fifteen hospitals in Shenzhen have established a multicentre research consortium focused on NEC. The participating centres are evenly distributed across various districts in Shenzhen, including Longgang District Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City (Longgang Maternity and Child Institute of Shantou University Medical College), Shenzhen Children’s Hospital, Nanshan Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital, Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, Shenzhen People’s Hospital (The Second Clinical Medical College, Jinan University; The First Affiliated Hospital, Southern University of Science and Technology), Shenzhen Nanshan People’s Hospital, Longgang District Central Hospital of Shenzhen, Shenzhen Baoan Women’s and Children’s Hospital, Shenzhen Luohu People’s Hospital, The University of Hong Kong‐Shenzhen Hospital, Southern University of Science and Technology Hospital, Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University), Shenzhen Guangming District People’s Hospital, People’s Hospital of Longhua, Shenzhen Longhua District Maternal and Child Health Center. Between 1 June 2024 and 1 June 2028 (a duration of 4 years), all newborns receiving NEC treatment at these facilities will be considered for inclusion in the study. Participation or withdrawal from the study will not interfere with standard clinical care for the patients.

Criteria

The inclusion criteria comprise NEC that meets stage 2 or higher (‘confirmed NEC’) according to the Bell staging criteria, with informed consent obtained from the parent.24 With reference to the characteristics of Bell I, ‘suspected NEC’ was defined as the presence of slow heart rate, apnoea, lethargy, increased gastric retention, positive faecal occult blood test, bloody stools, abdominal distension, vomiting and/or crying on abdominal palpation during quiet time in newborns initiating enteral feedings for no apparent reason, at which time plain abdominal radiographs may show a mildly dilated or normal intestinal tube. Cow’ milk protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome will be excluded. Individuals with fewer than one follow-up examination will also be excluded. Congenital gastrointestinal malformations, such as duodenal, jejunal atresias, Hirschsprung’s disease, and so on, will likewise be excluded.

Definitions of outcomes

During hospitalisation, the outcomes examined included severe NEC, survival status at discharge (either alive or deceased) and growth patterns at the time of discharge. Severe NEC was characterised by its requirement for surgery or its role as a cause of infant death.25 At the time of discharge, the evaluation indicators for growth patterns include height, weight, head circumference, the rate of growth patterns change during hospitalisation and extrauterine growth restriction.26 The follow-up results encompassed the rate of loss to follow-up, the postdischarge survival status, the difference in the changes of z-scores for weight, height and head circumference from discharge to the correction age of 18–24 months, healthcare needs between discharge and 18–24 months corrected age and a comprehensive neurodevelopmental evaluation at corrected age of 18–24 months. We will assess weight, height and head circumference of patients with NEC at corrected age 18–24 months and growth rate from discharge to corrected age 18–24 months.27 The rate of change in growth patterns is determined by the difference in z-scores for weight, height and head circumference between two distinct time points. Severe NDI was defined as bilateral blindness, hearing impairment requiring amplification, inability to walk 10 steps with support, cerebral palsy and/or total scores of Griffiths Development Scales-Chinese (GDS-C)<75.28 The assessment of healthcare needs includes postdischarge medical support (tracheotomy, ventilator, oxygen, gastrostomy, nasogastric tube feeding, apnoea, or cardiopulmonary monitor), postdischarge surgical procedures and any medical readmission requiring an overnight hospitalisation (except for surgical reasons such as respiratory conditions (including apnoea), nutritional or growth retardation (change in weight-for-age z-score of <−0.67 SD between birth and assessment was used to define growth retardation), seizure disorders, shunt complications, meningitis, urinary tract infection, gastrointestinal infection, respiratory infection or other).

Data collection and management

The study will recruit patients in a prospective manner and the recruitment phase is expected to end in 2028. Each participating centre and its patients will have a unique identifier. Clinical information and biospecimen storage serial numbers will be collected according to the Neonatal Necrotizing Enterocolitis Online Registry using the online database of Perinatal Cloud (Collaborative Perinatal Neonatal Platform, https://perinatalcloud.com/), which has been established by our collaboration group. In addition, all data in the online database will be entered by trained and qualified data collection recorders. Data checkers will provide regular quality control and feedback on the data. The online database allows for timely modification and updating of information, as well as real-time export of data for quality control and statistical analysis. In addition, the collaborative group developed a workflow for NEC that allows for more accurate collection of clinical information, biospecimens and follow-up results.

This study aims to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the information and data contained within the database. The findings will be published in a deidentified format to ensure confidentiality. In addition, the coordinating centre will ensure the management and operation of the online registry and data security. The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC), comprising the PI of the coordinating centre and the PIs of the 19 participating hospitals, is responsible for monitoring the implementation of the study in accordance with the study protocol. If necessary, the DMC will submit a plan for modification of the study design to the ethics committee and implement the modifications after approval. During the study period, regular meetings will be held to assess the progress of the study and whether there are any deviations from the protocol. The DMC is also responsible for recruiting participating hospitals, providing study materials, organising training meetings, checking and analysing data, and publishing study reports. The ethics committee, which is independent of the applicants and researchers, will review the progress of the study annually.

Data elements

In order to achieve our goal, we have carefully designed the Neonatal Necrotizing Enterocolitis Online Registry to collect the necessary information (online supplemental material 2), which will include the following:

1. Basic information: birth hospital, birth time, admission mode, infant’s registration number, infant’s name, infant’s mother’s registration number and infant’s mother’s name.

2. Mother-related information: gestational diabetes, pregnancy-induced hypertension, premature rupture of membranes ≥18 hours, multiple pregnancies, caesarean section, use of corticosteroids before childbirth, antibiotics before childbirth within 24 hours and chorioamnionitis.

3. Newborn-related information:

3.1 Birth information: sex, GA (weeks), birth weight (grams), small for GA, Apgar 1 min score, Apgar 5 min score and Apgar 10 min score.

3.2 Comorbidity information: neonatal pneumonia, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, respiratory failure, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, congenital heart disease (excluding PDA), PDA, shock or haemodynamic need for vasoactive drugs to maintain, hyperbilirubinaemia, gastrointestinal perforation or combined peritonitis, intracranial haemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia, sepsis (clinical sepsis or blood culture positive sepsis), sclerosis, metabolic acidosis and thrombocytopenia (<100×109/L).

3.3 Feeding information: feeding method within 24 hours before the onset of NEC symptoms (total enteral nutrition, partial enteral nutrition or total parenteral nutrition), feeding content within 24 hours before the onset of NEC symptoms (breastfeeding only, mixed feeding of breast milk and formula, formula feeding only) and feeding volume within 24 hours before the onset of NEC symptoms (mL/kg).

3.4 Laboratory data: Hgb (g/L) within 7 days before the onset of NEC, minimum Hgb (g/L) during NEC, minimum pH value within 7 days before the onset of NEC, Hgb (g/L) at the onset of NEC, lactate (mmol/L) at the onset of NEC and highest lactate (mmol/L) during NEC.

3.5 Diagnostic information: Bell Staging at NEC, severe NEC, surgical NEC, NEC that resulted in death, survival status at discharge and date of death.

3.6 Growth patterns information: the length and head circumference at birth, the weight, length and head circumference at 14 days after birth, the weight, length and head circumference at 28 days after birth, and the weight, length and head circumference at discharge.

3.7 Risk factors associated with treatment: number of blood transfusions, days of fasting, use of ibuprofen and days on mechanical ventilation and days of antibiotic use.

3.8 Hospitalisation information: date of discharge, hospital stay (days) and hospital costs (RMB) .

4. Registration of biological samples: blood samples, breast milk samples, faecal samples, urine samples and saliva samples. All samples are recorded biological sample number, time of samples collection and time of storage of samples.

5. Follow-up:

5.1 The rate of loss to follow-up (number of lost to follow-up/total number of follow-ups × 100%).

5.2 Survival from hospital discharge to corrected age of 2 years and date of death.

5.3 The alterations in growth patterns subsequent to discharge: weight at corrected age 18–24 months, length at corrected age 18–24 months, head circumference at corrected age 18–24 months and corrected age at measurement.

5.4 The assessment of healthcare needs: postdischarge medical support (tracheotomy, ventilator, oxygen, gastrostomy, nasogastric tube feeding, apnoea and/or cardiopulmonary monitor), postdischarge surgical procedures, and any medical readmission requiring an overnight hospitalisation (except for surgical reasons such as respiratory conditions (including apnoea), nutritional, growth retardation, nutritional, growth retardation, seizure disorders, shunt complications, meningitis, urinary tract infection, gastrointestinal infection, respiratory infection and/or other).

5.5 A comprehensive neurodevelopmental evaluation at corrected age of 18–24 months: severe NDI, blindness (one eye and/or both eyes), automatic auditory brainstem response, brainstem auditory evoked potential, hearing impairment requiring amplification, amplitude-integrated electroencephalogram, cranial MRI, inability to walk 10 steps with support, cerebral palsy and total scores of GDS-C.

Collection and processing of biological samples

Faecal, urine, saliva and plasma samples will be collected from infants with NEC, while breast milk samples will be obtained from their mothers. The collected samples will initially be stored at 4°C before being transferred to −80°C for long-term freezing. Stool, urine, saliva and plasma samples should be collected within 24 hours before the diagnosis of NEC (‘suspected NEC’ period), at the time of diagnosis of NEC, and at the time of diagnosis of severe NEC, respectively.29 Breastmilk samples are collected within 24 hours prior to the diagnosis of NEC (the suspected NEC period), provided the infant is breastfed prior to the suspected NEC. The number of the sample and the specific time of collection and storage will be documented in the sample container and in the online database. This study requires obtaining informed consent from both the mother and her infant on enrolment. Following the procurement of consent, we will collect biological samples expeditiously. Given the vulnerability of neonates, our objective is to limit blood draws to the essential minimum required for diagnostic tests. Should the infant’s health status contraindicate immediate phlebotomy, the child’s immediate medical welfare supersedes the need for blood samples. All samples are collected by the neonatal nurses and frozen by the researchers. To obtain baseline characteristics of the case sample information, we also select controls. Controls were selected after the diagnosis of NEC and matched to cases from the same centre on the basis of GA at birth (plus or minus 1 week), birth weight (plus or minus 100 g) and sex.29 Each case will be matched to 1–4 controls, with no fixed ratio. Preference will be given to NEC<Bell II or non-NEC infants who met the matching criteria from the suspected NEC population as controls, thus ensuring homogeneity of sample collection. If controls are to be selected from the non-suspected NEC population, samples will be collected at an age as close as possible to the onset of the matched case. The selection of the control group will be concurrently conducted with the collection of neonatal NEC cases. Currently, we are unable to use existing infant biobanks as a control group.

Sample size estimation

The primary objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive description of the prognosis of infants with NEC. Since this is an observational study, sample size estimation is not usually necessary. Therefore, we will calculate the sample size for the logistic regression of risk factors for severe NEC. We anticipate testing 5–10 variables as potential risk factors. Therefore, based on the criterion of at least 10 events per variable, we will need 100 infants with severe NEC.30 31 A systematic evaluation summarised an overall mortality rate of approximately 23.5% (lower than the surgical rate) among all neonates diagnosed with NEC.32 Therefore, assuming a loss to follow-up rate of 20%, a sample size of at least 532 NEC cases would be required. We anticipate that more than 600 patients will be recruited for this study.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, this will be presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), and for categorical variables, it will be presented as frequency or percentage. Independent Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test will be used for continuous variables and χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression will be used to assess the relationship between NEC and prognosis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis will be used to identify independent risk factors affecting the prognosis of NEC. When the outcome variable is missing, it should be removed directly. For other missing variables, k-nearest neighbours can be employed to fill in the gaps. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results by employing various statistical models and by excluding specific infant characteristics. Various machine learning methods such as logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, plain Bayes, artificial neural networks and support vector machines will be used to construct predictive models. To reduce systematic differences in the results, we will analyse the data as a whole and the participating centres separately. R-V.4.3.3 statistical software will be selected for statistical analysis and visualisation. P values <0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Other countries have established disease-specific online registries for NEC to continuously monitor its aetiology and prognosis.14 In China, however, there is no such online registry for NEC and there is a lack of multicentre studies focusing on NEC. The advantage of an online registration system for special diseases is that it can collect a large amount of data, which can help in the development of quality improvement programmes.33 Big data analysis that aggregates multicentre data also points to new directions for future NEC research. Therefore, we designed an online registry system for neonatal NEC in Shenzhen City. To advance research on the diagnosis and treatment of NEC in newborns, our team intends to share pertinent datasets with collaborating research groups in the future.

This protocol describes the online registration and biospecimen bank establishment of neonatal NEC in Shenzhen, aiming to investigate the risk factors and prognosis of NEC. Systematic documentation will help us to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the prognosis of NEC. This study will establish for the first time in China a prospective, multicentre, open, observational, disease-specific database of NEC, which will promote the process of homogenisation of NEC diagnosis and treatment and make the management of NEC more standardised. By exploring risk factors, predictive tools to prevent NEC, growth failure and severe NDI, this study will recommend intensive surveillance of high-risk infants. Through extensive mining of biological sample data, we can identify biomarkers that are pivotal for both the prevention and treatment of NEC. This approach not only enhances our understanding of NEC’s pathogenesis but also directs future research endeavours. In addition, this study will provide information on the long-term prognosis of patients with NEC treated with conservative medical and surgical treatments in order to optimise individualised treatment for patients with NEC.

Limitations of observational studies are unavoidable, such as the possibility of a large number of lost respondents. To address existing issues, this study is scheduled during routine health follow-up visits periods for high-risk infants, aiming to mitigate parental aversion resulting from frequent visits. In addition, parents were reminded by telephone if infants did not participate in follow-up examinations during the corrected age of 18–24 months.

This study aims to enhance our understanding of NEC risk factors and outcomes, with the goal of preventing severe cases, growth issues and NDI. Additionally, it seeks to reduce postdischarge healthcare needs and refine personalised care strategies.

Ethics and dissemination

Our programme was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Longgang District Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City. Each participating centre will be required to obtain approval from the centre’s ethics committee prior to beginning recruitment. All patient parents will be fully informed about the study and informed consent will be obtained and signed by the parent prior to recruitment. Participating hospitals will share the results and accomplishments of the study. The results of the study will be published in national conferences and peer-reviewed paediatric journals.
