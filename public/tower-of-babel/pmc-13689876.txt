Guiding Pacemaker Implantation With Multimodality Imaging in an Adult With Ventricular Septal Defect
Authors: Amarpal Karamjit Singh, Charles Eastwood, Chiew Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case SummaryA 57-year-old man with a history of congenital ventricular septal defect and bicuspid aortic valve with surgical repair of aortic coarctation presented with dyspnea and found to be in complete heart block with junctional escape rhythm necessitating pacemaker implantation.DiscussionPacemaker implantation in adult congenital heart disease and atrioventricular block requires preprocedural planning. Multimodality imaging is important in patients with complex septal anatomy, as it can reduce procedural difficulty and improve procedural safety by identifying optimal lead positioning strategies.Take-Home MessageMultimodality imaging can facilitate safer and more precise pacemaker implantation in adult patients with congenital heart disease with abnormal septal anatomy.

Case Summary

A 57-year-old man presented with progressive dyspnea and was found to have complete heart block (CHB). His background included surgical repair of aortic coarctation in infancy, with residual congenital cardiac abnormalities comprising a ventricular septal defect (VSD) and bicuspid aortic valve. Echocardiography demonstrated a small left-to-right VSD shunt with Qp/Qs ratio of 1.2 (Supplemental Figure 1) and normal left ventricular size and function. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated satisfactorily functioning bicuspid aortic valve (Figure 1A) and the long-axis view demonstrated ascending aortic dilatation of 47.5 × 46 mm and muscular VSD (orange arrowhead, Figure 1B) at the junction of the basal and mid anteroseptum. Two fixation zones with a narrow rim of viable myocardium superior (10-mm thickness) highlighted by a green arrowhead and inferior (13-mm thickness) highlighted by blue arrowhead to the VSD for ventricular lead deployment were identified on CMR. No late myocardial gadolinium hyper enhancement was detected to indicate replacement fibrosis or infiltrative disease as a cause for CHB. Computed tomography (CT) coronary angiography showed mild coronary artery disease only and provided high-resolution delineation of complex septal and great vessel anatomy for safe left subclavian approach. The ventricular lead (Boston Ingevity) was inserted into the right ventricular outflow septum, targeting the inferior VSD segment. After several failed attempts for a suitable landing site from advancement through the VSD and lack of current of injury, the ventricular lead was eventually deployed into the superior myocardial segment of VSD highlighted with the corresponding cross-sectional view (red arrowhead, Figure 1C). Current of injury was assessed from the lead-tip unipolar electrogram after lead fixation. Absence of ST-segment elevation indicated a lack of myocardial injury response and potentially inadequate tissue engagement. Atrial lead was inserted into the right atrial appendage (cyan arrowhead, Figure 1C). The position of the ventricular lead was confirmed on fluoroscopy (Figure 1D) with a left bundle branch pattern QRS morphology (Figure 1E) compared with intrinsic QRS morphology (Figure 1F). The VSD on a 3-dimensional-rendered image is shown in Figure 1G. Patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) with CHB are at risk of sudden cardiac death especially in complex cardiac anatomy such as transposition of great arteries and tetralogy of Fallot. Pacemaker implantation guided by multimodality imaging in ACHD is recommended and warrants careful consideration due to risks of lead malposition, systemic embolization, and procedural complexity related to abnormal septal anatomy, as seen in this case.1 Preprocedural planning with CMR and CT for potential landing zones of ventricular lead is essential here as several fixation attempts were expected due to the muscular VSD. Although not used in our case, CT and CMR 3-dimensional models can be integrated into the cath lab fluoroscopy for real-time navigation to guide lead implantation. This would negate a trial-and-error approach. Alternatively, real-time anatomical integration of VSD using intracardiac echocardiography may offer shorter procedure time with fewer fixation attempts.2 Last, leadless pacemaker performed via the femoral approach may be a promising choice over the transvenous pacemaker in cases of venous malformation.3

This case highlights the importance of multimodality preprocedural imaging for successful and safe pacemaker lead placement and avoidance of complications in ACHD.
