Epidemiological investigation of Chandipura virus disease outbreak, Gujarat, India, July 2024: a matched case-control study
Authors: Amit Pritam Swain, Betsy Varghese, Sowntappan Balasubramanian, Ngangom Kanchan, Aathira V Dev, Kamlesh Mandriya, Jariwala Devang Ashvinkumar, Jayesh M Katira, Harshad P Prajapati, Jayesh Solanki, Nilam Patel, Ramesh Chandra, Himanshu Chauhan, Atul Goel, Tanzin Dikid

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChandipura virus disease (CHPVD), caused by the sandfly-transmitted Chandipura virus, is a major public health concern due to its reported explosive outbreaks of Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES) and high mortality in children. We aimed to describe the clinico-epidemiological characteristics of the cases and identify risk factors, following laboratory confirmation of CHPVD in Gujarat, India, in July 2024.MethodsWe defined case as laboratory-confirmed CHPVD in clinical presentation of AES in a child aged under 15 years from Gujarat in July 2024. We identified cases through enhanced passive surveillance and compared them to age and place-matched controls. We randomly selected two controls (same and neighbouring village) with no fever and seizures or altered sensorium in previous month and aged +/-two years from case-age. We collected data using structured questionnaire. Severe underweight was weight-for-age z-score < -3 Standard deviation. We calculated geometric-mean (GM) for laboratory investigations, adjusted OR (aOR) using conditional logistic regression with Firth penalization. We conducted an entomological survey for sandflies in and around case household.ResultsWe identified 54 cases with median age of 4 years [Interquartile-range (IQR) = 2–7] and 59% males in 20/33 districts. Case fatality rate was 50%. Rural cases were 81%, 72% had domestic animals, and 34.8% were severely underweight. Clinical manifestations were fever (87%), convulsion (74%), vomiting (70.4%), altered sensorium (57.4%), and loose stools (48%). Median duration between symptom onset and hospital admission was 1 day (IQR:0–2), and from hospital admission to death was 3 days (IQR:2-5.75). WBC count (GM = 15295/mm3), SGOT (GM = 121.7U/L), APTT (GM = 48.9 s) and Lactate dehydrogenase (GM=657U/L.) were elevated. CSF showed lymphocytic predominance, and protein was 63 mg/dL. Underweight status in child (aOR = 4, 95%CI = 1.4–14.3) and poor housing structures and conditions (aOR = 2.66, 95%CI = 1.71–4.93) were significant risk factors. We collected sandflies (Sergentomyia species) from dwelling units while insecticidal control measures were underway.ConclusionThis investigation highlights widespread nature and high mortality from CHPVD outbreak among children in Gujarat. Clinical findings were consistent with acute encephalopathy and disseminated systemic infection. Being underweight and poor housing conditions were risk factors. Background

Chandipura virus disease (CHPVD) is an emerging arboviral disease caused by Chandipura virus (CHPV), which was first identified in 1965 in Chandipura village, Maharashtra, India [1]. This single-stranded RNA virus has been detected in regions of Sri Lanka, Africa, including Nigeria, and Senegal [2–4]. In India, outbreaks of CHPVD have been reported from Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Bihar in the past two decades [5–10]. Outbreaks typically manifest as Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES) in children under 15 years, characterized by the sudden onset of high-grade fever, altered sensorium, seizures, and rapid neurological deterioration [11, 12]. Rapid disease progression with a high case fatality of 28–78% is a characteristic feature of these outbreaks [12]. The disease spectrum and its epidemiology are currently not well understood.

CHPV has been occasionally isolated from sandflies (Phlebotomus argentipes and Sergentomyia), collected near human habitats, suggesting its role in transmission [11, 13–17]. There is no evidence of direct human-to-human transmission. Neutralizing antibodies to CHPV have also been documented in animals such as camels, horses, donkeys, buffalo, rhesus monkeys, sheep, and goats in endemic areas, highlighting its zoonotic potential [18]. Due to its outbreak potential and high case fatality among children presenting with acute encephalitis, CHPVD is a major public health concern.

Current investigation: On 16 July 2024, the state of Gujarat submitted a first information report of laboratory-confirmed CHPVD cases and deaths to the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP). These cases were identified through the IDSP-AES surveillance. Following the CHPVD outbreak confirmation, AES passive surveillance was enhanced in all tertiary and district hospitals. On clinical suspicion, AES cases were subjected to CHPV RT-PCR or IgM ELISA testing. On receiving the information, NCDC conducted a risk assessment and, based on the intensity of the outbreak, a multidisciplinary team of seven members was deployed on 23 July 2024. The team comprised of epidemiologists and entomologists to assist the state with the investigation in Gujarat.

We investigated the outbreak and aimed to describe the clinico-epidemiological characteristics of CHPVD cases. We conducted a case-control study to identify risk factors.

Methods

Descriptive epidemiology

Case definition for acute infections

We defined CHPVD cases as laboratory confirmed for CHPVD by either RT PCR or IgM ELISA in a child under the age of 15 years with clinical presentation of AES in Gujarat during 1 to 31 July.

Data collection

We extracted cases from the AES surveillance line-list that met the case definition. We reviewed their inpatient and laboratory records (parameters at three different time points) to gather data on clinical details, laboratory findings, referrals, complications, and outcomes. We used a semi-structured questionnaire, which was developed by the team for the outbreak investigation to collect information from the parents or caregivers regarding sociodemographic, household conditions, behavioural practices, and environmental exposures (Supplementary material 1).

Based on the descriptive epidemiology, we hypothesised that cases with behaviours and housing conditions favouring exposure to infected sandfly bites have higher odds of developing the disease compared to non-cases.

Matched case-control study

We conducted a 1:2 age-matched case-control study. A control was child aged ± 2 years of the case with no acute onset of fever or new onset seizure or altered sensorium in last one month, as reported by the parents/caregiver recall and triangulated with the fever survey records available with the field healthcare workers. Using the auxiliary nurse midwife family register for the sampling frame, we randomly selected one control from the same village (> 500 m) as the case and another control from a neighbouring village.

We used a semi-structured questionnaire to collect data on socio-demographics, immunisation, nutritional status, housing, domestic animals, sleeping habits, protective behaviours and peri-domestic environmental conditions. For nutritional status, we collected the weight of the child and calculated the weight-for-age and compared it with the WHO growth chart [19]. We assessed the type of housing construction, including material for flooring (cement/mud/tiled), roof (asbestos/concrete/thatched) and wall (plastered/mud wall/only brick) of the house. We also assessed for the presence of windows in each room, dampness and cracks in the house. We captured self-reported data for the presence of vegetations, farmlands, rat burrows, waste disposal, and firewood stacks peridomestically.

Sample size: We included all the CHPVD cases and two controls per case. A post-hoc power analysis was done to see the adequacy of the sample included by assuming 50% exposure prevalence among controls and a correlation coefficient of 0.2 for exposure between matched pairs, to detect an Odds Ratio (OR) of 3.0 at a 5% level of significance (α = 0.05).

Entomological survey

We conducted entomological survey in and around the house of the cases. Sandflies were collected using oral aspirator. Approximately one hour was spent in each house, and the sandflies collected were counted for calculating Per Man-Hour Density (PMHD). The sandflies were transferred to test tubes and sent to Gujarat Biotechnology Research Centre (GBRC)/National Institute of Virology (NIV). Sandfly samples from 4 districts were brought to NCDC, Delhi for identification. The sandfly samples brought to NCDC were mounted in a slide using Hoyers’ mounting medium and identified using the taxonomical identification Key by Lewis (1978) using a compound microscope [20, 21].

Data analysis

We analysed quantitative data using median with interquartile range (IQR) and qualitative data using frequency and proportions. We calculated the monthly per capita family income and categorized into four groups. We calculated weight for age scores for children below 10 years, and they were classified as normal weight, moderately underweight (z score=-2SD to -3SD) or severely underweight (z score < -3SD) based on WHO classification. We calculated the geometric mean for the laboratory data across different time periods (the first available laboratory results, the last available result and one result available in middle of the first and last reports) of the disease course for each case, and further representative geometric mean was calculated for different laboratory parameters for all cases. We compared the sociodemographic variables of cases and controls using conditional regression and a p-value of below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We used R software to calculate the univariate conditional odds ratio between exposure and outcome at 95% confidence interval (CI). Collinearity was assessed using Phi coefficient and Spearman correlation to identify correlated variables. Interrelated exposures were then aggregated into composite domains based on biological plausibility and public health actionability. The domains were housing quality (combining house with temporary structures, absence of windows in rooms, presence of exposed cracks in walls, presence of dampness in walls), domestic and peridomestic conditions (combining owning domestic animals, presence of cattle sheds inside homes, presence of vegetations around house, presence of firewood, disposed waste around house), high risk behaviour among children (combining children sleeping outdoors, not covering themselves while sleeping, child sleeping in same room as domestic animals, child going in shrubs for playing/defecation). Severely underweight and moderately underweight were grouped as underweight. The composite domains along with income (potential confounder) and nutritional status were included in the multivariable model. The model was developed by conditional logistic regression with Firth penalisation to address small sample bias. We calculated the adjusted conditional odds ratio (aOR) at 95%CI. The Events Per Variable (EPV) ratio was calculated by using the number of matched sets as events. Model discrimination was evaluated by the c-statistic (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), and goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Penalized Likelihood Ratio Test against the null model. We performed sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the associations in the final model. The full multivariable model was compared against multiple reduced models by sequentially omitting exposure variables of the final model (‘leave-one-out’ approach).

Ethical statement

Since the investigation was carried out with the purpose of identifying the risk factors of CHPVD during the outbreak and to institute immediate control measures to prevent morbidity and mortality, ethical approval was not required as the investigation was conducted as part of public health response and consistent with applicable state and central government law (Epidemic Diseases Act no.3, 1897). Strict data protection protocols reviewed by NCDC, ethical principles and guidelines by the Government of India, and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards were followed during public health response. Informed consent was taken from all the participants and from the parents or legal guardians for all participant under the age of 16 years before interview.

Results

Descriptive epidemiology of cases

We identified 54 CHPVD cases in this outbreak. These cases were distributed across 54 villages in 44 blocks of 20 districts (Fig. 1). No village had more than one case. One block (2.3%) had four CHPVD cases, seven blocks (15.3%) had two cases each, and the remaining 36 blocks (81.8%) had one case each.

Place distribution of CHPVD cases across multiple districts in Gujarat, July 2024

The index case had symptom onset on 1 July 2024 and was laboratory confirmed on 16 July 2024. Following this, the state health authorities issued letters and standard operating procedures for vector control measures (indoor residual spraying and malathion dusting) on 19 July 2024. The cases peaked on 20 July, followed by a gradual decrease by 31 July 2024. (Fig. 2). The case fatality rate was 50% (27/54).

Epicurve showing the time distribution of CHPVD cases based on the onset of symptoms in Gujarat, July 2024

The median (IQR) age of the cases was 4 (2-7) years, with 87% (47/54) of the cases under 10 years of age. Around 59% (32/54) of the cases were males, and 31.5% (17/54) belonged to the scheduled tribe. Severe underweight (weight-for-age) was observed in 34.8% (16/46) of the cases, and 30.4% (14/46) were underweight. Almost 82% (44/54) of the cases resided in rural areas, with 59.3%(32/54) of case households surrounded by agricultural farms. Domestic animals were owned by 72% (39/54) of the case households.

Clinical characteristics of cases

Among 54 cases, major symptoms reported were fever (87%), convulsions (74%), vomiting (70%), unconsciousness (59%), altered sensorium (57%) and loose stools (48%). For 61% cases, fever was the first symptom, then vomiting (18%) and new onset convulsions (6%). Seizures were generalized tonic-clonic and associated with up-rolling of eyeballs. Vesicular rashes were only reported in 7% cases.

We examined the aggregate laboratory parameters of the cases; a rise in the total leucocyte count (15294.5 per cu.mm), and liver function tests (SGOT = 121.7 U/L, SGPT = 104.3 U/L) was seen. Coagulation studies showed PT of 23.1 s with an INR of 1.7, and an APTT of 48.9 s. Inflammatory markers were elevated (LDH = 657.0 U/L, CRP = 4.0 mg/L, serum ferritin = 412.9 ng/mL, and D-dimer = 6.9). The CSF profile (median CSF cell count = 5, IQR = 3–13 cells/cu.mm, polymorphs = 0, glucose = 59, IQR = 44–81 mg/dl and protein = 62.8, IQR = 28–107 mg/dl) was consistent with viral etiology (Table 1).

The median duration between symptom onset and admission to tertiary care facility was 1 day (IQR = 0–2). Median duration from symptom onset to death was 3 days (IQR = 2-5.75) vs. discharge was 8 days (IQR = 4–12). Median number of health facilities visited before admission at tertiary care facility was 2 (IQR = 2–3). All the 27 deaths were reported in hospital. The sandflies were collected from 14 dwelling structures and 17 non-dwelling structures, which included cattle sheds, abandoned structures around the house, electric pump rooms, and toilets outside houses. Discussion

We conducted a clinico-epidemiological investigation of a CHPVD outbreak in Gujarat, western state of India, between 1 and 31 July 2024. The outbreak was defined by a rapid clinical course, high case fatality. Cases were geographically non-clustered, involving 20/33 districts. This study identified risk factors related to nutrition, and housing conditions. Entomological investigation demonstrated high sandfly density and predominance of Sergentomyia species in case households, suggesting potential ecological conditions conducive to CHPV transmission. Several studies are currently in progress to improve our understanding of the pathogenesis and epidemiology of CHPVD cases; this investigation provided information on the affected population group, risk factors and the likely vector habitats for targeted intervention to prevent future outbreaks.

The first documented outbreak of CHPVD in Gujarat was reported in the eastern districts of Panchmahal and Vadodara in July 2004, and since then, cases have been reported in the IDSP, indicating active transmission pockets in the area [8, 22]. This outbreak involved the majority of the districts, and cases were geographically non-clustered. Past outbreaks reported in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Gujarat reported a similar pattern [5–8]. This pattern may suggest the presence of undetected or asymptomatic infections; however, this was not evaluated in detail to ensure an active transmission chain. A similar epidemiology is seen in Japanese encephalitis outbreaks, which clinically presents as AES predominantly in children, serological surveys have observed large number of asymptomatic cases per AES case detected [23, 24].

We observed the median age of the affected children was four years, with more than half of the cases occurring under five years of age. This pattern of early childhood period being impacted aligns with findings from earlier studies [5, 7]. While the exact reasons are not known yet, the age-dependent susceptibility to CHPVD in young individuals can be explained immunologically to the reduced number and functionality of innate and adaptive immune cells, poorer production of immune or impaired responses to inflammatory stimuli, compared to adults. This immaturity can lead to an inability to effectively clear virus, allowing it to cross the blood-brain barrier, ultimately causing severe neurological symptoms [25, 26].

Our clinical and lab findings align with other outbreaks reported in India, with case fatality range from 29 to 78%, and in the majority of the death cases, there was rapid progression to death within 24 to 48 h [5–8, 10]. Laboratory investigations demonstrated deranged coagulation parameters and inflammatory biomarkers with CSF findings consistent with viral encephalitis. While the human molecular pathophysiology requires further research, experimental animal models suggest that the disease severity may have been influenced by trigger of the proinflammatory cytokines in response to CHPVD [26, 27].

Clinical diagnosis of CHPVD is challenging due to non-specific symptoms, further complicated by inadequate knowledge about CHPVD among treating physicians. Further clinical studies are required among hospitalized cases to identify blood markers of severity, and evaluating response to treatment modalities for mortality reduction.

This was the first systematic epidemiological study to identify risk factors for acquiring CHPVD. Cases had four times higher odds of undernutrition compared to controls, indicating that compromised immune function in undernourished children increases their vulnerability to infections [28].

A statistically significant higher proportion of cases than controls reported living conditions with absence of windows in living rooms, presence of visible cracks in the house and presence of vegetation in the surrounding. The living rooms were used for multipurpose activities including sleeping at night. Poor housing conditions with mud walls, especially those with cracks and holes, and inadequate ventilation provide ideal resting site habitat for vector insects like sandflies. These structures provide the necessary protection and microclimate that sandflies prefer, including humidity and warmth, while also shielding them from direct sunlight [29, 30]. Environmental factors such as the presence of vegetation and organic matter in the surroundings of the case habitat may have influenced sandfly density [31, 32].

The entomological survey from the peridomestic and domestic sites identified an abundance of sandflies of Sergentomyia genus. Surge in sandflies of Sergentomyia genus within outbreak clusters provides critical data on sandfly diversity and the entomological profile during the transmission period which was also seen in previous outbreaks in Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra [17, 33]. The identification of Sergentomyia babu in our investigation aligns with previous entomological assessments in neighbouring state of Maharashtra [33]. Sergentomyia species are largely zoophilic in nature; increase in its population density and breeding/ resting in close vicinity of humans increases possibility of anthropophilic adaptation [32]. We also observed sandflies above six-feet from ground, in contrast to their typical resting habitat, of up to six-feet from ground [34]. We collected sandflies from cattle sheds and abandoned structures around the house, which is similar to their usual ecology pattern during this season [31, 34].

Our study has a few limitations. We collated clinical data from multiple sources which had varying levels of completeness; to minimize errors and ensure completion, we consulted treating physicians for verification of clinical and lab findings. The absence of laboratory confirmation for controls raises the possibility of a non-random misclassification bias, which may result in underestimation of effect size between identified exposures and outcome. While enrolling controls from the same and neighbouring village, we may have inadvertently introduced spatial matching; although loss of statistical efficiency in identifying exposures as risk factors occurs, it does not threaten internal validity. Also, methodological strength of this investigation was matched case control analysis with development of concept-driven model paired with Firth’s penalized likelihood estimation to address the sparse-data bias. Entomological assessment conducted after indoor residual spraying may have underestimated the true sandfly density.

Public health action: We recommended sensitizing healthcare workers regarding early identification, health communication to address undernutrition and housing conditions, protection against sandfly bites, and enhanced vector control measures.

Conclusion

CHPVD is an emerging arboviral AES with outbreak potential, characterized by high case fatality due to multisystem involvement with children as at-risk group. Severe underweight and poor housing conditions were identified as significant risk factors. There is an urgent need to improve our understanding of the disease eco-epidemiology for targeted prevention and control, its pathogenesis for developing treatment strategies for better clinical outcomes. Augmenting surveillance and diagnostic infrastructure will improve early case detection.
