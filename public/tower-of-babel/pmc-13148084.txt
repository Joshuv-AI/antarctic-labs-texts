Effects of acute HMB-FA supplementation on antioxidant status and muscle damage in Elite Judoka: a randomized pilot trial
Authors: Seyed Morteza Tayebi, Meraj Mirzaei, Jeffrey R. Stout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHigh-intensity judo bouts induce oxidative stress and muscle damage, impairing recovery. This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial examined whether acute β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate free acid (HMB-FA) supplementation influences muscle damage, oxidative stress, and antioxidant biomarkers in elite judoka.MethodsTwenty-two male elite judoka were randomized to HMB-FA (1.0 g BetaTOR®) or placebo. Supplements were ingested after four simulated Olympic judo bouts. Blood was sampled pre-exercise, post-fourth bout, and 2 h post-bout for creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC). Twenty-one completed the protocol (one placebo dropout due to injury). Data were analyzed via 2 × 3 mixed ANOVA with partial η².ResultsNo group differences were seen for CK, MDA, or TAC (p > 0.05). Significant group × time interactions were observed for CAT (p < 0.001), GPX (p = 0.031), and SOD (p = 0.025), indicating greater enzyme increases in HMB-FA (CAT + 54%, GPX + 38%, and SOD + 16%) than placebo (+7–14%). LDH declined in both groups (interaction, p = 0.001), but more so in the placebo group (−29.9%) than in the HMB-FA group (−18.6%).ConclusionAcute post-exercise HMB-FA supplementation in elite judo athletes did not significantly influence muscle damage or oxidative stress markers but transiently increased antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, GPX, and SOD). These findings suggest an enzyme-specific effect and highlight the need for future studies using more precise oxidative-stress markers and cellular assays to better evaluate antioxidant responses; however, these effects should be interpreted with caution due to potential limitations related to assay sensitivity and the timing of measurements.

Introduction

Judo is recognized as a globally established sport, practiced by over 20 million people in more than 200 countries [1]. It is an Olympic high-intensity intermittent sport that places extreme physiological stress on athletes, often resulting in significant oxidative stress and muscle damage during competition. Intense efforts in repeated matches generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [2–4], which can impair muscle function and promote fatigue [5]. In pursuit of better recovery, athletes and sports scientists have turned to nutritional strategies to counteract exercise-induced oxidative damage and muscle protein breakdown.

β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate (HMB) is a leucine-derived metabolite that has been studied for nearly three decades for its muscle-protective effects [6,7]. Early work demonstrated that HMB can attenuate exercise-induced muscle proteolysis and damage, leading to improved training outcomes [7–9]. HMB is thought to work by both enhancing muscle protein synthesis and reducing protein breakdown [10]. Two supplemental forms exist: calcium-bound HMB (HMB-Ca) and free acid HMB (HMB-FA) [10]. Notably, HMB-FA achieves higher plasma concentrations more quickly than HMB-Ca (peak ~36 vs 131 min), which may be advantageous for acute recovery following intense bouts [11].

Despite HMB's documented benefits [10], most research has focused on continuous endurance [12–14] or resistance exercise [13–15]; no studies have examined the effects of HMB-FA in combat sports, such as judo. Judo's unique format (multiple matches in a day with short recovery intervals) presents a scenario where a fast-acting supplement could be impactful. Limited recent studies suggest that HMB-FA can reduce muscle damage indicators in athletes—for example, short-term HMB-FA administration lowered post-exercise CK and LDH levels in wrestlers and soccer players [16–18]—but its efficacy under judo-specific demands remains unknown.

Therefore, the purpose of this exploratory pilot designed study was to investigate whether acute HMB-FA supplementation could attenuate muscle damage and oxidative stress after a simulated Olympic judo competition (four successive bouts). In this study, CK and LDH (muscle damage markers) as well as TAC and MDA (oxidative stress markers) were considered as primary outcomes, while antioxidant enzymes (CAT, GPX, and SOD) were reported as secondary or exploratory outcomes. Such findings suggest that HMB-FA helps maintain redox balance and muscle integrity, potentially mitigating fatigue in elite judokas.

Methods

Participants and design

This study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial with repeated measures. A total of 24 male elite judo athletes were initially recruited from clubs in Tehran and Alborz provinces, of whom 22 (mean age, 20.5 ± 2.1 years) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study; two were excluded for not meeting the criteria. Inclusion criteria were ≥4 years of competitive judo training (national level) and age between 18 and 28 years. Exclusion criteria included any injury or condition preventing full effort in competition. Allameh Tabataba'i University approved the study, and all participants provided written informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [19]. Participants were randomly assigned (blocked randomization) [20] to HMB-FA (n = 12) or placebo (n = 10) using a computer-generated random list, with allocations sealed in opaque envelopes prepared by an independent co-coach. Based on practical feasibility and previous similar studies [21,22], we aimed to recruit ~20 athletes. Therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. An independent assistant prepared identical capsules for HMB-FA and placebo, ensuring blinding of both participants and investigators to group allocation. Participants were instructed to maintain their normal training load up to 48 h before the trial and to avoid any other supplements during the study.

Simulated competition protocol

We adapted the University of Mary Wrestling Anaerobic Performance Test (UMWAPT) [23] for judo to simulate the demands of an Olympic competition. The protocol consisted of four consecutive bouts of judo-specific activity, each separated by rest intervals similar to those found in tournament progression (30 min between Bout 1 and 2, 30 min between Bout 2 and 3, and 60 min between Bout 3 and 4). Each bout included: (a) Sled push (30 s)—athletes pushed a weighted sled (2.5 × body mass) as fast as possible in multiple directions, mimicking gripping, and driving against an opponent. (b) High-intensity throwing drills—immediately after sled pushes, athletes performed judo throws at maximal speed: Ippon Seoi Nage in Bouts 1–2 (2 min of repeated throws, ~20–25 throws), and Kesa Gatame transitions in Bout 3 (90 s, ~20 repetitions). The selected techniques—Ippon Seoi Nage and Kesa Gatame—were chosen based on their biomechanical advantages and high frequency of use in judo competitions. Biomechanically, these techniques enable the judoka to grip the opponent with one hand and utilize rotational movement, allowing for a quick transition to the next engagement. Additionally, due to their widespread application in competitive settings and the fact that most judokas can perform them effectively, these techniques were considered appropriate and relevant for inclusion in the training protocol [24]. (c) Active rest (20 s)—light movement between cycles. Each bout's total work time was ~3 min. Athletes completed all four bouts in a single day (approximately 9:00–16:00) with standardized warm-up and rest periods as noted. This protocol was designed to reflect the intermittent, high-intensity nature of judo competition and ensure all participants experienced comparable workloads.

Dietary control and supplementation

To minimize nutritional variance, all athletes received a standardized breakfast at 7:30 am on test day, consisting of whole-grain bread, boiled eggs, a banana, and milk (~550 kcal, 60% carbohydrate, 15% protein, and 25% fat) [25]. Light snacks (nuts, yogurt, and fruit) were provided in small portions between bouts to maintain energy without GI discomfort [26]. HMB-FA supplementation: Immediately after completing Bout 4 (the final bout), participants were administered two gelatin capsules under researcher supervision. HMB-FA group capsules each contained 500 mg of β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate free acid (BetaTOR®, Metabolic Technologies Inc.), for a total acute dose of 1000 mg. The dose and timing of HMB-FA administration were chosen to reflect practical, real-world supplementation scenarios in elite athletes, aiming to assess ecological validity rather than maximal efficacy. Placebo capsules contained 500 mg of inert contents (an edible filler with a few drops of water to match the weight/texture). Neither the athletes nor the assessors knew the contents of the capsule. This timing (post-bout 4) corresponds to approximately 2 h before an Olympic final match, thereby testing the supplement's effect on short-term recovery.

Blood sampling and outcome measures

Venous blood samples (5 mL) were drawn from an antecubital vein at three time points: (1) Baseline—30 min before Bout 1 (resting), (2) Post-Bout 4—within 5 min of completing the fourth bout (before supplement ingestion, if any), and (3) 2 h Post-Bout 4—two hours after the final bout (coinciding with the typical wait before a hypothetical final). Samples were collected with participants in a supine position for ~15 min, allowed to clot at room temperature, and then centrifuged (3500 rpm, 10 min) to isolate the serum. Serum aliquots were stored at –80 °C until analysis. We assayed the following biomarkers of muscle damage and oxidative stress: creatine kinase (CK, U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L) via enzymatic kinetic methods at 340 nm; malondialdehyde (MDA, nmol/mL) via thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay (absorbance 550 nm) as an index of lipid peroxidation; catalase (CAT, nmol/min/mL) by the Aebi method (peroxidatic activity, read at 540 nm); glutathione peroxidase (GPX, nmol/min/mL) via a NADPH-coupled reaction (340 nm); superoxide dismutase (SOD, nmol/min/mL) via inhibition of pyrogallol auto-oxidation; and total antioxidant capacity (TAC, mmol/L) via the ferric reducing ability of plasma (FRAP) assay at 593 nm. All assays used commercial kits (Faraz Sanat, and Iran) and were run in duplicate on a DIRUI CST-240 autoanalyzer, following manufacturer instructions. Intra-assay coefficients of variation for these assays ranged from 3% to 7%.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. We used a mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA framework to evaluate the effect of HMB-FA on each outcome over time. For each biomarker, a 2 (group: HMB-FA vs placebo) × 3 (time: baseline, post-4th bout, 2 h post-4th bout) mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to test for main effects of group, time, and their interaction. In addition, we conducted baseline-adjusted repeated-measures ANCOVA analyses, in which baseline values were included as a covariate and post-4th bout and 2 h post-4th bout values were entered as repeated measures, to account for potential baseline imbalances. Where Mauchly's test indicated no violation of sphericity (or in two-time-point analyses where it is inapplicable), no correction was applied. Significant group × time interactions were followed up with post-hoc comparisons (e.g. independent t-tests at specific time points or within-group changes) with a Bonferroni adjustment when multiple comparisons were made. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared (η²) for ANOVA/ANCOVA effects, with η² ≈ 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively. The alpha level was set at 0.05 for statistical significance. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp). A correction for multiple comparisons was not applied due to the exploratory nature of this pilot study, consistent with recommendations for small-sample preliminary research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Twenty-one athletes (12 in the HMB-FA group and nine in the placebo group) completed the study (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics did not differ between groups (Table 1). Mean ± SD age was 21 ± 1 vs 20 ± 1 years (placebo vs HMB-FA), and training status was comparable. Baseline serum markers of muscle damage and oxidative stress were also similar between groups (e.g. baseline CK ≈ 150 U/L in both; no significant differences, data not shown).

Muscle damage markers (CK, LDH)

Figure 2 illustrates CK responses. CK increased in both groups from pre- to post-exercise and remained elevated at 2 h, with no significant interaction or group effect [F (1, 18) = 3.913, p = 0.063, η² = 0.179]. HMB-FA group CK rose from ~478 U/L (post-bout) to 521 U/L at 2 h (+9%) (mean 298.1, 95% CI 287.5 to 308.7), while placebo rose from ~467 to 502 U/L (+7.5%) (mean 334.4, 95% CI 322.1 to 346.8), a statistically similar change. In contrast, LDH showed a significant group × time interaction [F (1, 18) = 16.50, p = 0.001, η² = 0.48]. Both groups had elevated LDH levels immediately after the bouts (~610–638 U/L), which declined by 2 h (mean 468.4, 95% CI 415 to 521.7) (Figure 3). However, the placebo group experienced a larger LDH reduction (from 638 to 447 U/L, –29.9%) (mean 420.9, 95% CI 359.2 to 482.7) compared to the HMB-FA group (610 to 497 U/L, –18.6%). At two hours post-exercise, LDH levels were 497 vs 447 U/L in the HMB-FA and placebo groups, respectively; the between-group difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.07).

Oxidative stress markers (MDA, TAC)

Neither lipid peroxidation (MDA) nor total antioxidant capacity (TAC) showed significant group differences over time. MDA levels actually decreased in both groups during recovery (interaction F (1, 18) = 0.012, p = 0.914, ƞ2 = 0.001). For example, HMB-FA MDA was 51.8 nmol/mL post-bout and 42.7 nmol/mL at two h (–17.6%) (mean 42.8, 95% CI 37.8 to 47.7), while the placebo went from 46.3 to 39.9 nmol/mL (–13.4%) (mean 39.2, 95% CI 33.4 to 45) (Figure 4). These similar declines indicate exercise-induced oxidative stress subsided similarly with rest in both groups. TAC increased over time in both groups (reflecting an expected acute phase response), with no significant interaction (F (1, 18) = 2.534, p = 0.129, ƞ2 = 0.123). HMB-FA group TAC rose from 1.4 to 1.8 mmol/L (+28.6%) (mean 1.7, 95% CI 1.6 to 1.9), and placebo from 1.6 to 1.9 mmol/L (+18.7%) (mean 1.8, 95% CI 1.7 to 2) (Figure 5). The slightly greater TAC increase in HMB-FA was not statistically significant.

Antioxidant enzymes (CAT, GPX, and SOD)

HMB-FA was associated with greater changes in antioxidant enzyme activities during recovery (Figures 6–8). Catalase (CAT) activity exhibited a significant group × time interaction [F (1, 18) = 20.48, p < 0.001, η² = 0.53]. Immediately post-exercise, CAT was ~2.4 U/mL (HMB) versus 2.7 U/mL (placebo); by 2 h, CAT surged to 3.7 U/mL in the HMB-FA group (mean 3.6, 95% CI 3.4 to 3.9), compared to a modest increase to 2.9 U/mL in the placebo group (mean 2.9, 95% CI 2.6 to 3.2). The 54% increase in CAT with HMB-FA was significantly greater than the 7% increase with placebo (p < 0.001 for interaction; between-group difference at 2 h: p = 0.002). Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) activity also rose more in HMB-FA (from 0.42 to 0.58 U/mL, +38%) (mean 0.49, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.52) than in placebo (0.42 to 0.48, +14%) (mean 0.44, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.48), with a significant interaction [F (1, 18) = 5.493, p = 0.031, η² = 0.23]. SOD showed a similar pattern: HMB-FA group increased from 365 to 425 U/mL (+16%) (mean 429.5, 95% CI 414.1 to 444.8), whereas placebo increased from 337 to 365 U/mL (+8%) (mean 367.7, 95% CI 349.8 to 385.5), interaction F (1, 18) = 5.965, p = 0.025, η² = 0.25. By the 2-h recovery mark, both GPX and SOD were higher in the HMB-FA group than in the placebo (GPX: 0.58 vs 0.48 U/mL, SOD: 425 vs 365 U/mL; interaction-driven differences, p < 0.05). These findings indicate greater post-exercise increases in CAT, GPX, and SOD in the HMB-FA group compared with placebo during the recovery period.

No other significant effects were observed. Notably, aside from the one injury-related withdrawal in the placebo group, no adverse events were reported. The HMB-FA supplement was well tolerated (no gastrointestinal issues or other complaints were noted).

Discussion

This randomized pilot trial explored the acute effects of HMB-FA on oxidative and muscle damage, as well as antioxidant markers in elite judo athletes, and provides preliminary evidence in this area. We observed indices of oxidative damage (e.g. MDA and TAC) changed similarly between groups. Additionally, HMB-FA did not significantly attenuate the rise in traditional muscle damage markers (e.g. CK), and LDH declined more in the placebo. By contrast, larger 2-h increases in serum CAT, GPX, and SOD activities were observed in the HMB-FA group compared with placebo following a single post-exercise dose. However, the observed enzyme elevations may partly reflect methodological or transient physiological effects rather than true enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defenses. Our hypothesis was thus only partially supported: findings indicate an enzyme‑specific pattern rather than a global enhancement of antioxidant defense or a significant effect on primary markers of muscle damage with acute HMB‑FA.

Judo competition places extreme demands on athletes [27], often leading to muscle damage and oxidative stress [27,28]. Nutritional countermeasures are of great interest. HMB has a well-established role in reducing exercise-induced muscle damage and promoting recovery in various settings [8,9]. Previous HMB-Ca studies demonstrated reduced post-exercise CK and soreness, especially in untrained individuals or with chronic supplementation [7,29]. More recent work with HMB-FA (the more bioavailable form) has shown promising results. For example, Wilson et al. [30] reported that trained men taking 3 g HMB-FA had significantly lower CK 48 h after intense resistance exercise and improved perceived recovery. Similarly, HMB-FA improved recovery of muscle function in a drop-jump protocol by 24 h post-exercise [31]. In team sports, short-term HMB-FA supplementation has been shown to attenuate increases in CK and LDH during intensive training in soccer players [16,17]. In our study, however, the acute low-dose HMB-FA did not meaningfully blunt CK or LDH elevations. This discrepancy may be due to the timing and dosage: we gave HMB-FA only after exercise, aiming to mimic practical supplementation rather than to achieve maximal effect. In contrast, many studies administer it before or throughout exercise and often at a higher total dose (3 g). HMB‑FA may require higher doses or longer exposure to influence membrane stability or proteolysis [32]. Additionally, the well‑trained status of the athletes may have limited detectable reductions in muscle damage [33].

The LDH findings were unexpected. We observed that the placebo had a slightly greater reduction in LDH over the 2 h than HMB-FA. HMB‑FA may theoretically reduce LDH leakage by stabilizing muscle membranes via the HMG‑CoA/cholesterol pathway [10,34,35]. Because both groups rested and received nutrition (snacks) post-exercise, LDH naturally declined, and any additional effect of HMB-FA was small. A single post‑exercise dose may also have been too late to influence LDH release. Some mechanism of HMB requires time—For instance, incorporation into muscle cell membranes or modulation of proteolytic pathways [32]—which an acute dose might not immediately achieve. Thus, our data do not provide clear evidence of an acute membrane‑protective effect. Future studies could assess LDH at earlier points (e.g. during exercise or <1 h post-dose) to see if HMB-FA can speed LDH clearance or reduce peak levels.

Notably, our CK results are consistent with reports showing limited effects of HMB in well‑trained athletes. For instance, Gonzalez et al. [36] found no reduction in inflammatory cytokines or CK with HMB-FA (plus cold-water immersion) after resistance exercise. Hoffman et al. [37] similarly reported no benefit of HMB-Ca on CK or performance during an intensive training camp in football players. Well‑trained athletes may require greater stimulus or longer supplementation to show benefits.

Regarding oxidative damage markers, MDA and TAC changed similarly between groups, which may reflect methodological limitations rather than a true absence of antioxidant effect. TBARS (used for MDA) is known to lack specificity and can be influenced by many non-lipid peroxidation compounds. The fact that MDA dropped similarly in both groups does not necessarily indicate comparable oxidative stress; it just means the method may not be sensitive enough to detect group differences [38]. Additionally, FRAP-based TAC is heavily influenced by uric acid (often 60%–70% of the signal). Post-exercise purine metabolism can increase urate independently of enzymatic antioxidant systems, potentially masking or mimicking treatment effects [39,40]. Consequently, the absence of group differences in MDA or TAC is therefore not strong evidence for or against an HMB-specific antioxidant effect.

On the other hand, an important preliminary observation of this study is the greater post-exercise increase in antioxidant enzyme activities following acute HMB-FA administration. However, these antioxidant enzyme outcomes are considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and not confirmatory. Further, given the multiple comparisons in this pilot study, the possibility of type I error inflation should be acknowledged, and these findings should be interpreted cautiously. To our knowledge, this study provides preliminary evidence of a possible association between acute HMB-FA ingestion and higher post-exercise serum CAT, GPX, and SOD activities in vivo in athletes, which requires confirmation in larger studies. It is tempting to attribute the acute enzyme changes to rapid up-regulation of cytoprotective programs (e.g. Nrf2-dependent genes, PGC-1α-linked signaling) [10,41]. However, the 2-h window after ingestion is typically too short for transcription–translation–maturation processes to produce measurable increases in circulating antioxidant enzyme activity in humans, particularly in serum rather than cellular fractions [42,43]. Red blood cells (a major source of SOD1, CAT, and GPx1) are anucleate and cannot mount de novo synthesis on this timescale [44]. Moreover, our post-exercise (pre-dose) sample serves as the true anchor for the treatment contrast; values at that draw differed slightly between groups for some enzymes, which increases the likelihood of regression-to-the-mean contributing to subsequent changes. Interestingly, a 6-week study by Arazi et al. [15] found that resistance training alone increased CAT and SOD, and adding HMB-FA did not further boost those adaptations. It should be noted that even mild hemolysis increases measured serum/plasma antioxidant enzyme activities and can interfere with spectrophotometric assays [45]. We did not quantify a hemolysis index (free hemoglobin), leaving residual confounding possible.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size (n = 21 completers) was small, which limits statistical power. The trial was intended as an exploratory pilot; therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in a larger cohort. Second, we examined only acute responses in a simulated setting. Real competitions involve psychological stress and varying opponent dynamics, which we did not replicate; whether HMB-FA would similarly aid recovery in actual competition conditions is unknown. Third, we did not measure actual performance outcomes or perceptual fatigue. While biochemical markers provide insight into recovery, the ultimate question is whether HMB-FA helps athletes perform better in subsequent matches—a question that future studies should address (e.g. via sustained sparring performance or time-to-exhaustion tests after supplementation). This limitation restricts the practical and real‑world applicability of our findings for sports nutrition and athletic performance. Fourth, our intervention consisted of a single 1 g dose administered post-exercise. This dosing was chosen to mirror a practical scenario, but it may be suboptimal. Chronic daily supplementation or pre-exercise dosing might yield different results. Fifth, only male judoka were studied; sex differences in response to HMB cannot be ruled out. Notably, a small study by Hung et al. [46] found no benefit of short-term HMB-Ca on power in female judoka undergoing weight cut. Female athletes and those in energy deficit might respond differently. Lastly, the biochemical assays have inherent limitations—e.g. the TBARS assay for MDA is not perfectly specific—and we relied on serum markers rather than direct muscle assays. Future work employing muscle biopsies could verify intramuscular effects on oxidative stress and damage (e.g. measuring 4-HNE or protein carbonyls in muscle tissue) [38]. FRAP is also a measure of reducing capacity dominated by uric acid; post‑exercise purine metabolism can elevate urate and inflate TAC independently of enzymatic defenses [39,40]. It is worth noting that serum activities of CAT, GPX, and SOD are vulnerable to hemolysis‑related release from erythrocytes and to matrix/optical interference; assays in erythrocyte lysates (normalized to hemoglobin) or standardized cellular fractions mitigate these issues.

Our study provides preliminary evidence of an association between acute HMB-FA supplementation and higher post-exercise serum antioxidant enzyme activities in combat sport athletes and should be interpreted with caution until confirmed in larger, adequately powered trials. In addition, given the above limitations, we advise caution. HMB-FA should not be seen as a standalone solution for recovery, but it could complement other strategies (proper diet, hydration, rest).

Conclusion

In summary, this double-blind randomized pilot trial provides preliminary evidence by exploring HMB‑FA supplementation in elite judo athletes. We interpret the larger 2‑h increases in serum CAT, GPX, and SOD after HMB‑FA cautiously, given the time course, matrix/assay considerations, and the lack of corroboration from oxidative‑damage and TAC indices.

Looking ahead, larger and adequately powered trials should explore both acute and chronic HMB-FA supplementation in athletes. In particular, it is recommended that incorporating a hemolysis index (or plasma-free hemoglobin) and applying pre-specified rules for sample exclusion or adjustment is essential. Furthermore, more specific oxidative-stress markers (e.g. F2‑isoprostanes or protein carbonyls) and cellular readouts (e.g. PBMC Nrf2-HO-1/NQO1 transcripts) should be included to more precisely assess antioxidant responses. Finally, assay interference tests by spiking pooled serum with HMB‑FA at expected concentrations and re-running CAT/GPX/SOD assays would strengthen the validity of the findings.
