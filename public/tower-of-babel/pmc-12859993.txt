Cryogenic microcarrier-assisted stem cell storage (Cryo-MASCS): a preservation method enabling scalable culture of human mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Carolina Rivera-Crespo, Leonel Velez-Román, Miosotis Acevedo-Esquilin, Heath J. Mills, Olivia Gámez Holzhaus, Maribella Domenech-García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microcarrier (µC) suspension systems enable closed, high-density manufacturing of human mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), but current workflows remain labor-intensive because they require post-thaw cryoprotectant removal and static culture steps to allow cell attachment. To address these bottlenecks, we developed a cryogenic microcarrier-assisted stem cell storage (Cryo-MASCS) workflow that integrates cell attachment, cryopreservation, and thawing directly on surface-engineered µCs. MSCs were pre-seeded onto µCs coated with stacked heparan sulfate-collagen bilayers, cryopreserved on-carrier in either conventional dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-supplemented medium or a DMSO-free, serum-free (SF) minimally supplemented medium, and then thawed and returned directly to suspension culture without intermediate processing. We optimized cell seeding density to maximize recovery. Following thaw, MSCs on engineered µCs and cryopreserved in either DMSO-containing or DMSO-free media retained viability comparable to traditional suspension cultures in DMSO-containing medium, while remaining attached to the carrier surface. Surface-engineered µCs show increased MSC yield within seven days in SF medium, significantly outperforming commercial collagen-coated µCs. Moreover, MSCs recovered metabolic activity and retained robust suppression of lipopolysaccharide-induced M1 macrophage polarization after IFN-γ priming. These findings demonstrate that direct cryopreservation of MSCs on heparan sulfate-collagen-coated µCs is compatible with both DMSO-free and DMSO-supplemented conditions and supports streamlined, scalable culture of undifferentiated MSCs for translational applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04871-9.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a prime cell source for regenerative medicine and tissue engineering, owing to their robust self-renewal capacity and multi-lineage differentiation potential [1–4]. With the recent FDA approval of Ryoncil, the first allogeneic bone marrow (BM) MSC therapy in the United States intended for graft-versus-host disease in pediatric patients, alongside hundreds of clinical trials exploring MSC-based treatments [5–7], the demand for large-scale, efficient cell expansion methods has never been higher. Historically, two-dimensional (2D) culture systems have served as the backbone of MSC cultivation; however, these approaches are time-consuming and labor-intensive, yielding relatively low cell numbers.

In response to escalating clinical needs, three-dimensional (3D) culture platforms, particularly those employing microcarriers (µCs), are increasingly recognized as a more scalable option for producing adherent cells [8, 9]. µCs provide a high surface area-to-volume ratio in bioreactors, enabling MSC proliferation within a more compact footprint. Despite these advantages, transitioning from small-scale laboratory experiments to clinically relevant production volumes remain a significant challenge. Ensuring robust cell attachment under agitated bioreactor conditions introduces additional complexity, as shear forces can disrupt MSC adherence and inhibit growth. Consequently, many researchers revert to static cultures, which prove unsuitable for large-scale manufacturing.

To address the need for amenable freezing methods on adherent cell cultures, Ji et al. [10] reported a cryopreservation technique for human embryonic stem cells in which cells were frozen as adherent, intact colonies on their culture plates rather than suspended colonies. This approach improved post-thaw survivability and reduced spontaneous differentiation; however, maintaining hESC colonies attached to plates at a large scale is not a practical use of freezer space, nor is it compatible with scale-up systems that use suspended cultures. In a related effort, Nie et al. [11] demonstrated that culturing and cryopreserving hESCs while attached to µCs is feasible. Their findings showed that the cells maintained self-renewal and pluripotency, with growth rates comparable to those achieved on traditional Matrigel-coated plates. Nonetheless, this method relied on dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a cryoprotectant, requiring additional steps to remove or dilute it to meet therapeutic standards.

Conventional protocols for seeding MSCs onto µCs or flat surfaces typically involve detaching cells from adherent cultures or harvesting them from suspension after cryopreservation in media supplemented with 5–15% DMSO. Each of these steps carries risks, including potential cell loss, contamination, and prolonged post-thaw recovery times, ultimately undermining cell viability, potency, and yields. Moreover, MSCs cryopreserved with DMSO have shown lower therapeutic activity than fresh cells due to increased apoptosis and impaired immunosuppressive activity [12, 13], which threatens critical therapeutic traits such as immunomodulatory function, differentiation potential, and trophic factor secretion. Consequently, a pressing need exists for streamlined cryopreservation strategies in adherent cell culture platforms that minimize handling, reduce cell stress, and preserve these intrinsic cellular attributes. The implementation of such methods will accelerate a truly scalable MSC production.

Building on our prior work with µCs engineered with collagen (COL)-heparan sulfate (HS) bilayers that preserve growth factors and support integrin-mediated adhesion and survival under serum-free (SF) conditions [14], we extend this chemistry to cryopreservation and present a µC-based workflow, named Cryogenic Microcarrier-Assisted Stem Cell Storage (Cryo-MASCS), that eliminates cryoprotectant washout and re-seeding steps. In this study, we focused on adipose-derived (AD-MSCs) and BM-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs), the two most widely used MSC tissue sources in both clinical trials in the United States and µC-based biomanufacturing pipelines [15–19]. In previous work, we established the performance of both MSCs on HS/COL-coated µCs [14], laying the foundation for their inclusion in the present Cryo-MASCS workflow. MSCs were inoculated onto either conventional COL I-coated plastic µCs or our previously characterized HS/COL-coated plastic µCs, then cryopreserved and evaluated for post-thaw recovery across media formulations (± DMSO, ±serum). After thawing, µC suspensions were returned directly to agitation and expanded to production density. Across conditions, post-thaw viability and growth kinetics matched or, in some cases, exceeded those achieved with standard suspension freezing in 10% DMSO. This method shortens the path from cryo-storage to suspension culture on µCs, providing a practical, scalable route to faster, more reproducible manufacturing of MSC therapeutics.

Methods

Multilayer deposition on µCs

Tissue culture-treated polystyrene (TCP) µCs were coated in multilayers of high-sulfated recombinant HS (HS29, TEGA Therapeutics) and lyophilized COL type I from the bovine tendon (Integra Lifesciences Holdings Corporation, Añasco, PR), through a Layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly. HS29 (4 µg/mL) and COL (1 mg/mL) were solubilized in acetate buffers prepared using sodium acetate and glacial acetic acid. The pH for HS29 and COL solutions was adjusted with ultrapure deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm) to 5 and 4, respectively. Multilayer coatings of HS29/COL were deposited over culture surfaces after an initial 15-minute incubation with solubilized polyethyleneimine (PEI) (P3143, Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in pH 5 buffer. Each polymeric layer was deposited and incubated for 5 min, followed by a 3-minute wash at pH 5. This procedure was repeated by alternating HS29 with COL for a total of nine layers (4.5 bilayers). Lastly, coated µCs were rinsed once with 1X PBS (D5652, Sigma-Aldrich) and UV-sterilized for ten minutes.

Cell culture

MSCs from three donors, AD-323 (C46001AD, RoosterBio), BM-277, and BM-310 (MSC-003, RoosterBio), were cultured and maintained according to the manufacturer’s recommendation until 80% confluency was reached. Cells at passage three were seeded onto TCP µCs (3779, Corning) previously coated with HS29/COL multilayers or onto commercial COL-coated µCs (3786, Corning), using a fixed µC concentration of 0.1 g mL⁻¹, and three initial seeding densities (350, 1.4k or 2.8k cells/cm2) as part of a preliminary optimization experiment conducted using donor BM-277 in SF medium. Based on these results, a seeding density of 1.4k cells/cm2 was selected for all subsequent cryopreservation and benchmarking studies. Cells were grown for three days in standard growth media composed of DMEM high glucose (D6429, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (25–514 H, Genesee Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P4333, Sigma-Aldrich). Environmental conditions were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for a three-day expansion period. The first 12 h were conducted under static conditions to allow initial cell attachment, followed by gentle agitation using a rocking shaker (SK-R1807-E, DLAB) set to 40 rpm (0–80 rpm) at a 7-degree angle to enhance carrier distribution throughout the remainder of the culture [14]. Following expansion, growth media was exchanged to standard growth media or SF media composed of DMEM high glucose supplemented with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A7906, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P4333, Sigma-Aldrich) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) at 20 ng/mL (CYT-218, ProspecBio).

Cell freeze and thaw

Following initial cell expansion, growth media was removed from the cells and a wash with 1X PBS was performed to remove any remaining media. Cells seeded on COL or HS29/COL-coated µCs were cultured in DMEM high glucose supplemented with one of three cryopreservation formulations: (I and II) 10% FBS +/- 10% DMSO, or (III) 0% FBS + 2% BSA supplemented with bFGF at 20 ng/mL (SF-media). MSCs were cryopreserved overnight (> 12 h) in a liquid nitrogen tank to ensure complete freezing. Following cryogenic storage, the samples were retrieved and thawed at ambient temperature (21 °C) for 3 h, then incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 to assess post-thaw recovery. After thawing, the culture media from all samples were removed. Samples previously frozen in DMSO-containing media were washed with 1X PBS and replenished with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, while samples assigned to the SF condition were directly replaced with fresh SF media formulation. Cells were expanded for up to seven days to assess viability, adhesion efficiency, proliferation, and metabolic activity.

Cell viability and proliferation

Cell viability was assessed using ViaStain Acridine Orange/Propidium Iodide (AO/PI) staining solution (CS2-0106, Nexcelom Bioscience). Specifically, 20 µL of cell suspension was combined with 20 µL of AO/PI solution, following the manufacturer’s recommended 1:1 ratio. Cell counts and viability assessments were performed using the Cellometer Vision (Nexcelom Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The proliferation of MSCs during initial expansion and after the freeze/thaw procedure was monitored using a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) quantitation assay (DNAQF, Sigma-Aldrich). For cell lysis, samples were washed with cold 1X PBS and resuspended in molecular biology-grade water. The lysis step was performed by exposing the cells to four alternating cycles of warming at 37 °C and cooling at −80 °C, each lasting 5 min. Quantification of dsDNA in lysed samples was performed by mixing 10 µL of sample with 190 µL of a detection solution prepared with molecular biology-grade water, fluorescent assay buffer (F7171, Sigma-Aldrich), and a fluorescent bisbenzimide H33258 solution (B1302, Sigma-Aldrich). Measurements were performed in black 96-well plates and analyzed for fluorescence using a multimode microplate reader (Spark, TECAN) at 360 nm excitation and 460 nm emission. All samples were run in technical quadruplicates, and background fluorescence was subtracted using blank wells with molecular biology-grade water.

Cell imaging

MSCs were washed with 1X PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (SC-281692, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 15 min. Cytoskeletal staining was performed using ActinRed™ 555 ReadyProbes reagent (R37112, Invitrogen) at the manufacturer-recommended dilution of 2 drops per mL for 30 min. Fluorescent images were subsequently taken at 20X (MRH00201, Nikon) magnification using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope.

Cell potency

THP1-ASC-GFP reporter cells (thp-ascgfp, InvivoGen) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 25 mM HEPES (H0687, Sigma-Aldrich), 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 µg/mL P/S, and 50 µg/mL Normocin™ (Ant-nr-05, InvivoGen). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator and passaged every 3–4 days. To maintain selection pressure, Zeocin® (Ant-zn-05, Invivogen) was added at 100 µg/mL after every other passage. Cells between passages five and nine were used for all experiments. MSCs were thawed from two cryopreservation conditions: (I) conventional freezing using standard media with 10% DMSO (II) 0% FBS (SF media). Following thawing, culture media were replaced by either fresh standard culture media or SF media. Cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured for five days at 37 °C.

To evaluate their immunomodulatory potency post-thaw, MSCs were primed with interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) at 20 ng/mL (300-02, PeproTech) for 48 h, while unprimed MSCs served as controls. Conditioned media (CM) were collected from each cell donor, centrifuged to remove debris, and stored at 4 °C for further use. THP-1 macrophages, previously polarized to an M1 phenotype by adding lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at a concentration of 1 µg/mL for three hours, were treated with MSC-derived CM for 24 h in a black-wall, clear-bottom 96-well plate. Treatment groups included CM from both IFN-γ-primed and unprimed MSCs, using either 0% FBS (SF media) or standard culture media (10% FBS). Anti-inflammatory effects were assessed by measuring GFP fluorescence intensity with a multimode microplate reader at 460 nm excitation and 505 nm emission.

Metabolic activity

To assess the metabolic activity of MSCs following cryopreservation, an MTT assay was performed using the Abcam Cell Proliferation Kit (ab211091, Abcam). MSCs cultured on COL or HS29/COL-coated µCs were subjected to conventional freezing in 10% FBS + 10% DMSO or SF media and thawed at 22 °C. Cells were subsequently transferred to 96-well plates under the same conditions as described above and expanded for five days in either standard (10% FBS) or SF media (0% FBS) at 37 °C. Following expansion, the media was removed and replaced with 50 µL fresh culture media and 50 µL of MTT reagent. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for three hours, after which 150 µL of MTT solvent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 15 min at ambient temperature. Absorbance was measured at 590 nm using a multimode microplate reader. All samples were run in three independent replicates. Background absorbance was subtracted using blank wells. For µC conditions, the blank consisted of culture media with the µCs without cells. For suspension controls, the blank consisted of media only without cells.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. One-way and two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were performed to evaluate significant differences among the tested conditions, with post hoc multiple-comparison tests applied where appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad software (GraphPad Prism 10, San Diego, CA). A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Method description and cell optimization

The Cryo-MASCS method comprises seven sequential steps, summarized in Fig. 1. Cells are attached to the µC surface for 12 h in static incubation (Step 1), followed by a short culture expansion step under rocking agitation (Step 2), both at 37 °C. The µC cell suspension is then transferred to a cryovial (Step 3), allowing the µCs to settle (< 1 min) before removing culture media, washing with 1X PBS (Step 4) and resuspending in culture media (Step 5). Vials are cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen (Step 6) and thawed for three hours at ambient temperature (Step 7). This brief ambient acclimatization step is based on the principles of hypothermic conditioning and has been shown to minimize cold‑induced ion imbalance, oxidative stress and apoptosis, while maintaining ≥ 80% MSC viability with preserved phenotype for up to four days at 2–32°C [20–22]. The result is that cells have time to restore ionic homeostasis and membrane integrity before re‑entering normothermic proliferation.

To determine the optimal seeding density for robust MSC recovery following cryopreservation, BM‑277 cells were cultured in SF on HS29/COL‑coated µCs at low (350 cells/cm2), medium (1.4k cells/cm2), and high (2.8k cells/cm2) densities. SF media without DMSO was used as the cryopreservation medium, and cell viability was measured before freezing (D0) and post-thawing (D1). Cell viability was highest in the medium‑density group, whereas low and high‑density cultures showed no appreciable change (Fig. 2A). Conditioned‑medium protein content, a proxy for secretory activity, was highest in the high‑density samples but fell in the low-density group after normalization to live cell numbers, indicating slower metabolic activity (Fig. 2B). The cell‑recovery fraction relative to initial cell seeding was > 75% for all seeding densities, being the highest for the medium cell seeding density (Fig. 2C). Overall, the 1.4k cells/cm2 seeding density, is the most supportive condition for MSC survival and functional recovery after freeze-thaw in SF.

Cell recovery is boosted by a combination of SF media and HS surface coatings

Building on prior optimization steps, we next examined how cryopreservation medium and µC surface chemistry influence MSC viability, recovery, and post-thaw proliferation. While seeding density shapes the cellular microenvironment before freezing, both the culture medium and the physicochemical properties of the culture substrate are also critical determinants of how cells recover from cryoinjury.

To isolate the influence of the culture substrate, we first used SF medium as the cryopreservation medium and compared HS29/COL-coated µCs to three benchmarks: commercial Synthemax-coated µCs presenting an RGD-based integrin-binding motif, commercial collagen-coated µCs that support COL integrin-mediated adhesion, and a µC-free suspension culture control. Post-thaw (D1) viability was similar between HS29/COL-coated µCs and the suspension control, but significantly reduced on both commercial µC types (Supplementary Fig. 1). By day 3 (D3), viability declined in the suspension group, whereas it remained comparable across all µC conditions. Notably, the HS29/COL group exhibited the highest growth, outperforming both commercial µCs and suspension-based cultures.

To assess the contribution of cryopreservation and culture medium, MSCs were then cryopreserved on HS29/COL-coated µCs and cultured in three media conditions: (i) standard culture medium (10% FBS), (ii) standard culture medium supplemented with 10% DMSO (10% FBS + 10% DMSO), and (iii) SF medium (0% FBS). To quantify the effect of Cryo-MASCS on MSC survival, cell viability was measured pre-freeze (D0) and post-thaw (D1), corresponding to steps 5 and 7 in Fig. 1, using cells from three independent human donors (AD-323, BM-277, BM-310). After thawing, MSCs were cultured for seven days at 37 °C in their respective basal media. For DMSO-containing samples, µC-attached cells were transferred immediately into fresh medium containing 10% FBS to minimize DMSO-induced cytotoxicity.

Post-thaw (D1) viability analysis showed that SF medium used during cryopreservation performed comparably to the standard DMSO-containing medium across all donors, as measured on µCs (Fig. 3A-C). In COL-coated µCs, MSCs showed reduced post-thaw performance. Specifically, D1 viability was significantly higher than pre-freeze levels in SF medium across all three donors, suggesting that SF media may better support early post-thaw recovery in this surface condition. In contrast, viability following cryopreservation in DMSO-containing medium fell below the 75% threshold for all donors, indicating suboptimal recovery. At 10% FBS, both BM-MSC donors (BM-277 and BM-310) also showed D1 viability below this threshold, while the adipose-derived donor (AD-323) improved post-thaw, highlighting donor-specific variability in serum. For HS29/COL-coated µCs, all donors exhibited post-thaw viabilities above the 75% threshold across all media conditions. Notably, viability consistently ranked highest in SF media, followed by DMSO-containing medium, and lowest in 10% FBS. This trend suggests that HS29/COL coatings offer protective advantages across media types and donors, potentially buffering against the detrimental effects observed in other conditions.

The proportion of MSCs recovered post-thaw, relative to pre-freeze levels, was also evaluated in cells cultured on HS29/COL-coated µCs. A consistent trend was observed across all three donors, with the lowest recovery observed in 10% FBS media (Fig. 3D). In donors AD-323 and BM-277, both DMSO-containing and SF media supported comparable recovery rates > 85%. In donor BM-310, recovery was highest in SF media, exceeding that in the DMSO condition. Results indicate that SF can match or exceed standard DMSO-based cryopreservation in supporting MSC recovery post-thaw on µCs, with minimal donor-dependent variation.

To better quantify cell yields, MSC proliferation was assessed by dsDNA quantification on days 1, 3, and 7 post-thaw to determine how media and surface conditions influenced expansion. For this assessment, cells were cryopreserved in SF or standard media with DMSO. DMSO-containing media were removed immediately after thawing, and cells were resuspended in 10% FBS medium for subsequent culture and analysis. Across all three donors, MSCs cultured on HS29/COL-coated µCs showed higher proliferation after seven days than those on COL-coated µCs (Fig. 4A–C). Within the HS29/COL condition, SF medium consistently supported greater cell expansion than standard 10% FBS, supporting that the sulfated surface enhances cell survival and recovery under SF conditions, a benefit not observed with COL-coated µCs. In contrast, media formulation had only modest effects on COL-coated µCs, with SF medium slightly outperforming 10% FBS at day 7 in two donors; overall, cell growth remained minimal, with no donor showing substantial proliferation relative to day 1. These trends were qualitatively supported by actin-stained immunofluorescence from a single donor (Fig. 5), which showed a visual increase in cell coverage over time on HS29/COL-coated µCs, while COL-coated µCs appeared to exhibit more limited surface colonization. Notably, the viability and recovery of MSCs cryopreserved for 1 month in SF medium further emphasized the advantage of HS29/COL µCs, with cell viability increasing post-thaw across all three donors (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and recovery rates relative to before freezing were the highest compared to COL-coated µCs and suspension controls (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Freeze‑thawed undifferentiated MSCs maintain metabolic activity and immunosuppressive function

Metabolic activity was evaluated with an MTT assay in MSCs that had been cryopreserved on HS29/COL-coated µCs either in SF medium without DMSO or in standard medium (10% FBS) supplemented with 10% DMSO and subsequently recovered in the corresponding SF (0% FBS) or standard (10% FBS) culture medium. All donors showed comparable mitochondrial activity under both conditions (Fig. 6A). The only statistically significant difference was a modest increase in absorbance for donor AD-323 in 0% FBS medium compared to 10% FBS, indicating that the freeze-thaw procedure did not impair metabolic function. To further evaluate the early recovery phase, metabolic activity was measured at days 0, 1, and 7 using donor BM-277. Both formulations showed the expected decrease in metabolic activity at day 1 post-thaw. However, this reduction was more pronounced in suspension than on µCs. By day 7, MSCs had gradually regained metabolic function as expected, but only HS29/COL-coated µCs showed metabolic activity significantly exceeding pre-freeze (D0) levels (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B), consistent with the higher cell yields observed.

To confirm preservation of functional potency, we evaluated the immunosuppressive activity of the MSC secretome [14, 23]. After thawing MSCs derived from each cryopreservation media, cells were cultured for 72 h, then either left unprimed or primed with IFN-γ for an additional 48 h. Conditioned media (CM) were collected and tested in a macrophage M1 polarization assay. Human THP1‑ASC-GFP reporter macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng mL⁻¹), to activate canonical NF-κB signaling and induce GFP expression characteristic of an M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype [24]. LPS-stimulated macrophages were then exposed to unprimed or IFN-γ-primed CM from each MSC donor as described by us [25]. Across all donors, both unprimed and primed CM significantly reduced GFP fluorescence compared with the M1 positive control (Fig. 6B-D), indicating robust inhibition of the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. The suppressive effects of CM generated from unprimed and IFN-γ-primed MSCs were highly similar in suspension and µCs, generating reductions in LPS-induced M1 polarization in both cryopreservation media. Representative fluorescence micrographs of CM from donor BM‑277 corroborate this finding, showing markedly reduced GFP signal in macrophages (Fig. 6E-G). While both cryopreservation media maintained robust immunosuppressive activity, standard medium conferred a modest advantage in limiting the pro-inflammatory M1 response. This advantage of the standard formulation was consistent across donors and observed for both unprimed and IFN-γ-primed CM. However, whether this effect translates into a meaningful difference in in vivo potency remains unclear, as standardized predictive potency metrics for MSC-based therapies are not yet established. Collectively, these data demonstrate that MSCs recovered directly from cryostorage onto HS29/COL µCs preserve both mitochondrial metabolic function and a potent capacity to suppress macrophage M1 activation, irrespective of whether they are expanded in serum-free or serum-containing media.

Discussion

Large‑scale MSC manufacture still depends on labor‑intensive plate cultures, whereas µCs permit suspension growth at higher cell densities and in closed‑loop systems; nevertheless, most µC workflows bypass cryopreservation because adherent cells survive freezing and thawing poorly [26, 27], making this step a persistent bottleneck that erodes viability, function and donor‑to‑donor consistency [28]. In this study, we demonstrate that the Cryo-MASCS method can be used for cold storage of MSCs. MSCs can be directly frozen on HS29/COL-coated µCs in an SF, chemically defined medium and then re-expanded without detachment, achieving post-thaw viability and proliferation kinetics that match or exceed those obtained with cell suspension cryopreserved in standard media supplemented with DMSO. The surface coating on µCs appears to safeguard essential cell-matrix contacts during cooling, thereby retaining cell attachment and supporting cell recovery and growth after thaw. Importantly, our data also underline that upstream culture variables profoundly influence cryo‑resilience in µC systems [29]. For donor BM-277, intermediate cell densities struck the optimal balance between cell attachment and viability post-thaw. Sparse cultures exhibited limited attachment and likely insufficient paracrine support, while overly confluent cultures may have experienced metabolic stress that heightened cryoinjury. Collectively, these findings indicate that successful MSC cryopreservation must be co‑optimized with the three‑dimensional micro‑environment rather than transplanted from 2‑D suspension protocols.

Extracellular matrix (ECM) cues are increasingly recognized as dual-purpose allies in cryopreservation, as they protect cells during freeze/thaw by maintaining adhesion-mediated processes and can accelerate growth after recovery. Prior studies showed that rat hepatocytes cryopreserved within a COL “sandwich” survive better than those frozen in suspension [30], and that embryonic stem cells frozen adherent to Matrigel‑coated µCs yield more undifferentiated cells than standard suspension methods [11, 31]. Similar benefits extend to MSCs, where 3D cultures that generate their own COL‑rich ECM preserve focal adhesions, integrin signaling and cytoskeletal integrity, retaining viability and osteogenic potential post‑thaw [32]. Building on this foundation, we demonstrate that coating µCs with HS29/COL stacked bilayers not only preserves these adhesion networks during freezing but also provides a biochemical boost once cells re‑enter culture. MSCs cultured on HS29/COL exhibited increased proliferation relative to COL alone, and this effect was most evident under SF conditions. Our prior work, as well as that of others, indicates that HS sequesters heparan-binding mitogens, both exogenously provided (e.g., bFGF) and endogenously secreted by cells (e.g., COL), shielding them from proteolytic degradation while concentrating them at the cell surface for efficient receptor engagement 14,28. In parallel, the sulfated domains cluster integrins and other receptors [33], activating MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways that orchestrate proliferation and survival [34] and foster assembly of a stabilizing pericellular matrix [35]. Together, these complementary biophysical and biochemical mechanisms create a regenerative-like niche that both protects MSCs during cryostorage and drives rapid cell recovery after thawing in SF media.

Functional potency is the ultimate benchmark for an “off‑the‑shelf” therapy, and transient loss of therapeutic potency has been reported during standard cryopreservation of cell suspensions in DMSO [12, 13, 36]. By contrast, MSCs cryopreserved directly on HS29/COL-coated µCs, in either a conventional DMSO-containing medium or our SF formulation, recovered with comparable metabolic activity and retained their ability to suppress LPS-driven M1 macrophage polarization after IFN-γ licensing. We observed a slightly reduced suppressive potency of MSCs on HS29/COL-coated µCs compared with standard suspension cultures; however, whether this modest decrease has a meaningful impact on their in vivo performance remains unclear, as standardized predictive potency metrics are not yet defined in a quantitative manner and are instead typically used as qualitative yes/no checkpoints. Overall, these findings reinforce the notion that the HS29/COL microcarrier platform supports a stable and resilient immunomodulatory phenotype after thawing.

The SF medium relied on 2% BSA as its sole macromolecular cryoprotectant. Although BSA cannot permeate the cell to block intracellular ice nucleation, it acts extracellularly to limit ice recrystallization, buffer osmotic shifts, and adsorb to the plasma membrane [37], thereby reducing mechanical shear during cooling and warming. BSA is widely recognized as a generic membrane‑stabilizing control in cryobiology [38], and its physicochemical mode of action is usually species‑independent, making it straightforward to substitute with recombinant human serum albumin (HSA) to achieve a fully xeno‑free, GMP‑compatible workflow [39]. In parallel, the sulfated GAG domains of HS29 cluster cell-surface receptors and concentrate cytokines and growth factors, thereby stabilizing the signaling activity in SF media [40, 41], which likely underpins the favorable response to IFN-γ licensing observed here. Collectively, the data indicate that HS29/COL-coated µCs support DMSO-free cryopreservation, rapid metabolic recovery, and full immunomodulatory potency, key prerequisites for scalable, Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)-grade MSC manufacturing [32, 42, 43].

Despite the promising results of our Cryo-MASCS method, several limitations must be addressed to refine MSC cryopreservation and culture. First, the polystyrene µCs used here resist fracture during freezing but generate plastic waste and require enzymatic/non-enzymatic cell harvest. Emerging biodegradable µCs that withstand cryo-stress while supporting MSC adhesion could mitigate both issues [44, 45]. Second, although our data supports the robustness of this workflow across three donors from AD- and BM-derived MSC sources, the number of biological replicates per tissue source is limited. As a result, we cannot definitively separate donor-, sex-, and tissue-specific effects. Future studies should therefore incorporate a larger number of donors per tissue type and extend to additional sources, such as umbilical cord-derived MSCs, to more comprehensively capture inter-donor and inter-tissue variability. Third, translation to industrial bioreactors will require precise control of cooling and warming profiles to ensure uniform cell recovery at scale. Finally, the initial cell seeding density was optimized using a single donor (BM-277) in SF medium as a representative preliminary condition to establish a consistent baseline for downstream comparisons. Although this density was applied uniformly across all subsequent conditions to enable controlled benchmarking, we acknowledge that optimal seeding may vary by donor. Future work should therefore not only refine donor-specific seeding strategies but also evaluate the in vivo performance of MSCs produced with this workflow and develop standardized potency assays to support clinically translatable manufacturing.

Conclusion

Our study shows that human MSCs can be frozen, stored, and thawed for expansion while still attached to HS29/COL-coated µCs in fully defined, SF and DMSO‑free medium. Because the cells remained on the µC surface, culture inoculation and expansion can be streamlined for scale‑up, eliminating the DMSO removal and static cell replating steps that add time and contribute to batch‑to‑batch variability. The workflow can therefore be aligned with GMP expectations for closed-loop manufacturing and minimal operator intervention, thereby reducing contamination risk while enhancing traceability and lot consistency. The same concept is readily transferable to unconventional settings, such as microgravity experiments, where adherent cell culture on µCs mitigates fluid management challenges during launch and in-orbit culture [46–48]. Taken together, the data position HS29/COL-coated µCs as a versatile platform for the scalable culture of undifferentiated MSCs for applications that benefit from robust, suspension-based adherent culture.
