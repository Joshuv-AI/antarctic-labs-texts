Clinical features and recurrence patterns of pediatric idiopathic orbital inflammatory disease
Authors: Zixuan Li, Yue Chen, Hongjuan Liu, Qiong Wu, Bentao Yang, Jing Zhang, Libin Jiang, Fred Kuanfu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic orbital inflammatory disease (IOID) is a non-specific orbital inflammatory condition that is uncommon in children. Due to limited data and the unique characteristics of the developing immune system in children, this study examined the clinical features, imaging subtypes, treatment outcomes, and recurrence patterns.MethodsPediatric patients (≤ 18 years) diagnosed with IOID at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital University between January 2020 and January 2025 were included in this retrospective review. Clinical data, imaging characteristics, treatment regimens, and recurrence patterns were collected. Cases were further classified into six anatomical subtypes based on orbital imaging.ResultsA total of 10 patients (12 eyes) were included, with a mean age of 9.9 ± 3.2 years. Imaging subtypes included diffuse (5, 50%), posterior scleritis (3, 30%), dacryoadenitis (1, 10%), and orbital apex involvement (1, 10%). Clinical symptoms were conjunctival injection (9, 90%), optic disc edema (8, 80%), decreased visual acuity (8, 80%), eyelid swelling (7, 70%), periorbital pain (6, 60%), ocular pain (4, 40%), and ptosis (4, 40%). Orbital MRI revealed posterior scleral and/or Tenon’s capsule thickening with enhancement (8, 80%), optic nerve or sheath enhancement (5, 50%), lacrimal gland enlargement (4, 40%), and cavernous sinus thickening or dural enhancement (2, 20%). All patients received systemic corticosteroids, with a mean follow-up duration of 17.9 ± 12.5 months. Nine patients experienced recurrence, with seven of them related to steroid dependence in diffuse-type cases. Final visual acuity was ≥ 0.3logMAR in all relapsed cases.ConclusionPediatric IOID frequently presents with diffuse or deep orbital involvement on imaging. While corticosteroid therapy is generally effective, these subtypes may be more prone to steroid-dependent relapse during tapering. The association between steroid tapering and relapse remains to be fully substantiated, and further studies are needed to explore this potential relationship. Imaging-based classification may help predict recurrence risk and guide individualized long-term management strategies for pediatric IOID.

Introduction

Idiopathic orbital inflammatory disease (IOID), also referred to as orbital pseudotumor, is a nonspecific inflammatory condition of unknown etiology that may affect one or multiple orbital structures.

IOID accounts for approximately 8–16% of orbital space-occupying lesions in adults [1, 2], but is exceedingly rare in pediatric populations [3]. Clinically, it presents with orbital pain, proptosis, conjunctival hyperemia and edema, restricted extraocular motility often accompanied by diplopia, and occasionally decreased visual acuity [4–6]. Although its precise pathogenesis remains uncertain, current evidence suggests that aberrant immune responses, potentially triggered by prior infections, may lead to cytokine dysregulation, immune cell infiltration, and progressive fibrosis, ultimately resulting in chronic, nonspecific inflammation of orbital tissues [7, 8]. Given its highly heterogeneous clinical presentation, IOID in an adult needs to be differentiated from a wide range of orbital disorders, including cellulitis, lymphoma, vascular malformations, optic neuritis, thyroid eye disease, and systemic autoimmune conditions such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis and sarcoidosis [9]. However, with the ongoing maturation of the pediatric immune system, the clinical manifestations, disease course and differential diagnoses in children presenting with IOID differ substantially from those observed in adults. Existing literature is primarily limited to case reports and small, heterogeneous case series, with few studies focusing on imaging-defined subtypes or long-term treatment outcomes in pediatric IOID.

This retrospective study aims to characterize the clinical and radiologic features of pediatric IOID, evaluate treatment outcomes with corticosteroids, and assess recurrence risk, thereby improving disease recognition and informing management strategies in this rare and understudied population.

Methods

This retrospective study included pediatric (≤ 18 years) patients diagnosed with IOID at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital University from January 2020 to January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of Beijing Tongren Hospital and adhered strictly to the Declaration of Helsinki principles (TRECKY 2020-045, January 2020). Informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 18. To minimize potential sources of bias, all eligible pediatric IOID cases during the study period were consecutively identified from the hospital electronic medical record system. Imaging findings were independently reviewed by two ophthalmologists and one radiologist, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. This study did not involve patients or members of the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of the research.

Patient inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for pediatric IOID were adapted from the international consensus diagnostic criteria of idiopathic orbital inflammation (IOI) proposed by JAMA Ophthalmology [10]. The criteria applied in this study were as follows:

Biopsy in areas such as posterior scleritis, orbital apex involvement, and diffuse inflammation is often not feasible due to the risk of complications, including visual impairment and extraocular motility dysfunction. Given that our patients showed a good initial response to corticosteroids, all legal guardians declined biopsy. As a result, no histopathological confirmation was obtained for this cohort. Despite this, the diagnosis of IOID in our cohort was based on characteristic clinical features and imaging findings, supported by a good response to corticosteroids, as recommended in previous studies [11].

Patient classification

Patients were categorized into six subtypes based on initial imaging findings, in reference to previously published anatomical classifications of orbital inflammatory disease [12, 13]. The classification aimed to facilitate assessment of clinical features, treatment response, and recurrence risk across distinct involvement patterns. The subtypes were defined as follows: (1) anterior involvement: inflammation confined to the eyelids and/or conjunctiva; (2) dacryoadenitis: predominantly affecting unilateral or bilateral lacrimal glands; (3) myositis: involvement of one or more extraocular muscles; (4) posterior scleritis: inflammation involving the sclera, uvea, or Tenon’s capsule, characterized by the “T-sign” on orbital ultrasound; (5) orbital apex involvement: lesions primarily located at the orbital apex, with potential extension to the optic nerve sheath or adjacent deep orbital structures; (6) diffuse involvement: simultaneous inflammation involving two or more of the above-mentioned anatomical regions, indicating multi-compartmental orbital disease. All imaging data were independently reviewed by two ophthalmologists with over ten years of experience and one senior radiologist, all of whom were blinded to patient outcomes and treatment details. Any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus discussion among the reviewers.

Clinical data collection and statistical analysis

The collected clinical data included age, sex, ethnicity, laterality, and duration of symptoms. Ophthalmologic assessments included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA in logarithm of minimum angle of resolution, logMAR), extraocular motility, external eye examination, slit-lamp examination, fundus examination, as well as orbital imaging (ocular ultrasound, computed tomography, CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, MRI). Treatment regimens and recurrence rates were also recorded. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v.23.0 (Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons between groups for continuous variables were made using the Mann–Whitney U-test, and Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and clinical features

This retrospective analysis included 10 pediatric patients (12 eyes) diagnosed with IOID. Among them, four (40%) were male, and eight (80%) were unilateral and two (20%) were bilateral (Table 1). The mean ± SD age at onset was 9.9 ± 3.2 years (range: 6 ~ 15 years), and the mean symptoms duration before diagnosis was 1.4 ± 1.4 months (range: 10 days to 5 months). BCVA (logMAR) at presentation ranged from 0.0 to 1.9. Based on clinical and imaging features, five cases (50%) were classified as diffuse type, three (30%) as posterior scleritis, and one each (10%) as dacryoadenitis and orbital apex type.

The most frequent clinical manifestations were ocular redness (9, 90%), optic disc edema (8, 80%), and visual impairment (8, 80%). Additional symptoms included eyelid swelling (7, 70%), periorbital pain (6, 60%), ocular pain and ptosis (4 each, 40%), proptosis, restricted ocular motility, and visual field defects (2 each, 20%). Conjunctival chemosis and keratic precipitates were each observed in one patient (10%).

All patients tested negative for rheumatologic markers, including ANA, ENA, ANCA, and IgG4. Further diagnostic tests, including full blood counts, chest CT, and IgG4 level testing, also returned negative results. These findings effectively ruled out systemic autoimmune conditions and malignancies. Mild elevations in inflammatory markers (e.g., leukocytosis, ESR, IL-6) were observed, further supporting the interpretation of IOID as a localized, idiopathic immune-mediated inflammatory process.

Subtype-specific presentations

Clinical features varied by IOID subtype. Diffuse-type patients exhibited the broadest symptom features, with all presenting restricted ocular motility (100%), periorbital pain (83.3%), ptosis (75%), eyelid swelling (71.4%), and visual field defects and proptosis in 50% of cases. Rates of optic disc edema and ocular redness were 50.0% and 55.6%, respectively.

In contrast, posterior scleritis cases (3, 30%) predominantly exhibited ocular pain (75.0%) and visual impairment (66.7%), along with visual field defects (50.0%) and optic disc edema (37.5%). The dacryoadenitis subtype (1, 10%) presented with localized eyelid swelling, pain, and redness, while the orbital apex case (1, 10%) showed proptosis, periorbital pain, and optic disc edema (Fig. 1).

Systemic manifestations and misdiagnosis

Systemic manifestations included sinusitis (3, 30%), anemia and headache (2 each, 20%), and oral ulcers, common cold, vomiting, somnolence, tuberculosis infection, and tonsillitis (1 each, 10%). Misdiagnosis at initial presentation was high (70%), with only 3 cases (30%) correctly diagnosed as IOID. Misdiagnoses included orbital cellulitis (3, 30%), conjunctivitis (2, 20%), optic neuritis (1, 10%), and orbital mass (1, 10%).

Imaging findings

All patients underwent orbital imaging. Six cases (60%) received enhanced MRI alone, while four (40%) underwent both orbital CT and contrast-enhanced MRI. Common MRI findings included posterior scleral or Tenon’s capsule thickening with enhancement (8, 80%), optic nerve or sheath enhancement (5, 50%), and lacrimal gland enlargement (4, 40%). Less frequent findings included cavernous sinus thickening and dural enhancement (2, 20%) (Figs. 2, 3, and 4). Among the eight patients who underwent ocular ultrasonography, all exhibited scleral thickening with a characteristic “T-sign,” and two had concurrent exudative retinal detachment.

Treatment and clinical outcomes

All patients received systemic corticosteroids, initially with intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisolone tapering. The initial IVMP dosage varied based on disease severity, ranging from 500 mg/day for severe cases to 30–80 mg/day for moderate and recurrent cases. After the IVMP regimen, patients transitioned to oral prednisolone, starting at 1 mg/kg/day, and the dose was tapered by 4 mg per week for 6 to 12 weeks based on clinical response, which aligns with the tapering period recommended by Fang et al. [7] and Montagnese et al. [14] after high-dose corticosteroid therapy. Eight patients were treated with corticosteroids alone, while two received adjunctive topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory eye drops (Table 3). The mean follow-up duration was 17.9 ± 12.5 months (range: 6 ~ 40 months).

Most of these patients had symptom durations exceeding 30 days before initiating corticosteroid therapy, with a mean duration of initial therapy in our recurrent cases (3.2 ± 1.6 months). Regarding recurrence, one patient (10%) remained relapse-free after steroid discontinuation, whereas nine patients (90%) experienced 1 ~ 4 episodes of relapse. Recurrent symptoms included ocular redness (6, 60%), ocular pain (4, 40%), optic disc edema (2, 20%), restricted ocular motility (1, 10%).

Seven patients relapsed when corticosteroid dosage was tapered to 4 mg/day. However, after increasing the dose to 12 mg/day, the symptoms were controlled again, suggesting a potential association with steroid dependence. Three patients (Case 2,5,9) experienced relapse during corticosteroid tapering despite concurrent use of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Two additional relapses occurred following respiratory infections after corticosteroid withdrawal (Case 7,8). Visual outcomes were generally favorable; apart from one case complicated by retinal detachment, all recurrent cases had final BCVA (logMAR) recovered to 0.3 (20/40) or better, with 90% recovering to 0.0 (20/20).

Discussion

We reported the most frequently observed ocular signs of IOID were conjunctival injection (90%), decreased visual acuity (80%), and eyelid swelling (70%) similar to previous reports [15–17]. In our cohort, we observed a higher frequency of diffuse-type IOID and posterior scleritis, with lacrimal gland involvement in 40% of patients. However, many of these cases also had posterior orbital involvement, leading to their classification as diffuse-type IOID. This may explain the higher incidence of optic disc edema (80%), which was more frequent than typically reported.

Compared with the earlier Chinese pediatric series by Yan et al. [18], which reported diffuse-type involvement in only 4% of cases, our findings indicate a significantly higher proportion. We think this pattern may be explained by several factors. First, there may be regional variations in disease presentation, with genetic and environmental factors potentially influencing the observed prevalence of certain subtypes. Second, the use of high-resolution MRI in our study may have allowed for more accurate detection of posterior orbital involvement, which may not have been captured in studies with lower-resolution imaging. Additionally, the diverse presentation of this idiopathic disease may contribute to significant variability in clinical manifestations between individuals, which may help explain the atypical findings observed in our cohort.

Retrospective imaging analysis in our cohort showed that 50% of patients had diffuse-type lesions involving multiple orbital structures. This finding is consistent with the study by Spindle et al. [16], who reported multi-structural involvement in 67% of pediatric IOID cases at initial presentation. MRI characteristics were heterogeneous, with T1-weighted images typically showing isointense to hypointense signals relative to orbital fat [19]. T2-weighted signal intensities varied depending on lesion composition, with lesions appearing hyperintense in the acute phase due to edema and inflammatory cell infiltration, and hypointense in chronic stages where fibrosis predominated [20]. Post-contrast enhancement showed variability, ranging from homogeneous to heterogeneous patterns. In our cohort, focal-type lesions exhibited well-defined margins with uniform enhancement, while diffuse-type lesions showed irregular, patchy enhancement. Although these imaging characteristics are consistent with known descriptions of IOID, we did not establish a direct correlation between these MRI features and clinical outcomes, such as relapse in our study. Further research is needed to explore this potential relationship.

Although orbital biopsy has been proposed as a useful tool for confirming the diagnosis of IOID and excluding malignancies or atypical lesions [16]. It is associated with substantial risks, including irreversible vision loss, persistent proptosis, and ocular motility disturbances. Mottow [15] et al. reported that 55% of pediatric IOID patients experienced visual impairment following biopsy. In our series, all patients demonstrated favorable responses to corticosteroid therapy without deterioration in visual function, and their guardians declined further biopsy procedures due to the potential risks involved. These findings suggest that in pediatric IOID cases demonstrating a favorable response to corticosteroids and lacking clinical or imaging evidence of infection, neoplasia, or systemic autoimmune disease, an empirical trial of corticosteroid therapy may serve as a reasonable initial diagnostic and therapeutic strategy. Orbital biopsy might be reserved for patients with inadequate treatment response or those exhibiting imaging features suspicious for atypical or non-inflammatory pathology.

Previous studies on pediatric IOID have reported varying recurrence rates. Mottow et al. [15] studied 29 pediatric cases and reported a recurrence rate as high as 76%, while Eshraghi et al. [21] reported a recurrence rate of 68.4%, with most patients showing clinical improvement on oral corticosteroids. In contrast, Spindle et al. [16] reported a recurrence rate of only 17%, with 83% of patients achieving complete resolution after treatment with steroids and steroid-sparing therapies.

In our cohort, the recurrence rate was notably high (90%), which could be related to the more diffuse and deeply situated nature of the lesions, making the disease more challenging to treat effectively.

Moreover, we found that 7 out of the 9 recurrent cases relapsed after their prednisolone dosage was reduced to 4 mg/day. Most of the recurrent symptoms were relatively mild and localized, such as ocular redness and pain, without significant visual impairment. After adjusting the steroid dose to 12 mg/day, the symptoms were controlled again, which is intriguing. This pattern suggests that steroid dependence might contribute to the recurrence of symptoms, possibly due to persistent inflammation in poorly vascularized regions like retrobulbar fat, Tenon’s capsule, and orbital apex. These areas may take longer to resolve, which could contribute to a “low responsiveness but high dependency” mechanism during steroid tapering. However, the exact underlying mechanism remains unclear, thus further studies are needed to explore and confirm the relationship between steroid tapering and recurrence.

In this study, three patients received a combination of corticosteroids and Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF) during steroid tapering. However, all patients experienced disease recurrence, despite adjustments to the corticosteroid dosage that controlled their symptoms. Two of these patients had diffuse-type IOID, and one had the orbital apex subtype. MMF, a purine synthesis inhibitor that suppresses B- and T-cell proliferation, is commonly used in managing various ocular and systemic inflammatory diseases. While MMF is considered a steroid-sparing agent, its role in preventing the recurrence of pediatric IOID remains uncertain. Previous studies have suggested that MMF may be beneficial as an adjunctive therapy for inflammatory ocular conditions, including IOID [22, 23]. Nonetheless, approximately one-third of patients have been reported to respond inadequately to immunosuppressive agents [24]. In our cohort, the combination of corticosteroids and MMF did not prevent recurrence, which may be due to suboptimal dosing, delayed onset of MMF’s effects, or the chronic and diffuse nature of the disease. This highlights the ongoing need for more targeted and effective treatment strategies. Future studies should explore the role of biologic agents, such as monoclonal antibodies, which may offer more effective options for managing steroid-resistant or steroid-dependent IOID.

Management of steroid-dependent or steroid-resistant pediatric IOID remains challenging. Biologic agents targeting CD20 or IL-6 pathways (e.g., rituximab, tocilizumab) have shown efficacy in adults [25, 26], while case reports suggest that TNF-α inhibitors (e.g., infliximab) may benefit pediatric patients refractory to conventional therapy [27]. However, these biologics are used off-label in children, with limited data on long-term safety and patient selection. Moreover, radiotherapy and other immunosuppressants have demonstrated only modest efficacy in refractory adult cases [28] and carry significant risks, including ocular surface toxicity, cataracts, retinopathy, and optic neuropathy [29]. In children, heightened tissue sensitivity and longer life expectancy substantially increase the risk of secondary malignancies—up to 14-fold higher than in the general population [30]. Given these limitations, corticosteroids remain the mainstay of treatment in our cohort, with all recurrent cases achieving final BCVA (logMAR) of 0.3 (20/40) or better, and 90% recovering to 0.0 (20/20). Future research should incorporate pediatric-specific immune profiles to refine relapse risk assessment and guide interventions, particularly for deep orbital involvement.

Our study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size, due to the rarity of pediatric IOID, limits statistical power and generalizability. Additionally, biopsies were declined by all families, which may have affected diagnostic completeness. However, despite these limitations, our findings provide important insights into the clinical features and steroid dependence of pediatric IOID, contributing to a better understanding of this rare condition. A larger sample size and future multi-center studies will be crucial to validate and further refine these results.

Conclusion

Pediatric IOID often follows a complex and relapsing course, especially in patients with diffuse or deep orbital lesions. While initial steroid therapy is generally effective, recurrence, typically mild but persistent, is common during tapering, indicating a possible link between imaging subtype and steroid dependence. Recognizing these patterns may help inform individualized management and long-term monitoring, which requires further investigation in larger cohorts.
