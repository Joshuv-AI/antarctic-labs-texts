Vision Language Model-based Testing of Industrial Autonomous Mobile Robots
Jiahui Wu, Chengjie Lu, Aitor Arrieta, Shaukat Ali, Thomas Peyrucain

Abstract
Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) are deployed in diverse environments (e.g., warehouses, retail spaces, and offices), where they work alongside humans. Given that human behavior can be unpredictable and that AMRs may not have been trained to handle all possible unknown and uncertain behaviors, it is important to test AMRs under a wide range of human interactions to ensure their safe behavior. Moreover, testing in real environments with actual AMRs and humans is often costly, impractical, and potentially hazardous (e.g., it could result in human injury). To this end, we propose a Vision Language Model (VLM)-based testing approach ( RVSG ) for industrial AMRs developed by PAL Robotics in Spain. Based on the functional and safety requirements, RVSG uses the VLM to generate diverse human behaviors that violate these requirements. We evaluated RVSG with several requirements and navigation routes in a simulator using the latest AMR from PAL Robotics. Our results show that, compared with the baseline, RVSG can effectively generate requirement-violating scenarios. Moreover, RVSG -generated scenarios increase variability in robot behavior, thereby helping reveal their uncertain behaviors.

Introduction

PAL Robotics (Spain)
[
1
]
is a leading service robot manufacturer, with deployments in diverse industries, including healthcare, retail, and agri-food.
Notably, their Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) excel at safe and efficient navigation in environments, e.g., offices and warehouses, automating tasks alongside humans.
Unpredictable human behavior and frequent interactions between humans and robots place high demands on AMR navigation
[
2
]
. To ensure dependable operation, PAL Robotics defines key functional and safety (F&S) requirements for various deployments that AMRs must meet.

Scenario-based testing offers a systematic approach to test AMRs’ navigation under various human-robot interactions
[
3
]
. Integrating F&S requirements into automated testing helps generate realistic testing scenarios that violate these requirements
[
3
]
.
Besides, to improve testing efficiency, a small set of test scenarios that capture diverse human behaviors shall be used, avoiding similar behaviors caused by the same faults
[
4
]
.
Moreover, given the high cost of testing in real environments with actual robots and humans, the impracticality (e.g., asking humans to exhibit a specific behavior), and the potential safety risks, such as collisions that could damage robots or injure humans, simulation-based testing provides a safe and cheap alternative
[
5
]
. However, ensuring realistic human behaviors in simulation remains a significant challenge
[
5
]
.

To address the challenge mentioned above, we use Vision Language Models (VLMs) to generate test scenarios, which may achieve human-level performance in commonsense reasoning about various tasks
[
6
]
.
VLMs can extract key information from both images and text, enabling contextual understanding and drawing logical conclusions.
Thus, we conjecture that VLMs are naturally well-suited to analyze textual requirements and exhibit strong capabilities in scenario interpretation and construction. This makes VLMs highly capable of analyzing F&S requirements and generating natural language descriptions of test scenarios and human behaviors.
An often overlooked but crucial aspect of scenario realism is that human behavior varies depending on the environment. For example, typical human behaviors in warehouses include lifting, sorting packages, and interacting with shelves.
In simulation-based testing, it is thus essential that modeled human behaviors remain consistent with the logical context of the given environment, which directly influences the configuration of such behaviors.
VLMs, using their commonsense reasoning capability, can generate contextually appropriate human behaviors tailored to specific environments, which is difficult to achieve with traditional methods, e.g., search algorithms.

To this end, we propose
RVSG
, a requirement-driven test scenario generation method for robot navigation tasks based on VLMs having two stages: (i) environment preprocessing and (ii) test scenario generation.
In the first stage,
RVSG
captures and labels images of the simulated environment, then uses the VLM to extract visual features to support scenario generation.
In the second stage, the prompt generator adapts pre-designed natural language prompt templates based on the given requirement and task.
Through multi-turn conversations, the VLM generates detailed scenario descriptions and human behavior configurations, which are decoded into executable human agents and run together with the navigation task in the simulator to obtain test results.
These results are then fed back to the prompt generator to optimize the human configurations, improving test scenarios.
Moreover,
RVSG
uses generated scenarios, human configurations, and feedback to guide the VLM in producing more diverse scenarios.
We evaluated
RVSG
and the baseline method on the latest AMR from PAL Robotics using the Gazebo simulator with different requirements and navigation routes. We also employed multiple metrics to assess the performance and diversity of the generated test scenarios.
Our results show that
RVSG
can generate more effective requirement-violating test scenarios with a certain degree of diversity, increasing the variability of robot behaviors that help reveal behavioral uncertainties. In addition, different navigation routes affect both the performance of the generated scenarios and the stability of scenario execution.

In summary, our contributions are:
(1) a novel requirement-driven VLM-based test scenario generation method for AMRs;
(2) a set of prompt templates that enable
RVSG
to understand the environment, generate test scenarios and human configurations, and dynamically incorporate simulation feedback and historical scenario information for improving scenario generation;
(3) evaluate the test scenarios generated by
RVSG
with multiple metrics on the latest AMR from PAL Robotics within the Gazebo simulator, covering a variety of requirements and navigation routes.

Industrial Context

This work is conducted in the context of the
RoboSAPIENS
European project
[

This study focuses on AMRs, which are deployed in environments such as offices and warehouses, where they navigate within a defined space and operate alongside humans. Naturally, the interaction between humans and AMRs must be safe. Typically, AMRs use two types of navigation algorithms: a global planner, which determines the optimal path to reach the destination, and a local planner, which accounts for dynamic obstacles in the environment (including humans) and adapts the path accordingly. To this end, an AMR is typically equipped with a set of LiDAR sensors to support various functions such as path planning, collision avoidance, and autonomous navigation. Fig.

Within the

[

, an extension of the well-established MAPE-K loop for self-adaptation in software systems. As presented in Fig.

Given the diverse deployment environments of AMRs, it is essential to ensure that they navigate efficiently, stably, and safely while posing no threat to humans
[

If a potential threat is detected, AMRs adapt their behavior, such as changing their path, stopping, or even communicating with nearby humans. However, due to unpredictable human behavior, it is unknown in advance how humans will interact in the environment where an AMR is deployed. Therefore, it is paramount to test AMRs under a variety of human behaviors, ensuring (to a certain degree) the dependability of their responses. Moreover, conducting tests in real-world environments with real humans is expensive and often impractical
[
5
]
. Instead, simulation-based testing offers an affordable alternative, which is the focus of this paper. In general, there is a clear need for systematic methods to test AMR behavior in simulation settings, where diverse human behaviors can be simulated to assess the dependability of AMRs. As Fig.

Plan

Legitimate

Overview

This map can then be labeled to highlight the spatial structure, facilitating subsequent layout analysis and task planning.
Based on the labeled map,

Both processed images are provided to the VLM for
Environment Description Generation
at
2
.
Within

Overview of

during the iterative testing of autonomous mobile robots.

For test scenario generation, at
3
,
RVSG
integrates the specific F&S requirement, robot navigation route, and the preprocessed map images and environment description. These inputs are structured into carefully designed prompts (detailed in Fig.
3
) for the VLM, guiding its automated understanding and analysis to generate human configurations.
At
4

Human Configuration Generation by Multi-turn Conversation
,
Prompt Generator
automatically selects appropriate initial multi-turn prompt templates based on the requirement and navigation route, filling them into predefined positions.
Similarly, map images and the environment description are incorporated to further enrich the prompt content, providing strong support for VLM reasoning.
These prompts are sequentially input into the VLM, enabling step-by-step reasoning through multi-turn conversations to produce intermediate outputs (e.g., scenario description) and final outputs (e.g., JSON format human behavior configuration).
Each turn of conversation builds on previous context, ensuring the VLM continuously aligns with the test objective, understands the requirement, and generates the required output for each step.

After completing all conversation turns,
RVSG
delivers the final formatted output to the
Configuration Decoder
, which interprets the human configuration generated by the VLM based on the specific requirement and robot navigation route.
The dialogue history is stored in the
Memory
module via
Dialogue History Storage
for future optimization.
Moreover, the
Scenario & Human Task Extractor
retrieves the scenario description and human tasks (e.g., workers assigned to packing and delivery in a warehouse setting) from the dialogue, and summarizes this information to support experience accumulation for
RVSG
scenario generation.

The decoded human configuration is used to generate executable human agents in the simulator. To ensure realistic human behavior,
RVSG
employs HuNavSim
[
9
]
, an agent controller based on behavior trees and the social force model, which closely simulates real-world human actions.
The AMR under test is loaded with its designated navigation route and uses its navigation algorithm for environment perception and navigation decisions.
Both human agents and the AMR perform their tasks in the simulator at
5
, enabling dynamic performance evaluation of the AMR along the navigation route, with time series data collected throughout the process.
The simulation environment is consistent with that used in preprocessing, and real-time communication is maintained via Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2)
[
10
]
.

The time series data collected during simulation is used by the
Objective Function Calculator
to compute an objective function value, quantitatively reflecting the evaluation results based on the initial requirement.
This result, together with the time series data, is submitted to the
Feedback Integrator
to generate feedback data.
The feedback data can be used to optimize the original prompts in the
Human Configuration Generation
module for more effective scenario configuration generation.
In addition, along with scenario and human task information, it is integrated into the
Memory
module through the
Memory Integrator
, which accumulates experience for VLM scenario generation, supporting prompt refinement and increased scenario diversity in future multi-turn conversations.

As shown at
6
, with simulation feedback from VLM-generated test scenarios and scenario generation experience stored in the
Memory
module, the
Prompt Generator
in
Human Configuration Generation
can continually refine prompts.
This enhances human configuration effectiveness for similar scenarios and increases the diversity of the generated test scenarios, enabling iterative testing.
Ultimately,
RVSG
flexibly produces a more effective and diverse set of test scenarios based on simulation feedback, scenario diversity, and predefined stopping criteria (e.g., generating 10 scenarios), thereby improving the comprehensiveness and effectiveness of AMR testing.

3.2
Environment Preprocessing

RVSG
uses a top-down view to capture the image of the simulation world, offering a clear representation of its structural and spatial relationships.
To further highlight the environment layout,
RVSG
labels the image either manually or automatically by drawing bounding boxes around each area and assigning a corresponding name as a label.
Since the VLM will analyze this labeled image and is highly adaptable in visual analysis, the labeling process is flexible and does not require strict standards, reducing the entry barrier for using
RVSG
.
Based on the labeled map image,
RVSG
constructs a grid map with precise waypoints, with the adjustable resolution and size tailored to different maps and testing requirements.
By default,
RVSG
uses evenly spaced square grids, where each grid point represents a unique 2D waypoint. For example, if rows are labeled with letters and columns with numbers, a specific waypoint, such as “E15”, refers to the point in row E and column 15.
Only valid waypoints (i.e., those not obstructed) are included to aid route planning.
In AMR testing, labeling is typically a one-time effort, allowing the same environment to be reused for testing different robots or for regression testing when their software is updated. As a result, manual labeling is more precise and not necessarily more costly than automated methods (e.g., AI methods), which could produce incorrect or imprecise results. Therefore,
RVSG
recommends using manual labeling methods for processing map and grid images.

Leveraging the VLM’s strong visual reasoning,
RVSG
conducts multi-turn conversations to analyze step-by-step the labeled map and grid images, extracting layout and navigation information.
As shown in the
Environment Description Generation
module of Fig.
2
,
RVSG
first provides the labeled map image and layout analysis instructions to the VLM to obtain a detailed analysis of environmental layout.
Next, using the preserved dialogue history,
RVSG
sends the grid image and corresponding grid analysis instructions to the VLM, along with the map image that emphasizes the basic environmental information, and generates descriptions of the grid and navigation layouts.
Finally,
RVSG
provides a summarization prompt to the VLM asking to summarize the key description for both map and grid images in JSON format.
This output is then processed by the
Environment Description Extractor
, which extracts and pairs the corresponding descriptions for each image, storing them together as the current environment description for subsequent testing and analysis.
Given the potential for hallucinations or inaccuracies in the VLM’s output, the final environment description is manually reviewed to ensure correctness.

3.3
Test Scenario Generation

As shown at
3
and
4
in Fig.
2
,
RVSG
utilizes multi-turn conversations to generate scenarios and human configurations based on system requirements, robot navigation routes, and environment descriptions.
By incorporating feedback from scenario simulations and memory of previous scenarios (at
6
),
RVSG
further optimizes configuration generation and enhances scenario diversity.
Specifically,
RVSG
provides multiple prompt templates for five key reasoning steps: (1)
Test Scenario Description Generation
, (2)
High-level Human Task Generation
, (3)
Low-level Human Waypoint Path Generation
, (4)
Human Configuration Validity Check and Revision
, and (5)
JSON Format Human Configuration Generation
.

Test Scenario Description Generation
analyzes the current environment and produces realistic textual descriptions of test scenarios involving human activities. These descriptions serve as the basis for subsequent human configuration.

High-level Human Task Generation
allocates specific human tasks according to the generated scenario description, detailing concrete workflows and outputting the names of key labeled areas involved in these workflows.

Low-level Human Waypoint Path Generation
builds on the high-level tasks, sequentially selecting valid waypoints from the preprocessed grid map based on the workflow and key areas to construct human waypoint paths.
The high-level human tasks and low-level waypoint paths together form the human configuration, which is then checked for validity by the
Human Configuration Validity Check and Revision
. Any problematic configurations are automatically detected and revised by the VLM at this stage. For example, the module verifies whether the workflow aligns with the scenario description and whether the waypoint paths are feasible. If inconsistencies are found, the workflow is modified and the waypoint paths are updated accordingly.
Finally,
JSON Format Human Configuration Generation
provides the final human configuration in JSON format.

Fig.
3
presents all seven prompt templates provided by
RVSG
. Each template focuses on one or two specific key reasoning step(s).
Notably, for
Test Scenario Description Generation
, the module has introduced an additional prompt version (Prompt2 in Fig.
3
) beyond the initial prompt (Prompt1), influenced by previous scenario memory.
Similarly, to regenerate the human configuration based on simulation feedback without modifying the underlying test scenario,
RVSG
integrates the prompts for
High-level Human Task Generation
and
Low-level Human Waypoint Path Generation
(Prompt3 and Prompt4), and provides a new
Human Configuration Regeneration
prompt (Prompt5).
Based on these templates,
RVSG
automatically fills in the provided information, such as requirements, into the corresponding fields to generate prompts for VLM reasoning and analysis.
For example, Prompt1 in Fig.
3
contains three fields: Environment Description, Robot Navigation Route, and Requirement.
RVSG
automatically populates each field with relevant content and outputs the final prompt.
Note that the content for fields, such as Requirement and Robot Navigation Route, does not adhere to a strict format and can be flexibly adjusted based on experience.

Prompt templates for multi-turn conversations and scenario generation workflow with feedback and memory in
RVSG
.

RVSG
combines different prompts to support iterative testing across various stages.
As shown in Fig.
3
, during the initial stage,
RVSG
selects the initial
Test Scenario Description Generation
prompt (Prompt1),
High-level Human Task Generation
prompt (Prompt3),
Low-level Human Waypoint Path Generation
prompt (Prompt4),
Human Configuration Validity Check and Revision
prompt (Prompt6), and
JSON Format Human Configuration Generation
prompt (Prompt7). These are combined to form the initial multi-turn conversation prompts that guide the VLM in generating the initial human configuration, which is then executed in the simulator to obtain simulation results.
Subsequently, the feedback trigger determines whether to initiate feedback-based optimization. If optimization continues,
RVSG
combines the dialogue history with
Human Configuration Regeneration
(Prompt5),
Human Configuration Validity Check and Revision
(Prompt6), and
JSON Format Human Configuration Generation
(Prompt7) to guide the VLM in refining the human configuration based on simulation feedback, without altering the initial scenario description.
The optimized configuration is then executed in the simulator, after which new feedback is obtained. This feedback includes the objective function value corresponding to the testing requirement, which is used to evaluate the effectiveness of the optimized configuration and determine the degree of optimization. If further optimization is needed, the generated feedback can be used iteratively in subsequent optimization steps.
If the feedback trigger indicates stopping feedback optimization (e.g., reaching a certain number of optimizations),
RVSG
then relies on the memory trigger to decide whether to use memory for generating more diverse scenarios.
If memory-based optimization proceeds,
RVSG
replaces the initial
Test Scenario Description Generation
prompt (Prompt1) with the
Diversity Test Scenario Description Generation
prompt (Prompt2), keeping the other prompts unchanged to guide the VLM in creating the new test scenario.
Moreover, in Prompt2, the
Historical Scenario Memory
field provides the descriptions of all previously generated test scenarios, along with their corresponding optimal configurations and objective function values, serving as references for generating more diverse scenarios.
Otherwise, the process terminates, and all generated scenarios are output.
All multi-turn conversation histories, simulation results, and the corresponding scenario descriptions and human tasks are stored in the
Memory
module, supporting VLM-based scenario generation at different stages.
Note that, throughout all multi-turn conversations,
RVSG
consistently provides labeled map and grid images either directly or indirectly. For simplicity, these details are omitted in the workflow shown in Fig.

Experiment Design

4.1
Subject System and Simulator

We test the latest logistics AMR robot, TIAGo OMNI Base from PAL Robotics
[
11
]
.
It has four mecanum wheels for omnidirectional movement and two LiDAR scanners that provide a 360° unobstructed field of view, enabling it to detect and autonomously avoid obstacles to ensure safe navigation.
To support simulation and testing, PAL Robotics offers an open-source TIAGO OMNI Base ROS 2 Simulation
[
12
]
.
The simulator is built on Gazebo classic 11
[
13
]
and provides a high-fidelity model of the TIAGo OMNI Base.
The TIAGo OMNI Base adopts Nav2
[
14
]
for both global navigation and local obstacle avoidance, while real-time communication is achieved through ROS 2 Humble
[
10
]
.
Considering the simulation platform and the ROS 2 version, we selected the compatible HuNavSim
[
9
]
as the default agent for human behavior control.
At the group level, HuNavSim uses the social force model
[
15
]
to simulate general crowd flow and avoidance behaviors in the presence of obstacles or other humans.
At the individual level, it employs behavior trees to define and control more complex, personalized human actions.
As a result, HuNavSim can provide realistic human behavior modeling, which benefits AMR navigation task testing and supports the development of navigation systems.

4.2
Experiment Setup

S&F Requirements and Objective Functions
. We consider three requirements provided by PAL Robotics: collision avoidance, stability, and efficiency. These requirements reflect essential safety and functional aspects of AMR navigation for PAL robotics and are listed as follows:

•

R1: Collision Avoidance – The robot must maintain a safe distance from other objects to prevent collisions.

•

R2: Stability – The robot must maintain operational stability, ensuring control is not lost due to external forces or loads.

•

R3: Efficiency – The robot should complete its assigned navigation task within a reasonable time, minimizing delays while maintaining safety and stability.

R1 is critical, as the risk of collision is a primary safety concern. To quantify the degree of violation of R1, we opt
Distance to Obstacles
(
DTO
)
[
16
]
as the objective function:

DTO=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbf{p}_{\text{robot}}^{t}-\mathbf{p}_{o_{i}}^{t}\right\|_{2},

(1)

where
\mathbf{p}_{\text{robot}}^{t}
and
\mathbf{p}_{o_{i}}^{t}
are positions of the robot and object
o_{i}
at timestep
t
,
n
is the number of objects in the environment, and
T
is the total number of timesteps.

DTO
measures the average minimum Euclidean distance from the robot to the nearest object during its navigation, where smaller values indicate a higher risk of collision.

R2 is also important, as a lack of stability may result in loss of control over the robot. To evaluate the violation of this requirement, we calculate
Jerk

[
17
]
as the objective function:

Jerk=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i+1}-\mathbf{a}_{i}}{\Delta t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is the acceleration of the robot at timestep
i
,
\Delta t
is the time interval between steps, and
T
is the total number of timesteps.
Jerk
measures the rate of change of acceleration over time; higher jerk values indicate more abrupt motion changes, indicating unstable behaviors.

R3 concerns the time cost required to complete an operational task. To assess the violation of this requirement, we use
Time-to-Reach-Goal
(
TRG
) as the objective function:

TRG=t_{\text{goal}}-t_{\text{start}},

(3)

where
t_{start}
and
t_{goal}
are the timestamps when the robot starts moving and reaches its goal.
TRG
measures the total duration taken by the robot to finish a given navigation route. Longer
TRG
indicates inefficiencies or suboptimal behaviors in the robot’s execution of the task.

VLM and Parameter Settings
. We employ GPT-4.1
[
18
]
as the VLM and integrate it into
RVSG
for scenario generation. GPT-4.1 is the latest VLM from OpenAI’s GPT series, which is designed for complex tasks. It excels at performing a variety of tasks, including coding and instruction following. Moreover, it supports up to 1 million contextual tokens and can better leverage these contexts, improving long-context understanding ability. The parameter settings of VLMs can significantly influence the performance. In our setup, we adopt the default settings from OpenAI for GPT-4.1.

RVSG Feedback and Memory Settings
. Based on a pilot study, we set the stopping condition for the feedback trigger to terminate after four configuration optimizations for the same scenario description, resulting in a total of five similar but distinct configurations per scenario. The stopping condition for the memory trigger was set to terminate after generating 10 sets of scenarios, each consisting of one scenario description and its corresponding optimal human configuration. Among these, the scenarios with the best and worst objective value were identified and repeated 30 times for further analysis.

Comparison Baselines
. Arcuri and Briand suggested
[
19
]
to use random search (
RS
) to check if a problem is complex enough to be solved by a complex algorithm. Thus, we performed a pilot study to compare
RVSG
with
RS
. Results in our online repository
[
20
]
show that
RVSG
is significantly better than
RS
in generating scenarios that violate a given requirement. Hence, our formal experiment excluded
RS
as a baseline. Instead, we employ a modified
RVSG
as the comparison baseline, i.e.,
\textit{RVSG}_{R}
.
\textit{RVSG}_{R}
shares the same architectural design as
RVSG
but excludes the feedback and memory components, resulting in unguided scenario generation by the VLM.

[

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

Objective Metric
. We adopted the same three objective functions as in Section
4.2
, i.e.,
DTO
,
Jerk
, and
TRG
, as the objective evaluation metrics to directly assess the effectiveness of the generated test scenarios in violating different requirements.

Performance Metric
. We incorporated four metrics from
[
9
]
to provide a performance assessment of the scenarios generated by
RVSG
for various requirement violations:

•

Robot on Person Collision
(
RPC
): count the number of times the robot collides with a person. This metric is calculated using the positions, orientations, and velocities of both the robot and humans to determine collision events and identify which party initiates the collision.

•

Cumulative Heading Changes
(
CHC
): measures the cumulative heading changes along the robot’s navigation, calculated as the sum of angles between successive waypoints. It reflects both path smoothness and motion stability, with higher values indicating less smooth and less stable movement.

•

Path Length
(
PL
): measures the distance the robot travels from the initial point to the goal position, which is used to assess navigation efficiency, with longer paths generally indicating less efficient planning.

•

Time Not Moving
(
TNM
): quantifies the seconds the robot remains stationary, which is used to evaluate the efficiency and continuity of the robot’s movement, with higher values indicating less continuous and effective navigation.

Diversity Metric
. To evaluate the diversity of the scenarios generated by
RVSG
, we defined the following metrics:

•

Scenario Description Diversity
(
SDD
): measures the diversity of natural language descriptions generated for scenarios, defined as one minus the average similarity computed by Sentence Transformers
[
22
]
. Higher values indicate greater diversity in scenario descriptions.

•

High-level Task Diversity
(
HTD
): measures the diversity of human high-level task descriptions by computing the average Levenshtein distance
[
23
]
between them, which quantifies how many single-character edits (insertions, deletions, or substitutions) are needed to change one string into another. Higher values indicate greater diversity.

•

Scenario Simulation Diversity
(
SSD
): measures the diversity of simulated scenarios by computing the average dynamic time warping (DTW) distance
[
24
]
between the time-series position data of the robot and humans. DTW calculates the distance between two temporal sequences, with larger distances indicating greater dissimilarity. Thus, higher values reflect greater diversity among simulated scenarios.

Threats to Validity

Concerning
conclusion validity concerns
, to draw a reliable conclusion, we employed appropriate statistical tests and a rigorous analysis based on established guides.
Regarding
internal validity
concerns related to parameters, we used the default parameters for the VLM from OpenAI and kept the same parameter settings for all approaches.
Concerning
constructive validity
threat, we used human-aware navigation metrics from HuNavSim
[
9
]
. These metrics are comparable and commonly applied in robot navigation
[
27
,
28
]
. To mitigate threats due to hallucinations and errors in VLM outputs, we manually reviewed a subset of the generated responses.
External Validity
concerns the generalizability of
RVSG
and the empirical evaluation. We employed three requirements and five robot navigation routes. The selected requirements represent key safety and functionality of the robot and are provided by PAL Robotics. The navigation routes are selected in consultation with PAL Robotics to reflect real-world operations and human-robot interactions. To address the simulator fidelity threat, we used Gazebo, a high-fidelity simulator that enables realistic modeling of robot dynamics, sensor noise, and human-agent interactions. Moreover, this simulator is used by PAL Robotics in their practice. Besides, to enhance the realism of human behaviors, we used HuNavSim
[
9
]
, which integrates the social force model and diverse human reactions to robot presence.

7
Lessons Learned and Industrial Perspectives

Benefits of VLMs in Test Scenario Generation
. VLMs offer several key benefits for automated scenario generation for AMR navigation testing. First, they can process multimodal inputs (e.g., visual and textual data) enabling them to generate environmental context-dependent scenarios (including human behaviors) that are logical for a given environment. For example, in a warehouse setting, a VLM can generate realistic human behaviors, such as workers picking items from shelves.
Second, VLMs possess commonsense reasoning capabilities
[
6
]
, allowing them to infer plausible risky human behaviors that could violate requirements. Third, their natural language understanding enables interpretation of high-level F&S requirements, ensuring that generated scenarios are focused on violating those requirements. Fourth, VLMs support diverse scenario generation, which enhances test coverage. With feedback and memory mechanisms,
RVSG
iteratively refines scenario quality, allowing learning from historical failures, adjusting human behavior configurations, and avoiding redundancy.

Practical Implications
. The practical adoption of
RVSG
can facilitate the development and testing of industrial AMRs.
RVSG
enables cost-effective and scalable testing by automating the generation of realistic, requirement-targeted scenarios without significant human effort. For example, a tester can specify requirements in natural language, which can then directly guide test generation, reducing both the need for technical expertise in robot testing and the overall implementation effort for coding tests.
VLMs are demonstrated for one testing application in
RVSG
; however, they can also support other testing activities, such as automated test oracle generation and regression testing. By interpreting high-level requirements and analyzing multimodal outputs, VLMs can help determine expected outcomes, thereby reducing manual effort to devise test oracles. Furthermore, VLMs can support AMR regression testing by automatically identifying updates in software across evolution and using them to guide regression testing (e.g., test case selection). Lastly, VLMs can support other AMR development phases, including requirements engineering (e.g., requirements identification), modeling and analysis (e.g., tools for automated creation of robot simulation models), and software development (e.g., automating code generation).

Related Works

Testing of Robotic Systems
.
Various robotic testing approaches have been proposed, e.g., simulation-based testing
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
and formal verification
[
33
,
34
]
. For example, Hutchison et al.
[

Brito et al.
[
36

To ensure the correct ROS operation, Santos et al.
[
32
]
presented a property-based testing approach for ROS system configurations, focusing on orchestration errors that occur when integrating different ROS components at runtime.
Compared to the above methods,
RVSG
focuses on system-level testing of an industrial robot to generate scenarios including human behaviors in simulation. It uses a VLM to search for semantically meaningful and realistic scenarios that challenge the robot’s behavior to violate its functional and safety requirements.

Foundation Models-based Test Generation
.
Foundation models (FMs), e.g., LLMs and VLMs, have shown benefits in many areas. Among them, FM-based test generation offers an effective way to generating tests
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. With techniques, e.g., prompting or fine-tuning, FMs support testing activities, including unit testing
[
39
,
40
]
and test oracle generation
[
41
,
42
]
.
Though FMs showed good performance in testing tasks like unit testing, their use to test complex software systems, e.g., robots, remains unexplored due to their complexity (e.g., real-time constraints and multimodal inputs), which pose unique testing challenges. Some works address these challenges for autonomous vehicles with FMs
[
43
,
44
]
.
In contrast,
RVSG
generates test scenarios for an industrial robot operating in dynamic environments.
Specifically,
RVSG
focuses on scenarios involving human-robot interactions, which are critical to both functionality and safety. Using VLMs,
RVSG
can synthesize realistic scenarios that expose potential violations of robots’ safety and functional requirements.

9
Conclusions and Future Work

We present
RVSG
, a VLM-based test scenario generation approach for robot navigation. Given a functional or safety requirement,
RVSG
generates scenarios violating the requirement.
RVSG
employs a VLM to extract visual features from an image describing a robot’s environment. Next, the extracted features are then used by the VLM to generate human behavior configurations along with the scenario description by iteratively querying the VLM with specifically designed prompts. The scenarios generated in each iteration are then executed in simulation, and the execution results are used as feedback for the next iteration to refine the scenarios. We evaluated
RVSG
in a warehouse environment and selected three requirements and five robot navigation routes for the experiment. The results show that
RVSG
can generate effective requirement-violating test scenarios that increase the variability of robot behaviors, helping reveal behavioral uncertainties. Meanwhile, the navigation routes affect the performance and stability of
RVSG
-generated scenarios.
Future works include studying the generalizability of
RVSG
by conducting experiments with other robots. Also, we plan to investigate multi-objective
RVSG
that can violate multiple requirements simultaneously.
Our open-source repository is now publicly available online
[
20
]
.
