Probing the Ground State of the Antiferromagnetic Heisenberg Model on the Kagome Lattice using Geometrically Informed Variational Quantum Eigensolver
Abdellah Tounsi, Nacer Eddine Belaloui, Abdelmouheymen Rabah Khamadja, Takei Eddine Fadi Lalaoui, Mohamed Messaoud Louamri, David E. Bernal Neira, Mohamed Taha Rouabah

Abstract
This work investigates the nature of the ground state of the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model on minimal kagome cells—a triangle and a star—using the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) algorithm on real quantum hardware. We demonstrate that the ground state preparation is achievable using a shallow hardware-efficient quantum circuit with a naturally Euclidean parameter space. Our custom ansatz is capable of accurately recovering essential properties such as the spin correlation terms for each edge without explicit error mitigation. These features are found to be less sensitive to noise. We exploited the Fubini-Study metric in constructing the ansatz, ensuring a singularity-free parameter space. With this ansatz design, the quantum natural gradient coincides with the normal gradient, complemented with a backtracking search for dynamic step size adaptation. We refer to this regime as the implicit adaptive quantum natural gradient descent. It achieves faster convergence in fewer iterations compared to simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (SPSA), while maintaining competitive runtime with the metric being analytically constant. We further apply error mitigation techniques, including zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) and qubit-wise readout error mitigation (REM). While ZNE does not obey the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle, the conditions under which REM preserves it are discussed.



I
Introduction

Finding the range of the ground state energy of a
k
-local Hamiltonian is known to be quantum Merlin Arthur hard (QMA-hard)
Kempe et al. 2004
, that is, the quantum analog of a classical NP-hard problem, mainly due to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space in interacting many-body systems resulting from their quantum nature, rendering the problem generally intractable for classical computers.
Despite the problem belonging to the QMA-hard complexity class, meaning that it is challenging to solve even when given a perfect quantum computer, the latter remains the most suitable approach to solve this class of problems because it follows the same laws
Feynman 1982
;
McArdle et al. 2020
, those of quantum mechanics
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.
However, despite recent advancements in the technology
Somoroff et al. 2023
;
Ganjam et al. 2024
, the current quantum computers are yet to reach their promised potential.
Short coherence times, high levels of noise, and limited connectivity, characteristics of
noisy intermediate-scale quantum
(NISQ) era computers
Preskill 2018
;
Bharti et al. 2022
, still pose a significant challenge and a roadblock to quantum advantage.

Identifying and preparing the ground state is crucial for studying many-body systems, enabling the understanding of their properties in the low-temperature regime.
For this task, the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) was introduced
Peruzzo et al. 2014
, a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm specifically designed to approximate the ground state energy using parametrized quantum circuits that are classically optimized.
Due to its flexibility and hybrid nature, this algorithm offers several potential advantages.
First, it can be scalable with a suitable choice of ansatz, thus handling larger systems with less overhead on the classical computer, effectively reducing the computational cost associated with the growth of the Hilbert space.
Secondly, the VQE is designed to be compatible with current NISQ devices
Cao et al. 2019
;
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
and their low quantum volume
Bharti et al. 2022
.
This is because it relies on relatively shallow quantum circuits to prepare the trial state, and the process of measuring observables is based on primitive sampling.
Estimating the energy with this algorithm comes at the cost of lower accuracy compared to quantum phase estimation (QPE)
Kitaev 1995
, a trade-off that is necessary in the absence of fault-tolerant quantum computers that are required to deal with the deep circuits of QPE.
Different methods have been developed to address this accuracy gap with NISQ computers.
Multiple error mitigation
Cai et al. 2023
and measurement optimization schemes
Yi et al. 2024
have been proposed to improve the estimates of VQE and its scalability, while alternative methods such as quantum sampling-based algorithms
Robledo-Moreno et al. 2025
;
Mikkelsen and Nakagawa 2025
have shown better scalability and higher resilience to noise.

This paper is concerned with the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
isotropic Heisenberg antiferromagnetic model on a kagome lattice (HAFK), a geometric arrangement of spins that has seen huge interest among researchers
Balents 2010
;
Semeghini et al. 2021
;

;
Kattemölle and van Wezel 2022
;
Sajjan et al. 2023
;

;
Wang et al. 2025
for its exotic magnetic properties and potential to manifest quantum spin liquids

Balents 2010
;
Semeghini et al. 2021

;
Mei et al. 2017
;
Tounsi et al. 2024
.
Due to the frustrated nature of this system, preparing its ground state is not a trivial task
Mendels and Bert 2011
;
Balents 2010
.

This system’s general ground state is not fully understood yet, especially in the thermodynamic limit ,i.e, an infinite lattice. Interestingly, numerical studies show very distinct results; initial numerical approaches of the DMRG showed a gapped ground state of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

I.1
Related Works

Quantum experiments on the kagome lattice are still very limited.
Theoretical proposals and classical simulations have been carried out. Some studies are devoted to optimizing the implementation of the Hamiltonian variational (HV) ansatz
Bosse and Montanaro 2022
;
Kattemölle and van Wezel 2022
, while some studies like
Guo et al. 2025
utilized the HV ansatz and showed phase transitions when considering different ratios of second-nearest neighbor interactions to first-nearest neighbor ones.
Although the HV ansatz is physically inspired, it is still too deep for NISQ devices.

To motivate our approach, we compare it to recent state-of-the-art efforts that explore similar directions from differing starting points.

Sajjan et al. 2023
follow a hybrid classical-quantum pipeline: they begin with DMRG computations on a reduced Hamiltonian to extract correlation data, then design an ansatz to replicate those features, and finally simulate and implement the VQE circuit, complemented by error mitigation techniques like T-REX and ZNE.
However, their implementation ultimately reduces the whole 12-qubit problem to an effective two-qubit ansatz.
Although this reduction is well motivated, it does not offer a useful benchmark for our setting, where our objective is to evaluate the VQE procedure under more agnostic and less informed conditions.
Conversely, we design a generic ansatz based on the hardware connections and native gates, then adapt it to give at least one ground state and analyze it with the Fubini-Study metric to avoid zero metric or undefined geometry in the energy landscape.
Despite the presence of noise, we are able to characterize the resulting quantum state and extract structural information.
Notably, our optimization starts from high-energy product states and still converges, highlighting the robustness of the variational landscape and the optimization strategy.

Weaving et al. 2025
use a symmetry-based qubit reduction strategy, identifying exact and approximate
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries to taper the Hamiltonian down to five qubits.
After validating the spectrum via DMRG, they apply VQE with extensive error mitigation, including REM, multi-fold ZNE, and symmetry verification of measurement outcomes.
While their method excels in preserving energy estimates under strong qubit compression, its scope is restricted to the energy alone.
Our aim, by contrast, focuses on the structure and correlations of the quantum state itself, which is essential for understanding ordering, entanglement, and potential quantum phases beyond the spectral analysis.

Semeghini et al. 2021
follow a different experimental direction by preparing RVB states using Rydberg atom arrays. They encode each edge of the kagome lattice as an atom and use adiabatic evolution with the blockade mechanism’s constraints to prepare dimer coverings, enabling the study of topological properties and quasiparticle excitations.
Despite the scalability of their platform and its suitability to probe RVB physics, it is confined to a specific candidate state and not the general solution of the Heisenberg model.

In this work, we focus on the VQE’s performance and its ability to characterize the ground state.
We design a custom shallow hardware-efficient ansatz to prepare the ground state of two systems: a single triangle and the six corner-sharing triangles making one kagome star.
We employ the Quantum Natural Gradient Descent (QNGD), an optimization technique designed to rescale gradient updates using the Fubini-Study metric tensor
Stokes et al. 2020
, thereby accounting for the local geometry of the parameter space. In our case, however, the ansatz is constructed such that the Fubini–Study metric is diagonal and constant. This renders the parameter space Euclidean, eliminating singularities and making the natural gradient coincides with the standard parameter-shift gradient. Consequently, our optimization is implicitly QNGD while reduced in practice to ordinary gradient descent, thereby avoiding the need to measure the expensive Fubini–Study metric at each iteration. To further enhance convergence, we combine it with a backtracking line search that dynamically adapts the step size
Atif et al. 2023
. We refer to this regime as Implicit-Adaptive Quantum Natural Gradient Descent (I-AQNGD), since it retains the benefits of AQNGD without incurring the overhead of explicitly measuring the metric.
In addition to this setting, error mitigation with low postprocessing cost is applied at the end of the VQE to improve the final ground energy estimate. Beyond of the energy estimation, we are able to characterize the dimer state by measuring the spin-spin correlations and the static spin structure factor, which exhibits resilience to noise.

III
Methods

In general, the parameters of the quantum circuit in variational quantum algorithms are optimized similarly to other classical optimization problems or machine learning models. However, the quantum nature of the circuit imposes some new challenges. To begin with, the quantum measurement outcome is intrinsically probabilistic even in the presence of an ideal quantum computer with absolutely no noise. In addition, current quantum devices suffer from an additional layer of noise coming from coherent and incoherent errors, state preparation errors, and measurement errors. Moreover, the ansatz requires a significant number of parameters to cover the Hilbert space region that includes the ground state. The cost to estimate the gradient in gradient-based optimizers is proportional to the number of parameters
d
; it is still possible, though, to optimize the number of shots for gradient estimation using the parameter-shift rule
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019
. Even though zeroth-order stochastic optimization methods incur only a fixed cost per iteration, the most efficient of these still have a convergence rate that grows polynomially with the number of parameters
d
. On the other hand, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) achieves convergence rates that do not depend on the number of parameters
d
in convex optimization problems
Stokes et al. 2020
. That said, quantum cost functions are generally non-convex. In such cases, classical machine learning has demonstrated the effectiveness of stochastic gradient methods in optimizing deep neural networks, which similarly involve minimizing non-convex objectives in high-dimensional parameter spaces
Stokes et al. 2020
.
Finally, the cost function landscape is complex, besides local minima, variational quantum algorithms can also face the problem of barren plateaus, when the gradient magnitude vanishes exponentially with the system’s size
Larocca et al. 2025
.

Given these properties, the VQE algorithm must be designed with careful considerations of the hardware capabilities and the problem’s characteristics to design a well-suited ansatz-optimizer pair.

III.1
Adaptive Quantum Natural Gradient Descent (AQNGD)

Searching for the correct parameters that represent the ground state is the central challenge for the classical part of VQE. For this task, various optimizers have been used, such as the normal gradient descent and stochastic perturbative ones; however, they do not take into account the geometry of the space of parametrized quantum states, called the Kähler manifold or the projective quantum space, considering it to be represented by an Euclidean space that is free of singularities. In quantum applications, the space of parametrized quantum states is rarely Euclidean
Kolodrubetz et al. 2017
;
Bukov et al. 2019
; it usually has a curvature featured by the Fubini-Study metric and hosts singularities and covariant parameters, which make the search for the optimal parameters very challenging
Yamamoto 2019
. Following the natural gradient approach from classical machine learning
Amari 1998
;
Stokes et al. 2020
, the quantum natural gradient Descent (QNGD) optimizer was proposed as its quantum analog. This optimizer uses the steepest gradient descent in the parameter space with an induced Fubini-Study metric, successfully avoiding singularities and considering parameters’ covariance. The transformation from the Euclidean space to the Riemannian parameter space is done by taking the inverse of the Fubini-Study metric tensor, which is the real part of the quantum Fisher information matrix. The Fubini-Study metric can also be used, as we will show later, to decide on an ansatz design with a naturally Euclidean parameter space that is more easily trainable at a lower cost than more complex ones. For more details on the QNGD, see Appendix
B
and the works of
Stokes et al. 2020
and
Yamamoto 2019
.

Although QNGD trains parameters faster and in the correct convergence direction, it still faces the problem of choosing the ideal learning rate, the algorithm can get stuck around the ground state and never reach the minimum if the geometry becomes tighter than the step size, which is common in entangled systems with a large learning rate
Yamamoto 2019
. On the other hand, choosing an extremely small step size will significantly slow the algorithm since it will be making really small steps when the geometry does not necessitate it.
Choosing a step size that monotonically decreases is not a reliable solution either as it is not geometrically informed and will be heavily problem dependent.

A way around this was presented in
Atif et al. 2023
, who used QNGD with a variable, geometrically informed, learning rate following the Armijo rule, which is an efficient backtracking rule with proven convergence. This allows us to adaptively choose a large step size when needed to converge faster without sacrificing the ability to navigate narrow parts of the geometry, thus avoiding stagnation by adapting the step size to the local geometry at each iteration. With this addition, we mitigate the dependenc of the QNGD’s performance on the chosen step size. Although this method introduces new hyperparameters to set, as we will see below, these are less problematic to choose than the step size.

This new adaptive quantum natural gradient descent (AQNGD) algorithm is detailed in Algorithm
2
. In the quantum natural gradient descent, the parameters are updated according to the gradient value, which is measured using the parameter-shift rule method that gives the Euclidean gradient, multiplied by the inverse of the Fubini-Study block diagonal metric, which squeezes and stretches the gradient according to the geometry of the parameter space. The latter is a practical approximation of the full Fubini-Study metric that would require exhaustive operations and techniques that are near impossible to implement on NISQ devices
Stokes et al. 2020
. The learning rate is adapted using the Armijo rule
Armijo 1966
as detailed in Algorithm

Ideally, the ansatz for a specific physical problem should satisfy three conditions: (i)
Expressivity
, i.e., the ability to prepare the system’s ground state. (ii)
Hardware efficiency
, ensuring that meaningful information survives the inherent QPU noise. (iii)
Trainability
, meaning the parameter space is well-behaved so that the classical optimizer can efficiently converge to the optimal parameters.

The best physically inspired ansätze designs for the kagome lattice so far have been based on the Hamiltonian variational ansatz (HVA), which can be viewed as a digital approximation of adiabatic evolution.
Park and Killoran 2024
proved that, in the absence of decoherent noise and under specific constraints, the HVA does not suffer from barren plateaus.
Bosse and Montanaro 2022
optimized the number of layers required for the HVA on QPUs with a square-lattice architecture.
Kattemölle and van Wezel 2022
proposed an HVA-inspired symmetry-preserving ansatz using dense layers of parametrized Heisenberg interaction gates starting from a dimer state, and further predicted the error rate and circuit depth required to achieve a given fidelity for arbitrary system sizes. A more hardware-friendly ansatz targeting valence-bond states (VBS) composed of dimer bonds was introduced by
Sajjan et al. 2023
.

In this work, our goal is to
develop an expressive, trainable, hardware-efficient ansatz that can be implemented on current QPUs without relying on heavy problem-specific reductions, but only hardware constraints. To achieve this, we construct an ansatz based on a simple pattern of parametrized single qubit rotations combined with entangling gates between physically connected qubits to avoid SWAP gates. Our ansatz, shown in Fig.
2
, is inspired by the well-known
RealAmplitudes
ansatz, but reduces the number of
R_{y}
gates. Specifically, we found that placing
R_{y}
rotations on every qubit both before and after the entangling layer was unnecessary. Instead, we alternate their placement, splitting them between the two sides of the entangling layer so that each qubit carries only a single
R_{y}
rotation. This choice serves to reduce the number of parameters, making the ansatz more trainable, while also mitigating some of the coherent noise coming from the entangling layer through the parametrized gates after it.

Beyond this structural simplification, a central feature of our ansatz is that it naturally has a parameter space with a Euclidean geometry. By analytically evaluating the Fubini–Study metric (see Appendix
B
), we show that it is diagonal and constant for our construction. This means that the parameters vary independently (i.e. no over-parameterization), the parameter space is free of singularities, and the gradients obtained via the parameter-shift rule coincide with the true natural gradients. In other words, our ansatz is not only hardware efficient, but also intrinsically trainable.

Altogether, this ansatz fulfills the three conditions outlined above: It is expressive, hardware efficient, and intrinsically trainable, thanks to its Euclidean parameter space. Our results confirm its expressivity, as it successfully prepares the ground state and reproduces physical observables such as the static spin structure factor
S(\boldsymbol{q})
. Looking forward, this construction opens the way for further investigations into scaling this family of Euclidean hardware-efficient ansätze to larger kagome lattices and, more broadly, to other frustrated quantum magnets.

III.4
Readout Error Mitigation

One of the main sources of noise in quantum devices is the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) errors. Specifically, the measurement or readout errors are well-known in many areas of physics such as astronomy and high-energy physics. The main assumption is that such readout errors are governed by the response matrix
R
, which is a stochastic matrix that stores the probability of measuring a specific state
\ket{{f}}=\ket{f_0 f_1 \cdots f_N}
given that the
N
qubits are prepared in a prior state
\ket{{i}}=\ket{i_0 i_1 \cdots i_N}
. Here,
i,f\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes N}
. Namely, the
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
components of the
R
matrix are

\displaystyle R_{{f}{i}}=P({f}|{i})

(4)

which is interpreted as the probability of measuring
\ket{{f}}\bra{{f}}
state given that the true state is
\ket{{i}}\bra{{i}}
. Therefore, the relation between the true and the noisy vectors of bitstrings
\mathbf{t}=\sum_{k}t_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}
and
\mathbf{n}=\sum_{k}n_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}
respectively are related as follows

\displaystyle n_{k}=\sum_{l}R_{kl}t_{l},

(5)

which suggests that the true readout vector is nothing but
\mathbf{t}=R^{-1}\mathbf{n}
. Traditionally, Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse is taken since the stochastic matrices are not generally invertible. Unfortunately, taking the inverse of an exponentially matrix is a hard computation overhead.
Nachman et al. 2020
suggested unfolding techniques borrowed from high energy physics. However, the characterization of the full
R
matrix by itself is not efficient since it requires
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
state preparations and the same amount of memory for storage. Nevertheless, the full characterization of the
R
matrix assumes correlated all-to-all cross-talking between the qubits.

Geller and Sun 2021
proposed an efficient measurement method based on the characterization of the
k
nearest neighbor qubits, requiring
O(4^{k}N^{2})
where
N
is the number of qubits, but also the classical post-processing of a
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
matrix which still not scalable.
In practice, we can assume that cross-talking occurs just locally between nearest neighbors in QPUs of restricted connectivity
Geller and Sun 2021
, so

\displaystyle P(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{N}|b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{N})

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle\in\mathcal{P}}P_{jk\cdots l}(a|b)

(6)

such that
P_{jk\cdots l}
is the probability of transition of the specific qubits labeled by
jk\cdots l
sharing the same neighborhood, and
\mathcal{P}
is the partition of the qubit register.

The immediate advantage of this model is the efficiency of taking the inverse of the response matrix since
P^{-1}=\bigotimes_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle\in\mathcal{P}}P^{-1}_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}(a|b)
. Moreover, the correction process for a given measurement result
\boldsymbol{m}=\sum_{k}m_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}
involves only the application of the inverse partitioned response matrix on the relevant sections of the qubit register. That is, the recovered measurement result is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle=R^{-1}\sum_{p}m_{p}\ket{p}\bra{p}=\sum_{p}m_{p}R^{-1}\ket{p}\bra{p}.

(7)

We use the partitioned response matrix. Hence,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=\sum_{p}m_{p}\bigotimes_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}R^{-1}_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}\ket{p_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}}\bra{p_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}}.

(8)

We apply each
R^{-1}
matrix partition to the corresponding
\ket{p}\bra{p}
partition as a probability vector using the inverse of Eq.(
5
). Hence,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=

\displaystyle\sum_{p}m_{p}\bigotimes_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}\sum_{q_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}}P^{-1}_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}(p|q)\ket{q_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}}\bra{q_{\langle jk\cdots l\rangle}}.

(9)

Since the number of measured samples of bitstrings in
\boldsymbol{m}
should be small in practice, the global sum is still practical.
However, the tensor products can yield exponentially large samples. Let
N_{p}=N/P
be the number of qubits, such that
P
is the number of partitions. Then, the inner sum spans
2^{N_{p}}
states, while the tensor product will yield
2^{N_{p}P}=2^{N}
states in the computational basis. Nevertheless, it is possible to truncate results with the largest probabilities. It is worth to note that the REM method faces the issue of negative probabilities since the inverse does not preserve the stochasticity of the response matrix. Even though the negative pseudo-probabilities are absorbed in the estimation of any physical observable, it is important to note that the positivity of the pseudo-probabilities is crucial for maintaining the variational principle of Rayleigh and Ritz to be valid. Otherwise, positivity can be retained by involving more efforts to minimize
||R\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{m}||

Geller and Sun 2021
or minimize
||\mathbf{t}-R^{-1}\mathbf{m}||
. The latter is computationally less demanding. In this work, we show the REM results with both scenarios: the positivity preserving and non-preserving REM since the former still valid since, generally, the negative pseudo-probabilities have small values.

III.5
Zero-Noise Error Mitigation

One of the efficient error mitigation techniques is the zero-noise error mitigation, which was initially developed by
Li and Benjamin 2017
and
Temme et al. 2017
. The basic idea behind this technique is the possibility to tune the quantum machine’s noise with a single parameter
\lambda
in such a way that the expectation value depends on this error parameter as follows

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle(\lambda)=E_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\lambda^{k}+O(\lambda^{n+1})

(10)

where
E_{0}
is the expected value without noise.
Li and Benjamin 2017
showed that this is true when the evolution of the quantum circuit is modeled with the master equation
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-[H,\rho]+\lambda\mathcal{L}[\rho]
where
\mathcal{L}
is the Linblad superoperator. Although originally tuning
\lambda
was performed by rescaling execution time in quantum computers
Li and Benjamin 2017
;
Temme et al. 2017
;
Kandala et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2023
, it is practically preferred to apply a unitary circuit or gate folding on digital quantum devices
Dumitrescu et al. 2018
;
Giurgica-Tiron et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
. Folding technique, despite its practicality, scales only the circuit internal decoherence noise, while in principle keeping the SPAM errors fixed.

In this work, unitary global folding is performed considering the folding points
1
,
3
and
5
. Previously, different extrapolation methods have been used;
Li and Benjamin 2017
used the Richardson’s deferred approach, while
Giurgica-Tiron et al. 2020
benchmarked different methods on IBMQ London five-qubit chip, showing unstability of Richardson’s extrapolation while better accuracy with exponential extrapolation. The choice of the extrapolation method still an ambiguous feature of ZNE method. Nevertheless, in this work, we choose the polynomial model of ZNE with the minimal degree that fits the points to avoid overfitting. In our case, the second-degree polynomial is used. Moreover, to infer the standard error of the extrapolated zero-noise energy, we use the Bayesian polynomial regression (BPR) with heteroscedastic noise technique assuming a Gaussian process
M. 2006
;
Gelman et al. 2025
.

Lastly, it is worth noting that ZNE does not guarantee an upper bound of the exact energy since it allows the violation of Rayleigh–Ritz variational principle.

V
Discussion

In Fig.
5(a)
, we can clearly see the comparable optimization performance of the I-AQNGD and the SPSA methods in real quantum devices featured by noise. I-AQNGD is a promising optimization method since it requires fewer iterations to converge compared to the inherently noise-adapted SPSA method. Even though the number of circuit executions is much more than what is required by an SPSA implementation, scaling linearly in the number of parameters, we should emphasize thatI-AQNGD is guaranteed to converge and thus more scalable than zeroth-order stochastic optimization methods.
To assess the role of adaptation in the I-AQNGD performance, it is sufficient to check the profile of the
k
values during the optimization. Fig.
4(b)
and Fig.
5(b)
show the variation of the
k
value in each iteration, illustrating that the step size adaptation is needed during the optimization, although the Armijo rule was not fulfilled in each iteration as doing so requires a large
k_{m}
and significantly more fault-tolerant hardware. Eventually, the built-in-ansatz quantum natural gradient of I-AQNGD, presented in Sec.
III.2
, did not negatively affect the optimization and retained the benefits of AQNGD without the measurement overhead.

In addition, as shown in Sec.
IV.1
and Sec.
IV.2
, despite the significant spectral gap yielded by the energy characterization of the ground state, the optimized parameters show impressive accordance with the exact ground state parameters without any reliance on error mitigation techniques. This observation can be quantified by evaluating the fidelity and energy values using the state-vector simulation of the ansätze. Namely, the state-vector fidelities are
99.98\%
in the case of one triangle, and
96.37\%
in the case of one kagome star, while the spectral gaps are
\Delta E=8\cdot 10^{-5}
and
\Delta E=0.14674
for the triangle and the kagome star, respectively.

Although this observation highlights the promising capabilities of real quantum devices and the VQE protocol
Sorourifar et al. 2024
;
Belaloui et al. 2025
, it becomes impractical and unscalable if such accurate states are inaccessible by quantum computing means. In this work, we show that characterizing the dimer state using spin correlation and spin structure factor is possible qualitatively even without error mitigation. This result is in agreement with the recent work of
Lively et al. 2024
who showed that the prediction of the energy derivative, two-site spin correlation functions, and the fidelity susceptibility from VQE results can be accurate even without error mitigation. For instance, local observables are less sensitive than non-local ones
Kim et al. 2023
. Nevertheless, quantum error mitigation (EM) techniques have great importance in this context. The result of error mitigation implemented on the last ansatz obtained from the VQE for one kagome star are detailed in Tab.
2
. Although the results are strongly stochastic, the accuracy of results is enhanced with different levels using different EM techniques. First of all, we notice that REM alone fails in one case to reach a significant accuracy despite its success in the case of three-site lattice as shown in Fig.
7(b)
. The combination of REM and ZNE undershoots in most cases, which may indicate an overlap between the effects of the two methods. Impressively, ZNE with quadratic extrapolation obtained repeatedly the best accuracy possible in different devices. We should mention that the coherent noise is not mitigated in our experiments using randomized compilation (RC) techniques. The numerical simulations by
Kurita et al. 2023
showed that the combination of RC and ZNE yields better accuracy without considering SPAM errors, while ZNE only undershoots generally in the absence of SPAM errors. If we assume that the readout error-mitigated energy values accumulate coherent noise errors only, it is possible to explain undershooting in our experiments by the presence of coherent noise. However, it is interesting that ZNE only performs better. Therefore, we hypothesize that the readout errors have rendered coherent noise into a stochastic one. This effect needs a careful dedicated study.
In the end, it is important to recall that REM can be implemented while preserving the variational principle, while there is yet no known way to do similarly with ZNE.

Lastly, it is worth noting that the preparation of the ground state of one kagome star is still compatible with the architecture of the heavy-hex topology of the quantum device. This shows again how important to solve for device-native problems to get meaningful results.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we focused on the preparation of the ground state of kagome lattice fragments on real quantum devices, showing that this can be achieved with shallow circuits using the
variational quantum eigensolver
(VQE). Remarkably, this holds even in the presence of significant noise and without applying error mitigation. Although noise limits the quantitative accuracy of energy estimation, essential qualitative features—such as the spin correlation structure of the dimer state—remain accessible, owing to the energy gap that protects certain observables from noise effects.

We exploited the Fubini-Study metric as a tool to analyze the geometry of the ansatz and designed it to have a Euclidean parameter space (ie. constant diagonal Fubini-Study metric), which allows for a smooth and easy optimization without facing singularities. Building upon this, we believe that this approach of carefully designing the ansatz’s geometry holds significant potential. We posit that such a design methodology can be exploited to design problem-tailored ansätze with analytically computable Fubini-Study metrics, which leads to expressive, hardware-efficient, and intrinsically trainable ansätze at low costs. This suggests that this kind of ansätze architecture could enable increasingly accurate and robust preparation of complex ground states for frustrated systems as quantum hardware continues to progress, including larger kagome lattices and quantum spin liquids.
We analyzed the performance of the
Implicit-adaptive quantum natural gradient descent
(I-AQNGD), where backtracking search is used, in comparison with SPSA, showing that I-AQNGD achieves convergence in fewer iterations while maintaining competitive runtime thanks to the constant metric. We showed that the adaptive feature through backtracking search enables faster convergence less dependent on the starting point.

Our results further reveal that error mitigation techniques can substantially improve the accuracy of observable estimates:
readout error mitigation
(REM) preserves the variational principle, providing reliable upper bounds, while
zero-noise extrapolation
(ZNE) does not respect this bound but often achieves the highest accuracy, suggesting potential for future studies. We also showed that certain observables, such as the static spin structure factor, are less sensitive to noise than the Hamiltonian, suggesting a good characterization of some of the ground state properties despite the challenges of the quantum hardware.

Finally, we highlight that solving device-native problems such as the one kagome star ground state on heavy-hex architectures not only enables realistic benchmarking but also paves the way for scaling to larger kagome lattices. Future work will focus on refining ZNE strategies, integrating randomized compilation to address coherent noise, and extending these methods to characterize the ground state of larger quantum spin liquid systems.

Appendix B
Block-Diagonal Fubini-Study Approximation

The infinitesimal Euclidean distance between two parameterized quantum states
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bra{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
and
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+d\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\ket{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta} + d\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bra{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta} + d\boldsymbol{\theta})}
, such that
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle d^{2}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+d\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}}g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})d\theta_{i}d\theta_{j}

(33)

where
g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\text{Re }G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and the quantum geometric tensor
G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is defined as
Stokes et al. 2020
:

\displaystyle G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rangle\left\langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right\rangle.

(34)

This tensor is computed based on the Fubini-Study distance
Stokes et al. 2020
.

In the case of ansatz involving layers of parametrized Pauli rotation gates, the quantum geometric tensor can be
simplified analytically since the parameterized ansatz form is given by:

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{0}=

\displaystyle V_{L}U_{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{L})V_{L-1}U_{L-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{L-1})\cdots V_{1}U_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})V_{0}\ket{0},

(35)

U_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-i \theta_l^j P^j_l)

is the unitary operator of the
l

N

V_{l}
is a fixed unitary operator that does not depend on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l}
.
Therefore, if the Fubini-Study metric is approximated by block matrices for each layer, the quantum geometric tensor can be approximated as
G_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\bigoplus_{l}G^{l}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})

Stokes et al. 2020
, where

\displaystyle G^{l}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{l}|P^{i}_{l}P^{j}_{l}|\psi_{l}\rangle-\langle\psi_{l}|P^{i}_{l}|\psi_{l}\rangle\langle\psi_{l}|P^{j}_{l}|\psi_{l}\rangle.

(36)

P_{l}^{i}
and
P_{l}^{j}

Appendix C
Measured Energy Variance

We show here how we compute the emperical variance and the standard deviation of energy value obtained from qubit-wise commuting Pauli words grouping. In the experiment, we showed that sampling three circuits in the
X
,
Y
, and
Z
basis is enough to estimate the energy as follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle H\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle H_{X}\right\rangle+\left\langle H_{Y}\right\rangle+\left\langle H_{Z}\right\rangle

(44)

such that

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{X}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{X}}\langle b|\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}|b\rangle,

(45)

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{Y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{Y}}\langle b|\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}|b\rangle,

(46)

\displaystyle\left\langle H_{Z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{Z}}\langle b|\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}|b\rangle.

(47)

where
\mathcal{S}_{X}
,
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{Z}
are independent sets of bitstrings that are measured in the
X
,
Y
, and
Z
eigenbases, respectively.
Statistically, since the sets of samples are independent, the covariance of the variables
H_{X}
,
H_{Y}
, and
H_{Z}
is zero. Hence, the variance of
H
is nothing but

\displaystyle\text{Var}(H)=\text{Var}(H_{X})+\text{Var}(H_{Y})+\text{Var}(H_{Z}).

(48)

The variance of each variable
H_{P}
can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\text{Var}(H_{P})=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{P}}\langle b|(\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}-\langle H_{P}\rangle)^{2}|b\rangle,

(49)

which leads to the reduced form

\displaystyle\text{Var}(H_{P})=\langle H_{P}^{2}\rangle-\langle H_{P}\rangle^{2}.

(50)

We can show using the identity projection
I=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\ket{b}\bra{b}
that

\displaystyle\text{Var}(H_{P})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{S}_{P}}(\langle b|\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}|b\rangle-\langle H_{P}\rangle)^{2},

(51)

which is nothing but the emperical variance of the energy value obtained from the
P
basis measurements. The standard deviation is then given by

\displaystyle\sigma(H)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(H)}.

(52)

The standard error is then

\displaystyle\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\text{Var}(H)}{N}}

(53)
