Bubble-wall speed with loop corrections
Andrii Dashko, Andreas Ekstedt

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the dynamics of the nucleating scalar field during the first-order phase transitions by incorporating one-loop corrections of classical fluctuations. We assume that a high-temperature expansion is valid—where the mass of the scalar field is significantly smaller than the temperature—so that we can treat the bubble-wall dynamics in a regime where quantum fluctuations can be integrated out. We present a systematic framework for calculating classical loop corrections to the wall speed; contrast our results with traditional methods based on the derivative expansion; show that the latent heat can differ from the effective-potential result; and discuss general hydrodynamic corrections. Finally, we show an application of the presented framework for a simple scalar field model, finding that the one-loop improvement decreases the wall speed and that an effective-potential approximation underestimates full one-loop corrections by about a factor of two.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have assessed the effect of loop corrections on the equation of motion of the nucleating scalar field during the first-order phase transition, focusing on the impact on the speed of the bubble wall.

The work is split into two main parts: first, the correlator of the nucleating scalar field fluctuations with an inhomogeneous background was calculated, and then the impact of these fluctuations on the bubble profile and bubble-wall velocity was determined. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of the effects of one-loop fluctuations of the scalar field on the bubble-wall speed.

To illustrate the calculations, we have further considered an example of a nucleating real scalar field in three dimensions. It was shown that one-loop corrections decrease the wall speed, reaching tens of percent correction to the tree-level velocity, giving the highest corrections at the border of perturbativity. The effective-potential approximation—leading-order derivative expansion term—underestimates the decrease of the wall speed by approximately a factor of two. Higher terms in the derivative expansion do not improve the situation. Thus signaling the failure of the validity of the aforementioned expansion for the bubble.

The findings presented in this paper may be used in further studies in more realistic scenarios, by including larger particle content and adding hydrodynamical effects. The framework for handling more complex cases, such as non-constant fluid velocity, multifield propagators, and hydrodynamical backreactions, has been presented in the appendices.

\displaystyle T^{\prime\prime}_{k}+2\sqrt{k^{2}+W(z)+F(z)}\,T^{\prime}_{k}+\frac{W^{\prime}(z)+F^{\prime}(z)}{2\sqrt{k^{2}+W(z)+F(z)}}\,T_{k}+F(z)\,T_{k}=0

\displaystyle\text{with}\ T^{\prime}_{k}(-\infty)=0\ \text{and}\ T_{k}(-\infty)=1,

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}_{k}-2\sqrt{k^{2}+W(z)+F(z)}\,H^{\prime}_{k}+\frac{W^{\prime}(z)+F^{\prime}(z)}{2\sqrt{k^{2}+W(z)+F(z)}}\,H_{k}+F(z)\,H_{k}=0

(32)

\displaystyle\text{with}\ H^{\prime}_{k}(\infty)=0\ \text{and}\ H_{k}(\infty)=1.

Here we have introduced an auxiliary function
F(z)
such that
F(z)+W(z)\geq 0
for all
z
; this is just to simplify the numerical evaluations, and the final result is, of course, independent of
F(z)
.

Finally, given
T_{k}(z)
and
H_{k}(z)
, the full propagator is given by

This follows after using the standard jumping conditions at

A.1
Handling zero-modes

Contrary to other particles, for Higgs and Goldstone propagators hit an additional obstacle due to the presence of zero-modes. Take, for example, the Higgs case, where
W(z)=V^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{4}
. The zero mode is then
\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)e^{-z\omega_{0}/2}
; the presence of zero modes is an artifact of the broken symmetry of the equation of motion by the background solution. A normal eigenfunction expansion then implies that

\displaystyle G_{k}(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{2}{k^{2}}\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{0}(z)e^{-z\omega_{0}/2}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z^{\prime})e^{-z^{\prime}\omega_{0}/2}}{N_{\phi}}+\ldots,

(34)

\displaystyle N_{\phi}=\int ds\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(s)^{2}e^{-s\omega_{0}}

(35)

The zero-mode term vanishes in the integral over
k
if we use dimensional regularization, but small
k
values are still troublesome numerically. To deal with this snag we subtract 0 from (
18
):

\displaystyle 0=-\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)^{2}e^{-z\omega_{0}}\int dkk^{-1}=-\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)^{2}e^{-z\omega_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{k_{\text{max}}}+\int_{k_{\text{max}}}^{\infty}\right]dkk^{-1}.

(36)

We then explicitly add the first term to equation (
18
) for each
k
, while the second term effectively gives
\frac{2}{N_{\phi}}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)^{2}e^{-z\omega_{0}}\log k_{\text{max}}
.

The procedure is the same for Goldstone modes; for example, for a complex scalar where
W(z)=\overline{\phi}_{0}(z)^{-1}V^{\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{4}
the zero mode is
e^{-z\omega_{0}/2}\overline{\phi}_{0}(z)
, and the procedure is identical to the Higgs case.

A.2
The WKB approximation

In case
k^{2}\gg W(z)
the
z
-dependence of
W(z)
is just a minor consideration, which allows us to solve

within the WKB approximation.
For
G_{k}^{</>}

\displaystyle\log G_{k}^{</>}\approx\pm\left[kz\pm\frac{1}{2k}\int dsW(s)\right]+\log\left|1-\frac{1}{4k^{2}}W(z)\right|.

(37)

Similar expansion can be made with the functions that appeared in our ansatz

\displaystyle\Psi^{</>}(z)=\exp\left[\pm\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{k^{2}+W(s)}ds\right]\approx\exp\left[\pm kz\pm\frac{1}{2k}\int_{0}^{z}dsW(s)\right].

(38)

Lastly, after imposing the boundary conditions, we find the asymptotic expansion for
T_{k}
and
H_{k}

\displaystyle T_{k}\approx 1-\frac{W(z)-W(-\infty)}{4k^{2}},

(39)

\displaystyle H_{k}\approx 1-\frac{W(z)-W(\infty)}{4k^{2}}.

(40)

Appendix B
Non-constant fluid velocity

B.1
Without one-loop hydrodynamic backreactions

Consider again the operator

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{H}\hat{\phi}=\left(-\partial_{z}^{2}-\vec{\nabla}^{2}_{\perp}+\omega_{0}(z)\partial_{z}+V^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))\right)\hat{\phi},

(41)

now with a z-dependent
\omega_{0}
, as occurs if we solve the full hydrodynamical equations. A first approximation is to incorporate
\omega_{0}(z)
without adding the
\hat{\phi}
dependence of the wall speed and temperature.

In this case, we can make this operator self-adjoint with the shift
\hat{\phi}\rightarrow e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z}\omega_{0}(s)ds}\hat{\phi}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{H}\rightarrow\left(-\partial_{z}^{2}-\vec{\nabla}^{2}_{\perp}+V^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{\prime}(z)\right).

(42)

The results in Section
3
are the same after defining
W(z)=V^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{\prime}(z)
. Note also that the new Higgs zero-mode is
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z}\omega_{0}(z)ds}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)
.

Finally, to find corrections to the leading-order wall speed we simply use
e^{-\int_{0}^{z}\omega_{0}(z)ds}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)
in place of
e^{-z\omega_{0}(z)}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}(z)
in equation (
11
).

B.2
Propagators with general equations of motion

Consider the general equation of motion

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)+V^{\prime}(\phi,\theta)+F(\phi,\phi^{\prime},\theta)=0,

(43)

where for example
\theta
might represent the fluid velocity or the temperature. After we expand around a leading-order solution,
\phi=\overline{\phi}+\hat{\phi}
, we then get

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\delta_{\phi}\left[V^{\prime}(\phi,\theta)+F(\phi,\phi^{\prime},\theta)\right]_{\phi=\overline{\phi}}\hat{\phi}(z)=0.

(44)

Here
F
might be a local friction term as in
section

4
, but
F
could also come from the solution of a Boltzmann equation
[
24
]
, and would then, in general, be a non-local function of
\phi
.
In taking the
\phi
derivative we have to take into account the
\phi
dependence of
\theta
; for example a field-dependent damping coefficient or the temperature. All in all, this ensures that
\overline{\phi}^{\prime}
is a zero-mode of the above operator.

The propagator can then be found as usual if we can evaluate
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\phi}
.

B.3
Adding one-loop hydrodynamic backreactions

As an example of the previous subsection, consider adding one-loop hydrodynamic corrections
8
8

8

We here neglect the bulk and shear viscosity. If these are present we would also expect velocity and temperature fluctuations.
. First, we need the hydrodynamical equations in the wall frame:

\displaystyle wv_{w}\gamma_{w}^{2}=\text{const},

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\phi^{\prime})^{2}-V+wv_{w}^{2}\gamma_{w}^{2}=\text{const},

(46)

where the enthalpy is of the form

. If we omit the second-term we can then solve for
v_{w}
and

\displaystyle\delta v=\underbrace{\frac{1}{aT^{4}\gamma_{w}^{2}v_{w}}}_{=a_{w}}\left[V^{\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0})\hat{\phi}-\overline{\phi}_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\phi}^{\prime}\right],\quad\delta T=-\underbrace{\frac{v_{w}^{2}+1}{4aT^{3}v_{w}^{2}}}_{=b_{w}}\left[V^{\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0})\hat{\phi}-\overline{\phi}_{0}^{\prime}\hat{\phi}^{\prime}\right]

(47)

Adding these corrections to the Higgs equation of motion we find

\begin{split}\widehat{L}_{H}\hat{\phi}=&\left(-\partial_{z}^{2}-\vec{\nabla}^{2}_{\perp}+\omega_{0}(z)\partial_{z}+V^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))\right)\hat{\phi}+\left[a_{w}\partial_{v_{w}}\omega_{0}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}+b_{w}\partial_{T}V^{\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))\right]\hat{\phi}\\
&-\left[a_{w}\partial_{v_{w}}\omega_{0}\overline{\phi}^{\prime}_{0}+b_{w}\partial_{T}V^{\prime}(\overline{\phi}_{0}(z))\right]\hat{\phi}^{\prime}.\end{split}

(48)

This operator can now be made self-adjoint as in the previous sub-section, and propagators for

and
\hat{\phi}(z)

2

Note that
\phi

Appendix C
Multifield propagators

The procedure to find multifield propagators numerically readily follows from the single-field case that we have delineated in
section

3
of this article. Consider for example an equation of the form

\displaystyle\left[-\vec{\nabla}^{2}\delta_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}+\omega_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)\partial_{z}+M^{2}_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)\right]G_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(\vec{x}-\vec{y})=\delta_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}\delta^{3}(\vec{x}-\vec{y}),

(49)

where
\theta_{i},\theta_{j}
runs over all relevant fields;
\omega_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)
and
M^{2}(z)_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}
are friction- and mass-matrices respectively.

The linear z-derivative terms can be removed by the shift

\displaystyle G_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}\rightarrow\left\{e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z}\omega}Ge^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z^{\prime}}\omega}\right\}_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}.

(50)

This brings the equation of motion to the form:

\displaystyle\left[-\vec{\nabla}^{2}\delta_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}+W_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)\right]G_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(\vec{x}-\vec{y})=\delta_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}\delta^{3}(\vec{x}-\vec{y}),

(51)

where

\displaystyle W_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z}\omega}\left[M^{2}_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(z)+\frac{1}{4}\omega^{2}_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}\omega_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}\right]e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{z}\omega}.

(52)

To find the propagators one can follow the same steps as before. Numerically, it is the simplest to first rotate to an eigenbasis of
W_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(-\infty)
, evolve the equations to
z=0
, rotate to an eigenbasis of
W_{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}(\infty)
, and then evolve the system to
z=\infty
. We note that an additional complication arises if mixing with vector fields is present, in which case non-trivial momentum dependence of mass matrix perplexes the calculation.

Appendix D
Derivative expansion

An analytic approximation to the propagator (derivative expansion) can be obtained under the assumption that the field-dependent mass is large:
\partial_{z}^{2}\ll M^{2}(z)
. To derive a derivative expansion recall the equation of motion for the propagator:

\displaystyle\left[-\partial^{2}_{z}-\vec{\nabla}_{\perp}^{2}+W(z)\right]G(\vec{x},\vec{x}^{\prime})=\delta^{3}(\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime}).

(53)

Then, following
[
41
]
, one can set
\vec{x}^{\prime}=\vec{x}+\vec{y}
and solve for
G
in powers of
\partial_{z}
. Subsequently, one finds

\begin{split}G(\vec{x},\vec{x})=-&\frac{1}{4\pi}\sqrt{W(z)}+\frac{1}{512\pi W(z)^{5/2}}\left(\partial_{z}W(z)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{328\pi W(z)^{3/2}}\partial_{z}^{2}W(z)\\
+&\frac{105}{32768\pi^{2}W(z)^{11/2}}\left(\partial_{z}W(z)\right)^{4}-\frac{77}{12288\pi^{2}W(z)^{9/2}}\left(\partial_{z}W(z)\right)^{2}\partial^{2}_{z}W(z)\\
+&\frac{3}{2048\pi^{2}W(z)^{7/2}}\left(\partial^{2}_{z}W(z)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{512\pi^{2}W(z)^{7/2}}\partial_{z}W(z)\partial^{3}_{z}W(z)\\
+&\frac{9}{2560\pi^{2}W(z)^{5/2}}\partial^{4}_{z}W(z)\ldots\>.\end{split}

(54)
