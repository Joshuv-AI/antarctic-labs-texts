The Safety Profile of Ticagrelor among Older Adults (Age ≥75 years): A Pharmacovigilance Analysis
Authors: Inbar Yacoby-Halevi, Boris Kruchin, Chen Gadot, Dmitri Guz, Assaf Rotmensh, Yonatan Shaleve, Hila Sharon, Noam Greenberg, Boris Fishman, Edward Itelman, Aviad Rotholz, Keren Skalsky, Katia Orvin, Alon Eisen, Ran Kornowski, Tsahi T. Lerman, Amos Levi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTicagrelor is a widely used potent P2Y12 inhibitor in patients aged ≥75 years with acute coronary syndromes. Despite its widespread use, data on ticagrelor’s safety in older adults remain limited.MethodsWe performed a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System from 2010 to 2024. Disproportionality analysis compared ticagrelor with clopidogrel, assessing predefined adverse events in adults (<75 years) and older adults (≥75 years). For each group, reporting odds ratios (RORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and differences between age groups were evaluated using interaction analysis.ResultsA total of 6,476 ticagrelor-related adverse event reports were included: 4,795 (74%) adults and 1,681 (26%) older adults. In older adults, ticagrelor was not associated with increased reporting of dyspnea, intracranial hemorrhage, complete atrioventricular block, or gout. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was the only event reported more frequently in older adults (ROR 2.3 [95% CI: 1.5–3.6] vs. 0.6 [95% CI: 0.4–0.9]; p < 0.001). Ticagrelor was associated with lower reporting of gastrointestinal bleeding, hematuria, hemoptysis, and anemia in both age subgroups. Bradycardia and syncope were reported more frequently among adults (p = 0.002 and p < 0.001, respectively).ConclusionTicagrelor appears generally safe in older adults (≥75 years), with no excess reporting of major adverse events compared to younger adults, aside from a higher reporting of AKI.

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for 13% of global mortality [1]. Advanced age is a major risk factor for coronary artery disease and is independently associated with worse outcomes [2]. Although 24–28% of ST-elevation myocardial infarction admissions involve patients aged ≥75 years who account for approximately 60% of acute coronary syndrome (ACS)-related deaths [3–5], this population is significantly underrepresented in clinical trials [6, 7].

P2Y12 receptor inhibitors are a cornerstone of antithrombotic therapy in ACS and after percutaneous coronary intervention, typically used alongside aspirin as part of dual antiplatelet therapy [8, 9]. The PLATO trial demonstrated that patients treated with ticagrelor, a potent P2Y12 inhibitor [10], as compared with clopidogrel, had lower rates of the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke in ACS patients and lower rates of stent thrombosis [11].

In a PLATO sub-study focusing on elderly patients, the benefit of ticagrelor over clopidogrel was consistent across age groups, with a greater absolute reduction in all-cause mortality seen in patients aged ≥75 years (2.6%) than in those aged <75 years (1.2%) [12]. Despite this, the Beers Criteria recommend using ticagrelor with caution in older adults due to a 20% increased bleeding risk versus clopidogrel [13, 14]. However, no significant difference in major or minor bleeding was observed in elderly ACS patients [15].

Given that prasugrel is relatively contraindicated in patients aged ≥75 years [16], ticagrelor remains the primary potent P2Y12 inhibitor option for this age group. However, its safety among older adults has not been adequately examined. In our study, we aimed to examine ticagrelor safety profile within the older adult population (≥75 years) using real-world post-marketing data.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance study utilizing the FDA’s database of safety reports (FAERS). FAERS is a comprehensive database that consists of adverse event (AE) reports associated with approved drugs. These reports are submitted by healthcare professionals, consumers, and manufacturers [17]. The database is widely used to evaluate post-marketing safety [18]. We employed OpenVigil FDA 2.1, a widely used and validated web-based pharmacovigilance analysis tool. This tool interfaces with the openFDA online platform to access pharmacovigilance data sourced from the Adverse Event Reporting System [19, 20]. We retrieved information starting from January 1, 2010, as the PLATO trial was published towards the end of 2009 [11]. OpenVigil incorporates a built-in deduplication process that identifies and merges duplicate reports, thereby reducing the risk of overlapping cases within the dataset.

Definition of AEs

FAERS classifies AEs using preferred terms (PTs) derived from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) terminology. Our analysis also incorporated the use of standardized MedDRA queries, an algorithm that combines various PTs to delineate a specific medical condition or area of interest [21]. Both standardized MedDRA queries and PTs are widely acknowledged and employed in research that analyzes the FAERS database. Outcomes of interest, according to previous safety reports, are dyspnea, bradycardia, complete AV block, intracranial hemorrhage, gastrointestinal (GI) hemorrhage, acute kidney injury (AKI), and gout, and the query was restricted to AEs for which ticagrelor or clopidogrel were reported as the primary suspected drugs by the reporter (healthcare professional or patient). In addition, as age was a key variable for case definition, the analysis was restricted to reports in which patient age was available.

Study Design

We employed disproportionality analysis comparing ticagrelor to clopidogrel. We evaluated the reporting of predefined AEs (dyspnea, bradycardia, complete AV block, intracranial hemorrhage, GI hemorrhage, AKI, and gout) in adults (<75 years) and older adults (≥75 years). To mitigate confounding variables, we searched for reports in which ticagrelor and clopidogrel were identified as the primary suspects for an AE. For each group, we calculated the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) and compared them by calculating p for interaction. We analyzed reports from January 1, 2010 to February 1, 2024.

Statistical Analysis

Our statistical analysis was based on disproportionality analysis, a well-validated method for detecting AE signals. In the primary analysis, we assessed the proportion of AE reports associated with ticagrelor treatment and compared it with the corresponding proportion in clopidogrel treatment. The analysis was performed twice, on both age groups. For each AE, we calculated the ROR, a commonly utilized index in pharmacovigilance studies. We conducted individual disproportionality analysis for each age subgroup and conducted interaction analysis to examine the effect of age. A p value for interaction of <0.05 was considered statistically significant [22, 23].

We compared baseline characteristics using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for dichotomous variables. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test [24]. Normally distributed variables were compared using Student’s t test and non-normally distributed variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test. While FAERS data differ from prospective clinical datasets, the analytical approaches are specifically designed and validated for use with spontaneous reporting system data and are consistent with current pharmacovigilance methodology guidelines [25]. We used R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), Excel 365 (Microsoft Corporation), and SPSS Statistics (version 29, IBM Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) to perform the statistical analysis.

Results

The FAERS included 21,166 patients treated with two P2Y12 receptor inhibitor drugs (Fig. 1): 6,476 (30.6%) patients with ticagrelor and 14,690 (69.4%) patients with clopidogrel. In the ticagrelor group, 4,795 (74%) subjects were adults (<75 years) and 1,681 (26%) were older adults (≥75 years). A total of 2,129 reporters (33.5%) were identified as female. Most reports originated in North America (42.9%), between the years 2015 and 2019 (54.3%). Median age (IQR) was 67 (58–75) years. Baseline characteristics are described in Table 1. AKI was the only AE reported more frequently among older adults. Among adults, AKI was reported in 34 cases (0.71%) with ticagrelor and 92 cases (1.11%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.43–0.95). In older adults, AKI was reported in 30 cases (1.78%) with ticagrelor and 50 cases (0.78%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.46–3.64), p of interaction <0.001.

When comparing age groups, reports of bradycardia and syncope were significantly more frequent among adults. For bradycardia, it was reported in 70 cases (1.46%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 21 cases (0.25%) with clopidogrel. In older adults, bradycardia was reported in 13 cases (0.77%) with ticagrelor and 30 cases (0.47%) with clopidogrel. The ROR was 5.84 (95% CI: 3.58–9.52) in adults versus 1.65 (95% CI: 0.86–3.18) in older adults, p = 0.002. For syncope, it was reported in 63 cases (1.31%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 73 cases (0.88%) with clopidogrel. In older adults, syncope was reported in 10 cases (0.59%) with ticagrelor and 106 cases (1.66%) with clopidogrel. The ROR was 1.5 (95% CI: 1.07–2.1) in adults compared to 0.36 (95% CI: 0.19–0.68) in older adults; p < 0.001.

Among older adults, ticagrelor was not associated with an increased reporting of dyspnea, intracranial hemorrhage, complete atrioventricular block (CAVB) and gout compared with adults (p of interaction = 0.11, 0.955, 0.369, and 0.986, respectively). Dyspnea was reported in 735 cases (15.33%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 364 cases (4.39%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 3.94, 95% CI: 3.46–4.50); in older adults, dyspnea was reported in 226 cases (13.44%) with ticagrelor and 288 cases (4.50%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 3.29, 95% CI: 2.74–3.96). Intracranial hemorrhage was reported in 54 cases (1.13%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 86 cases (1.04%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.77–1.53); in older adults, 25 cases (1.49%) with ticagrelor and 89 cases (1.39%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.68–1.67). CAVB was reported in 32 cases (0.67%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 2 cases (0.02%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 27.86, 95% CI: 6.67–116.30); in older adults, 9 cases (0.53%) with ticagrelor and 3 cases (0.05%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 11.47, 95% CI: 3.10–42.41). Gout was reported in 29 cases (0.60%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 13 cases (0.16%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 3.88, 95% CI: 2.01–7.46); in older adults, 2 cases (0.12%) with ticagrelor and 2 cases (0.03%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 3.81, 95% CI: 0.54–27.05).

Ticagrelor was associated with lower reporting of GI hemorrhage, hematuria, hemoptysis and anemia among both age subgroups (p of interaction <0.001, 0.614, 0.848, and 0.270, respectively). GI hemorrhage was reported in 131 cases (2.73%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 1,121 cases (13.51%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.15–0.22); in older adults, 68 cases (4.04%) with ticagrelor and 523 cases (8.18%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.37–0.61). Hematuria was reported in 33 cases (0.69%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 137 cases (1.65%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.28–0.60); in older adults, 28 cases (1.66%) with ticagrelor and 220 cases (3.44%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.32–0.71). Hemoptysis was reported in 25 cases (0.52%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 100 cases (1.20%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.28–0.67); in older adults, 12 cases (0.71%) with ticagrelor and 98 cases (1.53%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.84). Anemia was reported in 134 cases (2.79%) among adults treated with ticagrelor and 441 cases (5.32%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.42–0.62); in older adults, 86 cases (5.11%) with ticagrelor and 710 cases (11.10%) with clopidogrel (ROR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.34–0.54).

Discussion

Over the past two decades, studies evaluating ticagrelor have established its pivotal role as a P2Y12 platelet inhibitor in the antithrombotic management of ACS. These trials have demonstrated that ticagrelor, compared to clopidogrel, reduces the incidence of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke [11, 26]. In this large real-world pharmacovigilance analysis, we assessed the safety profile of ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel in older adults (≥75 years) using FAERS data. Older adults are a population that constitutes 30–40% of ACS hospitalizations and most of the ACS-related mortality [27], yet remain substantially underrepresented in clinical trials. Ticagrelor was not associated with increased reporting of dyspnea, intracranial hemorrhage, CAVB, or gout among older adults. AKI was the only AE reported significantly more frequently in this age group. Ticagrelor was associated with lower reporting of GI hemorrhage, hematuria, hemoptysis, and anemia in both age subgroups, and bradycardia and syncope were reported more frequently among adults aged under 75 years.

These findings are clinically relevant in the context of ongoing uncertainty surrounding ticagrelor use in older adults. While the PLATO trial and its dedicated elderly sub-study demonstrated a reduction in major cardiovascular events without increased major bleeding [11, 12, 27, 28], the POPular AGE trial reported significantly higher bleeding and discontinuation rates with ticagrelor in patients aged ≥70 years [29], and the SWEDEHEART registry found higher bleeding and mortality risk in patients aged ≥80 years [14]. The American Heart Association has accordingly suggested that clopidogrel may be preferable in older adults due to its more favorable bleeding profile [27]. Methodological differences between these trials, including the exclusion of anti-coagulated patients from PLATO and the high ticagrelor discontinuation rate in POPular AGE, may partly explain these discrepancies and further highlight the need for real-world safety data to guide clinical decision-making in this growing patient population.

In our study, AKI was the only AE reported significantly more often among older adults (p for interaction <0.001). Concerning renal safety, a small but significant difference was noted in serum creatinine rise between ticagrelor and clopidogrel arms during the PLATO trial (10% vs. 8%). This difference was sustained at 12 months but resolved 1 month after treatment discontinuation [11, 14]. On the other hand, studies that focused on older patients (POPular AGE and SWEDEHEART registry) did not report outcomes on renal function [14, 29]. Importantly, FAERS data cannot distinguish between pre-existing renal dysfunction and incident AKI, as baseline and longitudinal data are unavailable. Thus, the increased reporting of AKI in older adults may partly reflect a higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease rather than a purely drug-related effect. Our findings may reflect the reality of clinical practice, where ticagrelor is often prescribed to more complex patients with more risk factors for AKI – this signal should be carefully monitored in future studies. In light of the findings from the PLATO trial and our current analysis, we recommend careful monitoring of renal function in patients treated with ticagrelor.

Our analysis demonstrated that dyspnea was more commonly reported with ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel, but with no age interaction. In the PLATO trial, dyspnea was more frequently associated with ticagrelor than with clopidogrel (13.8% vs. 7.8% respectively; p < 0.001), requiring discontinuation of treatment in a small subset of patients (0.9% in the ticagrelor group vs. 0.1% in the clopidogrel group; p < 0.001) [11]. While no age-treatment interaction was noted in the PLATO sub-study on the elderly, dyspnea was the most important cause for ticagrelor discontinuation in the POPular AGE trial on patients aged ≥70 years [14, 29, 30]. Although ticagrelor-associated dyspnea is generally considered benign, the increased likelihood of treatment discontinuation may translate into a higher risk of adverse ischemic events, including stent thrombosis. This concern may be particularly relevant in older adults, who often have lower health literacy, potentially leading to premature or unsanctioned discontinuation of therapy.

Our study found that gout was overreported in adults with ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel, with no significant difference among older adults. In the PLATO trial, serum uric acid levels rose more prominently with ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel (14% vs. 7% increase after 1 month; p < 0.001), but the difference resolved 1 month after treatment discontinuation, and no clinical outcomes were reported [11]. The PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial later reported a significantly higher incidence of gout among patients receiving long-term ticagrelor compared to placebo (2.28% in the 90-mg group and 1.97% in the 60-mg group compared to 1.51% with placebo; p < 0.001) [31, 32]. A Swedish registry study reported an increased risk of gout with ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel, but did not address older adults separately [33]. Whether ticagrelor poses a clinically significant risk for hyperuricemic complications when compared to clopidogrel, particularly in older adults, remains an open question. In our study, the number of reports in older adults for both medications was probably too low to reach a clear conclusion regarding age interaction.

In our study, bradycardia was reported more frequently with ticagrelor in adults <75 (ROR = 5.84, 95% CI: 3.58–9.52), but not in older adults (ROR = 1.65, 95% CI: 0.86–3.18). Similarly, syncope was more common in adults aged <75 years taking ticagrelor, but not in older adults. In the PLATO trial, there was no significant difference in the incidence of bradycardia between treatment groups (4.4% with ticagrelor vs. 4% with clopidogrel; p = 0.21). Similarly, heart block occurred in 0.7% of patients in both groups, and pacemaker implantation was required in 0.9% of cases, with no statistically significant differences [11]. A sub-study that analyzed continuous electrocardiographic recordings from the PLATO trial revealed that ventricular pauses ≥3 s occurred more frequently in patients receiving ticagrelor compared to clopidogrel (84 [5.8%] vs. 51 [3.6%]; p = 0.006). Most of the pauses were asymptomatic, nocturnal, sinoatrial nodal in origin, and appeared in the early phase of ACSs. Furthermore, there was no significant difference between ticagrelor and clopidogrel in bradyarrhythmia-related complications, including syncope, pacemaker placement, and cardiac arrest [34]. Two additional studies comparing ticagrelor to placebo or clopidogrel did not find an increased risk for bradyarrhythmic complications with ticagrelor [29, 33].

Our findings may provide reassurance regarding the use of ticagrelor in older adults, specifically in the context of conduction abnormalities. They do, however, reaffirm the need for careful, age-adjusted assessment in future studies.

Our study has several important strengths that enhance its clinical relevance. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive real-world pharmacovigilance analysis focused specifically on the safety profile of ticagrelor in older adults (≥75 years). Given the consistent underrepresentation of elderly patients in major randomized controlled trials, such as PLATO and PEGASUS-TIMI 54, our findings help fill a critical gap in the current body of knowledge by providing age-stratified safety data derived from a large, real-world database.

Second, our study employed disproportionality analysis, a widely accepted pharmacovigilance method for signal detection. We further refined this approach by using “disproportionality by therapeutic area,” which limits comparisons to drugs used within the same clinical context, in this case, ticagrelor versus clopidogrel, both of which are P2Y12 inhibitors used for similar indications. This refinement alleviates concerns of indication bias and confounding by therapeutic setting, offering a more valid comparison of AE reporting rates [35].

Third, our analysis included a broad range of clinically meaningful AEs, including those with known mechanistic plausibility (e.g., dyspnea, AKI, bradycardia, syncope, bleeding, and gout). By providing a detailed age-stratified comparison of reporting odds, we were able to detect not only expected outcomes but also unexpected age-related differences, such as the higher reporting of bradycardia and syncope in younger adults and increased reporting of AKI in the elderly.

Several limitations should be mentioned. First, our study relied on spontaneously reported AE databases, limiting our ability to establish causality. Factors such as concomitant medications, underlying medical conditions, and latency periods may significantly impact these associations [36]. Furthermore, lack of medical confirmation in AE reporting raises significant concerns regarding reporter bias in pharmacovigilance systems [37]. Specifically, concomitant medications cannot be reliably attributed to individual patients, and potential drug-drug interactions may have influenced the observed reporting patterns. In addition, the observed lower reporting of bleeding-related events among older adults treated with ticagrelor may partly reflect selection bias, whereby clinicians may preferentially prescribe ticagrelor to less frail patients with lower perceived hemorrhagic risk. Moreover, reduced health literacy in the older adult population may result in underreporting of AEs, as patients experiencing drug-related symptoms may not recognize or report them through appropriate channels. While our findings did not demonstrate increased bleeding reporting in older adults, these results may be interpreted in the context of selective prescribing patterns and should be considered alongside other contributing factors when drawing conclusions regarding hemorrhagic safety. Additionally, reporting may be influenced by external factors such as published safety alerts, which may increase reporter awareness and lead to escalation of AE documentation, introducing notoriety bias.

Residual confounding due to comorbidities, polypharmacy, and underlying illness severity may have significantly influenced the observed associations and cannot be fully accounted for in this type of analysis. For example, dyspnea may be secondary to acute pulmonary edema in the setting of severe cardiac disease, or attributable to underlying pulmonary comorbidities, making causal attribution to ticagrelor particularly challenging.

Several limitations inherent to the FAERS database should be acknowledged. As FAERS data are incomplete, non-longitudinal, and subject to reporting bias, the application of statistical methods should be interpreted with caution, as these data differ fundamentally from those derived from controlled clinical datasets. Furthermore, an inherent methodological limitation of disproportionality analysis is that the denominator in all calculations represents the total number of reports in the database, rather than the number of patients exposed to the drug. Consequently, it is not possible to determine the true incidence of AEs, nor to account for differences in drug dosage or treatment duration across reports. Finally, AEs with a low number of reports may yield disproportionately high RORs with wide confidence intervals, reflecting statistical instability rather than a true clinical signal, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In this large, real-world analysis, ticagrelor use in older adults (≥75 years) was not associated with an increased reporting of most major AEs compared to younger adults, except for AKI. These findings suggest that, when carefully selected, older patients may tolerate ticagrelor similarly to younger individuals, particularly regarding bleeding and conduction-related events. However, given the inherent limitations in pharmacovigilance data and the underrepresentation of elderly patients in randomized trials, further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate the safety profile of ticagrelor in this growing patient population.
