Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing and Pulmonary Function for Assessing Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
Authors: Chunyan Yu, Yong Li, Shiwei Qumu, Jingen Xia, Jieping Lei, Xiaopan Li, Tianyi Yang, Zinan Wang, Shuhao Chen, Ke Huang, Ting Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the grading concordance between pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) in grading chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and to guide the clinical application of CPET in appropriate patient populations.Patients and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 152 patients with COPD who underwent PFT, CPET, the 6-minute walking test (6MWT), and body composition assessment. COPD was graded using forced expiratory volume in 1 second as a percentage of the predicted value (FEV1%pred) and Peak Oxygen Uptake per Kilogram of Body Weight (PeakVO2/kg), respectively. Grading discordance between the two systems was assessed, and clinical characteristics of patients with a higher disease severity grade on CPET were analyzed.ResultsAgreement between GOLD and peakVO2/kg-based CPET grading was slight (linear weighted Kappa = 0.182, 95% CI: 0.087–0.277; P < 0.001), with exact grade concordance observed in 47 of 152 patients (30.9%). Overall, 92 patients (60.5%) were assigned a more severe grade by peakVO2/kg-based CPET grading than by GOLD. Upward reclassification occurred in 49 of 67 patients with GOLD Mild disease (73.1%) and 41 of 62 patients with GOLD Moderate disease (66.1%). Compared with the non-upgraded group, patients in the CPET-upgraded group were older, had higher BMI, body fat percentage, and fat mass, and were more likely to have cardiovascular comorbidity (all P < 0.05). In partial correlation analyses adjusted for age, sex, and height, peakVO2/kg was negatively correlated with fat mass (r = −0.287, P = 0.002), whereas peakVO2%pred was not (r = 0.041, P = 0.657), indicating that the observed discordance was partly related to the body-weight normalization inherent to peakVO2/kg.ConclusionDiscordance between GOLD and peakVO2/kg-based CPET grading was common, particularly in patients with mild-to-moderate airflow obstruction, and was associated with age, adiposity, and cardiovascular comorbidity. CPET may provide physiological information complementary to spirometry; however, because peakVO2/kg-based grading is influenced by adiposity, a more severe CPET grade in overweight or obese patients should not necessarily be interpreted as indicating intrinsically greater cardiopulmonary impairment. PeakVO2%pred, body composition, and other clinical and physiological findings should be considered alongside peakVO2/kg when interpreting CPET results.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common respiratory condition that seriously threatens human health, affecting nearly 100 million individuals in China.1 It is the third leading cause of death among single diseases in China2 and imposes a substantial global economic burden, particularly within China.3,4 Accurate assessment of COPD grading and timely implementation of appropriate interventions are crucial to alleviate symptoms, delay disease progression, and reduce mortality. Dyspnea and exercise intolerance are common clinical features of COPD. Currently, COPD grading is primarily classified according to the GOLD criteria, which rely on forced expiratory volume in 1 second as a percentage of the predicted value (FEV1%pred) derived from static pulmonary function tests (PFTs). Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), a dynamic assessment method, provides more comprehensive information on cardiopulmonary function and Peak Oxygen Uptake per Kilogram of Body Weight (PeakVO2/kg) is the gold standard for assessing cardiopulmonary exercise function.5 In patients with COPD, a PeakVO2/kg below 15 mL/(kg·min) has been demonstrated to be associated with a significantly increased risk of mortality.6 In this study, patients were stratified according to the cardiopulmonary function grading criteria established by the American Medical Association (AMA), which classify cardiopulmonary function into four levels (from best to worst) based on peakVO2/kg measured during CPET.7 Previous studies have suggested that CPET may be superior to PFT in determining the degree of respiratory impairment, although the specific populations that benefit most remain unclear.8,9

CPET refers to an examination method conducted under progressively increasing exercise loads. It involves measuring a series of indicators—such as gas metabolism, heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygen saturation, and electrocardiogram—from rest to maximal exertion and then back to recovery. During the test, any corresponding symptoms exhibited by the subject are recorded. This approach objectively reflects physiological and pathological changes as well as the degree of functional impairment at different load levels, thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of the overall function and reserve capacity of organ systems such as the heart and lungs.10,11 Therefore, CPET provides more comprehensive and multifaceted clinical information reflecting disease characteristics than does PFT alone. The ATS notes that PFTs alone cannot accurately reflect a participant’s overall condition, whereas CPET provides information that is not obtainable from PFTs.12 Impairment of exercise capacity objectively measured by CPET is a robust indicator of compromised health status and a predictor of prognosis.6. Exercise limitation is a major contributor to morbidity in patients with COPD.6,13 Furthermore, peakVO2 has been demonstrated to be a stronger predictor of mortality in COPD than FEV1.6 We aimed to compare the consistency between PFT- and CPET-based COPD grading and to identify differences in patient assessment, thereby providing additional references for COPD classification and treatment.

Material and Methods

Study Participants

This retrospective study included 152 patients with stable COPD, who had not received any regular bronchodilators or corticosteroids prior to enrollment, and who underwent both PFT and CPET within a 12-month period at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital between June 2021 and July 2024. Participants with missing data on pulmonary diffusion function, body composition, smoking history, COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score, modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score, or St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) score were analyzed separately in subgroups. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (2022-KY-160), and all participants provided written informed consent for CPET.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Patients diagnosed with COPD according to the GOLD 202014 criteria and in a stable disease stage, with no acute exacerbations or lower respiratory tract infections for at least 4 weeks prior to the study.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients in the acute exacerbation phase and those with contraindications for CPET were excluded. Acute exacerbation was defined according to established criteria4 as a worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variations (eg, increased dyspnea accompanied by worsening wheezing, chest tightness, increased cough, or increased sputum volume) that required a change in medication.

CPET contraindications included:15 uncontrolled acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure, symptomatic severe aortic stenosis, severe coarctation of the aorta, descending aortic aneurysm, acute aortic dissection, acute myocarditis, pericarditis, or endocarditis, symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable severe arrhythmia, severe bradyarrhythmia, acute pulmonary embolism and pulmonary infarction, acute respiratory failure, uncontrolled asthma, resting peripheral oxygen saturation < 85%, acute deep vein thrombosis of the lower extremities, recent non-cardiac conditions affecting exercise capacity, and other conditions that could be worsened by exercise.

Data Collection

Demographic Data

Demographic data, including sex, age, height, and weight, were collected from the patients’ CPET reports.

Body Composition

Body composition was assessed using an MC-780MA body composition analyzer, which employs an 8-point tactile electrode method to directly measure the impedance of various body segments. The measurements collected included body fat percentage (BFP), fat mass (FM), fat-free mass (FFM), muscle mass (MM), right leg muscle mass (RLMM), and skeletal muscle mass (SMM), as well as three indices normalized by height squared: skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and right-leg skeletal muscle index (RLSMI).

PFT

PFT was performed using the MasterScreen system (Jaeger, Germany). Although some patients did not complete the diffusion function assessment, all underwent pulmonary ventilation capacity testing. The measured parameters included: forced vital capacity (FVC) and its percentage of predicted value (FVC%pred), FEV1 and its percentage of predicted value (FEV1%pred), FEV1/FVC ratio and its percentage of predicted value (FEV1/FVC%pred), peak expiratory flow (PEF) and its percentage of predicted value (PEF %pred), forced expiratory flow at 50% of FVC (MEF50) and its percentage of predicted value (MEF50%pred), maximal expiratory flow at 25% of FVC (MEF25) and its percentage of predicted value (MEF25%pred), mean forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of FVC (MMEF) and its percentage of predicted value (MMEF%pred), diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and its percentage of predicted value (DLCO%pred), and diffusion capacity per unit alveolar volume (DLCO/VA) and its percentage of predicted value (DLCO/VA%pred).

Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT)

The 6MWT was conducted according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines.16 The test was performed in a 30-meter straight corridor, with chairs placed at both ends and the midpoint for marking and participant rest. Before the test, participants were familiarized with the procedure and environment. They were instructed to walk back and forth along the corridor, avoid external distractions, and exert maximum effort. The test was terminated at 6 minutes, and the 6-minute walking distance (6MWD) was measured and recorded. Throughout the test, participants’ oxygen saturation and vital signs, including blood pressure and respiratory rate, were monitored. The test was immediately stopped if participants experienced significant discomfort, such as dizziness or severe shortness of breath.

CPET

CPET was conducted using a cycle ergometer from the MasterScreen CPX system (Jaeger, Germany). Room temperature and humidity were maintained at 20–25°C and 45–60%, respectively. Daily calibrations of ambient conditions, volume, and gas analyzers were performed to ensure measurement accuracy.

Pre-Test Preparation

Participants wore comfortable clothing and shoes. They were instructed to abstain from smoking and caffeine for at least 2 hours prior to the test and avoid any strenuous exercise beforehand. The CPET procedure and precautions were explained to the participants, and informed consent for CPET was obtained.

Testing Protocol and Procedure

The power increment rate was determined based on participants’ body size, disease status, and daily activity level, aiming for a total CPET duration of 8–12 minutes. Participants first sat at rest for 3 minutes while baseline cardiorespiratory data were collected. This was followed by 3 minutes of unloaded pedaling, after which the ramp exercise test began with a continuous, progressively increasing workload (5–25 W/min). Participants were instructed to maintain a pedaling rate of 55–65 rpm throughout the test, assisted by a metronome if needed. In the absence of termination criteria, participants were encouraged to continue exercising as long as possible. The test entered the recovery phase when participants experienced extreme fatigue or dyspnea that prevented them from maintaining the required pedaling rate. During the 3-minute recovery, the participants performed slow pedaling at a 20 W load at approximately 30–40 rpm. After stopping, participants’ heart rate, blood pressure, and other vital signs were monitored until they returned to pre-test levels.

Criteria for Terminating the CPET15

CPET was terminated if any of the following occurred:

(1) Occurrence of moderate to severe angina; (2) Manifestation of dizziness, ataxia, cyanosis or pallor, severe fatigue, or dyspnea; (3) ECG showing horizontal or downsloping ST-segment depression ≥ 0.2 mV in adjacent leads for 2 minutes or more, or ST-segment elevation ≥ 0.1 mV; (4) Occurrence of severe arrhythmias, including second- or third-degree atrioventricular block, ventricular tachycardia, frequent premature ventricular contractions, or newly onset atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate; (5) A decrease in systolic blood pressure ≥ 10 mmHg with increasing workload, or systolic blood pressure persistently below baseline levels; or systolic blood pressure ≥ 220 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg; (6) Significant decrease in pedaling rate due to lower limb weakness, muscle pain, or cramps; and (7) Participant request to stop the test.

Measured CPET Parameters

The measured CPET parameters included: peak oxygen uptake (PeakVO2) and its percentage of predicted value (PeakVO2%pred), PeakVO2/kg, peak workload (PeakLoad) and its percentage of predicted value (Peakload%pred), peak heart rate (PeakHR) and its percentage of predicted value (PeakHR%pred), peak oxygen pulse (PeakO2pulse) and its percentage of predicted value (PeakO2pulse%pred), peak minute ventilation (PeakVE) and its percentage of predicted value (PeakVE%pred), percentage of predicted anaerobic threshold (AT%pred), peak metabolic equivalents (PeakMETs), peak exercise breathing reserve (PeakBR), and carbon dioxide equivalent at anaerobic threshold (AT-EqCO2).

Symptom Assessment

Participants’ smoking history, CAT, mMRC, and SGRQ scores were collected.

Pulmonary Function and CPET Grading Methods

Pulmonary function grading, also known as GOLD grading, was based on the FEV1%pred according to the GOLD guidelines:17 mild: FEV1%pred ≥ 80%; moderate: 50% ≤ FEV1%pred < 80%; severe: 30% ≤ FEV1%pred < 50%; or very severe: FEV1%pred < 30%.

According to the American Medical Association (AMA) guidelines for pulmonary function grading, cardiopulmonary function is classified into four grades (from best to worst) based on peakVO2/kg measured during CPET:7 mild: peakVO2/kg ≥ 25 mL/(kg·min); moderate: 20 mL/(kg·min) ≤ peakVO2/kg < 25 mL/(kg·min); severe: 15 mL/(kg·min) ≤ peakVO2/kg < 20 mL/(kg·min); or very severe: peakVO2/kg < 15 mL/(kg·min).

Statistical Analysis

Assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.3 between FEV1%pred (a pulmonary function test parameter) and peak VO2 (a cardiopulmonary exercise testing parameter) in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the sample size was estimated using the “Correlation – Correlation – Pearson’s Correlation Tests” module in PASS 2021 (version 21.0.3). With a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 84 patients. A total of 152 patients with COPD were retrospectively enrolled in this study, which adequately met the statistical requirement.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, non-normal continuous data as median (P25, P75), and categorical data as n (%). For categorical proportions, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson score interval without continuity correction. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U-tests, or chi-square tests as dictated by data distribution.

The concordance between spirometry-defined GOLD airflow limitation staging and CPET-based exercise endurance impairment stratification was quantified with the linear weighted Kappa statistic. The results were interpreted according to the criteria of Landis and Koch (1977): <0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and >0.80, almost perfect agreement.18 A Sankey diagram was generated to visualize cross-classification distribution across the two grading systems.

To examine CPET upgrading status (yes vs no) between GOLD-based and CPET-based severity, a binary logistic regression analysis was performed with CPET-upgraded severity as the dependent variable. Candidate independent variables with P < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable model using a forward stepwise selection procedure (forward: LR). Prior to model entry, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF > 5 were excluded. The final model retained five independent predictors, which are presented with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Partial correlation analysis, adjusted for age, sex, and height, revealed that FEV1 (both absolute and %predicted) was significantly and positively correlated with all three peak oxygen uptake indices (Peak VO2 L/min, %pred, and/kg), with all P values < 0.001. Notably, the correlation between FEV1%pred and Peak VO2/kg, the two indices used for disease severity grading in this study, remained significant (r = 0.388, P < 0.001).

The traditional index, PeakVO2/kg, showed a significant negative correlation with fat mass (FM) (r = −0.287, P = 0.002), confirming that patients with higher adiposity were systematically penalized by this metric. In contrast, PeakVO2/kg was not correlated with skeletal muscle mass (SMM: r = −0.004, P = 0.976) or fat-free mass index (FFMI: r = 0.035, P = 0.707). By contrast, PeakVO2%pred showed no correlation with FM (r = 0.041, P = 0.657), demonstrating that the adiposity-driven bias was effectively eliminated. Furthermore, PeakVO2%pred exhibited significant positive correlations with both SMM (r = 0.269, P = 0.003) and FFMI (r = 0.304, P = 0.001).

Binary Logistic Regression Analysis of Factors Associated with CPET Upgrading

A binary logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors independently associated with CPET upgrading status (yes vs no), with the non-upgraded group serving as the reference category. As shown in Table 7, five variables remained independently associated with CPET-upgraded severity after adjustment for potential confounders. Higher BMI (OR = 1.413, 95% CI: 1.151–1.734, P = 0.001) and higher FEV1%pred (OR = 1.050, 95% CI: 1.018–1.082, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with increased odds of being in the more severe CPET group, indicating these factors as risk markers for greater severity. Conversely, higher DLCO (OR = 0.677, 95% CI: 0.484–0.949, P = 0.023), higher CAT score (OR = 0.886, 95% CI: 0.810–0.970, P = 0.008), and longer 6MWD (OR = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.984–0.999, P = 0.034) were associated with reduced odds of CPET-upgraded severity, suggesting protective effects. The model demonstrated good overall fit (Hosmer‑Lemeshow χ2 = 9.406, P = 0.309; overall classification accuracy = 77.2%).

Discussion

COPD is a chronic inflammatory airway disease that primarily affects the airways, alveoli, and microvasculature and gradually worsens over time. Exertional dyspnea is one of the main problems experienced by patients with COPD. As the disease progresses, dyspnea intensifies, leading to decreased exercise tolerance, limited physical activity, and a negative impact on prognosis.19 Currently, the diagnosis and assessment of COPD primarily rely on PFTs. However, in many patients, significantly disproportionate dyspnea and reduced exercise tolerance remain unexplained even after conventional spirometry. While PFT helps define the grading of airflow limitation, it has limitations in capturing the extensive and heterogeneous physiological impairments in COPD.

In this study, 152 participants with stable COPD were included. Based on the conventional criterion of FEV1 (% predicted) for assessing COPD grading, participants were classified as mild (n = 67), moderate (n = 62), severe (n = 14), or very severe (n = 9). Considering that FEV1%pred does not reflect cardiopulmonary reserve, participants were also classified according to the cardiopulmonary function grading criteria established by the AMA, which are based on peakVO2/kg measured by CPET,7 resulting in mild (n = 27), moderate (n = 43), severe (n = 59), and very severe (n = 23) groups. The consistency between the two grading standards was only 30.92%, consistent with the findings reported by Fink et al,20 Potential reasons for this discrepancy include the fact that FEV1%pred reflects only ventilatory capacity, whereas COPD is now recognized as a systemic disease. In addition to impaired ventilatory function, COPD can involve decreased respiratory and peripheral skeletal muscle function, cardiac dysfunction, nutritional imbalances, and psychological factors.21 PeakVO2/kg reflects the integrated performance of three major systems—pulmonary respiration (including ventilation and gas exchange), cardiovascular circulation, and skeletal muscle oxygen uptake—providing a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s condition.12 This also explains the common clinical observation that patients with similar FEV1%pred levels can have significantly different prognoses. As shown in Table 3, CPET upgraded the severity grade in 60.5% of all patients, with the highest upgrade rates observed in GOLD Mild (73.1%) and Moderate (66.1%) groups. Conversely, the higher concordance rates in GOLD Severe and Very Severe groups (64.3% and 66.7%) may be partially explained by a ceiling effect: once airflow obstruction is severe, there is limited room for CPET to assign an even higher grade. This does not imply that CPET grading is more “accurate” in severe COPD; rather, it reflects the compressed grading range at the upper extreme of disease severity.

CPET provides an integrated assessment of pulmonary, cardiovascular, and peripheral muscle responses to exercise that cannot be obtained from resting PFTs alone.12 Patients in the CPET-upgraded group (n = 92) exhibited the following characteristics compared with the non-upgraded reference group (n = 60): (1) higher rates of discordance were observed in those with mild or moderate GOLD stages (GOLD Mild and Moderate); (2) they were significantly older; (3) they had higher BMI, higher BFP, and higher FM; and (4) a higher prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidity. These results are consistent with the findings of Cotes et al,22 Cotes et al also noted that the best correlation between GOLD and CPET grading may occur in patients whose dyspnea is primarily due to lung disease rather than chest pain, muscle discomfort, exercise-induced asthma, or heart failure. Compared with the non-upgraded reference group, the CPET-upgraded group had a lower peak O2 pulse. They also had higher PeakBR, which may be due to differences in ventilatory limitations: individuals with worse GOLD grades often have poorer lung function and correspondingly lower breathing reserve compared with those classified as severe by CPET. This finding is consistent with that of Fink et al,20 Additionally, the CPET-upgraded group had higher AT-EqCO2 values than the non-upgraded reference group. AT-EqCO2 reflects ventilatory efficiency, suggesting less efficient ventilation in patients with poorer CPET performance. Furthermore, the CPET-upgraded group had a lower peakHR than the non-upgraded reference group. This observation differs from previous findings by Fink et al,20 which may be influenced by factors such as differences in participants, operators, and equipment, and requires further investigation in large-sample, multicenter studies.

Because greater adiposity was a prominent characteristic of the CPET-upgraded group, we further examined whether the use of body-weight-normalized peakVO2/kg contributed to the observed discordance. In the partial-correlation analyses, height was included because it is relevant to both height-indexed body-composition measures and predicted VO2 equations, whereas weight and BMI were excluded because body weight is already embedded in peakVO2/kg and adjustment could remove the adiposity-related normalization effect under investigation. Accordingly, peakVO2/kg-based grades should be interpreted cautiously in overweight or obese patients and considered alongside peakVO2%pred, which was not correlated with fat mass in our analysis. Because no validated four-level peakVO2%pred cutoffs corresponding to the AMA peakVO2/kg categories were identified, developing and validating such thresholds should be a focus of future research.

As shown in Table 7, higher BMI and higher FEV1%pred were associated with greater odds of CPET-upgraded classification, whereas higher DLCO, higher CAT score, and longer 6MWD were associated with lower odds. The association with BMI should be interpreted in light of the partial-correlation findings, because greater adiposity may reduce peakVO2/kg through body-weight normalization and thereby increase the likelihood of a more severe peakVO2/kg-based grade. The positive association with FEV1%pred may partly reflect the definition of the outcome, as patients with milder GOLD grades have greater opportunity to be reclassified upward by CPET. The inverse association with CAT suggests that perceived symptom burden did not necessarily parallel exercise-based impairment in this cohort; however, this unexpected finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single‑center study with a limited sample size and few GOLD stage 4 patients, restricting the generalizability of our results. Second, the ECCS 1993 reference equations (rather than the GLI equations) may have overdiagnosed airway obstruction in older patients, as the ECCS equations yield higher predicted values in the elderly, potentially leading to misclassification of normal cases as obstruction. Third, unmeasured confounders such as physical activity may have affected exercise test outcomes, and the severity of comorbidities was not collected either. Fourth, missing data for body composition (19.1%) and DLCO (22.4%) could have introduced selection bias under the complete-case analysis assumption. Fifth, the absence of routine CT imaging prevented the identification of emphysema‑predominant phenotypes and limited phenotype‑specific analyses.

Sixth, the non-upgraded reference group combined concordant and CPET-downgraded patients, who may be physiologically heterogeneous; however, the small number of downgraded patients precluded stable separate multivariable analysis. Given these constraints, our findings are hypothesis‑generating and should be validated in prospective, multicenter studies incorporating comprehensive physiological, phenotypic, and imaging data. Furthermore, as subgroup analyses were exploratory, no multiplicity adjustment was applied to avoid type II error, and all P values are nominal and interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated substantial discordance between GOLD grading based on FEV1%pred and CPET grading based on peakVO2/kg, particularly in patients with mild-to-moderate airflow obstruction. The discordance was more frequently observed in older patients, those with greater adiposity, and those with cardiovascular comorbidity. However, because peakVO2/kg was negatively associated with fat mass whereas peakVO2%pred was not, part of the apparent CPET upgrading in patients with greater adiposity may reflect body-weight normalization rather than intrinsically greater cardiopulmonary impairment. PeakVO2/kg-based grades should therefore be interpreted alongside peakVO2%pred and other clinical measures. The potential role of CPET in refining severity assessment remains hypothesis-generating and should be validated prospectively, together with the development of validated peakVO2%pred-based grading thresholds.
