Comparison of the effects of aquatic HIIT and MICT on central hemodynamics, endothelial function, and aerobic fitness in obese school-aged children: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Junhao Huang, Weiwei Huang, Yongpeng Zheng, Weishan Lu, Shen Wang, Min Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAquatic exercise may be a feasible strategy for improving cardiovascular health in children with obesity, but evidence comparing aquatic high-intensity interval training (aHIIT) and aquatic moderate-intensity continuous training (aMICT) remains limited. This pilot randomized controlled trial compared the effects of 8 weeks of aHIIT and aMICT on endothelial function, estimated cardiorespiratory fitness, central hemodynamic indices, arterial stiffness, and anthropometric indicators in school-aged children with obesity.MethodsThirty-three children with obesity were randomized to the aHIIT group (n = 11), aMICT group (n = 11), or non-exercise control group (n = 11). The two aquatic exercise groups trained 3 days/week for 8 weeks. The aHIIT protocol consisted of 10 × 30-s high-intensity aquatic exercise (such as freestyle swimming or freestyle kicking) bouts at 80%-90% HRmax, separated by 60-s recovery intervals, whereas the aMICT protocol consisted of two 15-min continuous breaststroke bouts at 60%-70% HRmax, separated by a 5-min recovery interval. Brachial artery flow-mediated dilation (FMD) was used as the principal vascular outcome. Central hemodynamic indices were assessed using applanation tonometry, and estimated VO2max was obtained from the 20-m shuttle run test. Intervention effects were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with the group × time interaction used as the primary test for differential intervention effects.ResultsSignificant group × time interactions were observed for estimated VO2max, FMD, AIx, and AIx@HR75. Estimated VO2max increased in both aquatic exercise groups compared with the control group. FMD improved in both exercise groups, with a greater increase observed after aHIIT. AIx and AIx@HR75 showed favorable changes after aMICT. No significant group × time interactions were observed for resting blood pressure, SEVR, ABI, cfPWV, or baPWV.ConclusionThis pilot trial suggests that both aHIIT and aMICT may improve estimated cardiorespiratory fitness in school-aged children with obesity. aHIIT may be associated with greater improvement in brachial artery endothelial function, whereas aMICT may have favorable effects on selected wave-reflection-related indices. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size, indirect estimated VO2max estimation, lack of maturation assessment, and unmatched exercise workload.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registration, ChiCTR2400080687.05/02/2024.

Introduction

Obesity among children has become a significant public health concern in modern societies. From 1990 to 2022, the global obesity rate among children and adolescents has surged dramatically.The obesity rate of girls and boys rose from 1.7% to 6.9% (an increase of approximately 5.2 percentage points) and from 2.1% to 9.3% (an increase of approximately 7.2 percentage points), respectively. By 2022, approximately 159 million children and adolescents worldwide were affected by obesity [1]. In children with obesity, excess adiposity is often accompanied by impaired endothelial function, elevated arterial stiffness, and reduced cardiorespiratory fitness, all of which may represent early targets for preventive intervention [2, 3]. Exercise is considered a first-line non-pharmacological strategy for improving cardiovascular health in children with obesity. Cardiorespiratory fitness, commonly expressed as maximal or estimated maximal oxygen uptake, is an important marker of health and cardiovascular risk. Because maximal oxygen uptake was estimated from the 20-m shuttle-run test in the present study, the term estimated VO2max is used throughout the manuscript to avoid implying direct gas-analysis measurement. Prior studies have shown that aerobic exercise can improve endothelial function and vascular health, but responses may vary according to exercise intensity, modality, baseline vascular status, and measurement procedures [4].

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has attracted increasing attention because it may induce larger cardiovascular and metabolic stimuli within a shorter exercise duration than moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT). Several land-based school exercise studies have reported favorable effects of structured exercise or HIIT on blood pressure, arterial stiffness, hemodynamic parameters, and heart-rate variability in children [5, 6]. However, most available trials have been conducted on land, and the transferability of those findings to aquatic exercise in children with obesity remains uncertain.

Exercise intervention represents an effective non-pharmacological strategy for improving cardiovascular health in children and adolescents with obesity [7–9]. Moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) are two widely used exercise modalities for cardiovascular and metabolic improvement [10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that both training approaches can improve cardiovascular-related outcomes, including reductions in blood pressure and improvements in arterial function. HIIT has received increasing attention because it may induce greater cardiovascular and metabolic stimuli within a shorter exercise duration. However, most existing studies have focused on land-based interventions, and high-impact exercise may impose greater mechanical loading and potential musculoskeletal stress in children with obesity.

Aquatic exercise may provide a suitable alternative for this population because buoyancy reduces weight-bearing stress and may enhance exercise tolerance, while hydrostatic pressure and thermoregulatory demands may influence venous return and cardiovascular responses. Previous studies have reported beneficial effects of aquatic HIIT and MICT on vascular and aerobic outcomes in inactive adults and adults with overweight or obesity; however, evidence in children with obesity remains limited [11, 12]. Therefore, direct comparisons between aquatic HIIT and aquatic MICT are needed to clarify their relative effects on vascular adaptations in children with obesity.

Accordingly, the primary aim of this three-arm randomized controlled pilot trial was to compare the effects of 8 weeks of aquatic HIIT and aquatic MICT on endothelial function in school-aged children with obesity, with brachial artery FMD selected as the principal vascular outcome. We hypothesized that both aquatic exercise interventions would improve FMD compared with the non-exercise control condition, and that aquatic HIIT would elicit greater improvements than aquatic MICT due to its higher intermittent cardiovascular stimulus and potential shear-stress exposure.

Estimated VO2max, central hemodynamic indices, arterial stiffness parameters, and anthropometric indicators were examined as secondary outcomes to provide an exploratory assessment of broader cardiometabolic and vascular adaptations. baPWV and cfPWV were used to characterize peripheral and central arterial stiffness, respectively, while ABI and resting hemodynamic variables were included to further describe vascular status. Given the pilot nature and relatively small sample size of this study, secondary and exploratory findings were interpreted cautiously.

Materials and methods

Participants

Obese school-aged children aged 9–13 years were recruited from local primary schools. The inclusion criteria were obese male children with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 21.4 kg/m2 and obese female children with BMI ≥ 20.4 kg/m2 [13], being able to swim yet lacking regular participation in physical activity (equivalent to less than 90 min per week) in the last three months. Participants diagnosed with metabolic diseases, serious organic pathologies of the heart, brain, lungs, kidneys, or locomotor system, with a history of serious surgery, as well as with sports injuries in the last six months, were excluded.

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1. Based on a repeated-measures ANOVA model with three groups and two measurements, a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 1 − β = 0.80, and a moderate effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.30), the minimum required sample size was 30 participants. Considering a potential dropout rate of approximately 10%, the initial sample size was set at 33 participants. Because this was a pilot trial and limited pediatric aquatic-exercise data were available for FMD, the sample-size calculation was based on a moderate repeated-measures ANOVA effect size rather than a definitive FMD-specific pediatric effect size.

Following baseline testing, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to the aHIIT, aMICT, or control group. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher using a computer-generated random number program. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared in advance and opened only after completion of baseline assessments. Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, participants and exercise instructors could not be blinded to group allocation. However, outcome assessors and ultrasound image analysts were blinded to group allocation and assessment time points whenever feasible. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Sport University (2024LCLL-04) and registered in Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400080687). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, each participant’s guardian voluntarily signed a written informed consent.

Study design

The exercise intervention protocol was developed with modifications based on our previous studies [11, 12]. Participants were randomly allocated to the aHIIT, aMICT, or control group. The two aquatic exercise groups participated in supervised training sessions three times per week for 8 weeks, with 24 planned sessions in total. An attendance rate of ≥ 90% was considered indicative of satisfactory intervention completion.

All training sessions were conducted at the West Swimming Pool of Guangzhou Sport University and supervised by trained instructors. During the intervention period, participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary patterns and usual physical activity behaviors. Body composition, estimated aerobic fitness, and vascular function were assessed before and after the 8-week intervention. Participants were instructed to fast for 8 h before vascular function assessments.

Before each session, participants in both aquatic exercise groups completed a standardized 5-min land-based warm-up followed by a 10-min aquatic warm-up at low intensity (approximately 50–55% HRmax). Each session ended with a 5-min cool-down period. HRmax was estimated using the following equation: HRmax = 216.6 − 0.84 × age [14].

The aHIIT protocol consisted of repeated high-intensity breaststroke bouts. During the first week, participants completed 8–10 bouts of 30-s swimming at 80%−85% HRmax, with 60 s of passive or low-intensity recovery between bouts, as a familiarization period. From weeks 2 to 8, participants completed 10 × 30-s high-intensity aquatic exercise (such as freestyle swimming or freestyle kicking) bouts at 80%−90% HRmax, with 60 s of recovery between bouts. The main aHIIT training component lasted approximately 15–17 min, including approximately 5 min of effective high-intensity swimming.

The aMICT protocol consisted of moderate-intensity continuous breaststroke swimming at 60%−70% HRmax. During the first week, participants completed 30 min of continuous swimming, including approximately 20 min of familiarization according to individual tolerance. From weeks 2 to 8, participants completed two 15-min continuous swimming bouts at 60%−70% HRmax, separated by a 5-min recovery interval. Exercise protocols

Participants in the control group were asked to maintain their usual daily lifestyle and avoid initiating regular structured exercise during the study period. The aquatic exercise protocols were prescribed according to target heart-rate zones and exercise duration. Training load was monitored using both objective and subjective indicators, including heart-rate responses and RPE.Training load was monitored using both objective and subjective measures. Heart rate was monitored in real time using a Polar Unite heart-rate monitor and was cross-checked, when necessary, by manual 15-s radial pulse measurement. RPE was recorded after each session to reflect subjective exercise intensity.

Estimated energy expenditure was calculated using heart-rate monitoring data, MET values from the Compendium of Physical Activities, the ACSM metabolic equation, and participants’ body mass. The estimated average energy expenditure was approximately 150 kcal per session in the aHIIT group and approximately 240 kcal per session in the aMICT group.Because excess post-exercise oxygen consumption was not directly measured and total workload was not strictly matched, the two protocols should not be considered energy-matched. The purpose of this study was to compare the physiological responses to different aquatic exercise modalities prescribed according to distinct intensity patterns rather than to compare identical exercise doses. Therefore, differences between aHIIT and aMICT should be interpreted considering both exercise intensity and the overall training stimulus.

Body composition

Height and weight were measured and BMI was calculated. Fat mass (kg and %), lean body mass, and body shape were recorded using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 370, Korea). All participants were instructed to stand barefoot on the analyzer while performing the test and wear minimal clothing until the recording was completed.

Aerobic fitness

Considering the feasibility and practicality of field-based assessments in school-aged children, the present study used the 20-m multistage shuttle run test (20-mSRT) to estimate cardiorespiratory fitness. Estimated VO2max was derived from 20-mSRT performance using the internationally validated Léger equation rather than directly measured by cardiopulmonary exercise testing. The 20-mSRT is a widely used field-based assessment in pediatric populations, and its validity and reliability for estimating aerobic fitness have been established in children and adolescents [15–18]. Participants were asked to wear athletic shoes and athletic clothing, and carry nothing on their person. Participants were instructed to listen to an audio signal to start the test with an initial speed of 8.5 km/h. After one minute, the tempo was increased by one stage and the speed was increased by 0.5km/h. The test was completed when participants were unable to cross the round-trip line before the audio signal sounded and the number of round-trip made was recorded as the final score. Estimated VO2max was calculated based on the participant’s 20-m round trip performance using the internationally accepted Leger formula: estimated VO2max = 31.025 + 3.238 × maximum running speed −3.248 × age + 0.1536 × age × maximum running speed. Maximum running speed = 8 + 0.5 × final intensity level [19].

Vascular function

Hemodynamic and arterial stiffness parameters were recorded noninvasively. Carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) and central hemodynamic indices were assessed using applanation tonometry (SphygmoCor; AtCor Medical, Sydney, Australia). Ankle-brachial index (ABI) and brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) were measured using an oscillometric device (boso ABI-system 100; BOSCH & SOHN, Jungingen, Germany).

Brachial artery flow-mediated dilation (FMD) was assessed using a high-resolution ultrasound system (UNEX EF38G, Nagoya, Japan) in accordance with current methodological recommendations for FMD assessment. Participants rested quietly in the supine position for at least 10–15 min in a temperature-controlled room at 22–25 °C before testing. The brachial artery was imaged longitudinally 5–10 cm above the antecubital fossa. After a stable baseline diameter was recorded, a pneumatic cuff placed on the forearm distal to the imaging site was inflated to at least 40 mmHg above systolic blood pressure and maintained for 5 min. After rapid cuff deflation, brachial artery diameter was recorded during the reactive hyperemia period, and the peak post-deflation diameter was identified.

To improve measurement consistency, all vascular assessments were performed by the same experienced investigator using the same ultrasound system under standardized laboratory conditions. Participants were instructed to refrain from vigorous physical activity, caffeine-containing beverages, and other potential acute vasoactive factors for at least 12 h before testing. The same testing procedures, participant positioning, cuff placement, inflation duration, and image acquisition settings were applied at baseline and post-intervention assessments.

Ultrasound images were recorded continuously throughout the assessment and analyzed offline by an investigator blinded to group allocation and testing time point. Vessel diameter measurements were performed using the built-in edge-detection software of the ultrasound system, and manual corrections were applied only when automated detection was visually inconsistent or when image quality required adjustment. Baseline diameter, peak post-deflation diameter, and reactive hyperemia responses were reviewed together to ensure that FMD interpretation was not based solely on percentage change. FMD was calculated as follows: FMD (%) = [(peak post-deflation diameter − baseline diameter)/baseline diameter] × 100. Mean shear rate (MSR) was used to characterize the shear stimulus during reactive hyperemia and, when blood velocity data were available, was calculated as 4 × mean blood velocity/arterial diameter. Because FMD can be influenced by baseline artery diameter and shear stimulus, FMD findings were interpreted together with baseline diameter and MSR rather than as an isolated vascular index.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection, and homogeneity of variance was examined using Levene’s test. Baseline differences among the three groups were tested using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

All analyses were conducted using a per-protocol approach, as all randomized participants completed the 8-week intervention and post-intervention assessments with no missing outcome data.

Intervention effects were examined using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance with group (aHIIT, aMICT, control) as the between-subject factor and time (baseline, post-intervention) as the within-subject factor. The group × time interaction was considered the primary statistical test for determining whether changes over time differed among groups. When significant main or interaction effects were detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed. Partial eta squared (ηp2) was reported as an effect-size index where applicable.

When baseline imbalance or baseline dependency was considered relevant, analysis of covariance was used as a sensitivity analysis, with the corresponding baseline value included as a covariate. Exploratory Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations among changes in selected indicators. Given the small sample size, mediation analysis was conducted only as a post hoc hypothesis-generating analysis. The PROCESS macro was used with 5000 bootstrap samples to estimate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects. These exploratory mediation results were not used to infer causality. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Intervention faithfulness and adherence

All participants were included in the final analysis of intervention adherence. Overall attendance was above 95% in the aHIIT, aMICT, and control groups. Participants in both exercise groups completed the planned 24 training sessions.

Heart rate monitoring showed that participants generally reached the prescribed intensity range. The mean exercise heart rate was 175.7 ± 9.6 bpm in the aHIIT group and 139.4 ± 14.7 bpm in the aMICT group (Table 1). No significant protocol deviations were recorded during the intervention, and no withdrawals or modifications to the protocol were required. During the 8-week intervention period, no exercise-related adverse events (e.g., musculoskeletal injury, dizziness, syncope, or cardiopulmonary symptoms requiring medical attention) were reported in either exercise group. Effects of aHIIT and aMICT on vascular function

For brachial endothelial function, peak diameter increased significantly within both the aHIIT and aMICT groups after the intervention. However, the time × group interaction for peak diameter was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), indicating that this change should be interpreted as a within-group or time-related change rather than clear evidence of a differential intervention effect. Basal diameter showed significant time and group effects, but the time × group interaction was not significant (p > 0.05).

A significant time × group interaction was observed for FMD (p < 0.001, effect size = 0.17). FMD increased significantly in both the aHIIT and aMICT groups after the intervention, while no significant change was observed in the control group. Post hoc comparisons showed that FMD was significantly higher in the aHIIT and aMICT groups than in the control group (p < 0.05) after the intervention. Moreover, the improvement in FMD was greater in the aHIIT group than in the aMICT group. These findings suggest that aHIIT may be associated with a greater improvement in endothelial function than aMICT, although this result should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size. No significant time, group, or time × group effects were observed for MSR.

In the pulse wave analysis, significant time × group interactions were observed for AIx (p < 0.05, effect size = 0.10) and AIx@HR75 (p < 0.01, effect size = 0.05), indicating that changes in these central hemodynamic indices differed among groups. AIx and AIx@HR75 decreased significantly within the aMICT group after the intervention. Post hoc comparisons further indicated that AIx@HR75 differed significantly between the aHIIT and aMICT groups after the intervention. These results suggest that aMICT may have favorable effects on selected wave-reflection-related indices.

Brachial DBP, aortic DBP, and SEVR showed favorable within-group changes in the aMICT group after the 8-week intervention. Specifically, brachial DBP and aortic DBP decreased, whereas SEVR increased in the aMICT group. However, the time × group interactions for brachial DBP (p > 0.05), aortic DBP (p > 0.05), and SEVR (p > 0.05) were not statistically significant.

No significant time × group interactions were observed for resting heart rate, brachial SBP, aortic SBP, ABI, cfPWV, baPWV Right, or baPWV Left. These results indicate that the 8-week aquatic exercise interventions did not produce statistically significant differential changes in these vascular parameters among groups.

Exploratory correlation and post hoc mediation analyses

Exploratory correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations among changes in selected anthropometric, fitness, and vascular indicators (Fig. 3). Changes in BMI and obesity level were positively correlated with changes in brachial DBP and aortic DBP, and negatively correlated with changes in estimated VO2max after the 8-week intervention. Changes in estimated VO2max were positively correlated with changes in FMD, peak diameter, and SEVR, and negatively correlated with changes in brachial DBP. Changes in FMD were negatively correlated with changes in brachial DBP and basal diameter. In addition, changes in AIx@HR75 were positively correlated with changes in AIx and negatively correlated with changes in SEVR. This pilot randomized controlled trial compared the effects of 8 weeks of aHIIT and aMICT on vascular function and estimated cardiorespiratory fitness in school-aged children with obesity.The main findings of this pilot randomized controlled trial were that both aquatic exercise interventions were associated with significant improvements in estimated VO2max and brachial artery FMD, supported by significant group × time interactions. In contrast, although several anthropometric indicators showed favorable within-group changes, most body-composition outcomes did not demonstrate significant group × time interactions and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. No significant intervention-related differences were observed for resting blood pressure, ABI, baPWV, or cfPWV. The post hoc exploratory mediation analysis suggested a possible indirect association between changes in BMI and FMD through estimated VO2max. However, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating only and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal mechanism.

Discussion

This three-arm randomized controlled pilot trial primarily examined the effects of 8 weeks of aHIIT and aMICT on endothelial function in school-aged children with obesity, with brachial artery FMD designated as the primary vascular outcome. Secondary outcomes included estimated VO2max, central hemodynamic indices, arterial stiffness parameters, and anthropometric indicators. Significant group × time interactions were observed for FMD, estimated VO2max, AIx, and AIx@HR75. Both aquatic exercise interventions were associated with improvements in estimated cardiorespiratory fitness and endothelial function compared with the control condition, while the increase in FMD was greater following aHIIT than aMICT. In addition, aMICT was associated with favorable changes in selected wave-reflection-related indices. In contrast, no significant group × time interactions were observed for blood pressure, SEVR, ABI, cfPWV, or baPWV. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence from a pilot randomized controlled trial rather than definitive evidence of superiority of one training modality over the other. Given the limited sample size, the study was primarily designed to explore potential intervention effects and was not sufficiently powered to detect small-to-moderate between-group differences across all secondary outcomes.The increase in estimated VO2max following both aquatic exercise interventions is consistent with previous evidence that structured exercise improves cardiorespiratory fitness in individuals with obesity [4, 20]. However, estimated VO2max in the present study was derived from the 20-m shuttle run test rather than directly measured using cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Although the 20-mSRT is a practical and widely validated field-based assessment for children, indirect estimation may be affected by non-metabolic factors, including motivation, pacing strategy, running economy, and familiarity with the test. Therefore, the observed improvements should be interpreted as changes in estimated aerobic fitness rather than definitive evidence of increased directly measured maximal oxygen uptake.. Although some anthropometric indicators changed within the exercise groups, several body composition outcomes did not show significant time × group interactions. Thus, changes in lean body mass, BMI, WHR, and obesity level should be interpreted cautiously. Previous work suggested that MICT may be more effective than HIIT for body-shape improvement [21], and BMI is negatively associated with estimated VO2max [22]. However, in children aged 9–13 years, normal growth and maturation may also contribute to these changes.

The most relevant vascular finding was the improvement in FMD. FMD is a non-invasive index of conduit-artery endothelial responsiveness and is influenced by nitric oxide bioavailability, shear stimulus, baseline arterial diameter, blood pressure, autonomic regulation, and vascular smooth muscle function. Previous studies examined land-based HIIT and MICT effects on endothelial function in people with obesity [23, 24]. The present finding that aHIIT was associated with greater FMD improvement is broadly consistent with evidence that HIIT may enhance FMD more than MICT in obese adults [24]. Overall, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and viewed within the context of a pilot randomized controlled trial.Our previous aquatic and acute exercise studies also suggested that aHIIT or land-based HIIT may induce stronger endothelial responses than MICT, possibly through greater shear rate, lactate, VEGF, and IGF-1 responses [11, 12, 25].

Another important consideration is that the aHIIT and aMICT protocols were not matched for total workload or energy expenditure. Although both interventions were prescribed according to relative heart-rate intensity zones, the accumulated exercise volume and estimated energy expenditure differed between modalities because of differences in exercise duration and structure. Therefore, the greater improvement in FMD observed after aHIIT cannot be attributed solely to exercise intensity. Instead, it may reflect the combined effects of intermittent high-intensity stimuli, repeated shear stress exposure, metabolic responses, and differences in overall training stimulus. Future studies should consider matching total workload, energy expenditure, or exercise dose when directly comparing HIIT and MICT modalities.

Nevertheless, the magnitude of FMD improvement observed in the present study, particularly in the aHIIT group, was relatively large compared with previous pediatric exercise intervention studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. FMD is highly sensitive to methodological factors, including cuff position, baseline arterial diameter, shear stimulus, image quality, edge-detection procedures, and identification of the true peak post-deflation diameter. In the present study, several procedures were implemented to minimize measurement variability, including standardized participant preparation, identical ultrasound equipment and testing conditions, blinded offline image analysis, and review of baseline and post-intervention images by the same experienced investigator.

However, formal study-specific reproducibility analyses, such as intra-observer and inter-observer intraclass correlation coefficients or coefficients of variation, were not performed. Therefore, although the observed group × time interaction suggests a differential endothelial response, the precision and reliability of the absolute magnitude of FMD change cannot be fully confirmed. Future studies should incorporate formal reproducibility assessments, repeated baseline measurements, and standardized vascular quality-control procedures to validate the magnitude of FMD responses observed in this study.The exploratory correlations suggested that changes in estimated VO2max were associated with changes in FMD. Similar associations between estimated VO2max and FMD have been reported in obese individuals, healthy individuals, people with diabetes, and patients with heart disease [26–28]. Exercise-induced endothelial adaptation may be related to repeated shear-stress exposure and enhanced nitric oxide production [29]. However, these correlations cannot establish causality. Associations among BMI, estimated VO2max, FMD, and hemodynamic variables may reflect parallel exercise-related adaptations rather than a mechanistic pathway.

The central hemodynamic findings were mixed. AIx and AIx@HR75 showed significant time × group interactions, suggesting that aMICT may have favorable effects on selected wave-reflection-related indices. This is partly consistent with our previous finding that aMICT improved central and peripheral pressure-related variables in inactive adults [11]. However, AIx and AIx@HR75 are indirect indices influenced by heart rate, vascular tone, arterial geometry, wave reflection sites, and autonomic regulation. Although brachial DBP, aortic DBP, and SEVR improved within the aMICT group, their time × group interactions were not significant. Thus, the data do not support a conclusion that aMICT is superior for lowering blood pressure or improving SEVR. No significant changes were found for cfPWV or baPWV, possibly because the participants were young and had relatively low baseline PWV values.

Growth and maturation are important confounders in pediatric exercise intervention studies. Participants were 9–13 years old, a period characterized by rapid changes in height, lean mass, fat distribution, vascular structure, blood pressure regulation, and exercise responsiveness. Therefore, changes in BMI, lean body mass, WHR, estimated VO2max, and vascular indices may have been influenced not only by the intervention but also by normal growth and biological maturation. Because pubertal status, maturity offset, or Tanner stage was not assessed, we could not determine the extent to which maturation contributed to the observed anthropometric, fitness, and vascular changes. Sex-specific responses may also be relevant during this developmental period, but the small sample size prevented meaningful sex-stratified analyses.

This pilot trial provides preliminary and population-specific evidence on aquatic HIIT versus aquatic MICT in school-aged children with obesity while simultaneously assessing endothelial function, selected central hemodynamic indices, arterial stiffness, and estimated aerobic fitness. This contribution is incremental, extending previous work on HIIT, MICT, obesity, and vascular outcomes [11, 12, 23, 24].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 11 participants per group. Although an a priori sample-size calculation was performed based on a repeated-measures ANOVA model, the calculation was not based on a definitive FMD-specific effect size because pediatric aquatic-exercise data were limited. Therefore, this pilot trial was primarily designed to provide preliminary estimates of intervention effects and feasibility rather than definitive hypothesis testing. The relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power to detect small-to-moderate between-group differences, particularly for secondary outcomes such as anthropometric variables, arterial stiffness indices, and central hemodynamic parameters. Another limitation is that the aHIIT and aMICT interventions were not matched for total workload or energy expenditure. Although both protocols were prescribed using relative heart-rate intensity targets, the overall training stimulus differed between groups due to differences in exercise duration, interval structure, and accumulated exercise volume. Therefore, direct comparisons between aHIIT and aMICT should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed differences may reflect both exercise intensity and differences in total training dose.Pubertal status, maturity offset, or Tanner stage was not assessed, which limits our ability to distinguish intervention-related changes from normal growth- and maturation-related changes. Estimated VO2max was derived from the 20-m shuttle run test rather than directly measured by cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Although this field-based method is commonly used in pediatric populations, it provides an indirect estimation of aerobic capacity and may be influenced by motivation, exercise familiarity, and running performance characteristics. Therefore, future studies should incorporate direct cardiopulmonary exercise testing to confirm whether aquatic HIIT and MICT induce comparable changes in maximal oxygen uptake.. Daily physical activity, diet, sedentary behavior, and sleep were not objectively monitored. Another important limitation relates to the unexpectedly large improvement in FMD, particularly in the aHIIT group. Although all ultrasound images and calculations were rechecked and no obvious measurement or processing errors were identified, the magnitude of change exceeded that reported in many previous pediatric exercise interventions. Because FMD assessment is sensitive to technical and biological variability, the possibility that methodological factors contributed to part of the observed improvement cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, intra-observer and inter-observer reproducibility assessments were not available in the present study. Future trials should incorporate repeated measurements, formal reliability analyses, and centralized quality-control procedures to further validate FMD changes following aquatic exercise interventions.

In conclusion, both aHIIT and aMICT may improve field-estimated cardiorespiratory fitness in school-aged children with obesity. aHIIT may be associated with greater improvement in brachial artery FMD; however, whether this difference is attributable to exercise intensity alone remains uncertain because the two protocols were not matched for total workload, whereas aMICT may have favorable effects on selected wave-reflection-related indices such as AIx and AIx@HR75. However, given the pilot nature of this study, the small sample size, and limited statistical power for secondary outcomes, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger adequately powered randomized controlled trials with prespecified primary outcomes are required to determine whether one aquatic exercise modality provides superior cardiovascular benefits.
