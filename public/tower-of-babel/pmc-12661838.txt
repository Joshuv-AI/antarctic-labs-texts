Reprogramming autoimmunity: inducing antigen-specific tolerance via apoptotic mimicry in an experimental model of multiple sclerosis
Authors: Herena Eixarch, Imane Boutitah-Benyaich, Jorge Plaza, Sílvia Rodríguez-Vidal, Míriam Salvadó, Montserrat Mancera-Arteu, Lidia Almenara-Fuentes, Martí Dalmases, Marta Vives-Pi, Xavier Montalban, Bruna Barneda-Zahonero, Carmen Espejo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated disorder characterized by demyelination, axonal damage, and neurodegeneration, leading to neurological disability in patients. It is considered a prototypic antigen-specific autoimmune disease, and therefore is a strong candidate for therapies aimed at restoring immune tolerance to self-antigens. However, the development of effective antigen-specific tolerization remains an important unmet medical need. In the present study, we administered phosphatidylserine (PS) liposomes, mimicking apoptotic bodies, containing a myelin antigen [35–55 myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG35 − 55) peptide] in the MOG35 − 55-induced experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), as a therapeutic strategy for the treatment of MS.MethodsMOG PS liposomes or empty PS liposomes were administered in a single administration before or after the appearance of EAE neurological symptoms. Intraperitoneal, intranasal, intradermal and intravenous administration routes were used. For mechanistic studies, peripheral T and B cell subpopulations of treated EAE mice were studied by flow cytometry. In addition, EAE mice treated with MOG PS liposomes received blocking antibodies to deplete Treg, B cells, and immunomodulatory mediators IL-10 and TGF-β. The efficacy of the therapy was compared with daily administration of fingolimod.ResultsWe demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of the MOG-loaded PS liposomes when administered intraperitoneally before onset, and the mechanism of action of MOG-loaded PS liposomes involved Treg and B cells, since blocking these populations and their related molecules IL-10 and TGF-β, abrogated the tolerogenic effect of the therapy. We also demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of the MOG-loaded PS liposomes when administered intraperitoneally after appearance of EAE neurological signs. Alternative routes of administration such as intravenous and intradermal, which are more suited for translation in clinical trials, were found to be efficacious before and after appearance of EAE neurological signs. Intravenous administration of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in established EAE reduced dendritic cell activation, decreased inflammatory cytokine secretion, induced T cell exhaustion and expanded regulatory B cells (Breg) and CD39+CD4+FoxP3+ T cells.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that antigen-specific therapy with PS liposomes mimicking apoptotic bodies downregulates the MOG-specific inflammatory immune response and expands Breg and Treg cells, offering a safe, versatile, and easily applicable approach that strongly supports its potential for near-future clinical translation in MS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03618-w.

Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease that is characterized by the destruction of myelin sheaths, neuroaxonal damage, glial cell activation and formation of demyelinated plaques in the central nervous system (CNS). Up to 2.8 million people worldwide live with MS, and it is the first non-traumatic cause of disability in young adults [1]. Although the aetiology of MS is not fully understood, several genetic, environmental and life-style factors have been associated with an increased risk to develop the disease [2–5]. Evidence supporting the autoimmune origin of MS includes its fulfillment of the three criteria proposed by Koch and Witebsky [6]; moreover, data from the animal model and studies with MS samples strongly support that CNS-antigen autoreactive CD4+ T cells play a key role in disease pathogenesis [5]. It is thought that CNS-antigen autoreactive T cells are activated in the periphery and then circulate into the CNS. Resident antigen presenting cells (APCs) present CNS-antigens to autoreactive T cells, inducing their reactivation and giving rise to a series of immune events that ultimately cause demyelination, axonal damage and finally neuronal loss [7]. In addition to the strong evidences that support the critical role of CD4+ T cells in MS immunopathogenesis, including those derived from the widely accepted MS experimental model experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [8], data from human tissues and clinical trials indicate that other immune cells including CD8+ T cells, B cells and myeloid lineage cells, as well as the interaction between them and with other immune cells, are also involved in the pathogenesis of the disease [9, 10]. In fact, B cells are emerging as key players in the pathogenesis of the disease, since B cell depleting therapies (anti-CD20 antibodies including Rituximab, Ocrelizumab and Ofatumumab) are highly efficacious in MS [11–13].

Due to the autoimmune nature of the disease, many disease modifying treatments (DMTs) including immunomodulatory and immunosuppressive therapies have been developed and launched into the market in the last two decades; some of these are highly effective in inhibiting relapsing-remitting phenotypes, but much less efficient in progressive clinical courses [14, 15]. It is important to note that DMTs are not antigen specific and have broad immunosuppressive properties [16]. Accordingly, serious side effects may develop when using DMTs including lack of protection to infections, appearance of secondary autoimmune diseases or higher prevalence of malignancies [12, 17–21].

To overcome this non-selective immunomodulation or immunosuppression, the induction of antigen-specific tolerance has been proposed as alternative for the treatment of autoimmune diseases including MS [22, 23]. Moreover, antigen-specific immune tolerance strategies offer the potential for a definitive cure for autoimmunity and are expected to exhibit a more favorable long-term safety profile compared to current DMTs. Towards this aim, numerous approaches that seek to restore tolerance to self-antigens have been developed [24].

Programmed cell death or apoptosis is a critical physiological process by which cells die in a non-immunological process and maintains homeostasis. Apoptotic cells maintain membrane integrity, which avoids that cellular content is released into the extracellular milieu. Tissue-resident professional phagocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), and non-professional neighboring phagocytes quickly recognize and engulf apoptotic cells or fragments of them, so called apoptotic bodies. The rapid clearance of apoptotic cells is crucial to maintain tolerance to self-antigens and impedes the recruitment to and activation of inflammatory cells in healthy tissues [25]. One key feature of apoptotic cells is the high proportion of phosphatidylserine (PS) on the outer leaflet of the membrane, which constitutes an “eat me” signal for phagocytes in an environment that is devoid of immunological danger [26, 27]. Engulfment of apoptotic cells trigger a series of signals inside the phagocytes resulting in the process and presentation of apoptotic cell-derived antigens to T cells in a tolerogenic manner [28]. In the present study we sought to take advantage of this physiologic tolerogenic mechanism, called efferocytosis, to induce immune tolerance to antigens involved in MS autoimmune attack. Building on the concept of apoptotic mimicry to restore immune tolerance, we developed and tested PS-coated liposomes loaded with the myelin oligodendrocyte oligoprotein (MOG) peptide 35–55 (MOG35 − 55) in the MOG35 − 55-induced EAE model. In a previous study, we demonstrated that mice administered with PS liposomes charged with 40–55 MOG peptide presented a milder EAE disease course and presented expansion of a subset of T cells with potential regulatory function [29]. In addition, our PS liposomes have been validated for other autoimmune diseases after encapsulating the corresponding autoantigen for each disease [30, 31]. In the current study, we used the 35–55 MOG peptide and aimed to reproduce the beneficial effect of MOG-charged PS liposomes testing different administration doses and we investigated the mechanisms by which MOG PS liposomes induce tolerance, focusing on peripheral immune modulation and key regulatory pathways. We also aimed to test the efficacy of the therapy once the clinical symptomatology was established, an issue that was not explored in the previous publication. Administered both before disease onset and after neurological symptoms emerged, this strategy not only attenuated disease progression but also revealed key immunomodulatory pathways. We further evaluated delivery routes compatible with clinical application, and finally, the therapy was validated by comparing its efficacy to a currently used DMT for MS, positioning this platform as a promising candidate for translation.

Materials and methods

Mice

C57BL/6J 8-week-old female and male mice were used and were purchased from Envigo (former Harlan Laboratories; Barcelona, Spain), and provided by the Netherlands’ facilities. Mice were housed under standard light- and climate-controlled conditions, and standard chow and water were provided ad libitum.

Sex as a biological variable

In this study, female animals were used due to their higher susceptibility to developing clinical signs in the experimental model employed, which mirrors the greater incidence observed in the human disease, where the female-to-male ratio is approximately 3:1. This increased susceptibility allows for a more robust detection of disease-related endpoints, thereby enabling a reduction in the total number of animals required to reach statistical significance, in accordance with the 3Rs principle—particularly the principle of reduction. Additionally, male animals are known to exhibit increased territorial aggression and a higher propensity for fighting when housed together, leading to elevated stress levels. As stress is a known modulator of immune responses and disease progression, its presence could introduce variability and confounding effects in the clinical outcomes assessed. Nevertheless, given the scientific relevance of confirming therapeutic efficacy across both sexes, the treatment was also tested in male mice. This step was taken to ensure that the observed therapeutic effects are not sex-specific and to strengthen the translational validity of the findings. The therapy demonstrated comparable efficacy in male animals, thereby supporting its potential applicability in both sexes.

EAE induction, OVA immunization and follow-up

For EAE induction, anesthetized mice were immunized by subcutaneous injections of 100 µl phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 100 µg of MOG35–55 peptide (Proteomics Section, Universitat Pompeu Fabra, Barcelona, Spain) emulsified in 100 µl complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 4 mg/ml Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37RA (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA). At 0- and 2-days post-immunization, mice were intravenously injected with 250 ng of pertussis toxin (Sigma-Aldrich). In the experiments in which we compared PS liposomes with fingolimod and in which we confirmed their efficacy in males, immunization conditions were adjusted because the reference of pertussis toxin that we usually used (P7208 from Sigma-Aldrich) had to be changed (516560 from Sigma-Aldrich) due to stock breakdown. Specifically, mice were immunized with 200 µg of MOG35–55 peptide. Mice were weighed and examined daily in a blinded manner for neurological signs using the following criteria: 0, no clinical signs; 0.5, partial loss of tail tonus for 2 consecutive days; 1, paralysis of whole tail; 2, mild paraparesis of one or both hind limbs; 2.5, severe paraparesis or paraplegia; 3, mild to moderate tetraparesis; 4, tetraparesis (severe in hind limbs); 4.5, severe tetraparesis; 5, tetraplegia; and 6, death [32]. Score 5 and weight loss over 30% were defined as endpoint criteria to minimize the suffering and to guarantee animal welfare. All data presented are in accordance with the guidelines suggested for EAE publication [33]. A set of EAE mice were immunized with 50 µg Ovalbumin (OVA; Sigma-Aldrich) emulsified in CFA 28 days after MOG-immunization, rechallenged after 14 days and euthanized one week later to collect sera.

Peptides and liposomes

Peptides were selected based on their reported relevance in autoimmune disease development. The peptides ‒MOG35−55 and MOG36−55‒ were purchased from the Peptide Synthesis Facility (Department of Experimental and Health Sciences, Pompeu Fabra University, Spain) with > 95% purity. MOG peptides were suspended with DPBS (from Fisher Scientific, Spain) to a 0.5 mg/ml concentration. Liposomes were manufactured in Ahead Therapeutic’s clean room (Arboç, Spain) under good manufacturing practices (GMP)-like conditions. Liposomes were composed of 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero3-phospho-L-serine (sodium salt) (DOPS; Lipoid GmbH, Germany) and 1,2-Dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC; Lipoid GmbH, Germany), and cholesterol (CH; Sigma Aldrich, MO, USA).

Liposomes were prepared at a final lipid concentration of 31 mM using the thin film hydration method; however, during the course of the study the manufacturing method was changed to the solvent injection-based methodology, which allows a scalable manufacturing process. Both methods have previously been described [31, 34]. For this last manufacturing method, we observed that the exposure of the 35–55 MOG peptide to certain solvents resulted in the oxidation and degradation of the first aminoacid (methionine); for this reason, we tested the encapsulation of a shorter sequence, the 36–55 MOG sequence, and found no changes in its efficacy. The solvent injection method yields smaller, more uniform liposomes with improved drug encapsulation, making it better suited for clinical applications than thin film hydration.

The particle size, measured as the hydrodynamic diameter, the polydispersity index (PDI) and the Z-potential were measured by dynamic light scattering using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, Instruments Ltd, UK).

Peptide encapsulation efficiencies (EE) were calculated indirectly via the equation EE (%) = [(total peptide – free peptide)/total peptide] × 100, where total peptide was the initial peptide concentration and the free peptide was the concentration of non-encapsulated peptide. To measure the free peptide, liposome emulsions were centrifuged at 3.700 g at RT for 20 min and the concentration of non-encapsulated peptide was assessed in the supernatants by PIERCE BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., IL, USA).

Treatment of EAE mice with PS liposomes

Mice received one dose of empty or MOG-loaded PS liposomes two days before EAE induction (preventive approach), five days after EAE induction (before onset, early therapeutic approach), or when EAE clinical symptoms were already established (therapeutic approach). Mice received 200 µl of PS liposomes via intraperitoneal injection, and MOG-liposomes were charged with 0.5 mg/ml of rat MOG35 − 55. To test the efficacy of higher concentration of antigen inside the PS liposome, PS liposomes were charged with 1 mg/ml of rat MOG35−55. To test the efficacy of the therapy with a low volume, 25 µl of PS liposomes was also administered. Apart from intraperitoneal administration, three different routes of administration were assessed including intranasal, intradermal and intravenous. In this set of experiments, MOG-liposomes with 0.5 mg/ml of rMOG35 − 55 were used and the volume of administration was adjusted for each route of administration (25 µl intranasal, 50 µl intradermally and 100 µl intravenous) (39). In the case of therapeutic intravenous administration, comparison with fingolimod and confirmation of efficacy in males, mice received an intravenous administration of 100 µl of PS liposomes charged with 0.5 mg/ml of rat MOG36−55, because they were manufactured under the solvent injection method, as specified above.

Treatment of EAE mice with fingolimod

Treatment with fingolimod was used as a gold standard to compare the efficacy of MOG-liposomes. Fingolimod (FTY20, Cayman chemical, Michigan, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 10 mg/ml and properly stored at −20 °C until its use. Female EAE mice were treated daily, starting at day 5 post-immunization, with 0.3 mg/kg of fingolimod diluted in saline via intraperitoneal injection. A vehicle group that received the same amount of DMSO diluted in saline as the fingolimod-treated group was included. Mice were followed for 30 days.

Motor function assessment

Motor performance was evaluated using a RotaRod apparatus (Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy) at the end of the clinical follow up period. The apparatus was set to accelerate one rotation per second from a speed of 4 to 40 rotations per minute. Each mouse was placed on the rotating cylinder and the time the animals remained walking on the cylinder without falling was recorded. Mice were given three trials. The time that each mouse remained in the cylinder was calculated as the mean of the three trials.

Samples

Spleens were obtained four days after PS liposome treatment in the early therapeutic approach, and at day 28 post-immunization in the therapeutic approach. Splenocytes were mechanically dissociated by grinding the spleens in a 70 μm cell strainer (Cultek, Madrid, Spain). After erythrocyte removal and washing, cells were counted for further use in flow cytometry studies. Serum was obtained 21 days after OVA immunization and was stored at −80 °C for further ELISA assay.

Splenocyte proliferation assay

Splenocytes were freshly seeded in 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher) at a concentration of 106 cells/ml in complete X-VIVO™ 15 medium (Lonza), and were stimulated with 5 µg/ml MOG35–55 (antigen-specific stimulation) or 5 µg/ml phytohaemagglutinin-L (PHA-L, Sigma; polyclonal stimulation). Splenocytes cultured in the absence of stimulus were included as the basal proliferation condition. After 48 h of culture, 1 µCi of [3H]-thymidine (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was added to each well, and cultures were maintained under the same conditions for additional 18 h. Splenocyte cultures were transferred into glass fibre filters using a FilterMate Universal Harvester (PerkinElmer) and embedded in Optima Gold F scintillation liquid (PerkinElmer). Then, the incorporated radioactivity was measured in a 1450 Microbeta scintillation counter (PerkinElmer). Results are expressed as the stimulation index (SI) of MOG35–55 or PHA-L conditions. The SI for each individual mouse was calculated by dividing the mean counts per minute (cpm) of MOG35–55 or PHA-L stimulated wells by the mean counts per minute (cpm) of the basal proliferation condition wells. Proliferation was considered as positive when the SI was > 2.

Cytokine production profile assay

After 48 h in vitro, supernatants from splenocytes rechallenged with MOG35–55 and PHA-L were harvested and stored at −80 °C to further determine cytokine secretion profile.

Cytokines, including BAFF, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1b, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17 A and IL-23, were quantified in those supernatants with a ProcartaPlex Multiplex Immunoassay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were acquired in a Luminex Magpix instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and data were analyzed with ProcartaPlex Analyst software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Flow cytometry

Freshly isolated cells from spleen were labelled with a proper combination of anti-mouse antibodies detailed in Table 1. Fc receptors were blocked with rat anti-mouse CD16/32 (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA). Death cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 or Fixable Viability Stain A700 (BD). Nuclear FoxP3 staining was performed with the FoxP3 staining set (eBiosciences, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were acquired in a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and analysed with the CytExpert 2.0 software (Beckman Coulter).

Blocking antibodies

Anti-mouse CD25 (clone PC-611.5.3; to block Treg cells), anti-TGF-β (clone 1D11.16.8) and anti-mouse IL-10 (clone JES5-2A5), all obtained from Bio X Cell (NH, USA), were administered twice a week to EAE mice treated with empty or MOG-PS liposomes, starting at day 6 post-immunization. A group of either empty- or MOG-liposome treated EAE mice were administered with a control G immunoglobulin (IgG; rat IgG anti-horseradish peroxidase, Bio X Cell) following the same pattern of administration as with the blocking antibodies [35]. Anti-mouse CD20 (clone 5D2, Genentech, CA, USA) or control IgG were administered at days 5, 9 and 13 post-immunization (p.i.) to EAE mice treated with either empty- or MOG-liposomes [35]. In all cases, mice received 250 µg of antibody diluted in saline per dose.

ELISA anti-ovalbumin IgG

The ELISA kit used was Mouse Anti-Ovalbumin IgG purchased from Alpha Diagnostic International (San Antonio, TX, USA). ELISA was performed following manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were diluted 1:100 in duplicates. Plate was read at 450 nm wavelength and background at 630 nm was subtracted. Results were analyzed with the Antibody Activity Threshold Index, in which values above 1 are a measure of Positive Antibody Activity and values below 1 are negative for antibody.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, data are represented as the mean ± SD. Quantitative variables were studied with two-tailed Student’s T-test or Mann-Whitney test when two groups were compared, and with One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons or Kruskal-Wallis with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons when more than two groups were studied. Crosstabs were used to analyze qualitative variables. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. SPSS Statistics v26 (IBM Corporation) was used for statistical analysis. Graphs were done with GraphPad Prism software v9.

Results

PS liposome preparation and efficacy assessment of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in EAE

We developed a technology platform based on PS liposomes that can efficiently encapsulate several autoantigens [29–31, 36]. The MOG35 − 55 was encapsulated in PS liposomes with equivalent results to MOG40 − 55, used in a previous study [29]. We encapsulated MOG35 − 55 at 0.5 mg/ml and 1 mg/ml and performed all the required tests to validate the technology and compile the needed information to define the toxicology and clinical batches (Table 2).

All liposomes’ preparations were monitored in terms of intensity weighted mean hydrodynamic size (nm), polydispersity index, stability (ζ-potential), and efficiency of peptide encapsulation. For the following experiments, PS liposomes that encapsulated the 36–55 MOG peptide were used: therapeutic intravenous administration, comparison with fingolimod and confirmation of efficacy in males.

In the present study, we sought to test the efficacy of PS liposomes charged with MOG35 − 55 administered in a single intraperitoneal dose of 200 µl at a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml in an early therapeutic approach [day 5 p.i., before disease onset], and demonstrated its efficacy since mice treated with a single dose of MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes showed a milder disease course compared to mice receiving empty PS liposomes (Fig. 1A). Importantly, a significant proportion of mice that had received a single administration of MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes (from now on, MOG-liposome group) did not develop neurological signs, while all mice receiving empty PS liposome (from now on, empty-liposome group) developed EAE (Fig. 1B, p < 0.05). When analyzing mice that did develop EAE clinical signs, the maximum clinical EAE score of MOG-liposome treated mice was similar to that of the empty-liposome control group (Fig. 1C left graph); however, disease onset was delayed (Fig. 1C right graph) and the clinical course was also milder (AUC score of mice that developed EAE: empty-liposome group 55.00±13.94 vs. MOG-liposome group 21.56±14.76 vs.; p = 0.001).

Next, we aimed to determine whether loading PS liposomes with 1 mg/ml of MOG35 − 55 peptide would improve the clinical benefit of the liposome-based therapy. Immunized mice were treated at day 5 p.i. (before disease onset, early therapeutic approach) with 200 µl of PS liposomes loaded with 1 mg/ml of the encephalitogenic peptide. Although the beneficial effect was maintained (Fig. 1A), 77.80% of 1 mg/ml MOG-liposome treated mice developed neurological signs (Fig. 1B), the maximum score of those mice that developed EAE was equivalent to that of the 0.5 mg/ml MOG-liposomes treated group (Fig. 1C left graph) and the delay in the clinical onset compared to EAE-developing empty-liposome controls was not as significant as that observed in mice treated with MOG-loaded PS liposomes containing 0.5 mg/ml of the peptide (Fig. 1C right graph), so the 1 mg/ml concentration was discarded and 0.5 mg/ml MOG-loaded PS liposomes were used in the following experiments.

We next demonstrated that a single intraperitoneal administration of a lower volume of MOG-loaded PS liposomes (25 µl) containing 0.5 mg/ml of MOG35 − 55 was sufficient to ameliorate EAE clinical outcome (Fig. 1D). The number of mice that developed EAE increased with less volume of administered MOG-loaded PS liposomes (Fig. 1E). However, mice that developed EAE still presented a milder disease in terms of maximum score reached (Fig. 1F, left graph)), onset of clinical symptoms (Fig. 1F, right graph) and clinical severity (AUC score for empty-liposome group 55.00±13.94 vs. AUC for 25 µl MOG-liposomes: 16.74±13.62; p < 0.0001) compared to empty-liposome group.

These results indicate that a single dose of MOG-loaded PS liposomes is efficient at reducing EAE development and severity. Moreover, intraperitoneal administration of 200 µl of PS liposomes loaded with 0.5 mg/ml of MOG35 − 55 ensures the maximum clinical benefit of the therapy.

MOG-loaded PS liposomes induce an immunomodulatory profile of T and B cells

We next aimed to elucidate whether the administration of MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes modified the frequency of different subsets of T and B cells as well as their activation status when given in the pre-onset phase of the disease. We obtained spleens four days after PS liposome treatment (day 9 p.i.). Then, immune cells were isolated and stained with specific antibodies, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Treatment with MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes increased the ratio CD4/CD8 (Fig. 2A). In addition, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were more activated than those from empty-liposome group (Fig. 2A). When analyzing CD8+ T cell subsets in more detail, an increase in the percentage of effector (CD62L− CD44+ CD127− KLRG1−) and effector memory (CD62L− CD44+ CD127+ KLRG1−) CD8+ T cells was observed in the spleens of MOG-liposome treated mice (Fig. 2B); indeed, a higher percentage of these effector/effector memory (CD62L− CD44+) CD8+ T cells expressed the inhibitory receptor PD-1 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, mice treated with MOG-loaded PS liposomes showed enhanced frequencies of (CD62L− CD44+ CD127− KLRG1−) and effector memory (CD62L− CD44+ CD127+ KLRG1−) CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2C, see gating strategy in Additional File 1, Fig. S1A) as well as higher percentage of effector/effector memory (CD62L− CD44+) CD4+ populations expressing the inhibitory receptors PD-1 and Tim-3 (Fig. 2C, see gating strategy in Additional File 1, Fig. S1B). Treatment with MOG-loaded PS liposomes also induced expansion of regulatory T cells (Treg) and increased the frequency of CD4+FoxP3+ cells expressing CD39, which has been associated with a higher capacity of immunoregulation via the secretion of IL-10 [37] (Fig. 2D).

Total B cell frequency decreased upon MOG-liposome treatment in EAE mice and B cells were less activated than those from empty-liposome treated mice (Fig. 2E). Indeed, the MOG-liposome treatment induced the reduction of B2 cells (B cells with activation dependency from CD4+ T cells) (Fig. 2E). MOG-loaded PS liposomes promote antigen-specific tolerance through B cell- and treg-dependent mechanisms

To further elucidate the mechanism of action of the MOG-loaded PS liposomes, we performed a series of in vivo experiments in which we blocked Treg cells and Treg-related cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β using specific blocking antibodies, as previously described in [35]. All blocking antibodies were administered intraperitoneally twice per week, starting one day after treatment with MOG-liposomes (i.e., on day 6 p.i.). Mice treated with empty-liposomes and an equivalent dose of irrelevant IgG were used as a reference group for EAE severity. A separate group of mice that received MOG-liposomes along with an equivalent dose of irrelevant IgG was used to assess therapeutic efficacy of MOG-liposome treatment. The effects of CD25 depletion (Treg cells) [38], TGFβ [39] and IL-10 [40] blockade on EAE have already been previously described in the literature. Depletion of Treg cells with the anti-CD25 antibody did not modify the beneficial effect of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in terms of incidence, because 20 days after immunization only 5/9 (55.60%) mice developed neurological symptoms in both MOG-liposome group treated with anti-CD25 or with control IgG compared to 8/8 (100%) in the empty-liposome group. The severity of the disease was also similar between the MOG-liposome group administered with anti-CD25 and the MOG-liposome control group; however, the disease began to worsen just before the 5th dose of anti-CD25 antibody administration (Fig. 3A, left graph). After the fifth dose of anti-CD25 (20 days post-immunization), a significant proportion of mice treated with the antibody died (6 out of 9, p = 0.001), and most of them hadn’t developed EAE symptomatology yet (Fig. 3A second graph). In fact, in our experimental model, blocking Treg goes beyond the reduction of the beneficial effect of MOG-liposomes, since deletion of Treg in the EAE model has already demonstrated to aggravate the clinical course of the disease [38]. We analyzed the clinical outcome of the three surviving mice that were administered with anti-CD25 (Fig. 3A, graphs on the right), and observed that the peak of the disease was reached at day 19 p.i., delayed respect to the Empty-liposome group (Fig. 3A, third graph). In addition, the severity of the symptomatology until the end of the clinical follow up was comparable to the Empty-liposome group and more severe than that of the MOG-liposome control group, as observed in Fig. 3A, right graph, and in terms of maximum score (MOG-liposome + anti-CD25: 4.50 ± 0.00 and MOG-liposome + IgG: 1.78 ± 1.86, p = 0.003) and AUC score (MOG-liposome + anti-CD25: 55.50 ± 11.27 and MOG-liposome + IgG: 17.83 ± 23.32, p = 0.013). Although the limited number of mice available for analysis at the end of the follow-up period does not allow for definitive conclusions, our data suggest that Treg cells may not be the only subset involved in the early phases of the beneficial effect of MOG-loaded PS liposomes. Rather, they may become particularly important in sustaining the therapeutic effect once tolerance has already been established. Blockade of TGF-β in MOG-liposome treated EAE mice partially reverted the beneficial effect of the therapy: the EAE symptoms worsened after the fifth dose of anti-TGF-β antibody (Fig. 3B), but the overall clinical course of the mice that developed EAE was not statistically different compared to MOG-liposome + IgG EAE-diseased mice (Fig. 3B). The blockade of the immunoregulatory cytokine IL-10 partially abrogated the beneficial effect of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in EAE from onset, but the overall clinical course was not statistically different from MOG-liposome + IgG treated mice (Fig. 3C).

B cells were depleted by the administration of anti-CD20 antibody at day 5, 9 and 13 after immunization as previously described [35]. Anti-CD20 was also administered to mice treated with empty-liposomes to control for potential antigen-independent effects of the antibody on EAE progression. The depletion of B cells worsened EAE symptoms in MOG-liposome group of mice from the onset, and the loss of the tolerogenic effect became more pronounced at day 20 p.i. (Fig. 3D). Indeed, the maximum score reached by mice that developed EAE was higher in the MOG-liposome treated mice that received anti-CD20 (MOG-liposome + IgG: 1.83 ± 0.68, MOG-liposome + anti-CD20: 3.44 ± 1.24; p = 0.0011), as well as the area under the curve (AUC) of the score (Fig. 3D right graph). The partial loss of the tolerogenic effect of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in the early stages of the disease indicates that other cell types may also contribute; however, the temporal progression supports a key role for B cells in both the induction and maintenance phases of tolerance induced by MOG-loaded PS liposomes.

Indeed, we also confirmed that MOG-liposome treatment does not induce a systemic immunosuppression: EAE mice treated with MOG-liposomes were able to mount a humoral response similar to that of EAE mice treated with empty-liposomes when immunized with another antigen, specifically OVA (Fig. 3E), indicating that the humoral response to other antigens is not dampened by the tolerance induction.

MOG-loaded PS liposomes do not prevent the immune response to MOG peptide

To determine whether MOG-loaded PS liposomes had the capacity to prevent the autoimmune response, 200 µl of 0.5 mg/ml MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes were injected intraperitoneally two days before EAE induction, since PS liposomes are rapidly distributed after intraperitoneal injection [31] and are phagocyted by DCs after 6 h of exposure and DCs acquire a tolerogenic phenotype within 24 h [41]. MOG-liposome treated mice developed EAE in a similar fashion as empty-liposome group (Fig. 4A); although MOG-liposome treated mice presented an earlier onset of the disease compared to controls (MOG-liposome: day 12.14 p.i.±1.68 and empty-liposome: 18.13 p.i.±5.11; p = 0.013).

MOG-loaded PS liposomes ameliorate EAE clinical course when neurological signs are already established

We next aimed to demonstrate the capacity of MOG PS liposomes to ameliorate the clinical symptomatology. To this end, 200 µl of 0.5 mg/ml MOG35 − 55-loaded PS liposomes were administered intraperitoneally to EAE mice that already presented neurological symptoms. Mice were randomized from day 12 to day 14 p.i. into equivalent groups of animals with respect to accumulated score, day of onset and clinical score at the day of treatment. A single dose of MOG-loaded PS liposomes was able to reverse the clinical symptomatology and ameliorate EAE clinical course in mice with already established neurological symptoms (Fig. 4B). Remarkably, 9 out of 11 mice (81.80%) showed a milder disease course or recovery after MOG-loaded PS liposome treatment (defined as a lower clinical score respect to the peak clinical score of a given mouse for at least three consecutive days, not considering 4.5 to 4 as recovery), while only 1 out of 11 empty-liposome treated mouse presented clinical recovery (p = 0.001). These data demonstrate the relevance of the proposed liposome-based antigen-specific therapy for the treatment of already established autoimmune disease and its potential of translation into clinical practice.

Candidate routes of administration for clinical translation

We next aimed to explore different routes of administration for further translation to the clinic of the therapy based on liposomes mimicking apoptotic bodies and charged with autoantigens, since the intraperitoneal injection is not performed in humans. Volumes for each administration route were chosen according to the literature [42]. One intranasal administration of 25 µl of MOG-loaded PS liposomes at the pre-onset phase of EAE (day 5 p.i.) was not effective and EAE mice treated with MOG-loaded PS liposome presented a severity similar to that of controls (Fig. 5A). However, a single administration of 50 µl of MOG-loaded PS liposomes via intradermal administration (Fig. 5B) or 100 µl of MOG-loaded PS liposomes via intravenous injection (Fig. 5C) resulted in reduced severity of neurological symptoms, and particularly in those mice receiving the MOG-liposomes via intravenous administration.

Intravenous administration of 100 µl of PS liposomes once EAE clinical symptoms were established, proved to be highly effective to revert neurological signs. Because the method of liposome manufacturing used was the solvent injection method, which is more suited for clinical applications, instead of the thin film hydration method, the 36–55 MOG peptide was used for this approach (see Material and Methods section for more detail). Indeed, all mice in the MOG-liposome group recovered from their peak clinical score (15/15), while only 4/15 did in the empty-liposome group (p < 0.001). Additionally, MOG-liposome group of mice presented a better motor function at the end of the clinical follow up, tested with the Rotarod paradigm (Fig. 6E).

Given that a single intravenous administration in EAE-diseased mice demonstrated superior efficacy compared to intradermal administration, we sought to examine the peripheral immune profile of mice therapeutically treated with MOG-liposomes at the end of the clinical observation period (14 to 17 days post-liposome infusion). Flow cytometry analysis revealed no differences in Treg cell frequencies, but CD4+FoxP3+ T cells exhibited elevated levels of the ectonucleotidase CD39, which enhances their immunosuppressive capacity (Fig. 7A). Additionally, the frequency of effector and effector memory T cells within the CD4 (Fig. 7B) and CD8 (Fig. 7C) compartments expressing inhibitory molecules was elevated in mice that received MOG-liposomes. Specifically, there was an expansion of Tim3+ and Tim3+PD-1+ CD4 effector/effector memory T cells, as well as a trend to increased Tim3+ and Lag3+ CD8 effector/effector memory T cells. Likely as a result of the anergic/tolerogenic state of the T cells, the frequency of B2 cells and activated B cells was reduced (Fig. 7D). In line with the observed immunosuppressive phenotype in the T cell compartment, an expansion of Breg cells was detected (Fig. 7D). Additionally, we evaluated the effect of liposome therapy on the activation status of DCs. Although major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II molecule expression was elevated in mice treated with MOG- liposomes, DCs exhibited reduced levels of the co-stimulatory molecule CD86 (Fig. 7E), suggestive of their tolerogenic polarization.

Splenocytes from intravenously MOG-liposome treated mice exhibited a trend to reduce MOG-specific proliferation (Fig. 7F). These splenocytes also released significantly lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-17 A, and GM-CSF) and the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 when rechallenged with MOG35 − 55 (Fig. 7G), with a trend towards reduced production of IFN-γ and IL-1β (see Additional File 2, Fig. S2A). MOG-liposome therapy is effective in male mice and shows comparable efficacy to fingolimod in EAE

To confirm that the therapeutic effect of MOG-liposomes was not restricted to female mice, we induced EAE in male mice and administered a single intravenous dose of 100 µl of 36–55 MOG-loaded PS liposomes on day 5 p.i., corresponding to the pre-onset phase of the disease. As observed in females, male mice treated with MOG-liposomes developed significantly milder disease compared to those receiving empty-liposomes (Fig. 8A, B).

In addition, the MOG-liposome therapy was compared to fingolimod, a sphingosine analogue that binds to sphingosine 1-phosphate receptors and restrains the migration of lymphocytes out of the lymph nodes. Fingolimod was used as the gold standard therapy that is currently used in MS. To this end, treatment with fingolimod was started at day 5 p.i., at the pre-onset phase of the disease, and was administered daily via intraperitoneal injection until the end of the clinical follow up. A group of mice were treated with a single intravenous dose of 100 µl of MOG-liposomes or empty-liposomes at day 5 p.i. As expected, fingolimod improved EAE outcome in a significant manner (AUC sore for fingolimod group: 10.45 ± 11.49, AUC score for vehicle group: 44.00 ± 22.48; p = 0.0002), and MOG-liposomes also showed a beneficial effect on EAE development (AUC score for MOG-liposome group: 0.25 ± 0.63 and AUC score for empty-liposome group: 26.85 ± 22.03; p = 0.0033). Notwithstanding, a single administration of MOG-liposomes performed better than daily administration of fingolimod (AUC score 0.25 ± 0.63 vs. 10.45 ± 11.49, respectively), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.4524) (Fig. 9A, B). Together with the evidence showing that the immune system is not broadly affected, MOG-liposome therapy stands out as a strong candidate for an alternative treatment strategy in MS.

Discussion

Apoptosis is a physiological mechanism of cell death during tissue turnover, which maintains immune tolerance to self-antigens. Thus, apoptotic cells present specific features to avoid an immune response to self and the recruitment of inflammatory cells into healthy tissues. Among other molecules, apoptotic cells present a high content of PS in the external leaflet of the cellular membrane. Once APCs engulf and clear apoptotic cells, tolerogenic mechanisms are triggered and lead to the presentation of self-antigens derived from apoptotic cells in a tolerogenic manner. Different approaches have been explored to take advantage of this physiological tolerogenic mechanism in the context of autoimmunity [43]. Indeed, we demonstrated that DCs pulsed with apoptotic pancreatic β-cells prevented the development of type 1 diabetes in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice [44]. In a first approach to translate the therapeutic immunomodulatory potential of apoptotic pancreatic β-cells into clinical practice, we developed synthetic vesicles (liposomes) mimicking apoptotic bodies loaded with autoantigens. These liposomes have been designed to contain a high proportion of PS in their surface in order to mimic apoptotic bodies. PS liposomes charged with insulin peptides administered in vivo reduced the incidence and severity of type 1 diabetes in NOD mice [30], most likely because DCs efficiently capture PS liposomes and acquire immunoregulatory features that allow them to present insulin autoantigens in a tolerogenic context [36]. The therapeutic potential and potential applicability of PS liposomes encapsulating autoantigens to other autoimmune diseases has led us to launch a platform devoted to treat autoimmunity based on the induction of antigen-specific tolerance with autoantigen-loaded PS liposomes. Towards this end, we have demonstrated the efficacy of the antigen-specific tolerogenic therapy with autoantigen-loaded PS liposomes in experimental models of myasthenia gravis, rheumatoid arthritis and MS [29, 31]. However, the therapeutic approach needed improvement in order to translate it from bench to bedside. The studies described here will serve as a starting point to establish the proper conditions for the translation of the therapy to a clinical trial.

Different approaches based on the induction of autoantigen-specific immune tolerance have been explored in the experimental model of MS [45–48] and in clinical trials [49–51]. In the present study we propose a nano-based therapy that has already proved to ameliorate the clinical outcome of EAE mice [29]. We aimed to further characterize the therapeutic potential of MOG-loaded PS liposomes in the EAE model and attempted to characterize the mechanisms by which MOG-liposomes induce tolerance, focusing on peripheral immune modulation and key regulatory pathways.

Surprisingly, MOG-loaded PS liposomes did not prevent EAE development when administered before disease induction. This could relate to a limited lifespan of the induced tolerogenic DCs [52–54] or to the loss of tolerogenic functions under inflammatory conditions [53, 55]. Another possibility relies on the absence of antigen-experienced T cells at that stage, given that triggering tolerogenic reprogramming requires a primed T cell repertoire [35]. Additionally, the timing of administration might have been insufficient for tolerogenic reprogramming to occur, as suggested by other preventive protocols using longer pre-immunization intervals [56, 57]. Nonetheless, it is important to emphasize that the therapy with MOG-liposomes proved highly effective once clinical signs were already established, significantly ameliorating neurological symptoms. This finding is particularly relevant from a clinical perspective, as autoimmune disorders—including MS—are typically diagnosed after symptom onset. Therefore, demonstrating that a therapeutic approach not only modulates the immune response in vivo but also reverses established autoimmunity is of critical translational value. However, it should be noted that in our study we evaluated the tolerogenic capacity of PS liposomes loaded with a single peptide. In contrast, in MS, multiple myelin antigens are involved in disease progression. Therefore, future approaches should consider PS liposomes carrying a broader panel of candidate peptides, such as the seven myelin peptides proposed by Bielikova and colleagues [58]. Another important consideration when translating therapeutic approaches to clinical practice is the route of administration. While intraperitoneal administration is commonly used in experimental models, it is not applicable to humans. Moreover, certain routes of antigen administration are known to be immunogenic, whereas others tend to be tolerogenic [59]. Also, the biodistribution differs between routes: intradermally administrated PS liposomes distribute slower than intravenously administered liposomes, and the quantity of PS liposomes that reach tissues and organs is much lower [31]. Taken together, these findings contribute to the differential tolerogenic effect of PS liposomes administered via distinct routes.

As another important step, we examined potential mechanisms underlying MOG-liposome–induced tolerance. We have already demonstrated that PS liposomes interact in vivo with macrophages and DCs in the spleen just six hours after injection [31]. Moreover, in vitro, DCs capture PS liposomes and acquire a regulatory phenotype [36]. Other groups reported that the administration of myelin antigens, soluble or coupled to carriers of various nature, induce tolerance through the generation of tolerogenic APCs and the expansion of antigen-specific Treg cells [60, 61]. Our results suggest that the administration of MOG-liposomes may elicit different tolerogenic mechanisms. In a first step, APCs would that take up MOG-loaded PS liposomes acquire a regulatory phenotype [36]. Tolerized APCs, and probably other cell types including B cells [62], would produce high amounts of IL-10 [63] in this first phase of inducing a state of immune tolerance. This is supported by the fact that when we blocked IL-10 the disease course was very similar to controls in the first days of disease development. Afterwards, the tolerogenic effect was only partially abrogated in the absence of IL-10, meaning that other mechanisms of suppression were also becoming relevant. In our experimental setting we found an expansion of Treg after administration of MOG PS liposomes, and Treg-expressing CD39 cells, which confers Tregs a highly immunosuppressive capacity and are described to produce high amounts of IL-10 [37]. However, the depletion of Treg cells in MOG-liposome-treated mice resulted in a maintained state of tolerance in the first stage of the disease, suggesting that other mechanisms or cell populations are the main suppressive mechanism at early stages of tolerance induction. Although twenty days after immunization 67% of MOG-liposome-treated mice died, those who survived presented a clinical course as severe as the control group, suggesting that Treg cells play a relevant role in the control of immune tolerance induced by MOG-liposome treatment. On the other hand, the death of a large proportion of mice after the fifth dose of anti-CD25 limits the robustness of the results, and further experiments would be required to more conclusively address the role of Treg cells in the mechanism of tolerance. Nevertheless, we also performed blocking studies targeting TGF-β, the key cytokine responsible for the differentiation of CD4 T cells into Treg cells. Blocking TGF-β abrogated the tolerogenic effect of MOG-liposomes in a later phase compared to IL-10 blocking, supporting the notion that differentiation of Treg cells is important in later phases of the tolerogenic state to further maintain the tolerogenic milieu rather than in the first phase of tolerance induction. However, we did not directly determine whether MOG-specific T cells differentiate into Foxp3⁺ Tregs or Tr1 cells after treatment. Such analyses would provide valuable insight into the mechanisms underlying tolerance induction, particularly by clarifying whether regulatory conversion of antigen-specific T cells contributes to the observed therapeutic effects through bystander suppression. While this was beyond the scope of our proof-of-concept in vivo study, it represents an important direction for future work to strengthen the mechanistic and translational basis of this approach. We also showed that the depletion of B cells resulted in the loss of tolerance from the beginning of the clinical follow-up. We showed that MOG-liposomes downregulate MHC-II on B cells four days after their administration, potentially limiting pathogenic antigen presentation while preserving regulatory function. Our data from B cell depletion experiment further supports the relevance of B cells in the mechanism of action of PS liposome-based therapy. The elimination of tolerized B cells serving as APCs in the first stages of the disease directly impact on the maintenance of Treg populations and probably in the suppression of inflammatory APC activity. Although CD5⁺CD1dhi Breg cells did not expand early after MOG-liposome administration, they increased at later time points, suggesting a delayed regulatory effect of Breg cells in the tolerogenic effect. We cannot, however, exclude the contribution of other B cell subtypes to tolerance, either via IL-10 secretion or through IL-10–independent mechanisms [65–67]. In fact, we have evidences in the NOD model that distinct Breg cell subsets interact with PS liposomes and produce IL-10 [31]. Moreover, B cells acting as APCs have been reported to induce antigen-specific tolerance through the induction of IL-10–producing Breg cell subsets and the expansion of Treg cells [68, 69]. We propose that both B cells and DCs process PS liposomes, present MOG in a tolerogenic context, and secrete IL-10, promoting Treg cell induction and the upregulation of inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules in the T cell compartment. The success of B cell-depleting therapies in MS highlights the important role of B cells in disease pathogenesis, beyond their traditional function as antibody-producing cells. In MS patients—especially those carrying the HLA-DR15⁺ haplotype—B cells have been shown to act as APCs that activate pathogenic CD4⁺ T cells [5]. In this context, PS liposomes may offer therapeutic benefit by both promoting B cell tolerance and expanding Breg cells. In summary, our mechanistic studies suggest that B cells are essential in the induction of autoantigen-specific tolerance mediated by MOG-loaded PS liposomes, and together with other tolerized APCs, B cells might induce tolerance mediated by IL-10, and secondly, the tolerogenic state is maintained by Treg cells (through the secretion of IL-10 and other suppressor mechanisms such as anergy, induction of apoptosis or exhaustion) (Fig. 10).

Conclusions

The induction of autoantigen-specific tolerance remains a key objective in the treatment of autoimmune diseases such as MS. Here, we present a nanotechnology-based approach using PS liposomes carrying a MOG peptide, which demonstrated robust efficacy in halting disease progression and ameliorating established clinical symptoms in the EAE model. Intravenous administration—compatible with clinical application—was identified as the most effective route. Mechanistically, the therapy induced a tolerogenic effect that involves the expansion of Treg and Breg cells, ultimately promoting antigen-specific tolerance without inducing global immunosuppression.

PS liposomes offer multiple advantages over current strategies, including a favorable safety profile, low immunogenicity, scalability, and ease of synthesis. Their production is independent of patient-derived materials and readily adaptable to incorporate diverse autoantigens, making this platform broadly applicable to various immune-mediated disorders. Together, these findings position PS liposomes as a promising and clinically translatable strategy for antigen-specific immunotherapy in neuroinflammatory diseases.
