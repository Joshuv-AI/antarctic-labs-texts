Delayed recognition and significant morbidity in adults with non–transfusion-dependent thalassemia
Authors: Aaron N. Cheng, Scott Peslak, Sujit Sheth, Farzana Sayani, Ashutosh Lal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•In this cohort, adults with NTDT had significant morbidity including iron overload, endocrinopathies, and cardiopulmonary disease.•Despite an initial diagnosis of NTDT, nearly half of patients were started on regular transfusions after referral to a thalassemia center.

Introduction

Thalassemia is an inherited hemoglobinopathy caused by mutations in the globin genes, leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and a variable degree of anemia. The disease spectrum ranges from transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT), in which early and regular transfusion therapy is required to prevent severe anemia and growth failure, to non-TDT (NTDT), a phenotype characterized by a milder anemia that does not require regular transfusions for survival.1 The umbrella term NTDT encompasses non–transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia as well as certain forms of α-thalassemia, such as hemoglobin H (HbH) disease.2

Despite advances in our understanding of NTDT pathophysiology and management, the epidemiology of adult NTDT in the United States remains poorly defined. Precise national incidence and prevalence estimates for NTDT are not available, partly because there is no national thalassemia registry, and partly because ascertainment and diagnosis are complicated by phenotypic heterogeneity and variable recognition of thalassemia outside specialized centers. Global burden of disease analyses provide only broad estimates for thalassemia as a whole; in 2021, the age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates in high-income North America were estimated at 1.53 per 100 000 and 0.10 per 100 000, respectively, but these estimates did not distinguish between TDT and NTDT phenotypes.3 Despite the absence of epidemiologic data for NTDT in the United States, the relevance of this population is growing over time, given changing migration patterns and the increasing recognition of morbidity in this subset of patients with thalassemia.4,5 Previous epidemiologic reports of NTDT have focused on either pediatric or mixed-aged cohorts, often with longitudinal follow-up from childhood.6 However, there are limited data characterizing the disease burden and treatment needs of people with NTDT who first present to comprehensive thalassemia centers in adulthood.

Whereas the treatment paradigm for TDT almost uniformly involves the early initiation of regular transfusions, typically in early childhood, the management of NTDT is far more variable and reflects the wide variation in disease severity.6 In severe forms of thalassemia, the need for regular transfusions usually becomes apparent early, and management strategies are now well described for this population, with established guidelines for treatment and surveillance that have improved outcomes. In contrast, NTDT may present more subtly and may remain underrecognized or undermanaged outside of specialty centers for many years, particularly in areas in which thalassemia is less prevalent. In that sense, despite the generally milder transfusion requirements that define NTDT, delayed recognition and less structured longitudinal management may contribute to substantial disease-related complications in adulthood. It may even be fair to say that because of optimized management of TDT, by comparison untreated NTDT may be at risk for more severe manifestations of thalassemia.

Historically, the management of NTDT focused on supportive care and treatment of established complications, with intermittent transfusions, occasional splenectomy, and consideration of adjunctive agents such as hydroxyurea selectively used for specific clinical indications such as poor growth, skeletal deformities, and sequelae of long-term ineffective erythropoiesis and extramedullary hematopoiesis.7 However, over the past decade, the clinical approach to NTDT has shifted toward earlier identification of complications and a lower threshold for consideration of transfusions. For example, there is now growing recognition of complications related to chronic hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis, such as extramedullary hematopoiesis, thromboembolism, pulmonary hypertension, osteoporosis, and endocrine dysfunction.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Recent guidelines have recommended surveillance strategies in recognition of these cumulative risks, including for thalassemia patients who are not receiving regular transfusions.13 Furthermore, the therapeutic landscape has been quite active, with the recent approval in January 2026 of mitapivat, an oral pyruvate kinase activator, for adults with non–transfusion-dependent α- and β-thalassemia in addition to those who are transfusion dependent.14 However, lack of access to expert care remains a major barrier for many people with NTDT. Many patients may not be referred to thalassemia centers until late in the disease course, at which point irreversible complications may have already developed. This highlights the ongoing critical need to understand the real-world burden of disease among adults with NTDT, and to assess how delays in referral may affect clinical outcomes and treatment decisions, including the initiation of regular transfusion programs.

This study is, to our knowledge, the first multicenter epidemiologic analysis specifically focused on patients with NTDT in the United States who present to specialized thalassemia centers in adulthood. Here, we present a cross-sectional assessment of clinical status and laboratory findings, as well as complications and treatment gaps at the time of initial referral for specialized thalassemia care. We hypothesize that a substantial subset of this population will require escalation to regular transfusions due to a significant burden of disease at the time of presentation. This analysis aims to inform future efforts for early identification, prompt referral before the development of complications, and comprehensive management of this underrecognized population.

Methods

Study design and data collection

We performed a multicenter, cross-sectional analysis of adult patients with a diagnosis of NTDT who were referred to 1 of 3 adult thalassemia centers of excellence in the United States between 1 January 2013, and 31 December 2023. The thalassemia treatment centers included in this study were the Penn Comprehensive Adult Thalassemia Program (Philadelphia, PA), the University of California, San Francisco Comprehensive Thalassemia Center (Oakland, CA), and the Weill Cornell Medicine Comprehensive Thalassemia Center (New York, NY). Institutional review board approval was obtained at each participating site. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the noninterventional and retrospective study design. All medical record data were deidentified.

Inclusion criteria for this study included age ≥18 years at the time of referral and a diagnosis of NTDT (defined as receiving <6 red cell transfusions per year) at the time of referral. Each patient’s chart was manually reviewed by the study authors, and the diagnosis was corroborated by notes from the patient’s treating hematologist. Baseline characteristics at the time of first visit were obtained, including blood counts, hemolysis markers, ferritin, available radiologic studies for iron quantitation, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and pulmonary artery systolic pressure on transthoracic echocardiography. For each patient, a transfusion history was gathered through a review of available notes and was recorded with indicator variables for whether the patient had ever gotten a blood transfusion in their lifetime, whether the patient had received >5 units of packed red blood cells in their lifetime, and whether the patient had ever received regular transfusions previously. Indicator variables for clinical complications were also recorded, corresponding to diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension, bone mineral density loss (osteopenia or osteoporosis), diabetes, arrhythmia, venous thromboembolism, and history of splenectomy. Pulmonary hypertension was defined by diagnosis on cardiac catheterization. Arrhythmias were defined based on routine clinical documentation. Finally, a separate variable was recorded for whether the patient was ultimately recommended to initiate a regular transfusion regimen, along with the reason (or reasons) for transfusion.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables used in the analysis include sex, α- or β-thalassemia genotype, and symptoms at first visit. Indicator variables were collected for history of splenectomy, paraspinal masses, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmia, low bone mineral density, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, diabetes mellitus, thrombosis, and iron chelator use. History of ever needing a blood transfusion, history of whether the patient had received ≥5 lifetime units of packed red blood cells, and history of being on a regular transfusion regimen were also encoded with indicator variables. For prespecified categorical clinical variables, absence of documentation in the medical record was coded as absence of the feature rather than as missing data. Continuous variables included Hb (grams per deciliter), lactate dehydrogenase (units per liter), nucleated red blood cells (number per 100 white blood cells), reticulocyte count (percent), total bilirubin (milligrams per deciliter), ferritin (nanograms per milliliter), soluble transferrin receptor (milligrams per liter), liver iron concentration (LIC; milligrams per gram), cardiac T2∗ (milliseconds), and LVEF on transthoracic echocardiogram (percent). A binary variable indicated whether the patient was started on regular transfusions after establishing care with the thalassemia center. Continuous variables were compared between α- and β-thalassemia using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, except Hb, which was compared using a t test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test. For these analyses, deletional and nondeletional HbH were classified as α-thalassemia; β/β thalassemia, HbE/β-thalassemia, and heterozygous β-thalassemia were classified as β-thalassemia. Analyses were performed using Stata/BE 18.0.

Results

Study population

We identified 82 patients with NTDT referred to any 1 of 3 US comprehensive thalassemia treatment centers between 2013 and 2023. The median age at referral was 36.8 years (range, 18-74), and 66% were female. Overall, 37 (45%) had α-thalassemia and 45 (55%) had β-thalassemia, and 76 patients had available genotype data (the 6 patients without genotypes were diagnosed based on electrophoresis and review by a thalassemia center). In the α-thalassemia group, 17 had deletional HbH disease, 17 had nondeletional HbH disease, and 3 had no available genetic testing. In the β-thalassemia group, 10 had HbE/β-thalassemia, 9 patients had heterozygous β-thalassemia, 23 had β/β thalassemia (which included β0/β0, β0/β+, or β+/β+ genotypes), and 3 had no available genetic testing. Prevalence of clinical complications

Individuals of all genotypes experienced complications related to chronic hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis (Figure 1; supplemental Figure 1). Pulmonary hypertension was observed in 13% of patients with β-thalassemia, compared with none in those with α-thalassemia (P = .021). Of those with pulmonary hypertension, 5 of 6 (83%) had a history of splenectomy. Paraspinal tumors were significantly more frequent among people with β-thalassemia (27% vs 3%; P = .003). Endocrine disorders were frequent among the entire group, with low bone mineral density (osteopenia or osteoporosis) being the most common endocrine complication, affecting 34% of patients overall (33% in β-thalassemia vs 35% in α-thalassemia; P = .86). Other endocrinopathies observed at lower rates were hypogonadism (5%), hypothyroidism (9%), and diabetes (5%). A history of thromboembolism was reported in 13% of patients with β-thalassemia, compared with none of those with α-thalassemia. A history of surgical splenectomy was present in 34% of patients, and was significantly more common in patients with β-thalassemia (53%) vs those with α-thalassemia (11%; P < .001).

Morbidity, defined as the presence of paraspinal masses, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmia, low bone density, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, and/or thrombosis, was present in 54.6% of patients with Hb <10 g/dL, and 25.0% of patients with Hb ≥10 g/dL. In a modified Poisson regression adjusting for age and sex, Hb >10 g/dL was associated with a lower prevalence of morbidity, although this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted prevalence ratio, 0.44; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.17-1.14; P = .09).

Laboratory findings, iron burden, and cardiac status

Baseline Hb at referral was lower among patients with β-thalassemia (mean, 8.5 ± 1.5 g/dL) compared with those with α-thalassemia (9.3 ± 1.34 g/dL; P = .015; Table 1; Figure 2A). Serum ferritin was significantly higher in those with β-thalassemia (median, 766.5 ng/mL [interquartile range, 386-1558]) vs α-thalassemia (median, 419.7 ng/mL [interquartile range, 220-657]; P = .002; Table 1; Figure 2B). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessment of liver iron was available in 58 (71%) patients, which demonstrated LIC exceeding 5 mg Fe/g dry weight in 41 (71%; Figure 2C). Only 33 patients (40%) had available cardiac T2∗ data, with a median value of 35 milliseconds (range, 5-71; Figure 2D). Significant myocardial iron loading, as indicated by cardiac T2∗ of <20 milliseconds, was observed in 3 patients (all with β-thalassemia). Among our cohort, 56 individuals had both available baseline ferritin and LICs. After excluding outliers with ferritin of >5000 ng/mL to remove patients who likely had significant transfusion exposure despite being classified as NTDT, ferritin and LIC were strongly correlated in both α-thalassemia (Pearson correlation coefficient, 0.82) and β-thalassemia (correlation coefficient, 0.84; supplemental Figure 2). For the diagnosis of LIC of ≥5 mg/g, a ferritin threshold of 800 ng/mL had a positive predictive value of 95.8% (95% CI, 78.9-99.9) and negative predictive value (NPV) of 48.3% (95% CI, 29.4-67.5). However, at a ferritin threshold of 200 ng/mL, the positive predictive value was 73.1% (95% CI, 59.0-84.4) and NPV was 100% (95% CI, 2.5-100).

Chelation status was generally poor among individuals with evidence of iron overload. Of 28 patients with ferritin of >800 ng/mL, only 7 (25%) were on chelation at the time of initial consultation. Of 41 individuals with an LIC of >5 mg Fe/g, 10 (24.4%) were on chelation. None of the 3 individuals with cardiac T2∗ of <20 milliseconds were receiving chelation therapy.

Transition to regular transfusions

Despite a diagnosis of NTDT at the time of referral, 49% of patients (n = 40) were ultimately recommended to initiate regular transfusion therapy after evaluation at a comprehensive thalassemia center. Among patients with available genotype data (n = 76), recommendations differed markedly by major genotype group. Rates of transition to regular transfusions were highest for β/β thalassemia (78%) and HbE/β-thalassemia (80%). Overall, 56% of people with heterozygous β-thalassemia were transitioned to regular transfusions, as compared with only 12% of patients with deletional HbH and 18% of patients with nondeletional HbH. Among 37 patients with documented reasons for initiating transfusions, 19 (51%) began transfusions due to symptomatic anemia or fatigue; 2 (5.4%) due to pregnancy; 15 (40.5%) due to extramedullary hematopoiesis or ineffective erythropoiesis; 1 (3%) due to leg ulcers; and 6 (16.2%) due to pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, or cardiopulmonary symptoms. Six patients (16.2%) had >1 documented indication. Additionally, 28 of 40 patients (70%) who were ultimately recommended regular transfusion therapy had previously received ≥5 lifetime units of packed red blood cells. Of 40 patients advised to initiate a regular transfusion program, 37 ultimately did so.

Heterozygous β-thalassemia

There is growing recognition that a subset of people who are heterozygous for β-globin mutations exhibit a significant thalassemia syndrome.15 Among our cohort of patients, 9 individuals were identified who were heterozygous for β-thalassemia mutations (Table 2). Of these, 7 had coinherited copy number variants of α-globin, which led to further α:β globin imbalance and a more severe thalassemia phenotype.16 One patient was heterozygous for a β0 mutation but had a normal α-globin genotype, suggesting that his thalassemia syndrome may have other untested or uncharacterized genetic modifiers; this patient was recommended to initiate regular transfusions. The remaining patient did not have an available α-globin genotype. Discussion

In this multicenter study of patients with NTDT referred to US thalassemia centers in adulthood, we observed a substantial burden of disease and late-stage complications at the time of first evaluation at a comprehensive thalassemia center. Our findings highlight the heterogeneity in NTDT disease trajectories and suggest that the absence of regular transfusions does not equate to benign disease.

Patients referred for care exhibited a high prevalence of thalassemia complications related to anemia and chronic ineffective erythropoiesis, including pulmonary hypertension, extramedullary hematopoiesis, iron overload, endocrine dysfunction, and chronic fatigue. These complications were particularly common in those with β-thalassemia, consistent with previous reports of more severe phenotypes in this subgroup.17

Notably, nearly one-third of patients with β-thalassemia had paraspinal extramedullary hematopoiesis, an indication of chronic ineffective erythropoiesis. In modern specialty care, symptomatic extramedullary hematopoiesis is an absolute indication to initiate regular transfusion therapy, and even when asymptomatic, paraspinal extramedullary hematopoiesis should prompt closer surveillance and consideration of escalation of therapy.13,18,19 Over half of patients with β-thalassemia had undergone splenectomy, which is currently discouraged in this setting, because it predisposes to more vascular disease including pulmonary hypertension, thromboembolic disease, and silent cerebral infarcts.20, 21, 22 Indeed, in this study, all but 1 patient with pulmonary hypertension had a previous splenectomy, further supporting the association between splenectomy and pulmonary hypertension in NTDT. Importantly, 11% of patients presented with Hb <7 g/dL, which is the absolute threshold to recommend initiation of regular transfusions in those without symptoms; of course, those with anemia-related symptoms or evidence of other thalassemia-related complications are recommended to initiate transfusions even if their Hb is above this threshold.18 In fact, there is growing evidence that Hb of 10 g/dL is a clinically significant threshold to prevent morbidity in patients with NTDT.23,24 Additionally, the most common endocrine complication, low bone density, may relate in part due to endocrine dysfunction, but also likely reflects sequelae of chronic ineffective erythropoiesis, which causes marrow expansion and cortical reduction and predisposes individuals to osteopenia and osteoporosis. Development of low bone density in this context serves as another important indicator that a patient with NTDT may benefit from regular transfusions to address ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis.

Among patients with available data, 71% had hepatic iron concentrations exceeding 5 mg/g dry weight, and 35% had serum ferritin levels of >800 ng/mL. Of 3 individuals with a cardiac T2∗ of <20 milliseconds, none were on chelation at the time of referral. In addition to dysregulation of the hepcidin axis leading to increased intestinal iron absorption, patients with severe iron overload in our study probably also had substantial transfusion-related iron exposure; this degree of iron overload is therefore unlikely to be attributable to increased absorption alone. These values point to possible missed opportunities for earlier monitoring and intervention. In a divergence from current standards of care, we show that a ferritin threshold of 800 ng/mL (which has been used as a threshold for diagnosing iron overload in the absence of MRI data), had a poor NPV for LIC of >5 mg/g in our cohort, underscoring the need for earlier MRI assessments.13,25 This finding corroborates previous reports indicating that ferritin-to-LIC ratio is lower in patients with NTDT compared with those with TDT.26,27 Our results suggest that screening for hepatic iron overload with MRI scan should be considered in patients with NTDT and ferritin consistently exceeding 200 ng/mL. Although not all patients in our cohort had LIC measurements and therefore may introduce potential selection bias, these findings remain clinically informative and set the foundation for future prospective investigations.

The most striking observation in our study was that nearly half (49%) of adults with NTDT were recommended to transition to regular transfusions soon after evaluation at a thalassemia center. This was most notable in those with β-thalassemia, although not negligible in those with α-thalassemia. Documented indications for transfusion initiation included symptomatic anemia, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and cardiopulmonary complications, all of which are well described sequelae of untreated ineffective erythropoiesis and severe anemia.28 Notably, some patients had multiple indications, supporting the notion that comorbidities can accumulate over time.

Nine individuals who were referred for heterozygous for β-thalassemia or thalassemia trait had manifestations (such as anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis, and iron overload) commonly seen in NTDT. Additional genetic characterization of these individuals at the thalassemia centers identified concomitant α-globin duplication, the consequent exaggeration of the α:β globin production leading to significant ineffective erythropoiesis and the thalassemic phenotype.15 This represents a missed opportunity for diagnosis of compound genotypes causing NTDT, and appropriate monitoring and intervention. Although this cross-sectional study was not designed to elucidate the reason for late referral, less common thalassemia genotypes that require additional laboratory analysis beyond first-pass genetic testing might contribute to underrecognition and delay in diagnosis.

Our study results support the emerging evidence that NTDT is not simply a milder subgroup of thalassemia compared with TDT. In many cases, the NTDT designation may reflect historical treatment decisions more so than actual disease biology. In this US adult cohort, patients presented with a high burden of anemia as well as high prevalence of complications, leading to a high rate of subsequent transfusion initiation. This suggests that without deliberate surveillance, patients may silently cross thresholds for anemia, iron overload, and end-organ damage without prompting an appropriate change in management. Furthermore, our analysis identifies crucial gaps in care and highlights the differences between current guidance on appropriate use of transfusions and iron chelation in NTDT and real-world management of patients with NTDT before they get to a thalassemia specialty center. The suboptimal adherence to modern-day guidelines may reflect a combination of improper diagnosis (for instance, mistaking a clinically significant thalassemia syndrome as thalassemia trait) and a lack of provider awareness of current transfusion and chelation guidelines in the thalassemia population. Together, these results argue for earlier referral to comprehensive thalassemia centers for longitudinal monitoring and timely recommendations regarding transfusion management.

This study has several strengths despite its retrospective design. It is the largest multicenter cohort to focus specifically on people with NTDT establishing thalassemia care in adulthood in the United States and captures a contemporary and geographically diverse population over a 10-year period. All diagnoses were manually validated, and chart abstraction was performed by trained hematologists with domain expertise. The cross-sectional design provides a real-world snapshot of disease burden at the critical juncture of specialty referral, a time point that has historically not been well characterized in previous literature. However, there are several important limitations. First, this retrospective and cross-sectional study is not designed to assess causal relationships. Second, reasons for delayed referral were not uniformly available, and selection bias may exist in those patients referred to tertiary centers. Third, because many patients were referred from outside institutions, not everybody was consistently screened for paraspinal masses, pulmonary hypertension, or arrhythmias unless they presented with relevant symptoms. In addition, for categorical clinical variables, absence of explicit chart documentation was coded as absence of the condition. This approach may have led to underestimation of the true prevalence of complications, particularly for asymptomatic conditions that were not systematically screened for, and introduces the potential for both misclassification and ascertainment bias. For instance, arrhythmias are common in thalassemia, and may be underestimated in this study because of heterogeneous screening practices.29 Prospective studies using standardized definitions will be important to better define the true prevalence of these complications in this population. Finally, the decision to initiate transfusions may have been influenced by patient preferences, which we could not fully standardize or capture.

In conclusion, adults with NTDT referred to comprehensive US. thalassemia centers frequently present with high rates of complications that warrant escalation to chronic transfusion therapy. These findings challenge the traditional binary classification of thalassemia as TDT vs NTDT, and emphasize that these categories are shaped not only by disease biology but also by clinical practice and may change over time. Disease monitoring and management should take a dynamic approach that accounts for the potential for disease evolution.30 Prospective studies are needed to better define optimal surveillance intervals and evaluate interventions aimed at mitigating or preventing long-term complications in this underrecognized population. Furthermore, initiatives to improve the dissemination and implementation of thalassemia guidelines are needed, such as improved provider education on transfusion and iron chelation practices and clearer referral criteria for thalassemia specialty centers. Because outcomes for patients with NTDT can be excellent when management is individualized, greater use of established resources and networks, such as the Cooley’s Anemia Foundation (https://thalassemia.org) and the Thalassemia International Federation (https://thalassaemia.org.cy), may help support provider education, facilitate referrals to specialty centers, and improve access to expert care.
