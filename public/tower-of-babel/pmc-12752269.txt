An oral engineered cerium-peptide composite microsphere inhibits inflammatory bowel disease and reverses chronic fibrosis​​
Authors: Wenyu Qiao, Luling Yang, Yu Liu, Huaiyuan Zhang, Zhihuan Zheng, Tianheng Gu, Jiaxi Zhu, Xinyi Hu, Gangze Li, Yuxia Zhang, Meng Xiao, Lin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and dysbiosis of the gut microbiota are pivotal contributors to the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and associated fibrosis. In this study, a microfluidic approach was utilized to fabricate a multifunctional therapeutic microsphere system by encapsulating hollow mesoporous cerium oxide nanoparticles (HCeO2) loaded with KP1 short peptides-specific inhibitors of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. The resulting composite formulation (KP1@HCeO₂@SAM) was designed to achieve synergistic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects. The developed microspheres exhibited prolonged intestinal retention and effectively modulated gut microbiota composition, increasing the relative abundance of probiotic species by over sevenfold. Moreover, they demonstrated remarkable reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging efficiency (> 95%) and significant inhibition of fibrotic signaling cascades. In dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced murine colitis models, treatment with the microspheres led to a substantial reduction in Disease Activity Index (DAI) (> 76%), restoration of intestinal barrier integrity (> 90%), and mitigation of fibrotic progression, as indicated by a 66% decrease in α-SMA expression. These findings establish a novel and integrative therapeutic platform for the effective management of IBD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03859-0.

Introduction

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic and recurrent inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, with lesions that may affect the entire digestive tract from the mouth to the anus [1–3]. In recent years, therapeutic paradigms for IBD have progressively evolved from conventional anti-inflammatory regimens—such as aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants—toward precision-based biologics and small-molecule therapeutics. Representative examples include anti–tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) antibodies (e.g., infliximab), anti-integrin agents (e.g., vedolizumab), and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib) [1, 4]. Although these agents effectively attenuate inflammation and alleviate clinical manifestations, they remain limited by heterogeneous patient responses, frequent disease relapse, and insufficient modulation of gut microbial dysbiosis [2]. Furthermore, the persistent inflammatory milieu characteristic of IBD promotes excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn activates profibrotic signaling cascades such as the TGF-β/Smad pathway [5]. This activation drives aberrant fibroblast proliferation and activation, leading to excessive extracellular matrix deposition, intestinal wall fibrosis, luminal narrowing, and functional impairment [6]. Consequently, there is an increasing emphasis on the development of multifunctional therapeutic strategies capable of concurrently mitigating oxidative stress, suppressing inflammation, inhibiting fibrotic progression, and re-establishing gut microbiota homeostasis, representing a key frontier in contemporary IBD research [7].

In active inflammatory lesions of IBD, infiltrating immune cells, especially macrophages and neutrophils, produce excessive ROS through the NADPH oxidase/myeloperoxidase pathways [8]– [9]. This oxidative stress damages epithelial tight junction proteins (e.g., ZO-1, occludin), compromises intestinal barrier integrity, and maintains a proinflammatory cytokine environment that accelerates tissue injury. Due to the complex and progressive nature of IBD, therapies targeting a single factor (gene modulation or selective cell depletion) have limited success [10, 11]. Instead, a comprehensive approach that restores immune balance, reduces oxidative damage, and reestablishes microbiota homeostasis is essential for effective disease management. Recent advances in tissue engineering and molecular biotechnology have increased interest in cerium oxide (CeO2) nanozymes as promising options for IBD treatment [12–14]. These nanomaterials show remarkable antioxidant potential due to their reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling, which imparts superoxide dismutase (SOD)-, catalase (CAT)-, and peroxidase (POD)-like functions [15–17]. This multifunctionality allows for effective ROS removal in inflamed intestinal tissues. However, traditional CeO2 nanoparticles are limited by poor gastrointestinal stability, reduced retention time, and susceptibility to aggregation/deactivation in acidic environments [18]. Moreover, their pure antioxidative capacity is insufficient to address the complex interaction between oxidative stress, microbial imbalance (e.g., pathogen enrichment), and late-stage complications (intestinal fibrosis/dysplasia) [19–21]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a multifunctional, biocompatible platform capable of modulating oxidative stress, repairing the epithelial barrier, and rebalancing gut microbiota to effectively treat IBD and its associated fibrosis.

Drawing inspiration from mesoporous silica–based drug delivery systems, we designed hollow mesoporous cerium oxide nanospheres (HCeO2) as versatile carriers for therapeutic peptides [22–24]. The hollow architecture and well-defined mesoporous framework of HCeO2 significantly increase the exposure of catalytically active sites, improving its intrinsic antioxidant capacity compared with bulk CeO2. Similarly, the high surface area and interconnected porosity enable efficient encapsulation of the short therapeutic peptide KP1 [25, 26]. KP1 exhibits strong affinity for transforming growth factor-β receptors (TGFβR), where it effectively interrupts downstream Smad signaling, thus suppressing fibrogenic activation and mitigating intestinal fibrosis [27, 28]. Owing to its low molecular weight (~ 2 kDa) and minimal immunogenic potential, KP1 demonstrates favorable biocompatibility and pharmacological safety, while its nanoscale encapsulation within HCeO2 facilitates stable loading, sustained release, and targeted therapeutic action [29]. In IBD, besides modulating local immune responses, restoring intestinal microbial balance is crucial. Microbial dysbiosis not only drives disease progression but also is a key hub mediating the vicious cycle of “inflammation fibrosis carcinogenesis” [30–32]. Conventional probiotic treatments remain less effective due to poor gastric stability and low efficiency in colonizing the gut. To address these limitations, sodium alginate hydrogel microspheres (SAM) were developed using microfluidic technology and sodium alginate (SA), an FDA-approved, food-grade biopolymer [25, 33, 34]. These hydrogels form a well-defined three-dimensional crosslinked network with high encapsulation capacity. Due to their pH-responsive swelling behavior and intrinsic mucoadhesive properties, the microspheres promote targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to inflamed intestinal sites and prolong gastrointestinal retention [33, 35]. Furthermore, SAM shows prebiotic activity by selectively promoting the growth of commensal bacterial genera, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, while simultaneously inhibiting pathogenic taxa such as Escherichia-Shigella, thus aiding comprehensive microbiota remodeling [19, 36].

Based on the aforementioned design rationale, a multifunctional oral delivery system was constructed by encapsulating KP1-loaded hollow mesoporous cerium oxide nanoparticles (KP1@HCeO₂) within a self-assembled polysaccharide matrix (SAM), resulting in the formation of the composite microsystem KP1@HCeO₂@SAM (Fig. 1A). In contrast to conventional single-function antioxidant nanoplatforms or absorption enhancer–dependent oral peptide systems (e.g., SNAC-based semaglutide formulations) [37, 38], this composite system was designed to integrate multiple synergistic therapeutic mechanisms within a single platform. The SAM layer was employed to provide gastric protection, enable colon-specific degradation, prolong intestinal residence, and modulate the gut microbiota composition. The HCeO2 core was utilized to alleviate oxidative stress through potent ROS scavenging, mitigating the inflammatory microenvironment. Simultaneously, the encapsulated KP1 peptide was released to exert antifibrotic effects via selective inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. Through this multi-module design, comprehensive therapeutic regulation, from inflammation suppression and barrier restoration to immune modulation, microbiota homeostasis, and fibrosis reversal, was achieved, overcoming the inherent limitations of monofunctional therapies. The therapeutic performance of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM was systematically evaluated in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mouse model, demonstrating significant efficacy across all assessed parameters (Fig. 1B). KP1@HCeO₂@SAM was validated as a safe, mechanistically versatile, and promising oral formulation for integrated treatment of IBD.

Results

Characterization of KP1@HCeO2@SAM

Mesoporous hollow cerium oxide nanoparticles (HCeO2) were synthesized through a template-free, one-pot hydrothermal process. During the solvothermal reaction, Ce³⁺ ions were oxidized to CeO2, while acetic acid underwent in situ esterification with ethylene glycol, subsequently adsorbing onto the nanoparticle surfaces to regulate crystal growth. The self-assembly of primary CeO2 nanocrystals led to the formation of compact, energetically stable spherical architectures. The short therapeutic peptide KP1, a specific inhibitor of the TGF-β/Smad signaling axis, was subsequently incorporated into the mesoporous framework to obtain KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres through uniform dispersion. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that both HCeO2 and KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres exhibited homogeneous spherical morphology with narrow size distribution (Fig. 2A). Elemental mapping further confirmed the uniform spatial distribution of C, O, Ce, and N in KP1@HCeO₂, in contrast to the Ce and O signals detected exclusively in pristine HCeO2 (Fig. 2B, S1). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) corroborated these findings, verifying successful peptide loading through the emergence of C and N peaks in KP1@HCeO₂ (Figure S2, S3). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images displayed well-defined lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.24 nm, characteristic of CeO2 crystallites (Fig. 2C). The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern exhibited concentric diffraction rings corresponding to multiple crystallographic planes, indicating a polycrystalline structure with randomly oriented nanocrystals (Fig. 2D). The absence of distinct diffraction spots confirmed uniform nanocrystal dispersion, while the clear and continuous rings reflected excellent crystallinity and small grain size, features that contribute to the increased surface reactivity and catalytic potential of HCeO2.

Sodium alginate hydrogel (SAH) was utilized as the matrix to fabricate microspherical carriers (SAM) through a controlled microfluidic emulsification technique. During this process, KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres were uniformly dispersed within the aqueous phase and subsequently encapsulated within the alginate network, resulting in the formation of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composite microspheres. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the obtained microspheres possessed uniform size distribution, smooth surface morphology, and interconnected porous structures (Fig. 2E). The presence of a fibrous internal framework was particularly advantageous for increasing drug-loading capacity and facilitating sustained release. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the coexistence of C, O, Ce, and N elements in the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composites (Fig. 2F), while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further validated these elemental constituents and verified successful composite formation (Fig. 2G, S4). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the hydrodynamic diameters of HCeO2 and KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres ranged from 440 to 520 nm, with an average value of approximately 480 nm, consistent with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results (Fig. 2H). The microfluidic emulsification process yielded highly monodisperse KP1@HCeO₂@SAM microspheres with an average diameter of 255 μm (225–285 μm), corroborating SEM and elemental mapping observations (Fig. 2I). Further structural verification was performed using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2J). In the SAM spectrum, the characteristic peaks at 3291 cm⁻¹, 1742 cm⁻¹, and 1608 cm⁻¹ corresponded to the stretching vibrations of –OH, C = O, and C = C groups, respectively. For HCeO2 nanospheres, a broad absorption band at 3435 cm⁻¹ was attributed to –OH stretching, whereas the bands at 781 cm⁻¹ and 495 cm⁻¹ were associated with Ce–O–Ce and Ce–O vibrations, respectively. The HCeO2@SAM composite exhibited typical C = O stretching at 1722 cm⁻¹ and preserved the characteristic Ce–O–Ce and Ce–O peaks at 792 cm⁻¹ and 492 cm⁻¹. The KP1 peptide displayed distinct absorption bands at 1650 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), 3281 cm⁻¹ (N–H₂ stretching), and 3435 cm⁻¹ (–OH stretching). After incorporation, the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composite exhibited characteristic peaks at 3261 cm⁻¹ (N–H₂ stretching), 2982/2875 cm⁻¹ (CH₂/CH₃ stretching), and 1725 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), along with Ce–O–Ce and Ce–O vibrations at 788 and 492 cm⁻¹, respectively. The coexistence and preservation of these signature peaks collectively verified the successful encapsulation of KP1@HCeO₂ within the SAM hydrogel network and the stable formation of the composite microspheres.

Sodium alginate–based hydrogels are well recognized for their ability to prolong gastrointestinal residence through mucoadhesive and viscosity-enhancing effects [33, 34]. Consistent with these findings, the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composite microspheres exhibited significantly extended colonic retention following oral administration, compared with the control group, facilitating sustained drug release and a prolonged therapeutic window (Figure S5). To evaluate in vivo biodistribution, mice were orally administered either free KP1 or KP1@HCeO₂@SAM, and plasma as well as colonic tissue samples were collected at defined intervals for quantitative ELISA analysis. The KP1@HCeO₂@SAM group demonstrated substantially higher KP1 concentrations within colon tissue and a prolonged retention profile relative to free KP1. The calculated area under the concentration–time curve (AUC₀–₂₄h) in colonic tissue was approximately 4.89-fold greater for the microsphere formulation. However, plasma AUC₀–₂₄h values remained minimal for both groups, indicating negligible systemic exposure and confirming the localized delivery advantage of the composite system (Figure S6). These results highlight that KP1@HCeO₂@SAM achieves a favorable pharmacokinetic distribution, characterized by increased local bioavailability and minimized systemic absorption. The release behavior of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM was further examined in simulated gastrointestinal fluids to mimic physiological transit. In simulated gastric conditions (0–2 h), cumulative KP1 release remained below 4%, indicating excellent structural stability in acidic environments. During the simulated intestinal phase (2–6 h), a gradual release profile was observed (cumulative release < 20%). Upon transition to simulated colonic conditions (6–72 h), a sharp increase in release occurred, reaching approximately 95% cumulatively (Fig. 2K). Optical microscopy corroborated these findings: SAM microspheres maintained structural integrity in artificial gastric fluid (AGF) and artificial small intestinal fluid (ASF) but underwent rapid disintegration within 10 min in artificial colonic fluid (ACF) (Figure S7). This selective degradation behavior underscores the functional mechanism of the SAM matrix, acting as a protective barrier throughout the upper gastrointestinal tract while undergoing targeted breakdown in the colon, enabling spatially and temporally controlled release of KP1 for site-specific therapeutic action.

In vitro biocompatibility and ROS scavenging

Biocompatibility is a critical determinant for the translational potential of nanotherapeutic systems. Calcein-AM/PI dual-fluorescence staining revealed that HCeO2 nanoparticles, at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg/mL, exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward NCM460 human colonic epithelial cells. (Fig. 3A). The CCK-8 assay for cell viability revealed that HCeO2 nanoparticles (at 0–100 µg/mL concentration), as well as the KP1@HCeO2@SAM complex and its component materials, demonstrated similarly positive biocompatibility profiles. (Fig. 3B, S8). The hemocompatibility of the synthesized materials was evaluated through a standard red blood cell (RBC) hemolysis assay, employing deionized water and physiological saline as positive and negative controls, respectively. As illustrated in Figure S9, none of the tested formulations, SAM, HCeO2@SAM, KP1@SAM, or KP1@HCeO₂@SAM, induced discernible hemolytic activity following incubation with RBCs. The hemolysis percentages for all samples consistently remained below the accepted threshold of 5%, demonstrating outstanding blood compatibility. These findings confirm that the integration of HCeO2 nanoparticles and the KP1 peptide did not adversely affect the hemocompatibility of the composite hydrogel system. Under oxidative stress induced by 3 mM H2O2, HCeO2 significantly enhanced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating its protective antioxidant activity (Fig. 3A). To further explore the ROS-scavenging mechanism, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe in a 1 mM H2O2-induced oxidative stress model. The H2O2-treated group showed significantly higher green fluorescence than the untreated control, indicating excessive ROS accumulation. A progressive reduction in fluorescence intensity was observed with increasing HCeO2 concentrations (0–100 µg/mL) (Fig. 3C and D). Considering the crucial role of mitochondria in ROS production and oxidative damage, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was examined using the JC-1 probe. Cells in the control group mainly showed red fluorescence due to JC-1 polymer aggregation under normal ΔΨm conditions, with minimal green fluorescence from the monomeric form. Exposure to H2O2 caused a significant shift toward green fluorescence, along with a substantial loss of red fluorescence, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. Treatment with HCeO2 reversed this change in a concentration-dependent manner, with the 100 µg/mL group restoring red fluorescence to levels similar to the control and substantially reducing green fluorescence (Fig. 3E). Quantitative analysis confirmed a ΔΨm recovery of 63.12% (Fig. 3F and G). These results underscore the cytoprotective efficacy of HCeO2, highlighting its ability to (i) maintain cell viability, (ii) clear intracellular ROS, and (iii) stabilize mitochondrial function. These properties form a mechanistic basis supporting the anti-inflammatory potential of the material.

Reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment

Excessive ROS production in colonic inflammation can induce macrophage polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, thus amplifying the local inflammatory cascade [39–41]. The intracellular ROS scavenging efficacy of the HCeO2 nanozyme was quantitatively evaluated by the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe in combination with flow cytometry. In macrophage populations, a significant elevation in intracellular fluorescence intensity was observed after lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, relative to the unstimulated control group, indicative of substantial ROS accumulation. No significant changes in fluorescence intensity were observed in cells treated with SAM or KP1@SAM. However, a significant reduction in fluorescence was detected in both the HCeO2@SAM and KP1@HCeO2@SAM groups. The KP1@HCeO2@SAM showed a significant decrease in fluorescence signal, denoting strong intracellular ROS scavenging potential (Fig. 4A). These results revealed the improved antioxidative capability of HCeO2-based nanoplatforms, with the KP1@HCeO2@SAM composite showing the highest efficacy. Based on the observed ROS-scavenging potential of KP1@HCeO2@SAM, it was hypothesized that this nanocomposite could modulate the inflammatory immune microenvironment by promoting macrophage phenotype switching. To test this hypothesis, macrophages were pretreated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce an early pro-inflammatory state, followed by treatment with various hydrogel formulations or PBS as a control. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that LPS stimulation increased CD86 (M1 marker) expression while suppressing CD206 (M2 marker) levels, indicating polarization towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Both HCeO2@SAM and KP1@HCeO2@SAM significantly reversed LPS-induced M1 polarization. The KP1@HCeO2@SAM group showed the most prominent effect, with a substantial increase in CD206 fluorescence (green) intensity and almost complete reduction of CD86 signal (red) (Fig. 4B and D). Cytoskeletal staining confirmed these results, with LPS-treated macrophages displaying M1-like characteristics (flattened shape and prominent pseudopodia). Simultaneously, cells treated with HCeO2@SAM and KP1@HCeO2@SAM were observed with a typical M2 phenotype, with fewer pseudopodial extensions (Figure S10). Flow cytometry further supported these results by quantifying a decreased number of CD86⁺ cells and an increased number of CD206⁺ cells in the KP1@HCeO2@SAM group compared to LPS controls (Fig. 4E and H). These results demonstrate that KP1@HCeO2@SAM effectively reprograms macrophage polarization from the M1 state to the M2 state through ROS scavenging, thus establishing an anti-inflammatory immune environment that promotes early intestinal recovery.

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms by which KP1@HCeO2@SAM promotes macrophage polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, comprehensive whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed. Differential gene expression analysis (|log2FC| >1, p-value < 0.05) identified 1,491 significantly upregulated and 1,644 downregulated genes in M2-polarized macrophages (Fig. 4I and J). Gene Ontology Biological Process (BP) enrichment revealed a predominant association with anti-inflammation, inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway and negatively regulating ROS metabolism (Fig. 4K). KEGG pathway enrichment further indicated suppression of pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and TNF signaling, alongside specific activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway in LPS-stimulated macrophages treated with KP1@HCeO2@SAM (Fig. 4L). These findings were consistent with Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), which showed significant inhibition of ROS-related NF-κB signaling and upregulation of the classical M2 polarization-associated PI3K-AKT pathway [42, 43], aligning closely with KEGG results (Fig. 4M and N). Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis further validated the regulatory shifts observed in transcriptomic data (Figure S11). Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed that KP1@HCeO2@SAM treatment significantly reduced the expression of M1-associated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), while enhancing the expression of M2-related anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4 and IL-10) (Fig. 4O). To further delineate the regulatory mechanisms governing macrophage polarization, the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway was selectively inhibited using LY294002. Importantly, even under PI3K–AKT blockade, macrophages exhibited residual M2-like polarization, indicating the contribution of an additional modulatory pathway. This remaining polarization is plausibly attributed to the intrinsic ROS-scavenging capacity of the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composite, which mitigates oxidative stress and consequently suppresses activation of the pro-inflammatory NF-κB pathway (Figure S12). These findings indicate that KP1@HCeO₂@SAM orchestrates macrophage phenotypic reprogramming through a synergistic dual mechanism, by concurrently inhibiting NF-κB–mediated inflammatory responses and stimulating PI3K–AKT–dependent reparative signaling (Fig. 4P).

KP1@HCeO2@SAM therapy for acute phase of DSS-induced IBD

Based on the previously validated in vitro antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and biocompatibility properties of KP1@HCeO2@SAM, this study further examined its therapeutic efficacy in an acute IBD model. Colitis was induced in C57BL/6J mice by administering 3% DSS for 6 days, followed by oral treatments on days 7, 9, 11, and 13, with the terminating point on day 15 (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that the Saline-treated mice showed significant body weight loss compared to the Control group (Fig. 5B). However, KP1@HCeO2@SAM treatment reduced this weight loss starting on the first day of treatment (day 7), showing greater efficacy than the clinical reference drug (5-ASA). Analysis of fecal blood symptoms indicated severe hematochezia in the Saline and SAM groups. Partial alleviation was observed in the 5-ASA, HCeO2@SAM, and KP1@SAM groups; however, KP1@HCeO2@SAM completely resolved fecal bleeding by day 15 (Fig. 5C). Disease Activity Index (DAI) was recorded throughout the experiment (Figure S13). KP1@HCeO2@SAM reduced the DAI score in DSS-treated mice (p < 0.01), showing greater efficacy than 5-ASA (p < 0.05), restoring DAI values close to baseline. Morphologically, the Saline group had shortened colons (4.21 cm vs. 7.52 cm in Control), while the HCeO2@SAM (6.38 cm) and KP1@HCeO2@SAM (7.47 cm) groups maintained colon length better than 5-ASA (5.94 cm) (Fig. 5D and E). This effect is likely due to ROS scavenging by HCeO2 and mucosal retention by SAM. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining confirmed minimal colon damage in the KP1@HCeO2@SAM group, with intact goblet cell structure, regular crypt morphology, and an unbroken mucosal layer (Fig. 5F and G). Furthermore, analysis of inflammatory markers showed that MPO activity, along with levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the colon, were significantly lower in the KP1@HCeO2@SAM-treated group compared to the Saline group, approaching levels observed in healthy controls (Fig. 6A and E). Immunofluorescence analysis of macrophage polarization in colon tissue revealed extensive infiltration of CD86⁺ (M1) macrophages in the lamina propria and submucosa of the Saline group. However, KP1@HCeO2@SAM treatment decreased the number and fluorescence intensity of CD86⁺ cells, while significantly increasing CD206⁺ (M2) macrophages, indicating a shift toward an anti-inflammatory and tissue-repairing phenotype (Fig. 6F, S14). Immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses of intestinal tight junction proteins showed a significant reduction in ZO-1 and occludin expression in the Saline group, reflecting severe intestinal barrier disruption. KP1@HCeO2@SAM treatment effectively restored the expression of these proteins to near-normal levels (Fig. 5H and M). This multi-level evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of KP1@HCeO2@SAM in acute colitis, mediated through synergistic anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and barrier-protective mechanisms.

KP1@HCeO2@SAM improves gut microbiota

To elucidate the regulatory effects of KP1@HCeO2@SAM on intestinal microbial composition, fecal samples from all experimental groups were analyzed through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Alpha diversity analysis with Chao1 and Shannon indices showed that mice with DSS-induced colitis significantly reduced microbial richness and diversity compared to the untreated Control and the KP1@HCeO2@SAM-treated groups (Fig. 7A). Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) revealed a clear separation in microbial community structure between the DSS and Control groups, while the microbial profiles of the KP1@HCeO2@SAM-treated mice closely resembled those of healthy controls, suggesting a significant reduction of dysbiosis (Fig. 7B). Venn diagram analysis of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) showed more overlap between the KP1@HCeO2@SAM and Control groups than between the KP1@HCeO2@SAM and DSS groups (Fig. 7C). Linear discriminant analysis coupled with effect size estimation (LEfSe) revealed several bacterial taxa that were significantly enriched in the DSS-treated group, each exhibiting an LDA score greater than 4, indicative of a predominance of pathogenic microbial populations. (Fig. 7D). At the phylum level, the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio in the KP1@HCeO2@SAM-treated group was restored to levels similar to those of the Control group (Fig. 7E). Genus-level heatmap (Fig. 7F) analysis further revealed a significant enrichment of beneficial microbial taxa, i.e., Lactobacillus (Fig. 7H), Bifidobacterium (Fig. 7I), and Akkermansia (Fig. 7J), all of which are associated with intestinal epithelial regeneration, exhibited a 7.0-fold, 11.6-fold, and 2.3-fold increase in relative abundance, respectively. In comparison, the relative abundance of pro-inflammatory and IBD-associated pathogens, such as Escherichia-Shigella (Fig. 7G), was significantly decreased after treatment, showing a 153.9-fold reduction. These results suggest that KP1@HCeO2@SAM effectively reduces microbiota dysregulation associated with colitis by reshaping the gut microbial ecosystem, promoting probiotic colonization, and suppressing pathogenic burden. This microbial rebalancing establishes a gut environment conducive to mucosal healing and reduction of intestinal fibrosis.

Oral KP1@HCeO2@SAM reverses intestinal fibrosis

Intestinal fibrosis, an important complication of IBD, is mainly characterized by thickened intestinal walls, weakened peristalsis, and intestinal lumen stenosis. Considering the therapeutic potential of the KP1@HCeO2@SAM in suppressing IBD by attenuating ROS and modulating dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota, a chronic IBD model was developed using a gradient DSS induction protocol [44]. On day 35, after euthanasia, pathological evaluations were conducted (Fig. 8A). The KP1@HCeO2@SAM treatment group showed a significant reduction in colonic MPO activity and reduced levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, relative to the saline-treated group, with values approaching those observed in the control group (Figure S15, S16). Persistent elevation of TNF-α and IL-1β is known to directly activate the TGF-β signaling cascade, a key pathway in fibrogenesis [45, 46]. Consistent with this, histopathological analysis revealed that the KP1@HCeO2@SAM group showed the mildest IBD-associated lesions, the most effective reduction of weight loss (Fig. 8B), and restoration of colon length and thickness to near-normal levels (Fig. 8C and D). H&E staining analysis of colon tissue damage revealed massive inflammatory cell infiltration in the Saline group and significantly increased muscularis propria, muscularis mucosae, and overall intestinal wall thickness (Fig. 8E and F). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed extensive and densely packed collagen accumulation, with significant submucosal thickening, disruption of mucosal architecture, and persistent inflammatory cell infiltration in the Saline-treated group (Fig. 8G and I, S17). Immunohistochemical analysis of key fibrotic markers, including alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen type I (Collagen I), revealed numerous activated myofibroblasts within the submucosa and surrounding the intestinal crypts, indicating active fibrogenic activity (Fig. 8G, S18). Their distribution areas highly overlapped with regions of severe Collagen I deposition, suggesting that abnormal proliferation and migration of myofibroblasts are the core mechanisms driving colonic fibrosis, pathological collagen matrix accumulation in the submucosa, and intestinal wall structural remodeling. Although KP1@SAM showed moderate efficacy in attenuating intestinal fibrosis, it did not significantly improve inflammatory pathology. However, oral administration of the KP1@HCeO2@SAM, with its combined multi-component functions, effectively reduced ROS-induced intestinal injury and suppressed fibrotic progression. Western blot quantification revealed a pronounced suppression of phosphorylated Smad2/3 expression in the fibrotic colonic tissues of colitis mice following treatment with the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM formulation (Fig. 8J and L, S19). This molecular evidence substantiates the efficient engagement of the KP1 peptide with its target, leading to direct inhibition of the canonical TGF-β/Smad fibrotic signaling cascade. Importantly, this antifibrotic action was maintained independently of anti-inflammatory processes, signifying that KP1@HCeO₂@SAM effectively impedes fibroblast activation and differentiation even within a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. In parallel, the anti-inflammatory capacity of the composite system contributes to fibrosis resolution by reducing the production of profibrogenic mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β, disrupting the persistent inflammatory stimuli that sustain fibroblast activation. These findings delineate a dual-modality therapeutic paradigm: HCeO2 acts to reestablish an anti-inflammatory milieu, while KP1 exerts a direct inhibitory effect on the fibrotic machinery. The cooperative interplay of these mechanisms culminates in a potent reversal of established fibrosis, underscoring the superior therapeutic potential of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM compared with anti-inflammatory monotherapy.

To further elucidate the mechanistic role of KP1-mediated antifibrotic activity, an in vitro fibrosis model was developed using NIH-3T3 fibroblasts, leveraging the inhibitory effect of KP1 on the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 9B). Scratch wound assay analysis showed that TGF-β1 stimulation enhanced fibroblast migration, with near-complete wound closure at 24 h (Fig. 9A and C). Moreover, the wound closure rate of the KP1@HCeO2@SAM group was not statistically significant compared with the untreated control group, indicating effective inhibition of TGF-β-mediated fibrotic activity. Overall, these in vivo and in vitro results suggest that KP1@HCeO2@SAM effectively blocks TGF-β-driven fibroblast activation, migration, and extracellular matrix deposition, providing multiple anti-fibrotic effects in intestinal fibrosis.

In vivo biocompatibility

To evaluate the systemic biosafety of the developed formulations, C57BL/6J mice were orally administered SAM, HCeO2@SAM, KP1@SAM, or KP1@HCeO₂@SAM once daily for 20 consecutive days. Subsequent histopathological examination of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no observable tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, or morphological abnormalities, indicating the absence of significant organ toxicity associated with KP1@HCeO₂@SAM or its individual components (Figure S20). Furthermore, comprehensive hematological and serum biochemical analyses performed on day 20 showed no statistically significant deviations in white blood cell (WBC) and RBC counts, nor in hepatic (ALT, AST) or renal (CREA, BUN) function indicators compared with the control group (Figure S21). These findings collectively confirm that the KP1@HCeO₂@SAM composite exhibits excellent in vivo biocompatibility and systemic safety, supporting its suitability for oral therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

The KP1@HCeO2@SAM composite microsphere shows a dual-stage therapeutic strategy for IBD by effectively suppressing acute inflammation and reversing chronic fibrosis. During the acute inflammatory phase, HCeO2 exerts potent antioxidant activity by suppressing excessive ROS production, promoting macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, thus restoring intestinal barrier integrity. In the chronic fibrotic phase, the sustained release of the KP1 short peptide specifically inhibits the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, effectively suppressing myofibroblast activation and collagen deposition, thus alleviating intestinal fibrosis. Despite the promising therapeutic efficacy of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM demonstrated in preclinical studies, several critical factors must be addressed before successful clinical translation can be achieved. From a manufacturing perspective, while the microfluidic encapsulation of SAM enables precise control over particle size and uniformity, challenges remain in scaling up production. Improving process throughput, equipment scalability, and overall cost-efficiency will be essential to meet industrial and regulatory manufacturing requirements. Further, ensuring long-term formulation stability represents another key consideration. The sustained catalytic activity of HCeO2, prevention of KP1 peptide degradation, and preservation of microsphere integrity during storage are vital for maintaining consistent therapeutic performance. Future research should therefore prioritize formulation refinement and the establishment of optimized lyophilization protocols to improve storage stability. Moreover, although KP1 is a short peptide sequence derived from endogenous human proteins and is expected to exhibit low immunogenicity, comprehensive safety evaluations, including immunogenicity and chronic toxicity studies in higher-order animal models such as non-human primates, will be required to confirm its long-term biosafety profile and translational potential.

Load efficiency of KP1

(1) Preparation of HCeO2 suspension: Fifty milligrams of hollow cerium oxide (HCeO2) nanoparticles were dispersed in 25 mL of enzyme-free deionized water. The dispersion was subjected to ultrasonication followed by magnetic stirring for 30 min to ensure homogeneous dispersion, resulting in a stable HCeO2 suspension.

(2) Loading of KP1 peptide:Fifty milligrams of KP1 peptide were added directly to the prepared HCeO2 suspension. The mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 12 h to facilitate adsorption and encapsulation of the peptide within the mesoporous HCeO2 structure, forming the KP1@HCeO₂ complex.

Preparation of HCeO2 and KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres

Hollow mesoporous cerium oxide (HCeO2) nanospheres were synthesized via a one-step, template-free hydrothermal process. In a typical procedure, cerium(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO₃)₃·6 H₂O, 1 g) was dissolved in a degassed solvent mixture of deionized water and ethylene glycol under probe ultrasonication (300 W, 15 min). Hydrolysis was initiated by the addition of glacial acetic acid (1 mL) as a catalyst, while polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, 0.8 g) served as a steric stabilizer and structure-directing agent. The resulting homogeneous solution was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 20 h to promote autogenous crystallization and hollow sphere formation.

After cooling to room temperature, the colloidal suspension was centrifuged (8,000 rpm, 10 min) and sequentially washed with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted ions and residual organics. The purified product was vacuum-dried at 60 °C to yield uniform HCeO2 nanospheres.

For peptide loading, the KP1 short peptide was incorporated into HCeO2 through electrostatically driven self-assembly. Briefly, HCeO2 nanospheres were dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with KP1 solution under gentle rotation at 25 °C for 24 h. The resulting KP1@HCeO₂ conjugates were isolated by centrifugation (8,000 rpm, 4 min, 4 °C), washed twice with PBS to remove unbound peptide, and stored at 4 °C as stable colloidal pellets.

Preparation of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM microspheres

To preserve the bioactivity of KP1 during oral administration, KP1@HCeO₂ nanospheres were encapsulated within sodium alginate methacrylate (AlgMA) hydrogels using a hydrodynamic microfluidic technique (EFL, China). Two syringe pumps were used to introduce the aqueous and oil phases into the microfluidic chip.

The aqueous phase consisted of 5 wt% AlgMA, 2.5 wt% KP1@HCeO₂ dispersed in PBS, and 0.5 wt% lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) as the photoinitiator. The oil phase contained 5 wt% Span 80 in mineral oil, serving as the continuous phase to stabilize droplet formation. Monodisperse emulsion droplets were generated within the microchannels and immediately crosslinked under UV irradiation to form solid microspheres.

The resulting KP1@HCeO₂@SAM microspheres were washed thoroughly with PBS to remove oil residues and surfactant, followed by centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) to collect the purified microcarriers.

Characterization of KP1@HCeO₂@SAM microspheres

The morphology and surface topography of the synthesized materials were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S4800). Nanostructural organization and nanoparticle dispersion were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X). Elemental distribution was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) equipped with an AZtecWave system.

Surface chemical composition and oxidation states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific NEXSA). Functional groups and chemical bonding were characterized through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Nicolet NEXUS 870) across the 400–4000 cm⁻¹ spectral range. Pore structure and distribution of the composite microspheres were evaluated using mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP, V9620, USA).

In vitro release of KP1 peptide

For the release study, KP1-loaded microspheres (100 mg each) were accurately weighed and placed in separate test tubes.

Stage I (Gastric Simulation): Samples were immersed in 1 mL of artificial gastric fluid (pH 1.0) and incubated in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 2 h.

Stage II (Intestinal Simulation): After 2 h, samples were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 5 min), and the supernatant was discarded. The remaining microspheres were resuspended in 1 mL of artificial intestinal fluid (pH 6.8) and incubated under identical conditions for 4 h.

Stage III (Colonic Simulation): Following the intestinal phase, the supernatant was removed after centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 5 min), and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of artificial intestinal fluid (pH 7.8). Incubation was continued for 72 h to simulate colonic release.

At predetermined time points (Stage I: 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 h; Stage II: 3, 4, 6 h; Stage III: 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72 h), 500 µL of the release medium was withdrawn and replaced immediately with an equal volume of pre-warmed fresh medium of the corresponding pH to maintain constant volume.

Each collected sample was centrifuged to remove suspended particles, and the supernatant was analyzed to determine the released KP1 content. The cumulative release (%) was calculated and plotted as a function of time to obtain the release profile.

RAW 264.7 macrophages (2 × 10⁶ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates for in vitro M1/M2 polarization. M1 polarization was induced by LPS, followed by treatment with designated biomaterials.

Bulk RNA-seq reveals differential gene expression in polarized RAW 264.7 macrophages

Total RNA was isolated from RAW 264.7 macrophages using TRIzol™ reagent (Ambion, Cat. No. 15596018) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Messenger RNA (mRNA) was purified from the total RNA using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads provided in the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA). The purified mRNA was fragmented and reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed to generate double-stranded cDNA. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® following the standard workflow and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end configuration. Raw sequencing reads were processed through quality control and filtering to obtain high-quality clean reads. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 algorithm, and downstream functional enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape.

Animals

All animals were appropriately randomized and housed in individual ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were maintained on irradiated chow and provided autoclaved water ad libitum, with environmental parameters controlled at 24–26 °C, 50 ± 15% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on: 7:30 AM–7:30 PM; lights off: 7:30 PM–7:30 AM). Euthanasia was performed by carbon dioxide asphyxiation following standard ethical protocols. C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, 19 ± 1 g) were obtained from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (Approval No. 2025-A019-01).

Delayed-treatment of the acute phase using KP1@HCeO2@SAM​

In the IBD model, 5-ASA was used as a positive control. C57BL/6J mice (n = 5/group) were randomly assigned into seven groups following a 1-week acclimation period. The Control group received normal drinking water, while all other groups were administered 3% DSS (days 0–6) to induce acute IBD. KP1@HCeO2@SAM was administered on days 7, 9, 11, and 13. This dosing regimen was established based on preliminary results and literature evidence to mimic therapeutic intervention after inflammation onset and to ensure adequate drug accumulation and release at the target site during peak inflammation [8, 44, 45]. Mice were euthanized on day 15.

Body weight, fecal consistency, and bleeding were recorded every other day. Colon tissues were collected for H&E, IHC, and IF staining. Inflammatory cytokines in colon homogenate supernatants were quantified by ELISA. Fecal samples (day 14) were subjected to 16 S rRNA sequencing using the NEB Next® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit. Library quality was verified by Agilent 5400 and qPCR, followed by sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Intestinal fibrosis of the chronic phase using KP1@HCeO2@SAM

C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into seven groups (n = 5/group) and acclimated for one week before experimentation. Intestinal fibrosis was induced through three consecutive cycles of gradient DSS exposure. Each cycle consisted of 5-day DSS administration at increasing concentrations (1.5%, days 0–5; 2%, days 10–15; 2.5%, days 20–25), followed by standard drinking water during inter-cycle intervals. Oral gavage of the designated drug formulations began on day 15 and continued until endpoint euthanasia. Body weights were monitored every 5 days throughout the experimental period. Representative colon segments were collected and subjected to H&E, modified Masson’s trichrome, and IHC staining for histopathological evaluation.

Histological examination

(1) At defined endpoints (day 15 for the acute model and day 35 for the chronic model), euthanized animals underwent laparotomy for colonic resection. Excised tissues were immediately immersion-fixed in neutral-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0099) at 4 °C for 36 ± 12 h, followed by graded ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding (Leica EG1160). Serial transverse Sections (5 μm) were obtained using a Leica RM2255 microtome. For the acute model, sections were subjected to H&E staining, dual immunofluorescence for epithelial barrier proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), and macrophage polarization co-staining (CD86/CD206). For the chronic model, sections were processed for H&E, modified Masson’s trichrome staining, and fibrosis-related IHC (α-SMA/Collagen I).

(2) At the chronic model endpoint (day 35), major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested to evaluate systemic biocompatibility. Gross pathological features were scored on a 0–4 scale (0 = no lesions; 1 = congestion; 2 = hemorrhage; 3 = necrosis; 4 = tissue disintegration). Collected organs were sectioned at 4 μm and subjected to standard H&E staining for histopathological examination.

Western blotting analysis of colon tissue proteins

Total proteins were extracted from colon tissues of both acute and chronic models using a commercial protein extraction kit and quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated on 7.5% SDS-PAGE gels (PG211; Epizyme) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies. Band intensities were quantified and normalized to GAPDH for densitometric analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experiments had 3 (cellular) or 5 (animal) replications. Data represent mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21 (USA). Intergroup differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA, and when significant, post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s HSD test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
