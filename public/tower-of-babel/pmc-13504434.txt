Physician Implicit Racial Bias and Guideline-Concordant Vascular Care
Authors: Erika T. Beidelman, Brian Witrick, Kerry A. Howard, Ashley Clark, Katharine L. McGinigle, Samantha D. Minc, Olamide Alabi, Caitlin W. Hicks, Andrew A. Gonzalez, Crystal W. Cené, Samuel Cykert, Corey A. Kalbaugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs physician implicit racial bias associated with the performance of evidence-based care and use of monitored guidelines for vascular care?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 218 physicians linked with procedure-level data for 21 632 patients, physicians’ implicit bias was not associated with adherence to established vascular care guidelines.MeaningThese findings suggest that clear and consistently monitored guidelines may reduce care disparities for Black and White patients with peripheral artery disease.

Introduction

Implicit racial bias is the unconscious preference for specific races, which may contribute to race-based health disparities by influencing clinical decision-making.1,2 However, evidence on the association of physician implicit bias with patient treatment and outcomes is limited and contradictory. Evidence has found that physicians with bias in favor of White individuals are less likely to prescribe Black patients appropriate narcotics for pain, less likely to initiate thrombolysis treatment for acute coronary symptoms, and more likely to have Black patients with postprocedure disability compared with physicians without this bias.3,4,5,6 Still, other studies on this topic have found no such associations.2,7,8,9 The bulk of this evidence comes from synthetic environment studies, such as clinical vignettes, which may fail to replicate actual clinical settings. Accounting for these stressors is important because physician stress and cognitive load likely activate implicit bias.1,2,10 Thus, studies in high-stress clinical environments are needed to establish the link between physician implicit bias and treatment disparities.

To address this gap, our team has focused on better understanding the management of peripheral artery disease (PAD), an atherosclerotic disorder of the lower extremities with well-documented racial disparities in disease burden,11,12,13,14 treatment,15,16,17,18 and outcomes.19,20,21,22,23,24 Our previous research found that physicians with implicit bias favoring White individuals were more likely to perform low-value infrapopliteal revascularization procedures for Black compared with White patients with intermittent claudication (IC).25 Infrapopliteal revascularization is discouraged as a first- or second-line treatment for IC due to unfavorable risk-benefit profiles,26,27 though recommendations lack clear Level 1A evidence. Historically, the decision to perform this procedure was characterized by multiple competing factors, including high reimbursements, limited inclusion in quality programs, and potential for immediate symptom relief, despite higher long-term amputation risks. In contrast, high-quality guidelines may streamline treatment decisions and reduce cognitive load and, thus, may reduce the impact of implicit biases.

In this study, we assess care measures with well-established and closely monitored guidelines, specifically pharmacological and behavioral guideline-directed medical care. We hypothesize that clinical guidelines may support decision-making to combat physician implicit bias by reducing cognitive load. Here, we investigate whether physician implicit racial bias is associated with care delivery in settings with well-defined Class I, Level A guidelines, hypothesizing no association with adherence to guideline-directed practices.

Methods

Ethical Approvals and Data Availability

The institutional review boards at Clemson University and Indiana University approved this cross-sectional study. Informed consent from physicians completing the IAT was obtained electronically. Informed consent for patients in the VQI was waived as this is a retrospective study of deidentified data. The dataset and analytic methods for this project are available to other qualified researchers on request pending approval by the Research Advisory Committee at the Vascular Quality Initiative. This study followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Conceptual Model, Study Sample, and Source Population

Our work adapts the Kressin and Peterson model, which was originally designed for understanding cardiovascular disease–related health care and outcomes disparities28 (eAppendix in Supplement 1). Here, we applied their framework to explore patient, physician, and system-level variables in the delivery of symptomatic PAD care. We sourced patient treatment data from the peripheral vascular intervention and infrainguinal bypass registries of the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) from 2011 to 2022. The SVS VQI is a procedure-level registry that documents common open and endovascular procedures across the US.29 This registry includes data from 960 VQI-participating centers in all 50 states as well as Washington, DC and Puerto Rico. We used data from the procedural dataset, including information on physician characteristics, patient characteristics, patient medical history, procedure characteristics, and postoperative characteristics.30

We invited all physician members documented in the SVS VQI registry to complete the web-based race Implicit Association Test (IAT). Physicians who completed the IAT and could be linked to procedure-level data were included in the analysis. The IAT was administered from October 2021 to October 2022, and procedures were included from 2011 to 2022. From this linkage, we defined 2 samples for our analysis: (1) a total sample including all linked procedures performed on patients identifying as Black or White (N = 21 632) and (2) a subset that included only procedures performed on patients with IC (n = 6588). We restricted the sample to White and Black patients because the race IAT only measures implicit bias in relation to White or Black individuals. We restricted our subsample to patients with IC to align with the sample used in our previous analyses and ensure comparability of results.25

Exposure: Implicit Bias

Our exposure of interest was physician implicit bias as measured via the race IAT, the most widely accepted tool for measuring unconscious biases.31,32 The IAT is a 15-minute computerized assessment that asks participants to use rules to classify images of Black and White faces against positive and negative attributes. D-scores were then used to categorize physicians across 7 levels of implicit bias: strong, moderate, or slight bias in favor of White individuals (ie, pro-White bias); no bias; and slight, moderate, or strong bias in favor of Black individuals (ie, pro-Black bias). We further reduced the implicit bias measure into a dichotomous variable due to low within-category sample sizes: no or pro-Black bias (reference group) compared with pro-White bias.

Outcome: Guideline-Driven Clinical Practices

We selected 3 clinical practices explicitly outlined in the 2016 AHA/ACC Guidelines on the Management of Patients With Lower Extremity PAD to serve as the outcomes in this analysis. These practices comprise Class I, Level A guidelines that should be performed prior to all procedures within our dataset. Though we use the 2016 AHA/ACC guidelines as the reference, recommendations for the specific prescriptions we examine as Class I, Level A recommendations were present in clinical guidelines dating back to 2000, substantially preceding 2011, which is the first year of procedures in our analysis.33,34 The clinical practices included preoperative prescription of (1) antiplatelets, (2) statins, (3) and antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors or β blockers). Additionally, as a fourth outcome, we examined the patient’s smoking status at the time of the procedure. This fourth guideline practice was chosen because the evidence shows that for patients with IC, active smoking at the time of procedure leads to worse outcomes in nonurgent care settings. Therefore, performing revascularizations on patients with IC who are active smokers has been discouraged.35 For consistency, we retained smoking as an outcome in the total sample model but note this is not a direct guideline practice for people with severe PAD. The smoking status outcome was coded to identify nonsmoking patients at the time of the procedure (0 = current smoker; 1 = non-smoker). Additionally, for analyses examining antihypertensives, the sample was restricted to patients reporting hypertension. Each clinical practice was defined as a binary variable indicating if it was performed for each procedure. Additionally, we calculated a summary index across 3 clinical practices (excluding antihypertensives), which is equal to the total number of practices performed for each procedure (minimum: 0; maximum 3).

Covariates

We included physician, patient, and procedural characteristics in our analyses to describe the sample and account for confounding. Physician-level characteristics included age (30-39 years, 40-49 years, 50-59 years, or ≥60 years), sex (male or female), specialty (vascular surgery or other), years of experience (0-5 years, 6-10 years, 11-20 years, or ≥21 years), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White or other [including American Indian, Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Hispanic, Pacific Islander, multiracial, and not specified responses; this category was collapsed due to sample sizes <5), and region (Midwest, Northeast, South, or West). Procedure-level characteristics included year (as a factor variable). Patient-level characteristics included age, sex (male or female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black or non-Hispanic White), diabetes status (yes or no), hypertension status (yes or no), and any prior cardiovascular disease (yes or no).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed for both the full sample and IC subsample. We used generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with a logit link and binomial distribution to assess the association between physician implicit bias and guideline-driven clinical practices. Separate models were fit for each clinical practice and represent population-average effects reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs). Next, we assessed the association between physician implicit bias and the summary index of clinical practices via a GEE model with a log link and Poisson distribution, with results reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs). All models used the physician as the clustering unit and specified an exchangeable working correlation structure to account for correlation between procedures performed by the same physician. All models used robust standard errors and were adjusted for the year of procedure, region, physician race and ethnicity, physician age, physician years of experience, patient race and ethnicity, patient prior cardiovascular disease status, patient diabetes status, and patient hypertension status. We examined effect modification by patient race and ethnicity via an interaction term with physician implicit bias. Sample weights were applied to all models to produce estimates reflective of the source population of VQI physician members and to reduce the potential impact of nonresponse and selection bias. Characteristics of the full physician sample as well as the weighted and unweighted analytic sample are presented in eTable 1 in Supplement 1. The weighting methods are described in detail in a previous publication and in the eMethods in Supplement 1.25

To contextualize our findings against our prior analysis of nonguideline infrapopliteal revascularization procedures in the same IC subsample,25 we estimated bias-by-race interaction contrasts for the guideline-driven clinical practice index. We derived estimated marginal means for each of the 4 combinations of physician implicit bias and patient race from the fitted model, then computed deviation contrasts comparing each combination’s estimate to the sample mean. Estimates are reported as IRRs on the response scale with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was set at an α of .05. All analyses were performed through March 2025 in RStudio, version 4.1.1 (R Foundation).36

Results

Sample Characteristics

Of 2512 invited physicians, 218 completed the race IAT. Across these 218 physicians, 157 (72%) presented with a pro-White implicit bias, and 61 (28%) with no or pro-Black bias. Physicians were linked to a median of 61 (IQR, 23-131) procedures; 198 (91%) were vascular surgery specialists; 160 (73%) were male and 56 (26%) were female; 144 (66%) were non-Hispanic White and 374 (34%) were of other race and ethnicity; and 125 (57%) had fewer than 11 years of experience. Physicians’ mean (SD) age was 45 (10) years (Table 1). A greater proportion of physicians with pro-White bias identified as non-Hispanic White (110 [70%]) compared with those with no or pro-Black bias (34 [56%]) (P = .07). The 218 physicians were linkable to 21 632 revascularization procedures, with 201 physicians and 6588 procedures constituting the IC subsample. In the total sample, patients had a mean (SD) age of 68 (11) years; 4992 (23%) identified as non-Hispanic Black and 16 640 (77%) as non-Hispanic White; and 8019 (37%) were female and 13 613 (63%) were male (Table 2). Diabetes (12 544 [58%]) and hypertension (19 270 [89%]) were prevalent in the total sample.

Rates of Guideline-Concordant Care

Unadjusted rates of guideline-concordant care varied across practices but were similar across physician implicit bias categories (Table 3). Across patient race and physician implicit bias strata, preoperative antiplatelet prescription ranged from 81.2% (n = 1264; no bias and Black patients) to 83.7% (n = 4809; no bias and White patients), statin prescription from 78.2% (n = 2684; pro-White bias and Black patients) to 78.9% (n = 4536; no bias and White patients), antihypertensive prescription from 93.0% (n = 824; no bias and Black patients) to 94.3% (n = 6090; pro-White bias and White patients), and nonsmoking status at the time of procedure from 62.1% (n = 2134; pro-White bias and Black patients) to 66.4% (n = 7231; pro-White bias and White patients). Among all physicians, differences by patient race were small but statistically significant for antiplatelets (13 788 [82.9%] White vs 4057 [81.3%] Black patients; P = .01) and nonsmoking status (10 972 [65.9%] White vs 3165 [63.4%] Black patients; P = .001). There was no significant difference by patient race for statins (13 107 [78.8%] White and 3905 [78.3%] Black patients; P = .42) or antihypertensives (9327 of 9924 [94.0%] White and 2690 of 2888 [93.1%] Black patients; P = .11). Discussion

Our group is, to our knowledge, among the first to link physician implicit bias data to clinical records in a live interventional setting. We sought to understand the potential role of physician implicit bias in treatment decisions governed by clearly defined and monitored practice guidelines for patients with PAD with and without claudication. We found that the performance of guideline-driven clinical practices was not associated with a physician’s measured racial implicit bias. Additionally, the likelihood of these practices being performed did not differ by patient race across the physician implicit bias groups.

Prior evidence on the association between physician implicit bias and clinical decision-making has predominantly relied on hypothetical scenario assessment, including standardized patients and clinical vignettes.3,4,5,7,8,9 The conflicting results of these studies may be due to their inability to reproduce contextual factors, such as stress and uncertainty, that influence the likelihood for implicit bias to impact treatment decisions. The few analyses using clinical data illustrate the persistence of this gap. Blair et al2 examined the association between physician IAT results and hypertension management from pharmacy fill and refill records in patients already receiving treatment, a measure that conflates prescriber intent with patient adherence and access. More recently, Ramaswamy et al37 examined physician implicit bias in the context of prostate cancer decision-making using electronic health records, but their analysis was limited to only 1 physician participant who demonstrated a pro-White bias, precluding meaningful inference about the relationship between bias and care. In contrast, our analyses address these gaps in the literature by linking implicit bias data from a robust sample of more than 200 physicians to patient-level registry data on procedures actually performed.

Our findings contrast sharply with our previous work, which identified that physician pro-White bias was associated with increased odds of low-value infrapopliteal revascularizations for Black patients with claudication, but not for White patients.25 In this clinical scenario where direct guidelines are lacking and oversight has been historically limited, we found Black patients treated by a physician with pro-White bias had a 67% increase in the odds of receiving infrapopliteal revascularizations.25 Conversely, in this current evidence-based guideline analysis, no differences were observed when comparing the performance of clinical practices for Black patients treated by physicians with pro-White bias against the full sample. Taken together, the results of our 2 studies suggest the presence of clear and monitored clinical guidelines may be the major distinguishing factor.

Additionally, the implementation of clinical guidelines alongside structured care tools, such as standardized care pathways, could reduce the opportunity for biased treatment selection. The Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, for example, works by creating care pathways that embed evidence-based practices and integrate care teams to support coordinated, standardized care delivery. In some cases, adherence to guideline-driven activities may be facilitated or even completed by other team members (eg, nurses or residents). These processes can minimize the influence of individual bias not only through streamlined decision-making but also by distributing mental and operational demands across the care team. ERAS has recently been introduced for lower extremity bypass surgery to reduce variation in the surgical care delivered to vulnerable patients with PAD.38 This structured, workflow-integrated pathway can help ensure that guideline awareness translates to consistent application in clinical environments.

Our findings illustrate that in the presence of defined guidelines, implicit bias could lose some of its power over patient-clinician interactions. However, even in the presence of these guidelines, we observed low overall rates of guideline-concordant care in comparison to guideline-expected rates of 100%. Prescription of preoperative antihypertensives among patients with hypertension was the only clinical practice that approached overall rates of 100%. This suggests that guidelines alone, even when reinforced by monitoring systems such as the VQI’s twice annual guideline adherence reports, may be insufficient to ensure consistent care. This indicates the need for systems of transparency and accountability in clinical settings to overcome low-value care. Real-time transparency can be achieved through an electronic health record derived warning system that alerts clinical teams to not only missed patient appointments but also missed expected milestones in care. Such systems have been shown to have the dual benefit of improving outcomes and reducing disparities.

Over more than 2 decades, our understanding of implicit biases has evolved.39 We now know that these biases are largely driven by structural factors—namely racism—operating through policies (written rules), practices (informal rules), values, and norms.40 Together, these factors create an environment where disparities in care are a reality for Black patients. For example, Black patients may be disproportionately cared for by lower-resourced hospitals delivering lower-quality care.41 Macro-level and health system–level policies around insurance coverage and reimbursement levels can also disproportionately harm Black patients who are often overrepresented in government-sponsored plans that reimburse less for higher-value procedures.42 To protect financial health, systems adopt strategies that limit access to lesser-reimbursed procedures, effectively reducing the proportion of Medicaid patients accepted. Regarding practices, clinical team conversations use subjective criteria that can disadvantage Black patients.43 Finally, implicit biases most closely operate through values and norms about Black individuals, which shape decision-making. Education and training alone are inadequate to change clinician behavior without also changing the structures, policies, and practices that allow biases to cause harm. For example, clinical societies should develop (while health care facilities implement) robust clinical guidelines and clearly delineated care pathways, particularly for conditions with strong evidence of disparities best explained by structural racism.

Proceduralists require substantial cognitive capacity to operate effectively and safely. At the same time, numerous on-the-job stressors can effectively reduce a surgeon’s available cognitive capacity and create opportunities for unconscious racial biases to impact surgeon decision-making.1,2,10,49 High cognitive load has been shown to exacerbate racial biases in multiple settings, including sleep deprivation among police officers,50 burnout among resident physicians,51 and stressors among emergency department physicians.52 Within surgical settings, a lack of supportive structures for clinical decision-making can increase cognitive load among physicians who are already predisposed to stress, fatigue, and burnout. Therefore, clinical guidelines are likely successful because they do not require the individual to identify and/or challenge their implicit biases in already stressful situations. We say this not to excuse implicit bias nor to discourage physicians from participating in implicit bias trainings. Physicians should indeed consider the role of implicit bias in their own practices. However, we contend that individualized approaches are unsuccessful in the long term because implicit bias reflects systemic racism that enables unconscious attitudes to create and perpetuate harm. In accepting implicit bias as a diagnostic marker of systemic racism, we can begin to address the health inequities that result.

Limitations

The IAT has documented limitations, including modest test-retest reliability and moderate predictive validity for individual-level behavior.44,45,46 However, these limitations are characteristic of implicit measurement more broadly, with other implicit measures such as the Go/No-Go Association Task, Affect Misattribution Procedure, and evaluative priming demonstrating comparable or lower reliability.46 Still, the IAT remains a widely used test for studying unconscious bias with moderate reliability and validity. Additionally, because implicit bias was measured in 2021 to 2022 and the procedures could date back to as early as 2011, we cannot guarantee that the measured levels of implicit bias were the same at the time of each procedure. However, the majority of procedures measured were temporally close to the IAT measurement, and even for procedures performed less recently, unconscious bias is unlikely to change over time without a formal intervention. Unconscious bias forms early in life and is well developed by the time we reach adulthood and enter the workforce.47,48 Another primary limitation of this work is the potential for selection bias. Only approximately 10% of eligible VQI physicians participated in the IAT assessment, and these physicians differed from the full physician population in important ways. Specifically, IAT participants skewed younger and were more likely to be female compared with all VQI physicians. If IAT participation is also associated with clinician delivery of guideline-concordant care, the estimated association between implicit bias and care delivery may be biased. To mitigate this potential bias, we applied sample weights to all models to align the analytic sample with the source population of VQI physicians on measured characteristics. Weighting successfully attenuated the differences observed across IAT participants and nonparticipants. Nonetheless, weighting only accounts for measured characteristics, and we cannot exclude the possibility that there are remaining unmeasured factors associated with both IAT participation and care delivery. Residual selection bias may therefore persist, and these findings are most directly applicable to clinicians who elected to participate.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study examining vascular specialists and the care they deliver, we found that physician implicit bias was not associated with any clinical practice outcome. Our findings suggest that clear and consistently monitored clinical guidelines may be a powerful tool to combat physician implicit bias.
