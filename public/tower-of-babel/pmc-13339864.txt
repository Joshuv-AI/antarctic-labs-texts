Single-cell and bulk omics uncover fibroblast heterogeneity and HSPH1 as a key driver in Barrett’s esophagus to esophageal adenocarcinoma progression
Authors: Lulong Tao, Yanting Guo, Fang Lu, Changyong Qi, Guoxin Zhang, Jin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis, often evolving from Barrett’s esophagus (BE). Understanding the molecular mechanisms driving this progression is critical for identifying diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk transcriptomic datasets to investigate fibroblast heterogeneity and epithelial–stromal crosstalk across disease stages. A disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) was established and applied for consensus clustering to define EAC molecular subtypes. HSPH1, the core prognostic gene, was identified using multiple machine learning algorithms and further analyzed for pathway enrichment, immune landscape, and drug sensitivity. Its expression and function were subsequently validated by immunohistochemistry and in vitro assays.ResultsDistinct fibroblast subpopulations were identified in BE and EAC, with specific clusters exhibiting enhanced interactions with epithelial cells. Ligand–receptor analysis revealed key signaling pathways, including COL1A1–SDC1, MDK–NCL, and ITGA2–ITGB1, potentially contributing to malignant transition. Molecular subtyping revealed two EAC clusters with distinct immune infiltration. Among the DAFS genes, HSPH1 was identified as a key prognostic marker associated with higher tumor mutation burden, reduced immune infiltration, and increased tumor purity. Functional assays confirmed that HSPH1 promotes EAC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. A progressive increase in HSPH1 expression from normal tissue to BE to EAC supports its potential utility as a biomarker for malignant progression.ConclusionOur study reveals critical fibroblast–epithelial interactions in NC-BE-EAC progression and identifies HSPH1 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target. These findings may facilitate risk stratification and personalized treatment strategies for EAC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04319-x.Keywords: Esophageal adenocarcinoma, Barrett’s esophagus, Fibroblast, Tumor microenvironment, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) is more prevalent in Western populations and is closely linked to risk factors like obesity, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and Helicobacter pylori infection [1, 2]. EAC typically develops in the distal esophagus and often invades surrounding tissues, creating challenges for surgical management. Its insidious onset and limited sensitivity to radiotherapy further result in delayed diagnosis and restricted treatment options. Consequently, the prognosis of EAC is significantly poorer than that of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 15% [3].

Barrett’s esophagus (BE), a precursor of EAC, is characterized by the replacement of squamous epithelium with columnar epithelium, primarily induced by chronic exposure to gastric acid and bile reflux [4]. The columnar mucosa exhibits both intestinal and gastric features, conferring a heightened risk of malignant transformation. Accordingly, regular endoscopic surveillance is essential for early detection and timely intervention.

Among the molecular drivers, the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in the development of BE [5]. Evidence shows that Hedgehog ligand expression in epithelial cells can induce stromal BMP4 secretion, which in turn alters esophageal epithelial identity [6]. In addition, chronic inflammation and activation of NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin pathways promote progression from BE to EAC [7–10]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying the sequential transformation from normal mucosa to intestinal metaplasia and ultimately to carcinoma remain poorly understood.

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have highlighted the critical role of stromal components, particularly fibroblasts, in tumor development. Under homeostatic conditions, fibroblasts maintain tissue architecture through extracellular matrix remodeling and paracrine signaling [11]. However, upon injury or chronic inflammation, fibroblasts undergo phenotypic reprogramming and generate distinct subpopulations that modulate the tissue microenvironment [12]. Loss of ANXA1 has been shown to drive the transition of normal fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), thereby contributing to ESCC development [13]. Similarly, POSTN+ myofibroblast can promote EAC cell invasion through periostin-dependent signaling involving integrins αvβ3/αvβ5 and downstream PI3K–Akt activation [14]. Consequently, gaining a deeper understanding of mesenchymal cell function may clarify the mechanisms driving malignant progression and help identify key subpopulations or driver genes for targeted therapy development.

In this study, we comprehensively characterized fibroblast heterogeneity in BE and EAC using integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses. We identified disease-specific fibroblast subtypes and their associated ligand–receptor interactions. A disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) was constructed and applied to define molecular subtypes of EAC. Furthermore, machine learning algorithms identified HSPH1 as a core prognostic gene, which was subsequently evaluated for its role in immune microenvironment modulation, therapeutic response prediction, and prognostic stratification.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for BE tissues were obtained from the Cellxgene Data Portal (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/a18474f4-ff1e-4864-af69-270b956cee5b), while EAC data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE173950, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). In addition, we collected multiple microarray datasets covering normal control (NC), BE, and EAC, including: GSE13083 (7 NC, 7 BE), GSE26886 (19 NC, 20 BE, 21 EAC), GSE39491 (40 NC, 40 BE), GSE37200 (31 BE, 15 EAC), GSE42363 (14 EAC), GSE13898 (28 NC, 15 BE, 75 EAC), and GSE37201 (14 EAC). RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for EAC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://cancergenome.nih.gov/).

Bulk and single-cell RNA-seq processing

For bulk datasets, batch effects were removed using the "ComBat" function from the "sva" package, resulting in a unified dataset termed "Merged_GEO"(Fig. S1A-D). The "Seurat" package was employed for scRNA-seq analysis. For the EAC dataset, cells with fewer than 500 or more than 4,000 detected genes and mitochondrial content exceeding 25% were excluded during quality control. Doublets were removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3), and batch effects were corrected using the "Harmony" package. Data normalization, scaling, and identification of the top 2,000 variable genes were conducted using "NormalizeData", "ScaleData", and "FindVariableFeatures" functions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, with the top 40 PCs used for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.1, and clusters were annotated based on CellMarker 2.0 and published markers. For the BE dataset, the file “UMAP of Cancer Data integration” was downloaded and used for analysis. Samples corresponding to normal esophageal tissue and Barrett’s esophagus (BE) were selected from the integrated dataset, and epithelial cells and fibroblasts were directly extracted from the original matrix for downstream analysis. In both datasets, fibroblasts were further extracted and subclustered to investigate their heterogeneity.

Cell–cell communication analysis

The "CellChat" package was employed to assess intercellular communication between fibroblasts and epithelial cells. After normalizing expression data, interaction networks were inferred, and significant ligand-receptor pairs and signaling pathways were identified. Group comparisons were conducted to detect differential communication profiles.

Identification of core prognostic gene using machine learning

Given the heterogeneity of disease-associated fibroblasts in BE and EAC datasets, we applied the "intersect" function to identify shared marker genes, defined as the disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS). To identify prognostically relevant genes, the DAFS genes were first subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis, and genes significantly associated with overall survival were retained as candidate prognostic genes. To further prioritize candidate prognostic genes, three machine learning algorithms were subsequently applied, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). For LASSO analysis, the "glmnet" package was used with a Cox regression framework, and the optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was determined by tenfold cross-validation. Genes with non-zero coefficients at the optimal lambda were retained. RF analysis was performed using the "rfsrc" function in the "randomForestSRC" package with 1,000 trees, nodesize = 14, and log-rank splitting, and candidate genes were ranked according to variable importance (VIMP) scores. XGBoost was implemented using the "survival:cox" objective with 100 boosting rounds and a learning rate of 0.1, and genes were ranked according to feature importance using the "xgb.importance" function. The overlapping genes identified by all three algorithms were considered robust candidate prognostic genes for downstream analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the "clusterProfiler" package [15], to identify relevant biological processes and molecular functions. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the Reactome database to compare pathway activity between high- and low-HSPH1 expression groups.

Consensus clustering and subtype-specific differences

Utilizing DAFS, unsupervised clustering of EAC samples was conducted via the ‘ConsensusClusterPlus’ package [16]. The optimal number of clusters was determined through an integrated evaluation of the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, delta area plot, tracking plot, and proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC) score, with k = 2 selected as the final clustering solution. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted using the "h.all.v7.0.symbols" gene set to explore functional differences between subtypes.

Gene set scoring analysis

For bulk transcriptomic data, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was used to calculate sample-level enrichment scores for DAFS. For single-cell data, AUCell analysis was performed to evaluate the enrichment of predefined gene signatures across fibroblast subclusters at the single-cell level. The scores were then used to compare the relative activity of different biological programs across molecular subtypes or cell subclusters.

Immune landscape analysis

To investigate immune cell dynamics within the tumor microenvironment, quantitative differences between groups were assessed using the "CIBERSORT" and "ssGSEA" algorithms. The "estimate" package was applied to calculate stromal scores, immune scores, and tumor purity, and group-wise differences were visualized with boxplots. Correlation analysis was performed between HSPH1 expression level, immune cell abundance, and immune/stromal scores to assess its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment.

Mutation profiling and drug response prediction

Mutation frequency and type were visualized using the "maftools" package, which generated a Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) waterfall plot to depict mutation distributions across samples. The top 15 genes with the highest mutation frequencies were ranked in both high- and low-HSPH1 expression groups. Drug response was predicted using the "OncoPredict" package, which maps gene expression profiles to drug sensitivity scores (IC50) based on established drug response datasets. Additionally, correlations between drug efficacy and gene expression were analyzed.

Diagnostic performance evaluation

To evaluate the diagnostic value of HSPH1 in distinguishing NC, BE, and EAC, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess diagnostic accuracy. ROC curves and AUC values were generated using the "pROC" package.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

HSPH1 expression was assessed in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital With Nanjing Medical University, including 6 NC, 9 BE, and 7 EAC samples. IHC staining was performed using a primary antibody against HSPH1 (ab109624, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:500 dilution), with heat-induced antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). An HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500 dilution) was used as the secondary antibody. PBS was used as a negative control in place of the primary antibody, and hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Quantification of HSPH1 staining was performed using ImageJ software.

Cell culture and siRNA transfection

The human esophageal adenocarcinoma cell line OE19 was obtained from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and OE33 was purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Both cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Wisent, Canada) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biochannel, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Biochannel, China). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified atmosphere and passaged every 2–3 days once reaching ~ 80% confluence. For gene silencing, OE19 and OE33 cells were transfected with HSPH1-targeting siRNA oligonucleotides (100 nM; Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd) using Lipofectamine™ 8000 (Beyotime, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences of all siRNAs used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Western Blot (WB)

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HSPH1 (ab109624, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and GAPDH (Beyotime, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system.

CCK-8, Colony formation, and transwell assays

The proliferative capacity of EAC cells was assessed using the CCK-8 and colony formation assays following the manufacturer’s instructions. Due to differences in growth characteristics, transfected OE19 and OE33 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at densities of 5,000 and 3,000 cells per well, respectively. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO, USA) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. For colony formation assays, transfected OE19 and OE33 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 1,200 and 1,000 cells per well, respectively, and incubated for 9 days based on their growth rates. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and manually counted under a microscope.

Cell invasion was evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning, USA) with 8.0 μm pores for OE19 cells and 12.0 μm pores for OE33 cells. Chambers were pre-coated with Matrix-Gel™ (Beyotime, China). Transfected OE19 (8 × 104 cells/well) and OE33 (5 × 104 cells/well) cells in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower chambers contained medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h (OE19) or 24 h (OE33), non-invading cells were removed from the upper membrane surface. Invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in five randomly selected fields.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman or Pearson coefficients, as appropriate. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell landscape of EAC and BE tissues

The overall study workflow is depicted in Fig. 1. After quality control, a total of 21,609 high-quality cells derived from 16 normal samples and 28 tumor samples were retained for downstream analysis in the EAC dataset. Unsupervised clustering identified 11 clusters, which were subsequently annotated into eight major cell types based on canonical marker genes, including epithelial cell (cluster 2; KRT7, KRT6A), fibroblast (clusters 0 and 10; PDGFRB, LUM), endothelial cell(cluster 6; VWF, PECAM1), T/NK cell (clusters 1 and 8; CD3D, CD3E, NKG7), B cell(cluster 9; BANK1, CD79A), plasma cell(clusters 3 and 5; MZB1, IGHG1), mast cell(cluster 7; GATA2, KIT), and myeloid cell(cluster 4; CD68, FCGR3A) (Fig. 2A; Figure S2A-B).The top differential genes for each cell type are shown in Fig. 2B. Fibroblasts, especially cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play pivotal roles in the tumor microenvironment by contributing to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, angiogenesis, and tumor invasion [17]. Given their potential relevance to EAC progression, fibroblasts were extracted for downstream analysis. In EAC samples, fibroblasts were subdivided into six distinct clusters (Fig. 2C; Table S2), and representative marker genes for each cluster are shown in Fig. 2D. Notably, Fibroblast Cluster 1 was predominantly enriched in EAC tissues.

To investigate epithelial–stromal communication in EAC, the CellChat algorithm was applied to infer intercellular signaling between epithelial cells and fibroblast subclusters. Compared with NC tissues, EAC tissues exhibited a remodeled epithelial–fibroblast communication pattern, characterized by a greater number of inferred interactions but relatively lower interaction strength (Fig. 2F, G). In addition, several signaling pathways, including non-canonical WNT (ncWNT), ESAM, ANGPTL, NOTCH, MK, and GDF, displayed altered information flow patterns (Fig. 2H). Among fibroblast subpopulations, Cluster 1, exhibited stronger interaction strength with epithelial cells in EAC tissues (Fig. 2I), suggesting its potential role in stromal–epithelial crosstalk during EAC initiation and progression. Furthermore, Cluster 1 highly expressed matrix-associated genes such as COL1A1, COL3A1, and POSTN, and showed relatively higher Myofibroblastic-like signature scores (Figure S3A; Table S4), indicating that this subpopulation represents a myofibroblastic fibroblast population.

From the original expression matrix, epithelial cells and fibroblasts were selected for downstream analysis. After quality control, a total of 30,309 high-quality cells were retained, including 27,095 epithelial cells and 3,214 fibroblasts (Fig. 3A). A disease-specific epithelial subpopulation was identified exclusively in BE tissues (Fig. 3B), potentially representing an early event in neoplastic transformation. Clustering analysis further identified five fibroblast subclusters (Fig. 3C; Table S3), with their marker genes and relative abundances shown in Fig. 3D-E. Similarly, Cluster 1 highly expressed genes such as LUM, DPT, POSTN and CCN2, and showed relatively higher Myofibroblastic-like signature scores (Figure S3B; Table S4), suggesting that this subpopulation exhibits a myofibroblastic-like fibroblast state.

Functional enrichment and disease-associated alterations in fibroblast subclusters

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the disease-specific fibroblast subclusters in BE and EAC tissues were both enriched in biological processes related to extracellular matrix organization, protein folding, and collagen metabolism, suggesting shared roles in stromal remodeling and microenvironmental regulation. Consistently, KEGG analysis showed enrichment in cytoskeleton-related pathways and antigen processing and presentation (Figure S4 A–D), further supporting the involvement of these fibroblast populations in matrix dynamics and immune-related interactions.

Despite these shared features, fibroblast subpopulations displayed distinct functional signatures in different stages. Compared with normal tissues, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from Fibroblast Cluster 1 were enriched in antimicrobial humoral responses, monocyte chemotaxis, and epidermal cell differentiation in BE tissues (Figure S4E), pointing to potential roles in inflammation modulation and epithelial barrier maintenance. In contrast, DEGs from EAC-derived fibroblasts were associated with purine ribonucleoside triphosphate metabolism and nucleoside biosynthesis, indicating a shift toward metabolic programs associated with tumor progression (Figure S4F).

Collectively, these results indicate that although fibroblast-driven stromal remodeling is a common hallmark during NC–BE–EAC progression, distinct biological programs are activated at different disease stages.

Establishment of Disease-Associated Fibroblast Signature (DAFS)

Given the functional similarity between fibroblast subpopulations in BE and EAC tissues, we identified a set of shared marker genes to establish a disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) (Fig. 5A; Table S5). Functional enrichment analysis showed that these genes were involved in extracellular structure organization, collagen-containing matrix components, and structural constituents of the extracellular matrix (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis showed enrichment in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, antigen processing and presentation, focal adhesion, and PI3K–Akt signaling pathways (Fig. 5C). To identify clinically relevant genes within the DAFS, univariable Cox regression analysis was performed on 78 EAC samples from the TCGA database. Six prognosis-associated genes were identified, including GEM, HSPA1B, HSPH1, CBX3, DNAJB4, and HSP90AA1 (Fig. 5D).

Molecular subtypes and tissue microenvironment of EAC

To further explore the role of DAFS in shaping the tumor microenvironment of EAC, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed to define molecular subtypes. Based on an integrated evaluation of clustering stability and interpretability, k = 2 was selected as the final clustering solution, stratifying 78 EAC samples from the TCGA cohort into Cluster 1 (n = 68) and Cluster 2 (n = 10) (Fig. 6A; Figure S5A-C). We next performed “ssGSEA” analysis using the DAFS signature to characterize the biological differences between the two subtypes. As shown in Figure S5D, Cluster 2 exhibited significantly higher DAFS signature activity than Cluster 1. Among the six DAFS-derived prognostic genes, GEM, HSPA1B, HSPH1, DNAJB4, and HSP90AA1 tended to be more highly expressed in Cluster 2, although only GEM and DNAJB4 reached statistical significance. In contrast, CBX3 expression was higher in Cluster 1 (Fig. 6B).

Immune cell infiltration patterns were evaluated using the CIBERSORT and ssGSEA algorithms. Cluster 2 showed relatively higher estimated infiltration of activated B cells, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, monocytes, macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 6D). In addition, activated mast cells appeared to be more abundant in Cluster 2, whereas resting mast cells were enriched in Cluster 1 (Fig. 6C). By contrast, neutrophil infiltration was significantly higher in Cluster 1, suggesting that the two subtypes may exhibit distinct immune characteristics. To assess functional differences, GSVA was conducted using the “Hallmark of Cancer” gene set. Cluster 2 was enriched in pathways related to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), myogenesis, angiogenesis, and IL2–STAT5 signaling, whereas Cluster 1 was relatively enriched in metabolic programs, including peroxisome, fatty acid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 6E). Immune checkpoint analysis revealed significant upregulation of CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, and TIGIT in Cluster 2 (Fig. 6F), suggesting a more immune-active phenotype in this subtype. Collectively, these findings suggest that DAFS-defined subtypes may represent distinct immune and metabolic states in EAC. However, given the limited samples, these characteristics require validation in larger independent cohorts.

Machine learning-based identification of key genes

To refine the selection of prognostic genes from the six DAFS-related candidates, three machine learning algorithms were applied. LASSO regression, a regularization method that penalizes less informative features, identified two key genes (Fig. 7A). XGBoost ranked candidate genes according to predictive importance and retained the top-ranked genes (Fig. 7B). Random forest analysis identified four important genes based on variable importance scores derived from ensemble decision trees (Fig. 7C). A Venn diagram was used to determine shared genes among the three models, revealing HSPH1 as the overlapping gene (Fig. 7D). Subsequent analyses showed that HSPH1 was significantly upregulated in EAC tissues, and high expression levels were associated with poorer overall survival (Fig. 7E, F), underscoring its potential as a prognostic biomarker in EAC. We next further characterized its cellular localization and found that HSPH1 was detectable across multiple cell types, rather than being selectively confined to the fibroblast (Figure S6A-D).

HSPH1-Driven gene expression alterations and functional insights

To gain deeper insights into the transcriptional features associated with HSPH1 in EAC, we stratified 78 EAC samples into high- and low-HSPH1 expression groups according to the median expression level. Differential expression analysis identified 295 HSPH1-associated genes, including 217 downregulated and 78 upregulated genes in the high-expression group (|log2 fold change|> 1, p < 0.05). GO enrichment analysis showed that the upregulated genes were mainly enriched in protein refolding (Figure S7A), suggesting an association with cellular stress-response programs. In contrast, downregulated genes were enriched in several immune-related biological processes (Figure S7C). KEGG analysis further revealed that upregulated genes were associated with the estrogen signaling pathway, antigen processing and presentation, and MAPK signaling (Figure S7B), whereas downregulated genes were enriched in pathways related to primary immunodeficiency, NF-κB signaling, and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Figure S7D). Collectively, these findings suggest that high HSPH1 expression is associated with altered protein homeostasis and immune-related transcriptional programs in EAC.

Mutation landscape and biological variations

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of the Cellular Response to Heat Stress pathway in the high-HSPH1 expression group. In contrast, the low-expression group showed relative enrichment of pathways related to Neutrophil Degranulation, Plasma Lipoprotein Remodeling, and Platelet Activation Signaling and Aggregation (Fig. 8A). Immune checkpoint analysis based on HSPH1 expression stratification showed significantly higher expression of CTLA4, LAG3, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2, and TIGIT in the low-expression group (Fig. 8B). Somatic mutation analysis using waterfall plots identified TP53, TTN, and SYNE1 as the most frequently mutated genes in both groups (Fig. 8C). In addition, the high-HSPH1 expression group exhibited a significantly higher overall mutation burden (Fig. 8C, D), suggesting a potential association between elevated HSPH1 expression and greater genomic alteration in EAC.

Impact of HSPH1 on tumor microenvironment

We further explored the association between HSPH1 expression and immune cell composition using the CIBERSORT algorithm (Fig. 9A, B). Compared with the low-expression group, the high-expression group showed a lower estimated abundance of plasma cells and a higher proportion of resting CD4⁺ memory T cells (Fig. 9C). Moreover, HSPH1 expression was negatively correlated with both stromal score and immune score, whereas tumor purity was positively correlated (Fig. 9D). Correlation analyses further showed that HSPH1 expression was positively associated with monocytes and neutrophils, but negatively correlated with regulatory T cells (Tregs) and naive B cells (Fig. 9E). These findings suggest that HSPH1 may modulate immune infiltration in EAC, contributing to immune evasion and tumor progression.

Drug sensitivity

To evaluate the therapeutic relevance of HSPH1, drug sensitivity profiling was conducted. In the high-HSPH1 expression group, MK-1775 showed lower predicted IC50 values and a negative correlation with HSPH1 expression, suggesting that tumors with higher HSPH1 expression may have greater sensitivity to this compound. In contrast, several compounds, like AZD8055, Elephantin, GSK269962A, PCI-34051, PF-4708671, PRIMA-1MET, PRT062607, and WZ4003 showed relatively lower predicted IC50 values in the low-expression group (Fig. 10A, B). These results underscore the potential of HSPH1 for guiding drug selection and personalized treatment strategies in EAC.

Diagnostic potential of HSPH1 in NC-BE-EAC progression

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic value of HSPH1 across disease stages. In the GSE26886 dataset, HSPH1 showed AUC values of 0.689 for NC vs BE, 0.887 for NC vs EAC, and 0.833 for BE vs EAC (Fig. 11A). In the Merged_GEO dataset, the corresponding AUC values were 0.570, 0.770, and 0.724, respectively (Fig. 11B) Overall, HSPH1 showed more consistent discriminatory ability in comparisons involving EAC than in distinguishing NC from BE, suggesting its greater diagnostic relevance for EAC detection.

Expression changes and experimental validation

To explore HSPH1 expression dynamics across the NC–BE–EAC progression, we analyzed multiple public datasets. Overall, HSPH1 expression increased progressively with disease advancement. Although no significant difference was observed between NC and BE in GSE13083, GSE13898, and the Merged_GEO dataset, a consistent and significant upregulation was observed in EAC compared to BE in GSE37200, GSE26886, and Merged_GEO (Fig. 12A–E), suggesting its potential role in malignant transformation. To validate these findings, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on clinical samples. The results confirmed elevated HSPH1 expression in BE tissues relative to NC and a marked increase in EAC tissues (Fig. 12F, G), reinforcing the clinical relevance of HSPH1 as a potential biomarker for tumor progression.

In vitro validation of HSPH1 function in EAC cells

To explore the functional role of HSPH1 in EAC, gene silencing was performed in OE19 and OE33 cells using siRNA constructs targeting HSPH1. Transfections were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol, and knockdown efficiency was confirmed by WB. Both cell lines exhibited a marked reduction in HSPH1 protein expression upon siRNA treatment (Fig. 13A). The siRNA sequence with the highest silencing efficiency was selected for subsequent functional assays. Following HSPH1 suppression, CCK-8 assays revealed a significant decrease in cell proliferation in both OE19 and OE33 cells (Fig. 13B), indicating that HSPH1 supports EAC cell growth. Consistently, colony formation assays demonstrated a marked reduction in clonogenic potential upon HSPH1 silencing (Fig. 13C), further supporting its role in promoting cellular proliferation. In addition, Transwell assays showed that suppression of HSPH1 significantly impaired the migratory and invasive capabilities of EAC cells (Fig. 13D). Collectively, these findings highlight HSPH1 as a critical regulator of EAC cell proliferation and invasiveness.

Discussion

As the precursor to EAC, BE warrants routine screening, particularly in high-risk individuals, such as those with GERD, smoking history, or obesity [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that undifferentiated gastric cardia cells can transdifferentiate into BE through transcriptional programs involving c-MYC and HNF4A [19]. Additionally, upregulation of KRT14 has been associated with enhanced motility and invasiveness of esophageal epithelial cells, promoting EAC development [20]. However, most existing studies have focused on single pathological stage, with limited understanding of the dynamic molecular alterations occurring during the NC–BE–EAC transition. To address this knowledge gap, we integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to identify fibroblast subpopulations associated with disease progression. We constructed a disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) and investigated its clinical relevance in EAC molecular subtyping, immune contexture, and drug response. HSPH1, a core component of the DAFS, was further validated for its diagnostic and functional importance using clinical samples and in vitro experiments.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have emerged as key regulators in tumor biology due to their ability in remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM), secreting cytokines and chemokines, and modulating immune responses [21]. CAFs exhibit phenotypic and functional heterogeneity across cancer types, including myofibroblastic (myCAFs), inflammatory (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting (apCAFs) subsets [17]. CD10⁺GPR77⁺ CAFs, for example, have been shown to promote cancer stem cell properties and therapeutic resistance through IL-6 and IL-8 secretion [22]. In our study, we identified disease-specific fibroblast subpopulations enriched in BE and EAC, characterized by involvement in ECM remodeling, antigen processing, and inflammatory pathways. Notably, POSTN was consistently highly expressed in both subpopulations, reinforcing its established role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, migration, and resistance [23, 24]. Mechanistically, POSTN has been shown to promote tumor progression via integrin–FAK–STAT3 signaling in thyroid cancer [25], and to mediate chemoresistance by interacting with CDK16⁺ LPC-like cells in breast cancer [26]. In BE-associated fibroblasts, elevated expression of FOSB, a member of the AP-1 transcription factor family suggests involvement in TGF-β-, IL-6-, and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling [27]. In contrast, EAC-associated fibroblasts showed upregulation of SFRP4, implicating Wnt-related mechanisms in tumor invasiveness [28].

Epithelial–stromal interactions play a pivotal role in carcinogenesis [13, 29]. By focusing on ligand–receptor pairs consistently involved in NC–BE–EAC progression, we identified persistent stromal signals that may promote epithelial transformation. SDC1 activates KRAS in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), facilitating nutrient uptake and tumor growth [30], whereas SDC4 enhances motility and invasion in gastric cancer [31]. These findings suggest that both proteins contribute to cancer progression through distinct mechanisms. Taken together with our results, sustained expression of collagen proteins like COL1A1 and COL1A2 may advance disease by reinforcing the extracellular matrix, supporting signaling in the tumor microenvironment, and interacting with glycosaminoglycan receptors (SDC1, SDC4) on epithelial cells. Additionally, CD44 and integrins (ITGA2, ITGB1) are key regulators of cell adhesion, migration, and signaling [32], while MDK-NCL may promote tumor migration and invasion via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [33]. These interactions offer attractive targets for early intervention aimed at halting BE-to-EAC progression.

Notably, we established a disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) and leveraged it to classify EAC into two subtypes. Cluster 2 exhibited increased immune infiltration and activation of EMT and immune-related pathways, suggesting a more immunologically active phenotype. In contrast, Cluster 1 was enriched for metabolic programs and characterized by reduced immune signatures. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of EAC and suggest that DAFS-defined subtypes may represent distinct tumor microenvironmental states. Although the precise molecular mechanisms responsible for the divergent immune infiltration patterns between these subtypes remain unclear, our data provide possible explanations. First, ssGSEA analysis showed that DAFS signature activity was significantly higher in Cluster 2, indicating enhanced activation of the DAFS-related transcriptional program in this subtype. Functional enrichment analysis of the DAFS genes revealed significant enrichment in extracellular structure remodeling, collagen- and integrin-related binding, protein folding, endoplasmic reticulum protein processing, and antigen processing and presentation. These results suggest that DAFS may contribute to remodeling of the EAC immune microenvironment through coordinated regulation of stromal architecture, cellular stress-response programs, and immune-related molecular processes. Such changes may partly account for the increased immune infiltration and elevated immune checkpoint expression observed in Cluster 2.

Among the DAFS genes, HSPH1 emerged as a consistently prioritized prognostic candidate. As a molecular chaperone involved in protein refolding and proteostasis, HSPH1 has been implicated in multiple malignancies. For instance, HSPH1 enhances Bcl-6 and c-Myc expression, promoting proliferation in aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma [34]. In carcinogen-induced gastric carcinogenesis, YTHDF1-regulated HSPH1 facilitates malignant transformation and tumor development [35]. In our study, high HSPH1 expression was associated with altered immune cell composition, including increased estimated infiltration of monocytes and neutrophils and decreased levels of regulatory T cells and naive B cells, suggesting that HSPH1 may be linked to remodeling of the immune microenvironment in EAC. Prior studies have shown that HSPH1 knockdown suppresses esophageal tumor growth in vivo [36], and our in vitro experiments further support a tumor-promoting role for HSPH1 by showing that its silencing impaired the proliferation and invasion of EAC cells. Clinically, high HSPH1 expression correlated with poorer prognosis and an altered immune regulatory state in EAC. Moreover, HSPH1 expression showed an association with predicted sensitivity to MK-1775 (Adavosertib), a WEE1 kinase inhibitor known to abrogate G2/M arrest and enhance radiosensitivity in esophageal cancer [37]. Taken together, these findings suggest that HSPH1 may have potential relevance as a prognostic biomarker and as a candidate for future therapeutic stratification studies, pending further validation.

The identification of novel biomarkers is essential for early detection, therapeutic decision-making, and prognostic evaluation. Metabolomics studies have linked the amino acid L-proline (LP), the ketone body 3-hydroxybutyrate (BHBA), and the carbohydrate D-mannose (DM) to EAC risk [38]. In our study, transcriptomic and immunohistochemical analyses revealed a stepwise increase in HSPH1 expression across the NC–BE–EAC sequence. While its ability to distinguish NC from BE was limited, HSPH1 showed strong discriminatory power between BE and EAC, making it a promising biomarker for identifying patients at risk of malignant progression. Future studies should evaluate the diagnostic performance of HSPH1 in combination with other molecular markers to improve early detection.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we identified disease-associated fibroblast subpopulations in BE and EAC tissues, their spatial distribution, phenotypic characteristics and predicted stromal–epithelial interactions were not directly validated. Second, while in vitro assays demonstrated that HSPH1 enhances the proliferative and invasive capacities of EAC cells, its fibroblast-related role and its contribution to stromal biology remain to be further clarified. Third, the clinical relevance of HSPH1, including its prognostic and diagnostic value, still requires validation in larger independent cohorts. Finally, speculations regarding immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, and potential therapeutic relevance were derived mainly from computational inference, and therefore should be supported by further experimental and clinical evidence.

Conclusions

In summary, this study uncovers the molecular and cellular dynamics underlying the progression from Barrett’s esophagus to esophageal adenocarcinoma. We identified disease-specific fibroblast subpopulations and key ligand–receptor interactions driving epithelial transformation. The development of a disease-associated fibroblast signature (DAFS) enabled the classification of EAC subtypes with distinct immune and metabolic phenotypes. Furthermore, HSPH1 was identified as a core oncogenic driver with diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance. These findings provide new insights into the tumor microenvironment and offer potential biomarkers and targets for the early detection and treatment of EAC.
