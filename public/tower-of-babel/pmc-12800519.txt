Subclavian artery injury following clavicle fracture successfully treated with an urgent conversion to endovascular method: a case report
Authors: Jiwon Kim, Jonghwan Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubclavian artery injury is a rare complication of clavicular fracture. This condition most often results from penetrating trauma but may also occur after blunt injury, when bone fragments cause rupture, pseudoaneurysm, dissection, or arterial thrombosis. Subclavian artery injury associated with clavicular fracture occurs in less than 1% of cases and may lead to life-threatening hemorrhage or limb ischemia. We report a case of subclavian artery injury secondary to a clavicular fracture that was successfully managed with endovascular intervention. A 48-year-old man presented to the emergency department after a downhill bicycle crash with a right midshaft clavicle fracture and was scheduled for open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) 11 days later. Intraoperatively, rupture of a subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm caused massive hemorrhage. Surgical dissection was complicated by severe perivascular inflammation and a high risk of iatrogenic subclavian vein injury. Immediate conversion to an endovascular approach allowed successful hemostasis through femoral artery access and covered stent deployment, after which ORIF was completed. The patient recovered without neurovascular complications and was discharged on postoperative day 5. At 12 weeks, he achieved full shoulder range of motion, and 2-year follow-up angiography showed no stent-related complications. This case underscores the effectiveness of emergent endovascular intervention for ruptured traumatic subclavian pseudoaneurysm when inflammation and risk of iatrogenic injury preclude safe open dissection. A hybrid or rescue endovascular strategy should be considered for similar complex trauma cases.Keywords: Subclavian artery injury, False aneurysm, Hemorrhage, Endovascular intervention, Case reports

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic subclavian artery injuries are rare, as the clavicles and ribs provide substantial protection to the subclavian arteries [1]. These injuries most frequently result from penetrating trauma, though blunt trauma can also cause arterial damage from bone fragments, leading to rupture, pseudoaneurysm, dissection, or thrombosis. Subclavian artery injury associated with clavicle fracture occurs in less than 1% of cases and can result in life-threatening hemorrhage or limb ischemia [2].

There is limited literature on the management of acute subclavian artery injuries caused by clavicle fractures, particularly regarding the use of endovascular [3,4] and open surgical approaches [5,6]. This report describes a rare case of subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm rupture due to a clavicular fracture, successfully managed with prompt conversion from open surgery to urgent endovascular treatment.

CASE REPORT

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ajou University Hospital (No. AJOUIRB-EX-2025-354). Written informed consent for publication of the research details and clinical images were obtained from the patient.

Patient information

A 48-year-old man presented to the trauma center after falling off his bicycle while riding downhill. He was wearing a helmet but briefly lost consciousness. Upon arrival, he reported chest and right shoulder pain. Radiologic evaluation revealed a right clavicle fracture, right hemothorax, and pulmonary contusion. He was hemodynamically stable, with a well-perfused right arm and a palpable radial pulse. Sensory function in the upper extremity was intact, and he could perform the thumbs-up and OK signs. The color and pulse of the right arm were preserved.

Diagnostic assessment

Anteroposterior x-ray and computed tomography (CT) of the right shoulder (Fig. 1A) demonstrated a comminuted midclavicular fracture with a small inferior “butterfly” fragment. A pseudoaneurysm was visible on the initial contrast-enhanced chest CT (Fig. 1B, C) but was overlooked during the primary survey. The patient was scheduled for open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the right clavicle 11 days after injury.

Initial computed tomography (CT) scans. (A) Shoulder CT scan (axial view). Contrast-enhanced chest CT scans of (B) axial view and (C) coronal view. Arrows indicate the missed right subclavian pseudoaneurysm.

Therapeutic intervention

During ORIF, the surgeon encountered a subclavian artery tear with a pseudoaneurysm related to the clavicle fracture. Upon exposure of the fracture site, the pseudoaneurysm ruptured, causing massive bleeding. Dissection of the injured subclavian artery was extremely difficult due to dense adhesion and fibrosis secondary to inflammation. Further isolation required dissection of the subclavian vein, but this was deemed hazardous because of severe inflammation and the high risk of venous injury. Therefore, conversion to an endovascular approach was undertaken. The patient was transferred from the operating room to the angiography suite while manual compression of the right subclavian artery was maintained (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative field image. Direct manual compression to the subclavian pseudoaneurysm can be seen. A vessel loop was used to retract the subclavian artery (arrowheads). The proximal subclavian artery was controlled by a vascular clamp. The arrow indicates the divided clavicle.

Endovascular repair began with percutaneous puncture of the right common femoral artery. A 9F sheath was placed in the common femoral artery, and angiography revealed rupture and contrast extravasation from the right subclavian pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 3A). A covered stent graft (8 mm×6 cm Covera Plus, BD) was deployed (Fig. 3B), followed by ballooning with a 7-mm distal and 8-mm proximal balloon to secure stent anchorage at the injury site. Post-deployment angiography and visual inspection confirmed cessation of bleeding (Fig. 3C). The patient was then returned to the operating room, and ORIF was completed using bone-fixation plate and cortical bone screws (Arix Hand System Bone Plate, Jeil Medical).

Angiographic findings. (A) Angiogram before stent graft insertion. Extravasation is indicated (arrow), and manual compression of injury site can be seen. (B) A stent graft was inserted. (C) Angiogram after stent graft insertion. (D) Upper extremity computed tomography angiography scan after 2 years.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient remained hemodynamically stable postoperatively and exhibited no neurological or vascular deficits upon awakening. He was transferred from the intensive care unit to the general ward on postoperative day 3 and discharged on day 5. At discharge, he was prescribed aspirin 100 mg daily and followed up at the outpatient clinic 4 weeks later. Physiotherapy commenced 4 weeks postoperatively, focusing on aggressive range of motion and gradual strengthening exercises. At 12 weeks, he regained full range of motion in the right shoulder. A CT angiogram of the upper extremity obtained 2 years after discharge showed no stent migration or vascular stenosis (Fig. 3D).

DISCUSSION

Subclavian artery injuries caused by either penetrating or blunt trauma are rare. According to the 2014 American Association for the Surgery of Trauma Prospective Observational Vascular Injury Treatment (PROOVIT) registry, subclavian artery injuries accounted for only 2.8% of all vascular injuries reported [7]. The incidence of concomitant axillosubclavian vessel injury in patients with clavicle fractures is 0.6% [8], and major neurovascular complications have been well documented in association with such fractures. These injuries may occur immediately at the time of fracture or manifest later as pseudoaneurysm, deep vein thrombosis, or thoracic outlet syndrome secondary to excessive callus formation [9]. Trauma to the subclavian artery remains one of the most challenging vascular injuries to manage, largely because of the difficulty in obtaining surgical access and the vessel’s proximity to vital anatomic structures. Traditionally, open exposure and direct vascular repair have been considered the standard treatment, using approaches such as median sternotomy, supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and transclavicular incisions [10].

Over the past three decades, the management of vascular trauma has evolved considerably with the advancement of endovascular techniques. Because of the technical challenges associated with achieving open vascular control and exposure, many institutions have recently reported successful use of endovascular repair for subclavian artery injuries, either as a standalone treatment or as part of a hybrid approach combining proximal endovascular balloon occlusion with open surgical repair [11]. Generally, endovascular treatment is reserved for hemodynamically stable patients undergoing conventional angiography and repair [12].

This case report describes a previously unrecognized subclavian artery injury associated with a clavicular fracture that was discovered intraoperatively as a ruptured pseudoaneurysm. The rupture resulted in massive hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability. Blood loss during surgery was estimated at approximately 3 L, and the patient’s blood pressure dropped to 76/40 mmHg. Although endovascular management of subclavian artery injury has been reported, its application in cases with delayed presentation within an orthopedic open surgical field has rarely been discussed.

The open approach in this case was further complicated by the patient’s anatomy and by fibrotic adhesions that had developed during the 11 days following trauma, making isolation of the subclavian artery extremely difficult. Additional dissection required for arterial control posed a high risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury, thereby making the endovascular approach a particularly appealing alternative for arterial repair.

This was an extremely urgent situation in which rupture of a pseudoaneurysm was encountered unexpectedly in the operative field. We initially attempted surgical exposure and repair of the subclavian artery; however, further dissection was impossible, and bleeding persisted. As a rescue measure, we rapidly converted to an endovascular approach. A hybrid strategy was implemented, involving placement of a covered stent in the subclavian artery after open exposure. Unlike prior reports that utilized proximal endovascular balloon occlusion for hemorrhage control, we employed direct manual compression.

This technique proved highly effective as a rescue method, achieving immediate hemostasis while minimizing the time required for vascular stent placement and reducing ischemic duration, since active arterial bleeding was already visible in the open field. In this patient, the covered stent graft was deployed at the transition between the subclavian and axillary arteries.

Stent graft placement in this region requires particular caution because of the potential risk of device-related complications. The close proximity of the stent to the clavicle or first rib raises concerns regarding fracture or stenosis due to shoulder motion [13]. Patel et al. [14] reported a case in which a stent graft used for penetrating subclavian artery injury fractured eight months after implantation. However, recent studies have demonstrated the long-term patency of axillary stent grafts for pseudoaneurysm exclusion and the durability of newer generation endografts [15]. Nevertheless, success is not universal, and meticulous patient selection with ongoing long-term surveillance remains essential. In our case, 2-year follow-up imaging confirmed the absence of stent fracture or stenosis.

Our case report also highlights the potential utility of the endovascular approach in patients with unstable trauma, particularly when open surgical dissection is complicated by distorted anatomy and hemorrhage into adjacent tissue planes. The benefits of rapid transition to endovascular management in emergent subclavian artery injury are clearly demonstrated here, and further evaluation is warranted regarding the role of endovascular therapy in managing traumatic vascular injuries.
