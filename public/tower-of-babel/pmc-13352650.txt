Analysis of factors associated with comorbid anxiety and depression in patients with functional dyspepsia: a cross‑sectional study
Authors: Jian Sun, Shalayiding Sufeila, Xiaoting Wang, Yongnian Ding, Ting Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore factors associated with comorbid anxiety and depression (CAD) in patients with functional dyspepsia (FD) and to compare the performance of logistic regression models (LRM) and decision tree models (DTM) in risk identification and assessment.MethodsA cross‑sectional design was used. A total of 1,227 FD patients were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Xinjiang between January 2023 and August 2024. Assessments included the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‑7 scale (GAD‑7), Patient Health Questionnaire‑9 (PHQ‑9), Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS), Multidimensional Health Locus of Control Scale (MHLC), and the Simplified Nepean Dyspepsia Index (SF‑NDI). Candidate variables were selected based on clinical knowledge and a causal framework (informed by directed acyclic graph principles). Binary logistic regression and a decision tree model using the CRT algorithm were used to analyse factors associated with CAD. To ensure fair comparison, the data were randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%), and both models were developed on the same training set and evaluated on the same test set. The decision tree was subjected to 10‑fold cross‑validation, and the logistic regression was assessed using the Hosmer‑Lemeshow goodness‑of‑fit test. A sensitivity analysis was performed using a higher cut‑off (≥ 10 on both GAD‑7 and PHQ‑9). ROC curves were plotted to compare the discriminative ability of the two models.ResultsThe detection rate of CAD was higher in female FD patients (33.9%) than in males (26.3%). Logistic regression showed that a higher score for powerful others health locus of control (PHLC) (OR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.001–1.043, P = 0.038), more severe gastrointestinal symptoms (OR = 1.050, 95% CI: 1.033–1.068, P < 0.001), and a higher SF‑NDI score (OR = 1.029, 95% CI: 1.012–1.048, P = 0.002) were associated with a higher likelihood of CAD; male sex, older age and higher education level were protective factors. In the decision tree model, SF‑NDI was the most important factor (normalised importance = 100%), followed by GSRS (68%), sex (52%), age (47%), education level (39%), chance health locus of control (CHLC, 33%) and PHLC (30%). On the test set, the LRM achieved an AUC of 0.710 (95% CI: 0.679–0.741) with a sensitivity of 74.1% and specificity of 58.1%; the DTM achieved an AUC of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.736–0.792) with a sensitivity of 71.4% and specificity of 66.6%. The mean AUC from 10‑fold cross‑validation was 0.708 ± 0.012 for the LRM and 0.759 ± 0.015 for the DTM. The Hosmer‑Lemeshow test gave P = 0.292, indicating good calibration of the logistic regression. In the sensitivity analysis using a cut‑off of ≥ 10, the direction and magnitude of effects for core variables remained largely unchanged (GSRS: OR = 1.047, P < 0.001; SF‑NDI: OR = 1.026, P = 0.003; PHLC: OR = 1.020, P = 0.048). Sex‑stratified analysis showed that SF‑NDI, GSRS, CHLC and age were the main factors in males, whereas PHLC and education level were additionally influential in females.ConclusionCAD in FD patients is clearly associated with sex, age, education level, health locus of control orientation, severity of gastrointestinal symptoms, and disease‑related quality of life. Logistic regression and decision tree models each have advantages in identifying associated factors and assessing risk; using them jointly can help clinicians identify high‑risk individuals early and develop personalised, integrated physical and psychological interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04928-w.Keywords: Functional dyspepsia, Comorbid anxiety and depression, Nepean Dyspepsia Index, Health locus of control, Logistic regression, Decision tree

Introduction

Functional dyspepsia is one of the most common functional gastrointestinal disorders in clinical practice. Patients typically present with postprandial fullness, early satiation, epigastric pain or a burning sensation [1, 2]. The global prevalence of FD exceeds 10%, and in Asian countries it ranges from approximately 5% to 30% [3–5], and it is also increasingly recognised in paediatric populations [6], placing a considerable burden on patients’ quality of life and healthcare systems. As the medical model shifts towards a bio-psycho-social perspective, the phenomenon of comorbidity has received increasing attention. Studies have shown that FD patients have significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression than the healthy population; approximately 80% of FD patients have an abnormal mental state, with 33.2% experiencing anxiety symptoms, 45.3% depressive symptoms, and 25.6% having both [7, 8]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis further confirmed that the prevalence and severity of anxiety and depression in FD are comparable to those observed in gastroparesis, underscoring the substantial psychological burden in this population [9]. The concept of comorbidity was first proposed by Feinstein in 1970, referring to the presence of two or more independently diagnosable diseases in the same individual [10, 11]. In the field of mental health, comorbid anxiety and depression (CAD) is one of the most common forms of comorbidity. CAD in FD patients is influenced by multiple factors, yet current research has focused mainly on pathological mechanisms, and the exploration of psychosocial factors remains insufficient.

The concept of locus of control originated from Rotter’s social learning theory [12]. Building on this, Wallston et al. introduced the concept of health locus of control, which refers to an individual’s beliefs about the determinants of their health, categorised as internal health locus of control (IHLC), chance health locus of control (CHLC) or powerful others health locus of control (PHLC) [13, 14]. Different health locus of control orientations affect how patients cope with disease and their emotional experience [15–17]. Many quantitative studies have examined the relationship between health locus of control and mental health [18], but the findings have been inconsistent. For example, some studies have shown that internal control is a protective factor associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression, whereas others have found no significant association or have suggested that external control is not a universal risk factor. At present, systematic investigations of the association between health locus of control and CAD specifically in FD patients are still rare.

FD is a chronic and relapsing functional gastrointestinal disorder [19] that not only causes persistent gastrointestinal discomfort but also easily triggers psychological disturbances such as anxiety and depression, as well as impaired social function, leading to a marked decline in quality of life. Although conventional drug treatment can partially alleviate physical symptoms [20], the accompanying psychological problems often persist. Therefore, it is of great clinical importance to comprehensively examine the relationships among gastrointestinal symptoms, health locus of control, quality of life and CAD.

Methodologically, a decision tree is a tree-structured classification and prediction model that can clearly display how variables influence the outcome and reveal complex interactions among variables [21]. Decision trees are non-parametric; they do not require a pre-specified functional form and can handle non-linear relationships and interaction effects, making them particularly suitable for exploring complex relationships that are difficult to express with simple functions [22]. In contrast, logistic regression is mainly used to quantify the independent effect of each factor, providing intuitive risk estimates such as odds ratios [23]. The two approaches are complementary: logistic regression excels at quantifying the strength of single factors, while decision trees are better at revealing conditional dependencies and subgroup heterogeneity.

Based on the above background, this study combined decision tree models and logistic regression models to systematically explore the associations among gastrointestinal symptoms, health locus of control, quality of life and CAD in FD patients, as well as sex differences, with the aim of providing references for early identification of high-risk patients and the development of personalised physical-psychological interventions.

Methods

Study population

This cross‑sectional survey was conducted in the Department of Gastroenterology of a tertiary hospital in Xinjiang from January 2023 to August 2024 using a convenience sampling method. A total of 1,250 FD patients were recruited. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) conscious, with basic literacy, able to complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance from the researcher; (3) voluntary participation and signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of serious organic gastrointestinal disease or other major physical illness; (2) previous diagnosis of psychiatric disorder or current receipt of professional psychiatric/psychological treatment; (3) severe cognitive impairment, visual/auditory impairment or language communication difficulties that prevented completion of the assessment; (4) refusal to participate or withdrawal during the survey. A total of 1,250 questionnaires were distributed, and 1,227 valid questionnaires were returned, giving an effective response rate of 98.1%. There were no missing data for any of the study variables in the final sample. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (approval No. XJYKDXR20241011004). All patients provided written informed consent, and the study procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement instruments

General information questionnaire: A self‑designed questionnaire was used to collect basic sociodemographic information including sex, age, place of residence, education level and total monthly household income. This questionnaire was specifically designed for the present study; its English version is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Assessment of anxiety and depression symptoms: Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the GAD‑7 scale [24], which has 7 items scored 0–3, total score 0–21, with a cut‑off of ≥ 5 for positive anxiety symptoms [25]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ‑9 scale [26], which has 9 items scored 0–3, total score 0–27, with a cut‑off of ≥ 5 for positive depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for both scales were 0.92. CAD was defined as GAD‑7 ≥ 5 and PHQ‑9 ≥ 5, reflecting the symptom burden of anxiety and depression rather than a formal psychiatric disorder. A sensitivity analysis was also performed using a higher cut‑off (≥ 10 on both scales).

Assessment of health locus of control: The MHLC scale [13] was used, which includes three subscales: internal health locus of control (IHLC), chance health locus of control (CHLC) and powerful others health locus of control (PHLC). It consists of 18 items, each rated 1–6, with higher scores indicating a stronger orientation toward that locus of control. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the three subscales were 0.80, 0.75 and 0.83, respectively.

Assessment of FD symptoms: The GSRS scale [27] was used to assess the severity of gastrointestinal symptoms over the previous week. It contains 15 items rated 1–4, with a total score ranging from 15 to 60; higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.85.

Assessment of health‑related quality of life: The SF‑NDI scale [28] was used to assess the impact of the disease on quality of life. It covers five dimensions (emotional state, daily activities, diet, disease perception, work/study) with 10 items rated 1–5; higher scores indicate poorer quality of life. The Cronbach’s α was 0.88.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent‑samples t‑tests. Non‑normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann‑Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi‑square test.

To avoid the limitations of relying solely on univariate significance for variable selection, we used a combination of clinical knowledge and a causal framework to guide variable inclusion. All clinically meaningful variables were entered into the multivariable models.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for all continuous independent variables, with VIF < 5 considered indicative of no serious multicollinearity.

To ensure a fair comparison between the logistic regression and decision tree models, the entire dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%, n = 859) and a test set (30%, n = 368) using a fixed random seed (20240504). Both models were developed only on the training set and evaluated on the same test set. The decision tree was built using the CRT algorithm, with a maximum tree depth of 5, a minimum parent node size of 50 and a minimum child node size of 10; 10‑fold cross‑validation was performed. Calibration of the logistic regression was assessed using the Hosmer‑Lemeshow goodness‑of‑fit test. ROC curves were plotted with CAD as the state variable, and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity and Youden index were calculated.

To examine the robustness of the findings to the choice of cut‑off, CAD was redefined using a higher threshold (GAD‑7 ≥ 10 and PHQ‑9 ≥ 10; CAD_10), and the main logistic regression analysis was repeated. All statistical tests were two‑sided, and the significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Logistic regression analysis

The logistic regression model developed on the training set and evaluated on the test set indicated that sex, age, education level, PHLC, gastrointestinal symptoms and SF‑NDI were independent factors associated with CAD in FD patients. Female sex, higher PHLC score, more severe gastrointestinal symptoms and higher SF‑NDI score were associated with an increased risk of CAD, whereas male sex, older age and higher education level were protective factors (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Decision tree model analysis

The CRT decision tree developed on the training set (with 10‑fold cross‑validation) had 5 levels and 17 terminal nodes. The variable importance ranking was: SF‑NDI (normalised importance = 100%), GSRS (68%), sex (52%), age (47%), education level (39%), CHLC (33%) and PHLC (30%). SF‑NDI was the root node, indicating that poorer quality of life (higher SF‑NDI score) was associated with a higher likelihood of CAD (Fig. 2).

Model performance comparison and internal validation

On the test set (n = 368), the logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.710 (95% CI: 0.679–0.741), a sensitivity of 74.1%, a specificity of 58.1% and a Youden index of 0.322. The Hosmer‑Lemeshow test gave χ² = 9.624, P = 0.292, indicating good calibration. The decision tree model achieved an AUC of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.736–0.792), a sensitivity of 71.4%, a specificity of 66.6% and a Youden index of 0.380. The mean AUC from 10‑fold cross‑validation was 0.759 ± 0.015, with a mean sensitivity of 70.9% and mean specificity of 66.2%; the Brier score was 0.183 (< 0.2), indicating good calibration. The cross‑validation results were highly consistent with the test‑set results, suggesting that model performance was stable and not overestimated due to a single data split. The decision tree had higher specificity and Youden index, while the logistic regression had higher sensitivity; the two models were complementary (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

When CAD was redefined using a cut‑off of ≥ 10 on both GAD‑7 and PHQ‑9, the prevalence of moderate‑to‑severe CAD was 11.8% (145/1,227). Multivariable logistic regression showed that the direction and significance of the core variables remained largely unchanged: GSRS (OR = 1.047, 95% CI: 1.028–1.066, P < 0.001), SF‑NDI (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.009–1.044, P = 0.003), PHLC (OR = 1.020, 95% CI: 1.000–1.041, P = 0.048), and the protective factors also showed the same direction as in the primary analysis. This indicates that the results are robust and not substantially influenced by the choice of cut‑off.

Sex‑stratified analysis

The CAD detection rate was 26.3% in males and 33.9% in females. Sex‑stratified logistic regression and decision tree analyses showed significant heterogeneity in factors associated with CAD between sexes. In males, SF‑NDI, GSRS, CHLC and age were the main associated factors; in females, in addition to these factors, PHLC and education level played a more prominent role (Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7). This finding also helps explain the higher CAD detection rate in females.

Discussion

Using cross‑sectional data from 1,227 patients with functional dyspepsia, this study systematically analysed the associations of demographic characteristics, gastrointestinal symptoms, disease‑related quality of life and health locus of control with comorbid anxiety and depression, and compared the performance of logistic regression and decision tree models in risk identification. The results showed that sex, age, education level, health locus of control orientation, severity of gastrointestinal symptoms and quality of life were clearly associated with CAD, and that there were significant differences between males and females. The decision tree model outperformed logistic regression in terms of specificity and risk stratification, whereas logistic regression had higher sensitivity; the two were complementary.

We found a higher risk of CAD in female FD patients compared to males (OR = 0.674), which is consistent with a previous study of 907 FD patients [29]. This sex difference may be related to differences in hormone levels, emotion regulation strategies and social role expectations. Oestrogen fluctuations can affect neurotransmitter systems involved in emotion, increasing sensitivity to negative emotions [30]; screening instruments have also demonstrated acceptable accuracy for identifying depression and anxiety in female populations [31]; women tend to use emotion‑focused coping and internalise stress, thereby increasing the risk of anxiety and depression [32, 33]. Moreover, sociocultural factors may make women more willing to express emotional distress, and their psychological symptoms may be more readily recognised when they seek medical help. Higher education level was associated with a lower risk of CAD, suggesting that education may play a protective role by enhancing health literacy, disease understanding and coping abilities [34, 35]. Previous studies have confirmed that female sex and low socioeconomic status (low education or income) are important predictors of psychological comorbidity in FD patients [36]. Therefore, clinical assessment and intervention should pay special attention to the emotional state of female FD patients and provide sex‑tailored psychological support; health education for patients with low education levels is also an important direction for CAD prevention.

Among disease‑related factors, quality of life as measured by SF‑NDI was the factor most strongly associated with CAD. In our decision tree model, SF‑NDI appeared as the root node and had the highest importance, which is consistent with the findings of Sperber et al. Previous studies have confirmed that FD patients often have impaired health‑related quality of life [37]. FD patients with better quality of life feel more confident in solving problems and tend to remain optimistic and adopt active coping strategies when facing the disease, thus having a lower risk of anxiety and depression [38]. The severity of gastrointestinal symptoms (GSRS) was also closely associated with CAD, in line with Ford et al. [39]. Some studies have shown that the “severity of gastrointestinal symptoms” itself is a strong predictor of anxiety, sometimes even surpassing SF‑NDI [40]. Physical discomfort can activate sustained stress response systems such as the hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis and autonomic dysfunction, thereby exacerbating mood disorders; conversely, anxiety and depression can aggravate gastrointestinal perception through the brain‑gut axis [41], creating a vicious cycle. Emerging evidence further suggests that mind–body interventions such as yoga may reduce anxiety and depression symptoms across a range of clinical populations, offering a potential adjunctive approach worth investigating in FD patients [42]. This suggests that healthcare providers should actively attend to patients’ mental health status and integrate psychological guidance into the diagnostic and treatment process, while also adopting interdisciplinary strategies combining gastroenterology and clinical psychology to simultaneously improve symptoms and mood.

Regarding health locus of control, we found that a PHLC orientation was associated with a higher risk of CAD (OR = 1.022), consistent with Li et al. [43]. Patients who tend to rely on doctors or other health authority figures have stronger dependence on healthcare providers, lower self‑initiative and sense of control over their illness, and are more likely to adopt immature coping strategies, which may exacerbate gastrointestinal symptoms and worsen anxiety and depression. Cheng et al. [44] also showed that patients with an external locus of control tend to adopt passive, laissez‑faire coping strategies and exhibit more negative emotions than those with an internal locus. In addition, our decision tree model also found that in male FD patients, CHLC (chance control) was a factor associated with CAD. Patients who attribute their health to luck or fate take a “let it be” attitude toward the disease, have low personal health responsibility and lack trust in medical staff, which may lead to worsening symptoms, accelerated disease progression and poorer mental health [45]. These findings suggest that in clinical practice, attention should be paid to assessing patients’ health locus of control orientation, and through health education and self‑management, patients should be gradually guided from an external to an internal locus of control, helping them develop correct health beliefs and promote healthy behaviours.

Methodologically, logistic regression and decision trees were highly complementary, which is consistent with previous studies [46, 47]. The CRT‑based decision tree clearly displayed interactions among variables and the importance of each variable in different subgroups [48, 49]. In contrast, binary logistic regression provided quantitative relationships (e.g., ORs) but was less capable of showing interactions [50, 51]. In this study, the LRM had an AUC of 0.71 and the DTM had an AUC of 0.764. The decision tree had higher specificity, indicating that it was better at correctly identifying non‑CAD patients, whereas the logistic regression had higher sensitivity, making it more suitable for initial screening. Therefore, we recommend using both models jointly in future similar studies to provide a more comprehensive evidence base for developing intervention strategies.

Limitations: This study has several limitations. First, the cross‑sectional design does not allow causal inference, and a bidirectional relationship between gastrointestinal symptoms and psychological distress (brain‑gut axis) has been established; reverse causality is possible, and longitudinal studies are needed to verify causal pathways. Second, the data came from a single‑centre convenience sample, and the study hospital was a tertiary referral centre, where patients are likely to have more severe symptoms and more active healthcare‑seeking behaviour than the general FD population; this may lead to overestimation of the CAD prevalence and some association measures, and caution is needed when generalising the results to community or primary care settings. Third, the exclusion of patients with known psychiatric disorders, while avoiding confounding by pre‑existing mental illness, may have resulted in an underestimation of the true association between FD and CAD. Fourth, all psychological and symptom measures were based on self‑report scales; although standardised and validated, recall bias and social desirability bias cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, in the sex‑stratified analysis, some subgroups (e.g., males aged ≥ 80 years) were small, leading to wide confidence intervals; the results should be interpreted cautiously. Sixth, we did not include potentially important variables such as sleep quality, social support, life events or medication history, which may play moderating or mediating roles in the relationship between FD and CAD. Future studies should use larger, multi‑centre samples, include a broader range of psychosocial variables, and apply path analysis or structural equation modelling to further elucidate the mechanisms.

Conclusion

In summary, sex, age, education level, orientation towards powerful others health locus of control, orientation towards chance health locus of control, severity of gastrointestinal symptoms, and the quality of life reflected by the Nepean Dyspepsia Index are all independent factors associated with the symptom burden of anxiety and depression in patients with functional dyspepsia, and their patterns of influence differ significantly between sexes. Logistic regression and decision tree models each have advantages in identifying factors associated with CAD; using them together provides more comprehensive risk assessment. We recommend integrating psychosocial assessment into routine clinical practice for FD and implementing sex‑specific interventions targeting health locus of control to improve patients’ physical and mental health and quality of life.
