Dermoscopic Characterization of Granular Parakeratosis: Toward a Recognizable Pattern
Authors: Caterina Mariarosaria Giorgio, Michela Liguori, Mariachiara Arisi, Giuseppe Argenziano, Gaetano Licata
Published: 2026

Introduction

Granular parakeratosis (GP) represents a rare, self-limiting keratinization disorder, most often encountered in intertriginous sites. Its clinical presentation, usually consisting of brownish hyperpigmented plaques with subtle scaling, may mimic several more common dermatoses, including acanthosis nigricans, inverse psoriasis, erythrasma, and candidiasis. While the histopathological features of GP—parakeratosis with retention of keratohyalin granules—are well established, its dermoscopic spectrum remains incompletely defined, with only a handful of descriptions in the literature to date [1,2].

Case Presentation

We herein report two additional cases that expand on these early insights and highlight reproducible dermoscopic findings that may support a noninvasive diagnosis. Both patients were middle-aged females who presented with slowly enlarging, bilaterally symmetrical axillary plaques. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of GP, but what proved particularly informative was the concordant dermoscopic profile. Using a polarized dermatoscope at 20× magnification, we observed multiple sharply demarcated brown-to-grayish globules, either isolated or confluent in reticulated fields. In addition, coiled terminal hairs forming circular “hair circles” were consistently visible beneath the parakeratotic surface. This phenomenon is thought to reflect a temporary obstruction of the follicular ostium by compact keratin, altering the trajectory of emerging hairs (Figure 1). Although hair circles have been described in other settings, their recurrent presence in GP suggests a mechanical correlate between abnormal cornification and transient follicular blockade.

The diagnostic value of these features lies primarily in their differentiation from the dermoscopic patterns of common mimickers. Unlike inverse psoriasis, no dotted vascular structure wasobserved, and in contrast to acanthosis nigricans, the pigmentation was globular rather than diffuse and velvety. Erythrasma, which fluoresces coral-red under Wood’s lamp, and candidal intertrigo, often characterized by pustules, were also excluded clinically. The absence of vascular elements, coupled with the prominence of pigmented globules and hair circles, therefore emerges as a distinctive constellation, consistent with prior observations [1,2].

Conclusion

We acknowledge that the limited number of cases precludes broad generalization. Dermoscopy in GP should not be considered pathognomonic, and the features described here may overlap with other disorders of cornification. Nonetheless, the reproducibility of these findings in both patients and their alignment with recently published case series [1,2] strengthen their potential role as useful diagnostic clues. In clinical practice, the ability to raise suspicion for GP without immediate biopsy can reduce unnecessary treatments, avoid patient anxiety, and guide more judicious use of invasive procedures. In conclusion, our observations add to the growing evidence that dermoscopy can assist in the recognition of granular parakeratosis. The combination of sharply demarcated brown globules and hair circles may represent characteristic, though not exclusive, dermoscopic signs. Further systematic investigations, ideally involving larger patient cohorts, are warranted to confirm their diagnostic specificity and to define their place in the broader spectrum of keratinization disorders.
