From Proxies to Fields: Spatiotemporal Reconstruction of Global Radiation from Sparse Sensor Sequences
Kazuma Kobayashi, Samrendra Roy, Seid Koric, Diab Abueidda, Syed Bahauddin Alam

Abstract
Accurate reconstruction of latent environmental fields from sparse and indirect observations is a foundational challenge across scientific domains—from atmospheric science and geophysics to public health and aerospace safety. Traditional approaches rely on physics-based simulators or dense sensor networks, both constrained by high computational cost, latency, or limited spatial coverage. We present the Temporal Radiation Operator Network (TRON) , a spatiotemporal neural operator architecture designed to infer continuous global scalar fields from sequences of sparse, non-uniform proxy measurements. Unlike recent forecasting models that operate on dense, gridded inputs to predict future states, TRON addresses a more ill-posed inverse problem: reconstructing the current global field from sparse, temporally evolving sensor sequences, without access to future observations or dense labels. Demonstrated on global cosmic radiation dose reconstruction, TRON is trained on 22 years of simulation data and generalizes across 65,341 spatial locations, 8,400 days, and sequence lengths from 7 to 90 days. It achieves sub-second inference with relative L 2 L_{2} errors below 0.1%, representing a > > 58,000× speedup over Monte Carlo-based estimators. Though evaluated in the context of cosmic radiation, TRON offers a domain-agnostic framework for scientific field reconstruction from sparse data, with applications in atmospheric modeling, geophysical hazard monitoring, and real-time environmental risk forecasting.

Introduction

Reconstructing high-resolution spatial fields from sparse and indirect observations defines a class of inverse problems spanning climate science, seismology, remote sensing, and environmental health. These inverse problems involve estimating latent physical states from limited, noisy proxies, often in real-time and across large, under-instrumented areas. While traditional simulation-based methods can model forward physics with high fidelity, they are often computationally prohibitive and brittle under data sparsity. This has motivated a shift toward flexible, data-driven frameworks capable of generalizing across space and time without explicit simulation pipelines.

Real-time, high-resolution reconstruction of global radiation fields from sparse observational data remains a fundamental challenge in space weather science and environmental health. At aviation altitudes and in space, the intensity of ionizing radiation can far exceed terrestrial levels—posing risks to passengers, flight crews, astronauts, and high-altitude infrastructure. During solar energetic particle (SEP) events or geomagnetic storms, radiation dose rates can spike by several orders of magnitude within hours, triggering operational concerns across civil aviation, satellite communication, and spaceflight. Although the health effects of acute high-dose exposure are well documented, the long-term consequences of chronic low-dose radiation remain ambiguous. Observational studies point to elevated cancer incidence, cardiovascular and neurocognitive effects, and genomic instability among high-risk cohorts
Cucinotta and Durante 2006
;
Durante and Cucinotta 2008
;
Cherry et al. 2012
;
Guéguinou et al. 2009
, yet the interpretation is complicated by latency, confounders, and uncertainties in dose reconstruction.

To manage these risks, international regulatory frameworks rely on models of effective dose and long-term biological impact. Chief among them is the Linear No-Threshold (LNT) model, which postulates that all ionizing radiation carries risk in direct proportion to dose, with no safe threshold
Beir 2005
;
National Council on Radiation Protection and Measurements 2001
. While operationally conservative, this model remains controversial. Recent biological evidence suggests the possibility of adaptive responses to low-dose radiation, consistent with hormesis or threshold-based models
Calabrese and Baldwin 2003
;
Wolff 1998
. Regardless of model preference, there is wide consensus on the need for large-scale, high-resolution, and temporally resolved monitoring of radiation exposure—both to refine dose-response models and to support real-time decision-making in operational domains.

However, real-time radiation monitoring poses unique challenges. Cosmic radiation cannot be measured directly at every location. Instead, global monitoring systems rely on ground-based neutron monitors that detect secondary neutrons produced when primary cosmic rays interact with Earth’s atmosphere
Simpson 2000
;
Usoskin et al. 2010
. These detectors provide sparse, location-fixed observations that act as indirect proxies for the global radiation field. Inferring effective dose from these measurements requires simulating the full cascade of cosmic ray propagation, atmospheric modulation, secondary particle generation, and energy deposition in biological tissue. This forward modeling process is computationally intensive, often requiring atmospheric models, geomagnetic cutoff calculations, and Monte Carlo particle transport simulations. In practice, these steps introduce latency and scalability constraints that make real-time global estimation infeasible.

A number of dose estimation systems have been developed to address this problem, each making trade-offs between physical accuracy and computational speed. CARI-7
Copeland 2017
, developed by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), uses precomputed lookup tables to estimate flight route doses under average conditions. While fast, it lacks responsiveness to transient space weather events. More recent frameworks like EXPACS (EXcel-based Program for calculating Atmospheric Cosmic-ray Spectrum) and PARMA (PHITS-based Analytical Radiation Model in the Atmosphere)
Sato 2015
;
Sato 2016
improve flexibility by incorporating analytical models of particle spectra and dose conversion factors derived from Monte Carlo simulations. However, they still rely on simplified assumptions about geomagnetic rigidity, atmospheric depth, and solar modulation, and require significant computation to produce high-resolution global maps. In dynamic operational environments, these methods remain bottlenecked by latency and rigidity, particularly when evaluating dose fields under changing conditions.

This case illustrates a more general class of problems, where the goal is not just to simulate physical processes forward, but to infer spatially distributed latent fields from sparse, temporally evolving proxy data. Unlike forecasting applications that rely on dense reanalysis datasets and field-to-field mappings, inverse field estimation demands a model that can reconstruct the underlying system state without direct access to all physical drivers.

The growing availability of sensor data and advances in machine learning have motivated new approaches to real-time, data-driven environmental modeling. Deep learning models have demonstrated the ability to approximate high-dimensional, nonlinear functions with remarkable fidelity, enabling surrogate models for complex scientific simulations across physics, chemistry, and climate science. Its ability to extract high-dimensional representations from structured and unstructured data has facilitated breakthroughs in weather forecasting
Hewage et al. 2021
;
Bi et al. 2023
, climate modeling
Rasp et al. 2018
;
Eyring et al. 2024
, and environmental monitoring
Han et al. 2023
;
Himeur et al. 2022
. These advancements stem from deep learning models’ capacity to approximate nonlinear mappings between input and output spaces, offering an alternative to computationally expensive numerical simulations. A key challenge in cosmic radiation modeling is predicting a single numerical output and an entire spatially distributed dose field that varies over time. Unlike typical deep learning tasks that classify images or forecast scalar time series, this problem requires models that generalize across spatial domains and capture temporal dependencies in radiation fluctuations. Various deep learning architectures have been proposed for spatiotemporal modeling, each with strengths and limitations.

In radiation modeling and other environmental forecasting domains, deep learning has primarily been applied to fixed-grid prediction and local dose estimation tasks. Traditional approaches rely heavily on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for spatial representation and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architectures, for temporal sequence modeling. CNNs excel at capturing local spatial dependencies through hierarchical receptive fields and have demonstrated effectiveness in a variety of geophysical applications. For example, George et al.
George et al. 2021
used CNNs to infer ocean eddy heat fluxes from satellite-derived sea surface height anomalies, reconstructing mesoscale turbulence with high spatial accuracy. Similarly, Panahi et al.
Panahi et al. 2020
applied CNNs to groundwater potential mapping, achieving superior performance over conventional regression models by learning nonlinear interactions among environmental variables.

RNN-based models have proven effective for learning temporal dynamics from sequential data streams, particularly in agricultural yield forecasting
Cheng et al. 2024
, traffic prediction
Guan et al. 2024
, and sensor-driven monitoring. In GT-LSTM
Cheng et al. 2024
, for instance, the authors coupled LSTM layers with remote sensing inputs to predict wheat yield across scales, capturing spatiotemporal variability driven by weather fluctuations. Guan et al.
Guan et al. 2024
introduced a hybrid GCN-GRU model to forecast traffic flow by combining graph-based spatial learning with temporal memory units, demonstrating the value of integrating topological structure with sequential modeling.

However, these successes are largely confined to well-instrumented domains with dense, gridded observations and direct mappings between inputs and outputs. When extended to under-instrumented or inverse settings—where inputs are sparse, indirect, and irregular—these models struggle to generalize spatially or capture system dynamics robustly.

Despite their successes, these architectures face fundamental limitations in field-based prediction tasks where both spatial generalization and temporal coherence are required. CNNs, by construction, operate over fixed-grid inputs and rely on localized filters, making them sensitive to spatial resolution and grid alignment. This restricts their ability to interpolate across irregular or sparse spatial observations—a common scenario in domains like radiation monitoring, where sensor coverage is nonuniform. Similarly, while LSTMs and GRUs effectively model temporal dependencies, they lack intrinsic spatial awareness and produce outputs that are not inherently structured over continuous fields. Hybrid models such as CNN-GRU or GNN-LSTM attempt to bridge these gaps by combining spatial and temporal modules, but they still operate over discrete input-output pairs and often require extensive tuning for each spatial configuration.

Moreover, the architectural coupling to grid-structured data means these models lack portability across different sensor layouts or spatial meshes—a limitation that undermines their use in flexible, scalable monitoring systems. In effect, they remain static models in a dynamic world.

Moreover, these models typically assume static architectures tied to specific sensor layouts or fixed input sizes, which undermines their capacity to generalize across evolving monitoring networks or varying sequence lengths. In radiation estimation, where input observations are sparse, temporally dynamic, and spatially coupled through physical processes, these limitations become especially pronounced. Consequently, while CNN- and RNN-based models offer useful tools for structured data forecasting, they fall short of providing the flexible, continuous, and generalizable representations needed for real-time, global field reconstruction from sparse measurements.

These challenges signal the need for a new class of models—ones that can infer latent spatial fields from temporally evolving, non-gridded, and proxy observations, without rigid assumptions on sensor layout or input dimensionality.

To overcome these limitations in spatial generalization and architectural flexibility, a new class of models—neural operators—has emerged as a transformative approach to learning continuous mappings between functions. Rather than treating input-output pairs as finite-dimensional vectors or tensors, neural operators model relationships directly in function space, enabling inference at arbitrary spatial coordinates and across diverse sensor configurations. This function-to-function learning paradigm allows models to operate over irregularly sampled inputs, handle varying discretizations, and generalize to out-of-distribution geometries—capabilities that are essential for scientific systems governed by physical laws or sparse measurements.

Among neural operator architectures, the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
Li et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
and Graph Neural Operator (GNO)
Li et al. 2020
have demonstrated strong performance in approximating partial differential equation (PDE) solutions over structured and unstructured domains, respectively. FNO leverages spectral convolution to learn translation-invariant operators, while GNO applies message-passing over graph structures to capture nonlocal interactions. However, both methods present key limitations in sensor-driven applications: FNO requires fixed grid resolutions, making it less adaptable to sparse or dynamically changing sensor layouts, and GNO depends on carefully crafted graph connectivity, which may not be well-defined or stable in real-world monitoring systems.

In contrast, the Deep Operator Network (DeepONet)
Lu et al. 2021
offers a more flexible and sensor-agnostic framework. Its architecture decouples the encoding of input functions and spatial query points via a dual-network structure: a branch network that processes the input function, and a trunk network that encodes the target spatial coordinates. This separation allows DeepONet to learn spatially continuous output fields conditioned on sparse, irregular inputs—making it particularly well-suited for problems such as sensor fusion, physical surrogate modeling, and field reconstruction. Recent applications of DeepONet to heat transfer, material deformation, and additive manufacturing
Koric and Abueidda 2023
;
He et al. 2024a
;
Koric et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024b
;
Kushwaha et al. 2024
illustrate its ability to replace expensive simulators while preserving high predictive fidelity.

However, DeepONet and other neural operator architectures have so far focused on static input-output mappings, limiting their applicability to problems where field evolution is driven by temporally structured proxy observations. In radiation and many other domains, the quantity of interest is shaped by cumulative or lagged drivers, making time-awareness a prerequisite for reliable inference.

While neural operator architectures such as DeepONet offer significant advantages in modeling spatially continuous functions from sparse inputs, they exhibit notable limitations when applied to temporally evolving systems. In their standard form, these models assume static input functions and are architecturally agnostic to temporal ordering, thereby discarding crucial sequence-dependent patterns. This design choice is particularly restrictive in systems where the present state is informed by a history of evolving dynamics–such as radiation exposure driven by cumulative solar modulation, atmospheric changes, or magnetospheric disturbances. In such cases, encoding only the current snapshot of observations erases the long-range temporal dependencies that govern dose field evolution. As a result, conventional DeepONet models struggle to distinguish transient events from persistent trends and are unable to learn the dynamical memory inherent in many geophysical and space weather phenomena.

Moreover, efforts to retrofit time-awareness into DeepONet by stacking snapshots or concatenating time steps into static vectors introduce artificial independence across temporal observations. These approximations undermine the architecture’s ability to represent history as a latent trajectory and often lead to overfitting, poor extrapolation, or degraded generalization to unseen temporal windows. More fundamentally, most existing operator networks are not designed to perform inference on function-valued sequences—that is, where the input itself is a time-indexed function. This mismatch between model design and data structure is a central limitation in extending neural operators to dynamic monitoring tasks.

Addressing this gap requires an integrated solution—one that preserves the spatial flexibility of operator learning while explicitly modeling temporal dynamics from sequential proxy observations. This calls for an architecture that is not only resolution- and layout-agnostic, but also temporally aware and dynamically conditioned.

TRON augments the classical DeepONet framework by embedding a recurrent neural encoder—either LSTM or GRU—within the branch network, enabling the model to ingest temporally ordered neutron monitor observations and encode their cumulative dynamics into a coherent latent representation. This temporal enrichment equips the model to capture long-range dependencies and event-driven modulations in space weather that static operator formulations inherently miss. The trunk network retains DeepONet’s core strength: it maps arbitrary spatial query coordinates to the continuous output field, allowing the model to produce dose estimates over unstructured or high-resolution grids without retraining or architectural modification.

This architectural fusion—temporal recurrence in the branch and spatial generalization in the trunk—recasts radiation dose estimation as a spatiotemporal inverse operator problem. Rather than simulating intermediate physical variables such as geomagnetic cutoffs, particle fluxes, or secondary cascade spectra, TRON directly maps temporal sequences of sensor data to global radiation fields. It bypasses simulation-based pipelines and avoids lookup-based surrogates, enabling near-instantaneous field reconstructions from sparse and irregular observations. Unlike CNN-RNN hybrids, which operate on discretized grid tensors, TRON is inherently resolution-agnostic and can predict at any spatial location with arbitrary precision. Moreover, the model generalizes across sequence lengths—from 7-day to 90-day histories—without retraining, making it adaptable to different operational and scientific forecasting horizons.

Although demonstrated here in the context of cosmic radiation, TRON offers a general methodology for real-time, proxy-to-field inference in sparse-data regimes. Its architecture is readily extensible to other domains requiring temporally conditioned spatial inference—such as airborne pollution tracking, seismic wavefield reconstruction, or epidemiological spread modeling from limited case data.

In this study, we apply TRON to the inverse problem of effective dose field estimation at sea level. Trained on 22 years of simulated radiation data from EXPACS—comprising 8,400 daily maps and over 65,000 global locations per day—TRON learns a temporally conditioned global operator that accurately reconstructs dose fields from just 12 fixed neutron monitor sequences. Its single-branch architecture jointly encodes sensor inputs into a shared latent space, preserving inter-sensor correlations that reflect the physical coupling of radiation modulation across Earth’s surface. This design outperforms multi-branch variants, which treat station inputs independently and suffer from fragmented representations. TRON achieves relative

Unlike prior works, which interpolate within dense fields, TRON extrapolates across space and time, enabling real-time dose reconstruction under unseen sequences and arbitrary spatial queries. This inverse operator formulation establishes a new class of models that fuse temporal memory, spatial generalization, and data sparsity resilience, positioning TRON as a foundation model for field estimation in under-instrumented regimes.

Whereas DGMR, MetNet-2, and NeuralGCM are primarily trained to interpolate or extrapolate within densely observed domains, TRON extrapolates across both space and time under extreme data sparsity. 2.1
Problem Formulation: From Physical Simulations to Inverse Estimation

Cosmic radiation poses a persistent environmental hazard with direct relevance to aviation safety, space missions, and high-latitude public health. Its global and temporal variability is influenced by solar activity, geomagnetic shielding, and atmospheric propagation effects, necessitating accurate field estimation for risk mitigation. Traditional forward modeling of this radiation environment remains computationally prohibitive due to the cost of high-fidelity physics-based simulations.

2.2
Spatiotemporal Operator Architecture: TRON

We introduce the
Temporal Radiation Operator Network (TRON)
, a novel spatiotemporal neural operator architecture purpose-built for high-resolution field reconstruction from sparse, time-varying observations. Unlike traditional forecasting models that assume dense spatiotemporal inputs, TRON operates in severely under-instrumented regimes, learning an inverse operator from sequences of proxy measurements to continuous output fields.

TRON extends operator learning principles to spatiotemporal inverse inference, drawing on conceptual ideas from frameworks such as the Deep Operator Network (DeepONet)
Lu et al. 2021
while addressing dynamic, underdetermined problems. Unlike conventional neural networks that operate on fixed-size input vectors, DeepONet learns an operator
\mathcal{G}
that maps an input function
u(x)
to an output function
v(y)
:

\mathcal{G}:u(x)\mapsto v(y).

(6)

The operator
\mathcal{G}
is approximated using two neural sub-networks. The
branch network
encodes samples of the input function
u(x)
evaluated at discrete sensor locations, producing a latent representation. The
trunk network
encodes the coordinates
y
at which the output function
v(y)
is evaluated. The final output is formed by combining these two representations through an inner product or element-wise fusion.

However, this original formulation assumes static inputs and lacks mechanisms for handling time-varying signals
Lu et al. 2021
;
He et al. 2023
;

In many real-world settings, including space weather and atmospheric monitoring, the input data are inherently sequential, capturing dynamic physical processes evolving over time. Neutron count sequences fall into this category, reflecting temporal modulation due to solar cycles and atmospheric variability. These time-dependent patterns necessitate an architecture that learns from evolving input sequences, rather than static snapshots.

TRON introduces a temporal learning component into the operator framework, enabling it to model spatiotemporal mappings from multivariate sensor sequences to target fields.

TRON consists of a branch encoder for temporal signals and a trunk encoder for spatial queries. These networks learn latent representations of historical neutron sequences and spatial coordinates, respectively—transforming the original DeepONet structure into a fully spatiotemporal neural operator designed for inverse field estimation.

The temporal encoder
\mathcal{B}

Each spatial point, represented in terms of Cartesian coordinates (Long, Lat), is fed into the trunk network. This component captures spatial structure and ensures that predictions reflect geographic trends and large-scale atmospheric variation. where

are the elements of the latent vector from the trunk network, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the learnable bias term.

\mathbf{X}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{P}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}

(15)

where
X_{j}

j

Performance evaluation across sequence lengths

To preserve temporal coherence and prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned chronologically into training, validation, and test subsets. The most recent 365 days were reserved for testing, ensuring that model performance was assessed on temporally unseen data. The remaining data were split evenly into training and validation sets. All neutron count measurements were normalized using min-max scaling parameters derived from the training set and applied consistently to validation and test data. The effective dose rates from EXPACS simulations were similarly normalized.

Each dataset was transformed into a sequential format to enable temporal learning. Sequences of length 7, 30, 60, and 90 days were constructed using a sliding window strategy, where each input comprised a temporal slice of neutron counts, and the target corresponded to the effective dose rate at the final time step. Spatial inputs–longitude and latitude coordinates for 65,341 evaluation points–remained fixed and were normalized to [0, 1] using global min-max scaling. This ensured consistency across all input formats and allowed for fair comparison across models.

Experiments were conducted to quantify the impact of input sequence length on predictive accuracy. Each model was trained and validated on its respective sequence-transformed dataset and evaluated on a hold-out test set. As longer input sequences reduce the number of available samples, strict numerical comparisons across sequence lengths are inherently biased. Nevertheless, these evaluations reveal consistent trends in model robustness and sensitivity to sequence duration. Configuration details for each dataset variant are included in Supplementary

3
Discussion

TRON introduces a new computational paradigm for inverse field estimation by framing the task as a spatiotemporal operator learning problem. This methodological shift enables inference in under-instrumented scientific domains where conventional field-to-field mappings or dense observational grids are unavailable. While demonstrated in the context of global radiation dose reconstruction, the architecture is broadly applicable to environmental, geophysical, and biomedical systems characterized by sparse, proxy-based measurements.

A primary source of TRON’s accuracy lies in the architecture of its single-branch variants, which preserve global coherence by jointly encoding temporal dependencies across all monitoring stations. By concatenating sensor inputs into a unified representation, the model internalizes shared temporal dynamics—such as those driven by solar modulation or geomagnetic disturbances—that manifest synchronously across the globe. This design facilitates cross-sensor correlation capture, enabling superior reconstruction of large-scale radiation fields.

Conversely, multi-branch configurations exhibit degraded performance, particularly for longer sequences, due to their structural constraint of treating sensors independently. The absence of inter-encoder communication imposes an artificial assumption of spatial independence, which limits the model’s capacity to learn coupled space-weather signatures. Late-stage latent fusion via element-wise multiplication compresses sensor-specific features without preserving their joint temporal evolution, ultimately suppressing predictive fidelity.

These architectural limitations are especially pronounced in cosmic radiation monitoring, where space weather phenomena—such as solar energetic particle (SEP) events and Forbush decreases—affect neutron monitors globally. Our empirical analysis confirms this hypothesis: neutron monitor time series exhibit synchronous perturbations during space weather events, validating the need for unified temporal representations. Architectures that embed this shared structure, such as the single-branch LSTM variant, consistently outperform those that assume statistical independence across sensors.

This global coupling hypothesis was validated through empirical correlation analysis of neutron count time series. The results confirmed synchronized perturbations across stations during major space weather events, supporting the theoretical motivation for a unified representation. Single-branch models, by design, internalize this shared structure and outperform models that treat sensors as independent inputs.

Beyond accuracy, TRON offers significant computational gains. Once trained, the model enables near-instantaneous inference, producing daily dose fields in milliseconds, achieving
>
58,000
\times
speedups relative to EXPACS-based simulations. This capability unlocks scalable, real-time monitoring for aviation, spaceflight, and terrestrial radiation safety applications. Unlike traditional methods that require retraining or re-simulation under varying input conditions, TRON maintains performance across temporal windows and spatial domains without adaptation.

Among all variants, the single-branch LSTM (S-LSTM) architecture achieved the most consistent performance, maintaining relative
L_{2}
errors below 0.1% across sequence lengths from 7 to 90 days. Its advantage over GRU-based and feed-forward baselines increases with sequence length, suggesting superior temporal memory retention and stability over extended histories. While the S-GRU variant provides competitive accuracy for short contexts, its performance plateaus at longer durations, consistent with known limitations in capturing long-range dependencies.

Recent advances in data-driven weather and climate modeling—such as DGMR, MetNet-2, and NeuralGCM—have demonstrated remarkable spatiotemporal accuracy, but operate under comparatively well-conditioned conditions. These models benefit from dense input-output pairs, uniform spatial grids, and direct observation of target variables. In contrast, TRON addresses a markedly more ill-posed and asymmetric problem: reconstructing radiation dose fields from sparse, proxy observations that are both indirect (neutron flux rather than dose) and highly underdetermined (only 12 ground-based monitors globally). This application domain serves as a stringent proving ground for a more general architecture. TRON’s architecture combines recurrent temporal encoding with neural operator-based spatial decoding, allowing for field reconstruction at arbitrary coordinates from temporally evolving, non-uniform data.

In addressing this challenge, TRON combines the strengths of neural operators with sequence modeling—effectively learning a latent representation of space weather dynamics from proxy signals. Unlike conventional forecasting or interpolation architectures, TRON enables real-time field estimation under extreme data sparsity, without requiring retraining for new locations or sequences. Its inverse formulation positions it as a general-purpose tool for domains where dense field measurements are unavailable.

In this ill-posed regime, TRON’s architecture—combining temporal recurrence with operator-based spatial generalization—learns a latent representation of space weather dynamics from sparse, asynchronous data. Unlike traditional architectures designed for interpolation or forecasting within dense grids, TRON enables resolution-agnostic, real-time inference across unseen spatiotemporal domains.

Nonetheless, the current implementation has limitations. First, TRON performs inference at the final timestep of a sequence and does not support forward extrapolation. While effective for nowcasting, it does not predict future states—a capability critical for early warning applications. Second, all experiments are limited to sea-level dose rates. Although this aligns with regulatory baselines and public health standards, radiation exposure in aviation and orbital contexts occurs at significantly higher altitudes. Extending TRON to multi-altitude inference or integrating satellite-based observations represents a key direction for future work.

In conclusion, TRON demonstrates that high-resolution, real-time field reconstruction is feasible even in severely under-instrumented regimes. It bridges the gap between physics-based simulation and data-driven inference by reframing inverse problems through the lens of spatiotemporal operators. TRON’s architecture—anchored by its S-LSTM variant—achieves near-instantaneous dose field estimation while preserving physical plausibility and spatial coherence. More broadly, this framework offers a scalable blueprint for real-time inference across scientific domains where field quantities must be reconstructed from sparse, indirect, and noisy measurements.

Rather than replacing simulation frameworks, TRON complements them—offering a fast, generalizable inference engine that can guide, augment, or initialize physics-based models in real-time operational workflows.

4.1
Simulation of reference dose rates using EXPACS

Reference effective dose rates were generated using the Fortran version of EXPACS
Sato 2016
;
Japan Atomic Energy Agency
;
Sato 2015
, compiled and executed by the authors to simulate daily ground-level radiation fields. EXPACS estimates cosmic ray fluxes and effective dose rates by applying the PARMA model, which parameterizes primary and secondary particle interactions with the atmosphere under geomagnetic and solar modulation.

The simulations were conducted over 22 years (2001–2023), covering the entire globe at sea level. The atmospheric depth was set to 1033 g/cm² to represent sea-level conditions, while the groundwater fraction was set to 0.15 to account for soil moisture effects. The spatial evaluation points,
\mathbf{r}_{j}
, were defined by a global grid of geographic coordinates, with longitudes ranging from
-180^{\circ}
to
180^{\circ}
and latitudes from
-90^{\circ}
to
90^{\circ}
, both with a grid step of
1.0^{\circ}
. This setup resulted in 65,341 evaluation points per day, totaling 8,400 days from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2023.

EXPACS automatically estimated the vertical cut-off rigidity at each point using its built-in database derived from MAGNETOCOSMICS
Desorgher et al. 2005
, accounting for the Earth’s magnetic field influence on charged particle trajectories. The simulation specified the actual date (year, month, and day) to capture solar activity variations, enabling the calculation of the solar modulation parameter (W-index) based on neutron monitor data.

The simulations considered contributions from neutrons, protons, muons (both positive and negative), electrons, positrons, photons, and ions from hydrogen to nickel (H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K, Ca, Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni). These particles include both primary cosmic rays and secondary particles generated through interactions with atmospheric constituents. The simulation output is the effective dose rate in units of
\mu
Sv/h, representing the radiation dose absorbed by biological tissue due to cosmic radiation exposure.

4.2
Implementation of TRON

The branch network in TRON is designed using either a single-branch or multiple-branch architecture, as illustrated in Fig.
6
.

In the single-branch configuration (Fig.
6
a), neutron count sequences from all
S
monitoring stations are concatenated into a matrix of shape
T\times S
, where
T
is the input sequence length. This joint representation is processed by a stacked S-LSTM or S-GRU network, consisting of 4 recurrent layers with 128 hidden units each. The final hidden state at the last time step is normalized using layer normalization and transformed through a fully connected linear layer to yield the latent temporal representation.

In contrast, the multi-branch configuration (Fig.
6
b) encodes each station’s temporal sequence independently using its own LSTM or GRU encoder. This design yields sensor-specific latent vectors, which are then fused through element-wise multiplication. While this architecture preserves per-sensor specialization, it restricts inter-sensor interaction during feature extraction, potentially underutilizing global modulation patterns.

The trunk network encodes spatial queries by transforming
(\text{longitude},\text{latitude})
coordinates into a 128-dimensional latent embedding. This transformation is performed by a two-layer fully connected neural network, each layer comprising 128 neurons with ReLU activation. The resulting spatial embedding aligns with the temporal feature dimension for fusion.

For comparative purposes, we implemented a static FNN-based DeepONet variant. Here, the Branch network consists of a four-layer feedforward neural network with hidden sizes [64, 64, 64, 128], using ReLU activations except in the output layer. The Trunk network follows the same spatial encoder design as the sequential models, enabling controlled comparisons across architectures.

The final dose rate estimate at each spatial location is computed by element-wise multiplying the branch (temporal) and trunk (spatial) latent vectors, followed by a summation over the hidden dimension (HD), as depicted in Fig.
6
c. This fusion process yields the final effective dose rate at each spatial point.

All TRON models were trained using a mean squared error (MSE) objective to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and simulated effective dose rates. The loss function for a given training batch is defined as:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{X}^{(i)}-X^{(i)}\right)^{2}

(16)

where
\hat{X}^{(i)}
and
X^{(i)}
are the predicted and reference effective dose rates for the
i
-th sample in the batch, respectively, and
N
is the batch size (i.e., 16). The model training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
. Early stopping based on validation loss was employed with a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting and to ensure convergence across varying sequence lengths and architectures.

Model performance was quantified using four complementary evaluation metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (
R^{2}
), and relative
L_{2}
error. These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of both absolute and relative predictive accuracy across spatially distributed outputs.

RMSE measures the average squared difference between predictions and references:

\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\hat{X}^{(i)}-X^{(i)}\right)^{2}}

(17)

The MAE measures the average absolute difference between predictions and reference values, providing a more interpretable error measure, and is expressed as:

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|\hat{X}^{(i)}-X^{(i)}\right|

(18)

The
R^{2}
score evaluates the proportion of variance explained by the model and is given by:

R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\hat{X}^{(i)}-X^{(i)}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(X^{(i)}-\bar{X}\right)^{2}}

(19)

The relative
L_{2}
error quantifies the Euclidean norm of the difference between the predicted and reference dose rates, providing an overall measure of prediction accuracy:

\text{relative }L_{2}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\left\|\hat{X}^{(i)}-X^{(i)}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{X}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}}

(20)

where
\bar{X}
denotes the mean of the reference effective dose rates across the entire test set, and
M
is the total number of test samples.
