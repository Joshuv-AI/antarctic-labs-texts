The association between maternal smoking during pregnancy and multimorbidity of non-communicable chronic diseases trajectory in offspring
Authors: Hang Wang, Xinyi Sun, Zheng Wang, Hui Liu, Jiaxin Huang, Yiwei Tang, Shanshan Wang, Conghui Qiao, Mengqi Su, Qianqian Lei, Wei Wei, Changhao Sun, Tianshu Han, Wenbo Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAlthough a few studies have found that maternal smoke during pregnancy (MSDP) is linked to a range of non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) in offspring, its association with the onset, progression, and prognosis of multimorbidity of NCDs (MNCDs) has never been studied.Study designPopulation-based prospective cohort study.MethodA total of 318,122 adults aged 18–87 years who were free of heart disease, stroke, and diabetes at baseline were selected. MNCDs was defined as the coexistence of two or three NCDs, including CVDs (coronary heart disease and stroke), cancer (digest, breast, lung, and urination), diabetes (type 1 and type 2), and CLRD (COPD and asthma). Then we used multi-state model to analyse the impacts of MSDP on the progression of MNCDs in the offspring. Associations between MSDP and transition trajectory were analyzed with results expressed as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI).ResultsDuring the 70.2 years of follow-up, a total of 112,894 participants developed first NCDs (FNCDs), among whom 28,195 developed MNCDs and 21,452 subsequently died. After adjustment for potential confounders, the MSDP was associated with the transition trajectory from baseline to FNCD (HR = 1.10, 95%CI:1.09–1.11) and from FNCDs to MNCDs (HR = 1.08,95%CI:1.06–1.10). Meanwhile, the MSDP was associated with the transition trajectory from MNCDs to death (HR = 1.07,95%CI:1.03–1.11). Further, these association became more pronounced among the offspring who smoke (HRbaseline→FNCDs=1.13, 95%CI:1.12–1.15; HRFNCD→MNCD=1.13, 95%CI:1.12–1.15; HRMNCDs→death=1.11, 95%CI:1.16–1.16); whereas among the offspring who do not smoke, only the transition trajectory from baseline to FNCDs was observed (HR = 1.07, 95%CI:1.05–1.09).ConclusionThis study suggested that the MSDP is associated with almost all transition phases of MNCDs development and increases the mortality risk of MNCDs in offspring who smoke, and these transition trajectories can be largely reduced by smoking cessation among offspring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20686-4.

Introduction

The multimorbidity of Non-communicable chronic diseases (MNCDs) refers to the co-occurrence of two or more non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs), generally including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), cancer, diabetes, and respiratory diseases, which are becoming a growing public health issue due to their rapidly increasing prevalence [1, 2]. The presence of MNCDs has been found to significantly increase mortality risk and result in a notable reduction in life expectancy when compared to NCDs occurring alone [3]. In many countries, it is estimated that nearly 50% of NCDs patients are affected by MNCDs [2, 4]. Thus, identifying potential risk factors for MNCDs is an important task in reducing the health burden associated with this condition. However, research regarding this issue is very scarce.

Current evidence suggests that the prenatal period is a critical stage that is highly sensitive to environmental stimulation, which can permanently alter metabolism and physiology [5–7]. Exposure to deleterious environmental stimuli during this period probably results in various diseases throughout adulthood [6, 8–10]. It is estimated that the proportion of women who smoked daily and continued to smoke daily during pregnancy was 30.6% in the European Region [11], and several epidemiological studies have found that maternal smoke during pregnancy (MSDP) is associated with the development of NCDs in offspring [12–14]. However, it is still largely unknown what health impacts of the MSDP may have on the development of MNCDs, as previous research has primarily focused on the transition from free of NCDs to single NCDs, leading to an underestimation of the disease burden attribute to the MSDP. Furthermore, to data, no studies have evaluated and compared the effects of MSDP on the incidence, progress, and prognosis of MNCDs simultaneously although such investigations could have significant implications for the development of evidence-based prevention and intervention strategies.

To address this evidence gap, this study aimed to examine the health impacts of MSDP on the trajectory of MNCDs. Specifically, we examined the transitions from being free of NCDs to developing a first NCD (FNCD), then to developing MNCD and ultimately to mortality in the UK Biobank cohort. Additionally, we intended to assess whether smoking behavior had a modifying effect on these transition trajectories.

Method

Study Population

The UK Biobank is a multicenter, population-based, prospective cohort study that has collected comprehensive data on individuals’ lifestyles, physical measures, biological samples, and health status. The primary objective of this study was to establish a resource for investigating the genetic and environmental factors that contribute to the development of complex chronic diseases in middle-aged and elderly populations, which has been described elsewhere [15, 16]. To summarize, the UK Biobank study enrolled approximately half a million UK residents aged 40 to 69 who lived within 25 miles of one of 22 research assessment centers across the country. Participants were recruited between 2006 and 2010 and registered with the UK National Health Service. A summary of the baseline characteristics of the cohort is available on the UK Biobank website’s data showcase (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk). All participants provided informed consent and our study complies with all relevant ethical regulations with approval obtained by UK Biobank from the National Health Service National Research Ethics Service (reference 11/NW/0382).

Assessment of the MSDP

Data on maternal smoking around the time of birth (Field ID 1787) were collected through the touchscreen questionnaire, which asked participants whether their mother smoked regularly at the time of their birth. The response options were ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘don’t know’, and the relevant information is available under the ‘Early life factors’ category (Field ID 100033) on the UK Biobank website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=100033), which has been utilized in recent studies to uncover the potential impacts of MSDP on the health of offspring [13, 17–19].

Ascertainment of outcome

The occurrence of the health conditions of interest and mortality were identified by linking the study participants’ information with disease and mortality registries, national health insurance claim databases, and local residential records, followed by annual verification. The coding of all health events was performed by trained staff who were unaware of participants’ baseline characteristics, in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). The MNCDs was defined as the occurrence of two or more NCDs including CVDs (coronary heart disease and stroke), cancer (digest, breast, lung, and urination), diabetes (type 1 and type 2), and CLRD (COPD and asthma) [2]. Coronary heart disease was defined with ICD-10 code of I20-I25, stroke with code of I60-I64, cancer with code of C00-C99 (digestive cancer with code of C15-C26, breast cancer with code of C50, lung cancer with code of C34, urinary tract cancer with code of C64-C68), CLRD with code of J40-J47 (COPD with code of J40-J44, asthma with code of J45). All-cause deaths were assessed from the UK Biobank Baseline Assessment Centre and linked to the UK Biobank National Mortality Record.

Definition of covariates

Additional information of covariates was also collected by the touchscreen questionnaire, which included age (years), sex (men/women), UK Biobank assessment center, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), body mass index (BMI), physical activity level (above/below moderate recommendation), ethnics (White/Mixed/Asian/Black/others), education (college degree or above/high school or below), drug use for lowing cholesterol, blood pressure or glucose (yes/no), mineral or vitamin supplementation (yes/no), smoking status (before/current/never) and alcohol use status (before/current/never).

Statistical analysis

We excluded participants whose data on diagnosed CVDs, cancer, diabetes, or CLPD were missing (N = 4,304) and excluded participants who did not know whether their mother smoked during pregnancy or preferred not to answer this question (N = 68,766). Additionally, we excluded participants who had missing information of covariates (N = 1,379 for race, N = 2192 for BMI, N = 533 for TDI, N = 6,457 for education, N = 75,685 for adequate physical activity, N = 836 for smoking status, N = 145 for alcohol drinking status, N = 1,657 for drug use for lowing cholesterol, blood pressure or glucose, N = 748 for mineral or vitamin supplementation, N = 21,551 for blood pressure). Finally, a total of 318,122 participants were included in the primary analysis. Participants were followed until death, loss to follow-up, or February 1, 2022, whichever came first. In main analyses, the participants without the MSDP were set as the reference group. All models were adjusted for age, sex, UK Biobank assessment center, TDI, BMI, physical activity level, ethnics, education, drug use for lowing cholesterol, blood pressure or glucose, mineral or vitamin supplementation, smoking status, alcohol use status, blood pressure, and healthy diet score.

In the initial analysis, we estimated the associations between the MSDP and FNCDs, MNCDs, and all-cause mortality using traditional Cox proportional hazards models. A Schoenfeld residuals test was conducted to evaluate whether our model meets the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Table 1). Afterwards, we extended our analysis by investigating the contribution of the MSDP to each transitional phase of MNCDs progression and prognosis. Specifically, we employed semi-Markov multi-state model (mstate package) to examine the associations of MSDP with the transition from free of NCDS to FCMD, MNCDs, and then to death. The multi-state model is an extension of the traditional Cox proportional hazards model that consider multiple subsequent or competing events as states of transitions. By including these events as states, multi-state models offer a unique advantage in investigating the influence of risk factors on different stages of disease progression simultaneously, while accounting for competing risks [20, 21]. Compared to the simple two-state model, which considers the transition from baseline to event only, multi-state models allow for a more comprehensive analysis of disease progression [20, 21].

In accordance with previous studies [22, 23], five transition stages (Fig. 1-a) were constructed as (1) baseline to FNCDs, (2) FNCDs to MNCDs, (3) baseline to death, (4) FNCDs to death, (5) and MNCDs to death. The participants with any states at baseline could remain in the entry state or transition to another. The entering date of MNCDs was defined as the date when the second NCD was diagnosed. It is impossible to emerge further transitions for participants once death occurs. Transition intensities were modelled using the ‘clock reset’ approach, where time was measured since entering each state. The ‘clock reset’ modelling approach of the semi-Markov multi-state model relaxed the Markov assumption based on the ‘clock forward’ approach by considering both the current state and the time spent in that state when predicting the future state, thus improving the biological plausibility of models. We calculated the entering date of the theoretically prior state for participants entering different stages on the same date as the date of the latter state minus 0.5 day based on previous studies. For example, for patients with first diagnosis as MNCDs, the entering date of FNCDs equaled the date of MNCDs minus 0.5 day [22, 23].

Further, we also examined possible differential associations of the MSDP with the progression by individual first NCDs, the multi-stage paths by subtypes of NCDs (CVD, cancer, diabetes and CLRD) were split, and 14 transitions were constructed (transition pattern B, Fig. 1-b). Moreover, we examined whether smoking behavior among offspring had a modifying effect on the health impacts of MSDP during the different transition stages of MNCDs. Therefore, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on offspring smoking behavior and applied the multi-state models again for each subgroup. Futhermore, interaction analysis was performed to explore the potential interaction between maternal and offspring smoking.

Also, several sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of our results. Firstly, we conducted a repeat analysis after excluding participants who were diagnosed with NCDs or died within two years of enrollment. This allowed us to evaluate whether delayed diagnoses of existing NCD conditions or serious illnesses could have influenced our results. Secondly, we retained the participants who entered in the different transition stages at the same day to further examine the exclusion of these participants would influence the results. We calculated the entering date of theoretically prior state as the date of the latter state minus 0.5 day/ 0.5 year, 1 year, 3 year and 5 year based on previous study [22, 23]. Besides, we performed the subgroup analyses by age, sex, BMI, drug use, physical activity, and healthy dietary quality to evaluate whether these covariates modified the association between the MSDP and the transition trajectories of MNCDs. Moreover, we performed multiple imputation on all covariates with missing values. we first excluded participants with missing data of MSDP. Then, we performed multiple imputation and generated 10 replicates, using the polytomous regression impression for unordered categorical data model “polyreg” to interpolate different unordered multiclass variables including age, gender, race, etc., and analyzed each generated dataset and merge finally. Besides, we conducted a repeat analysis after additionally adjusting the prenatal care condition. Lastly, we explored the potential competing risks of mortality due to causes unrelated to non-communicable diseases.

All the analyses in this study were performed using R-project 3.6.2 (www.r-project.org/). The multi-state models were constructed using the “mstate” package. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a probability value of P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics for the studying variables according to the status of MSDP is presented in Table 1. Among the total sample of the studying population, 28.4% of participants experienced MSDP. Compared to the participants without exposure, the participants exposed to MSDP were younger, more likely to be men, White European, former smokers, more daily physical activity and higher drinking rates with lower healthy diet scores and lower rates of vitamins and minerals supplements use (all the P < 0.05). During a median follow-up of 70.2 years, 112,894 (36.5%) participants developed at least one NCD. Among those with at least one NCD, 28,195 (26.1%) participants further developed MNCD. Further, 21,452 deaths were identified, of which 9,168 (8.1%) were attributed to FNCD and 7,420 (26.3%) were attributed to MNCD (Fig. 1a). When FNCD was further divided into specific NCDs, 33,074 (10.5%) participants had CVD, 17,925 (5.7%) had cancer, 15,040 (4.8%) had diabetes, 44,781 (14.2%) had CLPD, and 7,687 (23.2%), 3,612 (20.2%), 3,953 (26.3%) and 10,869 (24.3%) of them developed MNCD afterwards, respectively(Fig. 1b).

Multi-state analysis

In the traditional cox proportional hazards models, participants with MSDP were found to have a greater risk of developing FNCDs, MNCDs, and experiencing all-cause mortality, as compared to those without MSDP (Supplementary Table 2). By utilizing multi-state models, the MSDP had significant effects on each stage of the MNCD transition trajectories (Table 2). Specifically, the MSDP was associated with an increased risk of transitioning from baseline to FNCDs (HR = 1.10, 95%CI:1.09–1.11), as well as an increased risk of transitioning from FNCDs to MNCDs (HR = 1.08, 95%CI:1.06–1.10). For transition to death, the MSDP was associated with an increased risk of transitioning from MNCDs to death (HR = 1.07, 95%CI:1.03–1.11), but not from baseline or FCMDs to death (HRbaseline to death=1.05, 95%CI:0.99–1.12; HRFCMDs to death=1.03, 95%CI: 0.99–1.07).

Modification effects of smoking behavior in offspring

We performed interaction analysis to elucidate whether offspring smoking behavior had modification effects on the association between MSDP and the transition trajectories of MNCDs. The results showed that offspring smoking behavior could modify the association between MSDP and transition trajectories from free of NCDs to FNCDs, and from FNCDs to MNCDs(Table 4), especially the transition from CVDs and CLRD to MNCDs(Table 5). Therefore, we performed the subgroup analysis according to the offspring smoking behavior. The results showed that among the participants who still smoked during adulthood, the association between MSDP and transition trajectories from free of NCDs to FNCDs, and from FNCDs to MNCDs was more pronounced (HRbaseline to FNCDs=1.13, 95%CI: 1.11–1.15; HRFNCDs to MNCDs=1.13, 95%CI: 1.09–1.16) (Table 6). Moreover, compared to the total sample, we additionally observed the association between the MSDP and an increased risk of transition from free of NCDs to cancer (HR = 1.10, 95%CI:1.05–1.14) (Table 7). In contrast, among the participants who never smoke during adulthood, we only observed the association between the MSDP and an increased risk of transition from free of NCDs to FNCDs (HR = 1.06, 95%CI: 1.04–1.08), and the transition from FNCDs to MNCDs became non-significant (HR = 1.01, 95%CI: 0.98–1.06) (Table 8). For the transition trajectories of specific type of NCDs, the association between the MSDP and increased risk of transition from free of NCDs to CVD and CLPD was observed (HRbaseline to CVD=1.08, 95%CI: 1.04–1.12; HRbaseline to CLPD=1.11, 95%CI: 1.08–1.14). However, the association between MSDP and the transition trajectories from free of NCDs to cancer and diabetes became non-significant (HRbaseline to cancer=0.96, 95%CI: 0.92-1.00; HRbaseline to diabetes=1.03, 95%CI: 0.98–1.09). Additionally, while we found a significant association between MSDP and an increased risk of transition from diabetes to MNCDs (HR = 1.13, 95%CI: 1.11–1.15), the other MNCD transition trajectories became non-significant (Table 9).

Sensitivity analyses

The first sensitivity analysis showed that after excluding the participants who died within two years of enrollment, the results regarding the association of prenatally exposure to tobacco with risk of different transitions were still significant, indicating that serious diseases did not influence the results (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The second sensitivity analysis showed that after changing the entering date, the results were still significant (Supplementary Table 5). The third sensitivity analysis indicated that the associations were also significant after excluding participants who entered different transition stages on the same day (Supplementary Table 6). The fourth sensitivity analysis indicated that the associations remained relatively robust in the stratified analyses. The results showed that the associations of MSDP with the risk of transitions were more obvious among the participants whose age was below 60 years, or who were women or who had lower dietary quality, and the association attuned, and most was still significant among other participants. We observed significant effect modification of MSDP by age, alcohol drinking, economic levels and sex on one or more transitions. The older groups had higher risk of developing FCMD in association with MSDP. Excessive alcohol drinking amplified the impacts of MSDP on the transitions from baseline to FCMD, and from FCMD to death. Individuals with lower economic levels had a higher risk of death from baseline associated with MSDP. Males were more vulnerable to death from FCMD associated with MSDP compared to females (Fig. 2a-c). The conclusions from multiplicative interaction models were generally consistent with those from stratified analyses. Besides, we also observed that the associations were also significant after performing multiple imputation on all covariates with missing values (Supplementary Table 7) or additionally adjusting the prenatal care condition (Supplementary Table 8). Besides, we conducted a competitive risk analysis for various diseases. The results showed a significant relationship between influenza and pneumonia and the risk of death, which suggests that influenza and pneumonia cannot be ignored as competing risk factors when assessing the outcomes of deaths associated with MSDP (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

In this large scale and prospective cohort, this study investigated how the MSDP affects the entire course of MNCDs, including their onset, progression, and prognosis. The findings showed that the MSDP played a significant role in multiple transition stages of MNCDs, such as the transition from free of NCDs to FNCDs, FNCDs to MNCDs, and MNCDs to death. Taking disease-specific transitions into account, the MSDP had the strongest effects on CLPR during the transition from free of NCDs to FNCDs and from FNCDs to death, as well as on cancer during the transition from FNCDs to MNCDs. Additionally, the tobacco smoking behavior of offspring modified the association between the MSDP and multiple transition stages of MNCDs, with a more significant impact among those who smoked. Among offspring who never smoked, most of the associations except the MSDP and diabetes transition became non-significant.

MNCDs are a common occurrence, contributing a significant increase risk of mortality, but few studies have investigated the common risk factors contributing to this phenomenon. Therefore, the most significant finding of this study is that individuals whose mothers smoked during pregnancy are at higher risk for both FNCDs and the transition from FNCDs to MNCDs in adulthood, which are independent of a series of classical confounders of NCDs, suggesting that the occurrence and development of MNCD could be traced back to exposure to tobacco smoke in early life. Research conducted previously has revealed that maternal smoking can cause a significant increase in oxidative stress in the developing fetus [23], which may persist into adulthood and lead to direct damage to the mitochondria [24, 25]. This damage can then induce further oxidative stress and inflammation. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis study has shown that there is a linear, positive association between the number of cigarettes smoked during pregnancy and offspring overweight [26]. These studies support the result of our study, as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and overweight are common pathophysiological mechanisms of NCDs [27, 28].

Furthermore, we found that the impacts of MSDP on the transition trajectories from free of NCDs to FNCDs and from FNCDs to MNCDs varied depending on the specific types of diseases. Specifically, our data indicated that the individuals whose mothers smoked during pregnancy are at the highest risk for the transition trajectory from free of NCDs to CLPD, probably because fetal exposure to tobacco smoking could directly affect pulmonary intrauterine growth. Previous studies have shown that exposure to tobacco smoking during the fetal could permanently alveolar formation and growth through the reduction of forced expiratory flow and passive breathing compliance [29, 30], which may further support this transition trajectory. Meanwhile, regarding the transition trajectories from specific FNCDs to MNCDs, the strongest effects of MSDP were observed in the transition from cancer to MNCDs, likely due to the aggravating effects of oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and overweight on the occurrence of other NCDs in individuals with cancer.

In addition, prior studies have demonstrated a positive association between parental smoking and smoking behavior in offspring, indicating a higher risk of smoking initiation and tobacco use among children of parents who smoke. As such, our study explored whether smoking behavior modifies the association between MSDP and the transition trajectories of MNCDs. We found that among offspring who smoke, the health impacts of MSDP on MNCD transition trajectories become more pronounced. In contrast, among offspring who do not smoke, the health impacts of MSDP on most MNCD transition trajectories are attenuated. These findings suggest that the adverse effects of MSDP on all MNCD transition trajectories may be augmented by offspring smoking behavior but can also be mitigated by smoking cessation among offspring.

This study provided important public health implications. It is estimated that the prevalence of the MSDP is 30.6% in the European Region [11]. Consistent with these data, a total of 28.9% of participants reported that their mothers smoke during their born in the data of UK biobank. Meanwhile, the occurrence of MNCDs is gradually increasing around the world, and nearly 50% of patients with NCDs have at least two NCDs [4]. The findings of this study demonstrated the link between MSDP and all transition stages of MNCDs in the offspring, emphasizing the importance of the intervention of maternal behaviors in reducing the medical burden of NCDs in the offspring. Public health professionals and policy maker should be aware of the current findings from this study regarding the adverse effects of MSDP on the transition trajectories of MNCDs in the offspring.

Our study has numerous strengths. First, its prospective design allows for a better understanding of the perniciousness of the MSDP in real-life situations. Second, the large sample size provides sufficient outcome events for MNCDs, as well as statistical power to examine significant outcomes related to MSDP. Third, the detailed information on covariates enables comprehensive sensitivity and subgroup analyses, minimizing confounding factors. However, several limitations should also be considered. First, one potential limitation of the study is that participants, as mid-age adults, may not have accurate information on whether their mother smoked while pregnant with them. However, we anticipate that the inaccuracies in determining the exposure status are probably similar between exposed and non-exposed group, which may result in an underestimation of the true exposure rate and weaken the strength of the observed associations. Second, the information on prenatal exposure to smoke was not recorded in detail, such as the number of cigarettes prenatally smoked daily, the number of days smoked during pregnancy, the type of tobacco smoked, and secondhand smoke exposure. The lack of such information prevented us from assessing the dose-response relationship between MSDP and all transitions stages. Third, the possibility of residual confounders due to imprecise measurements or unknown factors cannot be excluded. Several residual confounders, such as paternal smoking, environmental tobacco exposure, and the quantity of maternal smoking during pregnancy, were not fully accounted for in our analysis. Fourth, UKB cohort consists predominantly of middle-aged and older individuals of European descent, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Last, the effects of second-hand or third-hand smoke at home or in the workplace in adulthood cannot be separated from smoking exposure in early life in observational studies, which may have influenced the observed associations despite adjustments for a range of confounders.

Implication

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the associations of MSDP with the transition from free of NCDS to FCMD, MNCDs, and then to death by using semi-Markov multi-state model. Thus, our study extends previous research by utilizing a large representative sample of UK biobank, controlling for relevant confounding variables, and exploring the role of MSDP in this respective relation. Policymakers should focus on several key areas regarding smoking during pregnancy. First, they should enhance public awareness campaigns to educate expectant mothers and their families about the harmful effects of smoking on fetal health. Second, it is essential to provide supportive services, such as smoking cessation programs, that offer professional psychological and medical assistance. Additionally, encouraging research on the impacts of MSDP, particularly across different socioeconomic backgrounds, can inform more effective interventions. Finally, collaboration with healthcare providers, community organizations, and educational institutions is crucial to promote healthy lifestyles and accessible cessation resources.

Conclusion

In summary, our study using data from a large, prospective cohort, provides evidence that the MSDP is associated with almost all transition phases of MNCDs development and increases the mortality risk of MNCDs in offspring, and these transition trajectories can be largely reduced by smoking cessation among offspring.
