Breastfeeding Challenges and Determinants among Mothers with Gestational Diabetes: A Systematic Review
Authors: Soltan Asghari, Nahid Jahani Shoorab, Samira Shahbazzadegan, Talat Khadivzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The prevalence of gestational diabetes has increased in parallel with risk factors such as obesity, a fam- ily history of diabetes, and advanced maternal age. Studies suggest that gestational diabetes may negatively affect breastfeeding outcomes. This systematic review aimed to investigate the relationship between gestational diabetes and breastfeeding, as well as the contributing factors influencing breastfeeding among affected women.Materials and Methods: Articles published up to January 2023, with an update through 2025, were included. A com- prehensive search was conducted across databases including Medline, PubMed, Scopus, and others, using relevant keywords such as gestational diabetes, breastfeeding, blood sugar, misconception, cultural factors, and predisposing factors. Quantitative studies were assessed using the STROBE checklist, and qualitative studies were evaluated using the CASP tool. Key challenges identified include delayed onset of lactogenesis, misconceptions regarding diabetes transmission, and insufficient support.Results: A total of 19 studies (16 quantitative and 3 qualitative) were included. Analysis revealed several trends: ten quantitative studies reported a reduction in exclusive breastfeeding duration; three indicated lower breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge; and three studies identified difficulties in breastfeeding initiation and continuation. Notably, three studies found similar breastfeeding continuation rates between mothers with gestational diabetes and healthy controls. Factors associated with reduced breastfeeding included maternal obesity, cesarean delivery, prematurity, advanced maternal age, racial disparities, insulin treatment, limited knowledge about complementary feeding, mis- conceptions about exclusive breastfeeding, and concerns over diabetes transmission through breast milkConclusion:Addressing breastfeeding barriers among mothers with gestational diabetes requires targeted education, social support, and attention to professional challenges. Additional barriers included insufficient organizational re- sources, lack of hospital policies supporting breastfeeding, inadequate post-discharge support, and poor coordination with other healthcare facilities. Strengthening post-discharge support and inter-facility collaboration is essential. Fu- ture longitudinal and qualitative research should integrate cultural and psychosocial dimensions into evidence-based practices.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a form of carbohydrate intolerance, can vary in severity and typically emerge during pregnancy. As risk factors such as maternal obesity, advanced maternal age, and a family history of diabetes have become more prevalent, the global incidence of GDM has also increased. In recent years, the worldwide prevalence of GDM has risen considerably. In the United States, it is estimated at approximately 7%, while in India it is reported at 7.1% (1, 2), In Iran, recent data from 2022 indicate a prevalence of 7.9% (3).

GDM poses substantial health risks to both mothers and their infants. Studies suggest that prolonged breastfeeding may reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes and other complications, such as overweight and obesity, in both mothers with GDM and their offspring (4). However, the literature remains inconsistent regarding breastfeeding outcomes in infants born to mothers with GDM.

The global increase in GDM highlights the urgent need for targeted strategies to support successful breastfeeding among affected mothers. Hospital-based interventions, such as the World Health Organization (WHO) Baby- Friendly Health Initiative and antenatal milk expression, have shown promise in promoting breastfeeding initiation and continuation (5-9). However, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the specific effects of GDM on breastfeeding patterns and outcomes. This systematic review aims to address these gaps by examining factors influencing exclusive breastfeeding practices among mothers with GDM, and by providing evidence-based insights to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Riddle and Nommsen-Rivers (10) associated GDM in first-time mothers with delayed onset of lactogenesis, an increased incidence of excessive infant weight loss, greater reliance on infant formula, and shortened breastfeeding duration. In that study, 44% of affected mothers experienced a delay in the initiation of lactogenesis. Furthermore, women with GDM are at increased risk of delayed secretory activation and reduced milk production, partly due to glucose intolerance, which may impair the timely onset of milk production and lower overall milk supply (6).

A 2019 systematic review by Nguyen et al. (11) found no significant difference in breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge between healthy mothers and those with GDM. However, the duration of exclusive breastfeeding was significantly shorter among mothers with GDM (11). A 2012 qualitative study by Hirst et al. (5), involving 34 Vietnamese women with gestational diabetes, highlighted that many of these mothers frequently experienced hunger-primarily due to limited knowledge of appropriate dietary alternative and concerns about transmitting diabetes to their infants. In addition, mothers with lactose intolerance type II require specialized guidance and support from hospital staff to effectively initiate and sustain breastfeeding, which is essential for preventing hypoglycemia in nursing infants.

Gunderson (4) demonstrated that prolonged breastfeeding among women with a history of GDM is associated with reduced risks of type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease. With the global prevalence of GDM continuing to rise, there is an urgent need to identify and implement strategies that support effective breastfeeding, particularly during in-hospital care. Initiatives such as the WHO Baby-Friendly Health Initiative, antenatal milk expression, and other evidence-based interventions have been shown promise in enhancing hospital-based support and improving breastfeeding outcomes (5-7, 9, 12). Despite growing recognition of the importance of breastfeeding in the context of GDM, gaps persist in understanding how GDM specifically influences breastfeeding patterns and outcomes. This review aims to systematically examine the factors that affect exclusive breastfeeding among mothers with GDM, with the goal of informing targeted interventions to optimize maternal and neonatal health. By synthesizing current evidence, the review seeks to clarify key determinants of breastfeeding success in this population and support the development of more effective clinical and public health strategies.

This study investigates the multifaceted factors influencing breastfeeding practices among mothers with GDM in hospital settings, aiming to address gaps in the existing literature. Unlike previous reviews that have primarily focused on lifestyle interventions or the prevention of GDM-related complications, this review critically explores a broader range of determinants associated with breastfeeding success. It highlights underexamined barriers-such as cultural misconceptions, maternal and neonatal complications, institutional constraints, healthcare provider limitations, and organizational challenges-that may negatively affect breastfeeding outcomes. By identifying these key factors, this study seeks to inform future strategies to promote effective breastfeeding and improve both maternal and infant health outcomes.

Materials and Methods

The review included articles published up to January 2023 and was updated to 2025. It was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 flowchart and reporting guidelines for systematic reviews. Eligible studies comprised prospective and retrospective observational designs (cohort and case- control), as well as qualitative studies investigating factors affecting breastfeeding in mothers diagnosed with GDM.

The participant, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) framework guided study selection: Participants were breastfeeding women diagnosed with GDM; no intervention was applied; the comparison group comprised breastfeeding women without GDM; and the primary outcome was the identification of factors influencing breastfeeding practices during hospitalization and after discharge among mothers with GDM.

Two independent reviewers screened the identified records and conducted a comprehensive search across multiple databases, including Medline (via PubMed), Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, and Persian-language data- bases such as the Scientific Information Database (SID), Magiran, and Iranmedex.

Grey literature-such as unpublished data, non–peer-reviewed materials, and sources unavailable for analysis at the time of review, including conference abstracts, dissertation/theses, reports, patents, and government and regulatory documents-was excluded.

The database search strategy utilized keywords including “GDM”, “breastfeeding”, “lactation”, “blood glucose”, “misconception”, “social support”, “maternal education”, “maternal obesity” and “systematic reviews”, along with their corresponding Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to refine the search strategy.

Key definitions used in this study

Exclusive

According to the WHO, exclusive breastfeeding refers to feeding infants only breast milk-either directly from the mother or expressed milk from the biological mother, a donor, or a wet nurse-without providing any additional food or liquids, not even water. Exceptions include oral rehydration salts, vitamins, minerals, or medications prescribed for medical reasons. This practice is recommended for the first six months of life to support optimal growth, development, and health.

Delayed initiation of breastfeeding

The WHO defines delayed initiation of breastfeeding as any introduction of food or liquid before the infant is breastfed within the first hour after birth. Such a delay may negatively affect neonatal health and the mother-infant bonding process. Notably, definitions of “delay” can vary across studies, ranging from more than one to four hours after birth.

Early weaning

Early weaning refers to discontinuing breastfeeding before the infant reaches six months of age. The WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, followed by the introduction of complementary foods alongside continued breastfeeding up to two years of age or beyond.

Gestational diabetes mellitus

GDM is a form of glucose intolerance first diagnosed during pregnancy, typically in the second or third trimester. Diagnosis is based on the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) as outlined by WHO or the American Diabetes Association (ADA). GDM is confirmed if any of the following thresholds are met: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 92 mg/dL, 1-hour plasma glucose ≥ 180 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 153 mg/dL

Risk factors for gestational diabetes mellitus

Risk factors for gestational diabetes mellitus Major risk factors for GDM include advanced maternal age (≥35 Y), a family history of type 2 diabetes in first or second-degree relatives, and elevated pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI): overweight (BMI >25 kg/m²) or obese (BMI >30 kg/m²).

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts retrieved through the search strategy, along with those identified from additional sources, were independently screened by two members of our review team to identify studies potentially meeting the inclusion criteria. Full texts of these potentially eligible articles (n=19) were then obtained and independently assessed for inclusion by a third reviewer. The authors independently evaluated the quality and eligibility of the articles, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with the third team member.

Data relevant to the selected studies were extracted using the PICO framework. Extracted information included the primary author’s name, article title, year of study, year of publication, research objectives, factors influencing breastfeeding practices, study location, sample size, study population characteristics, and key outcomes. All data were recorded in a standardized, predefined format.

PICO framework

Population: Women aged 18 years and older diagnosed with GDM, including those with impaired glucose tolerance or at elevated risk of developing diabetes, across diverse clinical and geographical settings.

Interventions: Breastfeeding support measures such as lactation counseling during hospitalization, post-discharge follow-up, glycemic monitoring, and assessment of maternal characteristics including BMI and ethnicity.

Control: Breastfeeding mothers without a diagnosis of GDM.

Outcomes: Primary outcomes comprised breastfeeding related indicators, including initiation, exclusivity, and duration rates. Secondary outcomes addressed barriers to breastfeeding, encompassing maternal and neonatal factors, healthcare professional practices, system-level challenges, cultural beliefs, and misconceptions regarding GDM and breastfeeding.

Inclusion criteria: Women with GDM carrying viable singleton pregnancies who received antenatal and postpartum care, intended to breastfeed, and participated in structured follow-up.

Exclusion criteria: Women with pre-existing diabetes, severe maternal or neonatal complications, those who did not initiate breastfeeding, cases with incomplete data, or those unable to meet language or consent requirements (5-10, 12-24).

Quality assessment

Two standardized tools were used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies. For quantitative studies, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist, comprising 27 criteria, was employed to ensure high-quality reporting of observational and cross-sectional research. Each item was scored as either 1 (yes) or 0 (no). Based on an 18-point scale, studies were classified as high quality (score < 14), medium quality (11 < score ≤ 14), or low quality (score < 11). Evaluation criteria encompassed study design, attrition rate, sample size, participant selection, appropriate use of statistical analysis with adjustment for confounders, and verification of key outcomes (25)

For qualitative studies, the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was applied, consisting of 10 questions scored as yes (2 points), unclear (1 point), or no (0 points). Overall scores were categorized as poor (0-7), average (8-14), or good (15-20) quality. The STROBE and CASP tools were used, respectively to assess quantitative and qualitative studies (26).

Results

This study examined global breastfeeding patterns among mothers with GDM. An initial search yielded 384 articles, of which 223 were excluded as duplicates, leaving 161 articles for screening. After title and abstract assessment, 130 articles were removed due to irrelevance, resulting in 19 eligible studies for inclusion. The majority of these studies met quality standards based on the STROBE and CASP checklists, with most reporting their methodologies and findings clearly. Overall, the methodological quality was deemed moderate.

Among the included studies, ten quantitative investigations reported reduced continuation of exclusive breastfeeding (8-10, 13, 15, 17-19, 23, 24), and three studies found a decline in breastfeeding rates during hospitalization (7, 12, 14). Specifically, in-hospital breastfeeding rates at discharge were lower among women with GDM in three studies. Notably, studies by Haile et al. (12), Matias et al. (7), and Chamberlain et al. (14) highlighted the importance of timely initiation and sustained exclusive breastfeeding. These studies emphasized the value of collaborative education during hospitalization and the support provided by healthcare professionals, policy makers, and family members (7, 12, 14).

In addition, three qualitative studies (5, 6, 21) reported lower initiation and continuation rates of breastfeeding among women with GDM compared to healthy controls. These women were at increased risk of delayed initiation and shorter durations of sustained breastfeeding. In contrast, three study (16, 20, 22) reported no significant differences in breastfeeding patterns between mothers with GDM and those without.

All included studies reported well-documented statistical methods and results. However, the generalizability of their findings may be limited due to differences in the diagnostic methods used to define gestational diabetes and variations in cultural attitudes across countries such as Thailand, Brazil, the United States, Australia, China, and Vietnam (5, 9, 14, 16, 19, 20, 22, 24) .

The earliest study, conducted between 1989 and 2000, was published in 2008. Overall, the included studies were published between 2007 and 2024.0 Table 1 presents the characteristics and quality assessment of these studies, categorized by geographic region (Asia, Europe, the USA, China, Brazil, Norway, and Australia) and study design (cohort or cross-sectional studies).

Several studies did not specifically investigate the impact of GDM on breastfeeding outcomes but instead explored broader influencing factors. Eight studies were conducted in the United States, including one qualitative study by Jagiello and Chertok (6) and six quantitative studies (7, 9, 14, 17, 23, 24). Various data collection methods were employed. For example, one large study based in Ohio extracted data from 10 birth certificates over a 6-year period across 16 U.S. states (9).

Breastfeeding outcomes in women with gestational diabetes

The results related to breastfeeding among women with gestational diabetes are presented in three sections: i. The relationship between gestational diabetes and exclusive breastfeeding, ii. Factors influencing breastfeeding in women with gestational diabetes, and iii. Breastfeeding outcomes in quantitative and qualitative studies.

This section summarizes studies examining the association between gestational diabetes and breastfeeding outcomes, including breastfeeding initiation at hospital discharge and the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding, based on both on quantitative and qualitative evidence.

The association between gestational diabetes and breastfeeding can be categorized into three components:

Initiation of exclusive breastfeeding at hospital dis charge

Initiation of any breastfeeding at discharge, including feeding with water, fruit juices, or oral rehydration solutions

Continuation of exclusive breastfeeding over time The section addressing the relationship between ges tational diabetes and breastfeeding summarizes findings on the initiation and continuation of exclusive breastfeed ing from both quantitative and qualitative studies. Three smaller studies indicated that gestational diabetes was associated with lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding at hospital discharge (7, 12, 14). Specifically, Matias et al. (7), Haile et al. (12) and Chamberlain et al. (14) reported that that mothers with gestational diabetes were less likely to initiate exclusive breastfeeding at discharge compared to healthy mothers.

For instance, Chamberlain et al. (14) reported exclusive breastfeeding rates of 80% among healthy mothers, 53% among Indigenous Australian women with gestational diabetes, and 60% among non-Indigenous women with gestational diabetes.

Haile et al. (12) reported that 62.2% of women with gestational diabetes exclusively breastfed at hospital discharge, compared to 75.4% of healthy women.

Furthermore, Matias et al. (7) found that maternal prepregnancy obesity and insulin treatment during pregnan cy were independently associated with delayed onset of lactation (lactogenesis II) within the first 2-3 days postpartum, affecting approximately 33% of women with gestational diabetes.

Initiation of any breastfeeding at discharge

Studies by Chamberlain et al. (14) and Nguyen et al. (8) indicated that the prevalence of any breastfeeding at discharge did not differ significantly across feeding types. In Vietnam, Nguyen et al. (8) reported that 94.4% of mothers initiated some form of breastfeeding at the time of discharge Similarly, Chamberlain et al. (14) found that the rate of any breastfeeding among mothers with gestational diabetes remained high, ranging from 82% to 96% during the 24 hours prior to discharge, based on data collected between 2007 and 2010.

In another study, Jirakittidul et al. (19) reported that among mothers with gestational diabetes, any breastfeeding initiation rates were 28.8% within 24 hours, 94% at six weeks, 71% at three months, and 49.8% at six months postpartum.

Continuation of exclusive breastfeeding

Several studies have examined the relationship between gestational diabetes and the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding. Laine et al. (20), in a Sweden study, observed continuation of exclusive breastfeeding up to six months postpartum. Similarly, Reinheimer et al. (22) reported that 93.1% of mothers with gestational diabetes initiated breastfeeding, and 96.1% continued it for up to one month-rates comparable to those observed among healthy mothers.

In contrast, Doughty et al. (16) found disparities in breastfeeding-related knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and experiences between women but reported no significant difference in breastfeeding continuation between women with and without GDM.

Conversely, a number of studies-including those by Nguyen et al. (8), Oza-Frank and Gunderson (9), Riddle and Nommsen-Rivers (10), Bærug et al. (13), Cherubini et al. (15), Glover et al. (17), Hummel et al. (18), Jirakittidul et al. (19), Verd et al. (23), and Wang et al. (24) suggested that mothers with gestational diabetes had lower rates of continued breastfeeding compared to their healthy counterparts.

Jirakittidul et al. (19) reported the rate of exclusive breastfeeding initiation among Thai women with gestational diabetes was 23%, which falls below the national target of 50%. The prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding among these mothers was 35.9% at three months, and 23% at six months postpartum.

In another study, Oza-Frank and Gunderson (9) found that 55.7% of women with gestational diabetes initiated breastfeeding within the first hour of birth, compared to 66.5% of healthy women. Additionally, 39.4% of affected women were exclusively breastfeeding during their hospital stay, in contrast to 52.7% of healthy women.

Oza-Frank and Gunderson (9) and Jirakittidul et al. (19) reported lower breastfeeding initiation rates among mothers with gestational diabetes compared to healthy controls. In another study, Hummel et al. (18) found that 75% of mothers with gestational diabetes initiated breastfeeding, compared to 86% of healthy mothers. The same study also observed a shorter breastfeeding duration-12 weeks among affected mothers with a BMI over 30, compared to 17 weeks in healthy mothers. Exclusive breastfeeding rates were 65% in the affected group versus 80% in the control group.

Nguyen et al. (8) reported that 72.9% of affected mothers continued breastfeeding at 12 months postpartum. In Norway, Bærug et al. (13) found that 56% of mothers with GDM continued breastfeeding at 12 weeks postpartum, compared to 67% of healthy mothers. Similarly, Verd et al. (23) reported that mild gestational glucose tolerance was associated with a 58% cessation rate of exclusive breastfeeding at 100 days postpartum, compared to 48% in healthy mothers.

Glover et al. (17), in a study conducted in the United States, reported that the discontinuation of exclusive breastfeeding among mothers with gestational diabetes was associated with elevated fasting glucose levels, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), BMI, and subscapular skinfold thickness.

Cherubini et al. (15) found that the likelihood of breastfeeding at approximately one year postpartum among mothers with gestational diabetes was 53.5%. Riddle and Nommsen-Rivers (10) observed that gestational diabetes was associated with delayed onset of lactogenesis and lower rates of breastfeeding continuation. In their study, the prevalence of diabetes was 14.9% in the case group compared to 6.1% in the control group.

In contrast, three studies (16, 20, 22) reported no significant differences in breastfeeding practices between affected and healthy mothers. For example, Doughty et al. (16), in a cross-sectional study in the United States, reported a 55% rate of rooming-in among affected mothers and their infants, Laine et al. (20) and Reinheimer et al. (22) documented similar initiation and continuation rates among women with and without gestational diabetes.

Finally, Wang et al. (24) indicated that educational level had the greatest impact on breastfeeding knowledge among pregnant women with gestational diabetes. Three qualitative studies (5, 6, 21) suggested that gestational diabetes may negatively affect the initiation and continuation of exclusive breastfeeding. In Vietnam, Hirst et al. (5) conducted a qualitative study involving 34 women with gestational diabetes, focusing on health-related attitudes and behaviors. Participants expressed concerns regarding the potential transmission of diabetes to their infants, which influenced their breastfeeding decisions.

Jagiello and Chertok (6), in a study involving 27 mothers with GDM, reported that 30% experienced delayed onset of lactogenesis II, and 44% noted insufficient milk supply within the first postpartum hour. Matsunaga et al. (21) explored barriers to breastfeeding initiation and continuation among women with gestational diabetes, identifying maternal and infant-related challenges, limited support from healthcare professionals, and institutional or organizational constraints.

Factors contributing to reduce breastfeeding among women with gestational diabetes

The contributing factors were categorized into five main domains: 1. Personal, 2. Prenatal (pre-delivery), 3. Intrapartum (during delivery), 4. Postpartum (after delivery), and 5. Managerial and health policy factors. These domains encompass physiological factors and three stages of delivery.

1. Personal factors

These were further divided into three subgroups, two of which are considered non-modifiable during pregnancy and lactation

A. Demographic factors, including maternal age, educa tion, race, residence, occupation, income, and pregnancy history

B. Lifestyle factors, such as BMI, smoking, physical ac tivity, medication use, and contraceptive methods.

C. Physiological and psychosocial factors, including perceptions of milk adequacy, attitudes and beliefs, cul tural norms and misconceptions, access to information, and breastfeeding intentions.

The remaining four categories address temporal and systemic aspects affecting exclusive breastfeeding among women with gestational diabetes: prenatal, intrapartum, postpartum, and managerial /health policy factors.

A. Demographic factors

i. Education and age: In this study, women with GDM who had a university education were more likely to initiate and continue exclusive breastfeeding compared to those with only a high school education. Several studies included in this review emphasized the protective role of higher education in promoting breastfeeding. Additionally, younger mothers tended to have shorter breastfeeding durations. Preterm labor and cesarean delivery were identified as two important factors negatively affecting breastfeeding outcomes (13, 15, 17-19, 23).

ii. Number of pregnancies: Primiparous mothers often face more challenges with breastfeeding, as reported in multiple studies (16). First-time mothers tend to breastfeed less frequently than multiparous women, potentially due to the lack of prior breastfeeding experiences, which may otherwise enhance confidence and skill in subsequent pregnancies (7, 21).

iii. Race: The impact of race on breastfeeding continuation has been extensively studied, with several reports indicating a potential negative influence on breastfeeding practices among minority groups (13-15). For example, minority populations have been identified as having a higher risk of not exclusively breastfeeding (15). South Asian mothers tend to cease predominant breastfeeding earlier than Western European mothers, whereas Middle Eastern mothers did not exhibit this trend (13). Differences in breastfeeding duration and continuation have been observed across racial and ethnic groups, with certain populations showing lower rates. Moreover, race and the presence of chronic health conditions have been shown to affect both breastfeeding initiation and duration (13). Chamberlain et al. (14) reported exclusive breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge of 53% among Indigenous women whit GDM, compared to 60% among non-Indigenous women. Cherubini et al. (15) found that Caucasian woman had lower breastfeeding rates.

iv. Place of residence: Chamberlain et al. (14) highlighted that geographical location-particularly in underdeveloped or remote areas with limited access to breastfeeding support services-significantly influences breastfeeding practices. Cherubini et al. (15) documented disparities in breastfeeding continuation rates based on place of residence, with mothers in certain regions facing challenges in sustaining breastfeeding. For example, woman with GDM living in two southern cities of Brazil had lower rates of breastfeeding compared to their counterparts in other regions.

v. Income and job level: Income and employment status significantly affect breastfeeding practices. Higher income levels and certain occupational roles have been associated with a reduced likelihood of exclusive breastfeeding for extended periods. Notably, working mothers face an increased risk of discontinuing breastfeeding within the first postpartum year. These findings underscore the importance of addressing socioeconomic determinants when designing interventions to support sustained breastfeeding. Hirst et al. (5), Cherubini et al. (15) and Wang et al. (24) all emphasized the role of income in breastfeeding practices.

B. Lifestyle and physiological factors

Maintaining a healthy lifestyle and effectively managing gestational diabetes are key to preventing severe complications. Relevant considerations include BMI, smoking, physical activity, medication use, and contraception methods.

i.BMI: Obesity is a well-established risk factor for gestational diabetes and may adversely affect breastfeeding outcomes. Studies indicate that mothers with a high BMI are less likely to breastfeed successfully. For example, Matias et al. (7) found a significant association between obesity and reduced breastfeeding rates. Additionally, women who were obese before pregnancy were less likely to exclusively breastfeed at discharge compared to those with normal pregnancy weight gain. Similarly, failure to adhere to the Institute of Medicine guidelines for gestational weight gain has been linked to lower rates of exclusive breastfeed (21).

In a study conducted among a diverse Australian population, Aboriginal women with diabetes mellitus demonstrated lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding (14). Glover et al. (17) reported that among mothers with gestational diabetes, higher fasting glucose, elevated hemoglobin A1c, and increased BMI were associated with early cessation of breastfeeding.

ii. In several studies conducted Smoking before and during pregnancy, and breastfeeding problems were significantly associated with time to weaning. For example Cherubini et al. (14) found signifighat associated smoking and Cassian of breastfeeding. Reinheimer et al. (22) indicated the important reason for not initiating breastfeeding was maternal smoking during pregnancy.

iii. Medication: The use of certain medications may pose challenges in initiating or maintaining breastfeeding among affected mothers. Some studies suggest that medication use may act as a barrier to breastfeeding initiation; however, the effects of specific medications remain unclear. Mothers with insulin-dependent gestational diabetes have been found to be less likely to breastfeed compared to those with other forms of the condition (12). Chamberlain et al. (14) reported no difference in breastfeeding initiation between mothers with GDM and healthy mother.

An Australian study that included a control group treated with diet or insulin did not report on the use of other medications. The classification of gestational diabetes and the effects of insulin on breastfeeding were not clearly determined.

iv: Pregnancy prevention methods: Jirakittidul et al. (19) reported that the use of hormonal contraceptives for six months adversely affected the continuation of breastfeeding.

C. Psychophysiological factors and beliefs

Psychophysiological factors and maternal beliefs significantly influence exclusive breastfeeding (19). Women’s knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs have been shown to affect exclusive breastfeeding both at the time of hospital discharge and during the postpartum period at home (12, 19). Specifically, factors such as lactogenesis II, milk production, breastfeeding knowledge, attitudes, intentions, self-confidence, self-efficacy, and support from family and friends play crucial roles in sustaining exclusive breastfeeding, particularly in hospital settings (10, 15, 17).

i. Lacto genesis and milk supply: Matias et al. (7) reported that approximately one-third of women with gestational diabetes experienced delayed breastfeeding initiation. A qualitative study by Jagiello and Chertok (6), involving 27 affected women, found that nearly 41% (40.7%) experienced delays in mature milk production and reduced breastfeeding continuity. These women often relied on strategies such as post-delivery breast milk pumping to support breastfeeding (6). Jirakittidul et al. (19) identified several factors influencing breastfeeding continuation before six months, including inadequate support from healthcare personnel, maternal perception of insufficient milk supply, intention to initiate breastfeeding within 24 hours, and the goal to sustain breastfeeding for up to six months. Additionally, Matias et al. (7) noted that delayed lactogenesis (33%) and was linked to prepregnancy obesity and infant’s age at breastfeeding initiation, with such delays being more pronounced among obese women.

ii. Attitudes and beliefs: Attitudes and beliefs about breastfeeding among women with GDM can significantly influence both the initiation and continuation of breast feeding. Doughty et al. (16) emphasized the pivotal role of maternal attitudes in establishing and maintaining exclusive breastfeeding. In their study, sustained exclusive breastfeeding was influenced by several factors. Mother expressed less inclination toward breastfeeding as the optimal choice, particularly when they perceived a preference for formula or mixed feeding from fathers or physicians, or when they felt uncomfortable breastfeeding in front of female friends. Key barriers included the belief that formula is nutritionally equivalent to breast milk and the influence of healthcare professionals. Notably, mothers with diabetes were less likely to room-in with their infants during hospitalization compared to non-diabetic mothers.

Doughty et al. (16) and Jirakittidul et al. (19) also reported that women who acknowledged the benefits of breastfeeding and regarded breast milk as the optimal form of infant nutrition were less likely to believe in the nutritional adequacy of formula. In a qualitative study, Hirst et al. (5) reported that fear of transmitting diabetes through breast milk and a lack of knowledge about appropriate maternal nutrition for successful lactation were important concerns. Wang et al. (24) identified persistent misconceptions, such as the belief that breastfeeding does not prevent infant overweight and that only 3-4 feeds per day are sufficient during the first 2-3 days postpartum.

Significant barriers to effective diabetes management during the postpartum period have been identified, including limited access to nutritional information and insufficient understanding of blood glucose monitoring. Many participants expressed uncertainty about how to perform and interpret blood glucose tests, and some reported concerns regarding the potential health implications of frequent blood glucose monitoring (5). Additionally, Wang et al. (24) reported persistent misconceptions among mothers, such as the belief that breastfeeding does not prevent infant overweight and that only 3-4 feeds per day are advisable during the first 2-3 days postpartum.

iii. Intention: The intention to breastfeed has been identified as the most critical predictor of breastfeeding initiation within the first 24 hours among women, according to Doughty et al. (16). Mother with GDM who had lower confidence in the benefits of exclusive breastfeeding and lacked adequate information were less likely to prepare for optimal feeding their infants. Additionally, studies by Matsunaga et al. (21) in Japan highlighted limited awareness and knowledge among healthcare professionals regarding GDM and breastfeeding. This gap led to insufficient support for all women, particularly those with GDM, and a lack of guidance on breastfeeding's role in preventing type 2 diabetes.

In the study by Hirst et al. (5), mothers expressed reluctance to breastfeed due to concerns about transmitting diabetes through breastfeeding. Early cessation of breastfeeding was attributed to various factors. A study involving 229 mothers who breastfed up to six months identified the intention to breastfeed as the primary predictor for sustaining breastfeeding (19). Conversely, Doughty et al. (16) found that many women with GDM did not have a strong predisposition to exclusively breastfeed prior to childbirth. Haile et al.'s (12) cohort study indicated that women with the condition were six times more likely than healthy counterparts to be unwilling to exclusively breastfeed. Evidence from. Jagiello and Chertok (6) and Doughty et al. (16) suggests that women’s intention to breastfeed is influenced by their knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and confidence regarding breastfeeding.

iv. Self-confidence: Women with low breastfeeding self-efficacy scores were more likely to defer to their husbands and healthcare providers regarding breastfeeding decisions. These women with GDM often expressed concerns about delayed lactogenesis and perceived insufficiency of breast milk, which negatively impacted their confidence as mothers (16). Factors influencing confidence included the intention to breastfeed prior to child birth (5), fears related to GDM and breastfeeding (5, 6), and concerns about newborn health (5, 6).

In one study by Hirst et al. (5) involving 27 affected women, participants indicated lower self-confidence compared to healthy counterparts. Some women were worried about transmission GDM through breast milk and, as a result, chose not to breastfeed. Similarly, in a separate study, Doughty et al. (16) reported that affected women experienced reduced self-confidence associated with challenges in feeding preterm infants.

Self-efficacy also plays a crucial role in blood sugar management, particularly among women with GDM who rely on dietary control. Hirst et al. (5) found that most mothers feared hospital settings for glucose regulation. Mothers with GDM often feared breastfeeding due to inadequate knowledge of diet, blood sugar control, and self- efficacy in managing infant feeding. Additionally, Jirakittidul et al. (19) reported that mothers expressed concerns about the adequacy of their milk.

2. Factors in pregnancy

Research findings suggest that prenatal education positively influences breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge, particularly among affected women. Jirakittidul et al. (19) emphasized the importance of healthcare providers delivering breastfeeding education, supporting mothers’ decision, and assisting with early breastfeeding initiation. Support from healthcare personnel during delivery also plays a crucial role in breastfeeding success (9, 10, 15).

i. Prenatal education

Hirst et al. (5) reported that misconceptions surrounding diabetes and breastfeeding may negatively affect breastfeeding practices among affected mothers. Jagiello and Chertok (6) underscore the importance of identifying and addressing barriers to exclusive breastfeeding.

ii. Support before delivery

In a review study, women affected by GDM reported that professional prenatal support helped them develop effective strategies for exclusive breastfeeding and enhanced their self-confidence during the postpartum period (27). However, they also expressed a need for continuous guidance and support from midwives and healthcare providers throughout pregnancy (9, 10, 12, 14).

For instance, a study conducted in Vietnam in 2012 involving 34 affected women found that although participants understood the health benefits of breastfeeding, they were less likely to breastfeed due to fears of transmitting diabetes through breast milk. These women reported feelings of guilt, confusion, and anxiety, and actively sought reassurance and information from healthcare professionals (5). Additional findings by Jagiello and Chertok (6) emphasized that such fears were often intensified by insufficient support and negative attitudes from staff.

3. Effective factors during childbirth

women with GDM include: a. Vaginal delivery (6, 14), b. Delivery of a full-term infant, and c. Immediate skin- to-skin contact. Additionally, blood glucose monitoring and staff expertise in managing GDM and preventing maternal complications play crucial roles. Adequate support from healthcare personnel is essential to facilitate successful breastfeeding (7, 12, 19).

i. Caesarean section and infant age: Caesarean delivery and premature birth are recognized barriers to exclusive breastfeeding. Nguyen et al. (8) reported that cesarean section was associated with reduced breastfeeding continuation [hazard ratio (HR): 1.22, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01-1.47]. Matias et al. (7) observed a slight decrease in breastfeeding rates among mothers who underwent cesarean sections [adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 1.18, 95% CI: 0.85-1.63]. Chamberlain et al. (14), in a study involving 982 infants born to affected mothers, emphasized that cesarean delivery, prematurity, and low birth weight negatively affected exclusive breastfeeding.

ii. Blood glucose intolerance: Riddle and NommsenRivers (10) demonstrated a significant association between blood glucose intolerance and early cessation of exclusive breastfeeding. Verd et al. (23) identified contributing factors including mild gestational glucose intolerance, maternal obesity, cesarean delivery, premature birth, low birth weight, early introduction of semi-solid foods, and infant illness.

iii. Gender of the baby: The gender of the infant may influence the initiation or continuation of breastfeeding. Laine et al. (20) reported that mothers facing breastfeeding challenges tended to breastfed boys for a longer duration than girls, although the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, both Matias et al. (7) and Verd et al. (23) found no gender-based differences in breastfeeding rates. Other studies did not specifically address gender-related breastfeeding outcomes (7-9, 13, 21, 23)

iv. Skin-to-skin contact: Skin-to-skin contact after birth was identified as a crucial factor positively influencing breastfeeding initiation in hospitals among affected women. Jirakittidul et al.’s study (19) involving 299 affected mothers, demonstrated that adequate support and guidance from healthcare providers significantly increased early breastfeeding initiation, minimized mother- infant separation, and promoted early skin-to-skin contact. In contrast, Jagiello and Chertok’s study (6) reported that skin -to- skin contact was not performed.

4. Hospital support factors postpartum

i. Frequency of exclusive breastfeeding: Jagiello and Chertok (6) found that frequent exclusive breastfeeding positively impacted breastfeeding practices in the hospital. An intervention study involving 139 infants born to mothers with diabetes demonstrated that feeding every 2-3 hours in the hospital, immediate skin-to-skin contact at birth, and reduced formula supplementation contributed to improved breastfeeding outcomes (16).

ii. Mother and baby rooming-in: Rooming-in of the mother and infant plays a critical role in the initiation and continuation of exclusive breastfeeding. Matsunaga et al. (21) emphasized the importance of both rooming-in and skin-to-skin contact as facilitators of breastfeeding initiation and maintenance. Jirakittidul et al. (19) suggested that improved breastfeeding and mother- infant roomingin help prevent hypoglycemia episodes.

In a phenomenological study by Jagiello and Chertok (6), mothers’ concerns about neonatal hypoglycemia prompted formula supplementation despite adequate milk production. Other mothers feared neonatal hypoglycemia due to delayed initiation and separation from their infant. Doughty et al. (16) reported that premature infants experienced feeding difficulties. Among infants who did not require Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission, those with low birth weight were less likely to be room-in iii. Management of neonatal hypoglycemia: Postpartum support from healthcare professionals plays a vital role in promoting exclusive breastfeeding. Several studies have reported insufficient hospital support for breastfeeding. Moreover, some healthcare providers have recommended the use of formula supplements (12, 16, 18, 22).

According to Oza-Frank and Gunderson (9), five key factors influence the initiation of exclusive breastfeeding among affected mothers in hospital settings: 1. Inadequate staff cooperation, 2. Increased reliance on formula, 3. Exclusive breastfeeding limited to the hospital, 4. Positive experiences with breastfeeding equipment, and 5. The use of breast pumps, which increased breastfeeding rates.

Hirst et al. (5) found that affected mothers lacked access to internists, nutritionists, and counselors. Chamberlain et al. (14) reported that affected indigenous mothers in remote areas faced significant barriers, including a lack of essential facilities to sustain breastfeeding.

iv. Training of personnel: Matsunaga et al. (21) identified three major categories of barriers to breastfeeding: 1. Mother–infant-related obstacles, such as medical interventions for the infant and maternal health-related breastfeeding difficulties, 2. Barriers related to healthcare professionals, including support for women with HIV at an individual level and challenges in interprofessional collaboration, and 3. organizational barriers, such as limited resources, inadequate support, and restrictive policies.

The study demonstrated that breastfeeding reduces the risk of type 2 diabetes in women GDM (21).

Discussion

GDM has been the subject of investigation for over half a century. Nonetheless, several aspects related to diagnosis, screening, management, and follow-up-particularly its effect on breastfeeding and maternal-infant complications- remain poorly understood. Mothers with GDM often face breastfeeding challenges due to inadequate targeted education, limited social support, and insufficient resources. Future research should incorporate cultural and psychosocial factors to strengthen evidence-based practices.

These gaps present challenges in clinical care, cultural beliefs, and systemic barriers, complicating efforts to optimize breastfeeding among mother with GDM.

The studies included in this systematic review consistently demonstrate an association between GDM and reduced rates of both breastfeeding initiation and continuation. Although most mothers with GDM exhibit high rates of any breastfeeding initiation, they show significantly lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding. This discrepancy underscores the need for standardized data collection and assessment methods to improve comparability and interpretability across studies.

Some studies have highlighted complex associations between GDM and breastfeeding. For instance, one study demonstrated a relationship between GDM, depression, childbirth experiences, and reduced breastfeeding rates (24). Blair et al. (28) found that lactating women with GDM had a lower prevalence of metabolic syndrome in the early postpartum period compared to their non-lactating counterparts. These findings suggest potential health benefits of breastfeeding among women with GDM and underscore the need for individualized support approaches.

Physiological challenges -such as delayed onset of lactogenesis II and perceptions of insufficient milk supply -along with psychosocial determinants, including maternal attitudes, beliefs, self-efficacy, and intentions, significantly influence lactation outcomes. Prepregnancy obesity and insulin use during pregnancy have been implicated in delayed lactation (7, 17). Both gestational diabetes and cesarean delivery can delay lactogenesis II and milk production, contributing to early cessation of breastfeeding. Additional barriers include complications related to midwifery and newborn care, prolonged mother- infant separation, and the use of powdered milk to prevent neonatal hypoglycemia (6, 16). Cordero et al. (29) specifically reported that mothers with GDM frequently used powdered milk for this purpose Infant weight loss is another influencing factor. Verd et al. (23) reported an average infant weight loss at discharge of 1.73 (95% CI, 1.26-2.36 AOR), an aspect often overlooked in other studies. Early promotion of skin-to-skin contact, minimizing mother-infant separation, and providing enhanced support for breastfeeding challenges are crucial improving outcomes (6, 30).

Fallon et al. (31) applied Maslow’s hierarchy of needs to conceptualize maternal requirements and propose supportive strategies. Chertok and Sherby (32) demonstrated that targeted education and support can enhance breastfeeding confidence by improving self-efficacy and addressing maternal concerns. Matsunaga et al. (21) noted that barriers to implementing breastfeeding guidelines may arise from healthcare provider practices, patient related factors, and health policy limitations. Overcoming these obstacles requires comprehensive strategies that strengthen maternal and infant support, improve healthcare professional training, and optimize organizational structures. Collaboration among all stakeholders is essential to achieve sustainable improvements.

For mothers with GDM, access to adequate resources, support systems, and appropriate equipment is critical to optimizing breastfeeding, reducing complications, and ensuring effective management (21). Current research has examined the diagnosis of GDM in parallel with therapeutic and supportive interventions during breastfeeding, taking into account physiological, demographics, cultural, attitudes, and biochemical factors.

However, several critical factors-such as infant weight at discharge, oxygen requirements, five-minute Apgar score, insulin treatment for GDM, maternal medication or drug use, and contraceptive methods during exclusive breastfeeding-require further investigation. Misconceptions about GDM and breastfeeding outcomes also persist. Although many studies have examined maternal medication use during lactation, evidence indicates that women with GDM who require insulin therapy are less likely to breastfeed than those who achieve glycemic control through dietary measures alone.

Qualitative studies by Matsunaga et al. (21) and Jagiello and Chertok (6) highlight the importance of identifying and addressing barriers to exclusive breastfeeding. These barriers encompass the need for targeted maternal education and support, interprofessional collaboration to facilitate timely breastfeeding initiation, staff training, policy development, and structured postpartum breastfeeding management. Matsunaga et al. (21) further emphasized the role of organizational factors, including hospital resources, staffing capacity, provider knowledge, and the value of post-discharge follow-up.

Early postpartum support-particularly within the first two weeks to months after delivery-is critical for helping mothers overcome initial challenges. Importantly, breastfeeding has been shown to reduce the long term risk of developing type 2 diabetes among women with GDM (21).

Insufficient communication between healthcare staff and mothers with GDM has been identified as a barrier to both the initiation and continuation of breastfeeding (21). Oza-Frank and Gunderson (9) underscored the importance of prenatal education, highlighting the need for enhanced efforts to support women with GDM throughout pregnancy and postpartum. Matias et al. (7) emphasized that early, targeted support is essential for women with GDM -particularly those with additional risk factors -to facilitate timely breastfeeding initiation and promote exclusive lactation.

Doughty et al. (16) reported differences in breastfeeding-related knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and experiences between women with GDM and their healthy counterparts, suggesting that these areas could be effectively addressed through tailored interventions. They further noted the influence of guidance services, rooming-in practices, and staff availability on exclusive breastfeeding rates.

A review by Cummins et al. (27). highlighted that breastfeeding intention, self-efficacy, and continuous support are critical determinants of successful breastfeeding for both women with GDM and healthy mothers. However, for women with GDM, immediate skin-to-skin contact after birth and frequent breastfeeding are particularly important to prevent delayed lactogenesis II, neonatal hypoglycemia, and delayed onset of mature milk production. Similarly, a systematic review by Manerkar et al. (33) reported higher in hospital formula supplementation rates, shorter breastfeeding duration, and reduced continuation rates up to 12 months postpartum among mothers with GDM.

Three qualitative studies by Hirst et al. (5), Matsunaga et al. (21), and Jagiello and Chertok (6) consistently reported a decline in breastfeeding among mothers with GDM . Matsunaga et al. (21) identified key barriers encountered by healthcare professionals, including: 1. Limited interdepartmental and interdisciplinary collaboration, 2. Inadequate breastfeeding support resources, and 3. Insufficient in-hospital- support systems. Jagiello and Chertok (6) highlighted the challenges mothers face in initiating and maintaining breastfeeding, underscoring the importance of targeted education, immediate postnatal breastfeeding counseling, and structured follow-up for mother-infant dyads. Both Hirst et al. (5) and Jagiello and Chertok (6) also reported a persistent misconception among mothers that breast milk could harm their infants, representing a substantial barrier to breastfeeding.

Furthermore, Jagiello and Chertok (6) and Matsunaga et al. (21) highlighted infant health challenges, particularly neonatal hypoglycemia, as significant obstacles to breastfeeding among mothers with GDM. They emphasized the importance of breast pump use as a strategy to maintain adequate milk supply in these mothers. Benton et al. (34) discussed the role of maternal beliefs in counteracting negative perceptions and stigmas related to GDM, which can impact both perinatal and postpartum mental and physical health.

Otter et al. (35) underscored the necessity of tailoring breastfeeding education and support to the unique needs and challenges of affected mothers, advocating for the involvement of family members and significant others in support strategies. To enhance the applicability of these findings, further research across culturally diverse populations and countries with varying income levels is warranted.

Kim et al. (36), in a systematic review and meta-analysis, highlighted the critical role of educational interventions in initiating and sustaining exclusive breastfeeding among mothers with GDM. Park et al. (37) demonstrated that targeted breastfeeding education for pregnant women with GDM not only mitigates long-term adverse effects but also reinforces positive health beliefs through enhanced breastfeeding experiences. To strengthen evidence in this field, multicenter trials employing consistent diagnostic criteria are needed to effectively monitor breastfeeding outcomes and validate the efficacy of such interventions.

Both Matsunaga et al. (21) and Jagiello and Chertok (6) independently underscored the necessity of comprehensive breastfeeding support. Jagiello and Chertok (6) emphasized the importance of specific follow-up recommendations for mother-infant dyads, while Matsunaga et al. (21) focused on the significance of education, staff training, and immediate postpartum counseling. However, challenges remain, particularly in providing follow-up support to mothers who face barriers such as geographical distance or insufficient assistance inter-facility coordination, which hinder access to continuous breastfeeding assistance.

Qian et al. (38) found that mothers with GDM often underestimate the long-term risks associated with the condition and benefits of breastfeeding, largely due to inadequate information, feeding choices, existing barriers, and a lack of social support.

Multiple studies included in this systematic review consistently demonstrate an association between GDM and reduced rates of both breastfeeding initiation and duration. The variability observed across studies underscores the need for standardized data collection and assessment methods. While initiation rates for any breastfeeding remain relatively high, exclusive breastfeeding rates among mothers with GDM are persistently low.

Key physiological barriers identified include delayed onset of lactogenesis II and perceived insufficient milk supply. Psychosocial determinants influencing lactation outcomes involve maternal attitudes, beliefs, self-efficacy, and intentions. Additionally, prepregnancy obesity and insulin treatment during pregnancy have been implicated in contributing to delayed lactation.

This study’s strengths include the incorporation of three qualitative analyses that specifically explore breastfeeding, focusing on family support, maternal attitudes, and barriers. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited by the predominant use of American samples, given the diverse cultural attitudes and healthcare resources globally. The heterogeneity in data collection tools and language differences across studies further constrained comprehensive analysis. Additionally, reliance on self- reported data from mothers with GDM and inconsistencies in breastfeeding definitions across studies may have influenced the outcomes.

Some studies (7, 10, 14) used patient records, which introduce challenges such as data integrity issues, coding discrepancies, and temporal variations that may affect the robustness of the findings. For example, one study included data collected prior to July 2007 (14), while another retrospective US study from 2014 (7) faced potential measurement bias.

This review also examined factors like diet, physical activity, and pregnancy weight gain and their possible influence on breastfeeding duration. Most maternal studies did not account for chronic diseases, although some relevant variables such as Apgar scores and oxygen requirements were considered. To minimize confounding effects of prior breastfeeding experience, most studies only primiparous mothers.

Conclusion

The global increase in GDM underscores the urgent need for targeted strategies to support breastfeeding among affected mothers. Prolonged breastfeeding has the potential to reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes and associated complications in this population. However, key barriers such as limited organizational resources, inadequate hospital support, restrictive policies, and insufficient post-discharge follow-up hinder breastfeeding success. This study highlights the significant impact of cultural misconceptions, maternal and neonatal complications, and systemic healthcare challenges on breastfeeding outcomes. To improve maternal and infant health, it is essential to these barriers through enhanced collaboration among healthcare providers and the implementation of tailored, culturally sensitive interventions
