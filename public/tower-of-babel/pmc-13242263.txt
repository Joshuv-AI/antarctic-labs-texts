Temperature-dependent growth and development of Theocolax elegans (Hymenoptera: Pteromalidae) parasitizing Lasioderma serricorne (Coleoptera: Ptinidae)
Authors: Qingjiao Yuan, Jianhua Lü, Chunqi Bai, Yafei Guo, Linshan Du, Jiaqin Xi, Christos Athanassiou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to explore the growth, development, and reproductive capacity of the parasitoid wasp, Theocolax elegans (Westwood) (Hymenoptera: Pteromalidae) reared on the pupae of Lasioderma serricorne (Fabricius) (Coleoptera: Ptinidae), the influence of temperature on lifespan, fecundity, development duration, development threshold temperature, and effective accumulated temperature of T. elegans was investigated at 20, 25, and 30 °C and 65 ± 5% relative humidity under dark conditions. Temperature significantly affected the development and reproductive capacity of T. elegans, and the optimum temperature range for T. elegans development was 25 to 30 °C. The adult longevity, developmental duration, and fecundity of T. elegans increased at 25 °C, and decreased at 30 °C, compared to that at 20 °C. The developmental threshold temperature of preoviposition was 9.65 ± 0.86 °C, and the effective accumulated temperature was 247.59 ± 34.47 d °C. The present results provided valuable information for the large-scale rearing of T. elegans with L. serricorne pupae as hosts.Keywords: Theocolax elegans, Lasioderma serricorne, growth, developmental duration, reproductive capacity

Introduction

Grain is the necessary material for human survival and healthy development. During storage, transportation, and processing of grain after harvest, occurrence of stored grain pest insects frequently results in serious quantity and quality losses (Upadhyay and Ahmad 2011, Pugazhvendan et al. 2012). At present, chemical pesticides are still mainly applied to control stored grain pest insects, which have caused serious “3R” issues (resistance, resurgence, and residue) (Ingegno and Tavella 2022). Thus, there is an urgent need for scientific, sustainable strategies to deal with stored grain pest insects. Biological control by utilizing dominant parasitic natural enemies is a sustainable, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly strategy for controlling stored-product pests, which has gradually become a research hotspot (Hansen 2007).

Theocolax elegans is a solitary ectoparasitoid and has a wide range of hosts primarily including Coleoptera and Lepidoptera species, such as Sitophilus oryzae (Linnaeus) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), Sitophilus zeamais (Motschulsky) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), Lasioderma serricorne (Fabricius) (Coleoptera: Ptinidae), and Prostephanus truncatus (Horn) (Coleoptera: Bostrichidae) (Bare 1942, Williams and Floyd 1971, Flinn et al. 1996, Helbig 1998). It can parasitize larvae or pupae of hosts (Van Den Assem and Kuenen 1958, Shariff 1972). After locating a suitable host, the female wasp injects venom inside the host to paralyze it and then lays a single egg on the paralyzed host (Wen and Brower 1995). T. elegans is safe for humans, environmentally friendly, and does not result in pest insect resistance when used to prevent the damage of stored-product insects (Hokkanen and Lynch 2010). It has great potential in controlling stored grain pest insects. Williams and Floyd (1971) found that T. elegans was able to suppress populations of the maize weevil in corn by 89%.

Lasioderma serricorne is one of the most damaging pest insects of stored products in the world. It can seriously decrease the quality of stored tobacco, tea, cereals, legumes, dried dates, oilseed crops, zoological and botanical specimens, cocoa beans, leather, and rattan products (Ebadollahi et al. 2010). The larvae of L. serricorne burrows down into the food, damaging tobacco by eating small galleries through the host material (Ashworth 1993). In addition, carcasses and excrement of L. serricorne contaminate tobacco leaves and manufactured tobacco products, thereby seriously compromising the usability of tobacco leaves and the overall quality of cigarettes (Arbogast et al. 2002). Studies have shown that T. elegans had strong potential to control L. serricorne (Yuan et al. 2025), but little is known about the biology of T. elegans to parasitize L. serricorne, and more information on the life history parameters of T. elegans is necessary for the large-scale reproduction and to evaluate the parasitic potential of T. elegans.

Considering that insects are poikilothermous, temperature is the most important factor affecting insect biology and ecology, such as growth and development, reproduction, population growth, distribution and population dynamics (Logan et al. 1976, Régnière et al. 2012). In addition, several crucial indicators for evaluating the biological control potential of natural enemies, such as parasitism rate, fertility, and host searching behavior are affected by ambient temperatures (Iturralde-García et al. 2020). Imamura et al. (2004) found that T. elegans appears to have huge potential as a biological control agent comparable to Anisopteromalus calandrae and Lariophagus distinguendus from the viewpoint of population increase at 25 °C. Furthermore, temperature is a key factor influencing the biological parameters of T. elegans, including developmental period, adult longevity, mean number of progeny, and so on. Therefore, deeply understanding influence of different temperatures on the growth and development of T. elegans is very important to improve its biological control effectiveness.

At present, most of researches on T. elegans mainly focus on using internal stored grain insects (the insects feed inside the grain), such as S. oryzae, S. zeamais, and Rhyzopertha dominica, as hosts when mass-rearing T. elegans. It is difficult to accurately control the number of hosts and observe the growth and development process of T. elegans during their artificial rearing. In addition, different hosts influence the parasitism rate and oviposition preferences of the wasp, it could affect the large-scale rearing of them, thereby impacting their potential for biological control (Maille et al. 2025). Our preliminary research showed that pupae of L. serricorne fed with whole wheat flour can be used as suitable hosts for rearing T. elegans, but it still lacks relevant basic biological parameters for artificial large-scale breeding. Therefore, the research aims to investigate the growth and development of T. elegans reared on pupae of L. serricorne at different temperatures.

Materials and Methods

Insects

T. elegans was obtained from the infested wheat grain in an experimental warehouse, and reared on L. serricorne pupae (aged 30 to 35 d after hatching) in the incubation chamber at 30 ± 2 °C, 65 ± 5% relative humidity (RH) under dark conditions. L. serricorne were maintained in the Institute of Stored Product Insects of Henan University of Technology, Zhengzhou, China. T. elegans and L. serricorne have been reared for more than 3 generations. To obtain L. serricorne pupae for the following experiments, about 300 L. serricorne adults were allowed to oviposit on 100 g whole wheat flour (Jinshahe whole wheat flour, Jinshahe Flour Manufacturing Co., Ltd.) for 3 d, then the adults were removed, and the whole wheat flour was kept in an incubation chamber at 30 ± 2 °C, 65 ± 5% RH, and dark for 35 d.

Temperature-Dependent Lifespan and Reproduction of T. elegans

Ten pairs of mated T. elegans adults (1 d old) and 10 L. serricorne pupae were released in a plastic container (top diameter: 3.8 cm, bottom diameter: 2.9 cm, height: 3.2 cm), respectively. All L. serricorne pupae were collected daily at the same time and replaced with new ones until the female wasp died. Then, the parasitized L. serricorne pupae were cultured in a dark controlled temperature chamber at different temperatures (20, 25, and 30 °C) and 65 ± 5% RH until no T. elegans adults emerged. The survival status of newly emerged T. elegans adults were regularly observed at 10:00 every day until they died in every box. The number and sex of newly emerged T. elegans adults in each plastic box were observed and recorded. Each treatment of different temperatures was repeated 5 times.

Temperature-Dependent Development of T. elegans

A group of 20 L. serricorne pupae parasitized by T. elegans (1 d old, 1 T. elegans egg per L. serricorne pupa) were transferred into a 25 ml plastic container, respectively. Then the containers were placed into constant temperature incubators at 20, 25, and 30 °C, and 65 ± 5% RH, respectively. The hatching of T. elegans eggs was observed every 24 h under a microscope. Then, the newly emerged T. elegans adults were observed and recorded daily. Twenty replicates were conducted.

Data Analysis

Mean values and standard errors of the adult longevity, the number of offspring, sex ratio, the developmental duration (N), daily egg production, and the number of newly emerged T. elegans adults per day were estimated by using SPSS Statistics 20. The adult longevity, number of offspring, developmental duration, daily egg production, and number of newly emerged T. elegans adults per day were transformed to log10(x + 1) scale, and sex ratio was transformed to arcsine square-root values before being subjected to 1-way ANOVA analysis. Results

Temperature-Dependent Adult Longevity and Reproduction of T. elegans Females

The temperature significantly affected the adult longevity (F = 6.83; df = 2, 12; P < 0.05), the number of offspring (F = 4.45; df = 2,12; P < 0.05), and sex ratio (F = 9.69; df = 2, 12, P < 0.05) of T. elegans (Table 1). T. elegans exhibited the shortest lifespan of 6.6 d at 30 °C, and the longest life of 11.00 d at 25 °C. The maximum number of offspring was 33.20 at 25 °C, and the minimum was 13.00 at 20 °C. The sex ratio of offspring had a maximum value of 75% at 25 °C. T. elegans females displayed high egg production during the first 4 d at 20, 25, and 30 °C, respectively, and the longest egg-laying duration was 15 d at 25 °C (Fig. 1). In a whole, the optimum temperature for breeding of T. elegans was 25 °C.

Temperature-Dependent Development Duration

The developmental duration significantly decreased with increasing temperature (F = 14.61; df = 2, 57; P < 0.05) (Table 2). The developmental threshold temperature was 9.65 °C, the effective accumulated temperature was 247.59 d °C, and the generation prediction formula was N=(247.59 ± 34.47)/(T-9.65 ± 0.86). The number of predictive generations per year at 20, 25, and 30 °C were in the range of 12 to 36. Environmental temperature significantly affected the number of newly emerged T. elegans (F = 6.88; df = 2, 12; P < 0.05). Adults of T. elegans emerged from 19th d to 28th d at 20 °C, from 15th d to 23th d at 25 °C, and from 15th d to 28th d at 30 °C, respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present results showed that 25 °C was the favorable temperature for T. elegans growth and development when considering adult lifespan, offspring, and higher sex ratio (female to total), and L. serricorne was a suitable host for mass-production of T. elegans.

The environment temperature significantly affects the growth and development of insects because insects are poikilothermic animals (Hagstrum and Milliken 1988, Mironidis 2014). T. elegans exhibited the longest lifespan at 25 °C in the present study, but Imamura et al. (2004) reported that T. elegans had the longest lifespan at 20 °C. The reason may be as follows: Imamura et al. (2004) did not provide food, water, and plenty of hosts, and this study observed the life span of T. elegans under adequate host supply, suggesting that providing sufficient hosts may have a significant impact on the lifespan of T. elegans (Flinn and Hagstrum 2002). Additionally, it might also be due to the different hosts used: the hosts are L. serricorne pupae in the current study, and S. zeamais in Imamura et al. (2004). The hosts may differ in body size, developmental stage, nutritional quality, and preferred developmental temperature, all of which could influence the development, fecundity, and parasitism performance of T. elegans. Another possible reason could be that the temperature intervals were set differently: 5 °C temperature intervals in the current study, whereas a smaller temperature interval of 2 or 3 °C in Imamura et al. (2004). Sex ratios (67.92% to 70.99%) of T. elegans at the range of 20 to 30 °C in the present study are the same to the results (67% to 71%) of Imamura et al. (2004). The development duration of T. elegans decreased with increasing temperature at the range of 20 to 25 °C, which is similar to previous research (Imamura et al. 2004). Development duration (18.74 d at 25 °C) of T. elegans in the present study is different from the result of Van Den Assem and Kuenen (1958). Their study showed that T. elegans could complete one generation development within approximately 25 d at 25 °C. This may be due to differences in hosts. The hosts are L. serricorne and S. granarius in the current study and Van Den Assem and Kuenen (1958), respectively.

The favorable temperature range and the number of offspring of T. elegans is different from A. calandrae (Howard) (Hymenoptera: Pteromalidae) when both reared on L. serricorne larvae (Guo et al. 2022). T. elegans performed a great control ability of L. serricorne when the temperature was lower (25 °C) in the present study, while A. calandrae had a better control effect at higher temperature (30 to 36 °C) (Niedermayer et al. 2013). The number of eggs (33.2) laid by T. elegans was less than that (55.3) laid by A. calandrae at 25 °C (Guo et al. 2019). This may be due to the smaller body size of T. elegans, which has less nutrients available for offspring production. In addition, the difference may also be due to the different hosts: the pupae were used in the present study, and larvae were used in Guo et al. (2019).

Temperature is one of the most important factors in determining the effectiveness of biological control of parasitoids (Hassell 1978, van Lenteren 1986, Mahdian et al. 2006). Toews et al. (2001) found that when the R. dominica reproduced at greater densities, T. elegans showed increased suppression at the lower temperature (27 °C) compared to that at the upper temperature (34 °C). Flinn (1998) indicated that T. elegans had a very high level of population suppression to R. dominica at 25 °C. Flinn and Hagstrum (2002) established a temperature-mediated functional response for T. elegans parasitizing R. dominica, showing that both parasitization rate and instantaneous search rate were the highest at 30 °C.

Inundation biological control is an environmentally friendly pest insect control method, and mass-rearing is important for inundated release of parasitoids (Eilenberg et al. 2001). The present study clarify L. serricorne is a suitable host for mass-rearing of T. elegans. However, there is still lack of the functional response of T. elegans to the hosts L. serricorne. Therefore, the potential limitations or variations in L. serricorne populations that could influence rearing success, for example, the variability in host quality or availability and how it might affect parasitoid performance under different conditions, need further research.

The current study just investigated the effect of temperature on growth and development of T. elegans. However, other environmental factors, such as light, humidity, feeding experience, host quality characters, volatile substances, and nutrition supply, etc., also affect the growth, development, and control potential of the parasitoid. Germinara et al. (2009) found that T. elegans readily perceives common grain volatiles, including valeraldehyde, maltol, and vanillin, which elicited dose-dependent behavioral responses to find hosts of T. elegans. Among the different host substrates (brown rice, maize, sorghum, and wheat), T. elegans preferred to oviposit on S. zeamais that developed on brown rice (Sitthichaiyakul and Amornsak 2017). Maille et al. (2025) found that T. elegans most preferred damaged grain infested by R. dominica in a 4-way olfactometer, and appeared most effective in suppressing R. dominica. The influence of these environmental factors on the T. elegans deserves to be fully explored in the future.

In conclusion, temperature significantly affected the growth, development, and reproductive capacity of T. elegans, and the optimum temperature for T. elegans development was 25 °C, while the longevity and development duration were shortened. The fecundity of T. elegans decreased with increasing temperature within a certain temperature range. The present results provided valuable information for the large-scale reproduction of the T. elegans reared on L. serricorne pupae.
