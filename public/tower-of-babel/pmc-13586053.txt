Corneal Sequestration and Bilateral Distichiasis in a Binturong (Arctictis binturong)
Authors: Vienna Lunking, Mandy Womble, Hannah L Smith, Ashley Souza, Sarah Ozawa, Tara Harrison, Elizabeth Rose, Annie Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo report the first documented case of corneal sequestration and distichiasis in a binturong ( Arctictis binturong ), discuss implications of disease prevalence in a non‐domestic feliform, and hypothesize on potential etiology and predisposing factors for corneal sequestration.Animal StudiedA 15‐year‐old intact male binturong.ProceduresAnesthetized physical examination and ophthalmic assessment were performed by board‐certified specialists. Corneal cytology, culture and sensitivity, debridement of the sequestra in lieu of keratectomy, histopathology, and cryoepilation were performed, followed by 4 weeks of topical therapy. The patient was euthanized 3 years later for unrelated reasons, and a necropsy was performed.ResultsExamination found bilateral distichiasis. In the left cornea, two brown, ovoid lesions flanked an area of yellow infiltrate, all overlying a raised, ulcerated granulation bed. Corneal cytology identified neutrophils, cocci, and rare rods. Histopathology of the debrided tissue showed coagulative to lytic necrosis with intralesional gram‐positive cocci, indicating sequestration and secondary infection. Aerobic culture yielded Staphylococcus delphini and Streptococcus acidominimus . Photo updates 4 weeks post‐operation showed resolution of clinical signs. Later, necropsy and histology showed chronic ocular changes without recurrence of sequestra.ConclusionsClinical and histopathologic findings identified corneal sequestration, with chronic irritation secondary to distichiasis as a suspected factor. Surgical debridement and cryoepilation achieved long‐term clinical resolution, with only mild distichiae regrowth and no sequestration recurrence after 3 years. Corneal sequestration is thought to predominantly affect domestic cats ( Felis catus ); this report expands the clinical relevance of corneal sequestration to an exotic species and supports chronic corneal irritation as a predisposing factor.Keywords: binturong, corneal pigmentation, corneal sequestra, distichiasis, feliform, infected ulcer

Introduction

Corneal stromal sequestration is an ocular disorder predominantly described in domestic cats ( Felis catus ) [1, 2], with rare reports in other species such as dogs ( Canis lupus familiaris ) [3, 4, 5] and horses ( Equus ferus caballus ) [6, 7]. Despite these exceptions, corneal sequestration remains overwhelmingly a feline condition, commonly diagnosed based on its characteristic clinical appearance: localized corneal coagulative necrosis and pigmentation of the diseased stroma that clinically results in a tan, brown, or black region of corneal discoloration that varies in size and depth [1, 2, 8, 9]. The exact etiology and pathogenesis of feline corneal sequestra remain unknown, though chronic irritation of the ocular surface has been proposed as a contributing factor [2, 8, 10]. Other potential predisposing factors include breed (with overrepresentation of Persians and Himalayans), brachycephaly, lagophthalmos, entropion, trichiasis, feline herpesvirus‐1, tear film abnormalities, chronic preexisting keratitis, corneal ulceration, and corneal trauma [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Although distichiasis is rare in cats, it has also been implicated as a possible cause [13]. While some superficial corneal sequestra may eventually slough over time or improve with medical management [14], literature from within the past decade generally favors surgical removal via keratectomy, with or without placement of an additional protective covering (e.g., graft or soft bandage contact lens) [8, 10, 11, 15, 16, 17].

This report describes the clinical presentation of a binturong ( Arctictis binturong ) diagnosed with corneal ulceration and sequestration in the left eye (oculus sinister, OS), as well as chronic corneal irritation and distichiasis in both eyes (oculus uterque, OU). The binturong, also known as a bearcat, is a predominantly frugivorous, nocturnal, and arboreal mammal endemic to the forests of south and southeast Asia [18, 19, 20, 21]. Despite favoring a fruit‐heavy diet, the binturong has omnivorous tendencies and is one of only two carnivorous species with a prehensile tail [19, 22]. It is the largest member of the Viverridae family, which belongs to the monophyletic suborder of Feliformia [17, 18, 19, 20]. Feliformia encompasses a variety of “cat‐like” carnivores, which is reflected in multiple conserved traits seen in both binturongs and felids such as semi‐retractable claws, similar dentition, and analogous grooming behaviors [21, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Despite these similarities, binturongs find themselves in a separate taxonomic family from domestic cats, with estimated divergence times of approximately 23 million years ago (Mya) for Viverridae and 11 Mya for Felidae [21, 27, 28, 29].

Binturongs are markedly underrepresented in the scientific literature concerning ecological, physiological, and clinical data; thus, normative data on ocular health and the prevalence of common ophthalmic conditions in binturongs remain poorly defined. In fact, PubMed searches for “binturong ophthalmology,” “binturong ocular,” or “binturong eye” yielded zero results. To date, only one publication has noted ocular pathology of any kind in this species [20]. That study reviewed medical records of 74 captive binturongs housed in North American zoological institutions between 1986 and 2019, broadly assessing morbidity and mortality not limited to ocular findings. Of these binturongs, only three individuals were reported to have ophthalmic disease—all cases involved conjunctivitis, with one also presenting with blepharospasm and increased intraocular pressure. These findings highlight the lack of ophthalmic information pertaining to the binturong, including data regarding their ocular anatomy, tear film composition, susceptibility to particular ophthalmic pathogens, or other factors associated with corneal sequestration. Thus, the lack of comparative data makes it difficult to know whether the domestic cat, or any other species, would make an appropriate analog from which to extrapolate. The objective of this case report is to contribute to the limited data available regarding ophthalmology of the binturong. Further, the novel finding of corneal sequestra in a non‐domestic feliform with concurrent ocular pathology (distichiasis) expands our understanding of disease prevalence beyond the domestic cat. Lastly, it also contributes to the discussion on possible etiologies of corneal sequestration, including both clinical and genetic considerations.

Case History

On July 17, 2022, 3 weeks prior to presentation at the North Carolina State University College of Veterinary Medicine (NCSU CVM), a 15‐year‐old male intact captive binturong was observed by his caretakers to have swelling above the left eye, suspected to be the result of a bee sting. Diphenhydramine was administered (route and dose are unknown) to treat a potential allergic reaction. While the swelling resolved by the following day, the patient developed blepharospasm OS over the subsequent week, accompanied by noticeable corneal cloudiness. The patient was evaluated by his primary veterinarian, who submitted a corneal swab for culture and sensitivity. Results came back positive for Staphylococcus delphini, with reported sensitivity to ofloxacin. The patient received oral meloxicam (dose unknown) once daily for 5 days and topical ofloxacin 0.3% ophthalmic solution twice daily OS for approximately 10 days, without resolution of clinical signs. Due to lack of improvement, the patient was presented to the NCSU CVM for further evaluation including physical examination, bloodwork, and an ophthalmology consult under general anesthesia. Apart from a history of hepatocellular carcinoma that was completely excised under general anesthesia in 2018 and confirmed via histopathology, the patient had no other reported major medical events.

The patient was brought to the NCSU CVM on August 10, 2022, at which time he was premedicated with 5.4 mg/kg of ketamine, 22.7 mcg/kg of dexmedetomidine, and 0.54 mg/kg of butorphanol intramuscularly (IM). Once sedate, intravenous (IV) catheters were placed in both cephalic veins, and the patient was given 0.18 mg/kg of midazolam and an additional 1.09 mg/kg of ketamine IV for induction. The patient was intubated with a size 6.5 mm endotracheal tube, anesthesia was maintained via 1.25% isoflurane and oxygen, and IV lactated ringer's solution was administered throughout the anesthetic event. One hypotensive event was managed with a constant rate infusion of norepinephrine at a rate ranging from 0.05 to 0.3 mcg/kg/min.

A complete physical examination and systemic health assessment were performed under general anesthesia by a veterinarian board‐certified in zoological medicine and revealed no overt abnormalities. Complete blood count and serum biochemistry profile were unremarkable apart from a suspected mild azotemia, suggestive of either dehydration or early renal disease. Blood urea nitrogen was measured at 55 mg/dL (feline reference interval 17–35 mg/dL), and creatinine was 2.3 mg/dL (feline reference interval 0.8–2.3 mg/dL); a literature search failed to identify any published binturong‐specific reference intervals, so values were extrapolated from ranges reported for adult cats, though the significance in binturongs is unknown [30]. Because blood could only be drawn after the patient was premedicated, results of the complete blood count and serum biochemistry were only returned after conclusion of the anesthetic event. Thus, no pre‐ or intra‐operative treatment changes were made based on the presumed mild azotemia.

Ophthalmic examination was performed by an ophthalmology resident and board‐certified ophthalmologist and included slit‐lamp biomicroscopy, indirect fundoscopy, fluorescein staining, and rebound tonometry. Intraocular pressures (Icare Tonovet, Icare) were measured at 18 mmHg in the right eye (oculus dexter, OD) and 14 mmHg OS. Due to time constraints under general anesthesia, Schirmer tear testing was not performed. Initial gross examination revealed miosis OU likely secondary to anesthesia, mild conjunctival hyperemia OS, and numerous distichiae present along the lateral half of the superior eyelids and several along the inferior eyelids OU (Figure 1). Detailed examination OS revealed dorsolateral superficial corneal pigmentation and dense circumcorneal neovascularization extending to the axial cornea (Figures 1A and 2). In the ventrolateral cornea, there was a slightly raised, fluorescein stain positive, ulcerated granulation bed measuring approximately 5 × 3 mm. Within this area, there was a circular focus of dense, light yellow‐tan infiltrate located within the superficial stroma that was flanked by two distinct, dark brown ovoid lesions (each approximately 1–3 mm in length), suggestive of corneal sequestra (Figure 1A). Trace aqueous flare was present, with only a limited view of the lens and vitreous. Fundic examination was restricted to confirmation of a tapetal reflection. In addition to distichiasis, examination OD revealed dorsolateral to ventrolateral superficial corneal pigmentation (Figure 1B), as well as axial anterior cortical and posterior subcapsular incipient cataracts.

The sequestra sloughed off with very little manipulation, and careful corneal debridement was performed until the granulation bed was visible (white arrowhead), ensuring that no pigmented or necrotic stromal tissue remained.

Under the same anesthetic event as the examination, sampling of the corneal ulcer for cytology and culture was performed following the application of proparacaine hydrochloride 0.5% ophthalmic solution. Cytologic samples were collected using the back end of a #15 blade and assessed following Diff‐Quik stain (Fisher Scientific). Samples for aerobic bacterial culture were sterilely collected and submitted to the NCSU CVM Clinical Microbiology Laboratory for plating and evaluation.

After preparation with dilute betadine and eye wash, the sequestra were inspected with colibri forceps. The sequestra felt rigid upon manipulation and easily sloughed off in two discrete, firm pieces with debridement using a 64‐beaver blade. Thus, excision via superficial keratectomy was not necessary. Further debridement of the anterior stroma was performed to the level of the underlying granulation bed (Figure 2). The sloughed tissue was placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin before being submitted to the NCSU CVM Histology Laboratory. Given the relatively shallow nature of the lesion after debridement, additional grafting was not required.

Following debridement, cryoepilation with liquid nitrogen was performed on both the superior and inferior eyelids OU to address the distichiasis. Two freeze–thaw cycles were completed per eyelid; the freeze cycle was performed until there was formation of an ice ball (approximately 45 s), which was allowed to thaw completely before carrying out the second cycle (around 25 s) [31]. This was followed by manual removal of distichiae using cilia forceps. Topical Neo‐Poly‐Bac ointment was applied OU and atropine 1% ophthalmic ointment was administered OS. Following the procedures, the patient received 0.54 mg/kg of ondansetron IV and 0.1 mg/kg of meloxicam subcutaneously (SQ). Anesthesia reversal was achieved with 0.02 mg/kg of flumazenil SQ, 0.01 mg/kg of atipamezole IM, and 0.01 mg/kg of naloxone IM, and the patient recovered uneventfully.

Corneal cytology showed neutrophils, cocci, and rare rods. Histopathologic analysis of the fixed sequestra samples was performed on slides stained with hematoxylin (Leica Biosystems) and eosin (Fisher Scientific), gram yellow (Agilent), and Grocott‐Gomori methenamine silver (Agilent) stains. Histology showed loss of corneal epithelium with coagulative to lytic necrosis of the underlying corneal stroma and abundant intralesional gram‐positive cocci (Figure 3), consistent with sequestration and bacterial infection; no fungal organisms were identified. An overt cause of the stromal discoloration, such as hemosiderin or melanin, was not identified. Aerobic culture ultimately grew a marked amount of Streptococcus acidominimus. Alpha‐hemolytic streptococci are considered susceptible to penicillins, and acquired antimicrobial resistance to these drugs is not expected. However, for convenient drug administration, sensitivity testing for oral antibiotics that achieve tear film penetration—tetracycline and minocycline, neither of which are penicillins—was completed [32]. Disk diffusion test results were reported as 22 mm and 25 mm zones of inhibition for tetracycline and minocycline, respectively. Published information regarding zone interpretations, minimum inhibitory concentration breakpoints, and antimicrobial sensitivity of Streptococcus acidominimus in binturongs does not exist. However, extrapolation and the results of the patient's first culture, which revealed that the previously isolated Staphylococcus delphini was susceptible to minocycline, led to the use of oral minocycline despite uncertainty regarding the true antimicrobial sensitivity or pharmacokinetic properties in this case [33].

Initial treatment involved moxifloxacin 0.5% ophthalmic drops OS three times daily until reassessment, 0.1 mg/kg of meloxicam per os (PO) once daily for 3 days, and 10 mg/kg gabapentin PO twice daily as needed. As mentioned, 10.8 mg/kg minocycline PO was added twice daily for 7 days after receiving culture and sensitivity results. To avoid the risks and challenges associated with repeat anesthesia in the binturong, in‐person recheck evaluations were not pursued. Instead, the keepers provided periodic photographs of the left eye to veterinarians at the NCSU CVM. Images shared 4 weeks post‐procedure showed that the primary lesion OS was smaller in size, flat, and white in color; the patient was also reported to be behaving normally, with no evidence of pawing, squinting, or other signs of ocular discomfort. At that time, all remaining medications were discontinued, and the keepers were instructed to continue monitoring for any abnormalities. Images from 6 weeks post‐debridement showed continued improvement, with the lesion remaining white, further reduced in size, and without changes in elevation or pigmentation (Figure 4A). No subsequent recheck photographs were provided, and the patient was not reported to have any further ocular concerns.

Three years later, the patient was euthanized at the NCSU CVM for reasons unrelated to his ocular health, allowing for a final ophthalmic assessment. Immediate post‐mortem examination OD showed improved dorsolateral and ventrolateral superficial corneal pigmentation, with only a few superficial corneal blood vessels and distichiae present (Figure 4B); there was no significant progression of the previously noted incipient cataracts OD. Given the patient's age and other ocular exam findings, these were presumed to be age‐related cataracts. In the left eye, there was focal corneal fibrosis and superficial pigmentation at the site of the previous sequestra, with a marked reduction in corneal vasculature and dorsolateral superficial corneal pigmentation. Regrowth of superior and inferior distichiasis was noted; however, the number of distichiae was significantly reduced (Figure 4C). Post‐mortem histological evaluation of the globe confirmed chronic mild focal corneal fibrosis with neovascularization, minimal neutrophilic keratitis, and multifocal pigment deposition OS—consistent with previous stromal injury presumably from the historically treated sequestra. Of note, there was no histologic evidence of active corneal sequestration in either eye at the time of death.

Discussion

This is the first published case of corneal sequestration and distichiasis observed in a binturong. As corneal sequestra are exceedingly rare outside of the feline population, this case provides an opportunity to examine and compare clinical features seen in this patient as a means of further investigating disease etiology and prevalence of corneal sequestra beyond the bounds of our domestic cat population. Several factors support a diagnosis of corneal sequestration in this binturong including clinical signs, patient history, and concurrent presence of chronic corneal irritation—a hypothesized predisposing factor for sequestrum formation in cats, dogs, and horses [3, 6, 7]. Further, stromal pigmentation and the presence of a non‐healing ulcer were evident on clinical examination, which are findings typical of feline corneal sequestration. Abundant intralesional bacteria were observed, but given the lack of significant stromal loss and surrounding malacia, these were thought to be secondary, opportunistic organisms as opposed to primary infectious agents—again a common finding with feline corneal sequestration [5]. Histopathologic analysis of the removed corneal tissue was also consistent with this diagnosis, as it showed coagulative, lytic necrosis with mild brown‐tinged discoloration [8]. Corneal discoloration within sequestra can range from light brown to dark black, which is theorized to be secondary to deposition of iron, melanin, or porphyrin pigments [8]. Histologic characteristics of the observed pigment in this case were not typical of iron or melanin pigments; however, further characterization through special stains was not pursued, as gross and histologic pigment visualization was deemed sufficient for diagnosis. The shallow lesion observed in this binturong also behaved similarly to very superficial sequestra seen in domestic cats, in so far as it appeared close to spontaneously sloughing given how little manipulation was required for its removal. This suggests similarities to some superficial feline sequestra, which can also slough on their own without surgical excision [10, 14].

Although the exact etiology of feline corneal sequestration remains unknown, chronic ocular surface disease is regarded as a contributing factor in its development [2, 8, 10]. This binturong's clinical findings of distichiasis and superficial corneal pigmentation OU—with the latter suggestive of chronic corneal irritation—support this hypothesis. Other proposed anatomical causes, such as entropion or lagophthalmos, were not observed. No published data currently exist regarding ocular anatomy or distichiasis in binturongs; thus, it is unknown whether the features observed in this patient should be considered pathologic versus normal breed characteristics. Though this case report does not detail normal binturong ocular anatomy, it will allow for comparative reference should future ocular examinations be performed in this species. While we cannot speak to the prevalence of distichiasis in the binturong, this condition is exceedingly rare in cats, having been reported just over a dozen times in literature [13, 34, 35]. In one review of fifteen affected cats, nearly two thirds of eyes with distichiae had some type of concurrent ocular pathology, including a corneal sequestrum in one case that was hypothesized to be a consequence of the distichiasis [13]. Breed predisposition was not readily apparent, though the low numbers of cats reported with distichiasis make it difficult to draw conclusions.

Distichiasis and other causes of chronic corneal irritation—such as trichiasis, eyelid margin masses, entropion, or keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS)—are frequently observed in other species, and yet corneal sequestra are rarely, if ever, observed in these contexts [2, 36, 37, 38]. This supports the hypothesis that a genetic component may underlie corneal sequestration in cats, and potentially other “cat‐like” members of the suborder Feliformia, such as the binturong [20, 39, 40]. Several cat breeds, including Persian, Himalayan, Exotic, Siamese, Birman, and Burmese, are reportedly predisposed to sequestrum formation [1, 14, 39, 40]. In one population of cats with presumed hereditary or breed‐related ocular diagnoses, corneal sequestration was the most prevalent diagnosis reported, leading the authors to recommend further investigation into genetic or conformational etiology [40]. As of yet, no genetic locus significantly associated with the development of corneal sequestra has been identified, but the heritable nature of this disease suggests a complex pattern of inheritance may still be at play [39].

With that in mind, this case of corneal sequestration in a binturong adds more context to the range of breeds and species affected, and it raises questions concerning disease prevalence throughout the suborder Feliformia. If similar cases are identified in other cat‐like species, there may be a stronger argument for a genetic component specific to feliforms. At present, however, the lack of published data leaves it unclear whether exotic feliform species are predisposed to corneal sequestration formation, as this case may suggest, or whether it represents a rarity like other individual reports of sequestra in non‐feline species [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

In addition to limitations associated with insufficient population data and a dearth of published information on Arctictis binturong , there are several other factors that somewhat curtail our assessment of this case. As with many solitary case reports, the findings identified in this binturong lack generalizability and could represent abnormalities isolated to this individual. Further, in‐depth ophthalmic evaluation could only be performed following induction of general anesthesia, precluding assessment of certain visual responses and introducing iatrogenic components (e.g., drug‐induced miosis) to the exam. Similarly, the Schirmer tear test was not performed due to time constraints under general anesthesia, thus impeding assessment of a quantitative tear film deficiency, which has also been proposed as a possible risk factor for corneal sequestration. However, even if Schirmer tear test data had been collected, its analysis would have been subject to the same limitation of a lack of species‐specific reference intervals as noted when reviewing the patient's other results, such as bloodwork and antimicrobial sensitivity reporting. When considering antimicrobial choice, susceptibility testing for doxycycline, another antibiotic with reported tear penetration [41], was unavailable at the time. To avoid repeated anesthetic events, rechecks were not performed at the NCSU CVM. Therefore, post‐operative follow up was limited to several blurry photographs and descriptions of patient behavior. The lack of scheduled recheck examinations with the NCSU ophthalmology department made it impossible to assess the true timeline of clinical resolution, monitor for subtle changes such as distichiae regrowth or corneal clarity, or use ophthalmic equipment and diagnostics (e.g., slit‐lamp biomicroscopy, fluorescein staining, or tonometry) to objectively track post‐operative recovery.

Though subject to various limitations, this case report suggests that our understanding of feline corneal sequestration, including investigation of disease etiology and a possible genetic component, could be expanded by further evaluating prevalence of corneal sequestration in non‐domestic feliform species. A retrospective review of ocular disease in over 200 aged, non‐domestic captive felids suggested that ocular disease seen in non‐domestic felids resembles that which is observed in domestic cats [42]. This notion is also supported by multiple individual case reports [42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. In the aforementioned retrospective review, reported pathology included suspected feline herpes virus‐1 related ulcerative keratitis, secondary cataract formation, and hypertension related ocular signs (hyphema, retinal detachment) [20, 42]. Further, corneal disease—in the form of ulceration, perforation, fibrosis, and/or keratitis—was the most common ocular pathology in this population, though none of the 17 observed corneal lesions showed sequestration [42]. While corneal sequestration was not observed in this population, the data still suggest that non‐domestic felids suffer from similar ocular pathologies as their domestic counterparts. Thus, the novel diagnosis of a corneal sequestrum in a feliform binturong warrants continued surveillance of corneal sequestra in the suborder Feliformia. Additional information concerning the prevalence of corneal sequestration in feliform species beyond the domestic cat may also help further our understanding of the etiology and clinical significance of this disease.
