Quality of Coverage (QoC): A New Paradigm for Quantifying Cellular Network Coverage Quality, Usability and Stability
Varshika Srinivasavaradhan, Morgan Vigil-Hayes, Ellen Zegura, Elizabeth Belding

Abstract
. Current representations of cellular coverage are overly simplistic; they state only the minimal level of available bandwidth (i.e., 35/3Mbps download/upload speed for 5G) and fail to incorporate a critical component of usability: network stability over space and time. Cellular coverage quality is complex given wireless propagation characteristics and relationships between network load and (often limited) network capacity. A more fine-grained characterization is essential. We introduce Quality of Coverage (QoC), a novel multi-dimensional set of key performance indicators (KPIs) that reflect actual measured performance quality, usability and stability. This representation of the coverage of the cellular network more fully captures temporal and spatial usability and resilience. We motivate and define a set of QoC KPIs and use three distinct datasets to analyze the ability of the KPIs to characterize network behavior, demonstrating the ability of QoC to offer a more fine-grained and useful representation of cellular coverage than possible with current metrics.



1.
Introduction

Traditional representations of cellular coverage over-simplify network behavior.
For instance, characterizations
of mobile broadband access, such as those released by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) in the National Broadband Map
(
FCC 2025
)
, indicate whether a location is provided a minimum speed (e.g. 35 Mbps download/3 Mbps upload speed for 5G) by a particular cellular provider. While this provides slightly more information than the historical binary
representation of cellular coverage (i.e., does a location have access from a specific provider-yes or no), such
a simplistic characterization of networked services over cellular networks is insufficient
(
Adarsh et al. 2021b
;
Mangla et al. 2022
;
Rural Wireless Association 2019
;
Government Accountability Office 2018
)
.
While
“coverage” may exist in a given location, the service quality can be so routinely poor as to be unusable. This can be true due to poor signal quality and/or insufficient network provisioning. As an example of the former,
cellular holes, e.g. areas that lack usable signal quality, can be common in urban and suburban regions
(
Bai et al. 2014b
;
Shayea et al. 2021
)
. In rural or tribal regions, where base station coverage areas are greater and weaker signals
are more common, cellular coverage is typically less reliable
(
Mangla et al. 2022
;
Adarsh et al. 2021a
;
FCC 2019
)
. On the other hand, a region may experience strong signal quality, yet performance can be unpredictable when utilization outpaces infrastructure capacity
(
Marinova et al. 2020
;
Adarsh et al. 2021a
;
Pew Research Center 2019
)
. Hence, in modern networks where application requirements can place significant demand on a network, it is critical to characterize not only whether coverage exists and "should" meet some minimal threshold, but whether that coverage is actually of sufficient quality over space and time to support the types of applications desired, or needed, by users
(
Nielsen Insights 2020
;
Samms 2020
;
Holpuch 13
)
.

Within this context, our goal is to break away from simplistic coverage representations and introduce Quality of Coverage (QoC), a novel means of more accurately characterizing cellular network quality that focuses on the usability and performance stability of the coverage.
Like quality of service (QoS) and quality of experience (QoE), we define QoC as a set of key performance indicators (KPIs) that reflect network performance.
However, in defining QoC, we pay careful attention to the unique characteristics of the cellular transmission medium. While modern wired networks have communication media properties that offer stable maximum bandwidths, cellular networks are inherently unstable due to wireless propagation
(
Zhou et al. 2004
)
. In fact, 5G networks that incorporate mmWave can have greater performance instability than their predecessors due to the short, easily obstructable wavelengths
(
Srinivasavaradhan et al. 2025
;
Narayanan et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2014a
;
Busari et al. 2018
;
Hassan et al. 2020
)
.

To this end, we define five KPIs to characterize the quality and stability of a cellular network: Usability, Persistence, Usable Performance Mean, Variability, and Resilience. Together, these five KPIs provide an in-depth profile of the QoC offered by a cellular network.
Crucially, we do not need to measure each of these KPIs independently; each KPI is a function of
network performance (e.g., download speed, latency) at a location over a period time.

We offer the QoC framework as a robust starting point that we do not claim is, nor do we want to be, immutable. Similar to the evolution of QoS and QoE with the development of network and application-layer technologies, we anticipate that QoC, too, will evolve. Our goal is to enable network researchers and practitioners to more meaningfully characterize cellular networks.
By addressing the shortcomings of existing mobile network evaluation methods and introducing a multi-dimensional framework for characterizing QoC, this paper sets a new standard in the field of mobile network characterization and assessment. We believe our approach
aligns more closely with the complexities of modern cellular network usage
than more simplistic representations.

By accounting for spatial and temporal fluctuations in coverage and performance, QoC could provide stakeholders with the insight needed
to improve network design and management and enhance user satisfaction. For instance, QoC could help network planners and local governments make more informed decisions about where new infrastructure is needed.
QoC could also help users make decisions about which provider offers the best local service. Further, as new, hyper-local cellular technologies, such as mmWave 5G and 6G, are designed to support throughput- and latency-sensitive applications, QoC could offer benchmarking opportunities tailored to the dynamic nature of technologies. In summary, our contributions are the following:

(1)

Definition of Quality of Coverage (QoC)
: We formalize
Quality of Coverage
as a multi
-

(2)

This collection of measurements illustrates the complexity of representing cellular coverage quality. Speed thresholds are often inaccurate depictions of actual attainable speeds. This is due to a variety of cellular characteristics, such as signal propagation, network utilization, and network provisioning. Even when the speed threshold is achieved, it may not be consistently at or above that threshold, and available bandwidth may be variable and unpredictable. This, in turn, can greatly influence the network’s ability to support bandwidth and/or latency sensitive applications at any particular moment. With this in mind, in this paper we develop and apply the QoC framework to more precisely and meaningfully characterize cellular quality, usability and stability.

3.
Quality of Coverage Framework

Cellular coverage quality is a continuous function of space and time, but this performance is practically measured at individual points in space through discrete, timestamped samples, i.e., a time series. Our QoC framework operates directly on these discrete measurements. Using the observed performance variations within each time series, the QoC framework characterizes the combined impact of multiple cellular-specific factors such as dynamic cell load, radio propagation, cell tower density, and scheduling policies. Capturing each of these influences individually is impractical, but their aggregate effect manifests as performance fluctuations in the time series data.

Cellular performance at individual locations often varies significantly over short time periods and within small geographic areas. To provide fine-grained characterization of these variations, our framework begins by defining specific KPIs. The KPIs enable representation of network behaviors such as periods of stable high-quality service, disruptions due to performance drops, and subsequent network recovery.
The KPIs implicitly incorporate variability caused by local infrastructure and environmental conditions.

After defining the KPIs, we develop a spatial QoC representation by aggregating point-level metrics across broader regions. This allows us to relate what happens at a specific location, such as whether performance remains stable over time at point
(x,y)
, to broader patterns within the region. By summarizing localized temporal dynamics and combining them across space, our framework supports structured, scalable comparisons across geographic units while preserving the behavior that defines QoC at the point level.

3.1.
QoC Key Performance Indicators

This section lays the QoC framework foundation by first defining QoC KPIs for performance at individual locations. A key step is determining whether observed performance exceeds a predefined
Usability Threshold
. This aligns with policy and measurement practices: the FCC
(
FCC 2025
)
uses 5/1 Mbps (download/upload speed) as a broadband benchmark for 4G and 35/3 Mbps as the 5G-NR benchmark; although the FCC does not provide an explicit latency benchmark for mobile networks, it mandates that at least 95% of latency measurements remain at or below 100 ms round-trip time for carriers that obtain fixed broadband funding
(
usa 2019
)
.
To emphasize observed performance quality,
Usability
serves as the basis for all QoC KPIs.

Understanding coverage quality, however, requires more than simply identifying whether a threshold is ever met. Early cellular reliability and stability studies
(
Baltrunas et al. 2014
;
Bischof et al. 2017
)

focused on availability and percentile-based summaries to characterize performance extremes and instances of unreachability. As a way to measure stability, the FCC, through its Measuring Broadband America program, reports "80/80-consistent speed"
(
Burger et al. 2023
)
, which is the minimum speed achieved by at least 80% of users for at least 80% of the time during peak hours. While these approaches reveal whether and how fast a network performs over time,
they overlook how long a usable connection can be sustained, and they fail to meaningfully distinguish between what could be vastly different user experiences that meet the same performance baseline; two networks represented by the same 80/80 speed could have very different performance, stability, and resilience profiles.

Hence, a meaningful assessment of QoC must also capture how stable network performance is over time. To this end, we introduce two complementary dimensions of stability:
Persistence
and
Resilience
.
We define
Persistence
as the average duration for which performance remains continuously above the
Usability Threshold
. Inspired from Paxson’s path-lifetime constructs
(
Paxson 1997
)
and complementing conventional metrics, such as mean time between failures, we focus on the longevity of usable service rather than the frequency of disruptions.
Resilience
, in contrast, captures how quickly performance recovers after falling below the
Usability Threshold
. Together,
Persistence
and
Resilience
characterize temporal stability of location-specific coverage beyond what point-based snapshots can provide.

Importantly, we distinguish our approach from metrics based on average performance, which can obscure periods of unusable performance by blending them with high-performing samples. Instead, we focus only on intervals where performance is above the
Usability Threshold
. Within these usable periods, we compute both the median performance and the degree of performance variation. These metrics provide a clearer picture of what users may experience when the network is nominally usable. Together, these QoC KPIs answer five key questions: (i)

When is the network usable?
; (ii)

How long do usable periods last?
; (iii)

What performance level is typical during usable periods?
; (iv)

What is the performance quality when the network is usable?
; and (v)

How quickly does the network recover from performance drops?

Finally, before defining the QoC KPIs formally, it is worth examining their
relationship to the concepts of QoS and
QoE. All three concepts comprise multiple metrics, recognizing that no single
metric completely characterizes quality. QoS metrics capture relatively low level
indicators of network infrastructure performance, such as path bandwidth, latency,
and packet loss, and are agnostic to specific applications. QoE
metrics, in contrast, focus on indicators that more closely reflect user experience
with applications. These metrics are typically defined
in the context of a particular application use case, e.g. the time-to-first-byte and rebuffering time to define streaming video.
Our QoC metrics share with QoS a focus on application agnostic metrics.
They share with QoE a focus on user experience, but
of the network as a holistic interaction

rather than of a specific application. A user cares about how often network
performance falls below a usable threshold or how quickly
good enough network performance is restored. It is this type of user
experience that QoC is meant to capture. As we show through our evaluations, tuning the
Usability Threshold
enables evaluation of the network’s ability to support specific applications. For instance, a
Usability Threshold
of 50 ms is needed for AR/VR applications
(
Teniou 2021
)
, and 100 ms is needed for cloud gaming
(
Kämäräinen et al. 2017
)
, whereas 150 ms is suitable for real-time conferencing
(
Jones et al. 2021
)
.

In the following definitions, at each geolocation
(x,y)
, let
X(x,y,t)
denote the network performance measurement (e.g., download speed or latency) at time
t
, such that all KPIs defined below are computed for the specific location
(x,y)
. Formally, all KPIs are defined in continuous time for generality, but in practice, measurements are obtained as discrete samples over finite time intervals; thus, integrals are realized as summations over
T
sampled time points.

3.1.1.
Usability (U)
:

Usability
measures the fraction of time the network delivers acceptable performance; we term these "usable periods."
Our goal is not to select an appropriate threshold, but rather to provide a framework for defining a
Usability
KPI. Let
\tau
be the predefined
Usability Threshold
for metric
X
(e.g. 100ms for latency or 35Mbps for download speed).
Over a continuous observation window of duration
T
,
Usability
is defined as:

U=\tfrac{1}{T}\int^{T}\mathbb{I}\big(\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]\big)\,dt,

where
\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[\cdot]
denotes the predicate that evaluates to
1
when the performance metric satisfies the usability condition (i.e.,
X(x,y,t)\!\geq\!\tau
for uplink and downlink bandwidth, or
X(x,y,t)\!\leq\!\tau
for latency and packet loss).

3.1.2.
Usability Persistence (P):

Analogous to mean time to failure (MTTF)
(
rel 2025
)
from classic reliability theory, Persistence reflects temporal stability by quantifying the average duration of continuous usable periods.
Suppose there are
N
continuous usable periods
during which
\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]
holds, and let
L_{j}
be the length of the
j^{th}
period, defined as

L_{j}=\int_{t_{j}^{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{j}^{\mathrm{end}}}\mathbb{I}\big(\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]\big)\,dt
.
Then:

P_{\text{usable}}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}L_{j}

3.1.3.
Usable Performance Mean (M):

While overall performance provides a baseline, the
Usable Performance Mean

offers a realistic view of typical user-experienced network performance during sustained usability periods. Suppose there are
N
continuous usable periods where

\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]
holds. Let
M_{j}
denote the median of performance values
within the
j^{th}
period. Then:

M_{\text{usable}}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}M_{j}

3.1.4.
Usable Performance Variability (V)
:

While all measurements within a usable period satisfy the usability condition, the performance may still fluctuate. Since our focus is on cellular coverage quality, we are interested in capturing the spread of performance during usable periods. Suppose there are
N
continuous usable periods where

\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]
holds and let
V_{j}
denote the variability of performance within the
j^{th}
period. Then:

V_{j}=\tfrac{P_{75}(X_{j})-P_{25}(X_{j})}{P_{50}(X_{j})}

where
P_{75}
,
P_{50}
and
P_{25}
are the 75th, 50th and 25th percentiles of performance within the
j^{th}
period, respectively. Overall
Variability
is:

V_{\text{usable}}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}

3.1.5.
Resilience (R)
:

Resilience
quantifies the network’s ability to recover from performance drops below the
Usability Threshold
, and is defined in terms of mean time to recovery (MTTR)
(
rel 2025
)
from classic reliability theory. Let there be
W
continuous unusable periods during which
\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)]
does not hold,
and let
D_{i}
denote the duration of the
i
th
unusable period, defined as

D_{i}=\int_{t_{i}^{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{i}^{\mathrm{end}}}\big(1-\mathbb{I}(\mathsf{usable}_{\tau}[X(x,y,t)])\big)\,dt
; this is the recovery time. Then
Resilience
is defined as the inverse of the mean recovery time:

R=\tfrac{W}{\sum_{i=1}^{W}D_{i}}\,.

(x,y)

3.2.
Spatial Aggregation of QoC Profiles

With the definition of QoC KPIs in place, we now consider how QoC of individual spatial units can be aggregated over larger regions. We are motivated by questions city planners, residents, or network researchers may ask, such as: (Q1)
What coverage quality does each provider offer within a community?
; (Q2)
Which regions have extended periods of poor network performance, indicating they need upgrades?
; and (Q3)
How homogeneous is the coverage quality within a census tract?

These questions are inherently regional in scope. To answer them, we can aggregate the QoC KPIs at individual units into meaningful region-level summaries.
Here, we use “region” to mean any spatial unit of interest: a census tract, neighborhood, uniform H3 hexagon (see Section
4.1
), or custom polygon. We apply the same aggregation logic to whichever unit best suits policy goals.

3.2.1.
Region-Level QoC Summarization.

Cellular networks exhibit
spatial heterogeneity
, as base station density, interference patterns, and user load change–potentially dramatically–between regions.
To extend the QoC framework from a individual spatial unit, such as a point location or census block, to regions, we employ a two-step aggregation strategy via simple scalar averages and
DDSketch

(
Masson et al. 2019
)
.
1
1

1

DDSketch is a fully-mergeable quantile sketch that can approximate full distributions with bounded relative error.
Originally developed at Datadog
(
dat 2010
;
dat 2019
)
for high-volume streaming and distributed databases, these sketches have been widely adopted in systems like Apache Druid for efficient quantile estimation
(
per 2025
)
and data summarization, specifically of latency distributions
(
dds 2024
)
, which can be skewed. While any mergeable quantile sketch can be used for distribution-preserving summarization,
DDSketch
offers the best tradeoff between accuracy, space, and merge efficiency for our dataset, as we show in Table
3
of the Appendix. This strategy preserves the richness of individual behavior yet offers the efficiency required for large-scale spatial analysis.

In our approach, each individual spatial unit
is associated with a QoC profile derived from its network measurement time series. To construct an aggregate QoC profile, we
serialize the distribution of each QoC KPI at the unit level into a DDSketch and then merge them independently to form region-level QoC sketches.
This two-step methodology offers several advantages:

•

Hierarchical mergeability:
DDSketches from smaller regions can be efficiently merged
into larger-region sketches.

•

Data reduction for large-scale analysis:
Cellular measurement datasets often span millions of points. DDSketches compactly estimate various quantiles of the full distribution of each QoC KPI (e.g., 10th, 50th, 90th percentiles) without retaining raw data,
2
2

2

We note that the QoC KPI at an individual unit level may itself be serialized into a compact DDSketch, preserving its full distribution. This enables each spatial unit to be expressed through any desired quantile of the underlying distribution (e.g., median or 90th percentile) without retaining raw measurements.
enabling efficient storage and fast percentile queries across arbitrary regions.

In addition to the sketch-based summaries, we compute the average of each QoC KPI across all constituent spatial units. For each QoC KPI
m
, the region-level average

\overline{m}_{\text{region}}=\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{i}
,
where
M
is the number of units in the region and
m_{i}
is the average per-unit QoC KPI value. This average vector complements the percentile-based sketch summaries by offering a representation of typical spatial QoC.
This two-fold approach provides flexibility in answering a variety of question types involving larger regions. Distribution sketches preserve the full shape of each QoC KPI, enabling fine-grained quantile queries (e.g., "What is the 10th percentile of Resilience across the region?"), while the means produce compact, interpretable summaries (e.g., "What is the average Persistence in the tract?"). This ensures that both distributional summaries and average trends are accessible without recomputation.
Finally, both the sketch-based and average-based aggregation methods preserve the five-dimensional structure of the QoC representation. Each region is summarized as the aggregated QoC profile for each constituent spatial region.

3.2.2.
Enabling Policy-Driven Analyses.

Region-level QoC profiles support a wide range of questions about spatial coverage. Revisiting the questions posed at the beginning of section
3.2
, we can provide answers as follows:

•

Region Summaries and Provider Evaluation (Q1):
By querying merged sketches or means from a provider, percentiles and averages of Usability, Persistence, and Resilience can be reported across any geographic region. By comparing each carrier’s spatial results, regions where one provider outperforms another can be identified. This empowers users to more quantitatively evaluate provider coverage, increasing their purchasing power when selecting a cellular provider.

•

Computing Coverage Quality (Q2 and Q3):

By modeling the study area as a graph of adjacent spatial units, we can identify and rank pockets of weak coverage by filtering nodes through QoC cutoffs, enabling targeted investments to the largest regions of greatest need. We can also apply spatial clustering algorithms to identify regions with (in)consistent quality profiles.

There are two primary goals for our QoC evaluation. The first is to study the ability of the QoC KPIs to characterize
network performance at both single locations and larger geographic regions. The second is to study the sensitivity of the QoC KPIs to temporal and spatial measurement density. Here we are motivated by real-world constraints of cellular network measurement, whereby it can be difficult to obtain temporally and spatially dense measurements throughout a geographic area. An ideal dataset for evaluation would consist of network measurements taken at regular, frequent time intervals at specific, densely-distributed locations within some geographic area. This would provide temporal and spatial data density needed to fully evaluate QoC. Unfortunately, we are not aware of any such datasets, at least in the public sphere. Thus, we leverage a three-step process.

First, we evaluate QoC using synthetic datasets generated with ideal temporal and spatial density. These datasets represent a set of controlled, pre-defined scenarios in which we can study how the QoC KPIs respond to and characterize different, predictable network behaviors. Second, we use two distinct datasets collected in production cellular networks to examine the ability of QoC to characterize network performance and stability in uncontrolled settings. These two production-network datasets together capture over 45 million measurements on three cellular carriers in nearly 60 locations in our community. Finally, we study the sensitivity of QoC KPIs to data sparsity by down-sampling synthetic and production datasets and analyzing the change in network characterization.

4.2.
Real-World Data

We complement our synthetic data with an extensive dataset of measurements collected on Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile production cellular networks in our local community. Unlike synthetic traces, real-world measurements capture operational complexity, such as unpredictable load and signal degradation patterns, device-level variability and imperfect probing. To this end, we conducted two distinct types of experiments to obtain a realistic view of instantaneous end-user performance: one with fine-grained, temporally frequent RTT measurements, and one of download speeds.
For each measurement location in the two datasets, both the National Broadband Map
(
FCC 2025
)
and official carrier coverage maps indicated all providers offered 5G coverage. The locations span diverse performance conditions and experienced a range of signal qualities, setting an expectation for varying performance.

4.2.1.
RTT Measurements.

We utilize 15 Android smartphones across five models (Google Pixel 7, Google Pixel 9, Samsung Galaxy S21, Samsung Galaxy S23, Samsung Galaxy S25). We procured five SIM cards per carrier with comparable cellular plans, and deployed three phones, one per carrier, at each of five indoor locations in our community. To control for device-related factors, each location used three phones of the same model. The phones were consistently plugged in and free of any other user interaction throughout the experiments.
We developed an Android measurement app to send lightweight UDP probes from each phone to a university-hosted echo server every 500 ms to measure RTT. In addition, the app loaded a 10 MB static webpage hosted on the same measurement server every five minutes to record QoE metrics, such as time-to-first-byte (TTFB), DOM content loaded time (DOM), and total page load time (PLT). In parallel, the app read the cellular signal state through Android Telephony APIs at the same cadence, producing time-aligned measurements of signal strength.
We chose RTT as the core performance indicator for this study since it offers a low-overhead, fine-grained view of end-to-end responsiveness and short-term interruptions, which are central to latency-sensitive mobile applications, e.g. 5G use cases such as AR/VR. Additionally, RTT measurements consume significantly less data than throughput tests or speed tests, making highly frequent measurements feasible.
We conducted experiments
from September 3 to October 1, 2025, capturing diverse diurnal and weekly patterns across carriers and locations. Occasional power outages and app crashes led to some unpredictable data omissions. Therefore, we analyze only days with complete 24-hour coverage at 500 ms granularity, resulting in roughly 15 measurement days per location and
243 day-level traces in total.

4.2.2.
Speedtest Measurements.

We complement our latency study with a focus on download speed as measured through
Ookla Speedtest at 52 locations in our community. We utilize three cell phone models: Samsung Galaxy S20+, S23, and Google Pixel 7; three units of each model were used, totaling nine phones. At each location, we assigned one phone of each model to each carrier, ensuring each location in our dataset had data from all three phone types for all three carriers. We procured nine SIM cards per carrier, all subscribed to identical cellular plans.
At each location, Speedtests were performed
sequentially on all nine devices, one Speedtest per device; we term these nine Speedtests as one round. Following each round, the SIM cards were systematically rotated between phones to decouple device idiosyncracies from carrier-specific performance. The total dataset consists of
\sim
6,000 Speedtests. The data was collected in 2024 and 2025.

5.
QoC Evaluation

We evaluate the QoC framework by analyzing the KPI characterizations of the synthetic and production network datasets and the ability of the spatial aggregations to represent coverage in larger regions; then in Section
5.4
, we evaluate the sensitivity of QoC to data sparsity.
Each KPI captures a distinct aspect of network stability and is measured on different scales and units. We retain raw units to preserve interpretability, when required; otherwise, we apply standard normalization techniques
(
min 2019
;
log 2023
)
, using natural log transformation and min–max scaling to map all KPIs to a common [0,1] interval, to enable direct comparison and joint analysis. For consistency in interpretation between QoC KPIs (higher scores denote better performance), we invert the normalized Variability KPI (1 - V) and utilize this value in our plots.
Our analysis demonstrates the ability of the KPIs to characterize fine-grained network behavior, an ability that commonly used average performance and availability measures lack. Further, we show that no single KPI consistently captures differences in all network scenarios, but together the KPIs present a detailed picture of network QoC.

Because our QoC framework measures stability over time and across space, it is at risk of needing dense measurement data for accurate computation. Indeed, our synthetic datasets offered ideal temporal and spatial richness for full QoC characterization, and our production network data similarly measured performance at regular, frequent intervals. While this richness is valuable for accurately characterizing nuanced network behavior, it is practically challenging. Local broadband organizations can strategically place stationary measurement units at key points of interest to obtain accurate QoC. Beyond these intentional campaigns, however, temporally and spatially dense measurements over long time periods can be difficult and/or resource-intensive to obtain. As a result, current representations of network coverage

6.1.
Temporal Sensitivity

We begin by evaluating how reducing temporal measurement density impacts QoC KPIs at
individual spatial units. We first use the synthetic datasets, and then the production network datasets, to perform two types of temporal down-sampling: (i) fixed-interval (fixed)
and (ii) random.
For fixed down-sampling of the synthetic datasets, we select one datapoint from the original datasets uniformly at random at different time intervals: 5 minutes, 1 hour, 6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, and 5 days.
For random down-sampling, we irregularly sample measurements by retaining a fraction
d\in\{0.5,0.25,0.1,0.01,0.001\}
of all measurements uniformly at random without replacement.
To quantify sampling bias and for statistical robustness, we repeat each down-sampling experiment 30 times. We recompute QoC KPIs for each down-sampled dataset.
We quantify fidelity loss by computing the normalized absolute errors between each down-sampled QoC metric and its corresponding original, full-data baseline.
In the following section, we evaluate the accuracy of QoC KPIs in fixed down-sampling. The results for random down-sampling demonstrated similar trends and are included in the Appendix.

6.2.
Spatial Sensitivity

We conclude our evaluation with a study of the how spatial data sparsity impacts the ability to accurately characterize QoC over larger geographic areas. We spatially down-sample by reducing the number of hex10 cells aggregated into each hex9 region; this models hex10s with missing time series data. For the homogeneous and heterogeneous synthetic spatial-region datasets described in Section
5.4
, we systematically down-sample hex10 cells, retaining

k\in\{6,5,4,3,2,1\}
hex10 cells per hex9 region (recall there are seven hex10s in each hex9). For each
k
, we repeat the down-sampling experiment 30 times to statistically characterize sampling randomness. We then recompute region-level QoC KPIs via scalar averages and merged DDSketches.
We quantify fidelity loss by computing the distribution
of the normalized absolute errors with 95% confidence intervals
between down-sampled aggregates and their corresponding full-data baselines.

The spatial down-sampling results are shown in Figure
14
. Here, the seven original synthetic network scenarios reflect the homogeneous spatial aggregations, while "heterogeneous" (the light blue bar) reflects the heterogeneous aggregation. For the homogeneous aggregations, errors increase only modestly (median error < 4%) for all QoC KPIs as the number of hex10s decreases. By contrast, in the heterogeneous aggregation,
tail errors climb steeply, from roughly 10% at k = 6 to nearly 70% at k = 1; median and mean errors remain at 30% even when k = 1. Reduced data in mixed-scenario regions can amplify error when key stability patterns from constituent sub-regions are missed. In real deployments, where spatial homogeneity seldom holds, limited region-level sampling can therefore produce inaccurate Persistence and Resilience estimates and mislead coverage assessments.

Takeaway:

QoC KPIs are more sensitive to spatial sparsity in heterogeneous regions; hence these regions require greater spatial measurement coverage to maintain fidelity.

This has key implications for stakeholders attempting to evaluate regional coverage,

Related Work

Mobile broadband assessments have primarily relied on KPIs like throughput, latency, and signal strength. The limitations of focusing solely on signal strength and the importance of context-aware measurements is emphasized in
(
Sonntag et al. 2013
;
Gember et al. 2012
)
. Prior research
(
Shakir et al. 2023
;
Elmokashfi et al. 2017
)
underscores challenges such as temporal variability and sampling biases in traditional measurement approaches. Other studies
(
Bauer and Lehr 2018
;
Frias et al. 2023
)
highlight difficulties in measuring mobile broadband performance, the evolving complexity of the mobile broadband ecosystem, and emphasize the need for a flexible measurement framework to support evidence-based policymaking.
Recognizing limitations of throughput and latency, QoE research has developed composite models: VoIP MOS frameworks jointly measure loss, delay, and recovery
(
Moura et al. 2007
;
Sengupta et al. 2008
;
Chen et al. 2011
)
; adaptive-video QoE combines startup delay, bitrate variability, and rebuffering
(
Barman and Martini 2019
;
Raake et al. 2017
)
. Studies of QoS and QoE in 4G LTE and 5G networks underscore the need for metrics such as session quality and recovery
(
Adarsh et al. 2021b
;
Raca et al. 2018
;
Mardian et al. 2019
;
Casas et al. 2016
;
Banović-Ćurguz and Ilišević 2019
)
.

The inherent instability and variability of network traffic have been well-documented. Paxson’s seminal work
(
Paxson 1997
)
introduced persistence and prevalence in network routing behavior. Subsequent studies
(
Willinger et al. 2004
;
Baltrunas et al. 2014
;
Lazarou et al. 2009
;
Snow et al. 2000
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Lehr et al. 2012
;
Bischof et al. 2017
)
documented the importance of reliability-based metrics for fixed and mobile broadband performance, with methods to quantify availability, burstiness, reachability, uptime, mean time between failures (MTBF), and mean time to recovery (MTTR).
Other work
(
Aceto et al. 2018
;
Dasari et al. 2018
;
Narayanan et al. 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2022
;

;

;

;
Chen et al. 2025
)
has shown the variability of cellular performance due to frequency band, device model, network load, handoffs, mobility, carriers, geography and environmental conditions.

Recent studies have integrated spatio-temporal modeling into performance analyses to quantify variability in network performance
(

)
. For example,

8.
Conclusion and Practical Implications

Cellular networks differ from fixed and WiFi networks in their bandwidth variability due to signal propagation characteristics, use of extreme frequency bands, insufficient network provisioning, and load sensitivity. Their coverage quality fundamentally extends beyond instantaneous availability or average speed; true quality depends heavily on stable connectivity over sustained periods and the network’s ability to quickly recover from (potentially frequent) disruptions.

We have defined a new standard for characterization of cellular network coverage quality, usability, and stability. The QoC framework can represent longitudinal cellular coverage at a single location, and it can be spatially aggregated to represent coverage of larger geographic areas. We have demonstrated the ability of QoC to go far beyond current representations of cellular availability to offer a fine-grained characterization of actual coverage over time and space. We offer five QoC KPIs as a robust starting point, and encourage the networking community to introduce additional KPIs as cellular networks and applications evolve.

The collection of temporally and spatially dense measurement data can be challenging in many scenarios. We have identified situations where the KPIs are more and less sensitive to data sparsity. Fortunately, quality and stability properties of cellular coverage do not need to be uniformly studied. Locations where high speed, stable coverage is critical can be identified and targeted for short-term placement of measurement devices such as Raspberry Pis. These locations could include bus stops, downtown walking areas, and urban spaces where people congregate, as well as smart city spaces that will require ultra-reliable low latency communication (URLLC) such as traffic intersections.

QoC KPI characterization could have important benefits in the FCC Mobile Challenge Process. Stakeholders could target specific measurement locations believed to have sub-par service, and then use the QoC KPIs to provide a more robust argument that the offered service does not match provider coverage claims. This could inform more strategic allocation of subsidies for network infrastructure improvements. Finally, better characterization of cellular coverage empowers average users to simply select the best local provider.

A.2.1.
DDSketch: Relative-Error Quantile Sketch.

DDSketch is a fast, fully mergeable quantile sketch designed to approximate arbitrary distributions with
provable relative-error
guarantees
(
Masson et al. 2019
)
. It discretizes the value domain into logarithmically spaced bins, parameterized by a user-defined relative accuracy
\varepsilon
, such that any estimated quantile
\hat{z}
satisfies
|\hat{z}-z|/z\leq\varepsilon
. This design enables DDSketch to maintain bounded error regardless of the input distribution, especially highly skewed or heavy-tailed distributions, such as those observed in network latency, while keeping memory usage minimal. DDSketches support efficient update and merge operations allowing them to be useful in sketching fast data streams. Overall, DDSketch provides a robust means for data summarization via quantile estimation when bounded relative error, fast and efficient sketching and merging capability, and high tail-accuracy are needed.
In Table
3
, we compare three quantile sketches across sketch size, compute and merge times, and absolute and relative errors across different percentiles on the
45
million real-world RTT measurements we collected; DDSketch shows the best overall performance. It is a close second to KLL sketches on compute and merge times and lower-quantile estimation accuracy, and a close second to T-Digest at upper-tail accuracy. Based on these tradeoffs, we select DDSketch for this work.
