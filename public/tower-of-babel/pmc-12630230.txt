External Application of Methyl Jasmonate Alters Monoterpene Composition and Induces Resin Duct Production in Engelmann Spruce (Picea engelmannii)
Authors: Thomas Seth Davis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The effects of exogenous application of methyl jasmonate (MeJa), a ubiquitous hormonal elicitor of plant defense, was tested for the ability to upregulate monoterpenes and induce production of traumatic resin ducts in Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii). MeJa treatments were applied a single time at one of three concentrations (1-, 10-, and 100-mM) and phloem monoterpene responses were evaluated during the same growing season and one year post-treatment. MeJa application affected monoterpene profiles in a time-specific manner. Total monoterpene concentrations were unaffected by MeJa in the year of application (P = 0.692) but concentrations of several monoterpenes including γ-terpinene, terpinolene, linalool were elevated for trees receiving the 100 mM application, indicating treatment affected monoterpene compositions. Both γ-terpinene (P = 0.008) and terpinolene (P < 0.001) were elevated within one week of application but later declined to control levels, whereas linalool was elevated two weeks after application and remained higher than control for the remainder of the growing season (P < 0.001). Evaluation of traumatic resin duct formation the following year revealed higher frequencies of traumatic resin ducts in growth increments for trees receiving 10- and 100-mM treatments. Occurrence of traumatic resin ducts moderately predicted variation in total monoterpene concentration the year following treatment (P = 0.069). Trees forming resin ducts bisecting between 20 and 90% of the previous year’s growth increment had ~ 39% higher mean phloem monoterpene concentrations than trees with fewer ducts or those that were completely occluded by ducts. These results indicate that exogenous MeJa application alters monoterpene profiles shortly following application and may have latent, resin duct-mediated effects on total monoterpene concentrations. Potential ecological impacts are discussed.

Introduction

The mechanistic drivers of plant chemical defenses are of long-standing interest to ecologists and provide invaluable insight into the evolutionary underpinnings of plant-insect interactions (Stamp 2003; Barton and Boege 2017; Züst and Agrawal 2017). In addition, understanding how to stimulate plant defenses has practical applications in agriculture and forestry. For example, manipulation of plant defense phenotypes to generate individuals that are resistant to herbivore damage could reduce the use of toxic or environmentally harmful pesticides that have off-target effects with unintended consequences for plant and insect biodiversity or negative impacts on human health (Mitchell et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2025). Moreover, understanding how to ‘induce’ herbivore resistance has useful applications in research, and can aid plant breeding programs by helping researchers quickly screen and generate varietals for inheritable traits associated with resistance (Wink 1988; Inger 2009).

Plant chemical resistance to herbivores presents in many forms but such defenses are often categorized as ‘constitutive’ or ‘inducible’ (Kaplan et al. 2008; Kessler 2015). Constitutive defenses typically refer to those that are pre-formed in the plant body prior to herbivore exposure, whereas inducible defenses usually refer to those that are upregulated in response to herbivory, usually via exposure to some elicitor (Benhamou 1996; Thaler et al. 1999; Thakur and Sohal 2013). In many instances, both constitutive and inducible defenses are defined by the concentrations of secondary metabolites, and a hyper-diverse array of secondary metabolites with defensive function are found in plants including latexes, alkaloids, glucosinolates, phenols, terpenoids, and many others (Wink 2008; Kessler and Kalske 2018). Such inducible compounds can readily be found in root, foliar, phloem, and xylem tissues (Kaplan et al. 2008), and especially in structures that are metabolically costly to produce or are vital for plant survival and reproduction (Ohnmeiss and Baldwin 2000). While the induction of chemical defenses can result in the production of qualitatively different compounds with toxic effects on herbivores, in some plants the same class of secondary metabolites may be present as both constitutive and inducible defenses with the only distinction being the quantity of secondary metabolites (Stamp 2003; Van Den Boom et al. 2004).

The latter is often the case with long-lived conifer species, which must contend with repeated and diverse biotic challenges. For example, most conifers in the Pinaceae family contain terpenoids as both constitutive and inducible secondary metabolites (Berryman 1972; Raffa and Smalley 1995). Although conifer volatile terpenoids can attract herbivores such bark beetles, they are also present constitutively in relatively high quantities in phloem tissues, which is the primary food source for larvae of conifer-infesting bark beetles. Attack by bark beetles or colonization by their symbiotic fungi is usually sufficient to elicit upregulation of terpenoids in phloem, and concentrations may increase several-fold during attack and induction (Cook and Hain 1986; Paine and Stephen 1987; Nebeker et al. 1993; Raffa and Smalley 1995). Trees that induce rapidly may resist colonization and repel beetles, while those that induce slowly may be colonized and eventually perish (Raffa and Smalley 1995; Wallin and Raffa 2000; Faccoli et al. 2005; Schiebe et al. 2012).

In conifers as well as many other plant species, the upregulation of secondary metabolites in response to herbivory is signaled by a relatively conserved set of phytohormones including jasmonic acid, salicylic acid, and abscisic acid (Bari and Jones 2009; Thaler et al. 1999). In conifers, jasmonic acid and in particular the bioactive derivative methyl jasmonate (MeJa) are among the key phytohormones that signal terpenoid production. Though the cellular mechanisms that control concentrations of and sensitivity to MeJa remains poorly understood in conifers, earlier studies demonstrate that external application can result in rapid induction of terpenoids with consequences for herbivory by bark beetles. For example, application of MeJa to the bark of Norway spruce (Picea abies L.) increased concentrations of phloem terpenes by 2.5-fold within three weeks of treatment, which was sufficient to reduce colonization and reproduction by the European spruce bark beetle Ips typographus L. (Erbilgin et al. 2006; Schiebe et al. 2012). The authors also reported a substantial increase in the density of resin ducts the year following MeJa application. Similar patterns of terpene upregulation, stimulation of resin duct production, and reduced herbivore feeding following external application of MeJa are also reported for pines, firs, and larches (Heijari et al. 2005; Huber et al. 2005; Moreira et al. 2009; Jiang and Yan 2018).

Here, I evaluate the potential for MeJa to induce production of terpenoids, specifically monoterpenes, in the phloem of Engelmann spruce (P. engelmannii Parry ex. Engelm.). Engelmann spruce is a widespread species in North America and in several regions, especially the southern Rocky Mountains, tree populations have experienced substantial mortality in recent decades due to outbreaks of the North American spruce beetle (Dendroctonus rufipennis Kirby, Coleoptera: Curculionidae) (Jenkins et al. 2014). Although both constitutive and inducible terpenoid-based chemical defenses inhibit the success of beetles and their symbiotic fungi and are important for predicting tree survival (Davis et al. 2018; Davis 2020; Ott et al. 2021), there are not yet any studies that describe whether hormonal elicitors are responsible for upregulation of terpenes in Engelmann spruce. However, a better understanding of the role of phytohormones such as MeJa as potential controls on Engelmann spruce inducible defenses could inform plant physiological research and aid in the development of pest management techniques. Accordingly, I test the following specific hypotheses: (1) external application of MeJa is associated with an increase in monoterpenes in Engelmann spruce phloem; (2) these effects are dose-dependent; (3) MeJa stimulates production of resin ducts, and (4) resin duct density predicts phloem monoterpene concentrations.

Methods and Materials

The present study took place during July and August in 2020 and during June of 2021 in the vicinity of Willow Creek Pass, Colorado (coordinates: 40.34656° N, −106.09600° W, elevation: 2905 m a.s.l., stand basal area: 34 m2 ha− 1) in the Arapaho-Roosevelt National Forest (Ott et al. 2021). In the selected study stand, Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii Parry ex Engelm.) was the predominant species with intermixed lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta Douglas) and subalpine fir (Abies lasiocarpa (Hook.) Nutt.) also present in the canopy. Blue spruce (P. pungens Engelm.) was also present in some places. Dominant and co-dominant mature Engelmann spruce trees that were free of apparent defect, injury or disease were selected for sampling (mean diameter at breast height: 36.9 cm ± 5.7 cm SE, mean tree age: 131.1 ± 3.7 years SE), and all sampled trees were within a 3-hectare area.

In June of 2020, forty study trees were selected and randomly assigned to receive one of four treatment solutions (n = 10 trees per group); treatment groups included exogenous application of methyl jasmonate at 1, 10, and 100mM concentrations, or deionized water as a treatment control. Treatment solutions were prepared by dissolving methyl jasmonate dissolved into water to the target molarity and adding 0.1% (v/v) of Tween 20 as a surfactant. Treatments were applied a single time on Jul 7, 2020. To apply treatments consistently, a 30 cm2 rectangle was first painted on the north side of the bole of each tree at a height 1.3 m above the ground. Treatment solutions were directly applied to boles using a hand-pump sprayer to the delineated area by saturating the bark until run-off occurred (approximately 300 ml of solution). Phloem punches were then collected for sampling monoterpene concentrations at time zero (immediately after application, Jul 7) and every 7–10 days thereafter for a period of one month (Jul 17, Jul 26, Aug 2, and Aug 9), which coincides with the end of the D. rufipennis flight period in the region (Davis and Hansen 2018; n = 5 collection periods in total). Phloem samples were collected using a 1.5 cm diam. bark punch, and collected samples were immediately placed in plastic bags and stored on dry ice for transport to the laboratory. Collected samples were then stored in a freezer at − 20° C degrees until processing and analysis.

Concentrations of six monoterpenes including (+)-α-pinene, (−)-β-pinene, β-phellandrene, (+)-Δ−3-carene, γ-terpinene, and terpinolene) and the monoterpene alcohol (+)-linalool were quantified from phloem samples based on the results of earlier studies reporting these compounds as the most common regional monoterpene components of P. engelmannii (Davis et al. 2018). The preparation of samples, extraction of monoterpenes, and their analysis are previously described and follow the general methods described in Erbilgin and Colgan (2012) and Davis et al. (2018). Phloem samples were prepared by thawing to room temperature, and phloem was excised from bark using a sterile scalpel and sliced into ~ 5 mm pieces. These were then placed in mortars and pulverized under liquid N until powder. A 100 mg subsample of each powdered phloem was weighed and placed in a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes, to which a 500 µl ml aliquot of hexane (99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA, lot STBG5019V) was added. Vials containing phloem suspended in hexane were vortexed at maximum speed for 30 s and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min; thereafter the supernatant was decanted into a new 2 ml borosilicate gas chromatograph autosampler vial for analysis. The process was repeated and the second extract was added to the first for a final volume of ~ 1 ml. Earlier studies using this approach report that the quantification of monoterpene concentrations are not sensitive to extraction time (Ott et al. 2021).

Analysis of extracts followed methods of Davis et al. (2018). The concentrations of selected monoterpenes in extracts were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7820 A; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to a mass spectrometer (Agilent 5977B; Agilent Technologies). A 1 µl volume of each extract was injected via autosampler onto an HP 5 ultra-inert column (Agilent Technologies, part no. 19091 S-433UI; dimensions: 30 m × 250 μm external diameter × 0.25 μm internal diameter) with a front inlet temperature 250° C, helium flow rate of 1.2 ml/min, and gas pressure of 53 kPa. The instrument was operated in splitless mode. The temperature program was 40° C for 5 min, then increasing by 10° C per minute to final temperature of 250° C and held at 250° C for 5 min. Total ion chromatograms were acquired using the Chemstation data analysis software (Agilent Technologies, version F.01.03.2357), and chromatograms were integrated using the MassHunter software (Agilent Technologies, version B.07.00). Monoterpenes in extracts were confirmed by comparing electron mass spectra and retention times against commercially-available authentic standards. The concentration of each monoterpene (mg monoterpene per g phloem, fresh weight) in extracts was determined by relativizing monoterpene peak areas in samples to peak areas of authentic monoterpene standards injected at known concentrations to quantify concentration in parts-per-million (µg/g phloem). Analytical standards and their sources included: (+)-α-pinene (98% purity, Sigma-Aldrich), (−)-β-pinene (99% purity, Sigma-Aldrich), (+)−3-carene (> 90% purity, Sigma-Aldrich), γ-terpinene (95% purity, Sigma-Aldrich), terpinolene (90% purity, Sigma-Aldrich), β-phellandrene (> 80% purity, Synergy Semiochemicals, Delta, British Columbia, Canada) and (+)-linalool (97% purity, Sigma-Aldrich).

In June of 2021, the year following application of treatments, each tree was visited again to collect xylem core samples for assessing traumatic resin duct production. Cores were collected from within the delineated treatment area, but distal to phloem sampling wounds, using a 4.3 mm diam. × 100 mm length increment borer (Haglöf, Sweden). Collected cores were returned to the laboratory, air-dried, mounted and progressively sanded to 500 grit following standard dendrochronological techniques (Fritts 1976). Cores were then scored for traumatic resin duct production in the 2020 growth increment following the methods described in DeRose et al. (2017) and Ott et al. (2021). Briefly, cores were mounted on a sliding stage micrometer and the 2020 growth increment was examined for the tangential alignment of resin ducts. Categories of traumatic resin duct formation were assigned on the basis of proportion of each growth increment bisected by tangentially aligned ducts. Increments were assigned to one of four categories including zero traumatic resin ducts (Class 0), Class I traumatic resin ducts (tangential alignment of two or more ducts bisecting up to 20% of the growth increment), Class II traumatic resin ducts (tangential alignment of resin ducts bisecting 21–90% of the growth increment, or Class III traumatic resin ducts (tangential alignment of resin ducts bisecting > 90% of the growth increment). A photographic guide to tangential duct alignment can be found in Ott et al. (2021). In addition, phloem was excised from the proximal end of growth cores and phloem samples were processed and monoterpenes extracted and analyzed as described above for the purpose of comparing traumatic resin duct formation in 2020 growth increments to monoterpene concentrations in the phloem in 2021 (i.e., to evaluate the effect of resin duct production on phloem monoterpenes in the following year).

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using the software JMP Pro V 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was assigned to modeled effects using a Type I error rate of α < 0.10. Three main hypotheses were tested. First, a nested two-factor ANOVA model was used to test the hypothesis that exogenous application of methyl jasmonate affects the concentration of phloem monoterpenes by analyzing the effects of date and treatment nested by date on the response of mean concentration of each target monoterpene, as well as total summed monoterpenes, in 2020 (the same year of application). Next, a chi-square test was used to test the hypothesis that the frequency of traumatic resin duct formation in growth increments varies due to the effect of treatment. Lastly, a one-way ANOVA model was used to test the hypothesis that traumatic resin duct formation is associated with variation in individual and total monoterpene concentrations the year following treatment by analyzing the effect of resin duct classification on monoterpenes in 2021 (the year following application).

Discussion

The experiment reported here demonstrates that exogenous application of MeJa to the boles of mature P. engelmannii has dose-dependent effects on monoterpene composition in the year of application and for some trees at least one year following application. Here, two monoterpenes (γ-terpinene and terpinolene) and one monoterpene alcohol (linalool) were responsive to MeJa application. In trees treated with a 100-mM MeJa solution, concentrations of γ-terpinene and terpinolene increased by ~ 43 and 66% on average (relative to control trees) within one-week post-application, but then later declined to the levels found in control trees. Similarly, concentrations of linalool increased by ~ 69% on average by two weeks post-application in the same treatment group and remained elevated for the remainder of the sampling period (Fig. 1). However, total monoterpene concentrations did not change during the sampling period (Table 1), indicating that MeJA treatment altered the relative composition but not the total concentration of phloem monoterpenes. This differs somewhat from results found in other spruce-dominant ecosystems. For example, several earlier studies show that 100-mM applications are sufficient to increase total monoterpenes in Picea abies (Norway spruce) and can elevate total monoterpene concentrations by two-fold (e.g., Martin et al. 2002; Erbilgin et al. 2006).

This discrepancy could be explained by the rapidity of resin duct formation in response to MeJa treatment. Picea abies forms resin ducts in response to MeJa during the same growing season of application (Franceschi et al. 2002; Erbilgin et al. 2006), but mature P. engelmannii does not appear to form resin ducts until the year following exposure to elicitors (e.g., Ott et al. 2021). It is possible that climatic drivers are responsible for this difference. In general, P. engelmannii in the present study occurs in a colder climate than P. abies from the above-cited earlier studies: mean winter low temperatures in the vicinity of Willow Creek Pass (Colorado, USA) are approximately − 17 °C whereas mean winter low temperatures in the vicinity of Ås (Norway) and Göttingen (Germany), where earlier studies on P. abies responses to MeJa occurred, are approximately − 6 and − 2 °C, respectively. Summer day lengths are also considerably shorter in the North American study system, consistent with an overall shorter growing season and more severe winter climate. Although I did not evaluate resin duct formation in response to MeJa in the same growing season of application, MeJa application had a clear dose-dependent response on the formation of traumatic resin ducts in the year following application (Fig. 2). Almost all trees treated with the 100-mM solution formed extensive traumatic resin duct systems in the application area; most trees treated with 1- and 10-mM solutions also formed resin duct systems in comparison to control trees.

These differences corresponded to variation in total monoterpene concentrations the year following treatment, and trees forming Class II resin ducts (i.e., tangentially-aligned resin ducts that bisected 20–90% of the growth increment) produced ~ 39% higher total monoterpene concentrations than the mean of the other treatment groups (Fig. 3). Similar relationships between inducible resin ducts and monoterpene concentrations are reported for other conifers. For example, feeding of Monochamus alternatus (Coleoptera: Cerambycidae) on sapling Masson’s pine (P. massoniana Lamb.) caused development of traumatic resin ducts within 15 d of feeding, and this was associated with a 1.7- and 2.1-fold increase, respectively, in concentrations of α- and β-pinene in the xylem proximal to feeding sites (Chen et al. 2018). In the more closely related P. abies, external treatment of seedlings with 10- and 100-mM MeJa was sufficient to induce traumatic resin duct formation with 9 d of application and by 15 d after application monoterpene content of xylem tissues tripled (Martin et al. 2002). In the present study, a less dramatic 1.3-fold increase in total monoterpene concentrations was observed in mature spruce trees, although extensive resin duct formation occurred in trees treated with 10 and 100 mM MeJa. Additionally, total or near-total occlusion of the xylem with resin ducts (i.e., trees that formed Class III resin duct formation) exhibited a reduced ability to upregulate monoterpenes, even in comparison with control trees that received no MeJa exposure (Fig. 3). This could suggest a reduced defensive competency in trees that ‘over induce’ in response to MeJa, and merits further study. The mechanisms underlying this pattern are unknown, but one hypothesis is that development of resin canals is metabolically costly, and trees inducing extensive duct networks may exhaust the carbohydrate reserves needed for subsequent secondary metabolite production (Christiansen et al. 1987). Since bark beetle attack is generally correlated with loss of photosynthetic function in conifers (Huang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2019), over-induction may more rapidly drive trees towards death by carbon starvation (Erbilgin et al. 2021). My data, similar to the results of Martin et al. (2002), therefore indicate that the greatest effect on defensive priming in P. engelmannii in subsequent growing seasons after MeJa application occurred for the 10 mM treatment group.

In general, the toxicity of monoterpenes to Dendroctonus bark beetles may provide some insight into whether changes in tree secondary metabolite profiles are likely to affect resistance (e.g., Cook and Hain 1988; Werner 1995; Chiu et al. 2017; and others). For D. rufipennis, a primary bark beetle that can kill many mature spruces during outbreaks, trees containing higher mean concentrations of terpinolene, linalool, and γ-terpinene in their phloem are more toxic than trees containing lower concentrations of these compounds (Davis 2020). Although these three compounds represent relatively minor components of P. engelmannii phloem and xylem and typically comprise only ~ 3–7% of the monoterpene profile (Davis et al. 2018), even small changes in their relative concentrations may be important for repelling D. rufipennis or preventing attacks. Here, all three of these secondary metabolites were upregulated in response to 10- and 100-mM MeJa application, and in considerable quantities (43–69%), suggesting that MeJa-driven changes in the monoterpene profile are potentially associated with resistance of P. engelmannii to primary bark beetles, as demonstrated elsewhere (Martin et al. 2002; Heijari et al. 2005; Erbligin et al. 2006). My data are consistent with a change in the overall relative composition of phloem monoterpenes important for resistance in response to MeJa, rather than a change in absolute monoterpene concentrations.

Summarily, how and whether plants re-program their defensive phenotypes appropriately in response to certain hormonal elicitors is a key factor that predicts survival of biotic challenges from pathogens and herbivores (Erb et al. 2012). Plant responses to jasmonic acid and methyl jasmonate (MeJa) in particular are conserved across a diversity of plant families, and these phytohormones reliably signal the induction of physical and chemical defenses important for resistance to herbivory in many conifer species (Franceschi et al. 2005; Heijari et al. 2005; Miller et al. 2005; Erb et al. 2012). Here, I show that P. engelmannii does not exhibit the strong short-term increases in total monoterpene concentrations reported for other spruce systems, but MeJa application nonetheless primed defensive capacity by altering phloem chemistry and promoting traumatic resin duct formation in subsequent years. This pattern may reflect a defensive strategy adapted to shorter growing seasons and harsher climatic conditions, in which delayed duct formation provides benefits against subsequent herbivore challenges. The observed shifts in monoterpene profiles toward terpinolene, linalool, and γ-terpinene—compounds with demonstrated toxicity to D. rufipennis—suggest that sensitivity to MeJa is associated with the induction of resistance traits of mature spruce. Additional work that directly links induced chemical and anatomical traits to realized bark beetle resistance is needed to evaluate this potential. The present study provides an experimental framework against which to test this, and varying MeJa inputs is associated with differences in the expression of resin duct formation. More broadly, the present study highlights the importance of species- and climate-specific contexts in interpreting the role of hormonal elicitors as drivers of conifer inducible defenses.
