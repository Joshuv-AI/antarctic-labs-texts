Longitudinal Assessment of T1 Mapping Trends Disease in Pediatric Patients With Heart Transplant
Authors: Nicolle M Ceneri, Ravi Vegulla, Nicholas Mouzakis, Karin Hamann, Devika Richmann, Joshua Kanter, John Berger, Tacy Downing, Yue‐Hin Loke, Steven J Staffa, David Zurakowski, Russell Cross, Laura J Olivieri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurveillance for heart transplant rejection by endomyocardial biopsy is invasive and may yield false negatives. T1 and T2 mapping from cardiac magnetic resonance can demonstrate elevations with rejection. We sought to evaluate longitudinal changes in T1 and T2 mapping in pediatric patients with heart transplant.Methods and ResultsA cohort study was performed of pediatric patients with heart transplant who underwent concurrent endomyocardial biopsy and cardiac magnetic resonance with T1 and T2 mapping from December 2019 to July 2024. Segmental values were measured and subsegmental elevations (ie, hotspots) were identified. Subjects were categorized as either treated rejection or no rejection. Peak and mean T1 and T2 values and number of hotspots at/between each time point for patient dyads were compared between the groups. A total of 21 subjects (7 treated rejection, 14 no rejection) with 68 total encounters met inclusion criteria. Peak and mean T1 values were higher in treated rejection patients during the rejection period and decreased with treatment (peak, 1086 versus 1052; mean, 1028 versus 1021), such that at last follow‐up when their rejection had resolved, there was no significant difference in values when compared with no rejection patients (peak, 1066; mean, 1016). The number of T1 hotspots decreased after rejection treatment (2 versus 1). There were no changes in peak or mean T2 values in the treated rejection group despite treatment, and peak and mean T2 values were similar to patients with no rejection through last follow‐up.ConclusionsElevated T1 values and hotspots observed during cardiac allograft rejection decline in response to treatment. Cardiac magnetic resonance may serve as a noninvasive monitoring tool for the development and resolution of rejection, as well as the effectiveness of rejection therapy.Keywords: cardiac magnetic resonance, heart transplant, parametric mapping, pediatricsSubject Categories: Transplantation, Heart Failure, Congenital Heart Disease, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In pediatric patients experiencing cardiac allograft rejection, quantitative cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can both measure and visualize active rejection with T1 mapping, which normalizes in response to therapy.

In pediatric patients experiencing cardiac allograft rejection, quantitative cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can both measure and visualize active rejection with T1 mapping, which normalizes in response to therapy.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Cardiac magnetic resonance T1 mapping may be used to discern rejection and its response to treatment in a noninvasive manner.

Cardiac magnetic resonance T1 mapping may be used to discern rejection and its response to treatment in a noninvasive manner.

Although heart transplantation offers lifesaving therapy for a myriad of pediatric heart conditions, allograft rejection remains an important cause of morbidity and mortality in this population. 1 Acute rejection accounts for >10% of patient deaths within 10 years after transplant, and patients treated for rejection within the first year after transplant have worse survival. 2 Because rejection has an insidious onset, children with heart transplants require lifelong screening for rejection.

Diagnosis of allograft rejection is complex, as the definition of rejection may vary by center, and it is often difficult to differentiate acute cellular rejection and antibody‐mediated rejection. 1 Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) has remained the gold standard for the diagnosis of allograft rejection in children, although newer methods, such as circulating cell‐free DNA levels, are increasingly incorporated into care. 3 EMB allows for direct pathologic evaluation of the myocardium for acute cellular rejection and antibody‐mediated rejection. 4 However, this procedure is invasive and carries cumulative risks associated with radiation, sedation, and the tissue procurement itself. 5 Furthermore, because tissue sampling via EMB is limited to random samples from the right ventricle, it frequently yields false negatives as accessible regions may not be representative of most of the myocardium and are subject to scarring from repetitive sampling. 6 , 7 , 8 Although cell‐free DNA serum markers are demonstrating excellent sensitivity for rejection detection, they are noted to be elevated in other conditions that may not be associated with immune‐mediated rejection and therefore may not indicate a need for rejection therapy. 9 , 10 Thus, a noninvasive diagnostic tool that can give a reliable global assessment for allograft rejection remains an unmet need for pediatric patients who have undergone heart transplantation.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is a noninvasive imaging modality that has demonstrated ability to detect the presence and extent of cardiovascular disease in pediatric cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, and injury and rejection in transplants. Numerous studies have demonstrated a reliable correlation between CMR‐based parametric mapping results and histopathology‐determined transplant rejection. More recently, the utility of a CMR‐guided approach to transplant surveillance in the first year after transplant demonstrated superior identification and treatment of rejection compared with standard surveillence. 11 However, these studies were performed in adult transplant recipients, and no data exist for this approach in pediatric patients.

Although there is emerging evidence that CMR T1 and T2 values are elevated in pediatric heart transplant (PHTx) rejection, 12 , 13 , 14 data are lacking on the performance of these measures over time in the pediatric population, which would inform their use as a tool to trend disease severity and treatment efficacy. Therefore, we sought to evaluate longitudinal changes in T1 and T2 values in pediatric patients with heart transplant with and without allograft rejection.

METHODS

Study Cohort

This study was conducted under institutional review board number Pro00011408. All patients at Children's National Hospital with a history of heart transplantation referred for at least 2 clinically indicated surveillance EMBs and prospectively enrolled in the CMR study with consent/assent between December 2019 and July 2023 were included. Patient CMRs with graft age <6 months after transplant were excluded because of recent cardiopulmonary bypass and its possible effects on T1 and T2. Clinical data were recorded, including patient demographics, transplant history, and rejection history. Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the corresponding author.

CMR Procedure

Patients underwent an abbreviated noncontrast CMR at 1.5 T (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), including breath‐held native modified Look‐Locker inversion recovery T1 and T2 mapping in 8 short‐axis slices to offer full myocardial coverage from apex to base, with parameters published previously. 12 For modified Look‐Locker inversion T1 map acquisition, the protocol was field of view 360 × 307 mm and phase field of view 50% to 80% based on the patient's body habitus. For patients with an RR interval >700 ms, matrix was 256, echo time was 1.12 ms, repetition time was 2.80 ms, and flip angle of 35°; for patients with RR <700 ms, matrix was 192, echo time was 1.06 ms, repetition time was 1.93 ms, and flip angle of 70°. Slice thickness and slice skip varied by patient size, ranging from 4 to 8 to 0 to 2 mm, respectively. For T2 map acquisition, the protocol was matrix 192, field of view 360 × 307 mm, phase field of view 50% to 80% based on the patient's body habitus. The echo time was 1.06 ms, repetition time was 185 ms, and flip angle 70°. Slice thickness and slice skip varied based on the patient's size, ranging from 4 to 8 to 0 to 2 mm, respectively. Children aged <10 years underwent CMR with general anesthesia, and those aged >10 years were offered the option of anesthesia, as medically indicated. The duration of the entire CMR examination was 24 minutes (range, 21–30 minutes).

Parametric Map Analyses

After excluding the most basal and apical slices and ensuring maps passed quality checks to evaluate for motion offset and reconstruction errors, the T1 and T2 maps were analyzed to create 2 types of measurements, as demonstrated in Figure 1. First, a traditional segmental analysis was completed using offline software (Medis Medical Imaging, Leiden, the Netherlands), where segmental T1 and T2 values corresponding to the American Heart Association 17‐segment model were measured using standard laboratory techniques, 12 , 15 taking care to exclude blood pool, epicardial fat, and other artifacts. From these 16 T1 and T2 values, mean T1 and T2, and peak T1 and T2 values were generated for each encounter. An intrarater reliability review was performed with 12 patients and 1 observer, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated for T1 and T2 values using a 2‐way mixed‐effects model.

Segmental analysis is demonstrated by the orange ROIs, which correspond to the American Heart Association 17‐segment model, wherein all values of T1 or T2 within the ROI are averaged.

Subsegmental analysis is demonstrated by the blue ROI, containing only pixels that demonstrate elevation (green arrow, orange arrow). A small subsegmental elevation may be clinically important but will have minimal impact on segmental T1 or T2 values; thus, both analysis methods are needed. ROI indicates region of interest.

Second, given the patchy nature of T1/T2 elevations previously noted in transplant rejection, 12 , 16 a subsegmental analysis of the parametric maps was also completed using 3‐dimensional segmentation software (ITKSnap, http://itksnap.org) 17 to identify regions of continuous (ie, >2 slices, >3 voxels), significant signal elevation beyond 4 SDs of our local normative values (T1 >1175 ms, T2 >60 ms), or so‐called subsegmental “hotspots.” These subsegmental hotspots could exist within or across the segments of the 17‐segment model and reflect the random and patchy nature of muscle changes with graft rejection. 12 , 18 This hotspot analysis generated a number of T1 and T2 hotspots for each encounter.

Categorization as Rejection or No Rejection

Graft rejection was defined by the primary team's clinical decision to treat for rejection according to our institution's protocol for treatment for rejection. Treatment decisions were blinded to CMR results. Patients with grade 2R or greater received treatment for rejection per protocol. Patients with grade 0 or 1R biopsy results received treatment for rejection according to institutional protocol based on the presence of hemodynamic compromise from additional clinical and hemodynamic data from echocardiography, cardiac catheterization, and laboratory studies. 12 Each encounter was categorized as either rejection (ie, patient received new treatment for suspected rejection, including both intravenous or oral medications according to institutional protocol) or no rejection (ie, patient received no new treatments).

After each encounter was categorized as rejection or no rejection, time point dyads were created and categorized into 1 of 2 outcome groups based on the longitudinal history. Subjects were categorized as no rejection (ie, 2 time points classified as no rejection) or as treated rejection (ie, started in the rejection group and progressed to no rejection at the subsequent time point). In cases where 1 subject had >2 study encounters, they were categorized as treated rejection if any rejection was noted during the study (using the rejection encounter as the first time point, and the most recent time point without rejection as the second time point), or as no rejection (using the earliest and latest time points). At the time of their last CMR, all patients were without clinical rejection.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic characteristics between the treated rejection versus no rejection dyads were compared using Fisher exact test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. Shapiro‐Wilk test for normality of data was used to determine the appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. The 6 CMR quantitative variables listed above (mean T1 and T2, peak T1 and T2, and number of T1 and T2 subsegmental hotspots) were noted for each encounter and compared longitudinally. For hotspot calculations, the second time point hotspot burden was subtracted from the first time point hotspot burden to calculate hotspot change. For paired CMR quantitative data, Wilcoxon signed ranks test or paired t‐tests were used to assess longitudinal changes within each group. CMR quantitative data were compared between rejection versus no rejection dyads using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for nonparametric testing or Student t‐test for parametric testing, as appropriate.

RESULTS

A total of 34 pediatric patients with heart transplant completed 95 unique encounters during the study period. Of these, there were 21 patients with ≥2 encounters who met inclusion criteria, totaling 68 unique encounters. There were 7 patients in the treated rejection group (33%) who accounted for 41% of total encounters, and 14 patients in the no rejection group (67%) who accounted for 59% of total encounters. Demographic data of the cohort are shown in Table 1. There were no demographic differences between the groups. The median follow‐up interval of the cohort was 24 months (interquartile range, 12–30 months) over which there was a median of 3 encounters per patient (interquartile range, 2–4).

DISCUSSION

This prospective clinical study of recipients of a pediatric heart transplant confirms the ability of noninvasive, noncontrast cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to diagnose rejection without an endomyocardial biopsy. Furthermore, this is the first study of its kind in children to find that T1 values return to normal following treatment for allograft rejection, offering a quantitative end point to track therapy and its effectiveness. Specifically, mean and peak native T1 values, as well as “hotspot” subsegmental elevations in native T1 values, correlate with clinical rejection and improve after treatment in pediatric patients with heart transplant. Together, these findings suggest for the first time that native T1 mapping may serve as a longitudinal test to identify and track allograft rejection and its response to treatment. With this, CMR has the potential to be a useful noninvasive tool in clinical decision‐making and longitudinal care of pediatric patients after heart transplantation.

Prior studies demonstrating the utility of CMR in identifying myocardial abnormalities in both adult and pediatric heart transplant recipients set the stage for further investigation. In their study, Ide et al were the first to validate native T1 times against the histologic (although not clinical) gold standard right ventricular septal EMB in pediatric patients with heart transplant. 19 The relationship between native T1 times and EMB has also been demonstrated in adults with valvular heart disease and forms of cardiomyopathy. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 However, it is known that EMB has a significant rate of false‐negative results, and frequently patients are treated for rejection with improvement of symptoms in the setting of a “negative” EMB. Additionally, studies regarding the application of parametric mapping in the monitoring of the PHTx population have been limited to date. Our group previously published the correlation between elevated native T1 values and the degree of clinical rejection and graft dysfunction in pediatric patients with heart transplant. 12 Husain et al found a similar relationship between T1 times and clinical rejection, as well as cardiac allograft vasculopathy history. 16 In the present study, which features a cohort composed exclusively of patients with longitudinal follow‐up, we again demonstrate this finding. Here, we show the correlation between elevated mean and peak native T1 values and the presence of clinical rejection (Table 2, Figure 2). Children with grafts aged <6 months were excluded in an effort to eliminate the confounding elevation in T1 measurements early on, as others have also done, 25 possibly associated with allograft ischemia‐reperfusion injury. In fact, Ide et al showed that native T1 in the interventricular septum is correlated with ischemia time. 19 Although elevations in T1 are complex and potentially multifactorial, the longitudinal elevations with rejection that decrease with treatment are more compelling to represent changes associated with rejection rather than more permanent changes associated with graft fibrosis and vasculopathy. Thus, our results suggest that providers can leverage the clinically significant differences in native T1 values (peak or mean) to identify patients with or without allograft rejection after the early posttransplantation period. In a sense, this is similar to findings in other acute inflammatory conditions, including the observation of significant and diffuse native T1 elevations in various types of myocarditis that eventually resolve once the disease process has resolved. 26 , 27 , 28 Thus, CMR may have clear advantages over traditional EMB because of its ability to provide a comprehensive assessment of the whole myocardium, rather than only accessible regions of the right ventricle. With these results, the feasibility of setting a peak and mean T1 threshold above which patients should be treated for clinical evidence of rejection is attainable. More studies are needed, ideally with multiple sites given how rare vasculopathy is in pediatric transplants.

Earlier research has also shown patchy abnormalities in heart transplant patients with rejection that often occur at the subsegmental, rather than segmental, level. 12 , 16 Therefore, differences in the total number of “hotspots” or subsegmental elevations were investigated longitudinally, and found to be a significant marker of rejection, with a decrease in the convalescent period following rejection therapy (Figure 4). On average, the number of T1 hotspots was higher in those with allograft rejection in comparison to those without (2 versus 1), although this did not reach statistical significance. Given that we have found subsegmental elevations in T1 among a background of global T1 elevation, this may signify that there is an area of myocardium where the rejection episode originates. This concept may help explain the false‐negative history of EMB; we may not be able to access the region of the myocardium where evidence of cellular or antibody‐mediated rejection is active, although its downstream effects are leading to local and diffuse fibrotic changes. Preliminary work by our group has shown that patients with T1 hotspots on CMR and guided EMB require rejection treatment more frequently than those with unguided biopsies. 18 Thus, not only do hotspot data offer additional information to assess for allograft rejection but they may also be leveraged to improve the diagnostic yield of EMB by targeting accessible hotspots.

The novelty of our study is its longitudinal design. We followed up patients over a median of 24 months (interquartile range, 12–30 months) and 3 separate CMR studies (interquartile range, 2–4), with no differences in the length of follow‐up or number of CMR encounters between treated rejection and no rejection groups. We observed a decrease in the peak and mean T1 values in patients experiencing rejection after treatment, approaching statistical significance. In fact, after treatment, there were no differences in the peak or mean T1 values between the 2 groups. Thus, we can hypothesize that it takes at most a median of 24 months for T1 to fully recover based on these data, with some patients recovering sooner. Future studies should focus on further defining the optimal interval between studies for surveillance and recovery, as they may differ from those observed by other mechanisms.

An elevation in native T2 values has also been shown in patients experiencing allograft rejection. In a smaller cohort previously published by our group, T2 values were elevated in patients with histologic or clinical evidence of rejection, including a monotonic trend where increased T2 values were associated with higher EMB grades. 13 Studies in adult patients with heart transplant have shown a similar relationship of elevated T2 in those with cardiac allograft rejection 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 and was even sensitive and specific in predicting a positive biopsy result. 31 In contrast, our study showed that the peak and mean native T2 values were similar between patients with and without clinical evidence of allograft rejection (Table 2, Figure 3). The current literature on the significance of T2 values in pediatric patients with heart transplant is limited and variable. Others have also observed no differences in global T2 values between PHTx with and without allograft rejection, 29 as well as between PHTx and healthy controls. 16 The longitudinal changes in native T2 values or T2 hotspots were not impressive. Peak and mean T2 values, as well as T2 hotspot numbers, did not change after treatment for allograft rejection. Although this study did not identify T2 as a strong predictor of rejection, it is possible that, because the focus is on longitudinal trends in T1 and T2 with rejection and its treatment, we could be underpowered to detect significant changes in T2 over time. T2 maps also have traditionally coarser spatial resolution, which could make identification of patchy elevation more difficult in smaller patients. It is possible that the increased size of our cohort and clinical definition of rejection may have contributed to these differences. Furthermore, there may be various physiological mechanisms of rejection at play in pediatric patients with heart transplant that result in less interstitial edema and therefore free water content, leaving T2 unchanged. A multisite study will be helpful in determining the true value of T2 in children with heart transplantation.

Other CMR components have been studied and may be used to complement parametric mapping findings and assist in the diagnosis of rejection. For example, CMR‐derived extracellular volume fraction has been found to correlate with the histologic degree of fibrosis in EMB 19 and to be related to the B‐type natriuretic peptide, a biological indicator of cardiac function. 25 Results of other noninvasive imaging tests, such as echocardiography, may also be used in the holistic assessment for rejection. Riesenkampff et al demonstrated that the native T1 times are higher in pediatric patients with heart transplant and are correlated with markers of echocardiographic diastolic dysfunction. 34 In addition, we previously showed that peak T1 values are also correlated with invasive testing measures, including right‐sided filling pressure, capillary wedge pressures, and increased B‐type natriuretic peptide. 12 Thus, we believe there is value in developing a novel CMR scoring criterion that includes an assessment of native T1 values and hotspots, as well as extracellular volume, to diagnose allograft rejection and provide prognostic information of myocardial tissue over time in pediatric patients with heart transplant. Ultimately, we foresee CMR becoming complimentary to EMB for the identification and monitoring of cardiac allograft rejection and may serve to reduce the number and frequency of EMBs performed.

LIMITATIONS

Our study is limited by a small group of patients with rejection within the PHTx cohort. This is a single‐center study, reflecting the clinical care at a single hospital and may therefore have limitations in generalizability to other centers with different treatment protocols and imaging capabilities. Further work is required to evaluate CMR imaging findings on multivendor platforms and prospective longitudinal assessments from multiple centers. Biopsy‐negative rejection is difficult to discern, which may weaken the parametric mapping results. Additionally, cardiac allograft vasculopathy and other causes of myocardial fibrosis could affect the ability to identify rejection given that these patients will likely have higher native T1 values. CMR does not differentiate acute cellular rejection from antibody‐mediated rejection, or differentiate histologic grades of rejection, which is another limitation given that treatment protocols differ based on the mechanism and severity of rejection. Further work is needed to elucidate whether CMR has this capability.

CONCLUSIONS

Parametric mapping regional T1 values are elevated during cardiac allograft rejection and decline in response to treatment. Thus, CMR has important utility in assessing the need for and effectiveness of rejection therapy and may serve as an indicator for alternate therapies. Identification of subsegmental hotspot elevations in T1 may be a more sensitive way to detect the development and resolution of allograft rejection. CMR is a promising noninvasive modality to longitudinally monitor for allograft rejection and resolution in pediatric patients with heart transplant.
