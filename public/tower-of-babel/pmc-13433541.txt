Possible underestimation of non-operative management success in perforated diverticulitis with distant free air
Authors: Muzaffer Atlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe review reported a 27.8% success rate for non-operative management in patients with distant free air and, on that basis, suggested that early surgery should be strongly considered. When we examined the published data, we were unable to reproduce this estimate. Recalculating from the information given in the review, the numerator appeared to include only studies that reported this outcome, whereas the denominator also included patients from studies in which it was not reported; on a like-for-like basis the success rate is closer to 73% than to the reported 27.8%. Several values in the distant-free-air column of Table 5 of the review also correspond exactly to the complementary failure rates, which is compatible with a labelling or reporting error. These observations concern the distant-air subgroup specifically and require verification against the original data rather than establishing that the pooled analysis is incorrect. Given the clinical weight carried by this subgroup, clarification from the authors, and a correction if the discrepancies are confirmed, would be valuable.

Dear Editors,

We read the systematic review by Cirocchi et al. [1] on non-operative management (NOM) of perforated diverticulitis with real interest. It is a careful piece of work, and the authors are open about heterogeneity, risk of bias, and the fact that “failure” is defined differently from one study to the next. Our reason for writing is narrower. The figure that carries their main recommendation, the success of NOM when distant free air is present, does not agree with the numbers in their own tables. Using the denominators reported in the review’s Tables 2, 3 and 5, we were unable to reproduce the pooled estimate despite repeated recalculation. We should say plainly that we did not attempt to re-run the random-effects meta-analysis, and that we had no access to the original data-extraction sheets or statistical files. We checked only whether the reported proportion is arithmetically consistent, and our observations therefore point to values that need verification against the original data rather than to a proven error.

The review reports that NOM succeeded in 27.8% of patients with distant free air (179/643), and concludes that early surgery should be strongly considered in this group. Two things trouble us.

The numerator and the denominator appear to come from different studies. In Table 5 of the review, the 179 successes come from the ten studies that reported outcomes for distant air. The denominator of 643, drawn from Table 2, also counts patients from studies that did not report this outcome. The largest of these is the cohort of Ocaña et al. [2], 369 patients with distant air, marked “Nr” in Table 5 of the review. This is not the complete-case, outcome-specific denominator the authors describe in their methods. Restrict both numerator and denominator to the reporting studies and the result is 179/243. Include the Ocaña cohort instead, using the success count given in Table 3 (269/369), and the result is 448/612. Both give a crude arithmetic success proportion of around 73%, rather than 27.8% (Table 1).

The per-study rates in the distant-air column of Table 5 of the review also do not appear to follow from their own numerators and denominators. In several studies the tabulated value corresponds exactly to the complement of the calculated success rate, that is, the failure rate (Table 2), while in others it matches the success rate. The column therefore seems to mix success and failure rates, a pattern that is compatible with a labelling, transcription, or data-extraction error rather than a single miscalculation. This interpretation would need to be checked against the original extraction records.

This is not merely a presentational matter, because the advice to consider early surgery whenever distant free air is seen goes beyond the more measured 2020 WSES guidance [3] and appears to be influenced by the reported 27.8% figure. If the subgroup proportion is closer to 73%, that would be more consistent with attempting NOM in selected, haemodynamically stable patients under close clinical and radiological surveillance. In practice this can be the difference between an early laparotomy and a period of close observation. We recognise the authors’ own cautions about selection bias, high heterogeneity (I² = 85%) and the shifting definition of failure, and we do not suggest that these concerns extend to the review as a whole; our point is confined to the distant-air subgroup and is, at its core, arithmetical.

We therefore offer these observations in the spirit of constructive scientific discussion. We would invite the authors to revisit the distant-air subgroup, verify the study-level values reported in Table 5 of the review, and clarify whether the summary estimate was derived directly from the meta-analytic model or from the aggregated tabulated data. Given the clinical importance of the conclusions regarding patients with distant free air, further clarification of this subgroup would be valuable and, if the discrepancies are confirmed, could inform an appropriate correction of the published results. These issues do not diminish the importance of the authors’ contribution to a clinically important topic; our aim is to help preserve the accuracy of the record.

Sincerely,
