Legal Δ \Delta : Enhancing Legal Reasoning in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning with Chain-of-Thought Guided Information Gain
Xin Dai, Buqiang Xu, Zhenghao Liu, Yukun Yan, Huiyuan Xie, Xiaoyuan Yi, Shuo Wang, Ge Yu

Abstract
Legal Artificial Intelligence (LegalAI) has achieved notable advances in automating judicial decision-making with the support of Large Language Models (LLMs). However, existing legal LLMs still struggle to generate reliable and interpretable reasoning processes. They often default to fast-thinking behavior by producing direct answers without explicit multi-step reasoning, limiting their effectiveness in complex legal scenarios that demand rigorous justification. To address this challenge, we propose Legal Δ \Delta , a reinforcement learning framework designed to enhance legal reasoning through chain-of-thought guided information gain . During training, Legal Δ \Delta employs a dual-mode input setup—comprising direct answer and reasoning-augmented modes—and maximizes the information gain between them. This encourages the model to acquire meaningful reasoning patterns rather than generating superficial or redundant explanations. Legal Δ \Delta follows a two-stage approach: (1) distilling latent reasoning capabilities from a powerful Large Reasoning Model (LRM), DeepSeek-R1, and (2) refining reasoning quality via differential comparisons, combined with a multidimensional reward mechanism that assesses both structural coherence and legal-domain specificity. Experimental results on multiple legal reasoning tasks demonstrate that Legal Δ \Delta outperforms strong baselines in both accuracy and interpretability. It consistently produces more robust and trustworthy legal judgments without relying on labeled preference data. All code and data are available at https://github.com/NEUIR/LegalDelta.

1
Introduction

The rapid development of LegalAI systems—referring to artificial intelligence technologies applied to legal practice and judicial processes—is fundamentally reshaping the legal landscape. By automating complex tasks such as legal information extraction
(
Rabelo et al. 2022
)
, intelligent case retrieval
(
Zeng et al. 2005
;
Zhang and Dou 2023
)
, judgment prediction
(
Liu et al. 2023b
)
, and legal question answering
(
Louis, van Dijck, and Spanakis 2024
)
, LegalAI significantly enhances the efficiency and accessibility of legal services. These technologies have demonstrated substantial value in real-world applications, including contract analysis, legal dispute resolution, and regulatory compliance. Given these promising developments, recent research has increasingly turned to Large Language Models (LLMs) as a foundation for building a more effective legal assistant.

Recently, researchers have increasingly adopted Reinforcement Learning (RL) frameworks such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which enables models to learn from feedback and optimize their behavior through trial-and-error. Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) like QwQ
(
Team 2025
)
and DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
have demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities across specialized domains including medicine
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, mathematics
(
Ahn et al. 2024
)
, and programming
(
El-Kishky et al. 2025
)
. Recent work has extended these ideas to the legal domain by distilling reasoning traces from powerful RL-trained models such as DeepSeek-V3
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, as in LawGPT
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
. However, these methods typically rely on rule-based rewards in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
(
Mroueh 2025
)
, which focus on output correctness while neglecting the quality and depth of the reasoning process itself. Therefore, how to effectively optimize Chain-of-Thought (COT) within the RL paradigm remains an open question.

To address these challenges, this paper presents Legal
\Delta
, a reinforcement learning framework designed to improve legal reasoning in LLMs by incorporating
Chain-of-Thought guided Information Gain
. Built upon the GRPO paradigm, Legal
\Delta
introduces an information-gain based reward mechanism into the RLVR setting, encouraging the model to explore diverse reasoning trajectories and develop robust reasoning strategies.
Specifically, Legal
\Delta
quantifies information gain by leveraging the mathematical equivalence between model logits and Q-values in reinforcement learning. By comparing model responses under direct inference and chain-of-thought prompting, it computes confidence differentials that capture both (i) pointwise mutual information between reasoning steps and final answer correctness, and (ii) global confidence shifts indicating whether reasoning improves decision reliability. This reward is then used to guide policy optimization, pushing the model to discover and reinforce high-quality reasoning behaviors that balance accuracy and prediction confidence.

Our experiments on various LLMs demonstrate that Legal
\Delta
outperforms all baseline models, achieving approximately 10% performance improvements across multiple legal reasoning tasks, while also exhibiting strong generalization to out-of-domain tasks.
Further analysis reveals that the information gain module significantly enhances model confidence levels through progressive logits improvements, while also reducing training batch variance for improved training efficiency and stability.
Additionally, comprehensive performance quality assessment demonstrates that Legal
\Delta
increases the prominence of legally tokens and generates reasoning chains with lower perplexity scores at the token-level, while consistently outperforming baseline models across different scenarios complexities at the sequence-level.

Related Work

Existing legal LLMs mainly rely on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to help models better understand legal semantics. These methods usually train the models on legal corpora using language modeling objectives. For example, models like LawGPT-zh
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
, HanFei
(
He et al. 2023
)
, and Lawyer LLaMA
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
adopt SFT and combine it with the Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) strategy to improve training efficiency
(
Hu et al. 2022
;
Cui et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2023
)
. One major challenge of SFT lies in the construction of high-quality training data. Recent work
(
Yue et al. 2023
)
addresses this by collecting existing publicly available legal datasets, crawling legal texts from the web, and incorporating instruction-tuning datasets
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
)
.
While these approaches improve performance to some extent, they typically require extensive labeled data and focus on end-to-end optimization over task-specific labels.
As a result, SFT-based methods are often susceptible to overfitting and catastrophic forgetting
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
, which undermines the retention of previously acquired legal knowledge.

To address this challenge, recent studies
(
Verma et al. 2025
;
Vara et al. 2025
)
have explored Reinforcement Learning (RL) to encourage LLMs to engage in broader exploration by specially designed reward mechanisms.
Intelligent Legal
Assistant
(
Yao et al. 2025
)
utilizes the coverage of case elements as the reward signal to guide clarification in legal question answering.
SyLeR
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
addresses the lack of structured reasoning paths in legal question answering by introducing a structure-aware similarity-based reward mechanism that guides LLMs to construct syllogistic reasoning paths.
However, its reliance on surface-level matching, making it difficult to capture the complexity and rigor of legal reasoning.
Simultaneously, Legal Mathematical Reasoning
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
employs result-oriented rewards to enhance mathematical reasoning, belonging to the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) framework.
However, this approach falls short in effectively evaluating intermediate reasoning quality.
Therefore, we propose Legal
\Delta
that integrates an information-gain-enhanced reward mechanism into the RLVR framework to provide fine-grained supervision for reasoning quality, enabling LLM to learn more reliable and confidence-aligned legal reasoning strategies.

3.1
Preliminary of LLM Reasoning in the Legal Tasks

Legal reasoning is a complex cognitive process involving fact analysis, application of legal principles, and structured logical deduction. Unlike standard QA tasks, legal reasoning requires multi-step analytical thinking, integration of domain knowledge, and the generation of interpretable argumentation. Our framework focuses on five representative legal tasks that demand such capabilities.

Given a legal query
q
, our model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{Legal}}
produces a structured output:

q\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{M}_{\text{Legal}}\rightsquigarrow o=(r,a)

(1)

where
r
denotes the step-by-step reasoning process and
a
is the final answer. This structured output ensures both interpretability and reliability, which are crucial in legal applications.
We then introduce our reinforcement learning based training strategy in Section
3.2
, which equips LLMs with legal reasoning capabilities. Following this, Section
3.3
presents an information-theoretic reward enhancement mechanism that refines
R_{\text{legal}}
into
R_{\text{info}}
via differential Q-value analysis, enabling more nuanced and reasoning-aware reward modeling for reinforcement learning.

3.2
Enhancing the Reasoning Capability of LLMs via Reinforcement Learning

General-domain LLMs often fall short in domain-specific reasoning tasks. Although supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
Gururangan et al. 2020
)
can adapt models to target domains, it also risks overfitting and catastrophic forgetting
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
, thereby weakening general reasoning capabilities.
To address this, we propose a RL-based training framework. The first stage employs SFT to guide the LLM to imitate the expert-like reasoning behaviors. The second stage applies reinforcement learning to further refine the reasoning ability based on performance feedback.

Supervised Fine-Tuning

We construct
\mathcal{M}_{\text{R1-Distill}}
via SFT using reasoning-enhanced legal data. Specifically, we leverage the high-quality reasoning chains generated by DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
to construct a dataset pairing legal queries with structured and expert-like reasoning steps.

The SFT loss is defined as the standard cross-entropy loss over the full reasoning sequence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{SFT}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(q,r,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_{train}}\left[\log P_{\theta}(r,a|q)\right],

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}_{train}
represents the training dataset,
o=(r,a)
denotes the complete output containing both reasoning chain and final answer, and
P_{\theta}(r,a|q)
represents the probability of generating the complete reasoning output given the query and task instruction.

Reinforcement Learning

Building upon the distilled model, we apply Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
to further enhance the model’s reasoning ability. Unlike traditional RL approaches that require separate critic models, GRPO compares intra-group outputs to estimate advantages, making it effective for the scenarios where outputs can vary widely in quality.

Within the GRPO framework, our policy model
\pi_{\theta}
generates multiple reasoning trajectories
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
for each legal query. Then we can use the GRPO training method to optimize LLMs:

\displaystyle J_{GRPO}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|q)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}L_{i}(\theta)\right]

(3)

\displaystyle-\beta D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}||\pi_{\theta}),

where
L_{i}(\theta)=-\log\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)\cdot A_{i}^{G}
, and
A_{i}^{G}
is the group-wise advantage. The KL term penalizes policy shifts to maintain training stability.

The effectiveness of GRPO critically depends on the quality of reward signals. Legal
\Delta
defines a structured reward function as:

R(o_{i})=R_{\text{format}}(o_{i})+R_{\text{legal}}(a_{i}),

(4)

where
R_{\text{format}}
ensures adherence to the structured output format, and
R_{\text{legal}}
evaluates the correctness of the legal reasoning process and conclusion.

Format Reward (
R_{\text{format}}(o_{i})
):
This component ensures outputs adhere to a structured format, with
<reasoning>
and
<answer>
tags separating the reasoning chain from the final decision. This format facilitates reward computation of both the reasoning process and the legal outcome.

Legal Reward (
R_{\text{legal}}(a_{i})
):

This component evaluates the correctness and legality of the final answer
a_{i}
using fine-grained, task-specific criteria. These legal criteria make the outcomes more verifiable and thus suitable for guiding GRPO-based training. Further details on the design of the legal reward are provided in Appendix
A.3
.

3.3
Information-Gain Enhanced Legal Reward Modeling

To move beyond terminal answer evaluation, we introduce an information-theoretic enhancement to reward design. Specifically, we replace the legal reward
R_{\text{legal}}
component with
R_{\text{info}}
for reward modeling:

R(o_{i})=R_{\text{format}}(o_{i})+R_{\text{info}}(a_{i}).

(5)

The reward
R_{\text{info}}
incorporates reasoning confidence through differential Q-value analysis to better evaluate the semantic contribution of reasoning processes in legal analysis:

R_{\text{info}}(a_{i})=R_{\text{legal}}(a_{i})\times\sigma\left(\Delta Q\cdot T\right),

(6)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function providing bounded amplification within (0,1) with monotonic enhancement properties,
\Delta Q
denotes the information quality differential, and
T
is the temperature parameter that controls the sensitivity of reward modulation for fine-grained control over training dynamics. The multiplicative structure preserves the primacy of correctness while enabling adaptive enhancement based on information content.

Recent work by Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2025
)
proves that logits from next-token prediction training are mathematically equivalent to Q-functions in offline inverse reinforcement learning(see detailed proof in Appendix
A.4
):

Q(q,a)=\text{logit}_{\theta}(a|q),

(7)

where
Q(q,a)
quantifies the expected value of generating answer
a
given query
q
, and
\text{logit}_{\theta}(a|q)
represents the average unnormalized log-probability scores across all tokens in answer
a
when generated autoregressively given context
q
. This theoretical foundation enables us to extract Q-values directly from any pre-trained language model and design a differential mechanism to quantify reasoning quality.
For answer
a
, the Q-value gain given by reasoning process
r
is expressed as:

\Delta Q(r)=\text{logit}_{\theta}(a|q,r)-\text{logit}_{\theta}(a|q).

(8)

This formulation measures the semantic shift in the model’s internal representation space induced by the reasoning process
r
, providing a quantitative measure of how much the reasoning contributes to the model’s confidence in generating the correct answer. We show our reasoning mode and direct mode prompt templates respectively in Appendix
A.6
.

From an information-theoretic perspective, given that logits follow the softmax probability distribution:

p_{i}(a)=\frac{e^{\mathrm{logits}_{i}(a)}}{Z_{i}},

(9)

where
Z_{i}=\sum_{a^{\prime}}e^{\mathrm{logits}_{i}(a^{\prime})}
,
a^{\prime}
is used to traverse all possible answer tokens. Through algebraic manipulation of the logit difference, we obtain:

\Delta Q(r)=\log\frac{p(a|q,r)}{p(a|q)}+\log\frac{Z_{q,r}}{Z_{q}}.

(10)

This decomposition reveals the information-theoretic interpretation of our approach.

The first term

\log\frac{p(a|q,r)}{p(a|q)}
represents the pointwise mutual information (PMI) between the answer token
a
and the reasoning process
r
, measuring the correlation between the reasoning chain and the specific answer: a positive value indicates that the reasoning process makes this answer more likely, a negative value means the reasoning actually decreases the probability of this answer, and zero implies no correlation.

The second term

\log\frac{Z_{q,r}}{Z_{q}}
serves as a global confidence regularizer that captures whether the reasoning process
r
enhances the model’s overall decisiveness: when positive, it indicates that
r
has sharpened the probability distribution, making the model more confident overall; when negative, it suggests the reasoning has introduced confusion. Within the GRPO framework, since rewards are utilized for comparative evaluation across multiple generated reasoning trajectories, the global normalization constant remains invariant across all candidates for the same query, allowing us to focus on the information-theoretic signal that truly differentiates reasoning quality.

Experimental Methodology

In this section, we describe the datasets, baselines, evaluation metrics, and implementation details in our experiments.

4.1
Datasets

In our experiments, we select training data from portions of the CAIL2018
(
Xiao et al. 2018
)
, JEC_QA
(
Zhong et al. 2019
)
, and LAIC2021
(
Zhong et al. 2019
)
datasets, which encompass diverse legal reasoning scenarios across multiple domains. We first use 450 high-quality reasoning samples distilled from DeepSeek-R1 to establish foundational legal reasoning capabilities. Subsequently, for GRPO training of Legal
\Delta
, we collect 6,981 samples for the training set and 563 samples for the validation set across all tasks in our experiments.
We select knowledge-intensive legal reasoning tasks for a comprehensive assessment, including legal article prediction (SPP-F), criminal charge prediction (CCP), sentencing prediction (SLP), similar case analysis (CAS) and financial amount calculation (CAC), which demand sophisticated reasoning over legal facts and regulations. To assess the generalization capability of our approach, we additionally evaluate performance on the out-of-domain DiscLaw benchmark, which contains various legal professional examinations distinct from our training domains.
Detailed dataset statistics can be found in the Appendix
A.1
.

4.2
Baselines

In our experiments, we compare Legal
\Delta
with existing law models: Fuzi-7B
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
, HanFei-7B
(
He et al. 2023
)
, LexiLaw-6B
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, ChatLaw-13B
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
, and DiscLaw-13B
(
Yue et al. 2024
)
. These law models vary in parameter scales and capabilities, all trained using various training methods on diverse legal datasets. To ensure fair and rigorous evaluation, we reimplemented a representative method (DiscLaw) based on the same Qwen2.5 series base models as our approach. Additionally, we compare with four baseline models: Zero-shot, R1-distill, DPO, and SFT (DiscLaw) for comprehensive evaluation.
By evaluating LLMs across different scales, we can systematically assess the generalization ability of various training strategies.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

We design comprehensive evaluation metrics for different task types following
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Yue et al. 2023
. For in-domain evaluation, we employ F1 and Jaccard (JAC) scores for SPP-F and CCP tasks, which assess the quality of multi-label classification. For SLP, CAS, and CAC tasks, we use accuracy scores. For out-of-domain evaluation, we assess generalization capability using accuracy scores across five tasks from the DiscLaw benchmark: PAE, NJE, PFE, UNGEE, and LBK.

4.4
Implementation Details

In our experiments, we adopt the Qwen2.5-Instruct series due to its superior reflective capabilities and cognitively informed reasoning behavior
(
Gandhi et al. 2025
)
, including the 3B, 7B, and 14B parameter models
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
, as backbone language models to ensure a comprehensive evaluation across different model scales.
During training, we use a batch size of 2 per GPU and a learning rate of 5e-5. For LoRA fine-tuning, we use a rank of 32. We use nucleus sampling with temperature of 0.9 and generate
G=6
samples per prompt. For information-gain, we set a temperature of
T=0.2
.
More training implementation details of Legal
\Delta
are presented in Appendix
A.2
.

Evaluation Result

In this section, we first evaluate the performance of various legal LLMs on legal reasoning tasks, demonstrating Legal
\Delta
’s effectiveness across different model scales and its generalization to out-of-domain scenarios. We then analyze the effectiveness of our information-gain-enhanced reward modeling by examining logits variations during training and the correlation between information gain and reward values. Following this, we conduct a comprehensive performance quality assessment from both token-level and sequence-level perspectives: at the token level, we analyze legal token prominence progression and assess reasoning chain quality through perplexity evaluation; at the sequence level, we examine performance improvements across different scenarios complexities. Additionally, we provide some case studies in Appendix
A.5

5.1
Overall Performance

This section evaluates the legal reasoning capabilities of Legal
\Delta
on both in-domain and out-of-domain tasks.

As shown in Table
1
, Legal
\Delta
achieves consistently superior performance across different model scales and evaluation settings. On in-domain legal reasoning benchmarks, Legal
\Delta
outperforms all baselines, including Zero-shot, SFT (DiscLaw), DPO, and R1-Distill, with an average improvement of approximately 10% across Qwen2.5 models of varying capacities. While DPO benefits from preference optimization and R1-Distill leverages high-quality reasoning traces, Legal
\Delta
outperforms both, especially on complex reasoning tasks such as SLP and CAS.

Furthermore, the advantages of Legal
\Delta
extend to out-of-domain scenarios, where it maintains strong performance across diverse evaluation benchmarks. This highlights the generalization capability of our approach, which dynamically optimizes reasoning processes and better captures nuanced legal logic by using the information-gain enhanced reward modeling method.

5.2
Ablation Study

To systematically assess the contribution of each core component in the Legal
\Delta
framework, we conduct a series of ablation experiments. The results are summarized in Table
2
.

Our reinforcement learning based training strategy brings substantial performance gains over the vanilla baseline. Removing the GRPO component (
w/o GRPO
) leads to a marked drop in performance, highlighting the critical role of reinforcement learning in enabling the legal reasoning capability of LLMs. This demonstrates that GRPO’s intra-group comparison mechanism effectively exploits quality differences among reasoning trajectories, making it essential for optimizing legal reasoning capabilities. Similarly, the
w/o SFT
setting confirms that both SFT and RL stages are jointly necessary for achieving optimal performance.

Among all components, the information-gain-enhanced reward mechanism contributes most significantly to performance improvements. To verify this, we evaluate a variant that excludes information gain and instead relies solely on traditional reward signals (
w/o Info-Gain
). The resulting performance degradation confirms the value of incorporating differential Q-value analysis, which enables fine-grained differentiation of reasoning quality. The gap between this variant and the full Legal
\Delta
model underscores the effectiveness of our reward enhancement in capturing the subtle characteristics of high-quality legal reasoning.

5.3
The Effectiveness of Information-Gain Enhanced Reward Modeling

In this section, we analyze the effectiveness of the information gain module during training through two perspectives: (1) its influence on model confidence via logits analysis, and (2) its impact on training efficiency, measured by batch reward variance.

In deep learning, logits often reflect the model’s confidence in its predictions. Higher logits indicate stronger confidence, resulting from more stable and consistent internal representations, which manifest as concentrated probability distributions. To evaluate confidence improvement, we compare the logits performance of two variants of Legal
\Delta
, depending on whether the information gain module is incorporated. As demonstrated in Figure
3
, in direct response scenarios (Figure
3(a)
), both models exhibit similar performance during early training stages. However, Legal
\Delta
with information gain module progressively develops higher confidence levels, with increasingly pronounced performance gaps in later stages. This improvement becomes dramatically more evident in Chain-of-Thought prompting scenarios (Figure
3(b)
), where Legal
\Delta
demonstrates substantial and consistent logits gains throughout training while the baseline variant maintains relatively low confidence levels.

The improved logit values confirm that our information gain mechanism successfully identifies high-quality reasoning patterns. This is further supported by the positive correlation between information gain and legal reward, as illustrated in Figure
4(a)
, where both information gain and reward values demonstrate consistent growth across training stages. This correlation indicates that samples with higher information gain consistently receive higher rewards, showing that information gain provides another way to evaluate the quality of generated responses during GRPO training.
Based on this positive correlation, our mechanism produces significant improvements in training efficiency and stability. As shown in Figure
4(b)
, Legal
\Delta
with information gain enhancement maintains more stable training dynamics with substantially reduced reward variance compared to the baseline variant, creating more consistent reward signals aligned with reasoning quality.

A.3
Legal Reward Construction

We design three distinct reward mechanisms based on the nature of expected outputs and evaluation requirements:

F1-Score Based Reward:
Provides balanced evaluation for multi-label scenarios:

R_{F1}=\frac{2\times\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(11)

This mechanism effectively penalizes both incomplete responses and excessive false positive predictions.

Distance-Based Reward:
Offers continuous evaluation for numerical predictions:

R_{dist}=\exp\left(-\frac{|\log(\text{pred})-\log(\text{true})|}{c}\right)

(12)

where
c
is a normalization constant set to 36 in our experiments. This design provides partial credit for predictions approximating the correct range.

Exact Match Reward:
Provides strict binary evaluation for categorical selections:

R_{exact}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if prediction matches ground truth}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(13)

These mechanisms are strategically applied:
R_{F1}
for criminal charge prediction and legal article identification,
R_{exact}
for financial amount calculation and multiple choice questions, and
R_{dist}
for sentencing prediction with wide value ranges.

A.4
Proof of Logits-Q Function Equivalence

Given dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(q^{i},a^{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the next-token prediction objective is:

\arg\max_{\pi}\sum_{i}\log\pi(a^{i}|q^{i})

(14)

while the offline inverse reinforcement learning objective is:

\arg\max_{Q}\sum_{i}\left[Q(q^{i},a^{i})-\alpha\log\sum_{a}e^{Q(q^{i},a)/\alpha}\right]

(15)

Since
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|q)=\text{softmax}(f_{\theta}(q,\cdot);\alpha)
, Eq. (
14
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\arg\max_{f}\sum_{i}\left[f(q^{i},a^{i})-\alpha\log\sum_{a^{\prime}}e^{f(q^{i},a^{\prime})/\alpha}\right]

(16)

Comparing Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
16
), we obtain:

Q(q,a)=f(q,a)=\text{logit}_{\theta}(a|q)

A.5
Case Study

In Table
5
, we compare the performance of Legal
\Delta
and zero-shot models in analyzing a cross-border civil procedure case involving a commercial dispute between Chinese and German companies. The zero-shot model exhibits several critical limitations that significantly impair its practical utility. The model not only conflates fundamental procedural distinctions between domestic and international litigants—particularly by erroneously equating the 15-day appeal period for Chinese parties with the 30-day requirement for foreign-related cases—but also fails to recognize valid exceptions in litigation representation rules. These shortcomings are further exacerbated by the model’s generation of verbose and unfocused legal citations, which obscures crucial jurisdictional differences.

Legal
\Delta
demonstrates markedly superior performance through its nuanced understanding of cross-border procedural law. The model accurately identifies and explains key asymmetries in legal treatment, including differentiated appeal periods and permissible representation forms. Its analytical approach represents a significant technical advancement, employing structured reasoning to strategically highlight legally determinative factors while maintaining clear and concise arguments. This sophisticated methodology stems from specialized training on party-specific rules and exceptions in international litigation. The model’s question-oriented output mechanism ensures direct relevance to the core legal issues, avoiding the digressive tendencies characteristic of zero-shot model responses. This demonstrates that Legal
\Delta
possesses stronger legal reasoning capabilities, enabling it to derive correct judgments and conclusions through key facts.
