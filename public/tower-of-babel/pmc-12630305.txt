Universal role of quinones in natural and artificial photosynthetic systems: overview from chemical properties to biological importance
Authors: Magdalena Łazicka, Maciej Garstka
Published: 2025

Chemical properties of quinones—redox reactions and their relationship with structural variability and surrounding environment

Quinones are a large group of small organic compounds assembled from diketone groups linked by π bonds into a benzoquinone ring. Carbonyl groups can occur in ortho or para positions, and the benzoquinone core may be part of polycyclic quinones composed of two or more hydrocarbon rings. Quinones are numerous and very diverse, both in terms of synthetic and naturally occurring molecules. The biologically most important quinones are para isomers belonging to three molecular classes: benzoquinones (BQ), naphthoquinones (NQ) and anthraquinones (AQ), containing one, two or three hydrocarbon rings, respectively. The diversity of functional groups attached to the basic quinone rings is highly variable and can significantly affect the properties of quinones.

Quinones redox reactions

Complete reduction of oxidized form of quinone (Q) to quinol (hydroquinone, H2Q) involves reduction by two electrons and uptake of two protons by single or multi-step reaction (Fig. 1). The course of these reactions depends on the characteristics of the environment surrounding quinones, mainly polarity and pH of solvent. In neutral aprotic media or at alkaline pH the reduction of para-quinones occurs by two successive one-electron steps to produce semiquinone radical anion (Q•−) and quinone dianion (Q2−). At neutral pH the intermediate quinone reduction can be a Q•−, as well as monoprotic neutral acid (HQ•) and, in smaller amounts, the diprotic cation (H2Q2+) formed by a two-electron or one-proton two-electron reaction, respectively. The reduction of Q by proton-coupled electron transfer (2e–/2H+) (PCET) or protonation of semiquinone radical may occur in an acidic environment. Generally, it can be stated that the fully reduced quinol, with its two hydroxyl groups (pKa ≈ 10) will tend to be fully protonated in protic solvents (H2Q), and fully deprotonated in aprotic solvents (Q2−) (Fig. 1) (Guin et al. 2011; Silverstein and Rosé 2022). When we know the potential and pH of electrolyte, we can infer the presence of specified forms of quinones. This relationship can be described by diagram of the dependence of potential on pH (Pourbaix diagram), showing the equilibrium between quinone and quinols, semiquinones and deprotonated forms (Gaudin and Aubry 2022).

Quinones functional groups

The functional groups on the quinone ring can change the properties of the molecules and consequently influence the potential of subsequent redox forms of quinones (Table S1). Electronegative functional groups e.g., chlorine increasing the potential of quinones, the one-electron reduction increases the E°(Q/Q•−) of dichloro-quinones fourfold in comparison to benzoquinone, + 470 mV vs. + 100 mV. But such a significant change is no longer seen when semiquinones are reduced to the hydroquinone form, E°(Q•−/H2Q), + 617 mV vs. + 473 mV. This is because the chloroquinone will be more electrophilic and easier to put electrons into Q to form Q•−. On the other hand, quinones with alkyl groups will be less electrophilic and one-electron reduction occurs at lower electrochemical potential, as in case of methyl-, dimethyl- and tetramethyl-benzoquinone where the potential values, E°(Q•−/H2Q), are estimated to + 23 mV, − 80 mV and − 240 mV, respectively (Song and Buettner 2010). A similar effect can be observed in the case of naphthoquinone (NQ), where presence of electron-donating alkyl groups decreases the midpoint potential of NQ derivatives, e.g. for 2,3-dimethyl-1,4-naphthoquinone, where Em is estimated to − 795 mV vs. − 610 mV for NQ. Contrary, the increase of potential to more positive values was noted in quinone derivatives containing the hydroxyl or chlorin groups (Coates et al. 2013).

Furthermore, the presence of specific groups may result in non-linear relationship between the one-electron and PCET reduction potentials (Huynh et al. 2016). Groups attached to quinone rings affect not only oxidoreductive properties but also the polarity of compounds, their dispersibility and ability to interact with other molecules. Among the modifications of key biological importance is the binding of the isoprenoid side chain to the quinone ring, which allows diffusion of molecules (prenylquinones) in the lipid bilayer. Thus, quinones structural and electrochemical diversity make them universal mediators of electron transfer between compounds with different redox potential and in different reaction environments.

Wide reactivity spectrum of quinones

Light reactions

The p-benzoquinone ring absorbs light in the range of 200–300 nm, but presence of various functional groups can shift the spectrum toward longer wavelengths (Görner 2003). Photoactivation of quinones usually leads to their reduction, but the course of these photochemical reactions depends on types of functional groups as well as on the reaction environment. Photoreactions of quinones are widely used in organic synthesis, e.g. reactions with aldehyde yielding acylated hydroquinone or reactions of intramolecular cyclization. In these reactions, most often carried out in an anhydrous environment, the transformation of the molecule takes place in the vicinity and with reduction of carbonyl groups of quinone (Ando and Suzuki 2018).

In aqueous solution, the triplet state (Q3*) formed after the conversion of the light-induced quinone singlet state (Q*), can undergo photoreduction in the presence of alcohols to the monoprotic neutral acid HQ• and finally into hydroquinone H2Q (Görner 2003, 2004). Photoactivation of benzoquinone can lead to water splitting by abstraction of hydrogen atom and formation of semiquinone radical (Q* + H2O → HQ• + OH•), but the mechanism of this reaction is unclear. Theoretical studies suggested, that in excited benzoquinone-H2O complex a charge-transfer state might exist, which enables formation of charge-separated state and subsequently transfer of proton from water to carbonyl group (Karsili et al. 2015).

Reactions with oxygen and oxygen radicals

The semiquinone radical (Q•−) can react with dioxygen to form superoxide (Q•− + O2 ↔ Q + O2•−). This reaction is reversible, and its equilibrium point is determined by ratio of quinone/semiquinone radical (E°(Q/Q•−) and oxygen/superoxide (E° (O2/O2•−) reduction potentials. The one-electron reduction potential for the O2/O2•− couple was estimated to about − 180 mV, therefore in the reaction of oxygen with quinones with lower potential E°(Q/Q•−) the formation of superoxide is favored. In the case of reaction with quinones that reveal the E°(Q/Q•−) greater than − 180 mV, the equilibrium point is shifted towards the formation of semiquinone radicals (Song and Buettner 2010). In protic aqueous solution the product of quinone reduction by superoxide (O2•−), the semiquinone radical (Q•−) undergoes protonation to give the hydroquinone (H2Q) as the main product of reaction (Zhu et al. 2020). Furthermore, this reaction may proceed in different manners depending on the hydrophobicity of the substitutes of quinones (Samoilova et al. 2011). The reduced quinones (H2Q) undergoes autoxidation, which reaction is pH-dependent and increases at higher pH. It reaction occurs by sequential steps, resulting first in the HQ− and then Q•−, which undergoes autoxidation, according to the equation Q•− + O2 + 2H+ → Q + H2O2 (Song and Buettner 2010).

Quinone coupled systems

A unique feature of quinones is the ability to form between their molecules a charge-transfer complex. A classic example of such complex is quinhydrone formed between an electron-rich hydroquinone and electron-deficient benzoquinone. Coupled molecules are stabilized by interaction between each ring and additionally by hydrogen bridges created between reduced and oxidized carbonyl groups of individual molecules (Kruk 1988; Payne et al. 2019). Such complexes may also be formed between molecules containing additional functional groups or between quinone rings present in one composed molecule (Anh and Williams 2012; Payne et al. 2019).

The complex consisting of porphyrin-type pigment (P) as an electron donor and quinone as an electron acceptor may carry out the photoinduced electron transfer (ET). The quinone acts as a quencher of excited pigments (P*), by formation of the coupled radical pair (P•+Q•−). Further charge separation reactions (P•+ + Q•−) enable the reduction of quinones in the form of semiquinone radical (Q•−). In the next step, it is possible transfer of another electron and attachment of protons to reduced molecule, which leads to formation of quinol (H2Q) (Mennenga et al. 2006; Yusa and Nagata 2017). This phenomenon is conditioned by differences in redox potentials between photo-excited dyes and quinones as well as efficiency of quinones as reversible electron relays. Photoreduction of quinones in the presence of dyes was observed in chlorophyll-porphyrin-quinone triads (Johnson et al. 1993), 2-porphyrin-2-quinone tetrads (Kurreck and Huber 1995) and in chlorins-quinone (Mennenga et al. 2006) or metallophthalocyanine-quinone systems (Yusa and Nagata 2017). It has been experimentally confirmed that porphyrins and quinones form coupled complexes (Mennenga et al. 2006), and, based on computational studies the mutual spatial geometry of molecules needed to ensure the occurrence of charge separation reactions was proposed (Karr et al. 2006; Kang et al. 2019). The construction of such self-organized or covalently linked porphyrin-quinone-type systems is modeled on the natural photosynthetic systems, in which pigments and quinones are bound to protein scaffold to form chlorophyll-protein (CP) complexes. Therefore, in both the artificial and natural photosynthetic systems the crucial role in initiation of ET plays the electronic coupling between the donors (dyes) and acceptors (quinones) entities (Nagata 2014; Karr et al. 2006).

Prenylquinones—most important small molecules in the cell bioenergetics

Natural quinone derivatives are an incredibly diverse group of compounds, estimated at more than two thousand molecular species found in all taxonomic groups (Thomson 1997). These compounds differ in the basic arrangement of hydrocarbon rings, substituents, and some quinone derivatives are made up of multiple ring arrangements. From a quantitative point of view, most quinones can be classified as secondary metabolites of bacteria, fungi, and especially of plants (Goel et al. 2020; Christiansen et al. 2021; Dahlem Junior et al. 2022). They play the role of pigments, antioxidants, proton/electron carriers and signaling molecules (Dulo et al. 2021; Bolton et al. 2018; Laohavisit et al. 2020). Furthermore, their importance in antibacterial, antifungal or antitumoral action has been demonstrated (Goel et al. 2020; Dahlem Junior et al. 2022). Natural quinone derivatives also differ significantly in their redox potential (E°) which varies from − 1.4 V to + 1.5 V vs. SHE (Kristensen et al. 2020).

Prenylquinones diversity

For the operation of the bioenergetics metabolism of the cell, the most relevant group of quinones are isoprenoid quinones (prenylquinones). They are composed of a polar quinone head group (the para benzoquinone or naphthoquinone) binding isoprenoid side chain. Polyprenyl chain is made from repeating 5-carbon units, in many cases containing a trans double bond in each unit, and with the length varying from 4 to 14 isoprene units. Such structure provides incorporation of polyprenyl chain into lipids bilayers and simultaneous interaction of quinone ring with aqueous environments or polar regions of proteins. This makes prenylquinones efficient redox transporters in the environment of biological membranes, especially as a PCET shuttle in respiratory and photosynthetic electron transport chain (PETC) (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018; Braasch-Turi et al. 2022).

Biological prenylquinones show large diversity, both in case of quinone ring modifications as well as in the lengths of polyprenyl tail. These structural as well as functional differences among organisms are especially evident in procaryotes (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Franza and Gaudu 2022). The quinone ring and the polyprenyl side chain are synthesized independently, and following prenyltransferase activity, condensed into a final product that can undergo further modifications (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018). The most important compounds from the prenylquinones group are ubiquinone (UQ), plastoquinone (PQ) and rhodoquinone (RQ), which are benzoquinone derivatives, and menaquinone (MK) and phylloquinone (vitamin K1, PhQ) containing a naphthoquinone ring (Table S1).

Prenylquinones in various types of photosynthesis

Menaquinone is found in bacteria and archaea where it acts as an electron carrier in procaryotic redox systems. In many anoxygenic photosynthetic systems MK is associated with photosynthetic reaction center (RC) complexes as well as acts as a membrane PCET transporter (Fischer et al. 2016). MK is an efficient electron shuttle under anaerobic conditions because of its low redox potential (E°, − 74 mV) but undergoes spontaneous, non-catalytic oxidation under aerobic environments (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018). The similarly is in the case of RQ (E°, − 63 mV), which is found in organisms living in low oxygen surroundings, among others in purple bacteria, some protists, and worms (Salinas et al. 2020). The next important prenylquinone containing naphthoquinone ring is phylloquinone, synthesized in organisms carrying out oxygenic photosynthesis and specifically bound in the reaction center of photosystem I. The structure of PhQ differs from MK only by replacing the polyprenyl side chain with a phytol residue (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018).

Ubiquinone with higher redox potential (+ 100 mV) is easier to reduce and more difficult to oxidize than MK. Therefore, UQ replaced MK with the emergence of oxygen as the ultimate electron acceptor and the origin of aerobic species in the course of evolution. UQ is a component of the electron transport chain in all types of organisms, acting as an electrons shuttle between individual respiratory complexes and ensuring the formation of a proton gradient in mitochondria. Furthermore, UQ is involved in reactions catalyzed by other oxidoreductases, e.g. glycerol-3′-phosphate (G3P) dehydrogenase (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018). UQ is also found in RCII-type reaction centers of purple bacteria (Cao et al. 2022).

An analogous role to that played by UQ in the respiratory chain, in systems of oxygenic photosynthesis is made by plastoquinone. PQ (E° around + 80 mV) is specifically found in photosynthetic membranes of cyanobacteria and as an evolutionary legacy in algae and plants. The main functional, dominant form of PQ contains 9-unit polyprenyl side chain (PQ), although homologous molecules with shorter side chain or varying numbers of methyl groups in the quinone ring have been detected in different plant species (Szymańska and Kruk 2018). Furthermore, a number of PQ derivatives have also been found in plants and cyanobacteria. Among these the non-enzymatically formed hydroxy derivative, plastoquinone-C (PQ-C) and its ester form, PQ-B. PQ-C contains the hydroxyl group in the polyprenyl side chain, which is acylated in PQ-B by saturated fatty acids (Dłużewska et al. 2015; Kruk and Szymańska 2021; Nowicka et al. 2021; Tanikawa et al. 2025). The hydroxyl residues of PQH2 can undergo acylation to form the acyl plastoquinols (APQ), a new class of derivatives found mainly in cyanobacteria. Similarly, the reduced form of PQ-B can be subject to additional acylation. The physiological role of these compounds is unclear, but they are representative of all oxygenic photosynthetic organisms (Ito et al. 2025; Tanikawa et al. 2025).

Biosynthesis pathways of plant prenylquinones and tocochromanols—connections and differences

Formation of quinone head groups

Prenylquinones biosynthesis is intensively investigated in all classes of organisms. The biosynthetic pathways, enzymes as well as differences between prokaryotes and eukaryotes have been well determined (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2016, 2018; Abby et al. 2020; Salinas et al. 2020). In plants the shikimate pathway, localized mainly in plastids and related to the synthesis of aromatic amino acids, supplies the precursors to formation of quinone head groups (Lynch 2022). The precursor of naphthoquinone ring of PhQ is a 1,4-dihydroxy-2-naphthoate (DHNA), derived from chorismate, whose formation is associated with enzymatic transformation of tyrosine or phenylalanine. Also formed by the transformation of chorismate is a 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (4HB) being a precursor of the benzene ring of UQ. The synthesis of the quinone group of plastoquinone is slightly different, whose precursor is homogentisic acid (HGA) formed from p-hydroxy-phenylpyruvate being a tyrosine derivative (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Martinis et al. 2011; Liu and Lu 2016) (Fig. 2).

Biosynthesis of polyprenyl side chains

In plants the precursors for polyprenyl side chain, the second component of prenylquinones, are formed in two separate metabolic pathways. In cytosol from acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA is formed mevalonate (MVA), the first metabolite of MVA pathway. As a result of a series of reactions taking place in peroxisomes, MVA undergoes transformations to isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP,C5) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and further after their condensation to farnesyl diphosphate. Next syntheses located in cytosol result in formation of geranylgeranyl-PP and further polyprenyl-PP. This component condenses in mitochondria with 4-hydroxybenzoic acid forming 2-methyl-6-solanyl-1,4-benzoquinol, which converts to UQ after further modifications of the benzene ring. Unlike UQ formation, the synthesis of precursors for polyprenyl side chain of PQ occurs in plastids through the 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway. The first reaction of this pathway is the synthesis of 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-phosphate (DOXP) from pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. The next intermediate product in the pathway is MEP, whose sequential transformations lead to the formation, similarly as in MVA pathway, common substrates in isoprene synthesis, IPP,C5 and DMAPP. Subsequent reactions result in the formation of geranylgeranyl-PP and further the solanesyl-PP, which is a donor of polyprenyl group for HGA. The final product, plastoquinone, is formed after methylation of benzoquinone ring (Martinis et al. 2011; Liu and Lu 2016; Gutbrod et al. 2019) (Fig. 2).

The naphthoquinone ring of PhQ binds the phytol, saturated isoprenoid which is produced by reduction of three double bonds in the geranylgeraniol. Attachment of the phytol side chain and formation of PhQ is catalyzed by prenyltransferase in reaction between phytyl-PP and the 1,4-dihydroxy-2-naphthoic acid. The phytol molecules incorporated into PhQ originate partly from molecules released during chlorophyll degradation and subsequently rephosphorylated (Gutbrod et al. 2019; Romer et al. 2024).

Antioxidant properties of prenylquinones and tocochromanols

In addition to the synthesis of PQ, metabolic pathways start from homogentisic acid and lead to the synthesis of tocopherols and tocotrienols, molecules containing chromanol ring with an isoprenoid side chain. Tocopherols are formed by the reaction between phytyl-PP and homogentisic acid, whereas in tocotrienols geranylgeranyl-PP is a side-chain donor for HGA. These reactions can result in the formation of four tocopherols with saturated side chains and four tocotrienols with unsaturated ones. The tocochromanol family also includes the plastochromanol-8 (PC-8), which is synthesized from reduced PQ by tocopherol cyclase, carrying out transformation of quinone ring of PQ-9 to chromanol one (Nowicka et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022; Romer et al. 2024; Gutbrod et al. 2019) (Fig. 2).

The main function of tocochromanols is the protection of chloroplast membrane lipids and proteins against the reactive oxygen species arising under photosynthetic reactions. Tocochromanols are efficient scavengers of singlet oxygen (1O2), superoxide (O2•−) and lipid radicals (L•, LO•, LOO•) (Nowicka et al. 2021). In addition to tocochromanols reduced plastoquinone (PQH2) also shows antioxidant properties by reacting with 1O2 or O2•− (Kruk and Trebst 2008; Borisova-Mubarakshina et al. 2018). Activities of these lipophilic quenchers lead to accumulation of many specific oxidation products, partly with antioxidant properties (Kruk and Szymańska 2021; Szymańska et al. 2014; Dłużewska et al. 2015). Antioxidant balance in chloroplast is maintained, among others, by the α-tocopherol redox cycle, where the first product of oxidation, α-tocopherol quinone is reduced to α-tocopherol quinol, followed by a dehydrogenation reaction to form trimethylbenzoquinone, and a subsequent cyclization reaction to reconstitute α-tocopherol (Piller et al. 2014). The full elucidation of the course of reactions involving PQH2, tocochromanols and their derivatives in response to abiotic stress, especially to high-light conditions, is still under intensive studies (Nowicka et al. 2021; Szymańska and Kruk 2010; Ferretti et al. 2018; Borisova-Mubarakshina et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2020).

The role of prenylquinones in photosynthetic systems

Photosynthetic electron transport chain (PETC)—functional and structural organization

The synthesis pathways, diversity, and properties of prenylquinones affecting the stability of chloroplast membranes have been covered in many reviews (cited in previous chapters). Also, quite a lot is known about the contribution of PQ and PhQ to the functioning of plant photosynthesis. However, the importance of quinones involvement in photochemical reactions and in the regulation of PETC are often discussed in passing. Therefore, in the following chapters we have tried to collect the newest but scattered information and present it in relation to the properties of PhQ and PQ.

Light-harvesting (LHC) and reaction center (RC) complexes

In photosynthesis sunlight is converted into electrochemical energy in a multi-step process occurring in hierarchically organized chlorophyll-protein (CP) complexes composed of trans-membrane proteins and pigments. The light energy is captured by light-harvesting complexes (LHC) and transferred as excitons to the reaction center (RC) complexes. Energy trapping and initiation of charge-separation reaction start in electronically coupled chlorophylls (Chls) which have the lowest excitation energy. In plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, i.e. in oxygenic organisms, the special Chls designated as the primary donor P680 and P700, are part of RC of Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI), respectively. The special bacteriochlorophylls forming P800, P840 or P870 are bound in RC of different anaerobic bacteria. RCs are also classified based on the type of terminal electron acceptor into Type I (Fe-S type, RCI) and Type II (quinone type, RCII) RCs. In both types, quinones play a key, albeit different role (Fischer et al. 2016). The PSI and PSII in oxygenic organisms belong to RCI and RCII types, respectively. Therefore, based on their examples, we will present the importance of quinones in charge-separation reactions inside RC complexes.

Structural and functional arrangements of photosynthetic complexes in thylakoid membranes

Plant PSI and PSII are assemblies of RCs and their respective light-harvesting complexes (LHCs), that form precisely organized supercomplexes (LHCII-PSII, LHCI-PSI) located in thylakoids, the inner membranes of chloroplasts (Dekker and Boekema 2005; Wei et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2021a). Thylakoids are arranged in a 3-dimensional (3D) network forming separated areas, an interior space (lumen), which is surrounded by the chloroplast stroma. The lateral surface of the lipid bilayer facing the lumen is referred to as the lumenal side (p-side) and the layer facing the stroma as the stroma side (n-side). Furthermore, thylakoids are organized into three distinct domains: stacked membranes, called grana, unstacked stroma lamellae and grana margins linking these regions. LHCII-PSII supercomplexes and trimeric forms of LHCII are concentrated in stacked grana, while LHCI-PSI are found in stroma lamellae. The orientation of CP complexes in the vertical and lateral planes of the membrane as well as their distribution in the individual thylakoid domains determines the course and regulation of light harvesting and electron transfer reactions (Ruban and Johnson 2015; Koochak et al. 2019; Johnson and Wientjes 2020; Wood and Johnson 2020). The integration of photosystems into a functional PETC is possible due to the plastoquinone molecules located in thylakoid membranes. The total amount of PQ in the thylakoids is referred to as the plastoquinone pool. The PQ-pool acts as an intramembrane PCET transporter between PSII, the main source of PQ photoreduction, and cytochrome b6f complex (cyt b6f). Subsequently, coupled with proton translocation, electrons are transferred from cyt b6f complex through the mobile Cu-protein, plastocyanin (Pc) to PSI and further to Fd and finally to NADP via ferredoxin reductase (FNR). This process is called linear electron transfer (LET) and enables production of NADPH and generation of protons motive force (pmf) driving ATP synthesis. The importance of plastoquinone is also apparent from the redox potential of the PQ/PQH2 pair estimated to be around + 117 mV (Malone et al. 2021; Tikhonov 2024; De Causmaecker et al. 2019). This value enables the PQ-pool to operate as an electron acceptor from PSII and simultaneously as an electron donor to PSI, combining the light reactions of both photosystems into a single photosynthetic chain (Govindjee et al. 2017) (Fig. 3).

Phylloquinone—electrons transfer cofactor in photosystem I

Structure and operation of PSI reaction center

In higher plants the LHCI-PSI supercomplex consists of core PSI monomer surrounded by four LHCI subunits arranged in a semi-ring (Wang et al. 2021a). The PSI monomer is composed of 12–13 polypeptides, where transmembrane PsaA/PsaB heterodimer is key subunit of RC complex. The electron transfer cofactors associated with PsaA/PsaB are organized into two pseudo-symmetric A and B branches (Brettel and Leibl 2001; Jordan et al. 2001). The special pair of chlorophylls a, P700 (PAPB corresponding to Chl1A, Chl1B) is located in the lumenal part of PsaA/PsaB heterodimer and connected to two branches that bind Chl2A, Chl3A (A0A) and Chl2B, Chl3B (A0B), respectively. Each branch binds low-potential phylloquinones, A1A and A1B, which connect both ET paths to the common acceptor, the Fe4S4 cluster, FX. The next Fe4S4 clusters, FA and FB are located in the PsaC polypeptide at stromal part of PSI, which allows the electrons to be transferred to the external stromal acceptor ferredoxin (Gorka et al. 2021) Electrons in the reaction center are replenished by transfer from the reduced Pc, which binds on the lumenal side to PsaA/PsaB and PSaF subunits (Caspy et al. 2021; Naschberger et al. 2022) (Fig. 4A).

As is currently suggested excitation of primary donor P700 on the femtosecond time scale caused the formation of a symmetric, tetrameric exciplex (Chl2APAPBChl2B)* mixed with two charge-separation states, P700+Chl2A- and P700+Chl2B-. The electron transfer to primary acceptors Chl3A and Chl3B in both branches, but in favor of the A-branch, creates P700+Chl3A- and P700+Chl3B- charge-separation states in picosecond time scale. The forward ET to secondary acceptors, low-potential phylloquinones (A1A, A1B), leads to the formation of semiquinone anion radicals (PhQ•−) and stabilizes the charge separation (P700+A1A-, P700+A1B-) in both branches (Cherepanov et al. 2021).

The role of phylloquinone in charge separation in PSI reaction center

The Em values of PhQ molecules in the A1 sites were estimated, dependent on calculation method and species, in the range from roughly − 630 mV to − 820 mV (Srinivasan and Golbeck 2009; Kawashima and Ishikita 2017; Makita and Hastings 2017; Santabarbara and Casazza 2019; Santabarbara et al. 2019; Cherepanov et al. 2024). Such low potentials are necessary for the sequential reduction of the low-potentials iron-sulfur clusters (E° ≈ − 630 to − 530 mV) (Santabarbara and Casazza 2019) and Fd (Em − 460 mV to − 300 mV) (Hanke and Mulo 2013; Mondal and Bruce 2018), that is, the maintenance of electron flow in PSI core complex (Brettel and Leibl 2001) (Fig. 4B). PhQ in PSI serves only as an intermediate in ET and is not normally fully reduced to the quinol form. The factors impacting the redox properties of A1 are related to: (i) the presence of water molecules and H-bond network in vicinity of leucine residues and PhQ, (ii) plane-to-plane π-stacking of tryptophan residue and PhQ, (iii) the H-bonds network connecting A1 neighboring amino acid (aa) residues with low-potential FX cluster (for details see Srinivasan and Golbeck 2009; Kawashima and Ishikita 2017; Makita and Hastings 2020; Agarwala et al. 2020)) (Fig. 4C). The A1 role in ET and importance of aa residues in maintaining quinones functionality have been investigated using mutants with disrupted PhQ synthesis, which enables introduction of non-natural quinones derivatives or PQ in A1 cavity. Results have shown that the orientation of carbonyl groups of incorporated quinone derivatives is similar to PhQ one, as well as their redox potential is decreased (Coates et al. 2013; Makita and Hastings 2017; Milanovsky et al. 2017; Agarwala et al. 2023). This indicates that the low A1 potential is achieved through interactions of quinones with the protein environment of their binding site.

Both PhQs bound in A1A and A1B sites are involved in FX reduction, but the oxidation kinetics of A1A is an order of magnitude slower than the A1B reaction, with lifetimes of ∼250 ns and ∼20 ns, respectively. Furthermore, the redox potential (E°) of the A1B-/A1B pair is more negative than that of A1A-/A1A and this difference was estimated to 40–100 mV (Santabarbara and Casazza 2019). The structure of both A1 binding sites reveals high symmetry, however, disrupted by two amino acid residues located near the FX cluster, the tryptophan from the PsaB subunit and its counterpart, glycine from the PsaA subunit. These subtle differences alter the distribution of hydrogen bonds and the ionization degree of aa residues, differentiating redox potential of PhQ and the electron flow kinetics in PSI (Srinivasan and Golbeck 2009; Santabarbara et al. 2019; Agarwala et al. 2020).

Plastoquinone – electrons transfer cofactor in photosystem II

Structure and operation of PSII reaction center

In higher plants, the most common form of LHCII-PSII is the C2S2M2 supercomplex consisting of a dimer of the PSII core complexes (C2), two monomeric CP26 and CP29 antennas per RC, and strongly (S) and moderately (M) bound trimeric LHCII (LHCII3) complexes (Kouřil et al. 2012; Su et al. 2017; Wei et al. 2016; Sheng et al. 2021). Furthermore, the LHCII-PSII can interact in thylakoid membranes forming different types of megacomplexes (Nosek et al. 2017; Shan et al. 2024). The monomers that form the C2 dimer are structurally and functionally identical and act as independent core complexes (Shevela et al. 2023). The PSII monomer key subunit, a heterodimer of D1 (PsbA) and D2 (PsbD) proteins, binds all the cofactors involved in initial photochemical reactions and charge separation. This subunit is referred to as the PSII reaction center. The D1/D2 heterodimer from both sides binds internal antennas, the CP47 (PsbB) and CP43 (PsbC) proteins. All these four membrane subunits are involved in binding three extrinsic proteins (PsbO, PsbP, PsbQ). These lumen-side located proteins together with aa residues of D1 and CP43 proteins stabilize the oxo-bridged tetra manganese calcium cluster (Mn4CaO5) which forms a complex spatial arrangement referred to as an open cube. Together with the surrounding proteins, the Mn4CaO5 cluster forms a unique environment, called the oxygen evolving complex (OEC), that enables water splitting and dioxygen release. In addition, the PSII core is comprised of at least 13 other small membrane intrinsic proteins among others, the two subunits of cyt b559 (Umena et al. 2011; Suga et al. 2015; Shen 2015; Shevela et al. 2023).

The cofactors involved in charge separation are located on D1/D2 heterodimer and include a Chl dimer (PD1/PD2), and two monomeric molecules each of Chl (ChlD1, ChlD2), pheophytin (PheoD1, PheoD2), plastoquinone (QA, QB) and tyrosine (YZ, YD). The cofactors are arranged symmetrically, but due to the mutual position of D1 and D2 proteins, plastoquinone QA is bound to D2 while QB to D1 (Fig. 5A). In PSII RC only one branch of electron transfer is active, excluding the PheoD2 and YD which probably only have a protective role of RC (Shevela et al. 2023) The light energy harvested by external LHC antennas is transferred to CP47 and CP43 (Croce and van Amerongen 2020) and then through by electronic coupled Chls located at the border of internal antennas and D1/D2 subunits to primary donor P680 (ChlD1, PD1/PD2, ChlD2) (Saito et al. 2023). Two charge-separation pathways sharing a common PheoD1 acceptor have been identified in PSII RC. A fast charge-separation pathway with ChlD1 as the primary electron donor forming ChlD1∙+PheoD1∙- charge-separation state and slow pathway where PD1/PD2 dimer is an primary electron donor (Shevela et al. 2023; Yoneda et al. 2022; Sirohiwal and Pantazis 2023; Nguyen et al. 2023) (Fig. 5B). The properties of the aa residues surrounding the cofactors determine these excitation asymmetries in RC PSII (Shevela et al. 2023; Sirohiwal et al. 2020).

Oxygen evolving complex—the PSII donor side

Both pathways lead to formation of PD1∙+PheoD1∙- state, where PD1∙+ cation radical revealed the high oxidizing potential (Em ~ + 1200 mV), which allows download the electron from the YZ tyrosine residue which potential Em(YZ∙/YZ) is estimated roughly to + 960 mV (Mandal et al. 2020) (Fig. 5B). YZ tyrosine mediates one-electron transfer from OEC to P680, but dioxygen formation by water splitting is a four-electron reaction. These reactions can be coupled because the oxidation of water is catalyzed by states, in which the accumulation of the next four oxidizing equivalents in the Mn4CaO5 cluster is linked to the next four charge separation reactions occurring in P680. The five-state kinetic model of oxygen evolution (S-cycle) assumes sequential oxidation of manganese ions and changes in geometric, electronic and spin structure of Mn4CaO5 cluster (Shevela et al. 2023). Transitions between S0 → S1 → S2 → S3 → S4 states are induced by four sequential flashes of light, leading to the transfer of one electron each from the Mn4CaO5 cluster to P680 through YZ tyrosine. The S0–S3 are semi-stable intermediate states, whereas S4 is transient state that precedes dioxygen release under the light-independent S4 → S0 transition. The use of advanced research techniques, including serial femtosecond X-ray crystallography and quantum mechanics (QM) computations, allows for the development of increasingly accurate models of S states of Mn4CaO5 cluster and its protein matrix. Currently the mechanisms for the attachment and deprotonation of water molecules, the formation of an oxygen molecule as well as durations of the particular reactions have been established (Kern et al. 2018; Bhowmick et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024). The Em values for individual S states fall within the range of + 730 mV to + 880 mV which allows for electrons transfer and its replenishment in P680 (Mandal et al. 2020). The OEC constitutes the donor side of the P680, whereas the roles of the acceptor side, i.e., transferring electrons from PheoD1 and moving them out of PSII, is performed by PQ molecules bound at the QA and QB sites.

The QA and QB plastoquinones—the PSII acceptor side

The QA-(NHI-HCO3−)-QB complex

The PSII acceptor side is a two-electron gate consisting of primary PQ permanently bound at the QA site and interchangeable secondary PQ at QB site. The PQ at the QA site accepts only one-electron at a time from PheoD1∙-, resulting in a formation of semiquinone radical (PQ•−). Therefore, the PQ in QB site is reduced in two consecutive reactions before released as a PQH2 to lipid bilayer (Shevela et al. 2023). The QA and QB plastoquinones form complex structure with non-heme iron (NHI), which is at an equal distance from both quinones. The Fe2+ ion is hexacoordinate by four Nε-atoms of histidyl ligands, two from each of D2 (His214, His268) and D1 (His215, His272) subunits, whereas the remaining two ligand positions are occupied by oxygen atoms of bicarbonate. This HCO3− molecule is stabilized by tyrosine residues from both subunits, Tyr244D2 and Tyr246D1. The QA and QB are linked with NIH through common histidyl ligands, His214D2 and His215D1 respectively, and the entire QA-(NHI-HCO3−)-QB complex is symmetrically located with respect to the central axis of RC PSII (Müh et al. 2012; Müh and Zouni 2013; Kato et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024) (Fig. 5C).

The QA is located inside the core of RC PSII, and the quinone ring is positioned by two hydrogen bonds, one with His214D2 and second with amide group Phe262D2 as well as by π- π stacking with the nearby Trp253D2 residue (Müh et al. 2012; Saito et al. 2024). These interactions result in stable PQ binding with an energy estimated to − 56.1 kcal/mol and preclude protonation (Hasegawa and Noguchi 2014). The QB site is connected with a cavity located inside the protein, which is filled with polar lipids. The phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG) are located on stromal, whereas the digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG) and monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) on the lumenal side of this interior cavity. Two or three channels connect the cavity with the lipid bilayer of thylakoids, Channel I and III targeted to the center of the membrane, and Channel II directed more toward the stroma side (Loll et al. 2007; Guskov et al. 2009; Umena et al. 2011; van Eerden et al. 2017). The QB quinone ring forms hydrogen bonds with His215D1, and is stabilized by conserved aa residues, the Ser264D1 and Phe265D1. Unlike QA, the QB surrounding consists of a number of protonatable aa residues and the polyprenyl tail of PQ is directed to channel II (Müh et al. 2012; van Eerden et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2024). Such structure of the QB binding site makes the quinone binding energies lower than in case of QA and is estimated to − 37.9 kcal/mol (Hasegawa and Noguchi 2014).

Mechanism of QA and QB reduction and exchange cavity

Reactions on the acceptor side of PSII RC can be divided into two stages, firstly the electron transfer from QA to QB and its reduction and thereafter PQH2/PQ exchange between QB site and PQ-pool. The rate constant of electron transfer from PheoD1∙- to QA occurs within 400 ps, and a key role in this reaction is played by Trp253D2, which is electronically coupled both with PheoD1 and QA (Saito et al. 2024). Formation of QA∙- leads to rotation of quinone ring and shift of NHI toward QA. Further electron transfer to QB, likely with NHI involvement, occurs within ~ 300 μs. This QB reduction causes changes in its position and returns QA to output state. The next reduction of QB∙- occurs more slowly (~ 700 μs) due to negative charge of QB∙- (Kern et al. 2018; Ibrahim et al. 2020; Shevela et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024). The driving force behind electrons transfer on the acceptor side of PSII is the difference in the redox potentials (Em) of successive cofactors, in the order between the of PheoD1∙-/PheoD1 (− 550 mV), QA∙-/QA (− 144 mV) and QB∙-/QB (+ 90 mV) (Fig. 5B).

Note also that maintaining a low redox potential for QA∙-/QA requires the binding to PSII both the Mn4CaO5 cluster and bicarbonate (Krieger and Weis 1992; Johnson et al. 1995; Kato et al. 2016; De Causmaecker et al. 2019; Kato and Noguchi 2022). On the other hand, the accumulation of QA∙- in the case of over-reduction of PQ-pool results in a weakening of HCO3− binding and its release from NIH as CO2 molecule. This reaction leads to an increase in the Em of QA∙-/QA couple by + 74 mV and formation of high-potential form of QA. The increased energy gap between QA∙- and PheoD1 prevents back-reaction routes and thus the possibility of singlet oxygen generation and Chl degradation. This is probably one of the photoprotective mechanisms linking PSII activity to CO2 concentration (Brinkert et al. 2016a; Shevela et al. 2020; Sugo and Ishikita 2022).

Complete QB reduction is a multi-step process that is linked to the S-cycle (Ibrahim et al. 2020) and putatively follows the following pattern. The QB∙- formed after the first flash of light is stable and does not undergo protonation until QA is reduced again by a second flash, which leads to the creation of QA∙-QB∙- pair. Subsequently the distal carbonyl group of QB∙- is protonated by Ser264D1 hydrogen bonded with His252D1. The resulting QBH∙ radical accepts electron from QA∙- transforming into the QBH-. In the next step the proton from the His215D1 protonated the proximal oxygen of quinone ring resulting in formation of unstable intermediate His-QBH2. The reprotonation of His215D1, which is possible with the involvement of bicarbonate or Ser268D1, facilitates the release of QBH2 from His residue and is related with structural rearrangements at QB site (Forsman and Eaton-Rye 2020; Kato and Noguchi 2022; Sugo et al. 2022; Kato et al. 2024). Furthermore, difference in redox potential values between QB/QBH2 (∼65 mV) and PQ/PQH2 (∼117 mV) indicate that PQ binds more strongly to the QB site than PQH2, which enables efficient release of plastoquinol into the PQ-pool (Causmaecker et al. 2019).

The dissociation of QBH2 and its replacement by PQ occurs with a time constant of about ∼490 ms (Kato et al. 2024), but the pathway for the release of plastoquinol from PSII is unclear. Studies using molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) have found different results. The single channel hypothesis assumes that PQH2/PQ exchange occurs via Channel II (Zobnina et al. 2017; Kulik et al. 2020), whereas the coarse grained MDS demonstrated the operation of three channels acting as both input and output channels (Eerden et al. 2017). The latter hypothesis assumes that PQ passes through the channels in any head and tail orientation, while adopting the correct orientation necessary for binding in QB occurs inside cavity. Furthermore, the PQ accumulates in exchange cavity as a plastoquinone reservoir (Eerden et al. 2017).

In addition to the stable QB site, crystallographic studies have revealed two other PQ binding sites, QC and QD. The binding energy of QC is weaker than QB and is calculated for − 30.1 kcal/mol. It is related to the fact that the quinone ring of QC interacts with lipids, but not with any aa residues. The polyprenyl tail of QC is located in Channel I, which may facilitate its diffusion from the cavity. QD is located more inward in close proximity to QC and is more stable because its quinone ring interacts with neighboring aa residues. (Guskov et al. 2009; Hasegawa and Noguchi 2014; Kamada et al. 2023). The role of these PQ binding sites in exchange cavity is still under debate. It is suggested that both sites may temporarily bind PQ before it is bound in the QB site or transported outside the exchange cavity (Kamada et al. 2023). Furthermore, it is hypothesized that QC and/or QD may be involved in establishing the redox potential and reduction kinetics of cyt b559 (Kruk and Strzalka 2001; Kaminskaya et al. 2007; Kaminskaya and Shuvalov 2013). Over-reduction of PQ-pool can induce the cyclic electrons flow inside PSII, i.e. direct backward of electrons from acceptor side to donor side bypassing P680. It seems that the QC plays a key role in this process transferring from QBH2 protons to lumen and electrons to OEC (Gates et al. 2023).

Plastoquinone pool (PQ-pool) in thylakoids lipids bilayer

Plastoquinone properties in lipids bilayer

The overall properties of PQ are hydrophobic, due not only to the polyprenyl side chain but also to the quinone ring binding two methyl groups (Braasch-Turi et al. 2022). Computational simulations of PQ in an environment mimicking the thylakoid membrane showed that the PQ quinone ring is positioned at the level of the glycerol linker of lipids, whereas the polyprenyl chain is located between acyl chains of lipids. PQH2 was similarly embedded in the membrane bilayer. Further analysis showed that PQ in membranes adopts preferred conformations that differ in the position and shape of the polyprenyl tail, as well as differ in the angle of position of the quinone ring relative to the side chain. The time-resolved simulation indicated that the PQ flips from one monolayer to another, and this process occurs at a much lower rate in the case of PQH2 (Eerden et al. 2015; Jong et al. 2015). These properties allow the PQ/PQH2 to diffuse in the horizontal and vertical planes of the lipid bilayer and become a necessary molecule enabling electrons transfer beyond the PSII and directed transport of protons to the thylakoid lumen (Havaux 2020).

Diversity of PQ-pool in thylakoids

The PQ-pool links the main linear electron transfer (LET) with other alternative pathways of electrons transfer, such as cyclic electron transfer around PSI (CET) or Plastid Terminal Oxidase (PTOX) reactions. Maintaining a balance between these pathways is dependent on the state of PQ-pool reduction (PQ/PQH2 ratio), which is at least partly controlled by diffusion rate of PQ between thylakoid complexes. However, the PQ lateral diffusion coefficient in thylakoid membranes is a significant lower compared to diffusion in artificial lipid liposomes (Kirchhoff 2014). This effect is due to the arrangement of membrane proteins, where roughly the 75% and 65% area of core grana and stroma lamellae, respectively, is occupied by photosynthetic complexes and the regions of non-bounded lipids are less than 30% of membrane surface (Koochak et al. 2019; Wood and Johnson 2020). Microscopic observations of grana membranes indicated that lipid areas are separated into small spaces by clusters of LHCII-PSII, LHCII trimers and cyt b6f complexes (Koochak et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2014). Thus, protein crowding may limit the long-distance diffusion of PQ, but increased ordering of proteins may also facilitate the short distance diffusion of small molecules. Such arrangement of complexes, computer models of grana and kinetics of inter-photosystems electron transfer suggest existence of functional protein/lipid microdomains (Kirchhoff 2014, 2018; Tietz et al. 2015; Höhner et al. 2020). As it has been proposed, close proximity between PSII and cyt b6f inside microdomains might enable PQ/PQH2 diffusion over short distances and efficient electron transfer (Johnson et al. 2014; Havaux 2020). The biochemical studies showed the presence of PQ also in the chloroplast envelope and plastoglobules as well as enabled the estimation of the amount of photochemically active and nonactive PQ-pools. Furthermore, in the thylakoid membranes the relatively constant amount of PQ was accumulated, whereas PQ abundance in envelope and plastoglobules were variable, usually larger than in thylakoids. Thus, these functional and structural analyses showed that only a minor part of the total amount of chloroplast PQ is directly involved in photochemical reactions (Havaux 2020; Ksas et al. 2018).

Plastoquinone as a PCET shuttle in thylakoids—the interaction with cytochrome b6f complex (PQ:Pc oxidoreductase)

Structure of cyt b6f

The photochemical reduction of mobile PQ takes place at the QB site of PSII, but the main site responsible for maintaining the redox balance of the PQ-pool in thylakoids is a cyt b6f complex, which catalyzes PQH2 oxidation coupled with the proton’s translocation from stroma to thylakoid lumen and carrying out a simultaneous reduction of plastocyanin. The cyt b6f appears as a dimer in which the core of each monomer is made up from four proteins, the two transmembrane, cytochrome b6 and subunit IV and two exposed to lumen, the cytochrome f (cyt f) and Rieske iron-sulphur protein (ISP). The four other single-helix transmembrane peptides (PetL, PetM, PetG and PetN) and single anchoring transmembrane helices of ISP and cyt f stabilize the structure of monomer (Fig. 6A). The cyt b6 binds on the lumen side of bilayer (p-side) the hem bp (Em − 150 mV), while on the stroma side (n-side), the hem bn (− 85 mV) and nearby the hem cn (+ 100 mV). The F2S2 cluster (+ 310 mV) of ISP is located near the polar phase of thylakoid bilayer when the hem f (+ 355 mV) is positioned outside of membrane in lumenal part of cyt f, where the binding and reducing site of Pc is also located (Fig. 6B). In addition to the groups involved in electron transfer, cyt b6f binds one molecule of chlorophyll a at subunit IV and 9-cis-β-carotene inserted between PetM and PetG. Establishment of a dimeric structure of cyt b6f leads to emergence of the internal large protein-free lipid space enabling diffusion of PQ/PQH2 molecules. Open to this area are located the two catalytic centers of cyt b6f complex, near the p-side the site of PQH2 oxidation (Qp, quinol oxidase) whereas on opposite n-side the site of PQ reduction (Qn, quinone reductase) (Cramer et al. 2011; Malone et al. 2021; Sarewicz et al. 2021; Proctor et al. 2022; Tikhonov 2024).

Mechanism of electron/proton transfer inside the cyt b6f—bifurcation model and Q-cycle

Such a complex structure of cyt b6f, three catalytic centers and the binding of low- and high-potential hemes makes it possible to divide the oxidation of PQH2 into two one-electron reactions and launch the so-called Q-cycle enabling the translocation of twice as many protons as the number of electrons supplied from the oxidation of quinol. The mechanism of electron bifurcation begins with the binding of PQH2 in Qp site where one of the two electrons from PQH2 is transferred into high-potential chain containing F2S2 and hem f whereas the other is directed to the low-potential (hemes bp, bn and cn) chain (Fig. 6C).

The Qp site is divided into two cavities, one directed to Fe2S2 center and another extended toward heme bp. First oxidation of quinol starts with the deprotonation of PQH2 where the proton is transferred to His128 residue coordinating the Fe2S2 cluster of ISP. The one-electron reduction of Fe2S2 is endoergic due to high redox-potential of the PQH2/PQ•− pair estimated to + 450 mV. But the occurrence of this reaction is possible by coupling with exoergic transfer of the second proton from semiquinone to Glu78 residue of cyt b6. Most likely these reactions are facilitated by structural changes within Qp site according to the following scheme: (i) the unstable PHQ•−–Fe2S2 transition state decays, (ii) the Fe2S2 reduced cluster, which is located on the mobile domain of ISP, moves rapidly from Qp site towards the cyt f, and (iii) PHQ•− radical moves to inside cavity towards heme bp and Glu78. Thereafter reduction of heme f by Fe2S2 is associated with proton release from His128 residue to thylakoid lumen. Further oxidation of cyt f by Pc enables transfer of one electron from PQH2 outside of cyt b6f complex towards the PSI (+ 480 mV). The electron from PQ•− might be transferred to heme bp according to potential difference (PQ•−/PQ ≈ − 280 mV) and H+ from Glu78 is transferred by proton channel to bulk phase of lumen (Tikhonov 2024; Sarewicz et al. 2017) (Fig. 6C).

The low-potential chain of PQH2 oxidation starts with reduction of heme bp and further the heme bn. This stage results in electron transfer in vertical plane of membrane and participates in generation of electric field component (Δψ). In the next reaction heme bn is oxidized by heme cn located inside Qn site on the stromal side of cyt b6f. In the Qn site the oxidized form of plastoquinone is bound, but spatial orientation of PQ allows it to simultaneously occupy only one of two Qn sites available in the cyt b6f dimer (Proctor et al. 2022). Concurrently oxidation of another PQH2 molecule occurs at the Qp site in the second, adjacent monomer. But the low-potential chain in this monomer is blocked due to unoccupied Qn site, what increases the probability of electron transfer between hemes bp in neighboring monomers. The binding of PQ in the first monomer promotes the reduction and protonation of heme cn, that increases the likelihood of reduction of heme bn by electron coming from PQH2 oxidation in the second monomer. As a result, the reduced pair bnred / cnred is formed in the first monomer what makes possible to reduce the bound PQ by protons-coupled electrons transfer (2e–/2H+) mechanism (Malone et al. 2021). Alternatively, the second electron may be delivered from PSI as a result of CET activity (Tikhonov 2024) (Fig. 6C). The protons to reduction of PQ are transferred by different pathways. The first proton is indirectly supplied by located nearby Qn site the Arg207 residue, which firstly protonates the reduced heme cn. Finally, the reduction of PQ by cnred is coupled with H+ transfer to quinone’s carbonyl group. The next proton from stroma side is delivered through a proton channel involving the highly conserved Glu29 and Asp35 residues of cyt b6 (Malone et al. 2021) (Fig. 6C). Analysis of cryo-EM structures of cyt b6f enabled to propose that PQ/PQH2 molecules can move between the Qn and Qp sites inside internal lipid cavity. This may facilitate turnover of the Q-cycle without an exchange of quinones with the external PQ-pool (Nawrocki et al. 2019; Malone et al. 2021). However, recently, it has been demonstrated that PQH2 diffuses from bulk lipid phase to Qp site by intraprotein channel by one-way traffic, and that the oxidized molecule returns back to the plastoquinone pool. Furthermore, chlorophyll phytyl tail occupies PQ-conducting channel and plays the regulatory role in restriction of PQH2 diffusion to Qp site (Sarewicz et al. 2023). But, regardless of the model proposed, the overall rate of PQ-pool turnover is predominantly determined by redox reactions at active sites of cyt b6f than by PQ/PQH2 diffusion rate in bulk lipid bilayer (Tikhonov 2024). Furthermore, the abundance of cyt b6f in thylakoid membranes relative to PSII and PSI is not the stoichiometrically equal but accounts roughly 36% and 57% of content of these photosystems. Concurrently, cyt b6f is rather uniformly distributed between stacked and unstacked regions of thylakoids (Höhner et al. 2020). These quantitative relationships make it possible to regulate the state of PQ-pool by cyt b6f and thereby control electrons transfer through the PETC (Svoboda et al. 2023; Johnson and Berry 2021).

Reduction pathways of thylakoids PQ-pool

Linear electron transfer (LET)

Oxidation of PQH2 by cyt b6f is possible when electron acceptors at the Qp site (Fe2S2 and heme bn) are in an oxidized state, and when His128 and Glu78 residues are available as proton acceptors. The ISP His128 residue can accept the proton from PQH2 when the Fe2S2 cluster is reduced, which is possible only above the pKa ~ 6.2, as was estimated for oxidized His-Fe2S2 cluster (Tikhonov 2014; Malone et al. 2021). Concurrently, the redox state of cofactors in catalytic centers of cyt b6f is directly dependent on the LET rate, i.e. capabilities of consumption of formed NADPH and ATP molecules by metabolic reactions, mainly by Calvin cycle (Degen and Johnson 2024) (Fig. 3). This means, that the redox equilibrium of the PQ-pool can be maintained in relative low light, where the LET, the proton gradient (ΔpH) formation and its discharge in the reaction of ATP synthesis by ATP-ase are well-balanced (Johnson and Berry 2021). At light intensities exceeding optimal conditions, excessive reduction of PSI and the possibility of it damage occurs. At the same time, the thylakoid lumen becomes acidified. Lowering the pH of lumen below 6.2 leads to protonation of ISP His128 residue and in consequence slowing the oxidation of PQH2 and reducing the rate of LET. As a result, the reduction degree of thylakoid PQ-pool increases, which can lead to over-reduction of QB site in PSII and block the electrons flow through this photosystem (Malone et al. 2021). Thus, the redox state of PQ-pool plays the central role in maintaining PETC performance under changing environmental conditions.

Cyclic electron transfer (CET)

Protection of PSI is ensured by returning electrons from Fd to the PQ-pool and further to cyt b6f and by Pc back to PSI, forming a cyclic electron transfer (CET) pathway. Under these conditions the proton gradient (ΔpH) and pmf-driving ATP production is generated without NADP reduction and LET is downregulated with simultaneous increase of ATP to NADPH ratio. In oxygenic photosynthesis two separate pathways participate in CET, one sensitive to antimycin A (inhibitor of Qn site), in which participate the Proton Gradient Regulation 5 (PGR5) protein (CET1) and the second insensitive to this antibiotic, route involving the NADH-dehydrogenase-like Type-1 (NDH-1) complex (CET2) (Nawrocki et al. 2019; Takahashi 2022) (Fig. 3).

PGR5/PGRL1 pathway (CET1)

Despite many evidences of the involvement of PGR5 in the course of CET and formation of ΔpH (Suorsa et al. 2013; Ogawa et al. 2023; Degen et al. 2024), the mechanism of CET1 operation is not clear. The PGR5 is a small, stroma-soluble protein that forms heterodimer with PGRL1 transmembrane protein, which protects them prior to degradation (Hertle et al. 2013; Ruhle et al. 2021). Recently many studies suggested that PGR5/PGRL1 does not directly mediate electron transfer from Fd to PQ-pool but performs a regulatory role in CET1. It is not excluded that CET1 can proceed in large structural arrangements containing complexes engaged to electron transfer from PSI to PQ. Such a structural composition is indicated by ascertainment of physical interactions between PGR5/PGRL1 and PSI (DalCorso et al. 2008), PGR5/PGRL1 and FNR (Mosebach et al. 2017), between PSI, FNR and cyt b6f (Iwai et al. 2010) as well as isolation of LHCI-PSI/cyt b6f supercomplexes (Steinbeck et al. 2018; Yadav et al. 2017). It is also suggested that PGR5 directly regulates the switching of the Q cycle into a CET-dependent mode, what allows the reduction of PQ in the Qn side of cyt b6f by electrons supplied from PSI (Buchert et al. 2020).

NDH-1 pathway (CET2)

The CET2-mediated reduction of PQ is much better defined structurally. The key component of this pathway is a protonmotive NADH dehydrogenase-like complex (NDH-1) which is ortholog of mitochondrial Complex I, but lacks the NADH binding domain (Zhang et al. 2023). In plants NDH-1 contains at least 29 subunits, eleven of which are encoded in chloroplast genome. Similarly to respiratory Complex I, the NDH-1 exhibits characteristic L-shaped structure, where seven transmembrane subunits form the membrane arm operating as a proton pump, while subunits located on the stroma side bind the three Fe4S4 clusters (N0, N1, N2) involved in electron transfer from reduced Fd to PQ. The ferredoxin-docking site in NDH-1 partially overlaps the structure of Fd-site in PsaC subunit of PSI and the first cluster N0 is located similarly as FB cluster in PSI (Zhang et al. 2020; Schuller et al. 2019). The reduction potential of N0 is estimated at roughly − 550 mV, whereas the N1, N2 potentials are designated to about − 230 mV, which allows preferential reduction of N1 and N2 clusters by electrons provided from Fd. It should be noted that isopotential properties of N1, N2 clusters indicate that the reduction of any of these clusters is not favored (Richardson et al. 2021).

Since the reduced Fd is a one electron donor at time and Fe4S4 clusters undergo one-electron reduction, for full PQ reduction are needed the sequential binding of two Fd. The PQ binding site in NDH-1 is located at the interface of transmembrane and hydrophilic subunits, but at a large distance of about 18 Å from the N2 cluster, preventing direct electron transfer (Laughlin et al. 2020). Thus, the reduction of PQ requires it to shift toward N2 and the recently proposed model links the N1, N2 reduction with structural changes. It assumes that the first electron supplied from Fd keeps in reduced state the N2 cluster, subsequently the second reduction induces the conformational changes in H subunit of NDH-1, unlocking PQ access to the terminal [4Fe–4S]. This makes possible fast successive PQ reduction limiting the duration of semiquinone radical (PQ•−) (Laughlin et al. 2020; Pan et al. 2020). The protons for reduction of PQ are delivered from the stroma side by aa residues nearby the N2 cluster. Concurrently the electron transfer from Fd to PQ by sequential reductions of [4Fe–4S] in hydrophilic subunits induces the four protons transfer from stroma to lumen side by transmembrane subunits (Saura and Kaila 2019). Microscopic studies have shown that the NDH-1 most often binds one or two LHCI-PSI, although more complex configurations have been observed (Kouřil et al. 2014; Yadav et al. 2017). Furthermore, the assembly of NDH-1 in thylakoids requires interaction with LHCI-PSI (Kato et al. 2018) and the non-canonical light-harvesting antenna of PSI, Lhca5 and Lhca6, play a critical role in formation of NDH-1/LHCI-PSI supercomplexes (Kouřil et al. 2014). Recently presented models have shown that NDH-1 is located between the two PSI-LHCI supercomplexes, wherein the typical Lhca2 antenna has been substituted by Lhca6 in one LHCI-PSI while in the other LHCI-PSI the Lhca4 has been replaced by Lhca5 (Otani et al. 2018; Su et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2022). That specific structure of NDH-1/(LHCI-PSI)2 supercomplexes and quantitative ratio of PSI to NDH-1 estimated to 1:100 has suggested the low abundance of such arrangements (Nawrocki et al. 2019). Likewise, despite the isolation of LHCI-PSI/cyt b6f supercomplexes (Steinbeck et al. 2018; Yadav et al. 2017) the functioning of hypothetical large complex containing LHCI-PSI/NDH-1/cyt b6f, as has been suggested by structural homology to mitochondrial respirasome (Zhang et al. 2023) is rather improbable (Nawrocki et al. 2019).

CET1, CET2 role in the course of photosynthesis

Although qualitative determination of the detailed mechanisms of CETs is still ahead of us, the role of both PGR5/PGRL1 and NDH-1 pathways in controlling the redox state of the PQ-pool and generation of pmf is undeniable. But the quantitative contribution of these pathways to CET is unequal with the predominant importance of CET1, as indicated studies with single and multiple mutants of Arabidopsis (Nakano et al. 2019; Degen et al. 2023). It has been presented that PGR5/PGRL1 alleviates the impact of fluctuations in light intensity and CO2 concentration on the photosynthetic processes in C3 plants (Degen et al. 2024; Suorsa et al. 2013), whereas the NDH-1 pathway substantially contributes to course of photosynthesis in C4 plants (Ogawa et al. 2023). The observed non-photochemical reduction of PQ under stress conditions is referred to NDH-1 activity and production of reducing equivalents by stromal metabolic pathways (Nellaepalli et al. 2012; Krysiak et al. 2024), but the mechanism of this phenomena is not fully understood (Fisher and Kramer 2014; Peltier et al. 2016). It has been stated that many abiotic stress factors such as high-light, drought and low or high temperature are linked to activity of both cycles (Yamori and Shikanai 2016; Nikkanen et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2020). Therefore, the interdependent role of CET1 and CET2 might be assumed in maintaining an optimal redox state of the PQ pool for the course of photosynthesis (Nawrocki et al. 2019).

Over-reduction of PQ-pool as a short-time regulator of light-harvesting of PSII

The LHCII-PSII supercomplex has a highly ordered structure consisting of the PSII core and light-harvesting antennas, six monomers (CP29, CP26, CP24) and four trimers (LHCII3) (Wei et al. 2016; Su et al. 2017). Moreover, the LHCII-PSII can loosely bind other LHCII3 complexes which allows to increase light harvesting efficiency by increasing the overall size of antennas (Kouřil et al. 2012; Ruban and Johnson 2015). However, the excess of excitation energy reaching PSII reaction centers can lead to their degradation. This situation occurs at high-light intensities or when the LET rate is reduced, and both are associated with the PQ-pool over-reduction. At the same time, it is the level of PQ reduction that triggers mechanisms to protect PSII including (i) thermal dissipation of excitation and (ii) disconnection of LHCII3 from reaction centers.

The photochemical quenching

The mechanism of harmless dissipation of excitation energy as heat (the energy-dependent non-photochemical quenching, qE) is one of the most intensively studied issues in photosynthetic research. The LHCII3, that is the most abundant complex in the thylakoids, was identified as the main site of this phenomenon and ΔpH formation was indicated as the triggering factor (Saccon et al. 2020; Ruban and Wilson 2021). Energy transfer between Chl-Chl or Chl-xanthophyll (most likely lutein) has been proposed as a molecular mechanism for Chl quenching, but the identity of the quencher is still under debate (Ruban and Saccon 2022). Furthermore, the effect of ΔpH on initiation of qE is complex and involves a number of occurrences and these relationships are not fully explained. One of these processes is the xanthophyll cycle involving reversible conversion of violaxanthin (Viol) to zeaxanthin (Zea). In darkness epoxidation of Zea to Viol takes place, whereas at high-light and low lumen pH the violaxanthin de-epoxidase is activated and the resulting Zea replaces Viol at LHCII3 (Barros and Kühlbrandt 2009; Demmig-Adams et al. 2020; Iwai et al. 2024). It has been proposed that this cycle is linked to qE through Zea's induction/stabilization of aggregated LHCII3 structures and/or by making conformation of LHCII more sensitive to ΔpH, both resulting in efficient excitation energy quenching (Ruban and Wilson 2021; Janik-Zabrotowicz and Gruszecki 2023). Also, the small transmembrane protein PsbS, which is a sensitive sensor of thylakoid lumen acidification, can be involved in the qE induction (Liguori et al. 2019; Iwai et al. 2024). The ΔpH-induced conformational changes of PsbS promote its binding to LHCII3 and formation of LHCII3 aggregates. Summing up, the synergistic action of Zea and PsbS as well as interactions with membrane lipids are necessary to initiate qE (Welc et al. 2021; Ruban and Wilson 2021).

State transitions

The next mechanism of PSII protection controlled by level of PQ reduction is a process of disconnecting LHCII3 complexes from PSII reaction centers and redirecting them to PSI. This mechanism called State Transitions (ST) begins when PSII is overexcited relative to PSI, which allows the maintenance of balance between PSII and PSI excitation, as well as regulation of LET and CET operation (Wood and Johnson 2020; Johnson and Wientjes 2020). Migration of antenna complexes occurs as a result of reversible phosphorylation of stromal N-terminus of LHCII3 proteins by the thylakoid-bound STN7 kinase and in consequence weakens their affinity to PSII and simultaneously enhances their ability to associate with LHCI-PSI complexes (Allen et al. 1981; Longoni and Goldschmidt-Clermont 2021; Wood and Johnson 2020; Wu et al. 2023). Since LHCII-PSII and LHCI-PSI are distributed in the stacked and unstacked regions of the thylakoids, respectively, the ST phenomenon is linked to dynamic changes of thylakoids framework (Johnson and Wientjes 2020; Hepworth et al. 2021). Activation of STN7 is dependent on interaction with cyt b6f, specifically with lumen-exposed Rieske iron-sulphur protein and transmembrane subunit IV, which involves the closeup of Qp site of cyt b6f to pair of cysteines of kinase. Binding at Qp site the PQH2 induces structural changes of kinase, formation of intermolecular disulfide bridges and activation of STN7 (Shapiguzov et al. 2016; Longoni and Goldschmidt-Clermont 2021). However, it remains unclear how these lumen-located interactions are moved to stroma-exposed kinase domain of STN7. Therefore, other mechanisms and sites of STN7 activation are also proposed (Wu et al. 2021; Riche et al. 2024; Ibrahim et al. 2025). Dephosphorylation by constitutively active phosphatase takes place when PSI is overexcited relative to PSII and PQ-pool is oxidized (Johnson and Wientjes 2020).

Oxidation pathways of thylakoids PQ-pool

Plastid terminal oxidase (PTOX) and chlororespiration pathway

Independent of the LET and CET pathways, short respiratory chain called chlororespiration can be distinguished in plant thylakoid membranes (Peltier and Cournac 2002). This pathway involves the electron transfer from NADPH/NADH to PQ-pool by NDH-1, followed by oxidation of PQH2 in reaction catalyzed by plastid terminal oxidase (PTOX), which is a homolog of plant mitochondrial alternative oxidase (Fig. 3). PTOX is a non-heme di-iron enzyme reducing molecular oxygen to water with the oxidation of two molecules of PQH2, according to the reaction 2PQH2 + O2 → 2PQ + 2H2O. This nucleus-encoded enzyme is composed of six α-helices, of which four form catalytic center that bind iron ions and an oxygen molecule whereas two α-helices can bind to the stromal side of thylakoids allowing the attachment of PQH2 molecules (Nawrocki et al. 2015). Sequential attachment of two PQH2 molecules is required for full O2 reduction, therefore, in the event of a limitation of PQH2 availability, the formation of radical oxygen species (ROS) by PTOX is possible (Yu et al. 2014; Krieger-Liszkay and Feilke 2015; Messant et al. 2024). It seems that primarily PTOX plays the important protective role against over-reduction of PQ-pool under abiotic stress as a high-light, drought or salt-stress (Nawrocki et al. 2015; Stepien and Johnson 2018; Messant et al. 2024). As suggested the PTOX in its inactive aggregated form is localized in stroma, and its activation involving association with thylakoid membranes is dependent on pmf formation (Bolte et al. 2020) and structural reorganization of thylakoid network (Stepien and Johnson 2018; Calzadilla et al. 2024). The proposed mechanism is as follows: (i) when the stroma is alkaline, i.e. PQ-pool is over-reduced, the PTOX may become associated with thylakoids in active form reacting with the PQH2, (ii) but when the stromal pH is neutral the PTOX remains in inactive form (Krieger-Liszkay and Feilke 2015; Bolte et al. 2020).

The PTOX also affects the oxidation state of PQ-pool in processes not directly related to photochemical reactions. This applies primarily to the first steps of carotenoid biosynthesis involve desaturation of phytoene and ζ-carotene leading to the formation of lycopene. These reactions are catalyzed by phytoene desaturase (PDS) and ζ-carotene desaturase (ZDZ) which use PQ as a terminal electron acceptor, thereby increasing the reduction of PQ-pool (Brausemann et al. 2017; Sandmann 2021). The efficient course of these reactions is determined by the reoxidation of PQ-pool by PTOX as has been shown examining the chloroplast’s biogenesis (Carol et al. 1999; Wu et al. 1999; Nawrocki et al. 2015; Johnson and Stepien 2016).

Studies also indicate that oxidized PQ-pool is necessary for the activity of protoporphyrinogen IX oxidase (PPX), the last common enzyme of heme and chlorophyll biosynthesis. In this case, PQ could act as an electron’s acceptor in the conversion of protoporphyrinogen to protoporphyrin, while PTOX activity could ensure the continuity of reaction (Brzezowski et al. 2019).

Moreover, in plants non-photochemical reduction of PQ-pool carried out by NDH-1 can generate pmf if electrons are transferred to oxygen by PTOX. In this way chlororespiration links the dark metabolic processes in stroma and overall cell with stable redox state of the PQ-pool (Johnson and Stepien 2016; Nawrocki et al. 2015). Basically, similar to chlororespiration in chloroplasts, takes place the electron transfer from NADPH to oxygen in membranes of non-green plastids, in etioplasts (etiorespiration) (Kambakam et al. 2016) and chromoplasts (chromorespiration) (Renato et al. 2015; Grabsztunowicz et al. 2019). The pmf generated during these processes appears to play an important role in the energetic metabolism of these organelles. It can be concluded that PTOX in mature chloroplast acts as a valve to protect the PQ-pool from over-reduction, whereas in darkness and in other plastids is the last enzyme of the respiratory pathways enabling the pmf formation.

PhQ and PQ dependent non-enzymatic generation and scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in thylakoids

The undesirable results of plant photosynthetic light reactions are transfer of energy or electrons to molecular oxygen, which causes generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The direct energy transfer from excited chlorophyll to oxygen, mainly in PSII reaction center, lead to formation of singlet oxygen, 1O2. The one electron reduction of O2 causes formation of superoxide anion radical O2∙-, which in further reactions can be reduced to hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, mainly in stroma by superoxide dismutases. This phenomenon of light-dependent O2 reduction discovered 75 years ago is named Mehler reaction (Foyer and Hanke 2022; Kozuleva 2022). Furthermore, in the presence of Fe ions H2O2 can be converted to highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (OH•) (Foyer and Hanke 2022). On the other hand, the H2O2 can be reduced to H2O in reaction of chloroplast ascorbate peroxidase or 2-Cys peroxiredoxins coupled with activity of other enzymes of ascorbate–glutathione (Asada–Halliwell–Foyer) cycle. The overall pathways of O2∙- conversion to H2O is named water-water cycle, because 4 electrons coming from oxidation of water in PSII is used to H2O2 formation and later its reduction to water (Foyer and Hanke 2022; Foyer and Kunert 2024). This cycle is one of valves against over-reduction of photosynthetic components and can be up to 30% of total electron transfer (Foyer and Hanke 2022).

ROS generation

The non-specific transfer of electrons to oxygen and generation of O2∙- can occur in a number of sites of PETC, especially when the primary or secondary electron acceptors are reduced or unavailable. This is related to the Em value for O2/O2∙- estimated within range of − 160 mV in water to − 550 mV in dimethylformamide (model of lipid membranes) (Kozuleva et al. 2020; Kozuleva 2022) which allows acceptance of electrons from cofactors with lower redox potential and located both inside and outside the thylakoid membrane. The rate of O2∙- formation is widely diverse (from 0.4 to 5 e−/s) and is highest at PSI and cyt b6f, middle at PQ-pool and lowest at PSII or Fd (Kozuleva 2022).

In PSII oxygen may be reduced by pheophytin, cyt b559 and at QA site. In the last case the O2∙- is formed by reaction with PQ•− because the Em value for QA/QA∙- in the presence of HCO3− is estimated to − 145 mV which is sufficient for electron transfer (Kozuleva 2022; Pospisil et al. 2006; Brinkert et al. 2016a). The electrons transfer from low-potential form of cyt b559 to molecular oxygen is preceded by a reduction of cytochrome by PQH2 molecule binding to non-canonical quinone-binding site in PSII core. Thus, cyt b559 could play the role of PQH2:O2 oxidoreductase producing the O2∙- inside the thylakoid membranes (Yadav et al. 2014; Khorobrykh and Tyystjarvi 2018). In cyt b6f complex the one-electron reduction of O2 probably occurs at Qp site, at lumenal side of membranes, as a result of the reaction with PQ•− (Kozuleva et al. 2020; Kozuleva 2022) which is an intermediate product of the PQH2 oxidation (Malone et al. 2021; Tikhonov 2024). At PSI, the formation of O2∙- are assigned to [4Fe–4S] clusters located at stroma part of complex as well as to A1 phylloquinone (Kruk et al. 2003; Kozuleva et al. 2021, 2020). In PSI core the two PhQs located in different branches at A1A and A1B sites differ in their PhQ/PhQ•−potential (Santabarbara and Casazza 2019), but their values are negative enough to reduce the O2 in the lipid phase of membrane as well (Kozuleva et al. 2020). It has been proposed that under high irradiance the photoreduction of O2 occurs primarily at A1A- due to its longer lifetime compared to A1B- and that the phylloquinone is the main site of Mehler reaction under these conditions (Kozuleva et al. 2021).

ROS scavenging

In thylakoid membranes the free plastoquinone (PQ-pool) may be present in oxidized (PQ), singly reduced (PQ•− or PQH•) and doubly reduced (PQH2) states. These properties explain the contribution of the PQ-pool to both generation and scavenging of ROS (Vetoshkina et al. 2017). The comproportionating reaction between both free PQ and PQH2 leads to formation of PQ•− molecule which can reduce the O2 and produce the O2•−, however with tenfold lower rate than in PSI. At higher pH the rate of O2 reduction by PQ•− increases, so it is likely that at high irradiance, when the thylakoid lumen is acidified, this reaction occurs on the stromal side of membranes (Vetoshkina et al. 2017; Kozuleva et al. 2020).

Much more important than the generation of O2•− by plastosemiquinone is its scavenging by PQH2 which takes place inside the thylakoid membranes and follows the scheme PQH2 + O2∙- → PQ•− + H2O2. The formation of hydrogen peroxide occurs productively according to the high difference in the redox potential of PQ•−/PQH2 (370 mV) and O2∙-/H2O2 (940 mV) (Borisova-Mubarakshina et al. 2018). The next important property of PQH2 is a quenching of singlet oxygen, 1O2, either by physical or chemical mechanisms (Kruk and Trebst 2008; Ferretti et al. 2018). Physical quenching occurs via the Dexter-type excitation energy transfer and further dissipation of the excitation energy as heat, on a similar basis to the interactions of carotenoids with 1O2 (Tamura and Ishikita 2020). Probably a more common occurrence is chemical quenching proceeds through multi-step PQH2 oxidation. It has been proposed that the oxidation of PQH2 by 1O2 forms hydroperoxyl radical (HO2•) and semiplastoquinone radical (PQH•), which after reacting with another 1O2 molecule is transformed into PQ. The resulting two HO2• molecules undergo dismutation to form H2O2, and the PQ can be reduced again at QB site in PSII. However, if the re-reduction of PQ occurs insufficiently fast, the PQ can react further with molecules of 1O2 to form sequential hydroxyplastoquinone forms (PQ(OH)–PQ(OH)3) (Ferretti et al. 2018; Kruk and Szymańska 2021).

The singlet oxygen formed during high-light stress reacts with PQH2 to form the hydroxyl derivative PQ-C, which can subsequently undergo acylation to form PQ-B. Both derivatives accumulated in small amounts in plant membranes under these conditions (Szymańska et al. 2014; Dłużewska et al. 2015; Kruk and Szymańska 2021). Simultaneously, under the high-light or salinity stresses the accumulation of PQH2 and an increase in the content of photochemicallly active PQ-pool owing to PQ transport from plastoglobules are observed (Szymańska and Kruk 2010; Ksas et al. 2018; Pilarska et al. 2023). Thus, it appears that the dynamic exchange of PQ/PQH2 between thylakoids and their storage sites enables efficient scavenging of ROS under stress conditions (Pralon et al. 2019; Havaux 2020).

Synthetic quinone derivatives (sQ) in photosynthesis research and in biophotovoltaic devices

The use of synthetic quinone derivatives has been and continues to be crucial in basic photosynthesis research but also is important in the design of biophotovoltaic and artificial photochemical systems. Therefore, below, we provided examples of their applications and probable mechanisms of action.

Quinone derivatives as electron acceptors in PETC

The quinone derivatives are commonly used as an artificial electron acceptor (AEA) in study of PSII activity by measurements of oxygen release or Chl fluorescence quenching. The essential criterion for the selection of AEA is that their redox potential is sufficiently higher to allow the uptake of electrons from the QB (Em + 90 mV) or PQ-pool (Em + 115 mV). Therefore, in the studies the compounds that are methyl, chlorine, bromine or phenyl derivatives of quinones with sufficiently high potential are being used. Here, we can mention frequently used 2,3-dimethyl-p-benzoquinone (DMBQ, Em + 174), 2,6-dichloro-p-benzoquinone (DCBQ, Em + 315 mV) or phenyl-p-benzoquinone (PPBQ, Em + 279–290 mV) (Wiwczar and Brudvig 2017; Beauzamy et al. 2023) (Table S1). The numerous functional measurements determining the electron transfer efficiency of PSII allowed to identify QB as the main binding site for AEA (Bukhov et al. 2003; Joly and Carpentier 2007; Shevela and Messinger 2012; Longatte et al. 2015). However, it has only now been possible to study structural relationships at the molecular level by X-ray crystallography. Such analyses of three quinone derivatives with different functional groups (2,5-dibromo-1,4-benzoquinone DBBQ, 2,6-dichloro-1,4-benzoquinone DCBQ and PPBQ), revealed that all are located at QB site in hydrogen bond length distance to His215D1, Ser264D1 and Phe265D1, similarly to PQ molecule. DBBQ and DCBQ, due to the presence of Br and Cl atoms in their molecules, can form additional halogen bonds, which affects their binding strength at the QB site. The PPBQ which forms only hydrogen bonds with nearby aa residues is bound weaker (Kamada et al. 2023). In isolated PSII complexes the oxygen-evolution activity is the highest in the presence of PPBQ in comparison to other AEA, which indicates reverse correlation between binding strength at QB site and PSII activity. This effect is probably dependent on the higher turnover efficiency of PPBQ (Shevela and Messinger 2012; Kamada et al. 2023). The sQ diffusing into the exchange cavity of PSII can also bind at a QD site distant from the QB site by 14 Å (Kamada et al. 2023). The direct electron transfer from QA∙- to exogenous quinones is unlikely due to isolation by aa residues forming a 10-Å sphere around QA. However, modification of the environment around QA, through substitution in PSII core subunits, makes it possible to bind DMBQ on the stroma side at a distance of less than 9 Å and electrons transfer from QA∙- (Fu et al. 2017).

The interaction of quinone derivatives with PQ-pool has been postulated, but never conclusively proven (Bukhov et al. 2003; Joly and Carpentier 2007). The site of PQH2 oxidation, Qp in cyt b6f, can be occupied by 2,5-Dibromo-6-isopropyl-3-methyl-1,4-benzoquinone (DBMIB), commonly used inhibitor of PETC (Roberts et al. 2004). However, DBMIB mechanism of action is complex and not fully clear. The DBMIB bound in Qp cavity directed to [2Fe–2S] cluster of ISP inhibits the ET, whereas the molecule located nearby the heme bp does not exhibit such properties. Furthermore, the inhibitory features of DBMIB depend on its redox state and protonation of aa residues of ISP. The DBMIB can also accept electrons from PSII and transfer them to a Pc or directly to PSI bypassing cyt b6f (Fitzpatrick et al. 2020; Vilyanen et al. 2024). Similarly, has not been determined the mechanism of DQ, which in oxidized or reduced form (DQH2) can be an electron acceptor or donor, respectively, for the PQ-pool (Garstka et al. 2004; Joly and Carpentier 2007).

Quinone derivatives as Chl quenching reagents

Quinone derivatives not only act as AEA, but also as quenchers of excited state of Chl. It has been demonstrated that sQ specifically quench the chlorophyll fluorescence in antenna of photosynthetic bacteria, cyanobacteria, and algae (Vasil'ev et al. 1998; Frigaard et al. 1999; Longatte et al. 2015; Baikie et al. 2023; Beauzamy et al. 2023) as well as in isolated thylakoids (Rajagopal et al. 2003; Bukhov et al. 2003) and LHCII complexes (Lambrev et al. 2011; Łazicka et al. 2022). The mechanisms of this phenomenon, which can be described as exogenous non-photochemical quenching, are poorly understood. It has been proposed that in supercomplexes composed of reaction center and light-harvesting complexes it is possible electron transfer from RC to peripheral Chls and subsequent reduction of the exogenous acceptor, DCBQ. This theory assumes the formation of a light-induced highly delocalized charge transfer state covering both RC and antenna chlorophylls (Baikie et al. 2023).

However, these assumptions do not apply to the quinone-induced Chl quenching observed in isolated LHCII complexes, which lack the special Chls arrangement necessary for formation of charge transfer state (Schlau-Cohen et al. 2009; Müh et al. 2010). The PPBQ used in this study is an effective quencher acting in proportion to the number of available quenching sites at LHCII, indicating a localized interaction between Chl and quinone (Lambrev et al. 2011; Łazicka et al. 2022). Furthermore, the voltammetric analyses indicated the formation of complexes between PPBQ and Chls in the ground and excited states (Łazicka et al. 2022). When combined these results with the data for the chlorine-quinone complexes (Mennenga et al. 2006; Razeghifard 2015), make it possible to propose the following quenching scheme: (i) the excited Chl* is quenched in a charge-transfer reaction, leading to formation of a coupled radical pair of the Chl cation and quinone anion (Chl•+Q•−), (ii) the charge-transfer state can be dissipated in a backward reaction or coupled radical pair can be split into Chl and quinone radicals (P•+ + Q•−), (iii) charge separation allows further transfer of electrons to other molecules. It is likely that these reactions take place in so-called terminal emitter domain of LHCII including a cluster of coupled Chl a of lowest excitation energy (Ostroumov et al. 2020; Łazicka et al. 2022). In this way, the plausible complex of Chl-Q may play a role similar to the primary donor in photosystems.

Quinone derivatives as an electron’s carriers in biophotovoltaic devices

Research concerning the construction of biophotovoltaic devices gathers information on the performance of nanostructured photochemical systems that can be used in the design of the next generation of photovoltaic systems with performance approaching that of photosynthetic complexes. Such photochemical systems, named also biohybrid devices, are created by combining biological photosensitive layers with solid electrochemical electrodes. Researchers are using a variety of strategies in the construction of such systems. As biological materials are used unicellular cyanobacteria or algae as well as isolated plant thylakoids and RC or LHC complexes. The substrates might be built of electrically conductive metal oxides or high electrical conductivity carbon nanomaterials and the structure of the electrodes can be monolayer or porous. Various methods of immobilization of biological material on electrode surfaces are also used (Zhang and Reisner 2020; Teodor and Bruce 2020; Ge-Zhang et al. 2023; Tschortner et al. 2019). However, the efficiency of the biophotovoltaic systems does not exceed a few percent. The limiting step is the transfer of electrons from the reaction centers to the electrode (Nawrocki et al. 2023). This problem attempts to be solved by improving either (i) direct electron transfer (DET), which involves connecting the electrode to the reaction center via a molecular wire, or (ii) mediated electron transfer (MET), which uses rapidly diffusing redox mediators with a suitable electrochemical potential, or (iii) a combination of both methods (Zhang and Reisner 2020; Teodor and Bruce 2020; Wang et al. 2021b). The sQ are not only AEA (Wiwczar and Brudvig 2017; Kamada et al. 2023) and photosynthetic energy quenchers (Łazicka et al. 2022; Lambrev et al. 2011) but also find applications in non-biological energy harvesting systems and rechargeable batteries (Son et al. 2016). Therefore, there is significant interest in their application in various types of biophotovoltaic devices.

An obvious way is to use the sQ as an AEA in systems that utilize PSII activity for photocurrent generation. Therefore, many types of sQ are applied as a MET both in systems based on isolated structures as well as those using suspensions of cyanobacteria or algae. It is assumed that the oxidized sQ are reduced at PSII and then re-oxidized at an electrode with an appropriately selected redox potential, which ensures the activation of electron flow. However, studies with thylakoids suspensions indicate that equally important feature is a chemical structure of sQ enabling interaction with PSII and efficient diffusion toward the electrode, e.g. in the case of DCBQ or p-BQ (Hasan et al. 2014; Pinhassi et al. 2015). The sQ are also used in the design of DET-based systems, where they are incorporated into linker between electrode and PSII complexes (Yehezkeli et al. 2013; Brinkert et al. 2016b), as for conductive polymer, poly(mercapto-p-benzoquinone), which can be electrons acceptor from QA∙- (Yehezkeli et al. 2012). Frequently a combination of electron transfer pathways is used, as for device using algal cells immobilized on conductive Os-Polymer (DET) in a solution containing BQ (MET) (Hasan et al. 2015).

Systematic studies on biophotovoltaic devices involving the suspension of unicellular green algae, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, and various types of sQ have revealed the complexity of these systems. Based on analyses of Chl fluorescence kinetics, it was found that the reduction of exogenous sQ is limited by endogenous electron flow, and that the effectiveness of sQ action depends on their concentration and illumination intensity (Longatte et al. 2015, 2016). Moreover, an additional factor affecting the efficiency of such systems is a sQ-induced quenching of excited state of Chl (Longatte et al. 2018). Simultaneous recording of fluorescence and photocurrent in algal suspensions allowed the ordering of sQ groups based on their oxidation properties, the stability of generated photocurrent and ability to Chl quenching (Beauzamy et al. 2020; Sayegh et al. 2021). Among the selected sQ were PPBQ and DCBQ, probably due to their affinity to the QB site (Kamada et al. 2023). The dual roles of sQ as Chl quenchers and electron acceptors have been successfully exploited in the design of biophotovoltaic devices involving immobilized LHCII3 and PPBQ as a MET reagent. Most likely, in this case, some PPBQ molecules form Chl*-Q complexes involved in quenching reactions, while others transfer the charge toward electrode which allows photocurrent to appear (Łazicka et al. 2022).

The sQ have also found applications in biophotovoltaic devices based on bacterial RCII type complexes, where ubiquinone, a RC terminal electrons acceptor, is replaced by its synthetic derivatives. In these systems, sQ act as a MET reagents, while the electrons in RCII are replenished by DET mechanism from electrode (Yaghoubi et al. 2015; Friebe et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2020). The naphthoquinone (NQ) derivatives are being applied in systems built on isolated thylakoids or PSI complexes. For example, in devices where thylakoids are immobilized in a structure of multi-walled carbon nanotubes, as the MET reagent, was selected the 1,2-naphthoquinone (Takeuchi et al. 2018). To produce a nanostructured complex that catalyzes the conversion of protons into dihydrogen, PhQ bound at the A1A / A1B sites of the PSI core was replaced with a phylloquinone derivative having a 15-carbon chain terminated by a thiol group. The resulting molecular linker was able to bind Pt or hydrogenase and directly transfer electrons from A1A / A1B to the bound molecules, enabling protons conversion (Gorka and Golbeck 2020). The examples given above show the variety of biophotovoltaic devices in which sQ play an important role in mediating the transfer of energy from the CP complexes to the electrodes. But it should be remembered that sQ are also successfully used in photosynthesis-inspired artificial systems (Son et al. 2016; Oyama et al. 2021).

Summary

Quinones are found in all taxonomic groups and their metabolic utility is due to redox properties with a wide range of potential (Thomson 1997; Kristensen et al. 2020). The special group of the isoprenoid quinones (prenylquinones) are able to diffuse in the environment of biological membranes and are crucial in the operation of the respiratory and photosynthetic chain (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Kawamukai 2018; van Eerden et al. 2015). The two quinones that are necessary for PETC action belong to different potential groups, the phylloquinone, PhQ, is classified as a low-potential quinone (LPQ) whereas the plastoquinone, PQ, is a high-potential quinone (HPQ). The LPQs, except for PhQ, are typical for anaerobic bacteria, whereas HPQs are present in aerobic organisms. From an evolutionary view, LPQs appeared earlier, about 3.5 billion years ago. Evolutionarily younger HPQs share a common ancestor with LPQs and arose about 3.3 billion years ago, before atmospheric oxygen concentrations began to rise (Elling et al. 2025). These hypotheses indicate that PhQ and PQ evolved simultaneously with other components of photosynthetic complexes, becoming a key redox element in the photosynthetic electron transfer (Oliver et al. 2023). With PhQ, it is possible to function high-potential PSI, while PQ has become a link between photosystems and a universal redox state regulator of PETC. It can be concluded that properties of PhQ and PQ are certainly one of the conditions for the existence of the universal Z-scheme of photosynthesis.
