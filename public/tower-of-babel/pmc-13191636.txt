Multi-omics analysis reveals flavor biosynthesis in Camellia seed oil: phenylpropanoid pathway activation and antioxidant modulation of lipoxygenase (LOX)
Authors: Zhihan Yan, Yanqiang Yao, Yue Li, Waqar Ahmed, Jin Zhao, Ya Liu, Jin Yu, Dongmei Yang, Wuping Yan, Huageng Yang, Yougen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCamellia is a widely cultivated woody oil plant in southern China that produces high-quality edible oil rich in unsaturated fatty acids and bioactive compounds. This study conducted a comprehensive multi-omics comparative analysis of oil quality (fatty acid composition, bioactive compounds, antioxidant activity, volatile flavor compounds, and metabolomic profiles) of camellia seed oils from Hainan, Jiangxi, and Guangxi. Results showed that Camellia hainanica (Hainan variety) had lower seed oil content (39.19%–46.24%) compared to Jiangxi (48.45%–52.99%) and Guangxi (47.16%–59.46%) varieties but significantly higher levels of polyphenols, flavonoids, squalene, carotenoids, and amino acids, resulting in a stronger antioxidant capacity. Fatty acid analysis revealed higher saturated fatty acid (SFA) levels in C. hainanica, while C. oleifera varieties from Jiangxi and Guangxi production areas were richer in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs). Volatile profiling identified 45 metabolites, with C. hainanica distinguished by unique terpenoids and a more complex aroma. Non-targeted metabolomics revealed two main mechanisms behind flavor differences: (1) tropical stress can cause overactivation of the phenylpropanoid pathway in Hainan oil-tea camellia, leading to the production of strong antioxidant phenolic compounds and phenylpropanoid matrices; (2) the LOX pathway facilitates the breakdown of polyunsaturated fatty acids and results in the accumulation of nonanal, which is the key process behind the characteristic “grass-like aroma” of Jiangxi tea seed oil. Additionally, the metabolism of this pathway may be heavily influenced by high antioxidant substrates produced endogenously.Keywords: Camellia seed oil, Antioxidant activity, Volatile organic compounds, Comparative metabolomics, Bioactive compounds, Geographical origin

Introduction

Genus Camellia, which belongs to the Theaceae family, is one of the world's four major woody oil tea crops. With a cultivation history of over two thousand years in the low mountainous and hilly regions of southern China, its oil is widely regarded as the “Oriental olive oil” due to its exceptional quality and notable health benefits (Li, Ma, et al., 2022; Li, Zhang, et al., 2022). The Camellia genus includes over 60 species, mainly featuring common oil camellia (C. oleifera), small-fruit oil camellia (C. meiocarpa), and Vietnam oil camellia (C. drupifera) (Wang et al., 2022). Li, Ma, et al. (2022) and Li, Zhang, et al. (2022) discovered that C. oleifera (CO) is primarily found across key producing regions in China, such as Jiangxi and Guangxi. Known for high oil yield and an unsaturated fatty acid content of over 80%, it leads the domestic bulk edible camellia oil market. In contrast, Hainan camellia (C. hainanica, HCO) is a unique endemic species cultivated only in the tropical climate of Hainan Province. It is abundant in bioactive compounds like polyphenols and squalene, exhibits strong antioxidant properties, and has a distinctive flavor with a mellow taste (Zheng et al., 2024). As a result, C. hainanica seed oil (HCOSs) commands a premium market price of $62 to $90 per kilogram, while standard ordinary Camellia oil is priced at $14 to $22 per kilogram (Shi et al., 2020).

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are crucial determinants of the sensory quality of camellia seed oil (COSO) (Feng et al., 2025). Xia et al. (2024) reported that HCOSs are high in heterocyclic compounds, aldehydes, and alcohols. Chen et al. (2024) showed a strong link between VOCs and both amino acids and fatty acids in pepper seeds, while Chen et al. (2024) emphasized the influence of lipid oxidation and free fatty acids on the formation of volatile compounds in grouper. Fatty acids are important precursors for VOCs and are mainly controlled by the LOX pathway. Enzymes in this pathway, such as LOX, produce hydroperoxides from fatty acids, which are then broken down by the HPL enzyme into volatile C6/C9 aldehydes (Tang et al., 2024). At the same time, amino acids function both as regulators in plant responses to environmental stress and as precursors for aroma compounds. Notably, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway serves as the central route for accumulating antioxidants and specific aromatic compounds (Sun et al., 2024).

Previous studies have established the basic chemical indices of various Camellia species (Li, Zhu, & Qin, 2024; Li, Zhu, Sun, et al., 2024). Moreover, Xia, Xiong, et al. (2023) used a combined metabolomics and network pharmacology approach to study Vietnamese tea trees from Hainan, Guangdong, and Guangxi provinces. Their research not only identified multiple bioactive compounds with potential health benefits but also uncovered significant region-specific metabolic differences. However, despite the large number of species within the Camellia genus, which have diverse genetic backgrounds, systematic reports explaining their interspecific biochemical quality differences, aroma-active components, and underlying metabolic mechanisms are still notably limited.

To address this important knowledge gap, the current study incorporated phenotypic traits, targeted bioactive analyses, volatilomics, and untargeted metabolomics to thoroughly assess the unique tropical HCO varieties alongside established Jiangxi and Guangxi varieties. The specific objectives were to: (1) Analyze phenotypic traits and measure key chemical components, including polyphenols, squalene, and fatty acids; (2) Identify the primary aroma-active VOCs; (3) Clarify the precursor-product relationships between key precursors (fatty acids and free amino acids) and distinctive aromas; (4) Understand the overall metabolic differences that contribute to these quality variations. Ultimately, discovering these species-specific and region-specific metabolic advantages will lay a crucial theoretical foundation and provide strong data support for future COSO processing and targeted quality enhancement research.

Materials and methods

Fruit sample collection

Fruit samples were collected from nine camellia varieties across three locations (Guangxi, Jiangxi, and Hainan) in China. On October 23, 2024, samples from C. oleifera varieties ‘Ganwu 2’ (GW2), ‘Ganxing 48’ (GX48), and ‘Ganshi 84’ (GS84) were collected at the Xinghuo Camellia Germplasm Resource Base in Yichun City (27°43′29.03″ N, 114°11′55.7″ E, altitude 132 m), Jiangxi Province. On October 25, 2024, samples from C. oleifera varieties ‘Cenruan 2’ (CR2), ‘Cenruan 3’ (CR3), and ‘Xianghua’ (XH) were obtained from the Camellia Germplasm Resource Nursery of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Forestry Research Institute in Nanning (22°55′3.7″ N, 108°21′19.13″ E, altitude 105 m), Guangxi Province. On October 28, 2024, samples from C. hainanica varieties ‘Haida 1’ (HD1), ‘Haida 2’ (HD2), and ‘Houchen 1’ (HC1) were collected at the Camellia Germplasm Resource Nursery in Yangjiang Town, Qionghai City (19°5′58.4″ N, 110°20′56.4″ E, altitude 40 m), Hainan Province.

Jiangxi and Guangxi have a subtropical monsoon climate with annual average temperatures from 16.3 to 25 °C, while Hainan features a tropical marine monsoon climate with higher annual averages between 22.5 and 25.6 °C. Meteorological data indicate significant differences in light conditions among the sites. Jiangxi and Guangxi experience moderate sunshine durations (approximately 1300–1737 h) and solar radiation of 4200–4600 MJ/m2. In contrast, Hainan, a typical tropical region with strong sunlight exposure, records over 2150 h of annual sunshine and solar radiation nearing 5000 MJ/m2 (https://www.weather.com.cn/). For each variety, four healthy plants were randomly selected as biological replicates, and a total of 50 complete, disease-free oil-tea camellia fruits were collected from 12 plants per variety. A total of 450 fruits were collected from 108 trees. Of these, ten fruits per variety were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for 4 min and stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomic analysis. The remaining fruits were kept for seed phenotyping and further research.

Oil sample preparation and yield analysis

COSO was extracted using the method described by Manto and colleagues (Manto et al., 2024). Briefly, seeds were dried in an oven at 80 °C for 36 h to a constant weight, ground into a fine powder, and 10 g of the powder was subjected to Soxhlet extraction with 150 mL of petroleum ether (boiling range: 30–60 °C) at 85 °C for 6 h. The oil content was calculated based on the weight of the extracted oil.

Determination of bioactive substances

The total polyphenol content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Sánchez-Rangel et al., 2013). Samples were extracted with 80% methanol using ultrasonic-assisted extraction, and quantification was based on a standard curve (Y = 0.0337 x − 0.0107, R2 = 0.9981) prepared with gallic acid. Similarly, the total flavonoid content was measured by the aluminum chloride colorimetric method. For this assay, samples were extracted with 60% ethanol in a water bath for 20 min, and a rutin standard curve (Y = 0.0051 x + 0.0067, R2 = 0.99) was used for Quantification (Kumar et al., 2021). Carotenoid content was determined by solvent extraction followed by spectrophotometric analysis (Jan et al., 2025). Briefly, samples were extracted with petroleum ether (boiling point range 30–60 °C), and the absorbance of the extract was measured at a specific wavelength (OD 470 nm). A blank control containing only the solvent was used for baseline correction. Squalene was quantified using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (Lu, 2024). Samples were dissolved in n-hexane for analysis. The GC–MS system was equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature 150 °C (held for 1 min), increased to 280 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, and finally held at 280 °C for 10 min. The injector and MS transfer line temperatures were set to 280 °C and 300 °C, respectively. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, with a split ratio of 20:1. Quantification was performed using an external standard curve prepared with an authentic squalene standard (Aresta et al., 2020).

Determination of amino acid composition in camellia oil

Amino acid composition was determined by liquid chromatography following derivatization (Jámbor & Molnár-Perl, 2009). Briefly, 1 g of tea oil was mixed with 10 mL of 10% sulfosalicylic acid and subjected to ultrasonic-assisted extraction for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was adjusted to pH 2.2, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and stored for further analysis. For derivatization, 10 μL of the prepared sample was mixed with 70 μL of borate buffer (pH 8.8) and 20 μL of AccQ-Tag reagent. The mixture was incubated at 60 °C for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid before injection. Chromatographic separation was performed according to the Chinese National Food Safety Standard (GB 5009.124–2016). Quantification was achieved using external standard curves, as detailed in Table S1. Determination of fatty acid composition

The determination of fatty acid composition and content follows the method described in GB5009.168–2016, “National Food Safety Standards-Determination of Fatty Acids in Food.” Take 80 μL of the sample, add 4 mL of n-hexane to dissolve it, and then add 200 μL of a 2 mol/L potassium hydroxide-methanol solution. Ultrasonicate for 5 min, then let it stand until it becomes clear. Add 1 g of sodium bisulfate, vortex mix for 10 s, then let it stand for 10 min. Take 1 mL of the supernatant for gas chromatography analysis. Each sample was analyzed three times. Analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890B—7000B gas chromatograph equipped with an HP-5MS elastic quartz capillary column (0.25 mm × 30 mm, 0.25 μm). The detection temperature is maintained at 250 °C, the column temperature rises at a rate of 6 °C/min from 60 °C to 270 °C, the carrier gas is high-purity helium (99.995%), the column pressure is 7.62 psi, the flow rate is 1.00 mL/min, the injection volume is 1.00 μL, and there is no split injection. Ion source (EI) temperature: 230 °C, quadrupole temperature: 150 °C, emission current: 34.60 μA, ionization voltage: 70 eV, multiplier voltage: 2000 V, mass range: 50–450 amu. Compounds were identified using the HPMSD chemical workstation and the NIST 2005 and Wiley 275 mass spectrometry databases. Their relative contents were calculated using the chromatographic peak area normalization method (Dou et al., 2024).

Detection of volatile organic compounds

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC/MS) (Thomas et al., 2021). For sample preparation, 1.5 g of each oil sample was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min to achieve volatile equilibrium. All measurements were performed in triplicate. Analysis was performed on a FlavourSpec SPME-GC/MS system (G.A.S. mbH, Dortmund, Germany) equipped with a CTC-PAL 3 autosampler (CTC Analytics AG, Zwingen, Switzerland). The autosampler injected 500 μL of headspace gas in splitless mode, with agitation at 500 rpm and a needle temperature of 85 °C. Separation was performed on an MXT-5 capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm × 1 μm; Restek) held at 60 °C, using high-purity nitrogen (≥99.999%) as the carrier gas. The flow rate was programmed as follows: 2.00 mL/min for 2 min, increased to 10.00 mL/min over 8 min, then to 100.00 mL/min over 10 min, and held for 10 min (total run time: 40 min). The injector temperature was 80 °C. The IMS was equipped with a tritium (3H) ionization source; the drift tube (53 mm) was held at 45 °C with an electric field of 500 V/cm. High-purity nitrogen drift gas flowed at 75 mL/min, and data were acquired in positive ion mode.

Identification of the key aroma-active compounds

The contribution of volatile compounds to the overall aroma of camellia oil was evaluated by calculating the relative odor activity value (ROAV) (Fang et al., 2022). Compounds can be identified as key aroma compounds if their volatiles ROAVs are ≥1. Here, ROAV = the relative concentration of volatile compounds/odor threshold in water (Xia, Xiong, et al., 2023). Relative concentration is the peak-area proportion of each volatile compound, calculated using the peak-area normalization method based on gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) data. Odor threshold values were referenced from the Handbook of Olfactory Thresholds: Compilation of Flavor Thresholds in Water and Other Media (Second Expanded Revised Edition).

Metabolite identification and quantification

In this study, non-targeted metabolomic analysis of the oil tea samples was conducted by Hangzhou LC-Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The procedure was as follows: mature seed samples of Jiangxi oil tea (GW2), Guangxi oil tea (CR2), and Hainan oil tea (HD1) were selected, with six biological replicates per variety. After collection, the samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a − 80 °C ultra-low-temperature refrigerator for storage. Approximately 100 mg of the frozen samples was weighed, and 500 μL of a pre-cooled methanol-acetonitrile-water extraction mixture (2:2:1, containing the internal standard L-2-Chloropropylalanine at 2 μg/mL) was added. The samples were ground and crushed using a high-throughput tissue grinder (60 Hz, 2 min) under an ice bath. After grinding, the samples were placed in a − 20 °C refrigerator to stand for 30 min, allowing for the complete extraction of metabolites. They were then centrifuged at 4 °C, 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and 300 μL of the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter membrane. The filtrate was transferred to a sample injection bottle for liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) detection. Detection was performed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC, Thermo Ultimate 3000) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (Q Exactive Focus, Thermo). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: the ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters) was used at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, an injection volume of 5 μL, mobile phase A of 0.1% formic acid water solution, mobile phase B of 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile. The gradient elution program 0–1 min 5% B, 1–9 min 5%–95% B, 9–12 min 95% B, 12–12.1 min 95%–5% B, and 12.1–15 min 5% B. The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: the ion source was an electrospray ionization source (ESI); detection was performed in both positive and negative ion modes, with spray voltages of 3.5 kV in positive ion mode and 3.2 kV in negative ion mode, and the ion transmission tube temperature was 320 °C. Raw data were processed with XCMS software to extract and align peaks, generating raw metabolite abundance profiles across all samples. The metaX software package was then used for data quality control and preprocessing. Low-quality peaks—defined as those missing in over 50% of quality control (QC) samples or over 80% of experimental samples—were eliminated. The remaining data were median-normalized, and residual missing values were imputed using the minimum imputation method.

Data processing and statistical analysis

All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between groups were evaluated for statistical significance using Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA in SPSS (v.26.0, IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), followed by the Tukey test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered significant. For multivariate data exploration, principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering heatmaps, and log2(fold change) analyses were performed using TBtools (version 0.66443). The KEGG data were cited from https://www.genome.jp/kegg/.

Results

Phenotypic traits and seed oil content of camellia

Phenotypic characteristics and seed oil content of C. oleifera varied significantly across regions, reflecting both genetic and environmental influences. Fruit skin color differed markedly: HCOs had yellowish-brown skins, whereas varieties from Jiangxi and Guangxi had green or red skins (Fig. 1A). Hainan fruits were also larger, with diameters ranging from 32.63 mm to 51.13 mm and single-fruit weights from 41.36 g to 73.59 g, exceeding those from Jiangxi and Guangxi. The “Xianghua camellia” variety yielded the highest seed percentage (59.46%), followed by HD1 (52.73%) (Table S2). Seed oil content across the three regions ranged from 39.19% to 52.99%. “Haida 2” (Hainan) had the lowest oil content (39.19%), whereas “Gan Shi 84” (Jiangxi) had the highest (52.99%). Overall, HCOs varieties had significantly lower oil content than those from Jiangxi and Guangxi (Fig. 1B).

Assessment of bioactive components

The concentrations of four key bioactive components (total polyphenols, total flavonoids, carotenoids, and squalene) were quantified in seeds of C. oleifera varieties collected from Hainan, Jiangxi, and Guangxi. Overall, Hainan varieties contained significantly higher levels of all four bioactive compounds than those from Jiangxi and Guangxi. Total polyphenol content varied widely across the nine varieties, ranging from 8.67 mg/g (“GS84”, Jiangxi) to 24.66 mg/g (“HD1”, Hainan). Varieties from Guangxi exhibited intermediate polyphenol levels, generally aligning with the range observed in Hainan and Jiangxi (Fig. 1C). Similarly, HCOs oil had the highest carotenoid content, led by “HD1” (3.17 mg/g). In contrast, the carotenoid content of the varieties from Guangxi and Jiangxi was lower, ranging from 1.52 mg/g to 1.88 mg/g (Fig. 1D). A notable difference was observed in squalene content. HCOs were exceptionally rich in squalene, with “HD1” containing the highest level at 75.41 mg/g. The Jiangxi variety “GW2” had the lowest (26.19 mg/g) (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, total flavonoid content ranged from 0.47 mg/g (“GS84”, Jiangxi) to 2.89 mg/g (“XH”, Guangxi), with the Hainan variety “HC1” also showing a high value of 2.88 mg/g (Fig. 1F).

Determination of amino acid concentration

Amino acids significantly influence the flavor profile and antioxidant properties of camellia oil. Analysis of nine samples from three regions identified 21 amino acids, with 14 exceeding 1 μg/g and selected for detailed comparison. Significant compositional differences were observed among the varieties (Table 1). HCOs oils exhibited the highest total amino acid content, ranging from 1528.67 to 2004.67 μg/g, while also maintaining a stable, high proportion of essential amino acids (181.67–226.01 μg/g). In contrast, oils from Guangxi and Jiangxi showed moderate total and essential amino acid levels. Amino acids were categorized by taste: umami, sweet, bitter, and flavorless. Umami amino acids were the most abundant class across all varieties, constituting over 65.53% of the total and being dominated by aspartic acid (ASP). Sweet and bitter amino acids, primarily represented by alanine (ALA) and isoleucine (ILE), were present at lower levels. Hainan oils contained significantly higher concentrations of key umami amino acids, aspartic acid and glutamic acid, than those from Jiangxi and Guangxi. Specifically, the Hainan variety HD1 showed the highest levels of ASP (1374.33 μg/g), ALA (135.33 ± 34.01 μg/g), and glycine (GLY, 93.67 ± 11.02 μg/g). The primary bitter amino acid, ILE, was also most abundant in HD1 (112.67 ± 4.93 μg/g) and lowest in GW2 (68.33 ± 7.64 μg/g). Jiangxi oils contained fewer sweet amino acids than Guangxi oils. Flavorless amino acids, represented solely by tyrosine (TYR), showed no significant differences among the varieties, with XH having the highest content (2.33 ± 1.53 μg/g) (Table 1).

Fatty acid composition of camellia seed oil

Camellia seed oil is distinguished by its high concentration of unsaturated fatty acids. To characterize this profile, we analyzed nine varieties from three regions using gas chromatography. A total of 16 fatty acids were detected, with seven major components (each exceeding 0.1% of the total content) comprising 97.82%–99.3% of the total fatty acid profile (Table 2). These seven major fatty acids included four saturated fatty acids (SFAs): palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), arachidic acid (C20:0), and behenic acid (C22:0), and three unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs): oleic acid (C18:1), linoleic acid (C18:2), and linolenic acid (C18:3). Unsaturated fatty acids constituted the vast majority in all varieties. Oleic acid (C18:1) was the predominant UFA, while palmitic acid (C16:0) was the most abundant SFA. Significant regional differences were observed. Hainan varieties exhibited a higher total saturated fatty acid content (13.71%–15.3%) than those from Jiangxi and Guangxi. The palmitic acid (C16:0) content was notably higher in Hainan, reaching a maximum of 12.18% in the HD1 variety. In contrast, the total unsaturated fatty acid content was consistently high in Guangxi and Jiangxi varieties (85.22%–88.35%), with oleic acid (C18:1) being the dominant component, as exemplified by variety XH (82.34%). Additionally, trace amounts of alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3, omega-3) were detected across samples, contributing to the diversity of the lipid profile.

Analysis of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are key determinants of oil quality and aroma. To profile the aroma compounds in C. oleifera seed oils, we analyzed nine varieties from Hainan, Jiangxi, and Guangxi using headspace solid phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (SPME-GC/MS). A total of 45 VOCs were identified across all samples (Fig. 2A), grouped into alkanes (18.18%), sesquiterpenes (13.64%), aldehydes (13.64%), acids (9.09%), alcohols (9.09%), alkenes (6.82%), naphthalenes (4.55%), and other minor classes. Significant variation was observed in both the number and concentration of VOCs among the varieties, with the Hainan variety HD1 containing the highest number (28) and others ranging from 20 to 23 (Fig. 2B). Multivariate analysis confirmed distinct regional profiles. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation among varieties, with the first two principal components (PC1: 35.20%, PC2: 16.97%) accounting for 52.17% of the total variance (Fig. 2C). A Venn diagram highlighted unique metabolites: Hainan varieties, particularly HD1 and HD2, possessed the most distinctive components (6 and 5, respectively), while seven aromatic metabolites were common to all regions, forming a shared aroma base (Fig. 2D). Heatmap analysis of VOCs revealed distinct compositional patterns across the cultivars (Fig. 2E). Alkanes, such as undecane, tridecane, and tetradecane, were ubiquitous and represented the most abundant VOC class. Six key aldehydes, including nonanal, decanal, and benzaldehyde, were identified as core flavor components in all regions. A defining characteristic of Hainan cultivars, particularly HD1 and HD2, was the exclusive presence of six terpene compounds. Four alcohols, including 2-ethyl-1-hexanol and phenylethyl alcohol, were also detected and likely contribute to flavor and oxidative stability. In contrast, Jiangxi and Guangxi cultivars were characterized by a greater abundance of olefins, naphthalenes, and nitrogen-containing compounds such as pyridines.

Classification of key aroma-active compounds and correlation analysis

To assess the impact of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) on the aroma profile of COSO, we calculated the Relative Odor Activity Value (ROAV) for key metabolites. Eight VOCs with ROAV values greater than 1 were identified as primary aroma contributors: octanal, benzylacetaldehyde, undecane, nonanal, phenylethyl alcohol, 1-nonanol, decanal, and benzothiazole (Table 3). Nonanal, detected in all nine varieties, was the most abundant core aromatic component, imparting a characteristic fatty, citrus, and green scent. Region-specific compounds defined unique aroma nuances: phenylethyl alcohol (Guangxi) contributed a sweet, rosy floral note; octanal (exclusive to CR3) provided a pronounced citrus fragrance; and 1-nonanol (GS84) offered grassy and citrus notes with woody undertones. Benzylacetaldehyde, found only in Hainan and Jiangxi oils, contributed nutty, roasted, and popcorn-like aromas. The overall profile of COSOs is therefore a complex blend dominated by fatty and green notes.

Non-targeted metabolomics reveals factors underlying quality differences in camellia

To elucidate the metabolic basis underlying regional differences in quality, in accordance with our previous physiological measurements, an untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on three widely cultivated and highly representative commercial camellia cultivars: HD1 (Hainan), GW2 (Jiangxi), and CR2 (Guangxi). A total of 2770 metabolites were detected and classified into 17 categories (Table S4, Fig. 3A). Lipids and lipid-like molecules were the most abundant class (26.47%), followed by organic heterocyclic compounds (22.50%), organic acids and their derivatives (13.76%), and phenylpropanoids and polyketides (12.06%). Multivariate analysis confirmed distinct metabolic profiles among the three varieties. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation, with the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounting for 59.34% of the total metabolic variance, indicating significant regional differences (Fig. 3B). A clustering heatmap visualized these distinct distribution patterns (Fig. 3C), and K-means analysis categorized the metabolic trends into six subtypes, each enriched in a specific cultivar (Fig. 3D). Specifically, HD1 was enriched in subtype 1, GW2 in subtype 6, and CR2 dominated subtype 2 (506 metabolites). A cluster heatmap of all samples further highlighted markedly different metabolic profiles. Comparative analysis identified 1095 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), which were classified into 16 chemical categories (Fig. 4A). Lipids and lipid-like molecules again constituted the most significant proportion (28.61%), followed by organic acids and their derivatives (21.62%) and phenylpropanoids and polyketides (14.53%). A Venn diagram (Weytung's analysis) of the DAMs provided insights into the metabolic divergence driven by geographical isolation (Fig. 4B). The largest number of unique DAMs (240) was observed between HD1 (Hainan) and GW2 (Jiangxi). At the same time, CR2 (Guangxi) and HD1 shared the most extensive set of metabolites (178), suggesting a closer metabolic relationship between the Hainan and Guangxi varieties than with the Jiangxi variety. A heatmap of the DAMs further detailed these differences, showing that lipids, phenylpropanoids, and polyketides were most abundant in HD1 and CR2, while organic acids and heterocyclic compounds were significantly upregulated in HD1 (Fig. 4C). Overall, these results demonstrate that HD1 from Hainan possesses the richest and most distinct metabolic profile, providing a biochemical basis for its unique quality characteristics.

Metabolic link between precursor substances and volatile compounds

To elucidate the biochemical origin of the unique aroma characteristics, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to link key nutritional precursors with characteristic volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Amino acid-derived aroma (Fig. 5A): A strong “precursor-product” relationship was observed. Phenylalanine (PHE) and aspartic acid (ASP) showed a highly significant positive correlation with phenylethanal and benzothiazole (p < 0.01). Because phenylalanine is a direct precursor biosynthesized via the shikimate pathway for phenolic substances, this correlation confirms that the abundance of specific amino acids in the Hainan variety is the material basis for its unique “nutty” and “roasted” flavors. In contrast, phenylethanol exhibited a different pattern, showing a negative correlation with these amino acids, indicating the existence of a competitive metabolic branch. Lipid-derived aroma (Fig. 5B): The lipid matrix significantly influences aroma formation. Unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), especially oleic acid (C18:1) and linoleic acid (C18:2), showed a significant positive correlation with nonylaldehyde (a key grass/fat odor), confirming its origin from lipid oxidation. Meanwhile, saturated fatty acids (SFAs) such as C18:0 and C22:0 showed a positive correlation with benzothiazole (p < 0.05), suggesting that saturated lipid matrices may be more conducive to the retention of these heterocyclic compounds than high-oleic acid matrices.

KEGG pathway enrichment and metabolic flux analysis

After mapping the differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) to the KEGG database, phenylalanine biosynthesis (map00910) and α-linolenic acid metabolism (map00592) were identified as key pathways influencing regional flavor differences. The results showed that within the shikimate pathway (Fig. 5C), the HD1 cultivar exhibited a significant buildup of shikimate and its derivative, shikimate 3-phosphate, which enhanced phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. As a result, benzeneacetaldehyde and benzothiazole levels were notably higher in HD1, while the mainland cultivar GW2 generally displayed lower metabolite levels across this pathway. Conversely, analysis of the α-linolenic acid pathway (Fig. 5D) revealed that HD1 had lower levels of α-linolenic acid, 9(S)-HPOT, 9(S)-HOT, 3,6-nonadienal, and (3Z)-hex-3-en-1-yl acetate.

This metabolic downregulation led to a reduced concentration of the terminal volatile product, nonanal, compared to the mainland cultivar. However, notably, HD1 accumulated a higher level of traumatic acid, a non-volatile branch product within the same pathway. As a well-known stress-responsive wound hormone in plants, the significant increase of traumatic acid in HD1 strongly indicates an adaptive metabolic shift: under tropical high-temperature stress, HCOs appear to preferentially direct limited lipid oxidation intermediates into the production of internal defense compounds rather than breaking them down into volatile aldehydes.

Discussion

Distinctive features of phenotypic physicochemical indices and nutrients

The fruit shape and weight of HCOs are significantly larger than those of the common camellia in Jiangxi and Guangxi production regions, due to both genetic and environmental factors. HCOs were bred from Camellia drupifera and retained the large fruit trait of the latter. However, the seed oil content of HCOs is usually lower than that of common camellia in Jiangxi and Guangxi primary production areas. This is mainly because of its relatively short breeding history and, more importantly, the strong sunlight in Hainan, which causes severe abiotic stress. Additionally, HCOSs are rich in bioactive compounds and antioxidants. We hypothesize that such intense light stress inhibits the flow of carbon toward primary lipid accumulation, thus reducing oil content (Miserere et al., 2023), while increasing the biosynthesis of protective secondary metabolites such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and squalene. These enriched bioactive compounds play important roles in both plant defense and oil quality (Al-Darmaki et al., 2012).

Free amino acids in camellia seeds are not only important nutritional indicators but also enhance flavor and serve as precursors to characteristic aroma compounds (Lan et al., 2025). HCOSs show remarkable nutritional benefits, with the highest contents of total and essential amino acids, and are rich in umami amino acids (e.g., aspartic acid, glutamic acid) and sweet amino acids (e.g., alanine, glycine). Studies have shown that umami and sweet amino acids improve the taste of oils, and amino acids act as precursors to aroma compounds (Yang et al., 2025). In contrast, COSOs from Guangxi and Jiangxi have relatively balanced amino acid compositions but lack prominent characteristic flavor amino acids (Yoon et al., 2024). The fatty acid composition of COSOs from the three production regions is dominated by monounsaturated oleic acid (C18:1), which provides health benefits such as cardiovascular protection and anti-inflammation, though there are notable regional differences in their nutritional focus and processing properties (Nocella et al., 2017).

COSOs from Guangxi and Jiangxi display higher levels of unsaturation and greater oleic acid content. Paula M M et al. found that high-oleic peanuts have higher stability and longer shelf life than regular peanuts, making them suitable for heat-resistant processing and long-term storage. HCOSs are significantly rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), especially linoleic acid and linolenic acid. As essential fatty acids that the human body cannot produce, they give HCOSs excellent nutritional value and antioxidant properties (Nayyar et al., 2024). In conclusion, the distinct amino acid profiles and abundant fatty acids in COSOs reveal notable regional differences in nutritional supplementation, flavor enhancement, and industrial processing applications.

Regional variations in flavor traits and their key biosynthetic pathways

Volatile flavor metabolites are key indicators for assessing the flavor and freshness of HCOSs. The aroma compounds identified in this study mainly included aldehydes, ketones, heterocyclic compounds, alcohols, esters, and acids, which aligns with the findings of Fanget al., (2022). Among them, HCOSs contain 14 unique flavor substances, are rich in characteristic sesquiterpenoids (e.g., cedrol, patchouli alcohol), and are enriched with heterocyclic compounds. This may be related to the high antioxidant activity of HCOSs. These compounds are simultaneously upregulated with the endogenous antioxidant defense network, collectively providing HCOSs with abundant antioxidant activity and nutritional components (Jiang et al., 2021). Zhao et al. (2018) confirmed that heterocyclic compounds can significantly enhance biological activity and antioxidant capacity, which matches the high antioxidant activity observed in HCOSs.

Seven volatile components were shared among the three production regions, with C6/C9 aldehydes serving as the main core compounds. These aldehydes are characteristic markers that distinguish COSOs from other oil crops and form the common basic flavor profile of all production areas. C6/C9 aldehydes (e.g., nonanal) mainly originate from the oxidative cleavage of unsaturated fatty acids (such as oleic acid and linoleic acid) through the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway. This lipid degradation process underpins the universal fresh and fatty aroma of COSOs (Peng et al., 2024). Further pathway localization offers a biochemical basis for regional flavor differences. Under the catalysis of the LOX pathway, polyunsaturated fatty acids are cleaved to produce volatile C6/C9 aldehydes. The study found a strong positive correlation between the LOX pathway and the final product, nonanal. In Jiangxi camellia, the LOX pathway was significantly activated, leading to extensive downstream cleavage of substrates like α-linolenic acid and a highly significant buildup of nonanal. This efficient lipid cleavage explains the “grassy aroma” of Jiangxi COSOs, aligning with Ning et al. (2024) finding that nonanal contributes to herbaceous aroma.

The initial substrates of the LOX pathway are polyunsaturated fatty acids like linoleic acid (C18:2) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3). In the LOX pathway, 9-lipoxygenase (9-LOX) are the core key enzymes catalyzing the production of nonanal. Therefore, linolenic acid in COSOs could be a key precursor for nonanal formation (Cao et al., 2025). Notably, traumatic acid and nonanal exhibit opposite trends in the LOX pathway, which may be closely linked to the very high levels of endogenous antioxidants (polyphenols, squalene) within its lipid matrix (Jabłońska-Trypuć et al., 2019). Additionally, we observed that some aldehydes are connected to the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which not only drives the accumulation of non-volatile bioactive antioxidants (e.g., polyphenols) but also functions as a precursor for benzene ring volatiles. Catalyzed by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), HCOSs produce abundant phenolic compounds through this pathway in response to tropical stress (Ma et al., 2025).

The conversion of phenylalanine leads to the significant build-up of benzaldehyde. This strong antioxidant network effectively manages the oxidative breakdown of polyunsaturated fatty acids, thereby avoiding the overproduction of off-flavor aliphatic aldehydes and giving HCOSs a distinct almond and nutty aroma (Li et al., 2023). This metabolic trait reflects an adaptive evolutionary strategy of HCOs to cope with tropical stresses such as intense ultraviolet radiation and high temperatures. The phenylpropanoid pathway is channeled to produce polyphenols and other compounds that provide crucial UV protection and antioxidant defense.

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis of camellia from different producing areas

Non-targeted metabolomics and multivariate analysis clarified the characteristic metabolic profiles of COSOs from the three main producing areas, revealing specific metabolite accumulation patterns driven by the combined effects of genetic background and particular ecological environments. This regional metabolic differentiation reflects the interaction between genetics and ecology. For example, several lipid-related differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) in Jiangxi camellia (GW2) were relatively downregulated, consistent with its lipid synthesis pattern in a subtropical environment. In contrast, HCOs were significantly enriched in organic acids and their derivatives, indicating that its central carbon metabolism (e.g., tricarboxylic acid cycle) may maintain high activity, thereby providing enough carbon skeletons to adapt to the tropical habitat and support the subsequent biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Jasinska et al., 2024).

Pathway analysis further confirmed that secondary metabolic pathways such as phenylpropanoids in HCOs exhibit a rapid turnover pattern characterized by “low precursors and high downstream volatile derivatives (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde).” This strongly suggests that HCOs adopt a distinct differential accumulation pattern in response to tropical high temperatures and strong UV stress, prioritizing the synthesis of antioxidants and protective substances. Therefore, the metabolic basis, shaped by the dual effects of genetics and environment, directly determines the industrial positioning of COSOs across different producing areas. The high levels of phenylpropanoids and polyketide secondary metabolites in ‘HD1’ and ‘CR2’ provide a solid foundation for developing high-antioxidant and characteristic-flavor COSOs along the southern China coast and in transition zones. In contrast, the primary lipid metabolic profile remains stable in high-latitude Jiangxi areas represented by ‘GW2,’ making them more suitable for producing large quantities of healthy, high-oleic edible oils.

Conclusions

This study used multi-omics methods to systematically compare tropical-endemic HCOSs with COSOS cultivated varieties in China. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) Nutritional and phenotypic differences: Jiangxi and Guangxi ordinary tea varieties contain higher levels of oil and unsaturated fatty acids, whereas the tropical HCO variety excels in the accumulation of bioactive substances, being rich in polyphenols, squalene, carotenoids, and umami- and sweet-tasting amino acids.This gives it excellent nutritional qualities and antioxidant activity. (2) Flavor characteristics and precursor association: By analyzing 45 volatile compounds and their relative odor activity values (ROAV), a significant precursor-product relationship was identified. Important precursors such as phenylalanine and certain fatty acids contribute to the synthesis of key aldehydes through shikimic acid and linolenic acid pathways. Additionally, HCO specifically enhances terpenoids, creating a unique natural, plant-like fresh aroma combined with roasted nut flavors. (3) Metabolic adaptation mechanism: Untargeted metabolomics reveals that tropical stress diverts HCO metabolism toward the phenylpropanoid pathway. This adaptation not only ensures a robust oxidative defense system in vivo but also directly contributes to its outstanding antioxidant capacity and the premium flavor of its oil. This study systematically evaluated the quality differences between HCOSs and COSOs from Jiangxi and Guangxi production regions. Future research will incorporate sensory analysis of COSOs from different varieties after processing and the dynamic changes in flavor during thermal processing (e.g., roasting). In summary, the species- and region-specific conclusions obtained in this study provide an important theoretical basis for the processing, utilization, quality improvement, and targeted breeding of excellent varieties of tea seed oil.
