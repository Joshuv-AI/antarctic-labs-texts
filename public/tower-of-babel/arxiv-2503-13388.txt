A mathematical model for a universal digital quantum computer with an application to the Grover-Rudolph algorithm
Antonio Falcó , Daniela Falcó–Pomares, Hermann G. Matthies

Abstract
In this work, we develop a novel mathematical framework for universal digital quantum computation using algebraic probability theory. We rigorously define quantum circuits as finite sequences of elementary quantum gates and establish their role in implementing unitary transformations. A key result demonstrates that every unitary matrix in U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) can be expressed as a product of elementary quantum gates, leading to the concept of a universal dictionary for quantum computation. We apply this framework to the construction of quantum circuits that encode probability distributions, focusing on the Grover-Rudolph algorithm. By leveraging controlled quantum gates and rotation matrices, we design a quantum circuit that approximates a given probability density function. Numerical simulations, conducted using Qiskit, confirm the theoretical predictions and validate the effectiveness of our approach. These results provide a rigorous foundation for quantum circuit synthesis within an algebraic probability framework and offer new insights into the encoding of probability distributions in quantum algorithms. Potential applications include quantum machine learning, circuit optimization, and experimental implementations on real quantum hardware.

Introduction.

Quantum computing promises revolutionary computational capabilities by harnessing the principles of quantum mechanics to tackle problems intractable for classical computers
[
13
,
2
]
. Early breakthroughs such as Shor’s prime factorization algorithm and Grover’s search algorithm exemplify this potential. However, further progress requires a more rigorous mathematical foundation for quantum computation. Current formulations, which typically rely on state-vector evolution in Hilbert space, often lack a seamless integration with classical probability theory. This gap hampers our ability to fully understand quantum algorithms in probabilistic terms and motivates the need for a unified framework.

To address these limitations, we introduce a novel mathematical definition of a quantum computer
grounded in
algebraic probability theory

[
5
,
9
,
11
]
.
This framework provides a unified probabilistic interpretation of quantum computation
that naturally extends classical probability concepts into the quantum domain.
Specifically, we define quantum computation as the action of the unitary group on
the manifold of rank-one density matrices, derived from solutions to the Liouville–von Neumann
differential equation.

This definition offers a geometric perspective on computation, capturing both quantum superposition
and probabilistic mixtures within a single formalism. Moreover, in our framework, and

elementary quantum gate
is rigorously defined as a fundamental unitary operation acting on a qubit.
Such a precise characterization establishes a standardized basis for comparing and classifying
quantum algorithms, laying the groundwork for a more principled analysis of quantum programs.

Our approach is general enough to encompass the standard gate-based circuit model of quantum computation. In the conventional circuit model, an algorithm is implemented as a finite sequence of discrete quantum gates acting on an initial state
[
10
,
8
]
. This gate-based paradigm provides an intuitive operational blueprint for designing quantum algorithms and underlies most current quantum hardware implementations. Notably, it is the foundation for today’s Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices. An alternative paradigm,
measurement-based quantum computing
(MBQC), takes a fundamentally different route by driving computation through adaptive measurements on highly entangled resource states
[
12
]
. MBQC decouples entanglement generation from the computation itself and is particularly promising for certain error-correction schemes and specialized architectures. While effective in their domains, both the circuit model and MBQC treat unitary evolution and measurement-based randomness in separate theoretical frameworks.

In contrast, our algebraic probability framework offers a unified and mathematically rigorous foundation that inherently incorporates both the coherent unitary dynamics of quantum circuits and the probabilistic nature of quantum measurements. By representing quantum states as density matrices and quantum operations as unitary actions on these states, our model seamlessly integrates state evolution with measurement outcomes within one probabilistic formalism. This unified perspective not only subsumes the conventional circuit model but also provides a natural setting for statistical analysis of quantum algorithms. Consequently, the framework enables more systematic comparisons between different quantum algorithms and supports the explicit construction of the unitary operators that implement them.

The key contribution of this work is a new theoretical framework for
universal digital quantum computing
that bridges quantum mechanics and classical probability theory. Our algebraic approach offers a novel lens through which to examine quantum algorithms, yielding both conceptual clarity and practical criteria for algorithm design and evaluation. To illustrate the power and generality of the framework, we derive a generalized version of the Grover–Rudolph algorithm within our model. This generalized algorithm, originally inspired by
[
3
]
, demonstrates how encoding a problem’s probability distribution into a unitary operator can be achieved explicitly in our formalism. The success of this construction not only extends a well-known quantum algorithm but also showcases the capability of our approach to analyze and generalize quantum algorithms in a principled way.

It is important to clarify the scope of our model within the landscape of quantum computing paradigms. Quantum computers can be broadly categorized as analog, digital, or fully error-corrected systems
[
4
]
. The
analog
approach exploits continuous physical interactions (e.g., adiabatic evolution or Hamiltonian simulations) to perform computations, whereas the
digital
approach implements algorithms via discrete gate operations on qubits. (Current NISQ devices fall under the digital category, albeit without full error correction.) Our work focuses exclusively on the gate-based digital paradigm, which encompasses present NISQ processors as well as future fault-tolerant machines. Accordingly, we refer to our model as a
digital quantum computer
to emphasize its foundation in discrete gate operations. This focus aligns our framework with the quantum computing architectures used in practice, while our theoretical advances provide deeper insight into the structure and analysis of such gate-based quantum algorithms.

Basic Notions of Algebraic Probability

This section introduces the fundamental notions and results in algebraic probability theory in a finite-dimensional setting.

Classical probability theory is traditionally formulated in terms of measure spaces. A classical probability space is defined as a triple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, where:

•

\Omega
is the sample space, representing all possible outcomes of a stochastic experiment.

•

\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{\Omega}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of events.

•

P:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
is a probability measure assigning probabilities to events.

A random variable
X:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\to(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
induces a probability measure
P_{X}
on
\mathbb{R}
, given by:

P_{X}(B)=P(X^{-1}(B)),\quad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

(2.1)

This measure is referred to as the
distribution
of
X
. In the multivariate case, the joint distribution of an
n
-tuple
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
of random variables is defined analogously.

On the other hand, quantum mechanics requires an extension of classical probability theory to incorporate non-commutative observables. In classical probability, observables are represented by real-valued random variables, which commute under multiplication, allowing them to be simultaneously measured with certainty. However, in quantum mechanics, observables correspond to self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, and these operators generally do not commute. This non-commutativity reflects the fundamental uncertainty in quantum measurements. To accommodate this structure, algebraic probability theory replaces classical probability measures with states in operator algebras, giving rise to the framework of algebraic probability spaces. This framework provides a mathematical setting where probability is defined over a non-commutative algebra rather than a classical sample space.

Definition 2.1
.

An
algebraic probability space
(or
quantum probability space
) is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
, where:

•

\mathcal{A}
is a unital associative
*
-algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.

•

\varphi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
state
, meaning it satisfies:

1.

Linearity:

\varphi(\lambda a+\mu b)=\lambda\varphi(a)+\mu\varphi(b)
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
.

2.

Positivity:

\varphi(a^{*}a)\geq 0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

3.

Normalization:

\varphi(\mathbb{I})=1
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the unit of
\mathcal{A}
.

In this setting, a
quantum random variable
corresponds to a self-adjoint element
X\in\mathcal{A}
. The state
\varphi
provides an expectation value, analogous to classical probability:

\mathbb{E}[X]=\varphi(X).

(2.2)

A classical probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
can be embedded into an algebraic probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
by defining:

•

\mathcal{A}=L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, the algebra of bounded measurable functions on
\Omega
.

•

The involution is pointwise complex conjugation:
f^{*}(\omega)=\overline{f(\omega)}
.

•

The state
\varphi
is given by expectation:
\varphi(f)=\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)dP(\omega)
.

Thus, classical probability emerges as a commutative subcase of the algebraic probability framework.

2.1
Finite-Dimensional Example: The Matrix Algebra
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given, and consider the finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space

\mathbb{H}_{N}:=\mathbb{C}^{N}.
Along this paper, vectors in
\mathbb{H}_{N}
are represented in Dirac notation. For example, a vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}
is written as

\ket{\Psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Psi_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

Its corresponding dual vector in
\mathbb{H}_{N}^{\star}
is identified with the conjugate transpose, namely

\ket{\Psi}^{\star}:=\bra{\Psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\Psi_{1}}&\cdots&\overline{\Psi_{N}}\end{bmatrix},

where
\overline{\Psi_{i}}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\Psi_{i}
. This notation allows the inner product of two vectors
\ket{\Psi},\ket{\Phi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}
to be expressed as

\bra{\Psi}\ket{\Phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\Psi_{i}}\Phi_{i}=\overline{\bra{\Phi}\ket{\Psi}},

with the associated norm given by

\|\Psi\|=\sqrt{\bra{\Psi}\ket{\Psi}}.

2.1.1
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
as a Tensor Product Space

In quantum mechanics, the outer product
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi}
is an element of the tensor product space
M_{N}(\mathbb{C}):=\mathbb{H}_{N}\widehat{\otimes}\mathbb{H}_{N}^{\star}
, which we denote by

\ket{\Psi}\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\bra{\Phi}:=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi}.

Explicitly,

\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Psi_{N}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\Phi_{1}}&\cdots&\overline{\Phi_{N}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{1}\overline{\Phi_{1}}&\cdots&\Psi_{1}\overline{\Phi_{N}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Psi_{N}\overline{\Phi_{1}}&\cdots&\Psi_{N}\overline{\Phi_{N}}\end{bmatrix}.

Recall that
the
trace
is a functional
\operatorname{tr}:\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}
given by:

\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf{A})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{jj},

(2.3)

for any matrix
\mathsf{A}=(a_{jk})\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. The trace can also be computed as:

\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf{A})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bra{e_{j}}\ket{\mathsf{A}e_{j}},

(2.4)

where
\{\ket{e_{1}},\dots,\ket{e_{N}}\}
is any orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{N}
.

The tensor product
\widehat{\otimes}
is bilinear and satisfies the trace property

\tr\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi}\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\Phi_{i}}\Psi_{i}=\bra{\Phi}\ket{\Psi}.

(2.5)

Note that rank-one matrices generate the matrix algebra
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{H}_{N}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{N}^{\star}
, that is,

\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{span}\Bigl\{\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi}:\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N},\;\bra{\Phi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}^{\star}\Bigr\}.

If
\{\ket{\Psi_{1}},\dots,\ket{\Psi_{N}}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{H}_{N}
, then
\{\ket{\Psi_{i}}\bra{\Psi_{j}}:1\leq i,j\leq N\}
forms a basis for
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Indeed,
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is a a unital associative
*
-algebra over
\mathbb{C}.

2.1.2
Hilbert-Schmidt Norm and the Spectral Theorem

Before introducing the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, we present the following notation. Given a matrix

\mathsf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1,1}&\cdots&A_{1,N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{N,1}&\cdots&A_{N,N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),

its conjugate transpose is defined by

\mathsf{A}^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{A_{1,1}}&\cdots&\overline{A_{N,1}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\overline{A_{1,N}}&\cdots&\overline{A_{N,N}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).

A matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is said to be
self-adjoint
(or
Hermitian
) if
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}^{\star}
.

The Hilbert-Schmidt norm on
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is defined as

\|\mathsf{A}\|_{HS}=\sqrt{\tr\bigl(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{A}\bigr)},

and, as shown in
[
6
]
,
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS})
is a tensor Hilbert space with inner product

(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})_{HS}=\tr\bigl(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{B}\bigr)\quad\text{for all }\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).

From (
2.5
) and the definition of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, it follows that for any vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}
,

\|\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\|_{HS}\;=\;\sqrt{\langle\Psi\mid\Psi\rangle}\;=\;\|\Psi\|.

Since
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS})
is a Hilbert space, the dual space

\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})^{\star}=\{\,\varpi:\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}\mid\varpi\text{ is complex linear}\,\}

is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
via the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. In particular, for every
\varpi\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})^{\star}
there exists a unique
\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\varpi(\mathsf{A})=(\rho,\mathsf{A})_{HS}\quad\text{for all }\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).

We thus identify the linear functional
\varpi
with the matrix
\rho\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
.

2.1.3
States on
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})

To characterize the states on
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, we use the following result (see Theorem 4.6.2 in
[
1
]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\varphi)
be an algebraic probability space. Then, there exists a unique matrix
\rho\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\varphi(A)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho A),\text{ for all }A\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C}).

(2.6)

The matrix
\rho,
called the
density matrix
of the state
\varphi,
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

\rho
is Hermitian (i.e.,
\rho=\rho^{\star}
);

(b)

All eigenvalues of
\rho
are nonnegative (i.e.,
\sigma(\rho)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
);

(c)

\tr(\rho)=1
.

We denote the set of all density matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
by

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})).

In order to decompose
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})),
we introduce the set of matrices with fixed rank. For
r=0,1,\dots,N
, let

\mathcal{M}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})):=\{\,\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\mid\mathrm{rank}\,\mathsf{A}=r\,\}.

An immediate consequence is that the set of rank-one matrices is given by

\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))=\Bigl\{\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\,\Big|\,\mathsf{A}=\lambda\,\ket{\Psi}\bra{\eta},\;\ket{\Psi},\ket{\eta}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}\setminus\{\ket{0_{N}}\},\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\Bigr\},

where
\ket{0_{N}}
denotes the zero vector in
\mathbb{H}_{N}
,
In particular, for any
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}\setminus\{\ket{0_{N}}\}
the rank-one matrix
\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
satisfies

\|\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\|_{HS}=\sqrt{\bra{\Psi}\ket{\Psi}}=\|\Psi\|.

Furthermore,
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
can be decomposed as the disjoint union

\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})=\bigcup_{r=0}^{N}\mathcal{M}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})).

Since the zero matrix
\mathsf{O}_{N}
is excluded as a state, we define, for each integer
r
with
1\leq r\leq N
, the subset of states of rank
r
by

\mathcal{S}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})):=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))\;\cap\;\mathcal{M}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})).

This construction partitions the set of all states according to their ranks, namely

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))=\bigcup_{r=1}^{N}\mathcal{S}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})).

We now introduce the unitary group on
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
:

\mathrm{U}(N):=\{\mathsf{U}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\mid\mathsf{U}^{\star}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}\mathsf{U}^{\star}=\mathsf{I}_{N}\},

where
\mathsf{I}_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix, that is, the matrix whose diagonal entries are all
1
and whose off-diagonal entries are all
0
.
For a Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{A}
, its
spectrum
is defined by

\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\{\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})\text{ is not invertible}\,\}.

For any
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})
, the corresponding eigenspace is

\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})=\{\,\ket{u}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}\mid\mathsf{A}\ket{u}=\lambda\ket{u}\,\}.

If we write

\mathsf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\ket{a_{1}}&\cdots&\ket{a_{N}}\end{bmatrix},

where
\ket{a_{i}}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}
for
1\leq i\leq N,
we denote the set of its column vectors by

\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{A}=\{\ket{a_{1}},\dots,\ket{a_{N}}\}.

By employing the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) (see
[
7
, Chapter 4]
), one may prove the following version of the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices.

Theorem 2.3

(Spectral Theorem)
.

Let
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be a Hermitian matrix with spectrum
\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\}
and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors
\{\ket{u_{1}},\ldots,\ket{u_{N}}\}
. Then, there exists a unitary matrix

\mathsf{U}=\bigl[\ket{u_{1}}\,\ket{u_{2}}\,\cdots\,\ket{u_{N}}\bigr]\in\mathrm{U}(N)

and a diagonal matrix
\mathsf{D}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{U}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{U}^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\ket{u_{i}}\bra{u_{i}}.

Furthermore, for
0\leq r\leq N
, the matrix
\mathsf{A}
has rank
r
if and only if

r=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})\setminus\{0\}}\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{P}_{\lambda}),

where

\mathsf{P}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{u}\bra{u}.

Thus,
\mathsf{A}
may be written as

\mathsf{A}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\lambda\,\mathsf{P}_{\lambda}.

(2.7)

A consequence of the above theorem is that
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
is a Hermitian matrix if and only if
\mathsf{A}=\lambda\,\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
for some unit vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{N}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.
Clearly,
\|A\|_{HS}=|\lambda|.

In particular, the extremal points of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
are given by

\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))=\Bigl\{\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))\mid\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi},\;\|\Psi\|=1\Bigr\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
are called
pure states
.

2.1.4
Random Variables, Events, and Laws

Next, we define a
random variable
in this setting.

Definition 2.4
.

A matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is called a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
if
\mathsf{A}
is Hermitian, i.e.,
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}^{\star}
.

Since
\mathsf{A}
is Hermitian, by Theorem
2.3
it has the spectral decomposition

\mathsf{A}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\lambda\,\mathsf{P}_{\lambda},

where

\mathsf{P}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{u}\bra{u}.

This decomposition allows us to define the
event
corresponding to the outcome
\mathsf{A}=x
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
. For a real number
x
, define the event
\{\mathsf{A}=x\}
as the random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
given by

\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-x\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{u}\bra{u},&\text{if }x\in\sigma(\mathsf{A}),\\[4.30554pt]
\mathsf{O}_{N},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Similarly, the events
\{\mathsf{A}<x\}
and
\{\mathsf{A}>x\}
may be defined.

Remark 2.6
.

The set of random variables
\{\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}:x\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})\}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
satisfy the orthogonality relation

\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}\,\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=y\}}=\delta_{x,y}\,\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}},

analogous to the indicator functions in classical probability.

Definition 2.7
.

The
law of the random variable

\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
is defined as the function

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=x):=(\rho,\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}})_{HS}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-x\mathsf{I}_{N})}\tr\bigl(\rho\,\ket{u}\bra{u}\bigr),&x\in\sigma(\mathsf{A}),\\[4.30554pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It follows that

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=\lambda)=1.

Remark 2.8
.

In
[
1
]
the law of a random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
is represented by the measure

\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A})=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\tr(\rho\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda\}})\delta_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\langle\rho,\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda\}}\rangle_{HS}\delta_{\lambda}.

Here
\delta_{\lambda}
is a Dirac delta considered as a positive and finite Radon measure.

To illustrate the concept of the law of a random variable in the algebraic probability framework, we now present two concrete examples. The first example considers a Bernoulli random variable, which provides an intuitive probabilistic interpretation of the outcomes of quantum measurement. The second example generalizes this notion to arbitrary random variables, demonstrating how the spectral decomposition of Hermitian matrices naturally extends classical probability distributions into the quantum domain.

2.1.5
Example (Bernoulli random variable)

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
, where

\mathsf{A}=\ket{u}\bra{u}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))

and

\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})),

with
\ket{\Psi}
being a unit vector in
\mathbb{H}_{N}
.
Since the spectrum of
\mathsf{A}
is
\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\{0,1\}
, the corresponding random variables are given by:

\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=1\}}=\frac{\ket{u}\bra{u}}{\|\ket{u}\|^{2}},\quad\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=0\}}=\mathsf{I}_{N}-\frac{\ket{u}\bra{u}}{\|\ket{u}\|^{2}}.

Here,
\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=1\}}
represents the projection onto the subspace spanned by
\ket{u}
, while
\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=0\}}
is the projection onto its orthogonal complement.
If
\theta_{\Psi,u}
denotes the angle between
\ket{\Psi}
and
\ket{u}
, then

\mathbb{P}_{\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}}(\mathsf{A}=1)=\tr\!\Bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\,\frac{\ket{u}\bra{u}}{\|\ket{u}\|^{2}}\Bigr)=\frac{|\bra{\Psi}\ket{u}|^{2}}{\|\ket{u}\|^{2}}=\cos^{2}\theta_{\Psi,u}=p,

and

\mathbb{P}_{\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}}(\mathsf{A}=0)=1-p=\sin^{2}\theta_{\Psi,u}.

2.1.6
Example (General random variable)

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
with spectral decomposition

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{U}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{U}^{\star}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})}\lambda\,\mathsf{P}_{\lambda},

where for each
\lambda
,

\mathsf{P}_{\lambda}=\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{u}\bra{u}.

Assume that
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
for some
1\leq r\leq N
. Since
\rho
is Hermitian, Theorem
2.3
guarantees that there exists
\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
and a diagonal matrix
\Sigma
such that

\rho=\mathsf{V}\Sigma\mathsf{V}^{\star}=\sum_{p\in\sigma(\rho)}p\,\mathsf{P}_{p},

with

\mathsf{P}_{p}=\sum_{\ket{\Psi}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{V}\cap\ker(\rho-p\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi},

and

\sum_{p\in\sigma(\rho)\setminus\{0\}}p=1.

Then, for each
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})
, the law of the random variable
(\mathsf{A},\rho)
is given by

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=\lambda)=\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\quad\sum_{p\in\sigma(\rho)}\quad\sum_{\ket{\Psi}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{V}\cap\ker(\rho-p\mathsf{I}_{N})}p\,|\bra{\Psi}\ket{u}|^{2}.

An important property of the law of a random variable in the algebraic probability framework is its invariance under unitary transformations. This invariance ensures that the probabilistic structure of a quantum system remains unchanged when evolving under a unitary transformation, a fundamental principle in quantum mechanics. The following theorem formalizes this property, demonstrating that the law of a random variable remains invariant under conjugation by unitary operators.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho).
The for each
\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
the law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
is the same as the law of the random variable
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\mathsf{V}\rho\mathsf{V}^{\star}).
Furthermore,
\sigma(\mathsf{A})=\sigma(\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star})
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=x)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{V}\rho\mathsf{V}^{\star}}(\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star}=x)
for all
x\in\sigma(\mathsf{A}).

Proof.

First at all, observe that for any
\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=x)=\langle\rho,\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}\rangle_{HS}=\langle\mathsf{V}\rho\mathsf{V}^{\star},\mathsf{V}\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}\mathsf{V}^{\star}\rangle_{HS}.

Thus, the theorem follows if we show that
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}\mathsf{V}^{\star}=\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star}=x\}}
for all
x\in\sigma(\mathsf{A}).
To prove it, let
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathsf{A})
and
\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N}).
Then
\mathsf{A}\ket{u}=\lambda\ket{u}
and
\mathsf{V}\ket{u}\in\ker(\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N}).
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=x\}}\mathsf{V}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{V}\left(\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{u}\bra{u}\right)\mathsf{V}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ket{u}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{A}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\mathsf{V}\ket{u}\bra{\mathsf{V}\ket{u}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ket{z}\in\mathrm{col}\,\mathsf{V}\mathsf{U}\cap\ker(\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{*}-\lambda\mathsf{I}_{N})}\ket{z}\bra{z}=\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{V}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{V}^{\star}=x\}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, with these fundamentals established, we next develop our unified quantum computing framework.

3
An Algebraic Probability Framework for Universal Digital Quantum Computers

In this section, we present a mathematical model for a universal digital quantum computer within the framework of algebraic probability. The model comprises two key components:

1.

An
n
-qubit Quantum Processing Unit (QPU):
This serves as the fundamental quantum computational resource.

2.

A dynamic state evolution mechanism:
Analogous to a Turing machine’s tape, here it is realized through the action of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(N)
on the space of quantum states.

Formally, a quantum computation is defined as a finite sequence of unitary operations:

\mathsf{U}\;=\;\mathsf{U}_{\ell}\,\mathsf{U}_{\ell-1}\,\cdots\,\mathsf{U}_{1}\;\in\;\mathrm{U}(N),

where each
\mathsf{U}_{k}
corresponds to a quantum gate. In practical implementations, these gates are typically
local
unitaries that act on a small number of qubits, usually one, two, or three. Within our algebraic probability framework, a quantum gate is formally defined as a unitary operation on a qubit that preserves the probabilistic structure of states. This definition provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for constructing and analyzing quantum circuits.
We conclude this section by establishing the formal properties of quantum gates within the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(N)
, thereby laying the groundwork for the design and classification of quantum algorithms.

3.1
A mathematical model of a Quantum Processor Unit

To define the model of a quantum computer, we first introduce the concept of a
quantum processor unit (QPU)
as a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\rho),
where
\rho
is a pure state.

Definition 3.1
.

A
single-qubit quantum processor unit
is defined as a random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\rho),
where
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is a pure state. Thus, there exists a unitary vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
such that
\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}.

To give a more detailed description of a single-qubit QPU, we begin with an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{H}_{2}
:

\mathcal{B}_{2}:=\Bigl\{\ket{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad\ket{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\}.

The spectral decomposition of
\mathsf{A}
implies that

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{U}(\lambda_{0}\ket{0}\bra{0}+\lambda_{1}\ket{1}\bra{1})\mathsf{U}^{\star},

for some
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
and
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}.
The corresponding events
\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{z}\}
are

\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{z}\}}=\ket{z}\bra{z}
for

z\in\{0,1\}.
The law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
is then given by

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{z})=\tr\bigl(\rho\,\ket{z}\bra{z}\bigr)=|\bra{z}\ket{\Psi}|^{2}\text{ for }z\in\{0,1\}.

To extend this model to a QPU with multiple qubits, we recall the definition and some useful properties of Kronecker product.

Let
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{M^{\prime}\times N^{\prime}}
. The
Kronecker product

\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B}
is a matrix defined by

\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1}\mathsf{B}&A_{1,2}\mathsf{B}&\dots&A_{1,N}\mathsf{B}\\
A_{2,1}\mathsf{B}&A_{2,2}\mathsf{B}&\dots&A_{2,N}\mathsf{B}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{M,1}\mathsf{B}&A_{M,2}\mathsf{B}&\dots&A_{M,N}\mathsf{B}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{MM^{\prime}\times NN^{\prime}}.

Some standard properties of the Kronecker product include:

1.

\mathsf{A}\otimes(\mathsf{B}\otimes\mathsf{C})=(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B})\otimes\mathsf{C}
,

2.

(\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B})\otimes\mathsf{C}=(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{C})+(\mathsf{B}\otimes\mathsf{C})
,

3.

\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{D}=(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{C})(\mathsf{B}\otimes\mathsf{D})
,

4.

(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B})^{-1}=\mathsf{A}^{-1}\otimes\mathsf{B}^{-1}
,

5.

(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B})^{\star}=\mathsf{A}^{\star}\otimes\mathsf{B}^{\star}
, and

6.

\tr(\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B})=\tr(\mathsf{A})\tr(\mathsf{B}).

7.

Assume that
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mathbb{C})
are diagonal matrices. Then
\mathsf{A}\otimes\mathsf{B}
is also diagonal.

When the dimension of the Hilbert space is
N=M^{q}
for some
M\geq 2
and
q\geq 2
, we can identify the tensor product space
\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}}
with the
q
-fold tensor product

\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}}=\underbrace{\mathbb{H}_{M}\otimes\mathbb{H}_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{H}_{M}}_{q\text{ times}}=\mathbb{H}_{M}^{\otimes q}.

If
\{\ket{e_{1}},\ldots,\ket{e_{M}}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{M}
, then an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}}
is given by

\bigl\{\ket{e_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{e_{i_{q}}}:1\leq i_{k}\leq M,\;1\leq k\leq q\bigr\}.

Since
\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}}
and its dual
(\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}})^{\star}
form a Hilbert space, the associated matrix algebra is

\mathbb{M}_{M^{q}}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}}\,\widehat{\otimes}\,(\mathbb{H}_{M^{q}})^{\star}=\mathbb{H}_{M}^{\otimes q}\,\widehat{\otimes}\,(\mathbb{H}_{M}^{\star})^{\otimes q}.

It can be shown that
\mathbb{M}_{M^{q}}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes q}
; in other words, the algebra of
M^{q}\times M^{q}
matrices is linearly isomorphic to the tensor product of
q
copies of the algebra of
M\times M
matrices. In particular,

\displaystyle(\ket{e_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{e_{i_{q}}})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,(\bra{e_{j_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bra{e_{j_{q}}})

\displaystyle:=(\ket{e_{i_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{e_{i_{q}}})(\bra{e_{j_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bra{e_{j_{q}}})

\displaystyle=\ket{e_{i_{1}}}\bra{e_{j_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{e_{i_{q}}}\bra{e_{j_{q}}}

\displaystyle=(\ket{e_{i_{1}}}\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\bra{e_{j_{1}}})\otimes\cdots\otimes(\ket{e_{i_{q}}}\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\bra{e_{j_{q}}}).

For the remainder of this paper, we assume that the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}_{N}
has dimension
N=2^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now, consider
\mathsf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{A}_{n}

n
-single-qubit QPUs, that is,
n
-random variables in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\rho).
where
\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
for some unit vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}.
Assume that the spectral decomposition of each
\mathsf{A}_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{k}=\mathsf{U}_{k}(\lambda_{0}^{(k)}\ket{0}\bra{0}+\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\ket{1}\bra{1})\mathsf{U}_{k}^{\star},

(3.1)

where
\mathsf{U}_{k}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
and
\lambda_{0}^{(k)},\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{0}^{(k)}\leq\lambda_{1}^{(k)}.

Definition 3.2
.

an
n
-qubit QPU is defined by the Kronecker product of
n
-single qubit QPUs, that is,
\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
where
\mathsf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{A}_{n}
are
n
-single-qubit QPUs. We remark that, given

\mathsf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{A}_{n}
random variables in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\rho),
where
\rho=\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
for some unit vector
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathbb{H}_{2},
then
\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
is a random variable in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C}),\rho^{\otimes n}),
where
\rho^{\otimes n}=(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi})^{\otimes n}=\ket{\Psi^{\otimes n}}\bra{\Psi^{\otimes n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})).

Let
\mathsf{A}:=\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
be an
n
-qubit QPU. By using (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}(\mathsf{U}_{k}\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{U}_{k}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{0}^{(k)}\ket{0}\bra{0}+\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\ket{1}\bra{1}\right)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}\left(\sum_{z_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{z_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\lambda_{z_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\lambda_{z_{n}}^{(n)}\ket{z_{1}}\bra{z_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{z_{n}}\bra{z_{n}}\right)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}\left(\sum_{z_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{z_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\lambda_{z_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\lambda_{z_{n}}^{(n)}\ket{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}\bra{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}\right)^{\star},

where

\ket{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}:=\ket{z_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{z_{n}}

for
z_{1}\cdots z_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

The above representation of the
n
-qubits QPU
\mathsf{A},
allows to introduce the following notation. Identifying
\{0,1\}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the set
\{0,1\}^{n}
corresponds to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. Next, we consider the natural bijection

b_{n}:\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},

given by

b_{n}(k)=b_{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\,2^{i-1}\right)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}.

Thus, to each integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
we can associate a basis vector in
\mathbb{H}_{2^{n}}
by

\ket{b_{n}(k)}=\ket{z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}=\ket{z_{1}}\otimes\ket{z_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{z_{n}}.

The canonical basis of
\mathbb{H}_{2^{n}}
is then

\mathcal{B}_{N}:=\{\ket{b_{n}(k)}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}\}=\{\ket{\mathbf{z}}:\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\}.

Now, we can represent
n
-qubits QPU as

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\lambda_{b_{n}(k)}\ket{b_{n}(k)}\bra{b_{n}(k)}\right)\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\mathsf{U}_{k}^{\star},

where

\lambda_{b_{n}(k)}=\lambda_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}:=\lambda_{z_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots\lambda_{z_{n}}^{(n)}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.
In consequence, the event
\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{b_{n}(k)}\}
corresponds to the random variable
\mathsf{P}_{\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{b_{n}(k)}\}}=\ket{b_{n}(k)}\bra{b_{n}(k)}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C}),\rho^{\otimes n}),
which is a projector onto the linear space generated by the basis vector
\ket{b_{n}(k)}
. Moreover, the law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C}),\rho^{\otimes n})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rho^{\otimes n}}(\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{b_{n}(k)})

\displaystyle=\tr\bigl(\rho^{\otimes n}\,\ket{b_{n}(k)}\bra{b_{n}(k)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=|\bra{b_{n}(k)}\ket{\Psi^{\otimes n}}|^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\bra{z_{j}}\ket{\Psi}|^{2}.

This completes the extension form a single-qubit QPU to an
n
-qubit QPU. From now on, to simplify notation we will write
\{\mathsf{A}=\lambda_{b_{n}(k)}\}
as
\{\mathsf{A}=k\}
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}.

3.2
Dynamics of State Evolution in Digital Quantum Computers

To develop our model of a
Universal Digital Quantum Computer (UDQC)
, we introduce a discrete unitary-evolution framework. Specifically, we define the map

\mathcal{N}:\mathrm{U}(N)\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))\,\to\,\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})),\quad(\mathsf{U},\rho)\;\mapsto\;\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathsf{U})\;:=\;\mathsf{U}\,\rho\,\mathsf{U}^{\star},

(3.2)

which is derived from the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(Liouville-von Neumann Equation)
.

Let
\mathsf{H}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be Hermitian, and consider the Liouville–von Neumann equation on

\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS}\bigr)
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}\;=\;-\,i\,[\mathsf{H},\,\rho],\quad\rho(0)\;=\;\rho_{0},

(3.3)

where
[\mathsf{H},\,\rho]=\mathsf{H}\rho-\rho\mathsf{H}.

Then there is a unique solution given by

\rho(t)\;=\;e^{-\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}\,\rho_{0}\,e^{\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}.

Moreover, if
\rho_{0}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{S}_{r}\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\bigr)

for some
1\leq r\leq N
, then
\rho(t)
remains in
\mathcal{S}_{r}\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\bigr)

for all real
t
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

The solution
\rho(t)=e^{-\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}\,\rho_{0}\,e^{\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}
to (
3.3
) evolves the quantum state according to the Liouville–von Neumann equation. This evolution is unitary and preserves the probabilistic structure of the state, making it a natural model for quantum dynamics.

In the context of complex
N\times N
matrices, a matrix
\mathsf{G}
is called

skew-Hermitian
if it satisfies

\mathsf{G}^{\star}\;=\;-\,\mathsf{G}.
A fundamental fact is that the matrix exponential of
every skew-Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{G}
is a
unitary matrix:

\mathsf{U}\;=\;e^{\mathsf{G}}.
Moreover, this correspondence is essentially
onto
: every unitary matrix
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)

can be written in the form
\mathsf{U}=e^{\mathsf{G}}
for some skew-Hermitian
\mathsf{G}
. In other words,

\mathrm{U}(N)\;=\;\bigl\{\,e^{\mathsf{G}}\,\bigl|\;\mathsf{G}^{\star}=-\,\mathsf{G}\bigr\}.

We are now in a position to define the map
\mathcal{N}
precisely. Take
(\mathsf{U},\rho)\in\mathrm{U}(N)\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
. Since
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
, there exists a skew-Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{G}
such that
e^{\mathsf{G}}=\mathsf{U}.
Setting
\mathsf{H}=i\,\mathsf{G}
makes
\mathsf{H}
Hermitian. By Theorem
3.3
, the unique solution of (
3.3
) with
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
is

\rho(t)\;=\;e^{-\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}\,\rho_{0}\,e^{\,i\,t\,\mathsf{H}}.

Evaluating at
t=1
, we obtain

\rho(1)\;=\;e^{-\,i\,\mathsf{H}}\,\rho_{0}\,e^{\,i\,\mathsf{H}}\;=\;e^{\,\mathsf{G}}\,\rho_{0}\,e^{-\,\mathsf{G}}\;=\;\mathsf{U}\,\rho_{0}\,\mathsf{U}^{\star}.

Hence we define

\mathcal{N}(\mathsf{U},\rho)\;:=\;\rho(1)\;=\;\mathsf{U}\,\rho_{0}\,\mathsf{U}^{\star},

and it follows that
\mathcal{N}
is well-defined and preserves the rank of quantum states.

Remark 3.4
.

The map
\mathcal{N}
can be viewed as a discrete dynamical system, where the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(N)
acts on the manifold
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
. Furthermore, each subset
\mathcal{S}_{r}(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}))
is invariant under this action.

Consider the
n
-qubit QPU given by
\mathsf{A}:=\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
. Each
\mathsf{A}_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq n
is treated as a random variable in the algebraic probability space
\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C}),\rho_{0}\bigr)
, where
\rho_{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
. Consequently, the tensor product
\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
is a random variable in the enlarged algebraic probability space
\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{C}),\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}\bigr)
. Here,

\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}\;=\;\ket{0^{\otimes n}}\bra{0^{\otimes n}}\;=\;\ket{b_{n}(0)}\bra{b_{n}(0)}.

For each
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2^{n})
, the map

\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}}(\mathsf{U})\;=\;\mathsf{U}\,\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}\,\mathsf{U}^{\star}

produces a new pure state in
\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{C})
. Hence, one may regard

\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}
as a random variable in the algebraic probability space

\bigl(\mathbb{M}_{2^{n}}(\mathbf{C}),\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}^{\otimes n}}(\mathsf{U})\bigr)
.

A Universal Digital Quantum Computer

We now define an

.

.

A

in

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{A}_{n}

Example 2 (Unitary matrix for a specific task).

Let
(\mathsf{A},\rho_{0},\mathcal{N})

The measurement process provides the law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the output the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)
that yields

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\tr\!\Bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\ket{b_{3}(k)}\bra{b_{3}(k)}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{3}\Bigl(\delta_{k,1}+\delta_{k,2}+\delta_{k,4}\Bigr),

for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{8}
. Hence, the outcome is the set
\{1,2,4\}
with full probability, demonstrating the effectiveness of the constructed pure state. The challenge, then, is to design a unitary matrix (i.e., an oracle or quantum algorithm)
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
that produces the state
\rho=\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U})
starting from the initial state
\rho_{0}=\ket{b_{3}(0)}\bra{b_{3}(0)}
.

From the above example we learn that unitary matrices are the quantum equivalent of classical computational algorithms. In this context, we need to introduce a notion of elementary computational unit in our quantum computational framework. Similar to the definition of QPU as a cluster of single qubits joined by the help of the Kronecker product, we assume that a quantum elementary operation corresponds to the action over a single qubit, by means the map defined in (
3.2
).

3.4
Elementary Quantum Gates and Quantum Circuits

We now introduce elementary quantum gates as the fundamental building blocks of quantum computation and formally define quantum circuits, which represent oracles or quantum algorithms within
\mathrm{U}(N)
.

To achieve this, we first recall that a function
f:\mathrm{U}(2)\to\mathrm{U}(N)
is a group homomorphism if it satisfies the condition

f(\mathsf{U}\mathsf{V})=f(\mathsf{U})f(\mathsf{V})\quad\text{for all }\mathsf{U},\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2).

A homomorphism is called a monomorphism if it is injective. We denote by
\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N))
the set of such group monomorphisms.
Before proceeding, we formally define the fundamental building blocks of quantum circuits: the elementary quantum gates.

Definition 3.6
.

A matrix
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
is called an
elementary quantum gate
if there exists a pair
(\mathsf{V},\mathfrak{i})\in\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N))
such that
\mathsf{U}=\mathfrak{i}(\mathsf{V})
. The set of all elementary quantum gates in
\mathrm{U}(N)
is denoted by
\mathrm{QG}(N)
.

From the above definition, it follows that
\mathrm{QG}(2)=\mathrm{U}(2)
.
The set of elementary quantum gates can be viewed as a
dictionary
.
More generally, a subset
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathrm{U}(N)
is called a dictionary
if it is nonempty and satisfies the property

\mathcal{D}^{\star}=\{\mathsf{U}^{\star}:\mathsf{U}\in\mathcal{D}\}=\mathcal{D}.

Remark 3.7
.

It is straightforward to verify that if
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{QG}(N)
, then
\mathsf{U}^{\star}\in\mathrm{QG}(N)
; hence,
\mathrm{QG}(N)
forms a dictionary in
\mathrm{U}(N)
.

For a given dictionary
\mathcal{D}
, we denote by
\langle\mathcal{D}\rangle
the group it generates:

\langle\mathcal{D}\rangle=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{D}^{(k)},\quad\text{where }\mathcal{D}^{(0)}=\{\mathsf{I}_{N}\},\quad\mathcal{D}^{(k)}=\{\mathsf{U}_{1}\mathsf{U}_{2}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{k}:\mathsf{U}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}\}.

A dictionary
\mathcal{D}
is said to be
universal
if
\langle\mathcal{D}\rangle=\mathrm{U}(N)
.

In a quantum computer with
n
qubits, a wire represents the action of
\mathrm{U}(2)
on a particular quantum processing unit (QPU), namely
\mathsf{A}_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Each wire can be represented by elements of
\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N))
. For
1\leq j\leq n
, we define the mapping

w_{j}^{(n)}:\mathrm{U}(2)\to\mathrm{U}(2^{n}),\quad\mathsf{U}\mapsto w_{j}^{(n)}(\mathsf{U}):=\mathsf{I}_{2}^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes\mathsf{U}\otimes\mathsf{I}_{2}^{\otimes(n-j)}.

The action of an elementary quantum gate
w_{j}^{(n)}(\mathsf{U})
on the initial state of a universal digital quantum computer in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\ket{b_{n}(0)}\bra{b_{n}(0)}}(w_{j}^{(n)}(\mathsf{U}))

\displaystyle=\ket{b_{j-1}(0)}\bra{b_{j-1}(0)}\otimes\mathsf{U}\ket{0}\bra{0}\mathsf{U}^{\star}\otimes\ket{b_{n-j}(0)}\bra{b_{n-j}(0)}

\displaystyle=\ket{b_{j-1}(0)}\bra{b_{j-1}(0)}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\ket{0}\bra{0}}(\mathsf{U})\otimes\ket{b_{n-j}(0)}\bra{b_{n-j}(0)}.

Thus, this corresponds to the action of
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2)
on the
j
th initial state.

To conclude, we define the set

\mathcal{W}_{N}:=\{w_{j}^{(n)}(\mathsf{V}):\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2),\,1\leq j\leq n\}\subset\mathrm{QN}(N),

which also forms a dictionary that generates the group

\langle\mathcal{W}_{N}\rangle:=\mathrm{U}(2)^{\otimes n}.

Although
\mathrm{U}(2)^{\otimes n}\subset\mathrm{U}(N)
, the set
\mathcal{W}_{N}
is not a universal dictionary. Since
\mathrm{U}(2)^{\otimes n}
is a subgroup of the group generated by all quantum gates, i.e.,

\mathrm{U}(2)^{\otimes n}\subset\langle\mathrm{QG}(N)\rangle,

a natural question arises: is
\mathrm{QG}(N)
itself universal in
\mathrm{U}(N)
? The next result provides a positive answer to this question.

Theorem 3.8
.

The set of quantum gates
\mathrm{QG}(N)
forms a universal dictionary for the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(N)
; that is, for every
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
, there exists an integer
m=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
such that
\mathsf{U}
can be expressed as a product of
m
elementary quantum gates.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
3.8
is that every unitary matrix in
\mathrm{U}(N)
can be represented as a finite sequence of elementary quantum gates. This observation naturally motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.9
.

A
quantum circuit
of length
\ell\geq 0
for a unitary matrix
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
is defined as follows:

\ell=0
if and only if
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{I}_{N}
. Otherwise, a quantum circuit is a finite sequence of elementary quantum gates

\{\mathsf{U}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{U}_{\ell}\}\subset\mathrm{QG}(N)\setminus\{\mathsf{I}_{N}\}
, satisfying

\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}_{\ell}\mathsf{U}_{\ell-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1},

with the additional condition that
\mathsf{U}_{i+1}\mathsf{U}_{i}\neq\mathsf{I}_{N}
for all
1\leq i<\ell
.

Thus, if
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{QG}(N)
has circuit length
\ell\geq 1
, then for each
1\leq k\leq\ell
, there exists a pair
(\mathsf{V}_{k},\mathfrak{i}_{k})\in\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N))
such that
\mathsf{U}_{k}=\mathfrak{i}_{k}(\mathsf{V}_{k})
, and

\mathsf{U}=\mathfrak{i}_{\ell}(\mathsf{V}_{\ell})\,\mathfrak{i}_{\ell-1}(\mathsf{V}_{\ell-1})\cdots\mathfrak{i}_{1}(\mathsf{V}_{1}).

It is important to note that a given
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N)
may admit representations as quantum circuits of different lengths, meaning that the representation is generally non-unique.

In particular, the identity matrix
\mathsf{I}_{N}=\mathsf{I}_{2}^{\otimes n}
is itself an elementary quantum gate (e.g.,
w_{1}^{(n)}(\mathsf{I}_{2})=\mathsf{I}_{N}
) and serves as the empty word in the formal language where elementary matrices act as symbols in the alphabet
\mathrm{U}(2)\times\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N))
. Consequently, the circuit length
\ell
can be interpreted as the number of elementary one-qubit operations required to implement a given quantum circuit.

4
The Grover-Rudolph Algorithm

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

(Grover-Rudolph)
.

Let
(\mathsf{A},\rho_{0},\mathcal{N})
be a universal digital quantum computer in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Given a non-negative function
\varrho:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
satisfying the normalization condition

\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1,

there exists a quantum circuit
\mathsf{U}:=\mathsf{U}_{\ell}\mathsf{U}_{\ell-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}
of length
\ell=N-1
such that the law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the outcome algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U}))
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U})}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\varrho(x)\,dx,\quad\text{for each }k\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}.

We proof the above theorem in two steps.

4.1
A Trigonometric Decomposition of the Target Integral

Our first goal is to express the integral

I_{k}=\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\varrho(x)\,dx,

for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
in a form suitable for implementation through elementary quantum gates. To this end, we introduce the function

\mathsf{T}:\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathsf{T}_{z}(x)=(\cos x)^{1-z}(\sin x)^{z},

which will serve as the building block in our decomposition.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\varrho:[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a nonnegative function satisfying the normalization condition

\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=1.

Then, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
with binary representation
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}
,
there exist parameters

\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}},\,\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}},\,\ldots,\,\theta_{z_{n}}

and a parameter
\theta=\theta(\varrho)
(independent of
k
) in the closed interval
\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\varrho(x)\,dx=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta).

(4.1)

Furthermore, the normalization condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta)=1.

(4.2)

To prove Theorem
4.2
, we partition the interval
[0,1]
into equal subintervals
2^{\ell}
for each
1\leq\ell\leq n
. For
0\leq k\leq 2^{\ell}-1
, define

z_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{k}{2^{\ell}}.

We note that
b_{\ell}(2^{\ell})
is not formally defined; however, for notational convenience, we assign
z_{b_{\ell}(2^{\ell})}^{(\ell)}:=1
.
Thus, for fixed
\ell
we have

0=z_{b_{\ell}(0)}^{(\ell)}<z_{b_{\ell}(1)}^{(\ell)}<\cdots<z_{b_{\ell}(2^{\ell}-1)}^{(\ell)}<z_{b_{\ell}(2^{\ell})}^{(\ell)}=1.

Next, we establish a series of technical lemmas (Lemmas
4.3
and
4.5
) to derive the recursive decomposition of the integral in terms of the functions
\mathsf{T}_{z}
and their corresponding angles.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given
1\leq\ell\leq n-1,
, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\ell}}
it holds
b_{\ell+1}(2k)=0b_{\ell}(k)
and
b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)=1b_{\ell}(k).
Furthermore,

b_{\ell+1}(2k+2)=0b_{\ell}(k+1).

Proof.

Assume that
k=z_{1}2^{0}+z_{2}2^{2}+\cdots+z_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
, that is,
b_{\ell}(k)=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell}.
Then
2k=z_{1}2^{1}+z_{2}2^{2}+\cdots+z_{\ell}2^{\ell}
and hence

b_{\ell+1}(2k)=0z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell}=0b_{\ell}(k).
Using
2k+1=1\,2^{0}+z_{1}2^{1}+z_{2}2^{2}+\cdots+z_{\ell}2^{\ell}
we obtain
b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)=1b_{\ell}(k).

The last statement is straightforward.
∎

Now, given
1\leq\ell\leq n-1,
then for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\ell}}
since

z_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)}=k\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}=2k\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1}}=z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k)}^{(\ell+1)},

and

z_{b_{\ell}(k+1)}^{(\ell)}=(k+1)\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}=(2k+2)\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1}}=z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k+2)}^{(\ell+1)},

holds, we consider the following decomposition

[z_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)},z_{b_{\ell}(k+1)}^{(\ell)}]=[z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k)}^{(\ell+1)},z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)}^{(\ell+1)}]\cup[z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)}^{(\ell+1)},z_{b_{\ell+1}(2k+2)}^{(\ell+1)}].

From now one we will use the following notation

I_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)}:=[z_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)},z_{b_{\ell}(k+1)}^{(\ell)}].

for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\ell}}
and
1\leq\ell\leq n.
Hence, from Lemma
4.3
we have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

Given
1\leq\ell\leq n-1,
then for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{\ell}}
it holds

I_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)}=I_{b_{\ell+1}(2k)}^{(\ell+1)}\cup I_{b_{\ell+1}(2k+1)}^{(\ell+1)}=I_{0b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell+1)}\cup I_{1b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell+1)}.

The above corollary allows us to write

\displaystyle\int_{I_{b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx=\int_{{I_{0b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell+1)}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx+\int_{I_{1b_{\ell}(k)}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx.

(4.3)

Recall that
\lfloor z\rfloor
denotes the integer part function. Now, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
be such that
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}.
Then for
1\leq s\leq n-1
we have

b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{s}b_{n-s}(\lfloor 2^{-s}k\rfloor).

Furthermore,
b_{n-s}(\lfloor 2^{-s}k\rfloor)=z_{s+1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-s}.

Proof.

Since
k=z_{1}2^{0}+z_{1}2^{1}+\cdots+z_{n}2^{n-1},
then
2^{-s}k=z_{1}2^{-s}+z_{2}2^{-s+1}+\cdots+z_{s}2^{-1}+z_{s+1}2^{0}+\cdots+z_{n}2^{n-s},
and
hence
\lfloor 2^{-s}k\rfloor=z_{s+1}2^{0}+\cdots+z_{n}2^{n-s},
because
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for all
1\leq i\leq n.
Thus,

b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{s}b_{n-s}(\lfloor 2^{-s}k\rfloor)
for all
1\leq s\leq n-1.

∎

To conclude this section, we present the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
be such that
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}.
Take
2\leq\ell\leq n-1,
from Lemma
4.5
, choosing
s=n-\ell
, then
b_{\ell}(\lfloor 2^{-(n-\ell)}k\rfloor)=z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}
and we can write (
4.3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx=\int_{I_{0z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx+\int_{I_{1z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx,

(4.4)

and if
\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx\neq 0,
(
4.4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{I_{0z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}+\frac{\int_{I_{1z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}=1.

(4.5)

Define

\displaystyle\theta_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\arccos\sqrt{\frac{\int_{I_{0z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}}&\text{ if }\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx\neq 0,\\
\frac{\pi}{2}&\text{ if }\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx=0\text{ and }z_{n-\ell+1}=0,\\
0&\text{ if }\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx=0\text{ and }z_{n-\ell+1}=1.\end{array}\right.

and now, if
0<\theta_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}<\frac{\pi}{2},
from equation (
4.5
) we have

\displaystyle\cos^{2}\theta_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}=\frac{\int_{I_{0z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}\text{ and }\sin^{2}\theta_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}=\frac{\int_{I_{1z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}.

In consequence,

\mathsf{T}_{z}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\int_{I_{zz_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell+1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx}&\text{ if }\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

By taking
s=n-1
in Lemma
4.5
, we have
b_{1}(\lfloor 2^{-(n-1)}k\rfloor)=z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2},
that is,

\int_{I_{z_{n}}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx\in\left\{\int_{I_{0}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx,\int_{I_{1}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx,\right\}

where it holds

\int_{I_{0}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx+\int_{I_{1}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx=1.

Define

\theta=\theta(\varrho):=\arccos\sqrt{\int_{I_{0}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx},

and hence

\cos^{2}\theta=\int_{I_{0}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx\text{ and }\sin^{2}\theta=\int_{I_{1}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx.

Thus,
\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta)=\int_{I_{z_{n}}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx.

Recall that
I_{b_{n}(k)}^{(n)}=[z_{b_{n}(k)}^{(n)},z_{b_{n}(k+1)}^{(n)}]=[\frac{k}{2^{n}},\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}],
and hence

\int_{\frac{k}{2^{n}}}^{\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}}\varrho(x)dx=\int_{I_{b_{n}(k)}^{(n)}}\varrho(x)dx.

Now, by using Lemma
4.5
, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{I_{b_{n}(k)}^{(n)}}\varrho(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}^{(n)}}\varrho(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{I_{z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}^{(n)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}^{(n-1)}}\varrho(x)dx}\cdot\frac{\int_{I_{z_{2}z_{3}\cdots z_{n}}^{(n-1)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}}^{(n-2)}}\varrho(x)dx}\cdots\frac{\int_{I_{z_{n-1}z_{n}}^{(2)}}\varrho(x)dx}{\int_{I_{z_{n}}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx}\cdot\int_{I_{z_{n}}^{(1)}}\varrho(x)dx

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta),

if

\int_{I_{z_{n-\ell+1}z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}}^{(\ell)}}\varrho(x)dx\neq 0\text{ holds for all }1\leq\ell\leq n.

Otherwise, if the above integral is zero for some
\ell,
then

\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-\ell+1}}(\theta_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}})=(\cos\theta_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}})^{1-z_{n-\ell+1}}(\sin\theta_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}})^{z_{n-\ell+1}}=0.

Thus,

\theta_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\pi/2&\text{if }z_{n-\ell+1}=0,\\
0&\text{if }z_{n-\ell+1}=1.\end{array}\right.

This proves the first statement. The second one an is straightforward consequence of the fact

\int_{0}^{1}\varrho(x)\,dx=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}}\int_{I_{b_{n}(k)}^{(n)}}\varrho(x)dx=1,

and the proof is complete.
∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Theorem
4.1
is a direct consequence of Theorem
4.2
together with the following result.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(\mathsf{A},\rho_{0},\mathcal{N})
be a universal digital quantum computer in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}).
Then, there exists a quantum circuit
\mathsf{U}
of length
N-1
such that the law of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the outcome algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U}))
satisfies the following condition for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
, where
b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n}
:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U})}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}^{2}(\theta),

(4.6)

where the parameters
\theta_{z_{k}\cdots z_{n}}
for
2\leq k\leq n
and
\theta
appear in the decomposition (
4.1
).

Next, we introduce a subset of elementary quantum gates called control quantum gates, that it will be useful to prove Theorem
4.1
.

4.2.1
Control quantum gates

Fix
1\leq\ell\leq n
and consider
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(2).

We define a map

\mathsf{C}^{(\ell)}:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\times\mathrm{U}(2)\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\quad(\mathbf{z},\mathsf{U})\mapsto\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U},

where
\mathbf{z}=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
as

\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}:=\ket{z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}}\bra{z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}}\otimes\mathsf{U}\otimes\ket{z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}\bra{z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n}}.

This matrix acts over

\ket{\mathbf{u}}=\ket{u_{1}\cdots u_{\ell-1}u_{\ell}u_{\ell+1}\cdots u_{n}}
for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}

as follows:

\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\ket{\mathbf{u}}=\scalebox{0.9}{$\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}w_{\ell}^{(n)}(\mathsf{U})\ket{\mathbf{u}}&\text{if }u_{1}\cdots u_{\ell-1}u_{\ell+1}\cdots u_{n}=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.$}

For
\mathsf{U},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{I}_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(2),
we have the following properties:

\displaystyle(\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U})^{\star}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}^{\star},

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\mathsf{V},

(4.8)

and

\displaystyle(\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U})^{\star}\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{I}_{2}.

(4.9)

Observe that if
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
satisfies
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}^{\prime},

since
\bra{\mathbf{z}}\ket{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}=\mathsf{O}_{N}
then

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{V}\cdot\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{O}_{N},

(4.10)

holds.
Now, for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
we define the following map

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}:\mathrm{U}(2)\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\quad\mathsf{U}\mapsto\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}:=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}+\sum_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\begin{matrix}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{z}\end{matrix}$}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{I}_{2}.

The above unitary matrix acts over

\ket{\mathbf{u}}=\ket{u_{1}\cdots u_{\ell-1}u_{\ell}u_{\ell+1}\cdots u_{n}}
for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}

as follows:

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\ket{\mathbf{u}}=\scalebox{0.9}{$\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{\ell}^{(n)}(\mathsf{U})\ket{\mathbf{u}}&\text{if }u_{1}\cdots u_{\ell-1}u_{\ell+1}\cdots u_{n}=z_{1}\cdots z_{\ell-1}z_{\ell+1}\cdots z_{n},\\
\ket{\mathbf{u}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.$}

Then, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

For each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}
and
1\leq\ell\leq n,
the map
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\in\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N)).

Proof.

First at all, given
\mathsf{U},\mathsf{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2),
by using (
4.8
) and (
4.10
) , we have that

\displaystyle(\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U})\,(\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{V})=\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}(\mathsf{U}\mathsf{V}).

(4.11)

From (
4.9
) we have

\displaystyle(\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U})^{\star}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}^{*}+\sum_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\begin{matrix}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}\\
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{z}\end{matrix}$}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{I}_{2}=\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}^{\star}.

(4.12)

Now, by using (
4.12
) and (
4.11
) we have

(\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U})^{\star}\,\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}(\mathsf{U}^{\star}\mathsf{U})=\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{I}_{2}=\mathsf{I}_{N}.

hence
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(N).
Since

\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{I}_{N}
if and only if
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{I}_{2},
then
\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{z}}^{(\ell)}\in\mathrm{Emb}(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N)).
This ends the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
with binary representation

b_{n}(k)=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{n},

the angles
\theta_{z_{j}\cdots z_{n}}
(for
2\leq j\leq n
) and
\theta
are the parameters that appear in the decomposition (
4.1
) of Theorem
4.2
.
To begin, we consider the elementary quantum gate

\mathsf{U}_{1}:=\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta),

then:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{1}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta))\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-1}\otimes(\cos\theta\ket{0}+\sin\theta\ket{1})

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-1}\otimes\displaystyle\sum_{z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\cos\theta)^{1-z_{n}}(\sin\theta)^{z_{n}}\ket{z_{n}}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-1}\otimes\displaystyle\sum_{z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}

Now, take the unitary matrix
\mathsf{U}_{2}:=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0}))(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{1}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{1})),
defined by the product of two elementary quantum gates. Then

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{2}\ket{\psi_{1}}=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0}))(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{1}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{1}))\ket{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0}))(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{1}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{1}))\left(\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\displaystyle\sum_{z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0}))\left(\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\ket{0}\otimes\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{0}(\theta)\ket{0}+\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes R(\theta_{1})\ket{0}\otimes\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1}(\theta)\ket{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0})\ket{0}\otimes\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{0}(\theta)\ket{0}+\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta_{1})\ket{0}\otimes\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1}(\theta)\ket{1}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\scalebox{0.87}{$\displaystyle\left(\cos\theta_{0}\ket{0}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{0}(\theta)\ket{0}+\sin\theta_{0}\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{0}(\theta)\ket{0}+\cos\theta_{1}\ket{0}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{1}(\theta)\ket{1}+\sin\theta_{1}\ket{1}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{1}(\theta)\ket{1}\right)$}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\scalebox{0.85}{$\displaystyle\left(\sum_{z_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\cos\theta_{0})^{z_{n}}(\sin\theta_{0})^{1-z_{n}}\ket{z_{n}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{0}(\theta)\ket{0}+\sum_{z_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\cos\theta_{1})^{z_{n}}(\sin\theta_{1})^{1-z_{n}}\ket{z_{n}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{1}(\theta)\ket{1}\right)$}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\displaystyle\sum_{(z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\ket{z_{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}.

Observe that we can write

\mathsf{U}_{2}=\prod_{z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{n}})):=(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{0}))(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{1}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{1})).

For the next step we take

\mathsf{U}_{3}=\prod_{(z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-3}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{n-1}z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}})),

which is the product of
2^{2}
elementary quantum gates, to obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{3}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{3}\ket{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{(z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}\scalebox{0.9}{$(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-3}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{n-1}z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}}))\left(\ket{0}^{\otimes n-2}\otimes\displaystyle\sum_{(z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\ket{z_{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}\right)$}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-3}\otimes\scalebox{0.87}{$\displaystyle\sum_{z_{n-2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\sum_{(z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}(\cos\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}})^{z_{n-2}}(\sin\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}})^{1-z_{n-2}}\ket{z_{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\ket{z_{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}$}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-3}\otimes\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\sum_{(z_{n-2}z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{3}}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-2}}(\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}})\ket{z_{n-2}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\ket{z_{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n}}$}

\displaystyle=\ket{0}^{\otimes n-3}\otimes\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\sum_{(z_{n-2}z_{n-1}z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{3}}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-2}}(\theta_{z_{n-1}z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}}(\theta_{z_{n}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{n-2}z_{n-1}z_{n}}$}.

Proceeding inductively, at the step
n
we
take

\mathsf{U}_{n}=\prod_{(z_{2}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}),

which is the product of
2^{n-1}
-elementary quantum gates, and then

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{n}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{n}\ket{\psi_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{(z_{2}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\left(\ket{0}+\sum_{(z_{2}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{z_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\sum_{(z_{2}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n-1}}(\cos\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})^{z_{1}}(\sin\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})^{1-z_{1}}\ket{z_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{T}_{z_{2}}(\theta_{z_{3}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{(z_{1}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}}(\theta_{z_{2}\cdots z_{n}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}}(\theta)\ket{z_{1}\cdots z_{n}}.

Let
(\mathsf{A},\rho_{0},\mathcal{N})
be a universal digital quantum computer in
\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
and consider the quantum pure state
\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}.
Assume that for
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
we have
b_{n}(k^{\prime})=z_{1}^{\prime}z_{2}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}.
Then the random variable
\mathsf{A}
has a law in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}})
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}}(\mathsf{A}=k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}\ket{b_{n}(k^{\prime})}\bra{b_{n}(k^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}\ket{z_{1}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}}\bra{z_{1}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=|\bra{\psi_{n}}\ket{z_{1}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}_{z_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{2}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{2^{\prime}}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{3}^{\prime}\cdots z_{n}^{\prime}})\cdots\mathsf{T}_{z_{n-1}^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta_{z_{n}^{\prime}})\mathsf{T}_{z_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}(\theta).

Since
\ket{\psi_{n}}=\mathsf{U}_{n}\mathsf{U}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\mathsf{U}_{n}\mathsf{U}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}\ket{b_{n}(0)},
put
\mathsf{U}:=\mathsf{U}_{n}\mathsf{U}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{1}
and hence

\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}=\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U}).
Thus,

(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}})=(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\mathcal{N}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathsf{U}))

is an output algebraic probability space obtained by means the unitary matrix
\mathsf{U}.

Now, for
2\leq\ell\leq n,
the unitary matrix

\mathsf{U}_{\ell}=\prod_{(z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\ell-1}}(\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-\ell}}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{n-\ell+2}\cdots z_{n}})),

is the product of
2^{\ell}
-elementary quantum gates and
\mathsf{U}_{1}=\mathsf{I}_{2^{n-1}}\otimes\mathsf{R}(\theta)
is a single elementary quantum gate. In consequence, the quantum circuit
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}_{n-1}\cdots\mathsf{U}_{2}\mathsf{U}_{1}
is the product of
2^{n}-1
- elementary quantum gates and hence, it has length
N-1.
This ends the proof.
∎

4.3
Constructing a Quantum Circuit for a Specific Task

We now apply Theorem
4.1
to construct a quantum circuit that accomplishes the task outlined in Example 2 of Section
3
. The objective is to construct a quantum circuit,
\mathsf{U}\in\mathrm{U}(8)
, such that

\ket{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\Bigl(\ket{b_{3}(1)}+\ket{b_{3}(2)}+\ket{b_{3}(4)}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\Bigl(\ket{100}+\ket{010}+\ket{001}\Bigr)=\mathsf{U}\ket{b_{3}(0)}.

where the measurement process determines the probability distribution of the random variable
\mathsf{A}
in the algebraic probability space
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\rho)

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\frac{1}{3}\Bigl(\delta_{k,1}+\delta_{k,2}+\delta_{k,4}\Bigr).

This probability can be rewritten as

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}(\mathsf{A}=k)=\int_{k/8}^{(k+1)/8}\varrho(x)\,dx,

where
\varrho(x)

\mathbf{1}_{I}(x)

\mathbf{1}_{I}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in I,\\

To construct
\mathsf{U}_{2}

where

\mathsf{R}(0)=\mathsf{I}_{2}

\mathsf{U}_{2}=(\mathsf{I}_{2}\otimes\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{0}\mathsf{R}(\pi/2)).

To complete the construction, we determine

\mathsf{U}_{3}=\prod_{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{C}^{(1)}_{z_{1}z_{2}}\mathsf{R}(\theta_{z_{1}z_{2}}),

where

\theta_{01}=\arccos\sqrt{\frac{\int_{1/2}^{5/8}\varrho(x)\,dx}{\int_{1/2}^{3/4}\varrho(x)\,dx}}=0,\quad\theta_{10}=\arccos\sqrt{\frac{\int_{1/4}^{3/8}\varrho(x)\,dx}{\int_{1/4}^{1/2}\varrho(x)\,dx}}=0.

is a quantum circuit of length
4.

Proof of Theorem

To prove Theorem
3.3
we use the boundedness and self-adjointness of the commutator map induced by a fixed Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{H}
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathsf{H}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
be an Hermitian matrix. Then the linear map

[\mathsf{H},\cdot]:(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS})\longrightarrow(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS}),

defined by
\mathsf{A}\mapsto[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{A}]
, is bounded and self-adjoint.

Proof.

Linearity is immediate from the definition of the commutator. Since
(\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C}),\|\cdot\|_{HS})
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, every linear map is bounded. To show that the map is self-adjoint, we must verify that

\langle[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{A}],\mathsf{B}\rangle_{HS}=\langle\mathsf{A},[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{B}]\rangle_{HS},

for all
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Indeed, using the definition of the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product,

\langle[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{A}],\mathsf{B}\rangle_{HS}=\operatorname{tr}\left([\mathsf{H},\mathsf{A}]^{\star}\mathsf{B}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left((\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{H}^{\star}-\mathsf{H}^{\star}\mathsf{A}^{\star})\mathsf{B}\right).

Since
\mathsf{H}
is Hermitian (
\mathsf{H}^{\star}=\mathsf{H}
), this becomes

\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{H}\mathsf{B}-\mathsf{H}\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{B}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{H}\mathsf{B}\right)-\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{H}\right).

By the cyclicity of the trace we have

\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{H}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{H}\mathsf{A}^{\star}\mathsf{B}\right).

Therefore,

\langle[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{A}],\mathsf{B}\rangle_{HS}=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathsf{A}^{\star}(\mathsf{H}\mathsf{B}-\mathsf{B}\mathsf{H})\right)=\langle\mathsf{A},[\mathsf{H},\mathsf{B}]\rangle_{HS}.

This shows that the map
[\mathsf{H},\cdot]
is self-adjoint.
∎

Inductive Step.

Assume the result is true for

(B.1)

m\;=\;\frac{(N-1)\,(N-2)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathsf{V}_{1},f_{1}),\ldots,(\mathsf{V}_{m},f_{m})\;\in\;\mathrm{U}(2)\,\times\,\mathrm{Emb}\!\bigl(\mathrm{U}(2),\mathrm{U}(N-1)\bigr)

\mathrm{down}_{N}\!\bigl(\mathsf{U}_{N-1}^{(N)}\bigr)\;=\;(\mathrm{down}_{N}\circ f_{m})(\mathsf{V}_{m})\,\cdots\,(\mathrm{down}_{N}\circ f_{1})(\mathsf{V}_{1}),
