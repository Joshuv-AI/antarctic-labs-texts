Introduction

White spot lesions, referred to as early caries lesions, are a common dental problem that affects a significant number of people.

These lesions indicate subsurface demineralization beneath an intact, highly mineralized enamel layer and may or may not progress to caries.1 Their prevalence has ranged between 10% and 49%. Dental caries is an active process with cycles of demineralization and remineralization of the tooth's mineral structure. The progression or regression of caries lesions depends on the balance between these two processes. Pathological factors such as cariogenic bacteria, fermentable carbohydrates, and salivary dysfunction favor demineralization, whereas defensive factors such as antimicrobial agents, salivary components, and mineralizing ions modulate the cariogenic process by shifting the equilibrium toward remineralization.2

Several remineralizing agents, including casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), fluoride, nano-hydroxyapatite particles, and bioactive glass (BAG), are widely used to prevent white spot lesions. However, these agents are associated with potential systemic effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances and conditions like skeletal or dental fluorosis.3,4 Moreover, conventional remineralizing materials often demonstrate limitations, including limited hardness, reduced mechanical stability, and high solubility in oral environments. Consequently, researchers have turned their attention to bioactive compounds of natural origin, derived from dietary sources, medicinal plants, flowers, and traditional herbs.5–8 Among these, plant-based antibacterial molecules represent promising alternatives for plaque control and caries management. In particular, alkaloids of plant origin exhibit antimicrobial properties, inhibit demineralization, and support remineralization.4

Ginger, the rhizome widely known as ginger root, has been traditionally used in India for centuries as both a culinary spice and a medicinal agent. Among natural food sources, it is distinguished by its notable antimicrobial activity. Moreover, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has considered it safe for consumption and has provided a “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) designation. The pungent oil within ginger contains a range of polyphenolic ketones, which exhibit numerous pharmacological effects. Ginger contains a considerable level of fluoride (79 mg/kg in 8 mg), which may contribute to its potential remineralizing effect on teeth.9,10 Literature search shows there is minimal evidence that assesses the remineralization potential of ginger. Thus, the objective of the present review is to systematically assess the effectiveness of ginger extracts in the remineralization of white spot lesions.

Materials and Methodology

Registration and Protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/NSYAT). This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA (preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses) guidelines. The PICO (population, intervention, comparison, and outcome) framework was used to formulate the research question, which asks: “Do ginger extracts demonstrate a significant remineralization potential in teeth with white spot lesions compared to other remineralizing agents?” This framework defines the inquiry by focusing on teeth with white spot lesions (population), assessing the effectiveness of ginger extracts as a remineralizing agent (intervention), comparing it with other remineralizing agents (comparison), and evaluating outcomes such as changes in calcium content, surface microhardness, color, and fluorescence (outcome).

Eligibility Criteria

In vitro studies on the remineralization effect of ginger extracts on white spot lesions, published in English from January, 2000 to August, 2024, were included. Articles with study designs such as case reports, review articles, letters, or descriptive studies, and those examining ginger extracts in combination with other natural herbal extracts, were excluded.

Search Strategy and Databases

Using the PICO framework, an extensive search methodology was formulated. Databases such as PubMed, SCOPUS, LILAC, Google Scholar, and grey literature were searched systematically for studies published from January, 2000 to August, 2024. Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were employed to refine the search. The following search terms were used: {[(“white spot lesions” OR “incipient caries”) OR “early enamel lesions”] AND “ginger extracts”}, {[(“white spot lesions” OR “incipient caries”) OR “early enamel lesions”] AND “natural remineralizing agents”}, {[(“white spot lesions” OR “incipient caries”) OR “early enamel lesions”] AND “natural herbal agents”}, {[(“white spot lesions” OR “incipient caries”) OR “natural remineralizing agents”]}, and {[(“white spot lesions” OR “incipient caries”) AND “ginger extracts”]}. References of included studies were manually examined for additional relevant literature.

Screening and Selection

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers to identify studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Full-text articles were reviewed for relevance, conflicts were resolved through consensus or, when required, by seeking the opinion of a third reviewer. Duplicates were removed, and potentially eligible studies underwent a detailed evaluation. The selection process was documented in a PRISMA flow diagram, ensuring transparency.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized form to capture key study characteristics, including sample size, methodology, interventions, and outcomes. Specific details collected included study design, demographic details of the samples, interventions and timing, and measures of remineralization potential (e.g., changes in calcium content, surface microhardness, color, and fluorescence). Two reviewers independently extracted the data, followed by cross-verification to ensure accuracy. Any disagreements were addressed through discussion.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using RevMan version 5.4 software. A meta-analysis was conducted for studies that assessed the remineralizing potential of ginger extracts on surface microhardness and mineral content. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the I² statistic and Q-test. The I² index quantified the percentage of variability across studies, while the Q-test assessed statistical significance. Results were presented in tabular and graphical formats to illustrate pooled effects and heterogeneity.

Results

Study Selection and Inclusion

The systematic review identified a total of 578 records through database searches. After the removal of duplicates and screening based on titles and abstracts, an eligibility assessment was conducted on ten full-text articles. Five articles did not meet the inclusion criteria, resulting in five studies being included in the final analysis. The five studies selected for inclusion were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating remineralization outcomes. These studies collectively assessed three primary outcomes: surface microhardness, mineral content, and changes in color or fluorescence. Characteristics of studies included in the systematic review

Surface Microhardness Outcomes

Three studies (Saleh et al., Celik et al., and Gomathi et al.) assessed surface microhardness using Vickers hardness testing. Saleh et al. and Celik et al. found that ginger-treated specimens showed the highest remineralization effectiveness, with a meta-analysis indicating greater mean hardness in the ginger group, though not statistically significant (p = 0.11, I² = 99%). Gomathi et al. found significantly increased microhardness with Dabur Red toothpaste compared to Patanjali Dant Kanti (p < 0.001).11–15

Mineral Content Assessment

Saleh et al. observed no significant changes in calcium or phosphorus levels across groups but reported higher fluoride levels in specimens treated with eggshell and ginger compared to controls.13

Color and Fluorescence Changes

Celik et al. reported improved enamel fluorescence recovery (F∆%) across all groups with no significant differences (p > 0.05). Hossam found a significant improvement in Diagnodent-measured remineralization for the ginger group (p = 0.017). Eliwa reported the highest ΔE (color change) values for ginger-treated specimens, significantly outperforming rosemary and sodium fluoride groups (p < 0.001).11,12,15

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated using standardized criteria (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Among the five studies, three have a low risk of bias (Celik et al., Saleh et al., Gomathi et al.), while two have a high risk of bias (Hossam and Eliwa). Overall, the included studies demonstrated sufficient methodological quality, with isolated concerns primarily related to incomplete data and selective reporting.11–15

These findings suggest that ginger exhibits potential as an effective remineralizing agent, particularly in improving surface microhardness and color outcomes. However, variations in study methodologies and high heterogeneity highlight the need for further standardized research.

Discussion

White spot lesions (WSLs) represent the initial stage of dental caries and are defined by subsurface demineralization beneath an intact enamel surface. Effective remineralization can reverse these lesions if the surface integrity is maintained. Conventional approaches, including fluoride-based therapies and synthetic remineralizing agents, have been effective but are associated with concerns such as toxicity, fluorosis, and systemic side effects.11 Consequently, natural alternatives like ginger, with its bioactive and antimicrobial properties, have garnered attention for minimally invasive management of dental caries.16

This systematic review highlights the promising remineralization potential of ginger extracts. The included studies consistently reported improvements in surface microhardness, mineral content, and color recovery.

The present study supports the use of ginger extracts as a natural alternative for remineralizing white spot lesions. The results are consistent with those of Celik et al., who observed high remineralizing potential in ginger extracts, comparable to fluoride toothpaste and MI Paste, indicating that ginger could serve as a fluoride-free alternative.11 Similarly, Gocmen et al. reported no significant difference between ginger and fluoride toothpaste in remineralization, attributing ginger's efficacy to its high fluoride content (79 mg/kg).17

Hassan et al. showed that ginger and rosemary extracts were effective in inhibiting the progression of early enamel lesions, highlighting their antimicrobial properties and safety (GRAS status) as natural products.18 In agreement with this, Eliwa found that ginger-treated specimens exhibited the highest mean color change (ΔE) values, surpassing rosemary and fluoride treatments, reinforcing ginger's superior remineralization capacity.15

Saleh et al. reported that the acidic pH of ginger extract (5.8) enhanced enamel surface remineralization by facilitating particle penetration. Furthermore, ginger demonstrated antibacterial activity against Actinomyces viscosus, outperforming eggshell or combined preparations.16 Similarly, Patel et al. noted that ginger showed greater efficacy against Streptococcus mutans compared to gentamicin, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent.16

Our meta-analysis also corroborates the findings of Gomathi et al., where Dabur Red toothpaste containing ginger showed better remineralization potential than Patanjali Dant Kanti, likely due to its active herbal components.14 This reinforces the hypothesis that ginger, a key ingredient in Dabur Red, has a pivotal function in supporting remineralization.

In summary, this systematic review emphasizes the potential of ginger extracts as a natural and effective option for remineralizing white spot lesions. Their antibacterial activity, rich fluoride and calcium content, and capacity to improve surface hardness position them as a promising substitute for conventional synthetic agents.

Limitations and Future Directions

While the evidence supports ginger's efficacy in remineralizing WSLs and its antibacterial action, some limitations persist. Natural products, including ginger, may exhibit slower action or lower efficacy compared to synthetic agents in certain clinical scenarios. Standardized formulations and dosages are needed to optimize their application in dental practice. This systematic review included only in vitro studies. Thus, the inclusion of in vivo studies and clinical research studies with long-term follow-up can improve the level of evidence to establish protocols and validate their widespread adoption in caries management.

Conclusion

Ginger extracts serve as a promising natural substitute for the remineralization of white spot lesions. Their antibacterial properties, high mineral content, and ability to improve surface hardness and color recovery make them a viable option for minimally invasive dentistry. While further studies are needed to establish standardized application protocols, the current evidence supports ginger's potential to serve as an effective and safe remineralizing agent.
