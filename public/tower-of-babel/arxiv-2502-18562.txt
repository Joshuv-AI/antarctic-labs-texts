3D Conformal Field Theory in Twistor Space
Aswini Bala, Sachin Jain, Dhruva K.S., Deep Mazumdar and Vibhor Singh

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to study three dimensional Lorentzian conformal field theories in twistor space. We formulate the conformal Ward identities and solve for two and three point Lorentzian Wightman functions. We found that the Helicity operators apart from the conformal generators play an important role in fixing their functional form. The equations take the form of first order Euler equations which in addition to the usual solutions that are polynomials, also possess weak solutions which are distributional in nature. All of these play an important role in our analysis. For instance, in the case of three point functions, the distributional solutions are indeed the ones realized by the CFT correlators. We also extend our analysis to parity odd Wightman functions which take an interesting form in twistor space. We verify our results by systematically analyzing the corresponding Wightman functions in momentum space and spinor helicity variables and matching with the twistor results via a half-Fourier transform.

The form of the symmetry generators do not depend on the signature of spacetime. Usually, one starts with a translation invariant ansatz that is also consistent with the helicities of the currents in the correlator. 2.2
Euclidean correlators

The study and classification of conformal correlation functions of conserved currents in three dimensions have been the subject of intense scrutiny.
The most general form of the two point function consists of a term that is even under parity and another one special to three dimensions that is odd:

\displaystyle\langle J_{s}J_{s}\rangle=c_{\text{even}}\langle J_{s}J_{s}\rangle_{\text{even}}+c_{\text{odd}}\langle J_{s}J_{s}\rangle_{\text{odd}}.

(9)

Their three point functions take the form (schematically)
[
46
]
,

\displaystyle\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle=c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(F)}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{F}+c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(\text{odd})}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{\text{odd}}+c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(B)}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{B}.

(10)

F
and
B
stand for the corresponding correlators computed in the free fermionic and free bosonic theories respectively and the
c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}
are OPE coefficients. The odd structure, although cannot be directly computed like the free theory results, it can be obtained via an

[
23
]

\displaystyle\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{\text{odd}}=\langle\epsilon\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{F}-\langle\epsilon\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{B},

(11)

where,

\displaystyle\epsilon\cdot J_{s}^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p_{1})=\frac{p_{1\beta}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta(\mu_{1}}}{p_{1}}J_{s}^{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{s})}~{}_{\alpha}(p).

(12)

An important fact to note is that the odd structure is compatible with current conservation only when the spin-triangle inequality
s_{1}+s_{2}\geq s_{3},s_{2}+s_{3}\geq s_{1},s_{3}+s_{1}\geq s_{2}
is satisfied. Using (
11

10

\displaystyle\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle=c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(h)}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{h}+c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(odd)}\langle\epsilon\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{h}+c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(nh)}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{nh},

(13)

where we have defined the homogeneous and non-homogeneous correlators
[
23
]
,

\displaystyle\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{h}=\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{F}-\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{B},

\displaystyle\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{nh}=\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{F}+\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{B},

where

\displaystyle c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(h)}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(F)}-c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(B)}),c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(nh)}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(F)}+c_{s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}}^{(B)}).

(14)

It turns out that the representation (
13
) in contrast to (
10

11
). Further, the
h
and
nh
correlators can be distinguished by their Ward-Takahashi identities. They satisfy,

\displaystyle\langle p_{1}\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{h}=0

\displaystyle\langle p_{1}\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{nh}=\text{Ward-Takahashi identity}.

(15)

In words, the homogeneous correlator has a trivial (zero) Ward-Takahshi identity whereas the non-homogeneous correlator saturates the Ward-Takahashi identity of the correlator.
This statement can also be written in spinor helicity variables as,

\displaystyle K^{\mu}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{h}=0

\displaystyle K^{\mu}\langle J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}\rangle_{nh}\propto\text{Ward-Takahashi identity}.

(16)

Another nice fact about these correlators is that from the AdS bulk perspective, the homogeneous correlators arise from higher order (non-minimal) interactions while the non-homogeneous correlators emerge from the leading order interactions.

The fact (
15
) in particular makes the homogeneous correlator relatively easy to bootstrap for all spins. In contrast, the non-homogeneous correlator is much more complicated and harder to solve for. Also, when the spin-triangle inequality is violated, we actually get no homogeneous solution but rather two non-homogeneous solutions which are quite complicated
[
24
]
.

2.3
Wightman functions

As we shall elucidate, if we work in Lorentzian, rather than Euclidean signature and focus in particular on Wightman functions, the Ward-Takahashi identity becomes trivial,

\displaystyle\langle 0|p_{1}\cdot J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}|0\rangle=0.

(17)

This is in contrast to time-ordered correlators, see appendix
I
for a discussion on this. In spinor helicity variables, the analog of the action of the SCT (

\displaystyle K^{\mu}\langle 0|J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}|0\rangle_{h}=0

\displaystyle K^{\mu}\langle 0|J_{s_{1}}J_{s_{2}}J_{s_{3}}|0\rangle_{nh}=0,

(18)

where we stick to the labels
h
and
nh
as these Wightman functions are the analytic continuations of their Euclidean counterparts as we shall discuss in much more detail in section
5

As a result many of the complications associated to the Euclidean non-homogeneous correlators discussed above disappear.
These Wightman functions as mentioned, are conformally invariant and identically conserved and hence are the correlators obtained from twistor space as discussed by
[
38
]
. With this in mind, let us set up and solve the conformal Ward identities in twistor space.

3
Conformal Wightman functions from twistor space

Our aim in this section is to formulate and solve for (parity even) two and three point correlation functions of conserved currents in twistor space. We shall adopt an approach that is in the spirit of Witten’s twistor space construction for four dimensional scattering amplitudes
[
41
]
. We first discuss the necessity of working in Lorentzian space-time to define real twistors. We then perform half-Fourier transforms to go to momentum twistor space where we shall solve the conformal Ward identities, accompanied by the all important helicity counting identities for two- and three-point functions of conserved currents.

3.1
Towards twistor space: The signature of space-time

In this subsection, we take first steps in defining twistor space. We shall see that in order to define
real
twistor space, one really needs to work in Lorentzian, rather than Euclidean signature.

Problem with defining twistor space correlators starting from Euclidean space

In principle, one would think that one can obtain a twistor space correlator starting from a 3d Euclidean CFT correlator in spinor helicity variables and performing a half Fourier transform on the
(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})
variables. However, the associated Euclidean reality conditions as discussed below, do not allow this. Let us understand this obstacle more in detail. The main difficulty with usual spinor helicity variables is that the translation generator is multiplicative whereas the SCT is a second order differential operator as in (
7
). It is very tempting to perform a half Fourier transform which will make the action of
K
first order (and
P
as well) hence simplifying the analysis. However, our hope is short lived as
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
are Hermitian conjugates:

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}^{\dagger}=\bar{\lambda}^{a}~,~\bar{\lambda}^{a\dagger}=\lambda_{a}.

(19)

See Appendix
D
for a more detailed discussion. This implies we cannot Fourier transform of
\lambda
or
\bar{\lambda}
without affecting the other.

This discussion makes it clear that because of the reality conditions, it is not straightforward to directly obtain a Twistor correlator starting from a Euclidean correlator
5
5

5

It would be interesting to reconcile this with the approach of Woodhouse
[
47
]
.
. One way to circumvent this issue is to complexify the spacetime which entails treating
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
as complex and independent and then defining the twistor transformations. Another simpler way, is to work in Lorentzian, rather than Euclidean signature where the associated reality conditions allow for the construction of a real twistor space.

How Lorentzian signature admits the construction of real twistor space

The analogue of the spinor helicity reality condition (
19
) in Lorentzian signature is given by,

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}^{*}=\lambda_{a}~,~\bar{\lambda}^{a*}=\bar{\lambda}^{a},

(20)

where we refer the reader to appendix
D
for more details.
This implies for real Minkowskian momenta, the spinors
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
are real and independent. This can be understood as a fact inherited via a dimensional reduction of a null Kleinian momentum as we discuss in appendix

\bar{\lambda}
while keeping

Which Lorentzian correlator?

Now that we have established that Lorentzian signature is most well suited to develop twistor space

6

, one can ask the question about what Lorentzian correlators are natural from this perspective. To elaborate, the fact is that in contrast to Euclidean signature, there exist many different types of Lorentzian correlators such as time-ordered correlators, anti-time-ordered correlators, retarded correlators and much more. The answer, it turns out is that the Wightman functions are most suitable. This is due to the following fact. The action of the special conformal generator

\displaystyle\langle[K_{ab},\frac{J_{s_{1}}^{h_{1}}(p_{1})J_{s_{2}}^{h_{2}}(p_{2})J_{s_{3}}^{h_{3}}(p_{3})}{p_{1}^{s_{1}-1}p_{2}^{s_{2}-1}p_{3}^{s_{3}-1}}]\rangle=\langle[2\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{a}\partial\bar{\lambda}^{b}},\frac{J_{s_{1}}^{h_{1}}(p_{1})J_{s_{2}}^{h_{2}}(p_{2})J_{s_{3}}^{h_{3}}(p_{3})}{p_{1}^{s_{1}-1}p_{2}^{s_{2}-1}p_{3}^{s_{3}-1}}]\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{1}^{2}}\zeta_{a}^{h_{1}}\zeta_{b}^{h_{1}}\zeta_{a_{1}}^{h_{1}}\cdots\zeta^{h_{1}}_{a_{s_{1}-3}}\zeta_{a_{s_{1}-2}}^{h_{1}}p_{1a_{s_{1}-1}a_{s_{1}}}\frac{\langle J_{s_{1}}^{a_{1}\cdots a_{s_{1}}}(p_{1})J_{s_{2}}^{h_{2}}(p_{2})J_{s_{3}}^{h_{3}}(p_{3})\rangle}{p_{1}^{s_{1}-1}p_{2}^{s_{2}-1}p_{3}^{s_{3}-1}}.

(21)

(see (

However, if the correlator has a zero Ward-Takahashi identity, the RHS is zero and then one need not worry about solving a complicated integral equation. The Lorentzian correlator that precisely has a zero Ward Takahashi identity is the Wightman function, see appendix

In short, the reason is that our twistor space construction yields identically conserved correlators (no Ward-Takahashi identitites) is due to the fact that we are dealing with Wightman functions. From the perspective of the Penrose transformation taken by

3.2
Twistor variables

The idea of the momentum twistor transform (the
Witten

[
41

) is to take the pair
(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})
and perform a

transform, that is, a Fourier transform with respect to either
\lambda
or
\bar{\lambda}

[

\displaystyle\text{Twistor}:\qquad

\displaystyle(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})\to(\lambda,\bar{\mu}),

\displaystyle\text{Dual-Twistor}:\qquad

\displaystyle(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})\to(\mu,\bar{\lambda}).

(22)

Note that for this to make sense, we must work in Lorentzian signature as only there are the two spinors real and independent as we discussed in (
20
). The first of these transformations is called the twistor transform whereas the latter is called the dual twistor transform.

Consider the rescaled currents
\frac{J_{s}^{\pm}(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})}{p^{s-1}}
. We have two options viz (
22
) for each current in performing the twistor transform. They are,

\displaystyle\bigg\{\hat{J}_{s}^{+}(\lambda,\bar{\mu})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bar{\lambda}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\bar{\lambda}\cdot\bar{\mu}}\frac{J_{s}^{+}(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})}{p^{s-1}}~\textbf{or}~\tilde{J}_{s}^{+}(\mu,\bar{\lambda})=\int\frac{d^{2}\lambda}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-i\lambda\cdot\mu}\frac{J_{s}^{+}(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})}{p^{s-1}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\hat{J}_{s}^{-}(\lambda,\bar{\mu})=\int\frac{d^{2}\bar{\lambda}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\bar{\lambda}\cdot\bar{\mu}}\frac{J_{s}^{-}(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})}{p^{s-1}}~\textbf{or}~\tilde{J}_{s}^{-}(\mu,\bar{\lambda})=\int\frac{d^{2}\lambda}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-i\lambda\cdot\mu}\frac{J_{s}^{-}(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})}{p^{s-1}}\bigg\}.

\displaystyle\langle 0|\hat{J}_{s_{1}}^{+}(\lambda_{1},\bar{\mu}_{1})\cdots\hat{J}_{s_{n}}^{+}(\lambda_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})|0\rangle=F_{n}(\{\lambda_{i}\cdot\bar{\mu}_{j}-\lambda_{j}\cdot\bar{\mu}_{i}\})=F_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}).

In those cases, we find the helicity identities take the form of the following ODE,

\displaystyle x_{i}\frac{df_{i}}{dx_{i}}+n_{i}f_{i}=0,~n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

This equation is of Euler type and has two classes of solutions, see appendix

\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{x_{i}^{n_{i}}}.

(32)

However there are solutions that are not functions, but are rather

For the cases

\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=\frac{\theta(x_{i})}{x_{i}^{n_{i}}(-n_{i})!},~n_{i}\leq 0.

The Euler ODE (

5
Wightman functions

Correlation functions of local operators are among the most fundamental and important observables in generic quantum field theories. In Lorentzian signature, the basic building blocks for all correlators are the Wightman functions. In this section, we begin with a study of some generalities about Wightman functions and discuss their relation to Euclidean correlators. We then specialize to a discussion on Wightman functions in conformal field theories.

5.1
Definitions and generalities

A Wightman function is the following vacuum expectation value:

\displaystyle W_{n}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\langle 0|O_{1}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})\cdots O_{n}(t_{n},\vec{x}_{n})|0\rangle,

(100)

where
O_{i}

are generic (spinning) operators. For an
n

n!
such objects as there are that many possible orderings of the operators. Note however, that not all of them might be independent. Further, if the separation between all operators are space-like, all Wightman functions are identical thanks to microcausality. Technically speaking, these “functions” are actually
tempered
distributions and must be integrated against Schwartz functions to obtain finite results, although we shall use the terms Wightman function and Wightman distribution interchangeably

14

14

See for instance David Duffins’ TASI lecture notes on Conformal Field Theory in Lorentzian Signature available on his
Caltech home page
.
.

In the Heisenberg picture we can write (
100

15

15

\displaystyle W(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\langle 0|O_{1}(0)e^{-iH(t_{1}-t_{2})-H(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})+i\vec{P}\cdot(\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2})}O_{2}(0)\cdots e^{-iH(t_{n-1}-t_{n})-H(\epsilon_{n-1}-\epsilon_{n})+i\vec{P}\cdot(\vec{x}_{n-1}-\vec{x}_{n})}O_{n}(0)|0\rangle,

\displaystyle W(f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n})=\int d^{d}x_{1}\cdots d^{d}x_{n}f_{1}(x_{1})\cdots f_{n}(x_{n})W(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}).

7
Discussion and future directions

In this paper, we have set up and solved the twistor space conformal Ward identities for two and three point correlators of conserved currents. It is interesting that these identities accompanied by the helicity operator lead to differential equations of the Euler type which has as its solutions not only usual classical polynomial functions, but also weak solutions such as distributional solutions. We found that all these classes of solutions play an important role and occur in different twistor space correlators. We also analyzed correlators that are odd under parity and proposed one solution as to how they can be represented in twistor space. Further, we also took steps in the paper to systematically analyze and solve for Wightman functions in momentum space and spinor helicity variables and found the results to be in perfect accord with their twistor space counterparts. There are many interesting avenues to explore further of which we list a few below.

Conformal blocks and higher point functions

We have explored the spinning three-point correlators in twistor space and seen its immense power in terms of the simplicity of correlators. It would be interesting to go beyond and explore spinning higher point functions using this approach. Although they are generally complicated, we believe twistor space might help in this pursuit. In this work, we have only performed the analysis for conserved currents. In order to explore higher point functions, one would have to do the same analysis for three-point functions with one non-conserved current. Twistor space

In this appendix, we shall present the twistor space construction that we employ for the analysis of three dimensional conformal field theories. There are a few different ways to define twistor space

\mathbb{CP}^{3}

which has two dimensional representations. Thus, we identify,

\displaystyle Z^{A}=\lambda^{a}\oplus\bar{\mu}_{a^{\prime}},

(155)

where
\lambda
and
\bar{\mu}
are both in the fundamental representation of
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
. The incidence relation that connects twistor space to position space is given by,

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{a^{\prime}}=x_{aa^{\prime}}\lambda^{a},

(156)

where

is valued in
M_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
. Taking the three dimensional Minkowski slice which corresponds to real
\lambda,\bar{\mu}

30
30

30

In the main text, we understood this as resulting from a dimensional reduction from four dimensional Klein space. Here, we obtain a more intrinsic three dimensional picture of the twistor space construction.
.

Let us now compare and contrast this construction with the four-dimensional case. There, one can define twistor space as the space of all complex lines through the origin, see for instance

The three dimensional momentum is then given by,

\displaystyle p_{ab}=-\frac{1}{2}\big(p_{(a}^{\dot{a}}\epsilon_{b)\dot{a}}\big).

(214)

In matrix form this procedure is as follows: First form,

\displaystyle p_{a}^{\dot{a}}\epsilon_{b\dot{a}}\sim\begin{pmatrix}p_{t}+p_{z}&p_{x}-ip_{y}\\
p_{x}+ip_{y}&p_{t}-p_{z}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-p_{x}+ip_{y}&p_{t}+p_{z}\\
-p_{t}+p_{z}&p_{x}+ip_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

(215)

Now symmetrize this matrix and multiply by a factor of
-\frac{1}{2}
. The result is,

\displaystyle p_{ab}=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-p_{x}+ip_{y}&p_{t}+p_{z}\\
-p_{t}+p_{z}&p_{x}+ip_{y}\end{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-p_{x}+ip_{y}&-p_{t}+p_{z}\\
p_{t}+p_{z}&p_{x}+ip_{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-p_{x}+ip_{y}&p_{z}\\
p_{z}&p_{x}+ip_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

(216)

Thus, we see that
p_{t}
has completely dropped out in this procedure and left behind a three dimensional general momenta. In the langauge of spinors we have,

\displaystyle p_{ab}=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{(a}\epsilon_{b)\dot{a}}\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{(a}\bar{\lambda}_{b)}\big),

(217)

where we have defined,

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{b}=\epsilon_{\dot{a}b}\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}.

(218)

The induced reality condition on the three dimensional spinors is,

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}^{\dagger}=\bar{\lambda}^{a},(\bar{\lambda}^{a})^{\dagger}=\lambda_{a}.

(219)

H.2
\mathbb{R}^{2,2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2,1}

The procedure is very similar to the above case. A null momenta in the four dimensional Klein space can be written as an outerproduct of two spinors,
p_{a}^{\dot{a}}=\lambda_{a}\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}
with a little group redundancy
\lambda\to\frac{1}{r}\lambda,\tilde{\lambda}\to r\tilde{\lambda}
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
. In matrix form we have,

\displaystyle p_{a}^{\dot{a}}\sim\begin{pmatrix}p_{t}+p_{z}&p_{x}-p_{w}\\
p_{x}+p_{w}&p_{t}-p_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

(220)

The
p_{w}
component that appears here is related to the corresponding Euclidean
p_{y}
via
ip_{y}=p_{w}
. The reality condition here is simply that the above matrix is real and hence gives
\lambda_{a}^{*}=\lambda_{a}
and
(\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}})^{*}=\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}
, that is, the spinors are real and independent.
One can now follow the same procedure as in the previous subsection, i.e., defining a special vector
\epsilon_{a\dot{a}}
(
213
) and defining a 3d momentum via (
214
). The difference now is that
p_{w}
is a timelike coordinate unlike
p_{y}
and thus what we end up with is a three dimensional Lorentzian momentum. Again, in the language of the spinors we have,

\displaystyle p_{ab}=-\frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{(a}\epsilon_{b)\dot{a}}\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\lambda_{(a}\bar{\lambda}_{b)}\big),

(221)

where we have defined,

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{b}=\epsilon_{\dot{a}b}\tilde{\lambda}^{\dot{a}}.

(222)

The reality condition induced on the three dimensional momenta is now,

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}^{*}=\lambda_{a},(\bar{\lambda}^{a})^{*}=\bar{\lambda}^{a}.

(223)

H.3
Understanding discrete symmetries in Klein space

We have seen in appendix
H
that the natural dimensional reduction of Klein space leads to three dimensional Minkowski space. It is thus interesting to ask how the discrete symmetries in Klein space reduce to those in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
. The starting point is the position bi-spinor:

\displaystyle x_{a}^{\dot{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}t+z&&x-w\\
x+w&&t-z\end{pmatrix}.

(224)

Parity is the transformation
z\to-z
whereas time-reversal is
t\to-t
. The alternate transformations
x\to-x
and
w\to-w
are connected to the above choices via
SO(2)
spatial and temporal rotations respectively. Our aim is to translate these transformations to spinor helicity variables. A null Kleinian momenta is given by,

\displaystyle p_{a}^{\dot{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{t}+p_{z}&&p_{x}-p_{w}\\
p_{x}+p_{w}&&p_{t}-p_{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}&&-\lambda_{1}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\\
\lambda_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}&&-\lambda_{2}\tilde{\lambda}_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(225)

Under parity,
p_{z}
flips to
-p_{z}
. This leads to the following transformation for the spinors:

\displaystyle P:\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1},&&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}-\tilde{\lambda}_{1},&&\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(226)

which is identical to the parity transformation on the spinors in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
(
170
) after a dimensional reduction.
What about under time-reversal? Lets say we flip
t\to-t
. Then should one flip
p_{x},p_{z}
and
p_{w}
to
-p_{x},-p_{z}
and
-p_{w}
? In Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, time-reversal is anti-linear and thus under
t\to-t
,
(p_{t},p_{x},p_{z},p_{y})\to(p_{t},-p_{x},-p_{z},-p_{y})
. This also ensures that energy, that is
p_{t}
is invariant under a time-reversal. Klein space is connected to Minkowski space by a Wick rotation
p_{y}=ip_{w}
. Thus,
p_{y}
flipping under time-reversal implies that
p_{w}
must not flip since
i\to-i
takes care of the sign flip. Therefore, the Klein space time-reversal should not change
p_{w}\to-p_{w}
. Thus we have time-reveral taking
(p_{t},p_{w},p_{x},p_{z})\to(p_{t},p_{w},-p_{x},-p_{z})
. On the spinors it amounts to,

\displaystyle T:\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1},&&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{2},&&\lambda_{1},\end{pmatrix}

(227)

and similarly for
\tilde{\lambda}
. This leads to the same time-reversal operation we defined in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
in (
165
) after a dimensional reduction.

In short, what we have shown is that the discrete symmetries of four dimensional Klein space naturally give rise to the discrete symmetries of three dimensional Minkowski space.

Appendix I
Derivation of Ward-Takahashi identity for a time ordered correlator

In this appendix, we show how the current conservation Ward-Takahashi identity for a time ordered correlator arises in the canonical approach.
A time ordered three point function of a
U(1)
current
J^{\mu}
and two charged scalars
\phi,\chi
is given by,

\displaystyle\langle 0|T\{J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\theta(t_{12})\theta(t_{23})\langle 0|J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})|0\rangle+\theta(t_{13})\theta(t_{32})\langle 0|J^{\mu}(x_{1})\chi(x_{3})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle+\theta(t_{21})\theta(t_{13})\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})J^{\mu}(x_{1})\chi(x_{3})|0\rangle+\theta(t_{23})\theta(t_{31})\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})J^{\mu}(x_{1})|0\rangle

\displaystyle+\theta(t_{31})\theta(t_{12})\langle 0|\chi(x_{3})J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle+\theta(t_{32})\theta(t_{21})\langle 0|\chi(x_{3})\phi(x_{2})J^{\mu}(x_{1})|0\rangle,

(228)

where we used the shorthand
t_{ij}=t_{i}-t_{j}
. Let us take the derivative of (
228
) with respect to
\partial_{1\mu}
. The only contributions are when it acts on the Heaviside theta functions as the six Wightman functions appearing on the RHS are identically conserved. The result is,

\displaystyle\partial_{1\mu}\langle 0|T\{J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta(t_{12})\theta(t_{23})\langle 0|J^{0}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})|0\rangle+\delta(t_{13})\theta(t_{32})\langle 0|J^{0}(x_{1})\chi(x_{3})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle-\delta(t_{12})\theta(t_{13})\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})J^{0}(x_{1})\chi(x_{3})|0\rangle+\theta(t_{21})\delta(t_{13})\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})J^{0}(x_{1})\chi(x_{3})|0\rangle

\displaystyle-\theta(t_{23})\delta(t_{31})\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})J^{0}(x_{1})|0\rangle-\delta(t_{31})\theta(t_{12})\langle 0|\chi(x_{3})J^{0}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle+\theta(t_{31})\delta(t_{12})\langle 0|\chi(x_{3})J^{0}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle-\theta(t_{32})\delta(t_{21})\langle 0|\chi(x_{3})\phi(x_{2})J^{0}(x_{1})|0\rangle.

(229)

Grouping like terms together, this quantity can be written as,

\displaystyle\partial_{1\mu}\langle 0|T\{J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle=\delta(t_{12})\langle 0|T\{[J^{0}(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2})]\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle+\delta(t_{13})\langle 0|T\{\phi(x_{2})[J^{0}(x_{1}),\chi(x_{3})]|0\rangle.

(230)

Recall that,

\displaystyle[Q,\phi(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})]=\int_{\Sigma_{t_{2}}}d^{d-1}\vec{x}[J^{0}(t_{2},\vec{x}),\phi(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})]=q_{\phi}~\phi(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2}),

\displaystyle[Q,\chi(t_{3},\vec{x}_{3})]=\int_{\Sigma_{t_{3}}}d^{d-1}\vec{x}[J^{0}(t_{3},\vec{x}),\chi(t_{3},\vec{x}_{3})]=q_{\chi}~\chi(t_{3},\vec{x}_{3}).

(231)

This implies,

\displaystyle[J^{0}(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2})]=q_{\phi}~\delta^{d-1}(\vec{x}_{12})\phi(x_{2})~,~[J^{0}(x_{1}),\chi(x_{3})]=q_{\chi}~\delta^{d-1}(\vec{x}_{13})\chi(x_{3}).

(232)

Substituting these commutators back in (
230
) we obtain the familiar charge conservation Ward-Takahashi identity:

\displaystyle\partial_{1\mu}\langle 0|T\{J^{\mu}(x_{1})\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle=\big(q_{\phi}\delta^{d}(x_{12})+q_{\chi}\delta^{d}(x_{13})\big)\langle 0|T\{\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle.

(233)

Integrating (
233
) with respect to
x_{1}
over the entire manifold
\mathbb{R}^{1,d-1}
, we obtain (after using Gauss’ theorem on the LHS),

\displaystyle 0=(q_{\phi}+q_{\chi})\langle 0|T\{\phi(x_{2})\chi(x_{3})\}|0\rangle\implies q_{\chi}=-q_{\phi},

(234)

thus imposing conservation of charge.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}d^{d}x_{3}e^{ip_{1}\cdot x_{1}+ip_{2}\cdot x_{2}+p_{3}\cdot x_{3}}~\mathbf{ff}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}).

\displaystyle x_{12}^{\mu}=x_{1}^{\mu}-x_{2}^{\mu},x_{23}^{\mu}=x_{2}^{\mu}-x_{3}^{\mu},X^{\mu}=\frac{x_{1}^{\mu}+x_{2}^{\mu}+x_{3}^{\mu}}{3}.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{d^{d}x_{12}d^{d}x_{23}e^{ip_{1}\cdot x_{12}+i(p_{1}+p_{2})\cdot x_{23}}}{\big(-t_{12}^{2}+\vec{x}_{12}^{2}+i\epsilon t_{12}\big)^{\alpha}\big(-t_{23}^{2}+\vec{x}_{23}^{2}+i\epsilon t_{23}\big)^{\beta}\big(-(t_{12}+t_{23})^{2}+(\vec{x}_{12}+\vec{x}_{23})^{2}+i\epsilon(t_{12}+t_{23})\big)^{\gamma}}.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}dt_{12}d^{d-1}\vec{x}_{12}\frac{e^{-ip_{1}^{0}t_{12}+i\vec{p}_{1}\cdot\vec{x}_{12}}}{\big(-t_{12}^{2}+\vec{x}_{12}^{2}+i\epsilon t_{12}\big)^{\alpha}\big(-(t_{12}+t_{23})^{2}+(\vec{x}_{12}+\vec{x}_{23})^{2}+i\epsilon(t_{12}+t_{23})\big)^{\gamma}}.

(242)

J.2
Wightman function conjugation property

Two point correlators

In position space, the two point Wightman functions of a scalar
O_{\Delta}
are given by

\displaystyle\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle=\frac{1}{(-t_{12}^{2}+\vec{x}_{12}^{2}+i\epsilon t_{12})^{\Delta}},\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})|0\rangle=\frac{1}{(-t_{21}^{2}+\vec{x}_{21}^{2}+i\epsilon t_{21})^{\Delta}},

(243)

\epsilon>0

It is clear that the complex conjugation of the first Wightman function results in the second as
(i\epsilon t_{12})^{*}=-i\epsilon t_{12}=i\epsilon t_{21}

\displaystyle\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle^{*}=\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})|0\rangle.

(244)

Thus we see that complex conjugation reverses the operator ordering. Let us derive the analogous statement in Fourier space. We have by definition,

\displaystyle\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(p_{1})O_{\Delta}(p_{2})|0\rangle=\int~d^{3}x_{1}\;d^{3}x_{2}~e^{ip_{1}\cdot x_{1}+ip_{2}\cdot x_{2}}\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle.

(245)

Taking complex conjugate on both sides we get,

\displaystyle\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(p_{1})O_{\Delta}(p_{2})|0\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle=\int~d^{3}x_{1}~d^{3}x_{2}~e^{-ip_{1}\cdot x_{1}-ip_{2}\cdot x_{2}}\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})|0\rangle^{*}

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x_{1}~d^{3}x_{2}~e^{-ip_{1}\cdot x_{1}-ip_{2}\cdot\vec{x}_{2}}\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(t_{2},\vec{x}_{2})O_{\Delta}(t_{1},\vec{x}_{1})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(-p_{2})O_{\Delta}(-p_{1})|0\rangle.

(246)

Thus, in Fourier space, we see that complex conjugation reverses the order of the operators and flips the sign of the momenta.
Let us illustrate this for the two point function in momentum space (
251

\displaystyle\langle 0|O_{\Delta}(p_{1})O_{\Delta}(p_{2})|0\rangle\propto(-p_{1}^{2})^{\Delta-\frac{d}{2}}\theta(-p_{1}^{0}-|\vec{p}_{1}|)(2\pi)^{d}\delta^{d}(p_{1}+p_{2}),

It is easy to see that the above reality condition is indeed satisifed.

K.3
Parity and time-reversal odd two point functions

In the special case of three dimensions, the two point function of conserved currents can acquire a parity odd contribution. In Euclidean momentum space it is the following:

\displaystyle\langle J_{s}(p_{1},z_{1})J_{s}(p_{2},z_{2})\rangle_{\text{odd}}=c_{s}^{(\text{odd})}\frac{\epsilon^{z_{1}z_{2}p_{1}}}{p_{1}}(z_{1}\cdot z_{2})^{s-1}p_{1}^{2s-1}.

This quantity can be obtained by epsilon transforming the parity even correlator

\displaystyle\langle J_{s}^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p_{1})J_{s}^{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}(p_{2})\rangle_{\text{odd}}\propto\frac{1}{p_{1}}{\epsilon^{p_{1}\alpha(\mu_{1}}}\langle J_{s}^{\alpha\cdots\mu_{s})}(p_{1})J_{s}^{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}(p_{2})\rangle_{\text{even}}.

The analytic continuation to obtain the Wightman function is identical to (

\displaystyle\langle 0|J_{s}(p_{1},z_{1})J_{s}(p_{2},z_{2})|0\rangle_{\text{odd}}=c_{s}^{(\text{odd})}\frac{\epsilon^{z_{1}z_{2}p_{1}}}{p_{1}}(z_{1}\cdot z_{2})^{s-1}\text{Disc}_{p_{1}^{2}}\bigg(p_{1}^{2s-1}\bigg).

(261)

Note however, that the

\displaystyle\langle 0|J_{s}^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p_{1})J_{s}^{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}(p_{2})|0\rangle_{\text{odd}}\propto\frac{1}{p_{1}}{\epsilon^{p_{1}\alpha(\mu_{1}}}\langle 0|J_{s}^{\alpha\cdots\mu_{s})}(p_{1})J_{s}^{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}(p_{2})|0\rangle_{\text{even}}.

(262)

K.4
General structure of Wightman function of currents in spinor helicity variable

Converting the above momentum space expressions into spinor helicity variables, we see that the general structure for the two-point function of conserved currents in the two helicity configurations, i.e. (- -) and (++) are respectively given as:

\displaystyle\langle 0|J_{s}^{-}(\lambda_{1},\bar{\lambda}_{1})J_{s}^{-}(\lambda_{2},\bar{\lambda}_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle\sim(c_{s}^{(even)}-\textit{i}c_{s}^{(odd)})\frac{\langle 12\rangle^{2s}}{p_{1}},

\displaystyle\langle 0|J_{s}^{+}(\lambda_{1},\bar{\lambda}_{1})J_{s}^{+}(\lambda_{2},\bar{\lambda}_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle\sim(c_{s}^{(even)}+\textit{i}c_{s}^{(odd)})\frac{\langle\bar{1}\bar{2}\rangle^{2s}}{p_{1}}.

(263)

L.6
An example with a scalar operator

Consider the correlator
\langle JJO_{1}\rangle
. In Euclidean space it is given by,

\displaystyle\langle J(z_{1},p_{1})J(z_{2},p_{2})O_{1}(p_{3})\rangle_{even}=

\displaystyle z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\Big(\frac{p_{3}^{2}-(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}}{2p_{3}E^{2}}\Big)-z_{1}\cdot p_{2}\;z_{2}\cdot z_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{3}E^{2}}\Big).

\displaystyle\langle 0|J(z_{1},p_{1})J(z_{2},p_{2})O_{1}(p_{3})|0\rangle_{even}=

\displaystyle z_{1}\cdot z_{2}\Big(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}}{E(E-2p_{1})(E-2p_{2})(E-2p_{3})}\Big)

(284)

A similar analysis can be performed for the parity odd case although we do not present the explicit details here.
