Effectiveness of large language model-based teaching interventions in medical education: a systematic review and stratified meta-analysis of 14 studies
Authors: Xiaoliu Liu, Jiayue Pan, Chengyi Zhang, Xiaomei Wang, Jinghua Wan, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, artificial intelligence has been adopted as an innovative educational approach and may substantially reshape health-care delivery systems. This systematic review and stratified meta-analysis compared large language model (LLM)-based teaching interventions with traditional teaching approaches in medical education.MethodsBased on a four-database search conducted through 31 March 2026, we included comparative studies and feasibility studies evaluating LLM-based teaching tools in medical education learners. This review was not prospectively registered. It evolved from a broader evidence-mapping exercise into a formal systematic review with meta-analysis as the intervention literature and analyzable outcomes became clearer, and no publicly archived protocol was available. Risk of bias was appraised with design-specific RoB 2-, ROBINS-I-, or JBI-informed domains. Domain-specific quantitative synthesis focused on strict knowledge acquisition, delayed retention, and structured clinical performance. An inverse-variance random-effects model was used for pooled SMD analyses. The broader post-course score-based synthesis was retained only as an exploratory summary across related but non-identical outcomes. Studies not directly compatible with a parallel-group SMD framework were retained through classification summary figures and single-study exploratory displays. Certainty of evidence was judged using a GRADE-informed domain-level approach.ResultsSearches of four databases identified 8103 records (PubMed n = 2203, Web of Science Core Collection n = 2388, Embase n = 1790, and Cochrane Library n = 1722). After removal of 5652 duplicate records and 1251 non-full-text items, 1200 records underwent title/abstract screening, 25 full-text articles were assessed, and 14 studies comprising 1207 analyzable learners were included in the systematic review. Across the domain-specific pooled analyses, structured clinical performance showed the clearest positive signal (2 studies; 38 vs. 39 participants; SMD 3.39, 95% CI 1.43 to 5.35), strict knowledge acquisition showed a smaller but still positive signal (5 studies; SMD 0.80, 95% CI 0.07 to 1.53), and delayed retention showed no clear difference (2 studies; SMD 0.15, 95% CI -0.50 to 0.80). This structured clinical performance estimate was based on a very small evidence base and may be inflated by small-study effects, selective reporting, or measurement artifacts. An exploratory pooled synthesis of post-course score-based outcomes included 7 studies (248 vs. 251 participants) and suggested an advantage for LLM-based teaching (SMD 0.90, 95% CI 0.18 to 1.62, I² = 92.5%, P = 0.015), although heterogeneity across constructs, learner groups, and intervention modes was substantial. Exploratory subgroup analysis suggested a larger effect in practice courses than in theory courses, although this signal was driven by only 2 practice-oriented studies and should be interpreted cautiously. Seven studies did not enter the pooled score-based synthesis directly; 1 was retained in the structured clinical performance supplementary meta-analysis and 6 were preserved through classification summary figures and single-study exploratory displays, thereby allowing all 14 included studies to remain within the overall results framework. GRADE-informed certainty ranged from very low to low across pooled domains.ConclusionsIn medical education, current evidence provides low-certainty signals that some LLM-based teaching interventions may improve structured clinical performance and certain short-term knowledge outcomes, whereas delayed retention remains uncertain. The broader pooled score-based synthesis should be interpreted only as an exploratory summary across heterogeneous educational constructs, learner groups, and intervention modes, not as a single universal endpoint. Because several pooled domains were based on only 2 to 5 studies and overall certainty remained low to very low, these findings should be regarded as cautious signals rather than definitive estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09879-0.Keywords: Large language models, Medical education, Meta-analysis, Systematic review, ChatGPT, Teaching interventions

Background

In recent years, rapid advances in large language models (LLMs) have moved their use in medical education from conceptual discussion to course-level implementation and outcome evaluation [1]. Unlike conventional artificial intelligence systems, LLM-based tools can generate learning materials, case scripts, and individualized feedback through natural-language interaction, and can also support teaching activities such as history taking, case discussion, clinical reasoning, and formative assessment [2]. In a field that depends heavily on knowledge integration, contextualized training, and continuous feedback, these tools offer new possibilities for improving the timeliness, personalization, and accessibility of educational support [3].

Existing studies suggest that the educational value of LLM-based tools in medical education may lie in at least three areas [4]. First, they can function as continuously available learning support tools for pre-class preparation, in-class interaction, and post-class consolidation, thereby improving access to immediate feedback and the efficiency of self-directed learning [5]. Second, in settings such as simulated patients, case-based progression, structured feedback, and clinical decision-making training, LLM-based tools may promote higher-order cognitive processing and skills practice through multi-turn dialogue and task guidance [6]. Third, where faculty time is limited or educational resources are unevenly distributed, LLM-based tools may reduce repetitive teaching burdens and broaden access to high-quality learning resources [1].

However, the true educational value of LLM-based teaching remains debated [1]. Previous meta-analyses suggest that LLM-based or broader AI-assisted teaching may improve immediate test performance and learner engagement [4]. Li et al. reported no significant advantage for theoretical knowledge scores (SMD 0.27) but a moderate benefit for practical skills (SMD 0.63) [3], whereas Peng et al. found substantial improvements in examination performance (SMD 2.06) and learning satisfaction (OR 5.80), although these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of high heterogeneity (I² = 94%) and small sample sizes [4]. Preiksaitis et al. also highlighted the potential of LLM-enabled tools to support active learning and immediate feedback [1]. At the same time, concerns remain regarding hallucinations, opaque reasoning pathways, and unstable output quality [7]. Differences in course type, teacher involvement, intervention duration, and outcome measures further limit comparability and generalizability across studies [2]. Moreover, some reviews have pooled image-recognition systems, adaptive learning platforms, and generative conversational tools together, which may obscure the intervention-specific effects of LLM-based teaching tools [4]. Interest in educational applications also accelerated after reports that ChatGPT achieved near-pass performance on the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) [8].

Against this background, we systematically reviewed and synthesized the available evidence comparing LLM-based teaching with traditional teaching in medical education. We prioritized domain-specific synthesis for strict knowledge acquisition, delayed retention, and structured clinical performance, while retaining a broader pooled synthesis of post-course score-based outcomes only as an exploratory summary when related endpoints could not be cleanly separated. Studies that could not be entered directly into pooled analyses because of crossover designs, team-level analyses, single-arm feasibility designs, or non-standard reporting were preserved through classification-aware and single-study exploratory displays. We aimed to answer two questions: whether LLM-based teaching shows measurable advantages over traditional teaching, and in which educational contexts and outcome domains these advantages are most likely to appear.

Compared with the recent meta-analysis by Li et al., which focused on randomized controlled trials in medical students and analyzed knowledge, skills, and satisfaction as separate conventional endpoints, the present review addressed a broader educational evidence base. We included medical students, nursing students, and residents; retained design-diverse studies beyond parallel-group RCTs; regrouped outcomes into four domains more closely aligned with the available data structure; and preserved studies that could not be validly pooled through design-aware exploratory displays. Our aim was therefore not simply to reproduce a previous pooled estimate, but to examine how a wider and more heterogeneous body of evidence on LLM-based teaching can be synthesized without discarding methodologically informative studies.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the PRISMA 2020 statement9. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary file 2. The review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, INPLASY, or OSF; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Because all analyses were based on previously published studies, ethics approval and patient consent were not required. The review evolved from a broader evidence-mapping exercise into a formal systematic review with meta-analysis as the intervention literature and analyzable outcomes became clearer. No prospectively registered or publicly archived protocol was available, and by the time the final review framework had stabilized, screening and outcome regrouping were already underway; we therefore avoided treating later synthesis choices as protocol-level prespecifications.

Search strategy and study eligibility

The search strategy covered PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, and the Cochrane Library through 31 March 2026. These searches identified 8103 records, from which 5652 duplicate records and 1251 non-full-text items were removed before title/abstract screening. The core concept was the use of LLM-based teaching tools in medical education learners. Primary outcomes were knowledge acquisition, examination performance, or clinical performance; secondary outcomes included delayed retention, self-directed learning, critical thinking, satisfaction, and cognitive load. Records were screened in two stages (title/abstract and full text), and citation chasing of relevant reviews and included studies was used to identify additional eligible articles. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary file 1.

Studies were eligible if they: (1) involved medical education learners, including medical students, nursing students, or residents; (2) evaluated an LLM-based educational tool or its curricular integration; (3) used a traditional teaching comparator, teacher feedback, or non-AI comparator, or were single-arm feasibility studies with extractable quantitative outcomes; (4) reported examination scores, knowledge acquisition, clinical performance, satisfaction, critical thinking, or related learning outcomes; and (5) were available in full-text English. Exclusion criteria included cross-sectional attitude/knowledge surveys, pure model-performance studies, commentary-style publications without extractable educational outcomes, and studies that did not clearly involve an LLM-based educational intervention. Eligible interventions could involve predominantly standalone learner-AI interaction, curriculum-integrated AI use, or AI plus explicit faculty guidance. We retained medical students, nursing students, and residents to preserve a broader medical-education evidence base, but this breadth introduced heterogeneity in curriculum structure, assessment expectations, and learning context. These learner groups were therefore not assumed to be educationally interchangeable when interpreting pooled estimates.

Data extraction, outcome regrouping, and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data according to the final review eligibility framework. The extracted information included first author, publication year, course title, sample size, learner characteristics, intervention method, study duration, and outcomes. The two reviewers then compared extraction results and resolved discrepancies by discussion, with a third reviewer adjudicating when necessary. We also classified the educational function of the AI intervention in each study, including tutoring, automated feedback, simulated patient interaction, clinical reasoning support, case generation, terminology learning, and broader learning support. We additionally recorded instructional mode, distinguishing predominantly standalone learner-AI interaction, curriculum-integrated use, AI-generated materials, and explicit AI-plus-faculty-guidance implementation.

Drawing on previous meta-analyses and the heterogeneity of the included evidence, we regrouped directly quantifiable outcomes into four domains: exploratory post-course score-based outcomes, strict knowledge acquisition, delayed retention, and structured clinical performance 3. The broad post-course score-based synthesis was treated as exploratory because it combines related score-based educational endpoints rather than a single latent construct. For studies that still could not be entered directly into pooled analyses, we defined three retention pathways: design incompatibility with a parallel-group SMD framework (for example crossover or sequential intervention studies); studies that stably reported direct effects but lacked key arm-level tables; and studies with outcome domains that were better represented at the team level or in single-arm form 10. Study-level analyzable totals were counted once per study, including for multi-arm and crossover designs, to avoid double counting in descriptive sample summaries. A study-by-study outcome regrouping table is provided in Supplementary file 3.

Risk of bias and statistical analysis

Methodological appraisal was design-specific. Randomized parallel-group studies were judged with a RoB 2-compatible framework, the crossover and team-randomized studies with RoB 2-informed crossover/cluster considerations, non-randomized comparative designs with ROBINS-I-informed domains, and the single-arm feasibility study with a JBI-style checklist11-13. For summary visualization, these design-specific judgments were mapped descriptively into six harmonized display domains: sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants/teachers, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and other bias. This mapping was used only to improve readability across mixed study designs and was not intended as a mathematically merged or separately validated single risk-of-bias tool. No Newcastle-Ottawa scale was used in this review. Funnel plots were used descriptively only for the exploratory pooled score-based synthesis; because only seven studies contributed, no formal asymmetry test was emphasized.

Quantitative synthesis prioritized interpretable outcome domains. Supplementary pooled analyses were conducted for strict knowledge acquisition, delayed retention, and structured clinical performance. An additional broader pooled synthesis of post-course score-based outcomes was retained only as an exploratory summary to avoid excluding score-based studies that reported related but not identical endpoints. When conceptually related score outcomes could not be separated into cleaner domain-specific pools, SMD was used as a shared metric for exploratory synthesis rather than as evidence that all outcomes measured the same latent construct. Pooled meta-analyses used an inverse-variance random-effects framework with Hedges g/SMD effect measures and a DerSimonian-Laird tau² estimator, and I² was interpreted cautiously in light of the marked educational heterogeneity across studies. Exploratory subgroup analyses within that pooled score-based synthesis were limited to course type (theory vs. practice) and learning duration (< 1 week vs. > = 1 week). Because the contributing studies were concentrated in similar regions and were mostly assessed immediately after the course, geographic location and assessment timing were not analyzed separately. Because the number of studies in several pooled domains was very small, subgroup findings and funnel-plot asymmetry were interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive inferential tests. For single-study exploratory results, only the originally reported MD, SMD, or proportion with 95% confidence intervals was displayed, without cross-study pooling. Certainty of evidence for the pooled domains was judged using a pragmatic GRADE-informed approach14. Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4 and Python.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Database searching identified 8103 records (PubMed n = 2203, Web of Science Core Collection n = 2388, Embase n = 1790, and Cochrane Library n = 1722). After removal of 5652 duplicate records and 1251 non-full-text items, 1200 records underwent title/abstract screening. Of these, 1175 were excluded and 25 full-text articles underwent eligibility assessment. Eleven studies were excluded, and 14 studies were ultimately included in the systematic review. The main reasons for exclusion were cross-sectional perception/attitude studies without an eligible comparative educational intervention (n = 7), the absence of a non-AI comparator or feasibility-only descriptions (n = 3), and interventions that were not clearly based on generative AI/LLM educational tools (n = 1). Across the 14 included studies, the analyzable study-level sample totaled 1207 learners. The included studies were published between 2024 and 2026 and involved undergraduate medical students, nursing students, and residents; learning contexts ranged from brief online terminology exercises to full 8-week courses. The included evidence was heterogeneous not only in learner group but also in instructional mode, ranging from predominantly standalone AI use to curriculum-integrated or faculty-guided implementation (Table 1). Before considering the broader exploratory pooled score-based synthesis, the domain-specific pooled analyses suggested the clearest signal in structured clinical performance, a smaller positive signal in strict knowledge acquisition, and no clear advantage in delayed retention. Figure 1 shows the study selection process. Representative examples across the included comparative studies were Kalam 2025, Kejingyun 2025, Hsu 2023, Gan 2024, and Çiçek 2024 15–19.

Exploratory pooled synthesis of post-course score-based outcomes

Seven studies (248 intervention participants and 251 control participants) entered an exploratory pooled synthesis of post-course score-based outcomes (Table 1).

The pooled analysis suggested that LLM-based teaching was associated with higher post-course score-based outcomes than non-AI control teaching, with a pooled effect size of SMD 0.90 (95% CI 0.18 to 1.62; I² = 92.5%; P = 0.015). However, because this synthesis combines related but not identical score-based educational constructs, it should be interpreted only as an exploratory summary rather than as a single universal endpoint. It should also not be read as a single overall effect of LLM-based or generative-AI teaching across medical education. Heterogeneity was high, suggesting that course type, learning duration, comparison framework, learner group, and instructional mode may all have contributed to the observed dispersion of effects (Fig. 2).

Table 2 presents a descriptive summary of methodological appraisal across design types. Because the included evidence was mixed rather than exclusively randomized, the table should be read as a descriptive display of design-specific judgments across broad domains rather than as the output of a single one-size-fits-all tool. No overall score was calculated, and the table does not represent a mathematical merger of different appraisal instruments; no Newcastle-Ottawa scale was used. Across the mapped domains, limitations were most visible in allocation concealment, participant/teacher blinding, and other design-sensitive sources of bias, whereas incomplete outcome data were generally handled better. Other bias mainly reflected baseline imbalance, design complexity, or inconsistency between the unit of randomization and the unit of analysis.

Figure 3 shows the funnel plot for the exploratory pooled score-based synthesis. Some asymmetry was visible, suggesting possible small-study effects. However, because only seven studies were included and heterogeneity was substantial, the funnel plot should be regarded as a descriptive quality assessment tool rather than direct evidence of publication bias. Methodologically, the included studies were also commonly affected by limited blinding of participants and teachers, insufficient reporting of allocation concealment, partial use of non-randomized designs, possible contamination in crossover or cluster-style interventions, baseline imbalance, and inconsistency in cluster- or team-level analytical methods. These factors may have influenced the pooled effect estimate and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Subgroup analysis

Exploratory subgroup analysis showed a pooled effect of SMD 0.60 (95% CI -0.10 to 1.30) for theory courses and SMD 1.60 (95% CI -0.21 to 3.41) for practice courses. The fixed-effect interaction test for course type suggested a statistical signal (Qbetween = 24.31, P = 8.2e-07), but this result was driven mainly by only two practice-oriented studies and within-group heterogeneity remained high. It should therefore be treated as an exploratory finding rather than a definitive conclusion. No clear interaction was observed for learning duration (Table 3).

Supplementary domain-specific meta-analyses

The seven studies that did not enter the pooled score-based synthesis directly were not excluded from the results framework. Brügge 2024 was incorporated into the supplementary meta-analysis of structured clinical performance. Daniel 2026 and Nissen 2025 were retained as exploratory displays of adjusted sequential differences and approximate paired crossover differences because their designs were not compatible with a parallel-group SMD framework. Shalong 2025 was retained as a direct-effect display because only between-group differences and 95% confidence intervals could be stably extracted. Gustafsson 2026, Arkan 2025, and Zhang 2025 were displayed separately or summarized through classification figures because they represented team-level results, self-rated problem-solving ability, or single-arm endorsement proportions. Through this strategy, all 14 included studies remained visible in the results section rather than being ignored simply because they could not enter the same pooled model. Representative retained studies included Gustafsson 2026 [20], Arkan 2025 [21], Zhang 2025 [22], and Daniel 2026 [23].

The strict knowledge acquisition sensitivity analysis included 5 studies (164 vs. 164 participants; Fig. 4) and yielded a pooled effect of SMD 0.80 (95% CI 0.07 to 1.53; I² = 88.6%). Delayed retention included 2 studies (66 vs. 82 participants; Fig. 5), with a pooled effect of SMD 0.15 (95% CI -0.50 to 0.80). Structured clinical performance included 2 studies (38 vs. 39 participants), with a pooled effect of SMD 3.39 (95% CI 1.43 to 5.35) (Fig. 6). A GRADE-informed certainty assessment suggested very low certainty for the exploratory pooled score-based synthesis, low certainty for strict knowledge acquisition, very low certainty for delayed retention, and low certainty for structured clinical performance (Supplementary file 1). The structured clinical performance estimate should therefore be interpreted as a small-study signal from only 77 participants rather than as definitive evidence of a large true effect; selective reporting and measurement artifact cannot be excluded.

Figure 7A and C summarizes three typical situations in which studies were not appropriate for direct inclusion in the pooled analyses. Zhang 2025 was a single-arm feasibility study and could only display the high endorsement proportions reported by the same 20 students for LLM-generated case-based learning. Nissen 2025 was a crossover study, and the accessible full text provided only the final sample size, mean/SD values for two item categories, and a rounded P value; accordingly, only an approximate paired 95% confidence interval could be reconstructed, and the result remained close to no clear difference. Gustafsson 2026 used teams as both the randomization and analysis unit, and the currently usable quantitative information was limited to the raw teamwork scores directly reported for four LLM teams and four control teams. Together, these three examples show that for single-arm studies, crossover designs, and cluster/team-level pilot trials, preserving design boundaries through exploratory display is more appropriate than forcing them into a parallel-group SMD model (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study adopted a strategy of narrative synthesis first and meta-analysis only for outcomes that were sufficiently homogeneous. The most interpretable quantitative findings came from the domain-specific analyses: strict knowledge acquisition showed a positive signal, delayed retention did not improve consistently, and structured clinical performance showed the largest pooled effect. The broader pooled score-based synthesis also suggested an advantage, but because it combines related score constructs rather than a single endpoint, it should be interpreted as an exploratory summary. Taken together, these findings provide only cautious, low-certainty signals of benefit in some short-term and structured-training settings, not a broadly transportable estimate across all learner groups, intervention modes, or educational constructs.

Broadly consistent with previous meta-analyses, the present review again supports the overall trend that LLM-based teaching may outperform traditional teaching, but it also emphasizes that this advantage is outcome-dependent [3, 4]. The advantage is most apparent in knowledge tests, standardized tasks, and some practice-oriented settings, whereas gains in higher-order clinical decision making appear more limited [4]. Li et al. found no statistically significant overall benefit for knowledge acquisition (SMD 0.27) but better practical skills and learning satisfaction with GAI [3]. Peng et al. likewise reported an overall benefit of AI-assisted teaching for examination performance and learning satisfaction among Chinese undergraduate medical students, although heterogeneity was high [4]. Related syntheses also suggest that the advantages of generative AI in open-ended clinical reasoning and complex problem solving are not stable [24]. In other words, gains are easier to observe in immediate performance, a pattern that is consistent with the active-learning literature [25]. The testing effect in medical education may also amplify short-term score improvements [26]. Accordingly, the broader pooled synthesis should not be read as a single overall effect of generative or LLM-based AI across medical education.

At the same time, the present review is not merely an expanded replication of Li et al. Their synthesis was intentionally restricted to randomized controlled trials in medical students and to three relatively conventional endpoint groups: knowledge, skills, and satisfaction [3]. By contrast, our review addressed a broader evidence base that also included nursing students and residents, incorporated design-diverse studies such as crossover, sequential, team-level, and feasibility designs, and reorganized outcomes into domains that better reflected how the available educational data were actually reported. This broader scope likely explains why our exploratory pooled score-based synthesis captures a wider performance signal while still showing uncertainty for delayed retention. The contribution of the present review is therefore complementary: it extends the question from whether LLM-based teaching improves conventional RCT-compatible outcomes to how heterogeneous educational evidence can be retained, interpreted, and compared without forcing methodologically incompatible studies into a single pooled model. This broader scope improved evidence coverage but also increased heterogeneity in learner background, course structure, assessment requirements, and delivery mode. Nursing students, medical students, and residents were therefore not treated as educationally interchangeable when interpreting pooled estimates.

Among the included studies, the clearest signals appeared in case-based progression, history-taking practice, simulated patients, and structured feedback training [6]. These settings share features of clearly defined tasks, immediate feedback, and repeated practice, which align closely with the technical strengths of LLM-based tools [27]. For example, GPT-based history-taking training showed favorable signals of feasibility and learning improvement [28]. Under simulated-patient and structured-feedback conditions, clinical decision scores improved among medical students [6]. In ultrasonography teaching, a combined AI-plus-faculty-guidance approach outperformed both AI-only and traditional teaching [29]. The subgroup findings still require cautious interpretation because they were derived from small samples and high heterogeneity [4]. At the same time, some studies suggest that overreliance on AI in the absence of teacher supervision may weaken independent critical thinking and introduce safety risks in higher-stakes decision making [30]. We therefore interpret these findings as directional signals rather than definitive conclusions [31]. This pattern suggests that some favorable effects may reflect guided integration of AI into teaching rather than exposure to AI alone, so AI-only and faculty-guided implementations should not be assumed to have equivalent educational effects.

This review preserved evidence types that could not be directly incorporated into pooled analyses, including crossover studies, sequential interventions, team-level randomized trials, and single-arm or other non-parallel feasibility studies, thereby reducing information loss [32]. This approach is more consistent with the actual evidence structure of emerging educational technology research [1]. Methodologically, the included studies were inconsistent in randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, and outcome reporting [33]. Differences in course duration, intervention intensity, comparators, and assessment tools were substantial and likely contributed to high overall heterogeneity [4]. These findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence on LLM-based educational interventions, particularly ChatGPT or similar systems. The overall certainty of evidence ranged from very low to low [14]. Pooled effect sizes should therefore be interpreted together with the risk-of-bias and certainty profile. In practical terms, LLM-based teaching is better positioned as an augmentative teaching tool rather than a replacement for teaching itself [34]. Educational design should emphasize faculty guidance, prompt governance, source verification, and academic-integrity management to reduce the risks of shallow dependence and internalization of AI-generated errors [31]. This review also has limitations: the review was not prospectively registered; several studies reported only partial arm-level data; pooled domains were often supported by small samples; and longer-term transfer beyond immediate or short-term post-course performance remains under-studied. More high-quality studies are needed to establish long-term effectiveness and safety in authentic educational settings [35]. Part of this heterogeneity likely reflects the coexistence of different learner groups and different instructional modes, from standalone AI use to faculty-guided integration. Because the review evolved from a broader evidence-mapping exercise, was not prospectively registered, and had no public protocol available, these pooled estimates should be interpreted conservatively alongside the low certainty profile.

Conclusions

In summary, current evidence provides low- to very-low-certainty and heterogeneous signals that some LLM-based teaching interventions may improve structured clinical performance and certain short-term knowledge outcomes, but these signals arise from small and methodologically diverse studies. The structured clinical performance estimate in particular was based on only 2 studies (38 vs. 39 participants) and may overstate the true effect. LLM-based tools are therefore best viewed as context-dependent adjuncts, especially when integrated with faculty guidance, rather than as replacements for conventional teaching.
