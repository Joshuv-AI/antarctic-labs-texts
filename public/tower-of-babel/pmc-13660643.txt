Deep learning-based metal artifact reduction in CT for head and neck radiotherapy
Authors: Ehsan Mihandoust, Peyman Sheikhzadeh, Saeedeh Navaei Lavasani, Hossein Arabi, Davoud Ahmadvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeMetal artifacts from dental fillings and implants compromise CT image quality, target delineation, and dose calculation accuracy in head and neck (H&N) radiotherapy. Previous reviews have largely focused on traditional metal artifact reduction methods and image quality metrics rather than radiotherapy-specific endpoints. This systematic review evaluates deep learning-based metal artifact reduction (DL-MAR) approaches for H&N radiotherapy, emphasizing technical advances and clinical dosimetric validation.Materials and methodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus were systematically searched (updated April 2026) for studies assessing DL-MAR methods in H&N CT imaging and radiotherapy. Of 227 identified records, 40 studies (11 traditional MAR and 29 DL-MAR studies) met the inclusion criteria.ResultsReported DL architectures included convolutional neural networks, generative adversarial networks, dual-domain networks, diffusion models, and task-oriented loss functions. Among studies with clinical dosimetric validation, GAN-based and task-oriented approaches consistently reported dose deviations below 3%. Gamma pass rates exceeding 95% (3%/3 mm) were reported in a subset of these studies. Dual-domain and diffusion-based methods were evaluated primarily using image quality metrics without direct radiotherapy-specific dosimetric validation.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that DL-MAR may achieve clinically acceptable dosimetric accuracy while improving artifact suppression and target visualization in H&N radiotherapy planning. Task-oriented loss functions currently demonstrate promising potential for clinical translation based on available retrospective evidence. Broader clinical implementation remains limited by reliance on synthetic training data, restricted access to sinogram data, and the absence of prospective multicenter validation studies.Keywords: Metal artifact reduction, Deep learning, Head and neck radiotherapy, Dosimetric validation, Adaptive radiotherapy, Patient-specific quality assurance

Introduction

Computed tomography (CT) is essential for modern radiotherapy planning, providing the anatomical information required for accurate tumor delineation, organs-of-interest (OOIs) sparing, and dose calculation [1], [2], [3]. In head and neck (H&N) cancer, the presence of metallic implants, most commonly dental fillings or implants, can severely compromise CT image quality [4], [5], [6], [7]. These high-density materials may cause photon starvation, beam hardening, scatter, and edge effects, often manifesting as bright or dark streaking artifacts and distorted Hounsfield units (HU) [2], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. In the complex anatomy of the head and neck, where organs of interest lie close to potential metal sources, HU inaccuracies may produce dose errors of 5%–20%, depending on implant size, composition, and location [13], [14], [15]. Such deviations may lead to clinically significant target underdosage or critical structure overexposure, compromising local control and increasing normal tissue toxicity [2], [16]. In current clinical practice, manual density overrides (i.e., manually assigning water-equivalent HU values to artifact-affected regions) are commonly employed to mitigate dose calculation errors caused by metal artifacts. However, these approaches are time-consuming, operator-dependent, and prone to inter-observer variability [2], [17], [18]. Traditional metal artifact reduction (MAR) algorithms, such as linear interpolation (LI) [19], normalized MAR (NMAR) [20], [21], and vendor-specific tools (e.g., O-MAR, iMAR, Smart MAR) [21], [22], [23], [24], aim to mitigate artifacts through sinogram inpainting or iterative reconstruction. While these methods can offer partial improvement, they may introduce secondary artifacts, require manual metal segmentation, or prove insufficient in severe cases involving multiple dental implants [25], [26]. Consequently, residual dosimetric errors have been reported to persist in clinical practice [2], [15], [25].

The emergence of deep learning (DL) has introduced new paradigms for MAR, offering data-driven approaches that learn complex artifact patterns directly from images [11], [27], [28], [29]. Various architectures, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and dual-domain networks, have demonstrated improved performance in image quality metrics such as peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [27], [28], [30], [31], [32]. In H&N radiotherapy, recent clinical studies have reported that DL-MAR can achieve dosimetric errors below 3% and gamma pass rates exceeding 95% (3%/3 mm), with emerging applications in cone-beam CT (CBCT) and synthetic CT generation that may facilitate adaptive radiotherapy workflows [31], [32], [33].

Despite these advances, previous reviews on MAR have primarily examined algorithmic performance metrics such as PSNR and SSIM, with limited synthesis of radiotherapy-specific endpoints such as dose deviation, gamma pass rates, and dose-volume histogram (DVH)-based validation. Moreover, existing reviews have largely focused on traditional MAR methods, while the field has rapidly shifted toward DL-based solutions with emerging dosimetric validation. To date, a comprehensive synthesis that integrates both technical innovation and structured radiotherapy outcomes is lacking for H&N cancer.

This systematic review addresses this gap by evaluating DL-MAR approaches published between 2015 and 2026 through the dual lens of technical advances and clinical dosimetric validation in H&N radiotherapy. Drawing on 40 curated studies, we provide three key contributions: (1) systematic quantification of dosimetric accuracy across DL-MAR architecture categories, (2) a clear distinction between methods with direct dose validation (GAN-based, task-oriented loss) and those evaluated solely on image quality metrics (dual-domain, diffusion), and (3) identification of critical gaps (multicenter prospective trials, sinogram accessibility) that currently limit clinical translation. Ultimately, this review provides radiotherapy and imaging specialists, including radiation oncologists and medical physicists, with an evidence-based framework for evaluating the readiness of DL-MAR tools for integration into clinical treatment planning workflows and quality assurance protocols in H&N cancer.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [34] (Fig. 2). The review protocol was not registered in a public registry before the review was conducted.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram illustrating the study selection process for this systematic review.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus databases in October 2025 and updated in April 2026. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to metal artifact reduction, deep learning, radiotherapy, head and neck cancer, and computed tomography using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The detailed, database-specific search strings are provided in Supplementary File 1. Titles, abstracts, and full-text screening were managed using Covidence systematic review software.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) proposed or evaluated a MAR method (traditional or DL-based); (2) focused on CT imaging in H&N cancer radiotherapy; (3) reported quantitative results on artifact suppression, image quality (e.g., PSNR, SSIM, artifact index), or dosimetric accuracy (e.g., gamma pass rate, D95% dose deviation); (4) were peer-reviewed original articles or review papers; (5) were published between January 2015 and April 2026; (6) were written in English. Exclusion criteria were: (1) conference abstracts, editorials, book chapters, or non-peer-reviewed publications; (2) studies using modalities other than CT as the primary imaging modality (e.g., PET-CT, SPECT-CT, or MRI-only without concurrent CT validation); (3) studies without explicit relevance to H&N radiotherapy; (4) duplicate publications. References cited in this manuscript with publication dates prior to January 2015 are included solely as foundational background literature to explain historical concepts or established baseline methods; they are not counted among the 40 primary studies included in this systematic review.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records, followed by independent full-text assessment. Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form covering first author, year, DL architecture, training strategy, dataset type, evaluation metrics, key findings, and limitations.

Quality assessment

For studies reporting clinical or dosimetric validation, methodological quality was appraised independently by two reviewers based on three criteria: (1) use of clinical rather than solely synthetic data, (2) comparison with a clinically relevant baseline (e.g., uncorrected CT or commercial MAR), and (3) reporting of standardized dosimetric endpoints (e.g., gamma pass rates, dose-volume histogram parameters). Studies were classified into three quality levels: ‘High’ (meeting all three criteria), ‘Moderate’ (meeting two criteria), and ‘Low’ (meeting one or no criteria). This classification was used to weight the narrative synthesis, giving greater emphasis to High- and Moderate-quality studies in the Discussion and Conclusions. The specific quality rating assigned to each included study is reported in Table 3. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity in study designs, evaluation metrics, and reported outcomes, a formal meta-analysis was not feasible. A narrative synthesis was performed, grouping studies by MAR method type (traditional vs. DL-based), DL architecture, and validation approach. To clarify the strength of support for clinical translation, studies were stratified into four evidence levels: Level 1 (prospective clinical validation linking DL-MAR to patient outcomes), Level 2 (retrospective clinical dosimetric validation), Level 3 (retrospective clinical image quality validation), and Level 4 (phantom-based or simulated data). This classification was consistently applied in Table 3, Table 4. Notably, no studies met the criteria for Level 1, highlighting a critical gap in prospective clinical validation. No gray literature search was performed, which may introduce publication bias.

Results

Traditional metal artifact reduction methods

Traditional metal artifact reduction (MAR) methods can be categorized into four main groups, as described by Gjesteby et al. [4]: acquisition-based methods, physics-based pre-processing, projection completion, and iterative reconstruction. These approaches aim to correct metal artifacts either during image acquisition, by modifying raw projection data, or through iterative reconstruction algorithms. However, each approach has inherent limitations when applied to radiotherapy. Projection completion methods (e.g., linear interpolation [LI]) may introduce secondary artifacts and Hounsfield unit (HU) errors that adversely affect dose calculation accuracy [17], [35]. Tissue modeling approaches (e.g., normalized MAR [NMAR], orthopedic MAR [O-MAR]) require manual segmentation and may produce secondary artifacts [36]. Iterative methods are computationally intensive and may over-smooth soft tissues [19], [22]. Physics-based corrections are effective only for mild artifacts and demand scanner-specific parameters rarely available clinically [4], [8].

Collectively, these traditional methods may leave clinically relevant residual dosimetric errors, and their operator-dependent nature underscores the need for data-driven deep learning (DL) approaches [13], [16], [28]. A detailed comparison of traditional methods is provided in Table 1.

Technical performance of deep learning-based metal artifact reduction methods

Deep learning-based MAR (DL-MAR) has evolved rapidly from simple convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to sophisticated dual-domain, generative, and task-oriented models. In this section, we focus on the technical performance and architectural innovations of these methods, recognizing that clinical relevance ultimately depends on HU accuracy and dosimetric stability, not solely on image quality metrics such as peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) or structural similarity index measure (SSIM). Trend 1: from single-domain to dual-domain and physics-informed models

Early DL models operated on CT images alone (image-domain) or on raw projection data alone (sinogram-domain). These single-domain approaches are easier to implement but may not fully correct metal-induced inconsistencies, because artifacts are inherently linked to both the physics of X-ray acquisition and the final image appearance [4], [45]. For example, the early CNN-based method (CNNMAR) [24] combined outputs from multiple simple corrections to produce a cleaner image, yet it struggled with widespread streaks from large implants due to its limited ability to enforce physical consistency between the sinogram and the image.

To address this, dual-domain networks (e.g., DuDoNet [27], IDOL-Net [41], InDuDoNet+ [8]) were introduced. These networks jointly process sinogram and image data by incorporating forward-projection and back-projection operators, thereby preserving the physical link between the two domains. Under controlled experimental conditions, these networks consistently achieve PSNR values above 40 dB and SSIM values above 0.98 on benchmark datasets. However, these dual-domain studies did not include direct dosimetric validation for radiotherapy planning [33]. More importantly, by maintaining sinogram-image consistency, dual-domain methods may directly support more accurate dose calculation in radiotherapy planning [8], [38]. Although clinical validation of dual-domain networks specifically for adaptive radiotherapy remains limited, their ability to preserve anatomical fidelity and physical consistency suggests potential applicability in adaptive replanning workflows, particularly when combined with accurate deformable image registration.

Trend 2: generative models for unpaired and unsupervised learning

Most DL methods for MAR rely on supervised learning, which requires paired training data: the same anatomical region with and without metal artifacts [16]. However, obtaining such paired clinical data is impractical because acquiring a metal-free CT after metal implantation is not feasible. Consequently, supervised approaches typically train on synthesized artifacts (metal artificially inserted into metal-free CTs), which may not fully represent the complexity of real clinical cases [16], [30], [46], [47].

To address this limitation, unsupervised methods have been developed that do not require paired data [48]. Instead, they learn to translate artifact-affected images to artifact-free images by leveraging the statistical distributions of both domains [28], [30]. Among these, generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been widely adopted. Starting with ADN [25] and extended by attention-guided β-CycleGAN [28] and Transformer-based CycleGAN [43], these models achieved PSNR gains of 1–3 dB over baseline CycleGAN while eliminating the need for paired training data.

However, most unsupervised studies lacked direct dosimetric validation, and several authors have cautioned that image-domain GANs may introduce false structures (hallucination) or fail when applied to clinical data that differ from synthetic training data (domain shift) [25], [28], [49]. These effects are rarely quantified but could compromise target delineation if not carefully validated. Nevertheless, preliminary studies have explored GAN-based MAR for radiotherapy workflows, including sCT generation from CBCT for dose calculation [31] and reduced contouring time in head and neck IMRT planning [30], though prospective validation for image-guided or adaptive radiotherapy remains limited.

Trend 3: diffusion models and neural fields for high-fidelity recovery

While GAN-based methods are powerful, they remain prone to hallucination and may struggle with uncertainty quantification. More recent approaches have explored diffusion-based and neural field methods as alternative frameworks for metal artifact reduction without requiring paired training data [42], [44].

DiffSeg, the first diffusion model specifically designed for metal segmentation, achieved a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 95.45%, significantly outperforming U-Net (93.52%) and Attention U-Net (94.21%) [42]. NAFMAR, a self-supervised neural attenuation field, attained SSIM values of 0.919–0.941 on dental CBCT without requiring any training data, surpassing all baselines in structural preservation [44].

These methods offer inherent uncertainty quantification and may reduce the risk of anatomical hallucination compared to GANs. However, they remain computationally intensive and are still in early stages of clinical translation, with no prospective radiotherapy-specific validation available to date.

Trend 4: task-oriented loss functions for clinical endpoints

Recent MAR research has increasingly shifted from pixel-wise losses, such as PSNR and SSIM, toward clinically meaningful objectives. Traditional loss functions aim to make the corrected image look similar to a reference image, but they do not necessarily indicate that the image is suitable for accurate dose calculation [14]. To address this gap, Ki et al. introduced a masked mean squared error (MSE) loss that restricts gradient updates to artifact-affected voxels, achieving mean dose errors of 0.8% in organs-of-interest (OOIs) and gamma pass rates >95% (3%/3 mm) in head and neck plans [14]. This approach directly optimizes dosimetric accuracy rather than image similarity, aligning with radiotherapy quality assurance goals.

It should be noted that while PSNR and SSIM remain useful for benchmarking algorithmic performance, their correlation with clinical dosimetric endpoints is not always linear [2], [16]. Studies that lack direct dose validation should be interpreted with caution; the ultimate measure of MAR success in radiotherapy remains the accuracy of dose calculation and target coverage. Notably, architectures that have undergone direct dosimetric validation, such as those incorporating task-oriented loss functions, appear to demonstrate the strongest potential for clinical translation based on the available evidence [14], [15].

Clinical and dosimetric validation in radiotherapy

While technical performance is a prerequisite, clinical and dosimetric validation is the ultimate measure of success in radiotherapy. Metal artifacts in CT scans used for radiotherapy simulation and verification can compromise treatment accuracy [50], [51], [52]. In head and neck (H&N) radiotherapy, artifact-induced inaccuracies in HU values distort electron density maps, which may lead to dose calculation errors exceeding 10% in regions adjacent to dental fillings or implants [1], [3], [9], [13]. Such errors may result in underdosing of planning target volumes (PTVs) or overdosing of OOIs [14], [15], [53]. Recent clinical studies have investigated whether DL-MAR can consistently reduce these dosimetric errors to clinically acceptable levels, as summarized in Table 3.

Among the five studies that included dosimetric evaluation [14], [15], [30], [31], [32]], DL-MAR was associated with gamma pass rates within clinically acceptable tolerance limits (e.g., >95% under 3%/3 mm criteria) and dose deviations considered acceptable for radiotherapy treatment planning (PTV D98% [dose to 98% of the PTV volume] difference ≤ 0.3 Gy; mean OOIs dose error 0.8%). Although AAPM Task Group 218 [54] primarily addresses measurement-based IMRT QA, these gamma pass rates suggest that DL-MAR may achieve clinically acceptable dosimetric stability in the studied H&N cohorts. Notably, methods incorporating task-oriented loss functions [14] or validated in volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and intensity-modulated proton therapy (IMPT) contexts [15] demonstrated favorable dosimetric results. Nevertheless, all included studies were retrospective with limited sample sizes (n = 10–63), and prospective multicenter validation remains essential before routine clinical adoption. Discussion

By synthesizing evidence from 29 deep learning studies, stratified into Level 2 (retrospective clinical dosimetric validation), Level 3 (retrospective clinical image quality validation), and Level 4 (phantom-based or simulated data), we identified three key advances and three persistent challenges that define the current state of the field.

Key advance 1: Clinically acceptable dosimetric accuracy.

The reporting of dose errors below 3% across several retrospective studies [14], [15], [30], [31], [32] represents a notable finding over traditional MAR methods, which often leave residual errors exceeding 10% [2], [17], [19]. However, the clinical significance of these differences depends on the endpoint and context. The shift from pixel-wise metrics to task-oriented validation, such as gamma pass rates and D95% coverage, has been instrumental in establishing clinical relevance [5], [14], [15], [31]. However, it should be noted that these findings are derived from Level 2 evidence (retrospective cohorts), and prospective multicenter validation remains necessary to confirm their generalizability.

Key advance 2: Architectural innovation toward clinical plausibility.

Architectural innovation has moved beyond single-domain CNNs toward dual-domain frameworks [8], [38], [40], [55] and generative models that jointly optimize sinogram consistency and anatomical plausibility. The interpretability of unfolding-based networks, as demonstrated by InDuDoNet+, may further enhance model transparency and clinical trustworthiness [8]. Self-supervised approaches such as NAFMAR [44] eliminate the need for paired training data by learning directly from measurements, offering a pathway toward more generalizable solutions. These advances address key limitations of traditional MAR methods, which often require manual metal segmentation and may introduce secondary artifacts.

Key advance 3: Task-oriented loss functions shift focus toward clinical rather than image-similarity endpoints.

The design of task-specific loss functions, such as masked MSE [14], has shifted focus toward clinical rather than image-similarity endpoints. By focusing optimization on regions of interest, including tumor targets and OOIs, these approaches have demonstrated the potential to achieve sub-3% dose error even when global image quality remains imperfect [14]. This paradigm shift aligns with the broader movement toward clinically meaningful objectives highlighted in recent reviews [2], [16], and may accelerate the translation of DL-MAR from research settings to clinical practice. Persistent challenge 1: Generalizability beyond synthetic data.

Most DL-MAR models are trained on synthetic data [16], [25], [41], [58], raising concerns about performance on real-world implants with unknown composition, geometry, and beam hardening profiles. Prospective multicenter validation with diverse implant types and patient populations is still needed to establish generalizability.

Persistent challenge 2: Sinogram accessibility in clinical practice.

Dual-domain methods require raw sinogram data, which is often inaccessible in clinical practice due to vendor restrictions [8], [38], [44]. Self-supervised approaches like NAFMAR mitigate this by learning directly from measurements, yet widespread implementation remains challenging [44].

Persistent challenge 3: Limited prospective clinical validation.

While phantom and retrospective studies validate dosimetric accuracy, prospective trials linking DL-MAR to improved patient outcomes (e.g., reduced normal tissue toxicity, improved local control) are absent [2], [16]. This limits conclusions regarding widespread clinical adoption.

Proposed clinical workflow and implementation

Fig. 5 illustrates a proposed workflow and implementation strategy for incorporating DL-MAR into H&N radiotherapy planning, bridging the gap between technical validation and routine clinical use. The workflow begins with CT simulation, where patients with dental fillings or other metallic hardware undergo standard CT imaging. The presence of metal implants typically produces severe streak and dark band artifacts, compromising image quality and subsequent treatment planning [2], [13], [59].

Proposed workflow for incorporating deep learning-based metal artifact reduction (DL-MAR) into head and neck radiotherapy planning. Steps include CT simulation, DL-MAR processing, target and OOIs delineation, treatment planning with dosimetric validation, and adaptive replanning using image-guided verification. CT, computed tomography; OOIs, organs of interest; PSQA, patient-specific quality assurance.

Following CT acquisition, DL-MAR processing is applied using architectures such as CycleGAN [30], 3D generative adversarial network (GAN) [15], dual encoder U-Net [33], or masked MSE U-Net [14]. These models reduce metal artifacts while preserving underlying anatomical structures. The corrected images then undergo image registration and contouring, where improved tissue conspicuity facilitates more accurate target and OOI delineation, with corresponding reductions in inter-observer variability [30], [45].

The artifact-corrected images are subsequently used for treatment planning (e.g., volumetric modulated arc therapy [VMAT] or intensity-modulated radiotherapy [IMRT]) and dose calculation. Several clinical studies have reported that DL-MAR-corrected images yield gamma pass rates exceeding 95% and dose errors below 3%, values that are within clinically acceptable tolerance limits [14], [15], [31], [32]. The resulting treatment plan is then submitted for patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) before treatment delivery.

During daily treatment sessions, image guidance (e.g., cone-beam CT [CBCT] or megavoltage CT [MVCT]) verifies patient setup and registration with the original planning CT [32], [51], [60], [61]. If anatomical changes such as tumor shrinkage or weight loss are observed, the acquired images may be used for adaptive radiotherapy [2], [60]. In this setting, the same DL-MAR processing can be applied to the treatment images, enabling recontouring, replanning, and dose recalculation without the need for a separate simulation CT. It should be noted, however, that clinical implementation of such a continuous workflow requires prospective validation, vendor integration, and quality assurance protocols specifically designed for DL-MAR-based planning.

Limitations of this review

This review has several limitations. First, the exclusion of non-English publications may introduce language bias, and the exclusion of conference abstracts and gray literature may contribute to publication bias. Second, the heterogeneity of study designs, metrics, and data sources precluded meta-analysis, limiting quantitative synthesis. Third, the majority of included studies were from single centers, which may affect generalizability. Fourth, the rapid pace of deep learning research means that newer architectures may have emerged after our search cutoff (April 2026). Fifth, the lack of standardized evaluation protocols and reporting metrics for MAR performance across the included studies limits direct quantitative comparisons between different DL-MAR architectures. Importantly, the majority of DL-MAR models were trained and tested on simulated artifact data, which may not fully replicate the complexity of clinical metal artifacts, particularly in terms of beam hardening and scatter effects.

In the retrospective cohorts examined, DL-MAR achieved dosimetric accuracy within clinically acceptable thresholds (dose errors <3%, gamma pass rates >95% under 3%/3 mm criteria), showing promise compared to traditional MAR methods [2], [16]. Architectural innovations, including dual-domain networks, task-oriented loss functions, and generative models, have contributed to improved target delineation and dosimetric stability. Based on Level 2 evidence from retrospective clinical cohorts, task-oriented loss functions currently demonstrate promising potential for clinical translation, although prospective validation is needed to confirm these findings. However, several gaps remain before routine clinical adoption: prospective multicenter validation (Level 1 evidence) with diverse implant types, improved access to raw sinogram data for dual-domain methods, and prospective clinical studies assessing patient outcomes. For medical physicists and radiation oncologists, current evidence supports careful integration of DL-MAR into treatment planning workflows with institution-specific validation, while remaining mindful of hallucination risks and domain shift when applying models trained on synthetic data to clinical cases. Future priorities should include multicenter prospective validation, open-source sinogram databases, vendor collaboration, lightweight real-time models for adaptive radiotherapy, regulatory frameworks for quality assurance, and health economic evaluations.
