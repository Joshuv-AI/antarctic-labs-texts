Acute aortic stenosis is a life-threatening emergency that requires expedited management to avoid heart failure, syncope, and mortality. The feasibility and ease of conventional and alternative access transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) have allowed patients with few options to receive this life-saving therapy despite significant comorbidities.1 However, some patients present with anatomic and procedural barriers that render any traditional or alternative access unfeasible.2,3 In this case, both medical and surgical comorbidities made traditional and alternative access unfeasible. A history of acute type A ascending thoracic aortic dissection that extended to both iliac arteries precluded the possibility of a transcaval approach. In addition, we were reluctant to perform a transapical approach given the patient’s low ejection fraction, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, frailty, chronic renal failure, and previous cardiac operations. After exhausting all traditional and nontraditional retrograde access options, we elected to pursue was an antegrade transseptal valve-in-valve TAVR.

Case Presentation

A 66-year-old man presents to the hospital with syncope, chest pressure, progressive shortness of breath, bradycardia, and pulmonary edema. His past medical history included end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis, ischemic cardiomyopathy with an ejection fraction of 20% to 25%, atrial fibrillation, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. His past surgical history included coronary artery bypass as well as a second sternotomy for repair of a type A aortic dissection that extended to the iliac arteries. Initial evaluation revealed anemia consistent with chronic kidney disease, elevated creatinine and troponin levels, and a right bundle branch block. Imaging studies were consistent with pulmonary edema, pleural effusions, and an enlarged aortic silhouette. Surgical aortic valve replacement was not an option because of the extremely high risk (18%) associated with his comorbidities and previous surgical history. Extensive dissection and severe vascular disease made transfemoral, transaxillary, and transcarotid access unfeasible (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Videos 1 and 2). Cardiomyopathy, acute-on chronic kidney disease, and severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease made transapical access unsafe. The residual aortic dissection involving the abdominal aorta precluded a transcaval approach. Procedure

A 7-F × 90-cm sheath was placed in the left common femoral artery and placed in the ascending aorta. A 27- to 42-mm EnSnare (Merit Medical) was deployed in the ascending aorta. Through the right common femoral vein, we performed transseptal puncture and placed a pigtail wire in the left atrium. We ballooned the septum with a 14.0-mm peripheral balloon and inserted a medium curl Agilis steerable sheath (Terumo) in the left atrium. We crossed the mitral valve antegrade with a pigtail and wired retrograde through the aortic valve with a 400-cm stiff angled glide wire (Terumo) (Video 3). We snared the wire in the ascending aorta and created an external veno-arterial rail (Video 4). Over the railed wire we inserted a 26-F × 65-cm Gore Dry Seal (Gore) across the septum and through the mitral valve to protect the mitral apparatus (Videos 5 and 6). From the arterial side of the rail, a 6-F multipurpose catheter was advanced over the glide wire just above the surgical aortic valve to aid in stabilization during deployment. We mounted a 23 S3 Ultra Resilia Valve (Edwards) directly onto the delivery balloon externally and inserted the delivery system through the 26-F sheath. Using the railed wire and delivery system we were able to deliver the valve retrograde across the previously replaced aortic valve and deployed the valve in the standard fashion (Videos 7 and 8). The initial mean gradient was 18 mm Hg so we postdilated with a 23-mm True Balloon (B. Braun) reducing the gradient to 7 mm Hg (Figure 4, Video 9). The bioprosthetic valve did not fracture but was modified significantly with improvement in gradient. The sheath was removed and transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed no significant damage to the mitral valve. Potential Pitfalls

The unique challenges of antegrade transseptal TAVR create a need for a preplanned, staged approach. The technique itself has potential complications (atrial perforation, pericardial effusion, and mitral apparatus injury) that mandate imaging guidance and proper placement of catheters. Also, when moving a large-bore catheter through the septum, leaflet entanglement or damage to the chordal structure can be encountered; this is mitigated by using a veno-arterial rail and the DrySeal damage-protective sheath. The risk of residual gradients resulting from underexpanded valves often necessitates balloon postdilation. Rarely, vascular complications and hemodynamic instability occur; therefore, a thorough evaluation of the computed tomography scan before the procedure and engagement of a heart team to strategize for emergency rescues is strongly advisable.

Discussion

In cases with poor femoral access, TAVR can be pursued by multiple alternative routes, most commonly transcarotid, subclavian, or transcaval.2,3 In this particular case, the patient's history of a hostile chest, dialysis-dependent acute renal failure, significant vascular disease, and severely depressed ejection fraction made surgical aortic valve replacement and commonly used alternative access strategies for TAVR unsafe. A transseptal valve-in-valve via an antegrade delivery method was chosen here, following the initial pathway described in 2002 by Cribier et al.3 Though initially abandoned for the now commonly used transfemoral approach, multiple case series have supported this option in select cases.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 The main drawback of this approach lies in the added procedural complexity (need for transseptal puncture, external rail) and the possibility of damage to the mitral valve apparatus. Although this case provides experience that supports a continuing role for the approach as a life-saving option in solely “no-access” situations, traditional alternative access sites have more established and better-documented evidence of safety and efficacy. The case adds to our knowledge of multidisciplinary and individualized clinical decision-making in patients with complex structural heart disease, with the potential to expand the role of various advanced modalities for select patients already considered inoperable.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that an antegrade transseptal valve-in-valve TAVR may represent a viable life-saving bridge for patients who have severe aortic valve disease with contraindication to surgery and traditional peripheral access. Although this method is not commonly used, an important role remains for this method in a small group of appropriately selected high-risk patients with few reasonable alternatives. Broader application should occur cautiously with limited evidence, and the complexity of outcomes must be weighed against the complexity of the procedure. Future reports with final actual data, larger series, and longer follow-up are still warranted to better define the safety, efficacy, and role of this alternative intervention in contemporary practice.
