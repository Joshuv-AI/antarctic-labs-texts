Universal Complexity Bounds for Universal Gradient Methods in Nonlinear Optimization
Yurii Nesterov

Abstract
In this paper, we provide the universal first-order methods of Composite Optimization with new complexity analysis. It delivers some universal convergence guarantees, which are not linked directly to any parametric problem class. However, they can be easily transformed into the rates of convergence for the particular problem classes by substituting the corresponding upper estimates for the Global Curvature Bound of the objective function. We analyze in this way the simple gradient method for nonconvex minimization, gradient methods for convex composite optimization, and their accelerated variant. For them, the only input parameter is the required accuracy of the approximate solution. The accelerated variant of our scheme automatically ensures the best possible rate of convergence simultaneously for all parametric problem classes containing the smooth part of the objective function.



Keywords:
Nonlinear Optimization, Composite Convex Optimization, Black-Box, Complexity Bounds, First-Order Methods, Fast Gradient Method, Universal Methods.

Introduction

Motivation.

A systematic study of worst-case efficiency bounds for numerical methods of Convex Optimization started from the famous monograph by Nemirovski and Yudin
[
5
]
, where the authors developed a comprehensive Complexity Theory for different parametric classes of optimization problems. Their classification was based on Hölder conditions for the first derivative of the objective function, parameterized by the Hölder degree
\nu\in[0,1]
and the corresponding Hölder constant:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{*}&\leq&L_{\nu}\|x-y\|^{\nu},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{array}

(notation
f\in\mathbb{C}^{1,\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
).

In
[
5
]
, we can find optimal methods for different
\nu\in[0,1]
. However, from the practical point of view, these methods were not perfect since very often they require an auxiliary line-search procedure or the knowledge of some additional information about the objective function.

The first optimal method without any additional requirements and without the line search for the most important practical case
\nu=1
was developed in
[
6
]
. Later, in
[
10
]
, there were presented the optimal methods for
\nu\in(0,1)
with automatic adjustment to the Hölder degree. However, theses methods need some additional information on the objective function (estimate of the Hölder constant, distance to the minimum). Moreover, for the proper choice of the method’s parameters, it is necessary to fix the number of steps of the method in advance.

An importance of automatic adjustment to the best-fitting Hölder power is evident since objective functions can be attributed simultaneously to several Hölder classes. It is almost impossible to decide in advance which of them is more appropriate to our particular objective.

Hence, several authors proposed different techniques for addressing this issue. In
[
3
]
, it was shown how this can be done in the framework of Level Method
[
4
]
. However, each iteration of this method is much more difficult than the usual step of gradients schemes.

In
[
2
]
, it was suggested to treat the Hölder condition in the framework of
inexact oracle
. In this approach, it is assumed that the answer of the oracle
(f(x),g(x))
at point
x
satisfies the following inequality:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(y)&\leq&\tilde{\delta}+f(x)+\langle g(x),y-x\rangle+{\tilde{L}\over 2}\|y-x\|^{2},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}

(1.1)

where
\tilde{\delta}>0
and
\tilde{L}>0
are some inexactness parameters. It was shown that these parameters can be chosen as appropriate functions of
\nu
. Therefore, the function from
\mathbb{C}^{1,\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be minimized by gradient methods for
\mathbb{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. However, the choice of parameters in these schemes is quite complicated.

Finally, in
[
8
]
, there were developed ”universal” gradient methods for solving the
composite miniization problem

[
7
]

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{x\in Q}\;[\;f(x)+\Psi(x)\;],\end{array}

where
\Psi(\cdot)
is a simple closed convex function. In this paper, the next point
x_{+}
has to satisfy the
Damped Relaxation Condition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(x_{+})&\leq&\delta+\min\limits_{y\in Q}\Big[f(\bar{x})+\langle g(\bar{x}),y-\bar{x}\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\hat{L}\|y-\bar{x}\|^{2}\Big],\end{array}

where
\delta={\epsilon\over 2}
and
\epsilon>0
is the required accuracy for the approximate solution of our problem in terms of the function value. In the previous papers
[
2
,
3
,
10
]
, the constant
\delta
was a complicated function of the class parameters.

After developments in
[
8
]
, one could have an impression that the problem of constructing the universal first-order methods is solved. Unfortunately, it is not the case. Indeed, all available complexity results are related to the functions from Hölder classes.

And what about the other problem classes?

We can imagine that there are many of them. Can we address the performance of our methods on other classes, which are may be even not known today?

On the other hand, there are serious doubts that the whole family of Hölder classes
\mathbb{C}_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\bigcup\limits_{\nu\in[0,1]}\mathbb{C}^{1,\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
represents well the class
\mathbb{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of all differentiable functions. Our concerns are supported by the following example.

Example 1

Consider the univariate function
f(x)=f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)
, where
f_{1}(x)=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}x^{2}
and
f_{2}(x)={2\over 3}|x|^{3/2}
with
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
f_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R})
. On the other hand, for
x=0
, any
h>0
, and any
\nu\in[0,1]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0\;\leq\;h^{-\nu}[f^{\prime}(0+h)-f^{\prime}(0)]&=&h^{-\nu}\Big[h+\sqrt{h}\Big]\;=\;h^{1-\nu}+h^{-\nu+1/2}.\end{array}

Hence, if
\nu\in[0,1)
, then the first term is this sum goes to infinity as
h\to\infty
. If
\nu=1
, then the second term goes to infinity as
h\to+0
. Therefore,
f\not\in\mathbb{C}_{H}(\mathbb{R})
.

\Box

Thus, we come to the same question again:

Can we get complexity bounds for wider classes of objective functions?

Our paper gives an affirmative answer to this question. We start from obtaining complexity bounds for some
internal characteristic
of our objective function, which has no relations to any standard problem class, and which is defined only by the topology of the function.
This bound serves as an interface between the method and the particular problem classes. When we decide on the problem class we are interested in, it is enough only to bound the growth of this characteristic using the properties of functions from this class. In this way, we get the complexity estimates for all methods supported by the above analysis.

In our paper, this internal characteristics is the
Global Curvature Bound
of the objective function (GCB). We are going to show how all of that work for several
universal
optimization schemes, some of which are new.

\hat{\mu}(\cdot)

1.1
) for the objective function. Our main statement relates the parameters
\tilde{\delta}
and
\tilde{L}
in (
1.1
) with the function
\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
.

3

\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
), or in an inverse form, using the

In Section
4

In Section
5

\begin{array}[]{rcl}2\hat{\mu}\left(2\sqrt{D\over k}\right)&\leq&\epsilon,\end{array}

D

\mathbb{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathbb{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

6

[
8

. Its direct complexity guarantee looks as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(k+1)\hat{\mu}\left(2\left({2\over k+1}\right)^{3/2}D^{1/2}\right)&\leq&\epsilon.\end{array}

7
, we discuss the presented results and the future research directions.

Notation.
In what follows, we work in a finite-dimensional linear space
\mathbb{E}
. Its dual space, the space of linear functions on
\mathbb{E}
, is denoted by
\mathbb{E}^{*}
. For
x\in\mathbb{E}
and
g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
, we denote by
\langle g,x\rangle
the value of linear function
g
at
x
. For the primal space
\mathbb{E}
, we fix an arbitrary norm
\|\cdot\|
.

2
Global Curvature Bound

In our approach, we do not fix in advance a particular parametric problem class containing the functional components of our optimization problem. A priory, the only fixed object is the norm
\|\cdot\|
for measuring the distances in
\mathbb{E}
, and its dual norm

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|g\|_{*}&=&\max\limits_{x\in\mathbb{E}}\{\langle g,x\rangle:\;\|x\|\leq 1\},\quad g\in\mathbb{E}^{*},\end{array}

ensuring Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
|\langle g,x\rangle|\leq\|x\|\cdot\|g\|_{*}
.

Let us introduce the notion of
Global Curvature Bound
(GCB).
For function
f(\cdot)
with
{\rm dom\,}f\subseteq\mathbb{E}
, it provides us with a uniform upper bound for the curvature of
f(\cdot)
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}_{f}(t)&=&\sup\limits_{x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f,\atop\alpha\in(0,1)}\Big\{{|\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)-f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)|\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}:\;\|x-y\|\leq t\Big\}.\end{array}

(2.1)

For convex functions, the absolute value in this definition can be dropped.
If function
f(\cdot)
is linear, then this bound is identically equal to zero. The Global Curvature Bound satisfies the following triangle inequality

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}_{f_{1}+f_{2}}(t)&\leq&\hat{\mu}_{f_{1}}(t)+\hat{\mu}_{f_{2}}(t),\quad t\in{\rm dom\,}\hat{\mu}_{f_{1}}\bigcap{\rm dom\,}\hat{\mu}_{f_{2}},\end{array}

which becomes equality when
f_{2}(\cdot)
is linear.

If no ambiguity arise, the lower index of function
\hat{\mu}_{f}(\cdot)
is dropped.
We will prove that GCB of any function
f(\cdot)

satisfies the following
growth condition
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}_{f}(\beta t)&\geq&\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}_{f}(t),\quad t\in{\rm dom\,}\hat{\mu}_{f},\;\beta\in[0,1].\end{array}

(2.2)

Our definition of GCB is similar to the notion of
uniform smoothness
, which was introduced in
[
1
]
for convex functions with
{\rm dom\,}f=\mathbb{E}
. However, in
[
1
]
, inequality (
2.2
) was derived by duality relations with the
exact modulus of uniform convexity

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu(t)&=&\inf\limits_{x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f,\atop\alpha\in(0,1)}\Big\{{\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)-f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}:\;\|x-y\|\geq t\Big\}.\end{array}

(2.3)

This modulus satisfies the opposite inequality (see
[
11
]
)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu(\beta t)&\leq&\beta^{2}\mu(t),\quad t\in{\rm dom\,}\mu,\;\beta\in[0,1],\end{array}

(2.4)

Unfortunately, for non-convex functions this logic does not work and we need to provide inequality (
2.2
) with a direct proof.

Denote
D={\rm diam}\,\left({\rm dom\,}f\right)
, and let
\Gamma=[0,D]
for
D<+\infty
and
\Gamma=[0,+\infty)
otherwise.
In what follows, we assume the following regularity condition.

Assumption 1

Function
\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
is well defined for all
t\in\Gamma
and
\lim\limits_{\tau\to+0}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau)=0
.

This assumption is not very restrictive. It can be ensured by different standard assumptions on function
f(\cdot)
. This can be seen by the following observation.

Lemma 1

Denote
\hat{\mu}_{1}(t)=\sup\limits_{x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f}\Big\{\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{*}:\;\|x-y\|\leq t\Big\}
.
Then, for any
t\in\Gamma
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}(t)&\leq&\int\limits_{0}^{t}\hat{\mu}_{1}(\tau)d\tau.\end{array}

(2.5)

Proof:

Indeed, for all
x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(x+(1-\alpha)(y-x))-f(x)&=&(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+\tau(1-\alpha)(y-x)),y-x\rangle d\tau,\\
f(y+\alpha(x-y))-f(y)&=&\alpha\int\limits_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(y+\tau\alpha(x-y)),x-y\rangle d\tau.\end{array}

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&|\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)-f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)|\\
\\
=&\alpha(1-\alpha)\Big|\int\limits_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+\tau(1-\alpha)(y-x))-\nabla f(y+\tau\alpha(x-y)),x-y\rangle d\tau\Big|\\
\\
\leq&\alpha(1-\alpha)t\int\limits_{0}^{1}\|\nabla f(x+\tau(1-\alpha)(y-x))-\nabla f(y+\tau\alpha(x-y))\|_{*}d\tau\\
\\
\leq&\alpha(1-\alpha)t\int\limits_{0}^{1}\hat{\mu}_{1}((1-\tau)t)d\tau\;=\;\alpha(1-\alpha)\int\limits_{0}^{t}\hat{\mu}_{1}(\tau)d\tau.\hfill\Box\end{array}

Thus, Assumption
1
is ensured, for example, by Hölder condition for the gradient of function
f(\cdot)
, or by uniform boundedness of the norm of its gradients on
{\rm dom\,}f
.

Let us prove the main property of GCB. Our proof is based on the ideas of
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2

For any
t\in\Gamma
and
\beta\in[0,1]
, we have
\hat{\mu}(\beta t)\geq\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}(t)
.

Proof:

Let us choose first
\mbox{${1\over 2}$}<\beta\leq 1
. For
t\in\Gamma
and a small
\epsilon>0
, let us choose points
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\rm dom\,}f
such that
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|\leq t
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\alpha f(u_{1})+(1-\alpha)f(u_{2})-f(\alpha u_{1}+(1-\alpha)u_{2})|&\geq&\alpha(1-\alpha)[\hat{\mu}(t)-\epsilon].\end{array}

(2.6)

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\alpha\in(0,\mbox{${1\over 2}$}]
.

For
\tau\in[0,1]
, denote
u_{\tau}=\tau u_{1}+(1-\tau)u_{2}
. Note that
\|u_{\beta}-u_{2}\|\leq\beta t
. At the same time, for
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{\alpha\over\beta}<1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma u_{\beta}+(1-\gamma)u_{2}&=&\gamma[\beta u_{1}+(1-\beta)u_{2}]+(1-\gamma)u_{2}\;=\;u_{\alpha}.\end{array}

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\beta f(u_{1})+(1-\beta)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\beta})|&\leq&\beta(1-\beta)\hat{\mu}(t),\\
\\
|\gamma f(u_{\beta})+(1-\gamma)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})|&\leq&\gamma(1-\gamma)\hat{\mu}(\beta t).\end{array}

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\alpha(1-\alpha)[\hat{\mu}(t)-\epsilon]&\leq&|\alpha f(u_{1})+(1-\alpha)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})|\\
\\
&\leq&\gamma|\beta f(u_{1})+(1-\beta)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\beta})|\\
\\
&&+|\gamma f(u_{\beta})+(1-\gamma)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})|\\
\\
&\leq&\alpha(1-\beta)\hat{\mu}(t)+\gamma(1-\gamma)\hat{\mu}(\beta t).\end{array}

Dividing this inequality by
\alpha(\beta-\alpha)
, we get
\beta^{-2}\hat{\mu}(\beta t)-\hat{\mu}(t)\geq-{1-\alpha\over\beta-\alpha}\epsilon
. Choosing
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain the desired inequality.

Finally, if
\beta\leq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}
, then we can represent it as
\beta=\bar{\beta}^{k}
with some
\bar{\beta}\in[\mbox{${1\over 2}$},1)
and
k\geq 1
, and repeat the above reasoning for
\bar{\beta}
recursively
k
times.

\Box

Let us prove now several useful inequalities for GCB.

Lemma 3

For any
x,y\in{\rm dom\,}f
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle|&\leq&\hat{\mu}(\|y-x\|),\end{array}

(2.7)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle|&\leq&2\hat{\mu}(\|x-y\|),\end{array}

(2.8)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle|&\leq&2\hat{\sigma}(\|x-y\|),\end{array}

(2.9)

where
\hat{\sigma}(r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau r)d\tau=\int\limits_{0}^{r}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau)d\tau
,
r\in\Gamma
.

Proof:

Indeed, in view of definition of
\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Big|f(y)-f(x)-{1\over 1-\alpha}[f(x+(1-\alpha)(y-x))-f(x)]\Big|&\leq&\alpha\hat{\mu}(\|y-x\|).\end{array}

Choosing
\alpha\to 1
, we get inequality (
2.7
). Adding two copies of (
2.7
) with
x
and
y
interchanged, we get an upper bound for the left-hand side of inequality (
2.8
). Finally, for
r=\|x-y\|
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Big|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\Big|&=&\Big|\int\limits_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(x+\tau(y-x))-\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle d\tau\Big|\\
\\
&=&\Big|\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\langle\nabla f(x+\tau(y-x))-\nabla f(x),\tau(y-x)\rangle d\tau\Big|\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GUp})}}{{\leq}}&\int\limits_{0}^{1}{2\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau r)d\tau\;=\;2\hat{\sigma}(r).\hfill\Box\end{array}

As compared with (
2.7
), inequality (
2.9
) is weaker.
However, under some natural conditions, function
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
is proportional to
\hat{\mu}(\cdot)
. Let us justify its useful properties.

Lemma 4

Function
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
is monotonically increasing on
\Gamma
and
\lim\limits_{r\to+0}{1\over r}\hat{\sigma}(r)=0
.
For all
r\in\Gamma
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}(\beta r)&\geq&\beta^{2}\hat{\sigma}(r),\quad\beta\in[0,1],\end{array}

(2.10)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}(r)&\leq&2\hat{\sigma}(r).\end{array}

(2.11)

Moreover, if
f(\cdot)
is convex and
{\rm dom\,}f=\mathbb{E}
, then for any
\beta\in[0,1]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\mu}(\beta r)&\leq&\beta\hat{\mu}(r).\end{array}

(2.12)

Consequently, in this case, function
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
is convex and for any
r\in\Gamma
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}(r)&\leq&\hat{\mu}(r).\end{array}

(2.13)

Proof:

The first statement of the lemma follows from l’Hopital rule and Assumption
1
. If
r\in\Gamma
, then

\hat{\sigma}(r)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau r)d\tau\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-ModUS})}}{{\geq}}\;\int\limits_{0}^{1}\tau\hat{\mu}(r)d\tau\;=\;\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\hat{\mu}(r)
,
and this is (
2.11
). By the same reason,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}(\beta r)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau\beta r)d\tau\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-ModUS})}}{{\geq}}\;\beta^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau r)d\tau\;=\;\beta^{2}\hat{\sigma}(r).\end{array}

Further, let
f(\cdot)
be convex and
{\rm dom\,}f=\mathbb{E}
. Let us fix an arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
. Then there exist points
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{E}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|\leq t
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\alpha f(u_{1})+(1-\alpha)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})&\geq&\alpha(1-\alpha)[\hat{\mu}(t)-\epsilon],\end{array}

where
u_{\alpha}=\alpha u_{1}+(1-\alpha)u_{2}
. Let us choose some
\gamma>0
and define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{u}_{i}&=&u_{i}+\gamma(u_{i}-u_{\alpha}),\quad i=1,2.\end{array}

Then
\|\bar{u}_{1}-\bar{u}_{1}\|\leq(1+\gamma)t
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\alpha\bar{u}_{1}+(1-\alpha)\bar{u}_{2}&=&\alpha(u_{1}+\gamma(u_{1}-u_{\alpha})+(1-\alpha)(u_{2}+\gamma(u_{2}-u_{\alpha}))\;=\;u_{\alpha}.\end{array}

At the same time, since
f(\cdot)
is convex, we have

f(\bar{u}_{i})\geq f(u_{i})+\gamma(f(u_{i})-f(u_{\alpha}))
,
i=1,2
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\hat{\mu}((1+\gamma)t)\;\geq\;{1\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}\Big\{\alpha f(\bar{u}_{1})+(1-\alpha)f(\bar{u}_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})\Big\}\\
\\
\geq&{1\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}\Big\{\alpha[f(u_{1})+\gamma(f(u_{1})-f(u_{\alpha}))]+(1-\alpha)[f(u_{2})+\gamma(f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha}))]-f(u_{\alpha})\Big\}\\
\\
=&{1+\gamma\over\alpha(1-\alpha)}\Big[\alpha f(u_{1})+(1-\alpha)f(u_{2})-f(u_{\alpha})\Big]\;\geq\;(1+\gamma)[\hat{\mu}(t)-\epsilon].\end{array}

Choosing now
\epsilon>0
arbitrarily small and denoting
r=(1+\gamma)t
with
\beta={1\over 1+\gamma}
, we get inequality (
2.12
).

We can see now that derivative
\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}(t)={1\over t}\hat{\mu}(t)
is non-decreasing on
\Gamma
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}(\beta t)&=&{1\over\beta t}\hat{\mu}(\beta t)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HMuUp})}}{{\leq}}\;{1\over t}\hat{\mu}(t)\;=\;\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}(t),\quad\beta\in(0,1).\end{array}

Hence, function
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
is convex. At the same time, by definition of
\hat{\sigma}(t)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\sigma}(r)&=&\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\hat{\mu}(\tau r)d\tau\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HMuUp})}}{{\leq}}\;\int\limits_{0}^{1}{1\over\tau}\cdot\tau\hat{\mu}(r)d\tau\;=\;\hat{\mu}(r).\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}

As we will see later, the right-hand side of inequality (
2.9
) is more convenient for the complexity analysis than that of inequality (
2.7
).

Let us prove now the following important property. Denote
\Gamma_{1}=\{\tau\geq 0:\;\sqrt{\tau}\in\Gamma\}
.

Lemma 5

On the set
\Gamma_{1}
, function
\hat{\xi}(\tau)=\hat{\sigma}(\sqrt{\tau})
is concave.

Proof:

Indeed, function
\hat{\xi}(\cdot)
is differentiable and
\hat{\xi}^{\prime}(\tau)={1\over 2\sqrt{\tau}}\,\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}(\sqrt{\tau})={1\over 2\tau}\hat{\mu}(\sqrt{\tau})
. Hence, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hat{\xi}^{\prime}(\beta\tau)&=&{1\over 2\beta\tau}\hat{\mu}(\sqrt{\beta\tau})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-ModUS})}}{{\geq}}\;{1\over 2\tau}\hat{\mu}(\sqrt{\tau})\;=\;\hat{\xi}^{\prime}(\tau).\end{array}

Thus, the derivative
\hat{\xi}^{\prime}(\cdot)
is non-increasing and therefore
\hat{\xi}(\cdot)
is concave.

\Box

Corollary 1

For any values
r,t\in\Gamma
, we have

Complexity of universal gradient methods

In order to use the bound (

and

Lemma 6

Let function
\psi(\cdot)
be defined on
\Gamma
. Assume that for some
r_{0}\in\Gamma
there exist constants
a,b>0
such that for any
r\in\Gamma
,
r\geq r_{0}
, and
\alpha\in[0,b]
with
r+\alpha\in\Gamma
, we have
\psi(r+\alpha)\geq\psi(r)-a\alpha^{2}
. Then
\psi(r)\geq\psi(r_{0})
for all
r\geq r_{0}
,
r\in\Gamma
.

Proof:

Let us fix some
\bar{r}>r_{0}
,
\bar{r}\in\Gamma
. Let us choose
N\geq 1
big enough to have
\alpha={\bar{r}-r_{0}\over N}\leq b
. Consider the points
r_{k}=r_{0}+k\alpha
. Then
\psi(r_{k+1})\geq\psi(r_{k})-a\alpha^{2}
,
k=0,\dots,N-1
. Hence,

\psi(\bar{r})=\psi(r_{N})\geq\psi(r_{0})-Na\alpha^{2}=\psi(r_{0})-{a\over N}(\bar{r}-r_{0})^{2}
. Thus, choosing
N\to\infty
, we get the result.

\Box

Now we can prove the following statement.

Lemma 7

On the set
\Gamma
, function
\delta^{+}_{f}(\cdot)
is non-decreasing, and function
L_{f}(\cdot)
is non-increasing.

Proof:

Consider
r\in\Gamma
and
\alpha>0
such that
r+\alpha\in\Gamma
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta^{+}_{f}(r+\alpha)-\delta^{+}_{f}(r)&=&2\int\limits_{r}^{r+\alpha}{1\over\xi}\hat{\mu}(\xi)d\xi-\Big[\hat{\mu}(r+\alpha)-\hat{\mu}(r)\Big]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-ModUS})}}{{\geq}}&{2\alpha\hat{\mu}(r)\over r+\alpha}-\Big[\left(1+{\alpha\over r}\right)^{2}-1\Big]\hat{\mu}(r)\;=\;-\alpha^{2}\Big[{2\over r(r+\alpha)}+{1\over r^{2}}\Big]\hat{\mu}(r).\end{array}

Hence, in view of Lemma
6
, function
\delta^{+}_{f}(\cdot)
is non-decreasing.

Finally, for
r\in\Gamma
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{f}(\beta r)={2\over\beta^{2}r^{2}}\hat{\mu}(\beta r)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-ModUS})}}{{\geq}}\;L_{f}(r).\end{array}

Hence, function
L_{f}(\cdot)
is non-increasing on
\Gamma
.

. Assume that it is valid for some
k\geq 0
. Then, for any
y\in{\rm dom\,}\Psi
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi_{k}(y)&\geq&\phi_{k}^{*}+\beta(v_{k},y)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-UFGM})}}{{\geq}}\;A_{k}\Big(\tilde{f}(y_{k})-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\epsilon\Big)+\beta(v_{k},y).\end{array}

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi_{k+1}(y)+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}A_{k}\epsilon&\geq&\beta(v_{k},y)+A_{k}\Big(f(x_{k+1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k}-x_{k+1}\rangle+\Psi(y_{k})\Big)\\
\\
&&+a_{k+1}[f(x_{k+1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y-x_{k+1}\rangle+\Psi(y)]\\
\\
&=&\beta(v_{k},y)+A_{k}\Big(f(x_{k+1})+\Psi(y_{k})\Big)\\
\\
&&+a_{k+1}[f(x_{k+1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y-v_{k}\rangle+\Psi(y)].\end{array}

In view of definition of the point
\hat{x}_{k+1}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi^{*}_{k+1}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}A_{k}\epsilon&\geq&\beta(v_{k},\hat{x}_{k+1})+A_{k}\Big(f(x_{k+1})+\Psi(y_{k})\Big)\\
\\
&&+a_{k+1}[f(x_{k+1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\hat{x}_{k+1}-v_{k}\rangle+\Psi(\hat{x}_{k+1})]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-BGrow})}}{{\geq}}&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|\hat{x}_{k+1}-v_{k}\|^{2}+A_{k+1}f(x_{k+1})+A_{k+1}\Psi(y_{k+1})\\
\\
&&+a_{k+1}\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\hat{x}_{k+1}-v_{k}\rangle.\end{array}

Since
\hat{x}_{k+1}-v_{k}={1\over\tau_{k}}(y_{k+1}-x_{k+1})
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi^{*}_{k+1}+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}A_{k}\epsilon&\geq&{1\over 2\tau_{k}^{2}}\|y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+A_{k+1}f(x_{k+1})+A_{k+1}\Psi(y_{k+1})\\
\\
&&+A_{k+1}\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
&=&A_{k+1}(f(x_{k+1})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}\rangle\\
\\
&&+2^{i_{k}-1}L_{k}\|y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}\|^{2}+\Psi(y_{k+1}))\\
\\
&\geq&A_{k+1}\Big(f(y_{k+1})-{\epsilon\over 2}\tau_{k}+\Psi(y_{k+1})\Big)\;=\;A_{k+1}\tilde{f}(y_{k+1})-{\epsilon\over 2}a_{k+1}.\end{array}

Thus, inequality (
6.4
) is proved for all
k\geq 0
.

Let us prove now the inequality (
6.2
).
If
k=0
, then
A_{1}=a_{1}
and
\tau_{0,i}=1
for all
i\geq 0
.
Hence, if
i_{0}=0
, then
L_{1}=L_{0}
and this inequality is valid in view of our assumption. If
i_{0}\geq 1
, then in view of Lemma
11
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mbox{${1\over 2}$}L_{1}&=&2^{i_{0}-1}L_{0}\leq\gamma_{f}(\epsilon).\end{array}

Hence, we get that (
6.2
) is valid for
k=0
.

Further, for some
A\geq 0
and
L>0
, consider the functions
a_{L}(A)
and
\tau_{L}(A)
, defined by the following equations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{a_{L}^{2}(A)\over A+a_{L}(A)}&=&{1\over L},\quad\tau_{L}(A)\;=\;{a_{L}(A)\over A+a_{L}(A)}.\end{array}

From the second equation, we get
a_{L}(A)={\tau_{L}(A)A\over 1-\tau_{L}(A)}
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau^{2}_{L}(A)A^{2}&=&{1\over L}(A+a_{L}(A))(1-\tau_{L}(A))^{2}\;=\;{1\over L}A(1-\tau_{L}(A)).\end{array}

Hence,
{\tau^{2}_{L}(A)\over 1-\tau_{L}(A)}={1\over LA}
, which implies that
\tau_{L}(A)
is a decreasing function of
LA
.

Assume now that inequality (
6.2
) is true for some
k\geq 0
, that is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{k+1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-AUBound})}}{{\leq}}&2\gamma_{f}\left(\epsilon\cdot\tau_{k}\right),\quad\tau_{k}\;=\;\tau_{L_{k+1}}(A_{k}).\end{array}

Let us look first at the case
i_{k+1}=0
. Then
L_{k+2}=L_{k+1}
and, since
A_{k+1}\geq A_{k}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau_{k+1}&=&\tau_{L_{k+2}}(A_{k+1})\;=\;\tau_{L_{k+1}}(A_{k+1})\;\leq\;\tau_{L_{k+1}}(A_{k})\;=\;\tau_{k}.\end{array}

Therefore, in view of inequality (
5.8
), we have
L_{k+2}\leq 2\gamma_{f}(\epsilon\cdot\tau_{k+1})
.

Consider now the case
i_{k+1}\geq 1
.
In view of Lemma
11
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}2^{i_{k+1}-1}L_{k+1}&\leq&\gamma_{f}(t_{k+1,i_{k+1}-1})\;\leq\;\gamma_{f}(t_{k+1,i_{k+1}}).\end{array}

Hence,
L_{k+2}\leq 2\gamma_{f}(\epsilon\cdot\tau_{k+1})
, and we conclude that inequality (
6.2
) is valid for all
k\geq 0
.

Since
L_{k+2}\geq L_{k+1}
, as a by-product of our reasoning, we get the following relation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau_{k+1}&=&\tau_{L_{k+2}}(A_{k+1})\;\leq\;\tau_{L_{k+1}}(A_{k})\;=\tau_{k},\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

which is the first inequality in (
6.3
).

For proving the second part of (
6.3
), note that

{a_{i+1}\over A_{i+1}^{1/2}}={1\over\sqrt{L_{i+1}}}
.
Since the univariate function
\xi(\tau)=\sqrt{\tau}
,
\tau\geq 0
, is concave,
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{i+1}^{1/2}-A_{i}^{1/2}&\geq&\xi^{\prime}(A_{i+1})(A_{i+1}-A_{i})\;=\;{A_{i+1}-A_{i}\over 2A_{i+1}^{1/2}}\;=\;{1\over 2\sqrt{L_{i+1}}},\quad i\geq 0.\end{array}

Summing up these inequalities for
k\geq 1
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{k}&\geq&{1\over 4}\Big[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}{1\over\sqrt{L_{i}}}\Big]^{2}\;\geq\;{k^{2}\over 4L_{k}}.\end{array}

This implies that
\tau_{k}={1\over\sqrt{L_{k+1}A_{k+1}}}\leq{2\over k+1}
, which gives us the second part of (
6.3
).

\Box

Let us derive some consequences of Theorem
6
.
Since
\phi_{k+1}(y)\leq A_{k+1}\tilde{f}(y)+\beta(x_{0},y)
for all
y\in{\rm dom\,}\Psi
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tilde{f}(y_{k+1})-\tilde{f}(x^{*})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-UFGM})}}{{\leq}}&{D\over A_{k+1}}+{\epsilon\over 2},\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(6.6)

where
D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\beta(x_{0},x^{*})
.

Now, we can get the direct complexity bounds for method (
6.1
).

Theorem 7

1. If for some
k\geq 0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{k+1}&\leq&{2\over\epsilon}D,\end{array}

(6.7)

then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{2\over\tau_{k}}\hat{\mu}\Big(2\tau_{k}^{3/2}D^{1/2}\Big)&\geq&\epsilon.\end{array}

(6.8)

2. Let the number of iterations
k\geq 0
of method (
6.1
) satisfy inequality

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(k+1)\,\hat{\mu}\Big(2\left({2\over k+1}\right)^{3/2}D^{1/2}\Big)&\leq&\epsilon.\end{array}

(6.9)

Then
A_{k+1}\geq{2\over\epsilon}D
and
\tilde{f}(y_{k+1})-\tilde{f}(x^{*})\leq\epsilon
.

Proof:

1. By the rules of Step 2 of method (
6.1
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{1\over A_{k+1}\tau_{k}^{2}}&=&L_{k+1}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-AUBound})}}{{\leq}}\;2\gamma_{f}(\epsilon\,\tau_{k})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-GammaF})}}{{=}}\;{2\epsilon\,\tau_{k}\over s_{f}^{2}\left(\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\epsilon\,\tau_{k}\right)}.\end{array}

Therefore,

{2\tau^{3}_{k}\over s_{f}^{2}\left(\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\epsilon\,\tau_{k}\right)}\geq{1\over\epsilon A_{k+1}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-AssA})}}{{\geq}}\;{1\over 2D}
.
In other words,
2D^{1/2}\tau_{k}^{3/2}\geq s_{f}\left(\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\epsilon\,\tau_{k}\right)
, which is the same as (
6.8
).

2. Let (
6.9
) hold for some
k\geq 0
. Assume that the strict variant of inequality (
6.7
) is valid.
Note that, by inequality (
2.12
), the left-hand side of inequality (
6.8
) is monotonically increasing in
\tau_{k}
. Then, using the second part of inequality (
6.3
), we come to the following relation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\epsilon&<&(k+1)\,\hat{\mu}\Big(2\left({2\over k+1}\right)^{3/2}D^{1/2}\Big),\end{array}

which contradicts to condition (
6.9
). Hence, our assumption is wrong and
A_{k+1}\geq{2\over\epsilon}D
. Thus, in view of (
6.6
), point
y_{k+1}
satisfies the desired inequality.

\Box

This theorem has several important consequences. In particular, we can estimate the total number of additional calls of oracle during the search procedure of Step 2.
Denote

\begin{array}[]{rcl}N_{k}&=&\sum\limits_{p=0}^{k}i_{p}\;=\;\sum\limits_{p=0}^{k}\log_{2}{L_{p+1}\over L_{p}}\;=\;\log_{2}{L_{k+1}\over L_{0}},\quad k\geq 0.\end{array}

Assume that
A_{k+1}\leq{2\over\epsilon}D
. Then, in view of inequality (
6.8
), we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\epsilon&\leq&{2\over\tau_{k}}\hat{\mu}\Big(2\tau_{k}^{3/2}D^{1/2}\Big)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HMuUp})}}{{\leq}}\;2\tau^{1/2}_{k}\hat{\mu}(2D^{1/2}).\end{array}

Therefore, we get the following bound:

\begin{array}[]{c}N_{k}\;=\;\log_{2}{L_{k+1}\over L_{0}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-AUBound})}}{{\leq}}\;1+\log_{2}{\gamma_{f}\left(\epsilon\tau_{k}\right)\over L_{0}}\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GNon})}}{{\leq}}\;1+\log_{2}\gamma_{f}\left({\epsilon^{3}\over 4\hat{\mu}^{2}(2D^{1/2})}\right)-\log_{2}L_{0}.\end{array}

(6.10)

7
Conclusion

Let us discuss some consequences of the main results of this paper.

1.
Condition (
6.9
) can be seen as a rate of convergence of method (
6.1
). In view of inequality (
2.12
), it admits the following sufficient condition:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{2^{3/2}\over\sqrt{k+1}}\;\hat{\mu}(2D^{1/2})&\leq&\epsilon,\quad k\geq 1.\end{array}

(7.1)

This is a very general rate of convergence, which does not depend on the problem classes containing
f(\cdot)
. It depends only on curvature of function
f(\cdot)
with respect to the chosen norm
\|\cdot\|
.

2.
Let us show how the condition (
6.9
) works for the standard problem classes. Assume that
f\in\mathbb{C}^{1,\nu}(\mathbb{E})
with
\nu\in[0,1]
. Then
\hat{\mu}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-MUp})}}{{\leq}}{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}t^{1+\nu}
. Therefore, a sufficient condition for inequality (
6.9
) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{L_{\nu}\over 1+\nu}\Big(2^{5/2}D^{1/2}\Big)^{1+\nu}\left({1\over k+1}\right)^{1+3\nu\over 2}&\leq&\epsilon.\end{array}

(7.2)

It is known that this is the optimal rate of convergence for the corresponding problem class
[
5
]
. Our method achieves it simultaneously for all
\nu\in[0,1]
.

3.
Comparing the bounds (
5.15
) and (
6.9
), we can see that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(k+1)\,\hat{\mu}\Big(2\left({2\over k+1}\right)^{3/2}D^{1/2}\Big)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HMuUp})}}{{\leq}}&2\,\hat{\mu}\Big(2\left({2D\over k+1}\right)^{1/2}\Big),\quad k\geq 1.\end{array}

Thus, the Fast Gradient Method is usually better than the simple gradient methods, especially on good problem classes. Its superiority is maximal for the class
\mathbb{C}^{1,1}(\mathbb{E})
, where
\hat{\mu}(t)\leq{L_{1}\over 2}t^{2}
. For the class
\mathbb{C}^{1,0}(\mathbb{E})
with
\hat{\mu}(t)\leq L_{0}t
, they are both optimal
[
5
]
.

4.
The advantages of our framework can be demonstrated by non-standard problem classes. For example, the complexity bounds for the problem class of Example
4
, can be readily derived by substituting the particular form of its modulus of uniform smoothness (
3.9
) into the inequalities (
5.15
) and (
6.9
).

5.
For the future research, the most interesting direction is related to development of universal method based on the exact modulus of uniform convexity.
