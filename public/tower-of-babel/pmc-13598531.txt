Dual clinical dilemmas in late pregnancy: A multidisciplinary conservative approach to high-risk pulmonary embolism with persistent category III fetal status
Authors: Mohammed Y. Abushamma, Omar M. Marouf, Abdullah Alawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pregnancy increases the risk of venous thromboembolism, and pulmonary embolism (PE) remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality. Diagnosis is challenging due to symptom overlap with normal pregnancy, and management is complex in severe cases without hemodynamic instability. A 32-year-old woman at 32 weeks’ gestation presented with dyspnea, chest pain, and hypoxemia. CT pulmonary angiography confirmed a saddle PE with right ventricular strain. Despite high oxygen requirements, she remained hemodynamically stable. She was managed in the intensive care unit with therapeutic anticoagulation and high-flow oxygen, while thrombolysis was deferred due to bleeding risk. Fetal monitoring showed intermittent nonreassuring findings likely related to maternal hypoxia. Following multidisciplinary discussion, conservative management was continued. The patient improved clinically, was discharged on low-molecular-weight heparin, and later delivered at 39 weeks with good maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state that significantly increases the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and pulmonary embolism (PE), with an estimated fivefold higher risk compared to nonpregnant women. The overall incidence of VTE during pregnancy is approximately 1-2 per 1000 pregnancies and is associated with a maternal mortality rate of approximately 14.9% in the United States [1].

Risk factors for PE can be classified as modifiable and nonmodifiable. These include obesity, immobility, smoking, venous stasis, vascular injury, and hypercoagulability. Pregnancy, malignancy, and inherited thrombophilias account for approximately 40% of PE cases in pregnancy [2].

Diagnosing PE in pregnancy remains particularly challenging, as up to 50% of its signs and symptoms overlap with normal physiological changes, especially in the third trimester. Common features such as dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, and lower limb discomfort or swelling can be misleading, thereby complicating timely diagnosis. In addition, concerns regarding imaging modalities during pregnancy may delay appropriate investigation and treatment.

Management of PE during pregnancy depends on hemodynamic stability. In hemodynamically stable patients, the first-line treatment is therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin (UFH), which can be continued throughout pregnancy, with reassessment of VTE risk at delivery. In hemodynamically unstable or severely hypoxemic patients, thrombolysis is recommended if no contraindications exist. Catheter-directed thrombolysis or surgical thrombectomy may be considered when bleeding risk is high or thrombolytic therapy fails.

We report a case of a third-trimester pregnant woman complicated by saddle PE with fetal compromise, managed conservatively through a multidisciplinary approach. This case highlights the complexity of clinical decision-making in such scenarios due to limited evidence regarding the management of massive PE in pregnancy.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old woman at 32 weeks of gestation, previously healthy with no relevant medical or surgical history and taking only pregnancy supplements, presented to the obstetric emergency department with shortness of breath at rest and chest pain. These symptoms were associated with upper respiratory tract infection features, including dry cough, sore throat, nausea, low-grade fever, and arthralgia.

On examination, the patient appeared ill and tachypneic, with a respiratory rate of 30 breaths per minute and use of accessory muscles. Blood pressure was 113/70 mmHg, pulse rate 121 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation was 80% on room air, improving to 86% on 5 liters of oxygen via nasal cannula.

Chest examination revealed normal vesicular breath sounds without added sounds. No clinical evidence of lower limb deep vein thrombosis was observed.

Electrocardiography demonstrated regular sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 130 beats per minute. Chest radiography revealed a prominent right descending pulmonary artery with no evidence of consolidation, pleural effusion, or cardiomegaly (Fig. 1). The case was immediately discussed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT), including internists, obstetricians, and intensive care specialists. The decision was made to admit the patient to the intensive care unit (ICU).

During her ICU stay, the patient was commenced on a heparin infusion at 18 IU/kg/hour, titrated according to activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), with a target range of 60-70 seconds. Given her increasing oxygen requirements, endotracheal intubation was initially considered. However, on the second day of admission, a decision was made to avoid intubation due to the risk of increased right ventricular afterload secondary to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance—which may precipitate right ventricular failure and increase myocardial oxygen demand. Instead, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy was initiated. Despite 60 L/min flow with 100% FiO₂, oxygen saturation remained at 90%, subsequently deteriorating to 85%.

Thrombolysis was proposed by the MDT; however, as the patient remained hemodynamically stable (BP 118/76 mmHg) and given the significant maternal and fetal bleeding risk, conservative management was continued despite the risk of cardiopulmonary collapse. Daily nonstress tests (NSTs) were performed and initially demonstrated a reactive Category I tracing.

On the third day, fetal monitoring showed recurrent spontaneous decelerations with reduced variability, attributed to maternal hypoxemia. The biophysical profile score was 6/8 because of reduced fetal movements, while the umbilical artery Doppler studies remained normal. Pelvic examination revealed a closed and unfavorable cervix. From an obstetric perspective, the tracing fulfilled criteria for a Category III NST and would ordinarily warrant urgent delivery. However, due to the high anesthetic risk associated with intubation and general anesthesia in the setting of severe pulmonary embolism, surgical delivery posed substantial maternal risk.

These findings were discussed extensively with the family, who elected to prioritize maternal stabilization and continue conservative management despite the acknowledged fetal risk.

During the fourth and fifth, the patient showed marked clinical improvement. Oxygen requirements decreased to HFNC 35 L/min at 100% FiO₂, with a respiratory rate of 17 breaths per minute, blood pressure of 134/80 mmHg, and pulse rate of 87 beats per minute. She was able to mobilize, speak in full sentences, and exhibited no signs of respiratory distress or accessory muscle use.

Throughout the sixth and seventh day, oxygen therapy was reduced to 3 L/min via nasal cannula. Heparin infusion was discontinued and replaced with therapeutic LMWH 80 mg twice daily. Vital signs remained stable (BP 125/84 mmHg, SpO₂ 92%, pulse 92 beats per minute). The patient continued to improve and was subsequently maintained on room air with oxygen saturation of 95%, blood pressure of 123/87 mmHg, and pulse rate of 95 beats per minute. She was then transferred to the obstetric ward.

Repeat echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 55%), normal left ventricular size, no significant valvular abnormalities, no pericardial effusion, and no evidence of persistent right ventricular dysfunction.

The patient remained clinically stable and was discharged on Hospital Day 8 on LMWH 80 mg twice daily with close outpatient follow-up.

She subsequently underwent cesarean delivery at 39 weeks’ gestation. Throughout her recovery, she remained clinically stable. Shared decision-making regarding the mode of delivery was undertaken, and although vaginal delivery with an expedited second stage and avoidance of maternal straining was offered, the patient elected to proceed with cesarean section.

Discussion

Pregnancy is a physiologically prothrombotic state, fulfilling all components of Virchow’s triad. Fibrinogen levels increase by approximately 30%-50%, while clotting factors VII, VIII, IX, and X rise by up to 200%-1000%, resulting in a markedly hypercoagulable state [3,4]. In parallel, free protein S levels decrease by nearly 40%-50%, and acquired resistance to activated protein C develops in approximately 40% of pregnant women, further reducing endogenous anticoagulant activity [3].

Venous stasis becomes particularly significant in late pregnancy, as uterine compression of the inferior vena cava reduces lower limb venous return by up to 50%, especially in the supine position [5]. Progesterone-mediated venous dilation further contributes to venous pooling and impaired circulation.

Endothelial dysfunction completes Virchow’s triad, with vascular activation during pregnancy and potential vascular injury during delivery. The postpartum period represents the highest risk phase, accounting for 50%-60% of all venous thromboembolism (VTE) events [4,6].

Overall, the risk of VTE increases by 4- to 5-fold during pregnancy and up to 20-fold in the postpartum period [1,5]. Pulmonary embolism remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, with up to 50% of cases occurring in women without identifiable traditional risk factors [4].

This makes the pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm one of the most practical and evidence-based approaches for evaluating suspected pulmonary embolism in pregnancy.

Management of pulmonary embolism according to maternal hemodynamic stability

In pregnancy, stable high-risk (sub-massive) pulmonary embolism with right ventricular strain is typically managed with therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin as first-line treatment. Observational data demonstrate maternal survival rates exceeding 98%-99% when treatment is initiated promptly, although there remains a 5%-10% risk of clinical deterioration, particularly in the presence of significant right ventricular dysfunction or delayed diagnosis [8].

Thrombolysis is reserved for hemodynamic instability or clinical collapse. In pregnancy, its use is associated with maternal survival rates of approximately 90%-95%, but it carries a substantial risk of major bleeding (18%-28%) and fetal loss of up to 20%, supporting current guideline preference for anticoagulation in stable patients [9].

Massive or saddle pulmonary embolism presenting with shock or cardiac arrest is a life-threatening obstetric emergency requiring immediate reperfusion therapy. Systemic thrombolysis in hemodynamically unstable pregnant patients has been associated with maternal survival rates of approximately 92%-94%, although maternal mortality remains around 6%-8%, particularly in cases with delayed diagnosis or peri-arrest presentation [9].

Major bleeding occurs in approximately 17%-28% of thrombolysed pregnant patients, with intracranial hemorrhage reported in less than 2%. The risk is higher in peripartum or postpartum administration (Leonhardt et al., Am J Obstet Gynecol, 2018; Martin et al., Chest, 2016). Fetal outcomes are primarily determined by maternal hypoxia and hemodynamic instability rather than drug transfer, with fetal loss reported in approximately 10%-20% and preterm delivery in 15%-30% of cases [9].

The decision to defer thrombolysis was based on the patient's preserved hemodynamic stability despite extensive pulmonary embolism and right ventricular strain. Current guidelines reserve thrombolytic therapy primarily for patients with shock, hemodynamic deterioration, or impending cardiovascular collapse because of the significant maternal bleeding and obstetric risks associated with treatment during pregnancy [[8], [9], [10]].

Therefore, therapeutic anticoagulation with close intensive care monitoring was considered the most appropriate management strategy.

Respiratory support was similarly individualized. Given the potential for endotracheal intubation and positive-pressure ventilation to worsen right ventricular function in severe pulmonary embolism, high-flow nasal cannula therapy was used to maintain oxygenation while avoiding the immediate hemodynamic risks of invasive ventilation.

The coexistence of persistent nonreassuring fetal monitoring created a challenging management dilemma.

As maternal hypoxemia can lead to secondary fetal compromise, the multidisciplinary team prioritized maternal cardiopulmonary stabilization rather than immediate delivery. The subsequent normalization of fetal monitoring following maternal clinical improvement supported this conservative approach and suggested that the fetal abnormalities were primarily related to reversible maternal hypoxemia.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that even extensive pulmonary embolism in pregnancy can be successfully managed without thrombolysis when the patient remains hemodynamically stable. Careful monitoring, multidisciplinary collaboration, and individualized decision-making are essential to achieving favorable outcomes for both mother and fetus.
