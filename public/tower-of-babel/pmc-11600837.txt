Physiological and molecular mechanisms of leaf response to high-temperature stress in high-temperature-resistant soybean varieties
Authors: Jiajia Li, Xianguan Zhi, Haoran Chen, Linying Chen, Yun Lu, Wei Liao, Zhuo Tian, Meiyan Wu, Yajing Shan, Heng Wang, Long Yan, Bingqiang Liu, Xiaobo Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith increasing global limate warm, high temperature (HT) is one of limiting factors for soybean yield and quality. Exploring HT resistance-related functional genes and their corresponding molecular mechanisms is of great value. In our previous report, compared with HD14 (HT sensitive), JD21 is an HT-resistant variety, and further analysis of the transcriptome and proteome has revealed the HT tolerance mechanism of JD21 anthers. We found that compared with those of HD14 (28.72%), the leaves of JD21 also exhibited HT resistance, and the degree of leaf wilting in JD21 plants after HT stress treatment was 11.02%; however, the regulatory mechanism of the response of JD21 to HT stress is still unclear.ResultsIn this study, comparative transcriptome analysis of JD21 and HD14 soybean leaves after HT stress and field control plants was performed by RNA-seq analysis. The results showed that the number of upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in JD21 and HD14 was greater than the number of downregulated DEGs after HT stress, and the number of up- or down-regulated DEGs in JD21 was higher than those of HD14. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that many DEGs were involved in various molecular functions and metabolic pathways. QRT‒PCR analysis verified that the gene expression pattern results determined via RNA–seq was reliable. In addition, through analysis of gene expression level and conserved domain, 18 key candidate genes related to the response of soybean leaves to HT stress were screened.ConclusionsThis study systematically revealed the regulation mechanism of soybean leaves molecular transcription level by RNA-seq, and several key candidate DEGs (transcription factor, HSPs, HSFs, GmCYP78A6, etc.) involved in the response to HT stress were identified based on the bioinformatics analysis. Background

Soybean is an important cash crop that can provide nutrients such as oil and protein for human and animals [1], but it is sensitive to changes in sunshine duration and temperature conditions [2]. High-temperature (HT) stress caused by climate change poses a threat to soybean at all stages of growth and development. This stress has become one of the essential factors restricting soybean yield and quality [3]. In recent years, soybean crops in the Huang-Huai-Hai region of China have frequently experienced continuous HT episodes during the flowering period, which is a crucial factor underlying large-scale yield reduction [2]. Materials with tolerance to extreme stress are widely used in HT, drought, and saline-alkali stress studies; the approach of screening of candidate HT-related genes by transcriptome sequencing of HT-resistant and HT-sensitive varieties is one of the essential methods for screening and analyzing the molecular mechanism and metabolic regulation network, which is involved in the corresponding HT stress response and for cultivating new HT-resistant soybean varieties [4]. Therefore, understanding the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and regulatory metabolic networks of extremely HT-resistant varieties is essential for cultivating HT-resistant soybean varieties.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) is the study of transcripts produced at specific developmental stages or under physiological conditions and can be used to reveal gene function [5]. With continuous technological development, an increasing number of transcriptomic studies related to abiotic stress have been conducted in recent years. Wang et al. used RNA-seq to analyze cotton methylation-related genes under salt stress and identified an essential gene, GhECT6, involved in the response to salt stress [6]. Through transcriptome analysis, Raju et al. found that 1137 genes were upregulated in sugarcane under HT stress and that the expression of the ferredoxin-dependent glutamate synthase and stress protein DDR-48 was significantly induced by HT [7]. Comparative transcriptomic and proteomic analysis of soybean root hairs under HT stress revealed 10 key genes involved in the response to HT stress [8]. Through transcriptomic sequencing of two melon varieties with HT tolerance and HT sensitivity, it was found that the tolerant variety exhibited stronger antioxidant capacity, while the sensitive variety showed significant suppression and damage in PN, Fv/Fm, and chloroplast structure, revealing the main reasons for the differences in their tolerance [9]. In addition, the metabolism of 35 soybean seed components differed under HT (36/32℃) stress. Compared with those in the control treatment, the levels of metabolites such as asparagine, histidine, and valine were significantly greater under HT stress, indicating that soybean seed components and their metabolite levels play essential roles in regulating HT stress [10]. The above results suggested that transcriptome sequencing has become an essential means for analyzing the molecular mechanism underlying the soybean response to abiotic stress.

In our previous study, we conducted a combined analysis of RNA-seq and iTRAQ data from anthers of HT-resistant (JD21) and HT-sensitive (HD14) soybean plants [4]. However, the molecular mechanism involved in the response to HT stress between JD21 and HD14 leaves remained to be further explored. In this study, comparative transcriptomic analysis was performed on the leaves of JD21 and HD14 plants after HT stress treatment and under natural field conditions to analyze the molecular mechanism involved in the response of these plants to HT stress. The results of this study will help us to further understand the molecular mechanism of soybean leaves under HT stress and provide basic data and some potential candidate genes for better understanding the effects of HT on soybean leaves at is transcriptional level and the underlying molecular mechanism.

Results

Physiological index analysis for JD21 and HD14

The nationally approved soybean variety JD21 exhibits a high and stable yield, early maturity and good lodging resistance, exceeding the national high-protein index [11]. Our early results showed that, compared with those in the field environment, the changes in the pollen germination rate, pod number per plant, effective pod number, seed number per plant and seed weight per plant in JD21 (HT-tolerant) plants after HT stress were less than those in HD14 (HT-sensitive) plants. The above results showed that JD21 plants had better HT resistance [2, 12]. Moreover, we constructed a gene expression network of JD21 and HD14 anthers under HT stress and identified the key candidate DEGs related to the anther response to HT stress by transcriptome and proteome sequencing and combined these findings [4]. The results showed that JD21 plants were more resistant to HT than HD14 plants, possibly due to the upregulation of the expression of more genes and the regulation of HSP family proteins and transcription factors to maintain key metabolic pathways, such as those involved in plant hormone signal transduction [2, 4]. Moreover, previous studies had shown that the reason why JD21 plants exhibit better HT resistance might be related to the resistance of leaves to HT stress (the degree of leaf wilting in JD21 plants after HT treatment was 11.02%, while with that in the sensitive HD14 plants was 28.72%) [2]. However, the molecular mechanism involved in the leaf response to HT stress remained unclear. To further analyze the regulatory mechanism underlying the leaf response to HT stress, Illumina sequencing technology was used to compare and analyze the transcriptomes of JD21 and HD14 plants and the control after HT stress treatment.

Sequencing data quality analysis

Illumina sequencing yielded a total of 88.8 G of clean bases. The practical data volume of each sample ranged from 6.95 G ~ 8.21 G, the Q30 base distribution ranged from 94.28%~94.57%, and the average GC content was 44.82%. By comparing the reads to the reference genome (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Traces/sra/), the genome alignment of each sample was obtained. The practical base percentage was between 90.36% and 93.29% (Table 1), which was above 90%, indicating that the sequencing results were of high quality. Based on the comparison results, the expression of protein-coding genes was analyzed.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

According to the expression levels of protein-coding genes in different samples, DEG screening was performed. There were four comparison groups: (1) JD21 leaves treated with HT and JD21 control leaves under natural conditions (TJL vs. CJL); (2) HD14 leaves treated with HT and HD14 control leaves under natural conditions (THL vs. CHL); (3) JD21 and HD14 leaves under HT stress (TJL vs. THL); and (4) JD21 and HD14 control leaves under natural conditions (CJL vs. CHL). There were 342, 192, 1500 and 1255 significant DEGs in the four comparison groups. Compared with the control, 228 genes were upregulated and 114 genes were downregulated in the TJL vs. CJL comparison, and 117 genes were upregulated and 75 genes were downregulated in the THL vs. CHL comparison. Compared with HD14, 842 genes were upregulated and 658 were downregulated (TJL vs. THL) in JD21 after HT stress. Compared with HD14, 778 genes were upregulated and 477 were downregulated (CJL vs. CHL) in JD21 under natural conditions (Fig. 1).

GO functional annotation of differentially expressed genes

To understand the functions of the DEGs, they were divided into three categories for analysis: BP, CC and MF. GO annotation revealed that 232 (67.84%) of the 342 DEGs in TJL vs. CJL were annotated into 42 GO functional terms, including 20 BP, 12 CC, and 10 MF terms. Of the 192 DEGs in THL vs. CHL, 122 (63.54%) were annotated to 41 GO functional terms, including 20 BP, 12 CC and 9 MF terms; 920 DEGs (61.33%) in TJL vs. THL were annotated to 52 GO functional terms, including 22 BP, 18 CC, and 12 MF terms; and 1255 DEGs (64.86%) in CJL vs. CHL were annotated to 47 GO functional terms, including 21 BP, 14 CC, and 12 MF terms. These GO terms were involved mainly in the apoplast (GO: 0048046), the flavonoid biosynthesis process (GO: 0009813), cutin transport (GO: 0080051), and other biological processes. Since these pathways were enriched by many DEGs, they may be involved in the response of soybean plants to HT stress (Fig. 2).

KEGG metabolic pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

To study the participation of DEGs in biological functions, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs from the four comparison groups. Among the 342 DEGs identified in the TJL vs. CJL comparison, 77 were enriched in 18 metabolic pathways, of which 49 DEGs were upregulated and 28 were downregulated, mainly involved in the ‘environmental information processing-signal transduction’, ‘metabolism-carbohydrate metabolism’ and ‘metabolism-biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites’ pathways. Among the 192 DEGs identified in the THL vs. CHL comparison, 38 DEGs were enriched in 13 metabolic pathways, 27 of which were upregulated and 11 were downregulated, mainly involved in the ‘genetic information processing-folding’, ‘sorting and degradation’, ‘metabolism-signal transduction’ and ‘metabolism-carbohydrate metabolism’ pathways. Among the 1500 DEGs identified in the TJL vs. THL comparison, 391 were enriched in 22 metabolic pathways, of which 183 DEGs were upregulated and 208 were downregulated, mainly involved in the ‘environmental information processing-signal transduction’, ‘metabolism-carbohydrate metabolism’ and ‘metabolism-biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites’ pathways. Additionally, ‘environmental information processing-signal transduction’ was one of the most enriched pathways in the three groups, being enriched by 6 DEGs in TJL vs. CJL, 9 DEGs in THL vs. CHL, and 48 DEGs in TJL vs. THL, indicating that the metabolic pathways may be involved in the regulation of soybean response to HT stress (Fig. 3).

qRT‒PCR validation analysis

To verify the reliability of the RNA-seq results, 14 DEGs were randomly selected for qRT‒PCR verification with Gmactin as the internal reference gene [13, 14]. The results showed that the expression patterns of 13 genes (92.86%) were consistent with the RNA-seq results, indicating that the RNA-seq results were reliable (Fig. 4, Supplement Fig. 1).

Mining of HT-resistant candidate genes in soybean

To further clarify the regulatory genes involved in the response of soybean plants to HT stress, the DEGs obtained by transcriptome sequencing were analyzed in depth, the genes with average expression levels in three biological replicates of JD21 and HD14 before and after HT stress treatment were selected, and the expression characteristics of each gene in soybean leaves, flowers, roots, stems, seeds and other tissues were examined. Because the RNA-seq sample used was leaf tissue, genes with a > 10-fold change in expression in the leaves (40 in TJL vs. CJL; 14 in THL vs. CHL, with Glyma.14G102900 present in both comparison groups) were screened to construct a heatmap of tissue expression levels (Fig. 5).

Conserved domain analysis of candidate genes responsive to HT stress

Conserved domains in genes are usually associated with specific functions, such as transcriptional activation/inhibition, nuclear localization, and protein interaction. To further screen the DEGs responding to HT stress, the conserved domains of DEGs with a greater than 10-fold change in expression were analyzed via RNA-seq. The conserved domains of these candidate genes were identified via the MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) website. The results showed that some conserved domains of these candidate genes were involved in the regulation of the plant response to HT stress (Fig. 6). For example, Glyma.13G225700 and Glyma.20G156800 had the conserved domain HSF, Glyma.16G196200 and Glyma.07G128800 had the conserved domain HSF_DNA-bind, and Glyma.13G135500, Glyma.10G047700 and Glyma.03G078200 had conserved domains such as GST_N_3 (Table S1). A total of 11 conserved domains of 18 genes were involved in the plant response to HT stress (Table 2). Further sequencing analysis of the CDS region of 18 genes revealed that four base mutations were found in the conserved domain of Glyma.07G052300 (GmCYP78A6), three of which were non-synonymous mutations (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Temperature greatly regulates plant growth and development. HT affects plant growth and has become one of the essential factors restricting plant yield and quality [15]. Our previous results indicated that, compared with those in the field environment, the germination rate, pod number per plant, effective pod number, and seed number per plant changed under HT stress. The number of seeds per plant and the seed weight per plant were found to be lower for JD21 after HT stress in comparison to HD14, and the anthers of JD21 and HD14 after HT stress and those of the control were sequenced. To clarify the regulatory mechanism of the soybean leaf response to HT stress, the HT-resistant (JD21) and HT-sensitive (HD14) varieties treated with HT stress and the control were analyzed by transcriptomics.

The RNA-seq results showed that the number of upregulated DEGs in JD21 and HD14 was greater than the number of downregulated DEGs after HT stress, and the number of up- or down-regulated DEGs in JD21 was higher than those of HD14. These findings suggested that soybean leaves respond to HT stress by elavating the expression level of these genes, which was consistent with our earlier results [4]. Pradhan et al. showed that there were more DEGs in LT-sensitive varieties after 6 h of low temperature (LT) stress at 4 °C [16]. There were more upregulated DEGs than downregulated DEGs in the LT-tolerant varieties, and more DEGs were downregulated than upregulated in the LT-sensitive varieties. The results revealed differences in the regulatory mechanism involved in the responses to HT and LT stress. In this study, there were 150 DEGs in the TJL vs. CJL comparison (HT-tolerant varieties) compared with the THL vs. CHL comparison (HT-sensitive varieties), suggesting that the resistance of HT-tolerant varieties can be easily improved via changes in the expression of additional genes.

Heat shock protein response to HT stress and leaf development

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are closely related to the plant response to HT stress and are the main participants in plant resistance to HT. HT stress stimulates the upregulation of HSPs. The genes that encode HSPs play essential roles in plants under stress conditions [17]. Studies have confirmed that HSP, hormone regulation, ROS and other metabolic pathways in plants are adaptive mechanisms involved in the response to HT stress [18]. Das et al. reported that the expression of EF-Tu and HSP-related proteins was significantly upregulated under HT and drought stress [9]. HSPs play a crucial role in mediating the plant response to HT stress. In the present study, the HSP17, HSP18, and HSP22 genes were upregulated 3-, 4-, and 10-fold, respectively, between TJL and CJL under HT stress; 8 HSP-encoding DEGs were enriched between THL and CHL, 7 of which were upregulated and 1 downregulated after HT stress. Five HSP DEGs were found in the TJL vs. THL group, 4 of which were upregulated and 1 of which was downregulated. Compared with those in HD14, most of the HSP genes in JD21 were upregulated after HT stress, indicating that soybean plants resist heat stress by activating the HSP family. The difference in HT-resistance between JD21 and HD14 may be related to the differential expression of HSPs.

Transcription factor response to HT stress

Many transcription factor families are closely related to abiotic stress. The WRKY family is a unique family of plant transcription factors that play vital roles in the response to biotic and abiotic stresses. AtWRKY25, AtWRKY26, and AtWRKY33 in Arabidopsis play a positive regulatory role in the synergy between ethylene-activated proteins and HSPs and mediate the response of plants to HT stress [19]. In this study, 8, 0, and 13 WRKY genes were significantly differentially expressed in the THL vs. CHL, TJL vs. CJL, and TJL vs. THL comparisons, respectively. These upregulated or downregulated DEGs helped to improve the tolerance of soybean plants to HT. In addition, MYB transcription factors have also been reported to be related to HT resistance. Using transcriptome sequencing, Zhao et al. identified 6 HT resistance genes in wheat, and further studies have shown that TaMYB80 improves the HT resistance and drought resistance of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana plants [20]. Ectopic expression of AtMYB68 in rapeseed can improve its HT resistance [21]. CmMYB012 expression is induced by HT stress, and this gene inhibits the biosynthesis of flavonoids by regulating CmFNS [22]. In the present study, MYB-encoding DEGs were enriched in each group. MYB11 showed a downward trend in THL vs. CHL and TJL vs. CJL after HT stress, suggesting that this gene may play a negative regulatory role in soybean under HT stress. Moreover, transcriptomic analysis presented the enrichment of NAC transcription factors. The proteins encoded by the rice genes OsNAC127 and OsNAC129 can form heterodimers during rice grain filling and coordinate multiple pathways to regulate seed development and the heat stress response in the reproductive stage [23]. LlNAC014 in lily enhances heat tolerance by sensing HT and translocating to the nucleus to activate the DREB2-HSFA3 module. These findings indicate that the NAC family is also involved in the plant HT resistance pathway.

Effect of plant hormone signal transduction under HT stress

Plant hormones are essential for the response to stress and in signaling networks [24]. Studies have shown that HT stress induces ROS production by increasing ABA levels and subsequently inducing the expression of NADPH oxidase in plants to increase antioxidant capacity, thereby improving the resistance of plants to HT [25]. Gray et al. demonstrated that, compared with 20 °C and 28 °C, HT stress can regulate IAA synthesis or catabolism to mediate hypocotyl elongation [26]. In addition, compared with those in the HT-resistant KSG1214, the accumulation of amino acids, ABA, and IAA conjugates in anthers of the HT-sensitive KSG1177 increased significantly under HT stress, indicating that these hormones play essential regulatory roles in enhancing the tolerance of wheat to HT stress [27]. In this study, 1 DEG involved in plant hormone signal transduction was enriched in the THL vs. CHL comparison, while 6 related DEGs were upregulated in the TJL vs. CJL comparison after HT stress. Five DEGs of this type were enriched between TJL and THL, of which 4 and 1 were upregulated and downregulated, respectively. Studies have shown that the exogenous application of IAA can effectively improve the tolerance of plants to HT, and the application of IAA can reduce the damage caused by HT stress during wheat anther development [28]. Plant hormones regulate plant adaptive responses by changing stress response genes, thereby improving plant tolerance to abiotic stress [29]. Growth under HT promoted an increase in auxin levels, increasing hypocotyl elongation [26]. In summary, plant hormone signal transduction may play an important role in the tolerance of soybean to HT stress.

Effects of other metabolic pathways under HT stress

Plants exhibit changes in transcription, translation, protein levels, enzyme activity, and plant hormone concentrations to achieve homeostasis for adaptation to HT stress [30]. Jin et al. found many DEGs enriched in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum in the transcriptomes of the resistant jujube variety ‘HR’ and the sensitive variety ‘HS’ under HT stress, indicating that this metabolic pathway is involved in the regulation of the HT stress response [31]. The MAPK signaling pathway is an essential transmitter of signals from the cell surface to the nucleus. The expression of the MAPK family member MPK increases under low-temperature, high-salt, and mechanical stress [32]. MPK3/MPK6 phosphorylation can mediate the negative feedback regulation of ethylene biosynthesis induced by B. cinerea and upregulate the expression of defensin genes to increase the resistance of Arabidopsis to B. cinerea [33]. Chen et al. [34] identified three Arabidopsis MAPK homologs, GhMAP3K14, GhMKK11, and GhMPK31, in cotton and demonstrated that these genes can regulate the tolerance of cotton to drought stress. The above findings illustrate the role of MAPK in the plant response to abiotic stress. Ca2+ can reduce the increase in cell membrane permeability and the accumulation of the membrane lipid peroxidation product MDA caused by HT stress [35]. The HT-resistant S1 mutant presented an increase in the Ca2+ content of mesophyll cells and subsequent damage to the cell membrane and chloroplasts [36]. Ca2+ is involved in the activation of MAPK cascades under various stimulation conditions and can bind to a variety of calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) to activate MAPKs or NADPH oxidase (RBOHD) [37]. Therefore, the Ca2+ signaling pathway is also an important factor involved in regulating the crop response to HT stress. These metabolic pathways play important roles in the response of plants to abiotic stresses such as HT; therefore, they may also be involved in the regulation of HT stress, and their specific functions need to be further verified.

Screening of candidate genes in soybean response to HT stress

In this study, 53 candidate genes (40 in TJL vs. CJL and 14 in THL vs. CHL, with Glyma.14G102900 present in both comparison groups) with expression levels greater than 10 were screened through transcriptome data analysis (Fig. 5). Further analysis of the conserved domains revealed that the conserved domains of 18 genes were involved in the plant response to HT stress (Fig. 6; Table 2; Table S1). Through transcriptome sequencing, He et al. revealed that HSF, WRKY, etc., are involved in the plant response to HT stress [38]. Related research has shown that GmHSFA2, a positive regulator, improved the HT tolerance of soybean CMS-based F1 plants [39]. The glutathione-S-transferase (GST) gene plays a major role in the response to oxidative stress. Studies have shown that by regulating HSP family proteins, GSTs, etc., HT-resistant plant species perform better than HT-sensitive plant varieties under stress [2]. In lettuce, the inhibition of LsDOG1 expression allows seeds to germinate at HTs and promotes early flowering [40]. Preeti et al. showed that TabZIP is induced by HT stress and can regulate the expression of oxidative stress-related genes and reduce the accumulation of ROS; moreover, TabZIP may play an important role in the crop response to HT stress [41]. In this study, we screened candidate genes containing conserved domains such as HSF, WRKY, and GST domains. Studies have shown that CYP family members play an important role in plant response to abiotic stress [42]. Kajino et al. [43] showed that EsCYP78A5 could increase the permeability of plants and improve the tolerance of plants to osmotic shock, salt shock, oxidation and heat stress by encoding AtKLU. In this study, three non-synonymous mutations in the conserved domain of GmCYP78A6 were found in HD14 and no mutation was found in JD21. It is speculated that this may be one of the reasons for the difference in HT resistance between JD21 and HD14, and that GmCYP78A6 may play an important role in soybean response to HT stress.

Conclusion

The soybean leaf gene expression networks of HT-resistant (JD21) and HT-sensitive (HD14) plants were constructed by RNA-seq under HT stress and control conditions. There was revealed the regulation mechanism of soybean leaf molecular transcription level and several key candidate DEGs (transcription factor, HSPs, HSFs, GmCYP78A6, etc.) involved in the response to HT stress were identified based on the bioinformatics analysis, The results provided a theoretical basis for studying the response mechanism of soybean leaf to HT stress.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The soybean cultivars “Jidou 21” (JD21; HT tolerant) and “Hedou 14” (HD14; HT sensitive) were grown in 30 cm×40 cm×20 cm plastic pots in 2019 at the Nongcui Garden Experimental Station of Anhui Agricultural University, China. An HT environment was created by artificial warming (by mulching with plastic greenhouse film) in the field at the flowering stage [2]. A temperature and humidity data logger (RC-4HC, Elitech, Berkhamstead, UK) was used to monitor the temperature stress for 7 days (09:00–16:00 daily), and the temperature control was higher than the field control by about 5 ℃. Plant leaf samples were collected the next morning after HT stress treatment, and the control plant leaf samples were collected simultaneously in the natural field environment. Samples of HD14 leaves in the HT stress treatment group (THL) and control group (CHL) and JD21 leaves in the HT stress treatment group (TJL) and control group (CJL) were collected after HT stress. A total of 12 libraries with 3 biological replicates were collected for transcriptomic data analysis.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and Illumina sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing was performed, total RNA was extracted from the sample, and the DNA was digested with DNase. Eukaryotic mRNA was enriched with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads (if the sample was prokaryotic, rRNA was removed with a kit to increase mRNA yield). The mRNA was disrupted into short fragments by the addition of a disrupting reagent. The disrupted mRNA was used as a template to synthesize single-stranded cDNA with a six-base random primer, a two-stranded reaction system was subsequently used to synthesize two-stranded cDNA, after which the double-stranded cDNA was purified using a kit. The purified double-stranded cDNA was subjected to terminal repair. An A tail was added, the sequencing adapter was connected, the fragment size was selected, and finally, PCR amplification was carried out. After the constructed library was analyzed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, sequencing was performed using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten to produce 150 bp paired-end reads. After quality inspection, Illumina sequencing was performed.

Quality control

With the Illumina platform, a large amount of sample paired-end sequencing data was obtained. Given the influence of the data error rate on the results, Trimmomatic software was used to preprocess the original data, and the number of reads in the whole quality control process was statistically analyzed. After undergoing quality control, adapter removal, low-quality read removal, and trimming of low-quality bases from the 3’ and 5’ ends, the original sequencing read number, sufficient sequencing read number, Q30 value, and GC content were determined and comprehensively evaluated. We used hisat2 to align clean reads with the designated reference genome to obtain location information on the reference genome or genes, as well as sequence feature information unique to the sequenced sample.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

The DEGs in the two samples were screened mainly based on the fold change (FC) and P value in the difference in expression of the same gene between the two samples. That is, |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and P value < 0.05 were used as the thresholds for judging the significance of gene expression differences.

Bioinformatics analysis of differentially expressed genes

The bioinformatics analysis of the transcriptome involved mainly Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) metabolic pathway enrichment analysis. GO annotation was performed at three levels: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). GO functional analysis of DEGs can explain the enrichment of DEGs and the difference in gene function between samples. KEGG is a large-scale database generated by genome sequencing and other high-throughput experimental techniques in which molecular-level information is used to understand the functions of genes in biological systems.
