Characterization and genetic dissection of the powdery mildew resistance in cultivated emmer wheat accession L424
Authors: Dongming Li, Yaoxue Li, Ningning Yu, Lige Geng, Yanmin Qie, Hongxing Xu, Bo Wang, Jun Guo, Jiatong Li, Nina Sun, Linzhi Li, Guantong Pan, Yintao Dai, Yuli Jin, Pengtao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPowdery mildew, caused by the fungal pathogen Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), is a widespread disease affecting wheat globally, exerting significant adverse impacts on both wheat yield and quality. The utilization of powdery mildew resistance genes represents the most effective and sustainable strategy for managing this disease. Cultivated emmer wheat (T. dicoccum, 2n = 4x = 28, AABB), recognized as one of the key ancestral species of common wheat, has emerged as a valuable genetic resource for enhancing powdery mildew resistance in modern wheat breeding programs.ResultsThe cultivated emmer wheat accession L424 exhibits a high level of resistance to multiple Bgt isolates under both seedling and adult stages. In this study, a novel gene PmL424 underlying this resistance was identified in L424. Using bulked segregant RNA sequencing (BSR-Seq) and molecular marker analysis, PmL424 was mapped to a 3.9 cM genetic interval on chromosome arm 2BS, flanked by the markers YTU424-L99 and YTU424-L10, corresponding to a physical interval of 1.09 Mb (21.63–22.72 Mb). PmL424 likely represents a new allele of Pm68, given its origin, resistance spectrum, and physical map with previously reported resistance genes on a similar chromosomal interval. Seven genes in this interval were associated with disease resistance and were considered as the candidate genes of PmL424. BSR-Seq analysis further revealed 2,325 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between resistant and susceptible bulks, which were then used for further Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. qPCR analysis of seven disease resistance-associated genes within the candidate interval demonstrated that three genes TRITD2Bv1G010130, TRITD2Bv1G010140 and TRITD2Bv1G010240 were significantly up-regulated in L424 compared to the susceptible parent Langdon after inoculating the Bgt isolate E09. Further analysis revealed that only genes TRITD2Bv1G010130 and TRITD2Bv1G010240 exhibit sequence variations between resistant and susceptible genotypes. Validation using 46 susceptible wheat accessions confirmed that the marker YTU024-L99 could accurately trace the presence of PmL424, providing practical tools for marker-assisted selection breeding of PmL424.ConclusionsIn the present study, a Pm gene PmL424 was identified using genetic analysis, BSR-Seq analysis and molecular markers in the cultivated emmer wheat accession L424, and three key candidate genes were also captured through time-course analysis post inoculation with Bgt isolate E09. Potential regulatory genes were also identified through DEG analysis using GO and KEGG pathway analysis, unraveling their potential transcriptional regulation mechanisms when fighting Bgt invasion. These multi-dimensional analyses provide valuable insights into the dissection of the molecular and genetic basis underlying the powdery mildew resistance, as well as establishing a foundation for MAS using PmL424.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07800-3.Keywords: Cultivated emmer wheat, Powdery mildew, Gene mapping, Molecular marker-assisted selection

Introduction

Common wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), a globally cultivated staple crop, plays a vital role in ensuring food security and alleviating global hunger [1]. However, wheat production faces numerous biotic and abiotic stresses [2–4]. Among the biotic threats, powdery mildew is one of the most devastating wheat disease that severely affect both yield and quality [5]. This disease, caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt), manifests as irregular white lesions on leaves, eventually forming powdery sporulation [6]. After years of mycosis transmission, it has occured worldwide, and the current prevention and control measures mainly rely on fungicides and host resistance [7, 8]. In comparison, the latter is considered to be one of the most effective and economical measures, and hence critical for sustainable disease control.

To date, over 140 powdery mildew resistance (Pm) genes derived from wheat and its relatives have been reported [9, 10]. However, evolving pathogens have rendered most of the Pm genes ineffective, necessitating continuous exploration of new resistance sources for wheat breeding programs [11, 12]. Compared with common wheat, its wild relatives evolved into more abundant genetic variations during the process of environmental adaptation, especially disease resistance ability. For powdery mildew resistance, many alien Pm genes have been reported, such as Pm7, Pm8, Pm17, Pm20 and Pm56 derived from Secale cereale (2n = 2x = 14, RR), and Pm21, Pm55, Pm62 and Pm67 derived from Dasypyrum villosum (2n = 2x = 14, VV). Notably, the ancestral species of common wheat, including Aegilops tauschii (2n = 2x = 14, DD), T. urartu (2n = 2x = 14, AA), T. monococcum (2n = 2x = 14, AA), T. dicoccoides (2n = 4x = 28, AABB), and T. timopheevii (2n = 4x = 28, AAGG), serve as another type of valuable genetic reservoirs for disease resistance due to their extensive genetic diversity and close evolutionary relationship with cultivated wheat. More than 50% of the reported Pm genes were introgressed from these species [9].

In the process of gene mining from wheat, its complex and large genome poses huge challenges for cloning resistance genes and dissecting disease resistance mechanisms in the past [13]. However, recent technical breakthrough led to rapid development of innovative approaches such as high-throughput sequencing and the continuous emergence of diversified reference genome of common wheat and its relatives, which greatly promoted the progress of wheat functional genomics. In the high-throughput sequencing, bulked segregant RNA sequencing (BSR-Seq) offers a cost-effective and efficient solution for genetic studies in polyploid crops. It requires only two bulked samples, making it particularly suitable for small populations while maintaining a streamlined workflow and rapid data generation [14]. This method has been widely applied in gene mining of wheat, such as the cloning of the PmAeu1 from Ae. umbellulata [15] and the identification of the PmQ from the wheat landrace Qingxinmai [16]. Except for wheat, BSR-Seq also facilitated gene cloning in other crops, such as the identification of maize glossy leaf gene gl3. This case also demonstrates that this technology is also applicable to other crops [17].

Cultivated emmer wheat (T. dicoccum, 2n = 4x = 28, AABB), a tetraploid species, is regarded as one of the key progenitors of common wheat due to its shared A and B genomes with common wheat [18, 19]. This species harbors abundant desirable traits, particularly demonstrating significant advantages in coping with biotic and abiotic stresses [20]. To date, four Pm genes, namely Pm4a [21], Pm49 [22], Pm50 [23], and Pm71 [10], have been identified in cultivated emmer wheat, which highlights the genetic potential of this species in disease resistance breeding. The continuous mining of novel Pm genes in this species is crucial for enhancing wheat improvement against powdery mildew. In this study, we employed a cultivated emmer wheat accession L424 to: (i) characterize its powdery mildew resistance and map the underlying resistance locus; (ii) identify candidate genes and assess their expression profiles following Bgt invasion; and (iii) develop closely linked molecular markers to facilitate marker-assisted selection (MAS) in wheat breeding programs.

Results

Evaluation and genetic analysis of powdery mildew resistance in L424

After inoculation, L424 was resistant to all the 15 tested Bgt isolates with infection type (IT) 0, suggesting its high and broad spectrum resistance to powdery mildew (Fig. 1a). Take the Bgt isolate E09 as an example, differential infection responses were observed among different plants: L424 displayed no any visible symptoms, with an IT 0, whereas the susceptible parent Langdon (LDN) exhibited severe spores with IT 4 (Fig. 1b). Genetic analysis of 196 F₂ plants derived from the cross L424 × LDN revealed 146 resistant and 50 susceptible individuals, fitting a ratio of 3:1 for Mendel monogenic inheritance (Table 1). Further validation using 190 F2:3 families harvested from the F2 population confirmed monogenic inheritance, with 47 homozygous resistant, 95 heterozygous resistant, and 48 homozygous susceptible plants, also fitting a ratio of 1:2:1 for Mendelian monogenic segregation (Table 1). In conclusion, the resistance to Bgt isolate E09 in L424 was governed by a single dominant gene, provisionally designated as PmL424.

To further clarify if the resistance to other avirulent Bgt isolates was also provided by PmL424, that was to say there were no other effective Pm genes were involved in L424, randomly selected seven Bgt isolates (F01, F02, F06, F08, F09, F17, F28) were used to infect randomly selected 10 homozygous resistant, 10 segregating, and 10 homozygous susceptible F2:3 families, similar responses as that of the Bgt isolate E09 were observed. This suggested that PmL424 conferred resistance to all the avirulent Bgt isolates tested, and no additional effective Pm genes were involved in L424.

BSR-Seq analysis revealed high-quality sequence data

BSR-Seq produced comprehensive high-throughput sequencing data with exceptional quality: 1) a total of 21.25 Gb clean data was generated from the resistant bulk, exhibiting a Q30 score exceeding 94% and GC content ranging from 51 to 69%; 2) the susceptible bulk produced 22.35 Gb of clean data with even higher quality (Q30 > 94%) and a GC content variation between 48 and 68%. After genome alignment, approximately 142.30 million reads from the resistant bulk and 149.40 million reads from the susceptible bulk were successfully mapped to the durum wheat reference genome (cv. Svevo). The obtained sequencing data met the stringent quality standards and were highly suitable for downstream analyses.

Molecular mapping

Upon BSR-Seq, SNP index analysis was carried out, and a total of 1,348 SNPs were identified between the resistant and susceptible bulks (Fig. 2a). Based on the ΔSNP index values, only one significant candidate region was detected, spanning the distal portion of chromosome arm 2BS. To validate this result, euclidean distance (ED) algorithm was also performed using these SNPs, which consistently identified the similar genomic region, further confirming the reliability of the candidate region (Fig. 2b). To genetically map PmL424, 12 previously reported markers in this candidate interval were selected for genotyping on the F2:3 families of L424 × LDN, and additional 38 newly developed markers were designed based on BSR-Seq for the same purpose. As a result, 10 of 50 markers exhibited consistent polymorphism between the resistant and susceptible parents and bulks (Table S1). Subsequently, 190 F2:3 families were genotyped using the ten polymorphic markers, enabling the construction of a genetic linkage map for PmL424. PmL424 was then locked within a 3.9 cM genetic interval flanked by the markers YTU024-L99 and YTU024-L10, corresponding to a 1.09 Mb (21.63–22.72 Mb) physical interval based on the reference genome of durum wheat Svevo (Ref Svevo v1) (Fig. 3, Table S2). Collinearity analysis was then conducted between durum wheat cv. Svevo and four bread wheat accessions using the flanking markers YTU024-L99 and YTU024-L10 of PmL424 (Fig. 4). This interval was 1.09 Mb referred to Ref Svevo v1. Meanwhile, in the reference genomes T. aestivum (10 + Genome Mace), T. aestivum (10 + Genome Jagger), T. aestivum (Fielder), and T. aestivum (IWGSC RefSeq v1.1), the corresponding intervals were 1.03 Mb, 1.04 Mb, 1.03 Mb and 1.1 Mb, respectively, and these intervals displayed high collinearity across different wheat genomes (Fig. 4).

Comparison of PmL424 with known Pm genes on chromosome arm 2BS

Multiple Pm genes, including Pm26/MlIW170, Pm42, PmXQ-0508, Pm68, PmWE99 and PmL709 have been previously reported on chromosome arm 2BS (Fig. 3c). To investigate the relationship between PmL424 and these reported Pm genes, 15 different virulent Bgt isolates were used to inoculate the donors of these genes. Only L424 and TRI1796 (carrying Pm68) exhibited resistance to all these isolates. In comparison, XQ-0508 (carrying PmXQ0508), B05329 (carrying Pm42), IWM170 (carrying MlIW170/Pm26), WE99 (carrying PmWE99), Pm26–40 (carrying Pm26/MlIW170) showed resistance to 80.0%, 86.7%, 60.0%, 73.0% and 93% of the tested Bgt isolates, respectively (Fig. 1a). These findings indicated that PmL424 exhibits a distinct resistance spectrum compared to other known Pm genes on the same chromosome arm, except for Pm68.

Analysis and identification of candidate genes

Gene annotation within the candidate interval identified seven disease resistance-associated genes, including TRITD2Bv1G010060, TRITD2Bv1G010110, TRITD2Bv1G010130, TRITD2Bv1G010140, TRITD2Bv1G010230, TRITD2Bv1G010240 and TRITD2Bv1G010300 (Table S3). Among them, TRITD2Bv1G010110 was annotated with low confidence, while the remaining six ones were assigned high confidence.

Then, we performed time-course infection process using the Bgt isolate E09. Microscopic analysis revealed distinct infection stages. The initial 0–0.5 h post-inoculation (hpi) period represented the primary hyphal growth phase, appressorium formation at 12 hpi, penetration at 48 hpi, and colony establishment at 72 hpi, with 0 hpi serving as controls (Fig. 5a).

The seven disease resistance-associated genes within the candidate interval were then selected for profiling their expression pattern following Bgt invasion to determine the potential as the candidate genes for PmL424. As a result, only three genes, TRITD2Bv1G010130, TRITD2Bv1G010140, and TRITD2Bv1G010240, showed up-regulated transcriptional levels in L424 but not in LDN. On the contrary, TRITD2Bv1G010110 exhibited significant up-regulation of transcriptional levels in LDN but not in L424 (Fig. 5b). These results indicated that TRITD2Bv1G010130, TRITD2Bv1G010140, and TRITD2Bv1G010240 could be initially regarded as the candidate genes of PmL424. We further analyzed the BSR-seq data, which revealed that only TRITD2Bv1G010130 and TRITD2Bv1G010240 genes exhibit sequence variations including SNPs and InDels between resistant and susceptible genotypes. In conclusion, we ultimately identified TRITD2Bv1G010130 and TRITD2Bv1G010240 as candidate genes.

DEGs enrichment analysis

To identify potential regulatory genes underlying the resistance process, 2,325 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between resistant and susceptible bulks using BSR-Seq, of which 1,696 ones were down-regulated and 629 ones were up-regulated using the expression index of susceptible bulk as a standard (Fig. S1). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed DEGs were mainly involved in two categories (Fig. S2a): (i) molecular function domain, particularly transmembrane transporter activity, monooxygenase activity, and oxidoreductase activity, suggesting that DEGs within the candidate region may play crucial roles in substrate transport, oxidative reactions, and metabolic processes, potentially contributing to the observed phenotypic differences between the resistant and susceptible bulks; (ii) biological processes related to acid/oxidative stress response and PAMP-triggered immunity, suggesting that the DEGs may contribute to powdery mildew resistance through pathogen recognition, defense signaling, and ROS-mediated defense mechanisms. Furthermore, kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis highlighted several disease resistance-related mechanisms including activation of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway to synthesize antimicrobial secondary metabolites (Fig. S2b), coordination of MAPK signaling and host–pathogen interaction pathways to regulate immune responses and glutathione-mediated antioxidant metabolism effectively neutralizes ROS accumulation triggered by pathogen infection (Fig. S3).

Breeding potential of L424 and evaluation of molecular markers for MAS

Cultivated emmer wheat L424 exhibits distinct agronomic traits compared to common wheat. It typically displays taller plant height, stronger tillering capacity, and denser spikes with two-grained spikelets (Fig. 6). While its yield potential is generally lower than common wheat, L424 demonstrates superior resistance against powdery mildew. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that L424 holds substantial breeding potential and could be utilized to further improve the quality of common wheat.

Agronomic performance of L424 and three wheat cultivars Huixianhong (HXH), Kenong 199 (KN199) and Tainong 18 (TN18). a Comparison of plant height; b Comparison of spike length; c Comparison of grain length and grain width

To facilitate MAS of PmL424, we evaluated four flanking markers across 46 susceptible wheat accessions. Genotyping analysis revealed that marker YTU024-L99 displayed consistent polymorphism between L424 and all these susceptible genotypes, while the remaining markers showed polymorphism in most accessions (≥ 90%). These result indicated that YTU024-L99 could serve as a robust marker for tracking PmL424 in MAS (Table 2). Furthermore, we have developed multiple advanced breeding lines carrying PmL424 in different elite genetic backgrounds, such as cultivars Huixianhong, Tainong 18 and Kenong 199. Field evaluations revealed that the introgression of PmL424 did not compromise key agronomic or yield-related traits, such as spike length, spikes per plant, spikelets per spike, kernels per spike, and thousand-kernel weight (Table S4). These results demonstrate stable performance and breeding value of PmL424 across diverse wheat cultivars.

Discussion

Cultivated emmer wheat serves as a valuable genetic resource harboring abundant Pm genes, making it a critical reservoir in wheat breeding programs. In wheat production, the continuous evolution of Bgt isolates has rendered many existing Pm genes ineffective, necessitating the mining of novel resistance genes. In this study, we identified and characterized the powdery mildew resistance in a cultivated emmer wheat accession L424, providing a potentially valuable genetic resource for wheat breeding against powdery mildew.

Through an integrated approach combining genetic linkage analysis, BSR-Seq and molecular markers, we identified a Pm gene, designated PmL424, underlying the powdery mildew resistance in L424. Precise genetic mapping confined PmL424 to a 1.09 Mb (21.63–22.72 Mb) physical interval on chromosome arm 2BS in the durum wheat reference genome. This region is known to harbor several previously reported Pm genes, including Pm26/MlIW170 [24, 25], Pm42 [26], PmXQ-0508 [27], Pm68 [28], PmWE99 [29] and PmL709 [6]. Among them, Pm26/MlIW170 and Pm42 were all derived from wild emmer wheat, while Pm68, PmL709, PmXQ-0508 and PmWE99 were derived from durum wheat, cultivated emmer wheat, common wheat and Thinopyrum intermedium, respectively. When aligned to the common wheat Chinese Spring reference genome (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1), the PmL424 interval (24.47–25.62 Mb) was clearly distinct from all documented Pm genes in this region, with the exception of Pm68 (24.44–26.64 Mb) [28]. Further supporting this distinction, comparative analysis of resistance spectra revealed notable differences between PmL424 and other reported Pm genes, though not with Pm68.

The substantial overlap between the genomic intervals of PmL424 and Pm68 raises intriguing questions regarding their genetic relationship. Recently, He et al. (2025) have cloned Pm68 from TRI 1796 using an integrated approach of genetic mapping, association analysis and PacBio sequencing. Transgenic assays demonstrate that Pm68 mediated resistance is controlled by a pair of genetically linked NLR genes, Pm68-1 and Pm68-2 [30], a mechanism consistent with another 2BS locus PmWR183 where two NLRs synergistically mediate resistance [31]. Two scenarios for PmL424 are plausible: First, it may represent a distinct yet closely linked resistance gene within the resistance gene cluster on chromosome 2BS. This hypothesis is supported by He et al. (2020), who identified eight NLR genes within the 1.78 Mb genomic interval of Pm68, indicating a complex arrangement of resistance genes in this region [28]. Alternatively, PmL424 could be an allelic variant of Pm68, analogous to the PmWR183/MlIW39 locus, which shares the core NLR-mediated resistance mechanism with Pm68 but exhibits a differentiated resistance spectrum [32]. Moving forward, future work, including allelic tests, fine-mapping, and functional validation, will clarify their relationship, aiding durable resistance breeding.

Of the 25 cloned powdery mildew resistance genes in wheat, the majority encode NLR proteins, with notable examples including Pm1a [33], Pm2 [34], Pm3 [35], Pm5e [36], Pm6/Pm52 [37], Pm8 [38], Pm12 [12], Pm17 [39], Pm21 [40], Pm41 [41], Pm55 [42], Pm60 [43], Pm68 [30] and Pm69 [44], reflecting conserved mechanistic paradigms in plant immunity [35]. Within the candidate interval of PmL424, we focused on seven disease resistance-associated genes and then analyzed their expression patterns following Bgt infection. Strikingly, three of them, including those encoding NBS-LRR proteins, exhibited significant up-regulation in the resistant genotype L424 compared to the susceptible genotype. The transcriptional induction is consistent with behavior observed in several functionally validated Pm genes, such as Pm60 from T. urartu [43] and Pm41 [41] from wild emmer wheat, where NLR gene expression was rapidly activated upon Bgt infection and essential for resistance establishment. Furthermore, the co-upregulation of multiple NLR genes within the narrow intervals is consistent with a genetic architecture characteristic of paired NLR systems, which are often composed of closely linked sensor and helper genes that are co-regulated to facilitate complex pathogen recognition [31], such as TdCNL1/TdCNL5 [24], Pm68-1/Pm68-2 [30], PmWR183-NLR1/PmWR183-NLR2 [31] and MlIW39 [32]. The observed NLR co-expression pattern in PmL424 may therefore represent a similar functional pairing mechanism.

Through integrated BSR-Seq analysis, we identified 2,325 DEGs between the resistant and susceptible genotypes, with significant enrichment in acid/oxidative stress response and PAMP-triggered immunity pathways. These findings align with transcriptomic results from Pm21-transgenic wheat, where ROS metabolic pathways were similarly upregulated to support broad-spectrum powdery mildew resistance [45]. KEGG analysis further revealed activation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and MAPK signaling pathways, consistent with reports of their essential functions in wheat-pathogens interactions [46]. These transcriptomic insights not only elucidate the regulatory framework of PmL424-mediated resistance but also align it with conserved defense pathways in wheat-Bgt interactions. The recurrent emergence of such NLR clusters across studies underscores their fundamental role in wheat immunity, providing important directions for future functional characterization of this resistance locus.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified a Pm gene PmL424 using genetic analysis, BSR-Seq analysis and molecular markers in the cultivated emmer wheat accession L424, and three key candidate genes were also captured through time-course analysis post inoculation with Bgt isolate E09. Potential regulatory genes were also identified through DEG analysis using GO and KEGG pathway analysis, unraveling its potential transcriptional regulation mechanisms when fighting Bgt invasion. These multi-dimensional analyses provide valuable insights into the dissection of the molecular and genetic basis underlying the powdery mildew resistance, as well as establishing a foundation for MAS using PmL424.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Cultivated emmer wheat L424, provided by Prof. Hongxing Xu from Henan University, showed high resistance to powdery mildew in the field for consecutive five years and was preserved in our lab. The susceptible durum wheat cultivar LDN was used as a susceptible parent to cross with L424 to develop F1, F2, and F2:3 progenies for subsequent genetic analysis and gene mapping. Tainong 18, a powdery mildew-susceptible wheat cultivar, served as the susceptible check. Six Pm genes donors including Pm26-40 (Pm26/MlIW170), G-303-1 M (Pm42), XQ-0508 (PmXQ-0508), TIR1796 (Pm68), IWM170 (MlIW170/Pm26), and WE99 (PmWE99) with L424 were tested against 15 Bgt isolates collected from different wheat producing regions in China: E09, E17, E21, F01, E05, F28, F17, E15, F08, F02, F23, F06, F09, E07, and E18. A panel of 46 susceptible wheat genotypes representing high yielding cultivars from principal wheat growing regions with optimal agronomic traits were selected to evaluate the polymorphisms of closely linked markers for MAS.

Phenotypic evaluation and genetic analysis

A total of 10 F1 plants, 196 F2 individuals, and their corresponding F2:3 families, as well as the parental lines L424 and LDN, were used for genetic analysis. More than 20 plants per F2:3 family were identified. Tainong 18 was randomly sown as the susceptible control. After sowing, these accessions were cultured under powdery mildew-free conditions with a cycle of 20 °C for 14 h of light and 18 °C for 10 h of darkness. When the seedlings reached the two-leaf stage, the Bgt isolate E09 was used to inoculate onto the seedlings using the brushing method. Ten days later, when obvious spores appeared on Tainong 18, the IT of each seedling was scored using a 0–4 scale [47–49]. Phenotypic segregation ratios were analyzed using chi-square (χ2) tests in SPSS Statistics version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

RNA extraction and BSR-Seq library preparation

Based on phenotypic results, 30 homozygous resistant and 30 homozygous susceptible F2:3 families (10 plants per family) of L424 and LDN were collected at 10 days post inoculation (dpi) and equally pooled to construct resistant and susceptible bulks. Total RNA of the resistant and susceptible bulks were extracted for BSR-Seq using an RNA extraction kit. Subsequently, mRNA was isolated using oligo(dT)25 magnetic beads through poly(A) + affinity purification. Binding was performed under gentle agitation at 18–20 °C for 15–30 min, followed by washing steps to remove non-mRNA components. The eluted poly(A) + RNA was fragmented into 100–300 nt segments via chemical fragmentation (using Mg2⁺-containing buffer at 94 °C) or enzymatic digestion (RNase III at 37 °C). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase and random hexamer primers. The cDNA fragments underwent end repair and adapter ligation, followed by PCR amplification to construct sequencing libraries. Library quality and quantity were validated to ensure compliance with sequencing standards.

Sequencing and data analysis

Raw reads were aligned to the durum wheat reference genome (cv. Svevo) using STAR [50]. SNPs and inDels were filtered (QUAL > 30, DP ≥ 5) using GATK v4.2.3.0. and Bcftools v1.9. Candidate regions were identified via ED analysis, retaining the top 1% SNPs.

Candidate interval analysis and gene mapping

Genetic variant detection was conducted using GATK v4.2.3.0 [51]. Subsequently, SNPs with a quality (QUAL) score > 30 and depth (DP) ≥ 5 were filtered using Bcftools (v1.9). To delineate the candidate gene interval, varBScore analysis was employed to compare SNPs and InDels between resistant and susceptible RNA bulks. Additionally, quantile-based thresholding was applied to evaluate the ED values of SNPs, retaining the top 1% of SNPs based on ED rankings to minimize noise interference originating from individual loci [52]. Following the identification of candidate intervals via ED analysis, SNPs and InDels within these intervals were screened across resistant and susceptible groups. Specific markers were designed based on these SNPs and InDels. InDels primers were generated using the PrimerServer function on the WheatOmics platform and Primer 5.0 software. The primers were subjected to polymorphism testing using DNA from resistant and susceptible parents, and those exhibiting polymorphism were retained. These primers were then utilized for genotyping the F2:3 families of L424 × LDN to map the gene location. Finally, the linkage relationship between the markers and the Pm gene in L424 were analyzed, and genetic linkage map were constructed using Mapmarker software [53].

Collinearity analysis

The Triticeae-GeneTribe database (https://wheat.cau.edu.cn/TGT/) was employed for collinearity analysis. Flanking marker sequences of the target genes were subjected to BLAST searches against the Triticeae multi-omics databases on WheatOmics 1.0 (https://202.194.139.32/) to retrieve the physical positions of homologous sequences in related Triticeae species. Subsequently, sequences with the highest sequence identity to the target genes were selected for detailed collinearity analysis.

Microscopic observation of the Bgt infection process

The resistant parent L424 and susceptible parent LDN were grown under aseptic conditions. When the seedlings developed to two-leaf stage, fresh spores of Bgt isolate E09 were inoculated onto the plants using the brushing method. Environmental conditions during inoculation were controlled at a temperature of 18–22 °C and a humidity of 60%−80%. Leaf segments (2–3 cm in length) were collected at 8 time points: 0, 4, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 120 hpi. The collected leaves were immediately placed in Carnoy’s fixative (prepared by mixing absolute ethanol and glacial acetic acid at a volume ratio of 3:1) and fixed for at least 48 h to completely remove chlorophyll. After fixation, the leaves were transferred to a 0.6% Coomassie Brilliant Blue solution (prepared by dissolving 0.6 g of Coomassie Brilliant Blue powder in 100 mL of absolute ethanol) and soaked for 1 min. Subsequently, the leaves were thoroughly rinsed with clean water multiple times to remove excess dye. Next, the leaves were placed face-up on a glass slide, and a drop of glycerol was added to complete the preparation of slide samples. Finally, the growth morphology of powdery mildew spores was observed using an Axioscope 5 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and relevant images were captured at a magnification of 400 ×.

qRT-PCR

After confirming the candidate interval, high-confidence genes related to disease resistance within this interval were annotated with reference to the reference genome of durum wheat (cv. Svevo) available on the WheatOmics platform (https://202.194.139.32/). qRT-PCR then was used to profile the expression patterns of candidate genes following the infection of Bgt isolate E09. Leaf samples of L424 and LDN were collected at 0, 0.5, 2, 4, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hpi after Bgt inoculation, and total RNA was extracted using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with TaActin serving for normalization. Each sample was tested in triplicate.

DEG analysis

Following BSR-Seq, transcript quantification was performed using featureCounts v2.0.1 to calculate gene expression levels by counting reads mapped to genomic feature regions. Differential expression analysis between resistant and susceptible bulks was conducted with EBSeq 3.5 software, with thresholds set at |log2(fold change)|≥ 1 (i.e., ≥ twofold change) and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional annotation of DEGs was carried out using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.0; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html), integrating GO (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/) databases. Enrichment analysis was further applied to identify statistically over represented biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions within the DEGs, thereby elucidating key regulatory pathways driving phenotypic divergence.

Validation of closely linked markers for MAS

Molecular markers closely linked to the Pm gene in L424 were used to genotype L424 and 46 wheat cultivars from diverse regions in China. Markers capable of amplifying polymorphic bands between L424 and the target genotypes were screened to validate their applicability for MAS across different genetic backgrounds. On the contrary, markers failing to generate polymorphic amplification products were considered unsuitable for tracking the target Pm gene in corresponding genetic backgrounds.
