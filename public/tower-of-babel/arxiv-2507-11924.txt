Advantages of Feedback in Distributed Data-Gathering for Accurate and Power-Efficient State-Estimation
Hyeongmin Choe, SooJean Han

Abstract
In distributed target-tracking sensor networks, efficient data gathering methods are necessary to save communication resources and assure information accuracy. This paper proposes a Feedback (FB) distributed data-gathering method which lets the central unit feed information back to the mobile sensors; each sensor then uses it to cancel redundant transmissions and reduce communication congestion. We rigorously compare its performance, in terms of mean-squared error (MSE) and cost of power per sensor, against more conventional Non-Feedback (NF) architectures by evaluating conditions of feasibility and advantage under different architecture specifications (e.g., communication delay rate, power cost rate, maximum backoff time, sampling period, observation noise). Here, we defined the advantage as the performance gain achieved by FB over NF, while FB is said to be feasible if the advantage region is nonempty. Our theoretical analyses show that the feasibility of FB depends more on the communication power cost, while the advantage depends on the sensors’ propagation delay per transmission interval; we derive concrete conditions under which these outcomes hold. Using extensive numerical simulations under a variety of settings, we confirm the accuracy of the derived conditions, and show that our theoretical results hold even for more complex scenarios where the simplifying assumptions no longer hold.



I
Introduction

Distributed sensor networks are widely used for target tracking and environmental monitoring where timely and accurate state estimation must be achieved under communication and power constraints (see
[
1
]
for survey). Conventional architectures typically rely on one-way communication from the sensors to a central unit. But in large-scale networks with overlapping sensing regions and non-negligible communication delays, these architectures often have redundant transmissions among sensors, inefficient power use, and increased estimation error due to large transmission delays
[
2
,
3
]
.

To address these challenges,
feedback
-enabled network architectures have been studied
[
4
]
, in which the central unit feeds information back to the sensors to guide how and when they should transmit new measurements. Such feedback mechanisms allow for dynamic sensor tasking, selective activation, and suppression of redundant data, thereby improving both communication efficiency and estimation quality
[
5
,
6
]
.
Previous literature
[
7
,
4
,
8
]
has empirically demonstrated the benefits of including feedback under
various design constraints, such as limited bandwidth, communication latencies, and partial observability of the environment.
However, determining
explicit conditions under which feedback-based architectures actually outperform traditional sensor networks without feedback remains largely unexplored.

This paper proposes a novel modular feedback (FB) distributed data-gathering architecture that can be used to concretely analyze its performance advantages over more conventional non-feedback (NF) architectures.
Each sensor uses this feedback to cancel redundant data and adjust its transmission schedule based on local rules that aim to reduce its power consumption while delivering its measurements to the central processor as quickly as possible.

Our contributions are as follows.
First, we derive criteria for
feasibility
, which determines whether there exists a region where FB can have a performance advantage over NF under the same network parameters (communication delay rate, power cost ratio, etc.).
Second, we rigorously analyze when data-gathering under FB is
advantageous
compared to NF, where no communication occurs from the central unit to the sensors.
To achieve this, we compare competing performance metrics, highlighting tradeoffs between accuracy (measured by the mean-squared estimation error) and efficiency (measured by the sensors’ power cost).
We support our analyses with extensive simulations across various scenarios, demonstrating that the theoretical benefits of FB persist even when simplifying assumptions are relaxed.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents the problem setup. Section
III
introduces the modular architectures used to model both FB and NF.
Section
IV
develops the theoretical results for both feasibility and advantage, while Section
V
validates the theory using simulations. Section
VI
concludes the paper.

II
Problem Setup

We consider a distributed sensor network which collects positional information about multiple targets, and a central unit that estimates the targets’ positions based on received data from the network.
For concreteness of our discussion, we assume
\mathcal{S}{\,\subseteq\,}\mathbb{R}^{2}
(a planar environment) and that each target moves with a basic period of
T_{e}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
, though we emphasize that our results can be easily extended to general aperiodic settings.
For
i{\,=\,}1,\cdots,N
, target
i
’s position,
{\bf x}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
, evolves according to a random walk given by

{\bf x}_{i}(t)={\bf x}_{i}(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta^{k}(t){\bf d}_{i}^{k},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

(1)

where
\delta^{k}(t)
is an indicator function that takes value
1
if
t{\,\in\,}[kT_{e},\infty)
, and
\{{\bf d}_{i}^{k}\}_{k}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{2}
are realizations of i.i.d. random variable stepsizes with each component
d_{i1}^{k}
and
d_{i2}^{k}
taking value
\Delta d
with probability
p
,
-\Delta d
with probability
1-p
.
The full environment state is the stack of all target positions
{\bf x}{\,=\,}({\bf x}_{1}^{\top},\cdots,{\bf x}_{N}^{\top})^{\top}
.

Our network consists of
M
sensors, where each sensor
j
has sampling period
T_{s}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
and its own observation region
\mathcal{R}_{j}{\,\subset\,}\mathcal{S}
.
Each sensor
j
measures the environment state
{\bf x}
via
{\bf y}_{j}{\,=\,}({\bf y}_{j1}^{\top},\cdots,{\bf y}_{jN}^{\top})^{\top}
, where

{\bf y}_{ji}^{k}=O_{ji}^{k}({\bf x}_{i}(kT_{s})+{\bf v}_{j}^{k}),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

(2)

is its observation on target
i
.
Here,
O_{ji}^{k}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is the identity matrix
I_{2}
if
{\bf x}_{i}(kT_{s}){\,\in\,}\mathcal{R}_{j}
and
\mathbf{0}
otherwise, and
\{{\bf v}_{j}^{k}\}_{k}{\,\sim\,}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{2})
is an uncorrelated noise process independent of the environment state.
We assume the central unit knows the locations and observation ranges of all sensors at the initial time, prior to deployment.

Remark 1
.

Since the environment evolves in continuous-time according to (
1
), each sensor
j
also has a continuous-time measurement
{\bf y}_{j}(t)
(
{\bf y}_{ji}(t)
).
However, it is piecewise-constant according to its sampling time, and so throughout this paper, we use the “discretized” notation
{\bf y}_{j}^{k}
(
{\bf y}_{ji}^{k}
) for each sampling interval
k
(i.e.,
t{\,\in\,}[kT_{s},(k+1)T_{s})
).

The sensors and the central unit communicate via
packets
.
We assume that the communication delay and power cost of transmitting a packet scale linearly with the number of individual vector components it contains, where we refer to the
v_{i}
of some vector
{\bf v}{\,=\,}(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})^{\top}
as its
components
.
The per-component delay and power costs are denoted by
\Delta t^{u}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\Delta p^{u}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
for sensor-to-central (uplink) transmissions, and by
\Delta t^{d}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\Delta p^{d}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{+}
for central-to-sensor (downlink or feedback) transmissions.

III
Proposed Feedback Architecture

Fig. 1:

The modular feedback (FB, left) and non-feedback (NF, right) architectures.
Each sensor observes the environment and uses a triggering rule (Def.
1
,
3
) to decide when to transmit. The transmitted data is fused at the central unit via the fusion rule (Def.
4
), and updates the global state estimate.
Under FB, the central unit additionally sends its state estimate as feedback (red, Def.
5
) to all sensors.
Each sensor uses this feedback to update its internal estimate of the central processor’s estimate via the estimation rule (Def.
6
).
Note that NF lacks this feedback loop; sensors rely solely on local information to create its internal estimate.

The overall objective of the distributed sensor network is to get the central unit to construct
\hat{{\bf x}}
, an estimate of the environment’s state
{\bf x}
, using only the measurements
\{{\bf y}_{1},\cdots,{\bf y}_{M}\}
transmitted to it by the sensors.
We especially aim to quantify the advantages of adding feedback capabilities to the central unit so that it can communicate information to the sensors, potentially reducing the amount of transmissions they need to send to ensure estimation accuracy.
Towards this goal, we design and compare two architectures: 1) the
feedback (FB) architecture
, which includes feedback, and 2) the
“non-feedback” (NF) architecture
, which is more commonly used in sensor fusion applications.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, the only distinction between FB and NF lies in the presence of the feedback rule, which enables the central unit to transmit information back to the sensors. All other components—the estimation, triggering, and fusion rules—are kept identical across both architectures.
While additional mechanisms such as consensus-based fusion
[
9
]
, and distributed scheduling
[
10
]
can enhance distributed estimation performance, our focus is solely on the impact of feedback. To isolate its effect, we deliberately adopt a minimal system design and use identical rules in both FB and NF, where applicable.

For a concrete comparison, we define each rule as follows.
In order for each sensor
j
to make informed decisions about what to transmit, it maintains its
own estimate

{\bf z}_{ji}{\,\in\,}\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the
central processor’s estimate

\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}
of target
i
’s position.
We provide a formal definition of
{\bf z}_{j}{\,\triangleq\,}({\bf z}_{j1}^{\top},\cdots,{\bf z}_{jN}^{\top})^{\top}
later in Definition
6
, after defining the other rules and their notations.

Definition 1
.

(Triggering Rule I)
If target
i
is detected by sensor
j
at time
t
, the
triggering rule
evaluates

\alpha_{ji}^{(1)}(t)=\mathds{1}\{\lVert{\bf z}_{ji}(t)-{\bf y}_{ji}^{k*}\rVert>\epsilon\}

(3)

where
k^{*}{\,\triangleq\,}\max\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:kT_{s}<t\}
and
\epsilon>0
is a user-chosen change-detection threshold.

If multiple sensors transmit their measurements immediately upon being triggered, issues such as false positives and packet collisions may occur. To mitigate this, each sensor schedules a random backoff time, defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Backoff Times)
.

Each sensor
j
generates a i.i.d.
random backoff time

b_{j}^{k}\sim\text{Unif}(0,T_{b}]
if it decides to transmit its measurement
{\bf y}_{j}^{k}
during sampling step
k
.
Here,
T_{b}{\,<\,}T_{s}
is the maximum possible backoff time.

While there are alternative backoff distributions and various other methods for avoiding collisions in the literature (e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
), we choose backoff times for concreteness and its simple implementation.

Definition 3
.

(Triggering Rule II)
If (
3
) yields
1
for sensor
j
time
t
, the triggering rule is checked again at time
t+b_{j}^{k^{*}}
in case there has been a new update to
{\bf z}_{ji}
:

\alpha_{ji}^{(2)}(t{\,+\,}b_{j}^{k^{*}})=\mathds{1}\{\alpha_{ji}^{(1)}(t)=1,\lVert{\bf z}_{ji}(t{\,+\,}b_{j}^{k^{*}})-{\bf y}_{ji}^{k*}\rVert>\epsilon\}

(4)

If (
4
) yields
1
, then sensor
j
finally transmits
{\bf y}_{ji}^{k*}
to the central unit.

Definition 4

(Fusion Rule)
.

For both FB and NF, the central unit applies a
fusion rule
to aggregate all received sensor data and update the estimate
\hat{{\bf x}}(t)
over time.
For concreteness, both FB and NF use a running arithmetic mean over all past received measurements of each component
x_{i}
in the packet; we refer to this as
averaging fusion
.

Definition 5

(Feedback Rule)
.

FB architectures employ the following
feedback rule
at each time
t
.
If the estimate for target
i
has been updated (i.e.,
\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t)\neq\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t-)
), and if
\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t)
lies within the observation range of at least two sensors, then the central unit feeds
\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t)
back to the entire sensor network.
Here, the notation
t-
is borrowed from math jargon to mean the time “just before
t
”.

Definition 6
.

(Sensor Estimation Rule)
Each sensor
j
implements an
estimation rule
to update
{\bf z}_{ji}
, its local estimate of the central unit’s estimate
\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}
, for each target
i
.
Its definition depends on the architecture type.
For FB, sensor
j
estimates the central unit’s estimate to be the later between the most recent broadcast received and the last transmission it sent:

\displaystyle{\bf z}_{ji}^{\texttt{FB}}(t)=\begin{cases}\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t^{*})&\text{if broadcast is more updated}\\
{\bf y}_{ji}^{k^{*}}&\text{if last transmission is more updated}\end{cases}

where
t^{*}
is the latest time before
t
when triggering rule II was equal to
1
, and
k^{*}{\,\triangleq\,}\max\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:kT_{s}<t,\alpha_{ji}^{(2)}(kT_{s}+b_{j}^{k}){\,=\,}1\}
.
In the NF case,
{\bf z}_{ji}^{\texttt{NF}}(t)={\bf y}_{ji}^{k^{*}}
.

The performance of the distributed data gathering architecture is evaluated using the
total power cost
(P) and the
mean squared error
(MSE) over the entire simulation duration
T_{\text{sim}}
.

\mbox{P}\triangleq\int_{0}^{T_{\text{sim}}}\left(\sum^{M}_{j=1}(P^{u}_{j}(t)+P^{d}_{j}(t))\right)dt

(5a)

\mbox{MSE}\triangleq\frac{1}{T_{\text{sim}}}\int_{0}^{T_{\text{sim}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert{\bf x}_{i}(t)-\hat{{\bf x}}_{i}(t)\lVert^{2}\right)dt

(5b)

where
P_{j}^{u}(t)
and
P_{j}^{d}(t)
are the power consumption of sensor
j
at time
t
from transmitting and receiving, respectively.
Note that
P_{j}^{u}\propto\Delta p^{u}
and
P_{j}^{d}\propto\Delta p^{d}
(see end of Section
II
).

IV
Theoretical Analysis

We now theoretically characterize the tradeoff space between the metrics (
5
): the MSE accuracy of
\hat{{\bf x}}
versus the total sensor power cost of transmitting
\{{\bf y}_{1},\cdots,{\bf y}_{M}\}
(and receiving
\hat{{\bf x}}
, in the FB case).
We emphasize that in this section only, we focus on a variety of simplified settings of the original problem setup from Section
II
for the purpose of deriving concrete results.
Later, in Section
V
, we numerically study our feedback architecture under the original setup (with all simplifying assumptions removed) and show that the results we derive in theory still hold empirically for more complex scenarios.

IV-A

Preliminary Definitions

Before beginning our comparisons, we introduce several definitions to facilitate our analyses.
Because feedback transmissions are made when targets are detected within a region of the environment shared by at least two sensors, it helps to partition the collective sensor observation regions by “collaborative sets”.

Definition 7
.

(Collaborative Set)
A
collaborative set

\mathcal{J}=\{j_{1},\cdots,j_{m}\}\subset\{1,\cdots,M\}
, for some
m\leq M
, is created for the sensors
j_{1},\cdots,j_{m}
iff
\cap_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{R}_{j}
is a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{S}
.
See Figure
3
for a visualization of all possible collaborative sets for an example of
M{\,=\,}3
sensors.

We also consider the distribution of all detected targets in the environment depending on whether they lie in a collaborative set or are uniquely detected by only one sensor.

Definition 8

(Collaborative and Unique Components)
.

Define, for each collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
,
n_{\text{c}}(\mathcal{J})
to be the number of
collaborative components
, which depends on the number of targets observed in
\cup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{R}_{j}
.
Further define, for each sensor
j
,
n_{\text{u}}^{j}
to be the number of
unique components
, which depends on the number of targets present in
\mathcal{R}_{j}\backslash\cup_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}\setminus\{j\}}\mathcal{R}_{j^{\prime}}
.

Each packet incurs power and delay costs proportional to the number of components it contains (see end of Section
II
).
Both FB and NF begin with the same number of components due to measuring the same
\{{\bf y}_{1},\cdots,{\bf y}_{M}\}
.
However, due to different estimation and triggering rules, FB can let each sensor additionally cancel the transmission of components using the central unit’s feedback.
See Figure
3
for an illustration of collaborative and unique components.

When a target enters a collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
, each sensor
j\in\mathcal{J}
responds differently to the trigger rule relative to the others. Under FB, this difference in behavior is further shaped by each sensor’s propagation delay, defined below.

Fig. 2:
A sample plot of communication events over time for three sensors in the same collaborative set, all triggered simultaneously at step
k
.
[Left] The lead sensor, sensor 1 (
(1){\,=\,}1
), transmits first (triangle), having the shortest backoff time.
Under FB, sensor 3 (white triangle) is informed by the feedback and thus does not transmit; sensor 2 (triangle) remains uninformed because it was scheduled during the lead sensor’s propagation delay
\tau_{(1)}^{k}
.
[Right] The equivalent NF case, shown for comparison.

Definition 9

(Propagation Delay)
.

Under FB only, given a collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
and sampling step
k
, the
propagation delay

\tau_{j}^{k}
of each sensor
j\in\mathcal{J}
is the sum of its transmission delay and feedback delay:

\tau_{j}^{k}=n\Delta t^{u}+m\Delta t^{d}

(6)

where
n
is the number of components in the transmission packet of sensor
j
at time
kT_{s}+b_{j}^{k}
, and
m
is the number of components in the feedback packet of the central unit at time
kT_{s}+b_{j}^{k}+n\Delta t^{u}
.

Fig. 3:
An example illustration of all possible collaborative sets
\{\mathcal{J}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{J}_{4}\}
(Def.
7
) and the number of collaborative/unique components (Def.
8
).
This sample environment
\mathcal{S}
(white square) has 3 sensors (gray circles) and 7 targets (red dots).

Definition 10
.

(Lead, Informed, and Uniformed Sensors)
Under FB only, for each collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
, its
lead sensor
at step
k
is the sensor with the shortest total delay, denoted using the index
(1)
:

(1)\triangleq\arg\min_{j\in\mathcal{J}}(b_{j}^{k}+\tau_{j}^{k})

(7)

If more than two sensors have the shortest delay, the lead sensor is the lowest-indexed one.

Informed sensors
are all sensors in
\mathcal{J}
that are informed about the environment state via a central processor feedback corresponding to the lead sensor’s transmission.
We denote

\mathcal{I}_{k}{\,=\,}\{j\in\mathcal{J}:b_{j}^{k}{\,>\,}b_{(1)}^{k}+\tau_{(1)}^{k}\}

to be the set of all informed sensors in
\mathcal{J}
at timestep
k
.
Finally,
uninformed sensors
are the remaining sensors in
\mathcal{J}
which are not lead or informed sensors.
See Figure
2
for a visualization of this classification of sensors and delays.

IV-B

Theoretical Performance Comparisons: FB vs. NF

We are now ready to compare the FB and NF architectures according to the efficiency and accuracy performance metrics (
5
).
We begin with two preliminary lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

At each step
k
, for each collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
, the power cost NF consumes more power than FB as given by

\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}=\sum_{\mathcal{J}}n_{c}(\mathcal{J})(\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert(\Delta p^{u}+\Delta p^{d})-\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert\Delta p^{d})

(8)

where each
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is the set of informed sensors of each
\mathcal{J}
at timestep
k
, and
n_{c}(\mathcal{J})
is the number of components in the collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
(see Definitions
10
,
8
).

Proof.

The power consumption contributed by the unique components
is identical for both FB and NF architectures.
For collaborative components
n_{c}(\mathcal{J})
,
the power consumption contribution is
n_{c}(\mathcal{J})\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert\Delta p^{u}
under NF, and
n_{c}(\mathcal{J})(\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert)(\Delta p^{u}+\Delta p^{d})
under FB. Subtracting them yields the desired result (
8
).
∎

As we will show in our later theorems, we are interested in deriving conditions for which
\boldsymbol{E}[\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}]>0
(and likewise for the MSE).
Note that
\Delta p^{u}
,
\Delta p^{d}
,
\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert
, and related parameters are predetermined via architecture specifications and sensor network layout.
Thus, a preliminary result that will help us is the expected number of informed sensors given these parameters.
To obtain interpretable closed-form expressions, we will operate under the following setting.

Assumption 1
.

At sampling step
k
, all sensors in collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
observe the same number of targets.

Useful consequences of Assumption
1
are as follows.
Since all sensors share the same targets in
\cup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{R}_{j}
, the number of unique components
n_{\text{u}}^{j}
is the same for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
.
More importantly, the propagation delays
\tau_{j}^{k}
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
are the same.
Therefore, the lead sensor is the sensor whose back-off time is shortest, and (
7
) is simplified as

(1)\triangleq\arg\min_{j\in\mathcal{J}}b_{j}^{k}

(9)

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
1
, the expected number of informed sensors in collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
at step
k
, given
\tau_{(1)}^{k}
, is given by

\boldsymbol{E}[\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]\propto x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}-\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert x+(\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1)

(10)

where
x\triangleq\tau_{(1)}^{k}/T_{b}
is the ratio of the propagation delay of the lead sensor and the back-off interval.

Proof.

As mentioned before, the lead sensor is the sensor whose back-off time is the shortest under Assumption
1
.
Using order statistics
[
13
]
, the distribution of the earliest back-off time
b_{(1)}^{k}
among a collaborative set is:

\boldsymbol{P}(b_{(1)}^{k}=t)=\frac{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}{T_{b}}\left(1-\frac{t}{T_{b}}\right)^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}

(11)

Given
b^{k}_{(1)}=t
and
\tau_{(1)}^{k}
, the conditional distribution of the number of informed sensors
\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert
is Binomial:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert=i|b_{(1)}^{k}=t,\tau_{(1)}^{k})=\binom{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}{i}p(t)^{i}\{1-p(t)\}^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1-i}

(12)

where

p(t)\triangleq\frac{T_{b}-\min(t+\tau^{k}_{(1)},T_{b})}{T_{b}-t}

(13)

The joint distribution can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(i,t|\tau_{(1)}^{k})

\displaystyle=\binom{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}{i}p(t)^{i}\{1-p(t)\}^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-i-1}

(14)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}{T_{b}}\left(1-\frac{t}{T_{b}}\right)^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}

where we use shorthand
\boldsymbol{P}(i,t|\tau_{(1)}^{k})=\boldsymbol{P}(\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert=i,b_{(1)}^{k}=t|\tau_{(1)}^{k})
for conciseness.
The expected number of informed sensors can be calculated as follows.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}[\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]=\sum^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}_{i=0}i\int_{0}^{T_{b}-\tau_{(1)}^{k}}\boldsymbol{P}(i,t|\tau_{(1)}^{k})dt

(15)

Substituting (
14
) into (
15
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}[\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{b}^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}}\sum^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}_{i=0}i\binom{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}{i+1}x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-i-1}(1-x)^{i+1}

(16)

and by change-of-variables
i+1\mapsto u
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}[\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}\rvert|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]=

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{b}^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}}\left(\sum^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}_{u=0}u\binom{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}{u}x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-u}(1-x)^{u}-1+x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}\right)

Since the first term is the expectation of
\text{Bin}(\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert,1-x)
, it evaluates to
\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert x
, and we get the desired result.
∎

Now we are ready to present our main theorems.
First, we make concrete the notions of
feasibility
and
advantage
.
The
feasibility
determines whether there exists a configuration where FB architecture can achieve lower power cost than the NF architecture.
The
advantage
refers to the conditions under which FB outperforms NF under either metric in (
5
).
In this language, for instance, Lemma
1
would suggest that the
power cost advantage condition
is equivalent to
\boldsymbol{E}[\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-\text{P}_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}]>0
.
Our first theorem rewrites this condition in a way that is more useful in practice, in terms of our system specifications.

Theorem 1

(Power Cost Advantage)
.

Over collaborative set
\mathcal{J}
and sampling step
k
satisfying Assumption
1
, given
\tau_{(1)}^{k}
, FB is
power cost advantageous
over NF on average if the following conditions are satisfied:

y>\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}

(18a)

(1+y)x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert}-\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert(1+y)x+(\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1)y-1>0

(18b)

where
y\triangleq\Delta p^{d}/\Delta p^{u}

and
x
was defined in Lemma
2
,
\tau_{(1)}^{k}
is the propagation delay of the lead sensor (see Definition
9
).

Proof.

By combining Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
, we can derive the expected power difference
\boldsymbol{E}[P_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-P_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]
. Conditioning
\boldsymbol{E}[P_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-P_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]>0
, we can get the advantage condition (
18b
). Differentiating
\boldsymbol{E}[P_{k}^{\texttt{NF}}-P_{k}^{\texttt{FB}}|\tau_{(1)}^{k}]
yields
\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert(1+y)x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}-\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert(1+y)
, which is zero at
x^{\lvert\mathcal{J}\rvert-1}=1
. Since the function is increasing over
(0,1]
, ensuring a nonempty solution set for the advantage condition requires the feasibility condition to hold at
x=0

).

Since both FB and NF aim for the central unit to produce an accurate estimate
\hat{{\bf x}}

, we also compare them under the MSE metric. The following theorem provides a sufficient

under which FB outperforms (is more advantageous over) NF in terms of the mean-squared error.

Theorem 2

(MSE Advantage)
.

Let
n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}\triangleq\min\{n_{\text{u}}^{j}:j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\}
be the minimum number of unique components among the informed sensors, where
n_{\text{u}}^{j}
is defined in Definition
10
.
Suppose that at every sampling step
k
, there exists at least one informed sensor
j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
with
n_{\text{u}}^{j}>1
. Then, FB is
MSE advantageous
over NF if the following condition holds:

\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}>\sqrt{\frac{2T_{s}}{\Delta t^{u}n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}}-1}

(19)

where
\epsilon
is the triggering rules’ threshold from Definitions
1
and
3
, and
\sigma
,
\Delta t^{u}
, and
T_{s}
are defined in Section
II
.

Proof.

In this proof only, we will enumerate all the collaborative sets and the corresponding sets of informed sensors using the superscript
\ell
, so that
\mathcal{J}\mapsto\mathcal{J}^{\ell}
,
\mathcal{I}_{k}\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}
, and
n_{\text{c}}(\mathcal{J})\mapsto n_{c}^{\ell}
.
With FB, for each informed sensor
j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}
within collaborative set
\mathcal{J}^{\ell}
, the number of scheduled components is reduced by
n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}
relative to NF.
Thus, under FB, each unique component is tracked at least
\Delta t^{u}n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}
faster, and NF accumulates at least

(\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2})n_{\text{u}}^{j}\Delta t^{u}n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}

additional contribution to its MSE compared to FB.
Across the entire sensor network, FB cancels a total of
\sum_{\ell}\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}
components. The resulting imbalances in MSE are distributed among all informed sensors.
Thus, the MSE advantage is bounded as:

\text{MSE}_{\texttt{adv}}\geq(\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2})n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}\Delta t^{u}\sum_{\ell}\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}.

(20)

Additionally, since FB has fewer packets in the collaborative set, the corresponding components in the state estimate will have higher variance under FB than NF when both are using the averaging fusion rule of Definition
4
(this is just the law of large numbers).
Thus, we must also account for its MSE
disadvantage
.
Within each collaborative set
\mathcal{J}^{\ell}
, for each
n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}
components, both FB and NF maintain the same MSE until
\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert
identical components—originating from different sensors in
\mathcal{J}^{\ell}
—are collected.

During this period, the MSE of FB remains either less than or equal to NF due to faster tracking.
However, beyond this point, FB ceases receiving additional data, resulting in a final error of
\sigma^{2}/(\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert)

Conversely, NF continues receiving data, further reducing its error to
\sigma^{2}/\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert
.
Thus, NF eventually achieves a lower MSE for the collaborative components.
The imbalance in MSE within a collaborative set persists for at most
2T_{s}
, as the backoff time is constrained by the sampling step
T_{b}<T_{s}
.
Thus, the MSE disadvantage is bounded as:

\text{MSE}_{\texttt{dsv}}\leq 2T_{s}\sum_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert}-\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert}\right)n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}\sigma^{2}.

(21)

Considering both advantages and disadvantages, the final condition for FB to be MSE advantageous over NF is
\text{MSE}_{\texttt{adv}}>\text{MSE}_{\texttt{dsv}}
, i.e.,

(\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2})n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}\Delta t^{u}\sum_{\ell}\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}>2T_{s}\sum_{\ell}\frac{\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert(\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert)}n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}\sigma^{2}

(22)

By assumption,
\sum_{\ell}\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}\neq 0
and
n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}\neq 0
,
which implies

(\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2})>\frac{2T_{s}}{\Delta t^{u}n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}}\frac{\sum_{\ell}\frac{\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert(\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert)}n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell}\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert n_{\text{c}}^{\ell}}\sigma^{2}.

Since

\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert/(\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert(\lvert\mathcal{J}^{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert))\leq\lvert\mathcal{I}_{k}^{\ell}\rvert
for each
\ell
, we obtain:

(\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2})>\frac{2T_{s}}{\Delta t^{u}n_{\text{u}}^{\text{min}}}\sigma^{2}.

and rearranging the terms yields our desired result.
∎

From an accuracy standpoint, FB allows each sensor to refrain from transmitting data it believes the central processor has already received,
which accelerates the delivery of new
information; this yields an advantage governed by
\epsilon
and
\Delta t
.
On the other hand, repeatedly fusing redundant data provides a benefit tied to
\sigma
and its time of persistence
T_{s}
until the next new sample.
Overall, FB improves accuracy when the trade-off between these effects favors faster access to new data, i.e., when
\epsilon/\sigma
satisfies the condition of Theorem
2
.

In summary,
FB can offer practical advantages over NF.
First, by eliminating redundant transmissions, FB reduces power cost under certain conditions (Theorem
1
).
Second, by prioritizing unseen data over redundant ones (Theorem
2
), FB lowers communication delay, which increases accuracy.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a modular distributed data-gathering architecture where the central unit is able to feed information back to the sensors.
Each sensor then uses this data to decide how and when to transmit new measurements of the environment to the central unit.
The main benefit of such a feedback (FB) architecture over more conventional non-feedback (NF) architectures is the reduction of the sensors’ total power consumption (thereby improving efficiency) and transmission of redundant data, which allows the central unit to attain higher estimation accuracy.
We rigorously derived feasibility and advantage conditions for FB to be able to outperform NF.
Because our conditions related user-chosen parameters with the immutable parameters of the network hardware, they can be used to guide algorithmic design choices and attain a better balance in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off space.
Our conditions were also validated empirically across various parameters, sensor configurations, and environment dynamics, and they were shown to hold even with the simplified theoretical assumptions removed.

This work provides the foundation to a more principled approach to designing scalable and resource-efficient sensor networks for distributed data-gathering and state-estimation problems, particularly in settings with significant communication delays and high sensor density. Future work includes extending the framework to more complex sensor rules, improving the estimation of informed agents, and designing more adaptive backoff mechanisms for faster and more reliable data acquisition.
