Integration of the circulating miRNome and clinical information to predict 90-day mortality in elderly COVID-19 subphenotypes

ABSTRACT
SummaryElderly patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) exhibit high mortality rates. We assessed whether microRNA (miRNA) profiling provides prognostic information in this population. This multicenter study included hospitalized COVID-19 patients aged ≥65 years (n = 763). Clinical subphenotypes were identified using clinical data through k-prototypes algorithm. Plasma miRNome was profiled using qPCR. Clinical and miRNA-based models predicting 90-day mortality were developed using Variable Selection Using Random Forests (VSURF). Median age was 79 years, 44.0% were female, and 90-day mortality was 26.7%. Although 13 candidate miRNAs were identified during screening (n = 39), none were associated with mortality in the full derivation cohort (n = 340), leading to a subphenotype-stratified analysis. Three subphenotypes (elderly COVID-1 [eCOVID-1], -2, and -3) with distinct clinical features and mortality risks were identified. In eCOVID-2, miR-106b-3p was the strongest predictor of 90-day mortality and improved model discrimination beyond clinical variables alone area under the curve (AUC: 0.75 vs. 0.66). Plasma miRNAs provide complementary prognostic information when integrated with clinical variables in elderly COVID-19 subphenotypes.

Introduction

The global increase in life expectancy, coupled with a decline in birth rates, has led to an increase in the proportion of older individuals. While in 2020, approximately one in 11 people worldwide were 65 years or older, by 2050, one in six individuals in the world will be over this age.1 This demographic shift, in combination with the fact that longevity is not necessarily accompanied by an extended period of good health, has now become one of the key challenges of contemporary health care.2

The deterioration of human health with age manifests as changes in a multitude of physiological and biological components,3 including the immune system.4 Among other factors, age-related immunological changes include a decreased ability to fight infections and a diminished vaccination response.5 These attributes consequently predispose elderly individuals to more severe infections, thereby increasing their risk of adverse outcomes and leading to higher rates of hospitalization and prolonged medical care.6 This escalation in health care utilization contributes to a growing burden on medical systems and rising expenditures.7 Importantly, the process of aging is not uniform. Older adults display considerable variability in health states, as demonstrated by differences in life care needs, the prevalence of chronic conditions, and mortality rates.8,9 This complexity has made the clinical management of this population challenging.

The burden of infectious diseases on the elderly has received increased attention because of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Extensive research in the field has identified age as one of the greatest risk factors for both the development of severe forms of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and the associated mortality.10 For instance, in a comparative observational study, the global mortality rate among hospitalized COVID-19 patients younger than 65 years was 6.8%, whereas it was 32.1% among patients older than 65 years11 The elevated mortality rate persists even after vaccination in older individuals.12 The clinical presentations of this condition are extremely heterogeneous across different age groups13 and are also influenced by the presence of chronic conditions in older adults.14 As a result, the elderly constitute the population sector that warrants the greatest health concern.

COVID-19 is not only a localized respiratory infection but also a multisystem disease that involves a complex interplay among immunological, inflammatory, and coagulation factors.15 In this context, there is considerable interest in deciphering the pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning the different clinical courses of the disease. Molecular profiling, particularly microRNA (miRNA) analysis, offers valuable insights into both disease origin and progression. miRNAs play crucial roles in cellular functions across the body and, as a consequence, their dysregulation has been linked to disease pathogenesis and progression.16 The discovery of stable forms of circulating miRNAs in body fluids17 and their cost-effective quantification using standardized clinical laboratory techniques18 have opened a promising avenue for the use of miRNAs as biomarkers in a variety of conditions, including infectious diseases.19,20,21 Collectively, miRNA profiling represents an innovative approach for developing mechanistic-based clinical biomarkers.22,23

Although circulating miRNAs have shown promise as biomarkers in COVID-19 patient studies from our group and others,24,25,26,27,28,29 their specific potential for predicting outcomes in elderly individuals remains unexplored. Notably, disease-specific alterations in aging profiles have been identified with miRNA sets differing between young and old patients across various diseases.30 Such miRNA profiling strategies could provide valuable insights for managing this high-risk population, which is particularly vulnerable to viral respiratory infections. Therefore, this study aimed to assess whether plasma miRNome profiling could offer prognostic information in elderly COVID-19 patients.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The study included 763 elderly hospitalized COVID-19 patients. The median [P25; P75] age was 79.0 [72.0; 87.0] years, and 44.0% of patients were female. The 90-day mortality rate was 26.7%. Nonsurvivors were older and had a higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease and chronic cardiovascular disease. The laboratory parameters at hospital admission highlighted disparities in creatinine, C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, and leukocyte, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts between survivors and nonsurvivors. During the hospital stay, nonsurvivors presented a higher incidence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). No disparities were noted between the study groups with respect to the need for intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Differences were identified in the ventilatory support modalities utilized during hospitalization; nonsurvivors exhibited a heightened requirement for high-flow oxygen therapy and noninvasive mechanical ventilation. Plasma miRNAs as biomarkers of 90-day mortality in elderly hospitalized patients

From the whole population, plasma samples were obtained from 340 patients (derivation cohort). Differences between patients with and without available samples were limited to hospital stay duration, systolic blood pressure, and pharmacological treatments (Table S1). Consistent with the two-phase profiling design, a screening phase was performed to identify miRNAs with potential as biomarkers for 90-day mortality (n = 39) (Table S2). Here, survivors and nonsurvivors were matched according to the age, sex, and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI). To maximize biomarker assessment, the selection criteria for candidate miRNAs in this phase were intentionally relaxed. The criteria included a fold change (FC) threshold of at least 1.5 (or less than 0.67). Eight miRNAs (miR-106b-3p, miR-199a-3p, miR-215-5p, miR-342-3p, miR-451a, miR-454-3p, miR-501-3p, and miR-584-5p) were identified as candidate biomarkers for 90-day mortality (Figures 1A and 1B).Figure 1MicroRNA profiling analysis in the screening phase(A) Volcano plot representing the differential expression of microRNAs (miRNAs) in the screening cohort. Second, these miRNAs were validated in the derivation cohort (n = 340) to explore their prognostic value in detail. In addition to this data-driven selection, five candidate miRNAs (miR-27a-3p, miR-34a-5p, miR-146a-5p, miR-150-5p, and miR-191-5p) were retrieved for the second phase due to their association with viral infection pathogenesis and disease severity in SARS-CoV-2 infection, based on results from our group and others.31,32,33,34,35 The differential miRNA expression between the study groups was set with an FC threshold greater than 1.5 (or less than 0.67) and a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 0.2. When the 13 candidate biomarkers were evaluated across the entire cohort, no significant differences in the plasma levels were observed between the survivors and nonsurvivors (Figure S1).

Subphenotype identification in elderly COVID-19 patients

Our group has previously demonstrated that, in certain clinical contexts, miRNAs have limited utility as biomarkers in the general population; however, they may hold significant potential in specific patient subgroups.36,37 On the basis of these results, we hypothesized that circulating miRNAs may exhibit distinct expression patterns among elderly COVID-19 patient subphenotypes, alongside differing prognostic values in each subgroup. Given the considerable variability in the host response to SARS-CoV-2, this hypothesis has particular relevance to the elderly population, where heterogeneity in health status is a prominent feature.

With that premise, we first explored the identification of clinical data-driven subphenotypes to address the heterogeneity of elderly COVID-19 patients according to their clinical status at hospital admission. Three subphenotypes were identified in the whole study population: elderly COVID-1 (eCOVID-1), elderly COVID-2 (eCOVID-2), and elderly COVID-3 (eCOVID-3) (Figure 2A and Table 2). Bootstrap-based stability analysis confirmed robust reproducibility of the identified subphenotypes (Table S3). Subphenotype-specific miRNA expression patterns in elderly COVID-19 patients and functional implications

Once data-driven subphenotypes were identified, we explored the candidate miRNA profile associated with each elderly COVID-19 patient cluster. The variables utilized for subphenotype construction demonstrated no significant differences between patients with and without sample within each subphenotype (Tables S4–S6), with the exception of the days between symptom onset and hospital admission in the eCOVID-2 subphenotype. Differential miRNA expression patterns between subphenotypes were observed (FDR less than 0.05) (Figure 2B and Table S7). Significant differences in miR-191-5p and miR-199a-3p plasma levels were detected between eCOVID-1 and eCOVID-2. Furthermore, miR-34a-5p was differentially expressed between eCOVID-2 and eCOVID-3. No differential miRNA expression patterns were identified between eCOVID-1 and eCOVID-3. The age-, sex-, and CCI-adjusted results are provided in Figure S2.

To explore the molecular differences underlying the identified subphenotypes, we analyzed the target genes regulated by miR-191-5p and miR-199a-3p. TargetScan Release 8.0 was employed to retrieve the predicted targets. The functional analysis was based on the intersection (I = 2) of the predicted target genes of miR-191-5p and miR-199a-3p, which resulted in six genes from a total of 474 miRNA targets (Figure 2C). The enriched biological and molecular pathways of the targetome mainly consisted of renal development and injury terms (e.g., metanephric glomerulus development, glomerular mesangium development, and renal sodium ion absorption), hormonal/metabolic processes (e.g., positive regulation of hormone metabolic process), and cell cycle pathways (e.g., Polo-like kinase mediated events and transcription of E2F targets under negative control by DREAM complex). To a lesser extent, terms related to the immune response, cell differentiation, and cellular hypoxic and hypotonic responses were also enriched for the targets of miR-191-5p and miR-199a-3p.

Plasma miRNAs as biomarkers of 90-day mortality in subphenotypes of elderly hospitalized COVID-19 patients

To evaluate the association between miRNA expression and 90-day mortality, each subphenotype was analyzed independently. The results of the clinical comparisons between survivors and nonsurvivors in each subphenotype are summarized in Tables S8–S10. Machine learning (ML) algorithms enable the discovery of complex interactions between miRNA features, clinical information, and outcomes.21,44 In eCOVID-2, the VSURF-based ranking of the clinical and miRNA data revealed that miR-106b-3p was the most important feature for the discrimination of the outcome (Figure 3B). To explore the prognostic value of this miRNA, we constructed a VSURF-based model integrating clinical predictors and the candidate miRNA. The ML-generated model selected the candidate miR-106b-3p together with three additional clinical predictors, namely PaO2/FiO2 ratio, age, and CRP (Figure 3C). Within the model, miR-106b-3p was the most significant feature, exceeding the contribution of the clinical predictors. Across 50 repeated feature selection runs, miR-106b-3p was selected in 100% of iterations, underscoring the robustness and stability of its contribution to the predictive model. To further enhance interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was performed. Indeed, miR-106b-3p contributed substantially to model predictions, with lower expression levels associated with the increased predicted mortality risk (Figure S4). The clinical and miRNA-based model yielded a cross-validated AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.71–0.80), demonstrating robust discriminative capacity. In contrast, the clinical model without miR-106b-3p achieved an AUC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.62–0.71). The inclusion of miR-106b-3p significantly improved model discrimination by 0.09 AUC units (95% CI: 0.02–0.15).

The optimal model for the eCOVID-1 subphenotype included age, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and lymphocyte count (Figure S3C). In the case of the eCOVID-3 subphenotype, the VSURF-based model integrated urea and neutrophil count (Figure S3D).

Functional implications of miR-106b-3p in elderly COVID-19 mortality

To identify potential biological mechanisms underlying the observed association between miR-106b-3p downregulation and 90-day mortality, we performed an exploratory enrichment analysis of its predicted targets. TargetScan Release 8.0 was used to retrieve the predicted regulatory targets of miR-106b-3p. The output consisted of 702 predicted target genes. The gene expression profile from an external RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset (GSE157103) was used to screen the expression of targets in a mortality analysis of elderly COVID-19 patients (Figure 3D).

Based on the miRNA-mediated downregulation of gene expression45 and the downregulation of miR-106b-3p among nonsurvivors, the analysis focused on the upregulated targets. Target genes displaying an FC exceeding 1.5 for upregulated genes and an FDR of <0.2 between the study groups were selected. A total of 591 genes were upregulated in the external dataset, and seven of these genes were identified as targets of miR-106b-3p: Aldo-Keto Reductase Family 1 Member C2 (AKR1C2), EF-Hand Calcium-Binding Domain 2 (EFCAB2), ETS Variant Transcription Factor 7 (ETV7), Helicase with Zinc Finger 2 (HELZ2), Heat Shock Protein Family A (Hsp70) Member 6 (HSPA6), Interferon-Induced Protein 35 (IFI35, also referred to as IFP35), and Ribosomal Protein S6 Kinase A2 (RPS6KA2).

Gene Ontology (GO) biological process, Reactome, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway databases were used to explore the underlying mechanisms of elderly COVID-19 mortality in the eCOVID-2 subphenotype (Figure 3E). The enriched biological processes and pathways were related to the immune and inflammatory responses. These included innate immune pathways such as macrophage activation, regulation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) 4 signaling, and response to prostaglandins, all of which are central to host defense and inflammatory signaling during viral infection. Beyond immune-specific processes, enrichment analysis revealed signaling pathways involved in cellular stress responses, growth, and transcriptional regulation. These included CREB1 phosphorylation through NMDA receptor-mediated activation of RAS signaling, ERK/MAPK targets, and nuclear events mediated by MAP kinases. In addition, cardiovascular homeostasis-related processes, including nervous system processes involved in the regulation of systemic arterial blood pressure and regulation of systemic arterial blood pressure via the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), were identified. Finally, enrichment was also observed in metabolic processes, including tertiary alcohol and polyketide metabolic processes, as well as bile acid and bile salt synthesis via the 7α-hydroxycholesterol and 24-hydroxycholesterol pathways.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to support the development of integrated clinical and miRNA-based prognostic models for elderly patients hospitalized for COVID-19. The findings underscore the potential value of more precise and personalized risk-stratification strategies in a vulnerable population within the context of respiratory infectious diseases.

Initially, we examined the use of circulating miRNAs as biomarkers for 90-day mortality in a cohort of elderly hospitalized COVID-19 patients. The analysis was conducted in two distinct phases: (1) the screening phase of the plasma miRNome (matching groups according to age, sex, and CCI), and (2) miRNA profiling in the derivation cohort (unmatched groups). The results from the screening phase revealed eight miRNAs as putative predictors of 90-day mortality. These candidate miRNAs, along with five additional miRNAs selected based on literature, were further assessed in the derivation cohort. None of the 13 miRNAs demonstrated biomarker value across the entire population. These results are consistent with previous studies that suggest a poor biomarker potential of miRNAs in whole cohorts but a possible value in specific subpopulations of patients. For instance, although plasma miRNAs failed to predict adverse outcomes in a cohort of patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing hemodialysis, regression tree models identified eight patient subgroups with distinct risk patterns, where miR-632 and miR-186-5p refined the risk assessment in two specific patient subpopulations.36 Similarly, although circulating miR-133a-3p did not enhance the established clinical prognostic model for patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and central sleep apnea, this miRNA facilitated risk stratification into specific subphenotypes when combined with clinical data.37 On the basis of previous and current evidence, we hypothesized that circulating miRNAs may show limited prognostic performance in the overall population but may be useful in specific patient subphenotypes.

The identification of data-driven subphenotypes is frequently observed in heterogeneous diseases, wherein clinical manifestations vary among distinct subgroups of patients. This approach has facilitated unraveling complex diseases, including ARDS,46 sepsis,47,48 heart failure,49 Alzheimer disease,50 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),51 and type 2 diabetes.52 Subphenotyping in COVID-19 has improved our understanding of the clinical heterogeneity of the disease.53,54,55 Despite the high interindividual variability among elderly patients, the presence of subphenotypes among this high-risk population remains a significant knowledge gap. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive assessment to identify clinical data-driven subphenotypes within our elderly cohort of patients hospitalized for COVID-19. Three distinct subphenotypes, eCOVID-1, eCOVID-2, and eCOVID-3, were derived from the study population, each characterized by unique baseline features, admission patterns, and disease progression. The eCOVID-1 and eCOVID-3 subphenotypes exhibited similar ages, baseline characteristics, and comparable admission PaO2/FiO2 ratios and ARDS development rates. However, eCOVID-1 patients displayed the lowest 90-day mortality rate, while eCOVID-3 patients presented the greatest deterioration in vital signs at the time of hospital admission and the highest 90-day mortality rate. In contrast, patients grouped in eCOVID-2—the eldest and most comorbid subjects—did not exhibit the highest mortality rate, despite their higher baseline clinical vulnerability. Overall, these findings suggest that mortality may not be directly proportional to baseline vulnerability, indicating the potential influence of other factors on disease progression and mortality.

To explore further, linear models with empirical Bayes statistics were used to evaluate the candidate miRNA profile of each subgroup, potentially uncovering molecular markers that could offer deeper insight into the observed differences in clinical outcomes. miRNA differential expression analyses revealed that miR-191-5p and miR-199a-3p were significantly upregulated in eCOVID-1, the subphenotype with the lowest 90-day mortality rate, compared with the most comorbid and eldest subphenotype, eCOVID-2. Functional analysis of these miRNAs suggested that the differential severity and evolution of these two subphenotypes may be associated with divergent hormonal/metabolic, cell cycle, and renal mechanisms. In addition, patients in eCOVID-2 presented significantly lower expression of miR-34a-5p than those in the eCOVID-3 subgroup, who experienced the most acute symptoms at admission and the highest mortality rate. The downregulation of miR-34a-5p has been shown to influence chemokine signaling, thereby affecting the immune cell response (e.g., CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and macrophages), which is crucial for the response to infections.56 Therefore, the observed differences in disease evolution between eCOVID-2 and eCOVID-3 may stem from modulations in the immune response dynamics.

Given that eCOVID-2 comprises the eldest and most comorbid patients and that no differences in miRNA expression were observed between eCOVID-1 and eCOVID-3 patients, it is important to consider whether the observed miRNA differences derive primarily from the age-related regulatory changes, rather than differences in disease evolution. Age is a well-known factor influencing miRNA expression,10 potentially shaping the molecular profile of this subgroup, independent of disease severity. However, although age-related effects cannot be ruled out, the functional roles of miR-34a-5p, miR-191-5p, and miR-199a-3p in key biological pathways suggest that other mechanisms beyond aging may contribute to the distinct clinical evolution observed across subphenotypes. Further studies are warranted to dissect the contributions of age- and disease-specific factors to miRNA expression patterns in elderly COVID-19 patients.

We next evaluated the prognostic value of candidate miRNAs within each patient subphenotype. Notably, eCOVID-2 was the only subgroup that exhibited differential miRNA expression between survivors and nonsurvivors, with miR-106b-3p downregulated in nonsurvivors. Within the multivariable model, miR-106b-3p showed the highest predictive contribution, exceeding that of the established clinical predictors such as age, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, and CRP. The inclusion of miR-106b-3p significantly improved model discrimination compared with the clinical-only model, increasing the cross-validated AUC from 0.66 to 0.75. Together, these findings indicate that miR-106b-3p adds prognostic value beyond conventional clinical variables and may improve risk stratification when incorporated into multivariable predictive models. In support of the findings discussed above,36,37 our subphenotyping strategy and ML-based multivariable modeling highlight the utility of miRNAs as biomarkers in specific subgroups of patients, rather than across the entire population. These data also point to the value of incorporating miRNA expression analyses in the study of complex conditions.

Functionally, upregulated targets of miR-106b-3p, identified in the external dataset, were linked to the immunoregulatory pathways, including macrophage activation and TLR4 signaling pathways. Studies using SARS-CoV-2-infected mouse models, macrophages, and human airway in vitro cultures have shown significantly increased IFI35 expression,57,58 indicating activation of interferon and innate immune responses to viral infections. Interestingly, IFI35 acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern that promotes inflammation by activating macrophages via the TLR4 and NF-κB pathways.59 Consistent with this pro-inflammatory role, clinical studies have shown that serum IFI35 levels in individuals with SARS-CoV-2 infection correlate with disease severity.58 Moreover, as interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), HELZ2 and ETV7 mediate interferon antiviral response, as demonstrated in Dengue virus infection and in models of influenza virus infection, respectively.60,61 In parallel, ETV7 has been shown to shape adaptive immune responses by driving CD8+ T cells from memory differentiation toward terminal exhaustion, thereby limiting the antiviral efficacy.62 In the context of COVID-19, where severe states and fatal outcomes, particularly in elderly patients, are characterized by impaired interferon responses and dysfunctional T cell phenotypes,63,64 ETV7 upregulation in leukocytes from nonsurvivors is consistent with a state of compromised antiviral host defense. Similarly, and beyond its canonical chaperone functions, Hsp70 also has a dual function in immune regulation: activating nonspecific immunity, such as the enriched TLR4 signaling pathway; and regulating the adaptive response by inducing the differentiation of regulatory T cells.65 Elevated plasma levels of Hsp70 have been associated with increased mortality in COVID-19 patients.66

Beyond immune-specific pathways, enrichment analysis revealed signaling programs linked to vascular regulation (i.e., regulation of systemic arterial blood pressure by renin-angiotensin) and stress-responsive transcriptional control (e.g., gastrin-CREB signaling via PKC and MAPK, CREB1 phosphorylation through NMDA receptor-mediated activation of RAS signaling, ERK/MAPK targets, and nuclear events mediated by MAP kinases). Notably, dysregulation of the RAS is a hallmark of SARS-CoV-2 infection, as viral hijacking of ACE2 perturbs vascular homeostasis and promotes inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and organ injury.67 In this context, increased expression of RPS6KA2, a member of the ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK) family of serine/threonine kinases, reflects sustained activation of downstream RAS-ERK-MAPK signaling cascade.68 Moreover, RPS6KA2-mediated phosphorylation of CREB1 links extracellular stress signals to the transcriptional programs involved in inflammation, cell survival, and vascular tone, processes that are critically altered in severe and fatal COVID-19.67 Upregulated target genes were also associated with metabolic processes such as bile acid synthesis and tertiary alcohol metabolism. Indeed, metabolomic analysis demonstrated that COVID-19 patients with elevated bile acid derivatives were more likely to progress to severe disease.69 These results suggest that metabolic dysregulation may contribute to severe COVID-19 outcomes in the eldest and most comorbid subphenotype.

In conclusion, the plasma miRNA profile provides complementary prognostic information when combined with clinical information for defined subphenotypes of elderly COVID-19 patients. These findings highlight the potential added value of incorporating miRNA signatures into risk-stratification models for this vulnerable population, while emphasizing the need for further studies to validate and refine the prognostic utility of miRNAs.

Limitations of the study

It is imperative to take the limitations of this study into account when interpreting the findings. With respect to the study design, the screening phase was conducted with the objective of identifying miRNA biomarkers at the general study population level, rather than for specific subphenotypes. The criteria for selecting candidate miRNAs in this phase were intentionally relaxed to maximize the potential for biomarker assessment success in the derivation cohort. Some degree of residual confounding from unmatched or unmeasured variables between the screening groups may have influenced the observed associations. Concerning the study outcome, we focused on 90-day mortality, excluding other clinically relevant endpoints. Clinical management during the recruitment period, carried out in the early stages of the pandemic, may also have influenced outcomes, particularly given the evolving therapeutic strategies and the emergence of new SARS-CoV-2 variants. Notably, the study cohort predominantly comprised unvaccinated patients. Further studies should be performed to validate the identified subphenotypes and the prognostic value of miR-106b-3p and the ML-based multivariable model within independent populations. In addition, certain treatments administered before or during hospitalization (such as monoclonal antibodies or convalescent plasma) were not systematically recorded, and their potential influence on clinical evolution or circulating miRNA levels cannot be fully ruled out. Similarly, post-discharge variables related to re-infections or re-admissions were not collected. The absence of sociodemographic variables such as ethnicity or socioeconomic status prevented adjustment for potential related biases. Furthermore, differences observed between patients with and without available samples may have introduced a degree of selection bias. Additionally, age differences across subphenotypes, particularly in the eCOVID-2 group, represent an inherent characteristic of this population. While age and comorbidities were intentionally included to derive clinically meaningful subphenotypes that reflect real-world patient stratification, we acknowledge that their inclusion might have shaped the cluster structure. The bootstrap-based stability analysis, which assesses cluster reproducibility under repeated data perturbation, confirmed highly stable recovery of eCOVID-2, supporting the robustness of this subphenotype beyond its demographic composition. Importantly, differential miRNA expression analyses were adjusted for age and CCI, reducing the risk of demographic confounding in the observed molecular associations. Notwithstanding, future studies applying alternative feature configurations, including physiological-variable-only clustering, would further clarify the relative contribution of baseline vulnerability versus acute disease severity to subphenotype assignment. Finally, experimental validation, together with matched miRNA and mRNA expression data in the same cohort, are required to establish direct mechanistic relationships between miR-106b-3p and its target genes. Therefore, the functional analyses presented here should be considered hypothesis generating. Despite these limitations, the real-world clinical foundation of this study simultaneously represents one of its key strengths.

Experimental model and study participant details

This is a substudy of the COVID-Ponent cohort registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04824677). COVID-Ponent is a multicenter observational retrospective/prospective study including hospitalized COVID-19 patients admitted to the Hospital Universitari Arnau de Vilanova-Santa Maria (Lleida, Spain), Clínica Perpetuo Socorro (Lleida, Spain), Hospital Comarcal del Pallars (Tremp, Spain) and Hospital del Río Hortega (Valladolid, Spain) between February 2020 and December 2021. Patients were enrolled if they met the following criteria: (a) admitted to the hospital with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection by qPCR of nasopharyngeal swabs; and (b) aged 65 years or older, following the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of elderly individuals in developed countries.1 For patients with multiple COVID-19 hospitalizations during the study period, only data from the first hospitalization were considered.

The study included elderly patients with a median [P25; P75] age of 79.0 years [72.0-87.0],), of whom 44.0% were female.

The primary study endpoint was 90-day mortality. Patients’ data were collected from the day of hospital admission to death or until 90 days of follow-up, whichever occurred first. A comprehensive array of sociodemographic, clinical, pharmacological and laboratory data was gathered at the time of admission. The pharmacological treatments administered and interventions performed during the hospital stay were collected. Complications and outcomes that occurred during the hospital stay were also recorded. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and entered into the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database,70 by trained data entry researchers. Any data that were incoherent or missing were checked by independent experienced data collectors.

All the subjects provided written informed consent for study participation. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics and Research Committees of the Hospital Universitari Arnau de Vilanova (CEIC/2279) and was performed in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national and international law on data protection.

Method details

Blood samples were obtained during the first 72 hours of hospitalization and processed under standardized operating procedures at each participating hospital. Blood samples (10 mL) were collected by venipuncture into EDTA-containing tubes (BD Vacutainer®, Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA). The plasma was obtained after centrifugation under standard conditions: 1500 xg for 10 min at room temperature. The resulting supernatants were transferred into RNase-free tubes and stored at −80°C before RNA extraction.

Samples collected at the Hospital Universitari Arnau de Vilanova-Santa Maria were obtained with support from the IRBLleida Biobank (B.000682) and Biobank and Biomodels Platform (ISCIII PT23/00032). Frozen plasma aliquots from other participating centers were shipped on dry ice and centralized to the IRBLleida Biobank (Lleida, Spain).

miRNA profiling was divided into two phases. First, the human plasma miRNome was evaluated in a screening phase to identify candidate miRNAs as biomarkers for 90-day mortality. In this phase, survivors and nonsurvivors were matched according to age, sex and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI).71 Second, the candidate miRNAs were assessed in the derivation cohort, defined as the subset of patients with available plasma samples for miRNA expression analysis.

Total RNA was isolated from 200 μL of plasma using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Advanced Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Synthetic Caenorhabditis elegans cel-miR-39-3p (1.6 × 108 copies/μL, Qiagen) and RNA spike-in templates (1 μL of UniSp2, UniSp4 and UniSp5 mix) (Qiagen) were added to each sample as quality controls (QCs) for the RNA extraction process. cel-miR-39-3p was also used for exogenous normalization. Moreover, MS2 bacteriophage RNA (1.25 μg/mL, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), an RNA carrier not containing miRNAs, was added to improve the RNA yield. The reagents were spiked into the samples at the beginning of the RNA isolation process in conjunction with the lysis buffer. The RNA was eluted from the column with 20 μL of RNase-free water and immediately stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis.

miRNA quantification was performed according to the protocol for the miRCURY LNA Universal RT microRNA PCR System (Qiagen) which offers optimal accuracy and reproducibility.72 From the isolated total RNA, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the miRCURY LNA RT Kit (Qiagen) in a total reaction volume of 10 μL following the manufacturer's instructions. QC of this process was monitored using 1 μL of the UniSp6 RNA spike-in template (Qiagen). The reaction mix was incubated for 60 min at 42 °C followed by 5 min at 95 °C for RT inactivation. Reactions were carried out using a Veriti™ 96-Well Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The synthesized cDNA was stored at −20 °C.

RT-qPCRs were conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol using locked nucleic acid (LNA)-based qPCR assays. In the screening phase, we used miRCURY LNA™ miRNA Focus PCR Panels (384-well plates) (Qiagen), which comprise the human plasma miRNome, consisting of the 179 most typically detected miRNAs in human serum/plasma. For the validation phase, miRCURY LNA™ miRNA Custom Panels (384-well plates) (Qiagen) were used to analyze the resulting screening candidates. The panels contained assays for candidate miRNAs as well as for synthetic spike-in controls used for monitoring the RNA extraction (UniSp2, UniSp4 and UniSp5) and cDNA synthesis (UniSp6) to assess technical variability. In addition, an interplate calibrator (UniSp3, IPC) was implemented to monitor inter-run variability and minimize the batch effect. For normalization purposes, the panels included miR-106a-5p and miR-484 assays as endogenous reference miRNAs, as previously reported.21

Both the screening and validation phases were performed using the miRCURY LNA SYBR® Green PCR Kit (Qiagen) with a total volume of 10 μL. qPCR assays were conducted using the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with the following temperature settings: 95 °C for 2 min and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 56 °C for 1 min, followed by melting curve analysis. Amplification curves were evaluated using QuantStudio Software v1.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Melting curve analysis was used to corroborate the specificity of the reactions. The quantification cycle (Cq) value was defined as the number of cycles required for the fluorescence signal to exceed the detection threshold value. This threshold was set to the log linear range of the amplification. Unusual amplification curves (e.g., low amplification efficiency) were excluded from further analysis.

QC of the miRNA profiling process was performed by systematically monitoring the Cq values of all synthetic spike-in controls. For each spike-in assay, Cq distributions were examined across samples to detect deviations from the expected homogeneous range. To minimize inter-run variability, interplate calibration was applied using the UniSp3 assays included in each qPCR plate. For each plate, an IPC was calculated as the difference between the plate-specific mean UniSp3 Cq and the overall mean UniSp3 Cq across all plates. Calibrated Cq values for each miRNA assay were then obtained by subtracting the corresponding IPC value from the raw Cq value. Hemolysis contamination of plasma samples was also analyzed using the ratio of miR-23a-3p (stable in plasma and serum and not affected by hemolysis) to miR-451a (an erythrocyte-enriched miRNA), as previously described.73 Samples in which the delta Cq (ΔCq) (Cq miR-23a-3p – Cq miR-451a) exceeded 7 were considered hemolyzed and were discarded for analysis. In this QC step, two samples were discarded because of hemolysis in the candidate validation phase.

Cq values above 35 cycles were considered undetectable and were censored at the minimum level observed for each miRNA. miRNAs expressed in <90% of samples were excluded from further analyses. Among the 179 miRNAs profiled in the screening phase, twelve did not meet the expression criteria and were therefore removed. All miRNAs included in the validation phase fulfilled the expression threshold.

Cq values of the miRNAs were simultaneously subjected to exogenous and endogenous normalization. First, exogenous normalization was conducted using cel-miR-39-3p. Second, endogenous normalization was performed by applying miR-106a-5p and miR-484 as endogenous reference miRNAs for SARS-CoV-2 infection severity, as previously described by Belmonte T & Perez-Pons M et al.21 Six samples from the derivation cohort lacking data for either of the endogenous reference miRNAs were excluded from further analyses. Overall, relative quantification of the miRNA data was performed using the 2-ΔCq method: ΔCq=CqmiRNA-Cqcel-miR-39-3p-Geometricmean(CqmiR-106a-5p·CqmiR-4842). The expression levels were log-transformed for statistical purposes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org/). The characteristics of the study population were summarized by descriptive statistics. Data are presented as the median [P25; P75] for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher’s exact test. The p-value threshold defining statistical significance was set at < 0.05. Linear models with Empirical Bayes statistics were used to assess differential miRNA expression between study groups. Comparisons between survivors and nonsurvivors were conducted using models adjusted for age and sex. For analyses involving population-derived subphenotypes, both unadjusted and adjusted models were performed; adjusted models included age, sex and the CCI as covariates.

In the screening phase, candidate circulating miRNAs were selected based on fold change (FC) differences between study groups, without applying statistical significance thresholds. This exploratory approach was intentionally adopted to maximize sensitivity and capture miRNAs showing biologically relevant expression shifts. The candidates were subsequently tested in the derivation cohort under more stringent statistical conditions, where differential expression was assessed using adjusted p-values corrected for multiple testing (Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate, FDR). This two-step strategy aimed to balance exploratory discovery breadth with statistical rigor in validation, thereby minimizing false discoveries while preserving potential biological signals.

A sample size calculation was performed to confirm that the available cohort provided sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful differences in miRNA expression between survivors and nonsurvivors. Assuming a two-tailed test with an alpha risk of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, a minimum of 27 nonsurvivors and 95 survivors were required to detect as statistically significant a fold-change difference greater than or equal to 1.25 (or 0.80 in the case of downregulated miRNAs).

Subphenotypes were built on sociodemographic (age and sex), clinical baseline variables (hypertension, type II diabetes mellitus, chronic respiratory disease, chronic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease and active neoplasia) and vital signs on hospital admission (heart rate, respiratory rate and temperature), as well as the days between symptoms onset and hospital admission. Subphenotypes were constructed using a multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) cluster aggregation strategy based on Aschenbruck et al.38 adapted for mixed data (continuous and categorical). A total of five imputed datasets were generated. Clustering was performed using the k-prototypes algorithm, which generalizes k-means to accommodate mixed data types by optimizing a within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS)-type objective that jointly minimizes dissimilarity across continuous and categorical variables. For each imputed dataset, clustering solutions were generated for k values ranging from 1 to 10. The optimal number of clusters was selected using elbow criteria based on WCSS, choosing the smallest k beyond which additional clusters yielded only marginal reductions in WCSS. The elbow criterion was defined before inspecting clinical outcomes to avoid data-driven tuning. Using the same WCSS objective for both clustering and k selection ensured internal consistency and preserved interpretability. Cluster stability was assessed by bootstrap resampling. Patients were repeatedly sampled with replacement; clustering was re-performed using k-prototypes and cluster reproducibility was quantified using the Jaccard similarity index relative to the original clustering solution. Clusters were considered highly stable for mean Jaccard values ≥0.85, stable for values between 0.75 and 0.85, moderately stable for values between 0.60 and 0.75, and unstable for values <0.60.

The random forest (RF) by VSURF method, a supervised machine learning (ML) algorithm for feature selection, was employed both for ranking clinical and miRNA data and for prognostic model construction.74 VSURF comprises three steps: (i) elimination of variables with low importance by ranking the average of the variable importance measure on 50 runs of RF; (ii) calculation of the out-of-bag (OOB) error rates of 50 RF runs for each model and variables with the lowest OOB error were selected; and (iii) selection of the final model by performing an ascending sequence of RFs that tested the inclusion of each feature to remove the most redundant. The data utilized for model construction included candidate miRNAs and previously proposed clinical predictors of COVID-19 mortality. These predictors included sociodemographic variables (age and sex) and clinical parameters at hospital admission (creatinine, urea, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet count, C-reactive protein (CRP) and partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) to fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio (PaO2/FiO2).75,76,77,78 To ensure the robustness and consistency of the feature selection process, we repeated each selection method 50 times. In every iteration, all candidate predictors were considered during the selection procedure. We then calculated the frequency with which each predictor was selected across the 50 independent runs. To enhance model interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed. SHAP analysis provides a unified framework to quantify the contribution of each feature to individual predictions. Beeswarm plots were generated to visualize both the magnitude and direction of feature effects on the outcome, allowing interpretation of the relationship between predictor values and mortality risk.

Clinical and miRNA-based model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The discriminative performance of the model was quantified as the area under the ROC curve (AUC), estimated using repeated 5-fold cross-validation (6 repetitions). To quantify statistical uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the cross-validated AUC were computed using bootstrap resampling of the cross-validated predictions. To assess the incremental discriminative value of miRNAs over the clinical model, the discriminative performance of the clinical model alone was also evaluated.

Bioinformatic analysis was performed using R software, version 4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org/). Conserved predicted biological targets of differentially expressed miRNAs were retrieved from TargetScan Release 8.0.79 Functional analysis of the miRNA targets was performed using the Bioconductor repository plugin in the R interface.80 Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes, Reactome and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway databases were analyzed executing ReactomePA and clusterProfiler packages.81,82

The differential expression of the targets of the miRNAs, identified as biomarkers for 90-day mortality, was evaluated using an external RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset (GSE157103) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The GEOquery package (Bioconductor) was used to extract the GSE157103 dataset.80,83 The dataset included 43 leukocyte samples derived from whole blood, comprising 26 samples from survivors and 17 from nonsurvivors, all from elderly patients hospitalized for COVID-19 (≥ 65 years). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package.84 On the basis of the miRNA-mediated downregulation of gene expression, functional analysis focused on upregulated or downregulated target genes of downregulated or upregulated miRNAs, respectively.
