Nonconvex Linear System Identification with Minimal State Representation
Uday Kiran Reddy Tadipatri, Benjamin D. Haeffele, Joshua Agterberg, Ingvar Ziemann, René Vidal

Abstract
Low-order linear System IDentification (SysID) addresses the challenge of estimating the parameters of a linear dynamical system from finite samples of observations and control inputs with minimal state representation. Traditional approaches often utilize Hankel-rank minimization, which relies on convex relaxations that can require numerous, costly singular value decompositions (SVDs) to optimize. In this work, we propose two nonconvex reformulations to tackle low-order SysID (i) Burer-Monterio (BM) factorization of the Hankel matrix for efficient nuclear norm minimization, and (ii) optimizing directly over system parameters for real, diagonalizable systems with an atomic norm style decomposition. These reformulations circumvent the need for repeated heavy SVD computations, significantly improving computational efficiency. Moreover, we prove that optimizing directly over the system parameters yields lower statistical error rates, and lower sample complexities that do not scale linearly with trajectory length like in Hankel-nuclear norm minimization. Additionally, while our proposed formulations are nonconvex, we provide theoretical guarantees of achieving global optimality in polynomial time. Finally, we demonstrate algorithms that solve these nonconvex programs and validate our theoretical claims on synthetic data.

System Identification, Hankel rank minimization, Sample Complexity.

Introduction

We consider the linear time-invariant system (
LS
) with time
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}
as hidden states,
{\mathbf{u}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
as control inputs,

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}
as outputs,
\zeta_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}
as output noise,
and system parameters
(A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
,
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}\times n_{u}}
,

C\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}\times n_{x}}
,
D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}\times n_{u}})
described as

\begin{split}&{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=A{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+B{\mathbf{u}}_{t};{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=0,\\
&{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=C{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+D{\mathbf{u}}_{t}+\zeta_{t}.\end{split}

(LS)

Linear
System IDentification
(SysID) aims to estimate these parameters

(A
,
B,

C,

D)
using finite rollouts with finite length trajectories,

\{({\mathbf{u}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{i})_{t=1}^{2L+2}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. However, the true system order or state dimension
n_{x}
is
typically unknown.
It is of practical interest to find systems that are
minimal order

as such models provide faster response times, simplified control designs, and improved robustness,
while mitigating overfitting during the learning phase.
This task can be formulated as a dimension minimization problem under an
L_{2}
norm constraint (see Program (
P0
)).
However, solving this problem is known to be NP-HARD
(
Vandenberghe and Boyd, 1996
)
.

In classical linear system theory, the order of the system is determined by the rank of the
Hankel matrix constructed from the Markov parameters
(CB,CAB,\dots,CA^{2L+1}B)

(
Sontag, 2013
, Corollary 6.5.7)
. Convex relaxations, such as minimizing the Hankel
nuclear norm instead of the rank, have been proposed to make this problem computationally tractable

(
Fazel et al., 2003
)
. However,
this presents two key challenges:
(i)
Computational complexity
: The number of parameters scales
linearly with the trajectory length
L
, making nuclear norm minimization increasingly difficult.
(ii)
Statistical inefficiency
: The high dimensionality leads to worse statistical error rates
and larger sample complexity requirements
(
Oymak and Ozay, 2019
)
.

In the literature on matrix sensing, nuclear norm minimization is tackled via nonconvex reparameterization
using its variational form with Frobenius norms
(
Burer and Monteiro, 2003
)
.
This reformulation enables the use of efficient first-order optimization methods,
such as gradient descent and Nesterov acceleration
(
Nesterov, 1983
)
, which converge
in
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
, and
\mathcal{O}(1/T^{2})
, respectively, where
T

is the number of iterations.
In contrast, direct nuclear norm minimization often requires computing proximal operators
(
Parikh et al., 2014
)
that
depend on SVD, making it less computationally efficient.
In this work, we propose two reformulations, the first of which is Hankel nuclear norm minimization for SysID
(see Program (
P1
)) using the BM factorization
(
Burer and Monteiro, 2003
)
.
Despite being nonconvex, this formulation eliminates the need for frequent SVD computations, significantly enhancing computational efficiency, and can still be provably globally optimized in polynomial time.

While this first reformulation offers clear advantages in terms of optimization efficiency, the statistical error rates
and sample complexities remain comparable to those of Hankel nuclear norm minimization. In particular, the statistical error rates scale with trajectory length, despite the number of system parameters remaining constant. Our second reformulation (see Program (
P2
)) proposes a method of SysID of a real, diagonalizable system
where we perform optimization directly over the system parameters space
(A,B,C,D)
with a structured
regularization similar to an atomic norm decomposition. Although this reformulation is also nonconvex,
we show that it can be efficiently optimized to global optimality. Furthermore, this approach achieves improved
statistical error rates and reduced sample complexity compared to existing Hankel nuclear
norm minimization heuristics.

Contributions.

Our key contributions are as follows:

1.

Nonconvex Relaxations for low-order SysID.
We propose two nonconvex reformulations (i) for
general linear systems via BM re-parametrization (
P1
) of the
Hankel matrix, (ii) by directly optimizing over
system parameters (
P2
), restricted to real, diagonalizable systems.
These nonconvex problems are first of their kind
and serve as relaxations to the NP-HARD problem (
P0
).

2.

Global Optimality Guarantees.
We provide guarantees of global optimality for the two nonconvex reformulations when they are solved using first-order optimization procedures.
Furthermore, we affirm that these nonconvex problems can be solved in polynomial time.
The second reformulation overcomes the quadratic dependency on trajectory length in the
computational efficiency of existing methods.

3.

Statistical Error Rates and Sample Complexities.
We provide statistical error rates and sample complexities for both the reformulations
while the first reformulation scales poorly compared to the second
due to its linear dependency on trajectory length.

4.

Numerical evidence.
We propose algorithms to tackle these
nonconvex programs, provide numerical simulations that corroborate our
theoretical insights. Under a fixed compute budget, our algorithms outperform existing methods.

These results highlight that for real, diagonalizable systems
the reformulation (
P2
) achieves both superior statistical efficiency and
reduced sample complexity compared to (
P1
),

Sun et al. (2022)
, and
Lee (2022)
.

Problem Formulation

Given a noisy linear time-invariant system (

. Formally, define

1

Due to the trivial estimation of

(1)

Our goal is to solve the optimization

\begin{split}\min_{n_{x},A,B,C,D}n_{x},\quad\text{such that }\frac{1}{4N(L+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\mathsf{vec}}(Y_{i})-G(A,B,C,D){\mathsf{vec}}(U_{i})\|_{F}^{2}\leq\epsilon,\end{split}

It is well known that solving (

) in general

(

)
. Numerous convex relaxations of (

) have been proposed to tackle this issue under certain conditions (beyond the scope of this work), such as minimizing the nuclear norm of the Hankel matrix

(

;
Recht et al., 2008
;
Recht et al., 2010
)
. To place this in context, define the Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}\times(2L+1)n_{u}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(L+1)n_{y}\times(L+1)n_{u}}
for a block sequence

as

\forall i,j\in[L+1],\left[\mathcal{H}\left(\begin{bmatrix}K_{1}&K_{2}&\dots&K_{2L+1}\end{bmatrix}\right)\right]_{1+(i-1)\cdot n_{y}:i\cdot n_{y},1+(j-1)\cdot n_{u}:j\cdot n_{u}}=K_{i+j-1}.

(2)

In
Sun et al. (2022)
the authors proposed to solve the reformulated problem

\min_{H,D}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\mathbf{y}}_{2L+1}^{i}-\begin{bmatrix}H&D\end{bmatrix}{\mathsf{vec}}(U_{i})\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathcal{H}(H)\|_{*}.

(P1’)

After obtaining the Markov parameters
H
they perform the
Ho-Kalman procedure
(
Ho and Kalman, 1966
)
to obtain the
system parameters
(A,B,C,D)
. The matrix
H
has

2(L+1)n_{y}n_{u}
number of unique parameters to estimate.
The time complexity of SVD on the matrix
\mathcal{H}(H)
is

\mathcal{O}(L^{2}n_{y}n_{x})

(
Xu and Qiao, 2008
)
,
so the quadratic dependence on

it causes a computational bottleneck when

is large.
Therefore, we propose adopting a BM type re-parameterization
(
Burer and Monteiro, 2003
)
in two ways: (i) by learning two low-rank matrices whose product forms a Hankel matrix, and (ii) by directly estimating the system parameters
(A,B,C,D)
. First,
recall the adjoint of the Hankel operator
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}:{\mathbb{R}}^{(L+1)n_{y}\times(L+1)n_{u}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}\times(2L+1)n_{u}}
as

\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}\left(\begin{bmatrix}[K_{i+j-1}]_{i\in[L+1]}\end{bmatrix}_{j\in[L+1]}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}K_{1}&K_{2}&\dots&K_{2L+1}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}\times(2L+1)n_{u}},

(3)

and note that
\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}={\sf id}
, and

\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}\circ\mathcal{H}={\sf id}
, where
{\sf id}
is the identity operator.

First formulation
. We define the optimization program (
P1
) as

\min_{n_{x},V,Z,D}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\mathbf{y}}_{2L+1}^{i}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}(VZ^{T})&D\end{bmatrix}{\mathsf{vec}}(U_{i})\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|Z\|_{F}^{2}\right],

(P1)

where
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(L+1)n_{y}\times n_{x}}
, and
Z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(L+1)n_{u}\times n_{x}}
.
Let the impulse response for this parameterization be

G^{\prime}(V,Z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2(L+1)n_{y}\times 2(L+1)n_{u}}
.

Program (
P1
) redefines the nuclear norm minimization via the sum of
squared Frobenius norms
\frac{1}{2}\left[\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|Z\|_{F}^{2}\right]
. In contrast to the nuclear norm regularization, the Frobenius norm minimization has time
complexity
\mathcal{O}(L(n_{y}+n_{u}))
, which is only linear in
L
.
However, in both Programs (
P1’
) and (
P1
), the number of parameters
scales with the trajectory length. For (
P1’
), it has been shown that the
sample complexity is
NL\gtrsim\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(L^{2}n_{u}(n_{y}+n_{u}))

(
Sun et al., 2022
)
. In contrast, we will see in Theorem

2
(
P1
) achieves a smaller sample complexity of

NL\gtrsim\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(Ln_{u}(n_{y}+n_{u}))
.
Nevertheless, there remains room for improvement in the sample complexity,
given that we have access to
\mathcal{O}(NL)
data points.
We consider another reformulation that ameliorates this suboptimal trajectory dependence. Unfortunately, the theoretical guarantees necessitate that the systems
be real, diagonalizable, which imposes limitations on the class of realizable systems

(
Fernández-Cara et al., 2015
)
. However, previous studies have shown that
estimation of non-diagonalizable systems is highly challenging

(
Tu et al., 2024
)
which is an avenue for future work.

Second formulation
. For real, diagonalizable systems
we optimize
over the system parameters and dimension directly via a regularization that can induce
low-order structure. We achieve this by considering the program

\min_{n_{x},{\mathbf{a}},B,C,D}\frac{1}{4N(L+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\mathsf{vec}}(Y_{i})-G({\sf diag}({\mathbf{a}}),B,C,D){\mathsf{vec}}(U_{i})\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\Theta({\mathbf{a}},B,C,D),

(P2)

where

, and
\Theta({\mathbf{a}},B,C,D)

3

Since we are dealing with real, diagonalizable systems the program (
P2

on the spectrum of state-transition matrix
A
, and eigen matrix is assumed to be absorbed in
B
, and
C
.
Although Program (
P2
) is nonconvex, specific choices of

\Theta({\mathbf{a}},B,C,D)
allow us to provide a certificate of optimality when employing
first-order optimization methods.
In this work, we provide certificates for global optimality and analyze statistical
recovery errors
for the programs (
P1
), and (
P2
) for a trivial feed-through matrix,
D=0

3
) relies in re-writing the programs
(
P1
), and (
P2

Haeffele and Vidal (2020)
. In §
4

we instantiate the approach of
Ziemann and Tu (2022)
to provide tight
(up-to log factors) error rates and sample complexities.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we focus on performing system identification with minimal state representation.
We propose two nonconvex reformulations: (i) a BM-style reparameterization of Hankel nuclear norm
minimization, and (ii) direct optimization over the system parameters. Despite their nonconvex nature, we design
algorithms with theoretical guarantees of
global convergence using first-order optimization methods.
Additionally, we derive near tight statistical error rates and sample complexity bounds. Our findings
suggest that directly estimating system parameters outperforms alternative approaches in both optimization
efficiency and statistical recovery. However, our analysis is currently restricted to systems that are real,
diagonalizable. Extending this framework to handle non-diagonalizable systems is an avenue for future work.

Appendix B
Proofs for statistical bounds

First we present Theorem
B.1
that
provides excess risk or recovery error bound
for time dependent data with compact hypothesis class.
These results take ideas from

Ziemann and Tu (2022)
;
Ziemann et al. (2024)
. While
their analysis was catered to more general hypothesis class.
However, our settings is applied to simple linear hypothesis
classes.

Now we state our assumptions on the time-dependent data
and the hypothesis class.

Assumption B.1

(Data assumption)
.

The sequence
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
are random variables such that
there exists a

such that for any

we have
that

(B.1)

This assumption trivially holds if

is a sub-Gaussian random
variable.

Assumption B.2

(Hypothesis class)
.

The parametric class
{\cal F}_{\theta}
, and function

\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{B}(r),\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}})\|={\sf poly}(r)

{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,\epsilon)\leq\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{{\sf dim}({\cal F}_{\theta})})

If the function

is linear in its inputs, then

B.2
holds, which is our primary concern.

{\cal F}_{\theta}
,
and predictor

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose
\hat{\theta}\in{\cal F}_{\theta}
is the global minimizer of the Equation (
B.3
), then we have

\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i},f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\rangle+\lambda\left[\Omega(f^{*})-\Omega(f_{\hat{\theta}})\right].

(B.23)

Proof.

By the definition of the global minimizer of ERM, we have that,

\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{i}-f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\Omega(f_{\hat{\theta}})\leq\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{i}-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\Omega(f^{*}).

(B.24)

From data the data generating mechanism we have that,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{NT}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})+{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\Omega(f_{\hat{\theta}})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})+{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\Omega(f^{*}),\end{split}

(B.25)

upon simplifying it further we have,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}&+\|{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+2\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i},f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\rangle\\
&\leq\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left[\Omega(f^{*})-\Omega(f_{\hat{\theta}})\right],\end{split}

(B.26)

which evaluates to,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}&\|f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{2}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i},f_{\hat{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\rangle+\lambda\left[\Omega(f^{*})-\Omega(f_{\hat{\theta}})\right].\end{split}

(B.27)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Next we link empirical recovery loss with population loss
for a fixed hypothesis class. We will directly apply
the result from
Ziemann et al. (2023)
.

Lemma B.2
.

(
Ziemann et al., 2023
, Lemma F.2)

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}
be
N
iid samples. Define

{\mathsf{c}ond}_{{\cal F}_{\theta}}:=\max_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\max_{t\in[T]}\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}\right]}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]}.

(B.28)

For any
\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}
and

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)\leq{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\frac{-N}{4{\mathsf{cond}}_{{\cal F}_{\theta}}^{2}}\right).

(B.29)

Lemma
B.2

Although
Ziemann et al. (2023)
provides a uniform bound, it
is restricted to finite hypothesis classes. The work
of
Ziemann et al. (2024)

focuses on Orlicz spaces and non-realizable settings. In contrast,

Lemma B.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}
be
N
iid samples such
that for a convex set
{\cal C}_{x}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}}^{\otimes T}
it holds
true that
\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{i\in[N]}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}\right)\leq\delta_{{\cal C}_{x}}
.

{\mathsf{c}ond}_{{\cal F}_{\theta}}:=\max_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\max_{t\in[T]}\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}\right]}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]},

(B.30)

and for a fixed
f^{*}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}

D_{2}({\cal C}_{x}):=\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\left|\|f_{\theta}-f^{*}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}-\|f_{\theta}\circ{\cal P}_{{\cal C}_{x}}-f^{*}\circ{\cal P}_{{\cal C}_{x}}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\right|.

(B.31)

If for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\cal C}_{x}
we have
that for some metric
d
in
{\cal F}_{\theta}

\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{\theta}:\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}})\|\leq K_{{\cal C}_{x}}d(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(B.32)

and
\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}})\|_{2}\leq B_{{\cal C}_{x}}
.
Then for any
u\geq 0
we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq u+D_{2}({\cal C})\right)\\
&\leq{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\ln({\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/4K_{{\cal C}_{x}}B_{{\cal C}_{x}}))-\frac{N}{4{\sf cond}_{{\cal F}_{\theta}}^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(B.33)

Proof.

We proceed with convex projection analysis similar to

Tadipatri et al. (2025)
. Denote

Z_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{z}}_{1}^{i}&\cdots&{\mathbf{z}}_{T}^{i}\end{bmatrix}
and define

g_{\theta}([Z_{i}]_{i\in[N]}):=-\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(B.34)

Now we estimate the Lipschtiz constant
g_{\theta}

in
{\cal F}_{\theta}
.

\begin{split}g_{\theta}([Z_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i}]_{i\in[N]})&=-\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(B.35)

From the problem setting we obtain that

\begin{split}|g_{\theta}([Z_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i}]_{i\in[N]})|&\leq 4K_{{\cal C}}B_{{\cal C}}d(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).\end{split}

(B.36)

In the similar analysis as Lemma
B.4
we
obtain for any
u\geq 0

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([X_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u+D_{2}({\cal C})\right)\leq{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C}))\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(g_{\theta^{\prime}}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))>0\right).\end{split}

(B.37)

From Lemma
B.3
we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([X_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u+D_{2}({\cal C})\right)\leq{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/4K_{{\cal C}_{x}}B_{{\cal C}_{x}}){\mathsf{exp}}\left(-\frac{N}{4{\sf cond}_{{\cal F}_{\theta}}^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(B.38)

∎

Up to this point we have linked empirical and population

).

Lemma B.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{v}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{1:t}
is sub-gaussian vector with proxy variance
\sigma^{2}I

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}
, where
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}
.
Consider the parameter class
{\cal F}_{\theta}
,
and corresponding function
f_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}

for all
\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}
. Let
f^{*}
be some fixed realizable function.
Suppose we have access to
N
iid samples
({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})
such that there is a convex set

{\cal C}\subseteq{{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}}^{\otimes T}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{y}}}^{\otimes T}
which satisfies
P(\cap_{i\in[N]}\cap_{t\in[T]}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})\notin{\cal C})\leq\delta_{{\cal C}}
.
Define a real-valued function

M(f,[({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[T]}]_{i\in[N]}):=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-f({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\rangle,

(B.39)

projection gap

D({\cal C}):=\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[M(f_{\theta}-f^{*},[({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[T]}]_{i\in[N]})-M(f_{\theta}-f^{*},{\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[T]}]_{i\in[N]}))\right]\right|.

(B.40)

If for all
(({\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{v}}_{t})_{t\in[T]})\in{\cal C}
for some metric
d
defined in
{\cal F}_{\theta}
it holds true that

\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{\theta};\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|\leq K_{{\cal C}}d(\theta,\theta^{\prime}),

(B.41)

and

\max\{\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2},\|{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\|_{2}\}\leq B_{{\cal C}}.

(B.42)

Then we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}M(f_{\theta}-f^{*}[({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[T]}]_{i\in[N]})>u+D({\cal C})\right)\\
&\leq\delta_{{\cal C}}+{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\ln\left({\cal N}\left({\cal F}_{\theta},d,\frac{u}{6K_{{\cal C}}B_{{\cal C}}}\right)\right)-\frac{NT}{\sigma^{2}}u\right).\end{split}

(B.43)

Proof.

The proof is similar to Proposition F.2 in
Ziemann et al. (2023)
. However, we follow the style of

Tadipatri et al. (2025)
proof analysis via projecting onto convex sets.

Denote
Z_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{z}}_{1}^{i}&\cdots&{\mathbf{z}}_{T}^{i}\end{bmatrix}
, and
W_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{i}&\cdots&{\mathbf{w}}_{T}^{i}\end{bmatrix}
. Define,

g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle.

(B.44)

We begin to estimate the local Lipschitz constant of
g_{\theta}
in
{\cal C}
. First
choose any
[Z,W]\in{\cal C}
since we have,

\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\cal F}_{\theta};\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|\leq K_{{\cal C}}d(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(B.45)

Then for any
[Z_{i},W_{i}]\in{\cal C}
we have,

\displaystyle\left|{g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-8.0pt\big|\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle

(B.46)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\hskip-8.0pt\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle\big|,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip-8.0pt\big|\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\hskip-8.0pt\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i}),f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\rangle\big|,

(B.47)

First we apply triangular inequality and then Cauchy-Schwartz inequality then obtain,

\begin{split}\left|{g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})}\right|\leq\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}&\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\|\times\Big[\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\|\\
&+\|{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{i})\right]\|\Big].\end{split}

(B.48)

Since
\forall[Z,W]\in{\cal C}
, and
\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}
it is the case that

\max\{\|f_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})\|,\|{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\|\}\leq B_{{\cal C}}
, via this assumption we obtain,

\left|{g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})}\right|\leq\underbrace{3K_{{\cal C}}B_{{\cal C}}}_{=:K^{\prime}({\cal C})}d(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(B.49)

Suppose that,
\theta^{*}
is the maximizer of
g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})
then
\exists\theta_{0}\in{\cal N}_{\epsilon}({\cal F}_{\theta},d)

such that
d(\theta^{*},\theta_{0})\leq\epsilon
therefore we have,

-K^{\prime}({\cal C})\epsilon\leq\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})-g_{\theta_{0}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})\leq K^{\prime}({\cal C})\epsilon.

(B.50)

By the monotonicity of the probability measure we have,

\mathbb{P}\left(g_{\theta_{0}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})+K^{\prime}({\cal C})\epsilon>u\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u\right),

(B.51)

further more by applying supremum over all the
\epsilon
-net then we have,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta_{0}\in{\cal N}_{\epsilon}({\cal F}_{\theta},d)}g_{\theta_{0}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u-K^{\prime}({\cal C})\epsilon\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u\right).

(B.52)

Now set
\epsilon=u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C})
then we have,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta_{0}\in{\cal N}_{u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C})}({\cal F}_{\theta},d)}g_{\theta_{0}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u/2\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u\right),

(B.53)

by sub-additivity of probability measure we have,

\sum_{\theta_{0}\in{\cal N}_{u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C})}({\cal F}_{\theta},d)}\mathbb{P}\left(g_{\theta_{0}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u/2\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u\right).

(B.54)

Now choose a fixed
\theta^{\prime}\in{\cal N}_{u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C})}({\cal F}_{\theta},d)
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u\right)\leq{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C}))\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(g_{\theta^{\prime}}([Z_{i},W_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u/2\right).

(B.55)

Observe that for
[X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}
(not necessarily in the convex set
{\cal C}
) we have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\big(&g_{\theta}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]})+\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]})\right]=g_{\theta}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))+\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))\right]\big)\\
&\geq 1-\delta_{{\cal C}}.\end{split}

(B.56)

By our assumptions we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]})\right]\leq B_{r}.

(B.57)

Therefore we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\big(g_{\theta}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]})\leq g_{\theta}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))+B_{r}\big)\geq 1-\delta_{{\cal C}}.\end{split}

(B.58)

First we apply supremum over
\theta
, then set
[Z_{i},V_{i}]={\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}])
. Finally by
combining Equations (
B.55
), and (
B.58
) we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}g_{\theta}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]})>u+D({\cal C})\right)\leq{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C}))\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(g_{\theta^{\prime}}({\cal P}_{{\cal C}}([X_{i},V_{i}]_{i\in[N]}))>u/2\right).\end{split}

(B.59)

By replacing all the variables back we have,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u+D({\cal C})\Big)\leq\delta_{{\cal C}}\\
&+{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C}))\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u/2\right).\end{split}

(B.60)

At this stage we have linked supremum process with a fixed parameter in
\epsilon
-net of
{\cal F}_{\theta}
.
Next we compute the probability of the event in the right side term of Equation (
B.60
) for a fixed
\theta^{\prime}
.
By applying Cramér-Chernoff bound for any
a\geq 0
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathsf{exp}}\left(a\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle\right)\right].\end{split}

(B.61)

By independence we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathsf{exp}}\left(a\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle\right)\right].\end{split}

(B.62)

Recall the martingale property of conditional
independence for a random variables
a_{1:T}

we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{t}\right)\times\mathbb{E}\left[a_{T}\mid a_{1:(T-1)}\right]\right]
. In the similar spirit we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[{\mathsf{exp}}\left(a\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle\right)\\
&\times\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\frac{a}{NT}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{T}^{i}\rangle-\frac{a}{NT}\|\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{i})\right]\|_{2}^{2}\right)\mid({\mathbf{x}}_{1:T-1}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}_{1:T-1}^{i})\right]\Big].\\
\end{split}

(B.63)

From our assumption we have that
{\mathbf{v}}_{T}^{i}|{\mathbf{x}}_{1:T}^{i}
is a sub-Gaussian vector then for any
vector
{\mathbf{y}}
, and
\lambda\geq 0
we have that
\mathbb{E}[{\mathsf{exp}}(\lambda\langle{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{v}}_{T}^{i}\rangle)\mid{\mathbf{x}}_{1:T}^{i}]\leq{\mathsf{exp}}(0.5\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2})
. Then applying this property to earlier conditional expectation
we obtain,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Big[{\mathsf{exp}}\left(a\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle\right)\\

(B.64)

Now repeated apply these argument for all
t\in[T-1]
then we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2N^{2}T^{2}}\sigma^{2}\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{a}{NT}\|\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right]\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\
&=e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a^{2}}{2N^{2}T^{2}}\sigma^{2}\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{a}{NT}\|\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right]\|_{2}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(B.65)

Recall that the choice of
a
was arbitrary in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
we set
a=\frac{2NT}{\sigma^{2}}
then we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u\right)\\
&\leq e^{-au}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a^{2}}{2N^{2}T^{2}}\sigma^{2}\|f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{a}{NT}\|\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\
&={\mathsf{exp}}\left(-\frac{2NT}{\sigma^{2}}u\right).\end{split}

(B.66)

Now by re-scaling
u
to
u/2
we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u/2\right)\leq{\mathsf{exp}}\left(-\frac{NT}{\sigma^{2}}u\right).

(B.67)

Finally we combine Equation (
B.60
), and (
B.67
) to obtain,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\theta\in{\cal F}_{\theta}}\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i}),{\mathbf{v}}_{t}^{i}-\left[f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})-f^{*}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{i})\right]\rangle>u+D({\cal C})\right)\\
&\leq\delta_{{\cal C}}+{\mathsf{exp}}\left(\ln\left(|{\cal N}({\cal F}_{\theta},d,u/2K^{\prime}({\cal C}))|\right)-\frac{uT}{\sigma^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(B.68)

∎

Appendix C
Related works

The word “
System Identification
” was first coined in
Zadeh (1962)
.
SysID deals with determination of outputs of a/many system given the inputs based on previous observations
of inputs and outputs. In this section, we disscus only the key relevant works while
the readers are referred to
Aström and Eykhoff (1971)
;
De Schutter (2000)
for an extensive survey.
Estimation of impulse response of a linear system can be re-written as a least squares
problem whose solution is obtained Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse of inputs
De Schutter (2000)
.
However, with classical methods from impulse response estimation of system parameters
required the knowledge of true-order of the system.

Minimal-State Realization.
Due to the basis invariance of linear system parameters, and unknown
system order, it has been
a great interest to identify structured realization of system parameters. In the seminal work of
Ho and Kalman (1966)

provided an algorithm to estimate lowest order realization of
(A,B,C)
from the Markov parameters alone.
This Ho-Kalman procedure requires to compute SVD of Hankel matrix with Markov parameters. This scales
quadratically with the trajectory length. N4SID method
(
Van Overschee and De Moor, 1994
)
performs
both least-squares to estimate the Markov parameters, and Ho-Kalman
procedure to estimate low-order system parameters. Similar SysID has been
viewed in frequency response by
McKelvey et al. (1996)
.
As pointed out earlier,
these methods does not scale for longer trajectories. There have been many works in literature under
name
Subspace-Identification
(see
Qin (2006)
for detailed survey).

Convex Relaxations.
From the optimization lens of low-order
SysID is posed as Hankel matrix-rank minimization problem
(
Fazel et al., 2001
)
,
while this NP-HARD to solve in general. Influential work of
Recht et al. (2008)

proposed convex relaxations of matrix-rank minimization via
a nuclear-norm based regularization procedure that recovery guarantees under
certain conditions. This procedure was then adapted many linear SysID problems
in
Mohan and Fazel (2010)
;
Shah et al. (2012)
;
Fazel et al. (2013)
;
Liu et al. (2013)
;
Chiuso et al. (2013)
;
Pillonetto et al. (2016)
;
Sun et al. (2022)
and for non-linear SysID in
Markovsky (2017)
.
Equivalent convex relaxation were also performed in

Smith (2014)
;
Honarpisheh et al. (2024)
. While these convex relaxation circumvents
the hardness of the problem, however, still necessitates the SVD computation
for the Hankel nuclear-norms.

Nonconvex Relaxations.
To circumvent the SVD computation authors
of
Campi et al. (2015)
re-forumulated the Hankel-rank minimization
to estimating the amplitude, frequency, and phase of the system response
making the problem nonconvex and computationally faster. However, the adopted algorithm
is greedy and have only probabilistic global optimality guarantees. For faster learning of
system parameters, Deep Learning (DL) methods very adopted in
Pillonetto et al. (2025)
.
However, the main critique of optimality certificates for DL make it not realiable control
applications. But in the literature of Machine Learning there are nonconvex problems whose
global optimality certificates are well established
Haeffele and Vidal (2017)
. For instance,
the necessity of SVD computation
for nuclear norm computation was greatly reduced by re-parameterzing the matrix
with a product of left and right matrices commonly known as
BM factorization
(
Burer and Monteiro, 2003
)
. By doing this, the
nuclear-norm can written as variational minimization of sums of square Frobenous norms of
left and right matrices
(
Recht et al., 2010
;
Giampouras et al., 2020
)
. This trick
has been adopted to many structured matrix factorization problems in
Bach (2013)
;
Haeffele and Vidal (2020)
.
These works form a basis for our proposed reformulations for SysID.

Statistical Analysis.
Classical works
used maximum likelihood estimation to perform SysID
that provided asymptotic convergences

(
Lennart and Caines, 1980
)
. However, they required
the prior information about the covariances
of the system parameters that posits a limitation
for unknown SysID.
Simchowitz et al. (2018)

analyzed recovery error rates but their
analysis was limited to fully observed system.

Tsiamis and Pappas (2019)
, and
Oymak and Ozay (2019)

were the one of the first ones to provide error rates
for partially observed systems. While
all the earlier mentioned works analyzed SysID without
the consideration of low-orderness.
Sun et al. (2022)

studied both optimization and statistical aspects
of low-order SysID, however the estimated sample
complexities scaled badly with the trajectory length.
Later work on SVD truncation of Hankel matrices yielded a
tighter error rates and sample complexities

(
Lee, 2022
)
. We compare our estimates statistical error rates and sample complexities
with earlier mentioned works in Table

.
