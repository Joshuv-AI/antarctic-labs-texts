Predicting drug solubility in binary solvent mixtures using graph convolutional networks: a comprehensive deep learning approach
Authors: Masoud Amiri, Farnaz Khaleseh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prediction of drug solubility represents a fundamental challenge in pharmaceutical development, with traditional experimental methods proving both time-intensive and resource-demanding. This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) for predicting drug solubility in binary solvent mixtures across diverse temperature ranges. We used an extensive dataset comprising 27,000 solubility measurements encompassing 123 small-molecule solutes, 44 solvents, and 110 binary solvent combinations measured across varied temperatures (273–373 K). Our GCN architecture incorporates multi-head attention mechanisms, hierarchical molecular representation learning, and sophisticated pooling strategies to capture complex molecular interactions. The proposed GCN model achieved exceptional performance with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.28 units, demonstrating a 15% improvement over traditional machine learning approaches. Through comprehensive ablation studies and attention visualization analyses, we demonstrate that GCNs excel particularly in modeling structure-solubility relationships for pharmaceutically relevant compounds. Prospective validation using four drug molecules confirmed the model’s predictive reliability, with experimental verification yielding MAE < 0.5 S for compounds structurally similar to training data. This research establishes GCNs as powerful tools for accelerating pharmaceutical formulation development, potentially reducing experimental requirements by 60–80% while providing interpretable molecular insights through attention mechanisms. This work demonstrates that thoughtful integration of established graph neural network techniques, specifically optimized for binary solvent systems, can substantially advance computational solubility prediction for pharmaceutical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28272-3.Keywords: Graph convolutional networks, Drug solubility, Binary solvents, Deep learning, Pharmaceutical informatics, Molecular property predictionSubject terms: Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Drug solubility constitutes a critical physicochemical parameter that fundamentally influences multiple aspects of pharmaceutical development, including drug discovery1–3, analytical chemistry4,5, and formulation design6–8. The accurate determination of solubility typically relies on established thermodynamic and kinetic methodologies9,10, where thermodynamic approaches measure equilibrium concentrations between dissolved and solid phases, while kinetic methods determine precipitation onset concentrations11.

Despite methodological advances aimed at enhancing experimental throughput12,13, significant challenges persist, particularly when dealing with high-value pharmaceutical compounds or when material availability is constrained. These limitations have catalyzed research into computational alternatives, with machine learning and deep learning approaches emerging as promising solutions14,15.

The majority of existing machine learning models focus on predicting solubility in single solvents16–25, primarily targeting aqueous systems due to abundant training data availability. While such solvent- specific models typically achieve higher accuracy by avoiding inter-solvent variability, their applicability remains limited to the specific solvents for which they were developed. Recent efforts have expanded toward multi-solvent systems, with notable contributions from Vasiliou et al.26, who compiled 714 solubility measurements across 75 solutes and 49 solvents, achieving MAE values ranging from 0.39 to 0.58 Yi et al.27. further advanced this field with a larger dataset of 5,081 measurements covering 266 compounds in 123 solvents at various temperatures, resulting in an optimized model with MAE of 0.47 S.

Pharmaceutical applications frequently require understanding of drug behavior in solvent mixtures28–32, which offer enhanced formulation flexibility through composition and ratio modifications33,34. However, the vast combinatorial space of solvent compositions, ratios, and temperatures significantly complicates both experimental measurements and computational modeling approaches.

Among deep learning approach, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) represent a paradigm shift in molecular property prediction by directly learning from molecular graph representations rather than relying on hand-crafted descriptors36,37. Unlike traditional approaches that immediately flatten molecular information into fixed-size descriptor vectors, GCNs preserve the inherent graph structure during the feature learning phase, where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds as edges. Through iterative message-passing operations, GCNs learn hierarchical representations that capture the graph topology before the final readout operation produces a fixed- size molecular embedding for downstream prediction38,39. The mathematical foundation of GCNs builds upon spectral graph theory, where the convolution operation on graphs is defined through the graph Laplacian matrix40.

This research introduces a thorough assessment of GCNs aimed at predicting drug solubility in binary solvent mixtures over a wide range of temperatures for the first time. We used a large dataset that includes 27,000 solubility measurements, which cover 123 small-molecule solutes, 44 solvents, and 110 binary solvent combinations, all measured at various temperatures41. This study demonstrates that Graph Convolutional Networks can provide improved performance for drug solubility prediction in binary solvent systems, with 15.2% reduction in MAE compared to traditional descriptor-based approaches on the evaluated dataset41.

While this study utilizes the comprehensive dataset compiled by Bao et al.41, our work makes several distinct methodological and analytical contributions to the field of computational solubility prediction:

Architectural innovation Bao et al.41 employed descriptor-based machine learning approaches (LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest) utilizing 362 hand-crafted molecular features derived from RDKit and MACCS fingerprints. In contrast, our work introduces a graph neural network architecture that learns molecular representations directly from graph structure, eliminating dependence on pre-defined descriptors. This fundamental shift from descriptor-based to structure-based modeling enables the capture of complex structural relationships that may not be adequately represented by traditional molecular descriptors.

Performance improvements Our GCN architecture achieves MAE of 0.28 LogS compared to 0.33 LogS reported for LightGBM in Ref 41, representing a 15.2% improvement. This performance gain is particularly pronounced for structurally complex molecules, where we observe 22.2% improvement for compounds in the highest complexity quartile, suggesting that graph-based representations excel precisely where traditional descriptor-based methods face limitations.

Interpretability through attention mechanisms Unlike the black-box nature of gradient boosting methods, our multi-head attention mechanism provides interpretable insights into molecular features driving solubility predictions. The attention weight analysis reveals that hydrogen bond donors/acceptors receive highest attention (0.45 ± 0.08), validating that the model has learned chemically meaningful patterns consistent with established structure–property relationships.

Methodological contributions We introduce several architectural innovations including: (1) hybrid pooling strategy combining mean, max, and attention-weighted aggregation to capture multi-scale molecular features; (2) cross-attention mechanism for integrating experimental conditions with molecular embeddings; (3) Monte Carlo dropout for uncertainty quantification, enabling confidence-based prediction stratification essential for pharmaceutical decision-making.

Comprehensive validation and analysis Our work provides systematic ablation studies identifying the contribution of each architectural component, performance stratification across molecular complexity tiers and experimental conditions, and prospective experimental validation demonstrating practical reliability.

These contributions collectively demonstrate that graph neural networks represent a valuable advance over descriptor-based approaches for molecular property prediction in complex solvent systems, while acknowledging that further validation across independent datasets is necessary to establish broader generalizability.

Graph convolutional network architecture

Molecular graph representation

Our GCN approach represents molecules as undirected graphs , where denotes atoms and represents chemical bonds. Each vertex is characterized by a 13-dimensional feature vector including atomic number, period, hybridization state (sp, sp2, sp3), formal charge, valence electrons, aromaticity, chirality indicators, and hydrogen bond donor/acceptor properties. Edge features include 6-dimensional vectors encoding bond type (single, double, triple, aromatic), bond conjugation, stereochemistry, and ring membership indicators.

Multi-layer GCN architecture

The architecture as shown in Fig. 1 employs a four-layer GCN design, comparable in depth to other established molecular property prediction models42,43, that processes molecular graphs through sequential feature transformations. Graph Convolutional Networks42 learn molecular representations by iteratively aggregating information from neighboring atoms through trainable weight matrices. Our implementation employs a four-layer GCN architecture with carefully optimized dimension specifications: 13 → 128 → 256 → 256 → 128. This expansion-consolidation pattern allows the network to first enrich representational capacity to capture complex molecular patterns, then distill these representations into the most salient features. For comprehensive theoretical foundations of graph convolution operations, we refer readers to Kipf & Welling42.

GCN architecture implementation

Molecular graphs were constructed using RDKit’s molecular graph representation, with atoms as nodes and bonds as edges. Node features (13-dimensional) included atomic number, period, hybridization state, formal charge, valence electrons, aromaticity, chirality, and hydrogen bonding properties. Edge features (6- dimensional) encoded bond type, conjugation, stereochemistry, and ring membership.

The GCN layers were implemented using PyTorch Geometric’s GCNConv modules with custom dimension specifications (13 → 128 → 256 → 256 → 128). The multi-head attention mechanism utilized 8 parallel attention heads, implemented following the Graph Attention Network framework44 with modifications for our specific pooling strategy. Global pooling combined PyTorch Geometric’s built-in global_mean_pool and global_max_pool functions with a custom attention-weighted pooling implementation.

Training configuration

Batch size of 256 was selected to optimize GPU memory utilization. Mixed precision training (FP16) was employed to accelerate computation while maintaining numerical stability. Gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) prevented gradient explosion. The Adam optimizer with β1 = 0.9, β2= 0.999, ε = 10−8 was used throughout. Learning rate scheduling followed exponential decay (Eq. 6) with initial rate 0.001, decaying by factor 0.95 every 50 epochs.

Computational cost analysis

Training time for the full GCN model was 8.5 GPU hours on A100 hardware (approximately 68 CPU hours equivalent). Traditional ML baselines required 0.8–2.1 CPU hours for training. Inference time per 1000 samples was 15.2 s on GPU (68.4 s on CPU) for GCN, compared to 1.8–3.4 s for traditional ML methods. Peak GPU memory consumption during training was 12.3 GB, with 4.2 GB required for inference.

Molecular complexity analysis

The stratified analysis of model performance across molecular complexity tiers reveals a compelling pattern that underscores the fundamental advantages of graph-based molecular representation. The complexity scoring framework (Eq. 7) integrates four critical molecular descriptors with carefully calibrated weights: molecular weight (MW, ) capturing overall molecular size, rotatable bond count (RBC, ) representing conformational flexibility, topological polar surface area (TPSA, ) quantifying polarity and hydrogen bonding capacity, and number of aromatic rings (NAR, ) indicating structural rigidity and π-electron systems. When molecules are stratified by this composite complexity score, a striking trend emerges: the Graph Convolutional Network’s performance advantage over LightGBM progressively amplifies with increasing molecular complexity. For low-complexity molecules (below 25th percentile, ), the GCN achieves MAE of 0.24 compared to LightGBM’s 0.28 , representing a modest but statistically significant 14.3% improvement. This advantage expands to 15.6% for medium-complexity molecules (25-75th percentile, ), where the GCN attains 0.27 versus 0.32 for LightGBM. Most remarkably, for high-complexity molecules (above 75th percentile, n = 6,750), the performance differential reaches 22.2%, with the GCN maintaining reasonable accuracy (MAE = 0.35 ) while LightGBM deteriorates substantially (MAE = 0.45 ). This monotonic increase in relative performance improvement—from 14.3% to 15.6% to 22.2%—demonstrates that the GCN architecture excels precisely where traditional descriptor-based methods falter: in capturing the intricate structural relationships, long-range dependencies, and emergent properties that characterize complex pharmaceutical molecules such as macrocycles, natural products, and multi-functional drug candidates. The consistent sample sizes across complexity tiers (6,750 for low and high, 13,500 for medium) ensure statistical robustness of these comparisons, while the absolute MAE increase with complexity for both models (0.24 → 0.27 → 0.35 for GCN, 0.28 → 0.32 → 0.45 for LightGBM) reflects the inherent challenge of predicting solubility for structurally complex molecules, making the GCN’s maintained relative accuracy particularly noteworthy for pharmaceutical applications where molecular complexity is often unavoidable.

Collectively, these polarity-related descriptors account for the majority of predictive power in the model.

Molecular weight (0.78) and LogP (0.75) complete the top five features, representing fundamental size and lipophilicity descriptors. The significant contribution of molecular weight reflects its relationship with cavity formation energy and entropic costs of dissolution, as formalized in the general solubility equation49,50. LogP’s importance validates its fundamental role in capturing the hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance that governs solubility across diverse solvent systems51.

Aromatic rings (0.71) occupy the sixth position, reflecting their dual nature in solubility phenomena. The moderate importance of aromatic systems reflects their contribution through π-π stacking interactions with aromatic solvent molecules and their effect on molecular hydrophobicity52,53. The feature importance ranking demonstrates that the model recognizes both the beneficial π-π interactions and the hydrophobic character of aromatic systems.

Secondary features and structural complexity indicators

The middle-tier features (ranks 7–15) encompass structural complexity and molecular architecture descriptors that provide important contextual information for solubility prediction. Rotatable bonds (0.68) capture molecular flexibility and conformational entropy contributions to dissolution thermodynamics54, while formal charge (0.64) affects ionic interactions and solvation energetics. Heteroatom content (0.62) contributes to dipolar moments and hydrogen bonding capacity, complementing the primary polarity descriptors.

Ring count (0.58), largest ring size (0.55), and SP3 carbon fraction (0.52) indicate that the model recognizes the importance of structural diversity and cyclic constraints in determining solubility behavior. The presence of acidic (0.49) and basic groups (0.46) influences solubility through ionization equilibria and pH-dependent dissolution behavior55, though their lower ranking suggests the model captures these effects partially through the higher-ranked polarity descriptors.

The hierarchical importance ranking learned by our GCN—prioritizing H-bonding > polarity > size > flexibility—mirrors the established hierarchy in medicinal chemistry and physical organic chemistry32,46–55. This alignment between machine-learned patterns and fundamental chemical principles provides strong evidence that the model has captured meaningful structure–property relationships rather than spurious correlations. Feature attribution analysis reveals that our GCN has independently learned to prioritize molecular descriptors in an order consistent with decades of solubility research, validating both the model’s chemical interpretability and the robustness of learned representations.

Confidence analysis

Following Gal & Ghahramani (2016)56, we employ Monte Carlo dropout during inference to quantify prediction uncertainty. This approach approximates Bayesian inference by maintaining dropout activation during test time, effectively sampling from an approximate posterior distribution over model weights. The method has been successfully applied to molecular property prediction57, providing calibrated uncertainty estimates essential for risk-aware deployment.

Differentiation from prior GCN-based molecular property prediction

While graph neural networks have been applied to various molecular property prediction tasks, previous GCN-based solubility studies have primarily focused on single-solvent systems. However, these approaches do not address the substantially more complex challenge of binary solvent mixtures where: (1) Solvent–solvent interactions must be modeled alongside solute–solvent interactions, introducing non-linear mixing effects and preferential solvation phenomena not present in single-solvent systems; (2) Composition-dependent solvation requires the model to capture how molecular features differentially contribute to solubility across the entire composition space (0–100% organic solvent); (3) Temperature-dependent behavior across broad ranges (273–373 K) demands modeling of entropic contributions and conformational effects that vary with both temperature and solvent composition; (4) Combinatorial complexity of 110 binary solvent systems with continuous composition variations creates a vastly larger and more heterogeneous prediction space than single- solvent benchmarks.

Our framework specifically addresses these challenges through:

Cross-attention integration of experimental conditions enabling dynamic, learned modulation of molecular features based on temperature and binary solvent composition (Table 10 shows 10.7–20.6% improvement over concatenation);—Systematic validation across diverse splitting strategies including solvent-based splits (withholding entire solvent systems) and temperature extrapolation (testing beyond training temperature range);—Comprehensive performance stratification across solvent compositions (water-rich, mixed, organic-rich) and temperatures;—Uncertainty quantification through Monte Carlo Dropout with confidence-based deployment guidelines, enabling risk-adjusted decision-making for pharmaceutical applications.

Key Findings:

Molecular descriptors provide superior generalization

The descriptor-based approach achieves lowest error on challenging solvent-based splits (MAE = 0.33 vs. 0.41 for composition-only and 0.47 for embeddings), demonstrating effective generalization to entirely novel binary solvent combinations not encountered during training. This 19.5% improvement over composition-only baseline and 29.8% improvement over learned embeddings confirms that explicit physicochemical encoding is essential for pharmaceutical applications requiring predictions in unexplored solvent systems.

Learned embeddings overfit to training systems

While embeddings achieve slightly lower random split error (MAE = 0.25), they exhibit worst performance on solvent-based splits (MAE = 0.47, 88% degradation from random split), indicating severe overfitting to specific binary systems present in training data. The learned 64-dimensional vectors capture system-specific idiosyncrasies rather than generalizable physicochemical principles, limiting their utility for pharmaceutical formulation development where novel solvent combinations are frequently explored.

Explicit encoding enables interpretability

Descriptor-based approach allows systematic analysis of which solvent properties drive predictions:

In aqueous systems (> 70% water): hydrogen bonding descriptors (HBD, HBA) receive highest feature importance (0.42 ± 0.08).

In organic-rich systems (< 30% water): LogP and polarity parameters dominate (0.38 ± 0.06).

Across all systems: HBA shows strongest correlation with prediction accuracy (Pearson r = 0.71, p < 0.001).

In contrast, learned embedding dimensions show no interpretable correlation with known physicochemical properties, providing no scientific insight into solvation mechanisms.

Minimal computational overhead

Adding solvent descriptors increases inference time by only 8% (16.4 ms vs. 15.2 ms per 1000 samples on NVIDIA A100 GPU) while providing substantial scientific benefits. Training time increases by 12% (9.6 GPU hours vs. 8.5 h) due to larger condition MLP input layer (13 vs. 3 features), but this overhead is negligible compared to experimental validation costs ($5,000–10,000 per measurement).

Statistical validation

Paired t-tests comparing descriptor-based approach against both alternatives show statistically significant improvements for solvent-based splits: vs. composition-only (t = 4.32, p = 0.003), vs. embeddings (t = 5.87, p < 0.001). For random splits, differences are not statistically significant (p > 0.05), confirming that improved generalization does not sacrifice interpolation performance.

Practical implementation and industrial impact

Confidence-based decision framework and computational resources

The practical deployment of the GCN model is underpinned by a sophisticated confidence-based decision framework that stratifies predictions into three actionable tiers, enabling risk-adjusted implementation strategies across pharmaceutical development workflows. High-confidence predictions, comprising 78% of all cases and achieving 92% reliability within ± 0.3 units, provide sufficient accuracy for direct integration into critical decision-making processes including lead optimization, formulation screening, and process design without additional experimental validation. Medium-confidence predictions (18% of cases) maintain useful predictive capability for preliminary screening applications, serving as effective filters to reduce experimental search space while acknowledging the need for subsequent validation before committing significant resources. Low-confidence predictions (4% of cases) serve a crucial safety function by explicitly flagging molecular structures where the model’s predictive capability is limited, directing these cases toward experimental characterization or alternative computational methods, thereby preventing costly errors from overconfident predictions. This tiered approach transforms the model from a binary prediction tool into a comprehensive decision support system that communicates both predictions and associated uncertainties, enabling pharmaceutical scientists to optimize resource allocation based on prediction reliability. The computational resource analysis reveals a clear trade-off between predictive accuracy and computational efficiency, with the GCN requiring 8.5 GPU hours for training compared to 0.8–2.1 h for traditional methods, and inference times of 15.2 ms per sample versus 1.8–3.4 ms for tree-based models. Despite these increased computational demands, the 15.2% improvement in prediction accuracy translates to substantial economic value in pharmaceutical applications where each experimental validation costs $5000–10,000, making the additional computational investment economically justified for projects involving high-value compounds or extensive screening campaigns.

Industrial implementation and validation

The industrial deployment case study reported by Bao et al.40 across 28 pharmaceutical compounds over 12 months demonstrated the practical utility of machine learning approaches for solubility prediction in pharmaceutical development. Their work documented 68% reduction in development time and 72% reduction in screening expenses using descriptor-based ML models (LightGBM, XGBoost). While this validation was conducted with their baseline models rather than our GCN architecture, it provides important context for the potential impact of computational solubility prediction in industrial settings. Building upon their validation could potentially offer additional advantages through its superior predictive accuracy (15.2% improvement in MAE), though industrial-scale validation of the GCN approach remains as future work requiring collaboration with pharmaceutical partners.

Discussion and conclusions

This work establishes a comprehensive computational framework for drug solubility prediction in binary solvent mixtures, demonstrating that thoughtful integration and domain-specific optimization of established graph neural network techniques can achieve substantial improvements over traditional descriptor-based approaches. Our primary contribution lies not in algorithmic novelty but in the systematic development, rigorous validation, and practical deployment guidelines for applying graph-based deep learning to this challenging pharmaceutical problem. The 15.2% improvement in MAE over state-of-the-art gradient boosting methods, combined with interpretable attention mechanisms, calibrated uncertainty estimates, and comprehensive generalization analysis, provides the pharmaceutical community with a validated, deployable tool rather than a proof-of-concept demonstration.

Honest Assessment of Solvent Encoding Evolution: Our initial implementation used only composition ratios (temperature, solvent mole fractions) without explicit solvent identity encoding, limiting both generalization to novel solvent combinations and scientific interpretability of solvent-specific effects. During peer review, careful examination of our implementation revealed this fundamental limitation. We subsequently developed and validated a molecular descriptor-based encoding approach (LogP, polarity, hydrogen bonding descriptors, molecular weight), which improved solvent-based split performance by 19.5% (MAE 0.41 → 0.33) while enabling interpretable analysis of physicochemical factors driving predictions. This experience reinforces an important methodological principle: while large-scale implicit pattern learning can achieve reasonable interpolation performance within covered chemical space, explicit physicochemical feature engineering remains essential for scientific interpretability and extrapolation to novel systems—critical requirements for pharmaceutical formulation development. We recommend that future molecular property prediction studies for multi-component systems prioritize transparent encoding of component physicochemical properties over purely learned representations, particularly when deployment to unexplored chemical space is anticipated. The ablation studies in Section "Condition-dependent attention analysis and substructure interpretability" quantitatively demonstrate that this approach provides superior generalization without sacrificing interpolation accuracy.

Comparison with prior GCN-based molecular property prediction frameworks

Our framework builds upon but substantially extends previous applications of graph neural networks to molecular property prediction. Key differentiating factors include: (1) Complexity of Prediction Task: Prior GCN applications to solubility prediction have focused primarily on aqueous solubility—a single-condition prediction task with abundant training data (> 10,000 compounds). In contrast, binary solvent solubility requires modeling across 110 solvent combinations, continuous composition ranges, and broad temperature variations (273–373 K), representing a substantially more complex prediction space with multifactorial dependencies. (2) Integration of Experimental Conditions: Previous molecular GNN architectures typically treat experimental conditions (if considered at all) as simple concatenated features or ignore them entirely for fixed-condition predictions. Our cross-attention mechanism enables learned, dynamic modulation of molecular features based on experimental context—for example, automatically emphasizing hydrogen bond donors in aqueous systems, aromatic rings in organic- rich solvents, or conformational flexibility at elevated temperatures. Ablation studies (Table 10) demonstrate this approach yields 10.7–20.6% improvements over concatenation, with largest gains under challenging conditions. (3) Comprehensive Generalization Assessment: Prior GCN studies typically report performance under random data splits, which primarily assess interpolation within covered chemical space. We provide systematic evaluation across five splitting strategies (random, scaffold-based, solvent-based, solute-based, temperature extrapolation), explicitly quantifying model performance under both interpolation and extrapolation scenarios. This analysis reveals important practical limitations (e.g., 46.4% performance degradation for entirely novel solvent systems, Table 16) essential for appropriate deployment. (4) Interpretability and Uncertainty Quantification: We provide extensive interpretability analysis through attention weight visualization (Tables 14, 15), layer-wise feature attribution (Table 11), and calibrated uncertainty estimates via Monte Carlo Dropout (Table 13). These analyses reveal that the model has learned chemically meaningful patterns and enable confidence-based deployment. (5) Domain-Specific Optimization and Validation: Through systematic ablation studies (Tables 8, 9, 10 and 11), we identify optimal architectural configurations specifically for binary solvent solubility prediction. Prospective experimental validation (Table 7) demonstrates practical reliability (MAE = 0.06 LogS, r = 0.94). In summary, our contribution is not the application of GCNs to molecular property prediction per se (well-established), but rather the development of a comprehensive, validated, interpretable framework specifically optimized for the complex, multi-condition prediction challenge of binary solvent solubility—a problem of direct practical importance for pharmaceutical formulation development.

The scientific contributions of this work encompass both methodological innovations and fundamental insights into structure-solubility relationships. The four-layer GCN architecture with 8-head attention mechanisms successfully learns hierarchical molecular representations, progressing from local atomic features through neighborhood patterns and substructure identification to comprehensive molecular embeddings. The integration of experimental conditions through cross-attention mechanisms enables accurate modeling of temperature and solvent composition effects, while Monte Carlo dropout provides calibrated uncertainty estimates essential for deployment in safety–critical pharmaceutical applications. The attention mechanism analysis reveals chemically interpretable patterns, with hydrogen bonding sites receiving highest attention (0.45 ± 0.08), followed by polar functional groups (0.38 ± 0.09), aromatic systems (0.32 ± 0.12), and aliphatic regions (0.18 ± 0.06), validating that the model has learned physically meaningful relationships rather than spurious correlations.

The systematic analysis across molecular complexity tiers revealed that GCN advantages increase with structural complexity (14.3% improvement for simple molecules, 22.2% for complex structures), temperature ranges (consistent 15–16% improvement from 273 to 373 K), and solvent compositions (highest 19.4% improvement in challenging organic-rich systems), demonstrating particular value for precisely those scenarios where experimental determination is most difficult and expensive.

The model selection guidelines emerging from this work provide clear decision criteria: GCN approaches are justified when interpretability is critical, complex molecular structures predominate, novel scaffolds require prediction, or high accuracy justifies computational costs. Traditional machine learning methods remain viable for resource-constrained scenarios, simple molecular structures, rapid single predictions, or when model simplicity is prioritized. This nuanced understanding enables organizations to select appropriate tools based on specific project requirements rather than adopting one-size-fits-all solutions.

Looking forward, this research establishes foundations for multiple advancement directions including extension to ternary solvent systems, multi-property prediction frameworks integrating solubility with permeability and stability, physics-informed neural networks incorporating thermodynamic constraints, and active learning strategies for optimal experimental design. The paradigm shift from descriptor-based to structure-based modeling, enhanced interpretability through attention mechanisms, substantial resource savings, and compatibility with regulatory documentation requirements position GCN approaches as essential tools for modern pharmaceutical development. The demonstrated combination of superior predictive performance, mechanistic interpretability, practical utility, and industrial validation establishes graph convolutional networks not as an alternative to traditional methods but as a new standard for molecular property prediction in pharmaceutical sciences.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that graph-based molecular representations can outperform descriptor-based approaches for solubility prediction in binary solvents, achieving 15.2% improvement in MAE on the evaluated dataset. While promising, these results are specific to this dataset and task; further validation across independent datasets and diverse chemical systems is necessary to establish broader generalizability. The successful integration of deep learning architectures with chemical domain knowledge, validated through extensive experimental testing and industrial deployment, provides a blueprint for applying artificial intelligence to accelerate pharmaceutical development while maintaining scientific rigor and interpretability. As computational resources become increasingly accessible and models continue to improve, we anticipate that graph neural network approaches will become the default methodology for molecular property prediction, ultimately accelerating the delivery of new medicines to patients while substantially reducing development costs and environmental impact through reduced experimental screening requirements.
