Brain network efficiency during social interaction is associated with real-world social network size
Authors: Mengfei Han, Xin Liu, Yongcong Shao, Hanxiao Ge, Feifei Chen, Jiyuan Li, Letong Wang, Xiao Zhong, Yiwen Hu, Yuyang Zhu, Liu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial ties critically influence individual health and well-being, raising important questions about why some individuals occupy advantageous social network positions. While social cognition is known to play a key role, it remains unclear whether neural differences during social information processing is associated with variability in the social network structure. Using the Prisoner’s Dilemma Game (PDG) combined with multilayered social network analysis, brain network analysis, and machine learning, we investigated how neural activation patterns relate to individual social network structures. The results revealed that individuals with a larger social network size were associated with (1) heightened nodal efficiency in the midcingulate cortex (MCC), (2) reduced efficiency in the inferior occipital gyrus (IOG), (3) enhanced functional connectivity between the anterior cingulate cortex-supplementary motor area (ACC-SMA) and olfactory-somatosensory regions, and (4) significantly negative predictive effects of nodal efficiency in both the IOG and hippocampus (as identified through machine learning). These findings demonstrate that efficient brain network organization, characterized by optimized integration in the cingulate cortex with a conflict monitoring function and selective suppression of visual attention and memory processing, is related to real-world social adaptation. Our study offers novel neurobiological insights into social networks, highlighting the crucial role of neural efficiency in social resource acquisition.Keywords: Social network, Cingulate cortex, Brain network analysis, Machine learning, fMRI

Introduction

Dynamic and varied social connections in daily life collectively shape social networks. Social networks play a vital role in determining physical and mental health by regulating the flow of information and providing a social support system [10, 20, 21, 77]. Social networks refer to the collections of relationships that individuals maintain throughout their lives, serving as essential sources of emotional, informational, and material support [34]. The social network structure affects both the accessibility and the quantity of resources available to individuals. Specifically, a more complex social network, characterized by features such as a greater number or diversity of social tie or higher density of connections, enhances an individual’s potential to access a wide range of beneficial supportive information, such as social support and health-related guidance. This type of network also promotes a stronger sense of social integration. Such supportive resources can help buffer the pressure of life, foster a greater sense of well-being, improve immune and cardiovascular function, and reduce the risk of disease [55]. This inevitably leads us to consider why some individuals occupy more advantageous social network structures, such as larger network sizes or more complex network connections, than others do. The social brain hypothesis proposes a possible neurobiological explanation, suggesting that individuals who navigate numerous complex social relationships may require or have a “larger” social brain to process intricate social information and adapt to their social environment [9]. Consequently, differences in brain activity patterns during social cognitive processing may be related to variations in social network structure among individuals.

Although previous studies support the contention that neural activity in social cognition-related brain regions can positively predict the complexity of individual social network structures, the role of mentalizing—a core component of social cognition—and its underlying neural mechanisms remains largely unexplored. Mentalizing (or theory of mind), the ability to understand and interpret the mental states of others [30, 46], plays a crucial role in the development and preservation of social relationships [72]. Theoretically, individuals who possess strong mentalizing skills are thought to have an enhanced ability to interpret others’ mental states, allowing them to adapt their behaviors to various social contexts [8, 57]. This adaptability is associated with the capacity to navigate complex social interactions, and this capacity may be further linked to the development of advantageous social network structures [24, 36, 61].

Emerging research has demonstrated that enhanced activation and functional connectivity in the mentalizing network during social tasks, particularly in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), temporoparietal junction (TPJ), and amygdala, are correlated with larger and more complex social networks [71]. Specifically, O’Donnell et al. [43] reported that individuals who act as key information brokers—connecting otherwise unconnected others in online social networks—show greater activation in these mentalizing regions when processing divergent feedback about peers’ evaluations of mobile applications. Similarly, another study utilizing a social exclusion paradigm revealed that stronger functional connectivity between bilateral TPJ regions positively predicted online social network density [51]. Research on offline social networks has reported similar findings. In a recent study using the same social exclusion paradigm, Baek et al. [5] demonstrated that individuals with more friends showed greater behavioral and neural sensitivity to stranger exclusion, including in key brain regions associated with negative affect (amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex [ACC]), social pain (e.g., the dorsal ACC, insula), and mentalizing (mPFC, TPJ); Notably, Baek et al. [4] indicated that central individuals (those frequently nominated as friends by their peers) exhibited more similar neural responses in mentalizing-related regions (e.g., the dorsomedial PFC and ventromedial PFC) when viewing naturalistic audiovisual stimuli than did their less central peers. Kirby et al. [26] found that, even in childhood, social network size is positively correlated with activation in the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) during biological motion encoding. Although these studies collectively demonstrate correlations between social network structures and neural activity elicited by social cognition tasks, the varied tasks and social network metrics employed have led to some variation in the findings. Further, these studies primarily used general social cognition tasks rather than directly addressing mentalizing processes and specialized tasks.

This study employed the prisoner’s dilemma game (PDG) to model dynamic social interactions and examine individuals’ ability to utilize mentalizing during interpersonal exchanges [35]. PDG is a game theory in which each player chooses between cooperating with or defecting against their opponent [38]. Award payoffs are determined by the interaction of both players’ choices, such that individual outcomes are maximized through defection (e.g., when one defect occurs while the opponent cooperates) rather than cooperation, mutual defection, or the player’s unilateral choice of cooperation [49, 56]. Throughout the game, players must infer their opponents’ beliefs, intentions, and strategic tendencies to adaptively optimize their decisions, thereby engaging in a mentalizing mechanism [37, 38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that neural activation elicited by the entire PDG task overlaps with key regions of the classical mentalizing network, including the TPJ, ACC, dmPFC, STS, and precuneus [18, 49, 60]. However, few studies have explored the correlation between PDG performance and social behaviors.

Previous studies have identified the important role of intuitive processing in social contexts [29]. According to the Social Heuristics Hypothesis (SHH), individuals internalize strategies that are typically advantageous in daily social interactions [47]. These strategies then become automatic, intuitive responses that are readily applied even in atypical contexts, such as structured laboratory experiments. Individuals with a complex social network structure are exposed to a wider variety of social scenarios, allowing them to accumulate richer experience in social information processing [43, 57]. This may provide them with an advantage in intuitive decision-making. Consistent with these studies, our previous electroencephalography (EEG) findings demonstrated that university students with higher betweenness centrality in their class exhibited greater susceptibility to others’ conflicting decisions as early as 180ms after stimulus onset [19, 21]. Thus, we introduced decision-making time constraints in the PDG task to simulate the cognitive shift from intuitive to deliberative processing in social contexts [29].

Furthermore, we employed a multilayered concentric social network structure to measure social connectedness, comprising three distinct layers: the outer layer as the online social network, quantified by the number of WeChat friends [77]; the intermediate layer as the real-world social network, defined by the number of contacts with whom an individual maintains regular interaction [13]; and the core layer as a close social network, characterized by the structural properties of significant interpersonal ties [31]. Collectively, this framework captures an individual’s generalized social competencies [24, 42].

To clarify whether an individual’s social cognitive processing is reflected in their social behavior, we progressively examined this relationship through both brain activation patterns and brain network topology. Furthermore, we employed machine learning to verify the predictive power of these neural mechanisms for social behavior. Specifically, we focus on how neural activation and brain network patterns elicited by the PDG, a classic interactive mentalizing task, correlate with multiple social network metrics. We hypothesized that individuals who demonstrate higher brain activation, greater functional network efficiency and stronger connectivity during mentalizing tasks are associated with more complex social networks. This advantageous neural pattern is expected to be more impactful during intuitive decision-making.

Methods

Participants

A total of 45 university students (13 females, 32 males; Mage = 21.00 years, SD = 2.27 years) participated in this fMRI study. fMRI data from 12 participants were excluded because of significant head motion (> 3 mm and/or 3°). Additionally, two participants were excluded because they failed to respond to the stimuli, and three participants were excluded because of incomplete responses to the social network questionnaire. The final analytical sample consisted of 28 participants (6 females; Mage = 21.36 years, SD = 2.26 years).

All participants were right-handed native language speakers, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and had no history of psychiatric disorders. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board of the Air Force Medical Center, Fourth Military Medical University. All the procedures were conducted in compliance with the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and subsequent revisions.

Procedure

fMRI paradigm

In the PDG, participants were informed that they would interact with a coplayer whose responses were purportedly derived from a real human participant who had previously enrolled in the study. In each trial, both players made simultaneous and independent decisions to select either option A (cooperate) or option B (defect). The payoff matrix is shown in Fig. 1, which determines the point allocations on the basis of joint decisions. For example, cooperation (A/A) yielded 10 points for each player, and mutual defection (B/B) resulted in 5 points per player. In comparison, asymmetric choices led to 15 points for the defecting player (B) and 0 points for the cooperating player (A). The payoff values vary across the game rounds. The participants were instructed to maximize their cumulative hypothetical points. Unbeknown to the participants, the response of the coplayer was generated by a computer algorithm implementing a probabilistic tit-for-tat strategy, which reciprocated the participants’ previous choices with a 67% probability [48, 56]. This algorithm aims to simulate real human behavior [12, 48, 49].

Experimental flowchart. A Measured multi-layered social network metrics. B Trial procedure of the Prisoner’s Dilemma Game (PDG) task. C Five unique payoff matrices

The present study employed a block design comprising five blocks, each characterized by distinct decision-making time constraints. In the first block, the participants faced time pressure and needed to make decisions within 2 s. The subsequent blocks imposed increasingly longer mandatory waiting periods of 2, 4, 6, and 8 s. This manipulation of time pressure was intended to simulate the shift from intuitive to deliberative decision making in the context of social cooperation [29, 47]. Each block comprised 10 rounds of PDG trials featuring 5 unique payoff matrices, each presented twice in pseudorandom order (see Fig. 1). Each trial started with a 2-second fixation cross. Following this, in blocks 2 through 5, the payoff matrix was presented for 2, 4, 6, or 8 s, respectively. In the first block, the trial advanced directly to the next screen after the fixation. Subsequently, a bell picture appeared in the top-left corner of the payoff matrix, serving as a reminder for participants to press a key to submit their decisions. This screen was presented for 2 s.

Afterward, a feedback screen displayed both players’ choices and their cumulative hypothetical points. Each block was followed by a fixation cross for 10 s, resulting in a total duration of approximately 594 s for the entire experimental procedure.

Social network metrics

All participants completed a set of questionnaires assessing their real-world social networks before fMRI scanning.

Real-world social network size: The measurement of social network size was adapted from the social network index (SNI), developed by Cohen et al. [13]. The SNI assesses the number of individuals with whom participants engage in regular contact (defined at least once every two weeks) across ten distinct relationship categories: romantic partners, parents, other close family members, friends, classmates/schoolmates, colleagues/workmates, neighbors, fellow volunteers, members of religious groups, and other significant relationships. The social network size was calculated by summing all these relationship categories. Furthermore, we perform three categorical aggregations to analyze relationships more thoroughly: (a) kinship network size, which combines parents and other close family members; (b) romantic network size; and (c) friendship network size, which aggregates the remaining seven relationship types, including friends, classmates, colleagues, neighbors, fellow volunteers, religious group members, and other relationships.

Close social network structure: The measurement of the close social network structure was adapted from the Social Network Questionnaire developed by Lewis et al. [31]. The participants were requested to identify their significant interpersonal relationships and to detail the connections between each pair of nominated individuals. Using these nominations, we constructed individual close social networks and subsequently calculated three key network metrics: (a) degree centrality, which quantifies the total number of nominated connections linked to an individual; (b) network density, which is calculated as the ratio of actual existing connections to the maximum possible connections within the network; and (c) betweenness centrality, which is measured by the proportion with which an individual appears on the shortest paths connecting other network members [19].

fMRI data acquisition analysis

Images were acquired via a Philips Ingenia Elition X scanner at the Beijing Shijitan Hospital Imaging Centre. The functional data were collected via a T2-weighted gradient echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle = 90°; field of view = 224 × 224 mm; voxel size = 3.5 × 3.5 × 3.5 mm3; matrix = 64 × 64 × 33; slice thickness = 3.5 mm; and number of slices = 33. T1-weighted high-resolution anatomical images were acquired with TR = 8.2 ms; TE = 3.8 ms; flip angle = 8°; field of view = 240 × 240 × 170 mm; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; matrix = 384 × 384 × 170; slice thickness = 1 mm; and number of slices = 170.

fMRI data analysis

Brain activation analysis

Functional data preprocessing was conducted via DPABI (Data Processing & Analysis of Brain Imaging, Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China; Yan et al., [73] and SPM 12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, Institute of Neurology, London, UK; Friston et al., [17]. The first five T2-weighted images from each participant were discarded to ensure signal stability. Slice timing was subsequently applied to correct the scanning time delays. Head motion was then estimated via realignment, and six head movement parameters were removed from the dataset. The middle slice (17th slice) served as the reference. Following this, the functional images for each participant were coregistered with their corresponding anatomical images. Both the anatomical and functional images were spatially normalized to the EPI template provided with SPM 12. Finally, the images were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 4 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM), resulting in a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³.

Task analysis was conducted via SPM12. Statistical analyses of the fMRI data were conducted via a two-level mixed-effects model implemented in SPM12. At the first level, we applied a general linear model (GLM) framework to estimate the responses for the PDG task and neutral control condition. The PDG task varied on the basis of five different decision-making time constraints: decisions made within 2, after 2, 4, 6, and 8 s. The control condition represented the intervals between the blocks and served as a baseline for further comparison. Six head motion parameters obtained during head motion correction were included as nuisance regressors. During parameter estimation, the data were subjected to high-pass filtering with a 128 s cutoff. Contrasts between the five conditions and the control condition were subsequently computed for each participant and entered into a second-level random-effects analysis via a flexible factorial design to conduct a repeated-measures one-way ANOVA. We examined both the main effect of time constraints and the average effect across the five conditions in the PDG task. All the results were FWE-corrected at p < 0.05, with a cluster threshold of 10 voxels, via the xjView toolbox (http://www.alivelearn.net/xjview).

To investigate the relationship between brain responses during social processing and individual social network structure, we conducted a region of interest (ROI) analysis via Marsbar and WFU_PickAtlas. The brain regions that exhibited significant activation during the PDG were identified as ROIs (Table 1). For each ROI, we applied a standard 10-mm sphere mask centered on the peak voxel coordinates identified in our study [59]. We then correlated the average contrast beta values for each participant within these ROIs via various social network metrics and Pearson correlation analysis. The false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment method was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Functional brain network analysis

Functional brain networks were constructed to investigate how brain activity efficiency during social processing predicts individual social network metrics. Brain network construction and the calculation of network properties were performed using the Graph Theoretical Network Analysis Toolbox (GRETNA) in Matlab (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna/). Based on the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas TzourioMazoyer et al., [64], the whole brain (excluding the cerebellum) of each participant was divided into 90 regions. For each region, the mean time series across all voxels within it was extracted as the regional time series. For the PDG task, the time series were concatenated from the five task blocks, whereas for the control condition, they were concatenated from the intervals between these PDG blocks. Consequently, time series for all 90 regions were obtained for each participant under both conditions. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between every pair of regional time series for each participant and each condition [15]. This procedure resulted in two 90 × 90 connectivity matrices per participant, representing the brain networks for the PDG task and the control condition, respectively.

Global brain network properties. We assessed the topological characteristics of the brain network via graph theory after applying Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to the correlated data. To ensure that the networks remained fully connected while preserving a small-world architecture, we derived topological characteristics from binarized brain networks with sparsity levels ranging from 0.05 to 0.4 in steps of 0.01 [76, 77]. We analyzed the area under the curve (AUC) for the following global network properties [20, 70]:

Global (Eglob): This reflects the brain network’s capacity to integrate information, which is calculated as the mean shortest path length between all node pairs.

Nodal brain network property. In addition to examining the global network properties, we evaluated nodal efficiency across all 90 nodes. For each node, we calculated its degree centrality, which was defined as the number of direct connections with other nodes in the whole-brain network.

For statistical analyses, paired t tests were performed to identify significant differences in both global and nodal network properties between the PDG task and control conditions. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to explore the potential associations between nodal/global network metrics during the PDG task and social network metrics. Prior to the statistical analysis, all the brain network properties and social network metrics were transformed into Z scores according to the means and standard deviations of all the participants. All the statistical tests were corrected for multiple comparisons via the FDR method (p < 0.05).

Functional connectivity analysis. The edges of the functional brain network represent the functional connectivity strength between every pair of brain regions defined by the AAL atlas. We conducted Pearson’s correlation analyses to assess the relationships between functional connectivity patterns and social network metrics. All the statistical comparisons were corrected for multiple tests via the FWE method (p < 0.05).

Machine learning analyses

We employed a machine learning approach to determine the multivariate predictive effects of functional brain network properties and connectivity on social network metrics. The analysis involved the following steps: feature extraction, feature preprocessing, model training, and model evaluation [14, 41].

Feature Extraction: Graph-theoretical measures derived from the PDG task-based functional brain networks were used as the feature set. This included three global network metrics (Eglob, Eloc, and Cp) as well as the nodal property of degree centrality across all 90 brain regions. We employed real-world social network size as the model’s label based on our initial correlation analyses (see Sect. 3.2.2–3.2.4), which identified it as the only social network metric significantly correlated with brain network properties.

Feature Preprocessing: Missing values are imputed using the median. The SelectKBest feature selection method combined with F-regression was applied to identify the top three most predictive features. The features were subsequently scaled via RobustScaler to ensure consistent feature magnitudes across the different attributes.

Model training: To evaluate predictive performance, we first randomly partitioned the entire dataset (including both features and labels) into training (70%) and held-out test (30%) sets. We implemented a linear kernel support vector regression (SVR) model, optimizing the hyperparameters through 10 iterations of 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. The grid search was performed by systematically sampling 50 logarithmically spaced values for the regularization parameter C (ranging from 1 × 10⁻⁴ to 10) and 20 linearly spaced values for the ε-insensitive parameter epsilon (ranging from 0.001 to 1). All possible parameter combinations were exhaustively evaluated, with selection on the basis of the maximal mean R2 in the cross-validation. The optimally parameterized model was then independently assessed via a 30% test set.

Results

Behavioral measures

Social network metrics

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationships among all social network metrics (Table 1). Specifically, for real-world social network size, individuals’ social network size was positively correlated with their friendship network size. Regarding the close social network structure, those with higher betweenness centrality tended to exhibit higher degree centrality and lower network density. These results indicate that individuals who acted as bridges between others tend to have more close social connections but a sparser overall close social network. The findings suggest that these social network metrics provide a multilayered perspective on an individual’s social network structure.

Prisoner dilemma game behavior

To examine the impact of decision-making time constraints on cooperation rates, a repeated-measures one-way ANOVA was performed with the cooperation rate defined as the proportion of cooperative rounds relative to the total number of game rounds. The analysis revealed no statistically significant main effect of decision-making time constraint (F (4, 108) = 1.07, p = 0.360; see Table 2). Given the nonsignificant main effect, we averaged the cooperation rates across the five blocks for subsequent analyses. Pearson correlation analysis was then conducted to investigate the relationships between cooperation rates and social network metrics; however, no statistically significant correlations were found.

fMRI results

Brian activations related to social network metrics

To investigate the neural correlates of social information processing and how decision-making time constraints modulate this brain activity, we analyzed both the main effect of decision-making time constraints and the average neural response across all conditions in the PDG task. Whole-brain analysis revealed no significant main effect of the decision-making time constraints. However, when the average neural response across all conditions was examined, we observed significant activation in several regions associated with social cognition and decision-making, including the inferior parietal lobule (IPL), fusiform gyrus, lingual gyrus, precentral gyrus, supplementary motor area (SMA), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and middle frontal gyrus (MFG) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Nodal brain network properties

Paired t tests revealed significant differences in nodal brain network properties between the PDG task and the control condition. Compared with the control condition, the PDG task was associated with significantly elevated degree centrality in the insula, midcingulate cortex (MCC), precuneus, superior temporal gyrus (STG), and middle temporal gyrus (MTG). Conversely, reduced degree centrality was observed in the inferior occipital gyrus (IOG) (see Table 5).

Discussion

This neuroimaging study employed fMRI combined with brain network analysis and machine learning to examine the neural basis of mentalizing in relation to multilayered social network structures. Our findings demonstrate that individuals with larger social network size were associated with enhanced nodal efficiency in the MCC and reduced efficiency in the IOG, as well as stronger functional connectivity between the ACC-SMA and olfactory-somatosensory regions. These results reveal that individual differences in social network structure are associated more critically with the topological properties of brain networks than with brain activation levels. They also highlight the important role of the cingulate cortex in maintaining social networks. Our study points to a potential mapping between functional brain networks and real-world social functions.

Neural activation during the PDG task

Our findings demonstrate that the PDG activates several brain regions overlapping with the mentalizing network, indicating that this task engages in social cognitive processing. Specifically, the opercular part of the IFG is involved in affective mentalizing, particularly processing others’ emotional perspectives and resolving self-other incongruity [3, 11]. Moreover, the IPL supports mental state inference, particularly in monitoring and executing movements on the basis of the observed actions or reactions of others [3, 6, 32, 52]. Additionally, the MFG, which includes the dorsal ACC [68] and SMA, plays a critical role in switching subsequent actions on the basis of estimated action values or influencing the production of an action [65]. Although the activation patterns we observed do not fully align with those in mentalizing networks, further functional brain network analysis revealed enhanced nodal efficiency within key mentalizing regions during the PDG, including the precuneus, STG, and MCC [52, 60]. These areas are central to inferring a partner’s feelings, thoughts, or intended actions by simulating one’s responses [27], thereby supporting prior evidence that the PDG modulates activity patterns in brain regions associated with mentalizing.

The observed discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies may be attributed to methodological variations in experimental design. We analyzed the task as an integrated process without separating the distinct phases of the PDG (e.g., decision-making, anticipation, and feedback; Thompson et al., [60]. Future research could benefit from examining phase-specific neural activity and its relationship with individual social network structures.

Nodal efficiency and social network size

Functional brain network analysis revealed that degree centrality in the MCC was positively correlated with social network size, whereas in the IOG, it was negatively correlated. Machine learning confirmed that the IOG, the calcarine cortex, and the hippocampus together negatively predict social network size. These findings suggest that individuals with larger social networks are associated with optimized brain network efficiency, characterized by enhanced key social cognition hubs and suppressed irrelevant visual and memory processing resources.

The MCC is involved in processing information about the outcomes of others’ decisions during social interactions and regulating self-action [1]. Specifically, when individuals recognize errors in their own decisions or experience non-reward outcomes (i.e., conflict monitoring), neural activity in the MCC increases [62]. Furthermore, the MCC serves as a key node bridging the default mode network and salience network [53, 67], dynamically coordinates and integrates cognitive and affective functions across brain regions [25]. The enhanced nodal efficiency of the MCC suggests that individuals may process others’ decisions or perspective discrepancies more efficiently. This neural adaptation is usually associated with a better understanding of others’ feelings and the facilitation of appropriate behavioral responses, which in turn correlates with the maintenance of social relationships.

However, our findings indicate that IOG degree centrality is negatively related to social network size. This suggests that individuals with larger social connections are related to optimized visual information processing [75]. The PDG task may require less integration of visual attentional resources. For individuals with larger social networks, this simplified connectivity pattern in the IOG could reflect a strategic reallocation of neural resources toward brain regions that support higher-order social cognition, such as the MCC, rather than maintaining extensive connections for basic social cue detection.

Subsequent machine learning analyses verified that decreased degree centrality in the IOG, calcarine cortex, and hippocampus collectively predicted a larger social network size. The IOG was consistently identified in both correlation analyses and the machine learning analyses as being associated with real-world social network size, which demonstrates the robustness and replicability of this result. The machine learning analysis captures synergistic activity patterns across multiple brain regions [66]. Although the calcarine cortex and hippocampus exhibited weak Pearson correlations with real-world social network size individually, they collectively form a significantly predictive brain activity pattern when integrated with the IOG. While the correlation analysis identified crucial neural nodes, the machine learning analysis reveals how these regions interact synergistically within a predictive framework.

The IOG processes basic social visual cues, the calcarine fissure is the primary visual area, and the hippocampus integrates social information into memory [58]. The reduced degree centrality in IOG, accompanied by synchronous decreases in the calcarine cortex and hippocampus, may indicate that individuals with larger social networks are linked to greater automation in processing visual social features and encoding/retrieving social information when handling complex social interactions. Coordinated optimization across these brain areas may be linked to more efficient transmission of social information to higher-order social cognition regions. These optimized neural transmission patterns, in turn, are associated with rapid adaptive responses in complex social environments [39]. Our results demonstrate that even basic perceptual and memory systems are associated with individual differences in social network characteristics. Although our study offers a novel perspective on the relationship between social cognitive processing and social networks, more empirical studies are needed.

Functional connectivity and social network size

Functional connectivity analyses further confirmed the crucial role of the cingulate gyrus in the maintenance of individual social networks. Specifically, the functional connectivity strength between the ACC and SMA was positively correlated with social network size. This result may reflect the regulatory control of motor preparation by the conflict monitoring system. As a core hub for social cognition, the ACC engages in error/conflict monitoring as well as cost‒benefit decision-making and further modulates behavioral preparation through the SMA on the basis of evaluative outcomes during social interactions [2, 40]. This connectivity pattern represents a component of the fronto-cingulate circuit, reflecting sensitivity to social signals and behavioral flexibility [22, 23]. The literature has established associations between social network size and the functional and structural connectivity of the ACC with other brain regions. For example, resting-state coupling between the ACC and mid-STS activity highlights social information valuation as a function of network size [42, 50]. Our findings further confirmed the critical role of the ACC in maintaining social networks.

Additionally, we found that the functional connectivity strength between the olfactory cortex and sensorimotor regions (e.g., the postcentral gyrus and paracentral lobule) was positively correlated with social network size. These results suggest the importance of multisensory integration for social network maintenance. Research has demonstrated that the olfactory system can modulate socioemotional processing through limbic circuits, even in odorless environments [7]. The left somatosensory cortex plays a crucial role in sensorimotor integration [28, 44, 54]. The enhanced functional connectivity observed in our study may reflect improved predecision integration of sensory information into motor execution. However, this finding requires further validation in future studies.

Previous studies have reported positive relationships between individual social network size and brain activity/volume in regions such as the amygdala, mPFC, OFC, and TPJ [42, 43, 63, 77]. However, our study highlights the critical role of the cingulate cortex, which is involved in processing conflicting information and expected value signals. This discrepancy may stem from our experimental paradigm. Our task required participants to focus on reward information in social interactions (i.e., the PDG required individuals to maximize their gains), making them particularly sensitive to conflict situations and nonreward feedback. This aligns with our previous findings, in which well-connected individuals exhibited larger ERP amplitudes (e.g., N200, P300, and LPP; Han et al., [19, 20] in response to self-other conflict during social influence tasks. Our results are also consistent with those of a recent study by Baek et al. [5], which revealed that individuals with more friends exhibited greater distress toward social exclusion. Together, these findings suggest that individuals who are more sensitive to self-other conflicts may be better equipped to maintain larger social relationships.

In addition, our study emphasized the importance of nodal efficiency rather than regional brain activation. A previous resting-state study by Lin et al. [33] also revealed no significant associations between social network structure and regional brain activation or volume. In combination with our results, these findings suggest that brain network properties may represent a more reliable neural correlate of individual differences in social network structure. Further studies are needed to verify this conclusion.

Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that, among the various social network metrics analyzed, only real-life social network size significantly predicts individual neural activity patterns. In contrast, neither the close social network structure nor the online social network size showed meaningful associations. Close networks tend to be overly concentrated with limited interindividual variability, whereas online networks are often vast, incorporating peripheral connections such as colleagues or commercial contacts. Collectively, these results suggest that an individual’s intermediate social layer, comprising routinely maintained relationships, may serve as a valid indicator of their mentalizing ability.

The current study revealed that the cingulate cortex’s local information integration efficiency and its functional coupling with motor regions are significantly related to social network size, extending the traditional “social brain” theory. These findings highlight that the maintenance of social networks is linked to optimized brain network efficiency, which involves the activity of conflict detection and reward valuation systems.

Limitations and future research

The present study clarified the relationship between the social network structure and neural activities underlying social interaction. Our findings demonstrate that individuals with larger social networks exhibit optimized brain network efficiency. Our findings offer profound insights into the neural underpinnings of variations in individual social network structures. However, this study has several limitations.

First, it relies on self-reported measures of social network structure, which may introduce variability due to factors such as response bias, recall accuracy, or participant fatigue. This methodological factor may account for both the partial discrepancy between our results and those of previous studies as well as the inconsistencies among prior findings. Future research could employ objective measurement methods to construct social networks, such as nominations of “particularly close” friends within defined groups.

Second, excessive head motion led to the exclusion of many participants, resulting in a final sample of 28 individuals. Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the gender imbalance in this sample, as the majority of participants were male. Therefore, future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are necessary to validate these results.

Third, we did not collect additional demographic details, such as race or ethnicity. All participants were Chinese university students recruited from the same institution (Beijing Sport University). Given their shared cultural and educational background, we believed that factors like race and ethnicity would have minimal impact on the cognitive and behavioral outcome in our study. Future studies should examine how demographic information moderate the relationship between mentalizing processing and social networks.

Fourth, a growing body of evidence suggests a nonlinear relationship between social network size and health outcomes, wherein both excessively large and highly restricted networks may pose health risks [16, 74]. The perceived social support potentially serving as a mediator [69]. However, the present study focused primarily on social network structure, without fully accounting for quality of social network such as perceived social support. Future research should further examine the interactive effects of social network structure and quality on individuals’ health and cognitive processing.

Fifth, although we incorporated time pressure to simulate the cognitive shift from intuitive to deliberative social decision-making, no significant main effect of time was observed. In the subsequent analysis, we did not differentiate between task phases (e.g., anticipation versus decision-making) or behavioral choices (e.g., cooperation vs. defection) in the PDG. Future studies should conduct phase-specific analyses to identify when social network differences emerge.

Finally, while few previous studies have used the PDG paradigm to investigate how mentalizing neural mechanisms relate to social network structure, our work represents a novel extension of prior research. However, our conclusions require validation via diverse experimental paradigms. Such efforts will clarify context-dependent relationships between mentalizing and social networks, ultimately advancing our understanding of the neural underpinnings of individual differences in social networks.

Conclusions

This study elucidates how individual differences in social network size are reflected in distinct patterns of brain network organization. By combining interactive mentalizing tasks with brain network analysis, we demonstrated that individuals with larger social networks are associated with optimized neural efficiency, characterized by enhanced nodal efficiency of the cingulate cortex, suppressed visual and memory-processing resources, and strengthened functional connectivity between the cingulate and motor execution regions. Our results bridge a critical gap between neurobiological mechanisms and real-world social adaptation, providing novel insights into a neurocomputational framework for future investigations of the dynamic interaction between neural patterns and social functioning.
