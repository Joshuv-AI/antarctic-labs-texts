Investigating impact of in-process cooling mediums on microstructural and mechanical properties of FSWed AA2219 T6 joints
Authors: Ghulam Hussain, Tauheed Shehbaz, Mohammed Alkahtani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of various in-process cooling techniques on the microstructural characteristics and mechanical properties of the friction stir-welded AA-2219-T6 alloy. The in-process cooling methods used include normal air, dry fan, an oil-water mixture (20:80), and water. The primary objective is to determine the optimal cooling strategy for enhancing the quality of the weldment. The characterization included light microscopy, tensile testing, X-ray diffraction (XRD), microhardness testing, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of fractured surfaces to evaluate the microstructure and mechanical properties. It was seen that in-process cooling methods significantly affect the microstructural features and mechanical properties of AA 2219-T6 alloy weldments. Findings indicated that the water-cooled method at 55 °C resulted in the highest density of Al2Cu particles, leading to superior hardness and mechanical properties compared to other in-process cooling methods. Specifically, the water-cooled samples exhibited a ~ 17% increase in hardness compared to air-cooled samples and achieved an ultimate tensile strength equivalent to 65.4% of the base metal. Furthermore, a notable volume of second-phase particles was observed on the fractured surfaces in the water-cooled condition, attributed to the lower in-process temperatures, thus underscoring the effectiveness of water cooling in friction stir welding.

Introduction

AA 2219 alloy, known for its lightweight nature and high-strength characteristics, is widely utilized in aircraft structures, including fuselage components, wing structures, and fuel tanks1–3. The 2219 aluminum alloy is a ternary alloy consisting of aluminum, copper, and manganese, with copper serving as a primary alloying element. Its precipitation sequence includes the formation of various phases: α, α + GP zones, α + θ″, α + θ′, and α + θ, where α represents the aluminum solid solution, θ′ and θ″ denote metastable phases, and θ signifies the stable phase (Al2Cu)4,5.

One of the primary reasons for the significance of welding AA 2219 alloy lies in its ability to maintain structural integrity and mechanical strength under extreme operating conditions typically encountered in aerospace environments6. This alloy exhibits excellent resistance to stress corrosion cracking7 and fatigue, making it suitable for prolonged service in demanding aerospace applications.

Various methods have been employed for the autogenous welding of AA2219-T6. Li et al.8 joined the 2219-T6 alloy using the VPTIG (variable polarity tungsten inert gas welding) and observed the macro and microporosity in the FZ (fusion zone) and HAZ. Zhang et al.9 studied the effect of weld penetration on tensile properties of AA2219 TIG welded joints and found deeper penetration of the weld in the toe region resulted in lower tensile strength owing to the stress that concentrated at the weld toe. Quan et al.10 reported the formation of eutectics, microporosities, and microsegregation in the fusion zone of the LPVPTIG (low frequency pulsed current variable polarity TIG) welded 2219-T6 alloy. However, the lower welding speed in the HPVPTIG weld reduced the Cu microsegregation, leading to increased tensile strength and elongation by 13% and 71%, respectively, compared to the LPVPTIG process.

Zhu et al.11 used pulsed Lorentz force during laser welding to study the properties and microstructural features of laser-welded 2219-T6 aluminum alloy, as well as the fracture mechanics of welded joint. Findings revealed that the strength of the weldments was 226.60 MPa compared to those of the BM (382.60 MPa) due to microstructural heterogeneity and the presence of coarse brittle eutectic phases. Hussain et al.6 investigated the tensile properties and microstructural features of the AA2219-T6 alloy using electron beam welding method and found 47.3% and 42.2% and 47.3% reduction in yield and tensile strength, respectively, of the joint than the base alloy, which is ascribed to the dissolution of the second phase particles.

Friction stir welding (FSW) has emerged as a revolutionary solid-state joining technique for lightweight alloys, offering advantages such as low distortion, improved mechanical properties, and enhanced joint efficiency. Researchers have extensively studied dissimilar alloys, revealing significant improvements in mechanical properties and microstructural integrity of the joints using FSW compared to traditional welding techniques12–15. Al alloys joined with dissimilar Mg alloys, and the majority of researchers believe that the eutectic reaction is the most important element in the creation of IMCs between Al-Mg FSW. Moreover, Constitutional liquation is believed to be the leading cause of excessive IMCs and the weakening of Al-Mg welds16. Sato et al.17 investigated the FSW of Al and Mg and observed a large volume of intermetallic compound Al12Mg17 in the irregular shaped region at the interface. It was also found that constitutional liquation formed IMC Al12Mg17 during the joining process.

Researchers have conducted numerous parametric studies on the FSW of different alloys. Yaknesh et al.18 studied the impact of the angle of tilt of the employed tool on the strength and microstructural features of the friction stir welded flat plates of AZ91C alloy and observed the large sized cored grains of the parent metal have been transformed into almost equally spaced small-sized grains, by the impact of the intermittent effective process of recrystallisation which increased the strength of the joint from 188 to 205 MPa (~ 86% of the parent metal). Similarly, Mehdi et al.19 studied the effects of three different pin profiles on the joints of AA6061 and AZ91D using FSW and found that the triangular pin profile had the highest ultimate tensile strength (UTS) at 144.32 MPa. On the other hand, the square pin profile generated a significant amount of heat, leading to severe plastic deformation in the SZ.

Based on the investigative data produced by the six parameters-five levels based factorial analysis, Gunasekaran et al.20 performed research to build empirical connections within the constraints of the FSW procedure and the tensile relevant strength of AZ80A joints. The joint exhibited a maximum tensile strength of 234.23 MPa under an axial downward force of 5.17 kN, achieved at a traverse speed of 1.304 mm/sec and a rotating speed of 1032.88 rpm.

Dissimilar aluminum alloys presented superior mechanical properties using the FSW process. The welding behavior of 2219 aluminum alloy, particularly in friction stir welding, has garnered significant attention from researchers worldwide because of reduced distortion, enhanced mechanical properties, and no fusion zone defect in comparison to fusion welding techniques21. Liu et al.22 studied the impact of welding parameters on the microstructural aspects and mechanical characteristics of the 2219-T6 alloy and reported the presence of recrystallized grains in the middle of the nugget zone. Furthermore, raising the welding speed from 60 to 220 mm/min increased the tensile strength of FSWed joints to 79% of the base metal. However, voids emerged at a higher welding speed of 300 mm/min, which was responsible for the reduction in strength.

Chen et al.23 investigated the precipitation development in the FSW of 2219-T6 and reported the existence of fine equiaxed grains in WNZ (weld nugget zone) and elongated grain structure in TMAZ with stable θ precipitates that are formed during welding from the plate-shaped morphology coherent θ″ and semi-coherent θ′ phase precipitates already present in base metal. Moreover, after ageing, the stable θ precipitates were still observed in both zones, with re-precipitation of some plate-shaped precipitates. Liu et al.24 examined the effect of welding speed on the microstructural characteristics and tensile properties of FSW of 2219-T6 assisted by an external non-rotational shoulder. The welding speed of 100 mm/min achieved a defect-free joint, and the tensile strength reached a maximum of approximately 69% of the BM. The decrease in strength was attributed to the change in energy input during the welding process, which resulted in grain size and microstructural variations.

In past research, various methods have been employed to improve the mechanical characteristics of FSWed precipitation hardenable aluminum alloy weldments. Li et al.25 used non-thinning and penetrating Friction Stir Welding (NTPFSW) and Penetrating Friction Stir Welding (PFSW), a novel FSW method for 2219-RT6 weldment, and reported the superior mechanical properties (2% higher) than PFSW process which was ascribed to the larger grain size in NTPFSW with a smaller number of θ′ precipitates. Benavides et al.26 conducted friction stir welding (FSW) on 2024 Al, employing liquid nitrogen (LN) cooling to decrease the starting temperature of the plates from 30 °C to -30 °C. The increase in hardness was observed in TMAZ and HAZ in comparison to the normal welded joint. However, the presence of void defects was observed in the nugget. However, the specific impact of the cooling method on the mechanical properties of the weldments was not discussed.

Sharma et al.27 examined the effect of compressed air, liquid nitrogen, and normal water on FSWed AA7039 and found that in process cooling limited the grain coarsening in HAZ and TMAZ, which resulted in higher HAZ hardness in comparison to conventional joints. However, the superior mechanical properties observed in normal weld joints can be attributed to the vigorous natural aging response following Friction Stir Welding (FSW). Zhang et al.28 studied the microstructural aspects and mechanical performance of the FSWed 2219-T6 joint, both in normal conditions and under cooling water, and reported that the enhancement in hardness is ascribed to the reduction in precipitate coarsening and the lessening in precipitate-free zone width, primarily influenced by the fluctuating welding thermal cycles modulated by the water-cooling effect. Moreover, the tensile strength of the joint in water cooling increased by 5% compared to a normal FSWed joint. Liu et al.29 found that the FSW AA2219-T6 joints produced with water as a cooling medium at a consistent rotational speed of 800 rpm and torch speed during welding ranged between 50 and 150 mm/min, resulting in fractures occurring at HAZ or at the NZ. However, increasing the welding torch speed from 150 to 200 mm/min resulted in the formation of voids as defects. Furthermore, mechanical strength initially rose with increasing welding speed but experienced a significant decline at 200 mm/min speed due to the presence of groove defects.

Numerous methods can enhance the mechanical properties of FSWed joints, according to a literature review. These methods include (i) varying process parameters, (ii) changing tool geometry, and (iii) increasing the cooling rate by employing external in-process water cooling. Although several studies have concentrated on alloys such as Al7075 and Al2024, the effects of in-process cooling media on friction stir welded Al2219 in T6 state are not investigated. Unlike prior research that primarily focused on singular cooling methods, our investigation offers a holistic view by incorporating a comparative analysis of multiple cooling strategies. This approach not only broadens understanding but also enables to pinpoint the most effective cooling method for improving weld quality, a gap not fully explored in the existing literature. The objective of the study was to introduce a methodology by systematically evaluating the effects of different in-process cooling mediums on the weldments of AA 2219-T6 alloy and a detailed comparative analysis of the microstructural features, mechanical properties, and fracture characteristics of weldments subjected to different cooling methods.

Materials and methods

This study used plates of AA2219-T6, measuring 120 mm in length and 60 mm in width with a five-millimeter thickness. Chemical composition (wt%) of the plates measured by EDS, revealed 4.2% Cu, 0.3% Fe, 0.2% Si, 0.3% Mn, 0.1% V, 0.1% Zn, 0.15% Zr, and trace amounts of Mg and Ti and Bal: Al. A vertical milling machine (Model: UH-1250 A) with a table size of 1370 × 360 mm, a spindle speed of 70–2052 rpm, a milling head swiveling capability of ± 30°, and a cross-power feed of 24–932 mm/min is used to friction stir weld the plates. Microstructural characterization

The specimens for metallography were prepared by sectioning the welded plate with an electric discharge machine (EDM). The metallographic samples were ground using non-wetting SiC papers and subsequently polished with diamond paste (6–0.25 μm) and etched with Keller’s reagent. An Olympus polarizing microscope (BH2-UMA)was used to take low magnification images of the samples. The weldment’s microstructural details and chemical composition were inspected and studied using a SEM (Carl Zeiss-EVO15) with an energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) attachment. X-ray diffractometry (XRD) was used to investigate the phases formed within the joints. The observations were made with a Proto X-ray diffractometer, which was operated at 40 kV and 25 mA with Cu Kα as a source target.

Results and discussion

Microstructure

Figure 2(a) shows the grain structure of the base metal. The microstructure of the base alloy displayed equiaxed and longitudinally elongated grains, a result of rolling-induced deformation. Furthermore, the grain structures indicated the presence of finely distributed second-phase particles, notably Al2Cu and S-phase (Al2CuMg), as illustrated in Fig. 2(a) and (b). The formation of Guinier-Preston zones (GPZ) within the grains is believed to contribute to the observed age-hardening phenomenon. Other researchers have observed similar findings35–37. The formation of GPZs caused age hardening, which in turn correlated with these precipitates (Al2Cu and S-phase).

The optical micrographs of cross-sections of friction stir welded (FSW) plates under varied in-process cooling mediums are shown in Fig. 3, with the cross-sections being perpendicular to the tool traverse direction. The impacts of four distinct cooling media—air cooled, a fan cooled, oil + water, and water cooled —were examined in relation to the process parameters. The rubbing action between the tool shoulder and the metal produced sufficient frictional and deformation heat to soften the base metal locally. Following its softening, the metal was extruded from the tool’s front to its rear, where it was forged to create a joint38. This joint consisted of the base metal’s stir zone (SZ), HAZs, and TMAZ.

The FSW joints AC, FC, OW, and WC encompass the SZ, TMAZ, HAZ, and base material (BM), as depicted in Figs. 3a and d and 4a, and 4d, respectively. The stir zone (SZ) has sustained high temperatures and substantial plastic deformation, resulting in a dynamically recrystallized, finer, equiaxed grain structure with the Al2Cu particles, as illustrated in the SZ zones of all specimens in Figs. 3c and f and 4c, and 4f. In the T6 condition of the alloy, the welding process generated insufficient heat to melt the second-phase particles, leaving them intact39.

The TMAZ is subjected to plastic shear stress around the material’s plastic flow, causing grain elongation in the direction of utmost shear stress (Figs. 3b and e and 4b, and 4e).

Figure 5 show secondary electron micrographs of second-phase particle distribution in NZ of all the samples. From the EDS analysis shown in Fig. 5 and the Al-Cu phase diagram, the particles are identified as Al2Cu in the Al matrix. It is also found that the second phase particles (Al2Cu) in samples AC and FC are relatively less than the OW and WC samples. The reduction in the particle density can be attributed to the temperature during the process, which is ~ 3.8 and 3 times higher in AC and DF than OW and FC, respectively. Moreover, in the AC and FC samples, the increase in temperature, which is close to the solution treatment temperature of the alloy, during FSW resulted in a dissolution and redistribution of the particles in the SZ of the weldment40. It is pertinent to mention that the majority of the particles are elongated in AC and DF, whereas round/spherical morphology can be seen in OW and WC. The cooling rate significantly affects the grain size in the SZ. Faster cooling rates, such as those provided by water cooling, result in rapid solidification, which restricts grain growth and leads to finer grains. Conversely, slower cooling rates, like air cooling, allow for more prolonged exposure to high temperatures, promoting grain coarsening due to extended recrystallization and grain growth periods. In addition, the cooling conditions affect the solute redistribution within the SZ, influencing the nucleation and growth of second-phase particles. Fast cooling limits the diffusion of solutes, which can lead to a more homogeneous distribution of fine precipitates and inhibit precipitate coarsening. Slower cooling may allow for solute segregation, resulting in the coarsening of particles and localized concentration of phases. The change in morphology is indicative of the deformation and high temperature transformation of the second phase particles associated with processing the conditions of the samples22.

Fig. 5Secondary electron micrograph of second-phase particles distribution the different NZ: (a) AC (b) DF (c) OW and (d) WC.

Secondary electron micrograph of second-phase particles distribution the different NZ: (a) AC (b) DF (c) OW and (d) WC.

The grain size in the SZ is significantly influenced by heat dissipation, heat input and plastic deformation of material during friction stir processing (FSW). Similar findings are reported by various researchers41,42. Reduction in heat input and increased heat dissipation or strain rate typically lead to grain refinement in the SZ. The application of forced cooling methods facilitates rapid heat extraction from the SZ, thereby significantly reducing the motivation for recrystallized grain to grow and limiting its formation. As a result, with an increase in cooling rate, the size of the fine equiaxed grains reduces at the expense of high cooling rates, resulting in enhanced heat dissipation capacity43. In the air-cooling (AC) method, the natural convection of air acts as the primary heat dissipation mechanism. The stir zone cools at a relatively slower rate compared to forced cooling methods. This slower cooling rate leads to coarser grains within the stir zone (SZ) and heat-affected zone (HAZ). As the metal cools, larger grains can form, resulting in decreased grain boundary area and potential softening of the weld zone. In addition, 2219-T6, which contains Al2Cu as a second-phase precipitate, air cooling may not provide enough rapid quenching to effectively suppress precipitate dissolution and reprecipitation, leading to a more homogeneous distribution but less refined precipitate structure44. Dry fan (DF) cooling involves forced air directed at the weld zone, increasing the convective heat transfer coefficient and thus cooling the weld faster than natural air cooling. Therefore, faster cooling than natural air-cooling led to finer grain structures, which are beneficial for increasing the strength and hardness of the weld45. The cooling rate is sufficient to maintain some degree of precipitate integrity while preventing excessive grain growth, which aids in strengthening the welded joints.

Figure 6 depicts the particle density across all samples. Post-FSW analysis using ImageJ software (ImageJ, Version 1.54i, URL:https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) revealed that sample AC exhibited approximately 65% lesser second-phase particles compared to the base material (BM). In contrast, sample DF showed an ~ 28% increase in particle density relative to sample AC. Furthermore, particle density in samples WC and OW increased by ~ 152% and ~ 85%, respectively. These variations in particle density are likely due to the melting and reallocation mechanisms induced by the thermal conditions in the FSW process. Moreover, using a mixture of oil and water as a cooling medium, the presence of oil acted as a lubricant, reducing frictional heat, while water facilitates rapid heat extraction46. This dual-phase cooling medium offers a balance between thermal conduction and heat dissipation, resulting in intermediate grain size reduction as the oil component moderates the extreme cooling effect of water alone, which prevents overly rapid quenching that could lead to undesirable microstructures. This cooling approach stabilizes the second-phase particles (Al2Cu), preventing excessive dissolution and enhancing the mechanical stability of the stir zone.

XRD

Figure 7 shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for all specimens using varied in-process cooling mediums. Constructive interference on different crystallographic planes yielded varied intensities in all specimens, representing the Al (α) phase matrix. Sharp peaks were seen at 2θ values of 38°, 44.5°, 66.5°, and 78.5°, indicating diffraction on the planes (111), (200), (220), and (311), respectively. Furthermore, at an angle 2θ = 47.5°, non-equilibrium coherent θ’ precipitates corresponding to the (202) plane were found, showing that the BM was age-hardened before FSW. It is obvious that Al2Cu remained present in all four situations, owing to the processing temperature being less than the solution treatment temperature47.

Microhardness

The microhardness measurements, performed at the mid-section of the friction stir welded (FSWed) joints in the transverse direction, as shown in Fig. 8, revealed insightful data about the hardness distribution across the different zones of the weldments. The microhardness measurements, performed at the mid-section of the friction stir welded (FSWed) joints in the transverse direction, as shown in Fig. 8, revealed insightful data about the hardness distribution across the different zones of the weldments. The AA 2219-T6 alloy’s W-shaped hardness profile, characteristic of friction stir welding (FSW), reflects the complex thermal cycles and mechanical deformation the material undergoes during the welding process.The SZ and TMAZ exhibited reduced hardness compared to the heat-affected zones (HAZ), for all in-process cooling mediums, indicating a softened region due to the intense plastic deformation, dynamic recrystallization and breaking of the second phase particle with redistribution allowed coarser grains and reduced precipitate strengthening. This softening can be attributed to the severe plastic deformation, and high temperatures in the SZ lead to dynamic recrystallization, resulting in grain refinement but also in the dissolution or coarsening of strengthening precipitates such as Al2Cu, which are crucial for maintaining hardness in the T6 condition. Moreover, high temperatures in the case of AC, DF, and OW in comparison to WC during FSW promoted the solubility of these particles in the aluminum matrix, leading to the breaking down of larger Al2Cu precipitates into finer solute atoms and redistribution.

The observed average microhardness values in the SZ were 52.5 Hv for air-cooled (AC), 55.7 Hv for dry fan (DF), 66.07 Hv for water-cooled (WC), and 61.3 Hv for oil-water (OW) mixture. These differences highlight the influence of cooling rates on precipitate stability and grain structure. In addition, the increased temperatures on the retreating side (RS) lead to precipitate coarsening, which contributes to the lower hardness observed on the RS compared to the advancing side (AS). This aligns with previous studies where higher plastic deformation on the RS resulted in a reduction of microhardness values due to precipitate dissolution48.

The HAZs demonstrated higher hardness than the SZ and TMAZ, a phenomenon often observed in precipitation-hardened alloys like AA 2219-T6. The highest hardness in the HAZ can be attributed to the thermal cycle during FSW, can lead to natural aging or precipitation hardening in the HAZ, where the temperatures are sufficient to promote the reprecipitation of strengthening phases without causing significant over-aging. This effect is more pronounced in cooling methods that maintain a moderate temperature range, allowing for optimal precipitation kinetics. The interaction between dislocations and fine precipitates during cooling enhances the hardness in the HAZ. The rapid cooling in water-cooled samples (WC) potentially preserves these precipitates better than slower cooling methods, resulting in a ~ 17% increase in hardness compared to AC samples.

The choice of cooling medium significantly impacts the microhardness profile across the weldment. The varying cooling rates and their effects on microstructure explain the observed hierarchy of hardness—WC > OW > DF > AC. Water cooling (WC) provides the highest cooling rate, leading to the retention of fine precipitates and a more refined grain structure in the SZ.

This aligns with the literature, which suggests that faster cooling rates boost the precipitation of strengthening phases, thereby enhancing hardness and mechanical properties. The (OW) Mixture and Dry Fan (DF) provide intermediate cooling rates, leading to moderate precipitate stability and hardness. The OW mixture, with a 20:80 oil-to-water ratio, achieves better thermal management than DF, resulting in higher hardness values. Tensile strength

Tensile properties of FSWed joints and BM were obtained using tensile tests, as illustrated in Fig. 9. It can be seen that joints with in-process cooling showed relatively lower tensile and yield strength than the BM. The tensile strength increased with decreasing the cooling medium temperature, owing to the disintegration of grain and second phase particles, the re-precipitation of strengthening phases, and the pre-existing dislocation during FSW49–51. The maximum tensile strength is found to be 321 MPa in the WC specimen equivalent to ~ 66% of that of the BM. Similarly, the joint yield strength seems to follow a similar behavior to the tensile strength, and the maximum yield strength, 230 MPa, is achieved at the WC ~ 61% of that of the BM. Moreover, the highest ductility ~ 15% is achieved at the WC, equivalent to 30% of the BM. The superior mechanical properties of the WC specimen can be attributed to the water cooling, which provides the most aggressive cooling method with a high rate of heat extraction due to water’s excellent thermal conductivity. This led to rapid quenching in the stir zone immediately after tool passage. Moreover, the rapid quenching enhanced the retention of fine second-phase particles (Al2Cu), which are crucial for precipitation hardening. The increased density of these particles in the microstructure significantly increased the material’s properties.

Further, grain refinement that occurred in the SZ of all the samples can be attributed to an increase in grain boundaries, resulting in preventing dislocation movement and thereby improving alloy strength according to the famous Hall-Petch relation52.

“Joint efficiency, as the ratio of welded joint strength to base metal strength, was used to evaluate the efficacy of the welding process53. Table 3 presents the impact of in-process cooling on joint strength efficiencies. The WC joint exhibited the highest strength efficiency at 65.4%, followed by the OW joint at 61.4%, owing to the fine-grained microstructures uniform second-phase particle distribution and minimized HAZ, while air cooling resulted in lower efficiency due to slower cooling and grain coarsening. In the case of OW, moderate joint efficiency, slightly lower than WC but higher than air-cooled methods, were observed. This can be attributed to the cooling method, which stabilized the temperature gradients, reducing thermal stresses and leading to a balanced microstructure. The DF joint resulted in lower joint efficiency compared to WC and OW due to the relatively slow cooling rate, which allowed for some grain growth and partial recovery of the microstructure. The AC joint demonstrated the lowest efficiency, ~ 41.6% of the base metal due to prolonged exposure to high temperatures, which results in larger grain sizes and uneven particle distribution, which weakened the joint. These findings indicate that the lower temperature in the WC joint during the FSW process facilitated the retention of a higher density of second-phase particles, thereby enhancing joint efficiency.”

Fracture surfaces

To determine the fracture mode and describe the fracture features, an SEM was used to study the fractured surfaces of the tested tensile specimens. The uppermost region of the fractured surface of each sample was selected to capture micrographs. The broken surface of 2219-T6 BM, which showed typical ductile fracture behavior, is presented in Fig. 10(a). As seen in Fig. 10(a), distinctive characteristics such dimples54, second-phase particles, and tear ridges were found. Round or elongated depressions on the fractured surface called dimples, indicated by arrows, show plastic deformation and energy absorbed during fracture, implying the material’s capacity to experience significant plastic deformation prior to failure55,56.

The fractured surfaces of the AC and DF weld joints exhibited a mixture of dimples of various sizes and depth, as can be seen in Fig. 10(b) and 10(c). The AC joint displayed a greater number of larger dimples compared to the DF joint, indicating more significant plastic deformation and ductility. This is likely due to the slower cooling rate in AC, which allows for the retention of some ductile characteristics. The elevated welding temperatures, which promote the dissolution and redistribution of these particles within the matrix, could account for the absence of pronounced second-phase particles in the AC and DF samples. This dissolution reduces the strength efficiency to 41.6% for AC and 58.1% for DF, as the absence of these particles minimizes the potential barriers to dislocation movement and crack propagation.

In contrast, the oil-water (OW) and water-cooled (WC) samples demonstrated more complex fracture behaviors. The OW fracture surface, presented in Fig. 10(d), exhibited smaller dimples than the WC, indicating more ductility and relatively less strength.

The rapid cooling effect of water retained a higher density of second-phase particles in the WC sample, as shown in Fig. 10(e). This retention of Al2Cu particles is critical as they serve as barriers to dislocation movement, thereby impeding crack propagation and enhancing fracture resistance. This results in the highest strength efficiency among the samples, as these particles contribute significantly to the overall mechanical integrity of the joint.

Additionally, tear ridges were also evident over the fractured surfaces, indicating that the OW and WC samples were tearing and separated. The morphology and alignment of Al2Cu particles could cause stress to build up in certain areas, which could help tear ridges spread. Furthermore, the presence of Al2Cu particles in the matrix contributed to dimple formation, where the matrix deforms plastically around the second-phase particles before void nucleation and growth occur. The presence of dimples, second-phase particles, and tear ridges, which are associated with localized plastic deformation, indicated a tearing mode of failure, consistent with ductile fracture behavior.

A notable feature in the WC samples was the presence of cracked Al2Cu particles, as highlighted in Fig. 10(e). This observation suggested that while these particles contributed to the strength of the material by acting as obstacles to dislocation movement, they also became initiation sites for cracks under sufficient stress. The cracking of these hard, brittle particles under load might lead to localized stress concentration, which subsequently triggered a fracture. However, the presence of these particles generally improves the weld’s toughness by hindering crack growth across the weld interface.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the influence of different in-process cooling methods on the microstructure and mechanical properties of friction stir welded (FSW) AA 2219-T6 alloy. The novel approach of comparing normal air, dry fan, oil-water mixture, and water-cooling methods provided valuable insights into optimizing FSW parameters for enhanced joint performance.

The microstructural examination revealed that the cooling method significantly affected the distribution and density of second-phase particles (Al2Cu). The water-cooled condition exhibited the highest density of Al2Cu particles, due to the lower in-process temperature (55 °C), which mitigated particle dissolution.
