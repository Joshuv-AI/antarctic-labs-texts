Abstract
. A reciprocal geodesic on a ( 2 , k , ∞ ) (2,k,\infty) Hecke surface is a geodesic loop based at an even order cone point p p traversing its path an even number of times. Associated to each reciprocal geodesic is the conjugacy class of a hyperbolic element in the ( 2 , k , ∞ ) (2,k,\infty) Hecke group whose axis passes through a cone point that projects to p p . Such an element is called a reciprocal hyperbolic element based at p p . In this paper, we determine the asymptotic growth rate and limiting constant (in terms of word length) of the number of primitive conjugacy classes of reciprocal hyperbolic elements in a Hecke group.

Introduction

Our goal in this paper is to find the growth rate and the asymptotic constants (in terms of word length) of reciprocal geodesics as well as the primitive reciprocal geodesics on Hecke surfaces. For

Let

be an even order cone point of the Hecke surface
X_{k}
. A
reciprocal geodesic based at
p\in X_{k}
is a geodesic that starts and ends at
p
, traversing its image an even number of times. Equivalently, such a geodesic lifts to a hyperbolic element that is the product of two distinct conjugate involutions whose fixed points project to
p
.
Such a hyperbolic element we call a

p
.
Since we are exclusively working with
(2,k,\infty)
surfaces, our reciprocal geodesics are either based at the order
k
(if
k
is even) or the order 2 cone points. The reciprocal geodesics based at the order
k=2m

A\in\Gamma_{k}

B^{m}\in\Gamma_{k}

3.
Outline of proof

For
p
an even order cone point on the Hecke surface
X_{k}
, our interest is in counting conjugacy classes of reciprocal hyperbolic elements based at
p
in the Hecke group

\Gamma_{k}
(see section 1 for the definiton); that is, reciprocal geodesics based at
p
or equivalently
conjugacy classes of reciprocal words in the abstract group

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{k}
, for
3\leq k\leq\infty
.

The counting problem for reciprocal geodesics in a Hecke surface starts out in a similar fashion as the counting problem for the modular surface (
k=3
case). Namely, we look for a normal form in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{k}
of the conjugacy class that represents a reciprocal geodesic on the surface. This is the set of elements
\mathcal{N}
discussed in
Lemma
4.1
. For
k
odd a reciprocal word is the power of a reciprocal primitive word, and the number of conjugate elements in normal form is 2.
However, in the
k\geq 4
even case
there are two complicating features. The first is that there exist non-primitive reciprocal geodesics based at
p\in X_{k}
that are not the power of a primitive reciprocal geodesic based at
p
. In this case, our standard argument for finding the growth rate of primitive geodesics dominating the non-primitive geodesics breaks down. One such example of this phenomenon occurs with the word

ab^{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{4}
. This word
is not a reciprocal word but its power
(ab^{2})^{2}=ab^{2}ab^{-2}

is a reciprocal word. Note that
ab^{2}
corresponds to a closed geodesic that passes through both cone points.

The second complicating feature has to do with
deciding how many normal forms are conjugate.
In the case of the modular surface, and in fact for
k
odd, it is precisely two (see
[
1
,
16
]
).
However, for Hecke groups when
k

is even there are normal forms that are not conjugate to any other normal form. Returning to the example in

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{4}
, the normal form of a reciprocal word based at the order two cone point is

[a,h]
,
h
a
(bb)
-word. Hence,

(ab^{2})^{2}=ab^{2}ab^{-2}
is a reciprocal word in normal form and it is easy to see there is no other conjugate one in this form. On the other hand,
abab^{-1}
and
ab^{-1}ab
are conjugate reciprocal words based at the order 2 in normal form. While this may not be a concern for rough bounds it is a serious concern to determine whether an asymptotic growth rate exists and what it in fact is.

\Gamma_{2m}
,
k=2m
.

According to Proposition
4.5
item (1), there are two normal forms that are conjugate in

\mathcal{N}_{2t}-\mathcal{B}_{2t}
and thus represent the same element in
R_{2t}
.
Hence

(6.6)

\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{N}_{2t}-\mathcal{B}_{2t}|\leq|R_{2t}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{N}_{2t}-\mathcal{B}_{2t}|+|B_{2t}|.

Dividing by
|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|
:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}-\mathcal{B}_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}\leq\frac{|R_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}-\mathcal{B}_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}+\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}.

Finally letting
t\to\infty
and using the two asymptotics in Lemma

6.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{|R_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}=\frac{1}{2}.

Lastly by Lemma
6.2

(6.7)

\frac{|R_{2t}|}{\beta_{2m}^{t}}=\frac{|R_{2t}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|}{\beta_{2m}^{t}}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\frac{e_{2m}}{2}

The same asymptotic holds for the primitive reciprocal geodesics based at the order
k
cone point since the
\mathcal{B}
-type ones by Lemma
4.3

are negligible since
\beta_{2m}>\sqrt{2}
and the non-primitive words in
\mathcal{N}_{2t}-B_{2t}
are powers of primitive

\mathcal{N}_{2t}-B_{2t}
(as in the

odd case).
∎

7.
Growth in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}

In this section we count conjugacy classes of reciprocal words in
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}
. Let
a
be a generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and we let
b
be a generator of
\mathbb{Z}
. Since reciprocal words have even length we use the parameter
2t
for ease of computation.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}
. Then

|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|=\frac{1}{3}(2^{t}+2(-1)^{t})
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that the following recurrence relation holds for reciprocal words in
G
.

(7.1)

|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|=|\mathcal{N}_{2(t-1)}|+2|\mathcal{N}_{2(t-2)}|

To prove this, we consider a mapping,
\varphi:\mathcal{N}_{2t}\to\mathcal{N}_{2(t-1)}\cup\mathcal{N}_{2(t-2)}
.

Let
w=ab^{x_{1}}ab^{x_{2}}\dots ab^{x_{n}}ab^{-x_{n}}\dots ab^{-x_{2}}ab^{-x_{1}}\in\mathcal{N}_{2t}
. Then we define
\varphi
as the following piecewise function.

(7.2)

\varphi(w)=\begin{cases}ab^{x_{1}-1}ab^{x_{2}}\dots ab^{x_{n}}ab^{-x_{n}}\dots ab^{-x_{2}}ab^{-x_{1}+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{2(t-1)},&\text{if}\,\,x_{1}>1\\
ab^{x_{1}+1}ab^{x_{2}}\dots ab^{x_{n}}ab^{-x_{n}}\dots ab^{-x_{2}}ab^{-x_{1}-1}\in\mathcal{N}_{2(t-1)},&\text{if}\,\,x_{1}<-1\\
ab^{x_{2}}\dots ab^{x_{n}}ab^{-x_{n}}\dots ab^{-x_{2}}\in\mathcal{N}_{2(t-2)},&\text{if}\,\,x_{1}=\pm 1\end{cases}

The mapping
\varphi
is surjective and the first two branches of the map are injective, while the last branch is clearly 2-1. This establishes (
7.1
).

Following the standard method for recurrence relations, we use equation (
7.1
) to get the corresponding characteristic equation,

x^{2}-x-2=0.

The roots are
x=-1
and
x=2
, and this yields the closed form

|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|=c_{1}2^{t}+c_{2}(-1)^{t}.

We then use initial values of the sequence (
|\mathcal{N}_{2}|=0
and
|\mathcal{N}_{4}|=2
) to solve for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
and we get,

(7.3)

|\mathcal{N}_{2t}|=\frac{1}{3}\left(2^{t}+2(-1)^{t}\right).

∎

Our goal in this section is to prove

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
R
be the set of conjugacy classes of reciprocal words in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}
. Then

(7.4)

|R_{2t}|\sim\frac{1}{6}2^{t},\text{ as }t\rightarrow\infty,

and the primitive reciprocal conjugacty classes have the same growth rate.

Denote the primitive and non-primitive reciprocal geodesics of length
2t
by
R^{p}_{2t}
and
R^{np}_{2t}
, respectively.

Lemma 7.3
.

For
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}
,

(7.5)

|R^{np}_{2t}|\leq\frac{1}{6}t2^{t/2}.

Proof.

Fix
t
a positive integer. All sums in this proof are over the proper divisors of
t
. Using the fact that non-primitive reciprocal words of length

2t
are in one to one correspondence with primitive reciprocals of word length
2s
where
s
divides

we have,

(7.6)

|R^{np}_{2t}|=\sum|R^{p}_{2s}|\leq\sum|R_{2s}|=\sum\frac{1}{6}(2^{s}+2(-1)^{s})\leq\frac{2}{6}\sum 2^{s}

Using the fact that the largest possible proper divisor of
t
is
s=t/2
, we have that the right hand side of equation (
7.6
) is,

(7.7)

\leq\frac{2}{6}2^{t/2}\sum 1\leq\frac{1}{6}t2^{t/2},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the most number of proper divisors an integer
t
can have is
t/2
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Using item (1) of Proposition
4.4
, we know that each normal form has a conjugate. Hence, we divide equation (

) by 2 to obtain the growth rate of the conjugacy classes of reciprocal words.

To prove the same asymptotic for the primitive reciprocal words, we apply Lemma

to get

(7.8)

|R_{2t}|-\frac{1}{6}t2^{t/2}\leq|R^{p}_{2t}|\leq|R_{2t}|,

and hence,

(7.9)

Using this inequality and the fact that

∎

8.
Properties of the polynomial families

Consider the two polynomial families,

(8.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr }P_{2m+1}(x)=x^{m+1}-2x^{m-1}-2x^{m-2}-\dots-2x-2,\text{ for $m\geq 2,k=2m+1$}\\
Q_{2m}(x)=x^{m+1}-2x^{m-1}-2x^{m-2}-\dots-2x-1,\text{ for $m\geq 2,k=2m$}\\
\end{array}\right\}

Lemma 8.1
.

Each of the polynomials
Q_{2m}
and
P_{2m+1}
in expression (
8.1
) have distinct complex roots and a unique positive real root which is dominant over its other roots. The polynomial
P_{2m+1}
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Let
P
be one of the two polynomial types.
Descartes rule of signs says that
a polynomial with
real coefficients has positive real zeros equal to the number of sign changes or is less than the number of sign changes by an even number. Since
P
has only one sign change we may conclude it has a unique positive root which we call
r
.

Next we show that
r
is the dominant root. It is a classical result of Cauchy, called the Cauchy bound, that a polynomial
of the form

x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+...+a_{0},

has zeros in the closed disc of radius
r>0
, where
r
is the unique positive real solution of

x^{n}-|a_{n-1|}x^{n-1}-...-|a_{0}|

Next we claim that no other root of
P
has absolute value greater than or equal to
r
. if
\xi
is a root of
Q_{2m}
with
|\xi|=r
, then

r^{m+1}=|\xi^{m+1}|=|2\xi^{m-1}+2\xi^{m-2}+\dots+2\xi+1|

\leq 2|\xi|^{m-1}+2|\xi|^{m-2}+\dots+2|\xi|+1

=2r^{m-1}+2r^{m-2}+\dots+2r+1=r^{m+1}

Now the only way the inequality is an equality for
m\geq 2
is if

\xi
is positive real. Hence
\xi=r
, and thus
r
is the dominant root for
Q_{2m}
.
The same argument works for the other polynomial family

\{P_{2m+1}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
. We have established that both families have a unique positive real root that is dominant over all roots.

P_{2m+1}
, by the Eisenstein criteria (
[
5
]
) is irreducible over

\mathbb{Q}
, hence over the integers.
Now any monic irreducible polynomial
f
over

\mathbb{Q}
has no repeated roots in
\mathbb{C}
. For otherwise, the greatest common divisor of
f
and its derivative
f^{\prime}
would be non-trivial implying that
f
is reducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Thus

P_{2m+1}
is irreducible and has distinct roots.

We next consider the polynomial
Q_{2m}
. Here the Eisenstein Criterion does not apply. Instead, we note that the characteristic polynomial,
Q_{2m}(z)
, in this case can be factored as

(x+1)(x^{m}-x^{m-1}-x^{m-2}-\dots-z-1)=(x+1)S(x).

Focusing on the second factor, we can rule out
-1
as a root of the second factor since upon substitution,

(8.2)

S(-1)=\begin{cases}\text{ }1,&\text{if m even}\\
-2,&\text{if m odd}\end{cases}

Thus we see that
S(x)
does not share a root with the factor
(x+1)
, and moreover, it has simple roots (see
[
21
]
). We can now conclude that
Q_{2m}
has distinct roots.
∎

Denote the positive real root of
P_{2m+1}
by

\alpha_{2m+1}
, and the positive real root of
Q_{2m}
by

\beta_{2m}
.
Thus we have the odd sequence of dominant roots

\{\alpha_{2m+1}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
and the even sequence of dominant roots

\{\beta_{2m}\}_{m=2}^{\infty}
.

Lemma 8.2

(Properties of the dominant roots)
.

Let
\alpha_{2m+1}
be the dominant root of
P_{2m+1}
, and
\beta_{2m}
the dominant root of
Q_{2m}
.

(1)

The dominant roots
\{\alpha_{2m+1}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of the polynomials
\{P_{2m+1}\}
form an increasing sequence and satisfy

\sqrt{2}\leq\alpha_{2m+1}\leq 2
, where

\alpha_{3}=\sqrt{2}
, and
\alpha_{\infty}=2

(2)

The dominant roots
\{\beta_{2m}\}_{m=2}^{\infty}
of the polynomials
\{Q_{2m}\}

form an increasing sequence and satisfy

\sqrt{2}<\beta_{2m}<2

(3)

\beta_{2m}<\alpha_{2m+1}
for
m=2,3,4,...
.

Proof.

Note that
P_{2m+1}(\sqrt{2})<0
and
P_{2m+1}(2)>0
coupled with the fact that the positive root
\alpha_{2m+1}
is unique imply

\sqrt{2}<\alpha_{2m+1}<2
. Now, a straightforward calculation shows that
P_{2m+3}<P_{2m+1}
for

0<x<2
, and thus the roots

\{\alpha_{2m+1}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}

are increasing. This proves item (1).

For item (2), a computation shows

Q_{2m}(\sqrt{2})<0
and
Q_{2m}(2)=3
. So,

\sqrt{2}<\beta_{2m}<2
. Also, a short computation yields

Q_{2m+2}<Q_{2m}
and thus

\{\beta_{2m}\}_{m=2}^{\infty}
is an increasing sequence.

Finally, item (3) follows from

P_{2m+1}(x)-Q_{2m}(x)=-1<0
, and so

P_{2m+1}(x)<Q_{2m}(x)
and therefore the unique positive zero of

Q_{2m}
must occur before the unique positive zero of
P_{2m+1}
.
∎

9.
The linear system associated to a recurrence relation (solving for coefficients)

Let
P
be one of the polynomials
P_{2m+1}
or
Q_{2m}
.
Then
P
has
m+1
distinct roots, and a dominant positive root (Lemma
8.1
). Denote the roots by
\lambda_{0},...,\lambda_{m}
and designate
\lambda_{0}
to be the dominant positive root. A general solution for the counting (linear recurrence) problem has the form

g(t)=a_{0}\lambda_{0}^{t}+...+a_{m}\lambda_{m}^{t}

Now assume the initial conditions are

g(0)=b_{0},g(1)=b_{1},...,g(m)=b_{m}

we obtain the
(m+1)\times(m+1)
linear system

(9.1)

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
\lambda_{0}&\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{m}\\
\lambda_{0}^{2}&\lambda_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{m}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{0}^{m}&\lambda_{1}^{m}&\cdots&\lambda_{m}^{m}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}\\
b_{1}\\
\vdots\\
b_{m}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Denoting the unique solution as
(a_{0},,,,a_{m})
, we see that
the constant in the asymptotic solution of the counting problem is the coefficient
a_{0}
.

Remark 9.1
.

The initial conditions need not start at

. In that case the above linear system should be adjusted appropriately.

Thus the general solution of this recursion problem is

, and

is the dominant root. It is approximately

. Now for

, we can read off the initial conditions as

,
