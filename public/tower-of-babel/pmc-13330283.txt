Compartment-specific phase compensation between pulmonary T-Cell immunity and waning systemic responses in BA.5 breakthrough infection
Authors: Peiyu Huang, Xinyue Mei, Hao Xu, Qi Wan, Xiaohui Wu, Jieqi Peng, Kunning Zhou, Gang Yang, Li Cheng, Shijun Bu, Zhishan Deng, Fan Wu, Cuiqiong Dai, Daxiang Chen, Qi Luo, Xi Sun, Jie Su, Shidong Deng, Siying Wang, Yufen Liu, Pixin Ran, Zhongfang Wang, Yumin Zhou

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpatiotemporal coordination of SARS-CoV-2-specific immunity across pulmonary and systemic compartments is poorly defined, especially how chronic lung diseases modulate this. We hypothesized that profiling virus-specific T cells (VSTs) and antibodies in respiratory vs. blood samples would reveal compartmentalized dynamics critical for viral control.MethodsIn 64 mild Omicron BA.5 breakthrough-infected participants (30 early [D7], 34 later [D14]), we performed: 1) Paired sputum/PBMCs flow cytometry for CD4⁺/CD8⁺ VSTs; 2) Systemic antibody titration (NAbs, anti-N IgG); 3) Compartmental cytokine profiling; 4) Stratified analysis of GOLD I-II COPD (n = 28) vs controls (n = 36).ResultsPulmonary CD4⁺ VSTs increased 2.8-fold from D7 to D14, contrasting with delayed CD8⁺ VSTs mobilisation, while no sputum-blood VSTs correlations existed in either phase (all P > 0.05). Phase-dependent immune synergy was observed in that early viral control was driven by blood CD8⁺ VSTs-NAbs coordination (CT value: r = 0.43; NAbs: r = 0.41; both P < 0.05), whereas later-phase pulmonary CD4⁺ VSTs expanded reciprocally to waning systemic antibodies (NAbs: r = -0.54, P < 0.01), indicating local compensation. Pulmonary cytokine dominance was evident as sputum IL-1β/IL-6/RANTES inversely correlated with viral titer (CT value) at D7 (all P < 0.05), exhibiting stronger virological associations than their plasma counterparts. Additionally, GOLD I-II COPD patients showed no impairment in viral clearance, NAbs titres, or VSTs magnitudes versus controls (all P > 0.05).﻿ConclusionsNon-invasive sputum analysis reveals dynamic SARS-CoV-2 immunity: systemic effectors dominate early control, while pulmonary CD4⁺ VSTs compensate during antibody decline, underscoring the need for phase-specific therapeutic regimens targeting distinct compartments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03701-7.Keywords: Virus-specific T cells, SARS-CoV-2, Compartmentalized immunity, Sputum-based detection, COPD

Introduction

The triumvirate of neutralizing antibodies (NAbs), anti-nucleocapsid (anti-N) IgG, and virus-specific T cells (VSTs) constitutes the cornerstone of SARS-CoV-2 containment [1–5] and facilitates coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) recovery [6], yet their spatiotemporal coordination across anatomical compartments remains enigmatic. While NAbs are established as critical mediators of viral neutralization and clearance [7, 8], emerging evidence highlights the prognostic significance of early VSTs responses. Moderbacher et al. elucidated that robust CD4+/CD8+ VSTs responses correlate with attenuated disease severity [9, 10], while Wang et al. identified associations between early convalescent CD4+ VSTs levels and sustained NAbs maintenance [11]. However, current understanding derives predominantly from systemic (blood or plasma) immune assessments, with limited characterization of local pulmonary immunity.

Previous investigations have discussed the phenotype of lung T cells and their distribution during acute infection, revealing the complexity of the local immune landscape in the lungs [12]. Phenotypic analyses reveal distinct T cell activation and partial exhaustion profiles in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of severe COVID-19 patients [13]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. found that neutrophils were dominant in respiratory samples from several patients with severe COVID-19, and the level of respiratory cytokines was much higher than that of plasma cytokines [14], which was similarly observed in H7N9 patients [15]. Crucially, no study has systematically mapped the dynamic interplay between pulmonary and systemic immunity across infection phases, nor defined how pre-existing chronic pulmonary conditions modulate this equilibrium.

Current models posit that respiratory viruses such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2 initiate infection through the invasion of respiratory epithelial cells, triggering the recruitment of local professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and engulfment of the antigen. Following this, APCs migrate to draining lymph nodes, where they prime and activate T cells [10, 16, 17]. Activated VSTs then proliferate and are recruited to the infection site, while some of them circulate into the blood [18]. However, emerging evidence suggests alternative viral antigen dissemination to distal lymphoid organs (spleen, bone marrow), potentially generating ectopic VSTs populations [19–22]. The temporal precedence of local T-cell immunity relative to systemic immune responses remains unresolved. Critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the spatiotemporal correlation between tissue-resident and circulating T-cell responses during viral infection. Specifically, whether this correlation exhibits temporal dynamics corresponding to the stage-dependent interplay between antiviral immunity and viral pathogenesis requires elucidation. Key questions include: (1) Do pulmonary-localized T-cell responses demonstrate earlier activation kinetics compared to their peripheral counterparts? (2) Does the local-systemic immune axis exhibit phase-dependent functional dominance? (3) How does pre-existing airway inflammation recalibrate this balance?

To investigate compartmentalized immune responses, we conducted the first comparative analysis of VSTs in sputum versus blood from this COVID-19 cohort study involving healthy controls and GOLD I/II chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients. Our dual-phase design examined early (around 7 days post-infection) and later (around 14 days post-infection) immunological events, specifically analyzing three critical interfaces: sputum-blood VSTs dynamics, correlations between pulmonary immunity, viral clearance efficiency, and systemic antibody responses, as well as COPD-mediated immunological alterations given its established association with pulmonary immune dysregulation [23–25]. By stratifying COPD patients as a model of local immune dysfunction, we further assessed how pre-existing lung immunity impacts SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell trafficking, antiviral coordination between pulmonary and systemic compartments, and ultimately viral persistence outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study investigated immune responses in 64 mild Omicron BA.5 breakthrough-infected patients from the ECOPD cohort [26], stratified by time post-symptom onset (referred to as post-infection, p.i.) to capture dynamic immunological changes. Patients were divided into two groups: (1) D7 group (n = 30) sampled at ~ 7 days p.i., including 4 normal lung function and 26 GOLD I-II COPD cases; (2) D14 group (n = 34) sampled at ~ 14 days p.i., with 7 normal and 27 COPD cases (Supplementary Table 1). COPD classification followed GOLD guidelines using post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.70 [27]. This study was approved by the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University Ethics Committee (No.2020–51), with written informed consent from all participants.

Sex as a biological variable

Sex was self-reported by the participants (D7 group: 26 males and 4 females, D14 group: 23 males and 11 females, see Supplementary Table 1). To assess potential imbalance between the two groups, we performed a chi-square test, which showed no statistically significant difference in sex distribution (p = 0.134). However, due to the limited number of female participants in D7 group (n = 4), statistical power is insufficient to conduct meaningful sex-based comparisons of immune responses during early infection. Therefore, while the overall sex composition is balanced between groups, conclusions regarding sex-specific differences in the early phase cannot be drawn from this cohort.

Lung function test

Lung function tests were performed using a conventional spirometer (CareFusion MasterScreen Pneumo, Germany) to acquire pre- and post-bronchodilator measurements. Participants received 400 μg salbutamol sulfate aerosol via metered-dose inhaler, with spirometric evaluations performed 20 min post-administration. All testing procedures strictly followed the joint technical standards established by the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society [28, 29].Final analyses utilized post-bronchodilator values as the primary pulmonary function parameters.

Viral RNA detection in throat swabs

Throughout the course of the infection (approximately 7 or 14 days p.i.), throat swab samples were collected from the patients for viral titer (CT value) quantification. RNA extraction and detection were performed using NMPA-approved nucleic acid detection kits (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China) targeting the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) gene. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) amplification was conducted following manufacturer protocols, with cycle threshold (CT) values interpreted as follows: CT < 40 was defined as positive detection, CT ≥ 40 or indeterminate readings were considered negative. Additionally, based on established literature linking CT values to infectious viral shedding [30, 31], a CT value > 35 was used as a reference in subsequent analyses to indicate a low likelihood of culturable virus.

Induction of sputum supernatant and sputum cell separation preparation

Following established protocols [32], participants underwent standardized sputum induction after oral rinsing with sterile water. A 3% hypertonic saline solution was aerosolized via ultrasonic nebulizer (output 1.5 mL/min) during 7-min inhalation sessions. Expectorated samples were immediately evaluated by light microscopy (10 × magnification), with specimens containing < 20% squamous epithelial cells selected for processing. Qualified samples were weighed and processed within 3 h of collection to preserve cellular integrity. For supernatant preparation, sputum aliquots were homogenized in PBS containing 0.2% dithiothreitol (DTT), vortex-mixed, then sequentially incubated on ice (15 min) and agitated (5 min, 300 rpm). The homogenate was filtered through 70 μm cell strainers (Falcon) and centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were aliquoted and cryopreserved at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1000 µL of complete RP10 (c-RPMI, RPMI 1640 medium [Gibco] enriched with supplements, including 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum [Biological Industries, Israel Beit-Haemek], 100 μM MEM nonessential amino acids [Gibco], 100 U/mL penicillin [Gibco], 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin [Gibco], 2 mM L-glutamine [Gibco], 25 mM HEPES [Gibco], 55 μM 2-mercaptoethanol [Gibco], and 1 mM sodium pyruvate [Gibco]) and maintained at 4 °C for immediate ex vivo stimulation experiments.

PBMCs isolation

PBMCs were isolated from heparinized whole blood within 24 h of collection using density-gradient sedimentation with Ficoll–Paque, following the instructions provided by the manufacturer (GE Healthcare, Cat# 17–1440-02). The isolated PBMCs, with a viability exceeding 90%, were cryopreserved for subsequent use. Briefly, cells were resuspended in freezing medium consisting of 90% FBS and 10% DMSO, placed in a controlled-rate freezing container, and stored at −80 °C. After 24 h, the vials were transferred to liquid nitrogen for long-term storage.

Peptide pool design and preparation

The design and synthesis of SARS-CoV-2-specific peptides were conducted as follows [33, 34]. Protein sequences were obtained from the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome (GenBank: MN908947.3). In total, 487 15-mer peptides were designed to encompass complete antigenic regions of the spike receptor-binding domain, spike protein (S), membrane protein (M), nucleocapsid protein (N), and envelope protein (E). These peptides overlapped by 11 amino acids and were generated using the online tool Peptide 2.0. The GL Biochem Corporation (Shanghai, China) synthesized these peptides with a purity level exceeding 80%. In addition, 110 18-mer peptides were synthesized by GL Biochem Corporation (Shanghai) to cover the ORF1ab region of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. These peptides overlapped by 10 amino acids and maintained purity above 80%. Each peptide was dissolved in DMSO and combined to form a stock solution, with each peptide at a concentration of 45 μM. Aliquots of the peptide stock pools were stored at −80 °C to ensure stability throughout the study.

In vitro stimulation of PBMCs and sputum cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (1 × 106) and sputum cells (1 × 106) were cultured in complete RP10 medium. For ex vivo stimulation, these cells were treated overnight with the peptide pool containing 487 15-mer peptides and 110 18-mer peptides (125 nM of each peptide) in the presence of 10 U/mL recombinant IL-2 (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) and 1 ng/mL GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA), maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 [33, 34]. For PBMC samples, parallel unstimulated control wells (no peptides) were included for each donor. For induced sputum samples, due to the inherently limited cellular yield, cells were primarily allocated to the peptide-stimulated condition; background fluorescence was rigorously controlled using a standardized fluorescence minus one (FMO) gating strategy during flow cytometry analysis. Additionally, positive control stimulations using anti‑CD3/CD28 antibodies were included in each experiment to validate T‑cell responsiveness.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs and sputum cells harvested from overnight-stimulated cultures were incubated with live or dead aqua V510 for 15 min at room temperature. Following this, the cells underwent surface staining for 30 min at room temperature using a panel of antibodies: anti-CD3-PECF594 (BD, Cat# 562,280, 1:200, clone UCHT1), anti-CD8-PerCP-Cy5.5 (Biolegend, Cat# 344,710, 1:100, clone SK1), anti-CD14-APC-H7 (BD, Cat# 560,180, 1:100, clone MjP9), anti-CD19-APC-H7 (BD, Cat# 560,177, 1:100, clone SJ25C1), anti-TCR γ/δ-BV421 (Biolegend, Cat# 331,218, 1:100, clone B1), and anti-CD4-BV650 (BD, Cat# 563,875, 1:200, clone SK3). After surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix and Perm (BD Biosciences, Cat# 554,714) on ice in the dark for 30 min. Intracellular staining then followed, also conducted on ice and in darkness for 30 min, utilizing anti-IFN-γ-AF700 (BD, Cat# 557,995, 1:200, clone B27) antibodies. After a final wash, the cells were resuspended in 200 µL of FACS buffer. Samples were acquired using a FACSAria III instrument (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8). Virus-specific T cells were identified as live, singlet, CD3⁺CD4⁺IFN-γ⁺ or CD3⁺CD8⁺IFN-γ⁺ cells. The absolute number of VSTs was calculated as follows: for peripheral blood, VSTs per mL of blood were derived by multiplying the flow cytometry frequency by the donor’s absolute lymphocyte count per mL from clinical blood tests; for induced sputum, VSTs per 10⁶ plated cells were calculated by multiplying the frequency by 1 × 10⁶ (the total number of cells placed in stimulation). For peripheral blood, VSTs frequencies were calculated after background subtraction using the matched unstimulated (negative) control for each sample; for induced sputum, a virus‑specific T‑cell response was considered positive if the frequency of IFN‑γ⁺ cells among CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cells reached ≥ 0.02%.

iFlash-SARS-CoV-2 IgG assay

Whole blood samples were centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min to obtain plasma samples. As previously described [33], the detection of anti-N IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 was performed using an iFlash Immunoassay Analyzer (Shenzhen Yhlo Biotech Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of plasma sample was incubated with SARS-CoV-2 antigen-coated paramagnetic microparticles to allow antibody binding. After adding an acridinium-labeled anti-human IgG conjugate and trigger solutions to the reaction mixture, the iFlash optical system was used to measure the anti-N IgG titer.

SARS-CoV-2 conventional virus neutralization test

A cytopathic effect assay was used to assess the neutralizing capacity of plasma against the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5 variant in a certified BSL-3 laboratory, as described previously [35]. Initially, 25 μL of plasma sample was diluted at a 1:8 ratio, followed by serial twofold dilutions across eight points. Each assay was performed in duplicate. The samples were combined with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5 viral solution containing 100 TCID50 of the virus. Following a 2-h incubation of the virus-plasma mixture at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the mixture was added to a 96-well plate containing 1.2 × 104 Vero E6 cells. The plate was subsequently incubated for 4 days at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The cytopathic effects of the plate were examined using a Celigo Imaging Cytometer (Nexcelom Bioscience, Lawrence, MA, USA). The presence or absence of cytopathic effects was determined by comparison with positive control wells (plasma samples exhibiting high neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2 in infected Vero E6 cells) and negative control wells (human serum samples negative for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and neutralization assay, as well as Vero E6 cells alone).

Cytometric Bead Array (CBA)

Cytokine quantification was performed using the CBA Human Soluble Protein Flex Set System (BD Biosciences) within a CBA framework using a BD FACS Verse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). This assay facilitated the detection of IL-1β, IL-6, MIP-1α, and RANTES levels in both plasma and sputum supernatants. The procedure adhered strictly to the manufacturer's protocol. The analyte signal intensities were quantified relative to the respective standards, with absolute concentrations of individual analytes calculated using BD FCAP Array Software (BD Biosciences, USA).

Statistical analysis

For continuous data, those that follow a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and intergroup comparisons are conducted using an independent sample t-test. For non-normally distributed quantitative data, the median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) (M (Q1, Q3)) is used to represent the data, and statistical significance was evaluated using a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test for multiple comparisons. Correlations were examined using two-tailed Spearman's correlation coefficient. Antibody titer is presented as geometric.

mean titer (GMT) with 95% confidence interval (Cl). All analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, GraphPad Software) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25). The plots were crafted utilizing the R software (version 4.2.2) packages "corrplot" (version 0.92) and "ggplot2" (version 3.4.2) via Hiplot Pro (https://hiplot.com.cn/) and the Scientific illustrations were generated using BioRender (https://biorender.com). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Cohort characteristics and immune profiling in mild SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5 breakthrough infection

To investigate the compartmentalized immune responses of the respiratory tract and blood during SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5 infection, a cohort of 64 participants with mild COVID-19 was established, stratified by disease progression timeline: 30 patients sampled around 7 days p.i. (D7 group) and 34 individuals around 14 days p.i. (D14 group). Comprehensive biospecimen collection included paired peripheral blood (29/30 in D7, 34/34 in D14) and induced sputum samples (19/30 in D7, 25/34 in D14), with throat swabs obtained from all participants concurrently at the time of sampling (Fig. 1a). Our multi-modal analytical approach comprised: 1) Viral RNA quantification via RT-qPCR of throat swabs; 2) Systemic immunity assessment through plasma NAbs, anti-N IgG titer, and cytokine/chemokine profiling; 3) Respiratory inflammation analysis via sputum cytokine/chemokine measurement; and 4) Leukocyte subset characterization using flow cytometry of both PBMCs and sputum-derived cellular suspensions.

Negative correlation between viral load and humoral response in the early phase

Virological monitoring of pharyngeal swabs revealed significant temporal dynamics in viral burden. Median cycle threshold (CT) values increased from 32.21 (IQR 28.11–35.36) at D7 to 36.60 (IQR 33.42–40.00) at D14 (P < 0.001), indicating a 4-log10 reduction in upper respiratory tract viral burden (Fig. 1b, left panel). The D14 CT values exceeded the diagnostic cutoff of 35 associated with non-infectious viral load,consistent with the established viral clearance timeline [30, 31]. Concomitant immunological analysis demonstrated a borderline significant elevation of plasma anti-N IgG titer in D14 versus D7 (P = 0.06; Fig. 1b, middle panel), while NAbs titer showed comparable upward trends (P = 0.12; Fig. 1b, right panel). Notably, early-phase (D7) analysis uncovered a positive correlation between anti-N IgG titer and virus titer (CT value) (Spearman r = 0.46, P = 0.01; Fig. 1g, top left panel), interpreted as humoral immunity being associated with more effective viral clearance.

No correlation between sputum and blood VSTs during early or later phases

To explore whether VSTs in the lung region and systemic compartment synergistically participate in the process of virus clearance, comparative immunophenotyping of VSTs in paired induced sputum and PBMCs specimens was performed, revealing distinct patterns of CD4+ and CD8+ VSTs activation during early infection (D7) and later phases (D14). Notably, CD4+ VSTs exhibited robust activation, with a 91.67% detection frequency in peripheral blood (median 87.90 cells/mL [IQR 21.30–238.11], 22/24) compared with 73.68% in sputum samples (13.80 cells/106 mononuclear cells [1.00–52.70], 14/19) at D7. This compartmentalized response showed dynamic evolution, with blood detection rates increasing to 93.93% (109.44 cells/mL [55.87–244.55], 31/33) and pulmonary responses reaching 68.00% (38.70 cells/106 mononuclear cells [1.00–116.00], 17/25) by D14. The 2.8-fold increase in sputum CD4+ VSTs magnitude from D7 to D14 coincided with the timeline of viral clearance. In contrast, CD8+ VSTs showed a delayed mobilization pattern with distinct differences between compartments. Early-phase detection was limited to 45.83% in blood (1.00 cells/mL [1.00–36.30], 11/24) and 26.31% in sputum (1.00 cells/106 mononuclear cells [1.00–3.26], 5/19) at D7. Remarkably, CD8+ responses underwent significant expansion by D14, with blood detection rising 1.39-fold to 63.64% (18.41 cells/mL [1.00–52.29], 21/33) and pulmonary responses increasing 1.37-fold to 36.00% (38.70 cells/106 mononuclear cells [1.00–116.00], 9/25). This temporal divergence between CD4+ and CD8+ VSTs kinetics suggests distinct mechanistic roles in viral containment versus clearance. Despite the same activation patterns, our analysis uncovered a striking spatial uncoupling of SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell responses. VSTs showed no significant cross-compartment correlations in either CD4+ (early phase D7: r = 0.23, P = 0.43, Fig. 1e; later phase D14: r = −0.03, P = 0.90, Fig. 1f) or CD8+ subsets (D7: r = 0.31, P = 0.28, Fig. 1e; D14: r = 0.11, P = 0.62, Fig. 1f).

Synergistic CD8+ T Cell-humoral crosstalk drives early viral control

Early systemic CD8+ VSTs were associated with humoral immunity and correlated with viral clearance. Early-phase (D7) blood CD8+ VSTs counts positively correlated with viral titer (CT value) (r = 0.43, P = 0.04, Fig. 1g, top right panel), anti-N IgG titer (r = 0.46, P = 0.02, Fig. 1g, bottom left panel), and NAbs (r = 0.41, P = 0.04, Fig. 1g, bottom right panel). These associations were further supported when participants were dichotomized by detectable versus undetectable circulating CD8+ VSTs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Comparison of the D7 and D14 groups revealed synchronized peaks of CD8+ VSTs (blood median: 18.41 cells/mL) and NAbs at D14, suggesting a pattern that could be consistent with proposed models of T cell–B cell cooperation and antibody-mediated modulation of cellular immunity [36].

Pulmonary CD4+ immunity emerges during later phase

In contrast to systemic responses, later-phase pulmonary CD4+ VSTs exhibited inverse relationships with virological and serological markers: sputum CD4+ VSTs negatively correlated with virus titer (CT value) (r = −0.40, P < 0.05, Fig. 1h, top left panel), anti-N IgG titer (r = −0.38, P = 0.06, Fig. 1h, top right panel), and humoral NAbs titer (r = −0.54, P < 0.01, Fig. 1h, bottom panel). This tripartite negative association suggests a potential compensatory dynamic in which elevated viral load (lower CT values) is associated with compensatory CD4+ VSTs expansion. A significant increase in CD4+ VSTs was noted on D14 compared with D7 in humans vaccinated with three doses of inactivated vaccines followed by BA.5 infection. Subgroup stratification by D14 NAbs titer (using the group median of 96) further validated this systemic-pulmonary dichotomy. The high-NAbs subgroup (NAbs ≥ 96) demonstrated marginally reduced viral burden (P = 0.073, Figure S2a) alongside elevated systemic immunity markers, including anti-N IgG (P < 0.001, Figure S2b), leukocytes (P < 0.05, Figure S2c), and neutrophils (P < 0.01, Figure S2d). Notably, peripheral CD4+/CD8+ VSTs showed no intergroup differences (P = 0.935 and 0.121, Figures S2e-f). Conversely, the low-NAbs subgroup (NAbs < 96) exhibited enhanced pulmonary CD4+ VSTs (P < 0.05, Figure S2g) without significant CD8+ VSTs variation (P = 0.780, Figure S2h). The observed dissociation between pulmonary CD4+ VSTs and systemic humoral immunity (NAbs: r = −0.54) supports a model of compartmentalized, compensatory regulation. In this model, lung-resident CD4+ T cells expand independently of waning antibody levels, potentially establishing an antibody-independent defense that contributes to sustained viral control during convalescence.

Sputum cytokine regulation affects virological control dynamics

To delineate pulmonary-systemic crosstalk in antiviral immunity, cytokine mapping across pulmonary (induced sputum) and systemic (plasma) compartments during early (D7) and later (D14) phases was performed. Striking compartmentalization emerged: proinflammatory mediators IL-1β, IL-6, and MIP-1α demonstrated higher concentrations in sputum versus plasma (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a), whereas the level of RANTES in plasma exhibited higher at both time points. (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a). Notably, pulmonary cytokines showed phase-dependent virological correlations. During early infection (D7), sputum IL-1β (r = −0.45, P = 0.01, Fig. 2b), IL-6 (r = −0.37, P = 0.05, Fig. 2b), and RANTES (r = −0.49, P = 0.01, Fig. 2b) inversely correlated with viral titer (CT values), suggesting their dual role in both inflammation and viral containment. This relationship inverted systemically at D14, where plasma RANTES was positively associated with residual virus (r = 0.37, P = 0.04). Notably, Multidimensional correlation analysis uncovered bidirectional cytokine networks. Irrespective of the cohort, whether Day 7 or Day 14, sputum IL-6 consistently showed a significant positive correlation with plasma IL-6 (r = 0.37, P = 0.05, Fig. 2c; r = 0.37, P = 0.04, Fig. 2d), indicating synchronization of the IL-6 axis and systemic propagation of mucosal inflammation. Meanwhile, a notable inverse correlation was observed in the D7 group between plasma IL-1β and sputum IL-6 (r = −0.38, P = 0.04, Fig. 2c), and a significant positive relationship was observed between plasma MIP-1α and both sputum MIP-1α (r = 0.39, P = 0.04, Fig. 2c) and IL-1β (r = 0.38, P = 0.04, Fig. 2c), suggesting chemokine-mediated myeloid cell trafficking during the infection.

No significant differential patterns in viral clearance or antibody responses in GOLD I-II COPD versus healthy individuals

The cohort was further subdivided into GOLD I-II COPD patients and healthy individuals to explore whether the presence of COPD affects viral clearance as well as the levels of NAbs and anti-N IgG titer in the blood. Findings indicated no discernible difference in viral titer (CT value) across various time points (P > 0.05, Fig. 3b) nor in levels of plasma anti-N IgG (P > 0.05, Fig. 3c, left panel) and NAbs titer (P > 0.05, Fig. 3c, right panel) between COPD patients and healthy controls.

No Significant Differences in VSTs Levels in GOLD I-II COPD Versus Healthy Individuals

To investigate whether aberrant local immunity in COPD could interfere with VSTs responses both locally and systemically, immune responses were further examined; however, no statistically significant differences in CD4+ VSTs (P > 0.05, Fig. 4b) and CD8+ VSTs (P > 0.05, Fig. 4d) responses were observed between the two groups. Furthermore, analysis of inflammatory levels revealed that aside from significant differences in plasma RANTES levels, no other inflammatory factors exhibited notable differences (Fig. 4e). Our data indicate that the underlying condition of COPD does not significantly alter viral clearance, overall levels of plasma binding antibodies and NAbs, or most VSTs response levels in COVID-19 patients.

Discussion

Our immune profiling of sputum and blood specimens from patients with mild COVID-19 delineated the temporal dynamic interaction between viral kinetics, humoral immunity, and tissue-resident versus systemic T-cell responses during various phases of BA.5 breakthrough infection. We have identified a dynamic reciprocity between local and systemic immune mechanisms: peripheral VSTs synergized with NAbs during early infection, while sputum-resident VSTs responses exhibited compensatory amplification during the later phase when NAbs titer declined below protective thresholds (Fig. 5). The study of pulmonary T-cell immunity typically depends on invasive and damaging methods, such as obtaining BALF and lung tissue samples. These methods, while effective, are challenging to implement broadly and face limited acceptance in clinical settings. In this study, we used induced sputum as a non‑invasive alternative to investigate VSTs in the proximal airways. While BALF remains the gold standard for distal lung sampling, induced sputum enables practical assessment of mucosal immunity in the proximal airways, which serves as a critical site of initial viral infection and host defense.

This schematic integrates key findings to propose a two-phase response. a Early Phase (Systemic Dominance): Individuals with higher neutralizing antibody (NAbs) titers achieve more rapid viral clearance, mediated primarily by systemic effectors including circulating NAbs and blood CD8+ virus-specific T cells (VSTs). b Later Phase (Pulmonary Compensation): In individuals with lower NAb titers, viral clearance is delayed. During this phase, control is associated with a compensatory expansion of pulmonary CD4+ VSTs, suggesting a shift from systemic humoral to local cellular dominance. The model is derived from the inverse correlations in Fig. 1 and the subgroup analyses in Supplementary Fig. 2. Scientific illustrations were generated using BioRender (https://biorender.com)

The spatial heterogeneity of antigen-specific T-cell responses has been extensively documented [37]. Baumgarth et al. demonstrated influenza-specific T-cell responses display distinct frequency kinetics across mediastinal lymph nodes, lung parenchyma, and airways in murine models [38]. Moreover, Wiley et al. reported that the dynamics and intensity of flu-specific CD8+ T cell responses vary across the cervical lymph nodes, nasal-associated lymphoid tissue and spleen [39]. Grau-Expósito et al. detected memory T cells in lung tissue samples from recovered patients that were not present in blood, suggesting that blood T cell levels may not accurately reflect lung-specific T cell responses during the memory phase [40]. More importantly, from the perspective of natural human infection, we have delineated the dynamic differences between the early and later phases: during the early infection stage, systemic immune responses including circulating NAbs and blood CD8+ VSTs contribute to viral control within the respiratory tract, potentially through the recruitment of immune cells to the site of infection or via systemic modulation of the inflammatory milieu; whereas in the later phase, the compensatory activation of local VSTs may bridge the immune protection gap caused by declining antibody levels. This discovery provides novel insights into the temporal regulation of mucosal immunity during respiratory viral infections.

Emerging evidence highlights the temporal orchestration of adaptive immune components in controlling SARS-CoV-2 infection. Recent vaccination-challenge studies in non-human primates revealed an inverse correlation between early viral replication kinetics (peaking at day 2 post-infection) and circulating NAbs, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cell levels [41]. Notably, two distinct immunodominant profiles emerged—animals with either high-titer NAbs/low CD8+ T cell responses or low-titer NAbs/high CD8+ T cell responses—both demonstrating comparable viral containment efficiency. This functional compensation is further supported by clinical observations where early recruitment of VSTs correlates with accelerated viral clearance [42, 43]. Zhao et al. demonstrated that CD4+ T cells alone confer protection against SARS-CoV in mouse models, even in the absence of antibodies or CD8+ T cells, with airway memory CD4+ T cells being particularly important [44], exemplifying the compartmentalized nature of immune protection. While these findings emphasize the critical timing of immune effector deployment, they leave unresolved the dynamic interplay between cellular immunity, humoral responses, and viral replication kinetics. Our findings indicate a previously unrecognized compensatory mechanism: during the later phase when NAbs titer decline, tissue-resident VSTs emerge as critical secondary defense effectors. This challenges the traditional understanding that more VSTs at the site of infection should lead to quicker viral clearance. Our results suggest that elevated viral antigen persistence in later infection phases may actively recruit VSTs to pulmonary sites—evidenced by the positive correlation between local CD4+ VSTs frequency and viral load, contrasting with their inverse relationship to systemic anti-N IgG and NAbs levels.

Emerging evidence establishes cytokine elevation (IL-1β, IL-6, MIP-1α) in systemic circulation as biomarkers of COVID-19-associated pneumonitis [1, 45, 46]. However, Wang et al. demonstrated that airway-luminal cytokines (IL-6 in sputum/BALF) exhibit superior correlation with disease severity versus serum counterparts in critical cases [47]. Extending these observations, our study identifies a compartmentalized cytokine signature during early infection: sputum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and RANTES show stronger covariation with viral load than circulating counterparts. This pulmonary predominance suggests that airway-luminal cytokine profiling may offer enhanced resolution for monitoring viral replication dynamics and localized immunopathology compared to blood-based assays.

Contrary to reports linking chronic airway diseases to impaired antiviral immunity [48, 49], our cohort revealed no statistically significant difference in viral clearance kinetics, NAbs titer, or anti-N IgG levels between GOLD I-II COPD patients and healthy controls following mild infection. This challenges the presumed deterministic role of mild-moderate COPD in shaping COVID-19 outcomes, particularly when confounders like inhaled corticosteroids (known to modulate infection risk [50]) are excluded—a critical methodological strength as the ECOPD cohort lacked regular inhaler use. These findings carry immediate clinical relevance: they suggest that stable GOLD I-II COPD may not necessitate escalated COVID-19 management protocols, potentially alleviating healthcare burdens.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the cohort size was modest, and the notable imbalance between subgroups—specifically, the much larger number of COPD patients than healthy controls—likely limited statistical power to identify more nuanced, group-specific immunological features. Second, key datasets, particularly for sputum CD8+ VSTs, contained a high proportion of zero values. While complementary dichotomized and non-parametric analyses supported the robustness of the observed trends, the zero-inflated nature of the data limits quantitative resolution. Future studies employing more sensitive assays or specialized statistical approaches for zero-inflated data will be important to better characterize these responses. Third, and most substantially, the study’s cross-sectional design for sequential time points (with D7 and D14 samples coming from different individuals) prevents the analysis of within-subject immune dynamics and hinders causal inference regarding the observed compensatory response. Fourth, the significant sex imbalance in the early-phase cohort precluded a meaningful investigation of sex as a biological variable in early infection. Finally, the lack of long-term follow-up leaves the durability and protective efficacy of the pulmonary compensatory response against reinfection unresolved. Although our integrated focus on mucosal immunity and comorbidity offers novel insights, these findings require validation in larger, longitudinally sampled cohorts to definitively map temporal immune trajectories.

In conclusion, by employing a non-invasive sputum sampling strategy, our study delineates the compartmentalized and temporal dynamics of antiviral immunity during mild Omicron BA.5 breakthrough infection. We identify a pivotal compensatory role for airway VSTs during convalescence, a phase when systemic humoral immunity wanes. These findings not only advance our understanding of the spatiotemporal coordination of immune defenses in COVID-19 but also underscore the importance of assessing mucosal immunity for a complete picture of host protection. Looking forward, future studies with longitudinal designs, balanced cohorts, and extended follow-up are warranted to validate the durability of this compensatory response, elucidate its precise molecular mechanisms, and explore its implications for vaccine design and therapeutic strategies against respiratory viral infections.
