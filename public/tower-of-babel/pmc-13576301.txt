Implementing integrated mental health and substance use disorder services in Indian primary care: a multi-state CFIR-based qualitative study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrating mental health & substance use disorders (MSUDs) care into other NCDs remains a priority for bridging India’s wide treatment gap. While national programs such as District Mental Health Programme and Ayushman Bharat have laid foundational pathways, translating policy into everyday practice continues to face substantial challenges. This study explored how mental health services are being implemented across seven Indian states, using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to unpack the multilevel factors influencing sevice delivery.MethodsWe undertook a qualitative implementation study in Assam, Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Punjab. The data included 211 in-depth interviews and 28 focus group discussions across seven states for the following cadres: medical officers (MO), community health officers (CHOs), Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA), nurses, clinical psychologists, patients, caregivers, and policymakers. A combination of CFIR framework-guided and inductive thematic analysis was used to generate both cross-cutting and site-specific insights.ResultsWhile most respondents supported mental health integration in principle, they reported barriers such as strained frontline capacities, absence of structured protocols, stigma-related avoidance, limited availability of medicines and inconsistent referral pathways. Training uptake was uneven, and consultation times were often too brief to manage mental health needs effectively. However, promising practices were identified, e.g. Gujarat’s “Therapeutic Thursdays,” Haryana’s use of teleconsultation, and community mobilization efforts in Odisha and Assam. Increased Workload, lack of mental health-specific incentives, and digital infrastructure gaps were common challenges. Engagement from local champions (Individuals who dedicate themselves to supporting, marketing, and ‘driving through’ an implementation) was perceived to help adress structural gaps, but was rarely systematized.ConclusionThis study highlights how integration efforts are shaped by the depth of contextual dynamics. Background

Mental health and Substance Use Disorders (MSUDs) are the leading cause of disability worldwide, accounting for 416 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), amounting to 16% of global DALYs [1]. In India, the prevalence of common mental disorders—such as depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders—remains high, yet the treatment gap exceeds 70% [2]. Integrating mental health care into primary healthcare systems has emerged as a global strategy to reduce this gap, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where specialist resources are scarce [3].

India has made incremental progress in integrating mental health services within the broader health system, guided by national initiatives such as the National and District Mental Health Programme (DMHP) and, more recently, the National Health Mission and Ayushman Bharat’s Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (AAMs) [4, 5]. These Policy reforms emphasize task-sharing approaches wherein non-specialist providers—including community health workers, nurses, and primary care physicians—are trained to deliver basic mental health services. Evidence from pilot projects and implementation studies from different parts of India supports the feasibility of such integration efforts, although scale-up and sustainability remain complex challenges [6].

There is global evidence from LMICs demonstrating several models and strategies for integration, from collaborative care to community-based psychosocial interventions. For example , non-specialist-delivered transdiagnostic psychological interventions have shown moderate-to-high effectiveness in reducing symptoms of common mental disorders [7]. A recent systematic review found that digital technologies, when combined with task-sharing approaches, may increase the reach and fidelity of mental health care in underserved populations [8]. However, successful implementation is often contingent on contextual factors such as community engagement, provider motivation, infrastructure, quality of training , and local leadership [9, 10].

In India, diverse state-specific contexts influence how integrated mental health care is conceptualized and operationalized. Case studies from 72 programs across India reveal variation in models, ranging from NGO-led initiatives to public-private partnerships, and highlight the need for flexible, locally responsive approaches [6]. Evaluations from states like Madhya Pradesh and Goa demonstrate that primary care-based screening and treatment can be effective, if there is adequate training, supervision, and support for frontline workers [11, 12]. Nonetheless, barriers such as stigma, workforce shortages, weak referral linkages, and inconsistent policy implementation continue to hinder the scaling up of such services.

Integrating the mental health care into the existing community-level non-communicable disease (NCD) service delivery framework is a potential way forward in the India. Mental health disorders are more likely to co-occur with other NCDs. A meta-analysis of studies from the Indian sub-continent showed prevalence of depression in up to 44% of patients with NCD [13]. Similarly, a study on a community-based sample from North India found that 34–42% population with CMD had hypertension and 32–71% had central or generalized obesity [14]. NCDs and mental health disorders share common risk factors, which explain the higher co-occurrence [15]. Therefore, an integrated treatment approach would be acceptable, feasible, and effective [16].

Several landmark Indian implementation studies provide important foundations for understanding what enables (and limits) scale-up of community and primary care mental health care. PRIME in Sehore district, Madhya Pradesh, demonstrated the feasibility of implementing a district-level mental health care plan using task-sharing and structured training/supervision, while also highlighting the importance of health system readiness and sustained implementation support [17]. VISHRAM, implemented in Vidarbha (Maharashtra), improved contact coverage for depression care through grassroots, community-based model, underscoring the value of community outreach and demand generation [18]. The Atmiyata programme in rural Gujarat demonstrated the effectiveness of community volunteer–led identification and psychosocial support for common mental disorders, emphasizing community embeddedness and lay-worker enablement [19]. Finally, the SMART Mental Health programme in Andhra Pradesh combined technology-enabled decision support with anti-stigma efforts and primary health care worker training, illustrating the potential role of digital tools to improve detection and linkage to care [20]. Together, these programmes highlight diverse implementation pathways in India and underscore the need for multi-site qualitative research to identify contextual determinants that shape real-world delivery. Given the complexity of implementing integrated mental health services in the existing care context, robust frameworks are essential for understanding what works, where, and why. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) offers a comprehensive structure for examining multilevel implementation determinants, including characteristics of the intervention, inner and outer setting, individuals involved, and implementation processes [21]. Through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with a diverse range of stakeholders, including primary care physicians, community health workers, nurses, patients, caregivers, and policymakers, we aim to explore the facilitators and barriers to implementing mental health care among other NCDs at the primary care level. By capturing the perspectives across varied geographies and health system contexts, our study contributes to the emerging evidence base on the contextual realities of mental health service integration in India. This research study aims to explore the facilitators and barriers influencing the implementation of MSUD services in primary health care settings across seven Indian states, using the CFIR. The objectives of the study include (1) To identify multilevel determinants affecting implementation across CFIR domains (outer setting, inner setting, intervention characteristics, individuals, and implementation process) (2). To describe stakeholder perspectives on workforce capacity, referral pathways, medicines availability, stigma, and digital support systems (3). To synthesize cross-cutting and site-specific implementation insights to inform scalable strategies for strengthening primary health care mental health services.

Methods

Study design and theoretical framework

The current study is a qualitative study constituting Phase-1 formative research of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR)-supported ICMR-MINDS implementation study titled “ICMR’s Multistate Implementation Research Study on Integration of Screening and Management of Mental and Substance Use Disorders with Other Non-Communicable Diseases” (ICMR-MINDS) [22]. The ICMR-MINDS Implementation study is designed as a multi-phase implementation research study design to examine implementation of integrated MSUD services at the primary health care level across seven states in India (Assam, Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Punjab). It comprises of three phases: Phase I (Formative research), Phase II (Model Optimization [Mx]), and Phase III (Full implementation and evaluation). The current study (Phase I) aimed to undertake a situation analysis to understand the local context, existing service delivery pathways, and implementation challenges relevant to integrating MSUD services within NCD care services. Findings from this formative phase will be used to inform the design, adaptation, and implementation strategies of the subsequent intervention phase of the ICMR MINDS study. The overarching aim of ICMR MINDS are to develop and assess the feasibility of an integrated service delivery model using integrated digital assessment tools to achieve a high coverage (≥ 70%) of screening, management, and linkage to care for common MSUDs among individuals seeking NCD care in public health facilities, and to evaluate the costs associated with implementation of the proposed model.

The study was anchored in the CFIR, which provides a structured approach to identify factors affecting implementation across five major domains: intervention characteristics, inner setting, outer setting, characteristics of individuals, and implementation process. CFIR was selected due to its strength in unpacking multilevel determinants in complex health systems, aligning with India’s decentralized primary care system.

The intervention in the ICMR MINDS study comprises a standardized, multi-component, service delivery model designed to, Integrate MSUDs care into primary health care settings. This model includes the use of integrated digital assessment tools to support screening, clinical decision-making, and follow-up, alongside structured workflows, capacity building, and community engagement strategies [22]. We have used COREQ checklist to improve the transparency, completeness, and rigor of reporting of qualitative study (Supplementary File 4).

Study settings and context

India’s states vary greatly in population, languages, cultures, and health-system capacity, which leads to significant variation in health needs and service delivery contexts. Demographic diversity—from densely urban to isolated rural and tribal regions— intersects with vast differences in workforce availability, infrastructure, insurance coverage, and public health investment. These variations influence disease patterns, access to care, and the feasibility of programme implementation, highlighting the need for context-specific, state-level planning in national health initiatives. The Mental health services at the primary care level in India are primarily provided through the District Mental Health Programme (DMHP), a component of the National Mental Health Programme (NMHP). These services are delivered through Ayushman Arogya Mandir (AAM), previously known as Health and Wellness Centres, India’s network of comprehensive primary care facilities [29, 30]. The DMHP’s objective is to decentralize mental health care by offering screening, basic treatment, referrals, and follow-up services within district hospitals, community health centres (CHCs), primary health centres (PHCs), and sub-health facilities [29]. As of 2024–25, the DMHP is operational in more than 767 districts throughout India, thereby encompassing roughly 90% of the nation.

Within the AAM, comprehensive primary health care includes screening and treatment for common mental disorders (CMDs), substance use disorders (SUDs), and neurological conditions, along with other non-communicable diseases [30, 31]. Service delivery at the primary care level is supported by standardized screening instruments, the availability of essential psychotropic medications, referral networks, and periodic specialist assistance provided by DMHP teams [30, 31].

Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) serve as community-based, frontline agents, concentrating on mental health awareness, stigma reduction, and the identification of individuals potentially experiencing mental health or substance use issues during routine household visits; they also facilitate care-seeking by connecting beneficiaries with AAMs or higher-level facilities [20, 32]. Furthermore, ASHAs contribute to treatment adherence and follow-up care through community engagement and home-based monitoring [32].

Community health officers (CHOs), stationed at AAMs, are tasked with opportunistic and focused screening employing standardized tools, delivering fundamental psychosocial support and brief interventions, overseeing stable patients, and directing moderate to severe cases to PHCs, CHCs, or DMHP specialists [29, 30].

Medical Officers (MOs) at PHCs and more advanced facilities undertake clinical assessment, diagnosis, the initiation and continuation of pharmacological treatment in accordance with national guidelines, the management of comorbidities, and coordination with DMHP psychiatrists for specialist consultation, referral, and supervision [24, 25, 30, 33].

Consequently, the DMHP–AAM platform operationalizes a task-sharing and stepped-care model, facilitating early identification, basic management, and the continuity of mental health care within India’s public primary health system [25, 29, 33].

Research team and reflexivity

The multidisciplinary research team of both genders comprised psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, public health experts, social work professionals, and experienced qualitative researchers, each contributing domain-specific expertise to the study’s conceptualization and execution. A total of 30 interviewers with diverse disciplinary backgrounds participated in data collection across the seven study sites. The age of the interviewers ranged from 23 to 64 years. Most interviewers were female (n = 22, 73.3%), while 8 (26.7%) were male. Interviewers held a wide range of professional qualifications spanning public health, social work, psychology, medicine, dentistry, nursing, anthropology, physiotherapy, and journalism. The most common highest educational qualification among the interviewers was Master of Public Health (MPH), however, several interviewers possessed postgraduate or doctoral-level medical and mental health qualifications, including MD Psychiatry and DM in Addiction Psychiatry, while others held master’s degrees in public health, social work, psychology, and related social sciences.

All investigators involved in data collection and analysis had postgraduate qualifications in their respective fields, with previous experience of conducting qualitative health research, especially in community and health systems settings. To ensure methodological consistency and analytical rigour across the seven study sites, a few centrally coordinated, residential workshops on interviewing and qualitative analysis, especially thematic analysis, were held in July and December 2024. These workshops included hands-on sessions on interview techniques, reflexivity, memo writing, coding procedures, and framework-based synthesis. It also provided a forum for aligning the understanding of key constructs and definitions, which in turn strengthened inter-coder reliability and enhanced analytical coherence.

Interviews and focus group discussions were conducted by trained team members at each site, none of whom had any prior therapeutic, supervisory, or personal relationship with participants. While rapport was established through initial introductions and informal conversation to foster trust and encourage open dialogue, every effort was made to minimize the influence of the interviewer bias during the data collection process. Reflexivity was actively practiced through the use of field memos and weekly debriefings, which allowed the interviewers and analysts to reflect on their positionality, preconceptions, and emotional responses during the research process. To maintain credibility and reduce bias, coding was carried out independently by two or more researchers at each site. All coders followed a pre-defined protocol, and discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus-building discussions with senior qualitative researchers or site co-principal investigators (Supplementary File 1).

Sampling and recruitment

A stakeholder matrix was used to identify and involve key informants across various levels, to achieve a comprehensive understanding of perspectives relevant to the study. We use purposive sampling to ensure diversity in terms of geographic locations, stakeholder roles, and health system levels. This is consistent with the study’s objective of capturing multi-perspective insights into the implementation of integrated mental health services in primary care.

Healthcare providers were selected based on their active involvement in the planning, delivery, supervision, or direct experience in the provision of integrated mental health care. Recruitment was facilitated by partnership with local health departments, program implementation teams, and community-based networks across the seven states participating in the study.

The final dataset consisted of 211 in-depth interviews (IDIs) and 28 focus group discussions (FGDs) at all levels of the health care delivery system conducted across Assam, Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Punjab. A total of 322 participants were included in the study. The ages of participants ranged from 24 to 66 years. The majority of the participants were females (n = 219, 68%), while 103 Healthcare providers (32%) were male.

The participant group included a wide range of roles within the health system. MOs, who are typically posted at PHCs and CHCs, play a pivotal role in care delivery. CHOs, who are mid-level care providers, primarily from a nursing background and are responsible for service delivery at the AAMs.

Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANMs) and General Nurse Midwives (GNMs) are essential front-line workers involved in community-based screening, psychoeducation, and linking individuals to facility-based care. They have frequent engagement with families and women during antenatal and postnatal care and can play a strategic role in the early identification of mental distress. ASHAs, who serve as community-based health workers, are often the first point of contact for individuals with emotional and behavioral problems. Community nurses and other allied health professionals such as clinical psychologists and psychiatric social workers (where available), were included to understand their contribution to psychosocial interventions and training support.

In addition to providers, we also included 80 patients receiving services and their caregivers were included to explore lived experiences, perceptions of care quality, and barriers to access and continuity. The patients age ranged from 23 to 81 years and consisted of 56 males (70%) and 24 females (30%). The most common diagnosis among patients was NCDs (n = 59), followed by substance use disorders (n = 17), severe mental disorders (SMD) (n = 3), and common mental disorders (CMD) (n = 1). Overall, the most frequently represented conditions were NCDs and substance use disorders.

Patients were approached using site-specific, facility-based sampling procedures. In Gujarat, patients attending outpatient departments were selected purposively. In Bhopal, individuals with diagnosed mental health conditions, substance use disorders, or NCDs were identified based on facility service records and contacted through field visits or telephonic calls to schedule interviews or FGDs. In Assam, medical officers provided lists of NCD and mental health patients, who were subsequently contacted by telephone. In Karnataka, participants were recruited from service users attending the respective facilities. In Bhubaneswar, NCD patients were recruited from Ayushman Arogya Mandirs in both rural and urban areas, while patients with mental disorders were recruited from the psychiatric outpatient department of the district headquarters hospital and the district’s tertiary-level facility. In some sites, eligible participants were referred by the district psychiatrist. In Punjab, participants were identified and assigned by the medical officer at the Primary Health Centre.

Across states, site-specific contributions to the dataset were as follows: Gujarat contributed 31 IDIs and 6 FGDs; Karnataka 19 IDIs and 4 FGDs; Madhya Pradesh (Bhopal) 26 IDIs and 4 FGDs; Punjab 17 IDIs and 4 FGDs; Delhi (Haryana site) 17 IDIs and 2 FGDs; Assam 20 IDIs and 3 FGDs; and Odisha 61 IDIs and 5 FGDs. The sample size at each site was determined by thematic saturation, which was assessed iteratively throughout the data collection process. In instances where new themes continued to emerge, sampling was extended to ensure comprehensive coverage. This approach offered depth and variation in data, capturing context-specific implementation dynamics and systemic influences shaping the integration of mental health care at the primary level. None of the participants declined (Supplementary Files 2 and 3).

Data collection

Semi-structured interview guides tailored to each stakeholder group were developed centrally and refined based on pilot testing. These guides included open-ended questions, prompts, and probes informed by the CFIR constructs and training evaluation objectives. Interviews and FGDs were conducted in local languages by trained interviewers and ranged from 30 to 75 min in duration. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent, and field notes were taken during and after each session to capture contextual nuances and non-verbal cues. Data were collected in private settings within health facilities, training sites, or community spaces, ensuring confidentiality and comfort. No repeat interviews were conducted as thematic saturation was reached, ensuring no new insights were emerging, and participants were not asked to review transcripts due to feasibility constraints.

The Assam site collected data from 20th August to 30th November 2024; Gujarat from 14th June to 18th September 2024; Haryana from 6th May to 16th October 2024; Karnataka from 31st December 2024 to 23rd January 2025; Madhya Pradesh from 20th August to 30th November 2024; Odisha from 8th February to 19th November 2024; and Punjab from 15th July to 30th November 2024.

Data management and analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English by trained local staff. Transcripts were anonymized and verified for accuracy by the research teams. Data were managed and analyzed using licensed ATLAS.ti software. A team-based coding strategy was employed, with each site having one or more trained coders. Coding was informed by both deductive categories derived from CFIR and inductive codes emerging from the data. Coders included AN, TM, and RS in Gujarat; JPR and BRG in Karnataka; RP and RS in Madhya Pradesh; PM and BG in Punjab; AS in Delhi; PB, GK, and TN in Assam; and SP and SM in Odisha. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus meetings led by designated senior analysts, including CP, JR, DV, AG, and AP. Themes were developed using a constant comparative method, and a thematic framework was iteratively refined to capture variation across states, stakeholder types, and implementation contexts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional ethics committees of the coordinating and participating organizations and the respective state governments. All participants were informed about the study, its purpose, voluntary nature, and confidentiality protocols. The participants provided informed consent before participation in our study. Audio data and transcripts were stored securely and with restricted access to ensure data protection and participant anonymity.

Results

Participant details

Participant information were collected from seven different sites across India to understand the diversity in gender and professional experience among various cadres of healthcare providers and service users.

Across the seven sites—Assam, Gujarat, Bhopal, Bhubaneswar, Delhi, Karnataka, and Punjab—a total of 59 MOs, 81 CHOs, 80 frontline health workers, and 197 individuals with lived experience of illness or care-seeking were interviewed. On average, MOs had 6.6 years of professional experience, CHOs had 3 years, and frontline health workers had 8.3 years of experience. Among individuals with lived experience who reported duration of illness, the average was approximately 2.7 years. Two caregivers were also interviewed, though their experience was not recorded. Public health system stakeholders included 6 public health administrators and 32 policymakers/state officials (senior-, mid-, and junior-level), interviewed across participating states. Overall, 38 public-sector decision-makers contributed to findings on governance, coordination, resourcing, and scale-up considerations.

The findings are structured using the CFIR, encompassing five domains: Intervention Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Process. Within each domain, themes were developed through inductive coding and mapped onto CFIR constructs. The section draws on cross-cutting patterns across all study sites and stakeholder groups, supported by illustrative quotations. Cross-cutting themes were those that appeared from three or more sites.

Cross-cutting themes

‘Intervention Characteristics’ (characteristics of the intervention being implemented into a particular organization).

The ‘intervention’ in ICMR MINDS study refers to a standardized, multi-component, service implementation package, includes (1) Integrated Mental Health Screening and Assessment Tools (2), Integrated Clinical Workflows and Care Pathways (3), Digital Platform and Smartphone Application (4), Capacity Building and Supportive Supervision and (5) Patient Education and Community Engagement [22]. Participants across all states consistently expressed that while integrating mental health services into primary care was essential, the intervention remained under-supported and difficult to execute.

The ‘evidence strength & quality’ (stakeholders’ perceptions of the quality and validity of evidence supporting the belief that the intervention will have desired outcomes) of the intervention were questioned due to the lack of specialized personnel and formal protocols. As a CHO noted, “From the government, we need proper specialists, such as psychiatrists and counsellors. Also, I think we need proper training to better understand these issues for effective services.” A MO similarly stated, “We can diagnose mental health disorders, but without counsellors or specialists, we can only refer patients.”

There was also a strong demand for ‘design quality’ (perceived excellence in how the intervention is bundled, presented, and assembled) and ‘adaptability’ (degree to which an intervention can be adapted, tailored, refined, or reinvented to meet local.

needs), especially for locally relevant tools and digital platforms. One health administrator highlighted, “There is no formal mechanism yet. We need screening protocols, referral protocols, and follow-up protocols developed at the state level.” A CHO suggested, “CBAC forms should include mental health questions,” while another suggested the use of animated videos to aid counselling. This suggestion reflects that the CBAC forms should include more comprehensive mental health questions, beyond the existing two-item depression screen (PHQ-2), highlighting the perceived need for additional screening prompts/tools for mental health and substance use in primary care. Participants frequently raised concerns around ‘complexity’ (Perceived difficulty of implementation, reflected by duration, scope, radicalness, disruptiveness, centrality, and intricacy and number of steps required to implement) reporting that constrained consultation time and unmet training needs were perceived as complex and demanding within routine service settings. A MO from Haryana remarked, “Proper treatment requires at least half an hour to 45 minutes for taking the history of a psychiatric or addiction patient. Unfortunately, we are only able to give them about 5 to 7 minutes.” The health administrators reported a need for training all the CHOs and it would be burden on them to take care of multiple health related problems including mental health. A health administrator acknowledged this limitation and noted, “Expecting CHOs and MOs to manage mental health, NCDs, and maternal-child care in 10-minute consultations is unrealistic without team support.” CHOs and AYUSH MOs also explored the ‘adaptability’ of alternative treatment approaches for mental disorders, including the use of Ayurvedic formulations for substance use management. “Many Ayurvedic medicines are effective. If such medicines were available in our supply, we could treat substance abuse more effectively,” one practitioner noted. A CHO reported a health misconception regarding substance intake: “Opium is commonly kept at home, and people take it for any kind of pain or heart disease. It’s quite common in Punjab, and they have the misconception.”

Outer setting

Outer setting means the economic, political, and social context within which an organization resides.

A range of factors related to the outer setting emerged, particularly within the constructs of ‘patient needs and resources’ (The extent to which patient needs, as well as barriers and facilitators to meet those needs are accurately known and prioritized by the organization), ‘peer influence’ (mimetic or competitive pressure to implement an intervention) and community engagement.

Stigma was an omnipresent theme and echoed by CHO, ANM and patients. One CHO explained, “People are afraid to go to hospitals or meet a psychiatrist. Even if they go, they do not openly discuss the issues they are facing.” An ANM echoed, “They think if people discover they visited a mental health doctor, it will hamper their reputation.”

Cultural narratives influence help-seeking behavior

A patient shared, “Mental illness is when someone fails to live by societal rules.”

There can be many barriers in healthcare setups, as a MO reported, “The main problem is that our setup doesn’t have anything yet. There’s no infrastructure, no manpower, and no medicines. The main thing is that at the PHC or district level, a department should be established. There should be a separate department that handles this treatment.”

Financial barriers were also prominent. “Everyone in this hamlet faces financial difficulties because the majority of people are not financially secure,” an ASHA noted. A health administrator suggested, “ASHAs are incentive-based, so they will do more work if they get more incentives.”

Transportation added another layer of access issues: “There is no particular ambulance for mental patients,” shared a MO. However, this can be resolved as ASHA suggested, “It will help if camps are conducted at villages, since there is no transportation, and it can be easily accessed, and treatment can be taken rather than the hospital.”

However, some positive changes were evident. A CHO stated, “Women have started coming. They even assign disorders to themselves—like saying they are suffering from anxiety or depression.” Community-level interventions, including school awareness drives and role-plays, were reported to reduce stigma and facilitate dialogue. A policymaker added, “Partnerships with education and rural development departments can help normalize mental health dialogue in schools and communities.”

Inner setting

Inner setting is the structural, political, and cultural contexts through which the implementation process will proceed.

Key themes within the inner setting included available resources, implementation climate, networks and communication, and digital infrastructure gaps.

There are general concerns that the health personnel have to don multiple hats due to lack of designated personnel. For example, a clinical psychologist noted, “We are handling multiple roles—nurse, psychologist, record keeper—all at once.” This lack of resources was also demonstrated by CHO: “There should be a psychiatrist here, doctors should be trained, proper questionnaires should be provided, and medicines should be available for treatment. We don’t have extra manpower here.” Building rapport can help increase awareness, as suggested by CHO, “ASHA workers refer patients to us. It depends on how you build rapport with patients, how you talk to them, and how you listen to their problems. This way, it becomes easy to refer patients and talk to them.”

Implementation climate was further influenced by a lack of incentives

‘Implementation climate’ is the absorptive capacity for change, shared receptivity of involved individuals to an intervention and the extent to which use of that intervention will be rewarded, supported, and expected within their organization. Most of the primary healthcare personnel reported that adding mental health would be a burden to them, this limits sustainability and demotivation in them. A health administrator emphasized, “Mental health tasks are being added to workers’ load without any new cadre or remuneration. This limits sustainability.” Another quote, “ASHAs are overburdened with many responsibilities. It is impossible to impose additional work.”

Digital readiness was also a barrier; from no specific apps to specific aspects of mental health such as screening, management and follow-up. An ASHA explained, “The NCD app has just one question on depression. There are no apps specific to mental health.” A policymaker stated, “Our e-health platforms must include mental health modules for screening, follow-up, and reporting.”

Coordination within teams remained variable. “CHOs and ANMs often clash over responsibilities, creating inefficiencies,” shared an NCD coordinator. Working at a ground level, as MO remarked, “Including ANMs and ASHA workers in apps will be more beneficial. If you can, put some videos, some animated videos on how to convince the patients.”

Referral processes were described as fragmented: “We refer to PHC Tuesday clinics. If no improvement, then NIMHANS,” explained an ASHA.

Characteristics of individuals

This domain reports stakeholders’ knowledge, beliefs, and self-efficacy significantly influenced the quality of implementation.

“Out of 50 ASHAs, only 5–6% really understand mental health. The rest don’t grasp it,” admitted a MO. As an ASHA remarked, “ASHAs are incentive-based. If we get incentives like for TB patients, it will motivate us”.

Still, others were optimistic. “After training, we understand how to counsel. Sometimes a good counsellor can reduce the need for medicine,” said a CHO. On the same note, MO said, “If you provide training along with manuals and materials, it would strengthen our ability to open new clinics.”

Language barriers and conceptual mismatches were common.

“Most likely, this is because when we use terms like ‘depression’ in English, they don’t grasp the concept”, noted a MO.

Transportation barriers were also noted. “It will help if camps are conducted at villages since there is no transportation, and it can be easily accessed, and treatment can be taken rather than the hospital.”

Motivational variability was also reflected in the quote: “Motivation is very important in the community. If programs are conducted for women, they should be taught not to fear but to control the situation, show love, and explain things to them.”

However, the role of champions emerged. “If you explain the harm of drugs, they understand. If not, they won’t follow treatment,” said a CHO.

Providers described variability in training exposure, confidence, and perceived capability to deliver mental health and substance use care. One health administrator reported, ‘Training is given to two CHOs; one of them was on maternity leave… so the two who have received the training do the assessment,’ highlighting gaps in training coverage and continuity. Another administrator noted that expecting CHOs and MOs to manage multiple service priorities within brief consultations was unrealistic without additional team support, reflecting reduced self-efficacy and perceived burden.

Process

The process reports the stages of implementation such as planning, executing, reflecting and evaluating, and the presence of key intervention stakeholders and influencers including opinion leaders, stakeholder engagement, and project champions.

Implementation processes were at varied stages across sites.

In Gujarat and Odisha, structured mechanisms—such as dedicated training sessions, referral checklists, and use of Sangath’s (NGO focused on mental health research and service delivery) materials—were underway. “Training is conducted in 30 batches. Afterward, checklists and formats are used for referrals,” explained a health administrator.

Awareness building and community engagement strategies were widely recommended. ASHAs and CHOs reported role-plays, health education drives, and ‘sanghas’ (community-based organization) as effective platforms for destigmatization. “Men’s sanghas can also be utilized to raise awareness,” suggested a patient in an FGD.

Formal leadership structures remained underdeveloped

Positive peer influence was also reported: “I found out about the services from my friends. I used to go to private services before, but they told me to use government services instead,” shared a patient.

“Currently, success depends on local leadership. We need formal institutional support,” emphasized a policymaker. Champions at various levels-initiated referral formats and supported teleconsultation linkages, but lacked systemic backing. “We initiated Thursday clinics and developed mental health referral formats,” shared a CHO.

Overall, the process was marked by grassroots innovation, administrative enthusiasm, and frontline challenges—reinforcing the need for multilevel coordination, institutional incentives, and formal integration within health system priorities (Table 2).

Site-specific themes

The site-specific findings complement the cross-cutting results and are also organized by the CFIR domains. They illustrate how contextual differences across Haryana, Punjab, Gujarat, Odisha, Assam, and Karnataka influence the implementation of integrated mental health services in primary care.

Intervention characteristics

In Haryana, participants emphasized the challenge of complexity, particularly due to diverse age groups and co-morbid conditions. “Elderly patients also come in. Children 6 months old to 60-70-year-olds come here,” explained a Medical Officer, reflecting on the complexity of delivering age-sensitive mental health services. In Punjab, awareness was raised about the effectiveness of treatment. Yet a MO added, “Proper treatment requires 30–45 minutes… but we get 5–7 minutes.” In Punjab, Evidence strength and Quality were emphasized by MO, stating that “Early diagnosis and treatment can significantly improve outcomes, and if people are more vocal about mental health issues.

In Gujarat and Odisha, ‘adaptability’ and design quality were highlighted. A Health Administrator from Gujarat proposed, “CBAC forms could include mental health… but other screening tools are missing.” A CHO from Gujarat stated, “Meditation, yoga, and wellness activities happen once a week. It’s done properly, and for those who have PNC like postpartum depression, or adolescents who are going through menarche and don’t enjoy anything, we make them do meditation and pranayama.” Similarly, a respondent from Odisha said, “First, a toolkit… even app-based questionnaires on substance abuse would help.” The need for specialist engagement was also raised: “If a psychiatrist could visit weekly or monthly, it would be very beneficial,” suggested a Medical Officer from Odisha.

Outer setting

Stakeholders in Gujarat, Karnataka, and Assam spoke about the influence of family and community in shaping treatment pathways. A CHO in Gujarat said, “Their family convinces them to leave alcohol and tobacco… even help them financially.” Yet challenges persist: “People still think addiction doesn’t happen because others do it regularly and are fine,” explained a MO. In Assam, a patient confirmed, “Yes, he did. He even brought me here,” showing the family’s active role.

Karnataka participants described gaps in specialist referrals: “If they do not improve at PHC, we refer to NIMHANS,” said an ASHA. But transportation and specialist availability were major barriers: “Camps may not be successful if there are no specialists,” said a nurse. In Punjab, a lack of mental health awareness programs was seen, as ASHA stated, “There hasn’t been any specific awareness program for mental health or substance use in the community.” A CHO from Gujarat also stated, “If a patient is slightly mentally unstable, their family might not understand that they need mental support, counseling, or treatment. They might simply dismiss it, saying things like ‘they’re mad. So, family awareness is equally important.”

Inner setting

The inner setting was marked by structural and human resource constraints. In Haryana, ASHAs and ANMs described their roles: “We go door to door and prepare a list for follow-up,” said one ANM. Yet they also face discomfort in handling psychiatric patients in crowded facilities. In Punjab, a CHO noted, “There should be hospitals, seating facilities, and water.” The absence of female providers and counsellors was also emphasized. ASHA showed willingness to provide care, stating, “People should receive treatment, and we should be provided with the right tools and materials. There should be full staffing and provision of medicines."

Karnataka stakeholders raised the need for an integrated digital infrastructure. “If you can put some animated videos—how to convince patients—that would help,” said a MO. Others asked for apps that provide summaries to aid service delivery.

“If we get incentives like they give for TB patients, it will motivate us to do our work better for mental disorders, also”. – ASHA.

Leadership and governance emerged as cross-cutting implementation determinants, with stakeholders emphasizing that progress often depended on “local leadership” and informal champions, but lacked formal institutional backing and structured coordination mechanisms.

Participants also highlighted system constraints, including insufficient infrastructure, workforce, and medicine availability, limiting implementation readiness at PHC and HWC levels.

Process

Several states described innovations and referral practices. Gujarat reported structured efforts like “Therapeutic Thursdays” clinics (Gujarat District Mental Health Programme once a month clinic on Thursdays at Community Health Center) and family-based psychoeducation. Karnataka has introduced “Super Tuesdays” as part of the Manochaitanya program, a specific initiative within the DMHP, where psychiatrists and other mental health professionals from DMHP, hospitals, or medical colleges provide specialized services to mentally ill patients at Taluka-level (sub division of a district or the administrative district in India) hospitals every Tuesday. A Health Administrator shared, “After doing IEC, I say this is my counselling center… we can refer from here.”

In Punjab, participants suggested increased engagement with schools and camps: “We address these issues in schools too,” said a MO. However, they also noted, “There hasn’t been any specific awareness program for mental health or substance use in the community. Some NGOs are working on substance use disorders, but not specifically on mental illness.”

Assam emphasized proactive ASHA engagement and family mobilization. “ASHA workers should visit every household,” said a CHO. In Karnataka, participants called for village-level camps and structured app-based follow-up tools.

In Odisha, respondents recommended embedding psychiatric care into existing systems. “If psychiatric medicines are available at sub-centers, patients don’t have to keep returning to mental hospitals,” said a health officer. “If a psychiatrist could visit our area once a week or even once a month to counsel patients, it would be very beneficial,” said a MO (Table 3).

Stakeholder-specific perspectives on implementation domains

Analysis of stakeholder-specific perspectives within the CFIR domains were identified to have different priorities, barriers, and contributions depending on their roles in the health system. Medical officers consistently emphasized system-level limitations and clinical infrastructure gaps. For example , under Intervention Characteristics, they recognized the urgent need for access to psychiatrists and mental health specialists, while also flagging time constraints under Inner Setting as a major barrier to delivering comprehensive care during routine consultations. They also described informal, often ad hoc, referral pathways under Process, which were not embedded in formal protocols.

CHOs played a key role in delivery of services and continuity of care. They called for the inclusion of mental health screening items in CBAC forms and noted the impact of recent training on their ability to counsel patients, reflecting on the Characteristics of Individuals. CHOs also described piloting clinic-based mental health initiatives such as checklists and fixed-day clinics, demonstrating ownership and engagement with the Process domain.

ASHAs/Frontline community workers provided critical community-level insights. They reported discomfort and lack of confidence in referring individuals with mental health problems due to limited training and support tools (Intervention Characteristics), as well as their feeling of being overburdened without designated incentives (Inner Setting). They also described community mapping exercises and observed stigma and social judgment impacting patient willingness to disclose symptoms (Outer Setting and Characteristics of Individuals).

Administrators focused on structural and programmatic enablers. They suggested practical innovations such as toolkit development and mobile apps to support CHOs and ASHAs (Intervention Characteristics), emphasized the need for dedicated staff or designated areas for mental health (Inner Setting), and stressed the importance of scaling up IEC campaigns to reduce stigma and promote help-seeking behavior (Outer Setting). They also noted concerns around frequent staff turnover and continuity of care (Characteristics of Individuals), and requested for establishing formalized referral and coordination structures (Process).

Finally, patients and caregivers offered insights into help-seeking motivations and barriers. Many described delays in diagnosis and initial care-seeking (Intervention Characteristics), often due to stigma and poor access. However, they also highlighted the influential role of family members in encouraging treatment (Outer Setting), and described disclosing mental health issues more openly when encouraged by peers or trusted health workers (Characteristics of Individuals). Informal community conversations and word-of-mouth were cited as key pathways to learning about available services, underscoring the role of informal networks in the Process of mental health care navigation (Table 4).

Discussion

The implementation of integrated mental health services within the NCD framework in Indian primary healthcare settings is shaped by a dynamic interplay of barriers and facilitators across CFIR domains, unfolding sequentially and interactively. Community-level stigma, low mental health awareness, treatment-related economic burden, and transportation barriers emerged as persistent outer setting challenges. However, these constraints were not experienced uniformly across settings. States with stronger community engagement structures and more active local leadership demonstrated comparatively greater acceptability of mental health screening and follow-up, whereas settings characterized by high stigma and fragmented service access reported greater resistance to disclosure, treatment continuation, and family engagement. This suggests that outer setting influences are not merely background contextual variables but actively influence implementation feasibility and services uptake.

Such contextual realities influenced interpretation and local adaptation of the intervention characteristics. . While the need for specialist support, patient-centered care, and improved infrastructure was consistently recognized, states operationalized these needs differently according to existing health system capacity. For example, Odisha demonstrated more structured integration efforts and toolkit-based implementation, whereas Haryana has put greater reliance on teleconsultation mechanisms to address for specialist shortages. These differences suggest that implementation success depended less on uniform intervention delivery and more on adaptive integration of the intervention into existing local systems. This finding reinforces implementation science perspectives emphasizing that intervention adaptability, rather than rigid standardization, is critical for scalability in heterogeneous health systems [3, 6].

The inner setting emerged as a major determinant of implementation effectiveness. However, our findings demonstrate that infrastructural and organizational constraints did not operate independently; rather, they interacted with workforce roles, local leadership, and institutional culture. States with relatively stronger primary healthcare systems, clearer referral pathways, and more stable staffing demonstrated better continuity of care, improved follow-up, and smoother integration of mental health services into routine NCD care. On the other hand, resource-constrained contexts experienced implementation fragmentation due to workforce shortages, competing programmatic priorities, and weak coordination mechanisms. Importantly, these variations influenced not only service delivery processes but also frontline worker confidence and legitimacy of their roles. In settings where supervisory structures and referral mechanisms were functional, providers reported greater ownership and confidence in delivery of mental healthcare, whereas poorly coordinated systems amplified uncertainty and ambiguity in the roles.

A key analytical contribution of this study lies in the identification of differences across cadres in how implementation responsibilities were understood and enacted. Although both ASHAs and CHOs were positioned as frontline actors within integrated care delivery, their perspectives reflected differing institutional locations and experiential realities. CHOs generally approached mental healthcare through a clinical and protocol-oriented lens, emphasizing screening, diagnosis, and referral responsibilities. Contrarily, ASHAs frequently described implementation through relational and sociocultural dimensions, including family resistance, stigma negotiation, trust-building, and sustaining engagement within communities. This distinction is important because it demonstrates that implementation is not experienced uniformly across cadres, even within the same health system structure. Rather than functioning as interchangeable task-sharing actors, different cadres played complementary but unevenly supported implementation roles. These findings suggest that current implementation models may overemphasize technical training while underrecognizing the relational and emotional labour required for sustained community mental healthcare.

The study also highlights under-examined aspects of implementation practice such as informal innovations, improvisational workarounds, and the tacit knowledge of frontline providers. Our findings show that frontline implementation was often based on tacit knowledge and adaptive practice rather than formal protocols alone. Tacit knowledge was evident in providers’ ability to recognize early or subclinical signs of distress using contextual and behavioral cues, adapt communication strategies to align with local sociocultural norms, and navigate complex family relationship and stigma. Frontline providers often relied on experiential judgment and informal communication strategies to sustain patient engagement, particularly in settings where formal referral systems or specialist support were weak. This experiential and context-sensitive knowledge enabled providers to make practical decisions regarding case identification, follow-up, and continuity of care in situations where formal guidelines were insufficient or difficult to operationalize. Examples such as “Therapeutic Thursdays” in Gujarat, “Super Tuesday” in Karnataka and teleconsultation-based coordination in Haryana further prove that these grassroots innovations emerged not as isolated successes, but as adaptive responses to systemic gaps in specialist availability, referral coordination, and service continuity. These locally evolved practices demonstrate how frontline adaptive capacity and informal problem-solving mechanisms can sustain implementation in low-resource settings, extending findings from prior implementation research [12]. At the same time, these findings highlight an important implementation paradox: while local improvisations may enhance responsiveness, continuity, and contextual relevance of care, their dependence on individual initiative may also reflect underlying institutional fragility, weak system integration, and the absence of standardized implementation pathways. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of recognizing and integrating frontline experiential knowledge, adaptive practices, and locally generated innovations into training, supervision, and implementation planning for integrated mental healthcare services.

Another important insight generated through the multi-state design was the uneven distribution of implementation readiness across regions. While prior Indian studies have identified barriers such as workforce shortages, inadequate training, and weak referral systems [4, 33], our findings demonstrate how these barriers interact differently across sociopolitical and infrastructural contexts. In some states, implementation challenges were primarily structural, driven by shortages of personnel, medicines, or digital infrastructure. In others, sociocultural barriers such as stigma, gender norms, and family-level resistance emerged more prominently despite relatively better infrastructure. This distinction has important implications for policy because it suggests that implementation failure cannot be understood solely as a resource deficit. Instead, implementation readiness reflects the interaction between system capacity, sociocultural context, and local governance structures. The inclusion of stakeholders across multiple levels of the health system also generated important analytical insights into how implementation priorities differed across hierarchical positions. Administrative stakeholders frequently emphasized scalability, program integration, and workforce deployment, whereas frontline providers focused on operational ambiguities, workload burden, and patient engagement challenges. However, patients and caregivers, identified barriers related to stigma, affordability, continuity, and trust in services. These differences reveal significant gaps between policy-level assumptions and frontline realities. While policies often conceptualize integration as a technical and organizational process, frontline experiences suggest that successful integration also depends on relational trust, community legitimacy, and sustained interpersonal engagement. The divergence in stakeholder perspectives therefore underscores the importance of incorporating bottom-up implementation feedback into programme design and scale-up planning.

Our findings are broadly consistent with implementation lessons reported from PRIME, VISHRAM, Atmiyata, and SMART Mental Health programmes in India [18–20, 33]. Similar to PRIME, we found that training alone was insufficient without supportive supervision, referral coordination, and sustained implementation support. However, our findings extend these programmes by demonstrating how implementation determinants vary across state contexts and stakeholder groups, thereby complicating assumptions regarding the transferability of implementation models across settings. The importance of community engagement and trusted local actors observed in our study aligns with findings from VISHRAM and Atmiyata, while the identified need for workflow integration and decision support resonates with SMART Mental Health [18–20]. A recent narrative synthesis of community mental health interventions in India similarly emphasizes that scalability depends on context-responsive adaptation rather than replication of uniform intervention models [34].

Taken together, these findings suggest that integrated mental healthcare within NCD platforms should not be conceptualized solely as a technical exercise of task-sharing and service expansion. Rather, implementation should be understood as a contextually negotiated process shaped by infrastructure, institutional culture, cadre-specific roles, sociocultural realities, and frontline adaptive practices. Future implementation strategies should therefore move away from standardized training approaches toward differentiated, context-sensitive models that incorporate supportive supervision, flexible workflows, local innovation mechanisms, and recognition of frontline experiential knowledge.

Policy and practice implications

To effectively scale up integrated mental health and NCD services within the primary health care system, India must invest in the following key areas. Firstly, human resource development is crucial. The capacity of CHOs, ASHAs, and MOs must be strengthened through regular training, supportive supervision, and provision of digital job aids. Consideration should also be given to mental health-specific incentives, similar to those used for TB or maternal health programs. Secondly, referral, back referral, and follow-up systems need to be strengthened. Developing standardized protocols and tracking mechanisms that link AAMs with higher-level mental health services and digital platforms (such as e-Manas or telepsychiatry services), where a clinical decision support system is available, will be essential for continuity of care. Thirdly, medications to treatment of common and severe mental health problems must be made available at the PHC. Strengthening essential psychotropic drug availability and supply chains is also fundamental, as participants repeatedly linked service credibility and continuity to the availability of medicines at the point of care. Fourthly, community engagement must be scaled up. Expanding targeted IEC campaigns—particularly in rural and underserved areas—and involving family networks in treatment pathways can help reduce stigma and enhance early identification. Fifthly, and importantly, systems for data and outcome monitoring need to be developed and embedded into routine practice. Finally, state-specific adaptation of implementation strategies will be essential to account for varying contextual barriers and facilitators.

Our findings underscore that integration is not sustained through training alone; it requires governance mechanisms (leadership engagement, clear accountability), financing levers (including incentive alignment for frontline workers), supportive supervision and job-aids, and functional information systems that can capture mental health indicators within routine digital platforms.

Suggestions to use Ayurvedic formulations reflect provider perceptions and local preferences rather than guideline-endorsed practice. Any future integration of AYUSH pharmacological formulations into substance use care would require robust evidence of effectiveness and safety, and formal inclusion in national guidelines, before programmatic scale-up.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was conducted in seven diverse states but the findings may not be fully generalizable to all states of India. Secondly, the absence of systematic exploration of implementation outcomes (e.g., fidelity, sustainability) reflects both the stage of implementation and a limitation in the interview guides. Thirdly, while a variety of stakeholder types within the public health system were included, perspectives from private sector providers were not examined. Although the study focused on public sector implementation, the absence of private sector perspectives may limit insights into broader care-seeking pathways and referral linkages that could indirectly influence service delivery in some contexts. Fourthly, translation and transcription may have introduced minor distortions in meaning, despite quality checks. Finally, social desirability bias may have influenced some participants’ responses, particularly among CHOs and administrators. Although interviews were conducted in a non-judgmental environment with assurances of confidentiality and anonymity, and probing techniques were employed to encourage honest disclosure on sensitive topics, the potential for underreporting or overreporting due to social expectations remains a recognized limitation of this study. Although findings were mapped to CFIR, Process-domain constructs (planning, tailoring, engaging, doing, reflecting, and evaluating) were under-represented, reflecting that integrated mental health and substance use care within primary care remains still in infancy stage in many settings and is often not yet embedded in routine workflows with structured monitoring and evaluation.

Conclusion

This study offers one of the most comprehensive qualitative explorations of integrated mental health and NCD service implementation in Indian primary care, using the CFIR framework various geographies and stakeholders. Findings demonstrate a cascade of interrelated barriers and enablers—from community stigma and systemic resource gaps to individual motivation and innovative practice models. Implementation success hinges on multi-level coordination, policy support, and sustained investment in the health workforce. Future efforts need to institutionalize learnings from grassroots practices and advance robust outcome measurement systems to achieve equity and continuity in mental health care.
