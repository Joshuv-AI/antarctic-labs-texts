Abstract
. We study large traveling surface waves within a two-dimensional finite depth, free boundary, homogeneous, incompressible and viscous fluid governed by Darcy’s law. The fluid is bound by a gravitational force to a flat rigid bottom and meets an atmosphere of constant pressure at the top with its free surface, where it does not experience any capillarity effects. Additionally, the fluid is subject to a fixed, but arbitrarily selected, forcing data profile with variable amplitude. We use the Riemann mapping to equivalently reformulate the resulting two-dimensional free boundary problem as a single one-dimensional fully nonlinear pseudodifferential equation for a function describing the domain’s geometry. By discovering a hidden ellipticity in the reformulated equation, we are able to import a global implicit function theorem to construct a connected set of traveling waves, containing both the quiescent solution and large amplitude members. We find that either solutions continue to exist for arbitrarily large data amplitude or else one of a finite number of meaningful breakdown scenarios must occur. This work stands as the first non perturbative construction of large traveling surface waves in any free boundary viscous fluid without surface tension.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Equations of motion and traveling ansatz

In this paper we study a two-dimensional finite depth layer of fluid existing within a porous medium. The fluid is bounded below by an impermeable, flat, and rigid bottom and, at the top, meets an atmosphere of constant pressure with a free boundary.

Let us denote the dynamically evolving fluid domains by
t\mapsto\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. At each time
t\geqslant 0
the boundary of
\Omega(t)
is assumed to have two connected components:
\partial\Omega(t)=\Sigma(t)\sqcup\Sigma_{0}
, with
\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{R}\times\{{0}\}
denoting the rigid bottom and
t\mapsto\Sigma(t)\subset\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
denoting the time-evolving free boundary.

The Eulerian description of the fluid motion consists of two unknowns: the vector velocity
V(t,\cdot):\Omega(t)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the scalar pressure
P(t,\cdot):\Omega(t)\to\mathbb{R}
. In addition to the gravitational force with acceleration
g\geqslant 0
in the
-e_{2}
direction, the fluid is permitted to experience a known vector applied force
F(t,\cdot):\Omega(t)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the bulk and a known scalar applied pressure
\Phi(t,\cdot):\Sigma(t)\to\mathbb{R}
on the surface. The equations in the bulk relating these functions are written as follows:

(1.1)

\nabla\cdot V(t,\cdot)=0\text{ and }(\mu/\iota)V(t,\cdot)+\nabla P(t,\cdot)=-\rho ge_{2}+F(t,\cdot)\text{ in }\Omega(t).

The first identity in (
1.1
) is the constraint of incompressibility. The second identity above is known as Darcy’s law
[
31
]
. This is an empirically observed relationship between the velocity and the pressure of a fluid flowing through a porous medium, and can be rigorously derived from Stokes equations via homogenization
[
6
]
. The constants
\mu,\iota,\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
are the dynamic viscosity, the quotient of permeability and effective porosity, and the density, respectively.

The bulk equations (
1.1
) are coupled to a series of boundary conditions. On the bottom we simply have the no penetration condition for the velocity,

(1.2)

e_{2}\cdot V(t,\cdot)=0\text{ on }\Sigma_{0},

while on the top free surface we have the kinematic condition and balance of pressure:

(1.3)

\mathcal{V}(\Sigma(t))(\cdot)=V(t,\cdot)\cdot n(t,\cdot)\text{ and }P(t,\cdot)=P_{\mathrm{ext}}+\Phi(t,\cdot)\text{ on }\Sigma(t,\cdot).

The object
\mathcal{V}(\Sigma(t,\cdot))
is the scalar normal speed of the free surface
\Sigma(t)
. The outward pointing unit normal vector field is
n(t,\cdot):\Sigma(t)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Finally,
P_{\mathrm{ext}}\in\mathbb{R}
is the fixed constant ambient exterior pressure.

The coupled system of equations (
1.1
), (
1.2
), and (
1.3
) comprise what is known as the one-phase Muskat problem
[
53
]
. For more information on Darcy’s law and flow through porous media, the interested reader is referred to the books of Bear
[
10
,
11
]
.

Our interest in this work regards the construction and analysis of traveling wave solutions to the above equations. By a traveling wave, we mean a solution to (
1.1
), (
1.2
), and (
1.3
) that is stationary when viewed in a system of coordinates moving at a constant velocity
\gamma
in the
e_{1}
direction. More precisely, we make the ansatz that there exists a stationary domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with boundaries
\Sigma_{0},\Sigma
satisfying
\partial\Omega=\Sigma_{0}\sqcup\Sigma
, such that

(1.4)

\Omega(t)=\{{x+t\gamma e_{1}\;:\;x\in\Omega}\},\;\Sigma(t)=\{{x+t\gamma e_{1}\;:x\in\Sigma}\},\text{ and }\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{R}\times\{{0}\}\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0.

On this stationary domain we have the time-independent traveling velocity and pressure unknowns
v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that are related to
V
and
P
via:

(1.5)

V(t,x)=v(x-t\gamma e_{1})\text{ and }P(t,x)=p(x-t\gamma e_{1})\text{ for all }t\geqslant 0,\;x\in\Omega(t).

Of course, we also make the ansatz that the supplemental forcing to the system is stationary in the traveling frame as well. More precisely, we suppose that
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\phi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
are given functions that satisfy

(1.6)

F(t,x)=f(x-t\gamma e_{1})\text{ and }\Phi(t,x)=\phi(x-t\gamma e_{1})

for all
t\geqslant 0
and appropriate
x
.

The traveling unknowns
\Omega
,
v
, and
p
are thus related to the given
f
and
\phi
via the traveling wave equations for free boundary incompressible Darcy flow:

(1.7)

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot v=0\text{ and }(\mu/\iota)v+\nabla p=-\rho ge_{2}+f&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
e_{2}\cdot v=0&\text{on }\Sigma_{0},\\
(v-\gamma e_{1})\cdot\nu=0\text{ and }p=P_{\mathrm{ext}}+\phi&\text{on }\Sigma,\end{cases}

where
\nu:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the outward pointing unit normal. We emphasize that while the above formulation is time-independent, the domain
\Omega
is still one of the unknowns of the problem.

Our goal in this work is to construct and analyze large amplitude solutions to system (
1.28
). We shall think of the supplemental forcing
f
and
\phi
as having an arbitrary but fixed profile
\mathsf{f}
and
\upphi
(not both identically zero), but tunable scalar strength
\kappa\geqslant 0
so that
f=\kappa\mathsf{f}
and
\phi=\kappa\upphi
. When
\kappa=0
the system (
1.28
) admits a unique quiescent and flat equilibrium solution. By an implicit function theorem argument,
[
60
]
proved that for small
\kappa>0
there is a locally unique curve of nontrivial and small amplitude solutions
\kappa\mapsto(\Omega_{\kappa},v_{\kappa},p_{\kappa})
. We shall use global continuation based on the Leray-Schauder degree to extend this local mapping to a global connected set of solutions. In doing so, we will obtain large traveling waves that are not close to any equilibrium; moreover, along the connected set of solutions we prove that either arbitrarily large waves continue to exist or a else a geometrically meaningful quantity related to the domain
\Omega
is becoming arbitrarily large.

Despite the superficially simple appearance of the system of equations (
1.28
), this task of constructing large solutions is not a trivial undertaking, even if we ignore the immediate complication that the domain itself is one of the unknowns. Note that there are not any effects of surface tension and so we do not have available the extra two derivative regularization present within the corresponding system with positive capillarity. In fact, in a certain sense to be elucidated later, system (
1.28
) is not even unconditionally ‘elliptic’. As a consequence, we require that certain inequalities (see (
1.25
)) relating the gravitational acceleration, the wave speed, and the forcing data
f
and
\phi
be satisfied in order to crucially exploit a ‘hidden ellipticity’ upon which our construction hinges.

1.3.
Nondimensional, conformal, and nonlocal reformulations

We now endeavor to reformulate system (
1.28
) into an equivalent manifestation of the problem that is more convenient for the subsequent analysis. This is also a necessary step for us to take to properly state the main results of this paper in the forthcoming Section
1.4
. We proceed through several steps.

Our initial reformulation of the equations is more cosmetic. It serves to eliminate one of the unknowns, namely the velocity, and to nondimensionalize the system. We are interested in solutions to (
1.28
) that are periodic in the
e_{1}
direction and we select our characteristic length scale
\mathrm{L}>0
to be a reference period length of the solution (say the minimal period length of the known data
(f,\phi)
). The characteristic velocity scale
\mathrm{U}>0
is then defined in terms of
\mathrm{L}
and the other physical parameters via
\mathrm{U}=\mu\mathrm{L}/\iota\rho
. We define the nondimensional unknown domain
\Upomega
and pressure
\Pi:\Upomega\to\mathbb{R}
via

(1.8)

\Omega=\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;:\;x/\mathrm{L}\in\Upomega}\}\text{ and }\rho\mathrm{U}^{2}\Pi(x/\mathrm{L})=p(x)-P_{\mathrm{ext}}+g(e_{2}\cdot x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

The inverse Froude number
\mathrm{g}
, nondimensional wave speed
\mathrm{c}
, and nondimensional forcing terms
\mathsf{f}
and
\upphi
are

(1.9)

\mathrm{g}=\rho g\iota/\mu,\;\mathrm{c}=\gamma\rho\iota/\mu\mathrm{L},\;f(x)=\kappa(\rho\mathrm{U}^{2}/\mathrm{L})\mathsf{f}(x/\mathrm{L}),\text{ and }\phi(x)=\kappa\rho\mathrm{U}^{2}\upphi(x/\mathrm{L}).

Note that in the above we have introduced a nondimensional forcing strength parameter
\kappa\geqslant 0
.

By taking the divergence of the second equation of (
1.28
), we compute that the equations to be satisfied by
\Upomega
,
\Pi
, and the nondimensional forcing terms are

(1.10)

\begin{cases}\Delta\Pi=\kappa\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f}&\text{in }\Upomega,\\
e_{2}\cdot\nabla\Pi=\kappa(e_{2}\cdot\mathsf{f})&\text{on }\Upsigma_{0},\\
(-\nabla\Pi+\kappa\mathsf{f}-\mathrm{c}e_{1})\cdot\upnu=0\text{ and }\Pi=\mathrm{g}(e_{2}\cdot\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}})+\kappa\upphi&\text{on }\Upsigma,\end{cases}

where we have decomposed
\partial\Upomega=\Upsigma\sqcup\Upsigma_{0}
with
\Upsigma_{0}=\mathbb{R}\times\{{0}\}
and denoted the outward pointing unit normal on
\Upsigma
by
\upnu:\Upsigma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

The dimensional velocity
v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
appearing in (
1.28
) can be recovered from the solution to (
1.10
) via

(1.11)

v(x)=f(x)-\rho ge_{2}-\nabla p(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

with
f
defined in terms of
\mathsf{f}
as in (
1.9
) and
p
defined in terms of
\Pi
as in (
1.8
).

As we have previously alluded, central difficulty one encounters with systems (
1.28
) and (
1.10
) is that the fluid domains
\Omega
and
\Upomega
themselves are among the unknowns of the problem. Our next reformulation of the system of equations is meant to take a first step in addressing this fact. We shall, in a procedure known as
flattening
, devise a change of coordinates depending on the unknown domain
\Upomega
itself that allows us to pull the system of equations back to a fixed flat slab domain. The differential operators are complicated by this process. In fact it will be revealed that system (
1.10
) is fully nonlinear.

Since we are working in two spatial dimensions, it is convenient to chart the configuration space of possible domains with the help of the Riemann mapping. We shall fix a nondimensional conformal depth parameter
h>0
and make the ansatz that there exists a biholomorphic mapping
R:\mathbb{R}\times(0,h)\to\Upomega
, extending to a diffeomorphism of the closures, such that

(1.12)

R(x+e_{1})=R(x)+e_{1}\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,h)\text{ and }R(\mathbb{R}\times\{{0}\})=\mathbb{R}\times\{{0}\}.

Thanks to the Cauchy-Riemann equations, such a mapping
R
is essentially entirely determined by the trace of its second component on the top boundary
\mathbb{R}\times\{{h}\}
. Under suitable regularity assumptions on
\Upomega
, there exists

(1.13)

\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\text{ satisfying }\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi=0\text{ such that }R(w,z)=(w,z)+(\mathcal{E}\psi)(w,z),\;(w,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,h),

where
0<\alpha<1
and
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
denotes the one-dimensional unit diameter torus. The operator
\mathcal{E}
in (
1.13
) is the Cauchy-Riemann solver, defined in terms of the horizontal Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}
via

(1.14)

\mathscr{F}[{\mathcal{E}\psi}](\xi,z)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{{0}\}}(\xi)\Big(-\mathsf{i}\frac{\xi\cosh(2\pi|\xi|z)}{|\xi|\sinh(2\pi|\xi|h)}e_{1}+\frac{\sinh(2\pi|\xi|z)}{\sinh(2\pi|\xi|h)}e_{2}\Big)\mathscr{F}[\psi](\xi)

for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}
and
z\in(0,h)
. In particular, we have
(\mathcal{E}\psi)_{2}(\cdot,h)=\psi
. The operator
\mathcal{E}
is studied in greater detail in Lemma
2.1
below.

The map
R
discussed above permits us to change coordinates in (
1.10
) and reformulate to an equivalent system on the fixed periodic domain
\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)
for the unknown function
\psi
as in (
1.13
) and the flattened pressure
q:\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
q=-\mathrm{g}h+\Pi\circ R
. This system for
(\psi,q)
reads:

(1.15)

\begin{cases}\Delta q=\frac{\kappa}{2}\char 12\relax{\nabla R}^{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times(0,h),\\
\partial_{2}q=\kappa(\mathsf{f}\circ R)\cdot(\partial_{1}R)^{\perp}&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\},\\
\partial_{2}q=\mathrm{c}\partial_{1}\psi+\kappa(\mathsf{f}\circ R)\cdot(\partial_{1}R)^{\perp}\text{ and }q=\mathrm{g}\psi+\kappa(\upphi\circ R)&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\},\\
R(w,z)=(w,z)+(\mathcal{E}\psi)(w,z)&\text{for all }(w,z)\in\mathbb{T}\times(0,h).\end{cases}

To conclude this subsection, we now need to discuss one final equivalent reformulation of the traveling wave problem. Remarkably, we are able to reduce (
1.15
) into a single fully nonlinear and nonlocal pseudodifferential equation for the domain coordinate function
\psi
. The description of this reduction procedure requires the introduction of two additional linear operators. These are the mappings

(1.16)

S:C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\text{ and }G:C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})

with actions

(1.17)

S(f^{1},f^{2})=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{2}u\text{ and }Gf^{3}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{2}v

where
u
and
v
are the unique solutions to the PDEs

(1.18)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=f^{1}&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times(0,h),\\
u=0&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\},\\
\partial_{2}u=f^{2}&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\},\end{cases}\text{ and }\begin{cases}\Delta v=0&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times(0,h),\\
v=f^{3}&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\},\\
\partial_{2}v=0&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\},\end{cases}

for given
(f^{1},f^{2})\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and
f^{3}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. Here
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{z}\}}
denotes the trace operator onto
\mathbb{T}\times\{{z}\}
for
z\in\{{0,h}\}
. Notice that
G
is the flat, finite-depth Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator

(1.19)

\mathscr{F}[Gf](\xi)=2\pi\char 12\relax{\xi}\tanh(2\pi h\char 12\relax{\xi})\mathscr{F}[f](\xi).

By using
S
and
G
as defined above, system (
1.15
) is seen to be equivalent to solving first for
\psi
in the identity

(1.20)

G(\mathrm{g}\psi+\kappa\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}})(\upphi\circ R)-\mathrm{c}\partial_{1}\psi-\kappa\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R)\cdot(\partial_{1}R)^{\perp}\\
+\kappa S((|\nabla R|^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R)\cdot(\partial_{1}R)^{\perp})=0,

with the understanding that
R
is defined in terms of
\psi
as in the final equation of (
1.15
), and then solving for
q
in terms of
\psi
and the data using the first, second, and fourth equations of (
1.15
). The latter task is inverting a simple linear elliptic PDE, and so the all of the remaining difficulty is in the analysis and construction of solutions to equation (
1.20
).

In order for the correspondence between the nonlinear pseudodifferential equation (
1.20
) and the free boundary system (
1.28
) to be invertible we require that the flattening map
R
, defined in terms of
\psi
via (
1.13
), is a diffeomorphism up to the boundary. However, the operator comprising the left hand side of the equation (
1.20
) continues to be well-defined for all
\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
with vanishing mean.

1.4.
Main results and discussion

Let us fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define the open set of admissible domain coordinate functions

(1.21)

\mathcal{U}=\Big\{{\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\;:\;\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi=0,\;R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times[{0,h}]\to\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)\text{ is injective and }\min_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}\det\nabla R_{\psi}>0}\Big\},

where for
\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
the function
R_{\psi}
is the associated Riemann mapping

(1.22)

R_{\psi}(w,z)=(w,z)+(\mathcal{E}\psi)(w,z)\text{ for all }(w,z)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]

and
\mathcal{E}
is the Cauchy-Riemann solver defined in (
1.14
). For each
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
the map
R_{\psi}
, being biholomorphic, is a smooth diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)
onto its image that extends to a
C^{1,\alpha}
-diffeomorphism between the closures. For each such
\psi
, the map
R_{\psi}
satisfies
e_{2}\cdot R_{\psi}=0
when restricted to the set
\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}
. Thus the bottom boundary of the image
R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
is always
\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}
while the other boundary component is always a
C^{1,\alpha}
-curve sitting above.

On the set
\mathcal{U}
we define the maximal distortion function

(1.23)

\mathscr{D}:\mathcal{U}\to(0,\infty)\text{ via }\mathscr{D}(\psi)=\sup\Big\{{\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}}(w_{0},w_{1})}{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}}(R_{\psi}(w_{0},h),R_{\psi}(w_{1},h))}\;:\;w_{0},w_{1}\in\mathbb{T}\text{ and }w_{0}\neq w_{1}}\Big\}.

Roughly speaking,
\mathscr{D}(\psi)
is a measurement of the Riemann mapping diffeomorphism’s complexity in the sense that
1/\mathscr{D}(\psi)
being smaller means that
\psi
is closer to the complement of
\mathcal{U}
. Notice that the distortion
\mathscr{D}(\psi)
as defined above only depends on the trace of the flattening map
R_{\psi}
on the top boundary
\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}
. Thus, since

(1.24)

\mathbb{T}\ni w\mapsto R_{\psi}(w,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

is a conformal parameterization of the top boundary component of
R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
, we can interpret
\mathscr{D}(\psi)<\infty
as a quantitative verification that (
1.24
) is injective and has nowhere vanishing derivative. See Proposition
4.1
below for more information on the set
\mathcal{U}
and the function
\mathscr{D}
.

We are now in a position to state our main results, whose proofs can be found in Section
4.2
below. The first main theorem discusses the large solutions to the reformulated problem (
1.20
) that we are able to construct.

Theorem 1

(Large traveling wave solutions to the reformulated equation)
.

Let
\mathsf{f}\in C^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\upphi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
, and
\mathrm{g},\mathrm{c}\in[0,\infty)
satisfy one of the following ellipticity conditions

(1.25)

[{\mathrm{g}>0\text{ and }\partial_{2}\upphi\geqslant e_{2}\cdot\mathsf{f}}]\text{ or }[{\mathrm{c}\neq 0\text{ and }\mathrm{c}(\partial_{1}\upphi-e_{1}\cdot\mathsf{f})\geqslant 0}]\quad\text{on~}\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then, there exists a connected solution set
(0,0)\in\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
(the overset circle denotes the average zero subspace) such that for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
the equation (
1.20
) is satisfied with
\kappa=\upkappa^{2}
. Moreover, we have that
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})\supset\{{(0,0)}\}
and for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
the function

(1.26)

\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}+\mathscr{D}(\psi)\in(0,\infty)

is unbounded, where
\mathscr{D}
is the distortion as in (
1.23
) and
\mathcal{U}
is as in (
1.21
). In the absence of the bulk force, i.e.
\mathsf{f}=0
, the blow-up quantity (
1.26
) can be improved in the sense that for all
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
the function

(1.27)

\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}+\mathscr{D}(\psi)\in(0,\infty)

is unbounded.

Theorem
1
tells us that the zero solution
(\kappa,\psi)=0
to equation (
1.20
) is part of a connected set of solutions to the same equation in which the blow-up quantity (
1.26
) (or (
1.27
) when
\mathsf{f}=0
) grows arbitrarily large. This means that one of three things has to happen along this solution set. It could be the case that the forcing amplitude parameter
\kappa
becomes arbitrarily large and yet solutions continue to exist. Otherwise, it is some measurement of the geometry of the domain
\Omega_{\psi}=R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\subset\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
that is becoming unboundedly large along this solution set: either some norm measuring the regularity of the free boundary’s conformal parametrization (
1.24
) (meaning
\lVert\psi\rVert_{X}\to\infty
for
X=H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})
or
X=C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})
) or else the distortion (meaning the conformal parameterization is degenerating due to
\mathscr{D}(\psi)\to\infty
). Finally, we note that the first ellipticity condition in (
1.25
) is satisfied if
\mathrm{g}>0
and
\upphi(x)=\upphi(x_{1})
, which is precisely the setup considered in
[
58
]
and
[
14
]
.

Our second main result simply checks that the reduction computations of Section
1.3
are valid along the solution set produced by Theorem
1
. Thus the objects produced by our first main theorem genuinely correspond to large solutions to the traveling wave equations of free boundary incompressible Darcy flow.

Theorem 2

(Transfer of solutions to the original free boundary problem)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
1
and let
\mathcal{C}
be the connected solution set produced therein. Then, for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
the following hold.

(1)

The flattening map
R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]\to\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)
is a class
C^{1,\alpha}
diffeomorphism onto its image that is smooth in the interior; let us denote
\Omega_{\psi}=R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
.

(2)

There exists
\mathrm{v}\in C^{\alpha}(\Omega_{\psi};\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\mathrm{p}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega_{\psi})
, class
C^{4}
in the interior
\Omega_{\psi}
, solving in the classical sense the nondimensional equations of traveling free boundary incompressible Darcy flow:

(1.28)

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\mathrm{v}=0\text{ and }\mathrm{v}+\nabla\mathrm{p}=-\mathrm{g}e_{2}+\upkappa^{2}\mathsf{f}&\text{ in }\Omega_{\psi},\\
e_{2}\cdot\mathrm{v}=0&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}\\
(\mathrm{v}-\mathrm{c}e_{1})\cdot\nu=0\text{ and }\mathrm{p}=\upkappa^{2}\upphi&\text{on }R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}),\end{cases}

with
\nu:R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denoting the outward pointing unit normal to the top boundary component of
\Omega_{\psi}
.

We say that the domains
\Omega_{\psi}
discussed in Theorem
2
are graphical if

(1.29)

\exists\;\eta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{T})\text{ such that }\Omega_{\psi}=\{{(w,z)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)\;:\;0<z<\eta(w)}\}.

This condition may fail for sufficiently large traveling waves. Indeed,
\Omega_{\psi}
is not graphical for
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
only if there exists a point
w\in\mathbb{T}
such that we have
e_{1}\cdot\partial_{1}R_{\psi}(w,h)\leqslant 0
. Since
e_{1}\cdot\partial_{1}R_{0}(w,h)=1
, the portion of Theorem
1
’s solution set
\mathcal{C}
within a neighborhood of
\psi=0
are necesarily graphical, but this may not remain true for the larger waves. While in this paper we do not explicitly prove the existence or lack thereof of traveling wave solutions to (
1.28
) that are not graphical, our conformal formulation and construction opens the door to future study on the possible development of overturning traveling waves along such solution sets.

Let us now enter a brief overarching discussion on the proofs of our main results. The strategy is to employ a global implicit function theorem (GIFT) based on the Leray-Schauder degree to the operator determined by the left hand side of the equation (
1.20
). Similar techniques have been used in recent years for the construction of large solutions to other physical nonlinear PDEs, e.g.
[
73
,
74
,
75
,
58
]
. However, the particular GIFTs implemented in these results, that are a special case of Theorem II.6.1 in Kielhöfer
[
45
]
, require the operators to manifest as nonlinear compact perturbations of the identity. Not all versions of GIFTs in the literature possess the aforementioned structural mandate; in particular, the class of analytic global implicit function and bifurcation theorems (see, e.g.
[
15
,
21
,
22
]
and references therein) are more flexible in the forms of the nonlinear operators that they can handle; but, as the name suggests, real analyticity of the operator is required. We emphasize that analytic GIFT methods were employed in the traveling surface wave problem for the viscous shallow water equations in
[
70
]
.

However, the particular nonlinear equation (
1.20
) is simply not within the scope of analytic GIFTs for the reason that the nonlinear operator fails to be analytic under natural smoothness assumptions on the data profiles
\mathsf{f}
and
\upphi
. The reason is that the numerous composition-type nonlinearities control the maximal order of Fréchet differentiability of the nonlinear operator in terms of the data profile regularity. We would have to then select real analytic data to get a real analytic operator, which is, in the authors’ opinion, an unnecessarily restrictive choice. Thus, wanting to use data of a reasonable finite amount of regularity, we are led back to the convenient choice of using a real-variable GIFT based on the Leray-Schauder degree. Working towards massaging the equations in the form of a compact perturbation of the identity, we first identify a ‘quasilinearization’ of the operator of (
1.20
). Letting
\kappa=\upkappa^{2}
and writing this equation abstractly as
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
, we then are able to decompose the operator
\mathbf{P}
as

(1.30)

\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\psi+\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)

with
\mathbf{A}
, defined in (
3.1
), valued in the space of linear operators
\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathbf{Q}
, defined in (
2.68
), a nonlinear and compact map
\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
.

As it turns out, the operator
\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)
appearing in (
1.30
) is invertible with suitable estimates if we have satisfied the hidden ellipticity conditions (
1.25
). In these cases, we can apply the inverse operator
[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}
to the equation
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
to derive the equivalent functional form

(1.31)

\mathbf{F}(\upkappa,\psi)=\psi+[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)=0.

We are then able to prove that the difference between
\mathbf{F}
and the identity is indeed a compact nonlinear operator. We also calculate that the Fréchet derivative
D_{2}\mathbf{F}(0,0):\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
is an isomorphism. Therefore, we can apply the global implicit function theorem recorded in Theorem 4.6 of
[
58
]
. Upon doing so we are granted a connected solution set

(1.32)

\mathcal{C}\subset\{{\mathbf{F}=0}\}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})

such that
\mathcal{C}
is either unbounded or
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{{(0,0)}\}
is connected. This latter alternative is what is known as the closed loop. We show that the only solution to
\mathbf{F}(0,\psi)=0
is
\psi=0
and deduce that the closed loop alternative does not occur. So indeed the former alternative holds and the function

(1.33)

\mathcal{C}\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\in(0,\infty)

is unbounded. Let us pause to make a comparison with the construction of large traveling waves with surface tension in
[
58
]
, that used the same GIFT. In the presence of surface tension, by inverting the capillary-gravity operator
H+\mathrm{g}I
,
H
being the mean curvature operator, the needed compactness is immediate due to the two derivative gain from the inverse
(H+\mathrm{g}I)^{-1}
. In the present work, as hinted above, the compactness is delicate and comes from the hidden ellipticity, which leads to the invertibility of
\mathbf{A}
, and the commutator structure of
\mathbf{Q}
.

The next step is to show that the initial blow-up quantity (
1.33
) can be refined by replacing the high norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}
appearing above with a weaker norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X}
such that
C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow X
. The goal is to take
X
as large as possible, as to learn as much as we can about the nature of the blow-up. We achieve this through careful paradifferential commutator estimates and are able to take
X=H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})
for any
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
. Under the additional structural assumption that the bulk force
\mathsf{f}
vanishes identically, we can sharpen even further via similar methods and take
X=C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})
for any
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
.

Finally, we are only interested in the members of
\mathcal{C}
that correspond to meaningful solutions to the original free boundary problem, and so we need to study
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the set of admissible domain coordinate functions (
1.21
). In other words, we need to understand what is happening with the Riemann mappings
R_{\psi}
at the possible scenario when large
\psi
in
\mathcal{C}
are leaving the set
\mathcal{U}
; when this is happening, the Riemann mappings are failing extend to the boundary. Using tools from classical complex analysis, we are able to deduce that
R_{\psi}
can
only
fail to be a diffeomorphism up to the boundary if the distortion
\mathscr{D}(\psi)
(defined in (
1.23
)) is becoming unboundedly large. Unpacking the meaning of
\mathscr{D}(\psi)\to\infty
, shows that either the top free boundary of
\Omega_{\psi}
becomes arbitrarily close to self-intersection or the conformal parametrizations of the free surfaces (
1.24
) degenerate via a vanishing derivative.

The remainder of the paper, following a brief digression on notation in Section
1.5
, is organized into three body sections. In Section
2
we record a flurry of technical estimates and mapping properties of the constituent pieces of the operator on the left hand side of equation (
1.20
). Having developed an understating of the nuances of the objects involved, we then proceed with an unencumbered construction and analysis of large solutions to the operator equation in Section
3
. Finally, in Section
4
, we explore conformal degeneracy and complete the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
.

Lemma 2.1

For any

Proof.

The identities (

The left hand mapping property claimed in (

We turn our attention to proving the right hand mapping properties asserted in equation (

Lemma 2.3

(Analysis of the lower-order bulk remainder)
.

Let
\mathsf{f}\in C^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
0<\alpha<1
, and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
. The mapping

(2.24)

\psi\mapsto\mathbf{K}(\psi)=S((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp})

is well-defined, continuously differentiable in the Fréchet sense, and maps bounded sets to bounded sets for the following domain and codomain pairs:

(2.25)

\mathbf{K}:\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),\;\mathbf{K}:\mathring{H}^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})\to W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}),\;\text{and }\mathbf{K}:\mathring{H}^{1+\alpha/2}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

Proof.

Let us begin with the proof of the left hand mapping property claimed in equation (
2.25
). We first note that by standard Schauder theory (see, e.g.,
[
40
,
61
]
), the linear map
S
(defined in (
1.17
)), maps boundedly

(2.26)

S:C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

and, therefore, it is sufficient to observe that

(2.27)

\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp})\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})

is a well-defined mapping that is class
C^{1}
and maps bounded sets to bounded sets. This latter fact, in turn, is a consequence of Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, simple algebra properties of the spaces involved, and the observation that

(2.28)

\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{R}^{2})

is, in fact, an affine linear smoothing operator.

We now focus on the claimed middle hand mapping property of equation (
2.25
). A key point is that the map
S
, now thought of as the function

(2.29)

S:L^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})\to W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})

remains well-defined and bounded, thanks to elliptic estimates in Lebesgue spaces and Sobolev space trace theory (see, e.g.,
[
40
,
49
]
). Since
\psi\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}R_{\psi}
is a smoothing operator, the second component of the map (
2.27
) again gives us no trouble in verifying that the function

(2.30)

\mathring{H}^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}\in W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})

has the desired mapping properties.

By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, the Sobolev embedding

(2.31)

H^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\hookrightarrow L^{2/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)),

and the boundedness of the product map

(2.32)

L^{2/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\times L^{2/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))\to L^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))

we deduce that

(2.33)

\mathring{H}^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto(\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi}\in L^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))

is a well-defined mapping that is Fréchet
C^{1}
and maps bounded sets to bounded sets. Upon synthesizing the above, we complete the proof of the middle mapping property of equation (
2.25
).

The proof of the final mapping property of (
2.25
) is much the same. By similar arguments, we deduce that the following mappings are well-defined and map bounded sets to bounded sets:

(2.34)

\mathring{H}^{1+\alpha/2}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp})\in L^{2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T})\times W^{(1+\alpha)/2,2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T})

and

(2.35)

S:L^{2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T})\times W^{(1+\alpha)/2,2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T})\to W^{(1+\alpha)/2,2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T}).

So the result follows by composing (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) and appealing to the embedding

(2.36)

W^{(1+\alpha)/2,2/(1-\alpha)}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

∎

The final result of this subsection develops important commutator estimates for certain simple Fourier multipliers that we introduce now. For suitable functions
\phi
we define
\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\phi
via

(2.37)

\mathscr{F}[\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\phi](\xi)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{N}^{+}}(\pm\xi)\mathscr{F}[{\phi}](\xi),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}_{+}\phi+\mathbb{P}_{-}\phi=\phi-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi
; moreover if
\phi
is
\mathbb{R}
valued, then
\mathbb{P}_{+}\phi
is
\mathbb{R}
-valued if and only if it vanishes identically.

Proposition 2.4

(Commutator estimates for
\mathbb{P}_{\pm}
)
.

For
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
,
\varepsilon<\delta
, there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(2.38)

\lVert\mathbb{P}_{\pm}(f\partial_{1}g)-f\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert g\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}.

For
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(2.39)

\begin{cases}\lVert\mathbb{P}_{\pm}(f\partial_{1}g)-f\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{B^{\beta+\gamma-1,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C\|f\|_{B^{\gamma,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert g\rVert_{C^{\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\text{if~}\beta<1~\text{and~}\beta+\gamma>1,\\
\lVert\mathbb{P}_{\pm}(f\partial_{1}g)-f\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{B^{\gamma,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C\|f\|_{B^{\gamma,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert g\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\quad\text{if~}\gamma>0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Before we enter the heart of the proof, we must first introduce the necessary notation associated with standard Littlewood-Paley decompositions. Let us fix a pair of smooth functions
\chi,\widetilde{\chi}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that:
\widetilde{\chi}(\xi)=0
when
|\xi|\geqslant 6/5
,
\chi(\xi)=1
for
1\leqslant|\xi|\leqslant 2
,
\chi(\xi)=0
for
|\xi|>11/5
and
|\xi|<4/5
, and

(2.40)

\widetilde{\chi}(\xi)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\chi(\xi/2^{j})=1\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

The associated sequences of Fourier multiplication operators
\{{\Updelta_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{{\Upsigma_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are then defined via

(2.41)

\mathscr{F}[\Updelta_{j}\phi](\xi)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{\chi}(\xi)\mathscr{F}[\psi](\xi)&\text{if }j=0,\\
\chi(\xi/2^{j-1})\mathscr{F}[\psi](\xi)&\text{if }j>0,\end{cases}\text{ and }\Upsigma_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\Updelta_{i}.

The identity (
2.40
) ensures that the Littlewood-Paley decomposition
\phi=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Updelta_{j}\phi
holds in a suitable sense for sufficiently regular
\phi
.

We only prove estimates (
2.38
) and (
2.39
) for the case
\mathbb{P}_{+}
; the analysis for
\mathbb{P}_{-}
is identical. Let us now write

(2.42)

\Lambda^{+}(f,g)=\mathbb{P}_{+}(f\partial_{1}g)-f\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}g

to denote the action of the bilinear operator under consideration. By Fourier support considerations, we deduce that (
2.42
) is truly a high-low paraproduct in the sense that

(2.43)

\Lambda^{+}(f,g)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Lambda^{+}(\Updelta_{j}f,\Upsigma_{j+10}g).

The
j^{\mathrm{th}}
term in the series above is supported in the centered interval of length
2^{j+20}
.

We first prove the claimed estimate (
2.38
). By using (
2.43
), support considerations, and Hölder’s inequality, we first estimate for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

(2.44)

\lVert\Updelta_{k}(\Lambda^{+}(f,g))\rVert_{L^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\sum_{j\geqslant\max\{{0,k-20}\}}\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{2/(1-2\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert\Upsigma_{j+10}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

Then, thanks to Bernstein-type inequalities, we may further bound for each
j

(2.45)

\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{2/(1-2\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 2^{\varepsilon j}\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\text{ and }\lVert\Upsigma_{j+10}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 2^{(1/2-\varepsilon)j}\lVert g\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}.

By combining (
2.44
) and (
2.45
) with the Littlewood-Paley norm on
W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})
(see (
1.34
)), we get

(2.46)

\lVert\Lambda^{+}(f,g)\rVert_{W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\lVert g\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(2^{\varepsilon k}\sum_{j\geqslant\max\{{0,k-20}\}}2^{j/2}\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\Big)^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}\Big)^{1-\varepsilon}.

In turn, Young’s convolution inequality grants us the bound

(2.47)

\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(2^{\varepsilon k}\sum_{j\geqslant\max\{{0,k-20}\}}2^{j/2}\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\Big)^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}\Big)^{1-\varepsilon}\lesssim\lVert f\rVert_{B^{1/2+\varepsilon,2}_{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})}

with the implicit constant depending in particular on
\varepsilon>0
. To conclude (
2.38
) from (
2.46
) and (
2.47
) we use
\delta>\varepsilon
and the embedding

(2.48)

H^{1/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow B^{1/2+\varepsilon,2}_{1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T}).

Next, let us justify the estimates in (
2.39
). Similarly to (
2.44
), we have

(2.49)

\lVert\Updelta_{k}(\Lambda^{+}(f,g))\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\sum_{j\geqslant\max\{{0,k-20}\}}\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert\Upsigma_{j+10}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

We have

(2.50)

\lVert\Upsigma_{j+10}\partial_{1}g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim 2^{j(1-\beta)}\lVert g\rVert_{C^{\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\text{ and }\lVert\Updelta_{j}f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 2^{-j\gamma}\lVert f\rVert_{B^{\gamma,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

It follows that

(2.51)

\lVert\Updelta_{k}(\Lambda^{+}(f,g))\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 2^{k(1-\beta-\gamma)}\lVert f\rVert_{B^{\gamma,\infty}_{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lVert g\rVert_{C^{\beta}(\mathbb{T})}.

This implies the estimates in (
2.39
).
∎

2.2.
Instantiation of the nonlinear operator and decompositions

This subsection’s first result formalizes the left hand side of identity (
1.20
) as an operator acting between Banach spaces. We recall that the operators
S
and
G
are those defined in equation (
1.17
).

Lemma 2.5

(Operator encoding)
.

Fix
0<\alpha<1
,
\mathsf{f}\in C^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\upphi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
, and
\mathbb{R}\ni\mathrm{c},\mathrm{g}\geqslant 0
. The map
\mathbf{P}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
with action given by

(2.52)

\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=G(\mathrm{g}\psi+\upkappa^{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\upphi\circ R_{\psi})-\mathrm{c}\partial_{1}\psi-\upkappa^{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}\\
+\upkappa^{2}S((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp})

is well-defined and continuously differentiable.

Proof.

We begin by noting that by standard Schauder theory (see, e.g.,
[
40
,
61
]
) the linear map
G
, defined in (
1.17
), maps boundedly
G:C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. Thus after heeding Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, we deduce that
\mathbf{P}
, when thought of as a mapping into the larger space

(2.53)

\mathbf{P}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),

is well-defined and continuously differentiable as soon as we verify that

(2.54)

\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}\in C^{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{T}\right)

has the same desired mapping properties. But this latter fact is a simple upshot of Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
and the fact that
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
is a Banach algebra.

To complete the proof we need to check that the map of equation (
2.53
) actually has image within the subspace of functions with vanishing zeroth Fourier mode. A straightforward computation using the chain rule and the Cauchy-Riemann equations satisfied by
R_{\psi}
shows that

(2.55)

(\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi}=\nabla\cdot([{\nabla R_{\psi}}]^{\mathrm{t}}(f\circ R_{\psi})),

with the superscript ‘
\mathrm{t}
’ denoting the matrix transpose. Now let
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
denote the solution to the left hand equations of (
1.18
) so that

(2.56)

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{2}u=S((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}).

Integration of the bulk equations satisfied by
u
over the set
\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)
when paired with identity (
2.55
) and the divergence theorem shows

(2.57)

\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{2}u-\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)}\Delta u=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)}\frac{1}{2}\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot\nabla R_{\psi}e_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot\nabla R_{\psi}e_{2}.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations satisfied by
R_{\psi}
also inform us that
\nabla R_{\psi}e_{2}=(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}
; hence, identities (
2.57
) and (
2.56
) imply that

(2.58)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}S((\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}/2)(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp})=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}.

The mapping of
\mathbf{P}
into the subspace
\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
is now established.
∎

Note that in the operator encoding (
2.52
) we have replaced the
\kappa
that appears in (
1.20
) by
\upkappa^{2}
. This is done purely for technical reasons related to the desire to have smooth dependence on
\upkappa
and the nonnegativity
\kappa\geqslant 0
.

Our next result constructs a critically important decomposition for the operator
\mathbf{P}
from (
2.52
). We shall split
\mathbf{P}
into the sum of a leading order term and a compact, lower order remainder. Recall the notation for the projection operators
\mathbb{P}_{\pm}
introduced in equation (
2.37
).

Proposition 2.6

(Operator decomposition)
.

Let
\alpha
,
\mathsf{f}
,
\upphi
,
\mathrm{c}
, and
\mathrm{g}
be as in Lemma
2.5
. For
\upkappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
we write

(2.59)

\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}=\upkappa^{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}_{1}-\partial_{1}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}-\mathrm{c}\text{ and }\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}=\upkappa^{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\partial_{2}\upphi-\mathsf{f}_{2})\circ R_{\psi}+\mathrm{g}.

The following hold.

(1)

For
\upiota\in\{{0,1}\}
the maps

(2.60)

\mathring{C}^{\upiota,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi},\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}\in C^{\upiota,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})

are well-defined, continuously differentiable, and map bounded sets to bounded sets.

(2)

There exists a continuously differentiable and compact operator
\mathbf{Q}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
such that

(2.61)

\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=\mathrm{Re}[{\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi)+\mathbb{P}_{-}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\psi)}]+\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)

for all
\upkappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
.

(3)

Assume that one of the hidden ellipticity conditions of equation (
1.25
) holds. Then, for all
\upkappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
the
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions
\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}\pm\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}
are nowhere vanishing and for
\upiota\in\{{0,1}\}
the maps

(2.62)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\upiota,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}\pm\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}),\;(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}\pm\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}\in C^{\upiota,\alpha}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})

are well-defined, continuously differentiable, and map bounded sets to bounded sets.

Proof.

The first and third items follow from arguments similar to that of the proof of Lemma
2.2
; we omit the repetitive details for brevity. Thus, we shall only focus on the second item.

We give an explicit presentation of the operator
\mathbf{Q}
. To begin, we define the Fourier multiplication operators
\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}
via

(2.63)

\mathscr{F}[\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}\phi](\xi)=2\pi|\xi|(\tanh(2\pi|\xi|h)-1)\mathscr{F}[\phi](\xi)\\
\text{and }\mathscr{F}[\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\phi](\xi)=\begin{cases}2\pi|\xi|(\coth(2\pi|\xi|h)-1)\mathscr{F}[\phi](\xi)&\text{if }\xi\neq 0,\\
\mathscr{F}[\phi](0)&\text{if }\xi=0,\end{cases}

for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}
and suitable functions
\phi
. Notice that the symbols above decay exponentially and so the operators
\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}
are smoothing. The purpose of their introduction is the following pair of useful decomposition identities:

(2.64)

G=\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}-\mathsf{i}\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}+\mathsf{i}\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-}\text{ and }\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{1}R_{\psi}=e_{1}+(-\mathsf{i}\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}+\mathsf{i}\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-})\psi e_{1}+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi e_{1}+\partial_{1}\psi e_{2},

where
G
is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator (
1.19
).

In the remainder of this proof,
\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi}
is evaluated at
z=h
. Using the aforementioned operators and decompositions along with
[\cdot,\cdot]
to denote the commutator bracket, we equate

(2.65)

(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp}=-\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{f}_{1}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{f}_{2})\circ R_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi)-\mathbb{P}_{-}((\mathsf{f}_{1}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{f}_{2})\circ R_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\psi)\\
+[(\mathsf{f}_{1}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{f}_{2})\circ R_{\psi},\mathbb{P}_{-}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\psi+[(\mathsf{f}_{1}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{f}_{2})\circ R_{\psi},\mathbb{P}_{+}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-}\psi+(\mathsf{f}_{2}\circ R_{\psi})(1+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi)\\
-\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathsf{f}_{1}\circ R_{\psi})\partial_{1}\psi+\mathsf{i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathsf{f}_{2}\circ R_{\psi})(\mathbb{P}_{-}-\mathbb{P}_{+})\partial_{1}\psi

and

(2.66)

G(\upphi\circ R_{\psi})=-\mathbb{P}_{+}((\partial_{1}\upphi+\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi)-\mathbb{P}_{-}((\partial_{1}\upphi-\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\psi)\\
+[\mathbb{P}_{-},(\partial_{1}\upphi+\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\psi+[\mathbb{P}_{+},(\partial_{1}\upphi-\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-}\psi\\
+\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}(\upphi\circ R_{\psi})+\mathsf{i}(\mathbb{P}_{-}-\mathbb{P}_{+})((\partial_{1}\upphi\circ R_{\psi})(1+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi)).

The first two terms on the right hand sides of (
2.65
) and (
2.66
) are the highest order contributions whereas the remaining terms are lower order. Substitution of (
2.64
), (
2.65
), and (
2.66
) into the definition of
\mathbf{P}
in identity (
2.52
) gives one the formula

(2.67)

\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi)+\mathbb{P}_{-}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\psi)+\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)

with the operator
\mathbf{Q}
explicitly written as

(2.68)

\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)=\upkappa^{2}[\mathbb{P}_{-},(\mathsf{f}_{1}+\partial_{1}\upphi+\mathsf{i}(\mathsf{f}_{2}+\partial_{2}\upphi))\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\psi+\upkappa^{2}[\mathbb{P}_{+},(\mathsf{f}_{1}+\partial_{1}\upphi-\mathsf{i}(\mathsf{f}_{2}+\partial_{2}\upphi))\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-}\psi\\
-\upkappa^{2}(\mathsf{f}_{2}\circ R_{\psi})(1+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi)+\kappa^{2}\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}(\upphi\circ R_{\psi})+\mathsf{i}\upkappa^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{-}-\mathbb{P}_{+})((\partial_{1}\upphi\circ R_{\psi})(1+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi))+\mathrm{g}\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}\psi\\
+\upkappa^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathsf{f}_{1}\circ R_{\psi})\partial_{1}\psi-\mathsf{i}\upkappa^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathsf{f}_{2}\circ R_{\psi})(\mathbb{P}_{-}-\mathbb{P}_{+})\partial_{1}\psi+\upkappa^{2}\mathbf{K}(\psi),

where
\mathbf{K}:\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
is the lower order operator introduced in (
2.24
).

To acquire the desired identity (
2.61
) from (
2.67
) and deduce that
\mathbf{Q}
is indeed
\mathbb{R}
-valued, we observe

(2.69)

\mathrm{Im}[{\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi)+\mathbb{P}_{-}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\psi)}]=0.

Indeed, identity (
2.69
) can be directly checked by showing the Fourier transform of the argument of the above imaginary part, call it
\phi
, satisfies the reality preservation condition
\mathscr{F}[\phi](-\xi)=\overline{\mathscr{F}[\phi](\xi)}
, with the overline bar denoting complex conjugation.

The final tasks of the proof are to check that
\mathbf{Q}
as defined in (
2.68
) is continuously differentiable and compact as a mapping between the stated spaces. Continuous differentiability is a consequence of composing the analysis of Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
with simple product-type nonlinearities; in particular, there is no need to actually exploit any of the commutator cancellations.

Compactness of
\mathbf{Q}
, on the other hand, does additionally require the commutator bounds of Proposition
2.4
; specifically, we need the second estimate of (
2.39
). But, once we are armed with this tool, we deduce actually that as the mapping into the smaller space

(2.70)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathring{C}^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})

the operator
\mathbf{Q}
remains bounded on bounded sets. The compactness of
\mathbf{Q}
as a mapping into
\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
is now a consequence of compact embedding
\mathring{C}^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
.
∎

The last result of this subsection records finer estimates available on the constituents of the decomposition of equation (
2.61
).

Proposition 2.7

(More precise operator decomposition estimates)
.

Let
\alpha
,
\mathsf{f}
,
\upphi
,
\mathrm{c}
, and
\mathrm{g}
be as in Lemma

and let
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
,

,

. The following hold.

(1)

The map
\mathbf{Q}
from the second item of Proposition
2.6
uniquely extends to well-defined continuous functions

(2.71)

\mathbf{Q}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{H}^{1/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T})\to W^{\varepsilon,1/(1-\varepsilon)}(\mathbb{T})\text{ and }\mathbf{Q}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{H}^{1+\alpha/2}(\mathbb{T})\to C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

that send bounded sets to bounded sets.

(2)

Assume that one of the ellipticity conditions of equation (

) holds. Then the maps

(2.72)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{H}^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}\pm\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}),\;(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}\pm\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}\in H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})

are well-defined and send bounded sets to bounded sets.

Proof.

The second item follows from arguments similar to that of the proof of Lemma

The first item is a direct calculation on the expression for

and

, and finally bounds of the type

We again elect to omit the routine verification.
∎

3.
Analysis of large solutions to the nonlinear operator equation

Armed with the preliminary analysis of Section
2
, we can now begin the production of large solutions to the equation (
1.20
). The strategy is to reformulate the operator equation as a nonlinear compact perturbation of the identity and import Leray-Schauder degree theory manifesting as a global implicit function theorem. This reformulation is possible when we assume the satisfaction of one of the ellipticity conditions of (
1.25
). After this initial construction of the solutions, we then determine the weaker norms in which they are large.

3.1.
Construction of the connected set of solutions

Recall that in Proposition
2.6
we decomposed our main nonlinear operator
\mathbf{P}
into the sum of a minimal principal part and a compact lower order remainder
\mathbf{Q}
. Our next result establishes some crucial properties for this principal part.

Proposition 3.1

(Properties of the principal part)
.

Let
\alpha
,
\mathsf{f}
,
\upphi
,
\mathrm{c}
, and
\mathrm{g}
be as in Lemma
2.5
and assume one of the ellipticity conditions (
1.25
) is satisfied. The following hold.

(1)

The operator valued function
\mathbf{A}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T});\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))
, with action

(3.1)

\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi=\mathrm{Re}[{\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi)+\mathbb{P}_{-}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}+\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\Psi)}]

for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and
\Psi\in\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
, is well-defined and maps bounded sets to bounded sets, and is continuously differentiable.

(2)

The map
\mathbf{A}
introduced in the previous item takes values in the set of invertible operators and the family of inverses

(3.2)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T});\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))

map bounded sets to bounded sets and are continuously differentiable.

(3)

The map
\mathbf{A}
satisfies for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
the equality

(3.3)

\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\psi+\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)

where
\mathbf{Q}
is defined in (
2.68
); hence, for all such
(\upkappa,\psi)
we have that
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
if and only if
\psi=-[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)
.

Proof.

The first item is a direct computation and, assuming the second item is true, so too is the third item. Thus, we only focus on the proof of the second item.

We begin with the proof of the following auxiliary claim. For each
1\leqslant\lambda<\infty
there exists
C_{\lambda}<\infty
with the property that for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying
|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\lambda
and all
\Psi\in\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
we have the closed range estimate

(3.4)

\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C_{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}.

As a first step to showing estimate (
3.4
) to be true, we derive a Sobolev space variant. By orthogonality and reality considerations, we have the equalities

(3.5)

-\mathrm{Re}\Big[{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\overline{(\mathbb{P}_{+}+\mathbb{P}_{-})\partial_{1}\Psi}}\Big]=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}(\char 12\relax{\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi}^{2}+\char 12\relax{\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\Psi}^{2})

and

(3.6)

-\mathrm{Im}\Big[{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\overline{(\mathbb{P}_{+}-\mathbb{P}_{-})\partial_{1}\Psi}}\Big]=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}(\char 12\relax{\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi}^{2}+\char 12\relax{\mathbb{P}_{-}\partial_{1}\Psi}^{2}).

The satisfaction of the ellipticity conditions (
1.25
) means that either
\char 12\relax{\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}}\geqslant\char 12\relax{\mathrm{c}}>0
or
\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi}\geqslant\mathrm{g}>0
. In either case, we can use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and identities (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) to deduce that the following weaker version of (
3.4
) holds

(3.7)

\lVert\Psi\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C_{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

We now shall use (
3.7
) with a parametrix and interpolation argument to boost up to the desired (
3.4
). Since

(3.8)

\mathbb{P}_{+}(\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi)=\mathbb{P}_{+}((\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi)

we can introduce a commutator and multiply by
(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}
to get

(3.9)

\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi=(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}(\mathbb{P}_{+}(\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi)+[{\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi},\mathbb{P}_{+}}]\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\Psi).

By taking the norm of (
3.9
) in the space
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and using the third item of Proposition
2.6
, the commutator estimate (
2.39
) from Proposition
2.4
, the boundedness of
\mathbb{P}_{+}
on the Hölder spaces, and noting that
\Psi
is
\mathbb{R}
-valued, we learn

(3.10)

\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}+\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}.

We pair inequality (
3.10
) with the embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow C^{1/2}(\mathbb{T})
, the estimate (
3.7
), and the interpolation bounds

(3.11)

\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1/2}(\mathbb{T})}^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}^{\frac{1}{1+2\alpha}}

to deduce

(3.12)

\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}+(\lVert\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)\Psi\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})})^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}(\lVert\Psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})})^{\frac{1}{1+2\alpha}}.

Estimate (
3.4
) now follows by Young’s inequality and absorption.

Upon having established the auxiliary claim, we realize that to complete the proof of the second item, it remains only to show that the pointwise inverse
[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}
exists for all
\upkappa
and
\psi
. Since we have the uniform closed ranges estimates (
3.4
), the sough after invertibility will follow from the method of continuity, see, e.g., Theorem 4.51 in Abramovich and Aliprantis
[
2
]
, and the establishment of the existence of
[{\mathbf{A}(0,0)}]^{-1}
. We compute explicitly that

(3.13)

\mathbf{A}(0,0)\Psi=-\mathrm{c}\partial_{1}\Psi-\mathsf{i}\mathrm{g}(\mathbb{P}_{+}-\mathbb{P}_{-})\partial_{1}\Psi

and so the inverse to
\mathbf{A}(0,0)
corresponds to the application of the Fourier multiplication operator with symbol

(3.14)

\mathbb{Z}\ni\xi\mapsto\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{{0}\}}(\xi)(2\pi\mathrm{g}|\xi|-2\pi\mathsf{i}\mathrm{c}\xi)^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}.

This inverse is well-defined since
\mathrm{g}\mathrm{c}\neq 0
under (
1.25
), and is bounded
\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
as claimed.
∎

The third item of Proposition
3.1
shows that solving the equation
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
is equivalent to finding the zero set of a compact perturbation of the identity. This latter formulation is particularly convenient for initializing our construction and analysis of large solutions, as it allows us to import Leray-Schauder degree theory. The subsequent result explores this direction.

Proposition 3.2

(Existence of a global connected set of solutions)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.1
. There exists a connected solution set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying the following properties.

(1)

We have
(0,0)\in\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

For all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
we have
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
.

(3)

The function

(3.15)

\mathcal{C}\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto\char 12\relax{\upkappa}+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\in\mathbb{R}

is unbounded.

Proof.

We use the global implicit function theorem for compact perturbations of the identity based on Leray-Schauder degree theory as formulated in Theorem 4.6 in Nguyen
[
58
]
; note that this is a special case of Theorem II.6.1 in Kielhöfer
[
45
]
.

The map
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
defined via

(3.16)

\mathbf{F}(\upkappa,\psi)=\psi+[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi),\;(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),

with
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{Q}
as in equations (
3.1
) and (
2.68
), respectively, is well-defined and class
C^{1}
in the Fréchet sense as a consequence of Propositions
2.6
and
3.1
. The third item of Proposition
3.1
implies that the zero sets of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{P}
coincide. Moreover, the difference between
\mathbf{F}
and the identity is compact, as it can be factored into the composition of the mapping

(3.17)

(\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni((\upkappa,\psi),\phi)\mapsto[{\mathbf{A}(\upkappa,\psi)}]^{-1}\phi\in\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),

that is bounded on bounded sets, and the compact mapping

(3.18)

\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto((\upkappa,\psi),\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi))\in(\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))\times\mathring{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

We also observe that the partial derivative of
\mathbf{F}
at the trivial solution is an isomorphism. Indeed, a direct computation unveils the identity

(3.19)

D_{2}\mathbf{F}(0,0)\Psi=\Psi+[{\mathbf{A}(0,0)}]^{-1}D_{2}\mathbf{Q}(0,0)\Psi

and hence, by taking Fourier transforms,

(3.20)

\mathscr{F}[{D_{2}\mathbf{F}(0,0)\Psi}](\xi)=\upmu(\xi)\mathscr{F}[\Psi](\xi)\text{ for }\upmu(\xi)=\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{{0}\}}(\xi)\Big(1+\frac{2\pi\mathrm{g}|\xi|(\tanh(2\pi|\xi|h)-1)}{2\pi\mathrm{g}|\xi|-2\pi\mathsf{i}\mathrm{c}\xi}\Big),\;\xi\in\mathbb{Z}.

The symbol
\upmu
in (
3.20
) is bounded above and below in modulus by positive constants (uniformly for
\xi\neq 0
) thanks to the satisfaction of condition (
1.25
); hence,
\mathrm{ker}D_{2}\mathbf{F}(0,0)=\{{0}\}
. Since
D_{2}F(0,0)
is Fredholm with vanishing index, being a compact perturbation of the identity, we conclude that
D_{2}\mathbf{F}(0,0)
is invertible.

The hypotheses of the global implicit function theorem as formulated in Theorem 4.6 in
[
58
]
are thus satisfied by the map
\mathbf{F}
(
3.16
). Therefore, if we let

(3.21)

\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{cl}\{{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\;:\;\mathbf{F}(\upkappa,\psi)=0}\}

denote the closure of the solution set
\{{\mathbf{F}=0}\}
and let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the connected component of
\mathcal{S}
that contains
(0,0)
, then either
\mathcal{C}
is unbounded or
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{{(0,0)}\}
is connected.

The proof is complete as soon as we show that
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{{(0,0)}\}
is disconnected. In fact, we claim that

(3.22)

\mathcal{C}\setminus\{{(0,0)}\}\subset(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{{0}\})\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),

which, in particular, shows that
\mathcal{C}\setminus\{{(0,0)}\}
is disconnected. We prove the contrapositive of (
3.22
). Suppose that
(\upkappa,\psi)\in(\{{0}\}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}))\cap\mathcal{C}
so that
\mathbf{F}(0,\psi)=0
. Then,
\psi+[{\mathbf{A}(0,0)}]^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(0,0)\psi=0
. In turn, for
\upmu
as in (
3.20
),

(3.23)

\forall\;\xi\in\mathbb{Z},\;\upmu(\xi)\mathscr{F}[{\psi}](\xi)=0\text{ and hence }\psi=0.

So indeed the claimed inclusion (
3.22
) holds.
∎

3.2.
Improvements on the blow-up quantity

In Proposition
3.2
we have constructed a connected set of solutions
\mathcal{C}
to the equation
\mathbf{P}=0
, where
\mathbf{P}
is the operator from (
2.52
), on which the function (
3.15
) is unbounded. The goal of this subsection is to sharpen as much as we can the quantity that is blowing up by showing that norms of much lower regularity are actually unboundedly large on the set
\mathcal{C}
.

Proposition 3.3

(Blow-up refinement, I)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.2
and let
\mathcal{C}
be the connected set of solutions constructed therein. For every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
the function

(3.24)

\mathcal{C}\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto\char 12\relax{\upkappa}+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}\in\mathbb{R}

is unbounded.

Proof.

We begin by proving an auxiliary estimate. We claim that for all
0<\beta<\delta\leqslant 1/2
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, with the property that for all
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
and

(3.25)

|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant M

we have that

(3.26)

|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+2\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant N.

So let us assume that
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
satisfy (
3.25
) and
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
. We use identity (
3.9
) with
\Psi=\psi
and
\mathbf{A}(\kappa,\psi)\psi=-\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)
to see that

(3.27)

\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi=(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}([\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi},\mathbb{P}_{+}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\psi-\mathbb{P}_{+}\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)).

The next step is to take the norm of identity (
3.27
) in the space
W^{\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})
. Thanks to the reality of
\psi
and to the continuity of the embedding and product maps

(3.28)

W^{1+\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow H^{1/2+2\beta}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})\text{ and }H^{1/2+\beta}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})\times W^{\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})\to W^{\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})

we get

(3.29)

\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+2\beta}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\lVert\mathbb{P}_{+}\partial_{1}\psi\rVert_{W^{1+\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T})}\\
\lesssim\lVert(\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi})^{-1}\rVert_{H^{1/2+\beta}(\mathbb{T})}(\lVert[\mathsf{X}_{\upkappa,\psi}-\mathsf{i}\mathsf{Y}_{\upkappa,\psi},\mathbb{P}_{+}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\Psi\rVert_{W^{\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T})}+\lVert\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)\rVert_{W^{\beta,1/(1-\beta)}(\mathbb{T})}).

Now we use the assumed bound (
3.25
) along with the first and second items of Proposition
2.7
and the commutator estimate (
2.38
) from Proposition
2.4
to deduce that the desired estimate (
3.26
) indeed holds with some
N
depending only on
M
,
\beta
, and
\delta
.

Next, we shall prove that the function (
3.24
) is unbounded. Let us assume, for the sake of reaching a contradiction, that there exists
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
such that

(3.30)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}(|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{1/2+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})})<\infty.

By repeated application of the auxiliary estimate established in the first part of the proof, we deduce that for all
0\leqslant s<3/2

(3.31)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}(|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})})<\infty.

We then return to identity (
3.27
), which continues to hold for
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
. We now take the norm in
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{C})
. After using estimates (
2.62
), (
2.39
), and (
2.71
) along with (
3.31
) for
s=1+\alpha/2
, we find that

(3.32)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}(\char 12\relax{\upkappa}+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})})<\infty,

which is a contradiction of the unboundedness of the map (
3.15
) from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

We now argue that within a restricted class of forcing data profiles, the blow-up quantity can be dropped even lower.

Proposition 3.4

(Blow-up refinement, II)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.2
and let
\mathcal{C}
be the connected set of solutions constructed therein and suppose additionally that
\mathsf{f}=0
. For every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\min\{{2\alpha,1/2}\}
the function

(3.33)

\mathcal{C}\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}\in\mathbb{R}

is unbounded.

Proof.

Let us argue by contradiction, and suppose that there exists
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1/2
such that

(3.34)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}(\char 12\relax{\upkappa}+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})})<\infty.

Let
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
\mathsf{f}=0
, the expression for
\mathbf{Q}
from (
2.68
) simplifies considerably:

(3.35)

\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)=\upkappa^{2}[\mathbb{P}_{-},(\partial_{1}\upphi+\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{+}\psi+\upkappa^{2}[\mathbb{P}_{+},(\partial_{1}\upphi-\mathsf{i}\partial_{2}\upphi)\circ R_{\psi}]\partial_{1}\mathbb{P}_{-}\psi\\
+\kappa^{2}\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}(\upphi\circ R_{\psi})+\mathsf{i}\upkappa^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{-}-\mathbb{P}_{+})((\partial_{1}\upphi\circ R_{\psi})(1+\mathbb{S}^{\uparrow}\psi))+\mathrm{g}\mathbb{S}^{\downarrow}\psi.

Hence, by using the commutator estimate (
2.39
) from Proposition
2.4
(with
\beta=1-\varepsilon/2
and
\gamma=\varepsilon
) along with the uniform bounds (
3.34
) and Lemma
2.2
, we find that

(3.36)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}}{1+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1-\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}}<\infty.

We now return to identity (
3.27
) for
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
. By taking the norm in
C^{\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})
and using again (
2.39
), Lemma
2.2
, (
3.34
), and (
3.36
) we get

(3.37)

\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{Q}(\upkappa,\psi)\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1-\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 1+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1-\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}

for a finite implicit constant that is uniform in
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, by the interpolation estimate

(3.38)

\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1-\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T})}^{2\varepsilon/(2-\varepsilon)}\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})}^{(2-3\varepsilon)/(2-\varepsilon)},

the bound (
3.34
), and an absorption argument, we find that

(3.39)

\sup_{(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}}(|\upkappa|+\lVert\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\varepsilon/2}(\mathbb{T})})<\infty.

This contradicts Proposition
3.3
.
∎

4.
Further refinements

4.1.
Conformal degeneracy

Combining Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
gets us most of the way to proving our first main result, Theorem
1
. To complete its justification, it remains to take the connected solution set
\mathcal{C}
produced by Proposition
3.2
and study its intersection with the set
\mathcal{U}
, defined in equation (
1.21
), of admissible coordinate functions. The content of this subsection is the following preliminary result that enumerates the essential properties of the set
\mathcal{U}
.

Proposition 4.1

(On the set of admissible coordinate functions)
.

The following hold where
\mathcal{U}
is as defined in equation (
1.21
).

(1)

The set
\mathcal{U}
is open and
0\in\mathcal{U}
.

(2)

For all
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
we have
\mathscr{D}(\psi)<\infty
, where
\mathscr{D}
is the maximal distortion that is defined in equation (
1.23
).

(3)

For all
\psi\in\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\cap\partial\mathcal{U}
we have that

(4.1)

\mathscr{D}(\widetilde{\psi})\to\infty\text{ as }\mathcal{U}\ni\widetilde{\psi}\to\psi.

Proof.

We begin with the proof of the first item. Since
R_{0}(w,z)=(w,z)
for all
(w,z)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
, we obviously have
0\in\mathcal{U}
.

The map

(4.2)

\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})\ni\psi\mapsto\det\nabla R_{\psi}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,h])

is continuous (thanks to Lemma
2.1
) and so for each
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
there exists
\epsilon_{\psi}>0
such that

(4.3)

\forall\;\lVert\psi-\widetilde{\psi}\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}<\epsilon_{\psi},\;\min_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}\det\nabla R_{\widetilde{\psi}}>0.

We now claim that for each
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists
c_{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the property that for all
p,q\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
with
p\neq q
we have

(4.4)

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(p,q)\leqslant c_{\psi}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(R_{\psi}(p),R_{\psi}(q)).

If this were false, then we could find convergent sequences
\{{p_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{{q_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
with
p_{n}\neq q_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and points
p,q\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
with
p_{n}\to p
and
q_{n}\to q
as
n\to\infty
and

(4.5)

\frac{1}{2^{n}}>\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(R_{\psi}(p_{n}),R_{\psi}(q_{n}))}{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(p_{n},q_{n})}.

If
p\neq q
, then passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
in estimate (
4.5
) shows
R_{\psi}(p)=R_{\psi}(q)
, which is impossible since
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
implies that
R_{\psi}
is injective. Thus it must be that
p=q
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, we can use the fundamental theorem of calculus and the Cauchy-Riemann equations satisfied by
R_{\psi}
to write

(4.6)

\frac{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(R_{\psi}(p_{n}),R_{\psi}(q_{n}))}{\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(p_{n},q_{n})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla R_{\psi}(\tau p_{n}+(1-\tau)q_{n})\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big|<\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

Passing to the limit
n\to\infty
in the above now shows that
\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}(p)}=0
. This is a contradiction of the fact that
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
implies that
\det\nabla R_{\psi}>0
on the whole of
\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
.

So indeed the claimed reverse-Lipschitz bound of equation (
4.4
) must be true. In turn we find that, given
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
and
c_{\psi}
obeying (
4.4
), there exists
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\psi}>0
such that

(4.7)

\forall\;\lVert\psi-\widetilde{\psi}\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}<\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\psi}\;\text{and}\;\forall\;p,q\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h],\;\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(p,q)\leqslant(c_{\psi}/2)\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]}(R_{\widetilde{\psi}}(p),R_{\widetilde{\psi}}(q)).

We may now conclude that
\mathcal{U}
is indeed open, since we have shown that for all
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
we have
\widetilde{\psi}\in\mathcal{U}
whenever
\lVert\widetilde{\psi}-\psi\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}<\min\{{\epsilon_{\psi},\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\psi}}\}
.

The second item is a special consequence of the preceding analysis since for all
\psi\in\mathcal{U}
we have
\mathscr{D}(\psi)\leqslant c_{\psi}
where
c_{\psi}
is the finite positive constant as in (
4.4
).

Let us turn our attention to the third item. We shall argue by contradiction. If (
4.1
) is false, then there would exist
\psi\in\partial\mathcal{U}
,
\{{\psi_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
, and
M<\infty
such that

(4.8)

\lVert\psi-\psi_{n}\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty\text{ but }\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathscr{D}(\psi_{n})\leqslant M.

We are going to associate with this sequence of real-valued functions a sequence of holomorphic
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions.

Define the (doubled) complex domain

(4.9)

O=\{{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\;:\;\char 12\relax{\mathrm{Im}\zeta}<h}\}

and the sequence of functions
\{{f_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
via

(4.10)

f_{n}(\zeta)=\begin{cases}e_{1}\cdot R_{\psi_{n}}(\mathrm{Re}\zeta,\mathrm{Im}\zeta)+\mathsf{i}e_{2}\cdot R_{\psi_{n}}(\mathrm{Re}\zeta,\mathrm{Im}\zeta)&\text{if }0\leqslant\mathrm{Im}\zeta<h,\\
e_{1}\cdot R_{\psi_{n}}(\mathrm{Re}\zeta,-\mathrm{Im}\zeta)-\mathsf{i}e_{2}\cdot R_{\psi_{n}}(\mathrm{Re}\zeta,-\mathrm{Im}\zeta)&\text{if }-h<\mathrm{Im}h<\infty\end{cases}\text{ for all }\zeta\in O.

Similarly, we can also define
f:O\to\mathbb{C}
in terms of
R_{\psi}
as above.

By virtue of the satisfaction of the Cauchy-Riemann equations by
\{{R_{\psi_{n}}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
R_{\psi}
and the Schwarz reflection principle (see, e.g., Section 1 in Chapter IX of Conway
[
26
]
), the maps
f
and
\{{f_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are holomorphic functions
O\to\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, these maps extend to class
C^{1,\alpha}
functions on the closure
\overline{O}
. Also, since
\{{\psi_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{U}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
it follows that
f_{n}:\overline{O}\to\mathbb{C}
is injective and
\min_{\overline{O}}\char 12\relax{f_{n}^{\prime}}>0
, where the prime denotes complex differentiation.

The assumed convergence on the left hand side of (
4.8
), implies that
f_{n}\to f
in
C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{O};\mathbb{C})
as well. We may then invoke variations on Hurwitz’s theorem (see, e.g., Theorems 5 and 6 in Chapter 3, Section 2 of Narasimhan and Nievergelt
[
54
]
), after noting that
f^{\prime}
is not identically zero, to conclude that: 1)
f
is injective in the interior region
O
and
2)
the complex derivative
f^{\prime}
is nowhere vanishing in
O
.

We unpack these conclusions for
f
to see what they imply about
R_{\psi}
. We deduce that: 1)
R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times[0,h)\to\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)
is injective and 2)
\det\nabla R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times[0,h)\to\mathbb{R}
is everywhere positive. Since
\mathcal{U}
is open and
f\in\partial\mathcal{U}
, we have that
f\not\in\mathcal{U}
. Synthesizing these observations, we conclude that one of the following cases must occur.

•

There exists
w\in\mathbb{T}
such that
\det\nabla R_{\psi}(w,h)=0
.

•

There exists
p\in\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}
and
q\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
such that
R_{\psi}(p)=R_{\psi}(q)

We can use the right hand side inequality of (
4.8
) to eliminate the first case above. Indeed, for every
w_{0},w_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
the contradiction hypothesis is that

(4.11)

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}}(w_{0},w_{1})\leqslant M\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)}(R_{\psi_{n}}(p,h),R_{\psi_{n}}(q,h)).

Taking the limit
n\to\infty
in (
4.11
) implies that
|\partial_{1}R_{\psi}(w,h)|\geqslant 1/M
for all
w\in\mathbb{T}
. So the first case does not occur since
\det\nabla R_{\psi}=\char 12\relax{\partial_{1}R_{\psi}}^{2}
; so
\det\nabla R_{\psi}
is everywhere positive on
\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}
and hence on
\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]
.

Thus we must have the existence of
p
and
q
as in the second item. It must be the case that
q\in\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}
as well, as can be deduced by considering

(4.12)

R_{\psi}(q)=R_{\psi}(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{\psi_{n}}(p).

If
q\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,h)
, then
f_{n}
, for sufficiently large
n
, would, because of (
4.12
) and the fact that
f:O\to\mathbb{C}
is injective with nowhere vanishing derivative, map
\partial O
into the interior of
f_{n}(O)
, which cannot happen since
f_{n}
extends to a
C^{1,\alpha}
diffeomorphism of the closures. In turn we get

(4.13)

R_{\psi}(p_{1},h)=R_{\psi}(q_{1},h)

which is also impossible given equation (
4.11
). Thus we have contradicted the assumption that the limit (
4.1
) was false.
∎

4.2.
Synthesis

We may now combine Proposition
4.1
with the results of Section
3
to complete the proof of our first main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Thanks to Propositions
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
we are granted a connected solution set
(0,0)\in\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathring{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
for the desired equation (
1.20
) such that the function (
3.24
) is unbounded and, in the special case
\mathsf{f}=0
, the function (
3.33
) is unbounded. We need only verify the blow-up of the quantities (
1.26
) or (
1.27
) on the restricted solution set
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
, with
\mathcal{U}
being defined in (
1.21
).

If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
, then of course there is nothing more to prove. Since
\mathcal{C}
is connected, the only other case that can occur is that
\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\partial\mathcal{U})\neq\varnothing
; more precisely, we must have
\overline{\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\partial\mathcal{U})\neq\varnothing
. But then, by the third item of Proposition
4.1
we find that the function

(4.14)

\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})\ni(\upkappa,\psi)\mapsto\mathscr{D}(\psi)\in\mathbb{R}

is necessarily unbounded.
∎

Our second main theorem is proved by simply taking our solutions constructed to the fully nonlinear pseudodifferential equation (
1.20
) and unpacking them to solutions to the parent free boundary problem (
1.28
).

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The mapping
R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times(0,h)\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
is smooth since the components of
R_{\psi}
satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations as a consequence of Lemma
2.1
. The same lemma also implies that
R_{\psi}:\mathbb{T}\times[0,h]\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
is class
C^{1,\alpha}
. The inclusion of
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
and the definition of
\mathcal{U}
(
1.21
) completes the verification of the first item.

The proof of the second item traverses in reverse Section
1.3
. Let us fix
(\upkappa,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{U})
. We shall first construct the flattened pressure variable
q\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
as to solve system (
1.15
). Let us define
f^{1}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
,
f^{2}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\})
,
f^{3}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\})
via

(4.15)

f^{1}=(\upkappa^{2}/2)\char 12\relax{\nabla R_{\psi}}^{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathsf{f})\circ R_{\psi},\;f^{2}=\upkappa^{2}(f\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp},\text{ and }f^{3}=\mathrm{g}\psi+\upkappa^{2}(\upphi\circ R_{\psi}).

By standard Schauder theory there exits a unique function
q\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
that is a solution to the equations

(4.16)

\Delta q=f^{1}\text{ in }\mathbb{T}\times(0,h),\;\partial_{2}q=f^{2}\text{ on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{0}\},\text{ and }q=f^{3}\text{ on }\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}.

Now we use that
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
, with
\mathbf{P}
as defined in (
2.52
), to check that the solution to (
4.16
) has the correct Neumann data on the top. Indeed, rearrangement of
\mathbf{P}(\upkappa,\psi)=0
and substitution of definitions (
4.15
) show that

(4.17)

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}\partial_{2}q=Gf^{3}+S(f^{1},f^{2})=\mathrm{c}\partial_{1}\psi+\upkappa^{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathbb{T}\times\{{h}\}}(\mathsf{f}\circ R_{\psi})\cdot(\partial_{1}R_{\psi})^{\perp},

where we recall that
G
and
S
are defined in equation (
1.16
). Thus we have a solution pair
(\psi,q)
to system (
1.15
) with
\kappa=\upkappa^{2}
.

Now we can straightforwardly define our solution to the equations of traveling free boundary incompressible Darcy flow (
1.28
). We let
\Omega_{\psi}=R_{\psi}(\mathbb{T}\times(0,h))
and
\mathrm{p}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega_{\psi})
and
\mathrm{v}\in C^{\alpha}(\Omega_{\psi};\mathbb{R}^{2})
be determined via

(4.18)

\mathrm{p}(x)=q\circ(R_{\psi})^{-1}(x)+\mathrm{g}(h-e_{2}\cdot x)\text{ and }\mathrm{v}(x)=\upkappa^{2}\mathsf{f}(x)-\nabla\mathrm{p}(x)-\mathrm{g}e_{2}

for all
x\in\overline{\Omega_{\psi}}
. It is then a simple exercise in local elliptic regularity to see that
\mathrm{p}
and
\mathrm{v}
are class
C^{4}
in the interior of their domain and solve the correct equations (
1.28
) in
\overline{\Omega_{\psi}}
, given that we already know
(\psi,q)
solve (
1.15
) with
\kappa=\upkappa^{2}
.
∎
