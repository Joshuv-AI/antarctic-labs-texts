Uniqueness of non-Euclidean Mass Center System and Generalized Pappus’ Centroid Theorems in Three Geometries
Yunhi Cho and Hyounggyu Choi

Abstract
. G.A. Galperin introduced the axiomatic mass center system for finite point sets in spherical and hyperbolic spaces, proving the uniqueness of the mass center system. In this paper, we revisit this system and provide a significantly simpler proof of its uniqueness. Furthermore, we extend the axiomatic mass center system to manifolds. As an application of our system, we derive a highly generalized version of Pappus’ centroid theorem for volumes in three geometries—Euclidean, spherical, and hyperbolic—across all dimensions, offering unified and notably simple proofs for all three geometries.

Introduction

The Greek mathematician Archimedes established the concept of the
axiomatic mass center system
in Euclidean space (see pages 502-509 of
[
2
]
). With the advent of non-Euclidean geometry, the concept of the mass center naturally extended to non-Euclidean spaces. However, surprisingly, the mass center in non-Euclidean spaces has not been extensively studied, with only a few results available. For instance, in

]
and
[

]
, the spherical mass center was considered, while in

[

]
and
[
9
]
have emerged, subtly relying on the concept of the non-Euclidean mass center. However, these papers often fail to recognize the correlation between the concepts they employ and the non-Euclidean mass center, instead treating it merely as an ancillary tool for their theoretical developments.

G. A. Galperin has set up the concept of axiomatic mass center system on the spherical space
\bm{\mathrm{S}}^{n}
and on the hyperbolic space
\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{n}
in his article
[
7
]
. He started from a system of axioms that a system so called a
mass center system
plausibly satisfies. Among the axioms, for example, one is that the mass center system must be isometry invariant. After introducing a system of axioms, he showed that there exists a unique mass center system satisfying the given system of axioms by using the D’Alembert’s functional equation defined on
\mathbb{R}
:
f(x+y)+f(x-y)=2f(x)f(y)
.

Archimedes also proposed the axioms for mass center. There is a notable thing that must be mentioned. He basically assumed that the mass of the mass center is same as the total mass of the given points. However, Galperin excluded this assumption from the system of axioms. This is important. In result we will see, it is shown that the mass of the mass center is different from the total mass for spherical or hyperbolic cases.

The system of mass center is very simple as it will be presented. In Euclidean space, it is hopefully natural that the mass of the mass center is same as the total mass of the concerned set. This is true in result. However, it will be turned out that the mass of the mass center is not necessarily same as the total mass in the cases for non-Euclidean spaces. At first glance, it seems surreal but after a while it becomes real. From now on, in this reason, we call the mass of the mass center the
centered mass
.

We will present an alternative proof for the uniqueness of the mass center for finite system. It seems that our proof is considerably simpler than the proof of Galperin. However, it is wholly a follower’s benefit. Galperin’s consideration on the concept of axiomatic mass center system is great and absolute. Additionally, we propose the axiomatic mass center system for manifolds and prove the uniqueness. By the way, Choe and Gulliver
[
4
]
reached to the concept of
modified volume
in their own study on the isoperimetric inequality. The modified volume is nothing other than the centered mass. We are not sure that they considered the concept of centered mass.
As far as we know, Galperin is the first one who showed the uniqueness of the system and explicitly presented the concept of centered mass.

More than thirty years has passed after professor Galperin’s work. There are not many applications of this work. We add one. That is
Pappus’ centroid theorem on non-Euclidean spaces
. In fact, already there is a very significant work of Alfred Grey and Vincent Miquel
[
9
]
on Pappus’ centroid theorem on non-Euclidean spaces. Their result contains much portion of ours but not all of ours. Citing Choe and Gulliver
[
4
]
, they used the concept of modified volume. It is also unsure that they considered the concept of centered mass.

Deriving volume formulas for geometric objects in non-Euclidean spaces is considerably more challenging compared to Euclidean space. Explicit volume formulas for non-Euclidean solids are generally unavailable, except in certain special cases, such as ideal polyhedra. In this study, we utilize the powerful tool of Pappus’ theorem to derive explicit volume formulas for four previously unknown types of non-Euclidean solids, including the right circular cone and the torus in three-dimensional space.

\displaystyle d\left([\bm{\mathrm{a}}],[\bm{\mathrm{b}}]\right)

\displaystyle\text{if }\mathbb{X}^{n}=\mathbb{E}^{n},

\displaystyle\cos\left\{d\left([\bm{\mathrm{a}}],[\bm{\mathrm{b}}]\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\bm{\mathrm{a}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\bm{\mathrm{a}},\bm{\mathrm{a}}\rangle\langle\bm{\mathrm{b}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}\rangle}},\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }\mathbb{X}^{n}=\mathbb{S}^{n},

(5)

\displaystyle\cosh\left\{d\left([\bm{\mathrm{a}}],[\bm{\mathrm{b}}]\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\langle\bm{\mathrm{a}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\bm{\mathrm{a}},\bm{\mathrm{a}}\rangle\langle\bm{\mathrm{b}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}\rangle}},\quad

2.3.
Totally geodesic submanifold, hyperplane in spherical and hyperbolic space and parametrization

It is well known that the intersection of a
(k+1)
-dimensional subspace of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the space
\mathbb{X}^{n}
results in a
k
-dimensional
totally geodesic submanifold
of
\mathbb{X}^{n}
. A
geodesic
is a 1-dimensional totally geodesic submanifold, and it is the intersection of a 2-dimensional subspace of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the space
\mathbb{X}^{n}
. A
hyperplane
in
\mathbb{X}^{n}
is a totally geodesic submanifold of codimension 1. A hyperplane is the intersection of an
n
-dimensional subspace of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the space
\mathbb{X}^{n}
. We define the unit vector orthogonal to this subspace as the
polar vector
of the hyperplane. The polar vector exhibits a sign ambiguity. A polar vector is a
positive vector, and every positive vector corresponds to a unique hyperplane.

From now until the end of this subsection, we will assume that the space
\mathbb{X}^{n}
refers to either
\mathbb{S}^{n}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
be a point in
\mathbb{X}^{n}
, and let
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
be a vector orthogonal to
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
. Then,
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
is a positive vector, satisfying the following conditions:

\langle\bm{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}\rangle=0,\quad\langle\bm{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\mathrm{p}}\rangle>0.

Let
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}}
denote the hyperplane in
\mathbb{X}^{n}
that passes through
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and has
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
as a polar vector. Since a polar vector uniquely determines a hyperplane, the notation
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}}
is, in fact, redundant. However, to emphasize that this hyperplane passes through
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
, we retain the point
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
in the notation.

2.3.2.
Orthonormal basis and parametrization of hyperplane

Consider a hyperplane
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}}

The set of vectors

3.3.
Existence of mass center system for multi-dimensional space

Proposition 5
.

There is a mass center system for finite collection of material point as stated below :

\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}+\dots+\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}.

Proof.

Apart from the
axiom of isometry invariance
, it is evident that this system satisfies the other axioms. We know that the isometry group of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\mathbb{H}^{n}
are, respectively, the groups
\text{O}(n+1)
and
\text{O}(n,1)
. These groups act not only on the considered spaces but also on the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Each element in these isometry groups is a linear map. The isometry group of
\mathbb{E}^{n}
is the semidirect product
O(n)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
and each element in this group is a linear map represented by the matrix of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}A&\bm{\mathrm{b}}\\
\hline\cr\bm{\mathrm{0}}&1\end{array}\right),\quad A\in O(n),~\bm{\mathrm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Hence, the system described in this proposition satisfies the
axiom of isometry invariance
.

We omit the proof for the fact that

Theorem 1
.

(Uniqueness of the mass center system for finite collections of material points)

Assume that

Proof.

We will prove the followings.

•

For all material vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}

\bm{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}

\bm{\mathrm{a}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

Theorem 2
.

There is a unique mass center system for
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Specifically, for material vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}
, we have

\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}+\dots+\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}
be material vectors with masses that are not necessarily the same. We construct two sequences
\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\}
of material vectors following the
split and merge process
described below.

•

Set
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{b}}
.

•

If
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
is heavier than
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
, split
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
as
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}=(1-k)\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}+k\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}

(0<k<1)
such that
m_{k\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}}=m_{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}}
, and merge
k\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
. Set

\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}=(1-k)\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1},\quad\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}=k\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}=k\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}.

It is obvious that

(29)

\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1},\quad\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1},

and since
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}
is the midpoint of
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
, we get

(30)

\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}}\subset\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}},\quad d(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}d(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}).

•

If
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
is heavier than
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
, execute a similar process to construct
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}
. Whichever of
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}
or
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}
is heavier, we can obtain the same formulas (
29
) and (
30
).

•

We can repeat the
split and merge process
to construct
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{3}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{3}
from
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}
.

•

Continue this process indefinitely.

•

If
m_{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{N}}=m_{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{N}}
for some
N\geq 1
for the first time, stop the process. In this case, we easily obtain that
\bm{\mathrm{a}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}
. Therefore, we need to consider only the non-stopping case, i.e., infinite sequences
\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\}
.

In summary, we have constructed the sequences
\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\}
satisfying the following properties:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{a}},\quad\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{b}},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{a}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i},\quad\forall i\geq 1,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i},\quad\forall i\geq 1,

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}\bm{\mathrm{b}}}=\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{1}}\supset\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{2}}\supset\overline{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{3}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{3}}\supset\cdots,

\displaystyle d(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{i-1}}d(\bm{\mathrm{a}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}).

We now aim to show that there exists a pair of convergent subsequences,
\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}\}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}\}
. To do so, we alter our perspective and treat the material vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
as vectors in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Observe that the parallelogram formed by the vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i+1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i+1}
is contained within the parallelogram formed by the vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}
for all
i
. Therefore, every vector in the sequences
\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i}\}
is contained within the parallelogram formed by the vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}
, which is a compact set.

By the
Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem
, we know that the sequence of pairs
\{(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i})\}
has a convergent subsequence, say
\{(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}})\}
. Using the
axiom of continuity
, we can then observe that

\bm{\mathrm{a}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\lim_{j\to\infty}(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}\oplus\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}.

Similarly, it is trivial to show that

\bm{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\lim_{j\to\infty}(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}+\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}.

Applying the
Nested Intervals theorem
, we conclude that the sequences
\{[\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}]\}
and
\{[\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}]\}
converge to the same point in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Therefore, the material points
\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}
overlap.

By the
axiom of overlapping
, we then have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}\oplus\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i_{j}}+\lim_{j\to\infty}\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{i_{j}}.

Thus, we obtain the equation

\bm{\mathrm{a}}\oplus\bm{\mathrm{b}}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}+\bm{\mathrm{b}}.

By induction, we conclude the result of this theorem.
∎

3.5.2.
Mass center system on a line

Now we consider the uniqueness problem of mass center system for each of
\mathbb{E}^{1}

\mathbb{S}^{1}

\mathbb{H}^{1}

Theorem 3
.

There are uncountably many mass center systems on
\mathbb{E}^{1}
and
\mathbb{H}^{1}
satisfying the six mass center axioms.

Proof.

For
N
material vectors
\{m_{i}(x_{i},1)\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{E}^{1}

\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(x_{i},1)\}_{i=1}^{N}

=\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k},1)

for

, where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i\neq j}m_{i}m_{j}\cosh k(x_{i}-x_{j})},&\text{if }k\neq 0,\\
\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i},\quad\qquad&\text{if }k=0,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k}\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{M}_{k}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\sinh(kx_{i})}\right),&\text{if }k\neq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\mathfrak{M}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}x_{i},&\text{if }k=0.\end{cases}

Then the mass center system
\mathcal{F}_{k}
in
\mathbb{E}^{1}
easily satisfies the all axioms except the
axiom of isometry invariance
. And that is sufficient to prove that the system is invariant under the reflection
g_{1}(x)=-x
and the translation
g_{2}(x)=x+p
.

Hence we have to show that

\mathcal{F}_{k}\{g_{1}(m_{i}(x_{i},1))\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(-x_{i},1)\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathfrak{M}_{k}(-\mathfrak{X}_{k},1)=g_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k},1))

and

\mathcal{F}_{k}\{g_{2}(m_{i}(x_{i},1))\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(x_{i}+p,1)\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k}+p,1)=g_{2}(\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k},1))

for
k\neq 0
.
Let us check the only non-trivial equality
\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(x_{i}+p,1)\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k}+p,1)
.

From the definition of
\mathfrak{M}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
, two relations
\mathfrak{M}_{k}^{2}=(\sum m_{i}\cosh(kx_{i}))^{2}-(\sum m_{i}\sinh(kx_{i}))^{2}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{k}\sinh(k\mathfrak{X}_{k})=\sum m_{i}\sinh(kx_{i})
are trivially derived, so additionally we obtain
\mathfrak{M}_{k}\cosh(k\mathfrak{X}_{k})=\sum m_{i}\cosh(kx_{i})
.

Hence we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(x_{i}+p,1)\}_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{M}_{k}}{\sum m_{i}\sinh(k(x_{i}+p))}\right),1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{M}_{k}}\left(\cosh(kp)\sum m_{i}\sinh(kx_{i})+\sinh(kp)\sum m_{i}\cosh(kx_{i})\right)\right),1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sinh^{-1}\left(\cosh(kp)\sinh(k\mathfrak{X}_{k})+\sinh(kp)\cosh(k\mathfrak{X}_{k})\right),1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sinh^{-1}\sinh(k\mathfrak{X}_{k}+kp),1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{X}_{k}+p,1).

For
N
material vectors
\{m_{i}(\sinh x_{i},\cosh x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
, we can define

\mathcal{F}_{k}\{m_{i}(\sinh x_{i},\cosh x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}=\mathfrak{M}_{k}(\sinh\mathfrak{X}_{k},\cosh\mathfrak{X}_{k})

for

, using the same
\mathfrak{M}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{X}_{k}
as in the
\mathbb{E}^{1}
case.
The material vector system
\mathcal{F}_{k}
in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
also satisfies our six mass center axioms in a manner similar to the case in
\mathbb{E}^{1}
.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{k_{1}}\neq\mathcal{F}_{k_{2}}
for different
k_{1},k_{2}\in[0,\infty)
, we conclude the proof.
∎

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\mathcal{F}_{-k}
. If we designate a system where everything else is fixed but replaced by

instead of

5.
Pappus’ Centroid Theorem and its application

5.1.
Pappus solid and the statement of the theorem

By a
hyperplanar section
, we mean an
(n-1)
-dimensional manifold contained in a hyperplane.

Definition
.

A bounded
n
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{X}^{n}
with a mass distribution
\rho
is called a
Pappus solid along a centroid curve

C
or simply a
Pappus solid
if the solid
\mathcal{P}
is laminated by the hyperplanar sections with the restricted mass distribution
\rho
satisfying the conditions explained below.

- The mass centers of all concerned hyperplanar sections are mutually disjoint and they form the curve
C
.

- The arclength parametrization of the curve
C
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-differentiable.

- The hyperplanar section deforms smoothly along the centroid curve and, hence, the hyperplane containing a hyperplanar section also does.

The centroid curve of a Pappus solid is not necessarily contained in the solid. The hyperplane which contains a hyperplanar section is not necessarily perpendicular to the centroid curve. We also simply call a hyperplanar section just a
section
. We describe a Pappus solid in detail:

•

In the above definition, let the centroid curve
C
is parametrized by the arclength as
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),~(t\in I\subset\mathbb{R})
. The velocity vector of this curve is a unit tangent vector. We denote this vector as
\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)
:

~~\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)=\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}(t),~\langle\bm{\mathrm{t}},\bm{\mathrm{t}}\rangle=1

•

The section mass-centered at
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
is denoted as
L_{t}
.

•

Let
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}
be the hyperplane containing the section
L_{t}
.

•

The polar vector
\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)
of this hyperplane is selected so that, for all
t\in I
,

\langle\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)\rangle\geq 0.

•

Let
\theta_{t}
be the slant angle of this hyperplane with respect to the centroid curve. By Equation (
10
), we see

\cos\theta_{t}=\langle\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)\rangle.

•

The Pappus solid
\mathcal{P}
described above is denoted as

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big].

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big]
be a Pappus solid with a mass distribution
\rho
. Then the total mass of this solid is the total mass of the centroid curve where the mass distribution
\tilde{\rho}(t)
at the point
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
is given by
\tilde{\rho}(t)=m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t}

:

(48)

m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t})\big],\rho\Big)=m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),\tilde{\rho}=m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t})\Big).

Section
5.2
and
5.3
consist of the proof of this theorem.

5.2.
Non-Euclidean Pappus’ Centroid Theorem on Volume

In this section, we will present the necessary tools and proof for the non-Euclidean version of Pappus’ centroid theorem.

5.2.1.
Moving basis and parametrization of Pappus Solid

The Pappus solid
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big]
immediately determines the velocity vector
\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)
and the polar vector
\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)
. Following the discussion in Section, we can construct an orthonormal basis
\{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t)\}
for the space
\text{Span}\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)\}^{\perp}

\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Then the set of vectors

\{\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)\}

is orthonormal with respect to the
2
-form
\langle~,~\rangle
for all
t\in I
. This is an
moving basis
associated to the point
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
. This moving basis is not a moving frame because the vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
is not a tangent vector and the vector
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
is not necessarily a tangent vector if the space is Euclidean. Others are tangential. This moving basis is assumably
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, even though we do not provide a verification of its existence. For each
t\in I
, define the square matrix
F_{t}
as

(49)

F_{t}=[\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)].

Consider Equation (
12
). It is clear that a point
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
in
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}
can be parametrized as

(50)

\bm{\mathrm{r}}=\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t,\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})=F_{t}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\Phi^{n-1}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})\end{bmatrix}.

The section
L_{t}
is contained in
\mathbb{X}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}
. The domain of definition for the Pappus solid
\mathcal{P}
is described like this : For each
t\in I
, the
(n-1)
-tuple
(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})
varies in some region
D_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
depending on
t
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\left.\bm{\mathrm{r}}=F_{t}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\Phi^{n-1}(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})\end{bmatrix}\,\right|\,t\in I,~(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})\in D_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\right\}.

This is a parametrization of a Pappus solid. Observe that this parametrization is regular.

For notational simplicity, we denote
\Phi^{n-1}
simply as
\Phi
and denote the Jacobian matrix

\left(\Phi_{\varphi_{1}}~\Phi_{\varphi_{2}}~\cdots~\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}\right)
of
\Phi
as
D\Phi
:

\Phi(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})^{T},\quad D\Phi=\left(\Phi_{\varphi_{1}}~\Phi_{\varphi_{2}}~\cdots~\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}\right).

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big]
be a Pappus solid with a mass distribution
\rho
. A parametrization is given by Equation (
50
) with the parameter domain
D_{t}
. For each leaf
L_{t}~(t\in I)
, we have

(51)

\int_{D_{t}}\rho f_{i}\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}=\begin{cases}0,&\emph{\mbox{if }}~i<n\\
m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho),&\emph{\mbox{if }}~i=n.\end{cases}

Proof.

The volume element
\mathrm{d}{V}_{n-1}
of the section
L_{t}
is given by

\mathrm{d}{V}_{n-1}=\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}.

The mass center of the section
L_{t}
is
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
. Hence the mass center vector is

\displaystyle~~m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{t}}\rho\,\bm{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}{V}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{t}}\rho(f_{1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}+\dots+f_{n-1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}+f_{n}\bm{\mathrm{c}})\,\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{t}}\rho\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_{i}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i}+f_{n}\bm{\mathrm{c}}\right)\,\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho f_{i}\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\right)\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho f_{n}\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\right)\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t).

The set
\{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)\}
is independent. This completes the proof.
∎

5.2.2.
Derivative of the basis matrix

In the next lemma, we investigate the relation between the basis matrix
F_{t}
given in (
49
) and its derivative
{F_{t}}^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{F_{t}}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}
.

Lemma 5
.

The basis matrix
F_{t}
given in
(
49
)
satisfies the system of differential equations

(52)

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=F_{t}J_{n+1}\Lambda_{t}

where
\Lambda_{t}
is of the form

\Lambda_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\alpha_{1}(t)&\cdots&\alpha_{n-1}(t)&{\cos\theta_{t}}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}-\alpha_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n-1}(t)\\
{-\cos\theta_{t}}\end{array}&\lambda_{t}\end{array}\right)

for some skew-symmetric matrix
\lambda_{t}
. Every entry functions of
\Lambda_{t}
is a continuous function depending only on
t
.

Proof.

The matrix
F_{t}
is orthogonal:

\left(F_{t}\right)^{T}J_{n+1}F_{t}=J_{n+1}.

Differentiating both sides of the above equation, we get

O=\left({F_{t}}^{T}\right)^{\prime}J_{n+1}F_{t}+{F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}{F_{t}}^{\prime}=\left({F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}{F_{t}}^{\prime}\right)^{T}+{F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}{F_{t}}^{\prime}.

This means that the matrix
\Lambda_{t}:={F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}{F_{t}}^{\prime}
is skew-symmetric.

Since
{F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}F_{t}J_{n+1}=J_{n+1}J_{n+1}=I_{n+1}
, we have
\left({F_{t}}^{T}J_{n+1}\right)^{-1}=F_{t}J_{n+1}
and

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=F_{t}J_{n+1}\Lambda_{t}.

Let’s look into this skew-symmetric matrix
\Lambda_{t}
in more detail. Since

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=[\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}}^{\prime},\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}}]=[\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}}^{\prime},\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{t}}}]

and

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=F_{t}J_{n+1}\Lambda_{t}=[\bm{\mathrm{p}},{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}},\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}},\delta_{X}\bm{\mathrm{c}}]\Lambda_{t},

we have that

(53)

[\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}}^{\prime},\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{t}}]=[\bm{\mathrm{p}},{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}},\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}},\delta_{X}\bm{\mathrm{c}}]\Lambda_{t}.

Let the last column of the matrix
\Lambda_{t}
be the vector
[d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{n-1},d_{n}]^{T}
.

From Equation (
53
), we can see that

\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)=d_{0}\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)+d_{1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t)+\dots+d_{n-1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t)+\delta_{X}d_{n}\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t).

Apply
\langle~,\bm{\mathrm{p}}\rangle
on both sides of the above equation. By the orthonormality of the basis
\{\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)\}
, we can see that

d_{0}=\langle\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t)\rangle=\cos\theta_{t}.

and , hence, the skew-symmetric matrix
\Lambda_{t}
is of the form

\Lambda_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\alpha_{1}(t)&\cdots&\alpha_{n-1}(t)&\cos\theta_{t}\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}-\alpha_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n-1}(t)\\
-\cos\theta_{t}\end{array}&\lambda_{t}\end{array}\right)

for some skew-symmetric matrix
\lambda_{t}
. This completes proof.
∎

.

\displaystyle D\bm{\mathrm{r}}

Lemma 6
.

J_{n}\lambda_{t}\Phi\in T_{\Phi}\mathbb{X}^{n}=\text{\emph{Span}}\{\Phi_{\varphi_{1}},\dots,\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}\},\quad J_{n}\lambda_{t}\Phi=a_{1}\Phi_{\varphi_{1}}+\dots+a_{n-1}\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}.

for some real numbers

Proof.

Since

\langle\Phi,\Phi_{\varphi_{i}}\rangle=0~\text{ for all }~i=1,2,\dots,n-1.

\Phi(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n-1})^{\perp}=\text{Span}\{\Phi_{\varphi_{1}},\dots,\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}\}.

To see this, observe that

\langle J_{n}\lambda_{t}\Phi,\Phi\rangle=\Phi^{T}J_{n}J_{n}\lambda_{t}\Phi=\Phi^{T}\lambda_{t}\Phi,

and symmetric property of

Hence, by Equation (

By the above lemma, we can make the Jacobian matrix

\hline\cr\bm{\mathrm{0}}&\Phi_{\varphi_{1}}&\cdots&\Phi_{\varphi_{n-1}}\end{array}\right).

\hline\cr\bm{\mathrm{0}}&D\Phi\end{array}\right)E

where the matrix

\hline\cr\bm{\mathrm{0}}&(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\end{array}\right)\right|

Hence, the volume element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\bm{\mathrm{r}})^{T}J_{n+1}D\bm{\mathrm{r}}\right\}\right|\,}\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}f_{i}+\cos\theta_{t}f_{n}\right|\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}{t}.

Since the parametrization of
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
is regular, the volume form never vanishes. Hence, for
\epsilon_{0}=1
or
-1
, we see

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}f_{i}+\cos\theta_{t}f_{n}\right|=\epsilon_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}f_{i}+\cos\theta_{t}f_{n}\right)

Now we can calculate the total mass :

\displaystyle m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\mathcal{P},\rho\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{D_{t}}\rho\,\epsilon_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}f_{i}+\cos\theta_{t}f_{n}\right)\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\int_{I}\alpha_{i}\,\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho f_{i}\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{0}\int_{I}\cos\theta_{t}\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho f_{n}\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\Phi)^{T}J_{n}D\Phi\right\}\right|\,}\,\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{\varphi_{n-1}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\int_{I}m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\quad(\text{\small by Equation (\ref{f57}) in Lemma \ref{lem4m}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.\quad({\text{\small line integral interpretation and $\epsilon_{0}$ must be
$1$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),\tilde{\rho}=m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t}\Big).

This completes the proof of Pappus’ theorem on volume.

5.3.
Euclidean Pappus’ Centroid Theorem on Volumes

In this section, we will present the necessary tools and proof for the Euclidean version of Pappus’ centroid theorem.

5.3.1.
Moving basis and parametrization of Pappus Solid

The Pappus solid
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t}\big]
immediately determines the velocity vector
\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)
and the unit normal vector
\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t)\in T_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}\mathbb{E}^{n}
to the hyperplane containing the section
L_{t}
. Following the discussion in Section, we can construct an orthonormal basis
\{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t)\}

\text{Span}\{\mathbb{E}_{n+1},\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t)\}^{\perp}

\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

Then the set
\{\bm{\mathrm{n}},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}\}
is a orthonormal basis for
T_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{E}^{n}
with respect to the dot product. Since each vector in
T_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{E}^{n}
is orthogonal to
\mathbb{E}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

\{\bm{\mathrm{n}},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1},\mathbb{E}_{n+1}\}

is orthonormal with respect to the dot-product.
Let

\mathbb{E}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{n}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}}

be the hyperplane of
\mathbb{E}^{n}
that passes through
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
and has
\bm{\mathrm{n}}
as a normal vector. Then a point
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
in
\mathbb{E}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{n}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}}
is of the form

(61)

\bm{\mathrm{r}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}+x_{1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}+\dots+x_{n-1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}+[\bm{\mathrm{n}},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1},\mathbb{E}_{n+1}]\begin{bmatrix}0\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Let
C
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
curve on
\mathbb{E}^{n}
parametrized by the arclength as

\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),\quad t\in I\subset\mathbb{R}.

Then it is clear that
\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)\in T_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}\mathbb{E}^{n}
and
\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{t}}(t)=1
for all

.
There exists a set of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
parametrized vectors
\{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t)\}
such that

\{\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\dots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\mathbb{E}_{n+1}\}

is orthonormal with respect to the dot-product for all
t\in I
. For each
t\in I
, define the square matrix
F_{t}
as

(62)

F_{t}=[\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t),\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t),\mathbb{E}_{n+1}].

From the expression (
61
), it is clear that a point
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
in
\mathbb{E}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}
can be parametrized as

\bm{\mathrm{r}}=\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})=\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)+F_{t}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

The section
L_{t}
is a subset of the hyperplane
\mathbb{E}^{n-1}_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}
, and the normal vector
\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t)
of the hyperplane intersects the curve
C
with a slant angle
\theta_{t}
at
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
.
This is nothing other than a parametrization of a Pappus solid
P(C,\mathcal{L},\rho)
. Following is a direct application of Theorem
4
.

Proposition 9
.

A parametrization
\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
for a Pappus solid along a centroid curve
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)
is shown as :

\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})=\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)+F_{t}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
x_{1}\\

x_{n-1}\\

for

(x_{2},\dots,x_{n})

for each

.

Proposition 10
.

The centered mass
m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t})
of the leaf
L_{t}
is

(63)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{t}}\rho x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}=0,\quad\emph{\mbox{if }}~i=1,\dots,n-1

(64)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{t}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}=m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t}).

Proof.

The mass center of the section

is
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)

\displaystyle m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{t}}\rho\bm{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{d}{V}\!\left(\mathbb{E}^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{t}}\rho\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)+x_{1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}(t)+\dots+x_{n-1}\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}(t)\}\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\right)\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\right)\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i}(t).

The conclusion comes from the linear independency.

5.3.2.
Derivative of the basis matrix

Lemma 7
.

The matrix
F_{t}
given in
(
62
)
satisfies the sytem of differential equations

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=F_{t}\Lambda_{t}

where the matrix
\Lambda_{t}
is of the form

\Lambda_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0&\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha_{1}&\cdots&\alpha_{n-1}\end{array}&0\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}-\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n-1}\end{array}&\lambda_{t}&\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr 0&\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}&0\end{array}\right)

for some skew-symmetric matrix
\lambda_{t}
.

Proof.

Set
\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{0}:=\bm{\mathrm{n}}
, then
\langle\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{j}\rangle=0
for different indexes
i,j
implies
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\langle\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{j}\rangle=\langle\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{j}\rangle+\langle\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{j}^{\prime}\rangle=0
. So skew-symmetric of

is obtained. And the relation

{F_{t}}^{\prime}=[\bm{\mathrm{n}}^{\prime}(t),{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}}^{\prime}(t),\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}}^{\prime}(t),\bm{\mathrm{0}}]=[\bm{\mathrm{n}}(t),{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{1}}(t),\cdots,{\bm{\mathrm{n}}_{n-1}}(t),\mathbb{E}_{n+1}]\Lambda_{t}

shows that the last column of

is the zero vector.
∎

5.3.3.
Proof of the Pappus Theorem

In order to calculate the volume of the Pappus solid
P(C,\mathcal{L},\rho)
, we need to find the volume element
\mathrm{d}{V}^{n}
with respect to the variables
t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
:

\mathrm{d}{V}^{n}=\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\bm{\mathrm{r}})^{T}D\bm{\mathrm{r}}\right\}\right|}\,\,\mathrm{d}{t}\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1},

where
D\bm{\mathrm{r}}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
.

Let us calculate the Jacobian matrix
D\bm{\mathrm{r}}
.

\displaystyle D\bm{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{t},\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{x_{1}},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{x_{n-1}})

\displaystyle=[\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{0}},\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{0}}]+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{F_{t}}^{\prime}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\\
0\end{array}\right]&F_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}\\
I_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=[\bm{\mathrm{t}},\bm{\mathrm{0}},\cdots,\bm{\mathrm{0}}]+F_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda_{t}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\\
0\end{array}\right]&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}\\
I_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{t}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\theta_{t}\\
\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{n-1}\\
0\end{array}\right],\bm{\mathrm{0}},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{0}}\right)+F_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda_{t}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\\
0\end{array}\right]&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{c}\\
I_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=F_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\cos\theta_{t}+\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n-1}x_{n-1}&\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}+\lambda_{t}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}&I_{n-1}\\
\hline\cr 0&\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\end{array}\right)=:F_{t}A

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{V}\left(\mathbb{E}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left|\det\left\{(D\bm{\mathrm{r}})^{T}D\bm{\mathrm{r}}\right\}\right|}\,\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left|\det(A^{T}A)\right|}\,\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=\left|\cos\theta_{t}+\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n-1}x_{n-1}\right|\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos\theta_{t}+\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n-1}x_{n-1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\mathrm{d}{t}.

In conclusion, the above
\mathrm{d}{V}\left(\mathbb{E}^{n}\right)
formula and Proposition
10
derives the Euclidean Pappus’ Centroid theorem:

\displaystyle m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\mathcal{P},\rho\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{V}\left(\mathbb{E}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{I}\int_{D_{t}}\rho(\cos\theta_{t}+\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n-1}x_{n-1})\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\mathrm{d}{t}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{I}\cos\theta_{t}\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\right)\mathrm{d}{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\int_{I}\alpha_{i}\left(\int_{D_{t}}\rho x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x}_{n-1}\right)\mathrm{d}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t)}m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t})\cos\theta_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t.

5.4.
Application of the Pappus’ Theorem

In this section, we will examine applications of the Pappus’ theorem in non-Euclidean spaces. While the Pappus’ theorem has been well studied in Euclidean spaces, in non-Euclidean spaces, there have been results only by Gray, Miquel
[
9
]
. Now, we will present several examples from the view-point of non-Euclidean mass center.

Here, we reconsider the Pappus’ centroid theorem (
5
) for Pappus’ solid
\mathcal{P}
:

\displaystyle m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\mathcal{P}\big[\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),L_{t},\theta_{t})\big],\rho\Big)

\displaystyle=m_{\text{tot}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{c}}(t),\tilde{\rho}=m_{\text{cen}}(L_{t},\rho)\cos\theta_{t}\Big).

If the density is trivial case, i.e.,
\rho=1
, we call
m_{\text{tot}}(\mathcal{P})
as volume of
\mathcal{P}
with notation
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{P})
. The following four sections show the use of Pappus’ theorem to determine the volume of Pappus solid with

. As far as we know, the following examples have not been previously documented, despite being very simple objects.

5.4.1.
Volume of a non-Euclidean solid torus

If the radius of its circular cross section is
r
, and the radius of the circle traced by the mass center of the cross sections is
R
, then the volume
V
of the Euclidean torus is well-known and that is
2\pi^{2}Rr^{2}
by the Euclidean Pappus theorem.

Pappus’ centroid theorem shows below volume formulas of non-Euclidean solid torus.

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(\text{spherical solid torus})=

\displaystyle\int_{S^{1}(R)}~m_{\text{cen}}(B^{2}(r))~\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi\sin R}\pi\sin^{2}r~\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi^{2}\sin R\sin^{2}r,

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(\text{hyperbolic solid torus})=

\displaystyle\int_{S^{1}(R)}~m_{\text{cen}}(B^{2}(r))~\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi\sinh R}\pi\sinh^{2}r~\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi^{2}\sinh R\sinh^{2}r.

P_{n}^{\mathbb{S}}(a)
, where
\overline{B^{\prime}C^{\prime}}=\frac{\gamma}{2}
and define
\overline{A^{\prime}C^{\prime}}=\alpha
. Then
\alpha
and the
\gamma
are obtained by

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\alpha=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\cot^{2}\alpha}=\frac{1}{1+\cot^{2}b\sin^{2}h\csc^{2}t},

\displaystyle\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}=

\displaystyle\tan\frac{\pi}{n}\sin\alpha.

Finally, we get the volume of the cone over the regular polygon
P_{n}^{\mathbb{S}}(a)
with height
h
as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\frac{n\gamma}{2}\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\cot\frac{\pi}{n}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{h}_{0}\frac{n}{\sqrt{1+\cot^{2}b\sin^{2}h\csc^{2}t}}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\tan\frac{\pi}{n}}{\sqrt{1+\cot^{2}b\sin^{2}h\csc^{2}t}}\right)~\mathrm{d}t.

For a hyperbolic right cone with a regular
n
-gonal
P_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}(a)
base and height
h
, the cone has the following volume:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\frac{n\gamma}{2}\tanh\frac{\gamma}{2}\cot\frac{\pi}{n}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{h}_{0}\frac{n}{\sqrt{-1+\coth^{2}b\sinh^{2}h\text{ csch}^{2}t}}\tanh^{-1}\left(\frac{\tan\frac{\pi}{n}}{\sqrt{-1+\coth^{2}b\sinh^{2}h\text{ csch}^{2}t}}\right)~\mathrm{d}t,

where
\coth^{2}b=\tan^{2}\frac{\pi}{n}\coth^{2}\frac{a}{2}+1
. This case is left as an exercise for the reader.

The non-Euclidean volume can be computed not only for the objects introduced above but also for various symmetric shapes. However, deriving the volume formula without relying on Pappus’ centroid theorem reveals the inherent difficulty of such calculations, even for simple shapes like a right circular cone or a solid torus.
