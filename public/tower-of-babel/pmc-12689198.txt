Macrophage membrane coating enhances the therapeutic effects of Houttuynia cordata exosome-like nanovesicles against triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Pei-Pei Wang, Cheng-Xun He, Yang-Yang Shuai, Jia-Xuan Wang, Hui Ao, Wei Peng, Hong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe clinical management of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is often limited by the adverse effects and suboptimal efficacy of conventional drugs. In this study, we isolated and purified natural exosome-like nanovesicles (CELNs) from Houttuynia cordata Thunb. (HCT), which primarily comprise proteins, lipids, and RNAs. Proteomic profiling confirmed that CELNs retain a wide spectrum of bioactive components from HCT. These nanovesicles demonstrated high biocompatibility and an enhanced capacity to modulate DNA template transcription. To improve targeted delivery, CELNs were coated with macrophage membranes, generating MCELNs, which significantly promoted their uptake by TNBC cells. Both MCELNs and CELNs effectively inhibited cell viability and colony formation, induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and suppressed TNBC growth, with MCELNs exhibiting stronger antitumor activity. Transcriptome analysis revealed that MCELNs treatment resulted in significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes related to DNA damage and repair pathways. Consistent with this, both MCELNs and CELNs upregulated the protein expression of γ-H2AX, p-ATR, p-CHK1, p21, cleaved PARP1, and cleaved caspase 3, while downregulating CDC25C, CDK4, and full-length PARP1. These effects were substantially reversed by the DNA damage protective agent MPA. Mechanistically, MCELNs and CELNs exert anti-TNBC effects primarily by triggering DNA damage to cause cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Collectively, the present study establishes the potential of Houttuynia cordata-derived exosome-like nanovesicles as a natural therapeutic platform against TNBC, with macrophage membrane coating further augmenting their efficacy through biomimetic targeting. MCELNs may represent a promising drug delivery strategy for enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity in breast cancer therapy.Keywords: Macrophage membrane, Natural medicine, Exosome-like nanovesicle, DNA damage, Apoptosis, Tumor

Introduction

Breast cancer stands as the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women worldwide [1,2]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) comprises approximately 20 % of all breast cancer cases, predominantly affecting younger women [3,4]. Unfortunately, TNBC is heterogeneous and highly infiltrative, leading to increased mortality in breast cancer patients [5]. The prognosis is usually poor, with a high rate of systemic recurrence, which is difficult to cure with conventional treatment, regardless of the adjuvant therapy chosen [6]. Therefore, TNBC is in urgent need of more effective treatment options. In recent years, research into the molecular mechanisms of breast cancer has deepened, and precision medicine has gained widespread clinical acceptance. Driven by these developments, targeted therapy and immunotherapy have emerged as promising new directions for improving patient survival rates [7,8]. However, the development of resistance to treatment and severe toxic side effects of drugs on the body are the main reasons for treatment failure [9].

It's no doubt that natural agents from plants are precious resources for development of new drugs treating various diseases [[10], [11], [12]]. Houttuynia Cordata Thunb. (HCT), a perennial herb belonging to the family of Saururaceae, is a medicinal and edible plant with the functions of “Clearing heat and detoxifying, diuretic and reducing swelling”, which is also considered as the preferred medicine for breast abscess in Chinese medicinal theory. Previous evidence has demonstrated that HCT has a variety of bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, polysaccharides, volatile oils, and flavonoids [13]. In various countries such as China's southwest region, Thailand, and Japan, HCT is frequently consumed as a vegetable that represents local characteristics [14]. In China, HCT is used both as a seasoning and in cold, stewed, porridge, soup, and stir-fry dishes. As an ingredient in herbal tea, it has a variety of uses. It is part of a “cool drink”, which has a heat-clearing and detoxifying effect [15]. HCT helps to treat coughs, colds and sore throats. In Japan, HCT undergoes fermentation to produce “dokudami”, a substance utilized as an ingredient in beverages [16]. In various Asian regions, HCT-derived powder is employed in the preparation of steamed buns and dumplings, while the leaves serve as an ingredient for salad dressings [17]. Beyond serving as a food source, HCT possesses numerous advantages and has garnered considerable attention in pharmacological research over recent decades. Studies have revealed that both crude and alcoholic extracts of HCT exhibit notable inhibitory activity against breast cancer cell lines [18,19].

It is well established that exosomes exhibit enhanced biocompatibility, owing to their phospholipid bilayer structure and their cargo of proteins, RNAs, and lipids [[20], [21], [22]]. They can carry high payloads while maintaining a suitable size and are well tolerated in vivo [23,24]. Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (ELNs) are a promising delivery platform due to their compositional similarity to animal exosomes [25,26]. They also offer practical advantages, including abundant sources, low cost, and high yield. Moreover, ELNs from specific plants such as grapes [27], broccoli [28], and mung bean sprouts [29] exhibit inherent therapeutic effects, which can generate synergistic interactions with the delivered drugs. Integrin α4 is a transmembrane protein highly expressed on the macrophage membrane. It can bind to VCAM-1 on cancer cells, thereby enhancing the targeting of macrophage membrane-coated nanodrugs to tumor cells [[30], [31], [32]]. Nevertheless, the potential of this strategy for improving the targeting of plant-derived ELNs remains unclear.

In our previous work, Houttuynia cordata-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (CELNs) demonstrated good inhibitory effects on breast cancer. In this study, we performed further isolation and purification of CELNs. It is well recognized that rational dosage form design can improve drug targeting, efficacy, and safety [33,34]. Inspired by immune cell membrane coating nanotechnology [35], we explored the use of macrophage membrane (MAM) encapsulation to enhance inhibitory effects of CELNs on breast cancer. These findings indicate that CELNs suppress breast cancer progression by inducing DNA damage and subsequent apoptosis, an effect significantly potentiated by MAM coating.

Materials and methods

Materials

The main materials used in this study included P-CHK1, P-ATR, CDK4, CDC25C, P21, γ-H2AX (Abclone), cleaved PARP1, full length PARP1, cleaved caspase 3, BSA (BioFroxx), BCA Protein Concentration Assay Kit (Biyotime Shanghai), SDS-PAGE protein loading buffer (BOSTER, Wuhan), Three-color prestained protein standards (Abclone), Cell Cycle Assay Kit (RedFluorescence) (Biyotime Shanghai), enhanced RIPA lysate (BOSTER, Wuhan), RPMI 1640 medium(Gibco, USA), PKH67 (MedChemExpress, USA), PEG8000 (Chron Chemicals), 5-Fluorouracil (MedChemExpress, USA), and Camptothecin (MedChemExpress, USA). The rhizomes of Houttuynia Cordata Thunb. (HCT) were identified by Professor Wei Peng from School of Pharmacy, Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Chengdu, China).

Cell culture

Cell lines RAW264.7, 4T1, and MDA-MB-231 were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences and BT-549 cells were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. These cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 complete medium at 37 °C and 5 % CO2.

Isolation and purification of MCELNs

Macrophage membrane (MAM)-encapsulated CELNs (MCELNs) was obtained through three steps: preparation of CELNs, isolation of MAM, and masking of CELNs with MAM (Fig. 1). Fresh HCT was rinsed with reverse osmosis water before juice extraction. The obtained leaf juice was sequentially centrifuged at 3000×g for 20 min and 10,000×g for 40 min for pellet large cellular debris. The supernatant was collected and further centrifuged at 15,000×g for 60 min to obtain a pellet. This pellet was resuspended in PBS and passed through a 1 μm membrane filter. Subsequently, PEG8000 was added to a final concentration of 8 %, and the solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS, and sterilized using a 0.22 μm membrane filter [36]. RAW 264.7 macrophages were disrupted through direct extrusion and centrifuged to obtain MAM.

A two-stage biofabrication process comprising: (1) optimized CELNs isolation and purification, and (2) precise macrophage membrane vesicle integration to engineer MCELNs.

MCELNs was prepared by encapsulating MAM on CELNs using direct extrusion. In order to synthesize MCELNs, a meticulous process was undertaken, which involved the initial blending of 1 × 10−8 cell-purified MAM with 1.0 mL of CELNs, possessing a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. Following blending, the resultant mixture was sequentially extruded through polycarbonate membranes: first through a 400 nm pore-size membrane, and then through a 200 nm one.

Proteomic analysis

Briefly, HCT and CELNs were prepared and subsequently sent to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (China) for proteomic analysis [37]. Proteins with quantitative fold change >1.2 or <0.8 and p-value <0.05 were considered as differentially expressed.

Characterization of CELNs and MCELNs

CELNs were suspended in ultrapure water for assessment of their particle size and zeta potential. Furthermore, agarose gel electrophoresis and polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis were employed. Subsequently, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to detect RNA and protein components within CELNs. To ascertain the presence of lipids in CELNs, thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was utilized. To this end, the samples were separated on silica gel TLC plates and then visualized using a staining solution composed of 10 % copper sulfate and 8 % phosphoric acid.

To verify the decoration of MAM, we evaluated the size and zeta potential of MCELNs and subsequently visualized the morphology of the newly synthesized MCELNs under a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-1400Plus, Tokyo, Japan) after staining with uranyl acetate. In addition, we examined RAW 264.7 cells and MCELNs proteins. The protein profiles of MAM and MCELNs were analyzed through SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. Proteins were isolated from MAM and MCELNs using RIPA lysis buffer, followed by electrophoresis for detailed characterization. As a control, CELNs devoid of membrane modification were employed.

Uptake of CELNs and MCELNs by cells

CELNs were labeled with the fluorescent dye PKH67 at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. Fluorescent MCELNs were prepared from the labeled CELNs and subsequently co-cultured with TNBC cells, alongside the labeled CELNs. Cellular uptake of both CELNs and MCELNs was recorded using confocal microscopy after 4 and 8 h of treatment. PKH67 fluorescence signals in TNBC cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Biocompatibility and cytotoxicity of CELNs and MCELNs

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells per well) for 24 h of culture. CELNs and MCELNs were diluted across a range of 0–1920 μg/mL to incubate cells for another 24 h. Subsequently, cell viability was evaluated by measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Blood compatibility was assessed using a hemolysis assay. Different concentrations of CELNs and MCELNs (10–1500 μg/mL) were added to 1 mL of fresh mouse blood, followed by measurement of the absorbance at 550 nm.

Colony formation assay

Cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at a cellular density of 1 × 103 cells per well. Following a 24-h incubation period, the cells were treated with CELNs and MCELNs. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced and thereafter changed every three days for an additional period. On the 14th day, visual records were obtained following fixation with paraformaldehyde and staining with crystal violet.

Calcein-AM and PI fluorescence staining assay

Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well. Following a 24-h incubation period, they were exposed to either CELNs or MCELNs. After an additional 24 h of incubation, cells were stained with Calcein-AM and PI adhering to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, cells were maintained at 37 °C in darkness for 30 min and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA extraction and sequencing

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and then treated with CELNs for 24 h, with a control group included in parallel. The treated cell samples were sent to novogene (Beijing, China) for further sequencing analysis.

Differential expression analysis

We performed a differential expression analysis of the two comparison pairs using DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0) and adjusted p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg program.

Differential gene enrichment analysis

The analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment for differentially expressed genes was conducted utilizing the clusterProfiler software, specifically version 3.8.1. Additionally, we examined the statistical enrichment of these genes within Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using TRIzol reagent. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was subsequently performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a QuantStudio real-time PCR system. The reaction conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and then 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method and normalized to the expression of GAPDH as an internal control. Detection of cell cycle

MDA-MB-231 cells underwent treatment with either CELNs or MCELNs for 24 h, followed by collection and preservation in 70 % ethanol for a duration of 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended, and subjected to the addition of 100 μL of RNase A solution. Afterward they were stained with propidium iodide (PI). Ultimately, flow cytometry was employed for cell analysis, and the resultant data was processed utilizing the Modfit LT software.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit. Briefly, MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well for 24 h of culture. Following treatment with CELNs or MCELNs for another 24 h, the cells were harvested, stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to the manufacturer's protocol, and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence

MDA-MB-231 cells were exposed to medium containing CELNs or MCELNs for a 24-h period and subsequently fixed using 4 % paraformaldehyde. Following this, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100, blocked with 5 % BSA at ambient temperature, and subsequently incubated with the primary and secondary antibodies successively. Lastly, the cells were examined using confocal microscopy following DAPI staining.

Western blotting

Briefly, following treatment with CELNs or MCELNs for 24 h, the cells were collected and their total protein was extracted using RAPI lysis buffer. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA protein assay kit. The protein samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. These membranes were blocked with BSA prior to overnight incubation with the primary antibody. After incubation with the secondary antibody, protein bands were detected utilizing ECL reagent, and their intensities were subsequently measured and quantified using ImageJ software. To evaluate the relative potency of MCELNs, two reference drugs dihydrotanshinone (DHTS, 5 μM) and camptothecin (CPT, 5 μM) were designated as positive controls to induce cell cycle arrest and DNA damage, respectively.

Animal experiment design

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee of SLAS under protocol number (IACUC Issue No): SYST-2024-017. For the xenograft tumor model, twenty 4-week-old SPF BALB/c female nude mice were provided by Chengdu Pharmachem Biotechnology Co. and kept at Sichuan Scientist Biological Testing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After one week of acclimatization feeding, each animal was injected subcutaneously with 4 × 106 MDA-MB-231 cells. When the tumors reached a volume of 50–80 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly allocated into one of four groups (n = 5 per group) using a computer-generated random number list to ensure unbiased distribution. The control group animals received daily intraperitoneal injections of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), whereas the positive group animals underwent intraperitoneal administration of 5-Fluorouracil (5-Fu) at a dosage of 25 mg/kg every two days. The animals in the treatment groups received daily tail vein injections of either MCELNs (50 mg/kg.d−1) or CELNs (50 mg/kg.d−1). Tumor length and width were measured daily by a single investigator using a vernier caliper. This investigator was blinded to the animal group assignments throughout the entire experiment. Tumor volume was calculated according to the formula, volume = 1/2 × length × width2. After 10 days of medication, the tumors were surgically removed and weighed.

To evaluate the tumor-targeting capability of CELNs and MCELNs, mice bearing tumors of approximately 750–850 mm3 were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 3) and received an intravenous injection of Dir-CELNs or Dir-MCELNs, respectively. In vivo images were acquired at designated time points using an IVIS imaging system. All mice were humanely euthanized 24 h after injection and the tumors as well as major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected for ex vivo imaging.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry

Following fixation in 4 % paraformaldehyde, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. They were then stained with H&E following established protocol. For immunohistochemical analysis, primary antibodies matching those used in western blotting were employed.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and digested with Proteinase K (37 °C, 25 min). After rinsing, slides were treated with 3 % H2O2/methanol, washed with PBS, and incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) and deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP) at 37 °C for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data was statistically analyzed and visualized utilizing SPSS software version 19.0 (provided by IBM, Chicago, USA) alongside ImageJ (specifically version 1.5.2a). All data is displayed as the mean ± standard deviation. The statistical significance of the results was assessed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-test. p-value <0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Characterization of MCELNs and CELNs

We first performed a comparative proteomic analysis of HCT and CELNs. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed the number of peptides and proteins, with 2495 proteins in CELNs and 3706 in HCT. Among these, 2373 proteins (95.11 %) in the CELNs group were also found in the HCT group (Fig. 2A–C). Subcellular localization analysis showed that CELNs were enriched in cytoplasmic and membrane-associated proteins compared with HCT, indicating excellent biocompatibility and enhanced endocytosis potential (Fig. 2D). In CELNs, about 74 % of the proteins had molecular weights ranging from 0 to 80 kDa, whereas approximately 26 % were larger than 80 kDa. Notably, the distribution of protein number and molecular weight differed in part from that of HCT (Fig. 2E and F). The protein-protein interaction network of the differentially expressed proteins, GO, and KEGG analyses showed that CELNs still retained most of the biological activities of HCT and had significant advantages in the regulation of DNA-templated transcription (Fig. 2G–K). CELNs contain numerous proteins involved in signal transduction and various metabolic pathways, indicating that they exhibit vesicle-like properties and possess a potential pharmacodynamic material basis (Fig. 2L and M).

SDS-PAGE analysis of Caumas Brilliant Blue staining showed that most of the molecular weights of the proteins within CELNs were concentrated at 10–100 kDa (Fig. 3A). The detection of RNA enzymes verified the RNA content in CELNs, predominantly found within a molecular weight range of 100–1500 base pairs (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, TLC analysis revealed that CELNs comprise various kinds of lipids (Fig. 3C). Electron microscopic observation showed that MCELNs had a nucleoshell-like yolk-like structure compared to CELNs (Fig. 3D). The laser particle size analysis showed that the average particle size and zeta potential of CELNs were 148.56 nm and −11.03 mV, respectively, while those of MCELNs were 179.1 nm and −20.78 mV, respectively (Fig. 3E and F).

Subsequently, the protein profiles of macrophages, MAM, and MCELNs were determined by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. Since most of the protein composition in MAM was retained in MCELNs (Fig. 3G), the resulting biomimetic nanoparticles exhibited inherent exosome-like properties. To assess the purity of MAM and its successful coating on CELNs, we analyzed the expression of sodium potassium ATPase (SPA), CD9, β-Actin, Lamin B1, GAPDH, and Integrin α4 in RAW 264.7 cells, purified MAMs, and MCELNs by Western blot. As a result, the specific MAM markers SPA, CD9, and Integrin α4 were detected in RAW 264.7 cells, purified MAM, and MCELNs. β-Actin, Lamin B1, and GAPDH were readily detected in RAW 264.7 cell lysates but absent or barely detectable in purified MAM and MCELNs (Fig. 3H). This demonstrated the high purity of the MAM preparation and its successful coating on CELNs.

Biocompatibility and targeting of MCELNs and CELNs

We examined the uptake of MCELNs and CELNs by 4T1, MDA-MB-231, and BT-549 cells using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry, respectively. The results showed that the cellular uptake of MCELNs was significantly higher than that of CELNs after 4 and 8 h of incubation. However, no notable difference in uptake was observed among three cell lines at any given time point (Fig. 4A–F). After co-incubation with blood cells, the hemolysis rate of CELNs was only 11.68 % even at a high concentration of 1500 μg/mL, and it decreased progressively with lower doses. At identical concentrations, MCELNs exhibited a lower hemolysis rate than CELNs (Fig. 4G and H).

To evaluate the targeting capability of MCELNs, fluorescence-loaded CELNs and MCELNs were intravenously injected into BALB/c tumor-bearing nude mice. As shown in Fig. 4(I&J), CELNs exhibited weak fluorescence in tumor tissue, which may be attributed to their poor circulatory stability and rapid clearance. In contrast, within 8 h after MCELNs treatment, the fluorescence intensity at the tumor site showed a time-dependent increase, highlighting the targeting advantage conferred by MAM encapsulation. Furthermore, the accumulation of MCELNs in tumor tissues was significantly higher than that in major organs, except for the liver, which can be primarily ascribed to their prolonged circulation time and enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect (Fig. 4I and J).

MCELNs and CELNs inhibited in vivo tumor growth

To observe the effects of MCELNs and CELNs in suppressing breast cancer in vivo, we performed subcutaneous MDA-MB-231 xenograft tumor experiments (Fig. 5A). Compared to the control group, there was no notable difference in body weight of mice treated with MCELNs or CELNs, suggesting good tolerance to these treatments (Fig. 5B). However, both MCELNs and CELNs demonstrated substantial suppressive effects on the growth of MDA-MB-231 xenograft tumors. The MCELNs group exhibited significantly smaller tumor volumes and lower tumor weights than the CELNs group (Fig. 5C–E). In addition, the toxicity of the treatment was assessed by major organ H&E staining. The findings indicated that no obvious necrosis was detected in the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, or kidneys of all tumor-bearing mice, thereby reaffirming the safety profile of the medication (Fig. 5F).

MCELNs and CELNs suppressed the growth of TNBC cells

MCELNs were more highly cytotoxic to 4T1, MDA-MB-231, and BT-549 cells than CELNs, both of which repressed cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner. The half inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of CELNs were 1905 μg/ml (4T1), 1405 μg/ml (BT-549), and 755 μg/ml (MDA-MB-231), respectively, after 24 h of treatment, which were significantly higher than those of MCELNs (Fig. 6A–C). Therefore, we chose the above concentrations for subsequent studies on MCELNs and CELNs. Their effects on cell proliferation were evaluated by clone formation assay. Compared to the control group, the CELNs treatment group showed a significant reduction in colony area. This inhibitory effect was even more pronounced in the MCELNs group relative to the CELNs group (Fig. 6D–G). Following treatment with CELNs and MCELNs for 24 h, 4T1, MDA-MB-231, and BT-549 cells were stained with Calcein-AM and PI dual fluorescence. As a result, the number of dead cells evidently increased and MCELNs induced the death more obviously (Fig. 6H–K).

MCELNs induced enrichment of differential genes in DNA damage repair pathway

To explore the cell death mechanism induced by MCELNs, we performed transcriptomic analysis of MDA-MB-231 cells. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that numerous genes were differentially expressed compared to the control group (Fig. 7A). KEGG enrichment encompassed such processes as DNA replication, excision repair of nucleotides, and the cell cycle arrest (Fig. 7B and C). GO enrichment analysis showed that MCELNs mainly affected pathways related to RNA nucleotide excision repair, DNA replication, and cell proliferation (Fig. 7D and E). GESA analysis identified significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes involved in DNA damage and cell cycle arrest (Fig. 7F–H).

To validate the transcriptomic results, quantitative real-time PCR was utilized to analyze the expression of several key genes associated with DNA damage repair and cell cycle arrest. Consistent with the transcriptomic analysis, MCELNs significantly upregulated the expression of MCM2, MCM7, CHEK1, GADD45G, CDC25A, CDK1, and CCND2 (Fig. 7I–L).

MCELNs and CELNs elicited DNA damage and cell cycle arrest in TNBC cells

An early event in the cellular response to DNA damage involves the phosphorylation of H2AX (γ-H2AX), which is catalyzed by ATM and ATR kinases [38]. We found that the fluorescence intensity of γ-H2AX, a DNA damage marker, significantly increased in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with MCELNs and CELNs compared to the control group. Elevated γ-H2AX expression was also more pronounced in the MCELNs group than in the CELNs group (Fig. 8A and B). DNA damage can induce the cessation of cell cycle, which was further detected through flow cytometry. Following 24-h treatment of MDA-MB-231 cells with MCELNs and CELNs, a notable increase in G0/G1 phase cells was observed in comparison to the control group. Concurrently, an obvious decrease was detected in G2/M phase cells. MCELNs blocked cell cycle more significantly than CELNs (Fig. 8C and D)

To explore the mechanism of action involved, we analyzed the expression of proteins associated with the cell cycle, such as p-ATR, p-CHK1, CDC25C, CDK4, and P21. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that drug treatment elicited a significant upregulation of p-ATR, P21, and p-CHK1, and concurrently depressed the expression of CDK4 and CDC25C (Fig. 8E–J). Western blot analysis yielded consistent results (Fig. 8K–R). These findings confirmed that CELNs induced G0/G1 phase arrest, while the effect was significantly potentiated by MAM encapsulation. Notably, MCELNs exhibited efficacy comparable to that of the positive control group (DHTS/CPT).

MPA reversed MCELNs-induced DNA damage and cell cycle arrest in TNBC cells

Given that MPA is a known protector against DNA damage, we used it to investigate whether DNA damage is the underlying mechanism through which MCELNs induce cell cycle arrest. The viability of MDA-MB-231 cells treated with MCELNs was significantly restored by MPA, with no significant difference observed compared to the control group (Fig. 9A). Flow cytometry showed that MCELNs significantly increased the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase, but effect was reversed by co-treatment with MPA (Fig. 9B and C). Immunofluorescence and WB assays demonstrated that MPA counteracted the MCELNs-induced effects, namely the enhancement of γ-H2AX, p-ATR, p-CHK1, and P21, and reduction of CDK4 and CDC25C (Fig. 9E–R).

MCELNs and CELNs induced apoptosis in TNBC cells

Apoptosis is the most prevalent form of cell death following DNA damage-induced cell cycle arrest. Therefore, we further examined apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining. The results showed that both MCELNs and CELNs significantly induced apoptosis, with MCELNs triggering a higher apoptosis rate (Fig. 10A and B). Notably, this pro-apoptotic effect was markedly reversed by MPA (Fig. 10C and D). Western blot analysis demonstrated that MCELNs upregulated the expression of cleaved PARP1 and cleaved caspase 3 while downregulating full length PARP1 (Fig. 10E–H), and these effects were completely abolished by MPA (Fig. 10I–L). These findings indicated that MCELNs induced apoptosis via DNA damage-triggered cell cycle arrest.

Discussion

Edible plant exosome-like nanovesicles have been shown to treat a variety of diseases in recent years [39]. We isolated edible CELNs from HCT as novel components containing lipids, proteins, and RNA. However, the therapeutic activity of drug delivery systems usually depends on passive diffusion and random drug release mechanisms. Therefore, the construction of stable and controllable drug carrier systems with active targeting capabilities is essential to ensure efficacy and minimize toxic side effects. With the increasing understanding of bionanoscience, plasma membrane-coated nano-delivery systems have attracted much attention in recent years. Several studies have revealed that encapsulating small-molecule drugs within various cell membranes can enhance drug efficacy [40]. To this end, numerous techniques have been devised for their preparation. One of notable examples is the synthesis of leukocyte membrane-coated nanovesicles, which is achieved through the persistent rotation of nanovesicles under the combined effects of electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. Conversely, MAM-coated emtansine liposomes are produced through direct extrusion techniques [41]. These biomimetic strategies exploit the function of biological membranes to help small molecule drugs to cross biological barriers and escape the control of the body's immune system. This increases drug enrichment in the lesion. By mimicking the structure of natural cells, plasma membrane-coated nanovesicles acquire enhanced drug stability, prolonged blood circulation, and a superior targeting ability, leading to increased affinity for target cells [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. This biomimetic camouflage represents a promising therapeutic strategy for tumor-targeted drug delivery.

Here, we report for the first time the successful preparation of MAM-encapsulated CELN nanovesicles (MCELNs) for the targeted treatment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). The significant expression of integrin α4 was detected in both extracted macrophage membranes and the resulting MCELNs, confirming the inheritance of the surface protein profile and bio-interface properties from source cells. The macrophage membrane coating not only protects synthetic nanovesicles from immune clearance but also enables precise recognition of tumor antigens through natural targeting mechanisms. Specifically, integrin α4 expressed on the macrophage membrane binds to VCAM-1 in cancer cells, facilitating tumor adhesion and infiltration [46]. In tumor-bearing mice, MCELNs led to a greater reduction in tumor size and weight compared to CELNs. The superior performance of MAM-coated nanovesicles is likely due to the combined effects of enhanced cellular uptake, prolonged systemic circulation, and their selective tumor-targeting capability.

Similar to red blood cell membrane-coated nanovesicles, MCELNs also demonstrated enhanced internalization efficiency in tumor cells [47]. In vitro studies demonstrated that the cellular uptake of MCELNs was significantly higher than that of CELNs, owing to the enhanced affinity of MAM coating for tumor cells. However, we observed that the overall internalization pathway and intracellular distribution pattern of MCELNs were not altered. MCELNs showed stronger cytotoxicity and anti-proliferative effects on cancer cells than CELNs, and these results were also consistent with the in vivo findings. This suggests that MAM-encapsulated nanovesicles exhibit improved effectiveness in both in vitro and in vivo settings, aligning with earlier research findings [48]. Based on our findings, MCELNs not only function as an effective therapeutic agent but also emerge as a promising nanocarrier platform [49]. This synergistic strategy could integrate the biocompatibility and active targeting properties of the biomimetic vehicle with the potent cytotoxicity of conventional therapeutics, thereby potentially overcoming major limitations of free drugs including poor solubility, systemic toxicity, and the emergence of resistance.

To reveal the mechanism of MCELNs to inhibit breast cancer progression, we conducted transcriptomics analysis. The differential gene screening and enrichment analysis primarily implicated DNA damage response and cell cycle arrest as central pathways. Key biological processes within these pathways included RNA splicing and DNA replication. It has been reported that RNA splicing can cause cell cycle abnormalities [50]. Genotoxic anticancer drugs work by damaging the DNA within cancer cells [51]. Although DNA damage in cancer cells causes genomic instability contributing to tumor development, it also provides opportunities for cancer treatment [52]. DNA damage can induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in addition to DNA repair and survival [53]. Clinical development of anti-tumor drugs targeting DNA damage is also increasing. Apart from chemotherapeutic agents, certain natural compounds possess the ability to induce DNA damage and have been formulated as anti-cancer medications, such as paclitaxel [54,55]. In this study, we found that MCELNs could cause DNA damage to induce cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase. The ATR-CHK1 axis is a key DNA damage response pathway, and its inhibition represents a promising approach for novel antitumor drug discovery [56,57]. In this study, MCELNs treatment increased p-ATR, p-CHK1 expression and downregulated CDC25C, consistent with cell cycle arrest being mediated through the ATR-CHK1-CDC25C pathway [58,59]. We also demonstrated that the ATR-dependent p21-CDK4 pathway was activated by CELNs-induced DNA damage. Following MAM modification, both DNA damage and subsequent cell cycle blockade were significantly enhanced. To establish the causal relationship between MCELNs-mediated DNA damage and pathway activation, we conducted antagonism experiments using MPA, a recognized DNA damage protective agent. As a result, MPA treatment effectively abrogated MCELNs-triggered DNA damage, restored proliferative capacity, alleviated cell cycle arrest, and normalized the expression of DNA damage response proteins (γ-H2AX, p-ATR, p-CHK1, CDC25C, P21, CDK4). Critically, this restoration was accompanied by a significant reduction in apoptosis, as evidenced by decreased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP1, confirming that the DNA damage-initiated cell cycle arrest ultimately leads to cell apoptosis. These findings indicate that MCELNs induce cell cycle arrest primarily through a DNA damage-mediated mechanism, which subsequently triggers the apoptotic pathway.

While our findings establish that MCELNs induce significant G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis primarily through a DNA damage-dependent mechanism, we recognize that DNA damage response (DDR) is a highly complex network of interconnected pathways [60]. For instance, beyond the ATR-CHK1 signaling highlighted here, other key regulators, such as those in the ATM-CHK2 axis, p53, and crosstalk with alternative repair pathways (e.g., homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining), also play critical roles [61]. Our current work focuses on elucidating the ATR-dependent pathway as a central mechanism underlying the effects induced by MCELNs. However, a more comprehensive mapping of upstream sensors (e.g., MRN complex), effector kinases, and their contextual interactions remains beyond the scope of this study. Future investigations will employ combined genetic and pharmacological approaches to systematically dissect the contributions of these additional DDR components. Furthermore, leveraging large-scale screening strategies and advanced single-cell technologies may help resolve heterogeneity in DDR activation and identify biomarkers predictive of therapeutic responsiveness.

Conclusion

In summary, we extracted and characterized CELNs which contain proteins, lipids, and RNA. We also constructed a MAM-modified liposomal formulation of CELNs, with enhanced inhibitory activity against TNBC. MAM were successfully grafted onto CELNs through a simple approach, enabling greater CELNs enrichment into tumors. CELNs and MCELNs effectively inhibited TNBC growth. Mechanistically, both CELNs and MCELNs trigger DNA damage, activating the ATR/CHK1 pathway, which results in cell cycle arrest and subsequent apoptosis in TNBC cells (Fig. 12). This proof-of-concept study demonstrates a promising nanomedicine therapeutic strategy by utilizing the properties of MAM.
