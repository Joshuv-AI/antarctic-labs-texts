Neuroprotective potential of esterified indole-3-propionic acid with curcumin against high glucose stress: targeting oxidative damage, Akt/mTOR, and BDNF/TrkB pathways
Authors: Jayanthi Sidhambaram, Chitra Loganathan, Penislusshiyan Sakayanathan, Palvannan Thayumanavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasingly linked to neurodegenerative changes driven by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulated signaling pathways, particularly under chronic hyperglycemia.AimThis study aimed to assess the neuroprotective effects of novel curcumin–indole-3-propionic acid conjugate (CUR-IPA) against high glucose (HG)-induced damage in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells.MethodsCells were exposed to HG to simulate diabetic stress and co-treated with CUR-IPA (6.25–25 µM). Cell viability, oxidative stress markers, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and apoptosis were evaluated. Flow cytometry was used to quantify the expression of apoptotic (p53, Bax, caspase-3, and Bcl-2), survival (p-Akt and mTOR), and neurotrophic (BDNF, p-TrkB, and CREB) proteins.ResultsHG induced oxidative stress, loss of MMP, apoptosis, and suppression of PI3K/Akt/mTOR and BDNF/TrkB/CREB pathways.ConclusionIn conclusion, CUR-IPA demonstrates strong neuroprotective potential under HG stress by modulating redox status, mitochondrial function, and survival signaling. The findings suggest its promise as a multi-target therapy for diabetes-linked neurodegeneration, and warrant an in vivo study.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents a multifaceted metabolic illness marked by chronically elevated blood sugar, typically arising from the body’s inability to produce or effectively respond to insulin [1]. Its global footprint is expanding rapidly; current estimates suggest nearly 589 million adults are living with the condition as of 2024, with projections pointing toward a staggering 853 million by 2050 [2]. The overwhelming majority of these cases, that is, about 90%, are attributed to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3]. However, a significant portion of individuals remain unaware of their diagnosis, leaving them vulnerable to preventable complications. The persistence of high blood glucose acts as the central factor behind a range of long-term complications, broadly categorized as either microvascular or macrovascular [4]. Damage to small blood vessels results in eye disorders like diabetic retinopathy, kidney impairments progressing to failure, and nerve damage that particularly affects peripheral sensation, frequently leading to chronic wounds and infections [5]. On the other hand, large vessel involvement escalates the risk of cardiovascular events, including heart attacks and strokes, and can severely impair blood flow to limbs, sometimes necessitating amputations [5]. T2DM is no longer confined to older adults; its onset is being increasingly observed in younger populations. Emerging studies also reveal a troubling link between diabetes and declining cognitive abilities, regardless of age [6]. With longer life spans and the growing prevalence of T2DM, diabetes-related cognitive impairments are placing an unprecedented strain on healthcare systems [7].

Neurons depend highly on glucose for function; however, sustained hyperglycemia disrupts normal glucose handling inside brain cells, causing significant neural damage [8, 9]. One of the most debilitating consequences is peripheral neuropathy, a condition not only prevalent but also contributing to chronic pain, disability, and a marked decline in quality of life [10]. This condition stands as a major driver of foot ulcers and the leading cause of limb amputations that are not trauma-related [11]. At the cellular level, prolonged high blood sugar triggers harmful events, including oxidative stress, alteration in calcium signalling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, especially affecting peripheral nerves and their supporting cells [9, 12–16]. Oxidative stress, defined as an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defense mechanisms, leads to macromolecular damage and plays a central role in hyperglycemia-induced neuronal dysfunction [17, 18]. The oxidative damage inflicted on mitochondria-packed axons ultimately leads to nerve cell death [19]. This mechanism, also involved in diseases like Alzheimer’s (AD) and Parkinson’s (PD), underscores the role of oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in broader neurodegeneration [20]. Although the impact of diabetes on brain function is still investigated, several key contributors have been consistently highlighted in experimental studies using diabetic animal models. These include an imbalance in oxidative processes, disruption of acetylcholine-mediated neurotransmission, chronic inflammation within the nervous system, and shifts in molecular signaling cascades [21–23]. Notably affected pathways include Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor/Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (BDNF/TrkB), which governs neuronal survival and plasticity; the B-cell lymphoma 2/Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bcl-2/Bax) axis, central to the regulation of programmed cell death; the Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) route involved in cell growth and metabolism; Wingless-related integration site/β-Catenin (Wnt/β-Catenin) signaling, essential for neurogenesis and synaptic integrity; and the Extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2/Mitogen-activated protein kinase (ERK1/2-MAPK) pathway, which plays a critical role in cell response to stress and injury [9, 24–27]. The growing prevalence of diabetes has been matched by rising healthcare costs, especially related to managing its neurological complications, ranging from peripheral nerve degeneration to cognitive decline. Importantly, these impairments stem not from a singular cause but from a complex network of biochemical and cellular disturbances. Given this multifactorial pathology, the development of therapeutic candidates capable of acting on multiple pathways simultaneously, while minimizing adverse effects, is urgently needed. Identifying such a multi-target agent holds promise for more effective and comprehensive treatment of diabetes-associated brain dysfunction.

In prior work, we synthesized and structurally confirmed a diester compound created by coupling curcumin (CUR) with indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), termed CUR-IPA [28]. CUR is widely recognized for its neuroprotective profile, which includes modulating inflammatory mediators, maintaining iron balance, regulating gene expression epigenetically, enhancing antioxidant defenses, and engaging signaling cascades, particularly the BDNF/TrkB pathway [28–30]. It has also been investigated in a wide array of neurological conditions, including AD, PD, depression, anxiety, stroke, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, and traumatic brain injury [31].

IPA, on the other hand, is a potent scavenger of hydroxyl radicals and has demonstrated inhibitory activity against β-amyloid aggregation, positioning it as a potential candidate for AD therapy [32, 33]. Despite its broad bioactivity, CUR has limited pharmacological utility due to poor solubility and bioavailability. To address this, structural modification strategies, particularly esterification of its phenolic hydroxyl groups, have been explored to improve its pharmacokinetics. In this context, the synthesis of CUR-IPA aimed to enhance CUR’s stability and biological efficacy [34].

Initial in vitro investigations revealed that CUR-IPA exhibits notable antioxidant capacity and inhibits key cholinesterases, namely acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE). Computational docking supported these findings, showing favorable binding of CUR-IPA within the active sites of both enzymes [28]. Given these observations and considering that both CUR and IPA individually exert neuroprotective effects, the present study investigates CUR-IPA’s protective role under high-glucose (HG) stress in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells. The study evaluated multiple cellular stress markers, including ROS accumulation, intracellular antioxidant levels, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and apoptotic changes. Furthermore, using flow cytometry, changes in the expression of critical proteins involved in neuroprotective signaling pathways, specifically BDNF/TrkB, p53, PI3K/Akt, and the apoptosis-regulating Bcl-2/Bax axis, were quantified.

Materials and methods

Materials

CUR and IPA were purchased from Sigma Chemical Company, USA. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) was sourced from Gibco, a division of Thermo Fisher Scientific located in Massachusetts, USA. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) used in the study was acquired from Invitrogen, under the same parent company. Various fluorescent probes including 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), JC1, acridine orange (AO), ethidium bromide (EtBr), and Hoechst dye were purchased from Cayman Chemical, USA. Assay kits designed to evaluate antioxidant activity were procured from multiple sources: superoxide dismutase (SOD, Cat. No. E-EL-H1113) and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1, Cat. No. E-EL-H5410) were obtained from Elabscience, USA. Catalase (CAT, Cat. No. KBH3053) and reduced glutathione (GSH, Cat. No. KBH1462) kits were purchased from KrishGen Biosystems, India. Antibodies for apoptosis and signaling studies were obtained from two suppliers. From BD Biosciences (California, USA), the following were purchased: Caspase-3 FITC (Cat. No. 559341), BCL2-PE (Cat. No. 340576), p53-FITC (Cat. No. 557026), and phospho-mTOR (Ser2448)-PE (Cat. No. 563489). Additional antibodies were sourced from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA), including Bax (B9) Alexa Fluor 647 (Cat. No. sc-7480 AF647), phosphorylated Akt1 (B-5) Alexa Fluor 647 (Cat. No. sc-271966 AF647), proBDNF FITC (5H8, Cat. No. sc-65514 FITC), phosphorylated Trk receptor PE (E-6, Cat. No. sc-8058 PE), and CREB (D-12) Alexa Fluor 647 (Cat. No. sc-377154 AF647). CUR-IPA synthesized from our previous study, were used for cell line analysis [28].

Cell line treatment

The SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line was sourced from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 25 mM glucose and 10% FBS, under standard incubation conditions of 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and maintained at high humidity. To examine the cytotoxic impact of hyperglycemia, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 10⁴ cells per well and allowed 24 h for attachment. The effect of CUR-IPA on cell viability was assessed by treating cells with graded concentrations of CUR-IPA (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 µM) for 24 h. For evaluating the protective efficacy of CUR-IPA against high-glucose-induced toxicity, the experimental design included three groups: a normoglycemic control maintained in 25 mM glucose, a hyperglycemic group exposed to 55 mM glucose (as established in prior reports), and a combination treatment group [9, 35]. In the latter, SH-SY5Y cells were pre-incubated with CUR-IPA at 5, 10, or 15 µM for 2 h, followed by the addition of 55 mM glucose along with the same CUR-IPA dose, with co-incubation proceeding for 36 h to evaluate cytoprotective outcomes.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the 3−(4,5 − dimethylthiazol − 2 − yl) − 2,5 − diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Following treatment, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in a CO₂ incubator with MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in DMEM). After incubation, the medium was removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (ELx800, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

ROS detection by confocal microscopy

Intracellular ROS generation was assessed using DCFH-DA staining. DCFH-DA, a cell-permeable probe, is deacetylated by intracellular esterases to form non-fluorescent DCFH. Upon oxidation by reactive oxygen species, DCFH is converted to fluorescent DCF, allowing quantitative assessment of intracellular ROS levels. After treatment, cells were incubated with 1 µM DCFH-DA in complete medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed with 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) and counterstained with Hoechst dye to visualise nuclei. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM710, Carl Zeiss, Germany) with the following excitation/emission settings: DCFH-DA at 488/525 nm and Hoechst at 350/461 nm.

Quantification of LPO products

After treatment, the cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. The cells were homogenized using a water bath sonicator for 2 min and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min. The LPO product was measured colorimetrically using TBARS (Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances) assay. The chromophore formed was measured using an ELISA reader at 535 nm [36].

Antioxidant detection by ELISA

The cells were washed with ice-cold 1x PBS (pH 7.4) and detached using trypsin. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed three times with pre-chilled PBS. For every 1 × 10⁶ cells, 150–250 µL of cold PBS was added to resuspend the pellet. Cells were lysed by repeated freeze–thaw cycles or sonication under ice-cold conditions until complete disruption. The lysate was then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the clear supernatant was collected for antioxidant analysis. Activities of SOD, CAT, GPx, and GSH levels were quantified using commercial kits as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

MMP detection by flow cytometry

MMP was assessed using the cationic dye JC-1. Following treatment, cells were harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature, and washed once with PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL of JC-1 staining solution (5 µg/mL in serum-free medium) and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator protected from light. After incubation, cells were washed gently with 1x PBS (pH 7.4) to remove excess dye. Fluorescence was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA) using FL1 (530 nm) for JC-1 monomers (green) and FL2 (590 nm) for JC-1 aggregates (red).

Apoptosis detection by AO/EtBr dual staining using confocal microscopy

Apoptotic cell death was evaluated using dual staining with AO and EtBr. Following treatment, cells were harvested using trypsin-EDTA, centrifuged, and resuspended in AO/EtBr staining solution (100 µg/mL each). After a 10-minute incubation in the dark, cells were washed with 1x PBS (pH 7.4) and mounted in a glass-bottom dish. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope with appropriate excitation/emission filters for AO (470/40 nm, 525/50 nm) and EtBr (560/40 nm, 645/75 nm).

Determination of protein level by flow cytometry

Following the treatment period, cells were fixed and permeabilized using ethanol. The permeabilized cells were then incubated with the respective primary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Approximately 10 µL of antibody (200 µg/mL) was used per 1 × 10⁶ cells. After incubation, the cells were washed with 1× PBS (pH 7.4) to remove unbound antibodies. Fluorescence-based protein expression analysis was carried out using flow cytometry. Fluorescence signals were detected using the following filter settings: FITC: Excitation at 494 nm, emission at 520 nm (FL-1 channel); PE: Excitation at 488 nm, emission at 578 nm (FL-2 channel); Alexa Fluor 647: Excitation at 650 nm, emission at 668 nm (FL-4 channel). Data acquisition and analysis were performed using CellQuest Pro Software (Version 6.0).

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was independently repeated three times (biological replicates). To assess the statistical relevance of observed differences among groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test to pinpoint specific group differences. Homogeneity of variance was confirmed using the Brown–Forsythe test (P > 0.05), validating the use of one-way ANOVA.

Results

Evaluation of CUR-IPA cytotoxicity and its protective effect against HG-induced damage in SH-SY5Y cells

In SH-SY5Y cells treated with increasing concentrations of CUR-IPA alone, cell viability remained comparable to control at lower doses (3.125 and 6.25 µM). Moderate declines were observed at 12.5 µM and 25 µM, while a marked cytotoxic effect was evident at 50 µM, where viability dropped to approximately 73%. Under HG conditions, viability significantly decreased to around 45%, indicating glucose-induced cytotoxicity. However, pre-treatment with CUR-IPA showed substantial protection in a concentration-dependent manner. In the presence of 25 µM CUR-IPA, improved viability (≈75%) was observed, compared to HG-treated cells (Fig. 1A and B).

ROS generation and LPO under HG and CUR-IPA-treated conditions

To assess oxidative stress, intracellular ROS generation was quantified using DCF fluorescence. Further, the cells were counterstained with Hoechst dye to visualise nuclei as well as understand the accumulation of ROS in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2A and E). SH-SY5Y cells exposed to HG showed a significant elevation in ROS levels (mean green fluorescence: 5.61 ± 0.94) compared to the control (0.20 ± 0.26) (Fig. 2F). Treatment with CUR-IPA led to a concentration-dependent reduction in ROS accumulation. Cells pretreated with 25 µM CUR-IPA exhibited DCF fluorescence intensity of 0.40 ± 0.1, indicating an antioxidant effect of CUR-IPA that counteracted HG-induced oxidative damage. LPO was assessed by measuring MDA levels. HG exposure increased MDA levels, while CUR-IPA treatment reduced them in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2G).

CUR-IPA modulates antioxidant defense enzymes under HG stress

To evaluate the impact of CUR-IPA on endogenous antioxidant mechanisms, key enzymatic and non-enzymatic markers of oxidative defense, CAT, GPx, GSH, and SOD were quantified under control, HG, and CUR-IPA co-treatment conditions (Fig. 3A and D). SH-SY5Y cells challenged with HG exhibited a pronounced suppression in all four antioxidant parameters. CAT activity dropped from 7.67 ± 0.17 ng/mL in control cells to 2.17 ± 0.16 ng/mL under HG conditions. Co-treatment with CUR-IPA reversed this decline in a dose-responsive manner, with 25 µM CUR-IPA restoring catalase activity to 6.87 ± 0.19 ng/mL. Similarly, GPx levels, which were substantially diminished under HG exposure (227.74 ± 10.67 pg/mL compared to 1224.29 ± 21.75 pg/mL in controls), showed progressive elevation with increasing CUR-IPA concentrations. The highest recovery was observed at 25 µM CUR-IPA (898.83 ± 10.43 pg/mL). GSH content followed a parallel trend. Cells exposed to HG showed a sharp decline (4.07 ± 0.16 pg/mL), whereas CUR-IPA administration reinstated GSH levels, peaking at 18.28 ± 0.39 pg/mL with 25 µM treatment, nearing control values (19.56 ± 0.21 pg/mL). SOD activity was significantly reduced in the HG group (22.69 ± 0.89%) compared to controls (83.09 ± 0.62%). CUR-IPA treatment mitigated this reduction, restoring SOD activity in a concentration-dependent fashion, with 25 µM CUR-IPA yielding 71.69 ± 0.52% activity.

CUR-IPA preserves mitochondrial membrane integrity under HG insult

MMP (Δψm) was assessed using JC-1 dye to determine the extent of mitochondrial damage induced by HG and the protective efficacy of CUR-IPA (Fig. 4A and F). In control SH-SY5Y cells, JC-1 dye predominantly formed red-fluorescent aggregates, indicating intact mitochondrial membranes. However, cells subjected to HG exhibited a profound loss of Δψm, with a fluorescence ratio shift towards green, signifying severe mitochondrial depolarization. Upon CUR-IPA co-treatment, a clear concentration-dependent preservation of mitochondrial potential was observed. Particularly, in the presence of 25 µM CUR-IPA, the mitochondrial damage was only 24.88 ± 2.6%. The substantial recovery at 25 µM CUR-IPA indicates that the compound effectively stabilizes mitochondrial membranes and mitigates HG-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

CUR-IPA mitigates HG-induced nuclear damage and apoptosis

To visualize nuclear integrity and evaluate apoptotic changes, SH-SY5Y cells were stained with acridine orange (AO) and ethidium bromide (EtBr) under control, HG, and CUR-IPA-treated conditions (Fig. 5A and E). Control cells exhibited uniformly green fluorescence with intact, round nuclei, indicative of viable, non-apoptotic cells. In contrast, cells exposed to HG exhibited prominent orange to red fluorescence, accompanied by nuclear condensation and fragmentation, which are hallmarks of late-stage apoptosis and necrosis. This confirmed that prolonged HG exposure severely compromised nuclear structure and triggered apoptotic pathways. Treatment with CUR-IPA exerted a protective effect, with a concentration-dependent reduction in EtBr uptake. At increasing CUR-IPA concentrations (6.25–25 µM), fewer cells exhibited red fluorescence, and nuclear morphology resembled that of the control group. The 25 µM dose notably maintained nuclear integrity and reduced chromatin damage, indicating a robust anti-apoptotic action.

Protein expression analysis by flow cytometry

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying CUR-IPA’s anti-apoptotic effect, the expression of key apoptotic and survival markers, p53, Bax, Bcl-2, caspase-3, mTOR, and p-Akt, was examined using flow cytometry (Fig. 6A and F). HG exposure markedly altered the balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic signaling, consistent with induction of programmed cell death. HG treatment led to a substantial upregulation of pro-apoptotic markers: p53 (72.16 ± 2.64), Bax (74.37 ± 2.71), and cleaved caspase-3 (72.16 ± 2.64), compared to negligible expression levels in control cells. Concurrently, anti-apoptotic markers were significantly suppressed, Bcl-2 dropped from 99.38 ± 0.23 (control) to 18.01 ± 2.12, and p-Akt and mTOR showed sharp declines to 13.44 ± 1.31 and 0.01 ± 0.01, respectively. Co-treatment with CUR-IPA reversed these alterations in a concentration-dependent manner. At 25 µM CUR-IPA, pro-apoptotic markers were markedly suppressed: p53 decreased to 16.59 ± 1.86, Bax to 12.57 ± 1.18, and caspase-3 to 16.59 ± 1.86. Meanwhile, expression of survival markers was significantly restored (Bcl-2 reached 69.40 ± 2.34, p-Akt increased to 86.63 ± 1.45, and mTOR to 69.40 ± 2.34).

To investigate whether CUR-IPA influences synaptic plasticity and neurotrophic signaling under HG stress, the expression of BDNF, CREB1, and phosphorylated TrkB was assessed using flow cytometry (Fig. 7A and F). In control SH-SY5Y cells, expression levels of BDNF, CREB1, and p-TrkB were robust, reflecting intact neurotrophic signaling. However, exposure to HG caused a sharp reduction in all three proteins: BDNF declined to 24.53 ± 1.19, CREB1 to 21.17 ± 1.65, and p-TrkB to 9.07 ± 6.01, indicating severe impairment of the neuroprotective axis. CUR-IPA treatment rescued these deficits in a dose-dependent manner. At 25 µM, BDNF expression rose to 87.46 ± 0.82, CREB1 to 93.35 ± 0.21, and p-TrkB to 71.62 ± 0.42, approaching control levels. Lower concentrations (6.25 and 12.5 µM) also improved expression, though to a lesser extent.

Discussion

HG is well-known for inducing cell death in neuronal cells [9, 35, 37]. ROS generation is the foremost culprit in bringing neuronal dysfunction under HG conditions. In general, the brain’s inherent vulnerability to oxidative stress is largely attributed to its limited endogenous antioxidant capacity compared to metabolically active peripheral tissues such as the liver. This deficiency is further complicated by the subdued activity of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2, a transcription factor critical for regulating antioxidant gene expression. Consequently, reduced GSH availability in neurons impairs the activity of GPX, a key enzyme in the detoxification of lipid hydroperoxides, and may underlie neuronal susceptibility to ferroptosis [38, 39]. Under hyperglycemic conditions, neuronal oxidative stress is largely driven by mitochondrial and NADPH oxidase-derived ROS. Elevated glucose levels enhance mitochondrial respiration, increasing electron flux through the electron transport chain. This heightened metabolic activity favors electron leakage, particularly at complexes I and III, resulting in elevated superoxide generation [40]. In parallel, NADPH oxidase, a membrane-bound enzymatic complex, becomes aberrantly activated by hyperglycemia through signaling cascades involving protein kinase C (PKC) and TGF-β1/Smad2 pathways, further amplifying cytosolic ROS production [41]. Adding to this oxidative burden, hyperglycemia induces the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and contributes to mitochondrial structural and functional deterioration, both of which reinforce ROS generation [42]. In addition to ROS generation, the depletion of endogenous antioxidants contributes to the oxidative stress under HG conditions [9, 43, 44]. The cumulative impact of these processes results in sustained oxidative stress, which compromises neuronal viability and function, underscoring the need for interventions that target multiple sources of ROS as well as preserving the endogenous antioxidants [40–42, 45, 46]. In certain studies, CUR is reported for pro-oxidant activity, whereas the advantage of IPA is that it does not have any pro-oxidant activity [47, 48]. A novel CUR derivative, palmitic acid CUR ester, has demonstrated neuroprotective potential in models of amyloid-β (Aβ) toxicity. In neuroblastoma cells challenged with oligomeric Aβ, a well-characterized pathogenic species in AD, this lipophilic CUR conjugate conferred significant protection. The compound’s structural modification, involving esterification with palmitic acid, likely enhances its membrane permeability and metabolic stability, thereby improving its efficacy against Aβ-induced oxidative and apoptotic damage. These findings underscore the therapeutic promise of lipid-conjugated curcumin analogs in mitigating amyloid-driven neurotoxicity [49]. Previously, it has been reported that CUR conjugated with alkyl-substituted amino acids, such as alanine, valine, serine and cysteine, has improved antioxidant activity in comparison to CUR [50]. Hence, conjugation of IPA with CUR would have enhanced antioxidant properties. CUR-IPA preserved the redox status of the neuron under HG conditions, which further led to the maintenance of MMP under HG conditions. In different types of cells, including neuronal cells, HG is implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction, which plays a central role in the pathophysiology of diabetes-related neuronal injury, with excessive ROS acting as a primary instigator [51]. One of the earliest manifestations of this oxidative stress is the collapse of the MMP, a vital electrochemical gradient necessary for ATP synthesis via oxidative phosphorylation. Disruption of ΔΨm reflects impaired proton motive force and is indicative of compromised mitochondrial integrity [52]. In addition to membrane depolarization, diabetes induces alterations in mitochondrial mass and biogenesis, which may vary depending on cellular context and disease duration, reflecting a maladaptive response to energy imbalance [53]. High glucose exposure also inflicts damage to mitochondrial DNA, leading to deletions, mutations, and reduced transcription of essential respiratory chain proteins [52, 54]. The cumulative effect of these mitochondrial alterations is a decline in ATP production, coupled with activation of inflammatory cascades and cellular stress responses. Together, these changes create a bioenergetic crisis that exacerbates neuronal dysfunction and accelerates the onset of diabetic complications such as cognitive impairment [55]. CUR and IPA individually are known for their mitochondrial protection [56–58]. In particular, IPA acting via receptor-mediated pathways enhanced mitochondrial respiratory efficiency, leading to elevated ATP production and stabilization of membrane potential. This improved bioenergetic profile is accompanied by a marked reduction in ROS generation, thereby preserving redox balance and protecting mitochondrial integrity. By mitigating oxidative stress and maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, IPA interferes with key pathogenic mechanisms implicated in AD progression, positioning it as a promising candidate for neurodegenerative disorder intervention [59, 60]. Similar to these reports, CUR-IPA has shown protection of MMP, thereby thwarting the apoptosis of neuronal cells under HG conditions in the present study. Taken together, in alignment with the known neuroprotective roles of CUR and IPA individually, the CUR-IPA ester likewise demonstrated significant protection against HG-induced neuronal stress in this study.

To further understand the molecular mechanism alterations that occur in the presence of CUR-IPA under HG conditions, the levels of various proteins involved in the apoptosis axis and PI3K/Akt pathways are studied. In addition, the level of p53 was measured. The tumor suppressor protein p53 plays a pivotal role in modulating cellular responses to diverse stressors, including genotoxic damage, hypoxia, metabolic dysregulation, and neurodegenerative diseases [61–63]. Within the context of neuronal health, p53 extends its influence to mitochondrial dynamics, coordinating processes such as fission and fusion that are essential for maintaining mitochondrial integrity and function [64]. Under HG exposure, due to heightened oxidative stress in neurons, the redox imbalance acts as a potent activator of p53, initiating transcription of pro-apoptotic mediators like PUMA and Bax, ultimately driving the cell toward programmed death [9, 65, 66]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia-induced metabolic disturbances impair ATP synthesis and disrupt cellular energetics, adding another layer of stress that potentiates p53 signaling [67]. In these contexts, the increased level of p53 under HG conditions in the present study would have pushed the neuronal cells towards apoptosis, which was reflected as decreased anti-apoptotic (Bcl2) and increased pro-apoptotic (Bax and active-caspase-3) proteins. However, CUR-IPA has suppressed such activation of p53 and further initiation of the apoptosis signal, thereby resulting in preservation of cell viability under HG conditions. Such an effect of CUR-IPA was further strengthened by its ability to preserve the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway by maintaining the level of p-mTOR and p-Akt equivalent to that of the control level under HG exposure. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway functions as a pivotal signaling mechanism that sustains cellular proliferation, survival, and metabolic activity. Activation of this cascade results in the phosphorylation of Akt, which subsequently activates mTOR, coordinating essential processes such as protein synthesis, cell cycle progression, and autophagy regulation [68]. Beyond their roles in growth signaling, Akt and mTOR act as inhibitors of apoptosis by modulating intracellular factors that suppress cell death, including the prevention of caspase activation [69]. When the phosphorylated forms of Akt and mTOR are diminished, this survival advantage is compromised, leading to the reactivation of apoptotic pathways. In addition, in type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance impairs the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling cascade, a critical pathway for glucose metabolism and cellular survival, contributing to secondary complications commonly observed under hyperglycemic conditions. Several naturally occurring bioactive compounds have demonstrated the capacity to restore or enhance this pathway, thereby offering cytoprotective benefits [70]. CUR, a polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa, has been widely studied for its antidiabetic potential. It has shown efficacy in reducing hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hepatic damage, as evidenced by studies in diet-induced obese mice and T2DM rat models. In particular, CUR and it’s metabolites efficacy in reducing hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia effects are largely attributed to the ability to restore PI3K and Akt levels, improve liver and pancreatic architecture, and enhance insulin sensitivity [71–73]. Furthermore, CUR has been reported to protect pancreatic β-cells against lipotoxicity by modulating both the PI3K/Akt/FoxO1 axis and mitochondrial survival pathways [74]. In accordance with these reports, the CUR derivative, CUR-IPA, was also able to reinstate p-mTOR and p-Akt levels under HG conditions, thereby promoting cell survival under these conditions.

Having established that CUR-IPA confers protection to neuronal cells against HG-induced apoptosis, the investigation was extended to examine key components of the BDNF signaling cascade, specifically BDNF, p-TrkB, and CREB. This pathway was selected due to its integral role in promoting neuronal survival and maintaining synaptic plasticity, both of which are essential for cognitive processes such as learning and memory retention [75]. Dysregulation of BDNF signaling has been implicated in the pathophysiology of several major neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Huntington’s disease, AD, and depression [75, 76]. Similarly, under diabetic conditions, a low level of BDNF is reported to be associated with cognitive decline [77]. The reduction in BDNF activity is often associated with neuronal atrophy, synaptic loss, and cognitive decline observed in these disorders [75–77]. Therefore, the ability of CUR-IPA to preserve the integrity of the BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling axis not only supports its role in protecting neuronal viability but also suggests its broader therapeutic relevance in enhancing cognitive function under hyperglycemic stress. Despite these promising findings, comprehensive in vivo investigations encompassing behavioral assessments, memory retention analysis, and histological evaluation of neural tissue are essential to substantiate and delineate the full neuroprotective and cognitive benefits conferred by CUR-IPA.

Conclusion

The present study systematically evaluated the neuroprotective efficacy of CUR-IPA in an in vitro model of HG-induced oxidative stress using SH-SY5Y neuronal cells. HG exposure induced pronounced cytotoxicity, elevated ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, nuclear fragmentation, and activation of pro-apoptotic signaling pathways. These pathological changes were accompanied by significant suppression of endogenous antioxidant defenses, PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling and neurotrophic signaling components, including BDNF, p-TrkB and CREB. Notably, CUR-IPA demonstrated a robust protective profile across multiple cellular endpoints, attenuating oxidative damage, stabilizing MMP, and restoring redox balance. Further findings indicate that CUR-IPA protects SH-SY5Y cells against HG-triggered apoptosis by downregulating intrinsic apoptotic signals and reactivating PI3K/Akt/mTOR-mediated survival pathways. Moreover, CUR-IPA modulated neurotrophic signaling pathways, suggesting a multifaceted mechanism through which it counters HG-induced neuronal injury. These findings position CUR-IPA as a promising candidate for mitigating diabetes-related neurodegeneration.
