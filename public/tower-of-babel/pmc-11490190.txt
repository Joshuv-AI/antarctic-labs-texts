Prevalence of schizophrenia spectrum disorders in the Lazio region, Italy: use of an algorithm based on health administrative databases
Authors: Anna Maria Bargagli, Silvia Cascini, Anna Forastiere, Enrico Calandrini, Giulia Cesaroni, Claudia Marino, Marina Davoli, Nera Agabiti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental healthcare provision is undergoing substantial reconfiguration in many regions of the world. Such changes require a broad, evidence-based approach incorporating epidemiological data and information on local needs. The objective of this study was to estimate the prevalence of schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) in the Lazio region and its geographical distribution using regional administrative healthcare databases.MethodsCases of SSD (15–64 years old) were identified using an algorithm based on data from the hospital discharge registry (ICD IX CM: 295, 297, 298 [excl. 298.0], 299) and the ticket exemption database [code 044], between 2006 and 2019. We calculated crude, age- and gender-specific prevalence estimates on December 31, 2019. We also calculated age- and gender-adjusted prevalence to compare prevalence in different regional areas.ResultsWe identified 18,371 cases. In general, the prevalence was higher in men for all the conditions but delusional disorders and brief psychosis.ConclusionsOur results show that the overall prevalence of SSD among adults in the Lazio region is similar to those published in previous reviews, but an uneven regional distribution was observed. Background

Mental healthcare provision is undergoing substantial reconfiguration in many regions of the world. For example, early intervention in mental healthcare services to provide multidisciplinary care for people with emerging psychosis is needed as undertreatment is associated with poorer health and social outcomes [1, 2]. Such changes need a broad evidence-based approach incorporating epidemiological data and information on local needs in different populations. Such a population-based approach is vital to effective public mental health.

Schizophrenia and other chronic psychotic illnesses, such as schizoaffective disorder, are severe and disabling mental illnesses [3]. Schizophrenia is a complex mental disorder with a typical onset in late adolescence or early adulthood. It is a chronic and severe condition characterised by the persistence of symptoms of altered cognitive and perceptual functions, behaviour, and affectivity, a strong maladaptation of the person or severity that limits or compromises normal life activities [4]. People with schizophrenia are 2–3 times more likely to die early than the general population [5, 6], and this is often due to preventable physical diseases like cardiovascular diseases, metabolic diseases, and infections.

Schizophrenia has a relatively low prevalence. The global age-standardised point prevalence in 2016 was estimated to be 0.28% (95% CI: 0.24–0.31), with no gender differences observed [7]. A recent systematic review [8], evaluated 101 prevalence estimates of schizophrenia and related disorders and estimated a pooled median point and 12-month prevalence for persons of 3.89 and 4.03 per 1000, respectively, while the median lifetime prevalence was 7.49 per 1000. Moreover, the review reported that investigations performed in the general population presented a higher prevalence than those conducted in people who attended health/social services.

In the current classification of the DSM-5 [9], schizophrenia (delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thought/speech, altered motor behaviour -including catatonia- and negative symptoms) belongs to the section “Spectrum of Schizophrenia and other Psychotic Disorders” (SSD) together with schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, schizotypal personality disorder, schizophreniform disorder, brief psychotic disorder, as well as psychosis associated with substance use or medical conditions. A cohort study in Spain [10] has recently addressed the epidemiology of schizophrenia spectrum disorders. The overall prevalence for SSD was 6.2 per 1000 persons, and SSD was 76% more prevalent in men than women. This study also indicates that the prevalence of SSD is higher than that of schizophrenia alone and shows a different pattern among men and women. The findings agree with the suggestion from Moreno-Küstner [8], who found that, compared to the diagnosis of schizophrenia only, prevalence was higher in the probable psychotic disorder and non-affective psychosis. In Italy, occurrence measures of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders are scarce and mainly focused on incidence estimates [11, 12]. Two recent studies evaluated the continuity of care and the quality of mental health care delivered to patients with schizophrenia and related disorders in four Italian regions [13, 14]. In these studies, prevalence estimates ranged from 0.23% (Lazio region) to 0.55 (Lombardy region), and the overall prevalence was 0.4%.

We designed the present study to provide information on the prevalence and distribution of schizophrenia spectrum disorders in the Lazio region. These data are essential for disease burden estimation, epidemiological surveillance, and planning health and social services, which are rather heterogeneous in quantity and structure within the region. The specific objectives were to identify individuals aged 15–64 years affected by schizophrenia and other chronic psychotic illnesses using an algorithm based on data from regional administrative healthcare databases and to provide age- and gender-specific prevalence estimates on December 31, 2019.

Despite the availability of more recent classification systems, such as DSM-5 and ICD-11, data recording in Italy is still carried out using the ICD-9 system. The ICD system, used by the World Health Organization (WHO), remains the standard for health data collection, although ICD-9 is now outdated compared to international standards like ICD-11.

Methods

Study area

Lazio is a highly populated region in central Italy. The region includes the metropolitan area of Rome with a high degree of urbanization but the majority of municipalities are located in rural areas. The Lazio region is divided into 10 Local Health Units (LHUs). LHUs are public bodies operating within the National Health Service and responsible for managing preventive and health care services at the local level. On December 31, 2019, the resident population was 5,755,700 inhabitants (2,779,181 males and 2,976,519 females, https://www.istat.it/en/).

Study design and sources of data

This population-based study is based on data extracted from the fully anonymized administrative healthcare databases of the Lazio region. To detect the presence of schizophrenia and other chronic psychotic illnesses in the population, we applied an algorithm using data from the Hospital Discharge Registry (HDR) and the Ticket Exemption Registry (TER). The Hospital Discharge Registry includes data from all public regional hospitals. It contains information on patients’ characteristics, admission and discharge dates, hospital wards, discharge diagnoses (up to 6), procedure codes (up to 6) according to the International Classification of Disease, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), and costs (based on DRG). Data are available since 1996. The Ticket Exemption Registry includes data on all residents who are entitled to a co-pay fee exemption for some special conditions, e.g., disability, chronic diseases, low income, or old age. In Italy, as for fee exemption for chronic disease, very stringent rules are in effect within the National Health System, entailing the patient’s obligation to provide exhaustive clinical documentation, including a definite diagnosis provided by a specialized physician.

All residents registered with the regional health service have a personal identification code recorded in all the regional healthcare databases. This anonymous identifier allows the identification of individuals uniquely within the health system and represents the key to link different databases.

Case ascertainment and study population

To identify prevalent cases on December 31, 2019, we used administrative databases from January 1, 2006, to December 31, 2019. From the HDR, we selected all individuals who were hospitalised at least once in the period 2006–2019 with a primary or a secondary diagnosis of schizophrenia spectrum disorders (ICD9-CM codes 295, 297, 298 [excl. 298.0], 299) (see Supplemental Material: Table 1, for more details on ICD-9-CM codes). From the TER, we selected all patients registered with the specific code for schizophrenia [code 044]. We selected each patient at the date of the first claim recorded in the healthcare databases. Then, we verified the vital status on December 31, 2019, and checked that each individual was covered by the regional health service at the same date. In the analysis, we included only patients aged 15–64 residing in the region on December 31, 2019 (prevalence date).

Statistical analysis

Crude, age- and gender-specific prevalence estimates on December 31, 2019, were calculated using official population denominators provided by the Italian National Statistics Institute (https://www.istat.it/en/). Comparisons of prevalence between different Local Health Units within the region were adjusted for age and gender using direct standardization. Data were analysed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

The Department of Epidemiology of the Regional Health Service is the regional reference center for epidemiological research. This study is part of the institutional activity entrusted by the Regional Authority to the Department of Epidemiology for monitoring and evaluating the health status of the resident population. The Data Protection Officer of the Local Health Unit Roma 1 (Lazio region) licensed the study protocol. Because of the pseudonymized and administrative nature of the data, the Officer waived the requirement for informed patient consent.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the process of selecting patients from regional healthcare databases.

Flow chart of SSD cases selection from regional administrative healthcare databases

A total of 44,011 records initially corresponded to 26,756 patients alive on December 31, 2019. After controlling the residence status and coverage from the regional health service, 23,612 patients remained. A total of 18,371 cases were identified within the range of 15–64 years (57.0% men and 43.0% women); 13,603 (74.1%) patients were present exclusively in the HDR and 2077 (11.3%) were found in the TER only. Finally, about 15% of cases was registered in both the databases.

The overall age- and gender-adjusted point prevalence was 5.03 per 1000 population (95% CI 4.96–5.10) (Table 1). Age-adjusted prevalence estimates were 4.18 (95% CI 4.09–4.27) per 1000 women and 5.92 (95% CI 5.81–6.04) per 1000 men. We found an increasing trend in prevalence by age in both genders, (overall, from 2.10 per 1000 in the age 15–24 years to 6.31 per 1000 in the 45–54 years age class), with a reduction only among men in the older age group.

We observed differences in SSD prevalence within the region, with estimates ranging from 4.25/1000 (95% CI 3.97–4.55) in the LHU of Viterbo to 5.45/1000 (95% CI 5.29–5.62) in the city of Rome (LHU Roma 2), and 6.02/1000 (95% CI 5.74–6.30) in the area of Frosinone (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This large population-based study provides prevalence estimates of schizophrenia spectrum disorders in a region of central Italy of about 5.7 million inhabitants (including the city of Rome). The study proposes a reproducible methodology based on health administrative data to identify SSD cases, possibly applicable in other contexts. At the end of 2019, the overall point prevalence was 5.03 per 1000 persons, with standardised prevalence for men being approximately 1.4 times higher than for women. The overall trend by age appeared to be driven by the prevalence trend among men: the prevalence of SSD increased from age 15 to 54 and then decreased in the age group 55–64. In the age above 54 years, SSD prevalence is still increasing for women but not for men. Although it is still controversial whether there are gender differences in mortality, this finding could be explained, at least in part, by the lower average life expectancy for men with SSD/schizophrenia compared to women, which was estimated to be 59.9 years vs. 67.6 years, respectively [15, 16]. Our study also showed geographical variations in the distribution of the disease within the region.

We estimated a higher prevalence of SSD compared to the studies in the Lazio region in 2015–2016 [13, 14]. The observed differences are consistent with the documented increase in the incidence of SSD [17]. However, differences in patient identification criteria and the analysis methods between these studies might explain the heterogeneity observed in the results.

The estimate found in this study was within the range of median point prevalence reported in previous systematic reviews (from 3.2 to 8.3 per 1000) [18–20] and entirely congruent with the published estimates of median life prevalence (4.0-4.8 per 1000) [18, 19]. However, our estimated prevalence was significantly higher than that reported by the Global Burden of Disease study in more recent years (2.8 per 1000) [7]. Prevalence tends to vary by study design, geographical area, time of assessment, definition of schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and case identification methods, making it difficult to explain any observed difference. However, since the burden of schizophrenia is increasing globally [7], epidemiological studies providing up-to-date information on the local prevalence of the disease, even at the small-area level, are a valuable tool for healthcare planning and resource allocation [21].

Unlike some previous studies and reviews that found no gender differences in the prevalence of schizophrenia [19, 22, 23], we observed a 42% higher estimate in males compared with females. Indeed, there is evidence of a higher incidence of the illness among men, while it is controversial whether there are gender differences in mortality [8, 15, 24]. We observed a higher prevalence among men in all age groups. The males-to-females ratio decreased progressively with age, starting from 35 years. These results align with previous evidence showing differences between genders in the age of onset for schizophrenia, with men more likely to get their first symptoms earlier than women [25, 26]. Since women experienced a more benign course of illness and lower levels of disability [27, 28], we cannot exclude that the higher prevalence observed among men is related to their increased access to care services, which can affect the performance of the case identification algorithm.

We observed an uneven geographical distribution of SSD prevalence within the Lazio region that deserves further investigation, e.g., specific analytical studies to detect possible schizophrenia and related disorders risk factors. Other studies have shown geographical differences in the occurrence of SSD, both incidence and prevalence, mainly related to economic status, level of urbanicity, and social inequality [29–31]. Future research should test socio-economic and environmental factors in our region, considering the heterogeneity in access to healthcare services and the different availability of specialistic centres within the region, which may affect case identification performance.

In our study, the three most common SDD subtypes were schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorders, and psychosis NOS. Most previous research has focused on schizophrenia or groups of conditions broadly defined as non-affective or affective psychosis [8]. Large-scale epidemiological studies on the occurrence of schizophrenia spectrum disorders subtypes are sparse, and prevalence estimates vary widely due to study design, type of prevalence measure, and criteria for case identification [8]. A recent publication estimated, the lifetime prevalence of schizoaffective disorder at 3 per 1000 people, with higher estimates among men [32]. We found prevalence estimates lower than those previously reported; however, we consistently observed a higher prevalence of schizoaffective disorders among men compared to women.

This study has both strengths and limitations. The methodological approach based on population-based administrative data presents several advantages: it is time efficient, quickly provides updated prevalence estimates, and prevents problems associated with the small sample size. In addition, many epidemiological studies conducted in the Lazio region have demonstrated the quality and completeness of the regional administrative databases and their important contribution to epidemiological research [33, 34]. The major limitation is the probable underestimation of SSD prevalence. Although combining multiple data sources (e.g., hospital discharge and ticket exemption) produces more reliable estimates, only cases diagnosed and accessing both inpatients and outpatients healthcare services could be registered in the regional administrative databases and captured by the algorithm. The availability of private healthcare could produce a selection bias, underestimating the prevalence in well-off population groups. However, the data suggest that this is not the case; the standardized prevalence across LHUs is higher in Roma 1, the wealthiest LHU in Rome, compared to some deprived provinces. Secondly, using administrative data instead of clinical information to identify the population affected by SSD limits its use for more analytical purposes. Finally, we did not consider the possible diagnostic transition for psychiatric disorders [35], which can be evaluated using a longitudinal approach. However, a recent population-based cohort study and a meta-analysis of 42 clinical studies, found a high diagnostic stability among individuals with a schizophrenia spectrum disorder (about 90%) [36, 37].

In conclusion, our results showed that the overall prevalence of SSD among adults in the Lazio region is similar to estimates published in prior studies and reviews. However, we observed gender differences and an uneven regional geographical distribution. While possible underestimation could occur, health administrative databases represent valuable information for epidemiological surveillance, healthcare planning, and resource allocation. Furthermore, comparing prevalence estimates across different geographical areas can help to understand the association of possible environmental factors with SSD occurrence.
