Dynatune: Dynamic Tuning of Raft Election Parameters Using Network Measurement Thanks: This work was supported by JSPS KAKENHI Grant Number JP22K11971.
Kohya Shiozaki, Junya Nakamura

Abstract
Raft is a leader-based consensus algorithm that implements State Machine Replication (SMR), which replicates the service state across multiple servers to enhance fault tolerance. In Raft, the servers play one of three roles: leader, follower, or candidate. The leader receives client requests, determines the processing order, and replicates them to the followers. When the leader fails, the service must elect a new leader to continue processing requests, during which the service experiences an out-of-service (OTS) time. The OTS time is directly influenced by election parameters, such as heartbeat interval and election timeout. However, traditional approaches, such as Raft, often struggle to effectively tune these parameters, particularly under fluctuating network conditions, leading to increased OTS time and reduced service responsiveness. To address this, we propose Dynatune, a mechanism that dynamically adjusts Raft’s election parameters based on network metrics such as round-trip time and packet loss rates measured via heartbeats. By adapting to changing network environments, Dynatune significantly reduces the leader failure detection and OTS time without altering Raft’s core mechanisms or introducing additional communication overheads. Experimental results demonstrate that Dynatune reduces the leader failure detection and OTS times by 80% and 45%, respectively, compared with Raft, while maintaining high availability even under dynamic network conditions. These findings confirm that Dynatune effectively enhances the performance and reliability of SMR services in various network scenarios.

Raft

SMR is a technique that replicates the service state across multiple servers to enhance fault tolerance.
Each server uses a consensus algorithm to agree on the order of client requests and processes these requests in the same order.
This ensures that each server maintains a consistent service state, allowing the service to continue even if some servers fail because the remaining servers can continue the service.

Raft servers play one of the three roles: leader, follower, or candidate.
One server acts as the leader, whereas the others act as followers.
The leader receives the client requests, determines the processing order, and sends them to the followers.
The followers execute requests according to the order determined by the leader.

The period from leader failure to the election of a new leader is called the

Therefore, reducing the OTS time is crucial for improving the responsiveness of SMR services.

II
Background

II-A

Raft Basics and Leader Election Mechanism

Raft
[
1
]
is a consensus algorithm that implements SMR.
This algorithm is resilient to up to
f

non-Byzantine failures
using
n=2f+1
servers.
Each server in Raft plays one of the three roles: leader, follower, or candidate.
In a normal case, one server acts as the leader, whereas the others act as followers.
When the leader receives a client request, it records the request as a log entry and sends a log entry addition message to followers.
Followers respond passively to messages from the leader.
Upon receiving a message, they add a log entry if there is no issue and respond to the leader.
Once the leader confirms that the majority of servers have added the log entry to their logs, it commits the request and responds to the client.
Each server maintains a consistent service state by executing requests according to log entries, allowing the service to continue operation even if some followers fail.

However, Raft cannot process new requests if the leader fails.
To address this, Raft uses heartbeats and leader elections to select a new leader, as shown in Figure
2
.
The leader repeatedly sends heartbeats to all other servers at intervals called
Heartbeat Interval

h
.
Followers have an election timer of the length
Election Timeout

E_{t}
and reset it when receiving a heartbeat from the leader.
If the leader stops sending heartbeats owing to failure, the election timer expires, and the follower detects the leader’s failure.
In this case, the follower transitions to a candidate state and immediately starts the election for the new leader.
In recent Raft implementations, a pre-vote phase was introduced immediately before the election.
During this phase, the candidate conducts a preliminary vote, and if the candidate secures permission from the majority of the servers, the leader election is initiated.
If the candidate receives votes from the majority of the servers, it becomes the new leader and resumes processing requests.

If multiple candidates start the election simultaneously, none of them may receive a majority of the votes because each server can vote only once during an election.
In this situation, known as a split vote, candidates must wait for their election timers to expire again before starting a new election.
This repetition of elections continues until a leader is elected, and increases the OTS time.
To decrease the frequency of the split vote, each server randomizes its timer value from the starting point of
E_{t}
each time a timeout occurs.
We refer to the actual timer value after randomization as
\mathit{randomizedTimeout}
.

II-B

Election Parameters and Network Conditions

The election parameters are critical to the availability and responsiveness of the service.
However, the methodology for effectively configuring these parameters is not well understood.
According to the original Raft study
[
1
]
, election timeout
E_{t}
should be set to satisfy the following inequality:
\mathit{broadcastTime}\ll E_{t}\ll\mathit{MTBF}
.
Here,
\mathit{broadcastTime}
is the average time required for a server to send RPCs in parallel to all other servers in the cluster and receive their responses.
This value is equal to the maximum RTT between servers.

\mathit{MTBF}
refers to the mean time between the failures of a single server.
The heartbeat interval
h
was set to be significantly smaller than
E_{t}
.
This ensures that the leader’s heartbeats reach the followers within the time limit.

The inappropriate election parameter settings may lead to significant issues.
The RTT between servers, particularly between the leader and the followers, affects
E_{t}
.
If
E_{t}
is shorter than the RTT, the follower’s timer expires before receiving a heartbeat.
This leads to a situation in which followers repeat elections continuously, and requests remain unprocessed, resulting in a loss of availability.
Conversely, if
E_{t}
is too long, the leader’s failure detection is delayed, increasing the OTS time.

Regarding the relationship between
h
and network conditions, Huang et al.
[
9
]
recommended increasing the number of heartbeats sent before the timer expires
K

(=E_{t}/h)
by shortening
h
.
However, frequent heartbeats strain the leader’s computational resources and network bandwidth, thus reducing request-processing capability.

Therefore, the election parameters in Raft must be carefully configured according to the network conditions, such as the RTT and packet loss rate, as they significantly impact both availability and responsiveness.

II-C

Network Fluctuations and Problems in WAN Environments

SMR services are deployed not only in LAN environments, but also across continents around the world.
This type of SMR is known as the geo-replicated SMR
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
10
]
.
Geo-replicated SMR involves the deployment of SMR servers across various geographical locations, as illustrated in Figure
2
.
This arrangement enables continued service even during major disasters.

In geo-replicated SMR, as the distance between servers increases, the RTT and packet loss rates tend to increase.
In such WAN environments, the RTT and packet loss rates fluctuate over long and short terms.
For example, traffic may surge owing to time-of-day variations or global events, leading to network congestion.
Additionally, routing changes caused by submarine cable failures or temporary degradation of communication quality due to maintenance or outages in public cloud services such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Google Cloud Platform (GCP) may also occur.

II-C
1
RTT Fluctuation

In WAN environments, the RTT is known to vary significantly over both long and short periods owing to changes in routing and network congestion
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
Høiland-Jørgensen et al.
[
16
]
reported that more than two-thirds of sample communication paths showed RTT variations over 100 ms.
Furthermore, network load increases may drive queuing delays to exceed 200 ms, demonstrating that RTT variability is substantial and demands attention.

In such environments, even if the election timeout
E_{t}
is set to match the maximum RTT during normal conditions to detect failures quickly, increases in the RTT may cause it to exceed
E_{t}
.
Consequently, each server repeatedly initiates elections, making the service unavailable until the RTT falls below
E_{t}
.
Conversely, setting
E_{t}
too high increases the time required to detect leader failures, resulting in an increased OTS time.
These reasons highlight the limitations of the static parameter settings and the need for dynamic adjustments.

II-C
2
Packet Loss Rate Fluctuation

Similar to the RTT, packet loss rates fluctuate in WAN environments.
Haq et al.
[
18
]
reported that inter-cloud data center communications via dedicated circuits typically have lower packet loss rates, ranging from 0.001% to 0.1%, whereas communications via public Internet infrastructure experience higher packet loss rates, ranging from 0.001% to 50%.
However, Mok et al.
[
19
]
observed that in some regions, even inter-cloud communications in GCP experience packet loss rates that increase from 3% to 50% during network congestion.

Typically, the heartbeat interval
h
is configured considering packet loss rates to ensure that a sufficient number of heartbeats,
K
, are received.
However, when packet loss rates fluctuate, they may exceed initial expectations, leading to a situation in which the leader’s heartbeats do not reach the followers in time.
This may cause the followers’ election timers to expire, triggering unnecessary elections.
Conversely, if
h
is set to be extremely small to accommodate potential spikes in packet loss, it may strain the leader’s computational resources and bandwidth, thereby reducing its request-processing capability.
For these reasons, static settings have limitations, and dynamic parameter adjustments are required.

III
Dynatune

In this section, we introduce Dynatune, which dynamically tunes Raft’s election parameters based on network measurements to safely and effectively reduce the OTS time.
This section is organized as follows.
First, we outline the assumptions in Section
III-A

Next, we provide an overview in Section
III-B

In Section
III-C

In Section
III-D

Finally, in Section

III-A

Assumptions

Dynatune operates under the following assumptions equivalent to those of Raft
[
1
]
because it does not modify Raft’s fundamental mechanisms, particularly the consensus on the request execution order and leader election.
Dynatune can tolerate up to
f

non-Byzantine failures
among
n=2f+1
servers.
Non-Byzantine failures include failure models such as crashes and crash-recovery, where a server may stop and restart without exhibiting malicious behavior.
We assume a
partially synchronous model
, where the system alternates between synchronous and asynchronous periods.
During synchronous periods, both message delays and the relative speeds of processes (e.g., clock drift) are bounded and known.
In contrast, during asynchronous periods, there are no such bounds on message delays or the relative speeds of processes, and message loss or reordering may also occur.

III-B

Overview

Dynatune was developed based on Raft’s design
[
1
]
.
Dynatune’s approach to reducing the OTS time involves decreasing election timeout
E_{t}
within a safe range, allowing for the quick detection of leader failures.
Even when network fluctuations occur, Dynatune adapts and maintains well-configured election parameters through real-time network measurements.

To maintain Raft’s simplicity and well-defined characteristics, Dynatune does not modify the basic mechanisms for request ordering and elections.
Instead, it embeds all necessary communications within heartbeats to measure network metrics and tune parameters.

In Raft, all the servers use the same election parameters.
However, in Dynatune, to maximize the effect of tuning, election parameters are tuned individually for each leader-follower communication path based on the network conditions of that path.
By conducting measurements and tuning processes for each leader-follower communication path, Dynatune faces an increased load as the number of servers increases, which may have a non-negligible impact on the request-processing capability.
Thus, Dynatune reduces overhead by offloading data storage and calculations to followers.

Dynatune tunes the election parameters as follows:

Step 0:

The leader sends heartbeats with additional metadata for network metrics measurement to followers. Each follower records received metadata repeatedly until it stores sufficient data.

Step 1:

Each follower analyzes the recorded metadata to determine the RTT and packet loss rate between itself and the leader.

Step 2:

Each follower determines
E_{t}
based on the RTT and then calculates
h
from the packet loss rate.

Step 3:

Each follower applies the tuned
E_{t}
to their election timers and sends the leader the tuned
h
piggybacked in the heartbeat response.
The leader applies the received
h
as the heartbeat sending interval to the corresponding follower.

By repeating Steps 1–3, Dynatune adapts to fluctuating network conditions.
When a new leader election is initiated because of a leader failure or sudden RTT increase caused by a network spike, each follower discards the collected RTT and packet loss data, and the process returns to Step 0 with a newly elected leader.
At the beginning of Step 0, the election parameters were set to their default values.
The default values are large and conservative, which ensures that even if the tuning fails and temporarily results in
E_{t}<\text{RTT}
, falling back to these default values maintains the availability.

III-C

Network Metrics Measurement

This section describes the method for measuring the RTT and packet loss rates using heartbeats.
Each follower maintains two lists: one for storing RTT values, referred to as
RTTs
, and the other for storing heartbeat IDs, referred to as
ids
.
Upon receiving these heartbeats, the follower adds the RTT to
RTTs
and the corresponding ID to
ids
, thereby collecting the data necessary to measure the RTT and packet loss rates.

III-C
1
RTT

We describe a method for measuring the RTT using heartbeats.
This measurement is performed by the leader attaching a timestamp to the heartbeat when sending it to the follower, and calculating the RTT upon receiving the follower’s response.
The specific measurement method is shown in Figure
3(a)
.
The leader records the sending timestamp in the heartbeat message and sends it to the follower.
Upon receiving the heartbeat, the follower immediately replies and the leader calculates the RTT by taking the difference between the sending and receiving timestamps.
The measured RTT is transmitted to the follower with the next heartbeat.
The follower stores the RTT in the
RTTs
list and updates the election timeout
E_{t}
using the method discussed later in Section
III-D1
.

This measurement method uses only the leader’s local clock, allowing for accurate RTT measurements even in partially synchronous networks.
When packet loss occurs, the leader does not need to manage the sending time of the heartbeat, thereby eliminating the need for loss detection and variable release.
In addition, in the case of packet reordering, the leader can accurately calculate the RTT by referencing the sending timestamp recorded in the received heartbeat, ensuring that the sending times are not confused.

III-C
2
Packet Loss Rate

The leader assigns a sequential ID to each heartbeat to measure the packet loss rate.
This ID allows followers to detect missing heartbeats and measure the packet loss rate, as illustrated in Figure
3(b)
.
Each follower has a list
ids
of the received heartbeat ID, and appends an ID to the end of the list when it receives a heartbeat.
The follower then calculates the packet loss rate
p
with
ids
by dividing the number of heartbeats received (
\mathit{length}(ids)
) by the expected number of heartbeats (
ids[-1]-ids[0]+1
).

The packet arrival order is not guaranteed in partially synchronous networks, and packets may be duplicated.
To address this, the follower inserts the IDs into the list in ascending order and ignores subsequent receptions when duplicate messages.

III-D

Tuning of Election Parameters

This section explains the method for tuning the election parameters based on the measured RTT and packet loss rate in Section
III-C
.
The election parameters include the follower’s election timeout
E_{t}
and the leader’s heartbeat interval
h
.
First,
E_{t}
is determined based on the RTT, and then
h
is adjusted according to the packet loss rate.
As discussed in Section
II-B
and
II-C
, choosing appropriate
E_{t}
and
h
is important for the availability and responsiveness of the SMR service.
The specific tuning methods are described below.

III-D
1
Election Timeout

E_{t}
needs to be larger than the RTT as described in Section
II-B
, while remaining as small as possible to shorten detection time.
Ideally, setting
E_{t}=RTT
would minimize the leader failure detection time.
However, due to fluctuations in real-world networks, these variations must be taken into account.
Thus, we set
E_{t}
based on the statistical characteristics of the RTT between the leader and follower, considering the trade-off between reducing detection time and minimizing the risk of false detection.
Specifically, we calculate
E_{t}=\mu_{\mathit{RTT}}+s\sigma_{\mathit{RTT}}
,
where
\mu_{\mathit{RTT}}
is the average RTT between the leader and follower, and
\sigma_{\mathit{RTT}}
is its standard deviation.
These values were calculated using the
RTTs
list described in Section
III-C1
.
The coefficient
s
, which is a safety factor, was set in advance by the practitioner.
This coefficient covers a wider range of the RTT distribution and specifies the tolerance for false detections caused by fluctuations in network delay.
When a new RTT was measured,
E_{t}
was recalculated using the updated average RTT and its standard deviation.
This approach reduces the risk of false detections while reducing the detection time.

III-D
2
Heartbeat Interval

We determined
h
based on
E_{t}
and the packet loss rate between the leader and follower, considering the trade-off between resource consumption and the risk of false detection.
First, we calculated the number of heartbeats
K
required to ensure that the probability of a follower receiving at least one heartbeat was greater than or equal to
x
, considering the packet loss rate.
We then set
h
such that
K
heartbeats were sent at equal intervals within
E_{t}
.

Specifically, under packet loss rate
p
, the condition for the number of heartbeats
K
required to satisfy the arrival probability
x
is
1-p^{K}\geq x
.
Under this condition, we determined the required number of heartbeats
K=\left\lceil\log_{p}(1-x)\right\rceil
.
Finally, to send
K
heartbeats within a period
E_{t}
,
h
should be
h=E_{t}/K
.
This
h
is recalculated each time a heartbeat is received, and the updated value is piggybacked based on the response to the leader, which then applies.
This method ensures that the probability of at least one heartbeat reaching the follower before the election timer times out is at least
x
, minimizing the risk of unnecessary elections owing to false detections and reducing resource consumption.

III-E

Implementation

We implemented Dynatune based on the widely used key-value store, etcd
2
.
etcd is open-source software that employs the Raft consensus algorithm.
We forked etcd and integrated the proposed method discussed earlier.
The Dynatune source code is publicly available online
3
.

In the original etcd implementation, all Raft communications utilized TCP.
However, we adopted a hybrid approach in Dynatune using UDP for heartbeat communication and TCP for other consensus-related communications.
This approach ensures the accurate measurement of network metrics necessary to tune the parameters, while reducing communication costs.

In addition, we added runtime arguments to configure Dynatune’s parameters.
Specifically, these arguments allow us to set the standard deviation
\sigma
used to calculate
E_{t}
and the probability
x
of
h
.
Followers can also configure the list size for recording network metrics, specifying both minimum (
minListSize
) and maximum (
maxListSize
) sizes.
If the list size is below
minListSize
, Dynatune remains in
Step 0
, and the followers continue recording the metadata until sufficient data are stored.
Once the list size exceeds
minListSize
, Dynatune transitions to
Step 1
and begins tuning the election parameters.
When the list size exceeds
maxListSize
, Dynatune discards the oldest data to prevent the list from growing indefinitely.

IV-A

Experimental Settings

We conducted the experiments by reproducing a geographically distributed server environment on a single physical machine.
Specifically, we deployed multiple Docker containers, with each container operating as a server of a key-value store service cluster, and injected delays and packet losses into the server communications within each container using the
tc

We used the etcd implementation for the key-value store, referring to the default etcd as
Raft

III-E

Dynatune

In these experiments, we measured the time of the leader’s failure, the time when the failure was detected, and the time when a new leader was elected from each server’s log files in order to calculate the detection and OTS times.
Using multiple physical machines may cause time measurement inaccuracies owing to discrepancies in hardware clock synchronization between servers.
To avoid this issue, we conducted the experiments on a single machine, ensuring that all servers shared the same hardware clock.
This setup allows for the accurate measurement of the detection and OTS times from the log timestamps of multiple servers.

The machine primarily used for the experiments was equipped with an Intel Xeon Silver 4310 2.10GHz CPU, with each Docker container allocated four cores and 4GB of memory.
We use a 1TB NVMe SSD for storage without imposing any usage I/O limits on the containers.

We configured the election parameters for Raft with default values set
E_{t}
to 1000 ms and
h
to 100 ms.
Dynatune uses the same default election parameters as Raft, and after an election timer expires that triggers an election, it falls back to these default election parameters.
In addition, we configured Dynatune with four runtime arguments as follows:

\mathtt{minListSize}=10

\mathtt{maxListSize}=1000

IV-E

Discussion

In the experiment described in Section
IV-B1
, we demonstrated that Dynatune reduced the detection and OTS times by 80% and 45%, respectively.
However, when comparing election times, the period between failure detection and leader election in Raft completed the process in 244 ms (
1449-1205
), whereas Dynatune took 560 ms (
797-237
).
This increase in election time occurred because Dynatune sets a smaller
E_{t}
, which narrows the randomization range and leads to multiple servers starting elections simultaneously.
Consequently, the probability of a split vote increases, resulting in repeated election attempts.
Although our approach effectively shortens the OTS time by reducing the detection time and maintaining availability, combining it with other methods designed to shorten the election time
[
3
,
5
,
4
,
6
]
could resolve this issue and achieve even greater reductions in the OTS time.

The results in Section
IV-B2
show that Dynatune achieves a peak throughput 6.4% lower than that of Raft.
This reduction in peak throughput likely occurred because of the overhead introduced by the tuning processes.
However, the slight reduction in peak throughput can be mitigated by scaling up the machine resources.
Furthermore, Dynatune achieves a remarkable 80% reduction in detection time and a 45% reduction in OTS time, which benefits that only Dynatune can provide, making it a compelling trade-off.

Additionally, this trade-off can be addressed through slight modifications to Dynatune.
One approach is to stop sending heartbeats when the leader processes client requests.
When the leader receives a request from a client, it resets the timer used to manage heartbeat transmissions and sends replication requests to the followers.
When followers receive these replication requests, they replicate the commands and reset their election timers.
This eliminates heartbeats during periods of high request load, improving the peak throughput.
Another approach is to consolidate the leader’s heartbeat timers into a single timer.
In Raft, the leader uses a single timer to send heartbeats to all followers simultaneously.
In contrast, Dynatune requires
n-1
separate timers because each leader-follower pair has a unique heartbeat interval based on its
h
value.
By aligning the heartbeat intervals to the minimum
h
among all followers, the leader sends heartbeats to all followers simultaneously, which allows the use of a single timer.
This reduces the computational load on the leader and improves the peak throughput.
Incorporating these approaches into Dynatune is left as future work.

V
Related Work

In this study, the proposed method Dynatune was implemented based on Raft, while there were several other candidates such as Paxos
[
20
,
21
]
.
This decision came from Raft’s simplicity and Paxos’s complexity regarding communications and server roles.
Raft was designed to be simple by centralizing functions in the leader and restricting followers to respond only to the leader’s communications.
Raft’s simplicity has been widely regarded
[
22
]
and has been adopted by many services.

Raft and Paxos are leader-based algorithms.
A single server manages the consensus using these algorithms, resulting in a relatively simple design.
However, many leaderless algorithms have also been proposed
[
13
,
14
]
.
These do not have a single leader, so even if some servers fail, there is no need for leader election, and therefore, no OTS time occurs.
However, they are more complex than leader-based algorithms, making them difficult to implement and limiting their industrial usage.

Given this background, we focused on Raft to preserve its simple and well-defined characteristics without altering its fundamental mechanisms.
By using heartbeats for network measurement and tuning election parameters, we aim to effectively reduce the OTS time.

Various approaches have been proposed to reduce the OTS time of Raft.
Fluri et al.
[
3
]
investigated the impact of packet loss and network partitioning on Raft’s election time, and proposed a new timeout policy that extends the timeout duration each time a split vote occurs, thereby improving the leader election time.
For example, Tang et al.
[
5
]
divided servers into normal and prioritized servers, concentrating votes on the prioritized servers to reduce the occurrence of split votes and election time.
Fu et al.
[
4
]
and Zhang et al.
[
6
]
improved Raft’s voting mechanism by completing an election in a single round, thereby reducing election time.
These studies focused on reducing election time, whereas our research focused on reducing the detection time.

Research on Raft’s election parameters has been widely conducted.
For example, Iosif et al.
[
7
]
proposed a randomization method for election parameters to equalize the probability of each server becoming the leader.
Choumas et al.
[
8
]
optimized election parameters to increase the probability of electing a server as the leader, which minimized request latency during non-failure periods.
Huang et al.
[
9
]
proposed a model to estimate the probability of network partitioning under high packet loss rate environments and discussed the appropriate ratio between the election timeout and heartbeat interval.
Although these studies primarily focus on enhancing responsiveness during non-failure periods, our research aims to reduce OTS time in the event of leader failures.

In this study, we implemented Dynatune by applying the proposed method to dynamically tune election parameters to Raft, but the method can potentially be applied to other distributed consensus algorithms such as Paxos
[
20
,
21
]
and PBFT
[
23
]
.
These algorithms also use timeout mechanisms to detect leader failures, so dynamically adjusting timeout parameters could improve the efficiency of their leader election processes.
However, the proposed method currently lacks Byzantine fault tolerance, making its direct application to BFT algorithms like PBFT challenging.
In particular, there is a risk that Byzantine follower nodes might send fraudulent calculated results to deceive the leader.
Addressing this issue requires future work focused on the design of new algorithms with Byzantine fault tolerance.

VI
Conclusion

In leader-based SMR, reducing the OTS time due to leader failure has been recognized as a critical challenge for improving service responsiveness.
To address this issue, we propose Dynatune, which dynamically tunes Raft’s election parameters based on the network conditions.
Dynatune utilizes heartbeats to measure network metrics, such as RTT and packet loss rates, adjusting the election parameters accordingly.
This allows Dynatune to adapt to various network conditions, maintaining high availability while reducing the detection and OTS times.
In the experimental evaluation, Dynatune was compared with Raft under various fault scenarios and network conditions.
The results demonstrate that Dynatune effectively reduced the detection and OTS times by 80% and 45%, respectively.
Additionally, Dynatune proved to be highly adaptable to network fluctuations, maintaining high election performance and improving service availability.

In the future, we plan to develop methods that further reduce the OTS times by integrating Dynatune with existing approaches designed to shorten election time.
Additionally, we aim to apply methods to improve peak throughput and achieve performance comparable to that of Raft.
