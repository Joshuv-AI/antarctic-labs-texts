QCD chiral phase diagram from weak functional renormalization group
Yuepeng Guan, Masatoshi Yamada

Abstract
We investigate the QCD chiral phase transition at finite temperature and finite baryon density using the functional Renormalization Group (fRG). While conventional fRG studies often employ techniques such as dynamical bosonization to regularize divergences, we instead pursue the weak solution of the fRG equations which allows for non-analytic behavior in the flow to compute the pure fermionic potential V k ​ ( ψ , ψ ¯ ) V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi}) within the local potential approximation. This approach enables us to explore the effects of purely quark-level fluctuations on dynamical chiral symmetry breaking without introducing any auxiliary bosonic fields. Based on this framework, we present the resulting chiral phase diagram as a function of temperature and baryon chemical potential.



I
Introduction

Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), as the asymptotically free gauge theory of the strong interaction, has achieved remarkable success in explaining the confinement and collective dynamics of quarks and gluons.
Its predictive power underpins modern nuclear and particle physics, from the structure of hadrons to the formation of quark-gluon plasma (QGP) in relativistic heavy-ion collisions.

Progress in elucidating the QCD phase diagram has revealed a rich landscape of nontrivial phenomena. At high temperature and low baryon chemical potential, lattice QCD simulations and heavy-ion experiments confirm the crossover transition from hadronic matter to a deconfined QGP
Jacob and Tran
Thanh Van 1982
;
Kapusta et al. 2003
.
Conversely, in the large baryon chemical potential region,
it is expected that a critical endpoint exists and first-order transitions take place at large baryon chemical potential, while the phase structure remains still enigmatic because Monte-Carlo methods falter due to the sign problem.
Recent advances in functional methods, such as the Dyson-Schwinger equations (DSEs)
Welzbacher et al. 2015
;
Gao et al. 2024
;
Gao and Pawlowski 2020
;
Lu et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
and the functional Renormalization Group (fRG)
Cyrol et al. 2018a
;
Cyrol et al. 2018b
;
Fu et al. 2020
, have provided complementary insights into chiral symmetry restoration and color superconductivity.

In QCD, especially in the chiral limit, i.e. the vanishing current quark masses, the chiral phase transition is dominated by collective mesonic fluctuations.
Naive pure quark-based fRG frameworks struggle to resolve the chiral phase transition due to the divergence of the four-fermion interaction, which reflects the massless nature of the collective mode.
To address the symmetry-broken phase within the fRG framework, we need to employ techniques which appropriately capture the fluctuations of emergent degrees of freedom.
Dynamical bosonization
Gies and Wetterich 2002
;
Gies and Wetterich 2004
;
Pawlowski 2007
;
Floerchinger and Wetterich 2009
;
Braun et al. 2016
;
Mitter et al. 2015
;
Cyrol et al. 2018b
;
Cyrol et al. 2018a
;
Alkofer et al. 2019
;
Denz et al. 2020
;
Fu et al. 2020
;
Goertz et al. 2025
addresses the chiral phase transition by explicitly introducing bosonic fields (mesons) as emergent degrees of freedom, enabling a more natural description of symmetry-breaking phenomena.
Alternatively, recent works also encounter the full momentum dependence in the four-quark vertex function
Fu et al. 2024
;
Fu et al. 2025
, overcoming limitations of early bosonization schemes that relied on local approximations. These advances allow us to precisely evaluate mesonic spectra and critical fluctuations near the QCD critical endpoint.

Another way to access the symmetry-broken phase is through the “weak fRG” formulation
Aoki et al. 2014
;
Aoki and Sato 2014
;
Aoki et al. 2018
.
The strategy of the method is to utilize the “weak form” of the fRG equations, which relaxes continuity requirements, allowing solutions with nonanalytic behavior (e.g., discontinuities in derivatives of the effective potential), which is critical for capturing spontaneous symmetry breaking.
This approach allows us to explore chiral symmetry breaking solely through fermionic fluctuations.

In this paper, we solve the flow equation of the fermionic potential
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
at finite temperature and quark chemical potential through the search for the weak solution of the fRG equation.
In particular, we evaluate the dynamical mass and obtain the QCD chiral phase diagram on the
(\mu_{B},T)
plane.
To derive the flow equation for
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
, we employ the ladder approximation and its non-ladder extensions, considering both cases—with and without the anomalous dimension of the quark field—in the chiral limit as well as for finite quark masses.
The flow equation for
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
is then reformulated into a weak form of a differential equation.
For the running gauge coupling, we adopt input data obtained from an independent fRG analysis
Gao et al. 2021
.
The resulting phase diagram is shown in

.

\displaystyle S_{\rm bare}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\int_{x}\,\biggl[\frac{1}{4}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2\xi}(\partial_{\mu}A_{\mu}^{a})^{2}

\displaystyle\Phi=(A^{a}_{\mu},\psi^{T},\bar{\psi})^{T}.

(5)

The gauge field strength is given by

The fermionic field

II.1
Truncated effective action

In this work, we employ the modified local potential approximation (LPA’) scheme to truncate the effective action
\Gamma_{k}
in a solvable way.
Performing the derivative expansion to the effective average action and keeping the lowest order, we make an ansatz for the effective action

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]=\int_{x}\,\biggl[\frac{Z_{k}^{A}}{4}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{Z_{k}^{A}}{2\xi}\int_{x}(\partial_{\mu}A_{\mu}^{a})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+Z_{k}^{\psi}\bar{\psi}({\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$\partial$\crcr}}}+ig_{s}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr$A$\crcr}}}-\mu_{q}\gamma_{4})\psi-V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})\biggl].

Here
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
is the fermionic potential given as polynomials of gauge-invariant bilinear operators
\bar{\psi}\Gamma\psi
where
\Gamma
represents the spinor basis such as
{\bf 1}_{\text{spinor}}
,
\gamma_{\mu}
, etc, and
Z_{k}^{\psi}
and
Z_{k}^{A}
are the field renormalization factors for the quark fields and for the gluon field, respectively.
In
section

II.1
, we have neglected the gluonic (Polyakov loop) potential, which implies that we do not consider the confinement effect.
Also, we do not introduce the thermal splitting of the Lorentzian tensorial structures as an approximation, which means that we introduce the unified wave function renormalization factor for the temporal and spatial components of the kinetic terms for both gluon and fermion.

For
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, we adopt regulator functions for the three-dimensional momentum, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}^{\psi}({p})=Z_{k}^{\psi}i{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hfil/\hfil\crcr${p}$\crcr}}}}r_{k}^{\psi}(|\bm{p}|/k),

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}_{k}^{A}(p))_{\mu\nu}=Z_{k}^{A}p^{2}\Pi^{1/\xi}_{\mu\nu}\,r_{k}^{\psi}(|\bm{p}|/k),

(14)

with the sharp-cutoff regulator function in momentum space

\displaystyle r_{k}^{A}=r_{k}^{\psi}=\frac{1}{\theta(|\bm{p}|/k-1)}-1.

(15)

Here,
\Pi^{1/\xi}_{\mu\nu}
is the tensor structure of the two-point function for the gluon field at the tree level (see
eq.

67

eq.

14

For this choice, the flow equation
6
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\text{shell}}\operatorname{str}\log\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}.

(16)

Here and hereafter we introduce a shorthand notation for the momentum integral

\displaystyle\int_{p,\text{shell}}\equiv T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}.

(17)

Note here that the regulators

II.2
Flow of the fermionic potential and approximations

We discuss the derivation of the flow equation for the fermionic potential
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
.
In this subsection, we present the necessary components to clarify the approximation scheme employed in the present work.
The complete derivation of the flow equation for the fermionic potential is provided in
Appendix

B
.

At each order in the fermionic field expansion, the potential
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})
contains various non-trivial tensorial structures.
In the chiral limit and ignoring the
U(1)_{A}
anomaly, chiral symmetry is exact at the level of the action and can only be broken spontaneously (at the vacuum level), so the operators in the effective action must be chirally invariant.
For instance, the fermionic effective potential includes four-fermion interactions that are invariant under flavor transformations:

\displaystyle V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})\ni\left(\bar{\psi}\lambda^{I}\psi\right)^{2}+\left(\bar{\psi}\lambda^{I}i\gamma_{5}\psi\right)^{2}+\cdots,

(18)

where
\lambda^{I}
denotes the generators of the flavor symmetry group, with
\lambda^{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{f}}}\mathbf{1}_{\text{flavor}}
.

Here, we adopt a further approximation by projecting the flow onto the subspace that contains only flavor and Dirac singlet channels, i.e., the subspace involving only the scalar bilinear
\sigma=\bar{\psi}\psi
.
In this case, the fermionic effective potential reduces to
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})\equiv V(\sigma;t)
.
Consequently, the potential respects a discrete chiral symmetry under
\psi\to\psi
and
\bar{\psi}\to-\bar{\psi}
, rather than a continuous one.
Thus, Nambu–Goldstone modes (pseudoscalar fluctuations) are not included.
Neglecting contributions from other tensorial structures, such as
(\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi)^{2}
, may lead to the so-called Fierz ambiguity (see, e.g.,
Jaeckel and Wetterich 2003
;
Braun et al. 2017
;
Braun et al. 2018
;
Braun et al. 2020
).
Nevertheless, our aim in this paper is to investigate the impact of non-ladder effects on the phase diagram.
Therefore, we argue that this treatment introduces only quantitative differences in the final results and does not affect the qualitative physics under discussion.

In the chiral limit, the discrete chiral symmetry forbids odd-power terms of
\sigma
in the fermionic potential.
However, going beyond the chiral limit due to the presence of current quark masses, the chiral symmetry is explicitly broken, and the fermionic potential then contains odd powers of
\sigma
.
Thus, the reduced fermionic potential formally reads

\displaystyle V(\sigma;t)=\sum_{n=1}\frac{G^{(n)}_{k}}{n!}\sigma^{n}=\sum_{n=1}\frac{G^{(n)}_{k}}{n!}(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{n}.

(19)

Note here that
G_{k}^{(2)}
corresponds to the four-fermi coupling for
(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}
.

When evaluating the Hessian for the fermionic part, we retain only the leading term in the large-
N
expansion, neglecting the diagonal elements in the fermionic sector. In this approximation, the flow equation for the fermionic effective potential depends solely on its first derivative with respect to
\sigma
, i.e.,
\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t)
; see
eq.

79
.
After performing the functional derivatives, we take the following external field backgrounds

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{a}=0,\quad\psi(x)=e^{i\pi Tx_{0}}\psi,\quad\bar{\psi}(x)=e^{-i\pi Tx_{0}}\bar{\psi},

(20)

where we take into account the lowest mode of the fermion thermal fluctuation according to its statistical feature.
Combining all those setups, the Hessian is obtained within the LPA’ framework, see
eq.

81
for the explicit expression.

After inserting the Hessian into the flow equation
16
, and performing the field renormalization as

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{a}\rightarrow A^{a}_{\mu}/\sqrt{Z^{A}_{k}},\quad\psi\rightarrow\psi/\sqrt{Z^{\psi}_{k}},\quad\bar{\psi}\rightarrow\bar{\psi}/\sqrt{Z^{\psi}_{k}},

(21)

the flow equation is then reduced to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V(\sigma;t)=\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\log{S^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})}+\log{(S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}}\biggl]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log\biggl\{\delta^{ab}\delta_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{B}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}\biggl\},

(22)

where the explicit definition of
S^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})
,
(S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}
,
\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}
and
\mathcal{B}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}
can be found in

81

83

90

91

In
eq.

22

\eta_{\psi}=-\partial_{t}\log Z^{\psi}_{k}
denotes the quark anomalous dimension, which is derived in
Section

B.4
.
Since we do not introduce the thermal split of the fermionic kinetic energy, which is the same setup utilized in Ref.
Fu et al. 2020

i\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}
to represent the flow of the quark wave function
Z^{\psi}_{k}

\displaystyle\eta_{\psi}=-\frac{1}{8N_{f}N_{c}}\lim_{\bm{p}\to 0}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial|\bm{p}|^{2}}{\rm Tr}\,\left(i\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}}{\delta\bar{\psi}(-p)\delta\psi(p)}\right).

(23)

For the purpose of solving the fermionic potential

with respect to the RG scale

To this end, we introduce the so-called “ladder” and “non-ladder” approximation schemes

II.2.1
“Ladder” case

The strategy of evaluating the
\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log(1+\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})
is to expand the expression in terms of
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{n}
corresponding to the
n

\sigma

Diagrammatically,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
denote two types of effective vertices of the gluon propagator dressing; we call
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})
the mixed quark-gluon vertex.
This is depicted diagrammatically in

When expanding
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{n}
, the terms
\mathcal{A}^{l}\mathcal{B}^{m}
(
l+m=n
) represent the different constructions of the gluonic loop with fermionic background fields.
Truncating or approximating this combination is to drop some types of loops in the flow.

The simplest approximation scheme is to keep only the loops with forward or reverse ordering of the color generator, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{n}+\mathcal{B}^{n}.

(24)

As an example, we schematically show the diagrams that are taken into account for the
4
-point function, i.e.,
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{2}

Figure

From this figure, this approximation scheme is called the “ladder” approximation.

Figure 3:
Sketch of
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{2}\to\mathcal{A}^{2}+\mathcal{B}^{2}

After taking this approximation and the general Fierz transformation toward the scalar channel, the vertex expansion is then able to be resummed in a closed form easily.
This results in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V(\sigma;t)=\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t)

\displaystyle\quad-T\sum_{n}\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\left(\omega_{\psi,n}^{2}+(\sqrt{k^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}-\mu_{q})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-T\sum_{n}\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\left(\omega_{\psi,n}^{2}+(\sqrt{k^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}+\mu_{q})^{2}\right),

(25)

where the dressed mass of quarks is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\equiv\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t)+C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4(k^{2}+\omega_{A,n}^{2})}\sigma,

(26)

with Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{\psi,n}=(2n+1)\pi T
and
\omega_{A,n}=2n\pi T

C_{2}=\sum_{a=1}^{N_{c}^{2}-1}T^{a}T^{a}
is the Casimir operator of the fundamental representation of
SU(N_{c})

II.3
Gluonic sector and running gauge coupling

The flow equation of the fermionic potential
V(\sigma;t)
is closed as a partial differential equation (PDE).
To evaluate it, we need to give the flow equation of the gauge coupling
g_{s}

As will be discussed in
Section

In contrast, the weak form of the flow equation for the gauge coupling, which receives contributions from the four-fermion coupling as shown in

In the present study, instead of adopting this approach, we make use of quantitative results obtained from other functional methods

;
Gao and Pawlowski 2020
;
Lu et al. 2024
;

;
Cyrol et al. 2018a
;
Cyrol et al. 2018b
;

;

To obtain the phase diagram, the model parameters must be fixed so that reliable values of IR observables--such as the light constituent quark mass--are reproduced at zero temperature and zero chemical potential. III
Weak solution and method of characteristics

We aim to obtain the dynamical mass (or chiral condensate) by solving the flow equations
25
and
27
.
However, the fermionic effective potential develops singularities (or discontinuities) at a certain energy scale as a result of the phase transition.
This implies that the flow of
V(\sigma;t)
terminates at an intermediate scale, making it difficult to extract physical quantities in the deep infrared regime (
k\to 0
).
In this section, we introduce the strategy to solve the ladder
25
and the non-ladder
27
flow equations with explicit discontinuity.
The method we will apply is within the framework of the weak solution
Aoki et al. 2014
in solving the first-order partial differential equations.

III.1
Weak solution of the flow equation

We start by considering the following first-order partial non-linear differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V(\sigma;t)=-F(\partial_{\sigma}V,\sigma;t),

(38)

which could be the generalized version of the flow equations
25
and
27
.
When flowing the scale down into IR, the four-fermi coupling tends to be divergent since it corresponds to the chiral susceptibility for dynamical chiral symmetry breaking of second-order.
Here, defining the mass function and the four-fermi coupling with respect to the background field

\displaystyle M(\sigma;t)\equiv\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t),\quad G(\sigma;t)\equiv\partial_{\sigma}^{2}V(\sigma;t),

(39)

the above fact can be translated into

\displaystyle|G(0^{\pm};t_{c}^{+})|\rightarrow\infty,

(40)

when the scale approaches the critical scale
t_{c}
.
Below the critical scale, the mass function (in the chiral limit) develops a discontinuity,

\displaystyle M(0^{-};t)=-M(0^{+};t)\neq 0,

(41)

which signals the onset of chiral symmetry breaking.

This discontinuity shows up during the integration of the RG flow and causes the flow to stop at that point.
Also, if the solution of the flow equation shows a discontinuity behavior
41
, the solution cannot be described by the original PDE.
In the case of a second-order phase transition, such a discontinuity appears at
\sigma=0
, whereas a first-order phase transition, which may occur at finite chemical potential, involves discontinuities at nonzero values of
\sigma
.
Thus, the PDE must be reformulated so as to accommodate solutions with singularities.

Let us now introduce the weak formulation for the PDE.
To this end, we consider the flow equation for
M(\sigma;t)
instead of that for
V(\sigma;t)
.
Taking the derivative with respect to
\sigma
on both sides of
eq.

38
, we obtain the PDE of the mass function

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M(\sigma;t)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\sigma}F\left(M(\sigma;t),\sigma;t\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial F}{\partial M}\cdot\frac{\partial M}{\partial\sigma}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}.

(42)

This equation is, in general, a conservation law, where the conserved “charge” is
M
and the associated “current” is
F
. A typical example of such a conservation equation is the Burgers equation in hydrodynamics
Burgers 1995
.
We integrate the PDE of the mass function multiplied by an arbitrary smooth function
\varphi(\sigma;t)
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\rightarrow\pm\infty}\varphi(\sigma;t)=0,\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\varphi(\sigma;t)=0,

(43)

and utilizing the integration by parts, we obtain the following form

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\text{d}t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\sigma\,\left(M\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}+F\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\sigma}\right)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\sigma\,\left(M\,\varphi\right)_{t=0}.

(44)

Since the derivatives with respect to
\sigma
and
t
are transferred onto the test function
\varphi
, the solution
M
is not required to be differentiable. We refer to
eq.

44
as the
weak flow equation
, and its solution is called the
weak solution
. For details on the weak solution, see Refs.
Aoki et al. 2014
;
Evans 2010
.

III.2
Rankine-Hugoniot condition

Next, we discuss the requirement of the singularity of the weak solution
M=M(\sigma;t)
following the discussion of Ref.
Aoki et al. 2014
.
Suppose the set of discontinuous points of the weak solution forms a curve
\sigma^{*}(t)
in the
(\sigma,t)
plane, which divides the weak solution into two regions on different sides, denoted as
M^{(L)}
and
M^{(R)}
.
According to the conservation law nature of the original equation, if we take a small area made of the variation of
\sigma^{*}
and
t
, at the boundary of this area we have

\displaystyle\left(M^{(L)}-M^{(R)}\right)\text{d}\sigma^{*}=\left[F(M^{(L)})-F(M^{(R)})\right]\text{d}t.

(45)

This condition is the Rankine–Hugoniot (RH) condition of the weak solution
M(\sigma;t)
.
For the convenience of finding the weak solution, we shall consider one step further.
We denote the limit values of the fermionic potential at the different side of the singularity trajectory
\sigma^{*}
as
V^{(L)}
and
V^{(R)}
, and we trace the variation of them
\text{d}V^{(L/R)}(\sigma^{*}(t))
on the trajectory, which reads from the original RG equation

\displaystyle\text{d}V^{(L/R)}(\sigma^{*}(t))

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{(L/R)}\text{d}\sigma^{*}+\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\right|_{(L/R)}\text{d}t

\displaystyle=M^{(L/R)}\text{d}\sigma^{*}-F(M^{(L/R)})\text{d}t.

(46)

Then, the difference between
\text{d}V^{(L/R)}(\sigma^{*}(t);t)
vanishes according to the RH condition
45

\displaystyle\text{d}V^{(L)}(\sigma^{*}(t);t)-\text{d}V^{(R)}(\sigma^{*}(t);t)=0.

(47)

Since the initial potential continues everywhere within our setup, this continuity will be kept even after the emergence of the singularity trajectory.
Thus we have

\displaystyle V^{(L)}(\sigma^{*}(t);t)=V^{(R)}(\sigma^{*}(t);t).

(48)

Here, we show a schematic example of the weak solution to the weak flow equation in the Nambu–Jona-Lasinio model at finite density and temperature
Aoki et al. 2018
in
Figure

8
.
On the left panel of
Figure

8
, we show the fermionic potential of the composite operator
V(\sigma;t)
at deep IR, which behaves with two singularity points.
Imposing the continuity condition of the fermionic potential, we take the superior values of this potential, and the weak solution would be obtained as in the right panel.
The method of finding this solution will be introduced in the next subsection.

III.3
Method of characteristics

Following the method of characteristics, we introduce the characteristic curves
\bar{\sigma}(t)
with initial conditions
\bar{\sigma}(t=0)=\sigma_{0}
, which satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bar{\sigma}(t)=\frac{\partial F(\bar{M},\bar{\sigma};t)}{\partial\bar{M}},

(49)

where
\bar{M}=M(\bar{\sigma}(t);t)
.
With the introduction of
\bar{\sigma}(t)
, we convert the original RG equation into an equivalent strong form by looking at

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bar{M}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial M(\sigma;t)}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{\sigma=\bar{\sigma}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bar{\sigma}+\frac{\partial M(\bar{\sigma};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\left.\frac{\partial F(M,\sigma;t)}{\partial\sigma}\right|_{\sigma=\bar{\sigma}}.

(50)

The coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
49
and
III.3
give a set of solutions
(\bar{\sigma}(t),M(\bar{\sigma}(t);t))
at different scale
t
with different initial conditions
\bar{\sigma}(t=0)=\sigma_{0}
and
M(\bar{\sigma}(0);0))=M(\sigma_{0};0))
, and they forms a unique strong solution of the RG equation.
The fermionic potential along the characteristic curve is integrated from the original RG equation

\displaystyle V(\bar{\sigma}(t);t)

\displaystyle=V(\sigma_{0};0)+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\left[\frac{\partial V}{\partial\bar{\sigma}}\frac{\text{d}\bar{\sigma}}{\text{d}t^{\prime}}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\right]

\displaystyle=V(\sigma_{0};0)+\int_{0}^{t}\text{d}t^{\prime}\left[\bar{M}\frac{\text{d}\bar{\sigma}}{\text{d}t^{\prime}}-F(\bar{M},\bar{\sigma};t^{\prime})\right].

(51)

Following the discussion on the previous example of the RH condition in
Figure

8
, we select the initial conditions
\{\sigma_{0}\}
such that the fermionic potential takes the superior values in the vicinity of each point
\{\bar{\sigma}\}
.
Then we trace the mass function
\bar{M}
from the selected initial points, and we obtain the weak solution like in the right panel of
Figure

8
.
This completes the current discussion on the search for the weak RG solution, which preserves the divergence nature of the four-fermi coupling in the chiral broken phase.

V
Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the QCD phase diagram without explicitly including mesonic fluctuations, employing the weak solution of the functional renormalization group (fRG) equation.
The aim of this work is to revisit the well-studied problem of the QCD phase diagram with light quark flavors and to elucidate the role of mesonic fluctuations by focusing solely on quark and gluon dynamics.
In addition, we investigated the impact of the “non-ladder” approximation scheme on the QCD phase transition in the vicinity of vanishing baryon chemical potential.

As an approximation, we introduced the ansatz
V_{k}(\psi,\bar{\psi})=V(\sigma;t)
, where
\sigma=\bar{\psi}\psi
represents the fermion bilinear.
When solving the flow equation for the effective potential
V_{k}(\sigma;t)
, the solution exhibits singularities (nonanalytic behavior), which signal the onset of spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking.
To address this, we employed the weak formulation of the partial differential equation, which accommodates solutions with such singularities.

From the solution of the fermionic mass function
M(\sigma;t)=\partial_{\sigma}V(\sigma;t)
, we extracted the QCD phase diagram for two quark flavors.
By comparing our results with those obtained from other continuum functional methods that include mesonic fluctuations, we found that both the critical temperature and the curvature of the
T_{c}(\mu_{B})
line near vanishing chemical potential yield reasonable agreement.
However, the prediction of the chiral criticality deviates significantly from those approaches, which is partly in line with our expectations.

Appendix A
Wetterich equation with sharp regulator

Starting with the IR-regulated Schwinger’s functional

\displaystyle e^{W_{k}[J]}=\int\mathcal{D}\Phi\,e^{-S_{\rm bare}-\Delta S_{k}+J\cdot\Phi},

(58)

and the quadratic regulator term is

\displaystyle\Delta S_{k}=\int_{x}\Phi^{T}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{k}(\partial)\cdot\Phi.

(59)

k\in[0,\Lambda]
denotes the RG scale.
The general structure of the regulator
\mathcal{R}_{k}(p)
in the momentum space is given by the following supermatrix form

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}(p)=\begin{pmatrix}R_{k}^{A}&0&0\\
0&0&R_{k}^{\psi,T}\\
0&R_{k}^{\psi}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

Given the definition of the 1PI effective averaged action
\Gamma_{k}
by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]=\sup_{J}\left(J\cdot\Phi-W_{k}[J]\right)-\Delta S_{k}[\Phi],

(61)

where
W_{k}[J]
denotes the Schwinger’s functional and
\Delta S_{k}[\Phi]
is the regulator term quadratic in fields,
we adopt the following conditions

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}\rightarrow\begin{cases}S_{\rm bare}&\quad k\rightarrow\infty,\\[4.30554pt]
\Gamma&\quad k\rightarrow 0.\end{cases}

(62)

This results in

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}\rightarrow\begin{cases}\infty&\quad k\rightarrow\infty,\\[4.30554pt]
0&\quad k\rightarrow 0.\end{cases}

(63)

After taking the derivative with respect to the RG scale
t
on both sides of
eq.

58
, and performing the Legendre transformation, the flow equation for the 1PI effective action, i.e., the Wetterich equation
6
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{STr}\left[\left(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}+\mathcal{R}_{k}\right)^{-1}\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{k}\right],

(64)

is found. See also
Ellwanger 1994
;
Morris 1994
.
The UV boundary condition of
eq.

64
is the bare action
S_{\rm bare}
.

In this work, we apply the following spatial sharp regulator to regulate the action at finite temperature

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}({p})=\mathcal{K}_{k}\,r_{k}(\bm{p}),

(65)

where
r_{k}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{\theta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}-1
.
The tree-level kinetic matrix is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}Z^{A}_{k}{p^{2}}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{1/\xi}&0&0\\
0&0&Z^{\psi}_{k}i\not{{p}}^{T}\\
0&Z^{\psi}_{k}i\not{{p}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(66)

where
\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{1/\xi}=\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}+\frac{1}{\xi}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\parallel}
, and

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)=\delta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{p^{2}},\qquad\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\parallel}=\frac{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{p^{2}}.

(67)

By differentiating the regulator with respect to the RG time
t
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{k}=\mathcal{K}_{k}\frac{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}{\theta^{2}\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)},

(68)

where we have ignored the explicit anomalous dimension contribution from
\eta\sim-\partial_{t}\log Z
in the loops.
Also, the Hessian matrix can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}=\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}.

(69)

By extracting the momentum integration of the supertrace in the flow equation, the flow equation is then deformed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{STr}\left[\left(\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}+\mathcal{R}_{k}\right)^{-1}\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\operatorname{str}\left[\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}/\theta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)+\mathcal{V}_{k}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{k}\frac{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}{\theta^{2}\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}\operatorname{str}\left[\theta^{-1}\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}\cdot\theta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\right)^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}\operatorname{str}\left[\theta^{-1}\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)-\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}\cdot\theta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\right)^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{k}\right].

(70)

The first term of the last line in
eq.

70
is a field-independent and divergent term, thus dropped there.
Following the
Morris-Lemma

Morris 1994
, we deform the second term as

\displaystyle-\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}\cdot\theta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\right)^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad=-\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\int_{0}^{1}\text{d}\theta\,\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}\cdot\theta\right)^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad=-\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)\log\left[\frac{\mathcal{K}_{k}+\mathcal{V}_{k}}{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right].

(71)

Thus, the Wetterich equation is reduced to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]=-\frac{1}{2}T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}\operatorname{str}\log\Gamma_{k}^{(2)}.

(72)

Hereafter, for simplicity, we introduce the following short-hand notation, replacing the integral/summation with

\displaystyle T\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\int\frac{\text{d}^{3}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}{k\delta\left(|\bm{p}|-k\right)}\rightarrow\int_{p,\rm shell}.

(73)

As the kernel of the flow equation, the Hessian has the following supermatrix form in QCD

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k,a,b}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{BB}&H_{BF}\\
H_{FB}&H_{FF}\end{pmatrix},

(74)

where
B
and
F
denote the bosonic and fermionic superfield components, respectively.

In this work, we adopt the following form of the flow equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{str}\log\Gamma_{k,a,b}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\biggl[\operatorname{tr}\log{H_{FF}}-\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log{(H_{BB}-H_{BF}H_{FF}^{-1}H_{FB})}\biggl],

(75)

where the prime on the trace indicates that the trace acts only on the Lorentz and adjoint color spaces.
The derivation of
eq.

75
can be found, e.g., in Appendix B of the Ref.
Huang et al. 2025
.
This expression shows a intuitive diagrammatical interpretation, i.e., the flow equation with external fermion field is given by the one-loop of the fermion propagator
H_{FF}^{-1}
and the one-loop of the gluonic propagator
H_{BB}^{-1}
with the dressing of the effective self-energy
H_{BF}H_{FF}^{-1}H_{FB}
.
The corresponding Feynman diagram representation is shown in

(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{p}-\bm{q})\delta_{n_{p},n_{q}+1}/T,&\quad\text{for $-$}.\end{cases}

B.2
Derivation of the flow equation

With the truncated Hessian
81
at hand, we now start to derive the closed form of the flow equation for the fermionic potential.
First, we focus on the right-hand side of the flow equation
75
.
The fermionic loop reads

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\log{H_{FF}}=\frac{\Omega_{4}}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\biggl[\operatorname{tr}\log{S^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})}+\operatorname{tr}\log({S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}}\biggl],

(85)

where
\Omega_{4}=\beta\mathcal{V}_{3}
denotes the spacetime volume, and the trace is taken in the spinor, flavor, and color spaces.
The bosonic loop is obtained as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log{(H_{BB}-H_{BF}H_{FF}^{-1}H_{FB})}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\Omega_{4}}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log\biggl\{(D^{-1})^{ab}_{\mu\nu}(p_{A})+g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\nu}T^{b}\psi+g_{s}^{2}(T^{a}\gamma_{\mu}\psi)^{T}S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi})(\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\nu}T^{b})^{T}\biggl\}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\Omega_{4}}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log\biggl\{(D^{-1})^{ab}_{\mu\nu}(p_{A})+g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\nu}T^{b}\psi+g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\nu}T^{b}S(-p^{+}_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}\psi\biggl\}

(86)

In the flow equation
16
, we have omitted the bare kinetic term contributions, i.e., the denominator in the logarithm of
eq.

71
.
These terms read

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{str}\log{\mathcal{K}_{k}^{-1}}=+\frac{\Omega_{4}}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\biggl[\operatorname{tr}\log{S_{0}^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})}+\operatorname{tr}\log{(S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}}\biggl]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log(D)^{ab}_{\mu\nu}(p_{A}).

(87)

Performing the field strength renormalization
A_{\mu}^{a}\rightarrow A_{\mu}^{a}/\sqrt{Z^{A}_{k}}
,
\psi\rightarrow\psi/\sqrt{Z^{\psi}_{k}}
and
\bar{\psi}\rightarrow\bar{\psi}/\sqrt{Z^{\psi}_{k}}
, the gauge coupling is replaced by the renormalized one, and the left-hand side of the flow equation
16
becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}=-\Omega_{4}\left(\partial_{t}V_{k}-\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V_{k}\right)+\cdots,

(88)

where
\eta_{\psi}=-\partial_{t}\log Z^{\psi}_{k}
is the fermion anomalous dimension, and will be derived in
Section

B.4
.

Combining
eq.

85
to
eq.

88
, the flow equation of the fermionic potential is then obtained as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{k}=

\displaystyle\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\log{S^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})}+\log{(S^{(T)}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}}\biggl]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log\biggl\{\delta^{ab}\delta_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{B}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}\biggl\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\log{S_{0}^{-1}(p^{-}_{\psi})}+\log{(S^{(T)}_{0}(p^{+}_{\psi}))^{-1}}\biggl],

(89)

in which we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}\equiv g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\rho}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})T^{b}\psi,

(90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{ab}_{\mu\nu}\equiv g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}T^{b}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})\gamma_{\rho}S(-p^{+}_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}\psi.

(91)

This is the explicit form of

B.3
Ladder and non-ladder Approximations

Although the flow equations with the same truncation and approximation schemes are derived in the vacuum case in Ref.
Aoki and Sato 2013
, there are still subtleties at finite temperature and quark chemical potential.
Thus, a detailed derivation from the beginning is necessary.

B.3.1
“Ladder” case

To close the flow equation, we compute the gluonic trace in
eq.

89
.
We expand the logarithm in terms of the polynomial of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log(1+\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}\right)^{n}.

(92)

This is essentially the vertex expansion of the bilinear quantities
\bar{\psi}\mathcal{O}\psi
.
The
n
-th order of the polynomial corresponds to the one-loop diagram with
n
-external background fields, and the order of contraction of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
represents how this loop is constructed.
We then take the following ladder approximation that the vertices are contracted in a certain order, i.e., we only consider the
\mathcal{A}^{n}
and the
\mathcal{B}^{n}
terms and ignore other terms such as
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}^{n-2}
in the current approximation scheme.
See also the discussion around
eq.

24
.
After that, the trace becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\log(1+\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})\quad\overset{\rm Ladder}{\longrightarrow}\quad-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{A}^{n}+\mathcal{B}^{n}\right).

(93)

Next, we project the operator into the subspace of the scalar coupling channel using the generalized Fierz transformation
Aoki and Sato 2013

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{1}\mathcal{O}_{1}\psi_{2}\bar{\psi}_{2}\mathcal{O}_{2}\psi_{3}\cdots\bar{\psi}_{n}\mathcal{O}_{n}\psi_{1}\quad\rightarrow\quad(-1)^{n+1}F_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\psi}_{i}\textbf{1}_{\rm spinor}\lambda^{0}T^{0}\psi_{i},

(94)

where
T^{0}\equiv\textbf{1}_{\rm color}/\sqrt{2N_{c}}
is the normalized identity in the color space and
\lambda^{0}\equiv\textbf{1}_{\rm flavor}/\sqrt{2N_{f}}
is the normalized identity in the flavor space.
The Fierz coefficient reads

\displaystyle F_{n}=\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\textbf{1}_{\rm spinor}\lambda^{0}T^{0}\mathcal{O}_{1}\cdots\textbf{1}_{\rm spinor}\lambda^{0}T^{0}\mathcal{O}_{n}\biggl],

(95)

where the trace is taken in the spinor, the flavor space, and the color space.
The Fierz coefficients of
\mathcal{A}^{n}
and
\mathcal{B}^{n}
are obtained by

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{A}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{4N_{c}N_{f}}\right)^{n}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[\gamma_{\mu_{n}}T^{a_{n}}S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu_{1}}D_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(p_{A})T^{a_{1}}\gamma_{\mu_{1}}T^{a_{1}}\cdots S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu_{n}}D_{\mu_{n}\mu_{1}}(p_{A})T^{a_{n}}\biggl]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{s}^{2}C_{2}}{4N_{c}N_{f}}\right)^{n}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[S(p^{-}_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu}D_{\mu\nu}(p_{A})\gamma_{\nu}\biggl]^{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{s}^{2}C_{2}}{4N_{c}N_{f}}\right)^{n}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[S(p^{-}_{\psi})\frac{(3+\xi)}{p_{A}^{2}}\biggl]^{n},

(96)

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{B}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{g_{s}^{2}C_{2}}{4N_{c}N_{f}}\right)^{n}\operatorname{tr}\biggl[S(-p^{+}_{\psi})\frac{(3+\xi)}{p_{A}^{2}}\biggl]^{n},

(97)

where
T^{a}T^{a}=(N_{c}^{2}-1)/(2N_{c})\mathbf{1}_{\rm color}\equiv C_{2}\mathbf{1}_{\rm color}
is the Casimir operator of the fundamental representation of
SU(N_{c})
.
Then the original operators are replaced by the projected ones

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\operatorname{tr}^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{A}^{n}+\mathcal{B}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\rm F.T.}{\rightarrow}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\left(\frac{g_{s}^{2}C_{2}}{4N_{c}N_{f}}\right)^{n}\Biggl(\operatorname{tr}\biggl[-S(p^{-}_{\psi})\frac{(3+\xi)}{p_{A}^{2}}\biggl]^{n}+\operatorname{tr}\biggl[-S(-p^{+}_{\psi})\frac{(3+\xi)}{p_{A}^{2}}\biggl]^{n}\Biggl)

\displaystyle\quad=-\operatorname{tr}\log\biggl(1-C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4p_{A}^{2}}S(p^{-}_{\psi})\frac{\sigma}{N_{c}N_{f}}\biggl)-\operatorname{tr}\log\biggl(1-C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4p_{A}^{2}}S(-p^{+}_{\psi})\frac{\sigma}{N_{c}N_{f}}\biggl),

(98)

where F.T. denotes the Fierz transformation.

Combining with the fermionic loop and the left-hand side of the flow equation we derived, it turns out that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V(\sigma;t)=\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\log\biggl(S^{-1}(p_{\psi}^{-})-C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4p_{A}^{2}}\sigma\biggl)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{p,\rm shell}\operatorname{tr}\log\biggl(-S^{-1}(p_{\psi}^{+})+C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4p_{A}^{2}}\sigma\biggl).

(99)

where we rescaled the
V
and
\sigma
by the factor
N_{c}N_{f}

\displaystyle\sigma\rightarrow N_{c}N_{f}\sigma,\qquad V\rightarrow N_{c}N_{f}V,

(100)

with
\partial_{\sigma}V
unchanged.
After taking the trace and integrating out the loop momentum, we obtain the flow equation of the fermionic potential within the ladder approximation
25

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V(\sigma;t)=\eta_{\psi}\sigma\partial_{\sigma}V

\displaystyle-T\sum_{n}\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\left(\omega_{\psi,n}^{2}+(\sqrt{k^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}-\mu_{q})^{2}\right)-T\sum_{n}\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\log\left(\omega_{\psi,n}^{2}+(\sqrt{k^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}+\mu_{q})^{2}\right),

(101)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\equiv\partial_{\sigma}V+C_{2}g_{s}^{2}\frac{3+\xi}{4(\omega_{A,n}^{2}+k^{2})}\sigma,

(102)

and

\displaystyle\omega_{\psi,n}=(2n+1)\pi T,\qquad\omega_{A,n}=2n\pi T.

(103)

Notice that the separation of the
\pm\mu_{q}
terms is physically clear, that each term individually corresponds to the fermion or the anti-fermion loop that gains the opposite contribution from the quark chemical potential.
This is the effect induced by the ladder approximation that we do not incorporate with the mixing between fermion and anti-fermion, i.e., the broken ladder.

B.3.2
“Non-ladder” case

Next, we go beyond the ladder approximation scheme by encountering the broken-ladder effect.
First, we consider the mixed quark-gluon vertex at vanishing quark chemical potential

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{ab}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=g_{s}^{2}\bar{\psi}\biggl[T^{a}\gamma_{\mu}S(p_{\psi})\gamma_{\rho}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})T^{b}+T^{b}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})\gamma_{\rho}S(-p_{\psi})\gamma_{\mu}T^{a}\biggl]\psi

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{s}^{2}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})}{p_{\psi}^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}V)^{2}}\bar{\psi}\biggl[T^{a}T^{b}\gamma_{\mu}\left(-i\not{p}_{\psi}-\partial_{\sigma}V\right)\gamma_{\rho}+T^{a}T^{b}\gamma_{\rho}\left(+i\not{p}_{\psi}-\partial_{\sigma}V\right)\gamma_{\mu}\biggl]\psi+(\text{$[T^{a},T^{b}]$ term}).

(104)

To balance the color factors between different orders of combinations expanded from
(A+B)^{n}
.
This point motivates us to ignore the
[T^{a},T^{b}]
term, which is also discussed around
eq.

27
.
Then we are able to deform the mixed quark-gluon by collecting the following expressions, where we define
M_{\psi}\equiv\partial_{\sigma}V

\displaystyle(\text{$M_{\psi}$ term})=-\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{p_{\psi}^{2}+M_{\psi}^{2}}\bar{\psi}T^{a}T^{b}\biggl[M_{\psi}\{\gamma_{\mu},\gamma_{\rho}\}D_{\rho\nu}(p_{A})\biggl]\psi=-\frac{2g_{s}^{2}}{p_{\psi}^{2}+M_{\psi}^{2}}\bar{\psi}T^{a}T^{b}\biggl(M_{\psi}D_{\mu\nu}(p_{A})\biggl)\psi,

(105)

\displaystyle(\text{$\not{p}_{\psi}$ term})

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{p_{\psi}^{2}+M_{\psi}^{2}}\bar{\psi}T^{a}T^{b}\biggl(ip_{\psi,\sigma}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\rho}-ip_{\psi,\sigma}\gamma_{\rho}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\mu}\biggl)\frac{g_{\rho\nu}-(1-\xi)\frac{p_{A,\rho}p_{A,\nu}}{p_{A}^{2}}}{p_{A}^{2}}\psi

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{(p_{\psi}^{2}+M_{\psi}^{2})p_{A}^{2}}\bar{\psi}T^{a}T^{b}\biggl(ip_{\psi,\sigma}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\nu}-ip_{\psi,\sigma}\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\mu}\biggl)\psi

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{g_{s}^{2}(1-\xi)}{(p_{\psi}^{2}+M_{\psi}^{2})p_{A}^{4}}\bar{\psi}T^{a}T^{b}\biggl(ip_{\psi,\sigma}p_{A,\rho}p_{A,\nu}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\rho}-ip_{\psi,\sigma}p_{A,\rho}p_{A,\nu}\gamma_{\rho}\gamma_{\sigma}\gamma_{\mu}\biggl)\psi.

(106)

For the last line in the

B.4
Quark anomalous dimension

To extract the flow of the two-point function with external fluctuations, we perform a formal vertex expansion of the gluonic loop with respect to the effective vertex
M_{BF}M_{FF}^{-1}M_{FB}
, carrying the external field dependence.
Since the fermionic one-loop, which results in the tadpole diagram, does not contribute to the anomalous dimension, we shall omit it in our formulation.
Also, we ignore the derivatives of the wave function with respect to the fields in the anomalous dimension, such as
Z^{(n)}(\sigma)=\partial_{\sigma}^{n}Z(\sigma)
, as an approximation, which stresses that the potential term would also be dominant in the flow of the wave function.
We then obtain the flow of the fermionic quadratic term at the vanished quark chemical potential as

\displaystyle\int_{x,y,p}\bar{\psi}(x)\biggl(\partial_{t}\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(\psi,\bar{\psi};p)\biggl)\psi(y)\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}

(141)

where in the last line, we have defined the shifted momentum
