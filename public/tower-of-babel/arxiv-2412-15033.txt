Linear response of rotating and flattened stellar clusters: the oblate Kuzmin–Kutuzov Stäckel family.
Kerwann Tep, Christophe Pichon, Michael S. Petersen

Abstract
This paper investigates the linear response of a series of spheroidal stellar clusters, the Kuzmin–Kutuzov Stäckel family, which exhibit a continuous range of flattening and rotation, extending from an isochrone sphere to a Toomre disk. The method successfully replicates the growing modes previously identified in published N N -body simulations. It relies on the efficiency of the matrix method to quantify systematically the effects of rotation and flattening on the eigenmodes of the galaxy. We identify two types of bi-symmetric instabilities for the flatter models – the so-called bending and bar-growing modes – the latter of which persists even for very round models. As anticipated, in its least unstable configurations, the system becomes flatter as its rotational speed increases. More realistic equilibria will be required to achieve a better match to the main sequence of fast-slow rotators. The corresponding code is made public.

III.3
Linear response of oblate spheroidal clusters

The matrix elements given in equation (
9
) take the form

\mathrm{M}_{pq}\left(\omega\right)\!=\!\delta_{m^{p}}^{m^{q}}\frac{32\pi^{4}G}{\Delta^{2}}\hskip-17.07164pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{u},k_{v}\\
\ell^{p}+m^{p}+k_{v}\,\mathrm{even}\\
\ell^{q}+m^{q}+k_{v}\,\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\hskip-17.07164pt\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{J}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial F/\partial\boldsymbol{J}}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{(p)}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{(q)},

(15)

where
{k_{\phi}\!=\!m^{p}\!=\!m^{q}}
. As a consequence, only matrix elements such that
\ell^{p}
and
\ell^{q}
have the same parity are non-zero. To compute the modes of the system, we must solve the equation

\det[\mathbf{E}(\omega)]=0;\quad\,\mathbf{E}(\omega)=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M}(\omega),

(16)

where
\mathbf{E}(\omega)
is the dielectric matrix and
\mathbf{I}
the identity matrix.

The system’s axisymmetry, which appears through the Kronecker symbol
\delta_{m^{p}}^{m^{q}}
, makes the response matrix a block-diagonal matrix. As such, one can decouple the system’s instabilities through their azimuthal number,
m
, and study separately the axisymmetric modes
m=0
, the lop-sided modes
m=\pm 1
, the bi-symmetric modes
m=\pm 2
, and so on. Nonetheless, contrary to the infinitely thin disc and the spherically symmetric sphere cases, the axisymmetric response matrix does not benefit from a two-dimensional reduction. As such, one has to face both theoretical and computational difficulties, such as the calculation of orbital frequency, of the angular Fourier transform of the basis elements, and the integration over the full three-dimensional action space.

III.4
Azimuthal response matrix

Owning to axial symmetry, we can decompose the dispersion relation over the azimuthal numbers
m
as

\det\mathbf{E}(\omega)=\prod_{m}\det\mathbf{E}^{m}(\omega),

(17)

with
\mathbf{E}^{m}(\omega)=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{M}^{m}(\omega)
. Here, we define the azimuthal response matrix
\mathbf{M}^{m}(\omega)
by

\mathrm{M}_{pq}^{m}(\omega)\!=\!\frac{32\pi^{4}G}{\Delta^{2}}\hskip-17.07164pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{u},k_{v}\\
\ell^{p}+m+k_{v}\,\mathrm{even}\\
\ell^{q}+m+k_{v}\,\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\hskip-11.38109pt\!\!\int\!\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{J}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial F/\partial\boldsymbol{J}}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega}}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{(p)}W_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{(q)},

(18)

where
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{u},k_{v}})

{k_{\phi}\!=\!m}
,
{p\!=\!(\ell^{p},n^{p})}

\mathrm{M}_{pq}^{-m}(\omega)^{*}\!=\!\ \mathrm{M}_{pq}^{m}(-\omega^{*}).

We can thus limit our exploration to the sampling of frequency space over
{\rm{Re}(\omega)\!\geq\!0}

III.5
Mode shapes

Finally, linear response theory enables us to represent the shape of the unstable modes using the eigenvectors of the response matrix. Specifically, when an unstable mode arises, we obtain an eigenvector,
\mathbf{a}=(a^{m}_{\ell n})
, such that

\mathbf{M}(\omega)\,\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}.

(21)

It follows that the shape of the density perturbation,

\displaystyle\delta\rho^{m}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell n}a^{m}_{\ell n}\,\frac{D^{\ell mn}(\xi)}{\,c_{\Delta}(\xi,\eta)}\,Y_{\ell}^{m}(v,\phi),

(22a)

\displaystyle\delta\psi^{m}(\boldsymbol{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell n}a^{m}_{\ell n}\,F^{\ell mn}(\xi)\,Y_{\ell}^{m}(v,\phi).

(22b)

VI
Conclusions and perspectives

Nearly 30 years after the seminal work of
[
62
]
, this paper extended linear response theory to rotating oblate Stäckel  clusters. Our implementation was validated against the results from
71
, showing that the converged
m=2
modes of flattened and rotating spheroids closely match
N
-body simulations at the
\sim
10% level in terms of growth rates (see Figs.
2
and
4
), pattern speeds, and shapes. When rotation is introduced, two families of fast and slow modes are found (Figs.
3
and
7
): the bending modes, which are saddle-shaped, and the bar-growing modes, which are spiral shaped. Our implementation also allows for analytic continuation so that certain weakly damped modes can be identified (see Fig.
8
). However, a more systematic study (for, e.g., the marginal stability of non-rotating clusters) would require a more careful treatment of the resonant denominator of the response matrix, which becomes sharp near the real frequency line.

Note that breaking spherical symmetry introduces various theoretical and computational challenges when applying linear response theory. While axial symmetry allowed us to decompose the response matrix into distinct azimuthal components, it also fundamentally transformed the computational problem into a three-dimensional one. Specifically, computing a
5\!\times\!5
frequency-space grid for the response matrix of a single cluster with
(\ell_{\max},n_{\max},k_{\max})=(30,10,10)
, and performing the action-space integration over
256\!\times\!256\!\times\!128
nodes, required approximately 38 hours across 512 CPU cores. Hence we had to carefully balance mode convergence with computational cost to achieve accurate predictions.
On the other hand, this formalism provides access to the system’s linear response across all values of
\alpha_{r}
, as per equation (
30
). Consequently, future studies may find it advantageous to revisit this type of analysis with improved computational resources.

Although this single-component asymptotically cold model is not realistic enough to compare against data
[e.g.
25
, and subsequent work]
and simulations
[
15
, e.g.]
, the formalism presented here may easily be extended to multi-component Stäckel systems, and therefore
eventually be applied to more realistic equilibria.

VI.1
Perspectives

One should first investigate the limitations of the specificities of this Stäckel model, which extends the one-component, isotropic isochrone sphere to flattened systems.
Further exploration would be beneficial to extend the computation of linear response to more realistic spheroids, incorporating three-integral distribution functions
[
23

, two- or three-component Stäckel  discs and halos
[

[

20

Furthermore, the transposition the present formalism into the time domain following, e.g.,
Rozier et al. [68]

Beyond linear response, the methodology could be naturally expanded to kinetic theory

Eventually, understanding statistically

Appendix B
Changes of coordinates

The transformation
(E,I_{3},L_{z})\mapsto(J_{u},J_{v},L_{z})
is carried out by a straight computation of the two action integrals. However, the inverse transformation lacks an explicit analytical expression. Nevertheless, it can be computed using Newton’s method.

Let us define the vectors
\boldsymbol{J}=(J_{u},J_{v},L_{z})^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=(E,I_{3},L_{z})^{\mathrm{T}}
. Let use also define the function
\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{w})=(J_{u}[\boldsymbol{w}],J_{v}[\boldsymbol{w}],L_{z})^{\mathrm{T}}-\boldsymbol{J}
.
We wish to solve
\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{w})=0
. We start from an initial guess
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=(E_{i},I_{3,i},L_{z})
with
I_{3,i}=L_{z}^{2}/(2\Delta^{2})-E_{0}
and
E_{i}=E_{s}(L_{z},I_{3,i})/2
, which always describes a bound orbit for a Kuzmin–Kutuzov potential. Then, we define for each step

\boldsymbol{w}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{w}_{n}-[J(\boldsymbol{w}_{n})]^{-1}\boldsymbol{j}(\boldsymbol{w}),

(B1)

where
J(\boldsymbol{w})
is the Jacobian given by

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{u}}{\partial E}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{u}}{\partial I_{3}}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{u}}{\partial L_{z}}}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{v}}{\partial E}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{v}}{\partial I_{3}}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial J_{v}}{\partial L_{z}}}\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(B2)

At each step, we should be careful not to go outside of the authorized region of
(E,L_{z},I_{3})
.

C.1
Boundaries of motion

We may define the effective potentials

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,I_{3},L_{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{z}^{2}}{2\Delta^{2}\sinh^{4}u}+\frac{U(u)+I_{3}}{\sinh^{2}u},

(C1a)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{z}^{2}}{2\Delta^{2}\sin^{4}v}-\frac{V(v)+I_{3}}{\sin^{2}v},

\displaystyle p_{u}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\Delta^{2}\sinh^{2}u\,(E-U_{\mathrm{eff}}[u,I_{3},L_{z}]),

(C2a)

\displaystyle p_{v}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\Delta^{2}\sin^{2}v\,(E-V_{\mathrm{eff}}[v,I_{3},L_{z}]).

(C2b)

{p_{u}^{2}[u,I_{3},L_{z}]\!=\!0}

\nabla^{2}_{\xi,\ell,m}F^{\ell mn}=D^{\ell mn},

(D2)

(D3)

We have also defined
c_{\Delta}(\xi,\eta)=\Delta^{2}(\xi^{2}-\eta^{2})

In particular, the

(D4)

\int\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\phi\sin v\,|Y_{\ell}^{m}(v,\phi)|^{2}=1.

(D5)

in this context.
Now, we define the scalar product

\langle\psi^{(p)},\psi^{(q)}\rangle=-\frac{1}{4\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{r}\,\psi^{(p)*}\nabla^{2}\psi^{(q)}.

(D6)

Requiring

(D8)

Then, the full scalar product is related to the radial scalar product through

\langle\psi^{(p)},\psi^{(q)}\rangle=\delta_{\ell^{p}}^{\ell^{q}}\delta_{m^{p}}^{m^{q}}\langle F^{\ell^{p}m^{p}n^{p}},F^{\ell^{p}m^{p}n^{q}}\rangle.

Then, the associated density elements are given by

\displaystyle D^{\ell mn}(\xi)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{(\xi-1)^{n-1}}{(\xi+1)^{n+3}}\big(C_{0,n}\!+\!C_{1,n}(\xi-1)\!+\!C_{2,n}(\xi-1)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle-\!\frac{(\xi-1)^{n-1}}{(\xi+1)^{n+1}}\bigg(\ell(\ell+1)(\xi-1)\!+\!\frac{m^{2}}{\xi+1}\bigg),\hskip-2.84526pt

(D11)

where we defined the constants

\displaystyle C_{0,n}

\displaystyle=8n^{2},

(D12a)

\displaystyle C_{1,n}

\displaystyle=2\big(1+2n(n+1)-3(2n+1)\big),

(D12b)

\displaystyle C_{2,n}

\displaystyle=2\big(1-2(n+1)\big).

(D12c)

Note that the
n=0
radial element should only be used if
m=0
.

D.2
Computing the inner product

The inner product can be written in the form

\displaystyle-\Delta\langle F^{\ell mk},F^{\ell mn}\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{0,n}\,I_{1}(k+n-1,k+n+4)

\displaystyle+C_{1,n}\,I_{1}(k+n,k+n+4)

\displaystyle+C_{2,n}\,I_{1}(k+n+1,k+n+4)

\displaystyle-\ell(\ell+1)\,I_{1}(k+n,k+n+2)

\displaystyle-m^{2}\,I_{1}(k+n-1,k+n+3),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\frac{(\xi-1)^{p_{1}}}{(\xi+1)^{p_{2}}}.

(D13)

Following appendix B of
Robijn & Earn [64]
, we can compute these functions analytically. Indeed,

\displaystyle I_{1}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}(p_{1}+1,p_{2}-p_{1}-1)\,2^{p_{1}-p_{2}+1},

(D14)

where
\mathrm{B}(x,y)
is the Beta function.

D.3
Radial basis orthogonalization

Consider the non-orthogonal radial basis elements
\{\widetilde{F}^{i}\}_{i}
. We define the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell m}
by letting

\langle\widetilde{F}^{\ell mk},\widetilde{F}^{\ell mn}\rangle=\mathcal{E}^{\ell m}_{kn}.

(D15)

Then, for a given harmonics
(\ell,m)
, we can write the scalar product of any radial functions

\langle G_{1},G_{2}\rangle=G_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell m}G_{2}.

(D16)

Let

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell m}
. Then
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell m}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}
, and we let
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}

\mathbf{Y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\ell m}\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Y}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y})^{T}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{I}.

(D17)

Therefore, the columns of

form the (triangular) orthogonalization of the basis elements

(D18)

where

(resp.
n_{0}=1
) if
m=0
(resp.

We drop the bar notation from now on in order not to overload notations. As an example, the Gram–Schmidt coefficients for

(D19)

Appendix G
Introducing rotation

G.1
Distribution function: Lynden-Bell daemon

Using the Lynden-Bell parametrization
[
49
]
, the density profile of the cluster does not depend on the rotation parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}
. Its derivatives w.r.t. the action variables read

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\partial J_{u}}

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\partial F}{\partial J_{u}}(1\!+\!\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{sgn}[L_{z}]),

(G1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\partial J_{v}}

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\partial F}{\partial J_{v}}(1\!+\!\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{sgn}[L_{z}]),

(G1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\partial L_{z}}

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\partial F}{\partial L_{z}}(1\!+\!\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{sgn}[L_{z}])\!+\!2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}F(\boldsymbol{J})\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(L_{z})\,,

(G1c)

where
F(\boldsymbol{J})=F(E[J_{u},J_{v},L_{z}],L_{z})
. Therefore, the computation of the response matrix requires slight modifications.

Most notably, one must take into account the Dirac delta contribution, whose implementation should be performed carefully. Indeed, the third frequency
\Omega_{z}
has two distinct left-sided and right-sided limits at
L_{z}\rightarrow 0
. We can remedy this issue by recalling that integration over
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
should be understood as the limit

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}x\,g(x)\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(x)=\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}x\,g(x)\eta_{a}(x).

(G2)

over a set of functions
g(x)
.
Here,
{\eta_{a}\!\rightarrow\!\delta_{\mathrm{D}}}
converges in the distributional sense, with
{\eta_{a}(x)\!=\!\eta(x/a)/a}
where
\eta
is an even function integrating to 1. Then

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}x\,g(x)\eta_{a}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}y\,g(ay)\eta(y)

(G3)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}y\,g(ay)\eta(y)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{d}y\,g(ay)\eta(y)
