Therapeutic Potential of Isoniazid in Inflammatory Skin Diseases: A Review
Authors: Shuokai Zhang, Yi Tang, Jiamin Wen, Jiaqi Song, Wenchen Gong, Ting Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIsoniazid (INH), a well-established anti-tuberculosis agent, has recently gained increasing interest because of its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. This review is intended to summarize the mechanistic basis of INH in inflammatory skin disorders and to assess its potential clinical applicability. However, most evidence discussed here derives from preclinical studies and remains largely hypothesis-generating.Materials and MethodsA literature search was performed in the CNKI and PubMed databases.ResultsThe anti-inflammatory activity of INH is mediated through several pathways, including regulation of the ROS-PPARγ-NF-κB/AP-1 axis, suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and modulation of the Th1/Th2 balance. Therapeutic potential has been observed in conditions such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, urticaria, and rosacea, particularly among patients with latent tuberculosis infection or those undergoing biologic therapy, supporting its dual “anti-inflammatory–antituberculosis” value.ConclusionINH demonstrates a multi-target mode of action and distinct dual clinical advantages in the management of inflammatory skin diseases, rendering it especially relevant for resource-constrained settings and selected patient populations. Additional clinical investigations remain necessary to determine the optimal treatment strategy and to further establish its safety profile. However, clinical evidence remains limited and further prospective studies are needed.

Introduction

Isoniazid (INH), a first-line anti-tuberculosis agent, produces its antibacterial activity through inhibition of mycolic acid synthesis. Emerging evidence has indicated that INH also exhibits notable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. The expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β may be reduced, and the Th1/Th2 balance may be influenced through modulation of the ROS-PPARγ-NF-κB/AP-1 axis and inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Such observations provide a mechanistic rationale for repurposing INH in inflammatory skin disorders. Early work by the Chinese immunologist Wu He on the use of INH in immune-associated diseases established an important foundation for this therapeutic perspective. In recent years, regenerative and immunomodulatory approaches—particularly those involving extracellular vesicles and injectable biologics—have gained traction in dermatology.1,2 These advances reflect a growing emphasis on biologically driven therapies. Against this backdrop, isoniazid (INH), a small-molecule agent with well-documented immunomodulatory properties, offers a mechanistically distinct and potentially complementary alternative, especially in resource-limited settings where advanced biologics remain inaccessible. This study provides a comprehensive review of the molecular mechanisms, clinical applicability, and future investigative priorities of INH in the management of inflammatory skin diseases.

Materials and Methods

A literature search was performed in the CNKI and PubMed databases (up to January 1, 2026). The search terms were divided into three sets: (1) isoniazid (isoniazid/INH) combined individually with atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, inflammatory skin diseases, urticaria, rosacea, Th1/Th2 balance, Th1, and Th2; (2) isoniazid combined with IL-1β; (3) IL-1β combined individually with the above-mentioned diseases and immune terms (ie, atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, inflammatory skin diseases, urticaria, rosacea, Th1/Th2 balance, Th1, Th2). The search was restricted to Chinese and English languages. In addition, the reference lists of relevant articles were also screened (snowballing).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria: Studies were included if they involved the anti‑inflammatory/immunomodulatory mechanisms of isoniazid or IL‑1β and focused on inflammatory skin diseases; studies not related to the topic were excluded.

Anti-Inflammatory Mechanism of INH

Inhibition of IL-1β-Related Inflammatory Pathways

IL-1β represents a central inflammatory mediator within the innate immune system. The expression and activation of IL-1β may be suppressed by INH through the mechanisms outlined below:

Regulation of the ROS-PPARγ-NF-κB/AP-1-IL-1β Axis

Evidence derived predominantly from in vitro systems and animal models indicates that immune cell tolerance to ROS can be enhanced by INH, thereby providing protection against oxidative stress–induced necrosis.3 In a zebrafish inflammation model, intracellular ROS production was markedly reduced following INH treatment.4 Lower ROS levels may promote PPARγ expression, which in turn effectively suppresses the transcriptional activity of NF-κB and AP-1. Given that NF-κB and AP-1 serve as major transcription factors governing IL-1β expression, upstream inhibition of IL-1β may ultimately be achieved through the pathway “ROS↓→PPARγ↑→NF-κB/AP-1↓”.4 Similar regulatory effects have also been confirmed in additional inflammatory models.5

Suppression of NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation and IL-1β Maturation

Preclinical evidence has established that the biological activity of IL-1β requires cytoplasmic cleavage of its precursor (pro-IL-1β), a process primarily mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome.6 By reducing intracellular ROS levels, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and caspase-1 may be indirectly inhibited by INH, thereby preventing the conversion of pro-IL-1β into mature IL-1β.7,8

Regulation of Th1/Th2 Immune Balance

Contextual Determinants of Th1/Th2 Balance Regulation

INH exerts bidirectional effects on the Th1/Th2 balance, with outcomes potentially influenced by dosage, treatment duration, and underlying immune status. Several factors may determine the direction of this immunological shift, including drug concentration, treatment duration, baseline immune status, and the specific tissue microenvironment.9,10 At lower concentrations or within a Th2-predominant milieu, INH may preferentially drive Th1 polarization; conversely, higher concentrations or Th1-skewed inflammatory conditions may tilt the balance toward Th2 predominance. This concentration- and context-dependent duality suggests that INH’s immunomodulatory actions reflect a dynamic equilibrium modulated by multiple interacting determinants, rather than fixed attributes. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to delineate the precise conditions under which each polarization trajectory unfolds.

Clinical Implications of Preferential Th1 Cell Apoptosis

Of particular interest is the observation that INH preferentially induces apoptosis in Th1 cells over Th2 cells.10 This selective elimination of Th1 cells may, under certain therapeutic scenarios, partially explain a shift toward Th2-dominated immune profiles. Interestingly, in Th2-driven dermatological conditions such as atopic dermatitis, the relative preservation of Th2 cells—coupled with preferential Th1 depletion—might paradoxically attenuate Th2 responses by disrupting the positive feedback loops that sustain Th2 polarization. This apparent duality highlights the complexity of INH’s immunomodulatory actions and suggests that clinical outcomes may vary depending on the baseline immune status of individual patients.

Building on these mechanistic considerations, the following subsections detail the evidence for INH-induced Th1 polarization and Th2 polarization, respectively, in the context of inflammatory skin diseases:

Promotion of Th1 Polarization

In clinical settings, certain patients with tuberculosis and atopic dermatitis (AD) have exhibited unexpected and marked improvement of AD skin lesions following INH-containing regimens. Such observations suggest that INH may suppress the Th2-dominant pathological process characteristic of AD. At the cellular level, INH, acting as a monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) inhibitor,11 may inhibit MAOA-dependent Th2 cell proliferation and the secretion of related cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13.12 At the cytokine level, Wang et al9 cultured cells with varying concentrations of INH and reported increased IL-12 levels, with the greatest elevation observed in the 5.00 μg/mL group. IL-12 serves as a key cytokine driving Th1 differentiation. Accordingly, a Th1 polarization may be promoted through suppression of Th2 cytokine production together with enhanced IL-12 expression.

Promotion of Th2 Polarization

At the cellular level, Tousif et al10 administered 50 mg/L INH in the drinking water of mice and observed reduced IL-12 and IFN-γ levels in splenic cells, both of which are major inducers of Th1 differentiation. Biraro et al13 similarly reported that INH prophylactic treatment (IPT) was associated with decreased Th1 cytokine production. Tousif et al10 further noted that INH may directly induce apoptosis of antigen-specific activated CD4⁺ T cells, with Th1 cells demonstrating greater susceptibility than Th2 cells. Consequently, relative Th2 predominance may be indirectly enhanced through preferential Th1 cell loss; however, in Th2-driven disorders such as AD, INH-associated T-cell apoptosis may conversely attenuate Th2 responses. At the cytokine level, Ssekamatte et al14 reported increased IL-13 production in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells among patients with latent tuberculosis following IPT, and IL-13 is a key mediator of Th2 differentiation. Therefore, Th2 polarization may be promoted through reduced Th1 cytokine production, enhanced Th1 cell apoptosis, and increased IL-13 expression.

Potential Application in AD

AD is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by intense pruritus and eczematous lesions, predominantly driven by Th2-mediated immune responses.15 IL-33 promotes the generation of mature IL-1β through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and IL-1β serves a central role in the initiation of inflammation, impairment of the skin barrier, Th2 immune polarization, and the development of persistent pruritus in AD.16,17 IL-1β induces the expression of thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), thereby contributing to an AD-like cutaneous phenotype,18 and its expression level is positively correlated with disease severity.17 With respect to barrier dysfunction, IL-4 and IL-13 secreted by Th2 cells not only amplify inflammatory responses but also disrupt epidermal barrier integrity.15 Regarding pruritus mechanisms, IL-4 and IL-13 act directly on sensory neurons, leading to heightened pruritic sensitivity.19,20 TSLP has likewise been identified as a mediator of pruritus in AD,21 and its expression correlates positively with disease severity.22 Notably, IL-1β not only induces TSLP expression but also directly targets sensory neurons, reducing the pruritus threshold and intensifying symptoms.23 In patients with AD, increased cutaneous sensitivity to pruritic stimuli promotes scratching behavior, which further aggravates itch and impairs the skin barrier. This disruption permits penetration of external allergens and irritants into the epidermis, thereby triggering or aggravating inflammation and establishing a self-perpetuating “pruritus–scratching” cycle.24

Based on the mechanisms described above, INH demonstrates theoretical dual interventional potential. On one hand, inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and suppression of IL-1β maturation directly interrupt its central role in inflammatory initiation and pruritic signaling, thereby attenuating TSLP-mediated neuronal sensitization. On the other hand, modulation of the Th1/Th2 immune balance enables therapeutic effects through suppression of Th2 cytokine secretion and relative enhancement of Th1 responses, contributing to correction of pruritus sensitization and excessive Th2 polarization in AD. In addition, apoptosis of activated T cells induced by INH may assist in reestablishing immune homeostasis. Consequently, inflammatory activity in AD may be controlled at its origin, while pruritic symptoms are alleviated and epidermal barrier function improved, supporting a distinctive role for INH in the comprehensive management of AD. Furthermore, a clinical trial evaluating topical 10% INH cream for melasma reported favorable local tolerability,25 suggesting that topical administration may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for AD, as direct application to lesional skin may reduce systemic exposure and associated risks.

Application Prospects in Other Skin Diseases

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis predominantly driven by the IL-23/Th17 axis.26 Dysregulated immune activation and aberrant differentiation of keratinocytes (KCs) are central to its pathogenesis.27 IL-1β functions as both an initiating and amplifying mediator in this disorder. It synergizes with IL-23 to activate T cells and promote IL-17 production; IL-17 subsequently stimulates KCs to secrete additional IL-1β, thereby establishing a vicious inflammatory loop.28 Elevated IL-1β expression has been documented in psoriatic lesional skin and serum and correlates with disease severity and therapeutic response.29,30 Furthermore, biologics targeting IL-1β have demonstrated favorable efficacy in certain patients with severe pustular psoriasis.31 Human skin organ culture models have confirmed that IL-1β can induce psoriasis-like immunophenotypes in normal skin.32

Through inhibition of IL-1β maturation and release, INH may directly disrupt the aforementioned inflammatory circuit. Although psoriasis is primarily mediated by the IL-23/Th17 axis, Th1 responses exert synergistic effects,33 and modulation of Th1 activity by INH may confer additional benefit. This theoretical rationale can be further reinforced by considering the IL-1β/IL-23/Th17 axis: IL-1β has been shown to indirectly drive Th17 responses by inducing IL-23 production from dendritic cells and macrophages. Accordingly, INH-mediated suppression of IL-1β maturation might not only mitigate IL-1β-driven inflammation directly but also attenuate IL-23/Th17-dependent psoriatic inflammation.

Clinically, case reports have described improvement of cutaneous lesions in patients with severe psoriasis following INH therapy.34 In particular, two patients with latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) achieved remission after receiving oral INH at 300 mg daily for 6 months.35 However, it should be acknowledged that this evidence is of very low quality, being derived from isolated case reports involving only two patients. Moreover, the well-recognized potential for spontaneous remission in psoriasis further complicates the interpretation of these observations.

Collectively, the available evidence indicates that INH may attenuate psoriatic inflammation via suppression of IL-1β-dependent pathways and potential regulation of aberrant Th1 responses, although controlled clinical studies are critically needed to validate this hypothesis.

Urticaria

Urticaria is characterized by pruritic wheals and is pathogenetically associated with mast cell activation and the release of multiple inflammatory mediators. Elevated IL-1β levels have been detected in peripheral blood and lesional skin of patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria and demonstrate a positive correlation with disease severity.36,37 In pediatric patients with acute urticaria, serum IL-1β levels did not exhibit significant changes; however, IL-1RA levels were increased, which may represent a compensatory response to IL-1β activity.38 Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in mast cells, followed by IL-1β production, is regarded as an important pathogenic mechanism in urticaria.39 Clinically, anti-IL-1β therapy has demonstrated favorable efficacy in autoinflammatory conditions such as Schnitzler syndrome presenting with urticaria-like eruptions.40

As an inhibitor of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release, INH possesses theoretical potential to interfere with the pathogenic processes of certain forms of urticaria. The primary rationale for this therapeutic hypothesis rests on IL-1β inhibition, given the established role of this cytokine in mast cell‑driven urticarial inflammation. Although urticaria is not conventionally categorized as a Th1- or Th2-dominant disorder, immune dysregulation is implicated in some chronic cases, and the immunomodulatory effects of INH on T‑cell balance may be considered a secondary or adjunctive mechanism that could contribute to relapse control. This conceptual distinction is important to avoid overinterpretation of the available evidence and to accurately reflect the weight of the current data.

Rosacea

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis characterized by persistent facial erythema, telangiectasia, and inflammatory papules and pustules. Its pathogenesis is closely associated with dysregulated innate immune responses. Within lesional skin, the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TNF-α is markedly increased,41,42 and IL-1β levels have been reported to correlate positively with disease severity.43 In addition, elevated ROS production and activation of the NF-κB pathway are regarded as key pathogenic processes in rosacea.44 Notably, one therapeutic mechanism of minocycline, a commonly used clinical agent, involves inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, with subsequent reduction of IL-1β and ROS levels.45

Given that the pharmacologic actions of INH align closely with major pathogenic pathways implicated in rosacea, a potential regulatory role has been proposed. However, contemporary clinical evidence for INH in rosacea remains extremely limited. A single historical report from 1970 described the use of INH in rosacea management;46 however, given its age and irretrievability for independent verification, this citation should be regarded as a historical observation rather than credible supportive evidence. Its inherent limitations—including the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria, unclear treatment protocols, and lack of comparator groups—must be explicitly acknowledged. Consequently, any potential role for INH in rosacea management remains entirely speculative at present and warrants rigorous investigation using modern study designs.

Special Advantages: A Dual Anti-Inflammatory and Antitubercular Effects

A distinctive feature of INH lies in its capacity to provide combined anti-inflammatory activity and tuberculosis prophylaxis in selected patient populations.

Patients with LTBI

LTBI confers an ongoing risk of progression to active tuberculosis. INH remains a standard agent for preventing tuberculosis reactivation47 and may concurrently ameliorate cutaneous inflammation through its anti-inflammatory mechanisms, thereby offering dual clinical benefit.

Patients Receiving Biologics

Multiple biologics used in the management of inflammatory skin diseases, including TNF-α inhibitors for psoriasis48 and anti-IgE antibodies for chronic urticaria,49 are associated with an increased risk of tuberculosis infection or reactivation. This risk is particularly relevant in psoriasis, which itself may predispose patients to tuberculosis infection.50 In individuals requiring biologic therapy who are also at risk for tuberculosis, adjunctive INH administration may not only reduce tuberculosis risk but also contribute to improved disease control through potential synergy between its anti-inflammatory effects and biologic agents.

Discussion

The present review has several limitations. Most of the mechanistic data discussed are derived from in vitro and animal studies, which may not fully translate to human skin diseases. Clinical evidence supporting the use of isoniazid in inflammatory dermatoses remains sparse, consisting primarily of case reports and small case series without control groups. No randomized controlled trials have been published to date. Therefore, the therapeutic potential outlined here should be considered hypothesis‑generating rather than clinically established. Well‑designed prospective studies are urgently needed to validate the anti‑inflammatory effects of isoniazid in dermatological practice.

This review summarizes the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions of INH through multiple molecular targets, providing a mechanistic rationale for its potential use in skin diseases. The principal strengths of INH include its broad spectrum of activity—including inhibition of IL-1β–related pathways, modulation of Th1/Th2 balance, and induction of activated T-cell apoptosis—as well as its distinctive dual clinical utility. Such attributes may be particularly advantageous in regions with high tuberculosis prevalence or in selected clinical contexts requiring cost-effective interventions. Nonetheless, broader dermatologic application remains constrained by several unresolved issues, including the absence of standardized dosing regimens and treatment duration, the risk of hepatotoxicity with prolonged systemic exposure, and the need for additional validation of topical efficacy. Future studies should prioritize well-designed clinical trials and mechanistic research to define its safety and therapeutic effectiveness across diverse skin disorders and to evaluate potential synergy with existing treatment approaches.

The findings of this review should also be considered within the broader context of emerging regenerative and immunomodulatory therapies for skin inflammation and wound healing. Recent experimental studies have demonstrated that extracellular vesicle-based approaches—particularly exosome injections derived from plant or human sources—can attenuate cutaneous inflammation, promote angiogenesis, enhance collagen remodeling, and accelerate tissue repair.1,2 These observations underscore the growing interest in biologically driven strategies for inflammatory and regenerative dermatology. Compared with these complex biologic products, which face substantial manufacturing and regulatory challenges, INH offers the advantages of a well-characterized safety profile, low cost, and global availability, though its recognized toxicities require careful monitoring. Whether INH could serve as an adjunct to, or an alternative to, such advanced therapies—particularly in resource-constrained settings—warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

INH, a well-established anti-tuberculosis agent, has generated renewed interest in dermatologic therapy because of its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. Its multi-target mode of action, combined “anti-inflammatory–antituberculosis” potential, and economic advantages support its promise for clinical translation in inflammatory skin disorders, particularly among patients at risk for tuberculosis or in resource-limited settings. Although several obstacles remain, continued clinical and mechanistic investigations are anticipated to support the rational incorporation of INH into dermatologic practice.

To translate the preclinical findings discussed herein into clinically meaningful applications, future investigations should prioritize several key directions. First, prospective clinical trials targeting specific disease entities, with atopic dermatitis and psoriasis as high-priority candidates given their robust inflammatory and immunological underpinnings, are urgently warranted. Second, comparative studies evaluating oral versus topical INH administration are needed to determine the most appropriate route of delivery for dermatological indications. Third, dose-escalation studies should be conducted to establish the minimum effective dose that maintains an acceptable safety margin. Fourth, systematic safety surveillance (particularly concerning hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity, both established concerns associated with systemic INH therapy) must be integrated into all future trial designs. Collectively, these efforts will be essential to determine whether the promising preclinical signals reviewed here can be successfully translated into tangible clinical benefits for patients with inflammatory skin diseases.
