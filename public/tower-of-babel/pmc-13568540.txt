Pulmonary talcosis: An interesting autopsy revelation
Authors: Priya Suneja, BR Ashwini, Jyoti Srinivasaiah
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Pneumoconiosis is a spectrum of pulmonary diseases caused by chronic exposure to mineral dust, talc being one of them. Pulmonary talc granulomatosis is known to be caused either by inhalation secondary to occupational exposure or, rarely, by the intravenous route in drug abusers.[1] Herein, we present a case of pulmonary talcosis in a deceased drug abuser, diagnosed on histopathological examination post-autopsy.

A 42-year-old male, a known intravenous drug abuser of unknown nature since his teenage years, was brought to the emergency with abnormal movements of the limbs. After a thorough examination, he was diagnosed with status epilepticus. The patient was critically ill and died soon after, following which a post-mortem examination was conducted. Multiple injection marks were noted on bilateral upper limbs, and on opening the thoracic cavity, lungs were seen tightly adhered to the chest wall [Figure 1a] with pale white material oozing from one of the lungs [Figure 1b]. All other organs appeared unremarkable, and their representative parts were sent for histopathological examination. On gross examination of the lungs, the external surface and cut surface showed areas of consolidation. Multiple haematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stained sections examined revealed perivascular ill-defined non-necrotising granulomas with foreign body type giant cells, along with neutrophilic exudate and fibrinous material [Figure 1c] in the alveolar lumen and bronchioles. Surrounding alveoli showed focal oedema and septal capillary congestion. Occasional refractile particles were seen in the granulomas, which were further evaluated under polarising microscopy. On polarising microscopy, multiple needle-shaped birefringent foreign body particles were appreciated in the granulomas, confirming the presence of talc particles based on their appearance [Figure 1d-f]. Thus, a diagnosis of pulmonary talc granulomatosis was rendered. All other organs submitted for histopathological examination were grossly and microscopically unremarkable.

Pulmonary talc granulomatosis (or pulmonary talcosis) is a rare type of pneumoconiosis. Initially, it was a result of talc dust inhalation, but with time, it became more common as drug addicts started injecting dissolved oral drugs for recreational purposes. Drugs intended for oral use can be injected intravenously by grinding the tablets into powder, which often contains insoluble fillers such as cornstarch, talc, and microcrystalline cellulose.[2] Out of these substances, talc (hydrated magnesium silicate) is a common bulking agent in oral medications as it increases the mass, weight, and cost of the medication. In such individuals who inject oral medications, small particles of talc reach the lungs, enter the pulmonary vasculature and can then move into the interstitium and cause a granulomatous reaction. These foreign body granulomas are mostly perivascular in location, suggesting that foreign material reached the lung via venous circulation, which is a typical finding in an intravenous drug abuser. These granulomas are typically composed of free or intracellular birefringent deposits accompanied by multinucleated giant cells with few surrounding histiocytes.[3] These birefringent talc crystals can be visualised with polarised light. Also, larger talc crystals and perivascular distribution of talc granulomas are suggestive of intravenous drug abuse, unlike in cases of inhaled talc, where the deposition is predominantly around respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts.[1] On polarisation, talc crystals appear needle-shaped, microcrystalline cellulose appears rod-like, while starch has a maltese cross pattern, whereas crospovidone is non-birefringent on polarisation.[4] Intravenous drug abusers who develop talcosis present with progressive dyspnoea and symptoms of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[5] Chest radiography is usually insensitive but can show a diffuse micronodular pattern. In cases of injected material, computed tomography shows innumerable submillimetre micronodules having a centrilobular pattern and occasionally, the nodules display high attenuation. In cases with inhalational exposure, conglomerate masses are uncommon and involved areas contain foci of high attenuation resembling calcium.[5]

Since pulmonary talcosis has a low incidence, it is a diagnostic challenge for medical practitioners that can become even more difficult due to a lack of clinical suspicion and inaccessibility to drug abuse history. Therefore, there is a strong need for an appropriate history along with radiological correlation to come to a proper diagnosis.

Pulmonary talcosis presents a diagnostic challenge, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation and consideration of both industrial and non-industrial sources of foreign material in the lungs. A thorough clinical history, radiological correlation, and histopathological analysis are crucial for accurate diagnosis and subsequent management.
