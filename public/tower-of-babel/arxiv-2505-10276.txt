Chiral near-field control of quantum light generation using magneto-optical graphene
Mikkel Have Eriksen, Joel D. Cox

Abstract
We theoretically explore strategies to actively control photon emission from quantum light sources by leveraging the large magneto-optical response of graphene. The quantum electrodynamic response of graphene—characterized by the Purcell factor and the Lamb shift of a proximal emitter—is analyzed for extended two-dimensional sheets, one-dimensional nanoribbons, and zero-dimensional nanodisks, all of which are endowed with an intrinsic chiral near-field response under a static perpendicular magnetic field. Using rigorous semianalytical models of these systems, we reveal that the emission properties can be readily tuned by variations in doping charge carrier density and applied magnetic field strength, both with respect to magnetoplasmon resonances (at infrared frequencies) and Shubnikov-de-Haas oscillations (entering telecommunication bands) associated with optical transitions between discrete Landau levels. Localized magnetoplasmons in graphene nanoribbons are predicted to induce large dissymmetry in the spontaneous emission from left-hand and right-hand circularly polarized transitions in a proximal quantum emitter, presenting applications for chiral quantum optical waveguiding. This chiral dissymmetry is further enhanced in gyrotropic graphene nanodisks, signaling that the spatial shaping of near-fields in nanostructured graphene can significantly boost the intrinsic chiral response induced by the magnetic field. These results indicate that magneto-optical graphene constitutes a versatile and highly tunable platform for quantum light generation and manipulation at the nanoscale.



I
Introduction

Photon generation is of key importance not only for fundamental explorations in quantum physics
Couteau et al. 2023a
, but also in a plethora of light-based technological applications, such as super-resolution imaging
Koenderink et al. 2022
, quantum cryptography
Bozzio et al. 2022
, and quantum information processing
Couteau et al. 2023b
. While the numerous available quantum light sources—from natural atoms and molecules to engineered quantum dots and optically active defects—exhibit diverse photon emission rates and operational wavelengths
Aharonovich et al. 2016
;
Toninelli et al. 2021
, their properties are not easily controlled in an active manner. Fortunately, the spectral and temporal dynamics of quantum light emitters (QEs), along with the polarization state and directionality of the emitted photons, depend crucially on their electromagnetic environment and the associated local photonic density of states (LDOS)
Novotny and Hecht 2012
;
Barnes et al. 2020
. The interaction of QEs with engineered optical resonators thus enables modification of their spontaneous emission rate and transition energy through the Purcell effect and Lamb shift, respectively
Hohenester 2020
;
Verlekar et al. 2025
, while the LDOS can be tailored in nanophotonic environments to cause preferential generation of photons with certain polarization and propagation characteristics
Lodahl et al. 2017
. However, the development of integrated nanophotonic devices that enable active tuning of quantum light generation still presents a fundamental challenge.

The relatively strong light-matter interaction and optoelectronic tunability of graphene renders the carbon monolayer an appealing material platform for integrated photonics
Bonaccorso et al. 2010
. In electrically doped graphene, the optical response is dominated by collective oscillations of conduction electrons, i.e., plasmons, at frequencies
\hbar\omega\lesssim{E_{\rm F}}
, while interband transitions
\hbar\omega>2{E_{\rm F}}
lead to broadband
\sim 2.3\%
optical absorption
Gonçalves and Peres 2016
. For achievable doping levels
{E_{\rm F}}\sim 1
eV, graphene plasmons can thus intensify impinging electromagnetic fields in the terahertz (THz) and infrared (IR) spectral regimes
García de Abajo 2014
, while the absorption of visible light is impeded by Pauli blocking below the
\hbar\omega\simeq 2{E_{\rm F}}
threshold
Nair et al. 2008
. Depending on their relevant dipole-active transition energies, either of these mechanisms can be leveraged to actively control the spectral and temporal dynamics of QEs in the presence of graphene by modulating the free charge carrier concentration, e.g., through electrostatic gating
Tielrooij et al. 2015
;
Cano et al. 2020
;
Eriksen et al. 2022
. Complementing its active electrical tunability, the patterning of graphene into nanostructures supporting localized plasmon resonances enables further passive control over the associated near-fields
Brar et al. 2013
. The coupling of QEs with localized graphene plasmons in one-dimensional graphene nanoribbons or zero-dimensional nanodisks is predicted to enable strong vacuum Rabi splittings
Koppens et al. 2011
, superradiance
Huidobro et al. 2012
, and efficient energy transfer
Karanikolas et al. 2016
.

In addition to the electrical tunability achieved by modulating charge carrier doping levels, a static magnetic field applied normally to a graphene sheet can further tune its optical response
Peres et al. 2006
;
Neto et al. 2009
. In particular, the symmetry-breaking induced by a finite magnetic field induces a gyrotropic optical response in graphene
Ferreira et al. 2011
;
Sounas and Caloz 2011
, leading to a impressively large Faraday rotation associated with light traversing the 2D layer
Crassee et al. 2011
. Naturally, the applied magnetic field can also be leveraged as an additional knob to control the properties of graphene magnetoplasmon resonances operating at IR and THz frequencies
Yan et al. 2012
, which are anticipated to strongly enhance Faraday rotation
Tymchenko et al. 2013
, control spontaneous emission from a nearby QE
Kort-Kamp et al. 2015
;
Ma et al. 2022
, and drive a large chiroptical response
Eriksen et al. 2025
. However, the light emission characteristics of QEs interfacing localized magnetoplasmons in low-dimensional graphene nanostructures, including graphene nanoribbons and nanodisks, remains unexplored.

In this paper, we explore possibilities to actively control photon emission from QEs in the presence of extended or nanopatterned magneto-optical graphene monolayers. To do so, we develop rigorous semianalytical models to describe the quantum electrodynamic response of gyrotropic 2D materials and their low-dimensional forms, adopting the magneto-optical conductivity of graphene in the local limit of the random-phase approximation (RPA) to account for intra- and inter-band electronic transitions among the quantized Landau levels (LLs) induced by an applied static magnetic field
Ferreira et al. 2011
. Intriguingly, the induced LLs perturb the spectrally flat interband optical response of graphene at photon energies
\hbar\omega>2{E_{\rm F}}
, producing sharp spectral features known as Shubnikov-de-Haas (SdH) oscillations that are associated with the quantization of cyclotron orbits
Tan et al. 2011
. Starting from the case of an extended graphene sheet in the presence of a perpendicularly applied static magnetic field, we study the effects of the induced Hall conductivity, magnetoplasmon polaritons, and SdH oscillations on the quantum electrodynamic response of a proximal QE. We then turn to low-dimensional structures by considering graphene nanoribbons supporting magnetoplasmon polaritons that are localized in one spatial dimension. Here, the interplay of geometrical near-field confinement and the intrinsic gyrotropic response of the 2D layer is found to produce large dissymmetry in the spontaneous decay of a left-hand circularly polarized (LCP) dipole versus that of a right-hand circularly polarized (RCP) dipole, indicating a large chiral response associated with the excitation of guided magnetoplasmon polaritons. This chiral dissymmetry is further enhanced in zero-dimensional nanodisks, leading to an even stronger distinction of LCP and RCP photon generation. Our findings hold promise for developing quantum light sources with actively controllable chiral degrees of freedom.

VII
Conclusions

We present a comprehensive investigation of the electromagnetic near-fields provided by the proportionately large magneto-optical response of graphene. Our findings indicate that magneto-optical graphene constitutes a versatile platform to actively control quantum light generation, with both the free charge carrier density and the strength of the applied magnetic field offering unprecedented tunability over the photon generation rate and the spectral properties of emitted photons. In extended graphene, magnetoplasmons provide large Purcell factors and Lamb shifts at low energies, but are less tunable with respect to magnetic field and carrier density. In contrast, Shubnikov-de-Haas oscillations induced by moderate static magnetic fields provide sharp spectral variations in the local photonic density of states at energies
\hbar\omega\gtrsim 2{E_{\rm F}}
, corresponding to near-IR or telecommunications wavelengths at doping levels
{E_{\rm F}}\sim 0.5
eV that are achievable via electrostatic gating. Moreover, our calculations reveal the important role of Landau level degeneracy in the active electrical control of magneto-optical graphene, particularly under cryogenic conditions, where the discrete energy spectrum of electronic states can produce discontinuities in the spectral response as the Fermi energy is continuously varied and a reservoir of charge carriers is available. However, these spectral jumps to not appear if the charge carrier density is conserved in a closed system.

Simulations of optical extinction from graphene nanoribbons reveal qualitatively similar behavior as in extended samples, with localized magnetoplasmon resonances sustained by these low-dimensional systems offering a greater degree of electrical and magnetic tunability owing to lateral spatial confinement. The quantum electrodynamic response of an emitter interfacing magneto-optical graphene nanoribbons is similarly dominated by magnetoplasmons at low energies and SdH oscillations in the interband electronic transition regime. Interestingly, the magneto-optical graphene nanoribbon is shown to produce a large chiral response, quantified by substantial dissymmetry in the emission from left-hand and right-hand circularly polarized dipoles, which is at odds with the negligible chiral dissymmetry predicted for an extended graphene sheet. These results indicate that the geometrical shaping of near-fields in patterned graphene enable access to chiral light-matter interactions at the nanoscale. In a
lower
-dimensional system, represented by a graphene nanodisk, simulations reveal much greater dissymmetry factors for circularly polarized dipoles, indicating that magnetoplasmons in nanopatterned graphene can enable strong chiral control of quantum emitters.

Our findings are based on versatile semianalytical formalisms that we consolidate here to describe nanoscale light-matter interactions in gyrotropic 2D materials. While we have focused here on graphene, the framework we introduce here to describe the near-field response of nanoribbons and nanodisks can be generally applied to other 2D materials characterized by a gyrotropic conductivity tensor. We envision that our findings could inspire experimental investigations of quantum light generation in actively tunable magneto-optical nanophotonic environments.

k_{{\rm m}z}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{\rm m}\mu_{\rm m}\omega^{2}-k_{\parallel}}
denotes the normal component of the optical wave vector
{\bf k}_{\rm m}
in medium
{\rm m}
, expressed in terms of the conserved in-plane wave vector component
k_{\parallel}=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}
, and we have introduced the reflection and transmission coefficients
r_{\rm ss}
and
t_{\rm ss}
that relate incoming and outgoing “senkrecht-” or s-polarized TE fields. Invoking Faraday’s law in the frequency domain,
\nabla\times{\bf E}={\rm i}\omega\mu{\bf H}
, we obtain the corresponding magnetic field

\displaystyle H_{1x}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1z}E_{0}}{\omega\mu_{1}}\mathopen{}\left({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{1z}z}-r_{\rm ss}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}z}\right)\mathclose{},

(37a)

\displaystyle H_{2x}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{2z}E_{0}}{\omega\mu_{2}}t_{\rm ss}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{2z}z}.

(37b)

Now, to account for the generation of “parallel” or p-polarized TM fields, i.e., where the associated magnetic fields are entirely polarized in
\hat{\bf y}
, by s-polarized electromagnetic waves due to the off-diagonal 2D conductivity tensor components in Eq. (
34
), we introduce the cross-polarization Fresnel coefficients
r_{\rm ps}
and
t_{\rm ps}
according to

\displaystyle H_{1y}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}^{-1}E_{0}r_{\rm ps}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}z},

(38a)

\displaystyle H_{2y}

\displaystyle=Z_{2}^{-1}E_{0}t_{\rm ps}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{2z}z},

(38b)

where
Z_{\rm m}=\sqrt{\mu_{\rm m}/\epsilon_{\rm m}}
denotes the impedance in medium
{\rm m}
. From Ampere’s law,
\nabla\times{\bf H}=-{\rm i}\omega\epsilon{\bf E}
, the corresponding electric fields are

\displaystyle E_{1x}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1z}}{Z_{1}\omega\epsilon_{1}}E_{0}r_{\rm ps}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}z},

(39a)

\displaystyle E_{2x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k_{2z}}{Z_{2}\omega\epsilon_{2}}E_{0}t_{\rm ps}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{2z}z}.

(39b)

Introducing the above cross-polarized fields in the boundary conditions of Eq. (
35
) we obtain

\displaystyle 1+r_{\rm ss}-t_{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=0,

(40a)

\displaystyle\frac{k_{1z}}{\epsilon_{1}Z_{1}}r_{\rm ps}+\frac{k_{2z}}{\epsilon_{2}Z_{2}}t_{\rm ps}

\displaystyle=0,

(40b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{1}}r_{\rm ps}-\frac{1}{Z_{2}}t_{\rm ps}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{xx}\frac{k_{1z}}{\omega\epsilon_{1}Z_{1}}r_{\rm ps}-\sigma_{xy}(1+r_{\rm ss}),

(40c)

\displaystyle\frac{k_{2z}}{\omega\mu_{2}}t_{\rm ss}-\frac{k_{1z}}{\omega\mu_{1}}(1-r_{\rm ss})

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{yx}\frac{k_{1z}}{\omega\epsilon_{1}Z_{1}}r_{\rm ps}-\sigma_{yy}(1+r_{\rm ss}).

(40d)

Solving this system of linear equations using, e.g., Cramer’s rule, we isolate the reflection and transmission coefficients

\displaystyle r_{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(-)}+\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}},

(41a)

\displaystyle t_{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mu_{1}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}k_{1z}}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}},

(41b)

\displaystyle r_{\rm ps}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k_{2z}k_{1}}{k_{1z}k_{2}}t_{\rm ps}=-\frac{2}{Z_{1}}\frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}},

(41c)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(\pm)}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{k_{1z}}\pm\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{k_{2z}}\pm\frac{\sigma_{xx}}{\omega},

(42a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(\pm)}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{k_{1z}}{\mu_{1}}\pm\frac{k_{2z}}{\mu_{2}}\pm\omega\sigma_{yy}

(42b)

to simplify the expressions.

A.1.2
TM polarization

The case of TM polarized electromagnetic waves follows in an entirely analogous manner from the derivation for TE polarization by using the ansatz

\displaystyle H_{1y}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}^{-1}E_{0}\mathopen{}\left({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{1z}z}+r_{\rm pp}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}z}\right)\mathclose{},

(43a)

\displaystyle H_{2y}

\displaystyle=Z_{2}^{-1}E_{0}t_{\rm pp}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{2z}z},

(43b)

\displaystyle E_{1y}

\displaystyle=E_{0}r_{\rm sp}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}z},

(43c)

\displaystyle E_{2y}

\displaystyle=E_{0}t_{\rm sp}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k_{2z}z},

(43d)

which leads to the Fresnel coefficients

\displaystyle r_{\rm pp}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(-)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}+\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}},

(44a)

\displaystyle t_{\rm pp}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{Z_{1}}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}/\omega}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}},

(44b)

\displaystyle r_{\rm sp}

\displaystyle=t_{\rm sp}=\frac{2}{Z_{1}}\frac{\sigma_{yx}}{\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{(+)}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}^{(+)}-\sigma_{xy}\sigma_{yx}}.

(44c)

The Fresnel coefficients above for TE and TM polarization are in agreement with those reported in Ref.
Kort-Kamp et al. 2015
.

A.2
Green’s dyadic

The electric field produced at
{\bf r}
in a medium with permeability
\mu
by a point dipole with moment
{\bf p}
, oscillating at frequency
\omega
, and located at
{\bf r}^{\prime}
can be expressed as

{\bf E}({\bf r})=\mu\omega^{2}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{0}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})+\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\rm ref}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})\right]\cdot{\bf p},

(45)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\rm ref}
denote the bare and reflected parts, respectively, of the classical dyadic Green’s tensor
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}=\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{(0)}+\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\rm ref}
. Following Ref.
Hohenester 2020
, we evaluate the Green’s tensor for fields in a medium with isotropic permittivity
\epsilon_{1}
and permeability
\mu_{1}
above a planar interface in the
z=0
plane characterized by Fresnel reflection coefficients
r_{\alpha\beta}
associated with incoming (outgoing)
\alpha
-polarized (
\beta
-polarized) light for
\alpha,\beta\in\{{\rm s},{\rm p}\}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\omega,ij}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})=-\frac{\hat{z}_{i}\hat{z}_{j}}{k_{1}^{2}}\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\frac{{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}k_{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}k_{y}\frac{1}{k_{1z}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\mathopen{}\left[k_{x}(x-x^{\prime})+k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})\right]\mathclose{}}\begin{Bmatrix}\left[{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}|z-z^{\prime}|}e_{i}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})+r_{{\rm ss}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}(z+z^{\prime})}e_{i}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\right]\cdot e_{j}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})\\
+\left[{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}|z-z^{\prime}|}e_{i}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})+r_{{\rm pp}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}(z+z^{\prime})}e_{i}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\right]\cdot e_{j}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})\\
+r_{{\rm ps}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}(z+z^{\prime})}e_{i}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\cdot e_{j}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})\\
+r_{{\rm sp}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k_{1z}(z+z^{\prime})}e_{i}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\cdot e_{j}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})\end{Bmatrix},

(46)

where

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm}\times\hat{{\bf z}}}{|{\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm}\times\hat{{\bf z}}|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}}(k_{y}\hat{{\bf x}}-k_{x}\hat{{\bf y}}),

(47a)

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm})

\displaystyle={\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm}\times\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm})=\frac{\pm k_{{\rm m}z}}{k_{\rm m}\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}}(k_{x}\hat{{\bf x}}+k_{y}\hat{{\bf y}})-\frac{1}{k_{m}}\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\hat{{\bf z}}

(47b)

are polarization vectors given in terms of
{\bf k}_{\rm m}^{\pm}=k_{x}\hat{{\bf x}}+k_{y}\hat{{\bf y}}\pm k_{{\rm m}z}\hat{{\bf z}}
, with the sign of
k_{{\rm m}z}=\sqrt{k_{\rm m}^{2}-k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2}+{\rm i}0^{+}}
corresponding to upgoing (
+
) and downgoing (
-
) waves in media with permittivity
\epsilon_{\rm m}
and permeability
\mu_{\rm m}
.

The reflected part of the Green’s tensor in Eq. (
46
) can be written in the form

\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\rm ref}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime})=\frac{{\rm i}}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}k_{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}k_{y}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\mathopen{}\left[k_{x}(x-x^{\prime})+k_{y}(y-y^{\prime})+k_{1z}(z+z^{\prime})\right]\mathclose{}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{M}^{\rm ss}+\mathcal{M}^{\rm pp}+\mathcal{M}^{\rm sp}+\mathcal{M}^{\rm ps}\right)\mathclose{},

(48)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{{\rm ss}}}{k_{1z}}\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\otimes\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})=\frac{r_{{\rm ss}}}{k_{1z}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}k_{y}^{2}&-k_{x}k_{y}&0\\
-k_{x}k_{y}&k_{x}^{2}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(49a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\rm pp}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{{\rm pp}}}{k_{1z}}\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\otimes\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})=\frac{r_{{\rm pp}}}{k_{1}^{2}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}\pm k_{1z}k_{x}^{2}&\pm k_{x}k_{y}k_{1z}&-k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
\pm k_{x}k_{y}k_{1z}&\pm k_{1z}k_{y}^{2}&-k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
\mp k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&\mp k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{2}/k_{1z}\end{bmatrix},

(49b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\rm ps}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{{\rm ps}}}{k_{1z}}\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\otimes\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})=\frac{r_{{\rm ps}}}{k_{1}k_{1z}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}k_{x}k_{y}k_{1z}&-k_{x}^{2}k_{1z}&0\\
k_{y}^{2}k_{1z}&-k_{x}k_{y}k_{1z}&0\\
-k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})&0\end{bmatrix},

(49c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\rm sp}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{{\rm sp}}}{k_{1z}}\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm s}({\bf k}_{1}^{+})\otimes\hat{{\bf e}}^{\rm p}({\bf k}_{1}^{\pm})=\frac{r_{{\rm sp}}}{k_{1}k_{1z}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})}\begin{bmatrix}\pm k_{1z}k_{y}k_{x}&\pm k_{1z}k_{y}^{2}&-k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
\mp k_{x}^{2}k_{1z}&\mp k_{y}k_{x}k_{1z}&k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(49d)

The dipole self-interaction is then described by evaluating the integral of Eq. (
48
) in cylindrical coordinates for
z=z^{\prime}
by choosing the lower sign in Eqs. (
48
). Assuming that the reflection coefficients are independent of the azimuthal angle, i.e.,
r_{\alpha\beta}(k_{\parallel},\phi)\to r_{\alpha\beta}(k_{\parallel})
, the Green’s tensor reduces to Eq. (
14
).

Appendix B
Near-field response of a one-dimensional ribbon

Transforming the external field in Eq. (
23
) as
{\bf E}_{q}^{\rm ext}(x,\omega)=\int{\rm d}y{\bf E}^{\rm ext}({\bf R},\omega){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}qy}
, we find

{\bf E}_{q}^{\rm ext}(x,\omega)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{1}}\tensor{g}_{q}(x,{\bf r}_{0})\cdot{\bf p},

(50)

where the tensor
\tensor{g}_{q}(x,{\bf r})
is defined in Eq. (
25
). To determine the Green’s dyadic for a ribbon, we compute the induced electric field acting back on the dipole at
{\bf r}_{0}
in a medium with permittivity
\epsilon_{1}
and permeability
\mu_{1}
according to

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{\rm ind}({\bf r}_{0},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\rm eff}}\nabla_{{\bf r}_{0}}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bf R}\frac{\rho^{\rm ind}({\bf R},\omega)}{\mathopen{}\left|{\bf r}_{0}-{\bf R}\right|\mathclose{}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{4\pi\epsilon_{\rm eff}\omega}\int{\rm d}x\int\frac{{\rm d}q}{2\pi}\tensor{g}_{q}^{*}(x,{\bf r}_{0})\cdot{\bf J}_{q}(x,\omega),

where we have again interchanged the induced charge and current densities using the continuity equation and integrated by parts. Inserting the current of Eq. (
21
) in the above expression, we isolate the reflected part of the Green’s dyadic from the definition
{\bf E}^{\rm ind}({\bf r}_{0},\omega)=\omega^{2}\mu_{1}\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\rm ref}({\bf r}_{0},{\bf r}_{0})\cdot{\bf p}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\rm ref}_{\omega}({\bf r}_{0},{\bf r}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{\rm eff}\omega k_{1}^{2}}\int{\rm d}x\int\frac{{\rm d}q}{2\pi}\tensor{g}_{q}^{*}(x,{\bf r}_{0})

\displaystyle\cdot\mathcal{M}_{q,\omega}(x)\cdot f(x)\sigma(\omega)\cdot\tensor{g}_{q}(x,{\bf r}_{0}).

(51)

Equipped with the above Green’s tensor, we compute the Purcell factor and Lamb shift experienced by a quantum emitter near a ribbon comprised of a 2D material with an arbitrary intrinsic optical conductivity.

Incidentally, the interaction between a dipole
{\bf p}_{1}
located at
{\bf r}_{1}
and another dipole
{\bf p}_{2}
at
{\bf r}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\rm ref}_{\omega}({\bf r}_{2},{\bf r}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{16\pi^{2}\epsilon_{\rm eff}\omega k_{1}^{2}}\int{\rm d}x\int\frac{{\rm d}q}{2\pi}\tensor{g}_{q}^{*}(x,{\bf r}_{2})

\displaystyle\cdot\mathcal{M}_{q,\omega}(x)\cdot f(x)\sigma(\omega)\cdot\tensor{g}_{q}(x,{\bf r}_{1})

(52)

that mediates the field produced by
{\bf p}_{1}

{\bf p}_{2}

B.1
Ribbon discretization scheme

The real-space discretization scheme used to obtain the current in Eq. (
21
) is based on the approach outlined in Refs.
Christensen et al. 2015
;
Rasmussen et al. 2023
. Specifically, we discretize the space along
x
into
L
equidistant points
\{\theta_{l}\}
, where
\theta=x/W
is the normalized coordinate, and include points beyond the ribbon to account for the vanishing current at the boundaries
Christensen et al. 2015
. The current is similarly discretized as
{\bf J}_{q,l}={\bf J}_{q}(\theta_{l})
, along with other spatially dependent quantities. Adopting a five-point stencil scheme (resulting in fourth-order accuracy in the step size
h=\theta_{l+1}-\theta_{l}
), the first- and second-order derivative operators away from the ribbon boundaries are

\displaystyle\partial_{x}{\bf J}_{q,l}\approx\frac{1}{12h}\left(\delta_{l-2,l^{\prime}}-8\delta_{l-1,l^{\prime}}+8\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}-\delta_{l+2,l^{\prime}}\right){\bf J}_{q,l^{\prime}},

(53a)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}{\bf J}_{q,l}\approx\frac{1}{12h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-\delta_{l-2,l^{\prime}}+16\delta_{l-1,l^{\prime}}-30\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\\
+16\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}-\delta_{l+2,l^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}{\bf J}_{q,l^{\prime}}.

(53b)

At the boundary of the computational domain, the five-point central-difference formulation above is not applicable, and so instead we implement one-sided first- and second-order derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{x}{\bf J}_{q,l}\approx\frac{1}{12h}\begin{pmatrix}-25\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}+48\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}-36\delta_{l+2,l^{\prime}}\\
+16\delta_{l+3,l^{\prime}}-3\delta_{l+4,l^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}{\bf J}_{q,l^{\prime}},

(54a)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}{\bf J}_{q,l}\approx\frac{1}{12h^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}35\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}-104\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}+114\delta_{l+2,l^{\prime}}\\
-56\delta_{l+3,l^{\prime}}+11\delta_{l+4,l^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}{\bf J}_{q,l^{\prime}},

(54b)

where the first-order derivative is of order four and the second-order derivative is of order three
Fornberg 1988
.

The integral kernel is similarly discretized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{q}{\bf J}_{q}(x_{l},\omega)=2\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}K_{0}\mathopen{}\left(|q||x_{l}-x^{\prime}|\right)\mathclose{}{\bf J}_{q}(x^{\prime},\omega).

(55)

Assuming a slowly varying
{\bf J}_{q}(x,\omega)
and equidistant discretization
\theta_{l}
, we decompose the operator
\mathcal{V}_{q}
as a matrix according to
\mathcal{V}_{q}{\bf J}_{q}(x_{l},\omega)=\sum_{l}\mathcal{V}_{q,ll^{\prime}}{\bf J}_{q,l^{\prime}}
, with matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{q,ll^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\theta_{l^{\prime}}-h/2}^{\theta_{l^{\prime}}+h/2}d\theta^{\prime}K_{0}\mathopen{}\left(|q||\theta_{l}-\theta^{\prime}|\right)\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\pi\sum_{\tilde{\theta}=\theta_{ll^{\prime}}\pm h/2}(\pm\tilde{\theta})\begin{bmatrix}K_{0}(|q\tilde{\theta}|)\mathcal{L}_{-1}(|q\tilde{\theta}|)\\
+K_{1}(|q\tilde{\theta}|)\mathcal{L}_{0}(|q\tilde{\theta}|)\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the modified Struve function of order
n

Olver et al.
. Note that in the limit of
q\to 0
, the kernel of the operator
\mathcal{V}
can be expanded as

K_{0}\mathopen{}\left(|q||\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\right)\mathclose{}\sim-\log\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right|\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}-\log(|q|)+\log(2)-\gamma_{\rm EM},

(56)

where
\gamma_{\rm EM}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Invoking the charge neutrality condition, we find that the
\theta^{\prime}
-independent terms can be ignored
Christensen et al. 2015
, leading to

\mathcal{V}_{0,ll^{\prime}}=2\sum_{\pm}(\pm)\mathopen{}\left(\theta_{ll^{\prime}}\pm h/2\right)\mathclose{}\mathopen{}\left(1-\log\mathopen{}\left|\theta_{ll^{\prime}}\pm h/2\right|\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}.

(57)

In practice, we obtain well-converged results using discretization of
L\sim 250
points.

Appendix C
Optical response of a gyrotropic disk

Following the prescription of Ref.
Eriksen et al. 2025
to compute the polarizability of a gyrotropic nanodisk, we express the 2D charge density in a disk of diameter
D
in cylindrical coordinates
{\bf R}=(r,\varphi)
and expand in Jacobi polynomials
P_{j}^{(|l|,0)}(r)
according to
Fetter 1986
;
Christensen et al. 2014

\rho^{\rm ind}({\bf R})=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}^{(l)}\tilde{r}^{|l|}P_{j}^{(|l|,0)}(1-2\tilde{r}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}l\varphi},

(58)

where
\tilde{r}=2r/D
is the normalized radial coordinate. Inserting the density into the Poisson equation and performing algebraic manipulations, we obtain a matrix equation for the vector
{\bf a}^{(l)}
containing the expansion coefficients
a_{j}^{(l)}
introduced above as

\mathopen{}\left[\frac{{\rm i}\epsilon_{\rm eff}\omega D}{\sigma_{\parallel}}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\parallel}-{\rm i}\,{\rm sgn}(l)\sigma_{\perp}}{\sigma_{\parallel}+{\rm i}\,{\rm sgn}(l)\sigma_{\perp}}\mathcal{A}^{(l)}+\mathcal{B}^{(l)}\right)\mathclose{}+\mathcal{C}^{(l)}\right]\mathclose{}{\bf a}^{(l)}={\bf b}^{(l)},

(59)

where the matrices
\mathcal{A}^{(l)}
,
\mathcal{B}^{(l)}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{(l)}
are comprised of elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(l)}_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{0j}\delta_{0j^{\prime}}}{8|l|(|l|+1)^{2}},

(60a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8(|l|+2j+1)(|l|+2j^{\prime}+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\delta_{jj^{\prime}}+\delta_{j,j^{\prime}+1}}{|l|+2j}\\
+\frac{\delta_{jj^{\prime}}+\delta_{j+1,j^{\prime}}}{|l|+2j+2}\end{pmatrix},

(60b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{j-j^{\prime}+2}}{\pi[4(j-j^{\prime})^{2}-1](|l|+j+j^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2})(|l|+j+j^{\prime}+\tfrac{3}{2})},

(60c)

while the vector
{\bf b}^{(l)}
contains entries

b_{j}^{(l)}=\frac{4\epsilon_{\rm eff}}{D}\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}\tilde{r}\tilde{r}^{|l|+1}P_{j}^{(|l|,0)}(1-2\tilde{r}^{2})\phi^{\rm ext}_{l}(\tilde{r})

(61)

that depend on the quantities

\phi_{l}^{\rm ext}(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\varphi}{2\pi}\Phi^{\rm ext}({\bf R}){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}l\varphi}

(62)

associated with the external potential acting on the points
{\bf R}=(r,\varphi)
within the nanodisk. Importantly, the matrix elements given in Eq. (
60
) are only valid for
l\neq 0
, whereas the
l=0
case omits the indices
j=j^{\prime}=0
, such that
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}=0
, while the other vector- and matrix-indices instead start at
j=j^{\prime}=1

Fetter 1986
;
Muniz et al. 2020
;
Eriksen et al. 2025
. For the purpose of this paper, we truncate the matrices at
j\sim 200
that ensures convergence.

Once the expansion coefficients of the charge density are determined, the scalar potential at any point in the environment can be computed from

\Phi^{\rm ind}({\bf r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\rm eff}}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bf R}^{\prime}\frac{\rho^{\rm ind}({\bf R}^{\prime})}{\mathopen{}\left|{\bf r}-{\bf R}^{\prime}\right|\mathclose{}}.

(63)

Expanding the spatial dependence of the Coulomb interaction according to Eq. (
27
) and the charge density in Eq. (
58
), we write the induced potential as
\Phi^{\rm ind}({\bf r})=\sum_{l}\phi_{l}^{\rm ind}(r,z){\rm e}^{{\rm i}l\varphi}
and use the orthogonality condition
\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\varphi{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\varphi(l-l^{\prime})}=2\pi\delta_{ll^{\prime}}
to isolate the quantities

\displaystyle\phi_{l}^{\rm ind}(r,z)=

\displaystyle\frac{D}{4\epsilon_{\rm eff}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}^{(l)}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\tilde{r}^{\prime}\tilde{r}^{\prime|l|+1}P_{j}^{(|l|,0)}(1-2\tilde{r}^{\prime 2})

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}kJ_{|l|}(k\tilde{r})J_{|l|}(k\tilde{r}^{\prime}){\rm e}^{-k(\tilde{z}_{>}-\tilde{z}_{<})},

where
\tilde{z}\equiv 2z/D
. Making use of the integral relation

\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}xx^{|l|+1}J_{|l|}(kx)P_{j}^{(|l|,0)}(1-2x^{2})=\frac{1}{k}J_{|l|+2j+1}(k),

(64)

the components of the induced potential reduce to

\displaystyle\phi_{l}^{\rm ind}(r,z)=

\displaystyle\frac{D}{4\epsilon_{\rm eff}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}^{(l)}

{\bf p}
located at

to identify the reflected Green’s tensor components.

In cylindrical coordinates, we evaluate the induced field

\displaystyle{\bf E}^{\rm ind}({\bf r})=-\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\rm eff}}\sum_{l,j}a_{j}^{(l)}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}l\varphi}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}kJ_{|l|+2j+1}(k){\rm e}^{-k|\tilde{z}|}\begin{Bmatrix}\mathopen{}\left[J_{|l|-1}(k\tilde{r})-J_{|l|+1}(k\tilde{r})\right]\mathclose{}\hat{{\bf r}}\\
+(2{\rm i}l/k\tilde{r})J_{|l|}(k\tilde{r})\hat{\varphi}\\
\mp 2J_{|l|}(k\tilde{r})\hat{\bf z}\end{Bmatrix},

(66)

where we have used the relation
\partial_{x}J_{\nu}(x)=\mathopen{}\left[J_{\nu-1}(x)-J_{\nu+1}(x)\right]\mathclose{}/2
for Bessel functions and assumed that the disk lies in the
z=0
plane, such that the upper and lower signs in the
z

\tilde{z}>0
and
\tilde{z}<0

\tilde{r}=0

J_{0}(0)=1
,
J_{1}(0)=J_{2}(0)=0
. In what follows, we consider the induced fields produced by orienting the dipole normally (
{\bf p}=p\hat{{\bf z}}
) or parallel (
{\bf p}=p\hat{{\bf x}}

|l|=1

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}xJ_{\nu}(\beta x){\rm e}^{-\alpha x}=\beta^{-\nu}\left(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}-\alpha\right)^{\nu}/\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}

(68)

for dipoles oriented parallel or perpendicular to the disk, respectively. Importantly, the Green’s tensor component

\mathcal{G}_{\omega,yx}^{\rm ref}
