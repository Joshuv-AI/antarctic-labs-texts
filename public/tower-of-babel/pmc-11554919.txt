Minimising Harm and Managing Pain: Deprescribing Opioids in Older Adults
Authors: Aili V Langford, Carl R Schneider, Emily Reeve, Danijela Gnjidic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximately one in three older adults (aged 65 years and over) experience pain, negatively impacting their quality of life. Opioid analgesics are commonly prescribed to manage pain; however, balancing the benefits and harms of these high-risk analgesics can be challenging for both healthcare professionals and patients. This is particularly true for older adults, as factors such as polypharmacy, age-related physiological changes and cognitive decline may impact upon opioid safety and efficacy. Deprescribing is the patient-centred process of reducing or discontinuing a medication that is no longer appropriate, or where the risks of continuation are deemed to outweigh the anticipated benefits. Opioid deprescribing has been proposed as a mechanism to reduce individual and societal opioid-related harm; however, to date, research has predominantly focused on the general adult population, rather than older adults. This current opinion aims to summarise the existing opioid deprescribing literature, discussing its applicability for older adults. Drawing on a non-systematic review of the literature, it identifies unique challenges and considerations for this population, highlights international initiatives to enhance opioid deprescribing in clinical practice and proposes future directions to advance the field.

Introduction

Pain and pain-related conditions are a leading cause of disability and disease burden globally [1], with one in five adults reporting persistent pain [2, 3]. While pain is ubiquitous and affects all populations, a range of social, physical and economic factors leads to a differential burden in pain prevalence and severity [4]. Disparities in the perception, assessment and treatment of pain exist, with an unequal burden placed on certain patient subgroups [5], including racial and ethnic minorities [6, 7], people living in rural and remote areas [8], those with a low socioeconomic status [9] and older adults [10]. One in three older adults (aged ≥ 65 years) and up to 80% of people residing in residential aged care facilities live with pain [11, 12], contributing to a range of serious sequelae, including, but not limited to, depression, disrupted sleep, social isolation and reduced quality of life [13].

It is imperative that pain is adequately managed to limit suffering, as well as reduce the societal burdens of decreased productivity and increased healthcare expenditure [14]. Pharmacological therapies, including opioid analgesics, are routinely prescribed to manage pain. However, balancing their potential benefit with the risk of medication-related harm can be challenging for healthcare professionals and patients alike [15, 16]. This is particularly true for older adults, where pharmacological pain management is often complicated by the presence of multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, physiological changes associated with ageing and cognitive decline, making some medications contraindicated and increasing the risk of adverse drug events [10].

Deprescribing, the patient-centred process of reducing or discontinuing medications that are no longer appropriate [17], is increasingly being recognised as mechanism to mitigate medication-related harm. This current opinion aims to explore the complexities of opioid deprescribing and unique considerations for the older adult population. Drawing on a non-systematic review of the literature, it discusses research and clinical practice initiatives implemented internationally to optimise opioid deprescribing and suggests future directions for advancing the field.

Pain in the Older Adult

Pain is a common symptom experienced by older adults and undertreated pain can have devastating consequences, including a reduced quality of life [18], changed behavioural symptoms [19] and increased care dependency [20]. The aetiology of pain is complex, presenting both as a symptom of an underlying disease or injury, or as a distinct pathological condition [21]. The multifactorial nature of pain and the inherent variability across the older adult population necessitate multi-modal and individualised management approaches, targeting all components of the biopsychosocial model of pain [22]. Pain management guidelines advocate for such an approach, prioritising non-pharmacological treatments (e.g. physical therapy, psychological therapy, pain education), followed by non-opioid analgesics, and opioids when other medications are ineffective or not tolerated [23, 24]. For certain pain conditions, other medications may be used as adjuvant therapies or to treat the underlying cause of the pain (e.g. carbamazepine in trigeminal neuralgia, gabapentanoids for neuropathic pain, or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs for rheumatoid arthritis).

Despite the demonstrated benefits of non-pharmacological pain management strategies for older adults [25], compared with younger adults, older adults are less likely to use these approaches to manage their pain [26]. Patient-related barriers to implementing non-pharmacological strategies include high treatment costs, low motivation, scepticism about efficacy, fear of adverse effects, as well as limitations in physical and cognitive capacity [27, 28]. In the residential aged care setting, additional barriers include a lack of staff training, time and funding to implement non-pharmacological care [29]. As such, single-modality analgesic treatments are often preferentially prescribed and continued. Cognitive impairment associated with dementia is also common among older adults and may hinder the ability to communicate pain effectively, leading to undertreatment. Conversely, over-reliance on pharmacological interventions in the absence of pain, especially opioids, may exacerbate cognitive decline and increase the risk of falls and other complications [30, 31].

The Role of Opioids in the Pharmacological Management of Pain

Analgesia has traditionally been prescribed in a stepwise manner, where non-opioid analgesics such as acetaminophen or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are initially prescribed (dependent on the individual’s precautions and co-morbidities) followed by the introduction of opioids, if required [32]. Regular use of oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs is not routinely recommended for older adults, owing to heightened gastrointestinal, cardiovascular and renal risks [33]. Similarly, whilst opioids are an effective component of acute pain management and integral to end-of-life care (for both pain relief and management of shortness of breath), for many, opioids do not provide longer term, clinically important improvements in pain or function compared to placebo or non-opioid medications [34, 35]. Additionally, opioids confer a significant risk of harm, with approximately 80% of people taking them for 3 months or more experiencing adverse effects, with a range from mild (e.g. nausea, constipation) through to severe (e.g. respiratory depression, impaired cognition, dependence, overdose) [36]. Older adults are more susceptible to these adverse effects [37], with factors such as reduced renal function, polypharmacy and multimorbidity leading to an increased susceptibility of opioid accumulation, drug or disease interactions, and a smaller therapeutic window between safe dosages and dosages associated with adverse effects [10, 38]. The American Geriatrics Association proposes that in properly selected and monitored patients, opioids constitute a potentially effective and, for some older adults, indispensable treatment for persistent pain [18]. Regular assessments of pain, functional status and quality of life for people prescribed opioids may assist with the monitoring and mitigation of opioid-related risks, as outlined in Fig. 1. However, as older adults are under-represented in clinical trials testing pharmacological pain treatments [39], the impacts of polypharmacy, frailty, cognitive impairment, and altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics on efficacy and side effect profiles are not well established [40, 41]. Thus, balancing possible benefits of pain reduction with known and unknown risks of treatment can be even more challenging, necessitating shared decision making between the patient and healthcare provider to determine the best treatment approach for each individual.

Guiding principles for opioid deprescribing in older adults

Over the last three decades, an increase in the use of prescription opioids has been observed [42], particularly in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries [43]. In the USA, approximately one in eight older adults are reported to take long-term opioid pain medications [44], and in Australia, long-term opioid use is 4.8 times higher among those aged 80 years and older compared with those aged 18–34 years [45]. Rates of opioid use may be even higher in aged care facilities, with a recent systematic review reporting that one in three residents are regularly charted opioids [46]. Therefore, for older adults in which risks of continuation are deemed to outweigh the benefits, there is an impetus for healthcare professionals and patients to engage in shared decision making and determine the appropriateness of trialling opioid deprescribing [47].

Deprescribing as a Strategy to Reduce Opioid Overuse and Harm

Deprescribing is a crucial component of rational prescribing [48], and refers to the patient-centred process of withdrawal of an inappropriate medication, supervised by a healthcare professional with the goal of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes [17], a concept now at the forefront of geriatric medicine globally. The potential harms, coupled with the uncertain benefits of long-term therapy in older adults, render opioids a key therapeutic target for deprescribing.

Opioids have been reported to be a particularly challenging medicine to deprescribe, owing to a range of factors, including the development of physical and psychological dependence and a lack of effective and accessible pain management alternatives. Additionally, the potential for opioid withdrawal symptoms is further heightened in older adults and may be more difficult to detect [49]. A growing body of qualitative research has explored the perspectives of stakeholders on opioid deprescribing, detailing a range of barriers to implementation in practice. A recent qualitative evidence synthesis identified five overarching barriers to deprescribing opioids for chronic non-cancer pain, namely; (i) limited alternatives to opioids, (ii) management of pain is a top priority, (iii) patient understanding, expectations and experiences, (iv) prescriber pressures and (v) reluctance to change [50]. Anticipated patient resistance to opioid deprescribing is commonly reported by healthcare professionals [16, 51–53], despite research continuing to purport patients willingness to engage in opioid reduction [15, 54, 55]. A recent study published in Drugs & Aging examined the perspectives of older adults residing in the USA [55], identifying similar facilitators to successful opioid deprescribing as previously reported by younger Australian adults [15]. Patient-provider trust, clear communication and expectations for deprescribing, gradual medication reduction, and support for patients and prescribers have been identified as key [15, 16, 50, 55]. A need for tailored resources to support patient and healthcare professionals to have conversations about the risks and benefits of continued opioid use and engage in shared decision making about opioid deprescribing has also been consistently identified [15, 54], with an opioid tapering conversation guide having been developed for this purpose [56].

Although there is substantial evidence demonstrating the potential harms of long-term opioid use, less is known about the clinical outcomes of opioid deprescribing. Recent reviews indicate that opioid deprescribing is feasible and may lead to improved or unchanged pain outcomes; however, the evidence is not specific to older adults and is predominantly low certainty [57–59]. While existing evidence does not indicate that opioid deprescribing increases adverse events [57, 58, 60], population-based studies have demonstrated an association between an opioid reduction and a higher risk of unintended severe harms, such as suicide and mental health crises, particularly when opioid deprescribing is unsolicited [61, 62]. This disparity may be because opioid deprescribing conducted under research conditions likely employs appropriate shared decision making, gradual tapering, close monitoring and support. In contrast, population-based studies may capture deprescribing occurring in real-world conditions, where there are significant limitations to deprescribing best practices.

Despite a lack of high-certainty evidence on the outcomes of opioid deprescribing in older adults, we propose a number of best-practice principles (Fig. 1). These guiding principles support a person-centred approach to opioid deprescribing, encouraging active patient involvement in creating a personalised deprescribing plan that aligns with the individual’s goals, values, preferences and clinical characteristics. They emphasise setting realistic expectations about the potential benefits and harms of opioids at the point of initiation, as well as identifying therapeutic goals, using a shared decision-making model. Additionally, they highlight the importance of regular monitoring and ongoing support throughout the deprescribing process.

International Opioid Deprescribing Initiatives

There are numerous efforts internationally to investigate and promote safe and effective opioid deprescribing, providing opportunities to learn from and leverage international progress in this field. In response to the identified need for greater opioid deprescribing guidance [15, 16, 63], the Australian Evidence Based Clinical Practice Guideline for Deprescribing Opioid Analgesics was published in 2023 [64]. This guideline was developed by a multidisciplinary team with extensive stakeholder input from healthcare professionals, patients, professional and consumer organisations, and government departments [15, 16, 65]. It presents recommendations on when, when not (e.g. end-of-life care) and how to deprescribe opioids in primary care, recognising the importance of shared decision making in all opioid deprescribing decisions [64]. It highlights that older adults are high consumers of opioids, have high rates of inappropriate long-term use and are at a greater risk of harm. Despite this, there are no recommendations targeting older adults, noting a lack of evidence pertaining to this population. Additionally, no evidence specific to people living with dementia or frailty, populations known to be at increased risk of opioid-related harm, was identified [64]. The USA’s 2022 National Centres for Disease Control (CDC) Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Pain has a broader remit, but similarly recommends the gradual tapering and discontinuation of opioids if risks of continuation outweigh benefit [24]. These guidelines address an important gap in providing evidence-based deprescribing advice for healthcare professionals [66]; however, further research is needed into how to best support their implementation in practice.

Internationally, large clinical trials investigating patient-directed opioid deprescribing interventions have yielded mixed findings. The I-WOTCH (Improving the Wellbeing of People With Opioid Treated Chronic Pain) randomised controlled trial, conducted in the UK, demonstrated that a multi-component opioid-tapering self-management intervention substantially reduced patient-reported opioid use compared with usual care [67]. After 12 months, 29% of people in the intervention arm (mean age of 62 years, standard deviation 11.9 years) had stopped taking opioids, compared with 7% of those in the usual care arm. At the follow-up, there was no difference between the two groups in terms of their pain, or how pain interfered with their lives [67]. This group-based educational intervention provided ongoing support from facilitators and peers throughout the tapering process, elements that were determined to be integral to the intervention’s success [68]. Conversely, in Canada, the TAPERING Trial (Trial Applying Policy to Eliminate or Reduce Inappropriate Narcotics in the General Population) evaluated the effect of implementing a direct-to-consumer educational intervention (20-page brochure mailed to participants) on the cessation and reduction of long-term opioid prescriptions [69]. This light-touch intervention produced no difference in the prevalence of opioid cessation compared with usual care (mean age of 60 years, standard deviation 14.4 years) [69]. More participants in the intervention group than in the control group reduced their dose; however, the authors postulate that a range of other system-level strategies implemented in Canada to curb opioid use at the time of the trial may have contributed to the lack of effect for the primary outcome of cessation [69]. These two trials were conducted in different countries with varying healthcare contexts, but it appears that the more intensive intervention, which provided ongoing healthcare professional and peer support throughout intervention delivery, was able to produce greater opioid reductions, whilst maintaining pain and functional outcomes. These trials were not specific to older adults, and it is possible that different approaches may be needed for this population when considering factors such as polypharmacy and cognitive impairment. Therefore, future trials targeting older adults will produce much needed evidence on the effectiveness of interventions and the outcomes of opioid deprescribing, specific to this population [70].

Future Directions

As opioid deprescribing continues to gain traction in research and in practice, we propose specific focus areas to advance the field.

Filling Critical Evidence Gaps

Opioid deprescribing evidence is primarily low certainty [59]. There are critical evidence gaps related to the effectiveness and safety of different opioid deprescribing interventions, as well as a lack of comparative studies to provide guidance on preferred deprescribing approaches. Evidence on how to prevent the transition from short-term to long-term opioid use, particularly at transitions of care, would similarly be of value. Robust clinical trials that evaluate deprescribing interventions will likely be informative; however, there is a need to purposively recruit older adults who are currently under-represented in research studies, including those living with frailty or cognitive impairment. Strategies to achieve this may include fostering collaborative partnerships with healthcare providers and community organisations that serve older adults, engaging family and caregivers, and tailoring enrolment, consent and participation processes to the specific needs of this population. Pragmatic trials will likely be useful in this context [71]. Given that clinical trials take significant time before the findings are known, robust cohort and longitudinal studies that utilise routinely collected data may also provide important insights into the long-term benefits and harms of deprescribing for these populations [72].

Moving Beyond Barriers and Facilitators

Successful opioid deprescribing requires an understanding of barriers and facilitators from the perspective of key stakeholders. Much work has been done to identify these factors, and it is now essential to progress by translating findings into changed practice and policy. Priority areas flagged in recent qualitative studies include the development of resources for older adults and clinicians, to ensure that both patients and providers share a common understanding of the goals of opioid therapy [73]. Additionally, developing and validating context-specific conversation guides to facilitate opioid deprescribing discussions will likely be beneficial [15, 54]. At a system level, stakeholders have called for reforms to health systems to allow for additional training and time to facilitate opioid deprescribing in practice. Research conducted to date will be informative in leveraging such changes at a national level.

Considering Context

It is broadly acknowledged and accepted that opioid deprescribing needs to be individualised to meet the needs, goals and preferences of individuals, as well as tailored for the context in which the individual is situated [65]. Given the heterogeneity of the older adult population, which includes both very robust and very frail individuals, individualised pain management and deprescribing approaches are likely even more relevant; however, supporting evidence is yet to be obtained. Similarly, many older adults receive care either at home or in residential care settings. Involving the broader multidisciplinary healthcare team, formal carers and informal carers (such as relatives and significant others) in pain management and opioid deprescribing decisions will be crucial. These individuals often have a deep understanding of the patient’s preferences and how signs of pain manifest. This may be particularly true for those with cognitive impairment who may be unable to communicate verbally about their pain. Future research should explore the role of interdisciplinary teams, including pharmacists, nurses and other healthcare professionals, as well as families and carers in supporting deprescribing efforts for older adults. In addition, it is necessary to strengthen the evidence base for pain prevention and treatment strategies, reduce disparities in pain treatment, improve access to services, and provide effective professional and public education and training to ensure accessible and safe pain relief for all older adults.

Conclusions

The intent of deprescribing is to optimise medication safety and appropriateness, using shared decision making to develop a plan for withdrawing inappropriate or unnecessary medicines. Opioid deprescribing, while complex, may play a crucial role in reducing the risk of medication-related harm from high-risk medicines such as opioids, and if implemented in a patient-centred manner, may mitigate harms that have been observed following inappropriate and unsolicited opioid tapering. The growing body of research on this topic is promising, enhancing the certainty of evidence on outcomes and determining the acceptability and feasibility of opioid deprescribing interventions for end users. However, additional evidence on the effectiveness of interventions and outcomes of opioid deprescribing in older adults is required. By leveraging international progress in this field, and focusing future efforts on populations with enhanced age-related risk, we can collectively contribute to addressing the World Health Organization’s “Medication Without Harm Global Patient Safety Challenge” to reduce severe avoidable medication-related harm, [74] and optimise safe and effective pain management for older adults.
