Percutaneous Closure of a Coronary-Pulmonary Artery Fistula due to a Mycotic Aneurysm: A Late Complication of Endocarditis
Authors: Hellmuth Weich, Johan Sothmann, Arlene Mazaza
Published: 2026

A 40-year-old male with an aortic valve replacement (using a St. Jude Medical mechanical prosthesis) for Bartonella endocarditis 3 years prior presented with significant heart failure symptoms (New York Heart Association class 3 dyspnea) and a new continuous murmur. He denied any angina or other cardiorespiratory symptoms. Echocardiography revealed a dilated left heart with moderate secondary mitral valve regurgitation. Turbulent flow was seen in the pulmonary artery (PA). Coronary angiography confirmed a large fistula between an aneurysmal proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and the PA, presumably from a ruptured mycotic aneurysm (Video 1). The anatomy of the fistula and distal LAD was not visualized sufficiently on angiography because of the high flow through the fistula. This was better delineated with computed tomography (CT) angiography (Figure 1). Infection was excluded. In a heart team meeting, the patient was turned down for surgery due to concerns about the tissue quality of the fistula and the fact that the mitral valve regurgitation did not justify surgery in its own right. A covered stent in the LAD across the fistula was considered, but due to the perceived risk of restenosis of this type of stent, we opted to implant an Amplatzer™ Vascular Plug II (AVP II) (Abbott, Illinois) with the distal disc in the PA and the proximal discs in the aneurysmal sac. The CT scan showed the exit into the PA to have an 8-mm diameter, and we selected a 12 mm-AVP II. The procedure was performed via femoral access, and a Terumo wire (Terumo Medical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced via a coronary catheter into the PA. The catheter was advanced into the PA over this wire, which was then exchanged for a standard J-tipped 0.035" wire. An Amplatzer TorqVue™ delivery sheath was advanced over this wire into the PA and used to deliver the 12-mm AVP II as planned (Figure 2, Video 2). Interaction of the device with the LAD lumen could not be evaluated angiographically despite numerous views (Video 3). Control of the AVP II was maintained via the delivery cable, and secondary femoral access was obtained to enable intravascular ultrasound, which revealed significant disc protrusion into the LAD lumen (Figure 3). The AVP II was released to enable placement of a 4.5-mm × 18-mm Resolute Onyx stent (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota) to cage the plug and secure the LAD lumen (Figure 4), confirmed angiographically (Videos 4 and 5) and on intravascular ultrasound (Figure 5). The patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy and warfarin (with a plan to discontinue the dual antiplatelet therapy at 6 months and continue aspirin up to 12 months). He was asymptomatic at 6-month follow-up. Repeat CT demonstrated a patent stent (Figure 6), and repeat echocardiography showed a reduction in secondary mitral regurgitation. Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated a reduction in mitral regurgitation severity, with decreases in the effective regurgitant orifice area (30 mm2 to 20 mm2), regurgitant volume (58 ml to 40 ml), and regurgitant fraction (53% to 42%). There was also a notable reduction in pulmonary arterial systolic pressures from 54.79 mmHg to 31.54 mmHg postclosure. No turbulent flow in the PA and no regional wall motion abnormalities of the left ventricle could be seen.

Discussion

Coronary–PA fistulae may be managed using a variety of transcatheter techniques, including vascular plugs, coils, detachable balloons, and covered stents. Successful transcatheter closure with an Amplatzer vascular plug has previously been described as a safe alternative to surgical intervention.1 In the present case, a hybrid approach incorporating both device closure and coronary artery stenting was successfully employed. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported use of this combined strategy for the management of a coronary–PA fistula. Bartonella species have also been implicated in vascular complications, including mycotic aneurysm formation, providing a plausible mechanism for aneurysmal degeneration in the setting of Bartonella infective endocarditis.2 Although such cases are uncommon, this association underscores the importance of considering vascular involvement in patients with Bartonella infection and endocarditis.
