Healthcare consumers’ acceptance and use of digital health technology in LMICs and the role of self-efficacy and facilitating conditions: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Minmin Wang, Xuetong Zhao, Kepei Huang, Xiangning Li, Laura Downey, Sondus Hassounah, Xiaoyun Liu, Yinzi Jin, Minghui Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe implementation of digital health technology (DHT) has not reached its full potential. Research on the factors influencing the acceptance and use of DHTs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is fragmented and sparse. This study aimed to identify the facilitators and barriers to DHT acceptance and use in healthcare consumers.MethodsThe systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to July 1st, 2025. Facilitators and barriers to the acceptance and use of DHT were extracted, and a modified framework was developed based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology. The strength of each pathway recorded in the framework was estimated through meta-analysis.FindingsA total of 78 publications were included in the systematic review, comprising 64 quantitative studies and 14 qualitative studies. Twelve dimensions were summarized. Factors demonstrating the highest positive associations with intentions to use DHTs included attitude (r = 0.5644; 95% CI: 0.4498, 0.6607), trust (r = 0.5239; 95% CI: 0.4243, 0.6109), performance expectancy (r = 0.4851; 95% CI: 0.4335, 0.5335), technology self-efficacy (r = 0.4430; 95% CI: 0.2504, 0.6018), facilitating conditions (r = 0.4345; 95% CI: 0.3526, 0.5098), effort expectancy (r = 0.4171; 95% CI: 0.3508, 0.4793), and habit (r = 0.4213; 95% CI: 0.1684, 0.6222). Social influence was also positively associated (r = 0.3582; 95% CI: 0.2823, 0.4297), as well as hedonic motivation (r = 0.3963, 95% CI: 0.2280, 0.5416). Price value showed a near-null, imprecise association (r = 0.0304; 95% CI: −0.1483, 0.2072). Perceived risk to patient privacy, patient security and telemedicine systems security, showed a small negative but imprecise association with intentions (r = −0.0402; 95% CI: −0.2169, 0.1391). Anxiety showed a non-significant negative association (r = −0.2071; 95% CI: −0.5108, 0.1426).InterpretationThe barriers identified in this analysis highlight the necessity of establishing and sustaining an integrated enabling environment as well as investing in digital health literacy to enhance healthcare consumers' self-efficacy. Findings of the study will facilitate the improvement of DHT implementation to address health concerns.FundingThis study was funded by Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project (2024ZD0524500, 2024ZD0524501), National Natural Science Foundation of China (72274005 and 72304013), Beijing Nova Program (20230484284).Keywords: LMICs, UTAUT, Healthcare consumers, Self-efficacy, Facilitating conditions

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for studies that conceptualize the factors influencing the acceptance and utilization of digital health technologies (DHTs) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) from its inception to July 1st, 2025. The following search terms were used: (telehealth OR telemedicine OR digital health OR digital medicine OR mobile health)) AND (acceptance OR usage OR utilization) AND (low and middle income countr∗). Although we identified several studies investigating determinants of DHT acceptance and use, the evidence base in LMICs remains limited, and we found no prior evidence synthesis on this topic.

Added value of this study

Applying the systematic review and meta-analysis, this study is to the best of our knowledge the first one to offer a comprehensive examination of the facilitators and barriers to the acceptance and use of DHT from the perspective of healthcare consumers in LMICs. By synthesizing the evidence from 78 publications, a total of twelve dimensions were summarized as enablers or barriers. Effort expectancy, performance expectancy, facilitating conditions, social influence, trust, habit, hedonic motivation, technology self-efficacy and attitude may enhance healthcare consumers’ intention to utilize DHT in LMICs.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of this study provide LMICs with actionable evidence to address healthcare consumers' concerns about digital health technologies and to implement national digital health strategies and policies more effectively. By identifying priority areas for intervention, i.e., modifiable determinants such as facilitating conditions (infrastructure, technical support, affordability) and consumers’ technology self-efficacy, countries at different stages of digital health development can plan, build, finance, and govern their digital ecosystems to improve health outcomes, optimize service delivery, and advance equity.

Introduction

In light of the accelerated advancement of digital technologies and the growing interconnectedness of global populations, digital health technologies (DHTs) have emerged as indispensable tools for strengthening health systems and reducing health inequities.1 The accelerated development of innovative DHTs has given rise to a multitude of applications across a range of healthcare domains, including emergency response, health monitoring, diagnostic support, remote consultations, and beyond. Notwithstanding their potential, the implementation of DHTs remains suboptimal, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where barriers such as, low acceptance, limited usability, insufficient technical support, and unstable network connectivity2,3 significantly hinder widespread adoption.

Previous research into the factors that facilitate or impede the acceptance and use of DHT from the perspectives of healthcare consumers remains insufficient, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Prior investigations have predominantly focused on specific diseases or technologies, such as hypertension4 or neurological conditions,5 providing a fragmented understanding of the systemic factors influencing the acceptance and use of DHT in healthcare consumers. As a result, these studies provide only partial insights into the structural and contextual barriers that must be addressed to foster enabling environments for DHT integration. Moreover, many studies fail to account for the diverse socio-cultural, economic, and infrastructural factors that vary across healthcare settings, especially in low-resource environments. This gap in the literature highlights the need for more comprehensive, context-sensitive research that considers not only the technological and clinical aspects of DHT adoption but also the wider systemic and environmental factors that shape its uptake and impact.

A crucial but often overlooked factor is the role of consumer behavior in influencing DHT acceptance. Consumer behavior is a key determinant, as individual decisions and actions directly impact the effectiveness and adoption of technology. We frame this study with the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT),6,7 which explains intention and use through four core constructs: performance expectancy (perceived benefits), effort expectancy (perceived ease of use), social influence (perceived normative pressure), and facilitating conditions (availability of support, infrastructure, and resources). In healthcare settings, these externally oriented determinants interact with internal psychological processes, particularly self-efficacy. We examined two domain-specific forms of self-efficacy: technology self-efficacy,8, 9, 10 defined as confidence in learning and using digital health tools (e.g., navigating apps, entering data, troubleshooting), and health self-efficacy,11,12 defined as confidence in managing one's health tasks (e.g., self-monitoring, adherence, decision-making, communicating with providers), including when supported by DHTs. Higher levels of these forms of self-efficacy are expected to increase both intention and actual use, especially in diverse socio-cultural and resource-constrained contexts.13 Positioning consumer behavior within this integrated framework clarifies the mechanisms through which interventions can improve uptake and sustained engagement with DHTs.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in line with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.14 We registered the review with PROSPERO (CRD42024559814). Patients were not involved in this review.

Eligibility criteria and outcomes

Both quantitative and qualitative studies were incorporated without imposition of language restrictions. Studies were deemed eligible for inclusion if they reported on the facilitators and barriers to the acceptance and utilization of DHT for healthcare consumers in LMICs. The income classification of included countries was determined using the World Bank's country income classifications at the time of the study, which categorizes economies into low-income, lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income, and high-income groups. For this review, we included only studies conducted in low-, lower-middle-, and upper-middle-income countries, excluding those from high-income countries.

The following criteria were used to exclude studies: 1) conducted in high-income countries; 2) solely reporting the effectiveness of DHTs without examining influencing factors; 3) comprising case studies, conference papers, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or bibliometric analyses.

The primary outcome of this mixed-method systematic review was to identify the factors that facilitate or impede to the acceptance and use of DHT among healthcare consumers in LMICs. The secondary outcome was to estimate the path coefficient between the influencing factors and the intention to utilize DHTs. Both qualitative and quantitative studies contributed to the primary outcome, while only quantitative studies underwent meta-analysis for the secondary outcomes. All studies deemed eligible for inclusion will contribute to the synthesis of the factors influencing the primary outcome, with data from the quantitative analysis informing the synthesis of the secondary outcomes.

Literature search, data extraction and risk of bias assessment

To be included in the review, studies were required to be randomized controlled trials, observational studies, cross-sectional studies, or cohort studies published in peer-reviewed journals. We searched the study from its inception to July 1st 2025, in three electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. A comprehensive literature search was conducted by using keywords and medical subject headings to identify studies related to digital health, technology acceptance, framework, and LMICs. We searched titles and abstracts for terms related to digital health and its synonyms, such as “telemedicine,” while using an all-fields search for LMIC-related terms. Our search was limited to database queries; we did not perform additional follow-up searches of reference lists or citation tracking of included studies. The detailed literature search strategy is presented in Supplemental Table S1. Three authors (K.H., X.L., and X.Z.) conducted independent screening of the titles and abstracts of retrieved citations to identify relevant studies. They then performed independent full-text evaluations of the selected articles, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

We extracted four parts of data from each study independently and in duplicate. These included: (1) general study information such as study design, sample size, and original country; (2) characteristics of the digital technology, i.e., targeted population, and classification of the DHT; (3) reported facilitators and barriers to the acceptance of DHT in the targeted population; and (4) strengths of the association, i.e., correlation coefficient (Pearson correlation coefficients) reported for the given influencing factor (for quantitative studies only). In this study, our focus was on we the healthcare consumers, which differs from the healthcare providers in the WHO's classification of the targeted primary user in digital health interventions. The term “healthcare consumer” encompasses individuals who are potential or current users of healthcare services, including those engaged in health prevention and wellness activities. In accordance with the recommendations set forth by the WHO in its classification of digital interventions, services and applications in health, the types of technology were classified.15

Two authors conducted an independent evaluation of the methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies, in accordance with the recommendations set forth by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ).16 The risk of bias for each study was subsequently classified as low, moderate, or high. The detailed information is presented in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3.

Synthesis of influencing factors

The principal objective of this mixed-method systematic review was to synthesize the facilitators and barriers influencing the acceptance and utilization of DHT in LMICs among healthcare consumers. Accordingly, all identified influencing factors from the selected studies (both quantitative and qualitative) will be grouped according to the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) framework,17,18 which provides a comprehensive model for understanding users’ acceptance and use of digital technology. The UTAUT framework is derived from a number of foundational theories related to the adoption of innovations, including the Theory of Reasoned Action, the Technology Acceptance Model, the Motivational Model, the Theory of Planned Behavior, and the Innovation Diffusion Theory.17,18 The framework offers an integrated approach to analyzing the acceptance and use of DHT and summarizes the influencing factors into four main dimensions: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Factors that were not included in the original UTAUT framework but align with the aforementioned four dimensions would be incorporated into the UTAUT framework as extensions.

Meta-analysis

The secondary objective of the study was to quantify the strength of the associations between each influencing factor and the acceptance and use of DHT by synthesizing the real-world evidence. The studies selected for meta-analysis were evaluated according to the following criteria: 1) They were empirical studies, as opposed to conceptual studies; 2) they reported Pearson correlation coefficients or other statistics that could be converted to correlation coefficients; 3) the method used to analyze the data was structural equation modelling, ensuring consistency of approach across all studies.

In the meta-analysis, the reported raw correlation coefficient (r) with a 95% confidence interval for the identified influencing factors were analyzed using a random-effects model. Fisher's Z transformation was first applied to the correlation coefficients to stabilize the variance. The pooled mean effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals were subsequently calculated using the inverse-variance method weighted by sample size. The heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using Cochran's Q test and the I2 index.19 To further evaluate the potential for heterogeneity, the studies were categorized into subgroups based on the income level of the countries in question: upper-middle-income countries and lower-middle- and low-income countries. The strength of the association will be classified according to the estimated correlation: small (r = 0.10–0.30), medium (r = 0.31–0.50), and large (r > 0.50).20

Funnel plots were constructed to ascertain the presence of potential publication bias. Furthermore, the Begg rank correlation test and Egger linear regression test were conducted to ascertain the presence of publication bias, with a P-value of less than 0.05 indicating a statistically significant bias in the publication of results.21,22 A sensitivity analysis was subsequently performed, including only studies with a low or moderate risk of bias.

Ethics approval and consent to participate/for publication

This study utilized exclusively publicly available data from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science, and did not involve any direct interaction with human subjects or animals. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not applicable.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Across all databases, our search yielded a total of 9138 citations. After removing duplicates, we screened 6018 titles and abstracts, and obtained 899 publications for full-text review. Of these, 78 publications met our eligibility criteria, including 64 quantitative studies for meta-analysis and 14 qualitative studies for thematic analysis (Fig. 1). Some publications23, 24, 25 which appear to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded because even though they are exploring the influencing factors of DHT acceptance and use, they were not consistent with UTAUT's model path. Others26, 27, 28 because they used the regression equation or other methods instead of structural equation model.

Barriers and facilitators of acceptance and use of digital health technology

The current study extracted barriers and facilitators of acceptance and use of DHT from all 78 studies included in the analysis (Table 1). The specific input data are presented in Supplemental Table S4 for quantitative studies and Supplemental Table S5 for qualitative studies and are summarized according to the UTAUT framework (Fig. 2). The template data forms, including data extracted from included studies, data used for analysis are presented in Supplemental Table S4. A correlation coefficient was applied through meta-analysis to quantify the strength of the relationship between the factor and DHT use.

UTAUT-guided model of facilitators and barriers of the acceptance and usage of digital health technologies for healthcare consumers.

Based on the UTAUT theory and the factors identified in the 78 studies as influencing the acceptance and use of DHT, a total of 12 dimensions were identified as potential barriers or facilitators for healthcare consumers in LMICs. These were performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, anxiety, habit, hedonic motivation, price value, attitude, trust, risk, and self-efficacy (Fig. 2). In particular, the construct of “self-efficacy” was described in two sub-domains: technology self-efficacy and health self-efficacy. This approach allowed for a more precise delineation of the specific connotations associated with each sub-dimension. Technology self-efficacy was defined as perceived behavioral control and innovativeness in technology use, where health self-efficacy referred to a person's confidence in their ability to manage and engage with health-related decisions.106

The strength of the association in each pathway, as recorded in the 64 quantitative studies, was estimated through meta-analysis (Table 2). A positive association was observed between technology self-efficacy and intention to use (r = 0.4430, 95% CI: 0.2504–0.6018), and a similar positive association was evident between health self-efficacy and intention to use (r = 0.2538, 95% CI: 0.0514, 0.4361). Additionally, performance expectancy (r = 0.4851, 95% CI: 0.4335, 0.5335), trust (r = 0.5239, 95% CI: 0.4243, 0.6109) and attitude (r = 0.5644, 95% CI: 0.4498, 0.6607) were the factors that demonstrated the highest associations with intentions to utilize DHT. Other factors demonstrated consistent estimation with the initial UTAUT theory, including effort expectancy (r = 0.4171, 95% CI: 0.3508, 0.4793), facilitating conditions (r = 0.4345, 95% CI: 0.3526, 0.5098), social influence (r = 0.3582, 95% CI: 0.2823, 0.4297), habit (r = 0.4213, 95% CI: 0.1684, 0.6222), hedonic motivation (r = 0.3963, 95% CI: 0.2280, 0.5416) and price value (r = 0.0304, 95% CI: −0.1483, 0.2072). Conversely, risk (r = −0.0402, 95% CI: −0.2169, 0.1391) and anxiety (r = −0.2071; 95% CI: −0.51108, 0.1426) may have a detrimental impact on this intension. The intention to use DHT (r = 0.6523, 95% CI: 0.5494, 0.7358) and the facilitating conditions (r = 0.5327, 95% CI: 0.2335, 0.7398) were found to be statistically significant predictors of actual usage of DHT in LMICs. Discussion

This study is to the best of our knowledge the first one to offer a comprehensive examination of the facilitators and barriers to the acceptance and use of DHT from the perspective of healthcare consumers in LMICs. The results indicated that effort expectancy, facilitating conditions, social influence, habit, hedonic motivation and price value may enhance healthcare consumers’ intention to utilize DHT in LMICs.To enhance the explanatory power of the UTAUT framework, two additional domains were introduced: technology self-efficacy and health self-efficacy. The findings provide an evidence base to inform policy and practice with regard to the effective integration and equitable adoption of DHTs in LMICs.

A distinctive contribution of this study is the conceptual clarification of self-efficacy into two domain-specific forms relevant to healthcare consumers: technology self-efficacy and health self-efficacy. Technology self-efficacy denotes confidence in learning, navigating, and troubleshooting digital tools and platforms.107,108 Higher digital self-efficacy is associated with stronger intentions to use and sustained engagement with DHTs, whereas lower digital self-efficacy can impede uptake.109 The digital nature of these technologies, which often rely on online interfaces rather than face-to-face interactions,110 means that the adoption of DHT is profoundly influenced by the ideological and psychological traits of the recipients.111 Health self-efficacy refers to confidence in carrying out the tasks required to manage one's health (e.g., self-monitoring, adherence, decision-making, communicating with providers), including when supported by DHTs.24,112, 113, 114 Higher health management self-efficacy similarly predicts greater acceptance and use, underscoring its central role in the successful implementation of DHTs.115

It is equally important to consider health self-efficacy. The quantified association strength through meta-analysis demonstrated that individuals with higher levels of health literacy exhibited greater acceptance and utilization of DHT in the broader population. This finding suggests a promising strategy for integrating DHT implementation with health promotion programs. For example, the use of mobile health applications, in conjunction with community-based health literacy programs has been demonstrated to improve diabetes management among patients.116,117 The programs provided patients with essential knowledge about diabetes management and simultaneously familiarized them with digital tools for monitoring and controlling their condition. Similarly, the integration of telemedicine platforms with educational workshops on maternal health in rural Bangladesh and Laos resulted in an increased uptake of telemedicine and a significant improvement in maternal health outcomes.118 This was due to the fact that women were better equipped to utilize these digital services effectively.118 The incorporation of DHT implementation within broader health literacy promotion strategies enables healthcare systems to cultivate a more informed and empowered patient population, particularly in LMICs, where limited health literacy represents a significant obstacle to effective healthcare delivery.

Our findings suggest that health self-efficacy is more strongly associated with DHT use intention in LICs and LMICs, while technology self-efficacy plays a greater role in UMICs. This may reflect contextual differences: in lower-income settings, individuals confident in managing their own health may be more motivated to adopt DHTs as a means to compensate for limited healthcare access. In contrast, in UMICs where digital infrastructure is more developed, confidence in using technology becomes a more decisive factor. These insights underscore the need for context-specific strategies that build either health or technology self-efficacy depending on local needs.

Importantly, these two domains are not mutually exclusive. Rather, their intersection forms the basis of what is now widely recognized as digital health literacy—the ability to seek, understand, evaluate, and apply digital health information and tools.119 Enhancing this combined literacy is essential to overcome barriers to DHT adoption, a point emphasized by the WHO and increasingly supported by global evidence.120 Moreover, the Lancet and Financial Times Commission advocates for the development of not only digital and health literacy, but also civic literacy, to equip individuals with the skills to critically engage with digital health systems, understand their rights, and participate in governance.121 The review revealed that facilitating conditions were a frequently addressed domain, demonstrating a significant association with healthcare consumers' behavioral intention. This highlights the importance of the enabling ecosystem in the implementation of DHT and digital transformation. In the UTAUT theory, facilitating conditions are defined as the extent to which an individual perceives the existence of an organizational and technical infrastructure that supports the utilization of the system.106 The adoption of virtual technologies is contingent upon the existence of a robust physical and digital infrastructure. Facilitating conditions are defined as factors such as internet access, electricity supply, and IT infrastructure. For example, a study from China indicated that both direct and indirect network externalities had a positive effect on users’ continued intention to use mobile health applications.43 Similarly, studies from Bangladesh41 and Pakistan54 found that facilitating conditions, such as stable internet connectivity and adequate equipment, significantly impacted the use intention and actual behavior of specific populations, including older adults. In resource-constrained settings, the absence of stable networks or adequate equipment severely limits the impact of other factors aimed at promoting DHT adoption. This underscores the necessity for governments and healthcare institutions to invest in essential medical and technological infrastructure. By doing so, healthcare consumers are more likely to engage with and adapt to DHTs, fostering their innovation and development while realizing their potential to transform healthcare delivery and outcomes.

Among the factors examined, habit, attitude, trust, and performance expectancy showed the strongest positive associations with the intention to use DHTs. This finding suggests that individuals are more likely to adopt digital health technologies when they are already accustomed to using similar tools, perceive them positively, expect them to be beneficial, and trust the systems and institutions delivering them. These insights underscore the importance of not only ensuring functional effectiveness of DHTs but also fostering a conducive environment for their sustained use. From a policy and practice perspective, promoting digital literacy and integrating DHTs into routine health interactions can help cultivate habitual use. Clear communication of the benefits and reliability of DHTs can improve consumers’ attitudes and expectations. Furthermore, building trust through transparent data governance, user protections, and culturally sensitive implementation is crucial, particularly in settings where skepticism toward digital systems remains a barrier. These factors should be prioritized in the design and scaling of digital health interventions to improve uptake and impact.

Agencies engaged in digital health governance have long acknowledged the significance of acceptance and use in attaining the anticipated benefits of DHT. In 2005, the World Health Assembly, through its resolution WHA58.28 on eHealth, urged Member States the formulation of a long-term strategic plan for the development and implementation of eHealth services, the advancement of the infrastructure for information and communication technologies for health, and the promotion of equitable, affordable, and universal access to the benefits of eHealth.122 At the 73rd World Health Assembly, the WHO endorsed the establishment of the Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025.123 The strategy is founded upon four core principles. A national system necessitates that countries determine and commit to it, recognize that successful digital technologies require an integrated strategy, promote the appropriate use of digital interventions for health, and address the significant impediments faced by least-developed countries implementing DHT. In this context, the findings of this study could contribute to the promotion of the implementation of WHO decisions on cybersecurity and investment in health facilities and training staff to enhance cyber-maturity. This would be achieved through with the development of a more specific implementation path for the Department of Digital Health and other relevant actors, with the aim of accelerating the achievement of the strategic objectives set out in the Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025.123 Furthermore, this study presents specific targets for LMICs, with the aim of improving health outcomes, optimizing health services, and promoting health equity. These targets assist countries at all stages of development in effectively planning, building, financing, and governing their digital ecosystems.

This study has several strengths. First, it employs a systematic approach to identify and synthesize potential facilitators and barriers to the acceptance and use of DHTs in LMICs from the perspective of healthcare consumers. The study proposes a novel scale-up model to elucidate pathways of action and key influencing factors. Secondly, the study introduces an innovative approach to self-efficacy, which is divided into two distinct constructs: health self-efficacy and technology self-efficacy. This distinction provides a more detailed and complex understanding of the primary factors influencing healthcare consumers’ acceptance processes. Thirdly, the study emphasises the pivotal role of enabling conditions in the adoption of DHTs, particularly in LMICs, where infrastructural challenges are pronounced. It should be noted, however, that this study is not without limitations. The analysis was constrained by the meta-analytic approach and guided by the UTAUT framework, with factors mentioned in at least two studies included in the review. While this approach ensures the reliability of factor identification, it may have excluded less frequently reported but potentially important determinants. Second, although a comprehensive search and data organization were carried out, most of the included studies were from Asian countries with large populations, which means interpretation of the findings needs to be cautious. Further research in underrepresented regions was needed to strengthen the global evidence base. Thirdly, 99 out of 899 searching articles were unavailable, and this constraint may have limited access to some relevant studies and could influence the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, this study reviewed key facilitators and barriers to the adoption of digital health technologies in LMICs from the perspective of healthcare consumers. Our findings underscore the urgent need for an enabling environment supported by strong digital infrastructure and coordinated efforts across health systems. We call for increased investment in digital health literacy and skills training to improve uptake and effectiveness. Additionally, more research is needed in underrepresented regions—such as South America—to strengthen the global evidence base and guide future implementation efforts.
