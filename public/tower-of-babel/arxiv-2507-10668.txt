Entanglement and Purity in Open Systems: A Breakdown of the Lindblad Approach
R. Serao, A. Quaranta, A. Capolupo, F. Franchini, S.M. Giampaolo

Abstract
We examine the effectiveness of Lindblad master equation in capturing the short-time dynamics of entanglement and purity in open quantum systems. Focusing on two interacting two-level systems interacting with a larger environment, we compare the Lindbladian approach to a full microscopic treatment. While the latter shows entanglement growth and a quadratic decay of purity, the Lindbladian method predicts a linear purity decay and can entirely suppress entanglement when the dissipative coupling exceeds the interaction strength. Introducing a time-dependent Lindbladian improves some predictions but causes inconsistencies in other observables. Our results highlight the limitations of effective models and the need for caution when applying them to describe subtle quantum behaviors.



I
Introduction

Even when we have a very powerful and descriptive theory at our disposal, it is often impossible to apply it directly to make predictions that can be compared with the experimental outcomes. In these cases, one usually resort to effective theories, but their regime of applicability and validity needs to be correctly ascertained. This constitutes one of the most difficult, and sometimes underestimated, challenges in modern physics, as it involves approximations and simplifications that needs to be reconciled with the physical reality of systems that are hardly completely isolated and under complete control.

This issue is especially pronounced when looking at quantum mechanics: even assuming that the unitary evolution provided by the Schrödinger equation is the fundamental, correct description, in principle one should include every degree of freedom and every interaction, rendering its predictive power useless. In some rare situations it is possible to design experiments where everything else but the “
system
” can be neglected, but in practice inevitable interactions with the surroundings effectively introduce non-unitary effects
OQS1
;
OQS2
, which can significantly impact the dynamics of the system and even affect critical quantum resources such as entanglement
Ent1
;
Ent2
, coherence
Coho1
;
Coho2
, and quantum computational resources like magic
Magic1
;
Magic2
.

The typical scenario we have in mind is the study of a many-body quantum system. Some models, such as the celebrated Caldeira-Leggett model
CaldeiraLegget
, describe how thermal behaviors can emerge from unitary dynamics, when some degrees of freedom are considered as an environment and traced out. In general, interactions with the environment cannot be properly accounted for, or sometimes one is interested in only a subset of the whole system.
This leads to a natural bipartition: the accessible, usually smaller, subsystem is treated as the system of interest, while the remainder effectively functions as an environment.
Even if the global system evolves unitarily, the reduced dynamics of the subsystem is effectively non-unitary. This phenomenon can be equivalently understood as due to the interaction between the subsystem and the environment that generates or destroys the entanglement between the two, or due to the action of tracing out the environment degrees of freedom in order to concentrate purely on the subsystem.
Either ways, decoherence emerges intrinsically from the internal structure of the system, without assuming any external noise or explicit dissipative processes.

Incorporating non-unitary effects in a coherent quantum mechanical description is non trivial. One of the most used approaches, due to its ability to preserve the structure of quantum mechanics, is that of the Lindblad master equation framework
Gorini
;
Lindblad
.
This effective approach incorporates dissipation via phenomenological parameters, empirically derived, that encode the system’s interaction with its environment, while ensuring compliance with fundamental physical principles
BE1
;
BE2
;
BE3
;
BE4
;
BE5
.

Because of its simplicity and flexibility, Lindblad formalism has been widely adopted by the community to describe non-unitary dynamics in an empiric and effective way
Rev1
;
Rev2
. In this work, we will show in a very simple setting that the dynamics obtained by tracing out part of a system’s degrees of freedom cannot be possibly captured by a Lindbladian dynamics, except that by allowing the microscopic empiric parameters to depend on time. Our exercise thus sheds doubts on the validity of the Lindblad formalism to describe any type of non-unitary dynamics that can emerge from quantum mechanics and calls for a critical analysis of such settings, especially as recently the community has been investing a continuously growing interest in non-hermitian dynamics for various reasons and motivations
Rev1
;
Rev2
.

In particular, we will consider a system composed of several two-level objects, interacting pairwise. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that the dynamic of the many-body systems is governed by a global Hamiltonian made of mutually commuting terms so that the eigenstates of the Hamiltonians can be written as tensor products of local states.
From this system, we isolate two subsystems and analyze the evolution of their entanglement and purity using both a first-principles approach and the standard Lindblad master equation formalism, considering the remaining two-level objects as the environment.

Although a full first-principles treatment lies beyond the scope of this work, a general analysis can be carried out in the short-time limit – specifically, when the product of time and the interaction strength is much smaller than one (
\hbar=1
).
In this regime, the two approaches yield markedly different predictions: assuming an initially separable state, while the microscopic many-body treatment shows that entanglement always develops, the Lindbladian approach predicts two distinct behaviors, with entanglement emerging only if the interaction is stronger than the environmental coupling.
This contrast highlights a significant shortcoming of the Lindblad formalism when applied to many-body systems, where internal correlations and structure play a central role in the dynamics.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
After this introduction, we begin by examining the scenario in which the two two-level systems are completely isolated, undergoing purely unitary dynamics.
This serves as a baseline for the subsequent analyses.
In Sec. III, we adopt a microscopic approach, leveraging the simplifying assumptions of our model to compute the entanglement between the systems and the purity of their joint density matrix.
These results are then contrasted with those obtained from the effective model explored in Sec. IV. Finally, in Sec. V, we discuss the observed discrepancies and present our conclusions.

II
The basic model: no environment

Let us consider two physical systems,
A
and
B
, and their collective eigenstates which can be written as tensor products
\ket{a_{i}}\otimes\ket{b_{j}}
, where
\ket{a_{1}}
and
\ket{a_{2}}
are states associated with system
A
, and
\ket{b_{1}}
and
\ket{b_{2}}
correspond to system
B
.
Under these assumptions, the Hamiltonian governing the joint system can be expressed as:

H_{AB}=\sum_{ij}g_{ij}\ket{a_{i}b_{j}}\!\!\bra{a_{i}b_{j}}

(1)

This model has wide-ranging applicability and encompasses several scenarios, including the well-known BMV experimental setup

;

;
BMV3

;
QG2
;
QG3
;

;

In this context, the basis states correspond to different spatial positions of the systems.
The model also extends to situations involving internal degrees of freedom, such as spin
BMV4
or mass eigenstates in the context of particle mixing
BMV5

By defining the Pauli operators
\sigma^{z}=\ket{a_{1}}\bra{a_{1}}-\ket{a_{2}}\bra{a_{2}}
and
\sigma^{z}_{B}=\ket{b_{1}}\bra{b_{1}}-\ket{b_{2}}\bra{b_{2}}
, we can rewrite the Hamiltonian as:

H_{AB}\!=\!c_{0}\sigma^{0}_{A}\otimes\sigma^{0}_{B}\!+\!c_{A}\sigma^{z}_{A}\otimes\sigma^{0}_{B}\!+\!c_{B}\sigma^{0}_{A}\otimes\sigma^{z}_{B}\!-\!\omega\sigma^{z}_{A}\otimes\sigma^{z}_{B}

(2)

Here,
\sigma^{0}_{j}
denotes the identity operator, and the coefficients

,
c_{A}
,
c_{B}
, and
\omega
are linear combinations of the
g_{ij}
parameters.
The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
2
) fully determines the time evolution of the system.
Therefore, starting from any initial state, we can compute the system’s state, and any related quantities at any later time
t>0
using the time evolution operator derived from this Hamiltonian.

However, since the primary focus of this work is the entanglement dynamics, we can streamline the analysis. Notably, all four components of Eq. (
2
) commute with one another, which allows us to factor the time evolution operator into a product of four unitary operators, each generated by one of the Hamiltonian’s terms.

Among these, the first term, being a multiple of the identity, yields only a global phase and thus has no physical effect.
On the other hand, the second and third terms correspond to local operations acting non-trivially solely on systems
A
and
B
, respectively.
As it is well known, local operations cannot modify the entanglement between the systems
Ent1
;
Local1
, so they can also be excluded from our entanglement analysis.
Consequently, to investigate the entanglement dynamics, it suffices to consider only the time evolution generated by the remaining term of the Hamiltonian:

\tilde{H}_{AB}=-\omega\sigma^{z}_{A}\otimes\sigma^{z}_{B}\,

(3)

where
\omega=\frac{1}{4}\left(g_{12}+g_{21}-g_{11}-g_{22}\right)
.

Given this Hamiltonian, it can be readily shown that if the system starts from a completely separable state, i.e. when
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{\psi_{A}}\ket{\psi_{B}}
, and each subsystem is initially prepared in a non-trivial superposition of the states
\ket{a_{i}}
and
\ket{b_{j}}
, then a nonzero amount of entanglement is dynamically generated.
For any time
t>0
, such entanglement is bounded from above by the one obtained in the case in which both subsystems are initially in the real symmetric superposition states

\begin{split}&\ket{\psi_{A}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{a_{1}}+\ket{a_{2}}),\\
&\ket{\psi_{B}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{b_{1}}+\ket{b_{2}}).\\
\end{split}

(4)

which exhibits an entanglement quantified by the concurrence
CON1
;
CON2
, given by
\sin(2 t |\omega|)
. At the same time, the purity
P=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2})

Ent1
;
Purity1
, which indicates the degree of mixedness of a quantum state, remains equal to 1 at all times.

III
The microscopic model

How does the situation evolve if, rather than treating systems
A
and
B
as isolated, we instead view them as subsystems within a larger many-body system?
To explore this case, we suppose that
A
and
B
are part of a composite system consisting of
N+2
identical components, all interacting via pairwise couplings of the same type as described by Eq. (
1
).
Given the special role played by
A
and
B
in our study, the total Hamiltonian of the system can be rewritten as the sum of four contributions:

H=\tilde{H}_{AB}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{e}+H_{Ae}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}+H_{Be}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A}+H_{E}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}\ .

(5)

This expression shows that the presence of the remaining
N
elements introduces three additional interaction terms.
The last one, denoted by
H_{E}
, accounts for all pairwise interactions within the system that exclude both
A
and
B
.
This term effectively describes the environment of
A
and
B
. As we will see, as long as
H_{E}
is time independent, we could drop it here, since it cannot affect the entanglement between the rest of the system’s components. However, for completeness let us keep it for now.

The other two new terms represent the interactions of
A
and
B
with the environment.
Since we only consider two-body interactions, those involving
A
are completely independent from those involving
B
, and vice versa.
Moreover, assuming these interactions follow the same structure as Eq. (
1
), they can be written as:

\begin{split}&H_{Ae}=\sum_{ik}h^{a}_{ik}\ket{a_{i}e_{k}}\!\!\bra{a_{i}e_{k}}\\
&H_{Be}=\sum_{jk}h^{b}_{jk}\ket{b_{j}e_{k}}\!\!\bra{b_{j}e_{k}}\ .\end{split}

(6)

where
\{\ket{e_{k}}\}
are a complete set of eigenstates of
H_{E}
with
k
running from
1
to
M=2^{N}
.

Having specified the full Hamiltonian for this collection of
N+2
two-level subsystems, we now turn to the study of the entanglement dynamics between
A
and
B
, and contrast these with the results from the isolated case.
For this purpose, as in the previous case, we take the initial state to be a fully factorized product state:
\ket{\psi(0)=\ket{\psi_{A}}\ket{\psi_{B}}\ket{\psi_{E}}}
.
The states
\ket{\psi_{A}}
and
\ket{\psi_{B}}
are chosen to be the real and symmetric superpositions in

and
B
in Eq (
4
).
On the other hand, the environment’s initial state
\ket{\psi_{E}}
is assumed to be

\ket{\psi_{e}}=\sum_{k}f_{k}\ket{e_{k}}.

(7)

where the coefficients
f_{k}
are only constrained by the normalization condition
\sum_{k}|f_{k}|^{2}=1
.

Within these assumptions, the reduced density matrix describing the joint state of systems

and

e^{\imath 2\omega t}\Gamma_{A}^{*}&\Lambda_{-}^{*}&1&e^{\imath 2\omega t}\Gamma_{B}\\
\Lambda_{+}^{*}&e^{-\imath 2\omega t}\Gamma_{A}^{*}&e^{-\imath 2\omega t}\Gamma_{B}^{*}&1\end{pmatrix}

(8)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}|f_{k}(t)|^{2}e^{-\imath(h^{\alpha}_{1k}-h^{\alpha}_{2k})t}

(9)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}|f_{k}(t)|^{2}e^{-\imath(h^{A}_{1k}-h^{A}_{2k})t}e^{-\imath[\pm(h^{B}_{1k}-h^{B}_{2k})]t}

In principle, Eq. (

) predicts that in general

and
\Lambda_{\pm}
depend on the dynamics of the environment under its internal evolution.
However, as the state in eq. (

, where

is the eigenvalue of

associated to the eigenstate

.
Thus, the evolution of any physical quantity defined over
A
and
B
, including their entanglement, is unaffected by the dynamics induced by

.
Additionally, we notice that the partial trace over the environment degrees of freedom used to derive eq. (

This implies that the same result would hold even if the environment was not in a pure state as in eq. (
7
), but rather in a thermodynamic mixture, where the off-diagonal elements, and thus the internal coherence of the environment

, are fully or partially suppressed, rather than in the coherent superposition considered so far.

We note that the density matrix in Eq. (
8
) would reproduce the same entanglement properties we observed in the previous section, that is as in the case where subsystems
A
and
B
are completely isolated from the rest of the many-body system, if all the functions in Eq. (

This condition is satisfied if

for all

and
\alpha
, meaning the interaction with the environment does not depend on the internal states of
A
and
B
.
In this scenario, the subsystem made of
A
and

However, this is not the general case. For example, suppose we consider gravitationally interacting mixing particles where the internal states differ either in spatial position or in mass, as in Ref.

.
In that case, the strength of the interaction with the environment becomes dependent on the internal configuration of the system.
As a result, in such cases, the functions

A complete analysis is made impossible by the large number of free parameters.

In contrast, the influence of the remaining components of the many-body system on the entanglement of the reduced density matrix is negligible, since in the short-time limit the leading term of the entanglement is

We can now consider a specific example to see this construction in action: we have

IV
The effective model: The Lindbladian Approach

As we discussed in the previous section, beyond the short-time regime, the expressions for the functions
\gamma_{\alpha}
and
\Lambda_{\pm}
become highly intricate, making it exceedingly challenging to obtain results that exhibit any form of universality.
As a result, instead of adopting the rigorous first-principles approach employed so far, it is often more practical to use a simplified method that facilitates the analysis of the system’s dynamics.
For this purpose, the most commonly employed framework is based on the well-known Lindblad Master equation
Gorini
;
Lindblad
.
In this formalism, the Schrödinger equation is replaced by the so-called Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) equation that describe the time evolution of the system as

\derivative{\rho_{AB}}{t}=-\imath\commutator{H}{\rho_{AB}}-2\mathcal{L}_{AB}(\rho_{AB})\ .

(12)

Here
\rho_{AB}
is the density matrix representing the state in the system made by
A
and
B
, and

\mathcal{L}_{AB}(\rho_{AB})
is the Lindbladian term describing the non-unitary evolution induced by the interaction between
A
and
B
and the environment.

Naturally, the evolution of the state
\rho_{AB}
and its physical properties depend on the specific structure of the Lindbladian.
In the present context, where the system consists of two subsystems, we assume that the total Lindbladian is given by the sum of individual Lindbladians acting on each subsystem, namely

\mathcal{L}_{AB}=\mathcal{L}_{A}\otimes I_{B}+I_{A}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}
.
This assumption is consistent with the considerations previously discussed.
Specifically, in the absence of three-body interaction terms, the coupling between the environment and the composite system
A
and
B
reduces to the sum of the couplings between the environment and each subsystem individually.
Moreover, for the sake of simplicity, we further assume that the two Lindbladians
\mathcal{L}_{A}
and
\mathcal{L}_{B}
are identical, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{A}=\mathcal{L}_{B}=\mathcal{L}
, which corresponds to the assumption that the interactions between each subsystem and the environment are statistically equivalent on average.

Nevertheless, this assumption alone does not fully specify the Lindbladian.
As it is well known
BE1
, the most general form of a Lindbladian acting on a two-level system, expressed in the spin operator basis, can be written as
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}D_{\lambda,\mu}\rho_{\mu}\sigma^{\lambda},

where
\rho_{\mu}=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\sigma^{\mu})
, and in the basis of Pauli operators
\{\sigma^{0},\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z}\}
, the matrix
D
with components
D_{\lambda,\mu}
takes the form

D=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&K_{x}&f_{xy}&f_{xz}\\
0&f_{xy}&K_{y}&f_{yz}\\
0&f_{xz}&f_{yz}&K_{z}\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

The parameters in this matrix are not arbitrary; they are subject to constraints that ensure the physical consistency of the evolution governed by equation (
12
), in particular that the resulting density matrix must remain positive semi-definite throughout its evolution.
However, even with these constraints, the Lindbladian is not uniquely fixed.

To specify it further, we rely on the known characteristics of the system, whose dynamics are to be approximated by the equation in Eq. (
12
).
For instance: as we assumed that all Hamiltonian terms commute with one another, the diagonal elements of the density matrix, expressed in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian, remain constant over time.
Thus, the quantum nature of the system manifests in its ability to sustain coherent superpositions between two basis states:
\ket{a_{1}}
and
\ket{a_{2}}
for subsystem
A
, and
\ket{b_{1}}
and
\ket{b_{2}}
for subsystem
B
.
The decoherence process, which underlies the transition from quantum to classical behavior, acts to suppress these coherences, transforming the pure state into a statistical mixture while preserving the probabilities associated with each basis state.
This behavior can be captured by Linbladian with matrix
D
for which all elements in the fourth row and fourth column vanish and the only non-zero elements are
K_{x}
,
K_{y}
and
f_{xy}
.
Among the various possibilities, the simplest choice corresponds to setting

K_{x}=K_{y}=\lambda
with
\lambda>0
and
f_{xy}=0
.
For the sake of clarity in presenting our results, in the following we will adopt this assumption.
Nevertheless, we emphasize that our results have a universal character, as similar behaviors have been obtained for different choices of the matrix

D
that satisfy the conditions outlined above.

Having specified the Lindbladian operator,
we begin, as we have done before, by considering an initial state which is separable, i.e., a product of the two individual states given in Eq. (
4
).
In this way, the density matrix of the system at a generic time
t>0
takes the form

\!\rho\!=\!\frac{1}{4}\!\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-2t(\lambda+\imath\omega)}&e^{-2t(\lambda+\imath\omega)}&e^{-4\lambda t}\\
e^{-2t(\lambda-\imath\omega)}&1&e^{-4\lambda t}&e^{-2t(\lambda-\imath\omega)}\\
e^{-2t(\lambda-\imath\omega)}&e^{-4\lambda t}&1&e^{-2t(\lambda-\imath\omega)}\\
e^{-4\lambda t}&e^{-2t(\lambda+\imath\omega)}&e^{-2t(\lambda+\imath\omega)}&1\end{pmatrix}

(14)

At first glance, Eq. (
8
)
and Eq. (
14
) appear quite similar, and one might initially expect that the evolution of entanglement in the short-time regime,
where a universal comparison is meaningful, would also be similar.
However, a more careful analysis reveals a significant difference between the two. Specifically, while the functions
\Gamma_{\alpha}
and
\Lambda_{\pm}
in eq. (
9
), which determine the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix, can be approximated in the limit
0<|\omega|t\ll 1
by exponentials with purely imaginary exponents, this is not the case in Eq. (
14
).
Instead, the exponentials appearing in that equation always have exponents that are either real or complex with a nonzero real part, but never purely imaginary.

By evaluating the concurrence, assuming the short-time limit
0<|\omega|t\ll 1
and using a Taylor expansion, the leading-order contribution in time is the one proportional to
t
that is equal to

\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+2\omega^{2}+\Delta}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+2\omega^{2}-\Delta}-2\lambda

(15)

where
\Delta=2\sqrt{\omega^{2}(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})}
.
This quantity is positive – and therefore indicates the presence of nonzero entanglement – if and only if
|\omega|>\lambda
.
Contrary to the many-body framework, in which entanglement emerges naturally at short times, the Lindblad formalism predicts an entanglement growth rate that depends explicitly on the dephasing parameter
\lambda
and also allows for a non-zero concurrence only if
|\omega|>\lambda
.
This reveals a significant limitation of the Lindblad model, which can oversimplify or inadequately represent critical features of entanglement dynamics in open quantum systems.

A similar issue arises in the analysis of purity, which, based on the state given in Eq. (
14
), becomes equal to
\cosh^{2}(2\lambda t)e^{-4\lambda t}
.
Thus, in the short-time regime, the deviation from a pure state scales linearly in
t
and it is not proportional to
t^{2}
, as derived from the microscopical treatment in the previous section.
This indicates an additional shortcoming of the Lindblad formalism in accurately capturing the system’s behavior at short times.

V
Conclusion

This work investigates the applicability of the Lindblad master equation in capturing the dynamics of fundamental quantum informational properties, namely entanglement and purity, in open quantum systems.
We focus on a system composed of two coupled two-level systems (qubits), where the interaction Hamiltonian allows for a basis of product eigenstates.
These two qubits are placed in a larger bath made of analogous systems, all interacting via similar pairwise interactions.

While a full characterization of such many-body dynamics typically requires an exact specification of numerous system and environmental parameters, a universal behavior emerges in the short-time limit.
This motivates a comparison between the outcomes derived from a microscopic model and those predicted by the Lindblad formalism.
Contrary to expectations, our study finds that the latter, despite its mathematical elegance and computational simplicity, fails to accurately reproduce the early-time dynamics of both purity and entanglement.

In the microscopic model, the growth of entanglement between the two main qubits proceeds similarly to the isolated case, and purity decays quadratically with time.
However, when applying the Lindblad approach, the purity exhibits a linear decay, and more crucially, entanglement can be entirely suppressed.
Indded, we observe a critical threshold: when the Lindbladian amplitude
\lambda
exceeds the coherent interaction strength, entanglement fails to develop.
It is worth noticing that this condition can be realized in densely connected or highly interactive systems and hence it cannot be downplayed as a patological situation.

An interesting workaround to this discrepancy involves assuming a time-dependent dissipative strength, such as
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}t
.
While this assumption restores the correct short-time decay behavior for both purity and entanglement, it introduces inconsistencies elsewhere.

Although the Lindblad master equation remains a central tool for modeling open quantum systems, especially when microscopic modeling or large-scale simulation is impractical, our analysis highlights its limitations.
Even cutting-edge numerical frameworks, such as tensor network techniques or variational neural-network methods, cannot overcome the scalability constraints of high-dimensional systems.
Hence, it becomes crucial to interpret the results from effective models like Lindblad with critical scrutiny, especially when dealing with subtle observables sensitive to quantum coherence and correlation structures.
