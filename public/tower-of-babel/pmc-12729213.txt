Evolving core values in family medicine across regions: a global scoping review
Authors: Jia Hua Low, Justin Wei Jun Ng, Shu Yun Tan, Kai Ping Sze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily Medicine (FM) core values shape clinical practice, medical education, policy, and research. While universally recognised FM principles exist, their interpretation and implementation may vary significantly across different contexts, countries and time.MethodsThis scoping review systematically examined literature from diverse international sources to identify and analyse the evolving core values of FM. Relevant studies were reviewed to determine common themes, variations, contextual influences shaping FM and emerging themes.ResultsTwenty-one studies were included. Core values such as person-centred care, continuity, comprehensiveness, and coordination featured prominently across the literature. Although these traditional values persisted, their interpretations and prioritisations varied significantly according to geopolitical and healthcare system structures. Regional differences reflected local workforce structures, healthcare challenges, and sociopolitical context. High-income regions emphasised values like relational care, stewardship, and advocacy reflecting evolving expectations towards social responsiveness. Lower-resourced areas prioritised accessibility, procedural competence, and team-based leadership, responding pragmatically to infrastructural constraints. Over time, new values such as sustainability, comfort with complexity, and social accountability have emerged, indicating global concern for equity, physician well-being and proactive public engagement.DiscussionThis review highlights a dynamic landscape of FM values, shaped by both universal principles and local adaptation. As countries refine their health systems and FM roles, there is a need for value frameworks that are both globally informed and locally grounded. Clear articulation of values will guide education, policy, research, and professional identity, ensuring that FM remains responsive and relevant amidst evolving healthcare landscapes.Keywords: Family medicine, Core values, Professional identity, Social accountability, Sustainability

Introduction

Family Medicine (FM) plays an essential role in healthcare systems worldwide, often serving as the first point of contact for patients [1]. Over the years, it has evolved alongside the communities it serves, adapting to shifting societal expectations, medical advancements and emerging healthcare challenges [2]. FM clinicians do not merely manage diseases; they also focus on how care is delivered – through first contact, continuity of care, comprehensiveness, and coordination (“the 4Cs”) [3]. It bridges the biological, psychological, and social domains to achieve lower mortality, better equity and higher patient satisfaction than specialist-centric models [4, 5]. This unique and highly extensive discipline is represented by practitioners with wide-ranging knowledge who can apply it across different settings of care [6].

Countless thought leaders have helped shape FM’s foundational philosophy [7–9], setting a bearing for the expectations of the profession and the training and development of future practitioners to keep FM relevant to the population she serves. These core values have often been re-evaluated during times of change, when there was a need to chart out a new direction or re-establish the importance of the existing core values in the face of new challenges [10].

International organisations and national colleges have since elaborated these ideas into value frameworks to guide curricula, professional standards, and service design [11–15]. This is an empowering and defining move, and is a move seen to address some of the moral distress faced by family physicians working within ever-changing and more demanding health landscapes [16, 17].

Defining Family Medicine (FM) core values clearly is essential not only for professional identity, but also for guiding clinical education, informing health policy, and enhancing service quality. In medical training, well-articulated values shape the attitudes and behaviours expected of future practitioners, anchoring them in person-centred, holistic care even as clinical demands evolve. At the policy level, core values provide a foundation for system design and offer benchmarks for quality improvement. Together, they keep FM responsive, ethical, and grounded in the unique philosophy of Family Medicine.

International colleges and organisations have elaborated FM’s values into frameworks that inform curricula, standards, and models of care [18]. For example, WONCA Europe has published core values relevant to EU countries [19]. Though core values of Family Medicine may be universal and Family Medicine institutions globally can easily adopt these values, there may be hierarchy of values and nuances in interpretation of terminology that may take on different meanings and applications.

Singapore faces rapid population aging, multimorbidity, and widening social needs that require a stronger primary care front door [20]. Current national initiatives such as Healthier SG signal a strategic shift towards empanelment, longitudinal relationships, team-based and community-linked care, proactive prevention, population health management, and digital enablement to support care continuity across settings [21]. In parallel, Family Medicine has been formally recognised as medical specialty in Singapore, elevating expectations for scope of practice, training pathways, and professional accountability [22]. Together, workforce transformation and specialty recognition constitute a rare policy window to align identity, education and service models around a contemporary value set and quality and regulatory frameworks.

Although multiple frameworks exist, three gaps persist. First, there is conceptual divergence and terminology drift: similar constructs are labelled differently across regions, such as “person-centredness” vs. “patient-centredness” or “comprehensiveness” vs. “generalist scope”, and the relative hierarchy of values is seldom made explicit, complicating translation into curricula and standards. Second, the temporal evolution of FM since 1990, spanning digital health, team-based practice, population-health management, and social accountability, has reframed earlier formulations; a longitudinal synthesis is needed to distinguish enduring anchors from necessary updates. Third, contextual fit for Singapore remains underdeveloped: most frameworks arise from high-income Western contexts, whereas Singapore’s mixed provider landscape, strong central stewardship, multicultural population and rapid digital adoption require a synthesis that is globally informed yet locally actionable.

This scoping review aims to map and synthesise contemporary literature that defines the core values of Family Medicine across different global contexts. Its primary objective is to examine how formal definitions of Family Medicine core values have evolved globally from 1990 to 2025, and to explore the similarities and differences observed across regions and stages of primary care system development. The secondary objective is to explore how contextual factors, such as health system design, sociocultural expectations, and policy changes, have influenced the regional articulation and emphasis of these values. Through this dual focus, the review seeks to uncover potential gaps in the synthesised understanding of Family Medicine values and identify areas for future research and contextual adaptation.

Methods

This scoping review systematically identified, mapped, and synthesised the expression of FM core values across different countries utilising methodology guided by Arksey and O’Malley [23] and Levac et al. [24]. The paper adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [25] (Fig. 1).

PRISMA-ScR Flow Diagram of Study Selection

Results and discussion are jointly presented to enable a thematic and interpretive synthesis of findings across diverse sources. Given the conceptual nature of this review – focusing on the evolving definitions and expressions of core values in Family Medicine – each thematic segment integrates both the descriptive mapping of values and a contextual interpretation of their significance. This approach aligns with the objectives of a scoping review as outlined by the PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidance, which support flexibility in reporting when exploring conceptual boundaries, emerging trends, and variations across settings.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian to identify peer-reviewed literature and grey literature that define the core values of Family Medicine. The search study was published on Open Science Framework [26]. The search was conducted on PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus. Grey literature sources included the websites of major Family Medicine professional organizations in various countries. If formal position statements were not available on the website, an email correspondence was sent to the secretariat of the national Family Medicine professional body (or the email contact available on the WONCA website [27]) for further information regarding the presence of core values espoused by the country.

The search strategy included a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to Family Medicine and core values. Boolean operators and truncation were used to optimize sensitivity and specificity. The search approach balanced comprehensiveness with precision by including and exploring the general index terms such as “principles” and “family medicine” with other free text search terms. Before finalising, the searches were checked for sensitivity and relevance and peer reviewed for accuracy and consistency. The final search was conducted on 18 May 2025.

The search string for PubMed was: (“Family Practice” [Mesh] OR “family practice” [tiab] OR “family medicine” [tiab]) AND (“professional values” [tiab] OR “core values” [tiab] OR “family medicine values” [tiab] OR “guiding values” [tiab] OR “professional identity” [tiab] OR “moral values” [tiab] OR “values in practice” [tiab] OR “philosophy of care” [tiab]). The full search strategies of the databases are illustrated in Appendix 1.

Equivalent terms such as “GP” or “general practice” were not included because they are used variably in the literature, sometimes referring to broader primary care contexts without explicit articulation of Family Medicine core values. Pilot searches showed they retrieved a high volume of irrelevant articles, reducing specificity of the review.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies which fulfilled the following criteria: (i) clearly defined FM values or position statements explicitly address core values.; (ii) editorials, opinion articles, or qualitative research primarily addressing FM values; and (iii) availability in English and full-text format in peer-reviewed journals or credible grey literature sources. For the purposes of this review, “formal definitions of FM core values” were operationally defined as explicit, authoritative descriptions of Family Medicine core values issued by recognised professional bodies, international organisations (e.g. WONCA, WHO), national colleges or associations, or peer-reviewed scholarly works where the authors clearly state such definitions as part of conceptual or policy frameworks.

Studies were excluded if they: (i) primarily discussed health systems broadly without specific reference to FM; (ii) focused on medical education interventions involving medical students without discussing core values; (iii) concentrated solely on specific medical interventions or clinical practices; and (iv) were published before 1 January 1990. These exclusion criteria were selected to limit the search to core values defined by family physicians for family physician. 1990 was established as a cut-off as the period following this marked a period of global expansion and formalization of the discipline.

Data extraction and analysis

The screening process was conducted using the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews [22]. Two reviewers (JH, TSY) first conducted screening of both titles and abstracts (agreement = 87%, κ = 0.53), and then full texts (agreement = 83%, κ = 0.65), with conflicts at both stages resolved by a 3rd reviewer (SKP).

Data extraction was conducted by all four reviewers (LJH, TSY, SKP, JN) using a standardised form including the issuing organization, country, year of publication, stated Family Medicine values, and contextual features of the originating country’s healthcare system. Data was synthesised descriptively, highlighting both shared principles and regional variations, to identify trends in the data. Countries were included if they had published formal definitions of Family Medicine core values meeting the eligibility criteria during 1990–2025, either in peer-reviewed literature or in policy/official documents. This ensured coverage of diverse health system contexts and regional variations in value articulation. Values were coded deductively to a priori domains and inductively for emergent themes. The team held regular discussions daily to refine codes and agree on emergent themes. Covidence was utilised for the screening and data extraction process [28].

A total of twenty-one studies out of 460 were identified to systematically map out explicit FM core values in existing literature. We proceed to describe the definition of main core values identified, the temporal trend in FM core values, regional variation in core values, changes in the definition of core values and contemporary concepts. The reasons identified for revisiting and redefining core values by the various countries largely stemmed from a few motivating factors, including to clarify and reaffirm professional identity amid systemic healthcare transformations [29, 30], address inadequacies in traditional definitions unaligned with local realities [31, 32], and to respond proactively to external pressures such as healthcare inequities, fragmentation, and evolving societal health challenges [30, 33–36].

Themes

Core values identified

Starfield’s 4Cs [7] of primary care were featured in most of the selected articles. These core values are comprehensive care, continuity of care and collaboration.

Traditionally, the definitions of the 4Cs [3] are broadly defined as follows:

A.Comprehensive care: The ability to manage a wide range of health problems, across all age groups, sexes, and conditions.

Comprehensive care: The ability to manage a wide range of health problems, across all age groups, sexes, and conditions.

B.Coordination of care: The integration and management of care across multiple services and levels of the healthcare system.

C.Continuity of care: The provision of ongoing care over time by a single provider or team, across different episodes of illness.

D.First contact: Serving as the initial point of entry into the health system for all new health concerns.

Temporal trends

In the 2000 s, discourse mostly focused on continuity, comprehensiveness and generalism [37]. Core values were defined in the face of system fragmentation and increasing complexity in the United States [37], while Africa had begun the process of identifying its core values [31].

In the 2010 s, the emphasis shifted towards relational care, social context, and teamwork. It is during this period that person-centredness also increasingly emerged in the literature – not as a replacement of Starfield’s 4Cs but as a complementary value that aligned with the evolving expectations of primary care physicians and patients. This shift reflects a growing prioritisation of values rooted in ethics, humanism, and professional identity, particularly in response to rising rates of burnout, moral distress of practicing in fragmented systems and calls for social accountability. The frame of reference expanded beyond the clinical encounter to include the physicians’ role in advocacy, leadership, and social justice [33, 36, 38–41].

There existed a wealth of data in the 2020s. A significant trend was the rapid re-evaluation of core values in response to the pandemic and to modern technology such as telemedicine. There was also greater regional and national ownership of core values, with clearly elucidated differences in core values, and how/why they differed from the traditional model [35].

Regional variations

European countries showed divergent maturity with advanced definition in Denmark, UK, Iceland and Netherlands versus ongoing development in Romania and Poland [35]. Many newer concepts arose from countries with significant and developed primary care systems and a robust specialist system [39], which are discussed later.

Underserved countries were likely to list skill competencies, accessibility, and comprehensiveness within their core values. In Ukraine, family physicians work under resource pressure and an urban-rural divide, resulting in core values focused more explicitly on maintaining basic continuity (“first recourse”) and comprehensiveness within constrained systems, rather than the broader community leadership role prominent in Nordic regions [32]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, most primary care tasks are led by nurses, while family physicians take on more team leadership and referral roles within regional and district hospitals. Their core values reflect this reality, emphasising procedural competence, teamwork, and leadership [31, 34, 40].

In addition, there was less emphasis on individual doctor-patient relationships in Africa [31, 34, 40], as most primary care tasks tend to be undertaken by nurses and teams, and patients are not registered to a single primary care provider.

Changes in definition of core values

Upon comparing publications from 1990 to present, there are interesting trends that show the changes in definitions of core values over time.

Changes in “continuity of care”

Continuity of care shows an evolution from its traditional form definition, and a distinction has been made between personal continuity of care, and managerial or informational continuity of care [42]. Patients only saw value in personal continuity of care in certain circumstances such as psychological disease or antenatal care, preferring accessibility in other contexts, deviating from the perceptions of family medicine physicians [43].

There were also marked differences in how core values were ranked by employed GPs, with a majority considering personal continuity to be less important, whilst other core values such as self-care, distance from patients and collaboration were considered more important [38]. While older GPs and countries with a long tradition of Family Medicine practice tended to value the personal continuity with the patient [32, 35, 36], this was seen to negatively impact accessibility.

Changes in “comprehensive care”

The definition of comprehensive care in Family Medicine has undergone significant transformation across global contexts, shaped by differing health systems, workforce structures, and societal expectation. Traditionally, “comprehensiveness in care” was defined as the ability to provide across patient demographics and health conditions. However, contemporary interpretations increasing view comprehensiveness as a relational, contextual, and moral commitment, rather than a fixed basket of services.

In high-income settings such as Canada, comprehensiveness is now associated with whole person care that spans biological, psychological, and social dimensions. Grierson et al. highlight how this redefinition has prompted a shift in educational frameworks, integrating social accountability and whole-person care into FM training [33].

In the U.S., comprehensiveness in care is seen as a bulwark against specialisation and system fragmentation. Martin et al. reconceptualised it within a “New Model” of care, which promotes a coordinated team-based care tailored to patient needs [37]. Hashim further critiques traditional notions of comprehensiveness, warning that the term may mislead both patients and professionals. He advocates instead for a “generalist” framing, emphasising wide-ranging clinical expertise, but without overpromising universal service delivery [26].

In contrast, lower-resource (health systems with constrained financial, human, or infrastructural resources) or transitional settings (countries undergoing major systemic shifts in their health systems) articulate comprehensiveness pragmatically. The African consensus describes it as a strategic response to fragmented infrastructure, realised through inter-hospital referrals and district-level procedural competence [31, 34]. Family physicians in these contexts serve not just as clinicians, but as leaders who bridge gaps in service delivery across teams and levels of care. An African Delphi study found that while surgical competence and team-based longitudinal care were acknowledged as being important, practical priorities reflected the realities of scarce specialist access and workforce gaps [31]. Consequently, comprehensive care, supported by broad procedural and district-hospital capabilities, was ranked highest with aspirations for expanded service models seen as largely theoretical under current constraints.

Comprehensiveness also plays a critical role in equity promotion. In underserved regions, defined broadly to include rural or remote areas with limited primary care access, socioeconomically disadvantaged urban communities and marginalised population who face systemic barriers to healthcare, it is a strategy to mitigate access disparities by ensuring that patients receive essential care – even if not every ideal service is available. In Ukraine, for instance, comprehensiveness is perceived as an aspirational standard to be gradually achieved as system capacity improves. In its current form, it is defined by a “do the best with what you have” ethos, reflecting the country’s evolving health financing and delivery structure [32].

Across these contexts, comprehensiveness remains a universally espoused but variably expressed core value. While some systems pursue it as an ultimate core value for care integration, others deploy it as a means of survival and equity. Ultimately, the evolution of comprehensiveness reflects the degree in which Family Medicine is responsive to context, where resource density, referral pathways, professional identity, and patient needs shape how this core value is interpreted, prioritised, and practised.

Changes in “coordination of care”

Coordination of care has deepened in response to the growing systemic complexity, workforce diversification, and patient transitions across care setting. In the Future of Family Medicine collaborative project [37], coordination was framed as essential for achieving continuity, especially amidst rising specialisation and fragmentation in the U.S. healthcare system. Coordination was positioned as part of a broader movement toward the “personal medical home”, where team-based care ensured seamless transitions and shared accountability.

Ventres [30] further expounded on this systems-based interpretation by applying the complexity and systems theory to highlight that coordination cannot be solely managed by the physician - it must be supported structurally across institutions. Her reflections highlighted that while physicians value relationship-centred care, real-world fragmentation often disrupts efforts to maintain it, thus requiring team leadership, shared information systems and shared accountability.

In sub-Saharan Africa, coordination carries a more pragmatic definition, shaped by workforce scarcities and a heavy reliance on nurses for first-contact care. Mash et al. and Namatovu et al. describe how coordination is tied to procedural competence and team functioning across different facilities, with family physicians often acting as referral leads and system navigators within district hospitals [31, 34]. Coordination of care here entails managing upstream referrals, supporting remote clinics, and bridging care gaps rather than managing transitions of care within a single practice.

Contemporary concepts

Social activism and advocacy

There is a growing recognition of the need for social accountability and advocacy in the face of widening health inequities. Core values are expanding beyond individual-level care to embrace the physician’s role as a community advocate and policy influencer. FM organizations are increasingly calling on physicians to reclaim the discipline’s “counterculture heritage [13, 41]” and engage in social activism, particularly in addressing the social determinants of health.

The Nordic core values state a “duty to speak out publicly on specific factors that cause or worsen disease, increase inequality in health outcomes, or make resources less accessible to certain people [41]”. This ethos is echoed in calls for “leaders who would be ready to influence people, communities and policy on health-related issues [44]” and in the expectation that family physicians “adopt the perspectives of that community… to engage in leadership and policy-making that will affect change… a commitment to social accountability and fostering changes that are relevant to the social determinants of health for the whole population [33]”.

These emerging values reflect a broader pivot that positions family physicians as agents of systemic change.

Evolving interpretations of appropriate care: from comprehensiveness to stewardship

Appropriate care in Family Medicine have evolved in response to advancing technology, rising medical complexity, and increasing costs of care. Contemporary core value statements have begun to reflect a heightened awareness of resource stewardship, risk management, and the need to avoid harm from overdiagnosis and overtreatment, acknowledging that “overexamination, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment can harm patients… [and] consume resources [42]”, underscoring the ethical and systemic implications of unchecked intervention.

The shift toward remote and hybrid care has further reshaped how appropriateness is defined. An international consensus on telemedicine in Family Medicine notes that care “can be delivered either in-person or by remote access,” based on “patients’ preferences, medical needs, and local health system organization.” This emphasises that adaptability is now central to how appropriateness is practically understood [45].

Managing complexity and ambiguity

There is a growing emphasis on comfort with uncertainty and complexity, recognising that clinical ambiguity is an inherent part of primary care. This shift is echoed in the educational domain, where “complexity and uncertainty” is identified as one of the four hallmarks of Family Medicine practice, arguing that comfort with clinical ambiguity must be deliberately cultivated through training – an area described as underexplored in current FM education research [33, 39].

Sustainability

Sustainability is increasingly framed as a core value in Family Medicine, encompassing resource stewardship, environmental responsibility, and health system resilience. The focus has expanded beyond individual patient outcomes and cost-effectiveness to environmental sustainability and appropriate care [46]. The Nordic statement similarly warns that “overexamination, overdiagnosis, and overtreatment… consume resources,” urging cost-effective, evidence-based decisions that protect both patients and the system [39].

Equally, sustainability includes clinician well-being as a foundation for safe, ethical care, largely the influence of a pivotal paper about the Quadruple Aim [12]. Luo et al. advocates for mindfulness, reflection, and peer support to prevent burnout and support professional identity [46]. Hashim emphasises “reflective mindfulness” as a core value, enabling presence and judgment in complex care [32]. The Nordic framework and Horn et al. echo this, highlighting the importance of resilience, emotional boundaries, and work-life balance [38, 39]. Across contexts, sustaining the caregiver is increasingly viewed as essential to sustaining care itself. Remote care was identified as potentially “interfering… with private work life balance [40]”, and a potential future threat to workplace resilience.

Discussion

This scoping review shows substantive evolution in the definitions and interpretations of Family Medicine (FM) core values globally the past three decades.

One key interpretation emerging from the review findings is a consistent tension encountered when defining FM core values across settings between what clinicians value, what patients prioritise, and what health systems incentivise. Patients often favour rapid access over personal continuity except in specific context (e.g. psychological care, antenatal care), whereas clinicians variably de-emphasise personal continuity in favour of autonomy, collaboration and boundaries; at the same time, systems foreground coordination and sustainability to manage fragmentation and cost. These patterns are visible in our results: (i) Changes in continuity – patients’ conditional preference for personal continuity and trade-offs with access; (ii) Regional variation – Africa’s team-led models and Ukraine’s “first recourse” continuity under constraint, and (iii) Contemporary concepts such as resource stewardship, telemedicine-enabled appropriateness, and clinician well-being. Taken together, the balance among clinician, patient, and system values is not theoretical but emerges directly from these triangulated findings. These competing interests necessitate a holistic and integrative approach to defining core values, a defining strength of FM. Addressing this tension explicitly allows for the development of contextually sensitive value frameworks that can help to reconcile individual care needs, community expectations, and systemic objectives.

Another critical insight from this review is that the transformation in FM values is not merely semantic but deeply pragmatic, shaped by systemic pressure, sociocultural expectations, and the dynamic interplay between clinician, patient, and health system priorities. The shift towards embracing social advocacy, stewardship of resources and comfort with clinical ambiguity reflects a discipline grappling with its identity amidst increasing healthcare complexities and inequities. Our findings above underpins treating tolerance of uncertainty a as a teachable core value. As shown in the “contemporary concepts” results section, several regions explicitly recast the family physician as a public advocate, grounding our call for social accountability. Likewise, contemporary concepts such as appropriate care or stewardship (including the telemedicine consensus) support reframing appropriateness around avoiding overuse and matching modality to need. This progression further highlights FM’s essential adaptability, positioning family physicians not just as medical providers, but as advocates, system navigators, and leaders in community health. Translating these values into actionable frameworks may help to narrow the gap between aspiration and practice, though empirical evaluation is needed to determine impact. Consistent with sustainability findings, value frameworks should also protect clinician well-being (as part of the Quadruple Aim), highlighting mindfulness, boundaries and resilience for safe, ethical care.

As WONCA codifies region-specific core, there is a need for frameworks that assess not just which values are named, but how they are enacted. Frameworks should assess not only which values are named but how they are enacted. We propose categorising values as “relevant in theory,” “relevant in action,” or “not relevant” which was useful in deriving the African consensus [31]. This proposal reflects our synthesis of the African consensus and its emphasis on pragmatic enactment. It also highlights the gap between formal statements and real-world practice and helps develop more grounded, context-sensitive value sets that reflect both regional ideals and implementation realities.

Our synthesis indicates meaningful regional divergence in how FM values are named, prioritised, and operationalised. These differences are reflected on health-system design, financing arrangements, workforce mix, population needs, and sociocultural norms. Notably, divergence occurs along three axes: (i) terminology (i.e. “patient-centred” vs. “person-centred”), (ii) priority and hierarchy among values (what is treated as foundational vs. enabling), and (iii) translation in practice.

In Europe, statements frequently place strong weight on relational continuity and person-centredness within list-based or empanelled systems, with coordination framed through shared care plans and information continuity [35]. The African consensus highlights community orientation, social accountability, task-sharing, and equitable access under resource constraints [31, 34]. Australia and New Zealand places visible emphasis on rural-remote access, indigenous health and cultural safety, and team-based comprehensiveness [47]. Across parts of Asia, values frequently stress coordination, family and community roles, and professionalism, with continuity variously conceived as relational or informational within mixed public-private delivery and rapid digitalisation. These examples are illustrative rather than exhaustive and substantial within-region variation remains.

Implication for practice and research

While embracing shared universal humanistic values and aspirations, each region must interpret these global values within its context. For example in Singapore, a very fast paced and rapidly aging country, we may need to uphold of spirit of care continuity in the form of informational continuity rather than personal longitudinal continuity. It is also possible for regional leaders to anchor and illuminate certain values, steering the global family medicine advancement as a harmonious movement with diversity of voices.

The next challenge lies not only in defining these values, but in ensuring their meaningful actualisation across all levels of the health system. For educators, this means designing curricula that reflect contemporary challenges, incorporating emergent values such as comfort with uncertainty, reflexive mindfulness, sustainability, and appropriate care in complexity. Skills training on digital consultations, and comfort with role fluidity would need to be incorporated. Policymakers must align incentives with values that reduce system burden, discourage over-investigation, and support care that is relational, not transactional. Researchers should explore how these values translate into measurable outcomes, such as continuity’s impact on hospitalisation rates or years of functional life. Together, these efforts will strengthen FM’s professional identity and prepare family physicians for leadership, advocacy, and long-term sustainability in the years to come.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. We excluded non-English language publications. This may have led to the omission of perspectives from non-English speaking countries, limiting the global representativeness of the study. Secondly, the authors are based in Singapore and primarily practice in settings staffed by Family Physicians with minimal overseas experience. As such, there is a possibility of interpretive bias, with findings viewed through a Singaporean cultural and healthcare lens. To mitigate this, we grounded our thematic analysis in widely recognised international definitions wherever possible. Thirdly, the review excluded studies that captured insights from patients, the public, allied health professionals, medical students, and practitioners from other specialties. This may have led to a narrower understanding of core values, particularly those shaped by interprofessional collaboration or patient expectations. Future work could incorporate broader stakeholder perspectives to enrich the findings. Next, many of the included studies are reflective or cross-sectional in nature. Longitudinal research capturing how core values evolve over time in response to system reforms or societal shifts was limited. We attempted to compensate by examining studies across a range of publication years to identify temporal patterns where available.

Some geographical regions, such as Latin America, were underrepresented in our sample. While we used broad database searches to capture a diverse range of studies, the dominance of studies from high-income countries may have influenced the thematic emphasis. In addition, the definition and operationalisation of key core values (e.g., continuity, holism, generalism) varied across studies. This definitional inconsistency may have posed challenges in synthesising findings. We addressed this by providing clear descriptions of each theme based on the most common interpretations in the literature. Lastly, although grey literature was included to capture policy documents and institutional perspectives, informal sources such as blogs or community advocacy publications were excluded to maintain methodological rigor. This may have limited the diversity of voices captured.

Despite these limitations, this scoping review provides a foundational synthesis of evolving core values in Family Medicine and offers insights for educational, clinical, and policy development.

Conclusion

This scoping review has systematically mapped out explicit FM core values in existing literature and highlights their significant evolution and regional diversity from 1990 to 2025. Traditional values of continuity, comprehensiveness and coordination remain foundational yet are redefined by local healthcare challenges, workforce dynamics and societal expectations. Emerging values such as social advocacy, stewardship, managing complexity, and sustainability have become increasingly prominent. For Family Medicine to retain its relevance and effectiveness, core values must be adapted contextually rather than universally adopted. Future efforts should focus on translating these core values into actionable policies, educational curricula, and measurable clinical outcomes, thus ensuring that they remain meaningful within diverse and evolving healthcare landscapes.
