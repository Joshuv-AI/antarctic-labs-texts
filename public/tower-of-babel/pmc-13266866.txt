Chikungunya fever in Guangdong, China, 2025: rapid spread and challenges
Authors: Zhijie Lin, Ruibin Liu, Xuanfeng Li, Zhiqi Zeng, Xiaoyan Deng, Zifeng Yang, Yihui Huang, Di Wang, Chitin Hon
Published: 2026

To the editor, chikungunya fever (CHIKF) is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by chikungunya virus (CHIKV), primarily transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus (1). Since its discovery in 1952, CHIKF has spread across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. China reported its first imported case in 2008. As of 2025, multiple provincial-level regions—including Guangdong—have detected imported cases, often linked to Southeast Asia (2,3).

In July 2025, Guangdong Province reported a surge in CHIKF cases, beginning with an imported case in Foshan. Within weeks, cumulative confirmed cases rose sharply from 481 (July 14) to 9,103 (August 9), with nearly all concentrated in Foshan, additional cases were reported in Guangzhou, Zhongshan, and Shenzhen (4,5). Macao SAR recorded 7 imported cases, four of which had recent travel history to Foshan. No fatalities were reported during the study period; however, CHIKF is characterized by severe and often debilitating polyarthralgia, which may persist for months or even years in a substantial proportion of patients, leading to long-term functional impairment.

CHIKV infects both humans and non-human primates. Infected individuals may experience high fever, severe joint pain, rash, or fatigue, though 15–35% of infections may be asymptomatic. Vulnerable groups—such as infants, the elderly, and those with comorbidities—are at higher risk of complications. The virus remains active year-round in tropical climates, with seasonal peaks in China between July and November (6).

As of 2023–2024, licensed vaccines against CHIKV have become available in certain regions, including the live-attenuated vaccine Ixchiq, representing an important new tool for outbreak prevention in high-risk populations. The availability of vaccination highlights the need to incorporate immunization strategies into future outbreak preparedness plans (7,8).

In terms of public attention, Guangdong Province experienced a severe dengue fever outbreak in 2024. As shown in Figure 1A, the Baidu index for “dengue fever” showed a trend closely aligned with the number of reported cases, rising sharply in late September and remaining high for about two months. As case numbers declined from November, public attention decreased correspondingly, reaching low levels by late December. Based on this temporal relationship, we conducted an exploratory comparison with the chikungunya outbreak in 2025 (Figure 1B). The analysis suggested that public attention may follow epidemic intensity; however, given that chikungunya represented a relatively novel and rapidly expanding outbreak in mainland China, the actual attention trajectory could differ depending on media coverage, government response, and perceived disease severity. Therefore, this analysis should be interpreted as a descriptive comparison rather than a deterministic prediction.

The 2025 Guangdong outbreak coincided with favorable environmental conditions. Aedes mosquitoes, under temperatures of 15–35 ℃, exhibit accelerated development and transmission (9). To provide an early situational forecast during the rapidly evolving outbreak, we developed a pragmatic hybrid forecasting framework integrating vector-informed transmission dynamics with time-series calibration. The mechanistic component incorporated key Aedes mosquito-related transmission parameters (including temperature-dependent vector activity and transmission potential) to characterize the underlying epidemic growth pattern. In parallel, real-time chikungunya case data were assimilated using a short-term time-series calibration module to improve near-term predictive accuracy. Instead of assuming identical epidemiological dynamics between dengue and chikungunya, the 2024 dengue outbreak (10) was used only as a reference temporal template to initialize the epidemic curve, while real-time chikungunya case data were used for calibration and adjustment.

Using case data available as of August 9, 2025, and integrating pathogen detection, climatic conditions, and vector distribution information into a multi-source heterogeneous database, we generated a short-term series forecasting for the Guangdong outbreak. The model estimated that daily new cases would decline below 10 by mid-November 2025, with a cumulative total of approximately 30,340 local infections [95% confidence interval (CI): 17,333–47,118] (Figure 1C).

A retrospective validation was conducted after the epidemic subsided. As of December 11, 2025, the officially reported cumulative number of confirmed local cases in Guangdong Province was 25,337. The predicted magnitude achieved an accuracy of 80.3%, and the projected epidemic end time was consistent with observed data, supporting the utility of this rapid forecasting framework for early outbreak situational awareness. To mitigate future outbreaks, we propose the following measures: (I) reduce mosquito breeding sites through community cleanup and targeted insecticide spraying; (II) enhance surveillance, including rapid diagnostic reporting and viral genotyping; (III) strengthen clinical response through healthcare worker training; (IV) expand public awareness campaigns; (V) consider targeted vaccination strategies for high-risk populations where licensed vaccines are available; and (VI) establish cross-regional coordination across the Greater Bay Area. The Guangdong CHIKF outbreak underscores the high transmission potential of arboviral diseases in densely populated regions with established vector presence. While current cases are mild, rapid spread and international spillover risk highlight the importance of early warning systems and integrated response mechanisms.
