Matrix Inversion by Quantum Walk
Alastair Kay, Christino Tamon

Abstract
The HHL algorithm for matrix inversion is a landmark algorithm in quantum computation. Its ability to produce a state | x ⟩ \ket{x} that is the solution of A ​ x = b Ax=b , given the input state | b ⟩ \ket{b} , is envisaged to have diverse applications. In this paper, we substantially simplify the algorithm, originally formed of a complex sequence of phase estimations, amplitude amplifications and Hamiltonian simulations, by replacing the phase estimations with a continuous time quantum walk. The key technique is the use of weak couplings to access the matrix inversion embedded in perturbation theory.



The Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm
Harrow et al. 2009
is one of the most celebrated results in quantum computing, marking a pivotal step toward quantum algorithms that outperform their classical counterparts. Intended to deliver a solution for systems of linear equations exponentially faster (subject to certain caveats), HHL is a fundamental element of quantum machine learning
Rebentrost et al. 2014
, solving differential equations
Leyton and Osborne 2008
;
Berry 2014
;
Montanaro and Pallister 2016
, quantum data fitting
Wiebe et al. 2012
, and quantum chemistry
Baskaran et al. 2023
, and has also inspired exciting dequantization strategies
Chia et al. 2020
providing quantum-inspired classical strategies with improved running times.

Despite its vast potential, the original HHL algorithm is difficult to implement, comprising a complex sequence of phase estimations, amplitude amplifications and Hamiltonian simulations that are largely prohibitive for implementation on near-term, very limited, devices (aside from token attempts
Cai et al. 2013
). Improvements in asymptotic performance
Ambainis 2012
;
Childs et al. 2017
only exacerbate this. In this work, we present a streamlined variant, eliminating many of these costly components and simplifying the error analysis. Our new approach relies solely on Hamiltonian simulation, generating a quantum walk
Farhi and Gutmann 1998
;
Farhi et al. 2008
;
Ambainis et al. 2007
. These have been invaluable in finding speed-ups in various contexts such as the glued-trees algorithm
Childs et al. 2003
, including experimental implementation
Shi et al. 2020
, and spatial search
Childs and Goldstone 2004
. Structural modifications of the simulated Hamiltonian directly control the error parameters.

For a (possibly non-square) complex matrix
A
, the HHL algorithm
Harrow et al. 2009
aims to tackle the problem of solving

Ax=b.

Such a proposition is impractical for a quantum computer as, even if one could prepare a state
\ket{x}
, reading it out would require unreasonably long. Instead, it focusses simply on producing the normalised state
\ket{x}
, having been provided with the state
\ket{b}
. The algorithm is founded upon certain key assumptions, namely, the ability to prepare the initial state
\ket{b}
and the ability to efficiently implement a Hamiltonian evolution
Aaronson 2015
. It also requires that the range of singular values of
A
is bounded between (non-zero)
\frac{1}{\kappa}
and
1
, where
\kappa
is known as the condition number of the matrix
A
. We rely on the exact same assumptions. If we allow
A
to be any arbitrary matrix,
Harrow et al. 2009
showed that the problem of creating
\ket{x}
is BQP-hard: at least as hard as any problem that is efficiently solvable on a quantum computer. However, the algorithm made the further assumption that
A
was sparse. This case has since been dequantized
Chia et al. 2020
, giving an efficient classical variant provided assumptions about data access can be fulfilled. While the HHL algorithm readily generalises to allow the evaluation of an observable of any state
\ket{x}=f(A)\ket{b}
, we focus entirely on the primary use case of
f(y)=y^{-1}
. Extensions, such as via linear combinations of unitaries
Childs and Wiebe 2012
or the techniques from the quantum singular value transformation
Gilyén et al. 2019
, will be possible, but we have no new development.

Our main insight is that by taking a matrix
H
into which
A
is embedded with the promise that all the eigenvalues of
H
are at least
\frac{1}{\kappa}
away from 0, then if we couple this using a perturbation
V
to a new system of close to 0 energy, i.e.
H\rightarrow H\oplus H_{0}+V
where
\|V\|\ll\frac{1}{\kappa}
, then we must analyse
H_{0}
under degenerate perturbation theory, finding that gaps open up relating to matrix elements of
H^{-1}
. Thus,
H^{-1}
determines the evolution of states initially supported on the
H_{0}
subsystem. The benefit of our approach is in the remarkable simplicity of implementation, removing the need for many repeated iterations of phase estimation. The basic version that we present first matches the asymptotic performance of the original version of the HHL algorithm. By introducing (mild) additional complexity in
Section

II
, we can match more recent improvements in terms of the scaling with the condition number and the accuracy
Ambainis 2012
;
Childs et al. 2017
.

I
Main System

Imagine that we are given an
A
which we need to invert. This has a singular value decomposition
\{\lambda_{n},\ket{\lambda_n},\ket{\eta_n}\}
, i.e.
A\ket{\lambda_n}=\lambda_{n}\ket{\eta_n}
, and we are promised that
A
has a condition number of
\kappa
, meaning that
\lambda_{n}\kappa>1
for all
n
. We assume that
A
is sparse, which will allow us to invoke a Hamiltonian simulation algorithm such as
Berry et al. 2015
.

Our aim is to solve the linear problem
Ax=b
: given a state
\ket{b}
, we produce a normalised version of the state

\ket{x}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\ket{\lambda_n}\innerproduct{\eta_n}{b}.

(1)

Since
A
may not be square, let alone Hermitian, it is necessary to embed it into a larger matrix for implementation as a Hamiltonian. We choose

H=\begin{bmatrix}0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&0\\
\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&A&0\\
0&A^{\dagger}&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}\\
0&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}

where
\gamma
is a small parameter.
H
is readily implemented using simulation techniques such as
Berry et al. 2015
since its sparsity of only 1 greater than that of
A
.

The plan is to initialise a state
\begin{bmatrix}\bra{b}&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}=\ket{1}\otimes\ket{b}
, evolve it under
H
for a suitable time
t
, and post-select on a state having arrived in the final subsystem,

\ket{\psi}=(\bra{4}\otimes\mathbb{1})e^{-iHt}(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{b}).

Analysis

In order to analyse the evolution of

II
Enhanced Performance

Errors in our protocol can be ascribed to two sources. In
Eq.

3
, the first term describes the evolution of a state initially supported on the low energy (
\sim 0
) eigenvectors, giving exactly the term we want at first order, but higher order terms (all odd) mark deviations. The second term describes the (small) probability of being found in a high energy (
\sim\lambda_{n}
) eigenstate, with unwanted evolution. This second term is the leading order error, and is readily controlled by further extending the Hamiltonian,

H=\begin{bmatrix}0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&0&0&0\\
\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&0&0\\
0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&A&0&0\\
0&0&A^{\dagger}&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0\\
0&0&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}\\
0&0&0&0&\gamma\mathbb{1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

The effective Hamiltonians

h_{n\pm}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\gamma&0\\
\gamma&0&\gamma\\
0&\gamma&\pm\lambda_{n}\end{bmatrix}

have eigenvectors of energy
\sim\lambda_{n}
which are supported on state
\ket{n_{1\pm}}
with a strength reduced to order
\gamma^{2}
(
Lemma

1
). The low energy eigenvalues are of the form

\gamma\left(\pm 1\pm\frac{\gamma}{2\lambda_{n}}\right)+\order{\gamma^3}.

We choose to evolve for a time
t=2\pi/\gamma
to ensure that the leading
\pm 1
terms vanish. We now have that

\alpha_{n}\propto\frac{\gamma}{\lambda_{n}}+\order{\gamma^3\kappa^3}.

The accuracy of the final normalised state is
\gamma^{2}\kappa^{3}
; selecting
\gamma=\delta/\kappa^{3/2}
improves the run-time to
\order{\kappa^{3/2}T/\delta}
.

Further refinements are possible: by extending
H
so that
h_{n+}
is an
(R+1)\times(R+1)
matrix, the effect of the high energy sector is suppressed to
\order{\gamma^{2R}}
(
Lemma

1
). Furthermore, expansion of
\alpha
for the low-energy sector retains only odd powers of
x
(see
Lemma

3
), and these coefficients are independent of
\lambda
. Thus, by selecting evolutions over multiple times, we can take a linear combination of unitaries
Childs and Wiebe 2012
and cancel all errors up to
\order{\gamma^{2R}}
. Accuracy
\varepsilon
only requires
R\sim\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
.

III
Conclusions

In this paper, we have demonstrated a new version of the algorithm for matrix inversion. Without the need for phase estimation, it is far more direct that HHL. At its core is an implicit perturbative analysis of a weakly coupled system, whose effective Hamiltonian for a subspace close to energy
0
is approximately
H^{-1}
. The simplest variant of this algorithm matches the
\order{\kappa^2}
scaling of the original algorithm
Harrow et al. 2009
while delivering a massive simplification of the implementation of the algorithm, making it vastly more realistic for short-term realisation on Noisy Intermediate Scale Quantum devices. With a mild increase in sophistication, we have also matched the best performance
Ambainis 2012
;
Childs et al. 2017
in terms of accuracy and condition number. However, we have left open the question (
1
) of whether the most direct version always has solutions that can be found efficiently.

We have not investigated functions
\ket{x}\propto f(A)\ket{b}
other than the inverse in this paper. Standard techniques such as linear combinations of unitaries
Childs et al. 2013
;
Chia et al. 2020
will suffice, although it would be more satisfying if the function could be directly integrated into the evolution.
