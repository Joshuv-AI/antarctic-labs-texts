Abstract
. We propose a novel zeroth-order optimization algorithm based on an efficient sampling strategy. Under mild global regularity conditions on the objective function, we establish non-asymptotic convergence rates for the proposed method. Comprehensive numerical experiments demonstrate the algorithm’s effectiveness, highlighting three key attributes: (i) Scalability: consistent performance in high-dimensional settings (exceeding 100 dimensions); (ii) Versatility: robust convergence across a diverse suite of benchmark functions, including Schwefel, Rosenbrock, Ackley, Griewank, Lévy, Rastrigin, and Weierstrass; and (iii) Robustness to discontinuities: reliable performance on non-smooth and discontinuous landscapes. These results illustrate the method’s strong potential for black-box optimization in complex, real-world scenarios.



1.
Introduction

Optimization constitutes a fundamental mathematical and computational discipline that arises across diverse fields including engineering, economics, machine learning, and operations research. Traditional optimization methodologies primarily focus on identifying the minimum or maximum of a given objective function, often subject to constraints. In its broadest sense, optimization refers to the process of systematically adjusting inputs to a system or model to achieve optimal performance as measured by a predefined criterion. This criterion is typically expressed through an
objective function
, which may be either maximized (e.g., profit, efficiency) or minimized (e.g., cost, error, time)
[
17
]
.

Formally, a standard optimization problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle x^{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\quad\text{or}\quad x_{*}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)

(1.1)

subject to

\displaystyle g_{i}(x)\leqslant 0,\quad i=1,\dots,m\quad\text{or}\quad h_{j}(x)=0,\quad j=1,\dots,p,

(1.2)

where
f(x)
denotes the objective function,
g_{i}(x)
represent inequality constraints, and
h_{j}(x)
equality constraints.

Optimization problems can be classified according to various criteria:

•

Linear vs. Nonlinear:
If both the objective function and all constraints are linear, the problem constitutes a linear program (LP); otherwise, it is a nonlinear program (NLP)
[
3
]
.

•

Convex vs. Non-convex:
A problem is convex if the objective function is convex and the feasible region forms a convex set. Convex problems generally admit efficient solutions with guaranteed global optimality
[
3
]
.

•

Unconstrained vs. Constrained:
Unconstrained optimization imposes no restrictions on variables, while constrained optimization requires solutions to satisfy additional equality or inequality constraints as specified in (
1.2
).

•

Deterministic vs. Stochastic:
Deterministic optimization assumes perfectly known parameters, whereas stochastic optimization incorporates randomness or uncertainty in model data
[
20
]
.

•

Discrete vs. Continuous:
Discrete optimization restricts variables to discrete values (e.g., integers), while continuous optimization permits variables to assume any real values within specified ranges.

The selection of an appropriate optimization methodology is primarily determined by the underlying problem structure:

•

Gradient-Based Methods:
These techniques utilize derivative information to guide the optimization process. Notable examples include:

–

Gradient descent: Iteratively moves in the direction of steepest descent

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{k}),

(1.3)

where
\alpha
denotes the learning rate.

–

Newton’s method: Incorporates second-order derivative information for accelerated convergence

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-[\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})]^{-1}\nabla f(x_{k}).

(1.4)

–

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): Introduces controlled noise for improved generalization

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{k})+\sqrt{\alpha\varepsilon}\xi_{k},

(1.5)

where
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are independent Gaussian random variables.

Comprehensive treatments can be found in
[
17
]
.

•

Derivative-Free Methods:
These approaches are particularly valuable when gradient information is unavailable or computationally prohibitive. Prominent methods include the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm, pattern search techniques, and Powell’s conjugate direction method
[
23
]
.

•

Metaheuristic Algorithms:
These high-level strategies excel in complex optimization landscapes characterized by non-convexity, non-differentiability, or high dimensionality. Representative techniques include evolutionary algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms), swarm intelligence methods (e.g., particle swarm optimization), and physics-inspired algorithms (e.g., simulated annealing). See
[
5
]
for a comprehensive overview.

•

Convex Optimization Techniques:
For problems exhibiting convexity, specialized methods such as interior-point algorithms and barrier methods provide theoretically guaranteed polynomial-time convergence
[
3
]
.

Despite their widespread utility, optimization methods face several fundamental challenges including computational complexity in high-dimensional spaces, convergence guarantees in non-convex landscapes, and sensitivity to noise and numerical instabilities. Contemporary research focuses on promising directions such as integration with machine learning paradigms, exploitation of problem structure (e.g., sparsity, separability), and development of parallel and distributed optimization frameworks
[
2
]
.

The increasing computational capabilities and algorithmic innovations have significantly expanded the application domains of optimization techniques. Today, they form the computational backbone of numerous fields including large-scale machine learning model training
[
8
]
, complex systems design and control, operations research and logistics planning, and quantitative finance and portfolio optimization. The continued development of efficient, robust, and scalable optimization algorithms remains a critical research frontier as we confront increasingly complex problems across science and engineering disciplines.

This paper develops a novel global zero-order optimization algorithm that builds upon recent advances in sampling techniques
[
24
]
. The proposed method offers three significant advantages over conventional optimization approaches:

(i)

Minimal regularity requirements:
The algorithm imposes only weak assumptions on the target distribution, making it applicable to a broad class of optimization problems.

(ii)

High-dimensional non-convex optimization:
The method maintains its effectiveness even for challenging, non-convex landscapes in high-dimensional spaces.

(iii)

Initialization robustness:
Unlike many existing methods, our approach demonstrates low sensitivity to initial conditions, ensuring more reliable convergence.

1.1.
Intuitive Foundation

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a probability density function. Given
N
independent samples
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
drawn from
f
, the maximum likelihood principle ensures that for any
\varepsilon>0
and sufficiently large
N
, the following holds with high probability:

\exists X_{k}\in\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\text{ such that }\|X_{k}-x^{*}\|<\varepsilon,

where
x^{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
denotes the global maximizer, and
\|\cdot\|
represents the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Defining the empirical maximizer:

\xi^{*}_{N}=\arg\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}f(X_{i}),

(1.6)

we establish the following convergence result (see Theorem
2.3
):

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{2/d}\|f(\xi^{*}_{N})-f(x^{*})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)<\infty,

(1.7)

provided
\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{\infty}<\infty
. However, this intuitive estimate becomes computationally infeasible for high-dimensional problems due to the excessively large sample size
N
required.

Instead, we consider the minimization problem of a potential function via exponential tilting. Let
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable function satisfying the integrability condition:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty.

For any
\theta\geqslant 1
, we define the probability measure:

\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

A fundamental fact is that:

\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{1/\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{-U_{*}},

where
U_{*}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(x)
. Moreover, as established in
[
9
]
, we have the weak convergence:

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}\xrightarrow{w}\mu\quad\text{as}\quad\theta\to\infty,

(1.8)

where
\mu
is supported on the minimizing manifold:

{\mathscr{N}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:U(x)=U_{*}\}.

The characterization of the measure
\mu
on
{\mathscr{N}}
was originally established in
[
9
]
through an analysis of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}U
. We note that while the existence result in (
1.8
) guarantees convergence, it does not yield explicit convergence rate estimates. Nevertheless, under the polynomial growth condition: for some
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},m>0
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}\leqslant U(x)\leqslant\kappa_{1}|x|^{m},

(1.9)

where
0
is the unique minimizer (without regularity assumptions on
U
), we derive quantitative bounds for the convergence of the objective function
U(x)
to its minimum value
U_{*}
along the sampling points, and the convergence of the empirical minimizers to the true minimizer
x_{*}
. More precisely, for i.i.d. samples
(X^{\theta}_{n})_{n=1,\cdots,N}\sim\mu_{\theta}
and any
p,\theta\geqslant 1
(see Corollary
2.9
):

\displaystyle\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\min_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{d}{\theta m}+\mathrm{e}^{-N(\kappa_{0}/\kappa_{1})^{d/m}/(2p)},

(1.10)

and for
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{m})
, there exists
C=C(p,d,\alpha,\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},m)>0
such that (see Theorem
2.11
)

\displaystyle\|X^{\theta}_{n}-x_{*}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\theta^{-\alpha},\quad\theta\geqslant 1.

(1.11)

In practical implementation, given a sufficiently large inverse temperature
\theta\gg 1
, we generate
N
independent samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from the Gibbs distribution
\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)
; compute the objective values
\{U(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
; and finally extract the empirical minimizer
\xi_{*}=\arg\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}U(X_{i})
as an approximation to the global minimizer
x_{*}
. Since single sampling may not guarantee convergence, we develop iterative schemes (detailed in subsequent sections) to refine the minimizer approximation.

1.2.
Sampling via Stochastic Differential Equations

The key computational challenge in our algorithm is sampling from the distribution
\mu_{\theta}
. The most common approach utilizes Langevin diffusion:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=-\nabla U(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2\theta^{-1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

where
W_{t}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. This SDE has
\mu_{\theta}
as its unique stationary distribution, satisfying the Fokker-Planck equation:

\partial_{t}\mu_{\theta}=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{\theta}\nabla U+\theta^{-1}\nabla\mu_{\theta}\right)=0.

Under dissipative conditions
\langle\nabla U(x),x\rangle\geqslant m\|x\|^{2}-b
, exponential convergence is guaranteed
[
4
]
:

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu_{\theta})\leqslant C\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t},

where
\mathcal{W}_{2}
denotes the 2-Wasserstein distance. However, this approach has limitations:

•

Requires
U
to be differentiable (or subdifferentiable).

•

Convergence rates depend strongly on
U
’s convexity properties.

This approach corresponds to continuous-time stochastic gradient descent.

To overcome these limitations, we employ a transport-based method introduced in
[
24
]
. Consider any target distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(not necessarily of Gibbs form). Let
\sigma_{t},\beta_{t}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be
C^{1}
-functions satisfying:

\displaystyle\beta_{t}=1-\sigma_{t},\quad\sigma_{0}=1,\quad\sigma_{1}=0,\quad\sigma^{\prime}_{t}<0.

(1.12)

For
\varepsilon,\gamma>0
, consider the following SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b_{t}(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_{t}^{\prime}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma I_{d}),

(1.13)

where the drift term
b_{t}(x)
is defined for
t\in(0,1)
and
\eta\sim\mu
as:

\displaystyle b_{t}(x)=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[(x-\eta)\mathrm{e}^{-\|x-\beta_{t}\eta\|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}]}{\sigma_{t}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{e}^{-\|x-\beta_{t}\eta\|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}}\quad\text{with}\quad\ell_{t}:=\varepsilon\beta_{t}+\gamma\sigma_{t}.

(1.14)

The following result is proven in
[
24
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\eta
is bounded by
K
. Then, for any
\varepsilon,\gamma>0
and
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma I_{d})
independent of
\eta
, the SDE (
1.13
) admits a unique solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
X_{1}\sim\eta
and

\|X_{t}-X_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\sigma_{t}\|\xi-\eta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\sqrt{d\varepsilon\sigma_{t}}.

Moreover, for
t\in[0,1)
, the distribution density of
X_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(x):=(2\pi\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})^{-d/2}\mathbb{E}e^{-\|x-\beta_{t}\eta\|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}.

(1.15)

Flexibility of the Sampling Scheme:

Theorem
1.1
imposes no regularity conditions on the target measure
\mu
, which yields two significant advantages:

•

It can handle
non-smooth
potential functions that violate standard differentiability requirements.

•

It naturally admits extensions to manifold-constrained optimization problems, which we will explore in future work.

Computational Consideration:

The drift term computation involves high-dimensional integration, a challenge we resolve in Section
3
through a provably convergent Monte Carlo approximation scheme. Specifically, our approach:

•

Maintains the theorem’s generality while ensuring computational tractability.

•

Provides explicit convergence rates under measurable conditions.

1.3.
Adaptive Zooming Algorithm

Our main algorithm employs an adaptive coordinate-wise scaling strategy. Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d})\in(0,1)^{d}
be a multi-index controlling coordinate scaling. Define the operations:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x:=(\alpha_{1}x_{1},...,\alpha_{d}x_{d}).

Given initial point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, scaling parameter
\theta\gg 1
, and multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in(0,1)^{d}
, our algorithm primarily involves sampling from the following probability density function via SDE (
1.13
):

f(x)=\frac{e^{-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x}.

Below is the pseudocode of our algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Adaptive Zooming Optimization

1:

Input:
Initial point
x_{0}
, scaling
\theta\gg 1
, multi-index
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0}

2:

Output:
Optimizer estimate
x^{*}

3:

for

k=0,1,2,...
until convergence
do

4:

Define density:
f_{k}(x)\propto\exp(-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\odot x+x_{k}))

5:

Sample
X_{k}\sim f_{k}
via SDE (
1.13
)

6:

Update:
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\odot X_{k}

7:

Adapt scaling: Update
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k+1}
(see Section 4)

8:

end

for

The algorithm iteratively:

•

Samples from increasingly localized distributions via (
1.13
).

•

Adapts the search scope through coordinate-wise scaling.

•

Combines stochastic exploration with deterministic refinement.

Section 4 provides complete implementation details, including the adaptive
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
update rule.

2.
Convergence rate estimate of optimization by sampling

In this section we present some quantitative convergence rate estimate for finding the minimum of a function by sampling.
We first show the following simple result about seeking the maximum of a function by sampling,
and provide the basic convergence rate estimate.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with distribution
\mu
.
Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a measurable function.
Suppose that

f^{*}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\in[0,\infty).

Then for any
p>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{p}\leqslant p\int^{f^{*}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

(2.1)

In particular, if
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is continuous, and satisfies

\mathfrak{m}_{f}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty,\ \ \ \lim_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)=0,

then for
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\mathfrak{m}_{f}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{p}=0.

(2.2)

Proof.

Note that

\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|=\inf_{n=1,\cdots,N}(f^{*}-f(X_{n}))=:Y_{N}.

Since
Y_{N}\in[0,f^{*}]
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{p}=p\int^{f^{*}}_{0}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{N}\geqslant r)r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

(2.3)

For
r>0
, by the independence of
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
, we clearly have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{N}\geqslant r)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbb{P}}(f^{*}-f(X_{n})\geqslant r)={\mathbb{P}}(f^{*}-f(X_{1})\geqslant r)^{N}

\displaystyle=\mu\{x:f^{*}-f(x)\geqslant r\}^{N}=(1-\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\})^{N},

which together with (
2.3
) implies (
2.1
) by noting that for
s\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle(1-s)^{N}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-Ns}.

(2.4)

Moreover, for any
r\in(0,f^{*})
, since
f
is continuous, we have

\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}=\int_{\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}}\frac{f(x)}{\mathfrak{m}_{f}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\geqslant\frac{f^{*}-r}{\mathfrak{m}_{f}}{\rm vol}\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}>0.

The limit (
2.2
) now follows by (
2.1
) and the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

A more explicit and rough estimate for (
2.1
) is as follows: for any
\varepsilon\in[0,f^{*}]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant p\left(\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\int^{f^{*}}_{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant p\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\left(p\int^{f^{*}}_{\varepsilon}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-\varepsilon\}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{p}+((f^{*})^{p}-\varepsilon^{p})\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-\varepsilon\}}.

Below we shall consider concrete cases for obtaining quantitative convergence rate.

When
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, we have the following quantitative convergence rate estimate.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a probability density function with
\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
.
Suppose that there is a maximum point
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of
f
so that

f^{*}:=f(x^{*})=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)<\infty.

Then for any
p\geqslant 1
,

\varlimsup_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{2/d}\left\|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1)^{1/p}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)^{2/d}\kappa_{f}}{2\pi(f^{*})^{2/d}},

where for
\alpha>0
,
\Gamma(\alpha):=\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-s}s^{\alpha-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s
is the Gamma function, and

\displaystyle\kappa_{f}:=\sup_{|\omega|=1}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}[-\mathrm{tr}(\omega\otimes\omega\cdot\nabla^{2}f(x))]\geqslant 0.

(2.5)

In particular, for
d\geqslant 2
and
p=d/2
, we have

\varlimsup_{N\to\infty}\left(N{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{d/2}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{\kappa_{f}}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)}{f^{*}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, the key point is to make an estimate for

\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}=\int_{\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\geqslant(f^{*}-r){\rm vol}\{x:f^{*}-f(x)<r\}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
x^{*}=0
. Otherwise, we may consider the function
\tilde{f}(x):=f(x+x^{*})
.
Since
f\in C^{2}
, we have
\nabla f(0)=0
.
By Taylor’s expansion, we have

\displaystyle f^{*}-f(x)

\displaystyle=f(0)-f(x)+x\cdot\nabla f(0)=-\int^{1}_{0}s\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{tr}(x\otimes x\cdot\nabla^{2}f(ss^{\prime}x)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s^{\prime}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant\frac{\kappa_{f}}{2}|x|^{2},

(2.6)

where
\kappa_{f}
is defined by (
2.5
).
Hence, for
r\in[0,f^{*}]
,

{\rm vol}\{x:f^{*}-f(x)<r\}\geqslant{\rm vol}\left\{x:\kappa_{f}|x|^{2}/2<r\right\}=\left(\frac{2r}{\kappa_{f}}\right)^{d/2}\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)}=K_{f}r^{d/2},

where
K_{f}:=(2\pi/\kappa_{f})^{d/2}/\Gamma\big(\tfrac{d}{2}+1\big).
Thus,

\displaystyle\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}\geqslant(f^{*}-r)K_{f}r^{d/2}.

(2.7)

For given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we split the right hand side of (
2.1
) denoted by
{\mathscr{I}}_{p,N}
into two parts:

{\mathscr{I}}_{p,N}=p\left(\int^{f^{*}}_{\varepsilon f^{*}}+\int^{\varepsilon f^{*}}_{0}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>f^{*}-r\}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=:{\mathscr{I}}^{(1)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)+{\mathscr{I}}^{(2)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon).

For
{\mathscr{I}}^{(1)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)
, by (
2.7
) with
r=\varepsilon f^{*}
, we have

{\mathscr{I}}^{(1)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)\leqslant\left(p\int^{f^{*}}_{\varepsilon f^{*}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\mu\{x:f(x)>(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}\}}\leqslant(f^{*})^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}(\varepsilon f^{*})^{d/2}}.

For
{\mathscr{I}}^{(2)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)
, by (
2.7
) with
r\in[0,\varepsilon f^{*}]
, we also have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}^{(2)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leqslant p\int^{\varepsilon f^{*}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N(f^{*}-r)K_{f}r^{d/2}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant p\int^{\varepsilon f^{*}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}r^{d/2}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant p\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}r^{d/2}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Using the change of variable
N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}r^{d/2}=t
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}^{(2)}_{p,N}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leqslant(N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f})^{-2p/d}\frac{2p}{d}\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{2p/d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t=(N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f})^{-2p/d}\Gamma\Big(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1\Big).

Combining the above calculations, we get for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

{\mathscr{I}}_{p,N}\leqslant(f^{*})^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}(\varepsilon f^{*})^{d/2}}+(N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f})^{-2p/d}\Gamma\Big(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1\Big).

Hence, by (
2.1
),

\displaystyle N^{2/d}\left\|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant(f^{*}N)^{2/d}\mathrm{e}^{-N(1-\varepsilon)f^{*}K_{f}(\varepsilon f^{*})^{d/2}/p}

\displaystyle+\Gamma\Big(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1\Big)^{1/p}/(K_{f}f_{*}(1-\varepsilon))^{2/d}.

By firstly letting
N\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain the desired upper limit bound.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Estimate (
2.6
) implies that
\nabla^{2}f(x^{*})
must be negative definite.
Moreover, the
C^{2}
-assumption of
f
is only used in (
2.6
). Of course, if we directly assume that

f(x^{*})-f(x)\leqslant\kappa_{f}|x-x^{*}|^{2}/2,\ \ \forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

then we have the same conclusion.
If in addition,
\mathfrak{m}_{f}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty
, then by considering

\tilde{f}(x)=f(x)/\mathfrak{m}_{f}
, we have the following convergence rate

\varlimsup_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{2/d}\left\|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1)^{1/p}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)^{2/d}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{f}}{f^{*}}\right)^{2/d}\kappa_{f}.

The following corollary is a direct application.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a
C^{2}
-function. Suppose that

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}U(x)=\infty,\ \ \mathfrak{m}_{U}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty,

and

\kappa_{U}:=\sup_{|\omega|=1}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left[\big(\nabla_{\omega}\nabla_{\omega}U(x)-|\nabla_{\omega}U(x)|^{2}\big)\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}\right]<\infty,

where
\nabla_{\omega}U(x):=\langle\omega,\nabla U(x)\rangle.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables with common
distribution
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
. Then for any
p\geqslant 1
, we have

\varlimsup_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{2/d}\left\|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}\mathrm{e}^{-U(X_{n})}-\mathrm{e}^{-U_{*}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{2p}{d}+1)^{1/p}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)^{2/d}}{2\pi(\mathrm{e}^{-U_{*}}/\mathfrak{m}_{U})^{2/d}}\kappa_{U},

where
U_{*}:=\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U(x)
.

Proof.

We now apply Theorem
2.3
to
f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}/\mathfrak{m}_{U}
. By the chain rule, we have

\nabla^{2}f(x)=[\nabla U(x)\otimes\nabla U(x)-\nabla^{2}U(x)]\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}/\mathfrak{m}_{U}.

Thus, we clearly have

\kappa_{f}=\sup_{|\omega|=1}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}[-\mathrm{tr}(\omega\otimes\omega\cdot\nabla^{2}f(x))]=\kappa_{U}/\mathfrak{m}_{U}.

The desired estimate now follows.
∎

Let
x_{*}
be the minimum point of

and

Remark 2.7
.

If we take
U(x)=|x|^{2}/2
, then
\mathfrak{m}_{U}=(2\pi)^{d/2}
and
\kappa_{U}=1
.
In this case, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{2/d}\left\|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}\mathrm{e}^{-|X_{n}|^{2}/2}-1\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\leqslant\Gamma(\tfrac{4}{d}+1)^{1/2}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)^{2/d}.

Since
X_{n}
are i.i.d. Gaussian, by tedious calculation, the above inequality indeed takes the equality.

A natural question arises regarding the optimality of the convergence rate
N^{-2/d}
. Indeed, this rate critically depends on the local behavior of the potential function
U
near its minimizer
x_{*}
. In our subsequent analysis, we specifically examine the case where the measure
\mu
takes the Gibbs form
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, with assumptions imposed directly on the potential
U
rather than its exponential transformation.

From a computational perspective, the dimension-dependent rate
N^{-2/d}
becomes particularly problematic in high-dimensional settings (
d\gg 1
). In such cases, achieving sufficient precision in locating the minimizer
x_{*}
would require prohibitively large sample sizes
N
. To address this fundamental limitation, we introduce a temperature parameter
\theta
(analogous to its role in Langevin Monte Carlo methods), which enables the following crucial non-asymptotic estimate:

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
be a
measurable function so that for fixed
\theta\geqslant 1
,

\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty.

Suppose that
U_{*}:=\inf_{x}U(x)\in(-\infty,\infty)
. For
r\geqslant 0
, define

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}(r):=\int_{\{x:0\leqslant U(x)-U_{*}<r\}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

(2.8)

Let

X^{\theta}_{1},\cdots,X^{\theta}_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables with common distribution

\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\varpropto\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.
Then for fixed
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
p\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\Big\|1-\mathrm{e}^{U_{*}-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\varepsilon+\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)/p}.

(2.9)

Moreover, if for some
\alpha,\kappa>0
,

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}(r/\theta)\geqslant\kappa r^{\alpha},\ \ r\in[0,1],

(2.10)

then for any
p\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\varlimsup_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{1/\alpha}\Big\|1-\mathrm{e}^{U_{*}-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\frac{p}{\alpha}+1)}{\kappa^{1/\alpha}\theta}.

(2.11)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
U_{*}=0
. Otherwise, we may consider

\widetilde{U}(x):=U(x)-U_{*}.

Applying (
2.1
) to
f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}
and
\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X_{n})}\right)^{p}={\mathbb{E}}\left|1-\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}\mathrm{e}^{-U(X_{n})}\right|^{p}\leqslant p\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta_{\theta,r}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,

where

\delta_{\theta,r}=\mu_{\theta}\Big\{x:\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}>1-r\Big\}=\int_{\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}>1-r}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Noting that
\mathrm{e}^{-s}\geqslant 1-s
for
s>0
, by the definition (
2.8
) of
\ell_{\theta}
, we have

\delta_{\theta,r}\geqslant\int_{\{x:U(x)<r\}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\ell_{\theta}(r),\ \ \forall r\in[0,1].

Hence, for any
\theta\geqslant 1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X_{n})}\right)^{p}\leqslant p\int^{1}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(r)}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\qquad=p\left(\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\int^{1}_{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(r)}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

(2.12)

We first look at (
2.9
). For
I_{1}
, we clearly have

I_{1}\leqslant p\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=\varepsilon^{p}.

For
I_{2}
, since
\ell_{\theta}(r)
is increasing in
r
, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}\leqslant p\left(\int^{1}_{\varepsilon}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}.

(2.13)

Hence,

{\mathbb{E}}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X_{n})}\right)^{p}\leqslant\varepsilon^{p}+\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}.

Thus we obtain (
2.9
). Next we look at (
2.11
). Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/\theta)
.
For
I_{1}
, by (
2.10
) and the change of variable
N\kappa(r\theta)^{\alpha}=t
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}\leqslant p\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-N\kappa(r\theta)^{\alpha}}r^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=\frac{p/\alpha}{(N\kappa)^{\frac{p}{\alpha}}\theta^{p}}\int^{N\kappa(\varepsilon\theta)^{\alpha}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{\frac{p}{\alpha}-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{p}{\alpha}+1)}{(N\kappa)^{\frac{p}{\alpha}}\theta^{p}}.

(2.14)

Combining (
2.12
), (
2.13
) and (
2.14
), we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right)^{p}\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{p}{\alpha}+1)}{(N\kappa)^{\frac{p}{\alpha}}\theta^{p}}+\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}

and

\displaystyle\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{\Gamma(\frac{p}{\alpha}+1)^{1/p}}{(N\kappa)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\theta}+\mathrm{e}^{-N\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)/p}.

(2.15)

The proof is complete.
∎

Example 1:

Suppose that
U(x)=\kappa|x|^{m}
for some
\kappa,m>0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}(r)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\{x:\kappa|x|^{m}<r\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\{x:|x|^{m}<r\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r^{1/m}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s/\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t/\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t

\displaystyle=\gamma(d/m,r\theta)/\Gamma(d/m),

where the second equality follows from a change of variable, the third uses spherical coordinates, and the fourth is obtained via the substitution
t=\theta s^{m}
.
Note that
\frac{\gamma(\alpha,\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}>\frac{1}{2}
(see (
1.16
)). Therefore, for any
\theta\geqslant d/(m\varepsilon)
,

\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\gamma(d/m,\varepsilon\theta)}{\Gamma(d/m)}\geqslant\frac{1}{2},

and hence by (
2.9
),

\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\dots,N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{d}{m\theta}+\mathrm{e}^{-N/(2p)}.

Moreover, for
r\in[0,1]
, it is straightforward to show that

\ell_{\theta}(r)\geqslant\frac{mr^{d/m}}{d\,\Gamma(d/m)}.

This example illustrates that the curse of dimensionality can be mitigated by choosing a sufficiently large
\theta
.

The following corollary is more general and provides useful information.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
0\leqslant\kappa_{0}<\kappa_{1}
and
m>0
.
Suppose that for some
R\in(0,\infty]
and
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle U(x)=+\infty,\ \ |x-x_{*}|>R,

(2.16)

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}|x-x_{*}|^{m}\leqslant U(x)-U(x_{*})\leqslant\kappa_{1}|x-x_{*}|^{m},\ \ |x-x_{*}|<R.

(2.17)

Then for all
p\geqslant 1
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it holds that for
\theta\geqslant d/(m\kappa_{1}R^{m})
,

\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{U(x_{*})-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{d}{\theta m}+\mathrm{e}^{-N(\kappa_{0}/\kappa_{1})^{d/m}/(2p)}.

In particular,

\lim_{\theta\to\infty}\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-N(\kappa_{0}/\kappa_{1})^{d/m}/p},

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X^{\theta}_{n})}\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{d}{\theta m}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
x_{*}=0
and
U(x_{*})=0
.
Let
\varepsilon=\frac{d}{\theta m}
.
Notice that by the change of variable,

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{x:U(x)-U(0)<\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\{x:\kappa_{1}|x|^{m}<\varepsilon,|x|\leqslant R\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\int_{|x|\leqslant R}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\int^{(\varepsilon/\kappa_{1})^{1/m}\wedge R}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s/\int^{R}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}^{d/m}}{\kappa_{1}^{d/m}}\int^{(\varepsilon\theta)\wedge(\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m})}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s/\int^{\theta\kappa_{0}R^{m}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\kappa_{0}^{d/m}}{\kappa_{1}^{d/m}}\cdot\frac{\gamma(\frac{d}{m},\frac{d}{m}\wedge(\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{m})}.

For
\theta
being large enough so that
\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m}>d/m
,
by (
2.9
) and (
1.16
), we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a nonnegative measurable function so that for any
\theta\geqslant 1
,

\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty.

Suppose that there are
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
such that
for
|x-x_{*}|\leqslant\delta
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}|x-x_{*}|^{m}\leqslant U(x)-U(x_{*})\leqslant\kappa_{1}|x-x_{*}|^{m},

(2.18)

and for
|x-x_{*}|>\delta
,

\displaystyle U(x)-U(x_{*})\geqslant\kappa_{2}.

(2.19)

Let
\mu_{\theta}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}
and
X_{\theta}\sim\mu_{\theta}
.
Then for any
p>0
and
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{1}{m})
,
there is a constant
C=C(d,m,p,\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\alpha)>0
such that for all
\theta\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{\theta}-x_{*}|^{p}\leqslant C\theta^{-\alpha p}.

(2.20)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume
x_{*}=0
and
U_{*}=0
. Otherwise, we may consider

\widetilde{U}(x):=U(x+x_{*})-U(x_{*}).

Fix
p>0
. Note that by definition,

{\mathbb{E}}|X_{\theta}|^{p}=p\int^{\infty}_{0}\mu_{\theta}\{x:|x|>\lambda\}\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda=\frac{p}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}}(I_{1}+I_{2}),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}:=\int^{\infty}_{\delta}\!\!\int_{\{x:|x|>\lambda\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda,\ \ \ I_{2}:=\int^{\delta}_{0}\!\!\int_{\{x:|x|>\lambda\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda.

For
I_{1}
, by (
2.19
), we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}\int^{\infty}_{\delta}\!\!\int_{\{x:|x|>\lambda\}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\!\int_{\{x:|x|>\lambda\}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}{\mathbb{E}}|X_{1}|^{p}\mathfrak{m}_{1,U}/p.

For
I_{2}
, by (
2.18
) and (
2.19
), we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{p}}{p}\int_{\{x:|x|>\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x+\int^{\delta}_{0}\!\!\int_{\{x:\lambda<|x|\leqslant\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\lambda^{p-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\lambda

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\delta^{p}\mathfrak{m}_{1,U}}{p\mathrm{e}^{(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}}+\frac{1}{p}\int_{\{x:|x|\leqslant\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}|x|^{p}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta)
.
By the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{x:|x|\leqslant\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}|x|^{p}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\{x:|x|\leqslant\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}|x|^{p}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x+\int_{\{x:\varepsilon<|x|\leqslant\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}|x|^{p}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon^{p}\int_{\{x:|x|\leqslant\varepsilon\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\int_{\{x:\varepsilon<|x|\leqslant\delta\}}|x|^{p}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\left(\varepsilon^{p}\int^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}s^{p+d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{p}\Gamma(d/m)}{(\theta\kappa_{0})^{d/m}m}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\frac{\delta^{d+p}}{d+p}\right).

Combining the above calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{\theta}|^{p}\leqslant\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1,U}({\mathbb{E}}|X_{1}|^{p}+\delta^{p})}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}\mathrm{e}^{(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}}+\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}\Gamma(d/2)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{p}\Gamma(d/m)}{(\theta\kappa_{0})^{d/m}m}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\frac{\delta^{d+p}}{d+p}\right).

(2.21)

On the other hand, by (
2.18
) and the change of variable, we also have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\{x:|x|<\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\geqslant\int_{\{x:|x|<\delta\}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\int^{\delta}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}\gamma(\tfrac{d}{m},\theta\kappa_{1}\delta^{m})}{m\Gamma(d/2)(\theta\kappa_{1})^{d/m}}.

If
\theta\kappa_{1}\delta^{m}>d/m
, then by (
1.16
), we further have

\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}\geqslant\frac{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{m})}{m\Gamma(d/2)(\theta\kappa_{1})^{d/m}}.

Substituting this into (
2.21
), we obtain that for
\theta>d/(m\kappa_{1}\delta^{m})
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{\theta}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1,U}({\mathbb{E}}|X_{1}|^{p}+\delta^{p})}{\mathfrak{m}_{\theta,U}\mathrm{e}^{(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}}+\frac{2m(\theta\kappa_{1})^{d/m}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{m})}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{p}\Gamma(d/m)}{(\theta\kappa_{0})^{d/m}m}+\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\frac{\delta^{d+p}}{d+p}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1,U}m\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})(\theta\kappa_{1})^{d/m}({\mathbb{E}}|X_{1}|^{p}+\delta^{p})}{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{m})\mathrm{e}^{(\theta-1)\kappa_{2}}}+2\varepsilon^{p}\left(\tfrac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{0}}\right)^{d/m}+\frac{2m(\theta\kappa_{1})^{d/m}\delta^{d+p}}{\mathrm{e}^{\theta\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{m}}\Gamma(\frac{d}{m})(d+p)}.

From this, by choosing
\varepsilon=\theta^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{m})
, we derive the desired estimate.
∎

From a theoretical standpoint, the SDE (
1.13
) provides a universal framework for sampling arbitrary distributions. However, the drift term
b_{t}(x)
depends nonlinearly on the target distribution
\mu
, making its exact computation prohibitively expensive in practice.
When
\mu
admits a density
\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, we can employ Monte Carlo approximation for
b_{t}(x)
. Specifically, through the change of variable technique, we obtain:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\tau_{t}:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
be any function. The drift
b
can be written as

\displaystyle b_{t}(x)=\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}\left[\xi{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right]/\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi),

(3.1)

where
\xi\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,y):=\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}|x+\tau_{t}\beta_{t}y|^{2}/(2\ell_{t})+|y|^{2}/2}f(x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}y).

(3.2)

Moreover, we also have

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(x):=(2\pi\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})^{-d/2}{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-|x-\beta_{t}\eta|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}=\left(\tau_{t}\sqrt{\sigma_{t}/\ell_{t}}\right)^{d}\mathbb{E}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi).

(3.3)

Proof.

By the change of variable
y=x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}z
and using
\sigma_{t}+\beta_{t}=1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(x-\eta)\mathrm{e}^{-|x-\beta_{t}\eta|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(x-y)\mathrm{e}^{-|x-\beta_{t}y|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{1+d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}z\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}|x+\tau_{t}\beta_{t}z|^{2}/(2\ell_{t})}f(x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z

\displaystyle=(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{1+d}(2\pi)^{d/2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\xi{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right],

where
\xi\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,y)
is defined by (
3.2
).
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-|x-\beta_{t}\eta|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}=(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{d}(2\pi)^{d/2}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi).

(3.4)

By the definition (
1.14
) of
b_{t}(x)
, we complete the proof.
∎

To evaluate the drift term computationally, we employ a Monte Carlo approximation of the integral. Let
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
be independent Gaussian random vectors. The Monte Carlo approximation
b_{t}^{N}(x)
of the drift
b_{t}(x)
is given by:

b_{t}^{N}(x)=\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,\xi_{i}).

For notational convenience, we define the auxiliary function:

\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,y):=(x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}y)\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y)

(3.5)

which yields the equivalent representation:

b_{t}^{N}(x)=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{t}}\left[x-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{G}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})}\right].

(3.6)

Consider the approximate SDE driven by this drift:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}^{N}=b_{t}^{N}(X_{t}^{N}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{\epsilon\beta_{t}^{\prime}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad X_{0}^{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma{\mathbb{I}}_{d}).

(3.7)

Below we make the following assumptions:

•

f
has compact support in the ball
B_{K}
,

•

\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\beta_{t}^{\prime}>0
.

In particular, we have the uniform bound:

|b_{t}^{N}(x)|\leqslant\frac{|\sigma_{t}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{t}}(|x|+K).

(3.8)

By standard SDE theory, there exists a unique strong solution to (
3.7
). Moreover, we establish the following convergence rate estimate:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\gamma]
,
\lambda\geqslant 9
, where
\ell_{t}=\varepsilon\beta_{t}+\gamma\sigma_{t}
. Let
\tau_{t}=\sqrt{\lambda\ell_{t}/\sigma_{t}}
.
Suppose that
|\eta|\leqslant K
.
Let
\mu^{N}_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
be the laws of
X^{N}_{t}
and
X_{t}
, respectively.
Then for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in(0,1)
,

\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{8}K\big(9\pi\lambda\gamma/4\big)^{d/4}\mathrm{e}^{3K^{2}/(\varepsilon\sigma_{t})}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(d-2)N}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

By the classical Csiszár–Kullback–Pinsker (CKP) inequality (see
[
22
, (22.25)]
)
and the entropy formula (see
[
14
]
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2{\mathcal{H}}(\mu_{t}|\mu^{N}_{t})=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\mathbb{E}|b^{N}_{s}(X_{s})-b_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}}{\varepsilon\beta^{\prime}_{s}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Note that by (
3.6
) and
\sigma^{\prime}_{s}=-\beta_{s}^{\prime}\leqslant 0
,

\displaystyle b^{N}_{t}(x)-b_{t}(x)=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\left[\frac{{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi)}{{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})}\right]=:-\frac{\beta^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}).

Thus, by the independence of
X_{\cdot}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
as well as
X_{s}\sim\phi_{s}(x)

(see Theorem
1.1
), we further have

\displaystyle\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}^{2}\leqslant\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\beta^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(X_{s},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|^{2}}{\varepsilon\sigma^{2}_{s}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\beta^{\prime}_{s}}{\varepsilon\sigma^{2}_{s}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathbb{E}|{\mathcal{E}}_{s}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|^{2}\phi_{s}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(3.9)

Now, we devote to make an estimate for
{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|^{2}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i}){\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i}){\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})|\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right|.

Since
f
has support in
B_{K}
, by the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, we have

|{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})|\leqslant\frac{K\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right|+\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi)\right|}{{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)}.

Since
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi_{i})
are i.i.d. random variable, we further have

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(x,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{K\|{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}(x,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{N}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)}\leqslant\frac{2K\|{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{N}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)}.

By (
1.15
), (
3.9
), (
3.3
) and
\tau_{t}=\sqrt{\lambda\ell_{t}/\sigma_{t}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}^{2}\leqslant\int^{t}_{0}\frac{4K^{2}\beta^{\prime}_{s}\lambda^{d/2}}{\varepsilon\sigma^{2}_{s}N}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{H}}_{s}(x,\xi)|^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{s}(x,\xi)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

By (
3.4
) and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{d}(2\pi)^{d/2}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)\geqslant\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathbb{E}}|x-\beta_{t}\eta|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})},

and by the change of variable
x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}y=z
and
\tau_{t}=\sqrt{\lambda\ell_{t}/\sigma_{t}}
,

\displaystyle(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{d}(2\pi)^{d/2}{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\tau_{t}\sigma_{t})^{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma_{t}|x+\tau_{t}\beta_{t}y|^{2}/\ell_{t}+|y|^{2}/2}f^{2}(x-\tau_{t}\sigma_{t}y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-|x-\beta_{t}z|^{2}/(\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})+|x-z|^{2}/(2\lambda\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z.

Thus, by Fubini’s theorem, we get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)|^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{H}}_{t}(x,\xi)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{g_{t}(x,z)/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z,

where

g_{t}(x,z):={\mathbb{E}}|x-\beta_{t}\eta|^{2}-2|x-\beta_{t}z|^{2}+\tfrac{|x-z|^{2}}{\lambda}.

Note that

\displaystyle g_{t}(x,z)

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|x|^{2}+2\langle x,\beta_{t}(2z-{\mathbb{E}}\eta)-\tfrac{z}{\lambda}\rangle+\beta_{t}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}|\eta|^{2}+(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-2\beta_{t}^{2})|z|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|x+a_{\lambda}(z)/(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}|\eta|^{2}+(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-2\beta_{t}^{2})|z|^{2}+\tfrac{|a_{\lambda}(z)|^{2}}{1-1/\lambda},

where

a_{\lambda}(z):=\beta_{t}(2z-{\mathbb{E}}\eta)-\tfrac{z}{\lambda}.

Since
|\eta|\leqslant K
, it is easy to see that for
\lambda\geqslant 9
and
|z|\leqslant K
,

\displaystyle g_{t}(x,z)

\displaystyle\leqslant(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|x+a_{\lambda}(z)/(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|^{2}+(1+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}+\tfrac{(3+1/\lambda)^{2}}{1-1/\lambda})K^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant(\tfrac{1}{9}-1)|x+a_{\lambda}(z)/(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|^{2}+(1+\tfrac{1}{9}+\tfrac{(3+1/9)^{2}}{1-1/9})K^{2}

\displaystyle=-8|x+a_{\lambda}(z)/(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)|^{2}/9+12K^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{g_{t}(x,z)/(2\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-4|x|^{2}/(9\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})+6K^{2}/(\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\big(9\pi\ell_{t}\sigma_{t}/4\big)^{d/2}\mathrm{e}^{6K^{2}/(\ell_{t}\sigma_{t})}.

Combining the above calculations, we obtain

\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}^{2}\leqslant\int^{t}_{0}\frac{4K^{2}\beta^{\prime}_{s}\lambda^{d/2}}{\varepsilon\sigma^{2}_{s}N}\big(9\pi\ell_{s}\sigma_{s}/4\big)^{d/2}\mathrm{e}^{6K^{2}/(\ell_{s}\sigma_{s})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z.

Noting that for
\varepsilon\in(0,\gamma]
, by
\sigma_{s}+\beta_{s}=1
,

\gamma\geqslant\ell_{s}=\varepsilon+(\gamma-\varepsilon)\sigma_{s}\geqslant\varepsilon,

we have

\displaystyle\|\mu^{N}_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{{\mathrm{var}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4K^{2}\big(9\pi\lambda\gamma/4\big)^{d/2}}{\varepsilon N}\mathrm{e}^{6K^{2}/(\varepsilon\sigma_{t})}\int^{t}_{0}\beta_{s}^{\prime}\sigma_{s}^{d/2-2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8K^{2}\big(9\pi\lambda\gamma/4\big)^{d/2}}{\varepsilon N(d-2)}\mathrm{e}^{6K^{2}/(\varepsilon\sigma_{t})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z.

The proof is complete.
∎

4.
Sampling-based zero-order optimization algorithm

In this section, based on Corollary
2.9
, Theorems
2.11
and
3.2
, we develop a zero-order optimization algorithm. To this end, we first introduce necessary notation.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\in(0,1)^{d}
be a multi-index that controls the scaling of the target function along different coordinates. For any
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the following operations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x:=(\alpha_{1}x_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}x_{d}),\ \ |\boldsymbol{\alpha}|:=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{d}.

Let
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
denote the objective function whose minimum we seek. Throughout this section, we maintain the fundamental assumption:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x<\infty.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\theta\gg 1
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in(0,1)^{d}
.
Our algorithm mainly consists of sampling from the following probability density function:

\displaystyle f(x):=\frac{\exp\big(-\theta\,U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})\big)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\big(-\theta\,U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot y+x_{0})\big)\,\mathrm{d}y}.

(4.1)

Figure 1
.
Illustration of parameter effects. Larger
\theta
values yield steeper density functions
f(x)
, while smaller
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
components produce flatter densities.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f
be given in (
4.1
). Under (
2.17
), for
\theta\geqslant d/(m\kappa_{1}R^{m})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z\leqslant 4m|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|(\theta\kappa^{2}_{1}/(2\kappa_{0}))^{d/m}/\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{m}).

(4.2)

Proof.

By the definition and the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f^{2}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\int_{|x|\leqslant R}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta\kappa_{0}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\left(\int_{|x|\leqslant R}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}|x|^{m}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\int^{R}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta\kappa_{0}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s/\left(\int^{R}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta\kappa_{1}s^{m}}s^{d-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|m(\theta\kappa_{1})^{2d/m}}{(2\theta\kappa_{0})^{d/m}}\int^{2\theta\kappa_{0}R^{m}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t/\left(\int^{\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m}}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-t}t^{d/m-1}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|(\theta\kappa^{2}_{1}/(2\kappa_{0}))^{d/m}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{m})/\gamma(\tfrac{d}{m},\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m})^{2}.

In particular, for
\theta\kappa_{1}R^{m}\geqslant\tfrac{d}{m}
, by (
1.16
), we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The convergence rate in Theorem
3.2
depends on the
L^{2}
-norm of the density. From (
4.2
), it can be observed that smaller values of
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
lead to faster convergence in the particle approximation.

The following lemma is direct by the change of variable.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot x+x_{0})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Then

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\odot X+x_{0}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Our proposed algorithm implements an adaptive zooming strategy through the iterative update rule:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\odot X_{k},

(4.3)

where

•

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the adaptive zooming parameter vector that dynamically controls the exploration scope.

•

X_{k}
is sampled from the probability distribution:

f_{k}(x)\propto\exp\left(-\theta U(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\odot x+x_{k})\right).

(4.4)

From Lemma
4.3
, we derive the key distributional property:

x_{k+1}\sim\frac{\exp\left(-\theta U(x)\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\theta U(x)\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x}.

(4.5)

The algorithm’s convergence is governed by two fundamental mechanisms:

(1)

Progressive zooming
: The parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
decay asymptotically (
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\to\mathbf{0}
as
k\to\infty
), enabling increasingly refined local search.

(2)

Temperature-controlled concentration
: For large
\theta\gg 1
, the distribution exhibits exponential concentration around the global minimizer
x_{*}
, ensuring (see Theorem
2.11
):

•

x_{k}\to x_{*}
(convergence to optimum).

•

\|X_{k}\|\to 0
(vanishing exploration steps).

4.1.
Zooming Parameter Update Strategies

In the above algorithm,
the adaptive control of the zooming parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
governs the algorithm’s exploration-exploitation trade-off. We formalize two update methodologies:

(i)

Exponential Decay Update (EDU):

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{1+k},\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}\sim\mathcal{U}\left([\alpha_{\text{min}},\alpha_{\text{max}}]^{d}\right),

(4.6)

where
\alpha_{\text{min}}>0
prevents degenerate collapse, and
\alpha_{\text{max}}<1
guarantees contraction,
{\mathcal{U}}
is the uniform distribution.

(ii)

Sample-adaptive Variance Update (SVU): for given samples
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}=\{Y_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
at iteration
k
,
we compute component-wise variances:

\sigma_{ki}:={\mathrm{var}}(\{Y_{ni}\}_{n=1}^{N}),\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,d\}.

Then we update
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
by

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k},

(4.7)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}
is the normalized variance vector:

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}:=\left(\frac{\sigma_{k1}}{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\|},\dots,\frac{\sigma_{kd}}{\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\|}\right),\quad\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\|:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{ki}^{2}}.

In the above SVU,
\sigma_{ki}
reflects the concentration of each component.

In this section, we evaluate the proposed algorithm on a suite of standard benchmark functions commonly employed in the global optimization literature (see
[
10
]
). These test problems are carefully selected to assess algorithmic performance across several critical dimensions of optimization difficulty:

•

Modality:
This refers to the number of local minima and maxima in the objective function. Multimodal functions pose significant challenges due to the increased likelihood of algorithms becoming trapped in suboptimal local minima.

•

Separability:
A function
U(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is said to be separable if it can be expressed as

U(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}(x_{i}).

In such cases, each variable can be optimized independently, greatly reducing the problem’s complexity.

•

Differentiability:
The differentiability of a function determines whether gradient-based optimization methods, such as Gradient Descent (GD) or Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), are applicable.

•

Discontinuity:
Functions exhibiting discontinuities typically preclude the use of gradient-based techniques, necessitating derivative-free approaches such as genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, or Bayesian optimization.

•

Scalability:
This dimension evaluates the algorithm’s capacity to maintain performance as the problem dimensionality or structural complexity increases. Scalability is influenced by both the intrinsic properties of the objective function and the design of the optimization method.

5.2.
Comparative Analysis of Optimization Algorithms

We present a systematic evaluation of six established optimization algorithms against our proposed sampling-based method. This analysis focuses on both solution quality and computational efficiency across diverse benchmark functions.
The six baseline methods considered are:

•

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)

[
12
]
: PSO simulates a swarm of particles (candidate solutions) that traverse the search space, influenced by both their personal best-known positions and the global best-known position. The collective dynamics guide the swarm toward promising regions.

•

Differential Evolution (DE)

[
21
]
: DE evolves a population of candidate solutions via mutation, crossover, and selection. Mutations are based on the differences between randomly selected individuals, promoting diversity and exploration.

•

BFGS Method

[
17
]
: The Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) method approximates the inverse Hessian matrix using only first-order gradient information. It is well-suited for smooth and convex optimization problems and typically exhibits rapid convergence.

•

Simulated Annealing (SA)

[
13
]
: SA explores the solution space by probabilistically accepting worse solutions in the early stages to avoid local minima, with the acceptance probability decreasing over time according to a cooling schedule.

•

Stochastic Hill Climbing (SHC)

[
19
]
: SHC performs a local search by evaluating random neighbors of the current solution, accepting improvements. Although simple to implement, it can easily become trapped in local optima.

•

Adam Optimizer

[
11
]
: Adam combines momentum-based acceleration and adaptive learning rates using running averages of gradients and squared gradients. It is widely adopted in machine learning due to its effectiveness and efficiency.

Each of these algorithms serves a specific purpose: PSO and DE excel in global derivative-free optimization, BFGS is effective for smooth gradient-based problems, SA is robust on rugged landscapes, SHC offers a lightweight local search mechanism, and Adam is a go-to optimizer in deep learning contexts.

5.3.
Discussion

The proposed Sampling-based Optimization (SO) algorithm consistently achieves superior or near-optimal objective values across a diverse set of challenging benchmark functions. It performs particularly well on complex, multimodal, and deceptive landscapes such as the Griewank, Rastrigin, and Schwefel functions. Although it is marginally slower than certain local optimization methods like BFGS on relatively simple functions (e.g., Sphere), the SO algorithm strikes a compelling balance between solution quality and robustness, positioning it as an effective and dependable optimization strategy.

The numerical experiments highlight the effectiveness and versatility of the proposed method across a broad spectrum of problem types. Several key observations emerge from our empirical study:

•

Dimensional Scalability:
The SO algorithm maintains consistent performance across both low-dimensional and high-dimensional optimization problems, indicating strong scalability with respect to problem dimensionality.

•

Robustness to Landscape Complexity:
The algorithm demonstrates high resilience to multimodal, non-convex, and deceptive objective landscapes. It effectively mitigates the risk of premature convergence and consistently explores globally optimal regions.

•

Adaptability to Smoothness:
The method performs reliably on both smooth and non-smooth objective functions, including those that are discontinuous or non-differentiable, suggesting broad applicability across various classes of optimization problems.
