Bayesian Optimization with Lower Confidence Bounds for Minimization Problems with Known Outer Structure
Katrin Baumgärtner katrin.baumgaertner@imtek.uni-freiburg.de, Moritz Diehl moritz.diehl@imtek.uni-freiburg.de

Abstract
This paper considers Bayesian optimization (BO) for problems with known outer problem structure. In contrast to the classic BO setting, where the objective function itself is unknown and needs to be iteratively estimated from noisy observations, we analyze the case where the objective has a known outer structure —given in terms of a loss function—while the inner structure —an unknown input-output model—is again iteratively estimated from noisy observations of the model outputs. We introduce a novel lower confidence bound algorithm for this particular problem class which exploits the known outer problem structure. The proposed method is analyzed in terms of regret for the special case of convex loss functions and probabilistic parametric models which are linear in the uncertain parameters. Numerical examples illustrate the superior performance of structure-exploiting methods compared to structure-agnostic approaches.



Katrin Baumgärtner and Moritz Diehl

Introduction

In recent years, Bayesian optimization (BO) has received a lot of attention from various fields.
In machine learning, BO has been successfully used to optimize the performance of learning algorithms by selecting optimal hyperparameters
(
Falkner et al. 2018
)
.
Similarly, BO has been used in control engineering to tune controllers in closed-loop simulations and experiments
(
Berkenkamp et al. 2016
;
Berkenkamp et al. 2023
;
Kudva et al. 2024
)
.
In process control, where the system dynamics can often not be modeled accurately or accurate models are too expensive to be used for real-time feedback control, model-plant mismatch can significantly deteriorate the control performance.
In this context, BO has been used to optimize a control objective while taking into account model uncertainties
(
del Rio Chanona et al. 2021
)
.
For many control applications, however, the objective function is often not completely unknown, as is assumed in the standard BO framework.
Typically, the
outer
loss function is known—often even chosen by the control engineer—, while the
inner
function, the system model, is unknown.
This motivates the extension of the BO framework with novel approaches exploiting this known outer structure.
Before introducing this problem setup in more detail, we briefly review the problem assumptions underlying standard, in the following also referred to as
structure-agnostic
, BO approaches.

In the classic BO setup, an optimization problem of the following form is considered:

\displaystyle u_{*}\in\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\penalty\ \varphi_{*}(u)

(1)

with compact domain
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and continuous objective function
\varphi_{*}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Crucially,
\varphi_{*}
is not known a priori and we can only learn about
\varphi_{*}
from noisy observations
y^{\varphi}=\varphi_{*}(u)+v^{\varphi}
where
v^{\varphi}
denotes zero-mean noise.
In particular, no gradient information is available.
The problem might thus be referred to as zero-order black-box optimization.

As minimizing the unknown objective directly is not possible, BO algorithms compute the next evaluation point by instead optimizing a surrogate objective, in the literature referred to as
acquisition function

(
Mockus et al. 1978
)
.
For the classic setting (
1
), various acquisition functions have been considered in the BO literature.
We refer the interested reader to the excellent tutorial on BO by
Brochu et al. 2010
for a detailed overview on commonly used acquisition functions.

Most relevant for this paper is the lower confidence bound (LCB)
1
1

1

Or
upper confidence bound
(UCB) if a maximization problem is considered which is the more common case in classic BO.
.
This acquisition function corresponds to a probabilistic lower bound on the true objective
\varphi_{*}
which is minimized in each iteration in order to determine the next evaluation point
u
.
Intuitively, the motivation behind the LCB approach—sometimes referred to as
optimism in the face of uncertainty
—is the following:
If we evaluate at the minimum of the LCB acquisition function and the lower bound is tight at this minimum, then we have evaluated at a point with low objective value (
exploitation
).
In contrast, if the lower bound is underestimating the true objective value at the point of evaluation, then we are able to improve the estimated model of the objective (
exploration
).

In contrast to the classic BO problem (
1
), we consider a slightly different setup in this paper:
The unknown function, in the following referred to as model or output function,
f_{*}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m},u\mapsto z,
is potentially multivariate and enters the optimization problem via a known loss function
l
, i.e.
we consider the problem

\displaystyle u_{*}\in\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\penalty\ \varphi_{*}(u)\quad\text{where}\quad\varphi_{*}(u)=l(u,f_{*}(u)),

(2)

with known loss function
l:\mathcal{U}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and unknown output function
f_{*}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Both
l
and
f_{*}
are assumed to be continuous.
As before, we may learn about
f_{*}
via noisy observations
y=z+v
with zero-mean output noise
v
.
Note that in contrast to the standard setting (
1
) where observations of the scalar objective are given, we assume that (potentially) multivariate observations of the model outputs—but not of the objective—are available.
This setup, which might be classified as grey-box optimization, is a generalization of the classic black-box setting outlined above which we obtain for
l(u,z)=z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Similarly to the classic black-box setting setting, where a probabilistic model of the objective is iteratively updated with new observations, we will assume that a probabilistic model of the output function is available which allows us to compute parametric model confidence bounds of the form:

\displaystyle\Prop\left[f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\penalty\ \forall u\in\mathcal{U}\right]\geq 1-p,

(3)

where we call
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
the model confidence set
and
1-p
the confidence level.
The scalar hyperparameter
\gamma
allows us to scale the confidence set such that the prescribed confidence level is met.
In addition, the confidence set will be conditioned on the available observations, i.e. at time instant
n
, the observations
(u_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(u_{n},y_{n})
are given and summarized by
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.

Such a model confidence bound is for instance available if we consider parametric models of the form
z=f(u,\theta)
where
f(u,\theta)=A(u)\theta
is linear in the unknown parameters
\theta
(
linear-in-parameters
, LIP).
In this case, the model confidence sets can be described by ellipsoids parametrized by the mean and covariance associated with the uncertainty in the parameters
\theta

(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
.
Similarly, model confidence bounds might be derived for Gaussian processes (GP) which are a widely used model class in classic BO
(
Brochu et al. 2010
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
)
.

The main contribution of this paper is an extension of the lower confidence bound (LCB) algorithm to minimization problems with known outer problem structure as given in (
2
).
The method relies on the assumption that a tractable representation of the model confidence sets (
3
) is available.
We analyze the method in detail providing a regret analysis for the case where LIP models and convex costs are considered.
In numerical experiments, we compare the proposed approach to a zero-order iterative learning approach, which is based on a deterministic model, Thompson sampling and the classic LCB approach.

1.1
Related Work

The investigated grey-box setting is typical for control and robotics applications where the loss as a function of the controls inputs
u
and the system outputs
y
is known—and might be even chosen or tuned by the control engineer—while an accurate system model relating the control inputs to the system outputs is not available.
Control applications covered by this problem setup comprise steady-state optimization, as analyzed for example within the field of modifier adaptation
(
Marchetti et al. 2009
)
, and iterative learning control (ILC) where typically both
u
and
y
are control and output trajectories arising from the discretization of a continuous-time state-space model
(
Bristow et al. 2006
;
Volckaert et al. 2010
;
Schoellig et al. 2012
)
.

An important special case of the investigated problem is the linear bandit setting which is obtained for
l(u,z)=z
and LIP models.
Stochastic linear bandits were introduced by
Abe and Long 1999
considering a finite number of actions,
|\mathcal{U}|<\infty
.
A similar setup is analyzed by
Auer 2002
who was first to propose an algorithm for linear bandits based on the principle of
optimism in the face of uncertainty
.
Further, crucial contributions to the analysis of LCB-type algorithms for linear bandits, which our regret analysis builds on, have been made in
Dani et al. 2008
;
Rusmevichientong and Tsitsiklis 2010
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
.

Similarly, Gaussian process bandit optimization can be interpreted as a special case of the investigated problem if Gaussian processes are considered as model class and
l(u,z)=z
.
Gaussian process bandit optimization based on confidence bounds has been analyzed in terms of regret by
Srinivas et al. 2010
.
An excellent review on Bayesian optimization using Gaussian processes is given by
Brochu et al. 2010
.

At first glance, the investigated setting might resemble generalized linear bandits
(
Nelder and Wedderburn 1972
)
for which a confidence bound algorithm has been introduced by
Filippi et al. 2010
.
With generalized linear bandits, the objective function takes the form
\varphi_{*}(u)=\pi(f_{*}(u))
with a so-called
link function

\pi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is a scalar invertible function and assumed to be known.
While we consider more general loss functions
l:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as known outer structure, the main difference is the assumption on which quantity can be observed:
While we assume that potentially multivariate measurements of the outputs,
y=f(u)+v
, are given, the only information available in the generalized linear bandit setting are (noisy) observations of the scalar objective.

Very much related to our problem setting is the setup investigated by
Paulson and Lu 2022
:
Similar to our approach, they consider the concatenation of a known loss function and an unknown model function.
In addition, they allow for (known) constraints on inputs and outputs, while our method is restricted to input constraints.
They focus on Gaussian processes as model class and propose to use stochastic gradient descent to find a local optimum of a modified expected improvement acquisition function.

2
A Lower Confidence Bound for Problems with Known Outer Structure

In the following, we introduce the lower confidence bound (LCB) acquisition function for grey-box optimization with known outer structure, given in terms of a loss function, and unknown model.
To this end, we introduce the sequence of input-output observations available at time step
n
, which we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}
.
This sequence is recursively defined via
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}=(\mathcal{I}_{n},u_{n+1},y_{n+1})
.

The proposed algorithm builds on the main assumption that a probabilistic model of the model outputs is available.
Crucially, we assume that this probabilistic model allows us to compute model confidence sets
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
for each input
u
and given the sequence of current observations
\mathcal{I}
.
The hyperparameter
\gamma
allows us to scale the confidence set such that a prescribed confidence level
1-p
is met.
This is formalized in the following assumption:

{assumption}

[Model confidence set]
Let
\mathcal{I}
be a sequence of input-output observations.
For each confidence level
1-p
,
p\in(0,1]
,
there is
\gamma
such that the following model confidence bound holds:

\displaystyle\Prop\left[f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\penalty\ \forall u\in\mathcal{U}\right]\geq 1-p.

The set
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{0},\mathcal{I}_{0})
is the prior model confidence set which is independent of any observations,
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\emptyset
.
We furthermore use the shorthand
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}(u)\colon=\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
.

The hyperparameter
\gamma
scales the model confidence sets and needs to be chosen based on the prescribed confidence level
1-p
.
In the following, we will assume nonnegativity of
\gamma
,
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
, as well as monotonicity of the model confidence sets in
\gamma
:

{assumption}

[Monotonicity in
\gamma
]
We assume that the following implication holds for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and any sequence of observations
\mathcal{I}_{n}
:

\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma\leq\gamma^{\prime}\Longrightarrow\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}_{n})\subseteq\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma^{\prime},\mathcal{I}_{n}).

Given a computationally tractable representation of the model confidence sets
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
, we now introduce the novel LCB method for optimization problems with known outer structure:
At iteration
n+1
, given the observations
\mathcal{I}_{n}=(u_{1},y_{1},\ldots,u_{n},y_{n})
and hyperparameter
\gamma_{n}
, the next evaluation point
u_{n+1}
is selected by solving the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle u_{n+1}\in\arg\penalty\

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\scriptstyle{u,z}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad l\left(u,z\right)

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\scriptstyle{u,z}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}),

(4b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{U}.

(4c)

The constraint (
4b
) ensures that the output
z
lies within the model confidence set
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
with
\gamma_{n}
such that a prescribed confidence level
1-p
is met.
Evaluation at
u_{n+1}
yields the noisy observation
y_{n+1}=f_{*}(u_{n+1})+v_{n+1}
and the procedure is repeated with updated confidence set
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n+1},\mathcal{I}_{n+1})
with
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}=(\mathcal{I}_{n},u_{n+1},y_{n+1})
.

In order to show that the above algorithm is indeed a LCB method and nicely fits into the BO framework, we introduce the acquisition function
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
.

Definition 1

(Acquisition function)
.

The proposed structure-exploiting LCB-based BO approach is defined in terms of the acquisition function
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
given as

\displaystyle Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}):=\penalty\

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\scriptstyle{z}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad l\left(u,z\right)

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\scriptstyle{z}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}).

(5b)

Following the BO framework, the next evaluation point
u_{n+1}
, given as a solution to (
4
), can equivalently be defined as a minimizer of the acquisition function
Q(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
:

\displaystyle u_{n+1}\in\arg\penalty\

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\scriptstyle{u\in\mathcal{U}}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad Q(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}).

The following lemma shows that a model confidence bound as introduced in Assumption
2
directly implies a corresponding lower confidence bound on
\varphi_{*}(u)
given in terms of the acquisition function.

Lemma 2

(Lower confidence bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a sequence of input-output observations.
Consider the confidence level
1-p
,
p\in(0,1]
and corresponding hyperparameter
\gamma
such that Assumption
2
holds.
Then, the acquisition function
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
is a lower bound on
\varphi_{*}(u)
on the domain
\mathcal{U}
with probability at least
1-p
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Prop\left[Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\leq\varphi_{*}(u)\penalty\ \forall u\in\mathcal{U}\right]\,\geq\,1-p.

Proof.

Consider
u\in\mathcal{U}
and let
z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
be a solution to (
5
).
If
f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
, then
z_{*}=f_{*}(u)
is a feasible point of (
5
).
This implies

\displaystyle Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle=l(u,z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}))

\displaystyle\leq l(u,f_{*}(u))

\displaystyle=\varphi_{*}(u).

By assumption,
f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
holds true with a probability of at least
1-p
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Under the assumption that the model confidence sets contain the expected output
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{y}\right]
and furthermore assuming convex costs, the proposed LCB method is
optimistic
in the sense that the acquisition function underestimates the expected costs under the given probabilistic model.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
l(u,z)
is jointly convex in
u
and
z
.
Furthermore, we assume

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}}\left[\hat{z}\right]\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{U}.

Then, the acquisition function
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
is a lower bound on the expected costs, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}}\left[l(u,\hat{z})\right]\geq Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{U}.

Proof.

Under the assumption that
l(u,z)
is convex in
z
, Jensen’s inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}}\left[l(u,\hat{z})\right]

\displaystyle\geq l(u,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}}\left[\hat{z}\right])

\displaystyle\geq\min_{z\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})}l(u,z)=Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}),

where the second inequality follows from the assumption that the mean
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{z}}\left[\hat{z}\right]
is contained in the model confidence set
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
.
∎

The acquisition function furthermore preserves boundedness properties of the loss:

Lemma 4

(Boundedness)
.

If
l(u,z)
is upper (lower) bounded for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
is upper (lower) bounded for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

This directly follows from
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})=l(u,z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}))
where
z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
is a solution to (
5
). ∎

Boundedness of the acquisition function does in general not hold in the classic BO setting, where the acquisition function will tend to
-\infty
as
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
.
This will be illustrated in the next section where we compare the proposed method to the classic, structure-agnostic LCB-based BO approach.

3
Illustrative Example: Linear Models

In the following, we consider parametric models which are linear in the parameters (LIP).
Under the assumption of a Gaussian prior for the unknown parameters
\theta
, as well as Gaussian measurement noise, the uncertainty associated with the parameters can be propagated analytically as more and more output measurements become available.
Crucially, this class of probabilistic models furthermore allows for a computationally tractable representation of the model confidence sets
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
in terms of ellipsoids.

Before demonstrating differences of the proposed method to the classic approach on a simple illustrative example, we formally introduce the class of LIP models and discuss some properties of the structure-exploiting LCB method for this class of probabilistic models.

{assumption}

We consider a parametric model of the form
z=f(u,\theta)
with

\displaystyle f(u,\theta)=A(u)\theta

with parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
A(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
potentially nonlinear in
u
.
We assume that there is
\theta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the true model satisfies
f_{*}(u)=f(u,\theta_{*})
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

Starting from
\mu_{0}^{\theta}
,
\Sigma_{0}^{\theta}
, we recursively update our parameter estimate based on new output observations as follows:

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{n+1}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma_{n}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}+A(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!(\Sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{n+1}),

(6)

\displaystyle\mu_{n+1}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n}^{\theta}+\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}A(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!\left(A(u_{n+1})\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}A(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!+\Sigma^{v}\right)^{\shortminus 1}\left(y_{n+1}-A(u_{n+1})\mu_{n}\right).

(7)

The probabilistic model for the outputs
z
is then given in terms of the mean
\mu_{n}(u)=f(u,\mu_{n}^{\theta})=A(u)\mu_{n}^{\theta}
and covariance
\Sigma_{n}(u)=A(u)\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}A(u)^{\!\top}\!
.
As we will show in the next section, a convenient parametrization of the model confidence set
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
is in this case given by the ellipsoid
\Ell\!\left(\mu_{n}(u),\gamma_{n}^{2}\Sigma_{n}(u)\right)
.
Note that Assumption
2
, i.e. monotonicity of the model confidence sets with respect to the hyperparameter
\gamma
, holds in this case.

If the loss function
l(u,z)
is linear in the outputs
y
, we recover the classic LCB-based method for the linear bandit problem
(
Dani et al. 2008
)
:

Lemma 5

(Linear loss)
.

Assuming a loss function which is linear in
z
, i.e. of the form
l(u,z)=l_{u}(u)+z
, the acquisition function
Q(u,\gamma,\mathcal{I})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle Q(u,\gamma,\mathcal{I})=l_{u}(u)+\mu(u,\mathcal{I})-\gamma\sqrt{\Sigma(u,\mathcal{I})},

where
\mu(u,\mathcal{I})
and
\Sigma(u,\mathcal{I})
parametrize the confidence ellipsoid associated with the scalar output
\hat{z}
.

Proof.

Under the above assumptions, the acquisition function is given as:

\displaystyle Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I}):=\penalty\

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\scriptstyle{z}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad l_{u}(u)+z

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\scriptstyle{z}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\in\Ell\!\left(\mu(u,\mathcal{I}),\gamma^{2}\Sigma(u,\mathcal{I})\right).

The minimization of a linear function over an ellipsoid can be performed analytically
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
yielding
Q(u,\gamma,\mathcal{I})=l_{u}(u)+\mu(u,\mathcal{I})-\gamma\sqrt{\Sigma(u,\mathcal{I})}
.
∎

Another important special case, which yields a convex acquisition function, is obtained if we additionally assume linearity of the model in
u
, as well as jointly convex costs:

Lemma 6

(Convex subproblems)
.

If the set
\mathcal{U}
is convex,
l(u,z)
is jointly convex in
u
and
z
, and
f(u,\theta)
is linear in
u
, then
Q(u;\gamma,\mathcal{I})
is convex in
u
.

Proof.

Convexity of the acquisition function follows directly from partial minimization of a jointly convex function of two arguments over a convex set, compare e.g.
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
, Chapter 3.2.5.
∎

Remark 7

(Gaussian processes)
.

A natural generalization of LIP models are Gaussian processes (GP), which are a widely used class of probabilistic models in the classic BO setting.
Crucially, the uncertainty in the model outputs can again be described by ellipsoidal model confidence sets if GPs are used.
Analogous to linear models, the proposed acquisition function reduces to the standard LCB acquisition function used for Gaussian Process optimization
(
Brochu et al. 2010
)
if the loss is linear in
y
.

Let us now consider a simple illustrative example which will allow us to contrast the proposed structure-exploiting grey-box approach with the classic, structure-agnostic LCB-based BO approach.

Example 8
.

Regard the parametric LIP model
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle f(u,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}u+\theta_{2}\\
\theta_{3}u+\theta_{4}\end{bmatrix}\!.

(8)

The true system is
f_{*}(u)=f(u,\theta_{*})
with true parameter
\theta_{*}=(-1.1,0.4,-0.45,0.55)
.
The known loss function is
l(u,z)=z_{1}^{2}+0.1z_{2}^{2}
.
The scalar input
u
is constrained to be in the set
\mathcal{U}=[-1,1]
.

In order to compare the proposed structure-exploiting LCB method to the classic LCB-based BO approach, we will first assume that there is no measurement noise, i.e.
we directly observe
y=f_{*}(u)
and
y_{\varphi}=l(u,f_{*}(u))
, respectively.
If there was noise on the model outputs, the objective function evaluated at the noisy measurement,
y_{\varphi}=l(u,f_{*}(u)+v)
, would no longer be normally distributed due to the nonlinearity of the loss function, thus violating a crucial assumption of the classic LCB-BO approach.
We point out that even for the simple case of a quadratic loss function, the distribution of
l(u,f_{*}(u)+v)

Regret Analysis for Linear Models

In the following, we derive a regret bound for the structure-exploiting LCB-method assuming LIP models.
The proof is based on established results from linear bandit theory.
In particular, we adapt results from
Dani et al. 2008
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b
;
Agarwal et al. 2019
and
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
.
It is important to keep in mind that the analysis is based on the assumption that we are able to compute a global minimizer of the acquisition function in each iteration.
Apart from the rare special case of convex subproblems, cf. Lemma
6
, satisfying this assumption remains a major challenge in practice.

We first point out that the Bayesian linear regression update for the covariance matrix assuming multivariate observations can equivalently be expressed as multiple scalar-valued updates if we additionally assume that
\Sigma_{v}
is a diagonal matrix.

Lemma 9
.

Consider the multivariate setup defined in Assumption
3
and assume a prior
\mu_{0}^{\theta},\Sigma_{0}^{\theta}
is given.
Furthermore, assume
\Sigma^{v}=\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})=\mathrm{diag}\left((\sigma^{v}_{1})^{2},\ldots,(\sigma^{v}_{m})^{2}\right)
is diagonal.
The Bayesian linear regression update for the covariance is then

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{n+1}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}=(\Sigma_{n}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}+A(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{n+1})

(10)

The covariance matrix
\Sigma_{n+1}^{\theta}
can equivalently be computed with
m
scalar updates:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n,1}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{n}^{\theta},

(11)

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{n,k+1}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma_{n,k}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}+\frac{a_{k}(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!a_{k}(u_{n+1})}{(\sigma^{v}_{k})^{2}},\quad k=1,\ldots,m,

(12)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n+1}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{n,m+1}^{\theta},

(13)

where
a_{k}(u_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
denotes the
k
-th row of
A(u_{n+1})
.
Similarly, the mean update can be equivalently computed from
m
scalar updates.

The above lemma will allow us to reuse results from linear bandit theory which consider the scalar case only.

Furthermore, we simplify the analysis by considering the regret induced by the true system outputs, not the noisy measurements, i.e. we define the instantaneous regret
r_{n}=r(u_{n})
induced at iteration
n
as

\displaystyle r(u_{n})=\varphi_{*}(u_{n})-\varphi_{*}(u_{*})

(14)

where
\varphi_{*}(u):=l(u,f_{*}(u))
.
The cumulative regret at iteration
N
is
R_{N}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}r(u_{n})
and will be abbreviated as
R_{N}
.
In addition, we make the following assumptions.

{assumption}

The loss
l(u,z)
is convex in
y
for each
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

{assumption}

Let
\mathcal{Z}=f_{*}(\mathcal{U})
.
We assume
l
is once differentiable in
y
and there exists a constant
L_{z}
satisfying

L_{z}=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U},z\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\frac{\partial l}{\partial z}(u,z)\|
.

The regret analysis comprises two main steps:
In a first step and given
\delta\in(0,1]
, a nondecreasing sequence of hyperparameters
\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\ldots
is derived such that a simultaneous model confidence bound of the following form holds:

\displaystyle\text{{With a probability of at least} }1-\delta\!:\penalty\ f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})\text{ {for all} }u\in\mathcal{U}\text{ {and all} }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}.

The above is called a
simultaneous
model confidence bound as it considers all time steps
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
.
In a second step and assuming that the true model dynamics
f_{*}(u)
are contained within the model confidence sets
\hat{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}(u)
for all
u
and
n
, a deterministic bound on the cumulative regret is derived.

We start by deriving the simultaneous model confidence bound.
To this end, we make the following assumptions which will allow us to build upon results from the linear bandit literature:

{assumption}

The mean and covariance updates use the following values
\Sigma_{v}=\mathbb{I}
,
\Sigma_{0}^{\theta}=\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathbb{I}
,
\sigma_{0}>0
.
The noise
v_{n,i}
is 1-subgaussian and in particular independent of

,
i\neq j

(Simultaneous model confidence bound for LIP models)

Suppose Assumption

Proof.

Numerical Example

In the following, we illustrate the proposed LCB method on an iterative learning control (ILC) example.
The proposed approach is compared against Thompson sampling, originally proposed in
Thompson 1933
for a two-armed bandit problem, and a deterministic zero-order ILC method
(
Baumgärtner and Diehl 2020
;
Baumgärtner and Diehl 2023
)
, which only estimates affine model correction terms and does not maintain a stochastic model.
Furthermore, we compare against the classic, structure-agnostic variant of Thompson sampling and the LCB method.

Problem setup.

We consider open-loop control of a simple oscillator.
The example problem is adopted from
Baumgärtner and Diehl 2020
.
The continuous-time plant dynamics are given by

\ddot{y}+\dot{y}+y=u
, while the nominal model assumes
\ddot{y}+\dot{y}+y=0.5u
.

We consider a control horizon of
T=4
and discretize the continuous-time system dynamics using
M=15
piecewise constant controls on a uniform discretization grid and a Runge-Kutta integrator of order 4.
Both the plant and nominal input-output systems are affine in
u
, i.e.
f_{*}(u)=B_{*}u+b_{*}
with
B_{*}
lower block-triangular which is due to the time discretization.
We therefore use a parametric model adhering to the same structure:

\displaystyle f(u,\theta)=Bu+b+D(\theta_{1})u+d(\theta_{2})

(33)

with both
B
and
D(\theta_{1})
lower block-triangular.
The nominal dynamics
Bu+b
are obtained from discretization of the nominal continuous-time model using the same integration scheme which is used for the plant dynamics.
All nonzero components in
D(\theta_{1})
and
d(\theta_{2})
are fully parametrized with the unknown parameters
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, yielding in total
\dim(\theta)=135
parameters, and allow us to correct the nominal model
Bu+b
.
The loss function is given by

\displaystyle l(u,z)=\|z-z^{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2}+10\|u\|^{2}+100\|z_{\mathrm{terminal}}-z_{\mathrm{terminal}}^{\mathrm{ref}}\|^{2}

where
z_{\mathrm{terminal}}
is the last component of the
z
trajectory.
We use a constant reference of
z^{\mathrm{ref}}=z_{\mathrm{terminal}}^{\mathrm{ref}}=0.5
for both the intermediate reference trajectory as well as the terminal reference.
We assume there is no measurement noise in order to be able to compare structure-agnostic and structure exploiting methods.
Note that the concatenation of the loss function and the input-output model yields a quadratic function.

Structure-agnostic methods.

Both structure-agnostic methods assume the following parametric model:

\displaystyle\varphi(u,\theta^{\varphi})=l(u,Bu+b)+q(u,\theta^{\varphi})

where
q(u,\theta^{\varphi})=\frac{1}{2}(u,1)^{\!\top}\!H(\theta^{\varphi})(u,1)
with
H(\theta^{\varphi})
a symmetric matrix parametrized by
\theta^{\varphi}
, i.e. the objective is given as the sum of the loss function evaluated for the nominal model and a correction which is a quadratic function in
u
and linear in the unknown parameters
\theta^{\varphi}
where
\dim(\theta^{\varphi})=136
.
For structure-agnostic methods, we assume that the cost value (and not the model outputs) can be observed.
While the structure-agnostic LCB method minimizes the LCB acquisition function used in the linear bandit setting, cf. Lemma
5
, Thompson sampling samples a value
\bar{\theta}^{\varphi}
from
\hat{\theta}^{\varphi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\theta^{\varphi}},\Sigma^{\theta^{\varphi}})
and minimizes
\varphi(u,\bar{\theta}^{\varphi})
.

Structure-exploiting methods.

Both structure-exploiting BO methods, LCB and Thompson sampling, use the parametric model given in (
33
).
To determine the next input, the structure-exploiting variant of Thompson sampling minimizes
l(u,f(u,\bar{\theta}))
over
u
where
\bar{\theta}
is sampled from
\hat{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\theta},\Sigma^{\theta})
.
The zero-order ILC method does not maintain a probabilistic model, but instead uses a simple affine model correction.
In particular, the method minimizes
l(u,Bu+b+c)
where the correction
c
is obtained from the observed model-plant mismatch:
c_{0}=0
and
c_{n}=(1-\alpha)c_{n-1}+\alpha e_{n}
with observed error
e_{n}=y_{n}-Bu_{n}-b
and damping factor
\alpha=0.8
.
We refer to
Baumgärtner and Diehl 2023
for more details on the effect of damping for the zero-order ILC method.

We point out that for both the structure-exploiting and the structure-agnostic probabilistic model there are parameters
\theta_{*}
and
\theta^{\varphi}_{*}
such that the true system dynamics and the true objective are recovered.
This is not true for ZOO-ILC.
Both LCB methods use the hyperparameters
\gamma_{n}=\log(e+n)
.
For both variants of Thompson sampling, we perform 100 independent repetitions.

6
Conclusions and Outlook

This paper presents an extension of the classic lower confidence bound (LCB) method to minimization problems with known outer structure, i.e. problems where the objective is given in terms of the concatenation of a
known
loss function and an
unknown
output model.
In contrast to the classic Bayesian optimization setting, where noisy observations of the objective are available, we assume that the potentially multivariate model outputs are measured.

The proposed LCB method is agnostic of the underlying probabilistic model class and solely requires a tractable representation of the corresponding model confidence sets.
Furthermore, the proposed approach reduces to the standard LCB method in the linear bandit and Gaussian process bandit settings.
For the special case of LIP models and convex costs, an in-depth regret analysis is provided which extends established results from linear bandit theory and provides an update rule for the hyperparameter
\gamma
based on a prescribed confidence level.
A numerical example illustrate the effectiveness of the structure-exploiting approach compared to structure-agnostic methods.

Future research includes the extension of the method to problems with output constraints requiring additional measures to ensure safe exploration in the presence of uncertainty
(
Sui et al. 2015
;
Berkenkamp et al. 2023
)
, as well as a comprehensive regret analysis for other model classes such as multivariate Gaussian processes.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose
\theta\in\Ell\!\left(\mu^{\theta},\gamma^{2}\Sigma^{\theta}\right)

\|A(u)(\theta-\mu^{\theta})\|\leq\gamma\sqrt{\|A(u)\Sigma^{\theta}A(u)^{\!\top}\!\|}

We have

\displaystyle\|A(u)(\theta-\mu^{\theta})\|

\displaystyle=\|A(u)(\Sigma^{\theta})^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma^{\theta})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\theta-\mu^{\theta})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A(u)(\Sigma^{\theta})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\cdot\|\theta-\mu^{\theta}\|_{(\Sigma^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sqrt{\|A(u)\Sigma^{\theta}A(u)^{\!\top}\!\|}

where the last inequality uses
\theta\in\Ell\!\left(\mu^{\theta},\gamma^{2}\Sigma^{\theta}\right)
as well as
\|M\|=\sqrt{\|MM^{\!\top}\!\|}
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
\bar{r}=\max(1,\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\varphi_{*}(u)-\varphi_{*}(u_{*}))
denote an upper bound on the maximal regret.
Suppose
f_{*}(u)\in\hat{\mathcal{Z}}(u;\gamma_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n})
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
.
Assume
\gamma_{n}
is a non-decreasing sequence satisfying
\gamma_{0}\geq 1
.
The cumulative squared regret at time instant
N
is then bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}r_{n}(u_{n})^{2}\leq 8\gamma_{N-1}^{2}L_{z}^{2}\bar{r}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\left(1+\|a_{i}(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

Proof.

As a first step, we derive a bound on the instantaneous regret
r(u_{n})
:
Starting from the intermediate result (
27
) and leveraging Lemma
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle r(u_{n})

\displaystyle\leq L_{z}\|f_{*}(u_{n})-z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u_{n},\gamma_{n-1},\mathcal{I}_{n-1})\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{n-1}L_{z}\sqrt{\|A(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}A(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|}

as
f_{*}(u_{n})-z^{\mathrm{sol}}(u_{n},\gamma_{n-1},\mathcal{I}_{n-1})
can be written as
A(u_{n})(\theta_{*}-\mu_{n-1}^{\theta})+A(u_{n})(\mu_{n-1}^{\theta}-\tilde{\theta})
for some
\tilde{\theta}\in\Ell\!\left(\mu_{n-1}^{\theta},\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}\right)
.

Now regarding the cumulative squared regret, we directly obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}r_{n}(u_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\min\left(\bar{r}^{2},4\gamma_{n-1}^{2}L_{z}^{2}\|A(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}A(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{N-1}^{2}\bar{r}^{2}\max(1,4L_{z}^{2})\sum_{n=1}^{N}\min\left(1,\|A(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}A(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

where we used the fact that the sequence of hyperparameters
\gamma_{n}
is nondecreasing and
\gamma_{0}\geq 1
.
Together with

\displaystyle\|A(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}A(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|a_{i}(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|,

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}r_{n}(u_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{N-1}^{2}\bar{r}^{2}\max(1,4L_{z}^{2})\bar{r}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\min\left(1,\|A(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}A(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{N-1}^{2}\bar{r}^{2}\max(1,4L_{z}^{2})\bar{r}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min\left(1,\|a_{i}(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{N-1}^{2}\bar{r}^{2}\max(1,4L_{z}^{2})\bar{r}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\left(1+\|a_{i}(u_{n})\Sigma_{n-1}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n})^{\!\top}\!\|\right)

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\ln(x+1)\geq\frac{x}{2}
for
0\leq x\leq 1
and monotonicity of the natural logarithm.
∎

Lemma 15
.

We regard the covariance update as defined in Lemma
9
.
The sequence of covariance matrices
\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\det\left((\Sigma_{n+1}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)=\det\left((\Sigma_{n}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1+\frac{a_{i}(u_{n+1})\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!}{(\sigma_{i}^{v})^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We first consider
\Sigma_{i+1}^{\theta}
as defined in Lemma
9
.
Let
a_{i}=a_{i}(u_{n+1})
to simplify the notation.
We have

\displaystyle\det\left((\Sigma_{i+1}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left((\Sigma_{i}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}+\frac{a_{i}^{\!\top}\!a_{i}}{(\sigma_{i}^{v})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(S_{i}^{\shortminus 1}\left(\mathbb{I}+S_{i}\frac{a_{i}^{\!\top}\!a_{i}}{(\sigma_{i}^{v})^{2}}S_{i}\right)S_{i}^{\shortminus 1}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left((\Sigma_{i}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)\det\left(\mathbb{I}+s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!\right)

where
S_{i}=\sqrt{\Sigma_{i}^{\theta}}
and
s_{i}=\frac{S_{i}a_{i}^{\!\top}\!}{\sigma_{i}^{v}}
.
From

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{I}+s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!\right)s_{i}=s_{i}+s_{i}(s_{i}^{\!\top}\!s_{i})=s_{i}\left(1+\|s_{i}\|\right),

we conclude that
1+\|s_{i}\|
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{I}+s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!
.
As
s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!
is rank one, all other eigenvalues of
\mathbb{I}+s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!
are 1 implying
\det(\mathbb{I}+s_{i}s_{i}^{\!\top}\!)=1+\|s_{i}\|
.
Together with
\|s_{i}\|=\frac{a_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{\theta}a_{i}^{\!\top}\!}{(\sigma_{i}^{v})^{2}}
, the result follows by induction.
∎

Corollary 16
.

We regard the covariance update as defined in Lemma
9
.
The sequence of covariance matrices
\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{\det\left((\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}{\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}=\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1+\frac{a_{i}(u_{n+1})\Sigma_{n}^{\theta}a_{i}(u_{n+1})^{\!\top}\!}{(\sigma_{i}^{v})^{2}}\right).

Proof.

The result directly follows by induction from Lemma
15
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

We regard the covariance update as defined in Lemma
9
.

Let
\bar{A}=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\|A(u)\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\nu_{v}=\trace\left(\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}\right)
.
It holds that:

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\det\left((\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}{\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}\right)\leq d\ln\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}\nu_{v}\bar{A}^{2}N}{d}\right).

Proof.

First, note that
(\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}
can equivalently be expressed in terms of an
information update
as follows:

\displaystyle(\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\mathbb{I}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}A(u_{i})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{i}).

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
be the nonnegative eigenvalues of
\sum_{i=1}^{N}A(u_{i})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{i})
.
Together with
\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)=(\sigma_{0}^{2})^{-d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\det\left((\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}{\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\sigma_{0}^{2}(\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\mathbb{I}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A(u_{i})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\sigma_{0}^{2}\lambda_{j}\right).

Taking the natural logarithm on both sides yields

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\det\left((\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}{\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}\right)=d\ln\left(\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\sigma_{0}^{2}\lambda_{j}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)\leq d\ln\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\sigma_{0}^{2}\lambda_{j}\right)\right)\!,

where the final inequality follows from the AM-GM inequality.
Together with the following result on the sum of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=\trace\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}A(u_{i})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\trace\left(A(u_{i})^{\!\top}\!\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus 1}A(u_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\Sigma^{v}(\sigma^{v})^{\shortminus\frac{1}{2}}A(u_{i})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{v}N\bar{A}^{2},

with
\bar{A}=\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\|A(u)\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and where we used
\|M\|^{2}_{F}=\trace\left(M^{\!\top}\!M\right)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\det\left((\Sigma_{N}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}{\det\left((\Sigma_{0}^{\theta})^{\shortminus 1}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\ln\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(1+\sigma_{0}^{2}\lambda_{j}\right)\right)\leq d\ln\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}}{d}N\nu_{v}\bar{A}^{2}\right).

∎
