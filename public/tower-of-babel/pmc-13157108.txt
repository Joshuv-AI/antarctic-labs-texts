Toward large-scale utilization approaches for phosphogypsum with low-carbon paradigm
Authors: Haojie Wang, Yuwei Xiang, Xuquan Huang, Guangkun Wang, Guojing Wang, Lei Chen, Bing Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLarge-scale utilization of phosphogypsum (PG) is crucial due to its massive production, potential value, and environmental risks. Although various recycling technologies exist, they vary widely in scale and profitability. This work first proposes a reclamation-purification-stabilization trinity technological framework to bridge fundamental research and large-scale engineering. Based on a bibliometric analysis of 4,766 studies, it analyzes PG’s basic characteristics, environmental risks, and the interplay between its minerals and impurities. Key parameters, including mixing ratios, large-scale application indices, and carbon footprint, are quantified for different technologies. This study also evaluates the strengths, limitations, and application scenarios of the existing methods. Finally, it highlights that the quantitative carbon footprint analysis will be vital for guiding strategic decisions in PG’s low-carbon transformation. This work aims to advance the research focus from isolated technological breakthroughs toward optimizing the entire process chain, thereby accelerating the low-carbon transition of PG utilization.Subject areas: ecology, materials science

Introduction

Phosphogypsum (PG), as a quintessential byproduct of the phosphorus chemical industry, represents a significant environmental and resource challenge in terms of its harmless treatment and extensive resource utilization.1,2 Globally, cumulative stockpiles of PG have exceeded 6 billion metric tons on annual basis, with China alone contributing approximately 80 million metric tons and its resource utilization rate standing at around 50%.3,4,5 This industrial solid waste not only occupies vast tracts of land but also poses a multi-faceted threat to the environment, contaminating aquatic, terrestrial, atmospheric, and endangering biodiversity. In China, the situation is particularly acute in the Yangtze River basin, where PG discharge and accumulation have triggered severe phosphorus and fluorine pollution at the source, leading to numerous environmental and public health crises.6,7,8,9 Consequently, PG management has emerged as a critical flashpoint in the intersection of environmental protection and social development. Addressing this issue requires the development of large-scale PG utilization strategies, which are essential for curbing the root causes of environmental pollution and enhancing the effectiveness of basin-wide environmental governance.

Though PG mainly contains substantial amounts of calcium sulfates (CaSO4·nH2O, n = 0, 1.5, 2) as the main available resources,10,11 it also comprises a heterogeneous mixture of impurities,12 including fluorophosphate minerals,6,13 heavy metals,14,15 rare earth elements (REEs),16,17 and primary collophane. Such a complex composition endows PG with intricate chemical properties, presenting both challenges and opportunities for its large-scale utilization across diverse industries.6 Current research on the resource-oriented utilization of PG primarily focuses on partial substitution for cement in the building materials sector,18 gypsum application in board production, soil conditioners,19 and REE extraction. While conventional treatment methods have gently alleviated the stockpiling pressure, they still face significant challenges in terms of processing efficiency and cost when addressing the requirement for large-scale consumption of PG. Furthermore, certain techniques are associated with high energy consumption and a substantial carbon footprint. Simultaneously achieving large-scale utilization and low-carbon processing constitutes the core dilemma and critical bottleneck of the PG recycling field. Thus, the systematic adoption of a pathway for large-scale PG utilization under the low-carbon paradigm has become an imperative,20,21 which not only enables the reduction of life cycle carbon emissions, aligning with the international green development agenda, but also presents a new opportunity for the technological transformation and industrial upgrading of the phosphorus chemical sectors.

In an era marked by the exponential growth of scientific production, bibliometric analysis (BA) has emerged as an indispensable tool for understanding the dynamics of research fields. As a scientific and effective research method, BA can systematically sort out the research context, distribution of research hotspots, and development trends in a specific field through quantitative statistics and analysis of a large number of documents in that field.22,23 In the research category of integrating large-scale utilization of PG with the low-carbon concept, applying the BA method can comprehensively integrate scattered research achievements, tap potential research directions, and provide a macro perspective and theoretical support for a deeper understanding of this complex topic.

In the study, 4,766 research and empirical investigations were collected and employed to conduct an in-depth BA of the mineralization composition and environmental risks. An integrated technical framework involving reclamation, purification, and stabilization technologies for PG is proposed for the first time in the literature. Then, we summarize the environmental-resource application scenarios, strengths, limitations, future prospects, and carbon footprint of various PG utilization technologies. Finally, based on the data acquired and analyzed, several insights were derived to guide the practical implementation of PG utilization. The work aims to synergistically achieve large-scale disposal and low-carbon and high-value utilization of PG, thereby offering a novel pathway to alleviate the environmental and resource pressures associated with PG stockpiles.

Mineralogical composition of PG

PG contains approximately 20% impurities,26 which directly determine the potential utilization pathways of PG and serve as a critical link between laboratory research and engineering applications. The dominant mineral components of PG include CaO, SO3, SiO2, Al2O3, P2O5, and total F. Notably, we employ this oxide-based representation for quantitative analysis, while subsequent discussions will refer to actual mineral forms.

In this work, six mineral components of PG were selected for analysis, and relevant data were extracted from the retrieved publications and are presented in the Figure 2. The box plots demonstrate the content ranges of these components as follows: CaO, from 30.99% to 38.13%; SO3, from 40.86% to 51.11%; SiO2, from 1.59% to 6.55%; Al2O3, from 0.20% to 0.99%; P2O5, from 0.87% to 1.82%; and total fluorine (F), from 0.19% to 1.15%. In conclusion, the gypsum content accounts for 71.85%–89.24%, with the remainder being impurities. Given the heterogeneous nature of PG, an optimal treatment strategy must be grounded in the characteristics of the raw material. Therefore, it is essential to quantify the content and characterize the occurrence forms of valuable components to facilitate effective recycling and utilization. Furthermore, the analysis of key impurities is equally critical, which involves the regional environmental risks and hazards associated with PG.

Environmental risks of PG

The environmental problems resulting from the stockpiling of PG, such as land occupation, dust pollution, and soil and water contamination, have raised significant concern among both the public and governmental agencies.24 PG impurities include phosphates,27 fluorides,12 soluble sulfates,28 heavy metals (As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, and Zn),29 residual acids,12 naturally occurring radionuclides (226Ra and 232Th),30 REEs,31 and other trace elements. The concentrations of these impurities vary depending on the source of PG, the type of wet phosphoric acid production process, or storage conditions as well as aging time.2,14,32 Therefore, elucidating the environmental risks of PG is imperative, as it can play a critical role in preventing pollution dispersion and facilitating its safe and effective utilization.

The diverse impurities present in PG give rise to a complex spectrum of environmental risks, as summarized in Table 1. The release of fluorides causes toxicity to aquatic organisms by inhibiting photosynthesis and enzyme activity and induces human skeletal fluorosis.27 Phosphates primarily cause eutrophication in water bodies, heavy metals can disrupt soil aggregate structure and inhibit microbial activity, and long-term ingestion of heavy metal-contaminated substances may lead to neurological damage, cancers, and other diseases. Additionally, PG is acidic due to residual sulfuric acid (with a pH as low as 1.5), and the main hazards of this acidity lie in exacerbating the migration of pollutants and ecological damage. In terms of radiation exposure and pollution diffusion, 226Ra decays to produce 222Rn45 (an International Agency for Research on Cancer [IARC] group I carcinogen), which can be released into the atmosphere through gaps in PG stockpiles, and long-term inhalation of 222Rn increases the risk of lung cancer.

The environmental risks during the comprehensive utilization of PG require stringent control over impurity transformations and a novel technology framework based on impurity management. To address this, the present work pioneers a novel classification framework that systematically integrates reclamation, purification, and stabilization technologies for PG. This framework explicitly defines and distinguishes three core technological pathways: the recovery of valuable impurities, the removal of detrimental impurities, and the stabilization of residual components.

Calcium source recovery

PG contains approximately 24% calcium, primarily in the form of gypsum, fluorite, and apatite, which endows PG with substantial potential as an alternative source of calcium-rich raw materials.58 These calcium-rich chemical products include calcium sulfide, calcium oxide, calcium hydrosulfide, calcium carbonate, calcium chloride, calcium hydrogen phosphate, and potassium-calcium fertilizer.58 Currently, to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions, PG is frequently employed as a calcium source for CO2 mineral sequestration, enabling the production of high-purity crystalline calcite through carbonation valorization.55,59,60 Specifically, ammonia-enhanced PG mineral carbonation achieves a peak carbonation efficiency of 91% within 30 min.61 Under optimal conditions, the carbonation rate even reaches 98.32%, with 1,000 kg of PG yielding 510 kg of high-purity CaCO3 and sequestering 224 kg of CO2.62 Vaterite can be successfully synthesized by adjusting the reaction conditions and NaCl cycling times.63 In addition, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB)35 and the Lysinibacillus sphaericus strain21 can effectively bio-transform PG into crystalline, bead-shaped CaCO3 precipitates, which exhibit favorable technical properties and are applicable as raw materials for papermaking, plastics, and rubber industries.64

However, the fundamental bottleneck for the efficient recycling of PG lies in the synergistic adverse effects arising from the impurity-induced poisoning of crystal growth, the high-energy consumption nature of the processes, and the unfavorable economic model. Future breakthroughs must focus on source reduction of impurities via cleaner phosphorus production processes, coupled with a multi-component, cascading resource recovery strategy that prioritizes the extraction of valuable elements to synergistically facilitate calcium resource recycling. Ultimately, achieving the targeted synthesis of high-value functional materials through systemic innovation is key to resolving the long-standing challenge of synergistically balancing economic viability and environmental benefits.

Sulfur source recovery

PG contains approximately 20% sulfur, and the principle of sulfur recovery involves converting hexavalent sulfur in calcium sulfate into valuable sulfur species (e.g., SO2, H2SO4, and elemental sulfur) via chemical or biological methods. These technologies are categorized into three main types: high-temperature pyrolysis reduction (HTPR),65 wet chemical conversion, and biological transformation.

As the most mature industrialized route, the HTPR process involves reacting PG with reducing agents (e.g., coal, coke, and biomass) via high-temperature calcination to achieve the targeted conversion of sulfur, thereby enabling direct sulfur recovery. The primary reactions are shown in Equations 3, 4, 5, and 6, through which sulfuric acid could be produced concurrently with cement.56 Life cycle assessment indicated that HTPR can achieve dual environmental and economic benefit, making it a promising technology for large-scale implementation.66 Furthermore, the conversion rate of PG to sulfur dioxide reached 92.2%, with a desulfurization rate of 95.16%65 in the carbothermal reduction smelting experiments. SO2 and O2 adsorbed on the surface of P2O5 undergo catalytic oxidation to form SO3, which further reacts with water vapor to produce H2SO4. Notably, it was found that Fe3+ facilitates the catalytic oxidation of SO2 and O2 by lowering the reaction energy barrier,67 thereby promoting the formation of SO3. High energy consumption poses a major constraint on scaling this process.

Wet chemical conversion enables sulfur recovery via solution-phase reactions at ambient temperature, featuring low energy consumption and compatibility with low-grade PG. In this process, PG is leached with aqueous ammonia, allowing SO42− ions to dissolve and form (NH4)2SO4. The key reactions are presented in Equations 7 and 8; under optimal conditions, the maximum SO2 recovery rate of 97% can be achieved, and NH3 can be recycled for CO2 co-sequestration.68 In addition, the solid-liquid equilibrium approach has been employed to convert PG into potassium sulfate, which is further processed into K2Mg(SO4)2 as a binary fertilizer.69 Low energy consumption and operational simplicity offer significant benefits for scaling this process. However, the requirement for industrial ammonia and the adverse effects of impurity ions considerably undermine its practicality.

Biological transformation has garnered significant attention over the past few decades owing to its potential efficiency, cost effectiveness, and, most importantly, environmental benignity.28,70,71,72 Specifically, PG valorization can be achieved via SRB and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB): SRB reduce sulfate to sulfide, while SOB oxidize sulfide to elemental sulfur (S0). Stirred tank reactor experiments were conducted for PG leaching to form the scope for suitable sulfuric acid concentration.31 However, key challenges involve the impact of PG impurities on the metabolism and growth of microorganisms, and the inhibitory effects of high sulfate concentrations in PG and H2S.73

Sulfur recovery from PG offers the strategic advantage of converting calcium sulfate into valuable chemical products like elemental sulfur and sulfuric acid, thereby establishing an alternative sulfur source while eliminating sulfate ion pollution. The primary challenges involve the high energy consumption and severe corrosion associated with conventional high-temperature reduction processes, coupled with the low efficiency and high costs of emerging technologies. Future development will focus on green and low-carbon pathways, aiming to achieve breakthroughs in mild catalytic conversion and bioleaching technologies to achieve a high-value, co-production model that simultaneously recovers sulfur, phosphorus, calcium, and REEs.

REE extraction

REEs, a group of critical metals essential to the electronics and renewable energy system, have attracted increasing attention due to their extremely high economic benefits,74 even when they are present at concentrations ranging from 0.36% to 0.52% in PG. In PG, REEs primarily exist in three forms: mineral inclusions, isomorphous substitution of Ca2+ in gypsum lattice, and dispersed soluble salts.75,76 Numerous REE extraction methods have been proposed, including direct acid leaching, complexation leaching, and external field-assisted strategies such as mechanical activation and microwave-assisted techniques.77

Notably, mechanical grinding,78 ultrasonic treatment,79 and resin-in-pulp80 process have been shown to significantly enhance REE leaching efficiencies from PG, increasing them from 15% to over 70%. A hydrometallurgical process was developed to leach yttrium (Y), neodymium (Nd), and dysprosium (Dy) from PG, using nitric acid, hydrochloric acid, and sulfuric acid, and the process achieved a leaching efficiency of 94.6% for Nd and 86.1% for Dy under optimum operating conditions.81 In addition, ZSM-5 zeolite acts as a high-performance multi-functional adsorbent for Ce3+, La3+, and Nd3+ from PG leachates, exhibiting adsorption capacities of 99.42, 96.43, and 118.10 mg/g, respectively,82 while chitin and chitin aerogel are effectives for Y3+ adsorption.57 Furthermore, biogenic silica and metakaolin have been employed to fabricate magnetic geopolymer for the adsorption of La3+, Ce3+, Pr3+, Sm3+, and Nd3+ from real PG leachates, with the highest recovery efficiency observed for Nd3+, followed by Ce3+.83 Enrichment of undissolved independent REE inclusion is achieved through wet sieving, shaking tables, or hydro cyclones, leveraging the density difference between REEs and the gypsum matrix. Inorganic acids are employed to disrupt the gypsum lattice, thereby releasing REEs present in the isomorphous substitution state (where Ca2+ is replaced by REE3+) and as dispersed soluble rare earth salts. The central challenge in the hydrometallurgical recovery of REEs from PG revolves around the effective breakdown or phase transformation of the gypsum matrix, with the objective to liberate REEs that are primarily encapsulated within the crystal lattice via isomorphous substitution for calcium ions. A critical aspect of this process involves implementing strategies to mitigate re-immobilization or loss of the liberated REEs during subsequent processing steps.84,85,86

In addition, microbial-based extraction involves microorganisms metabolically producing gluconic acid to dissolve REEs or altering the pH via microbial metabolism.87 Aspergillus niger has achieved a total REE leaching efficiency of 74%, targeting key elements such as Y, lanthanum (La), cerium (Ce), and Nd. Notably, the leaching efficiency of A. niger culture filtrate was 63.3% higher than that of a simulated organic acid-mix formulated with the main organic acid components identified in the A. niger leachate.88

Currently, the lab-scale extraction of REEs has been successful, but the technology needs to be scaled up to the pilot scale. The ultra-low grade of REE (typically <0.1%) leads to prohibitively high costs for energy and reagents per unit recovered, thereby hindering the scaled application of recovery technologies. Complex impurity matrices (e.g., high concentrations of calcium, phosphorus, and fluorine) severely compromise the efficiency of selective REE separation, and secondary waste residues generated during the recovery process may pose new environmental risks. Future breakthroughs should focus on low-energy, integrated activation-separation technologies, novel methods for targeted REE enrichment, and comprehensive component valorization strategies, enhancing overall economic viability by prioritizing REE extraction while coupling it with calcium and sulfur resource recovery to balance environmental benefits, ultimately aiming to establish a green and low-carbon REE recovery technological framework.

Purification technologies for PG

Purification means the removal of impurities. PG contains a complex and diverse array of impurities, which primarily include phosphorus, fluorine, organic matter, heavy metals, radioactive elements, and other inorganic minerals.89 Among these, the removal of lattice-bound (co-crystalline) phosphorus is particularly challenging due to its persistent detrimental effects, and the low whiteness of PG is primarily attributed to its raw ore minerals, which severely restrict its utilization as a substitute of natural gypsum.90 Based on the removal mechanisms for different impurities, the purification technologies for PG (PG-PTs) are presented in Figure 4.

Washing process

Washing, as the most straightforward pretreatment technique, aims primarily to reduce soluble impurities in PG by removing soluble phosphates, fluorides, fine slimes,92 and partially soluble organic compounds/salts93 from PG via water, acid, or alkaline solutions.94 Washing methods can be categorized into two types: (1) simple water washing, which relies solely on the dissolution effect of water to remove soluble impurities95; (2) chemical-aided washing, which employs chemical reagents to enhance the removal of refractory impurities such as eutectic phosphorus and certain heavy metals. Oxalic acid (H2C2O4) was used to remove the insoluble phosphate impurities within the gypsum crystals by partially dissolving the PG matrix.96 However, for complete removal of lattice-bound phosphorus, a phase transition approach is essential.97,98,99 This process involves dissolution-recrystallization of gypsum, typically induced by temperature or salt concentration modulation, which enables the in-depth liberation of occluded phosphorus. Crucially, this phase transformation not only facilitates impurity extraction but concurrently enables the synthesis of high-value-added gypsum products, thereby integrating purification with material valorization. In practical applications, cyclic water washing enhances impurity removal efficiency through cumulative dissolution effect, while reducing wastewater discharge by reusing washing water multiple times. Notably, the traditional method of washing PG would generate a large amount of waste water,33 which poses a critical challenge requiring urgent attention.

Flotation process

Flotation is an efficient and industrially feasible technology for PG purification, and this process includes reverse flotation,100 direct flotation,101 reverse-direct combined flotation,102 graded flotation,90 column flotation,103 and coupled flotation.104 These flotation methods are designed to selectively and deeply remove siliceous impurities, organic impurities, eutectic phosphorus, and metal ions from PG, with the specific method determined by the impurity composition, particle size distribution, and purity requirements of the target product.90 Reverse flotation is first employed to remove organic matters and fine slimes, followed by direct flotation for silica impurity removal, and then, through a closed-circuit flotation process, whiteness of the PG concentrate is increased from 33.23 to 63.42.101 In addition, surfactants (e.g., Span-80, TX-100, and Tween-80) are utilized to construct colloidal-mechanical microenvironments, thereby promoting the whiteness of PG through the synergistic effect of micelle formation and mechanical force, while improving the removal efficiencies of soluble phosphorus and fluorine.33

Flotation purification technology is a primary pretreatment method due to its high selectivity for surface impurities, relatively low energy consumption, and scalability for industrial application. However, its inherent limitations—including the inability to remove lattice-bound phosphorus, significant reagent dependency, and constrained product purity enhancement—restrict its efficacy. In addition, challenges such as high reagent cost and residues remain to be addressed. In the future, the development of green collectors and optimization of equipment105 (e.g., flotation columns) are expected to further improve its economic viability and environmental friendliness. This integrated approach will facilitate a paradigm shift from conventional bulk impurity removal toward precision purification, ultimately enabling high-value resource utilization of PG.

Calcination process

PG calcination achieves two key objectives through controlled thermal treatment. The first is the transformation of the gypsum crystal structure via progressive dehydration of gypsum from the dihydrate to hemihydrate form and ultimately to anhydrite.106,107 Anhydrite and hemihydrate have a higher cementitious activity and can be used as raw materials for constructing cementitious materials91,108; the reactions are shown in Equations 9, 10, and 11.

The second objective involves the removal of impurities: soluble phosphorus and co-crystal phosphorus are converted into inert phosphates, while organic impurities are decomposed into CO2 and H2O.109 Notably, the presence of soluble P2O5 components is a key factor limiting the application of PG as a cementitious material. Therefore, removing phosphates is critically important during the calcination process. At elevated temperatures (≥200°C), soluble phosphates are converted into insoluble calcium phosphate salts, reducing their inhibitory effect on cementitious properties. For example, calcination at 800°C for 1 h can reduce soluble P2O5 content from 0.581% to 0.007%.110 Fluoride impurities may volatilize or react with other components at high temperatures to form stable compounds, thereby mitigating their corrosive effects. In addition, organic impurities undergo oxidative decomposition into CO2 and water at high temperatures, alleviating their adverse impacts on material properties.111 Unlike the thermal decomposition process for sulfur recovery, calcination of PG does not require additional reducing agents.106,112

Calcination purification technology demonstrates unique advantages in decomposing organic matter and immobilizing lattice-bound phosphorus under high-temperature conditions, coupled with its direct resource conversion capability for producing building material precursors. However, its scale-up application remains constrained by critical bottlenecks including excessive energy consumption, significant secondary pollution risks, and inefficacy against soluble impurities. Future advancement of this technology must transition from mere impurity removal toward a paradigm of low-carbon transition and quality enhancement, which can be achieved through innovations in low-temperature energy-efficient processes, stringent pollution control measures, and intelligent integration with pretreatment technologies, ultimately optimizing calcination as a crucial deep-treatment step within the high-value utilization chain of PG.

Biological process

Biological purification utilizes the metabolic activity of microorganisms to eliminate harmful components in PG, thereby improving its physicochemical properties and enhancing its potential for resource utilization.29 Compared with physicochemical methods, biological purification is characterized by low energy consumption, environmental friendliness, and mild reaction conditions, making it an emerging research focus in the field of PG harmless treatment.

Microorganisms are employed to metabolize or immobilize hazardous substances in PG, including fluoride ions, heavy metals, and organic pollutants, thereby converting these contaminants into less toxic forms.12 Rho (a microbial strain) can adsorb fluoride ions in PG through extracellular polymer, while accelerating the release of phosphorus from PG.113 For the bacterium Sporosarcina pasteurii, bio-washing with this strain achieves a 50%–90% reduction in the concentrations of 12 heavy metals in PG samples.29 Through enzyme-induced carbonate precipitation (EICP), modified acidophilus bacterial solutions have been applied to remove phosphorus and fluorine in PG, achieving approximately 13% increase in the optimal impurity removal ratio. The high acid resistance of both the urease enzyme and acidophilic bacteria improves their growth and metabolic activity, leading to approximately 22% increase in the biomineralization rate.36 Microbial strategies for immobilizing harmful substances in PG are discussed in detail in “stabilization technologies for PG.”

Biological process for impurity removal offers a promising alternative pathway for green and low-carbon purification, leveraging its unique advantages of mild operating conditions, high selectivity, and environmental compatibility. However, its industrial implementation remains constrained by core bottlenecks including low reaction rates, poor environmental adaptability, and significant scale-up challenges. Future advancements must undergo a paradigm shift from pursuing “single-strain efficiency” toward an integrated “multi-technology synergy and intelligence” approach. This entails employing synthetic biology to engineer robust microbial strains, developing synergistic multi-consortium systems, constructing bio-physicochemical hybrid processes, and incorporating process intensification with intelligent control. Ultimately, this evolution will position bioremediation as a precise and sustainable key unit within the future high-value PG utilization chain.

Alkaline slag neutralization

Alkaline slags34,117,118,119 are rich in alkaline components like CaO and Ca(OH)2, which supply OH− to neutralize the acidic environment and immobilize cation heavy metals, while providing Ca2+ to promote the precipitation reactions of fluorides and phosphates and significantly reducing their leachability. Alkaline slags exhibit the dual function of acid neutralization and contaminant immobilization, demonstrating significant potential for the large-scale treatment of PG. l.

Bayer red mud, a typical alkaline slag, can accelerate hydration reactions and enhance the structural strength of PG by 3-fold.117 In addition, steel slag can provide free CaO (f-CaO) and silicate minerals for PG stabilization, yielding a product with a strength of 56 MPa.118 Stabilization with fly ash can enhance mechanical strengths and mitigate issues like shrinkage or swelling.120 When using ground granulated blast furnace slag for PG stabilization, Fe3+, Al3+, and F− can form [AlF6]3- and [FeF6]3- complexes, enabling the fixation and stabilization of free fluoride ions.34 What’s more, iron/aluminum salts in alkaline slag also immobilize phosphate ions via ion exchange or adsorption to form insoluble salts, while adsorbing heavy metal ions to form stable complexes. Geopolymer stabilization lies in constructing a three-dimensional network structure through alkali-activated reactions with alkaline slags, thereby achieving the synergy of harmlessness and resource utilization of PG. This process forms a consolidated matrix predominantly composed of ye’elimite, larnite, ternesite and anhydrite.121 Notably, raw PG can be fully utilized in the alkaline slag neutralization process with little to no pretreatment, and the proportion of PG in the mixture ranges from 90% to 95%, indicating substantial application potential.

By leveraging its synergistic waste-to-waste approach and cost effectiveness, the alkali residue neutralization technology serves as a practical solution for large-scale harmless disposal of PG. However, its inherent limitation of providing a symptomatic rather than a fundamental solution, coupled with the risk of introducing new impurities, confines its primary application to low-value sectors such as roadbed filling. The future advancement of this technology critically depends on its deep integration with purification and activation processes, which can drive its evolution from simple stabilization toward targeted resource conversion.

Mineral carbonation

Mineral carbonation is typically coupled with alkaline slag neutralization, as raw PG is in an acidic environment where carbonation reactions cannot proceed. During the mineral carbonation process, abundant calcium sources in PG react with CO2 under specific conditions to form stable calcium carbonate,122 and impurities are immobilized within the calcium carbonate matrix through lattice substitution, co-precipitation, or encapsulation, thereby achieving in situ stabilization of the impurities, by efficiently regulating reaction conditions.123

When applying bi-directional carbonation curing with Na2CO3 as an internal carbon source, the immobilization efficiencies of Pb2+ and Cr3+ are increased by 80% and 87%, respectively. This also enhances the early-stage compressive strength of PG and promotes the formation of a denser spatial structure for trapping heavy metals, owing to the formation of nano-CaCO3 crystals.40 In addition, nanoscale CaCO3 crystals contribute to pore refinement and adhere to the surfaces of PG particles, which significantly improved the water resistance of carbonated PG paste.124 Notably, phosphate impurities within PG have an adverse effect on CO2 uptake, which inhibit the precipitation of hydration products and instead promote the formation of calcium phosphate species that retard the dissolution of cementitious particles. By contrast, fluoride impurities in PG accelerate hydration reactions and consequently expedite the carbonation process.27 CO2-assisted alkali activation of PG significantly reduces the leaching of Phosphates and Fluorides, and their concentrations decrease from 146.5 and 87.63 mg/L, respectively, in raw PG to approximately 1 mg/L in the hardened PG matrix (HPGM). In addition, heavy metals are effectively immobilized within ettringite (AFt) and calcium-aluminum-silicate-hydrate (C-A-S-H) phases, demonstrating the potential of HPGM for safe and sustainable utilization of PG in construction.125 In total, the carbonation product CaCO3 plays a triple role—filling pores, promoting crystal nucleating, and facilitating chemical activation during accelerated carbonation and subsequent hydration.41 The microstructural analysis results indicate that carbonation converts tricalcium silicate (C3S) and dicalcium silicate (C2S) into CaCO3 and silica gel, forming a dense, stable matrix that encapsulates PG particles and suppresses sulfate leaching. Subsequent hydration refines the pore structure and restores reactivity via the growth of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gels.126 However, carbonation tends to further loosen the microstructure of the PG hardened paste matrix (HPGM), due to the complete decomposition of ettringite and the severe decalcification of gel products.127

PG mineralization technology achieves fundamental stabilization through phase transformation and opens a green pathway for high-value resource utilization. However, the technology faces core challenges including sluggish reaction kinetics, product layer encapsulation inhibition, and difficulties in controlling impurity migration. Future development will focus on process intensification, targeted product regulation, and intelligent multi-technology integration to drive its transition from proof-of-concept toward efficient industrial application. Ultimately, it aims to become a key component in the PG resource technology portfolio, enabling simultaneous carbon fixation and product value enhancement.

Microbial mineralization

Impurities in PG are stabilized via oxidation, adsorption, encapsulation, chemical-enhanced stabilization, and pH regulation facilitated by the microbial mineralization process.36,73,116

A typical microbial mineralization method, microbial-induced carbonate precipitation (MICP), involves catalyzing urea hydrolysis using urease secreted by urease-producing bacteria such as Bacillus pasteurii128 and Bacillus cereus.37 This reaction generates CO32− and an alkaline environment, driving the formation of CaCO3 precipitates that improve the mechanical properties of PG. Additionally, MICP synergistically reinforces the cementation effect when combined with reactive magnesium oxide cement,37,129 while assisting in heavy metal immobilization to reduce environmental risks, ultimately realizing the harmless resource utilization of PG; the mechanism of MICP is illustrated in Equations 12, 13, 14, 15, and 16. Eco-friendly MICP technology using PG has been applied to achieve PG mineralization and stabilization.128 Microbially induced stabilization of heavy metals130 (HMs) involves oxidizing As(III) to As(V),42 and stabilizing As(V) via anion exchange using PG is more rapid and straightforward than using binding carbonation, though the resulting As(V) complexes are less stable.131

Furthermore, microbial metabolism effectively degrades PG, transforming pH from acidic to alkaline and significantly enhancing CO2 sequestration capacity,132 which fills pores and binds sand particles, significantly enhancing the strength of the sand mass and decreasing fluoride content.133 In addition, extracellular polymeric substances produced during microbial metabolism act as natural binders, enhancing particle aggregation and structural densification of PG. The carboxyl, amino, and other functional groups on the microbial cell membrane adsorb fluoride ions, and the generated CaCO3 encapsulates or co-precipitates harmful elements, preventing their leaching.128 Bio-cemented PG backfill maintained a neutral pH of 7.16, showing excellent fluidity, which is suitable for efficient pipeline transportation in underground mining, meeting the standards for mining backfill applications with unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of 947 kPa.116 The low environmental robustness of microbial agents, coupled with challenges in operational scalability, currently limit their application in industrial settings. Biomimetic mineralization134,135 is an emerging future technology that aids in microbial stabilization of PG by simulating biological mineralization pathways, achieving efficient immobilization of P, F, and heavy metals, realizing resource utilization, and better accommodating large-scale application.

Microbial mineralization technology for PG leverages its mild reaction conditions, environmentally benign process, and unique potential for precisely regulating mineral nucleation, offering an innovative approach for low-carbon PG valorization. However, this technology is currently constrained by several critical challenges, including low reaction efficiency, poor microbial adaptability to complex environments, and significant scale-up difficulties. Future advancements will inevitably rely on the deep integration of synthetic biology and process engineering. This entails engineering robust microbial strains, developing intelligent multi-technology synergistic reaction systems, and thereby facilitating the transition from lab-scale proof-of-concept to industrial application.

The schematic for the classification framework for PG reclamation, purification, and stabilization technologies, proposed for the first time in this study, is presented in Figure 6. Currently, the utilization rate of PG reclamation technologies generally remains below 45%, with a processing scale limited to approximately 1 million tons. Although REE-derived products have an exceptionally high market value, nearly 1,000 times the unit price of conventional materials, their practical application scale is five orders of magnitude lower, resulting in limited overall economic viability. Furthermore, this approach fails to address the persistent challenges of PG stockpiling and associated environmental impacts. Purification technologies for PG achieve a utilization rate ranging from 65% to 75%, with an operational scale of up to 40 million tons, making them the most mature and widely implemented process to date. In contrast, stabilization technologies demonstrate the highest PG utilization rate, exceeding 80%–95% at a production scale of over 10 million tons. These methods are already being used in mining operations and road construction due to their compatibility with in situ treatments. Moreover, the stabilized products maintain potential for further resource utilization. With continuing technological innovation, these approaches are expected to expand into broader application scenarios and higher value in the near future. Therefore, this classification framework based on application scale demonstrates significant potential for guiding the practical implementation of pilot-scale projects.

Schematic of the classification framework about PG reclamation, purification, and stabilization technologies

Furthermore, cost-benefit comparisons and quantitative carbon footprint assessments for PG treatment technologies represent critical factors influencing the scalability of PG management. As summarized in Table 3, several practically applicable technologies have been evaluated as representative case studies, with analyses focusing on their respective economic benefits and carbon emissions. A qualitative assessment based on the benefit-cost ratio (BCR) demonstrates that the selected viable technologies are generally economically favorable, indicated by BCR values exceeding unity (BCR > 1). Notably, although fluctuations in the market value of output products (e.g., sulfuric acid or REEs) can significantly impact BCR calculations, current evaluations consistently yield BCR of >1 under prevailing market conditions. The carbon footprint is expressed in emissions per metric ton of the product produced. Among the technologies assessed, REE extraction exhibits the highest carbon intensity, reaching up to 141.82 t CO2 equivalent per ton of product, a level that is counterbalanced by the high economic value of REEs. In contrast, the carbon footprints of other baseline technologies typically range around 300 kg CO2 equivalent per ton. Of particular significance is the mineral carbonation technology that can achieve a carbon-negative profile, which renders it crucial for supporting low-carbon transitions. A key limitation identified is the scarcity of comprehensive carbon footprint data for alternative technologies. Future research should prioritize quantitative carbon emission analyses across diverse technological pathways, which will yield essential data to inform strategic decision-making for low-carbon transformation.

This framework systematically integrates reclamation, purification, and stabilization technologies. Key parameters for each technological route, including mixing ratios, scalability, and carbon footprint, were quantitatively assessed.

Limitation of study

While the reclamation-purification-stabilization framework proposed in this study aims to provide a systematic and integrated analysis, its findings remain largely based on a synthesis of published literature and have not yet been validated through large-scale engineering applications. The quantitative relationships between key parameters, such as mix proportions and carbon footprint, the practical synergies, and potential conflicts arising from multiple treatment steps require further verification via pilot- or industrial-scale demonstrations. Furthermore, evaluations of techno-economic viability and environmental performance are limited by the scarcity of commercially deployed cases and inconsistent data availability, which add uncertainty to assessing the scalability of certain technologies, especially those targeting higher-value products. These limitations also point to clear pathways for future work, including integrated system validation, rigorous life cycle assessment, and interdisciplinary analysis that links technical development with policy and market dynamics.
