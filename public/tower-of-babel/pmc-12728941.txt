Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood
Authors: Hans Vaish, Vipin Vaish, Shalini Suri, Vasudha Tomar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA boy in late childhood presented with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain and respiratory distress, which temporarily improved after aspiration. The pleural fluid was consistently dark red. Due to repeated effusions, a right intercostal drain was placed. History revealed recent abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle. A CT scan of the chest showed right patchy consolidation, hydropneumothorax and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. Both serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were elevated. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed bilateral (right greater than left) pancreatopleural fistulas. The patient was treated with nil per oral status, total parenteral nutrition and Octreotide. Over a period of 10 days, drainage decreased, the chest tube was removed, and the child was discharged.Keywords: Pancreatitis, Paediatric intensive care, Radiology (diagnostics), Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Medical management

Background

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an extremely rare entity, which presents with recurrent, massive, blood-stained pleural effusion.1 It is observed in cases of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic trauma.2 It can be a confusing entity to deal with as it presents with signs of respiratory distress due to recurrent pleural effusions; however, the pathology lies in the pancreas,3 thereby delaying its diagnosis as all efforts go into finding a thoracic pathology. Only 47 cases of PPF have been reported in the paediatric age group, whereas more than 300 cases of PPF have been reported among adults.4

Case presentation

A child came to me in the outpatient department accompanied by his mother with complaints of difficulty in breathing and right-sided chest pain for 1 week. As per the mother, the distress had increased progressively over 7 days. There was no history of any consanguinity. Mother gave a history of Koch’s contact with his grandfather having pulmonary Kochs for which he was treated appropriately. The child had a dietary history notable for frequent consumption of processed and high-calorie foods, with limited intake of fresh fruits, vegetables and balanced home-cooked meals. His weight was 44 kg, with a height of 112 cm and body mass index of 35. On examination, the child had a respiratory rate of 20 /min with mild tachypnoea. The child had reduced chest movements on the right. Stony dull percussion was present on the right subscapular, right infra-axillary and axillary regions. Vocal fremitus was significantly weaker on the right side than on the left. Breath sounds were decreased on the right subscapular, right infra-axillary and axillary regions. There was no pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing or lymphadenopathy. The abdomen was soft with no hepatosplenomegaly. The child was conscious and oriented to time, place and person. The abdomen was soft with no hepatosplenomegaly, Cardiovascular system examination revealed tachycardia with no murmurs. The child had oxygen saturation of 88%–89% on room air with a heart rate of 110 /min. In view of tachypnoea and respiratory distress, the child was shifted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit and was started on oxygen by mask @5 l/min. A chest X-ray was ordered and was started on empirical antibiotic (piperacillin-tazobactam) after sending blood cultures. Chest X-ray revealed right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of right-sided costophrenic angle (figure 1) (Table 2). Bedside lung ultrasound was done which revealed pleural effusion of around one litre (figure 2) (Table 2). Right-sided pleural effusion was aspirated under all aseptic precautions. Around one litre of dark red pleural fluid was aspirated. Tachypnoea settled after thoracocentesis & his oxygen saturation on room air improved to 95%–98%. Child’s chest pain improved and was off oxygen support within 24 hours. Subsequently, he was shifted to the ward the next day and discharged. Pleural fluid that was aspirated was sent for examination. The child, however, was again admitted after 6 days with similar complaints of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea and difficulty in breathing. The child was shifted to the PICU again, where the CXR again revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis was done again and around one litre of dark red coloured pleural fluid was again aspirated. Pleural fluid (Adenosine Deaminase) ADA was slightly elevated (Table 1) and the child had a positive history of Koch’s contact, thus he was started on antitubercular drugs empirically. This child, however, again got admitted for the third time after 5 days, with similar complaints of right-sided chest pain and difficulty in breathing. We again reviewed the history from the child and he told us that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from the bicycle handle 1 month back. A contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was ordered before inserting an intercostal drainage (ICD). CT was suggestive of patchy areas of air space consolidation in the right lung with right-sided hydropneumothorax and elongated lobulated multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraoesophageal level to the upper left-sided retroperitoneum till the tail of pancreas (Table 2) . An ICD was inserted, and we drained around one litre of dark red pleural fluid via the ICD (figure 3). CT findings, along with a dark red coloured pleural aspirate and history of abdominal trauma, pointed us towards the diagnosis of a PPF. MRCP was thus planned the same day, which revealed pancreatitis with tubular cystic collection seen in the distal body and tail of pancreas extending posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and then tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow communication with the left pleural cavity was also seen. The collection is seen communicating with the distal pancreatic duct with narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the communication. MRCP pointed towards the diagnosis of PPF (figures46) (table 1). Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels were also sent. Pleural fluid analysis revealed significantly elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) concentrations (table 2), supporting the diagnosis of PPF. Antitubercular drugs were stopped, and the child was put on nil per oral and total parenteral nutrition. Octreotide infusion was started in the dose of 30 µg/kg/day (10–40 µg/kg/day). Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was also started. Over ten days, fluid drainage from the ICD gradually decreased and stopped. Subsequently, the ICD was removed, and the child was discharged.

Differential diagnosis

The patient presented with pleural effusion and chest pain with a definite history of abdominal trauma 1 month back. Other than post-traumatic pancreatitis, we had ruled out other causes of pleural effusion. This child did not have any history of fever, chronic cough or weight loss. History of Koch’s contact in the family was present and pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated; however, GeneXpert for MTB was negative. Pulmonary Koch’s was thus ruled out. There were no signs of fever or any neutrophilic exudate in the pleural fluid, thus ruling out empyema. Malignancy (lung, leukaemia, lymphoma, metastatic tumours) can also have amylase-rich pleural fluid; however, absence of fever and absence of weight loss, normal blood picture and normal peripheral smear ruled out malignancy. Pleural fluid cytology also did not reveal any malignant cells. Boerhaave’s syndrome (oesophageal perforation) can also have amylase-rich pleural fluid. This condition was ruled out as the child was passing stools normally and did not have any history of chest pain or vomiting. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia was ruled out as abdominal organs were not visible in the chest on imaging. Hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out as the liver function tests and liver imaging were normal, and the child did not have any history of chronic liver disease. Normal renal imaging and renal functions ruled out renal pathology/hydronephrosis.

Treatment

Respiratory support in the form of humidified oxygen by mask was given in the PICU. Ultrasound guided thoracocentesis was done twice for this case. Each time, around one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid was aspirated. Empirically, antitubercular drugs were started, which were stopped as soon as we confirmed our diagnosis on MRCP. On the third episode of admission, an ICD was inserted under all aseptic precautions on the right side and around one litre of blood-stained pleural fluid was again drained in the ICD bag. PPF, being a rare and uncommon entity in the paediatric population, lacks definite treatment guidelines. Treatment is based on various case reports. Conservative treatment being opted for first, the child was put nil per oral and was started on Octreotide infusion in the following dose 30 µg/kg/day (10–40 µg/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition and pancreatic enzyme replacement was started. Gradually over 10 days, output of fluid from the ICD started decreasing and finally stopped. ICD was removed and the child was thereafter discharged from the hospital.

Outcome and follow-up

After discharge from the hospital, the child has been symptom-free. He is on a regular monthly follow-up with me. There have been no new episodes of recurrent pleural effusions since discharge (S17). He has modified his lifestyle and is now on a balanced diet with regular exercise as advised to him. He has returned to his daily activities and is attending school regularly. He can do strenuous activities like running and playing without any difficulty.

Discussion

PPF is an extremely rare and infrequent complication of pancreatic disease in the paediatric population. Until now, we have only 47 documented cases of PPF in the paediatric age group.4 It can be observed in acute and chronic pancreatitis or paediatric trauma.2 Exact incidence is unknown in children; it, however, complicates around 0.4% cases of pancreatitis overall.5 6 Serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1 (SPINK1) and PRSS1 mutations feature prominently, accounting for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis.5 7 8 Blunt abdominal injury is also an important non-hereditary trigger, comprising 32% of the 25-patient cohort analysed by Yang et al6 Idiopathic cases continue to appear which may be due to probable under-recognised genetic or ductal variants.4 Pancreatic fluid can track from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses, usually the aortic or oesophageal hiatus, or less commonly via direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion, creating a fistulous connection to the pleural cavity.5 7 Most of these cases are missed as most of these cases present with thoracic symptoms, and all resources are mostly used in finding a pulmonary pathology, whereas the pathology actually lies in the abdomen. The literature search identified 21 relevant reports, in which 68% cases had predominant respiratory symptoms such as massive, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnoea and cough. Less than 20% had abdominal symptoms such as epigastric pain, vomiting and weight loss. 24% of cases had constitutional symptoms like fever.6 Case reported by Zi Yin Shang in Frontiers in Paediatrics 2023 also described a case of PPF in an 8-year-old boy who only presented with a bulging left-sided chest for 2 weeks. Cases with bulging chests as the first symptom are extremely rare. In this case, the pleural effusion likely developed progressively following abdominal trauma. Compensatory respiratory function of the unaffected lung allowed the patient to gradually adapt, resulting in the absence of typical respiratory symptoms such as cough or dyspnoea. In addition, the child suffered from bullying at school and deliberately concealed the history of abdominal trauma. This child was worked up on tuberculosis and cancer-related tests for haemorrhagic pleural effusion, but the diagnosis went in a different direction.9 In our case, the child also had recurrent pulmonary symptoms in the form of chest pain and recurrent right-sided pleural fluid collections. The history of abdominal trauma came out after repeated sessions of history from the boy and MRCP findings guided us towards a pancreatic pathology. Thus, this disease entity can have a ‘Pulmonary mask’ and can misguide the treating physician to a completely different diagnosis unless we take a detailed history and get relevant imaging. The source of accumulation of fluid in PPF is due to external forces or bacterial infection, resulting in the rupture of the pancreatic duct or the pancreatic pseudocyst and leakage of the pancreatic fluid. If the rupture occurs in the anterior part of the pancreas, pancreatic fluid will leak into the abdominal cavity, resulting in ascites. If the rupture occurs in the posterior part of the pancreas, pancreatic fluid will instead leak into the retroperitoneal space, following the path of least resistance upward, enter the mediastinum through the aorta or oesophageal hiatus, and break through the pleura to form a pleural effusion.10 11 An internal pancreatic fistula which acts like a leaky pipe, diverting pancreatic secretions into the chest. In adults’ alcohol dominates as the cause of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF. Uchiyama et al conducted the most comprehensive single-country review, documenting 113 cases from Japanese literature alone. This landmark study revealed that 99% of cases were related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, with chest symptoms predominating in 68% of patients.12 On the contrary, in children, genetic causes predominate in the causation of chronic pancreatitis. Mutations such as the PRSS1 gene, CF transmembrane regulator gene, SPINK1 gene and chymotrypsin C gene are well-known to cause hereditary pancreatitis.13 The most representative of these are mutations of R122H and N291 in exon 3 and exon 2, respectively, of the PRSS1 gene.14 If paediatric pancreatitis recurs or appears to be chronic, a thorough family history should be conducted and the possibility of hereditary pancreatitis should be considered. Diagnosis is generally delayed as most of these cases undergo extensive pulmonary workup before pancreatic origin of the disease is suspected. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF.4 6 Tuberculosis, oesophageal perforation and malignant tumours of the lung or pancreas should be excluded as these conditions also have an elevated level of pleural fluid amylase. Pancreatic type amylase in the pleural fluid distinguishes PPF from other conditions. Other conditions, as discussed above, which also have high pleural fluid amylase levels, have a salivary type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase typing can also be a part of diagnostic workup for a case of suspected PPF.15 MRCP offers 70%–80% sensitivity for visualisation of the fistula and duct anatomy.4 15 CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts or ductal dilation but depicts the actual fistula in only 33%–47%.16 Endoscopic cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning. Fistula visualisation rate in children varies from 38% to 78% via ERCP.6 17 Treatment of PPF can be divided into medical, endoscopic and surgical measures. Due to the rarity of occurrence of PPF, there are no standardised guidelines for its treatment. If there is no anomaly of the pancreatic duct, medical management is recommended initially. Bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (Octreotide), PERT and thoracic drainage are the conservative measures advocated initially. Conservative measures are to be followed for 2–3 weeks. Octreotide administration suppresses exocrine function and bowel rest allows the pancreas to rest and close the fistulous tract.2 The success rate of conservative methods is only 12% in paediatric cases.6 If conservative management fails, we resort to endoscopic methods (ERCP) for trans-papillary stenting. In the presence of ductal stenosis, an endoscopic approach can be used initially by placing a stent or balloon dilatation, due to its minimal morbidity and mortality. Stent insertion reduces intraductal pressure and leakage. Endoscopic intervention is generally known to close the fistula within 2–3 weeks in 48% of patients, and the stent usually lasts from 4 weeks to 6 months.18 ERCP had a success rate of 100% in paediatric cases,6 and a long-term success rate of >90% in mixed age cohort.19 If stent placement is not possible due to severe duct obstruction or a distally located fistula, or if there is no response to medical management for 3–4 weeks, surgery such as distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) or cystojejunostomy can be considered.6 Surgery showed a 93% success rate in 15 paediatric surgeries.6 Operative mortality is <5%, and recurrence is about 11%.6 In our case, the patient presented with a history of abdominal trauma followed by episodes of recurrent right-sided pleural effusions. Thoracocentesis revealed a dark red pleural fluid, whose amylase levels were elevated (6118 U/L). MRCP confirmed the diagnosis of PPF, which developed after abdominal trauma. ICD was inserted for drainage and medical management was started with somatostatin analogues and pancreatic enzyme replacement. The child was kept nil per orally and the drainage gradually decreased over the next 10 days. ICD was thereafter removed and the child has had no recurrent pleural collections since discharge. This case posed an important clinical question in our minds: ‘How does a pancreatic injury manifest as respiratory distress in a young boy?’ It is important to emphasise that since this disease has a pulmonary mask, these cases are easy to miss. In this case, we were looking for a pulmonary pathology and we went in a different direction, starting antitubercular treatment empirically, until we diagnosed a PPF that was the reason for recurrent effusions. Thus, early diagnosis can prevent recurrent pleural effusions and can prevent unnecessary pulmonary interventions and sepsis.

Patient’s perspective

I am the brother of this patient, and I am highly satisfied with the treatment my brother has received. My brother was admitted repeatedly with complaints of difficulty in breathing and right-sided chest pain in the PICU. On each admission, he had fluid collected in his lungs which the doctor aspirated. After the fluid was removed, my brother’s symptoms improved; however, again after 5 days fluid in the lungs got re-accumulated. Initially, the doctors thought that my brother had some lung disease and was started on empirical anti-tubercular medications; however, my doctor, seeing the peculiar red-coloured fluid from the lungs, was still sceptical. Further investigation of the lung fluid and MRCP revealed this rare diagnosis of a fistula between two organs. My brother was kept nil by mouth and was started on conservative management with oral and intravenous medications for this condition. I was told that if he does not respond to medications, he might have to undergo an endoscopic procedure or even a surgery to close the fistula. I was a bit hesitant to go for the endoscopic procedure, but I am thankful that my brother responded to the doctors’ medications and did not have to undergo any invasive procedure. My brother has been on regular follow-up with the doctor monthly and has had no re-accumulation of fluid in the lungs.

Learning points

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a very rare disease in the paediatric age group wherein pancreatic fluid, rich in digestive enzymes, erodes through tissue planes, creating a fistulous tract to the pleural cavity, leading to recurrent massive pleural effusions.
