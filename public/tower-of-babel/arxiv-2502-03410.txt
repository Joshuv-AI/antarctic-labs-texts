The Thermodynamic Cost of Ignorance: Thermal State Preparation with One Ancilla Qubit
Matthew Hagan, Nathan Wiebe

Abstract
In this work we investigate a model of thermalization wherein a single ancillary qubit randomly interacts with the system to be thermalized. This not only sheds light on the emergence of Gibbs states in nature, but also provides a routine for preparing arbitrary thermal states on a digital quantum computer. For desired β \beta and random interaction G G , which has Haar random eigenvalues and I.I.D Gaussian eigenvalues, the routine boils down to time independent Hamiltonian simulation and is represented by the channel Φ : ρ ↦ 𝔼 G ​ Tr Env ​ [ e − i ⁡ ( H + α ​ G ) ​ t ​ ( ρ ⊗ e − β ​ H E 𝒵 ) ​ e i ⁡ ( H + α ​ G ) ​ t ] \Phi:\rho\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{G}\Tr_{{\rm Env}}\left[e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}\left(\rho\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta H_{E}}}{\mathcal{Z}}\right)e^{i(H+\alpha G)t}\right] . We rigorously prove that these dynamics reduce to a Markov chain process in the weak-coupling regime with the thermal state as the approximate fixed point. This allows us to upper bound the total simulation time required as a function of the Markov chain spectral gap λ ⋆ \lambda_{\star} , which we are able to compute exactly in the ground state limit. These results are independent of any eigenvalue knowledge of the system, but we are further able to show that if one has knowledge of eigenvalue differences λ S ​ ( i ) − λ S ​ ( j ) \lambda_{S}(i)-\lambda_{S}(j) , then the total simulation time is dramatically reduced. The ratio of the complete ignorance simulation cost to the perfect knowledge simulation cost scales as O ~ ​ ( | | H S | | 7 δ min 7 ​ ϵ 3.5 ​ λ ⋆ ​ ( β ) 3.5 ) \widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{7}}{\delta_{\min}^{7}\epsilon^{3.5}\lambda_{\star}(\beta)^{3.5}}\right) , where δ min \delta_{\min} is related to the eigenvalue differences of the system. Additionally, we provide more specific results for single qubit and harmonic oscillator systems as well as numeric experiments with hydrogen chains. In addition to the algorithmic merits, these results can be viewed as broad extensions of the Repeated Interactions model in open quantum systems to generic Hamiltonians with unknown interactions, giving a complete picture of the thermalization process for quantum systems.

However attempts to naively port the classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm to quantum computers has been rather difficult due to inherent difficulties with quantum information, such as no-cloning. Initial attempts

One of the main drawbacks to the above approaches is the sheer complexity of the resulting algorithms. These algorithms tend to rely on coherently weighted sums of Heisenberg evolved jump operators and the construction of circuits to simulate the resulting Linbladians is nontrivial, as mentioned in Section 1.2 of

Another algorithm that avoids some of the drawbacks of Linbladian simulation is the thermal state preparation routine by Shtanko and Movassagh
[
26
]
. They propose two algorithms, one tailored to a specific class of Hamiltonians and another more general algorithm. The first couples the system to a large bath of ancilla qubits via random
k
-local Pauli terms but is only proven to work for input Hamiltonians satisfying the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH). The second couples the system to a bath of ancilla qubits via random circuit rotations. This second algorithm works for more generic Hamiltonians, but has much weaker guarantees on the convergence time needed and involves an extra rejection step similar, but not identical to, Temme et al
[
13

. Further, both of these algorithms have runtimes with explicit
\beta

\beta\to\infty

[
26

.

In this paper, we propose a novel thermal state preparation routine inspired by the classical Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm (
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
), which is also known as Hybrid Monte Carlo. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo resolves some of the previously mentioned issues, specifically the poor scaling with high dimensional systems, by introducing extra variables that serve as momentum and using time dynamics to generate state transitions. These extra momentum variables are sampled from their thermal distribution
\propto e^{-\beta p^{2}}
and the total system is then simulated with classical Hamiltonian dynamics to generate updates to the state. Our channel was developed out of efforts to port this philosophy of using extra variables and time dynamics to prepare thermal states over to quantum systems. The extra states that we add are a single ancilla qubit prepared in a thermal state and we simulate the system-ancilla pair with a randomized interaction term
G
, see Section
II.1
and Eq. (
7
) for more details.

We analyze the resulting channel in a weak-coupling regime and obtain a reduction of the quantum dynamics to an underlying Markov chain over the eigenstates of the system. This allows us to show the correctness of the procedure fairly easily by finding the fixed point of the Markov chain directly. We explicitly do not rely on KMS conditions, instead directly proving conditions on the fixed points that more resembles classical detailed balance, see Eq. (
63
). One potential downside to this approach is that the convergence time of our channel is now dependent on the mixing time of a classical Markov process, which is near impossible to know a priori. This is a feature of virtually all previous thermal state preparation routines and computing the spectral gaps that dictate these relaxation times is a major open problem. We are unable to compute these spectral gaps for general systems and finite
\beta
, but we resolve both open problems posed by Shtanko and Movassagh
[
26
]
by extending the range of systems that can be thermalized to all non-degenerate Hamiltonians and by computing the spectral gap of the Markov chain in the ground state,
\beta\to\infty
, limit.

Although our channel was inspired by the classical Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm, the end result bears a striking resemblence to the Repeated Interactions (RI) framework studied in open quantum systems
[
29
]
. This framework involves coupling small environments, typically a single photon prepared in a thermal state, with the system and repeating the process over and over. This is then studied in thermodynamic limits of infinite time, infinite interactions, and weak coupling, which serves as a key difference with our work as we are concerned with bounding finite resources for use in an algorithm. Our work could be viewed as an extension of the RI framework to arbitrary Hamiltonians with completely unknown interactions. This also highlights a key difference between our work and existing RI literature, as previous RI work is typically concerned with specific systems and interactions
[
30
]
. The RI framework has recently been fully worked out in the single qubit instance
[
31

where Prositto, Forbes, and Segal bound finite work and energy requirements needed for thermalization of a qubit with a single ancilla qubit. Their results are complimentary to our single qubit results in Section
III.1
. Recently there have been algorithms developed to take Linbladian simulations and convert them to RI simulations on quantum computers
[
32
]
. It remains to be seen if the previously developed Linbladian based algorithms, in particular the single qubit ground state prep algorithm by Ding et al
[
19

, resemble our results if this machinery is used to obtain the RI-equivalent algorithm.

I.1
Main Results

The remainder of the paper is split into three main parts. Section
II
contains a derivation of the weak-coupling expansion in Lemma
2
and outlines the underlying Markov chain behavior in Section
II.3
. This weak-coupling expansion is presented in as much generality as possible as it may be of use in other applications beyond our thermalization procedure, such as thermometry or spectroscopy. Section
III
has two theorems concerning single qubit systems and harmonic oscillators, Theorems
10
and
11
respectively, as well as numerics exploring the
\beta
and
\epsilon
dependence of the channel. Section
IV
contains our most general results in Theorems
12
and
13
in which we show that the thermal state is an approximate fixed point for arbitrary Hamiltonians, bound the runtime in terms of a Markovian spectral gap, and finally compute this spectral gap for the ground state limit. The main difference between these two theorems is that one requires only an uppper bound on the spectral norm
\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|
while the other takes advantage of eigenvalue knowledge. Lastly numerics on small Hydrogen chains that explore strong coupling error rates and the effects of noisy eigenvalue samples on thermalization are presented in Section
IV.3
.

One of the key aspects of our thermalization procedure is that the analysis is dependent on the ability to tune the environment gap
\gamma
to match the system energy differences. One of our main results in Theorem
12
shows that even if the user cannot tune
\gamma
at all and is reduced to uniform guessing within an interval containing all the differences
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
, then thermalization can still occur. We show that the thermal state at finite
\beta
is an approximate fixed state, with the error going to 0 as the coupling constant
\alpha\to 0
. This zero coupling limit can be taken with the opposite limit
t\to\infty
to yield a nonzero simulation time for the random interaction
G
. Further, we show that the ground state is exactly the fixed point in the
\beta\to\infty
limit. In this limit we are also able to bound the total simulation time required as
L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{16}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{7}}{\delta_{\min}^{8}\epsilon^{6}}\right)
, where
\delta_{\min}
represents a “resolution” type distance and is the smallest difference between two distinct eigenvalue differences
|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\Delta_{S}(k,l)|
. When preparing finite
\beta
thermal states we pick up an extra factor of
\frac{1}{\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{7}}
related to the spectral gap of the transition matrix.

Although Theorem
12
is likely to be the more applicable result for most scenarios, as eigenvalue knowledge is just as difficult to obtain as preparing thermal states, in Theorem
13
we are able to quantify the effects of this knowledge on the total simulation time needed by the channel. We obtain stronger results than the zero knowledge Theorem
12
: we find that the thermal state is the exact fixed point at all
\beta\in[0,\infty]
and that the amount of simulation time needed scales as
L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{16}}{\delta_{\min}\epsilon^{2.5}\lambda_{\star}^{3.5}}\right)
. This represents a reduction over the zero knowledge scenario of
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{7}}{\delta_{\min}^{7}\epsilon^{3.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{3.5}}\right)
. We remark that although these asymptotic scalings are not optimal, they are qualitatively interesting as they represent the first analysis for thermal state preparation that eigenvalue heuristics have a quantifiable impact on.

We explored this reduction in total simulation time numerically in Section
IV.3
with small Hydrogen chains of 2 or 3 nuclei. We started off by measuring the total simulation time needed to reach a fixed
\epsilon=0.05
with perfect samples of eigenvalue differences
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
. We then tracked the total simulation time as we added noise to these guesses and find that low noise guesses can reduce the total simulation time by a factor of 2. These results that indicate that even very noisy guesses can still lead to thermalization are also complimented by our study of the error convergence for hydrogen chain systems, as for these tests we chose
\gamma
from a Gaussian centered at the average energy
\Tr\left[H_{S}\right]/\dim_{S}
with a standard deviation of
\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|/2
. Even with this heuristic choice for
\gamma
we can achieve fairly low errors of
\epsilon=0.03
in as little as
L=1500
interactions.

Weak Interaction Expansion for

II.1
Preliminaries and Notation

We will be working with a bipartite Hilbert space consisting of a system space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
with dynamics governed by the Hamiltonian
H_{S}
and an environment space
\mathcal{H}_{E}
with Hamiltonian
H_{E}
. The total space is
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
with Hamiltonian
H=H_{S}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}+\mathds{1}_{E}\otimes H_{E}=H_{S}+H_{E}
. We will assume without loss of generality that our spaces are encoded in qubits so that
\mathcal{H}_{S}=\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{E}=\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}}
. We use
\dim_{S}
to refer to the dimension of the system’s Hilbert space (
2^{n}
),
\dim_{E}
the environment, and
\dim
the total Hilbert space. As for the basis we will use for our spaces, we will work directly in the eigenbasis of each Hamiltonian. Besides simplifying our calculations, we can do so because the interaction term we will introduce later is unitarily invariant. We denote these basis in a 1-indexed fashion as

H_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\lambda_{S}(i)|i\rangle\!\langle i|~,~H_{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{m}}\lambda_{E}(j)|j\rangle\!\langle j|~,~H=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{m}}\lambda(i,j)|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|,

(1)

where
\lambda(i,j)=\lambda_{S}(i)+\lambda_{E}(j)
and we will sort the eigenvalues in nondecreasing order such that
i>j\implies\lambda_{S}(i)\geq\lambda_{S}(j)
. We note that the ground state in our 1-indexed notation is therefore
|1\rangle\!\langle 1|
. We also make use of the following notation for the energy differences of the system-environment Hamiltonian and just the system

\Delta(i,j|k,l)\coloneqq\lambda(i,j)-\lambda(k,l),\quad\Delta_{S}(i,i^{\prime})=\lambda_{S}(i)-\lambda_{S}(i^{\prime}),

(2)

and because our eigenvalues are sorted
i>j\implies\Delta_{S}(i,j)\geq 0
. We will need a few other notations for eigenvalue differences. First we denote the degeneracy of an eigenvalue
\lambda(i,j)
using
\eta(i,j)
and the number of times a system eigenvalue
difference
is present as
\eta_{\Delta}(i,j)
. For example, in a truncated harmonic oscillator with 4 energy levels the lowest gap
\Delta
is present 3 times, so
\eta_{\Delta}(1,2)=3
. The second is that we will need to eventually analyze interferences between eigenvalue differences of the system, so we define

\delta_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{\Delta_{S}(i,j)\neq\Delta_{S}(k,l)}\left|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\Delta_{S}(k,l)\right|.

(3)

Note that nothing in this definition prevents one of the summands, say
\Delta_{S}(k,l)
, from being 0. This implies that
\delta_{\min}\leq\Delta_{S}(i,j)
for all
i
and
j
.

Currently our dynamics involved a system separated from the environment, so we need to fix this by adding an interaction term
G:\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}\to\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
. We will choose
G
randomly via the eigendecomposition

G=U_{\text{Haar}}DU_{\text{Haar}}^{\dagger},U_{\text{Haar}}\sim{\rm Haar}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})\text{ and }D_{ii}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(4)

where the eigenvectors are Haar distributed and the eigenvalues I.I.D. normal Gaussian variables. We then add this random interaction term to our system-environment dynamics with a coupling constant
\alpha
, yielding a total dynamics governed by
H_{S}+H_{E}+\alpha G
. We define the following rescaled coupling constant

\widetilde{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{\alpha t}{\sqrt{\dim+1}},

(5)

where the
\dim
is the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
dimension. The rescaling with respect to
\dim
is to capture the factors of
1/(\dim+1)
in the transition amplitudes that appear later and leads to much more compact expressions.
This gives a decomposition of expectation values over
G
into two parts

\mathbb{E}_{G}f(G)=\mathbb{E}_{\text{Haar}}\mathbb{E}_{D}f(G),

(6)

where the two expectations on the right commute with each other
\mathbb{E}_{\text{Haar}}\mathbb{E}_{D}=\mathbb{E}_{D}\mathbb{E}_{\text{Haar}}
.

We will use this interaction term to couple our system to an environment prepared in the thermal state
\rho_{E}(\beta)=e^{-\beta H_{E}}/\mathcal{Z}_{E}(\beta)
, where
\mathcal{Z}_{E}(\beta)=\Tr\left[e^{-\beta H_{E}}\right]
, and then trace out the environment. This gives the definition of our thermalizing channel
\Phi:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
as

\Phi(\rho;\alpha,\beta,t):=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[e^{+i(H+\alpha G)t}\rho\otimes\rho_{E}(\beta)e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}\right].

(7)

We will typically drop the implicit parameters of
\alpha,\beta
and
t
. Our goal is to show how this channel can be used to prepare the system in the thermal state
\rho(\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{S}}}{\mathcal{Z}(\beta)}
. It will be useful to introduce a fixed-interaction channel
\Phi_{G}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
over the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as

\Phi_{G}(\rho\otimes\rho_{E};\alpha,t)\coloneqq e^{+i(H+\alpha t)}\rho\otimes\rho_{E}e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t},

(8)

giving us
\Phi(\rho;\alpha,\beta,t)=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}\mathbb{E}_{G}\Phi_{G}(\rho\otimes\rho_{E}(\beta);\alpha,t)
. Another alternative notation for
\Phi
that we will use is whenever
\mathcal{H}_{E}
is a single qubit with energy gap
\gamma
we will use
\Phi_{\gamma}
to draw attention to this specific energy gap. We will also make frequent use of indicator functions, denoted
\mathbf{I}[P]
, which is 1 if the predicate
P
is true and 0 if
P
is false.

II.2
First and Second Order Terms

In order to understand our thermalizing channel
\Phi
we will compute a Taylor Series for the output of the channel with respect to the coupling constant
\alpha
. We will perform the
\alpha

\alpha=0
and we will use the mean value form of the remainder, in which we are guaranteed a special value
\alpha_{\star}\in(0,\infty)
such that the final derivative evaluated at

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\rho;0)+\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Phi(\rho;\alpha)\big|_{\alpha=0}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi(\rho;\alpha)\big|_{\alpha=0}+R_{\Phi}(\rho;\alpha_{\star})

\mathcal{T}(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi(\rho;\alpha)\bigg|_{\alpha=0}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi_{G}(\rho;\alpha)\big|_{\alpha=0}\right]

(10)

to denote the transition terms, as it will be revealed that the first two terms do not cause transitions in the system state, and

Further we will often leave the dependence on the

We start off with the

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho;0)=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}\int e^{i(H+\alpha G)t}\rho\otimes\rho_{E}(\beta)e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}dG\bigg|_{\alpha=0}=e^{iHt}\rho e^{-iHt}.

(11)

We then see that if

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi_{G}(|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|)\big|_{\alpha=0}\right]

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(\sum_{(a,b)\neq(i,j)}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right)\right)|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{(a,b)\neq(i,j)}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right)|a,b\rangle\!\langle a,b|,

(21)

which also demonstrates that
\Tr\mathcal{T}(\rho)=0
for
\rho
such that
[\rho,H_{S}]=0
.

The proof of this Lemma uses similar techniques to Theorem
1
, and formal proof can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

Next we will compute the effects of the channel on just the system alone which involves computing the partial trace
\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}
. We can either do this for a generic environment, which results in summations over
\mathcal{H}_{E}
floating around, or specialize to a specific choice of
\mathcal{H}_{E}
and compute the summation. For the remainder of this paper we will choose the latter option with a single qubit environment
\mathcal{H}_{E}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and denote the Hamiltonian
H_{E}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\gamma\end{bmatrix}
. Our environment input states then become

\rho_{E}(\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{E}}}{\mathcal{Z}_{E}(\beta)}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\eqqcolon q(0)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+q(1)|1\rangle\!\langle 1|,

(22)

where we will use the environment qubit probabilities
q(0)
and
q(1)
in calculations for brevity. It will turn out that the value chosen for
\gamma
is highly critical to the convergence of our algorithm, tuning it to match eigenvalue
differences
of the system
H_{S}
will allow us to analyze the convergence of the algorithm. As we can see in Eq. (
20
) there will be a lot of
\sinc
functions used, we will characterize a
\sinc
function as being on-resonance or off-resonance if the inputs are sufficiently close to zero (the max for sinc). As for how close “sufficiently close” actually is will depend on various parameters, such as
t,\alpha,\epsilon
, and the spectral properties of
H_{S}
.

Theorem 3

(Second-Order Expansion Term
\mathcal{T}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the second-order correction for a weak coupling expansion for a thermalizing channel
\Phi
with a single qubit environment. Let 0 and
\gamma
be the eigenvalues of the environment hamiltonian
H_{E}
and the state of the environment be given by
\rho_{E}(\beta)=e^{-\beta H_{E}}/\mathcal{Z}_{E}(\beta)
. The following properties hold for the second order correction terms.

1.

The transition element from system state
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
to
|j\rangle\!\langle j|
, for
i\neq j
, is given by

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\Biggr(

\displaystyle\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)\Biggr).

(23)

2.

For same-state transitions
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
to
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
we have

\langle i|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle=-\sum_{j\neq i}\langle j|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle,

(24)

which follows from
\Tr\mathcal{T}(\rho)=0
as shown in Lemma
2
.

3.

There are no coherences, or off-diagonal density matrix elements, introduced to the system up to second order in
\alpha

j\neq k\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle=0.

(25)

In the proof of the theorem, as well as in subsequent propositions, it is useful to introduce the concept of on- and off-resonant couplings which we give below.

Definition 4

(Definition of Transition Matrix Elements)
.

The transition elements in Eq. (
23
) can be divided into on-resonance and off-resonance terms based on the arguments to the sinc function. We define the on-resonance transitions as

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(26)

and the off-resonance terms as

\begin{split}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle&\coloneqq\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|>\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)\\
&~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|>\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)\\
&~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right).\end{split}

(27)

For same-state transitions
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
to
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
the on- and off-resonance transitions are equal to

\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle=-\sum_{j\neq i}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle\text{ and }\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle=-\sum_{j\neq i}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle.

(28)

We will now use these definitions to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

The bulk of this proof will be based on straightforward reductions from Eq. (
20
). To start we will first show that no off-diagonal elements are introduced to the density matrix. By taking the
(j,k)
matrix element of the output from Eq. (
20
) we see

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{E}(m)}}{1+e^{-\beta\lambda_{E}(m)}}\langle j,l|\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi_{G}(|i,m\rangle\!\langle i,m|)\big|_{\alpha=0}\right]|k,l\rangle

(29)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l,m}q(m)\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}(\chi(i,m)+\chi(i,m)^{*}+t^{2}\eta(i,m))\langle j,l|i,m\rangle\langle i,m|k,l\rangle

\displaystyle~+\sum_{l,m}q(m)\sum_{a,b}\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,m|a,b)t}{2}\right)\langle j,l|a,b\rangle\langle a,b|k,l\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle=0,

(31)

where we introduce
q(m)
for
m=0,1
to be a placeholder for the prefactors in Eq. (
20
) and the last equality is due to the fact that
j\neq k
implies that
\langle j,l|i,m\rangle
and
\langle i,m|k,l\rangle
cannot both be nonzero and likewise for
\langle j,l|a,b\rangle
and
\langle a,b|k,l\rangle
.

Since we have shown that coherences are not introduced to our system we can focus on the transitions from diagonal entries to diagonal entries in
\rho
. We make heavy use of Eq. (
21
) which tells us that for
i\neq k
the system-environment transition amplitude is

\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle k,l|\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi_{G}(|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|)\big|_{\alpha=0}\right]|k,l\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|k,l)t}{2}\right).

(32)

Now because all the operations present in the above expression are linear we can compute this map for the initial environment state
\rho_{E}(\beta)
straightforwardly. Taking the output of this linear combination and computing the trace over the environment then gives us the expression for
\mathcal{T}
using the assumption that the environment is a single qubit we find using the definition of
\gamma
and
\Delta_{S}
in (
2
)

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}q(k)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\langle j,l|\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi_{G}(|i,k\rangle\!\langle i,k|)\big|_{\alpha=0}\right]|j,l\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{k,l}q(k)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,k|j,l)t}{2}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,0|j,0)t}{2}\right)+q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,0|j,1)t}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,1|j,0)t}{2}\right)+q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,1|j,1)t}{2}\right)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)+q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)+q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)\right),

(36)

where we see that combining the terms with
\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)
, as
q(0)+q(1)=1
, we immediately get Eq. (
23
).

To classify these terms as on-resonance or off-resonance we will focus on the argument to the sinc function, which is of the form
\Delta_{S}(i,j)t/2
or
(\Delta_{S}(i,j)\pm\gamma)t/2
. The idea is that we will take
t
large enough so that only the energy differences that are less than
\delta_{\min}
, as defined in Eq. (
3
), will be non-negligible. Clearly the term
\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)
will always be off-resonance, as
\delta_{\min}\leq\Delta_{S}(i,j)
.

Now we have three terms to classify as either on-resonance or off-resonance, we refer to each term by their argument to the
\sinc
function. The first we can categorically declare as being off-resonance is the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
term. By Lemma
14
we know
\sinc^{2}(\Delta_{S}(i,j)t/2)\leq 4/(\delta_{\min}^{2}t^{2})
, which we will make arbitrarily small in later sections. The other two can only be classified as on or off resonance depending if
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
is positive or negative. If
i>j
then we know that
\Delta_{S}(i,j)\geq 0
and therefore
\sinc^{2}((\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t/2)
term can be close to 1 if
\gamma\approx\Delta_{S}(i,j)
, which also shows the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma
term is off-resonance for all
\gamma
. We say that the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma
term in this scenario is on-resonance if
|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}
. This classification is best described symbolically as

i>j\text{ and }|\Delta(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right).

(37)

The
q(0)
prefactor indicates that the ancilla started in it’s low energy state and since
\sinc^{2}
is symmetric we can write the argument as
\gamma-\Delta_{S}(i,j)
which can be remembered as the ancilla gaining
\gamma
amount of energy and the system losing
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
. In this scenario the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma
term is therefore put in the off-resonance map

\displaystyle i>j\text{ and }|\Delta(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)+q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)\right).

(38)

Now for
i<j
we find that the on-resonance term is

i<j\text{ and }|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}q(1)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right).

(39)

Similarly to before the
q(1)
prefactor tells us the ancilla starts in the excited state. This matches with the energy argument by noting that
\Delta_{S}(i,j)\leq 0
and that the argument to
\sinc
is symmetric, which allows us to write it as
|\Delta_{S}(i,j)|-\gamma
; indicating that the system gains energy
|\Delta_{S}(i,j)|
and the ancilla energy drops by
-\gamma
. In this scenario the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma
term is off-resonance and we have

\displaystyle i<j\text{ and }|\Delta(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\left(\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{S}(i,j)t}{2}\right)+q(0)\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)\right).

(40)

Now to compute the
i=j
case, it is sufficient to utilize our results from the
i\neq j
scenario. This is because our second order correction has zero trace
\Tr\left[\mathcal{T}(\rho)\right]=0
from Theorem
3
, so we can define the on-resonance and off-resonance terms as the following

\displaystyle\langle i|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{k\neq i}\langle k|\mathcal{T}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{k\neq i}\langle k|\left(\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)+\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)\right)|k\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle+\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle.

(43)

By plugging in Eqs. (
37
) and (
39
) we are done with the self-transition terms.
∎

II.3
Markovian Dynamics and Error Terms

Now that we have fully computed the significant contributors to the output of our channel
\Phi
, we move on to characterize the behavior of the channel as a Markov chain with noise.
A Markov chain is a random process that involves a walker transitioning to vertices on a graph wherein the probability of transition does not depend on the history of the walker. Specifically, in this context we view the vertices in this graph as the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian. The repeated interaction model because of the lack of coherences in the weak coupling limit can be interpreted as a Markov process over these eigenstates with transitions probabilities given by the above analysis.

Specifically, the Markov chain is dictated by the
\Phi(\rho;0)
and
\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}
terms in the weak-coupling expansion, for
[\rho,H_{S}]=0
we showed that
\Phi(\rho;0)=\mathds{1}(\rho)
, so from now on we will specifically only deal with such density matrices and characterize the zeroth order term as an identity map. As for the Markov chain, we will use normal font to denote matrices, such as
I
for the identity matrix and
T
for the transition term added on. We use
e_{i}
to denote the basis vector associated with the quantum state
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
and
p
to denote the probability vector for
\rho
associated with its eigenvalues.

Lemma 5

(Quantum Dynamics to Classical Markov Chain)
.

Let
T
be the matrix defined by

e_{i}^{T}Te_{j}\coloneqq\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|i\rangle.

(44)

The matrix
I+T
is a column stochastic matrix and models the Markovian dynamics of our thermalizing channel up to
O\left(\alpha^{2}t^{2}\right)
,

\langle j|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=e_{j}^{T}(I+T)^{L}e_{i}.

(45)

By linearity of
\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}
this identity extends to any diagonal density matrix input
\rho=\sum_{i}p(i)|i\rangle\!\langle i|
.

Proof.

We prove this inductively on
L
. The base case of
L=1
is trivial from the defintion of
T

\displaystyle\langle j|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=\delta_{i,j}+\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=e_{j}^{T}(I+T)e_{i}.

(46)

For the inductive step we will rely on the fact that there are no off-diagonal elements for diagonal inputs.

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle\implies\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle.

(47)

This is again by induction where the case
L=1
is proved in Theorem
3
and the inductive step is

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|k\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)\right)|k\rangle

(48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(|m\rangle\!\langle m|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|n\rangle\!\langle n|\right)|k\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\delta_{m,n}\langle m|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|m\rangle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(|m\rangle\!\langle m|\right)|k\rangle

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\langle m|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|m\rangle\delta_{j,k}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(|m\rangle\!\langle m|\right)|j\rangle

(51)

\displaystyle=\delta_{j,k}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle.

(52)

This argument points the way towards how we will prove the inductive step in our stochastic conversion, starting with

\displaystyle\langle j|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle j|\left((\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\circ(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)\right)|j\rangle

(53)

\displaystyle=e_{j}^{T}(\mathds{1}+T)^{L-1}e_{i}+\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\circ(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle.

(54)

We can use the inductive hypothesis on the term on the left and we now have to break down the
\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}
term.

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\circ(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T})^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(|m\rangle\!\langle m|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L-1}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|n\rangle\!\langle n|\right)|j\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}\left(|m\rangle\!\langle m|\right)|j\rangle e_{m}^{T}(I+T)^{L-1}e_{i}

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}e_{j}^{T}Te_{m}e_{m}^{T}(I+T)^{L-1}e_{i}

(57)

\displaystyle=e_{j}^{T}T(I+T)^{L-1}e_{i}.

(58)

Substituting this into Eq. (
54
) yields

\langle j|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=e_{j}^{T}(I+T)^{L}e_{i}.

(59)

Our final step in the proof is to show that
I+T
is column-stochastic. This is straightforward from our definition of
T

\sum_{i}e_{i}^{T}(I+T)e_{j}=1+\sum_{i}\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|i\rangle.

(60)

Now we use the fact that
\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|j\rangle=-\sum_{i\neq j}\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|i\rangle
from Eq. (
28
) to conclude that
I+T
is column stochastic.
∎

Since we will be effectively reducing our quantum dynamics to classical dynamics over the eigenbasis for
H_{S}
we will need bounds on the convergence of Markov chains. This is a very deep area of research, with many decades of results, so we point interested readers to the comprehensive book by Levin and Peres
[
33
]
. As we will be dealing with non-reversible Markov chains we unfortunately cannot use the relatively well-developed theory for reversible Markov chains. Luckily, we will only need the following theorem due to Jerison.

Theorem 6
.

(Jerison’s Markov Relaxation Time
[
34
]
)
Let
M:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an ergodic Markov transition matrix acting on an
N
dimensional state space with absolute spectral gap
\lambda_{\star}\coloneqq 1-\max_{i>1}|\lambda_{i}(M)|
, where the eigenvalues of
M
are ordered
1=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{N}\geq-1
. Given this gap, if the number of steps
L
in the Markov chain satisfies the following bound

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N}{\lambda_{\star}}\left(2\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}}+4(1+\log 2)+\frac{1}{N}(2\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)-1)\right)\eqqcolon\frac{N}{\lambda_{\star}}J,

(61)

where
J
is the collection of logarithmic and constant terms that we will typically ignore in asymptotic notation, then the resulting state
M^{L}\vec{x}
is
\epsilon
close to the fixed point

\forall\vec{x}\text{ s.t. }x_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{i}x_{i}=1,\quad\left|\left|\vec{\pi}-M^{L}\vec{x}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(62)

We use
\vec{\pi}
to denote the unique eigenvector of eigenvalue 1 for
M
.

Now that we have an idea of how long it takes for our Markov chain to converge to the fixed points we need to show which states are actually fixed points. We demonstrate that for finite
\beta
any fixed point must satisfy a summation of detailed-balance terms. This fixed point is unique if the Markov chain is ergodic, which we do not argue in this lemma as an arbitrary thermalization channel
\Phi
may not be ergodic. For the ground state limit of
\beta\to\infty
we show that the Markov matrix
I+T
is upper triangular, which is crucial to our analysis of the spectral gap of the Markov chain in later results. We also demonstrate that the ground state is a fixed point in this limit nearly trivially.

Lemma 7

(Markov Chain Fixed Points)
.

Let
T
be the transition matrix with sum zero columns
\sum_{j}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}
for all
i
, negative diagonal entries
e_{i}^{T}Te_{i}\leq 0
, and off-diagonals smaller than 1
e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}\leq 1
for
j\neq i
, associated with the on-resonance term
\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}
of an arbitrary thermalizing channel
\Phi
. A vector
\vec{p}
is a fixed point of the Markovian dynamics
I+T
if and only if it is in the kernel of
T
. This holds for finite
\beta
if the following is satisfied for all
j

\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}Te_{j}=0.

(63)

In the
\beta\to\infty
limit the ground state
e_{1}
is a fixed point and
T
is upper triangular

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}(I+T)e_{1}=e_{1}\text{ and }i>j\implies\lim_{\beta\to\infty}e_{i}^{T}Te_{j}=0.

(64)

Proof.

To show that the thermal state is the fixed point of the zero knowledge thermalizing channel we need to show that

T\vec{p}_{\beta}=0
and that the Markov chain is ergodic. Ergodicity will be easy to prove so we focus on showing that
T\vec{p}_{\beta}=0
. This condition can be expressed as

e_{j}^{T}T\vec{p}_{\beta}=\sum_{i}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}=0.

(65)

We can make a quick substitution as we know the diagonal elements must equal the sum of the remainder of the column

e_{i}^{T}Te_{i}=-\sum_{j\neq i}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i},

(66)

which we can then pull out the
i=j
term from the sum in Eq. (
65
)

\displaystyle e_{j}^{T}T\vec{p}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\sum_{k\neq j}e_{k}^{T}Te_{j},

(67)

which is 0 if and only if
\vec{p}_{\beta}
is a fixed point of
I+T
.

We now show the
\beta\to\infty
case. We can show that
T
is upper triangular using Theorem
3
which gives us the on-resonance transition amplitude. We assume
i<j
, which implies
\Delta_{S}(i,j)\leq 0
, and get

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}e_{j}^{T}Te_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

(68)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\left[\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)\right]

(69)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}

(70)

\displaystyle=0.

(71)

This further shows that the ground state is a fixed point, as every other eigenvector must have higher energy and therefore all on-resonance transitions
from
the ground state must be 0

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}e_{1}^{T}Te_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\langle 1|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|1\rangle\!\langle 1|)|1\rangle=-\sum_{j>1}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|1\rangle\!\langle 1|)|j\rangle=0.

(72)

This then shows that the ground state is fixed

(I+T)e_{1}=e_{1},

(73)

and completes the proof.
∎

Using the decomposition from Theorem
3
and intermediate expressions in its proof we can now show why the off-resonance map
\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}
is named “off-resonance”; even in the worst case scenario of choosing a bad value of
\gamma
such that all terms in
\mathcal{T}
end up in
\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}
the trace norm of its output is always controllably small via
\alpha
.

Corollary 8
.

The induced trace norm of the off-resonance map
\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(\rho)
, for all density matrices
\rho
such that
[\rho,H_{S}]=0
and
\rm{dim}\geq 2
, is upper bounded for all choices of the environment Hamiltonian
\gamma
by

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\leq\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}

(74)

Proof.

This result follows from applying bounds on the sinc function from Lemma
14
(given in Appendix
A.1
) to the worst-case scenario off-resonance terms given in Eq. (
27
).

\displaystyle i\neq j\implies\left|\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{4}{\delta_{\min}^{2}t^{2}}\left(1+q(0)+q(1)\right)=\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}(\dim+1)}.

(75)

This allows us to bound the off-resonance self-transition term in Theorem
3
as

\displaystyle\left|\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-\sum_{j\neq i}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle\right|\leq(\dim_{S}-1)\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}(\dim+1)}\leq\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}.

(76)

Now we can use this, along with our no off-diagonal output elements of
\mathcal{T}
, to compute the trace norm of the off-resonance map

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left|\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(\rho)|j\rangle\right|

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i,i}\left|\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle\right|

(78)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\rho_{i,i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}|\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle|+|\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle|\right)

(79)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\rho_{i,i}\left((\dim_{S}-1)\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}(\dim+1)}+\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}.

(81)

∎

The last result in this section that we will need is a bound on the trace norm of the remainder term, which we state in the following theorem.

Theorem 9

(Remainder Bound)
.

Let
R_{\Phi}(\rho)
be the remainder term for the second-order Taylor series expansion
of the quantum channel
\Phi
acting on an input state
\rho
about
\alpha=0
defined via

\Phi(\rho;\alpha)=\Phi(\rho;0)+\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\Phi(\rho;\alpha)\big|_{\alpha=0}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\Phi(\rho;\alpha)\big|_{\alpha=0}+R_{\Phi}(\rho;\alpha_{\star})

where the Schätten 1-norm of the remainder operator is bounded above by

\left|\left|R_{\Phi}(\rho;\alpha)\right|\right|_{1}\leq\frac{16\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\dim_{S}(\alpha t)^{3}.

(82)

The proof of the remainder bound follows from the triangle inequality and remainder bounds on Taylor series and is given in Section
A.3
.

III
Specific Systems

In this section we analyze the dynamics of the weak-coupling expansion from Section
II
as a thermal state preparation routine for specific systems. We keep these specific systems separate from the generic results in Section
IV
for a few reasons. The first being that these systems are simple enough that most readers should have a solid grasp of their spectrums. The second being that they are interesting in their own right and may be of use for researchers in other fields than quantum algorithms design who are looking to implement thermal state preparation for their projects. For example, even the single qubit scenario in Section
III.1
is already complex enough that there is active research on this Hamiltonian in the field of open quantum systems
[
31
]
. Lastly, these scenarios are simple enough that they allow for concrete computation of their spectra, which can be a helpful warm-up for the more abstract settings later.

For a single qubit we are able to give asymptotic runtime bounds for preparing arbitrary
\beta
thermal states with specific values of
\alpha
and
t
to guarantee a trace distance error of
\epsilon
from the thermal state. Further, our results hold without exact knowledge of the system energy gap
\Delta
. We do require knowledge of a window of width
2\sigma
that contains
\Delta
, and our runtime is parametrized in terms of
\sigma
with a simple expression in the exact knowledge limit of
\sigma\to 0
.

After we develop the theory for a single qubit we extend our sights to a truncated harmonic oscillator in Section
III.2
. This section can be viewed as a warmup to the proofs that work for general systems in Section
IV
. One unique aspect of the harmonic oscillator is that all of the energy eigenvalues are integer multiples of single difference
\Delta
, which means that by tuning our environment gap
\gamma
to
\Delta
we should be able to thermalize. One difficulty with this however is that ergodicity is now a bit harder to show, as with randomly choosing
\gamma
there is always a nonzero probability to transition between arbitrary states
i
and
j
but with the harmonic oscillator multiple “hops” may be necessary if the states are not adjacent in energy eigenvalues.

Lastly, another reason to study these two specific systems is that they serve as crucial test beds for numerical studies. As our analysis tends to work with upper bounds to obtain realistic estimates of total simulation times we need to simulate the channel numerically. Although we do study more realistic systems numerically in Section
IV.3
, larger systems are more costly in terms of classical computation needed to simulate. Because of the reduction in classical compute time needed, we are able to get a more complete view of our channel through the lens of the small systems in Section
III.3
.

III.1
Single Qubit

The first system we study is the qubit
\mathcal{H}_{S}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
. This system is simple enough that we can explicitly write the dynamics as a
2\times 2
transition matrix, which makes it easy to compute required simulation times and easy for the reader to follow. Although this system could be viewed as a warmup to the more general systems in Section
IV
, as the proof techniques are very similar, we remark that this system does have some unique properties. The biggest difference is that we do not assume any kind of belief distribution of the eigenvalue gap
\Delta
of the system. We only require that a window of width
2\sigma
is known that contains
\Delta
. We can then characterize the runtime in terms of
\sigma
and in addition to determining runtime we find it determines an upper bound on the
\beta
that can be prepared at low error.

The other unique phenomenon with the single qubit scenario is that the total simulation time needed is
independent
of
\beta
. Although this may seem incorrect, as most existing thermal state preparation algorithms tend to scale at least linearly with
\beta
, this is in fact a property of the underlying Markov chain. The rate of convergence of the Markov chain is dictated by the spectral gap, which for this system is shown to be
\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}
. The only aspect of the Markov chain that changes with
\beta
is what the fixed point is and the Markov Relaxation Theorem
6
provides relaxation guarantees regardless of initial or final state.

Theorem 10
.

Let
H_{S}
be an arbitrary single qubit Hamiltonian with eigenvalue gap
\Delta
,
\rho
any input state diagonal in the
H_{S}
basis,
[\rho,H_{S}]=0
, and
L
the number of interactions simulated. Given a window of width
2\sigma
that is promised to contain
\Delta
and satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\sigma\leq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{2\beta},\Delta\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right\},

(83)

then the following parameter choices

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t^{3}(\Delta+\sigma)^{2}},

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\in\frac{1}{\sigma}\left[\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}},\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}}\right],

\displaystyle\text{and }L

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{10}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}\left(2\log\frac{5}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}\sinc^{2}(|\Delta-\gamma|t/2)}+4(1+\log 2)-\frac{1}{2}+\log\frac{2}{\epsilon}\right)\right\rceil

(84)

are sufficient to guarantee thermalization of the form
\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
. In the limit as
\sigma\to 0
, the total simulation time required for all
L
interactions required scales as

L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\epsilon^{2.5}}\right).

(85)

Proof.

The proof will be structured into three parts. First, we will need a bound on how close the fixed point of the Markov chain is to the thermal state, because the fixed point is exactly the thermal state only when
\gamma=\Delta
and our window of width
\sigma
is sufficiently small given our error budget. Second, once we have these bounds we then need to determine the number of interactions
L
that will be necessary to reach the fixed point within trace distance
\epsilon
. Lastly, we use this value of
L
to bound the accumulative error from the off-resonance mapping
\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}
and remainder term
R_{\Phi}
.

We start by breaking down the trace distance into three components, one for the fixed-point distance from the thermal state, one for the Markov dynamics distance to the fixed-point, and lastly the remainder terms

\displaystyle\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)-\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)-\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)-(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(\rho)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)-\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)-(\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on})^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}+L\left(\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1}\right).

(88)

We proceed with the leftmost term first. The trace distance can be computed explicitly for a single qubit state as

\displaystyle\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)-\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle-\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|1\rangle\right|+\left|\langle 2|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|2\rangle-\langle 2|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|2\rangle\right|

(89)

\displaystyle=\left|\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle-\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|1\rangle\right|+\left|1-\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle-1+\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|1\rangle\right|

(90)

\displaystyle=2\left|\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle-\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|1\rangle\right|.

(91)

Now we expand
\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle
about
\gamma=\Delta

\displaystyle\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\gamma)|1\rangle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\Delta}}+(\gamma-\Delta)\beta\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}

(92)

\displaystyle=\langle 1|\rho_{S}(\beta;\Delta)|1\rangle+(\gamma-\Delta)\beta\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}},

(93)

where
\gamma_{\star}
denotes the special value of
\gamma
that is guaranteed to make the above equation hold by Taylor’s Remainder Theorem.
Since the rightmost factors can be upper bounded by
\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma_{\star}}}\leq 1
, this can be rearranged and plugged into Eq. (
91
) to give the upper bound

\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta,\gamma)-\rho_{S}(\beta,\Delta)\right|\right|_{1}\leq 2\beta|\Delta-\gamma|.

(94)

Since we require this distance to be less than
\epsilon
, we can upper bound
|\Delta-\gamma|\leq\sigma
and require

\sigma\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\beta}.

(95)

Now we move on to the second stage of the proof: computing the number of interactions needed to reach the fixed point of the Markov chain. As the Markov transition matrix is only
2\times 2
we will compute it explicitly. To do so, we need the matrix elements for
T
, which can be computed using Theorem
3

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{1}^{T}T\vec{e}_{1}=\langle 1|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|1\rangle\!\langle 1|)|1\rangle

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(-\Delta+\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(96)

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{2}^{T}T\vec{e}_{1}=\langle 2|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|1\rangle\!\langle 1|)|2\rangle

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(-\Delta+\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(97)

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{1}^{T}T\vec{e}_{2}=\langle 1|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|2\rangle\!\langle 2|)|1\rangle

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta-\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(98)

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{2}^{T}T\vec{e}_{2}=\langle 2|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|2\rangle\!\langle 2|)|2\rangle

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta-\gamma)t}{2}\right).

(99)

This gives us the total Markov chain matrix as

\displaystyle\mathds{1}+T

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta-\gamma)t}{2}\right)\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\begin{bmatrix}-e^{-\beta\gamma}&1\\
e^{-\beta\gamma}&-1\end{bmatrix},

(100)

where it can be seen that
the solution of

(\mathds{1}+T)\vec{p}_{\beta,\gamma}=\vec{p}_{\beta,\gamma},

(101)

is

\vec{p}_{\beta,\gamma}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\vec{e}_{1}+\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\vec{e}_{2}

(102)

In other words,
\vec{p}_{\beta,\gamma}
is the fixed point of the Markov chain. To show convergence we will need the spectral gap of Eq. (
100
), which is given as
\lambda_{\star}=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta-\gamma)t}{2}\right)
. Plugging this in to the Markov Relaxation Theorem
6
we can compute a lower bound on the number of interactions needed

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}(|\Delta-\gamma|t/2)}\left(2\log\frac{1}{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}(|\Delta-\gamma|t/2)}+4(1+\log 2)-\frac{1}{2}+\log\frac{2}{\epsilon}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\frac{2}{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sinc^{2}(|\Delta-\gamma|t/2)}J,

(104)

where
J
captures the logarithmic factors. We then choose
L
to satisfy this lower bound.

Our next goal is to simplify these bounds so that we can propagate them to our final error requirements. We first use Lemma
14
to produce a bound on
\sinc
whenever
\gamma
is within our window and
|\Delta-\gamma|\leq\sigma

\displaystyle\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{|\Delta-\gamma|t}{2}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2},

(105)

provided that
t\sigma\leq\sqrt{2}
to make the bound meaningful. Recalling that the dimension of the system is
4
, we can then create a new lower bound for
L
by plugging this expression for sinc in to to Eq. (
104
) and use the definition of
\tilde{\alpha}
to get the following sufficient condition on
L

L\geq\frac{10}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}J

(106)

which is larger than the lower bound in (
104
). If we choose
L
to be twice this lower bound then we will for sure meet the Markov chain error.

The third stage of the proof utilizes the above bound on
L
to bound the off-resonance and remainder terms. The magnitude of the total off-resonance contribution is
L\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\frac{8}{\Delta^{2}}
, given by Corollary
8
, and the remainder term is
L\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1}\leq 32\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\alpha t^{3}
, from Theorem
9
.
set
\alpha=\frac{1}{t^{3}(\Delta+\sigma)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{t^{3}\Delta^{2}}
. This then allows us to make the following inequalities

\displaystyle L(\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{20}{t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}J\left(\frac{8}{\Delta^{2}}+32\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\alpha t^{3}\right)

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{20}{t^{2}\Delta^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}J\left(8+32\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\right).

(108)

The last step is then to show the above is contained in
\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
. As
J
contains only log factors, it is sufficient to show that there exists a
t
such that
\frac{1}{t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}\leq\epsilon
. Rearranging yields a quadratic in
t^{2}
that must satisfy the following

\Delta^{2}t^{2}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\geq 0.

(109)

The roots of this quadratic are

\displaystyle t^{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}\right),

(110)

meaning if
t
lies within these two roots then our expression will be satisfied. Our first observation is that in order for any solution to the inequality to exist we require
\sigma\leq\Delta\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}
, otherwise the roots are complex. As
\sigma\to 0
we note that the larger root
\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(1+\sqrt{1-2\sigma^{2}/\Delta^{2}\epsilon})
approaches infinity and the smaller root approaches
\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}
.

Now that we know the inequality in Eq. (
109
) has valid solutions, provided
\sigma
is small enough, we can now bound the remainder and off-resonance errors by
\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
.
We now will summarize the results. First our distance from the target state can be upper bounded in three contributions, which is done in Eq. (
88
).
Provided
\sigma\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\beta}
, then the distance from our Markov chain fixed point to the desired thermal state is less than
\epsilon
. Second, if
L\geq\frac{10J}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}
, then the output state of our channel will be less than a trace distance of
\epsilon
away from the Markov chain fixed point. Lastly, by choosing
\alpha=\frac{1}{t^{3}(\Delta+\sigma)^{2}}
and
t\in\left[\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}},\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}}\right]
we can guarantee that the remainder and off-resonance error terms are of order
\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
. We choose the lower limit for our choice of
t
because it does not diverge as
\sigma\rightarrow 0
. Adding these contributions together gives us
\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
. The total simulation time needed can then be bounded

\displaystyle Lt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{10Jt}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2)}\geq\frac{10Jt^{5}(\Delta+\sigma)^{4}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}}\geq\frac{10J\sigma\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}\right)^{5/2}(\Delta+\sigma)^{4}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}\epsilon}}}.

(111)

If we make the assumption that
\sigma\to 0
then we can use the fact that the lower bound for
t
approaches
\frac{1}{\Delta\sqrt{\epsilon}}
, giving

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}Lt\geq\frac{10J}{\Delta\epsilon^{2.5}}.

(112)

The final result then follows by noting that
J
is polylogarithmic in
\Delta
and
\epsilon
for our choice of
t
and
\alpha
.
∎

III.2
Harmonic Oscillator

Now that we have explored the thermalization channel completely for the single qubit case we turn our attention to a more complicated system: a truncated harmonic oscillator. For this scenario we will assume that the oscillator gap,
\Delta
, is known. This is mostly to simplify proofs of ergodicity and should not be an issue in practice, as evidenced by later theorems that show thermalization without eigenvalue knowledge. The reason behind this proof requirement is that by tuning
\gamma
to be the spectral gap we can create a “ladder” transition matrix in which states can move one level up or down. The proof of ergodicity relies on this ladder. Once we remove knowledge of
\Delta
if
\gamma
has some probability of being close to
2\Delta
this special ladder structure is destroyed. To avoid this annoyance and focus on the special structure granted by the harmonic oscillator we will assume
\gamma=\Delta
.

This system also represents a transition from the single qubit to more general settings discussed later as the guarantees on total simulation time as a function of
\beta
are similar. For the harmonic oscillator we are only able to bound the spectral gap in the ground state limit as
\beta\to\infty
, meaning that the convergence time for finite
\beta
has to be characterized in terms of the spectral gap of the Markov chain. For infinite
\beta
we are able to compute the spectral gap exactly, as the Markov transition matrix is upper triangular. The following theorem introduces this technique in a straightforward setting before it is used later for more complicated transition matrices.

Theorem 11
.

Let
H_{S}
denote a truncated harmonic oscillator with
\dim_{S}
energy levels that are (up to an irrelevant constant offset)
\lambda_{S}(i)=i\Delta
for
1\leq i\leq\dim_{S}
,
\gamma
be chosen such that
\gamma=\Delta
be the known energy gap, and let
\rho
be any input state that commutes with
H_{S}
. Setting the following parameters for the thermalizing channel
\Phi

\alpha=\frac{\epsilon^{1.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{1.5}\Delta}{\dim_{S}^{4}},t=\frac{\dim_{S}}{\Delta\sqrt{\epsilon\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}},\text{ and }L\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}t^{2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}\right),

(113)

where
\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)
denotes the spectral gap of the scaled transition matrix
T/\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}
, is sufficient for thermalization for arbitrary
\beta
as

\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta)-\Phi^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right).

(114)

This gives the total simulation time required as

L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{9}}{\Delta\epsilon^{2.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{2.5}}\right).

(115)

In the limit as
\beta\to\infty
the above settings for
\alpha,L
, and
t
are valid for preparing the ground state with the spectral gap of the rescaled transition matrix given by

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)=1.

(116)

Proof.

We first show that the thermal state is the unique fixed point for finite
\beta
. This will be done by computing the nonzero on-resonance transitions and plugging in to Lemma
7
. As
\gamma=\Delta
,
\Delta_{S}(i,j)=(i-j)\Delta
, and
\delta_{\min}=\Delta
we can deduce that the on-resonance transitions will only be nonzero for adjacent states
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
and
|i\pm 1\rangle\!\langle i\pm 1|
. This can be seen explicitly for
i\neq j
by evaluating the transition elements given by Definition
4
.

\displaystyle\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

(117)

IV
General Systems

We now extend our thermalization techniques to arbitrary Hamiltonians with no degenerate eigenvalues. The first major difficulty that we run into is how to choose our environment gap
\gamma
. If one does not have any knowledge whatsoever about where the eigenvalues of
H_{S}
may lie then we are reduced to uniform guessing. In Section
IV.1
we show that even in this scenario the thermal state is an approximate fixed point for finite
\beta
and the exact fixed point for ground states and we provide a bound on the total simulation time required. However, we show that this generality does come at a cost. If one has complete knowledge of the eigenvalue differences we show in Section
IV.2
that the total simulation time markedly decreases. Further, with complete knowledge the thermal state is an exact fixed point for all
\beta
. Finally, in Section
IV.3
we study these impacts on small Hydrogen chain systems and observe the quantitative effects of noise added to
\gamma
.

The assumption on non-degenerate eigenvalues is required for fairly technical conditions. In the
\beta\to\infty
limit for our proof of the spectral gap we use the fact that the transition matrix
T
is upper triangular in Lemma
7
. This is where the non-degeneracy is required because degenerate eigenvalues always have a non-zero transition amplitude that scales as
O\left(\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\right)
without a factor of sinc. This means within the degenerate subspace in the transition matrix there is a uniform block. This makes computing the transition matrix spectrum a little more complicated than necessary, so we avoid this issue by requiring no degeneracies. This restriction could likely be lifted through an intelligent choice of eigenbasis for the degenerate subspace, or through better spectrum calculations of the resulting transition matrix, but we leave such explorations for future work.

IV.1
Zero Knowledge

We now move on to show how our channel performs if one has no knowledge about the eigenvalue differences
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
apart from a bound on the maximum value of these differences. This is represented by choosing
\gamma
uniformly from the interval

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(155)

\displaystyle~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(156)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(157)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{1}{4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{0}^{4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)d\gamma

(158)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\delta_{\min}}^{\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\delta_{\min}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\gamma)t}{2}\right)d\gamma

(159)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du.

(160)

The exact same calculation holds for
i<j
, which after repeating the steps that led to (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(j,i)-2u/t)}}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(j,i)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du,

(161)

as we pick up a factor of
q(1)
as opposed to
q(0)
. Note that we have also shown that
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}
is ergodic, as there is a nonzero probability for any state
|i\rangle\!\langle i|
to transition to any other state
|j\rangle\!\langle j|
in one iteration on average over
\gamma

For finite
\beta
the condition for
\rho_{S}(\beta)
being a fixed point is given in Eq. (

\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}=0,

(162)

for all

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\left(e^{-\beta\Delta_{S}(i,j)}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle-\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|i\rangle\right)

(163)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}e^{-\beta\Delta_{S}(i,j)}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{1-e^{\beta 2u/t}}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du

(164)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{1-e^{\beta 2u/t}}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du.

(165)

For
i<j

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{e^{\beta 2u/t}-1}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(j,i)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du.

(166)

Unfortunately these integrals are not 0, which can be verified numerically, and it is unclear how to make the summation over

equal to 0.

Our work around this is that instead of showing that the thermal state is exactly the fixed point we can use these results to show that it is an approximate fixed point. There are a few ways we could proceed. The first way could be to compute a Taylor series for the integrand and isolate the limits in which the remainder goes to 0. Unfortunately due to the
\sinc^{2}(u)=\sin(u)^{2}/u^{2}
term this means that the overall scaling will go like
1/t
, making the total expression independent of

. Instead the route we will take will be to upper bound the norm
\left|\left|\vec{p}_{\beta}-\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}(I+T_{\gamma})\vec{p}_{\beta}\right|\right|_{1}=\left|\left|\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}\vec{p}_{\beta}\right|\right|_{1}
, as this norm is only 0 if
\vec{p}_{\beta}
is a fixed point. We reduce this to computations we have already performed as

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}\vec{p}_{\beta}\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left|e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}\vec{p}_{\beta}\right|

(167)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}\right|

(168)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}\right|.

(169)

This is essentially the derivation for the fixed point conditions described in Lemma
7
. We now plug in Eqs. (
165
) and (
166
) into the above and upper bound the integral as

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}\right|+\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i>j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{j}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{i}-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}e_{i}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}e_{j}\right|

(170)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{e^{\beta 2u/t}-1}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(j,i)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i>j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\frac{1-e^{\beta 2u/t}}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-2u/t)}}\sinc^{2}(u)du\right|

(171)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\sum_{j}\sum_{i<j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\left|\frac{e^{\beta 2u/t}-1}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(j,i)-2u/t)}}\right|\sinc^{2}(u)du

\displaystyle+\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\sum_{j}\sum_{i>j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\left|\frac{1-e^{\beta 2u/t}}{1+e^{-\beta(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-2u/t)}}\right|\sinc^{2}(u)du

(172)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}e^{\beta\delta_{\min}}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\sinc^{2}(u)du\left(\sum_{j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\sum_{i<j}1+\sum_{j}\sum_{i>j}\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\dim_{S}}{t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}e^{\beta\delta_{\min}}\pi

(174)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}te^{\beta\delta_{\min}}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{-1}\pi.

(175)

For this we can have
\alpha t
, which represents the total simulation time multiplied by the strength of the random interaction
G
, be constant and still take
\alpha\to 0
to achieve arbitrarily small error.

Now we turn to bounding the total simulation time. We will let
\rho_{\rm fix}
denote the fixed point of the dynamics. As before, we break the error into two pieces

\left|\left|\rho_{\rm fix}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma}\right)^{\circ L}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\left|\left|\rho_{\rm fix}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}\right)^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}+L(\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1}).

(176)

Let
\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)
denote the spectral gap for the rescaled transition matrix
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}\cdot\left(\frac{2\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|(\dim+1)}{\alpha^{2}t}\right)
, as this is the dimensionful prefactor in front of the transitions derived in Eqs. (
160
) and (
161
). Jerison’s Markov Relaxation Theorem
6
tells us that taking

L\geq\frac{\dim_{S}}{\lambda_{\star}}J\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\alpha^{2}t\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}\right)

is sufficient to guarantee
\left|\left|\rho_{\rm fix}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\mathds{1}+\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}\right)^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
. Now we balance the off-resonance and remainder errors

\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}+16\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\dim_{S}(\alpha t)^{3}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}\left(8+16\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\dim_{S}\alpha\delta_{\min}^{2}t^{3}\right),

(178)

and we see setting
\alpha=\frac{1}{\dim_{S}\delta_{\min}^{2}t^{3}}
makes the parenthesis a constant.
To bound the total off-resonance and remainder error we take the product

L(\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1})\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\alpha^{2}t\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\delta_{\min}^{2}}\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{t\delta_{\min}^{2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}\right).

(179)

Observe that setting

t=\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\epsilon\delta_{\min}^{2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)}

(180)

is sufficient to make the above product
L(\left|\left|\mathcal{T}_{\rm off}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1})\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)
.

We now turn to the
\beta\to\infty

7

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}
is upper triangular. We will show the ground state is unique by computing the spectrum of
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}

\beta\to\infty

160

161

given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\sinc^{2}(u)du

(181)

and for
i<j
from Eq. (
161

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle=0.

(182)

We denote the
\sinc

I_{\sinc}(t)\coloneqq\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\sinc^{2}(u)du,

(183)

and we will show later that this is constant for
\dim_{S}\geq 3
. Now these transitions allow us to compute the diagonal elements

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|i\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\neq i}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

(184)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j<i}\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle

(185)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}(i-1)I_{\sinc}(t).

(186)

This gives a spectrum for
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}
as 0 and
-\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}{2t\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}(i-1)I_{\sinc}(t)
for

. This shows the ground state is the unique fixed point as 0 has multiplicity 1 in the spectrum. Further the spectral gap of the rescaled transition matrix
\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)
is then given by

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)=I_{\sinc}(t).

(187)

We can repeat the analysis for finding suitable values for
\alpha,t,
and

to guarantee thermalization and we find that

\alpha=\frac{1}{\dim_{S}\delta_{\min}^{2}t^{3}},~t=\frac{4\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\epsilon\delta_{\min}^{2}},\text{ and }L\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\alpha^{2}t}\right)

(188)

are sufficient to guarantee
\left|\left||1\rangle\!\langle 1|-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma}\right)^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)

L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{16}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{7}}{\delta_{\min}^{8}\epsilon^{6}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{7}}\right)

(189)

as stated in the second claim in the theorem.

Our final task is to justify the third claim of the theorem, which involves showing that
I_{\sinc}(t)
as constant is valid. Using the choice of

directly above

\displaystyle I_{\sinc}(t)=\int_{-\delta_{\min}t/2}^{\delta_{\min}t/2}\sinc^{2}(u)du=2\int_{0}^{\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\epsilon\delta_{\min}}}\sinc^{2}(u)du.

(190)

Now we note that this integral is monotonic with respect to the upper limit of integration with a final value of
\lim_{t\to\infty}I_{\sinc}(t)=\pi
. We note that we can capture a significant amount of this integral by just requiring the upper limit to be greater than the first zero of sinc located at
\frac{\pi}{2}
, which is true if
\epsilon\leq\frac{4\dim_{S}^{2}\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\pi\delta_{\min}}
. This value can be computed as
2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sinc^{2}(u)du\geq 2.43
. This can be guaranteed by noting that
\epsilon
can be at most 2, so the upper limit in Eq. (
190
) is satisfied if

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq 2\leq\frac{3^{2}}{\pi}\leq\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}}{\pi}\leq\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|}{\pi\delta_{\min}},

(191)

as
\delta_{\min}\leq 4\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|
. This shows that for our choice of
t
then
|I_{\sinc}(t)-\pi|\leq 0.71
, rendering it asymptotically constant as claimed.
∎

There are a few points that need to be addressed with the above theorem. The first is that our proof of the approximate fixed point utilizes rather poor bounds, resulting in diverging behavior as
\beta\to\infty
. For finite
\beta
our bounds on the change in the thermal state scales as
e^{\beta\delta_{\min}}
, which diverges as
\beta
goes to
\infty
, but in this exact same limit we are able to show that the ground state is the
exact
fixed point of the Markov chain. This clear divergence in approximation error is a result of loose bounds and could be a potential avenue for improvement. The second point we would like to address is the rather high asymptotic scaling. This is the byproduct of a few things, the most important of which is the introduction of a
1/t
in the reduction of the
\sinc
integral. This causes a downstream effect of increasing the degree of each asymptotic parameter. To improve this one would need some kind of knowledge of the eigenvalues to prevent a uniform integration of each
\sinc
term. We study the limiting case of this by assuming sample access to the exact eigenvalue differences
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
in Section
IV.2
and obtain much improved scaling. The second source of inflation in our asymptotic scaling could be our weak-coupling approach to studying the channel. As explored numerically in Section
III.3
we find that using different
\alpha
scalings with respect to
t
can greatly effect the
\epsilon
scaling of the total simulation time
L\cdot t
. A higher order analysis of this channel could lead to anytically better guarantees on the thermalization time required, even in this zero knowledge scenario.

IV.2
Perfect Knowledge

Oftentimes when studying a system some knowledge of the eigenvalue gaps may be present. Our goal in this section is to study the extreme case of this scenario where one has knowledge of the exact eigenvalue differences. This is unlikely to happen with realistic quantum materials but instead serves as an ideal scenario for our channel to benchmark the effects of eigenvalue knowledge. Further, we note for some computational tasks, such as amplitude amplification, the eigenvalues may be explicitly computable and the real task is to find the dominant eigenvectors. A more realistic model for studying the impacts of eigenvalue knowledge on the total simulation time might be to place Gaussians at each of the
\Delta_{S}(i,j)
values with some width
\sigma
. This is the model we use for numeric investigations in Section
IV.3
, but we were unable to compute the total simulation time required analytically. We find that our model of perfect knowledge allows us to show a reduced total simulation time budget, with the ratio of zero knowledge to perfect knowledge scaling as
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\left|\left|H_{S}\right|\right|^{7}}{\delta_{\min}^{7}\epsilon^{3.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{3.5}}\right)
,
which gives an explicit worst-case simulation time bound for ground state preparation.

Theorem 13

(Perfect Knowledge Thermal State Prep)

H_{S}
be a Hermitian matrix of dimension
{\rm dim}_{S}
with no degenerate eigenvalues,
\rho
any input state that commutes with
H_{S}

\left|\left|\rho_{S}(\beta)-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma}\right)^{\circ L}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\in\widetilde{O}\left(\epsilon\right)

(192)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{\min}\epsilon^{1.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{1.5}}{\dim_{S}^{7}},t=\frac{\dim_{S}^{2}}{\delta_{\min}\epsilon^{0.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{0.5}},\text{ and }L\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{14}}{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{3}}\right),

(193)

where

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\binom{\dim_{S}}{2}}{\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}}

L\cdot t\in\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\dim_{S}^{16}}{\delta_{\min}\epsilon^{2.5}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)^{3.5}}\right).

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)=\min_{i>1}\sum_{j<i}\eta_{\Delta}(i,j)\geq 1.

(195)

Proof.

\displaystyle~+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\frac{e^{-\beta\gamma}}{1+e^{-\beta\gamma}}\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma|\leq\delta_{\min}]\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)+\gamma)t}{2}\right)

(196)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{\Delta_{S}(k,l)}{\rm Prob}\left[\gamma=\Delta_{S}(k,l)\right]\frac{\mathbf{I}[|\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\Delta_{S}(k,l)|\leq\delta_{\min}]}{1+e^{-\beta\Delta_{S}(k,l)}}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta_{S}(i,j)-\Delta_{S}(k,l))t}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\frac{\eta_{\Delta}(i,j)}{\binom{\dim_{S}}{2}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\Delta_{S}(i,j)}}.

(198)

i<j

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(i)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\langle j|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|i\rangle\!\langle i|)|j\rangle-\frac{e^{-\beta\lambda_{S}(j)}}{\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\beta)}\langle i|\mathcal{T}_{\rm on}^{(\gamma)}(|j\rangle\!\langle j|)|i\rangle

(202)

For

V
Conclusion

Thermal state preparation is likely to be a crucial preparation step for the simulation of quantum systems on digital quantum computers. We have presented a thermalization routine for this task that has an optimally minimal number of overhead ancilla qubits and compiles to remarkably simple circuits of time independent Hamiltonian evolution of the unprocessed system Hamiltonian, with no filtering or rejection steps and no Fourier weighted jump operators of Linbladians. Our routine is based on relatively recent classical Monte Carlo techniques, specifically Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
28
]
and the end result bears striking resemblance to the Repeated Interactions framework in open quantum systems
[
31
]
. In the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm thermal states over a position coordinate
q
is prepared by sampling momentum
p
from the Boltzmann distribution for Gaussians
e^{-\beta p^{2}/2m}
followed by time evolution. Classical Hamiltonian dynamics is enough to couple the position and momentum, leading to the Boltzmann distribution over
q
with enough time and samples. In the repeated interactions framework a quantum system interacts with many small environments, typically a single photon, that is repeated until the system thermalizes.

Our work extends these procedures to quantum algorithms. For Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, instead of adding in momentum variables we add in a single ancilla qubit to serve as our extra state space. We do not have the luxury of classical Hamiltonian dynamics that couples these two spaces or registers, so we add in a randomized interaction term to the Hamiltonian. After simulating the time dynamics of this system-ancilla pair and repeating multiple times we are able to thermalize the system to the same
\beta
. On the other hand, the Repeated Interactions framework typically is concerned with thermodynamic limits, such as infinite time or interactions, and specific system-interactions pairs. As our procedure is intended to be used as a subroutine for quantum computers our techniques work for arbitrary, non-degenerate, Hamiltonians and purposefully use randomized interactions as opposed to a fixed interaction model.

One benefit of our thermalization procedure is that it can be compiled all the way down to the circuit level with minimal overhead in complexity. The elements of the channel that need to be compiled are: the ancilla qubit state preparation, the initial state preparation for the system, the time evolution of
H+\alpha G
, and the partial trace. The ancilla state preparation can be done starting from the ground state with a Pauli-
X
rotation. The initial system state can be any state that commutes with
H_{S}
, the two leading choices are the maximally mixed state or a Haar random pure state. The maximally mixed state can be prepared using just CNOT gates at a cost of higher qubit count and a Haar random pure state can be prepared with no additional qubit overhead by applying a deep enough random circuit
[
42
]
.

The time evolution of
H+\alpha G
can be broken down into simulation of
H_{S}
and then
\alpha G
by one application of Trotter. The simulation of
H_{S}
can be implemented in two different ways depending on the access model for the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
. If
H_{S}
is provided as a block-encoding then there exist optimal simulation techniques that add only a single extra ancilla qubit
[
43
]
. If
H_{S}
is provided as a sum of
k
-local Pauli strings or as a sparse matrix then product formula techniques can be used
[
44
]
at zero extra overhead in ancilla qubits. To simulate the time evolution of
\alpha G
we can use the following breakdown
e^{i\alpha Gt}=e^{i\alpha U_{\text{Haar}}DU_{\text{Haar}}^{\dagger}t}=U_{\text{Haar}}e^{i\alpha Dt}U_{\text{Haar}}^{\dagger}
. This unitary can be implemented with a 2-design to approximate the
U_{\text{Haar}}
, this is due to the fact that we only expand the channel to second order in
\alpha
. Random Clifford circuits
[
45
]
are sufficient for this purpose. To simulate
e^{i\alpha Dt}
random
Z
rotations and controlled-
Z
rotations should be sufficient, as we only ever rely on the eigenvalues being pairwise independent in our analysis. In the worst case the number of random gates in the
Z
basis that would need to be applied would scale with the dimension of the system, adding an overall factor of
\dim_{S}
to the preparation. In the product formula case we further remark that
H+\alpha G
could be simulated in total with a composite technique of using Trotter for
H
and randomized compilation for
\alpha G

[
46
]
.

In classical Hamiltonian Monte Carlo it is well known that sharp gradients in the Hamiltonian require longer simulation time and more samples to converge. Our quantum routine has a much more subtle dependence on the structure of the Hamiltonian. As our single ancilla qubit only has one energy difference
\gamma
, we have to tune this energy difference to allow for energy to be siphoned off from the system into the ancilla. This would present a conundrum, as knowing spectral gaps is as difficult or harder than preparing ground states of arbitrary quantum Hamiltonians, but we are able to prove that our routine is robust to complete ignorance of these differences. We show that this ignorance comes at an asymptotic cost in the amount of resources needed to prepare the thermal state. We numerically verify that knowledge of the eigenvalue differences can be used to speed up the total simulation time, as demonstrated in Figure
4
. We posit that this behavior serves as a crucial entry point for heuristics about Hamiltonian spectra into thermal state preparation algorithms. No prior thermal state preparation routines have had such an explicit demonstration of the utility of such knowledge. It was our hope to analytically quantify the speedups gained as a function of the relative entropy between a heuristic guess for the eigenvalue differences and the true spectra, but our numeric evidence will have to suffice until future work can clarify this dependence.

We would like to make a few remarks on potential improvements for the analysis of this channel. As we have demonstrated numerically, our guaranteed analytic values of
\alpha
and
t
that lead to thermalization are drastically overestimated. We conjecture that this is due to our truncation of the weak-coupling expansion and in Figure
3
we demonstrate that taking
\alpha\propto 1/t
and
t\propto 1/\sqrt{\epsilon}
drastically outperforms our analytically derived bounds of
\alpha\propto 1/t^{3}
, by almost 4 orders of magnitude at
\epsilon\approx 0.005
. It is an open question of how to analyze this channel in the strong-coupling regime, and our numeric results suggest that such an analysis may indicate better performance of our protocol than a weak-coupling expansion can show. It is also an open question of whether dynamically chosen values of
\alpha
and
t
, such as having strong coupling and low time at the beginning and gradually decreasing
\alpha
and increasing
t
, can outperform static
\alpha
and
t
. We also suspect that the Markov relaxation theorem we used greatly overestimates the number of interactions needed. It remains to be seen if better Markov theory is needed or if the convergence time could be characterized based on the overlap of the initial state with the thermal state, which is a property that a few ground state preparation algorithms demonstrate. Another potential avenue for improving the analysis of this channel is whether different randomized interactions or even eigenvector heuristics can be beneficial. For example, in the harmonic oscillator if one has knowledge of the creation and annihilation operators
a^{\dagger}
and
a
, could one simply use the interaction
a^{\dagger}\otimes(X+iY)+a\otimes(X-iY)
instead of involving a randomized
G
that relies on a Haar average? The last potential improvement is to extend our spectral gap computations using perturbation theory. We are only able to compute the spectral gap
\widetilde{\lambda}_{\star}(\beta)
in the limit of
\beta\to\infty
, but it should be possible to compute a perturbation on the order of
1/\beta
. This would give the simulation time needed to prepare low-temperature thermal states as opposed to zero-temperature states.

Lastly, we would like to speculate on possible applications of this routine to other quantum information processing tasks. The first question that arises is if these techniques could be used in the training of quantum Boltzmann machines, which are essentially thermal states. It is an open question if our thermalizing techniques could be used to either train models or to generate output samples from an already trained model. Through the process of demonstrating that this channel prepares the system in the thermal state we have calculated the output of our channel for both the system and the environment registers, and for much larger environments than single qubits. We can turn this protocol on it’s head and ask how much information about the system are these ancilla qubits carrying away with them? Preliminary explorations suggest that given knowledge about eigenvalue gaps one can use transition statistics in the ancilla qubits to infer what the inverse temperature
\beta
is of the system, assuming the system is in a thermal state. Could this thermalizing channel instead be used to develop a Bayesian model to update beliefs about Hamiltonian spectra and system temperatures? This would represent an interaction agnostic model for performing quantum thermometry or spectroscopy, which to the best of our knowledge has not been developed yet.

-\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}\sum_{(a,b)\neq(i,j)}\sinc^{2}(\Delta(a,b|i,j)t/2)&(i,j)=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\end{cases}.

(298)

The
(i,j)\neq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
case should be apparent, the first term with the coherence factors
\chi
are zero and the second term is what remains. The
(i,j)=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
case can be seen as follows. For the first term we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha^{2}e^{i\Delta(i,j|i,j)t}}{\dim+1}\left(\chi(i,j)+\chi(i,j)^{*}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}(\eta(i,j)+\eta(i,j)\right)|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|.

(299)

We first compute the sum
\chi(i,j)+\chi(i,j)^{*}
as

\displaystyle\chi(i,j)+\chi(i,j)^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b:\Delta(i,j,|a,b)\neq 0}\frac{1-i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t-e^{-i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}}{\Delta(i,j|a,b)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a,b:\Delta(i,j,|a,b)\neq 0}\frac{1+i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t-e^{+i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}}{\Delta(i,j|a,b)^{2}}

(300)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b:\Delta(i,j|a,b)\neq 0}\frac{2-e^{-i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}-e^{+i\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}}{\Delta(i,j|a,b)^{2}}

(301)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b:\Delta(i,j|a,b)\neq 0}t^{2}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right),

(302)

where the last step follows from a trigonometric identity (see Eqs. (
295
) - (
297
) in Appendix
A.2
for details). Since
\sinc(0)=1
the
\eta(i,j)
term can be expressed as
\eta(i,j)=\sum_{a,b:\Delta(i,j|a,b)=0}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right)
. Plugging this into Eq. (
20
) gives

\displaystyle\int\langle i,j|\mathcal{T}(|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|)|i,j\rangle dG

(303)

\displaystyle=\langle i,j|\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}t^{2}}{\dim+1}\sum_{a,b}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right)|i,j\rangle\!\langle i,j|+\sum_{a,b}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right)|a,b\rangle\!\langle a,b|\right)|i,j\rangle

(304)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}t^{2}}{\dim+1}\sum_{(a,b)\neq(i,j)}\sinc^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta(i,j|a,b)t}{2}\right).

(305)

As a by-product of this computation we have just shown that
\Tr\left[\mathcal{T}(\rho)\right]=0

A.3
Weak-Coupling Remainder Bound

Proof of Theorem
9
.

First we note that although
R_{\Phi}(\rho)=\frac{\alpha^{3}}{6}\partial_{\alpha}^{3}\Phi(\rho)\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}
for a specific
\alpha_{\star}>0
our proof will hold for any
\alpha_{\star}
. To compute the trace norm we will use the triangle inequality, unitary invariance of the Schätten norms, and submultiplicativity. To start,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\alpha}^{3}\Phi(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial\alpha^{3}}{\rm Tr}_{H_{E}}\int e^{i(H+\alpha G)t}\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}dG\bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial\alpha^{3}}{\rm Tr}_{H_{E}}\int e^{i(H+\alpha G)t}\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}dG\bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\|{\rm Tr}_{H_{E}}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial\alpha^{3}}\left(e^{i(H+\alpha G)t}\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}\right)\bigg|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right\|_{1}dG.

(306)

To proceed, Proposition 1 of
[
48
]
allows us to eliminate the partial trace using the relation

\left|\left|\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{E}}\left[X\right]\right|\right|_{1}\leq\left|\left|X\right|\right|_{\dim_{E}}\leq\left|\left|X\right|\right|_{1}
. Further we use the decomposition of the second derivatives from the proof of Lemma
2
, specifically Eq. (
245
) for the definition of each term, as
\partial_{\alpha}^{2}\Phi_{G}=(A)+(B)+(C)+(D)+(E)+(F)
. This gives the following

\displaystyle\left|\left|R_{\Phi}\right|\right|_{1}\leq\frac{\alpha^{3}}{6}

\displaystyle\int\left|\left|\partial_{\alpha}((A)+(B)+(C)+(D)+(E)+(F))\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right|\right|_{1}dG

(307)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{3}}{6}\int\left|\left|\partial_{\alpha}(A)\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right|\right|_{1}+\left|\left|\partial_{\alpha}(B)\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right|\right|_{1}+\ldots+\left|\left|\partial_{\alpha}(F)\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{\star}}\right|\right|_{1}dG

(308)

We will demonstrate how this can be computed for the first term
\partial_{\alpha}(A)
. Using Eq. (
249
)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}(A)

\displaystyle=-t^{2}\partial_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}e^{is_{1}s_{2}(H+\alpha G)t}Ge^{is_{1}(1-s_{2})(H+\alpha G)t}Ge^{i(1-s_{1})(H+\alpha G)t}\rho e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}s_{1}~ds_{1}ds_{2}.

(309)

Due to the multiplication rule the resulting derivative will have 4 terms that each need one application of Duhamel’s formula. We will show one of these terms, starting with the leftmost one

\displaystyle-t^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\alpha}\left(e^{is_{1}s_{2}(H+\alpha G)t}\right)Ge^{is_{1}(1-s_{2})(H+\alpha G)t}Ge^{i(1-s_{1})(H+\alpha G)t}\rho e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}s_{1}~ds_{1}ds_{2}

(310)

\displaystyle=-it^{3}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(e^{is_{1}s_{2}s_{3}(H+\alpha G)t}Ge^{is_{1}s_{2}(1-s_{3})(H+\alpha G)t}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times Ge^{is_{1}(1-s_{2})(H+\alpha G)t}Ge^{i(1-s_{1})(H+\alpha G)t}\rho e^{-i(H+\alpha G)t}s_{1}^{2}s_{2}~ds_{1}ds_{2}ds_{3}.

(311)

Our goal is to compute the 1-norm of the above expression at
\alpha=\alpha_{\star}
. We can do so using the triangle inequality to move the norm of the matrices in the integrand. To proceed we will set
\alpha=\alpha_{\star}
and reduce the norm of the matrices in the integrand using submultiplicativity and unitary invariance of the Schätten 1-norm as

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left(e^{is_{1}s_{2}s_{3}(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}Ge^{is_{1}s_{2}(1-s_{3})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}\right)Ge^{is_{1}(1-s_{2})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}Ge^{i(1-s_{1})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}\rho e^{-i(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}\right|\right|_{1}

(312)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left|e^{is_{1}s_{2}s_{3}(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}G\right|\right|_{1}\left|\left|e^{is_{1}s_{2}(1-s_{3})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}G\right|\right|_{1}\left|\left|e^{is_{1}(1-s_{2})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}G\right|\right|_{1}\left|\left|e^{i(1-s_{1})(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}\rho e^{-i(H+\alpha_{\star}G)t}\right|\right|_{1}

(313)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}\left|\left|\rho\right|\right|_{1}=\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}.

(314)

Similar computations can be carried out for the other three terms for the derivative acting on
(A)
which yields the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{3}}{6}\int\left|\left|\partial_{\alpha}(A)\right|\right|_{1}dG

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{3}t^{3}}{6}\int\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}(s_{1}^{2}s_{2}+s_{1}^{2}(1-s_{2})+s_{1}(1-s_{1})+s_{1})~ds_{1}ds_{2}ds_{3}dG

(315)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{6}(\alpha t)^{3}\int\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}dG.

(316)

Now that we have computed the norm of the derivative acting on term
(A)
we only have terms
(B)
through
(F)
to compute. These can all be checked to satisfy the same bound on
(A)
from Eq. (
316
), and as there are six terms in total we have the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\left|R_{\Phi}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 4(\alpha t)^{3}\int\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}dG,

(317)

which we note holds for all inputs
\rho
. Therefore our last problem is to compute the expected norm of our interaction to the third power. We will decompose
G=U_{\text{Haar}}DU_{\text{Haar}}^{\dagger}
to get

\displaystyle\int\left|\left|G\right|\right|_{1}^{3}dG

\displaystyle=\int\left|\left|D\right|\right|_{1}^{3}~dU_{\text{Haar}}dD=\sum_{i=1}^{2\dim_{S}}\int\left|d_{i}\right|^{3}dd_{i}=2\dim_{S}\mathbb{E}(\left|y\right|^{3}),

(318)

where

\mathbb{E}(|y|^{3})=2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}
, yielding the final inequality

\displaystyle\left|\left|R_{\Phi}(\rho)\right|\right|_{1}\leq 16\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\dim_{S}(\alpha t)^{3},

(319)

thus completing the proof.
∎
