Behavioral problems in children with unoperated rheumatic heart disease at national referral hospital in Ethiopia: an observational study
Authors: Addishiwot Melesse, Rediet Fasil, Dejuma Yadeta, Henok Tadele
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe behavioral disorder is characterized by a profound abnormality in an individual’s cognition, emotion or behavior that reflects in psychological, biological or developmental dysfunction. Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is a long-term sequel of single or recurrent acute rheumatic fever. Children with RHD are thought to be at increased risk for behavioral problems due to autoimmunity seen in pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections (PANDAS). Behavior disorder is not well studied among children with RHD. Hence, this study aimed to assess the pattern and predictors of behavioral problems among children with RHD enrolled in chronic care at a national cardiac referral Hospital.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used a validated parental-reported Amharic Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) to assess behavior problems. Children with RHD aged 6–17 years on chronic outpatient cardiac care at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia between February and May 2023 were included. Factors associated with behavior problems were assessed using logistic regression models.ResultsThe study included 166 children with a median age of 15 years [Interquartile range (IQR):13–17 years]. One-fourth of children, 24.7% (95% CI: 18.34–31.98), had behavioral problems. Of these, 46.4% (77) emotional, 35.5% (59) peer, 18.1% (30) conduct and 6.6% (11) hyperactivity problems were documented. Children who had good medication adherence, adjusted odds ratio AOR 0.29(95% CI: 0.09–0.94, P 0.040) and on New York Health Association (NYHA) class I, AOR 0.28(95% CI: 0.11–0.79, P 0.016) had lower odds for behavioral problems.ConclusionOne-fourth of children with RHD had behavioral abnormalities. Medication adherence and NYHA predicted behavioral abnormality. Better physical symptom control was associated with fewer behavioral problems. Behavioral abnormalities should be considered and assessed among children with RHD. Further multi-center mixed studies are recommended to uncover the unseen behavioral problem and guide health policy action on modalities of integration of behavioral problems assessment tools in clinical care.

Background

Behavioral disorder, together with mental and neurodevelopmental disorders, is characterized by a profound abnormality in an individual’s cognition, emotion or behavior that reflects in psychological, biological or developmental dysfunction. It results in distress or impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational or other aspects of functioning [1, 2]. Globally, one in seven adolescents has mental health problems, behavior problems included, and behavior problems are common in young adolescents [3].

Children with chronic physical health conditions are reported to have behavioral problems. Extra healthcare stress, physical activity limitation and dependency place these children in emotional and behavioral problems [4]. While there are several behavior assessment tools, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) is the most widely used and validated behavior screening tool among children in several countries and cultures. SDQ is available as a self-report (SDQ-S), parent/caregiver (SDQ-P) and teacher report version (SDQ-T). The self-report SDQ, which is used among children aged 11–17 years, increases the involvement of adolescents in their health while SDQ-P provides additional information for comparison with self-report [5–9]. Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD) is the sequel of single or recurrent rheumatic fever with damage to heart valves [10]. It is prevalent in developing continents like Africa with limited access to health care interventions [11, 12]. Ethiopia is among the highest RHD or RHD-endemic nations in the world [13, 14]. Adult RHD studies documented a significant burden of behavioral problems among RHD patients [15, 16]. Children with RHD are thought to be at increased risk for behavioral problems due to autoimmunity seen in pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections (PANDAS) [17–20]. To the best of our knowledge, a single study among children with RHD reported an obsessive-compulsive behavior problem [21]. There is generally a scarcity of data on the pattern and burden of behavior problems among children with RHD. The study aimed to assess the burden and predictors of behavior problems among children with RHD enrolled in chronic outpatient cardiac care in the national cardiac referral hospital.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH). TASH is the teaching hospital of Addis Ababa University, College of Health Sciences and School of Medicine. It is also the national cardiac referral hospital in Ethiopia with several specialties and sub-specialties. Definitive cardiac surgical interventions are available in Ethiopia through three means: mission-based, abroad referral and local service. Four local centers provide cardiac surgical interventions in Ethiopia and TASH is the only public national cardiac referral hospital while the rest are either private or charity-based organizations [22].

Pediatric cardiology and adult cardiac clinics are the follow-up clinics for outpatient pediatric patients with rheumatic heart disease. There were 290 children with majorly non-intervened RHD between the ages of 6 and 17 years enrolled in chronic outpatient cardiac care in 2022. The clinics, which are run over five working days a week, are attended by trained nurses, pediatric and internal medicine residents, pediatric and adult cardiology fellows, and consultant pediatric and adult cardiologists. Pediatric and adult cardiologists confirm the diagnosis of RHD using transthoracic echocardiography (GE or Samsung Ultrasound Machine) based on the World Heart Federation Criteria. Children with RHD diagnosis are transitioned to adult cardiologist care beginning from the age of 14 years to establish smooth transfer to adulthood care. On chronic outpatient cardiac care, children with RHD receive different medication combinations which include diuretics, Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, Aldosterone antagonists and Benzathine Penicillin injections for rheumatic recurrence prophylaxis.

Study design and period

This study was conducted between February and May 2023, and an observational cross-sectional study design was used.

Study population

All children, between the ages of 6 and 17 years with RHD and their caretakers, enrolled in chronic outpatient cardiac care for at least 6 months in pediatric and adult cardiology clinics.

Sample size and sampling procedure

A sample of 166 was calculated using the EPI info statistical package for sample size and power calculation using assumptions of 50% of behavioral problems among children with RHD, 95% confidence level with 5% margin of error and a power of 80 correcting it for the finite population. A proportion of 50% of behavior problems were used due to a lack of enough evidence on the subject of behavior problems among children with RHD. From the eligible 290 children with RHD, all children showing up for the follow-up with secured consent were included in the study until the final sample size was attained. A proportional sample allocation was made between pediatric and adult cardiology clinics.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All children between the ages of 6 and 17 years, with established echocardiographic diagnosis of RHD and enrolled in chronic outpatient cardiac care for at least 6 months, and with no known previous psychiatric care or follow-up for behavioral issues were included in the study. Children with no consent and incomplete data were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire prepared for the study and pretested on nonparticipating children. Behavioral abnormalities were assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) administered by parents/caretakers. It is a brief behavior screening tool that covers common areas of emotional and behavioral difficulties. It also asks whether the informant thinks the child has a problem in those areas and their perceived impacts. The SDQ was developed by Goodman and colleagues in the United Kingdom as an open-access and downloadable screening tool. It is available as a self-report (SDQ-S), parent/caregiver (SDQ-P) and teacher report version (SDQ-T). We used the SDQ-P [5]. A translated and validated Amharic SDQ was made available for use in Ethiopia [23]. The SDQ is composed of 25 items, in a Likert response format with three options, which are grouped into five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, peer problems, and prosocial behavior. The first four subscales form a total difficulties score. SDQ also includes items that identify the impact of the psychological attributes on the child or adolescent as well as the caretaker. The responses of the primary caregivers of the study participants were labeled as 0 if the answer was “not true”, 1 if the answer was “somewhat true” and 2 if the answer was “certainly true”. For each of the 5 scales, the score can range from 0 to 10 if all items were completed. The total SDQ, prosocial and impact scores are calculated by adding each response and grouped into close to average, slightly raised/slightly low, high/low, or very high/very low. A total SDQ score of 14 or more is considered raised or abnormal. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected by three medical doctor interns who received one-day training on the data collection tool prepared for the study. This data was collected through face-to-face interviews and a review of the electronic medical records of the study participants. The data quality was checked daily by the investigators.

Variables

The dependent variable was the total SDQ score. Age, age at RHD diagnosis, gender, monthly family income, malnutrition, RHD intervention, medicine administration responsible person, medication adherence, RHD severity, NYHA class, respondent, respondent marital status, distance from the follow-up facility, maternal education and occupation, paternal education and occupation and child’s educational status were independent variables.

Operational definitions

New York Health Association class was a functional status class of a child based on symptoms [24]. RHD disease was grouped into mild, moderate and severe based on the World Heart Federation criteria [25]. Medical adherence was assessed by self-report/caretaker report and grouped as good (≥ 80%), satisfactory (60–80%) and poor (< 60%) based on the percentage of medications taken before the follow-up date. Malnutrition was assessed based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification for Body Mass Index BMI) for age and gender [26]. Comorbidities are additional diseases existing among RHD patients.

Data analysis

Data completeness was checked manually and analysis was done using Statistical Package Software for Social Sciences (SPSS) V26. Univariate and multivariable analysis was made using binary logistic regression. Variables with a P value less than 0.05 on univariate analysis were entered in multivariable analysis to ascertain for confounders. Association between the dependent variable and independent variables was reported using odds ratio and 95% confidence interval. Statistical significance was considered when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and SDQ scores of children with RHD

One hundred seventy-eight children with RHD were approached. Twelve participants were excluded (there were 5 cases of non-consent and 7 questionnaires were incomplete). Finally, this study included a total of 166 children. The median age of the children was 15 years [Interquartile range (IQR):13–17 years]. About half of the children were females, 51.2% (85). The majority of children with RHD lived more than 100 km away from the study health facility (TASH), 68.1% (113). Sixty-seven percent (112) of the children had a monthly family income of less than 2000 Ethiopian birr. Ninety-one percent (151) of the children had primary or secondary-level education (Table 1). No difference was evident between boys and girls in terms of total SDQ scores. None of the sociodemographic variables showed an association with total SDQ scores (Table 2).

Clinical characteristics and SDQ scores of children with RHD

The mean age at RHD diagnosis was 10.6 ± 3.19 years. Half of the children were in NYHA class II and above category 50% (83). Seventy-seven (46.4%) of children had moderate or severe malnutrition. The majority of children had moderate to severe RHD as assessed by echocardiography, 77.7% (129). Most of the children didn’t have any associated comorbidities, 95.2% (158). However, the few reported comorbidities include anemia 1.2% (2) , renal problems 1.8% (3) , abnormal uterine bleeding 0.6% (1) , peptic ulcer disease 0.6% (1) and syncope 0.6% (1) .

Of the 166 children, 16 (9.6%), had RHD complications: 7.8% (13) had only atrial fibrillation (AF) while 1.8% (3) had a cardioembolic stroke in association with AF. All of the children were on medications for RHD and half of the participants reported self-administration of the medication/s they were taking, 53.6% (89). The majority had good medication adherence 89.8% (149). Only 5.4%( 9) of the participants had a history of surgical or device intervention for their RHD (Table 3). All children reported a previous history of heart failure. From clinical variables, NYHA class, medical adherence and RHD severity showed associations with total SDQ score (Table 2).

Strength and difficulties questionnaire score

The study documented that 24.7% (95% CI: 18.34–31.98) of children had behavioral problems with a slightly raised, high, or very high total SDQ score. Twelve percent (95% CI: 7.52–17.99) of children had slightly lowered, low, or very low prosocial scale and 5.4% (95% CI: 2.51–10.04) of the participants had a high impact score. Of the 166 study participants, 46.4% (77) had emotional problems, 35.5% (59) had peer problems, 18.1% (30) had conduct problems and 6.6% (11) had hyperactivity problems (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with abnormal behaviors among children with RHD

On multivariable regression analysis, children who were in NYHA class I had reduced odds for behavioral problems, 0.28(0.11–0.79) P 0.016. Children with good medication adherence also had reduced odds for behavioral problems, 0.29(0.09–0.94) P 0.04(Table 4). RHD severity showed an association on univariate analysis with P 0.000 (Table 2) but didn’t show any association when cofounders were controlled (Table 4).

Discussion

We report behavioral abnormalities among school-aged children with RHD enrolled in chronic care. One-fourth of children with RHD had behavioral problems with emotion, peer and conduct being most affected. Children who were in NYHA II and above class with poor medication adherence were at increased odds of behavior problems.

In this study, one-fourth of children with RHD had behavioral problems. Though guarded by a small sample size an Egyptian study documented behavioral problems among children with RHD [21]. Even though we couldn’t make further comparisons due to a lack of published data, we believe this is significant given the burden of RHD in Ethiopia and most Low-and Middle-Income countries (LMICS). Additionally, a previous study among pediatric and adult RHD patients in the same setup documented a due burden of poor subjective well-being, a proxy for depression, and the studies might signal the tip of an iceberg of mental health issues among RHD patients calling for more attention and action [27]. Possible autoimmunity is ascribed to incite the pathogenesis of neuropsychiatric and behavioral disorders including the pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections (PANDAS) [17–20].

Emotion, peer and conduct problems were the commonly reported behavior problems in this study. Adult studies and a single pediatric study documented obsessive-compulsive disorders as a common behavior problem among RHD patients [15, 19, 21, 28]. The diagnosis of RHD and subsequent health care has long been postulated to put these children in emotional disturbance [29]. Emotional disturbance together with peer and conduct problems affects the daily life of a school-aged child making a significant productivity loss at school for the present and economic loss in the future [2, 3, 30]. The findings demand due attention and control of symptoms given their impact on the child, family and society at large.

Asymptomatic children with RHD had reduced odds of behavioral problems in this study. This further reinforces established evidence of a high prevalence of anxiety and depression/other neuropsychiatric disorders among symptomatic cardiac patients [31, 32]. The mechanism for biological and behavioral interaction is interrelated, and an absence of heart disease symptoms is translated to a stable mental health state [32]. This study’s finding encourages physical symptom control through modalities like access to definitive care or stopping RHD severity progression for better mental health.

In the current study, good medication adherence was negatively associated with behavioral problems. We couldn’t get behavioral problem studies done among RHD patients for comparison. However, this is in agreement with non-behavioral problem studies that documented improved overall health outcomes among cardiac patients with good medication adherence [27, 33]. The absence of an association between medication adherence and clinicopsychological factors was reported in another study [34]. Negative behavioral and psychological factors are postulated to lead to cardiovascular disease progression mainly through behavioral effects like medication nonadherence, and pathophysiological mechanisms like hormonal imbalances and inflammation [35]. The finding calls for behavior problem assessment among RHD patients as it is related to poor medication adherence and adverse health outcomes.

The strengths of this study are the inclusion of a significant number of non-intervened RHD patients, the use of a validated Amharic SDQ tool, and the collection of prospective data. This study has several limitations. These include a single-center study, lack of qualitative data, and assessment of medication adherence by patient or caretaker report, which could overestimate it. Additionally, other factors like parental substance abuse were not assessed. However, it provides a glimpse of the unseen burden of behavioral problems/ mental illnesses among children with RHD. It highlights the association between physical symptoms and behavioral problems among children with RHD. It calls for timely assessment and attention to control of behavior problem symptoms among RHD patients. It demands policy attention for treatment, prevention and control of RHD given the time-sensitive productive gain losses that could happen in the school-aged child with RHD.

Conclusion

In this study, one-fourth of children with RHD had behavior problems. NYHA class and medication adherence predicted the occurrence of behavior problems. Better physical symptom control was associated with fewer behavioral problems. Behavioral abnormalities should be considered and assessed among children with RHD. Integration of routine behavioral problems assessment for children with chronic illnesses and further multi-center mixed studies are recommended to guide health policy action.
