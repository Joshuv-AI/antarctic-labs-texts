Abstract
We give new, improved bounds for approximating the sparsest cut value or in other words the conductance ϕ \phi of a graph in the 𝖢𝖮𝖭𝖦𝖤𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{CONGEST} model. As our main result, we present an algorithm running in O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n / ϕ ) O(\log^{2}n/\phi) rounds in which every vertex outputs a value ϕ ~ \tilde{\phi} satisfying ϕ ≤ ϕ ~ ≤ 2.01 ​ ϕ \phi\leq\tilde{\phi}\leq\sqrt{2.01\phi} . In most regimes, our algorithm improves significantly over the previously fastest algorithm for the problem [Chen, Meierhans, Probst Gutenberg, Saranurak; SODA 25]. Additionally, our result generalizes to k k -way conductance. We obtain these results, by approximating the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian matrix L := I − Deg − 1 / 2 A Deg − 1 / 2 L:=I-\Deg^{-1/2}A\Deg^{-1/2} , where, A A is the adjacency matrix and Deg \Deg is the diagonal matrix with the weighted degrees on the diagonal. We show our algorithms are near-optimal by proving a lower bound for computing the smallest non-trivial eigenvalue of L L , even in the stronger LOCAL model The previous state of the art sparsest cut algorithm is in the technical realm of expander decompositions. Our algorithms, on the other hand, are relatively simple and easy to implement. At the core, they rely on the well-known power method , which comes down to repeatedly multiplying the Laplacian with a vector. This operation can be performed in a single round in the 𝖢𝖮𝖭𝖦𝖤𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{CONGEST} model. All our algorithms apply to weighted, undirected graphs. Our lower bounds apply even in unweighted graphs.

Introduction

A graph cut is a partition of the vertices into two disjoint subsets, where the size of the cut is the total number of edges crossing between them, and the sparsity of the cut measures how small this cut is relative to total edge weight of the smaller side, i.e., in comparison to the sum of its vertex degrees.
Graph cuts are a central object in theoretical computer science and algorithm design and have been extensively studied in various settings and models of computation, both explicitly studied, e.g., in
[
Kar00
,
ARV09
,
SGP09
,
SMPU15
,
KM15
,
DEMN21
,
CKGS23
]
, and as central subroutines, e.g., in
[
ST04
,
SW19
,
CMGS25
]
.

Cuts are especially important in communication networks because they represent the capacity for information to flow between different parts of the system. A sparse cut—where only a few edges connect two large subsets—can become a bottleneck, severely limiting bandwidth or throughput. A central question for guaranteeing the performance of network algorithms is to determine the minimum sparsity of cuts in a given communication network. For example the runtime of the famous PUSH-PULL protocol depends on the sparsest cut value
[
Gia11
]
.
More generally, graphs without sparse cuts support fast mixing of random walks
[
SNPT13
]
, and enable efficient routing due to their strong connectivity
[
GKS17
,
GL18
]
.
This motivates the use of expander decompositions, which partition a graph into
high-conductance
clusters (i.e, no sparse internal cuts), and sparse inter-cluster connections.
Such decompositions are powerful algorithmic tools: thanks to efficient routing, many algorithms run significantly faster on high-conductance subgraphs, allowing problems to be solved locally within clusters before handling the sparse inter-cluster parts. This strategy has been used in many applications, see, e.g.,
[
CPZ19
,
CS19
,
DHNS19
,
EFFK+22
,
CGL20
]
.
The distributed expander decompositions
[
CPZ19
,
CS19
,
CS20
,
CMGS25
]
are all constructed by repeatedly computing sparsest cuts.

In this work, we present simple, time and bandwidth efficient distributed algorithms to approximate the sparsest cut value. Our randomized algorithms run in the classic
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model of distributed computing where a communication network is abstracted as an
n
-vertex graph with vertices being computing entities and edges serving as bandwidth-limited communication links. Communication happens in synchronous rounds, in each of which each vertex can send a
O(\log n)
-bit message to each of its neighbors and the complexity measure is the number of communication rounds until the vertices have computed their outputs, e.g., until they have output an approximate value of the sparsest cut.

Formally, the
sparsity
of a set (or cut)
S\subseteq V
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is defined as as

\phi(S):=\frac{|E(S,V\setminus S)|}{\min\{\Vol(S),\Vol(V\setminus S)\}}~,

where the volume
\Vol(S)
of a set of vertices
S
is the sum of of the degrees of the vertices in the set
\sum_{v\in S}\deg(v)
. The value
\phi(S)
is also known as the
conductance
of the set
S
. The
sparsest cut
\phi(G)\in[0,1]
of a graph
G
is the minimum sparsity of a subset
S\subseteq V
. This is also known as the
conductance
of the graph.

The relative notion
\phi(G)
can be viewed as a normalized quantification of cut size.
In contrast to computing minimum cuts, which quantify the absolute size of a cut, determining the sparsest cut or the conductance is not only NP-complete
[
MS90
]
but also APX-hard under the unique games conjecture
[
CKKR+06
]
. Consequently, although no polynomial-time algorithm can approximate the optimum arbitrarily well (unless
P=NP
), a variety of approximation algorithms with provable—albeit weaker—guarantees have been developed across different computational models.
Next, we present our results and compare them with the state of the art in the field. As the sparsest cut and the conductance have been extensively studied we provide further background and related work in
Section

1.3
.

Our Contributions on Spectrum Approximation

Our core technical contributions are efficient
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms to compute the approximate (partial) spectrum of a graph. In essence,
Theorems

1
and
2
then follow as direct consequences via Cheeger’s Inequality. Nevertheless, we chose to present these theorems first, as they are more accessible to a broader audience and can be compared with prior work.

Eigenvalues of the Normalized Laplacian.

The normalized Laplacian of a graph is the matrix defined as
L:=I-\Deg^{-1/2}A\Deg^{-1/2}
, where
A
is the adjacency matrix and
\Deg
is the (weighted) degree matrix. See
Section

2.1
for more details. Its spectrum captures many central properties of the graph, e.g., various types of its connectedness like
k
-way conductance
[
LGT12
]
and small set expansion
[
ABS15
]
, or how close it is to being bipartite
[
BJ09
,
Liu15
]
. We provide the following theorem to compute an approximation of that spectrum.

Theorem 5

(Eigenvalue estimation)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a parameter and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be a constant. There are randomized
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms that, given an undirected, weighted graph
{G=(V,E)}
, with high probability approximate the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
of the graph’s normalized Laplacian as follows:

1.

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
: an additive
\varepsilon
-approximations in
O(\log^{2}n\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
rounds,

2.

\lambda_{2}
: an additive
\varepsilon
-approximation in
O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
rounds,

3.

\lambda_{n}
: a multiplicative
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximation in
O\left(D+\tfrac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}\right)
rounds.

Eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
is the eigenvalue approximating the sparsest cut via Cheeger’s Inequality. It could either be approximated via the second algorithm, or slightly slower via the first.

For the smaller eigenvalues,
Theorem

5
states additive approximations avoiding the diameter dependence, e.g., when
\varepsilon
is an arbitrarily small constant
Theorem

5
approximates
\lambda_{2}
in
O(\log^{2}n)
rounds even though the diameter may be up to linear in
n
.

As
\varepsilon
can be arbitrary in
Theorem

5

\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{\prime}\lambda_{2}
for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0

Theorem

Theorem

Lemma

holds for any graph, so the diameter term is then always asymptotically smaller than the overall runtime of the algorithm.

The main prior work for distributed eigenvalue approximation is by Kempe and McSherry
[
KM08
]
. They provide a distributed algorithm that, for constant

k

O(\tau_{\rm{mix}}\log^{2}n)

We note that the mixing time can be as large as

Furthermore, an important caveat here is that Kempe and McSherry do not obtain their result in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model: they do not discuss the bandwidth constraint. They do specify that they only need
\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(k)

Later, Becchetti et al.
[
BCNP+20
]
gave an algorithm for finding

An alternative approach to distributed eigenvalue computation is via computing a spectral sparsifier, i.e., a sparse subgraph

Lower Bound.

All of our multiplicative upper bounds take diameter time. This means that at the end of the algorithm, every vertex can easily output the same eigenvalue or sparsest cut estimate. One may ask whether one can obtain faster algorithms if the output is more local. Perhaps every vertex outputs the sparsest cut it sees in some neighborhood, and there is a guarantee that at least one vertex will see an approximately sparsest cut. In
Theorem

3

\lambda_{2}

\Omega(D)

Any algorithm that with probability

ensures that all vertices output a value

\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\leq 2\lambda_{2}
requires
\Omega(D)
rounds.

An

Related Work

Sparsest Cut.

As mentioned there are two approaches to solving the sparsest cut problem. The
spectral approach
used in this paper and the
flow approach
. The spectral approach encompasses techniques like Cheeger’s Inequality, spectral sparsifiers (discussed above), and also random walks. For instance, the results of
[
DS15
,
KM15
,
FV18
,
BTT24
]
rely on random walks, which are considered spectral in nature since their convergence analysis depends on spectral properties. This approach is inherently limited by Cheeger’s Inequality, capping the approximation factor at
\sqrt{2\phi}
, the guarantee we match in this work.

The flow approach, based on the
cut-matching game
, computes a number of flows that will either guarantee expansion or find a sparse cut. Theoretically, this can give a
(\phi\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}\log n)
-approximation. In the sequential setting, the state-of-the-art is a
O(\phi\sqrt{\log n})
-approximation by Arora, Rao, and Vazirani
[
ARV09
]
. In the distributed setting, the state-of-the-art is the aforementioned
O(\phi\log^{2}n)
-approximation
[
CMGS25
]
. Before this recent result, the flow based gave worse approximations:
[
CPZ19
,
CS19
]
gave a
\tilde{\phi}\leq O(\phi^{1/3}\log^{5/3}n)
-approximation in
O(D\cdot\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}\log n,\phi^{-1})
rounds, for
\phi=O(1/\log^{5}n)
. Additionally, Chang and Saranurak
[
CS20
]
gave a deterministic version of that result with additional sub-polynomial factor in the round complexity.

Laplacian Paradigm.

The
Laplacian paradigm
is an umbrella term for algorithmic techniques relying on the Laplacian and its properties. This line of research was initiated by Spielman and Teng
[
ST04
]
, who showed that linear equations in the Laplacian matrix of a graph can be solved in near-linear time. Algorithms within the Laplacian paradigm combine numerical techniques – from the matrix point of view – with combinatorial techniques – from the graph point of view. Nowadays, it encompasses techniques like solving Laplacian systems, spectral sparsifiers, electrical flow, effective resistance, expander decompositions, continuous optimization, interior-point methods, gradient descent, and preconditioning. This has had many applications, including but not limited to flow problems
[
M
,
She13
,
KLOS14
,
Ma
,
Pen16
,
CMSV17
,
LS20
,
LS20a
,
AMV20
]
, bipartite matching
[
BLNP+20
]
, and (parallel) shortest paths
[
Li20
,
ASZ20
]
.

Also in the distributed world, this has booked many successes, e.g.,
[
GKKL+18
,
BFKL21
,
FGLP+21
,
ALHZ+22
,
FV22
,
FV23
,
Vos23
]
. With this paper, we hope to contribute to a better understanding of the Laplacian matrix and its applications in a distributed setting.

Comparison to the Min-Cut Problem.

In the minimum cut (min-cut) problem, the goal is to find a cut
S
of minimum value
|E(S,V\setminus S)|
. Although the problem statement seems similar to the sparsest cut problem, it has a very different computational complexity. As said, in the sequential setting, it is NP-hard to approximate the sparsest cut (under the unique games conjecture)
[
MS90
,
CKKR+06
]
. On the other hand, there are linear time algorithms for computing the exact min-cut
[
Kar00
]
.

In the distributed setting, we show that the
\Omega(\sqrt{n}+D)
lower bound does not apply for approximating the sparsest cut problem. The min-cut problem however has a complexity of
\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n}+D)

[
SHKK+12
,
DEMN21
]
,
where the lower bound holds even for a
\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(n)
-approximation.

Technical Overview

As mentioned,
Theorems

1
and
2
follow as direct consequences from our eigenvalue estimation results via Cheeger’s Inequality. Here, we focus on our core technical contribution: efficient
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms to compute the approximate (partial) spectrum of a graph.
The main ingredient of our algorithms is the well-known
power method
for approximating the largest eigenvalue of a matrix.

The Power Method Algorithm.

Let
M
be any matrix. The power method, also known as
power iteration
or
Von Mises iteration
, starts with a random vector and converges to the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
M
. It is a standard technique in numerical linear algebra, see, e.g.,
[
GVL13
]
. The procedure is as follows.

Power Method

1:

Input:
Matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, number of iterations
k
.

2:

Initialize:
A random vector
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

3:

for

i=1
to
k

do

4:

x_{i}\leftarrow Mx_{i-1}
.

5:

Output:

•

Approximate dominant eigenvalue:
\lambda\approx\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}
;

•

Approximate dominant eigenvector:
x_{k}
.

With constant probability over the randomness of the start vector
x_{0}
, the power method outputs a vector
x_{k}
whose
Rayleigh coefficient

\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}
is close to the
largest
eigenvalue of the matrix
M
. The main intuition why this happens is as follows: Any vector can be written as a combination of the matrix’ eigenvectors and when you apply the matrix multiple times, the contribution from the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue (in magnitude) dominates. So, over time, the method “filters out” all directions except the one corresponding to the largest eigenvalue. This works only if the random start vector
x_{0}
has a nonzero component in the dominant eigenvector’s direction; beyond the choice of this initial vector, the process is fully deterministic.

In this paper, we only need the power method for
positive semi-definite (PSD)
matrices, which are symmetric matrices with non-negative eigenvalues. Then all eigenvalues are nonnegative and we can assume the eigenvectors to be orthonormal. The power method also converges slightly faster on PSD matrices – by a factor
2
to be precise.

Lemma 7

(Power Method)
.

Let
M
be a positive semi-definite matrix with eigenvalues
0\leq\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}
.
Let
x_{0}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}
be a uniformly random vector and
x_{k}=M^{k}x_{0}
.
For
\varepsilon\geq 16/n
and
k\geq\Theta(\log n/\varepsilon)
, it holds with constant probability that

(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\leq\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}\leq\mu_{n}.

The power method is suitable for distributed applications, since matrix vector multiplication is easy in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model: this can be done in
O(1)
rounds where each vertex of the communication network holds an entry of the vector, and each vertex knows the entries of the matrix in its row and column. Important is that the only non-zero entries in a row or column correspond to a neighboring vertex. This is exactly the case when the matrix is closely related to the adjacency matrix – such as the Laplacian.

Only the final step, of computing the Rayleigh coefficient of
x_{k}
takes
O(D)
time.
We note that the power method is a widely used routine. Also decentralized implementations have been known for a long time, e.g,
[
BT15
,
PS14
]
or for the special case of PageRank
[
SMPU15
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, the procedure and its extensions have not been studied in the context of the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model.

One difficulty with implementing the power method in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model as is, is that values grow exponentially with
k
and hence after a few iterations and they cannot be send to neighbors using
O(\log n)
bits per message. Fortunately, the method is extremely robust. It still converges quickly after truncating each value after the
O(\log n)
most significant bits; if one additionally remembers the order of magnitude of the values one can approximately compute the Rayleigh coefficient at the end of the algorithm – see
Lemma

11
for a formal statement.
More involved methods, like the Lanczos method (see, e.g.,
[
GVL13
]
) might be able to return similar results. However, the Lanczos method is inherently unstable. It is unclear whether this can be adapted to work (efficiently) in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model.

Averaging Dynamics.

In community detection, average dynamics is a well-known concept. The idea is that each node updates its value to the average of its neighbors. If
G
is connected and not bipartite, this always converges.
The update step is comparable is the same in spirit as a step in the power method, but not entirely equal.
In particular, Becchetti et al.
[
BCNP+20
]
use average dynamics in the distributed setting. In case of a sufficiently small balanced cut, and some additional technical assumptions, they can provide an approximate sparsest cut. Since the approximation factor depends on the technical assumptions, we will not state their exact guarantees here.
With a similar approach, Beccheretti et al.
[
BCNP+17
]
can find a sparse cut efficiently if both of the parts do not contain sparse cuts. Moreover, the average dynamics has been studied as a tool for community dynamics in related models that do not directly compare to the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model, e.g., the asynchronous, opportunistic communication model
[
BCMN+18
]
and the asynchronous gossip model
[
MMM18
]
.

The Graph Laplacian Matrix.

We denote
\mathds{1}
for the vector with 1 in each entry and
\sqrt{\deg}=\Deg^{1/2}\mathds{1}
for the vector with
(\sqrt{\deg})_{v}=\sqrt{\deg(v)}
on index (equals vertex)

Recall that the normalized Laplacian
L:=I-\Deg^{-1/2}A\Deg^{-1/2}
has smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=0
.
This can be seen since
\sqrt{\deg}
is an eigenvector of
L

\displaystyle L\sqrt{\deg}

\displaystyle=(I-\Deg^{-1/2}A\Deg^{-1/2})\sqrt{\deg}=\sqrt{\deg}-\Deg^{-1/2}A\mathds{1},

Note that
A\mathds{1}
counts the number of 1s in each row

In weighted graphs, this is the sum of the weights in each row. Since the degrees are also weighted degrees, the proof remains the same.
, so this exactly sums up to the degree:

L\sqrt{\deg}=\sqrt{\deg}-\Deg^{-1/2}\deg=0
.
Hence, the smallest interesting eigenvalue to study is
\lambda_{2}
.

Approximating
\lambda_{2}
.

To approximate the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian
L
, we consider the matrix
M:=2I-L
, which has eigenvalues
0\leq\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}
, satisfying
\mu_{i}=2-\lambda_{n-i+1}
. In particular, this means that
\mu_{n}=2
and
\mu_{n-1}=\lambda_{2}
.
In order to compute
\lambda_{2}
, one would like to subtract the components in the direction
v_{n}=\sqrt{\deg}
, the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
. As a result, the prior largest eigenvalue is shifted to
0
, and the power method converges to the second largest eigenvalue instead.

As per the discussion above, we know the largest eigenvector
v_{n}=\sqrt{\deg}
exactly. We can also subtract the components in this direction exactly. However, a significant facet here is the bit complexity. As we discussed before, small errors incurred from rounding are not an issue when we are approximating the
largest
eigenvalue. However, due to rounding when we are approximation the second-largest eigenvalue, our vector will not remain perpendicular to
v_{n}
. Hence, given enough iterations, the process will always converge to the largest eigenvalue.
Naively, we can combat this by projecting the current vector
x
on the subspace orthogonal to
v_{n}
in every step, or in other words, by removing components in direction
v_{n}
. This requires computing the inner product between
v_{n}
and
x
. The latter is no problem in the centralized setting, but computing this inner product requires
\Omega(D)
rounds in
\mathsf{CONGEST}
. Hence we want to limit how often we do this. We show that the error blows up at a rate that means that we only need to project periodically, in total at most
\lceil\tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\rceil
times. In order to perform this efficiently, we show that all interesting cases actually have a diameter small enough to repeatedly perform these projections without increasing the runtime too much.

Approximating
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
.

We compute the eigenvalue approximations sequentially. For approximating
\lambda_{i}
, we use the same general approach as for
\lambda_{2}
. Instead of projecting on the subspace orthogonal the largest eigenvalue, we project on the subspace orthogonal to the largest
i-1
eigenvalues, and remove the resulting parts from consideration. The key challenge is that we do not know the corresponding eigenvectors exactly but they are only approximately known from our prior steps. We show that the power method still converges despite these inaccuracies in the eigenvectors that we project on. This is one of the most technical parts of this paper and appears in
Section

2.5
.

Probabilistic Guarantees.

The power method as stated in
Lemma

7
is correct with constant probability. This means that we can for example can also obtain our sparsest cut result,
Theorem

1
, in
O(\log n/\phi)
rounds but with
constant
probability. To boost the probability, we run
O(\log n)
independent instances of the power method and return the maximum. Since we are looking for the largest eigenvalue, and we know that we cannot overshoot (see
Lemma

10
), this gives the required approximation with high probability.

Lower Bound Constructions.

We construct graphs by combining well-understood components—such as cliques, paths, and star graphs—where the sparsest cut is easy to determine. Using these building blocks, we define two graph families with significantly different sparsest cut values, yet with locally identical neighborhoods around certain vertices. As a result, these vertices cannot distinguish between the two families and thus cannot accurately approximate the sparsest cut without global information. This implies that approximating the sparsest cut requires
\Omega(D)
rounds in our constructions.

It is challenging to use a similar approach to obtain a lower bound for eigenvalue approximation, as determining the eigenvalues of any graph is highly non-trivial. The eigenvalues of few specific graphs have been computed, like paths, cycles, and cliques, but determining them remains hard in general. This stems partly from the fact that gluing together graphs can have big effects on the eigenvalues. Cauchy’s interlacing eigenvalue theorem
[
Hwa04
]
gives some bounds, but this is unfortunately of no help. Another approach is to use the fact that we know the sparsest cut, together with Cheeger’s Inequality. Rephrased Cheeger’s Inequality gives
\phi^{2}/2\leq\lambda_{2}\leq 2\phi
. So it gives a linear upper bound on the eigenvalue given the sparsest cut, but it has a square in the lower bound. At the end of the day, this means that only using Cheeger’s would result in a lower bound of the form
\Omega(\sqrt{D})
. For our
\Omega(D)
lower bound, we need a better upper bound on the eigenvalues of the graphs used in the lower bound construction. We exploit that eigenvalues can also be bounded by specific vectors. The simplest such statement is that the largest eigenvalue of a matrix
M
equals
\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tfrac{x^{T}Mx}{x^{T}x}
. Hence any
x
can provide a lower bound for the largest eigenvalue. We use a similar statement for the second smallest eigenvalue and construct an appropriate vector
x
to prove a tighter upper bound on the eigenvalues of our constructed graphs. In total, we are able to provide upper and lower bounds that characterize the eigenvalues of the graphs used in our lower bound construction up to a constant factor.

Sparsest Cuts and Cheeger’s Inequality.

Finally, let us explain how the eigenvalue results enable us to derive an approximation of the sparsest cut.

Let us obtain some intuition why eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
relates to the connectedness of the graph. In the extreme case that
\lambda_{2}=0
holds the graph is disconnected: On each connected component, the degree vector is still an eigenvector with eigenvalue
0
. Hence a vector that has
\sqrt{\deg}
on one connected component, and all
0
s on the other connected component is also an eigenvector – and linearly independent of
\sqrt{\deg}
. The reverse direction, i.e., that the graph is connected if
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
holds, is also true but its proof is slightly more involved, so we omit it in this overview, see e.g.
[
Chu97
]
.

Cheeger’s Inequality
[
AM85
,
Alo86
,
SJ89
]
gives a more precise statement about the connectedness of the graph and the second smallest eigenvalue: it relates
\lambda_{2}
to the sparsest cut
\phi
.

Theorem 8

(Cheeger’s Inequality)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected, weighted graph, then

\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\leq\phi\leq\sqrt{2\lambda_{2}}~.

The fact that
\lambda_{2}=0
holds if and only if the graph is disconnected is a corollary of
Theorem

8
.

Since Cheeger’s Inequality states that the value of
\lambda_{2}
approximates
\phi
, we can find an approximation
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}
for
\lambda_{2}
with
Theorem

5
and use it to output an approximation
\tilde{\phi}=\sqrt{2\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}
of the sparsest cut, establishing
Theorem

1
.

For larger values of

, similar statements hold. First, we remark that
\lambda_{k}=0
if and only if the graph consists of at least
k
connected components. One direction of this statement is again easy to see: as before, on each connected component the
\sqrt{\deg}
vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
0
. In total, this gives
k
independent eigenvalues with eigenvector
0
– in other words,
\lambda_{k}=0
.

Similar as for
k=2
, this generalizes by relating
\lambda_{k}
to
\phi_{k}

Lee, Gharan, and Trevisan showed the following theorem which we then use to establish

Theorem 9

(Higher-Order Cheeger’s Inequality,
[
LGT12
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected, weighted graph, then

\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\leq\phi_{k}\leq O(k^{2})\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}~.

The Graph Laplacian Matrix.

First, we will give some definitions and notation. For a more thorough introduction of spectral graph theory, we refer to, e.g.,
[

]
.

The normalized Laplacian is defined as
L:=I-\Deg^{-1/2}A\Deg^{-1/2}
, where
\Deg
is a diagonal matrix with the (weighted) vertex degrees on the diagonal, and
A
is the adjacency matrix. Let us inspect some of its properties. First of all, we note it is
symmetric
, i.e.,
L^{T}=L
. We know that all symmetric matrices allow for a set of
orthonormal
eigenvector
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. That means the eigenvectors are
orthogonal
:
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
, and have norm 1:
||v_{i}||=1
for all
i
. Moreover, any Laplacian matrix is
positive semi-definite
. This means that all its eigenvalues are nonnegative. Equivalently, we have for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that
x^{T}Lx\geq 0
. More precisely, it has eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 2
. The eigenvalue
0
can easily be verified since
Lv_{1}=0
for
v_{1}:=\sqrt{\deg}
, the vector with in each entry the square root of the degree of the corresponding vertex.

As explained in
Section

1.4
,
\lambda_{2}=0
if and only if the graph is disconnected. More generally,
\lambda_{k}=0
if and only if the graph consists of
k
connected components. In the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model, we may assume a connected graph, or alternatively apply the results on each component of the communication network separately. Hence we have
\lambda_{2}>0
.

Notation.

Throughout,
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
denote distinct vectors. We write
(x)_{v}
for the
v
-th entry of a vector
x
, i.e., the entry of the vector
x
that is hold by vertex
v
.

We reserve
0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}\leq 2
for the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian. For a general
n
by
n
matrix
M
, we denote its eigenvalues by
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}
. We note that in the following
M
and
L
might differ, however, they will always have the same eigenvectors. Denote these as

, corresponding to
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}

2.2
Power Method

The core of our eigenvalue approximation is an efficient CONGEST implementation of the power method. We recall its main measure of progress.
See
7

Most results in this section have appeared in the literature before and we do not claim any novel contribution. To be self-contained and as our statements slightly differ from statements in the literature we present proofs of all presented lemmas in
Appendix

A
. To the best of our knowledge, the only exception is the bit-precision lemma that we present at the end of the section. It is central to implementing the power method in CONGEST and essentially says that rounding intermediate values does not significantly affect the performance of the power method; also see the paragraph on ‘exploding values’ in
Section

1.4
. It may be slightly stronger than what is needed in the centralized setting and potentially also of independent interest.

Lemma

7
states that there is a constant probability that the power method fails to find the largest eigenvalue. Hence, we need to run
O(\log n)
instances of it and return the largest eigenvalue found during any of the independent runs. The following lemma shows that we never return any value larger than the largest eigenvalue. It is also needed to control the growth of numbers which is essential to implement the method in CONGEST.

Lemma 10
.

For any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we have that
\frac{x^{T}Mx}{x^{T}x}\leq\mu_{n}
.

Regardless of whether in the centralized setting or in CONGEST, the intermediate vectors may consist of quickly growing values that cannot be efficiently represented. The following lemma states that truncating all values after
O(\log n)
bits does not affect the outcome of the power method by too much. Note that the statement may sound trivial as a precision of
1/\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}n
for each single number seems to simply result in an imprecision of
k/\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}n
after
k
steps. A closer look at the power method reveals that errors may amplify and exponentially grow over time. Hence, the next lemma requires an actual proof that appears in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
k\geq i\geq 1
. Throughout the power method, rounding each element of
x_{i}:=M^{i}x_{0}
to the
\Theta(\log n)
-th bit, does not impact the outcome of
\frac{(x_{k})^{T}Mx_{k}}{(x_{k})^{T}x_{k}}
by more than a factor
(1\pm n^{-\Theta(1)})
.

2.3
Warm-up: The Largest Eigenvalue

First, we show how the power method applied directly to the Laplacian gives an approximation of the largest eigenvalue.

Theorem 12

(Approximating
\lambda_{n}
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There is a randomized
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm that, given an undirected, weighted graph
G=(V,E)
, with high probability computes a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximation of the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of the normalized Laplacian. The algorithm takes
O(D+\log^{2}n/\varepsilon)
rounds.

Proof.

We use the power method,
Lemma

7
, with
M=L
and
k=\Theta(\log n/\varepsilon)
iterations. Each iteration consists of a matrix-vector multiplication, in which each vertex
v
needs to send its current value of
(x)_{v}
to its neighbors.
After this, we perform one additional matrix-vector multiplication and compute
\tfrac{x^{T}Mx}{x^{T}x}
in
O(D)
rounds.

Bit precision.

Lemma

11
states that we can truncate each value after the
O(\log n)
most significant bits. We additionally remember the order of magnitude of the values. Since we have
k=\Theta(\log n/\varepsilon)
iterations, and our numbers are of size
2^{k}
, we need at most
\log\log(2^{k})=\log(\log n/\varepsilon)=O(\log n)
bits to store the order of magnitude. Here we use that without loss of generality
\varepsilon=\Omega(1/n^{2})
.
We conclude that we can communicate each value up to the required precision in
O(\log n)
bits.

Success Probability.
Note that
Lemma

7
only gives constant probability where the probability is over the choice of the random start vector. Repeating the process
O(\log n)
times and take the maximum to obtain the result with high probability, since we cannot overshoot by
Lemma

10
.
The matrix-vector multiplications take
O(\log n\cdot\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon})
. However, for every instance, we also need to compute a final inner product that takes
O(D)
time each.
We can pipeline these inner product for the
O(\log n)
instances to obtain congestion plus dilation time for this part:
O(\log n+D)
. Combining the running times, we obtain

O(\log^{2}n/\varepsilon+D+\log n)=(\log^{2}n/\varepsilon+D)
rounds.
∎

2.4
The Smallest Non-Zero Eigenvalue

Next, we consider the smallest non-zero eigenvalue. The main idea is to consider
M=2I-L
, such that the smallest eigenvalue becomes the largest, and then apply the power method. However, we are looking at the
smallest non-zero
eigenvalue. So we take additional measures to avoid finding the smallest (
=0
) eigenvalue.

Theorem 13

(Approximating
\lambda_{2}
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There is a randomized
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm that, given an undirected, weighted graph
G=(V,E)
, with high probability computes an approximation of the smallest non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
of the normalized Laplacian. The algorithm takes

O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

rounds. At the end of the algorithm, every vertex outputs an estimation
\lambda_{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\leq\lambda_{2}+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We check if
D>\Theta(\log n/\varepsilon)
holds in
O(\log n/\varepsilon)
rounds. If so, then we have
\log n/\varepsilon<\Theta(D)\leq\Theta(\log n/\phi)
by
Lemma

21
, or equivalently
\phi<\Theta(\varepsilon)
. By Cheeger’s Inequality,
Theorem

8
,
\lambda_{2}\leq 2\phi\leq 2\varepsilon
. So we simply output
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}=\Theta(\varepsilon)
and we are done.

So for the remainder of the proof, we may assume
D=O(\log n/\varepsilon)
. The algorithm is similar as in
Theorem

12
, however, we now need to find the
smallest
(nonzero) eigenvalue. To flip smallest and largest, we use a standard trick and consider the matrix
M=2I-L
(instead of
M=L
as in
Theorem

12
).
M
is still a PSD matrix, now with largest eigenvalue
2
, for which we also know the largest eigenvector
v_{n}:=\sqrt{\deg}
. We denote the eigenvalues of
M
by
0\leq\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}=2
.
If we were to apply the power method on
M
, we would find this largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n}=2
. Our goal is, however, to find
\mu_{n-1}
. Thus, we aim to use it on the subspace orthogonal to
v_{n}
by nullifying contributions in direction
v_{n}
. Formally, we change the matrix
M
yet again.

To this end consider
M^{\prime}:=M-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T}
. All eigenvectors and most eigenvalues stay the same: for
i<n
we have

(M-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T})v_{i}=Mv_{i}-2v_{n}\langle v_{n},v_{i}\rangle=Mv_{i}=\mu_{i}v_{i},

by orthogonality of the eigenvectors. Only eigenvector
v_{n}
changes its eigenvalues from
2
in matrix
M
to
0
in matrix
M^{\prime}
:

(M-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T})v_{n}=Mv_{n}-2v_{n}\langle v_{n},v_{n}\rangle=Mv_{i}=2v_{n}-2v_{n}=0.

This means that the largest eigenvalue of
M^{\prime}
is
\mu_{n-1}
Suppose now we have found
\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}
such that
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n-1}\leq\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}\leq\mu_{n-1}
holds. Then also
\mu_{n-1}=2-\lambda_{2}
holds, so we output
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}:=2-\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=2-\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}\geq 2-\mu_{n-1}=\lambda_{2};\text{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=2-\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}\leq 2-(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n-1}=2-(1-\varepsilon)(2-\lambda_{2})=(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{2}+2\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{2}+2\varepsilon.

Setting
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/2
gives the result.

Finding the largest eigenvalue of
M^{\prime}
.

Again, we use the power method,
Lemma

7
, to approximate
\mu_{n-1}
.
We use the next claim to prove that for most steps, multiplying with
M^{\prime}
is actually the same as multiplying with
M
.

Claim 1
.

For all
x
, we have

1.

M^{\prime}x=Mx-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T}x=Mx-2v_{n}\langle x,v_{n}\rangle
;

2.

\langle M^{\prime}x,v_{n}\rangle=0
.

Proof.

The first part of the claim is immediate. For the second part we compute

\displaystyle\langle M^{\prime}x,v_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(M-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T})x,v_{n}\rangle=\left\langle(M-2v_{n}v_{n}^{T})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle x,v_{i}\rangle v_{i},v_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\langle x,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}-2v_{n}\langle x,v_{n}\rangle,v_{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\langle x,v_{i}\rangle\langle v_{i},v_{n}\rangle-2\langle x,v_{n}\rangle\langle v_{n},v_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\langle x,v_{n}\rangle-2\langle x,v_{n}\rangle=0.

\displaystyle\hfill\vartriangleleft

By
1
part 1, we can compute
x_{1}=M^{\prime}x_{0}
by a multiplication with
M
and inner product between
x_{0}
and
v_{n}
. The latter can be computed in
O(D)
time. Due to
1
part 2, we have that the inner product between
x_{i}
and
v_{n}
is zero for all
i\geq 1
. Hence, after the first iteration we obtain
M^{\prime}x_{i}=Mx_{i}
and we can simply multiply with
M
instead of
M^{\prime}
.

Bit precision.

As in
Theorem

12
, we have by
Lemma

11
that
O(\log n)
bits suffice to store each value.

However, rounding might introduce a small error in the direction of
v_{n}
. As opposed to
Lemma

11
, this error could blow up over time: it scales with
\mu_{n}^{l}
after
l
rounds, while the vector’s component we want scales with
\mu_{n-1}^{l}
. The number of iterations
l
after which this becomes a problem that depends on the ratio
\mu_{n}/\mu_{n-1}
. We will let this error blow up for
l
iterations, and then project again on the subspace orthogonal to
v_{n}
, using diameter time as above. Before we describe that, we prove how large
l
can be.

Formally, we prove the following claim, where
\delta v_{n}
denotes the error. For errors perpendicular to
v_{n}
, we can refer to
Lemma

11
, which also shows rounding errors are of size at most
\delta\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||
.

Claim 2
.

Let
x_{i}
be a vector such that
\langle x_{i},v_{n}\rangle=0
, and
0<\delta\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||
be a parameter. Then for
l\leq O(\tfrac{\log n}{\lambda_{2}})
we have

\left|\frac{(x_{i}+\delta v_{n}))^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})}{(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})^{T}M^{2l}(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})}-\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}}{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l}x_{i}}\right|\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}}{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l}x_{i}}.

Proof.

We first investigate the first term, i.e., the Rayleigh coefficient including the error after
l
iterations

\displaystyle\frac{(x_{i}+\delta v_{n}))^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})}{(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})^{T}M^{2l}(x_{i}+\delta v_{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}+\delta^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l+1}}{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l}x_{i}+\delta^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l}}\leq\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}+n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l+1}}{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l}x_{i}-n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l}},

where the equality follows from orthonormality of the eigenvectors, and the inequality follows from our bound on the error. If we now show for the numerator that
n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}
, then this also holds for the denominator (since this is a factor of
M
less), and hence we obtain that the Rayleigh coefficient after
l
iterations changed by a factor at most
(1+n^{-\Theta(1)})
. Hereto, we inspect
x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}
. We note that
|\langle x_{0},\rangle|\geq\tfrac{3}{16}
. We obtain

\displaystyle x_{0}^{T}M^{2(i+l)+1}x_{0}=x_{0}^{T}M^{2(i+l)+1}x_{0}\geq\mu_{n-1}^{2(i+l)+1}|\langle x_{0},\rangle|^{2}\geq\mu_{n-1}^{2(i+l)+1}\left(\frac{3}{16}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, we have that

||x_{i}||^{2}=x_{0}^{T}M^{2i}x_{0}\leq\mu_{n-1}^{2i}\cdot||x_{0}||^{2}=\mu_{n-1}^{2i}\cdot n.

Putting this together, we obtain that

\displaystyle n^{-\Theta(1)}||x_{i}||^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l+1}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}\cdot\mu_{n-1}^{2i}n\cdot\mu_{n}^{2l+1}=n^{-\Theta(1)}\cdot\mu_{n-1}^{2(i+l)+1}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n-1}}\right)^{2l+1}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n-1}}\right)^{2l+1}\cdot x_{0}^{T}M^{2(i+l)+1}x_{0}.

If we now show that

\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n-1}}\right)^{2l+1}\leq n^{\Theta(1)},

then we are done. Here we use that
l
is bounded: after many iterations, the largest eigenvector will always dominate. By assumption, we have
l=\Theta(\tfrac{\log n}{\lambda_{2}})
. Using this, together with
\mu_{n}=2
and
\mu_{n-1}=2-\lambda_{2}
, we see

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n-1}}\right)^{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{2-\lambda_{2}}\right)^{l}\leq(1+\lambda_{2})^{l}\leq\exp(\lambda_{2}\cdot l)=\exp(\Theta(\log n)=n^{\Theta(1)}.

\displaystyle\hfill\vartriangleleft

This claim shows that for
l=\Theta(\log n/\lambda_{2})
iterations, the rounding error does not propagate too much.
After every
\Theta(\log n/\lambda_{2})
iterations, we orthogonalize again. We can simply do this by computing
x-\langle x,v_{n}\rangle v_{n}
, since
v_{n}
is known:
v_{n}=\sqrt{\deg}
. However, this leads to
O(\left\lceil\tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil)
additional inner products.

Success Probability.

Again, we note that
Lemma

7
only gives constant probability. We can simply repeat the process
O(\log n)
times and take the maximum to obtain the result with high probability, since we cannot overshoot by
Lemma

10
. As before, we can stream the
O(\lceil\lambda_{2}/\varepsilon\rceil)
inner products, so the total time becomes:

O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\log n\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil+D\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right).

We can also simplify this to

,
using that
\lambda_{2}\leq 2
.

2.5
The
k
Smallest Eigenvalues

In this section, we generalize the method from
Section

2.4
. The general idea remains to change the matrix by projecting on the space orthogonal to the found eigenvectors. However, the found eigenvectors are now no longer exact, but approximate. We first show that the power method can work with such approximations as well.
The following lemma captures the main result of this section.

Lemma 14
.

Let
k\geq 0
be a constant.
Suppose
\tilde{\mu}_{i},\tilde{v}_{i}
for
i\geq n-k
are orthonormal vectors that approximate the eigenvalues thus-far:
\tilde{\mu}_{i}=\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}M\tilde{v}_{i}
and
(1-\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(\varepsilon))\mu_{i}\leq\tilde{\mu}_{i}\leq\mu_{i}
.
Performing
O(\log n)
instances of the power method – each with
O(\log n/\varepsilon)
iterations – with the matrix

M_{\rm{approx}}:=M-\sum_{i>n-k}\tilde{\mu}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}
gives w.h.p. either a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation
\tilde{\mu}_{n-k}
of the
k
-th largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n-k}
, together with a corresponding vector
\tilde{v}_{n-k}
, or we can output that
\mu_{n-k}<\varepsilon
.

Suppose we know the exact eigenvalues and eigenvectors
\mu_{i},v_{i}
for
i>n-k
, consider
M_{\rm{ideal}}:=M-\sum_{i>n-k}\mu_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}
. This matrix has the same eigenvectors
v_{i}
, but now largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n-k}
. This follows from orthonormality of the eigenvalues. Indeed we see for
i>n-k

\displaystyle(M-\sum_{i>n-k}\mu_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T})v_{j}

\displaystyle=Mv_{j}-\mu_{j}v_{j}=0

and for
i\leq n-k

\displaystyle(M-\sum_{i>n-k}\mu_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T})v_{j}

\displaystyle=Mv_{j}=\mu_{j}v_{j}.

So if we obtain a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation of the largest eigenvalue of matrix
M_{\rm{ideal}}
, we are done. We will show that the largest eigenvalue of
M_{\rm{approx}}
is a
(1-\varepsilon)
approximation of the largest eigenvalue of
M_{\rm{ideal}}
. The power method gives with constant probability a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation of the largest eigenvalue. Repeating this
O(\log n)
times and taking the maximum over these iterations gives the result with high probability. Note that we cannot overshoot by
Lemma

10
, hence taking the maximum indeed works. We can stream these iterations such that the
\log n
-factor does not multiply with
D
.

We note that a big difference to
Theorem

13
is that we now do not have exact orthogonality
at any point
in time. This also means that rounding does not change this fact. Such errors are corrected by taking inner products out
every step
– rather than periodically.

Furthermore, the reader might notice that we state
Lemma

7
for positive semi-definite matrices, and we no longer claim that
M_{\rm{approx}}
is positive semi-definite. There are two solutions for this: 1) the power method also works on general matrices
[
GVL13
]
. The running time is a constant factor slower, and it finds the
dominant
eigenvalue rather than the largest. 2) We note that
M_{\rm{ideal}}

is
positive semi-definite. Since we show that the eigenvalues of
M_{\rm{ideal}}
and
M_{\rm{approx}}
are close, this means that the eigenvalues in
M_{\rm{approx}}
are at least
-\varepsilon
. By running the power method on
M_{\rm{approx}}+\varepsilon I
, we have a PSD matrix again, and can use
Lemma

7
as before. The additive matrix
+\varepsilon I
has a linear effect on the eigenvalue approximation, so we can simply remove it at the end.

It remains to show that
M_{\rm{approx}}
approximates
M_{\rm{ideal}}
, which we do in
Section

2.5.1
. Then we show how this applies to the Laplacian in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model in
Section

2.5.2
.

2.5.1
M_{\rm{approx}}
approximates
M_{\rm{ideal}}
.

First, we note that we can assume the spectrum to be relatively simple.

Lemma 15
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the eigenvalues take values in
\{\mu_{n},(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n},(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\mu_{n},\dots\}
.

Proof.

Instead of analyzing the matrix
M
, with orthonormal eigensystem
\{(\mu_{i},v_{i})\}
, we analyze the following system. We round every eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
down to
\hat{\mu}_{i}
, the next value in
\{\mu_{n},(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n},(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\mu_{n},\dots\}
. Then we consider the matrix

M^{\prime}:=M-\sum_{i\in[n]}(\mu_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i})v_{i}v_{i}^{T}.

By orthonormality of the eigenvectors,
M^{\prime}
has the same eigenvectors. Its eigenvalues, however, have changed to
\hat{\mu}_{i}
. This gives an error of at most
\epsilon\mu_{i}
for each eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
. Setting
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/2
, makes that this additional error does not impact the final answer.
∎

In particular, this means that the second largest eigenvalue is at most
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}
. This gives us the following lemma, stating that the previous iterations are well aligned with the eigenvectors.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose
||y||=1
and
\frac{y^{T}My}{y^{T}y}\geq(1-\delta)\mu_{n}
. Then

\langle y,v_{n}\rangle\geq\sqrt{1-\delta/\epsilon}.

Proof.

Since
|y||=1
, we have
y^{T}My\geq(1-\delta)\mu_{n}
. We use this to see:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle y,v_{i}\rangle^{2}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle y,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}\right)^{T}M\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle y,v_{i}\rangle v_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=y^{T}My\geq(1-\delta)\mu_{n}.

Since the eigenvalues are
(1-\varepsilon)
apart, let
j
the highest index such that
\mu_{j}<(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}
, which means that
\mu_{i}=\mu_{n}
for
i>j
. So

\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}\langle y,v_{n}\rangle^{2}\mu_{n}+\sum_{i\leq j}\langle y,v_{n}\rangle^{2}(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta)\mu_{n}.

Using that
y
has norm 1, and that
\mu_{n}>0

\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}\langle y,v_{n}\rangle^{2}+(1-\sum_{i>j}\langle y,v_{n}\rangle^{2})(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\delta).

Rearranging gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}\langle y,v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta/\epsilon.

However,
v_{i}
for
i>j
all have the same eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
, so we can just rearrange the orthonormal basis such that
\langle y,v_{n}\rangle\geq\sqrt{1-\delta/\epsilon}
.
∎

Intuitively, this means that if we set
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}
, we get
\langle y,v_{n}\rangle\geq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\geq 1-\varepsilon
. In reality, the analysis is a bit more involved – so we carry the
\delta
for now.

Next, we show that if the inner product of two vectors is small, the difference in their projections is also small. Here, we denote
||A||
for the
matrix norm
or
operator norm
of a matrix
A
, defined by
||A||:=\max_{||x||=1}||Ax||=\max_{||x||=1}x^{T}A^{T}Ax
. Note that in our case of symmetric, positive semi-definite matrices, the norm of a matrix is bounded by its largest eigenvalue squared.

Lemma 17
.

If
||v||=||w||=1
,
\langle v,w\rangle\geq 1-\eta
and
a,b\geq 0
,
0<\eta<1
such that
(1-\eta)a\leq b\leq a
, then we have

||avv^{T}-bww^{T}||\leq 5\eta b
.

Proof.

First we note that

\displaystyle||avv^{T}-bww^{T}||

\displaystyle=||(a-b)vv^{T}+b(vv^{T}-ww^{T})||\leq||(a-b)vv^{T}||+||b(vv^{T}-ww^{T})||

\displaystyle=|a-b|\cdot||vv^{T}||+b||vv^{T}-ww^{T}||\leq b(\eta+||vv^{T}-ww^{T}||).

Next, we bound
||vv^{T}-ww^{T}||
:

\displaystyle||vv^{T}-ww^{T}||

\displaystyle=||(v-w)v^{T}+w(v-w)^{T}||\leq||(v-w)v^{T}||+||w(v-w)^{T}||

\displaystyle=||v-w||\cdot||v||+||w||\cdot||v-w||=2||v-w||,

where we know that

\displaystyle||v-w||

\displaystyle=\langle v-w,v-w\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle+\langle w,w\rangle-2\langle v,w\rangle=2(1-\langle v,w\rangle).

Putting this together we obtain

\displaystyle||avv^{T}-bww^{T}||\leq b(\eta+||vv^{T}-ww^{T}||)\leq b(\eta+2||v-w||)\leq b(\eta+4(1-\langle v,w\rangle))\leq 5\eta b.

∎

Finally, we use the following theorem from perturbation theory (see, e.g.,
[
HJ12
]
).

Theorem 18

(Weyl’s Inequality)
.

Let
A,E
be real, symmetric
n
by
n
matrices. Let
\mu_{i}(A),\mu_{i}(A+E)
denote the
i
-th eigenvalue of
A
,
A+E
respectively, for
i=1,\dots n
. Then we have

|\mu_{i}(A+E)-\mu_{i}(A)|\leq||E||.

We apply
Lemma

16
,
Lemma

17
and
Theorem

18
to our case:

\displaystyle|\mu_{i}(M_{\rm{ideal}})-\mu_{i}(M_{\rm{approx}})|

\displaystyle\leq||M_{\rm{ideal}}-M_{\rm{approx}}||

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\sum_{i>n-k}\tilde{\mu}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}-\sum_{i>n-k}\mu_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i>n-k}\left|\left|\tilde{\mu}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}-\mu_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i>n-k}5\cdot\delta/\varepsilon\cdot\mu_{i}

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot 5\cdot\delta/\varepsilon\cdot\mu_{n}.

We need this to be at most
\varepsilon\mu_{n-k}/2
, where the factor two comes from
Lemma

15
. Hereto, we set
\delta_{i}:=\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{10}\tfrac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{n-k}}
. Finally, we use that
\mu_{n}\leq 2
and that we can assume
\mu_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon
. The latter holds, because if
\mu_{n-1}<\varepsilon
, we still have that
\tilde{\mu}_{n-1}\leq\mu_{n-1}
. It might be below
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n-1}
, but we can recognize this case, as needed for

.
So we can bound

by

2.5.2

k
-th Smallest Eigenvalue in
\mathsf{CONGEST}

Theorem 19

(Approximating
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a parameter and
k\geq 1
be a constant. There exists a
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithm that, given an undirected, weighted graph
G=(V,E)
, with high probability finds
k
approximations
\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\tilde{\lambda}_{2},\tilde{\lambda}_{3},\dots,\tilde{\lambda}_{k}
of the first
k
eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian such that

\lambda_{i}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon,

in
O(\log^{2}n\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

Proof.

First, we show that we can assume the diameter to be small. We check in
O(k\log n/\varepsilon)
rounds if the diameter is bigger than
\Theta(k\log n/\varepsilon)
.
As seen in
Lemma

21
, if
D>\Theta(k\log n/\varepsilon)
, we have that
\phi_{k}\leq\varepsilon
.
By
Theorem

9
, we have that
\lambda_{k}\leq 2\phi_{k}\leq 2\varepsilon
. So we set
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/2
. And can then output
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=\varepsilon
for all
i\leq k
. For the remainder, we can assume that
D=O(k\log n/\varepsilon)
.

By
Lemma

14
, we need to run the power method with the matrix
M_{\rm{approx}}=M-\sum_{i>n-k}\tilde{\mu}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}
. We need to run
O(\log n)
instances to obtain our result with high probability, and each instance needs
O(\log n/\delta)
iterations, with
\delta=\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(1/\varepsilon)
. Note that each iteration requires the previous iteration to be more precise. In the end reaching a bigger
\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(\varepsilon^{-1})
.

Note that we are not running the power method with a locally defined matrix anymore. We use
M_{\rm{approx}}:=M-\sum_{i>n-k}\tilde{\mu}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}^{T}
, where
M=2I-L

is
locally defined. Compute
M_{\rm{approx}}x
consists of multiple steps. First we compute

, which can be done in
O(1)
rounds. Next, we need to compute each of the at most
k

\lambda_{1}=0
, with eigenvector
\sqrt{\deg}
. For
M_{\rm{approx}}
, this means the largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n}=2
with eigenvector

. We know this vector exactly, and do not need to use an approximation.

So we get total time
O(\log n\cdot\log n/\delta+\log n/\delta\cdot k\cdot(D+\log n)=O(\log^{2}n/\delta+\log nD)
.
Filling in

and
\delta=\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(1/\varepsilon)
gives the result.
∎

v\perp w
we have

\lambda_{2}\leq\frac{v^{T}L(G_{k,l})v}{v^{T}v}.

We have
\phi(G_{k,l})=\Theta(1/k)
, there are two types of cuts to inspect to proof this: cutting through the cycle or cutting through the clique. Cutting through the cycle is most balanced, hence sparsest, if the cut
S
is closest to the cycle. In this case we have

\phi(S)=\frac{|E(S,V\setminus S)|}{\min\{\Vol(S),\Vol(V\setminus S)\}}=\frac{2}{\min\{\Theta(l^{2}),2(k-1)\}}=\Theta(1/k).

Cutting through the clique always gives a bigger value: say we have
l^{\prime}<l
vertices of the clique in
S
, then

\phi(S)=\frac{|E(S,V\setminus S)|}{\min\{\Vol(S),\Vol(V\setminus S)\}}=\frac{l^{\prime}(l-l^{\prime})}{\min\{l^{\prime}(l-1),(l-l^{\prime})(l-1)+2k\}}\geq\frac{l-l^{\prime}}{l-1}\geq\frac{1}{l-1}=\Theta(1/n).

Since Cheeger’s Inequality,
Theorem

8
, gives us that
\lambda_{2}\geq\phi^{2}/2
, we get
\lambda_{2}(G_{k,l})=\Theta(1/k^{2})
.

Claim 2.
We have
\lambda_{2}(G_{k,l,l^{\prime}})=O(1/k^{2})
.
The upper bound follows from the same argument as in Claim 1, with the additional observation that
v_{k/2}=0
as well.

Suppose a vertex
v
sees its
r
-hop neighborhood, for some
r<k/2
. This means the vertex sees at most one clique, say of
l
vertices. The graph can still be
G_{k,l}
or
G_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
. For any
k\geq 2r
(and
k\leq n-l
). So we need to output
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}\geq\Theta(1/r^{2})
. However, the graph can also be
G_{2D,l,l^{\prime}}
with
\lambda_{2}(G_{2D,l,l^{\prime}})\leq O(1/D^{2})
. So we need
\Theta(1/r^{2})\leq(1+\varepsilon)O(1/D^{2})
, so
r=\Omega(D)
.
∎

2.7
Approximating the Entire Spectrum

Via similar methods, one can also approximate the entire spectrum. However, the output will not reveal how many eigenvalues there are of a certain approximation, i.e., it gives the spectrum
without multiplicity
.

Remark 20
.

We can compute a multiplicative approximation of the entire spectrum (without multiplicity) in
n^{o(1)}(\sqrt{n}+D)/\varepsilon^{2}
rounds.

Sketch.

Note that this statement does not have a linear dependency on
n
, even though there are
n
eigenvalues and from a high level point of view we would need to address them sequentially with the power method. The linear dependency does not appear in the runtime as we only compute the spectrum approximately and without multiplicity. Thus, essentially we only need to test
O(\log(Wn)/\varepsilon)
regimes in
[0,2]
for the existence of an eigenvalue.

The algorithm is now as follows. One can also find an eigenvalue with
\sigma
by the power method on
(L-\sigma I)^{-1}
, with
\tilde{\sigma}
some estimate of
\sigma
. By running this with
\sigma=2/(1+\varepsilon)^{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,O(\log(Wn)/\varepsilon)
, we cover the entire spectrum. Now we have to perform matrix-vector multiplication with
(L-\sigma I)^{-1}
, which takes
n^{o(1)}(\sqrt{n}+D)
rounds in each step
[
FGLP+21
]
. Multiplying this by
O(\log n/\varepsilon)
iterations, we obtain in total
n^{o(1)}(\sqrt{n}+D)/\varepsilon^{2}
rounds.
∎

3
Approximating the Sparsest Cut

In this section, we use the approximate eigenvalues from
Section

2.7
to approximate the sparsest cut in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model. We also give the complexity of the problem in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model and we provide a lower bound in
unweighted
graphs.

k
-Way Sparsest Cut and Diameter

We first observe that the diameter of a graph is related to its
k
-way sparsest cut. The result follows from a ball growing argument, that is (at least) known for the case
k=2
. We prove the generalization for completeness.

Lemma 21
.

If
G=(V,E)
has
k
-way sparsest cut
\phi_{k}
, then we have
D=O(k\log n/\phi_{k})
.

Proof.

Suppose not. This means that from any vertex
v_{1}
, there are vertices up to distance
\Theta(k\log n/\phi_{k})
. Select vertices
v_{2},v_{3},\dots,v_{k}
such that
d(v_{i},v_{j})>\Theta(\log n/\phi_{k})
for
i\neq j
. Now, for any
v_{i}
, suppose for contradiction that there are no cuts
S
within a distance
\Theta(\log n/\phi_{k})
with
\phi(S)\leq\phi_{k}
. Then for any
j\leq\log n/\phi_{k}
, the
j
-hop neighborhood
N_{j}(v_{i})
satisfies
|E(N_{j}(v_{i}),V\setminus N_{j}(v_{i}))|\geq\phi_{k}\Vol(N_{j}(v_{i}))
, so within
\log_{1+\phi_{k}}n=O(\log n/\phi_{k})
steps, we cover the entire graph, a contradiction. ∎

Appendix A
The Power Method

The power method is a standard technique in numerical linear algebra, see, e.g.,
[
GVL13
]
. Although it works for any type of matrix, both its statement and analyses are simpler for positive semi-definite instances. Since this suffices fo our purposes, we restrict to this case. In
Section

A.1
, we prove the known
Lemma

7
, following
[
Tre15
]
.
In
Section

A.2
we prove the bit precision lemma (
Lemma

11
) required for an efficient CONGEST implementation.

A.1
The Power Method (Proof of Lemma
7
)

Algorithm.

The power method starts with initial vector
x_{0}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}
, chosen uniformly at random vector.
We then compute
x_{k}:=M^{k}x_{0}
. The goal is to show that the Rayleigh coefficient of
x_{k}
approximates the largest eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
well:

(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\leq\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}\leq\mu_{n}.

Start vector.

Notably, the start vector
x
has to have a significant inner product with the largest eigenvector
v_{n}
.
Choosing
x
uniformly at random ensures that this is true with constant probability. For this proof, we use the Paley-Zygmund Inequality.

Lemma 22

(Paley-Zygmund Inequality)
.

Let
X\geq 0
be a random variable with finite variance.
For all
0\leq\delta\leq 1
holds that

\mathbb{P}[X\geq\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}(X)]\geq(1-\delta)^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}(X)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}.

The power method fails to work if the random start vector is orthogonal to the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue. The following lemma states that it is unlikely to happen.

Lemma 23
.

Let
x\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}
be a uniformly random vector and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a vector with
\lVert y\rVert=1
.
Then we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\lvert\langle x,y\rangle\rvert\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\right]\geq\tfrac{3}{16}.

Proof.

Let
X=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}y_{i}
be the random variable defined by the inner product
\langle x,y\rangle
.
As
X
is not necessarily non-negative, we base our calculation on
X^{2}
which is always non-negative.
First, we consider the second moment of
X
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}y_{i}\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}y_{i}y_{j}\mathbb{E}(x_{i}x_{j})=\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{2}\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{2})+\sum_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}y_{i}y_{j}\mathbb{E}(x_{i}x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}y_{i}y_{j}=\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{2}=1

In the fourth step, we apply
\mathbb{E}(x_{i}x_{j})=0
and
\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{2})=1
.
The last equality follows from the fact that
\lVert y\rVert=1
.
Next, consider the fourth moment of
X
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{4})

\displaystyle=3\cdot\sum_{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{2})y_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}(x_{j}^{2})y_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{4})y_{i}^{4}=3\cdot\sum_{i,j\in[n]}y_{i}^{2}y_{j}^{2}-3\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{4}+\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle=3\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}-2\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{4}=3-2\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{4}\leq 3.

For the sake of simplicity, the first step leaves out any summations that contain
\mathbb{E}(x_{i})
as all of them are equal to zero.
In the second step, we use the fact that
\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{2})=1=\mathbb{E}(x_{i}^{4})
.

Now, we are in the position to apply the Paley-Zygmund inequality.
Let
\delta=1/4
.
As
X^{2}
is a non-negative random variable with finite variance, it follows from
Lemma

22
that

\mathbb{P}[X^{2}\geq\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}(X^{2})]=\mathbb{P}[X^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{4}]\geq(1-\tfrac{1}{4})^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}(X^{4})}=(\tfrac{3}{4})^{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}=\tfrac{3}{16}.\qed

Lower Bound.

Lemma

7
states that the Rayleigh coefficient output by the power method is lower bounded by
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}
. Next, we prove a slightly weaker lower bound; the original lower bound is obtained in the proof of
Lemma

7
by adjusting
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set
x_{k}=M^{k}x
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
and
k\geq\log n/\varepsilon
, we have

\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{\langle x_{0},v_{n}\rangle^{2}}(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}}.

(2)

Proof.

We start by dividing the eigenvalues
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\dotsc\leq\mu_{n}
of
M
in two groups.
Let
j\in[n]
be the first index for which
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\leq\mu_{j}
holds, then we have
\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mu_{j-1}<(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}
and
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\leq\mu_{j},\dotsc,\mu_{n}
.
As a direct consequence, it holds that

\sum_{i\in[j-1]}\mu_{i}^{2k}\leq n(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\leq\frac{\mu_{n}^{2k}}{n}.

Intuitively, the contribution of the small eigenvalue group to the spectral decomposition of
M^{2k+1}
is insignificant for large
k
.
With this insight, we use the spectral decomposition of
M^{2k+1}
to derive a lower bound for the nominator

\displaystyle x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}=x_{0}^{T}M^{2k+1}x_{0}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{T}\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i}^{2k+1}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\right)x_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i}^{2k+1}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k+1}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}.

Second, we derive an upper bound for the denominator

\displaystyle x_{k}^{T}x_{k}=x_{0}^{T}M^{2k}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}+\sum_{i\in\{1,\dotsc,j-1\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\sum_{i\in\{1,\dotsc,j-1\}}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}+(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2},

where the last inequality follows from
\sum_{i\in[j-1]}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}\leq\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}
. Using both upper and lower bound, we can bound the ratio as follows

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}=\frac{x_{0}^{T}M^{2k+1}x_{0}}{x_{0}^{T}M^{2k}x_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}+\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}}{1+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}}{\sum_{i\in\{j,\dotsc,n\}}\mu_{i}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{i}\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}}{1+\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\mu_{n}^{2k}\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}}{\mu_{n}^{2k}\langle x_{0},v_{n}\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}}{1+(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\frac{\lVert x_{0}\rVert^{2}}{\langle x_{0},v_{n}\rangle^{2}}}.\qed

Upper Bound.

Next, we show that we cannot overshoot, i.e, that the output of the power method is upper bounded by
\mu_{n}
.
See
10

Proof.

We decompose
x
into the eigenvectors, i.e., let
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
be
\alpha_{j}:=\langle x,v_{j}\rangle
, and hence
x=\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}v_{j}
. Now we observe

\displaystyle x^{T}Mx

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}v_{j}\right)^{T}\left(M\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}v_{j}\right)\right)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}^{2}\mu_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\max_{k\in[n]}\mu_{k}\right)\alpha_{j}^{2}=\mu_{n}\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}^{2}=\mu_{n}\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}v_{j}\right)^{T}\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}v_{j}\right)=\mu_{n}x^{T}x.\qed

Conclusion.

We conclude by putting the pieces together.
See
7

Proof.

Due to
Lemma

23
and
\lVert x\rVert^{2}=n
, we have
\lVert x\rVert^{2}/\langle x,v_{1}\rangle^{2}\leq 4n
.
Thus, the right denominator in
Equation

2
can be bounded by

1+\frac{\lVert x\rVert^{2}}{\langle x,v_{1}\rangle^{2}}(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\leq 1+4n\cdot e^{-2\log n}\leq 1+\tfrac{4}{n}.

For any
\varepsilon\geq 8/n
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}
,
Equation

2
simplifies to

\frac{x_{k}^{T}Mx_{k}}{x_{k}^{T}x_{k}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{4}{n}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{n}\cdot(1-\tfrac{8}{n})\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\mu_{n}.

By
Lemma

10
, we get the upper bound.
∎

A.2
Bit Precision Lemma (Proof of Lemma
11
)

The following lemma shows that truncating or rounding after the
\Theta(\log n)
-th bit only impact the outcome by an inverse polynomial factor. This means we can round up to
\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(n)
times without any issues. In particular, we can round in every step of the algorithm with a negligibly small effect on the outcome.

See
11

Proof.

The power method starts with
x_{0}\sim\{+1,-1\}
uniformly at random, so
||x_{0}||=\sqrt{n}
, and with constant probability,
Lemma

23
, we have
|\langle x_{0},v_{n}\rangle|\geq\tfrac{3}{16}
. In which case the power method gives a good approximation. We investigate the numerical stability of this case.

We need to compare before and after rounding for

\frac{(x_{k})^{T}Mx_{k}}{(x_{k})^{T}x_{k}}=\frac{x_{0}^{T}M^{2k+1}x_{0}}{x_{0}M^{2k}x_{0}}=\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}}{x_{i}M^{2l}x_{i}},

where
l:=k-i
. Denote the difference occurred by rounding as
y
, i.e., after rounding we have
x_{i}+y
and since we round after
\Theta(\log n)
bits, we have
|(y)_{v}|\leq\delta|(x_{i})_{v}|
for some
\delta=n^{-\Theta(1)}
. In particular
||y||\leq\delta||x_{i}||
.

Now, let use make some observations. First of all,

\displaystyle|\langle x_{i},v_{n}\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{n}^{i}|\langle x_{0},v_{n}\rangle|\geq\mu_{n}^{i}\left(\frac{3}{16}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle||x_{i}||

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{n}^{i}||x_{0}||=\mu_{n}^{i}\sqrt{n}.

Next, we use this to bound our main inner product:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\langle x_{i},v_{n}\rangle^{2}\geq\mu_{n}^{2k+1}\cdot\frac{3}{16}.

Or equivalently,
\mu_{n}^{2k+1}\leq\Theta(1)x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}
. Next, we use Cauchy-Schwarz to express the incurred error in terms of this inner product.

\displaystyle|y^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq||y||\cdot||M^{2l+1}x_{i}||\leq\delta||x_{i}||\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\leq\delta\mu^{2k+1}\sqrt{n}\leq\Theta(\sqrt{n})x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}

\displaystyle|x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}y|

\displaystyle\leq||M^{2l+1}x_{i}||\cdot||y||\leq\delta||x_{i}||\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\leq\delta\mu^{2k+1}\sqrt{n}\leq\Theta(\sqrt{n})x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}

\displaystyle|y^{T}M^{2l+1}y|

\displaystyle\leq||y||^{2}\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\leq\delta^{2}||x_{i}||\mu_{n}^{2l+1}\leq\delta^{2}\mu_{n}^{2k+1}\sqrt{n}\leq\delta\mu_{n}^{2k+1}\leq\Theta(\sqrt{n})x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i},

Now, we are ready to consider
\frac{(x_{i}+y)^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+y)}{(x_{i}+y)M^{2l}(x_{i}+y)}
.
We do this in two steps: numerator and denominator. First we note for the numerator that

\displaystyle(x_{i}+y)^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+y)

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}-|y^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}|-|x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}y|-|y^{T}M^{2l+1}y|

\displaystyle\geq(1-3\delta\Theta(\sqrt{n}))x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}.

For the denominator, we obtain via similar arguments that

(x_{i}+y)M^{2l}(x_{i}+y)\leq(1+3\delta\Theta(\sqrt{n}))x_{i}M^{2l}x_{i}.

So in total we have

\frac{(x_{i}+y)^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+y)}{(x_{i}+y)M^{2l}(x_{i}+y)}\geq\frac{1-3\delta\Theta(\sqrt{n})}{1+3\delta\Theta(\sqrt{n})}\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}}{x_{i}M^{2l}x_{i}}.

For
\delta\leq n^{-\Theta(1)}
, we obtain

\frac{(x_{i}+y)^{T}M^{2l+1}(x_{i}+y)}{(x_{i}+y)M^{2l}(x_{i}+y)}\geq(1-n^{-\Theta(1)})\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{T}M^{2l+1}x_{i}}{x_{i}M^{2l}x_{i}}.

Similar arguments give the upper bound with
(1+n^{-\Theta(1)})
.
∎
