Prevalence and predictors of anxiety and depression among breast cancer patients in low-and middle-income countries: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zoia Sharif, Muhammad Ashfaq, Qasim Khan, Ayesha Khan, Ghazala Bhatti, Muhammad Junaid Hassan Sharif, Muhammad Junaid Farrukh, Ayesha Siddiqua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer diagnosis and treatment can be profoundly traumatic for women, often affecting psychological well-being, with many experiencing anxiety and depression especially in resource limited settings. This study examines the prevalence and predictors of anxiety and depression in breast cancer patients in Abbottabad, Pakistan.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted on 96 female breast cancer survivors receiving treatment (October 2023 to May 2024) at the Islamabad Diagnostic Center Breast Cancer Clinic and Ayub Teaching Hospital MTI. Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 Questionnaire (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).ResultsThe study population was characterized by a high proportion of patients with no formal education (52.1%) and unemployment (91.7%). The prevalence of anxiety and depression was substantially high at 41.7% and 79.2%, respectively. Regression analysis identified age and stage of cancer as significant predictors of anxiety and depression. Multivariate logistic regression identified younger age (20-40 years) and Stage 2 Cancer (compared to Stage 4) as significant predictors of anxiety, while age (41-60 years) was the main significant predictor of depression. ConclusionThe high burden of depression (79.2%) and anxiety (41.7%) among breast cancer patients in this low-income setting is alarming. Age and cancer stage are critical predictive factors, underscoring the need for targeted mental health interventions and support strategies for younger patients and those in earlier stages of treatment.

Introduction

Breast cancer constitutes a significant global health burden, representing the most diagnosed cancer worldwide and accounting for approximately 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths in 2020 [1, 2]. The complex processes of diagnosing and treating this malignancy elicit intense emotional and physiological responses in patients. Consequently, depressive and anxiety symptoms are prevalent comorbidities among individuals with oncological illnesses [3, 4].

One of the most prevalent psychological symptoms among patients with breast cancer is anxiety, which affects around 10–30% of patients [5]. It is a complex concept that compromises a patient’s quality of life and treatment outcomes [6]. Furthermore, depression is also commonly experienced, characterized by symptoms such as sleeplessness, suicidal thoughts, exhaustion and psychomotor issues [7].

These phycological symptoms often arise from disease-related factors such as disability, progression of pain, and treatment-related factors such as side effects, alongside psychological issues like grief and fear [7]. While the impact of the disease varies with stage and prognosis, it is associated with a higher risk of depression and anxiety compared to other chronic illnesses. These mood disorders can significantly affect treatment adherence and increase mortality [8]. Therefore, early interventions are crucial for addressing these psychosocial challenges between individuals and their partners, eventually improving their quality of life [9]. Despite the availability of effective psycho-oncological interventions, these disorders often remain untreated [10].

While the burden of breast cancer is global, Low and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) face unique challenges that exacerbate its impact. In Pakistan, one in every nine women is affected by breast cancer, giving the country a high incidence rate which is worsened by widespread poverty, limited access to affordable screening, and low awareness about early detection [11, 12]. These systemic barriers lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, contributing to persistently high mortality rates compared to developed nations [2].

Despite existing evidence linking breast cancer to mental health concerns, a critical gap remains in understanding the precise risk factors for anxiety and depression within the LMICs. Identifying predictors of anxiety and depression is essential to guide mental health interventions and improve quality of life for breast cancer patients. Hence, this study aims to explore the prevalence of anxiety and depression along with predictive factors in breast cancer patients.

Methodology

Study design & setting

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at the Islamabad Diagnostic Center Breast Cancer Clinic (IDC) and the Ayub Teaching Hospital (ATH), Abbottabad, Pakistan, between October 2023 and May 2024. The study included patients who had been diagnosed with breast cancer, were currently receiving breast cancer treatment, and were 18 or more years of age, whereas patients under age 18 were excluded.

Sampling technique and calculation

Data was collected using a convenience sampling technique due to ease of access and cost effectiveness, despite its limitations. All patients meeting the study criteria were included within the study period. A sample size of 101 participants was estimated to be appropriate for the study.

Data collection tool and procedure

Validated structured questionnaires (Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 Questionnaire (GAD-7)) were used for data collection. Verbal consent was obtained from the patients, and their rights were explained to them, which also included confidentiality of information and their freedom to discontinue at any time. The researcher questioned the patients in a separate examination room at the oncology department and recorded their responses.

Part A: Demographics: This section included information about social and demographic factors like age, marital status, occupation, education, stage of breast cancer, and therapy received.

Part B: The PHQ-9 is a structured, self-administered scale to assess the level of depression. It is comprised of 9 items [13]. Each question has 4 responses: “0: not at all,” “1: a few days,” “2: more than half the days,” or “3: almost every day.” The whole score ranges from 0 to 27. A score of ≤ 10 was considered the absence of depression, whereas a score of ≥ 10 was considered the presence of depression.

Part C: The GAD-7 is a self-administered scale to assess symptoms of anxiety [14]. There are four possible answers for each of the seven items in the list: “0-not at all,” “1-several days,” “2-more than half the days,” or “3-nearly every day.” The total score has a range of 0 to 21. A score of ≤ 10 was then considered as an absence of anxiety, whereas a score of ≥ 10 was considered as the presence of anxiety.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) v21. Frequencies and percentages were used as descriptive statistics. Relationships between the dependent variables, (presence/absence of depression and anxiety), and the independent variables were analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression.

Ethical approval

The ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Ayub Medical Teaching Hospital, Abbottabad, Institutional Medical and Ethics Review Committee (Ref.No.EA-2024/008). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards, whereas verbal consent was obtained from participants to participate in the survey due to the sensitivity of the study population. All information obtained ensured the confidentiality and security of the participant’s identification.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

This study included 101 patients, out of which 96 valid responses were recorded. The majority participants were middle aged women (41–60 years), who were largely married and resided in urban areas. The sociodemographic data indicated the low resource setting with more than half of participants being illiterate or lacking formal education and nearly all were unemployed. Among their clinical characteristics majority patients were in Stage 2 and Stage 3 of cancer. Patients receiving more than one treatment were categorized as their primary or most recent treatment type with most patients (47.9%) receiving chemotherapy, with only 6.3% on radiotherapy. Discussion

Prevalence of anxiety and depression in breast cancer patients

Our study confirms that anxiety and depression are mental illnesses that significantly affect the health of cancer patients. Symptoms of anxiety & depression often co-exist among patients with oncological diseases, and from the time of pre-diagnosis until the end of their lives, breast cancer patients may encounter these mental health problems. In our study, there was a high prevalence of anxiety (41.7%) and depression (79.2%) among women diagnosed with breast cancer, which is more severe than results from existing literature, where the range of anxiety and depression among breast cancer patients was reported to be from 17.9% to 33% and 9.4% to 66.1% respectively. This may indicate an increasing tendency toward anxiety and depression among breast cancer patients in recent years [15–17]. The elevated rates could also reflect more stress due to the complexity of modern treatments such as chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy [18, 19]. Considering the coexistence of these psychological symptoms, attention to mental health should be an essential component of breast cancer care.

Predictors of anxiety and depression

Cancer stage

Our findings suggest that the stage of cancer influences psychological well-being. Patients with stage IV breast cancer had higher levels of anxiety and depression compared to those in earlier stages. This is consistent with previous studies indicating that advanced cancer stages are associated with greater psychological distress [20, 21]. This association might be due to multiple factors such as poor prognosis, fear of death, physical symptoms burden, reduced social support and spiritual distress. However, while the trend was more pronounced in advanced stages patients in stage II also exhibited moderate anxiety levels. This indicates that even those in earlier stages experience significant psychological stress possibility related to diagnostic shock and treatment related uncertainty. That psychological support should be extended across all cancer stages, not just to those with advanced disease.

Age

Younger patients in our study reported higher levels of anxiety and depression and then older patients. Apoorva et al. validated these findings suggesting that majority breast cancer patients experiencing psychological symptoms were within the age range of 20–40 years as compared to those aged around 41–60 years [22]. Other studies also suggest a negative correlation between age and phycological symptoms [22, 23]. A recent observational study found that, in comparison to older patients, younger patients had higher rates of anxiety and depressive symptoms [24]. Younger adults may experience higher distress due to disruptions in life plans, fertility concerns, and body image issues, whereas older adults may benefit from greater emotional resilience and experience in coping with illness.

Education

Our study revealed some interesting findings that patients who were less educated had a lower likelihood of experiencing anxiety and depression as compared to those with higher education. However, this association was not statistically significant, and existing literature suggest the opposite trend, that women with less formal education experience higher rates of psychological symptoms [25, 26]. One possible explanation is that less educated patients may rely more on spiritual coping mechanisms and have limited exposure to anxiety inducing medical information. Nonetheless, these interpretations should be reviewed cautiously, as the relationship was not significant and requires further research.

Employment and treatment type

Psychological symptoms were more prevalent in unemployed patients, supporting findings by Hassan et al., who observed that women with low income were more susceptible to anxiety and depression due to their limited access to treatment [27]. This is probably because prolonged treatment may also lead to social isolation or job loss further exacerbating psychological distress.

Additionally, anxiety and depression were observed more frequently among patients receiving chemotherapy than those on other treatment regimens. While preclinical data show cytokine mediated hero inflammatory pathways may contribute to mood disturbances [28] and clinical evidence links chemotherapy with depressive symptoms [29], these associations in our study were not statistically significant and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Larger and longitudinal studies are warranted to explore these trends further.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, the study has several limitations. Time and resource constraints limited our ability to assess the long-term impact of predictive factors on psychological symptoms. As a result, some associations may be underestimated. The cross-sectional design of this study also restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between predictors and their psychological outcomes observed. Future cohort studies with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes could provide more definitive evidence. Additionally, the clinical presentations of patients were not recorded which might lead to inaccurate depression and anxiety screening as it is not enough to totally rely on psychometric scales. The Mental Health Literacy Questionnaire has demonstrated robust validity and reliability in university students [30], but oncology-specific screening tools remain scarce; future work should adapt or validate such instruments for breast cancer populations.

Implications for practice

Our finding suggests an urgent need for routine mental health screening and intervention within oncological settings in lower- and middle-income countries. High prevalence of anxiety and depression among certain groups necessitates targeted psychological support programs. Healthcare professionals at primary and tertiary care level should be equipped to screen and identify these psychological morbidities throughout the course of cancer treatment from diagnosis to palliative care. Furthermore, integration of early phycological assessment and continuous monitoring into clinical guidelines for cancer care is crucial to improve patient quality of life and treatment adherence.

Conclusion

Our results indicate that psychological illnesses like anxiety and depression are highly prevalent and significant comorbidities among breast cancer patients. Age and disease stage emerged as key predictive factors, with younger and late staged patients being more vulnerable to psychological distress. These findings underscore that cancer care management can become complicated when mental health challenges are overlooked, ultimately compromising patients overall wellbeing. Clinically, the findings support the integration of structured mental health care programs into oncological care, emphasizing early psychological screening, targeted interventions for high-risk groups and continuous psychological support throughout the treatment to enhance patient treatment adherence, outcomes and quality of life. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating culturally appropriate and resource sensitive intervention models, particularly for breast cancer patients in LMICs, to ensure equitable access to comprehensive cancer care.
