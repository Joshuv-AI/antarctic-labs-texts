Dentinal loss and cleaning efficiency of retreatment files with irrigation activation: An in vitro study
Authors: Rajvi D. Doctor, Shikha K. Kanodia, Navya Haritha Nandina, Hardik C. Parupaliya, Hiteshree Trivedi, Javnika Goswami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study aimed to compare dentinal loss during retreatment using ProTaper Universal retreatment file versus NeoEndo retreatment file systems via cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). In addition, it evaluated the efficacy of conventional needle irrigation, passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation (PUI), and laser-activated irrigation (LAI) in removing bioceramic sealer, utilizing both CBCT and SEM analysis.Subjects and Methods:Sixty mandibular premolars were obturated with bioceramic sealer, retreated using ProTaper Universal and NeoEndo systems, followed by irrigation activation techniques (conventional, ultrasonic, and Er:YAG laser). Dentinal loss was assessed via CBCT, and bioceramic sealer removal was evaluated via scanning electron microscope (SEM).Results:ProTaper Universal group showed a greater dentinal volume reduction (25%; P < 0.001), while NeoEndo group showed a reduction (18.18%; P < 0.001). Among irrigation protocols, Er:YAG laser activation produced a significant sealer reduction (44.44%), followed by ultrasonic (31.25%) and conventional irrigation (22.22%) (P < 0.001).Conclusion:ProTaper Universal showed a greater dentinal loss than NeoEndo, though not statistically significant. Er:YAG LAI achieved significant sealer removal, followed by passive ultrasonic, both outperforming conventional irrigation with statistically significant cleaning efficacy.

INTRODUCTION

Endodontic therapy remains a cornerstone of contemporary dental practice, aiming to eradicate infection from the complex root canal system and preserve the natural dentition. The long-term success of root canal treatment is largely dependent on effective chemomechanical debridement and three-dimensional obturation of the canal space.[1] Despite high reported success rates of up to 90%, posttreatment apical pathology can still occur, often necessitating nonsurgical endodontic retreatment as the preferred intervention for re-establishing periapical health.[23]

Failure of primary root canal treatment is commonly attributed to persistent or secondary intraradicular infection. Persistent infection arises from inadequately cleaned or obturated canal spaces, whereas secondary infection typically results from coronal leakage, missed anatomy, or delayed definitive restoration.[4] According to the Glossary of Endodontic Terms (AAE), nonsurgical retreatment involves the removal of existing filling materials, followed by reinstrumentation, redisinfection, and reobturation of the canal system.[5] A major challenge during retreatment particularly in anatomically complex or oval-shaped canals is the complete elimination of filling materials, as untouched recesses may harbor biofilms that compromise healing.[6]

Residual gutta-percha or sealer remnants act as physical barriers, limiting irrigant penetration into dentinal tubules, isthmuses, and lateral canals. The removal of sealer is especially critical, as it directly influences intracanal disinfection and periapical healing.[1] Although Grossman’s criteria outline the ideal properties of root canal sealers, newer calcium silicate-based (bioceramic) sealers have gained prominence due to their bioactivity, biocompatibility, and ability to chemically bond to dentin. Their major drawback, however, is the difficulty they pose during retreatment because of their strong adhesion and deep penetration into dentinal tubules.[78]

Numerous mechanical, chemical, thermal, and laser-assisted techniques have been introduced to enhance the removal of filling materials.[9] NiTi retreatment instruments, while efficient and flexible, can contribute to excessive dentin removal, particularly in curved or narrow canals potentially weakening the tooth and compromising prognosis.[10]

Research also suggests that mechanical instrumentation alone is insufficient for thorough removal of gutta-percha and sealer remnants. Hence, supplementary irrigant activation methods such as sonic, ultrasonic, and laser-activated irrigation (LAI) have been advocated to enhance debridement.[11] Passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation (PUI) improves cleanliness through cavitation and acoustic streaming, yet studies demonstrate that neither PUI nor NiTi files can achieve complete removal of obturating materials.[12]

Among advanced technologies, erbium lasers (Er:YAG) have shown superior efficacy due to their high absorption in water and hydroxyapatite. PIPS and the more recent SWEEPS generate powerful shockwaves that improve cleaning in complex, narrow canal morphologies.[1314]

Accurate evaluation of retreatment outcomes requires reliable imaging modalities. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) offers precise, three-dimensional, nondestructive assessment of dentinal volume changes, enabling objective comparison of retreatment systems before and after instrumentation.[15] Meanwhile, scanning electron microscope (SEM) remains the gold standard for visualizing residual sealer within dentinal tubules, providing high-resolution confirmation of irrigation efficacy.[9]

Given the increasing use of bioceramic sealers and their recognized retreatment challenges, there is a growing need to evaluate both mechanical systems and irrigant activation techniques for effective removal of these materials.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This in vitro study was conducted at the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, Government Dental College and Hospital, Ahmedabad.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on a power analysis using data from Arun N et al. (2024) regarding the standard deviation of gutta-percha remnants.[16] With a 95% confidence interval, 80% power, and a meaningful difference of 1.53, a minimum of 55 samples was required. To ensure statistical robustness, 60 samples were utilized.

Specimen standardization and preparation

Extracted teeth were cleaned of organic debris using an ultrasonic scaler (ACTEON® SATELEC®) and disinfected in 10% formalin for no longer than 2 weeks. The samples were then stored in 0.9% normal saline at room temperature.

Access cavities were prepared using Endo Access burs (#1, Dentsply Maillefer). Apical patency was established with a #10 K-file (Mani, Inc.). The working length (WL) was established by subtracting 0.5 mm from the measurement taken, when the #10 K-file tip was just visible at the apical foramen under 3.5× magnification.

Chemomechanical preparation and obturation

Chemomechanical preparation was done using the ProTaper Gold rotary system (Dentsply Maillefer) up to size F3 (#30, 0.09 taper) at 300 rpm and 3.0 Ncm torque. Irrigation was performed after each instrument change with 2 mL of 3% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd.). Final irrigation was carried out with 3 mL of 3% NaOCl (30 s), 2 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Prime Dental Products Pvt. Ltd.). (1 min) and 3 mL of 0.9% normal saline using a 30G side-vented needle.

Canals were dried with paper points and obturated with Bio Active RCS, bioceramic sealer (Safe Endo Ind. Pvt. Ltd., India) [Figure 1b], and a ProTaper Gold F3 master gutta-percha cone. The sealer was applied using a Lentulo spiral (Mani Inc.), and obturation was done with warm vertical compaction using Fast Pack Pro-Down Pack Device (Orikam Healthcare India). Samples were stored at 37°C and 100% relative humidity for 14 days to ensure complete sealer setting.

Retreatment protocol and dentinal loss assessment

Baseline dentinal volume (Phase 1) was recorded using CBCT (Vatech India). The 60 specimens were randomly divided into two groups (n = 30) for gutta-percha removal:

A crown-down technique with circumferential filing was employed until no filling material was visible on the file flutes. A second CBCT scan (Phase 2) was performed to evaluate dentinal loss during the retreatment procedure.

Statistical analysis

Data were coded and entered into Microsoft Excel 2019. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, and proportions) were calculated. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, revealing nonnormal distribution (P < 0.05); hence, nonparametric tests were applied. Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while comparisons between independent groups used the Mann–Whitney U-test. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for multiple group comparisons, followed by post hoc Bonferroni correction when significant. Proportions were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, with post hoc Bonferroni correction for interroot third comparisons. Statistical analysis was performed using (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States), with significance set at 5%.

DISCUSSION

Nonsurgical endodontic retreatment remains the preferred approach for managing persistent or recurrent periapical disease following primary root canal therapy. Although primary treatments demonstrate favorable success rates ranging between 86% and 98%, a subset of cases continues to develop or maintain apical pathology due to persistent intraradicular infection, inadequate debridement, incomplete obturation, or coronal leakage.[18] The findings of this study provide insight into the impact of file design, metallurgy and irrigant activation techniques on dentin preservation, removal of obturation materials, and the challenges of retreating bioceramic-based obturations.

Removal of existing filling materials is crucial in retreatment, as residual gutta-percha and sealer can harbor bacteria and hinder irrigant penetration; however, complete removal remains challenging.[19] Numerous studies including Schirrmeister et al., Hammad et al., and Bernardes et al. have consistently demonstrated that irrespective of the file system, technique or irrigant used, complete removal of filling remnants is seldom achieved.[202122] This difficulty is exacerbated in teeth with oval canals, where buccolingual extensions, isthmuses, and fins cannot be adequately instrumented by traditionally round file systems. Mandibular premolars, selected for this study, are particularly susceptible to these limitations, making them a clinically relevant model for evaluating retreatment efficacy and dentin preservation.[23]

The present study demonstrated statistically significant intragroup dentinal loss following retreatment in both the ProTaper Universal and NeoEndo retreatment systems. The ProTaper Universal group displayed a greater median percentage loss (25%) compared to NeoEndo (18.18%), although the intergroup comparison was statistically nonsignificant difference [Table 1]. The increased dentinal loss associated with the ProTaper Universal system can be attributed to several design characteristics. Its progressively increasing taper, triangular cross-section, and active cutting edges confer high cutting efficiency but at the cost of increased dentin removal. The frictional heat generated by the rotary action also aids in gutta-percha softening, facilitating material removal but potentially contributing to deeper dentin engagement.[2425]

Conversely, NeoEndo retreatment files exhibited a more conservative dentin removal pattern. This may be ascribed to the proprietary heat-treated controlled memory alloy that enhances flexibility and reduces file engagement with canal walls. The parallelogram cross-section and limited active cutting zone encourage controlled dentin removal while preventing excessive transportation or gouging.[26] These findings support the observations of Baranwal et al., who similarly noted that NeoEndo instruments demonstrated less dentinal loss during retreatment than PTUR files.[2728]

The complexity of retreating bioceramic sealers must also be considered, they exhibit superior sealing ability, dimensional stability and bioactivity due to their ability to form a hydroxyapatite-like mineral infiltration zone within tubules.[29] While these characteristics enhance primary treatment outcomes, they significantly hinder retreatability. The strong micromechanical and chemical adhesion to dentin makes dislodgement challenging, often resulting in persistent remnants along canal walls even after extended instrumentation.[30] Baranwal et al. and Da Silva et al. have emphasized that bioceramic sealers can penetrate tubules up to 2000 µm; distinctive setting mechanism of bioceramic sealers is driven by a hydration reaction that initiates upon contact with moisture, resulting in the formation of calcium phosphate mineral plugs that resist mechanical removal. Therefore, increased dentinal loss during retreatment may reflect not only file aggressiveness but also the inherent difficulty in detaching the sealer layer integrated with the dentin.[2831]

Given these limitations, supplementary techniques for irrigant activation have gained prominence in enhancing removal of residual filling materials. In this study, passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation (PUI) and LAI were incorporated as adjunctive methods. Ultrasonic activation has been extensively demonstrated to improve the efficacy of irrigants by transmitting ultrasonic energy from an oscillating file to the irrigant, producing acoustic streaming and cavitation.[32] These effects lead to the formation and collapse of microbubbles, thereby increasing the irrigants mechanical and chemical effectiveness, promoting better penetration into irregular canal spaces.[3334] Studies by Neelakantan et al. and Cavenago et al. indicated that ultrasonic agitation significantly enhances smear layer removal and aids in dislodging filling remnants, particularly in complex anatomies such as oval canals. In our protocol, the use of Talal’s ultrasonic tip allowed efficient activation of irrigants without causing additional mechanical enlargement, potentially preventing unnecessary dentin loss while improving debridement.[3536]

LAI, particularly with the Er:YAG laser provides additional advantages. Unlike diode and Nd: YAG lasers which act primarily through thermal effects and cannot effectively remove smear layer the Er:YAG system leverages photomechanical dissolution and water-mediated cavitation.[37] SWEEPS® enhance Er:YAG LAI by delivering paired ultrashort, low-energy pulses with the second pulse timed during the collapse of the primary cavitation bubble. This interaction intensifies bubble implosion, generating secondary cavitation and high-energy shock waves that amplify acoustic streaming and shear forces. The rapid expansion and collapse of these secondary bubbles produce pronounced acoustic streaming and localized shear stresses along the dentinal walls. In the confined geometry of the root canal system, these intensified shock waves propagate efficiently, improving irrigant penetration and enhancing the removal of smear layer and debris from anatomically complex areas such as fins, isthmuses and lateral canals.[14]

One limitation of traditional laser sapphire tip is the restricted cavitation range (2-3 mm) and linear emission. However, the side-firing spiral Endo tip used in this study significantly enhanced the distribution of laser energy along canal walls. The tip measures 25 mm in length and comprises three zones: a 4 mm cylindrical sleeve for handpiece attachment, a 3 mm flexibility zone to adapt to canal curvatures, and an 18 mm functional zone with six circumferential spiral slits. The slit width and spacing vary, with narrower apical slits to match canal taper and optimize energy transmission.[38] This tip’s circumferential slits and flexible design created shock waves and vigorous intracanal fluid motion, improving disruption of smear layer and residual bioceramic sealer without requiring insertion deep into the canal. This design enhances irrigant activation and debris/biofilm removal while reducing the risk of apical extrusion. Such findings align with the work of Sahar Helft S et al., who reported enhanced irrigant dynamics and effective debridement using this novel side-firing tip.[3940] Kapetanović Petričević et al. demonstrated that SWEEPS significantly improves the removal of filling remnants, supporting the findings of the present study.[6]

From a clinical perspective, preservation of remaining dentin is paramount to ensuring long-term tooth survival. Excessive dentin removal particularly in already weakened, previously treated teeth significantly compromise fracture resistance. Kulkarni et al. emphasized that dentinal preservation directly correlates with improved fracture resistance in endodontically treated teeth.[41] The findings of this study suggest that while both ProTaper Universal and NeoEndo retreatment systems are effective for retreatment, NeoEndo may offer an advantage in terms of conservative dentin removal. However, it must be noted that the statistically nonsignificant intergroup difference indicates that both systems perform similarly in clinical contexts and the choice may be more strongly influenced by operator preference, canal anatomy and severity of initial obturation [Table 2].

CBCT enabled nondestructive, three-dimensional assessment of dentin removal during gutta-percha retreatment and quantification of residual bioceramic sealer after irrigation activation. Compared with conventional sectioning or two-dimensional radiography, CBCT minimizes specimen distortion and material loss when providing reproducible volumetric data, thereby strengthening the validity of the results.[4243]

Quantitative analysis of residual bioceramic sealer volume demonstrated a significant reduction following irrigation activation across all groups (P < 0.001). CNI showed a mean percentage reduction of 22.22%, while passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation achieved a higher reduction of 31.25%. The Er:YAG LAI group exhibited the greatest cleaning efficacy, with a mean reduction of 44.44% [Table 3]. Intergroup comparison revealed statistically significant differences emerged following irrigation activation (P = 0.01), indicating the influence of irrigation activation technique on sealer removal [Table 4]. Post hoc pairwise analysis confirmed a statistically significant difference between Er:YAG laser activation and conventional irrigation (P = 0.01), whereas comparisons between Er:YAG and PUI, as well as between PUI and conventional irrigation, statistically insignificant [Table 5].

For qualitative evaluation, scanning electron microscope (SEM) was employed, as it remains the most effective technique for assessing dentinal surface cleanliness and smear layer removal. The SEM evaluation revealed clear qualitative trends in smear layer and bioceramic sealer removal among the irrigation techniques. CNI demonstrated consistently poor performance, with 80% of specimens in the coronal third exhibiting a score of 3, while all samples in the middle and apical thirds scored 4 and 5, respectively, indicating heavy smear layer persistence and minimal dentinal tubule exposure [Figure 2a-c]. These findings underscore the limited hydrodynamic effect of conventional irrigation, particularly in the apical region. Passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation showed measurable improvement, with 100% of coronal third samples scoring 2; however, the middle third displayed heterogeneous outcomes (scores 2–4), and the apical third predominantly showed higher scores (3–5), reflecting a decline in cleaning efficacy with increasing canal depth [Figure 2d-f]. In contrast, Er:YAG LAI achieved superior and more uniform smear layer removal, with 90% of coronal specimens scoring 1 and the remaining showing score 2. In the middle third, 70% of samples scored 2 and 30% scored 1, while in the apical third, 70% achieved score 2 and none exceeded score 3 [Figure 2g-i]. The statistically significant differences among groups (P < 0.001) and root-third-specific post hoc comparisons corroborate that laser activation provides enhanced disruption and removal of bioceramic sealer remnants, particularly in the apical third where conventional and ultrasonic techniques demonstrated limited effectiveness [Table 6].

The combined use of CBCT and SEM provided complementary quantitative and qualitative data, allowing a comprehensive evaluation of cleaning efficacy and dentin preservation among the irrigation activation techniques.

The study suggests future research using micro-CT for detailed evaluation of dentinal loss and residual sealer, especially in complex anatomies. Further assessment of various retreatment systems and bioceramic sealers, along with in vivo/ex vivo studies, is needed to improve clinical relevance. In addition, exploring advanced irrigation techniques, including lasers and novel irrigants, with combined imaging and SEM analysis, may enhance sealer and smear layer removal while preserving dentin.

CONCLUSION

This in vitro study assessed the efficacy of two rotary retreatment systems ProTaper Universal and NeoEndo and three irrigation activation protocols CNI, passive ultrasonic-activated irrigation (PUI), and Er:YAG laser activation in the removal of bioceramic sealer remnants. NeoEndo exhibited a more conservative retreatment approach, resulting in less dentinal loss than ProTaper Universal, although statistically insignificant. Among the irrigation methods, Er:YAG laser activation demonstrated statistically significant superior effectiveness in reducing residual sealer volume and smear layer, particularly in the apical third, outperforming both PUI and conventional irrigation. These results highlight the clinical value of Er:YAG LAI and suggest that its combination with minimally invasive retreatment systems can optimize cleaning efficacy while preserving dentinal integrity, thereby supporting a more conservative and biologically sound approach to endodontic retreatment.
