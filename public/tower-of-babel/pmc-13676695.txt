Protocol for quantifying chromatin DNA replication by multiplex labeling and single-molecule localization microscopy
Authors: Zhiguang Xiao, Xiaodong Guan, Yujie Sun, Mengling Zhang, Qian Peter Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol for imaging and quantifying chromatin DNA replication-associated signals in synchronized mammalian cells. We describe steps for cell-cycle enrichment, nucleoside analog-based metabolic labeling, and fluorescence detection. We then detail the procedure for dual-color STORM imaging and SR-Tesseler-based cluster analysis. It can be adapted to distinct questions in DNA replication biology by modifying synchronization schemes, pulse durations, labeling strategies, fluorescence detection channels, or analysis parameters.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhang et al.1Subject areas: Biophysics, Cell Biology, Genomics

Before you begin

Accurate control of DNA replication is essential for faithful genome duplication and maintenance of genome stability.2,3 Disruption of this process can cause replication stress, DNA damage, and chromosomal instability, and is therefore closely linked to cancer and other genome instability-associated diseases.3,4 Because replication is coordinated within higher-order chromatin and nuclear architecture, methods that can resolve its spatial organization and activity are important for understanding how replication is regulated in different cellular states.5,6,7 Conventional diffraction-limited fluorescence microscopy typically provides a lateral resolution of approximately 200–250 nm, which may be insufficient to distinguish closely spaced replication-associated foci as separate structures.8,9 By contrast, under appropriate labeling and acquisition conditions, single-molecule localization microscopy can localize individual fluorophore-emission events with a lateral precision of several tens of nanometers, enabling the resolution and quantitative analysis at the level of individual replication-associated foci, including the resolution of closely adjacent foci. Therefore, STORM imaging is needed to distinguish adjacent replication foci and quantify the nanoscale spatial organization of DNA replication.1

Many replication-related questions require cells to be enriched at defined cell-cycle windows before labeling and imaging.1,10,11 The synchronization strategy, release duration, and labeling window should be selected according to the biological question being addressed. For example, some experiments may require enrichment near the transition into DNA synthesis, whereas others may compare different periods of S phase or examine replication-associated signals after genetic, chemical, or environmental perturbations. These parameters should be optimized for each cell type and experimental condition before downstream super-resolution imaging.

The protocol below is organized as a practical workflow for labeling replication-associated DNA synthesis in defined cell-cycle windows and correlating these signals to fixed-cell fluorescence imaging and quantitative spatial analysis. It can be used to examine localization density, number of localization events, distribution, clustering, and spatial relationships, and can be combined with immunofluorescence labeling to relate DNA synthesis patterns to chromatin organization, nuclear architecture, replication-associated proteins, or DNA damage responses.

Innovation

This protocol provides an integrated strategy to convert temporally controlled DNA synthesis labeling into nanoscale spatial information in fixed cells. A key advantage is that replication-associated signals captured in defined pulse windows can be directly compared with DNA or protein markers in the same imaging context. Coupling dual-color STORM with SR-Tesseler-based segmentation further enables standardized quantification of signal number, size, density, distribution, and the spatial relationships between replication-associated localization clusters detected in the two fluorescence channels. This design makes the workflow suitable for analyzing the nanoscale organization of replication-associated signals under different cellular states or perturbation conditions.

Cell culture and seeding

Timing: 1 day

This section describes the culture and seeding of HeLa cells on glass-bottom dishes for subsequent synchronization, labeling, and STORM imaging.

Note: This cell culture and seeding procedure is generally applicable to adherent mammalian cells, but the seeding density should be optimized according to the proliferation rate and adhesion properties. In the example workflow described here, HeLa cells are maintained in 10 cm dishes in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells are passaged every 2–3 days at approximately 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin. For imaging experiments, cells are seeded on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes with No. 1.5 coverglass (approximately 0.17 mm thick) at a density that yields 20%–30% confluence on the next day. Optional poly-L-lysine coating can improve cell adhesion during repeated washing and staining steps.

Preparation of dye-conjugated secondary antibody

Timing: 2–3 h

This section describes the in-house preparation and purification of a Cy3B-conjugated secondary antibody for immunofluorescence staining and STORM imaging. The conjugation procedure and representative purification outcomes are shown in Figure 1.

Note: Although commercially available dye-conjugated secondary antibodies can be used after validation, in this example, we prepared the Cy3B-conjugated secondary antibody in-house to improve quantitative consistency. Using the same antibody stock and dye across experiments helps reduce batch-to-batch variation. After purification, the labeling quality can also be checked by absorbance measurement. This is useful for STORM-based quantification because differences in labeling efficiency or dye performance may affect fluorescence intensity, localization density, and segmented signal clusters.

Replication-associated metabolic labeling in synchronized cells

Timing: 2–3 days

This section describes synchronization of HeLa cells, release into a defined replication window, and pulse labeling with nucleoside analogs for downstream fluorescence imaging.

Note: The goal of this section is to describe how cells can be enriched at a selected cell-cycle stage, released into a defined replication window, and pulse-labeled with nucleoside analogs for downstream fluorescence imaging and spatial analysis. This strategy can be adapted to distinct questions in DNA replication biology by changing the synchronization method, release duration, pulse length, or nucleoside analog combination.

Replication studies often require cells to be compared at specific cell-cycle or S-phase windows. For example, cells can be enriched near the G1/S transition and then released into S phase, or release timing can be adjusted to examine different S-phase stages. In the example workflow below, HeLa cells are synchronized at G1/S transition using a thymidine block, release, and aphidicolin block as outlined in Figure 2A. Sequential thymidine and aphidicolin treatment was used to improve synchronization efficiency in HeLa cells before nucleoside-analog labeling.11,15 This design is used as a representative workflow and should be re-optimized when applied to other cell lines, perturbations, or labeling windows.

Expected outcomes

Successful execution of this protocol should yield cell-cycle-enriched samples with nucleoside analog-labeled replication-associated signals. Before super-resolution imaging, conventional fluorescence inspection should reveal clearly detectable signals in the intended imaging channels within the same nucleus, with preserved nuclear morphology and low nonspecific background (Figure 4B). When two replication labeling windows are used, the two signal populations may differ in intensity, size, density, or spatial distribution depending on the synchronization strategy and pulse duration. Fluorescent beads added before STORM imaging should appear as bright stationary puncta in the FOV. The bead localizations from the yellow dashed boxes in Figure 4B are used to estimate lateral x-y drift and to register the 561- and 647-nm channels.

A representative single raw STORM acquisition frame is shown in Figure 4E. Bead-based drift correction should improve localization stability and channel alignment, as illustrated in Figure 4C. Reconstructed drift-corrected dual-color STORM images should reveal discrete replication-associated signal clusters in both imaging channels, allowing the spatial relationship between the two labeled signal populations to be evaluated at the nanoscale (Figures 4F and 4G). The STORM images before and after x-y drift correction are shown in Figure S3 in Methods S1.

Representative SR-Tesseler segmentation results are shown in Figure 5F. The larger blue objects in the upper panel represent larger, longer-pulse signal structures (561 channel), whereas the smaller, compact blue puncta in the lower panel represent short-pulse signal clusters (647 channel).

In addition to reconstructed images and segmentation overlays, the completed analysis workflow generates multiple output files, including localization files, object-level measurements, per-cell quantitative summaries, and statistical outputs. These files can be used for downstream analysis, quality control, troubleshooting, or re-analysis with alternative segmentation criteria.

Limitations

This protocol depends heavily on robust and reproducible cell-cycle enrichment. Because replication-associated signals are captured through synchronization, release, and pulse labeling, small differences in cell density, washing quality, release timing, or pulse duration can substantially alter nucleoside analog incorporation and increase cell-to-cell heterogeneity. Therefore, all sample conditions should be processed in parallel under tightly matched culture, synchronization, labeling, and staining conditions. When the workflow is adapted to other cell types or biological questions, seeding density, synchronization duration, release timing, pulse-labeling conditions, and detection methods should be re-optimized.

A second limitation is the strong dependence of the assay on a well-optimized dual-color STORM imaging platform. This requirement reduces immediate transferability to laboratories without stable high-power lasers, sensitive single-molecule detectors, and experience with STORM acquisition. Although the upstream labeling and staining steps can be reproduced more broadly, the full quantitative output depends on successful nanoscale imaging and therefore remains instrument intensive.

This protocol is also limited by the sensitivity of STORM reconstruction and downstream segmentation to data quality and analysis parameters. The final measurements depend on fluorophore blinking behavior, imaging-buffer quality, localization density, signal-to-background ratio, acquisition stability, and accurate channel alignment. In addition, downstream quantification is not entirely parameter free. Localization filtering, reconstruction settings, and clustering thresholds in ThunderSTORM and SR-Tesseler influence the measured number, diameter, density, and spatial assignment of labeled signal clusters.

The present workflow is designed for two-dimensional STORM imaging and x-y localization analysis. In principle, this approach could be extended to three-dimensional imaging using astigmatism-based localization. Such an extension may provide additional information on the spatial organization of replication-associated DNA synthesis. However, it would require additional optical calibration, z-position calibration, and more complex data analysis than described here.

Manual selection of cell and bead ROIs remains a potential source of operator-dependent variability. Although consistent ROI criteria and identical localization and segmentation parameters are applied to all samples, variability introduced during manual ROI selection cannot be completely excluded. Future implementations may incorporate semi-automated nuclear segmentation, standardized image-based screening criteria, and inter-operator validation to improve reproducibility.

Finally, this protocol provides a fixed-cell, endpoint, and largely two-dimensional view of replication-associated DNA synthesis rather than a direct live-cell measurement of replication dynamics. Live-cell imaging of fluorescently tagged replication proteins provides a complementary approach. GFP-PCNA imaging can be used to monitor replication-foci dynamics,17 whereas single-particle tracking PALM of mEos2-PCNA can characterize PCNA mobility at the single-molecule level.18 However, these approaches report the behavior of replication proteins indirectly. They do not directly label newly synthesized DNA, as achieved by nucleoside analog labeling in the present fixed-cell protocol. The assay can resolve spatial organization and inter-channel relationships between labeled signal populations at high resolution, but it cannot directly capture the temporal sequence of origin licensing, origin firing, fork progression, or local chromatin remodeling in real time. Because representative FOVs and analyzable nuclei must be selected for imaging and reconstruction, the workflow remains relatively low throughput and may be affected by sampling bias if image-selection criteria are not predefined and applied consistently.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Cells fail to display the expected labeling pattern for the selected cell-cycle window, and nucleoside analog-labeled signals appear highly heterogeneous across nuclei (Steps 1–15).

Potential solution

This problem is most commonly caused by differences in cell density, incomplete synchronization, residual synchronization drugs after washout, or inaccurate release timing. Ensure that cells are evenly seeded and reach approximately 20%–30% confluence before synchronization. Avoid overconfluent cultures, as high density can reduce synchronization efficiency and increase cell-to-cell heterogeneity.

In practice, absent or extremely weak nucleoside analog labeling often indicates that the synchronization drug was not washed out thoroughly enough. During each wash after thymidine or aphidicolin treatment, gently add pre-warmed PBS, swirl the dish thoroughly to ensure complete exchange of medium across the glass area, and remove the wash completely before adding fresh medium. Residual aphidicolin can strongly suppress S-phase entry and result in weak or absent nucleotide incorporation. In addition, release and pulse-labeling times should be strictly controlled and kept identical across all samples. If the labeling pattern remains heterogeneous, re-optimize the initial seeding density, synchronization duration, release timing, and pulse-labeling conditions for the specific cell batch or cell type used.

Problem 2

Nucleoside analog-labeled signals are absent or very weak even though the synchronization and release steps have been confirmed to work properly (Steps 1–12 and 16–35).

Potential solution

If synchronization quality, washing, and release timing have already been verified, weak or absent nucleoside analog-labeled signal is more likely caused by problems with the labeling or detection reagents. First check whether the nucleoside analog working solutions were prepared correctly, stored properly, and used within an appropriate time frame. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles, prolonged storage, or incorrect dilution may reduce labeling efficiency.

If click-based detection is weak, prepare the click reaction mixture freshly immediately before use and check each component carefully, especially the fluorescent azide, copper reagent, and reaction buffer. If antibody-based detection of halogenated nucleoside analogs is weak, verify that HCl denaturation is sufficient to expose the incorporated analogs and that the primary and secondary antibodies are active and used under optimized conditions. When necessary, test the click reaction reagents and antibody staining reagents separately using a positive-control sample that previously gave reliable signal.

Problem 3

Nucleoside analog fluorescence appears uneven across the conventional fluorescence reference image, with strong signal in some regions and weak or absent signal in others (Steps 16–35).

Potential solution

Uneven staining usually results from incomplete coverage of the glass surface by the reaction solution, sample drying during incubation, or inconsistent denaturation and antibody exposure. Make sure that all solutions fully cover the imaging area and that the dish remains level throughout incubation. When small staining volumes are used, avoid evaporation by keeping samples in a humidified chamber. During HCl denaturation, ensure that the solution is evenly distributed across the sample and that the incubation time is consistent between dishes. During antibody incubation, gently dispense the solution so that it spreads uniformly over the sample instead of remaining localized in one area. Also avoid allowing the sample to dry during washing or solution exchange, as even brief drying can create severe local staining artifacts.

Non-uniform illumination across the FOV may also cause spatial variability in signal intensity or localization density. This can be assessed by imaging a homogeneous fluorescent sample, such as a fluorescent calibration slide, under the same optical configuration.

Problem 4

Background fluorescence is high, making it difficult to identify nucleoside analog-labeled or protein immunofluorescence structures under conventional fluorescence microscopy (Steps 16–35).

Potential solution

High background usually reflects insufficient blocking or washing, excessive antibody concentration, residual fluorescent reagents, or sample deterioration during storage. Increase the blocking time if needed and verify that blocking buffer and antibody dilution buffer are freshly prepared. Reduce the concentration of the primary or secondary antibody if the target signal remains detectable while the background remains high. Extend washing time and perform washes more gently but more thoroughly, especially after click chemistry and secondary antibody incubation. If samples are not imaged immediately, store them under appropriate conditions and avoid prolonged storage before STORM acquisition, as background and nonspecific fluorescence may increase over time.

Problem 5

Single-molecule blinking is poor during STORM acquisition, resulting in low signal-to-background ratio, insufficient localization density, or blurred reconstruction (Steps 37–52).

Potential solution

Poor STORM image quality is often caused by suboptimal fluorophore performance, degraded imaging buffer, weak initial staining, or unstable imaging conditions. Prepare the STORM imaging buffer fresh on the day of use and begin STORM imaging within 15–30 min after preparation of the working imaging buffer. Also verify that the STIB is adjusted to pH 8.0 and that the reducing agent and oxygen-scavenging system are suitable for the fluorophores being used. In this workflow, β-ME and GLOX were used to support fluorophore switching. If alternative blinking-inducing compounds are used, their identity and concentration should be optimized together with the buffer pH. Before starting STORM acquisition, confirm under conventional fluorescence that the intended labeled signals are clearly detectable and that the selected cell or nucleus shows the expected signal pattern for the chosen labeling window. Optimize the excitation power, exposure time, and acquisition length so that fluorophores enter a suitable blinking regime without causing photobleaching. Also confirm that the microscope is in stable focus and that mechanical or thermal drift is minimized during acquisition. If signal remains weak, re-evaluate upstream labeling and staining quality before attempting to optimize the imaging settings further.

Problem 6

Spectral crosstalk is observed between the 561- and 647-nm channels, making it difficult to distinguish the two labeled signal populations (Steps 39–47).

Potential solution

Spectral crosstalk may result from insufficient spectral separation between the fluorophores or inappropriate emission filters and acquisition settings. Confirm that the selected filters and acquisition settings minimize bleed-through between the fluorophores, such as AF647 and Cy3B in the validated workflow. Image single-labeled control samples to determine whether fluorescence from one fluorophore is detected in the other channel. Adjust the optical filters or acquisition settings if substantial bleed-through is detected.

Problem 7

Poor spatial registration is observed between the 561- and 647-nm channels, making the spatial relationship between the two labeled signal populations unreliable (Steps 53–58).

Potential solution

Poor channel registration may result from chromatic aberration, optical misalignment, or inaccurate estimation of channel displacement. Insufficient or unevenly distributed fiducial beads do not themselves cause channel misregistration, but they reduce the accuracy of estimating and correcting channel displacement. Use fiducial beads detected in both channels to estimate and correct the spatial displacement. Ensure that multiple well-isolated beads are available for correction. Apply the same registration procedure to all samples.

Problem 8

SR-Tesseler segmentation does not match visual inspection of the reconstructed images, leading to obvious over-segmentation or under-segmentation of labeled signal clusters (Steps 53–60).

Potential solution

Segmentation errors are often caused by poor raw image quality, inconsistent localization filtering, or inappropriate density thresholds. Before changing segmentation parameters, first verify that the reconstructed images are based on good-quality localization data with sufficient point density and accurate drift correction. Then apply the same localization filtering and SR-Tesseler analysis pipeline to all samples. Do not adjust thresholds separately for different conditions unless there is a clearly justified and documented reason. If the segmentation result is unstable, inspect several representative nuclei and determine whether the density factor is too low, causing merged structures, or too high, causing fragmentation of a single structure into multiple clusters. The Cut distance parameter should also be evaluated because it controls the spatial connectivity of localizations. Depending on the selected value, it may contribute to merging or fragmentation of neighboring clusters. Quantitative comparisons should only be performed after identical analysis parameters produce segmentation results that are visually consistent with the reconstructed images across all sample conditions.

Problem 9

Too few cells meet the quality criteria for downstream analysis, leading to poor reproducibility or insufficient sample size (Step 60).

Potential solution

This problem is typically caused by a combination of suboptimal seeding density, cell loss during repeated washing and staining, low synchronization quality, and the inherently low-throughput nature of STORM imaging. To improve yield, optimize the initial seeding density so that cells remain well separated but abundant enough after synchronization and staining. Handle samples gently during all washing steps to reduce detachment. Establish predefined image-selection criteria, such as nuclear morphology, expected labeling pattern for the selected cell-cycle window, signal quality, bead distribution, registration quality, and absence of obvious artifacts, and apply these criteria consistently across all sample conditions. In addition, acquire data from multiple independent experiments rather than relying on a single imaging session, as this improves robustness and reduces sampling bias.
