Composition-tunable polycarboxylic acid-stabilized ultrasmall amorphous paramagnetic nanoclusters as multifunctional MRI contrast agents
Authors: Shilin Xiao, Tao Sun, Liang Zhang, Yan Yang, Xiaofeng Yang, Xu Liu, Yue Zhao, Zhipeng Zhang, Wansu Zhang, Chunyu Zhou, Mingfu Gong, Dong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional crystalline nanoscale magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents (CAs) suffer from low longitudinal relaxivity (r1) and complex synthesis processes, limiting their clinical translation potential. Here, we report that polycarboxylic acid-stabilized ultrasmall amorphous paramagnetic nanoclusters (UAPNCs), fabricated via a one-step co-precipitation method, are proposed to simultaneously increase the number of inner-sphere coordinated water molecules (q), elevate the second-sphere hydrogen-bonded water molecules (q'), and prolong the rotational correlation time (τR), thereby achieving consistently excellent r1 (>20 mM−1s−1; 3.0 T) with a maximum of 41.27 ± 0.33 mM−1s−1 and low r2/r1 ratios (<3). Importantly, we propose a mechanism that non-paramagnetic metal doping further boosts r1 by approximately 40% via elevating q′. In vivo MRI reveals that manganese-based UAPNCs hold dual potential for hepatocyte-specific MRI and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (CE-MRA), whereas gadolinium-based UAPNCs excel in CE-MRA. Overall, this work demonstrates the universality and superiority of UAPNCs as CAs, providing a new paradigm for designing next-generation MRI CAs.Keywords: Magnetic resonance imaging, Contrast agents, Ultrasmall amorphous paramagnetic nanoclusters, Polycarboxylic acid

Introduction

Contrast agents (CAs) can effectively enhance the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, commercially available gadolinium-based CAs (e.g., gadolinium diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-DTPA) and gadoxetic acid disodium (Gd-EOB-DTPA)) typically demonstrate low longitudinal relaxivity (r1), necessitating high dosages in clinical practice. Theoretically, the r1 of CAs is jointly governed by inner-sphere, second-sphere, and outer-sphere relaxation [[1], [2], [3], [4]], which can be boosted by increasing the number of inner-sphere coordinated water molecules (q), elevating the second-sphere hydrogen-bonded water molecules (q'), prolonging the rotational correlation time (τR), and so on [[5], [6], [7]]. Based on these principles, extensive research has been devoted to developing high r1 CAs with tailorable compositions (e.g., gadolinium ion (Gd3+) [8], manganese ion (Mn2+) [9], ferric ion (Fe3+) [10], other lanthanide metal ions [11], or even paramagnetic metal-free systems [12,13]), diverse structural forms (chelates [14] or nanomaterials [6,15,16]), novel architectural designs (core-shell structure [17] or hollow mesoporous structure [18]), engineered surface modifications [19], and so on. Notably, ultrasmall magnetic nanoparticles, featuring large specific surface area, tunable composition, and versatile surface modification capabilities, hold great promise as next-generation CAs [20,21]. Nevertheless, two critical challenges remain unresolved: (i) Traditional crystalline Mn- and Fe-based CAs still possess relatively low r1, especially, those that exceed 20 mM−1s−1 (3.0 T) remain scarce [9,22]. Even for some CAs with sufficiently high r1, their concomitantly elevated transverse relaxivity (r2) leads to excessively high r2/r1 ratios (typically >10), rendering them unsuitable for T1 CAs. (ii) Complicated synthesis protocols constitute a major barrier to industrial-scale fabrication, as high-quality monodisperse nanoparticles are frequently prepared via intricate and costly thermal decomposition routes, which further require additional surface modification to enable biological application [22].

Some amorphous nanoparticles (e.g., amorphous calcium carbonate (CaCO3, ACC)) inherently contain abundant pores and intrinsic water channels, with water molecules even serving as integral structural components [23,24], significantly facilitating the interaction between metal ions and water molecules. Furthermore, these nanoparticles feature long-range atomic disorder and unique high coordination unsaturation [[25], [26], [27]], which enable metal ions to possess more water coordination sites. Accordingly, amorphous nanoparticles, especially ultrasmall amorphous nanoparticles, not only promote the interaction of nearly all metal ions with water molecules, but also endow each of them with a greater number of bound water molecules, representing a cascaded amplification effect. In other words, ultrasmall amorphous nanoparticles containing paramagnetic metal ions may serve as distinctive CAs that intrinsically possess a large q, which can overcome the limitation imposed by thin surface disordered shells on the r1 of crystalline nanoparticles [28,29], thereby yielding superior r1. Moreover, polycarboxylic acids (e.g., polyacrylic acid (PAA) [30] and polymaleic acid (PMA) [31]) are widely employed for nanoparticle surface modification due to their excellent hydrophilicity and biocompatibility, with particularly outstanding performance for ultrasmall nanoparticles [32,33]. Their flexibly tailorable molecular weights allow rational optimization of τR for nanoparticles, further contributing to high r1. More importantly, their abundant carboxyl groups can form hydrogen bonds with surrounding water molecules, potentially enhancing the r1 by acquiring a large q'. Additionally, polycarboxylic acids also play a crucial role in maintaining the structural stability of ultrasmall amorphous nanoparticles [34]. Leveraging these advantageous features, it can be inferred that polycarboxylic acid-stabilized ultrasmall amorphous nanoparticles would deliver higher r1 than conventional crystalline nanoparticles.

Herein, we fabricate a series of polycarboxylic acid-stabilized ultrasmall amorphous paramagnetic nanoclusters (UAPNCs) via a facile one-step co-precipitation method. Benefiting from the significantly enhanced q enabled by the ultrasmall amorphous structure, the substantial elevation of q′ from the hygroscopic effect of carboxyl groups in polycarboxylic acid, and the prolonged τR afforded by polycarboxylic acid modification, they exhibit uniformly high r1 (>20 mM−1s−1; 3.0 T) with a maximum of 41.27 ± 0.33 mM−1s−1 and low r2/r1 ratios (<3). More importantly, we propose that the introduction of non-paramagnetic metal ions with intrinsically high hydration capacity may also increase q′ of UAPNCs, leading to an approximately 40% enhancement in r1. And this enhancement is more pronounced than that arising from conventional ligand-water interactions because of the shorter spatial distance between such non-paramagnetic metal ions and paramagnetic nuclei. On this basis, we establish optimal synthetic conditions, laying a reliable foundation for scalable production. Finally, in vivo MRI studies reveal that amorphous manganese carbonate nanoclusters (AMC) show great potential for hepatocyte-specific MRI and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (CE-MRA), while amorphous gadolinium carbonate nanoclusters (AGC) deliver excellent CE-MRA performance. Overall, this study systematically investigates the mechanism, universality, and superiority of UAPNCs as high-performance CAs, providing a new paradigm for the rational design of advanced T1 MRI CAs.

Materials and methods

Materials

Polyacrylic acid (PAA, Mw = 1800, code: 323667), oleyl alcohol (85%), benzyl ether (98%) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd. PAA with Mw = 3000 and 5000, gadolinium chloride hexahydrate (GdCl3·6H2O, 99.9%), manganese chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O, 99.9%), anhydrous calcium chloride (CaCl2, 99.9%), magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl2·6H2O, 99.9%), anhydrous zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 99%), anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), anhydrous sodium silicate (Na2SiO3), anhydrous sodium sulfite (Na2SO3), anhydrous sodium phosphate (Na3PO4), polymaleic acid (PMA, code: P823909), polyaspartic acid (PASP, Mw = 4000), erucic acid (80%), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and n-hexane were procured from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Absolute ethanol and methanol were acquired from Chongqing Chuandong Chemical Co. Ltd. Commercially available Gd-DTPA was provided by Guangzhou Consun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., and the Gd-EOB-DTPA was supplied by Bayer Aktiengesellschaft. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

Samples preparation

Amorphous manganese carbonate (AMC) nanoclusters were synthesized via a modified one-step co-precipitation method based on the previous report [35]. Typically, PAA-1800 (200 mg) and MnCl2·4H2O (47.5 mg, 0.24 mmol) were first dissolved into 10 mL of deionized water (DI) in a 100 mL beaker under magnetic stirring (500 rpm) for 3 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of Na2CO3 solution (0.2 M) was rapidly added to the mixture, followed by continuous magnetic stirring for 1 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 20 mL of ethanol, and the mixture was immediately centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The colorless transparent gelatinous precipitate was resuspended in DI via ultrasonication, then purified by ultrafiltration (5 kDa, BKMAMLAB, China) to remove free PAA. Finally, a portion of the concentrated suspension was lyophilized using a freeze dryer (Lichen LC-10N-80C, China) and stored at room temperature, while the remaining suspension was diluted and stored at 4 °C.

The synthesis of amorphous gadolinium carbonate (AGC) and amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC) followed the same procedure as AMC, except that MnCl2·4H2O was replaced by an equimolar amount of GdCl3·6H2O or CaCl2, respectively. Note that the total dosage of Na2CO3 was adjusted to 2 mmol for AGC. Similarly, ultrasmall amorphous manganese silicate (MnSiO3), manganous sulfite (MnSO3), and manganese phosphate (Mn3(PO4)2) nanoclusters were prepared by substituting Na2CO3 with an equimolar amount of Na2SiO3, Na2SO3, or Na3PO4, while other reaction conditions remained unchanged. Correspondingly, the analogous Gd2(SiO3)3, Gd2(SO3)3, and GdPO4 nanoclusters were synthesized using the same approach. For Mg2+-, Ca2+-, and Zn2+-doped nanoclusters, the synthetic procedure was identical to that of AMC and AGC with the total molar concentration of metal ions kept constant. Furthermore, when investigating the effects of each component on the synthesis and performance of UAPNCs, only one reaction variable was altered at a time while the others were fixed to ensure experimental reliability. For comparison, unmodified calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and gadolinium carbonate (Gd2(CO3)3) were prepared by directly mixing stoichiometric molar amounts of CaCl2 or GdCl3·6H2O and Na2CO3 in the absence of PAA. After centrifugation, the precipitate was lyophilized and stored at room temperature.

For the direct preparation of hydrophilic ultrasmall amorphous gadolinium oxide (Gd2O3-W) nanoclusters, PAA-1800 (200 mg) and GdCl3·6H2O (89.2 mg, 0.24 mmol) were first dissolved in 10 mL of DI in a beaker. The solution was magnetically stirred in a 60 °C water bath for 10 min, followed by the addition of 5 mL of NaOH solution (0.5 M). The mixture was then continuously stirred in a 60 °C water bath for another 30 min. After adding 20 mL of ethanol, the mixture was immediately centrifuged. The resulting precipitate was purified by ultrafiltration and lyophilized.

Hydrophobic ultrasmall manganese ferrite (MnFe2O4) nanoparticles were synthesized according to our previous study [36]. And hydrophobic ultrasmall amorphous Gd2O3 nanoclusters (Gd2O3-O) were synthesized via thermal decomposition of gadolinium-erucate, which was prepared following a similar protocol to iron-erucate. Briefly, 3 mmol of gadolinium-erucate, 6 mmol of oleyl alcohol, and 20 mL of benzyl ether were loaded into a three-neck flask. The mixture was heated to 265 °C at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min and maintained at this temperature for 1 h under continuous nitrogen flow and magnetic stirring (500 rpm). After cooling to room temperature, the as-prepared Gd2O3 nanoclusters were washed twice with ethanol and centrifuged, and the precipitate was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 1 h. For hydrophilic modification, 200 mg of PAA-1800 was dissolved in 10 mL of DI, and the pH of the solution was adjusted to 10 by adding NaOH solution. Then 10 mL of THF containing 100 mg of MnFe2O4 or Gd2O3-O nanoclusters was added dropwise to the PAA solution under ultrasonication. The mixture was heated in a 60 °C water bath for 2 h under magnetic stirring, and then centrifuged with ethanol. The resulting hydrophilic nanoclusters (PAA-MnFe2O4 or Gd2O3-O/W) were purified by ultrafiltration and finally lyophilized.

Characterizations

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired using a Hitachi HT-7700 TEM at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV. High-angle annular dark field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images and corresponding energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) mapping were obtained using a FEI Talos F200X high-resolution TEM. X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer. Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were determined by a Malvern Zetasizer nano-ZS90 instrument. Magnetic hysteresis loops (from −2 to 2 T at room temperature) were obtained with a Lake Shore 8604 vibrating sample magnetometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were performed on a Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi photoelectron spectrometer. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker ALPHA II spectrometer at room temperature. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out on a Mettler Toledo TGA2 thermogravimetric analyzer at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) were performed on a Thermo iCAP 7200 ICP-OES and a PerkinElmer NexION 300D ICP-MS instrument, respectively.

In vitro MRI and relaxivity measurement

In vitro MR imaging was performed using a clinical 3.0 T MRI scanner (GE Signa HDx 3.0T, USA) equipped with a matched 8-channel head coil. Briefly, samples dispersed in DI at gradient concentrations (0.0125, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 mM) of Mn or Gd were placed in 2 mL microtubes, which were then positioned on a 48-hole centrifuge tube rack and immersed in a plastic box filled with DI. Imaging was conducted using the following conventional sequences: (1) Fast spin echo T1-weighted imaging (FSE T1WI): repetition time (TR) = 450 ms, echo time (TE) = 8.5 ms, field of view (FOV) = 160 × 160 mm2, matrix = 320×192, slice thickness/spacing = 1.0 mm/0.3 mm, number of excitations (NEX) = 4; (2) T1 mapping: TE = 9 ms, TR = 150, 300, 600, 900, 1200 ms, FOV = 160 × 160 mm2, matrix = 256×256, slice thickness/spacing = 1.0 mm/0.3 mm, NEX = 1; (3) FSE T2WI: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 50 ms, FOV = 160 × 160 mm2, matrix = 192×160, slice thickness/spacing = 1.0 mm/0.3 mm, NEX = 4; (4) T2 mapping: TR = 1500 ms, TE = 8.8, 17.6, 26.3, 35.1, 43.9, 52.7, 61.4, 70.2 ms, FOV = 160 × 160 mm2, matrix = 160×128, slice thickness/spacing = 1.0 mm/0.3 mm, NEX = 1. T1 and T2 values were calculated using the system's built-in post-processing software.

Cell lines and culture

The AML-12 cell line and SLC39A14-knockout AML-12 cell line (AML-KO) were purchased from Guangzhou Ubigene Biosciences Co., Ltd. The Hepa 1-6 cell line, Hepa 1-6-specific medium, and AML-12 complete medium were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All cells were routinely cultured in the corresponding medium in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

A standard Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of AMC and AGC. Briefly, AML-12 and Hepa 1-6 cells were first seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. Subsequently, the original medium was replaced with fresh medium containing AMC or AGC at serial metal (Mn or Gd) concentrations (0, 0.03125, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 mM), followed by further incubation for 24 h. Each concentration was tested in triplicate. The medium was then replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium supplemented with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China), and the cells were incubated for an additional 1.5 h. Cellular uptake assay

AML-12 cells were first seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with 2 mL of fresh medium containing AMC (0.5 mM Mn), and the cells were co-incubated for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h, respectively. Each group was performed in triplicate. After incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The cells were digested with trypsin, harvested by centrifugation, counted with a hemocytometer (SCC-M630, Servicebio, China), and then subjected to ICP-MS analysis for the quantification of cellular metal uptake. Similarly, the uptake of AGC and Mn-AGC by AML-12 cells, as well as the uptake of AMC by AML-12, AML-KO, and Hepa 1-6 cells, was evaluated using the same protocol. For these additional assays, the metal concentration was maintained at 0.5 mM, and the co-incubation time was fixed at 8 h.

Western blot assay

The expression of SLC39A14 protein in the three cell lines was determined by Western blot (WB) analysis. Briefly, AML-12, AML-KO, and Hepa 1-6 cells were separately cultured in T-25 flasks. Total cellular proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, China). Protein concentrations were quantified using a Bradford protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were denatured with SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, China) by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min. The denatured proteins were separated via electrophoresis on a precast polyacrylamide gel (Tris-Gly, 4-15%, 15-well, Beyotime, China) at 120 V for 5 min, followed by 200 V for 25 min. Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (0.45 μm, Millipore, USA) at 400 mA for 30 min. After blocking with tris-buffered saline with tween-20 (TBST, 10×, Servicebio, China) for 1 h at room temperature, the membrane was cut into two sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-SLC39A14 polyclonal antibody (1:2000, Cat No. 26540-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) and HRP-conjugated anti-GAPDH recombinant antibody (1:2000, Cat No. ZB15004-HRP-100, Servicebio, China). After three washes with TBST, the membrane probed with anti-SLC39A14 antibody was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:2000, Cat No. SA00001-2, Proteintech, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime, China) with a gel imaging system (GE ImageQuant LAS4010, USA).

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (Protocol No.: AMUWEC20245286). Male C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks old, body weight 16–20 g) were purchased from Chongqing Tengxin Experimental Animal Sales Co., Ltd. and housed in the Experimental Animal Center of Xinqiao Hospital under standard laboratory conditions.

Hepatocellular carcinoma-bearing mouse models

Hepatocellular carcinoma-bearing mouse models were established using Hepa 1-6 cells and C57BL/6J mice. Briefly, freshly harvested Hepa 1-6 cells were adjusted to a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL, and kept on ice for subsequent inoculation. Mice were anesthetized by continuous inhalation of isoflurane, fixed in a supine position, and their abdominal hair was shaved, followed by disinfection with povidone-iodine. A small incision parallel to the abdominal midline was made on the right side of the xiphoid process to expose the liver lobes without injuring adjacent tissues and organs. A total of 50 μL of Hepa 1-6 cell suspension was slowly injected into the liver parenchyma using a microsyringe, and the needle was maintained in place for at least 30 s post-injection to minimize cell leakage. Finally, the abdominal wall and skin incisions were sutured and disinfected again. Mice were housed under normal conditions, and MRI was performed weekly to monitor tumor development.

In vivo MRI

Hepatocyte-specific MRI and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (CE-MRA) in mice were performed using a clinical 3.0 T MRI scanner (Philips Ingenia CX 3.0 T, Netherlands) equipped with a dedicated 8-channel rat coil (MS80-3T; Suzhou Medcoil Healthcare, China). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and fixed on a stereotaxic frame (R510IP, RWD Life Science, China). Whole-body coronal images were acquired using a three-dimensional fast field echo (3D FFE) sequence, while liver transverse images were obtained using an enhanced-T1 high resolution isotropic volume examination (e-THRIVE) sequence before and after tail vein injection of the samples. For hepatocyte-specific MRI, the tested samples included AMC (25, 10, and 5 μmol Mn/kg), Gd-EOB-DTPA (25 μmol Gd/kg), and other Mn-based UAPNCs (25 μmol Mn/kg). For CE-MRA, samples included AGC (20 μmol Gd/kg), Gd-DTPA (200 μmol Gd/kg), and other Gd-based UAPNCs (20 μmol Gd/kg). Detailed sequence parameters were as follows: 3D FFE: TE = 3.9 ms, TR = 8.2 ms, FOV = 80 (FH) × 40 (RL) × 25 (AP) mm3, voxel = 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.5 mm3, matrix = 268 × 136 × 100, gap = −0.25 mm, number of signal averages (NSA) = 2, flip angle = 20°; e-THRIVE: TE = 4 ms, TR = 10.7 ms, FOV = 30 (RL) × 30 (AP) × 30 (FH) mm3, voxel = 0.25 × 0.3 × 1 mm3, matrix = 120 × 100 × 60, gap = −0.5 mm, NSA = 5, flip angle = 30°. Vascular images were reconstructed via maximum intensity projection (MIP) using the system's built-in post-processing software. Pharmacokinetics and half-life measurement

To investigate the pharmacokinetic profiles and half-life of AMC and AGC, 10 μL blood samples were collected from the mouse tail vein before injection and at 1, 3, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min after intravenous administration of AMC (25 μmol Mn/kg) or AGC (20 μmol Gd/kg). Mn and Gd concentrations were quantified by ICP-MS. Blood concentration-time curves were plotted, and the distribution half-life (t1/2, α) and elimination half-life (t1/2, β) were calculated using a two-compartment model.

Metabolism analysis of AMC and AGC in mice

To clarify the metabolism of AMC and AGC, urine samples were continuously collected in metabolic cages for 3 days after the intravenous injection of AMC (25 μmol Mn/kg) or AGC (20 μmol Gd/kg). These urine samples were subjected to TEM observation, EDS mapping, and Mn content quantification. Meanwhile, major organs were harvested, and their Mn/Gd concentrations were analyzed by ICP-MS.

Safety evaluation in vivo

For in vivo safety evaluation, mice in the experimental groups were intravenously injected with AMC or AGC (100 μmol Mn or Gd/kg), while the control group received normal saline. Mice were euthanized at 1, 7, 14, and 28 days post-injection. Blood samples were collected for routine blood analysis and biochemical assays, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and UREA. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain) were harvested at 14 days post-injection and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were analyzed using two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed via ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and all figures were plotted with OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of UAPNCs

As illustrated in Scheme 1, UAPNCs can be readily synthesized via a one-step co-precipitation method at room temperature. Notably, representative AMC and AGC both appeared as white powders after freeze-drying (Fig. S1a), and their resuspension in deionized water (DI) at a metal ion concentration of 100 mM formed a colorless, transparent dispersion with an obvious Tyndall effect (Fig. S1b), thus greatly facilitating their transportation and long-term storage. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and corresponding size distribution histograms revealed that AMC and AGC featured narrow size distributions and favorable monodispersity, with average diameters of 2.0 ± 0.32 and 1.98 ± 0.37 nm, respectively (Fig. 1a and b), while their hydrodynamic sizes determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) were 3.6 ± 0.51 and 4.2 ± 0.53 nm (Fig. 1c), confirming their ultrasmall size.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy demonstrated that the peak at 1712 cm−1 in PAA, corresponding to the stretching vibration of carboxyl groups (-COOH), was red-shifted to 1560 cm−1 in AMC, AGC, and ACC, which was assigned to the antisymmetric stretching vibration of carboxylate groups (-COO-) (Fig. 1d), aligning well with the previous report [37]. In contrast, this characteristic peak was absent in unmodified CaCO3 (without PAA), confirming the successful modification of PAA onto the UAPNCs. Notably, two additional peaks at 1456 and 1407 cm−1 were observed in AMC, AGC, and ACC, which are absent in pure PAA, and were attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of carbonate ions (CO32−) in amorphous structures [35,38], confirming the carbonate-based composition of the nanoclusters. X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) analysis was further conducted (Fig. 1e), and no distinct characteristic diffraction peaks were observed for AMC, AGC, and ACC, verifying their amorphous nature. In comparison, unmodified CaCO3 displayed a typical crystalline pattern, suggesting that PAA acts as a stabilizer to promote the formation of a stable amorphous structure in UAPNCs. Therefore, AMC and AGC possessed markedly negative zeta potentials of −35 ± 1.9 and −33 ± 1.2 mV, respectively (Fig. 1f), indicating their outstanding colloidal stability in DI.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectra of AMC revealed characteristic signals for Mn 2p, O 1s, C 1s, and Na KLL (Fig. S2a). In the high-resolution Mn 2p core-level spectrum (Fig. 1g), two prominent peaks at 641.6 and 653.7 eV were assigned to Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 spin-orbit splitting states [39]. A satellite peak was also observed in the high-energy region, which was ascribed to charge-transfer satellite transitions [40]. Furthermore, the energy splitting between the Mn 3s3/2 and Mn 3s1/2 peaks was determined to be 6.0 eV (Fig. 1h), demonstrating that Mn existed predominantly as Mn2+ in AMC [41]. Similarly, XPS survey spectra of AGC showed characteristic peaks for O 1s, C 1s, Gd 4d, Na KLL, and Cl 2p (Fig. S2b). In the high-resolution Gd 4d core-level spectrum (Fig. 1i), two distinct peaks at 141.9 and 144.1 eV were assigned to Gd 4d5/2, while two additional peaks at 147.6 and 149.2 eV corresponded to Gd 4d3/2, in good agreement with the spectral features of Gd3+ [42]. Meanwhile, the high-resolution O 1s spectra of both AMC and AGC displayed three well-resolved peaks at approximately 536.0, 532.6, and 531.6 eV, attributed to adsorbed water, single-bonded oxygen (C-O), and double-bonded oxygen (C=O), respectively (Fig. S2c and S2d). This spectral profile may originate from the similar chemical environments of carboxylate and carbonate moieties, leading to the overlap of their characteristic O 1s photoelectron signals [43]. In addition, the detectable Na KLL and Cl 2p signals in AMC and AGC may arise from the adsorption of sodium and chloride ions on the nanocluster surfaces.

Unlike conventional crystalline nanoparticles, UAPNCs displayed pronounced paramagnetic behavior rather than superparamagnetism (Fig. 1j), which is beneficial to their T1-enhanced MRI performance. Accordingly, to further evaluate the relaxivity of UAPNCs, the nanoclusters were dispersed in DI at gradient concentrations and imaged using a 3.0 T clinical MRI scanner with standard fast spin-echo (FSE) T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), FSE T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), T1 mapping, and T2 mapping sequences. As evident from the images, the T1WI signal exhibited a gradual brightening trend with increasing UAPNCs concentration (Fig. 1k), whereas the T2WI signal showed a slight darkening effect (Fig. 1l). Linear fitting of concentration-dependent 1/T1 and 1/T2 values derived from T1 mapping (Fig. 1m) and T2 mapping (Fig. 1n) afforded r1 of 26.71 ± 0.63 and 38.64 ± 0.42 mM−1s−1 for representative AMC and AGC, respectively (Fig. 1o and Table S1), with corresponding r2 of 74.08 ± 0.78 and 66.87 ± 0.71 mM−1s−1 (Fig. 1p). Obviously, the r1 of AGC was nearly 1.5-fold that of AMC, which can be rationalized by the larger number of unpaired electrons and higher magnetic moments of Gd3+ relative to Mn2+ [6], and also consistent with the stronger magnetization of AGC observed in magnetic hysteresis loops. In contrast, the r2 of AMC was slightly higher than that of AGC, in line with the intrinsically greater r2 of free Mn2+ compared with Gd3+ (Fig. S3a). Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. S3b, the r1 of AMC was nearly 5-fold higher than that of Gd-EOB-DTPA (5.7 ± 0.03 mM−1s−1), while the r1 of AGC was more than 8-fold that of Gd-DTPA (4.58 ± 0.10 mM−1s−1), confirming the excellent r1 of UAPNCs. Of particular significance, the r2/r1 ratios of AMC and AGC were as low as 2.8 and 1.7, respectively, well satisfying the criteria for ideal T1 CAs [44].

Collectively, these results verify the successful fabrication of UAPNCs through a facile synthetic strategy, in which PAA serves as a stabilizer to endow the nanoclusters with an ultrasmall amorphous structure, homogeneous morphology, and favorable colloidal stability. Importantly, the high r1 and low r2/r1 ratios render UAPNCs promising candidates for high-performance T1 MRI CAs.

High hydration endows UAPNCs with a large q and q'

First, a series of control samples were prepared to investigate the origin of the exceptionally high r1 of UAPNCs. As seen in Fig. 2a, the r1 of free Mn2+ was only 5.44 ± 0.03 mM−1s−1 and remained nearly unchanged upon simple physical mixing with PAA (5.79 ± 0.05 mM−1s−1). Intriguingly, the r1 surged to 31.08 ± 0.34 mM−1s−1 when Mn2+ met ACC, even surpassing that of AMC. In contrast, co-mixing of Mn2+, PAA, and unmodified CaCO3 yielded only a modest increase in r1 (9.77 ± 0.56 mM−1s−1). Analogous results were obtained for AGC (Fig. 2b), suggesting that the ultrasmall amorphous structure may be indispensable for efficient r1 boosting. To further validate the universality of this structure-property relationship, other inorganic salts (e.g., sodium silicate (Na2SiO3), sodium sulfite (Na2SO3), and sodium phosphate (Na3PO4)) were employed to construct UAPNCs. The resultant MnSiO3, MnSO3, and Mn3(PO4)2 nanoclusters, which were verified to be amorphous by XRD analysis (Fig. S4a), delivered comparable r1 of 28.37 ± 0.21, 23.38 ± 0.18, and 25.77 ± 0.24 mM−1s−1, respectively (Fig. 2c). For Gd-based analogs, the r1 of Gd2(SiO3)3 and Gd2(SO3)3 nanoclusters (39.65 ± 0.29 and 35.33 ± 0.35 mM−1s−1) were similar to that of AGC (Fig. 2d), whereas GdPO4 nanoclusters exhibited a substantially lower r1 (15.92 ± 0.24 mM−1s−1). As revealed by TEM images, there were negligible morphological differences between GdPO4 and AGC (Fig. S4b), ruling out morphology as the main cause. Therefore, this discrepancy in r1 mainly stems from the mixed crystalline-amorphous phase of GdPO4, while Gd2(SiO3)3 and Gd2(SO3)3 remained purely amorphous (Fig. 2e). In brief, the ultrasmall amorphous structure acts as the dominant factor responsible for the high r1 of UAPNCs.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was further performed to unveil the underlying mechanism of relaxivity enhancement endowed by the ultrasmall amorphous structure. As depicted in Fig. 2f, pure PAA initiated decomposition at approximately 180 °C, and the weight loss below this temperature was ascribed to water evaporation, demonstrating its strong hygroscopic nature. Evidently, unmodified crystalline CaCO3 contained neither water nor PAA, with decomposition commencing at around 550 °C. In contrast, unmodified Gd2(CO3)3, verified to be amorphous (Fig. S5a), exhibited a water content of about 11%, highlighting the superior hydration capability of the amorphous structure. Consequently, on the basis of dual hydration of amorphous structure and PAA, the water content of ACC, AMC, and AGC reached up to 20%, in good agreement with the previous report [35]. Of note, the decomposition temperature of PAA in UAPNCs was elevated to nearly 350 °C owing to the formation of carboxylate-metal coordination, which is consistent with the decomposition behavior of pure sodium polyacrylate (PAA-Na, Fig. S5b). In addition, a further weight-loss step was observed above 600 °C in the TGA profiles of AMC, AGC, and ACC, which was attributed to the decomposition of the carbonate components, further corroborating the carbonate-based composition of the UAPNCs. Similarly, other Mn-based UAPNCs also displayed high water content (Fig. 2g). More importantly, compared with other Gd-based UAPNCs, GdPO4 nanoclusters showed a drastically reduced water content (Fig. 2h), which is consistent with the interpretation that the ultrasmall amorphous structure enhances r1 by increasing the water content of the UAPNCs. To rule out the influence of τR on r1, the mass fraction of metal ions, which indirectly reflects their molecular weight, was calculated from inductively coupled plasma (ICP) results after weighing each lyophilized powder sample. As shown in Fig. S6, the Mn content was comparable among all Mn-based UAPNCs, and an identical trend was observed for Gd-based samples. These results indicate that the distinct r1 performance between crystalline and amorphous nanoclusters arises primarily from their differential hydration capacities, which can to some extent endow UAPNCs with a large q.

Moreover, in order to clarify the contribution of PAA hydration to the high r1 of UAPNCs, ultrasmall manganese ferrite (MnFe2O4) nanoparticles were synthesized according to our previous study [36]. As displayed in Fig. S7, MnFe2O4 nanoparticles possessed a diameter of 3.0 ± 0.35 nm and a crystalline structure. Although PAA-modified MnFe2O4 (PAA-MnFe2O4) nanoparticles showed a relatively high r1 of 10.03 ± 0.13 mM−1s−1 compared to that of tetramethylammonium hydroxide-modified MnFe2O4 (TMAH-MnFe2O4) nanoparticles (4.18 ± 0.09 mM−1s−1), their r1 was still markedly lower than that of AMC (Fig. 2i). Meanwhile, PAA-MnFe2O4 nanoparticles exhibited a water content of approximately 11%, which was exactly comparable to that of unmodified Gd2(CO3)3, whereas the water content of TMAH-MnFe2O4 nanoparticles was less than 3% (Fig. 2j). These results suggest that PAA may endow nanoparticles with a large q', thereby enhancing their r1. Nevertheless, despite the similar hydration capacity of the amorphous structure and PAA, the r1-boosting effect of the amorphous structure is significantly stronger, in line with the SBM theory [1] wherein the increase of q contributes far more efficiently to r1 than that of q'. Accordingly, we tentatively attribute the remarkable r1 enhancement enabled by the amorphous structure to an increase in q rather than q'.

Hydrophilic and oil-to-water phase-transferable ultrasmall amorphous gadolinium oxide (Gd2O3) nanoclusters (denoted as Gd2O3-W and Gd2O3-O/W) were synthesized to further validate the above findings. As presented in Fig. 2k, the r1 of Gd2O3-W (42.68 ± 0.41 mM−1s−1) was nearly identical to that of Gd2O3-O/W (42.78 ± 0.42 mM−1s−1) and even higher than that of AGC. Their r2 were also comparable (70.08 ± 0.63 and 74.11 ± 0.87 mM−1s−1, Fig. S8a), with similar water (Fig. 2l) and Gd (Fig. S8b) content. XRD analysis further verified their amorphous nature (Fig. S8c). Additionally, the diameter of Gd2O3-O was approximately 1.5 nm and increased slightly to ∼2 nm after PAA modification, matching the size of Gd2O3-W (Fig. S8d). These results indicate that Gd2O3 nanoclusters manifest comparable performance to AGC, further supporting the crucial role of the superior hydration ability originating from the amorphous structure and PAA of UAPNCs.

Taken together, regardless of paramagnetic metal species, anion types, and chemical forms (i.e., inorganic metal salts or metal oxides), all nanoclusters featuring an ultrasmall amorphous structure and PAA modification exhibit high water content, which is proposed to confer large q and q' and thereby contribute to exceptional r1, highlighting the remarkable superiority and universality of UAPNCs as novel MRI CAs.

Synthetic optimization and τR modulation of UAPNCs

To explore the effects of various components and synthetic parameters on the r1 of UAPNCs, a series of samples were synthesized under controlled conditions. As shown in Fig. 3a and Table S2, the r1 of AMC decreased from 36.47 ± 0.18 to 17.40 ± 0.30 mM−1s−1 with increasing sole Mn2+ dosage from 0.01 to 0.96 mmol, while their r2 first decreased from 93.15 ± 0.24 to 62.35 ± 1.93 mM−1s−1 and then slightly increased (Fig. S9a). For AGC, both r1 and r2 declined progressively with the elevation of Gd3+ dosage (Fig. S9b and S9c). This phenomenon is likely attributable to the reduction in the τR of AMC as the Mn2+ dosage increased, which is corroborated by the gradually enhanced Mn content (Fig. 3b), whereas no significant variations in water and PAA content were observed (Fig. 3c). In other words, despite the constant total amount of PAA, the molecular weight of each individual nanocluster was reduced, which is clearly exemplified by AGC prepared with 0.96 mmol of Gd3+ (AGC-0.96). As revealed by the TEM images (Fig. S9d), compared with AMC-0.96 and AGC-0.72, AGC-0.96 exhibited obvious aggregation due to insufficient PAA, accompanied by a sharp decline in r1. Thus, these results indicate that the r1 of UAPNCs is modulated by τR, which may be regulated by PAA dosage, and distinct PAA dosage requirements exist for different UAPNCs.

Hence, a series of AMC samples with varying PAA dosages were subsequently fabricated. In contrast to altering the Mn2+ dosage, the r1 of AMC first increased from 14.11 ± 0.11 to 26.31 ± 0.24 mM−1s−1 and then slightly decreased with augmenting PAA dosage (Fig. 3d and Table S3), even though the Mn content decreased continuously (Fig. 3e). An analogous trend was observed for AGC (Fig. S10a), yet a higher PAA dosage was required to achieve the maximum r1. Notably, AMC modified with 50 mg of PAA (AMC-50) showed apparent darkening due to deficient PAA (Fig. S10b). The addition of anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) rendered the reaction solution alkaline, which induced the oxidation of Mn2+ and thus resulted in a lower r1. Unexpectedly, as presented in Fig. 3f, the water content of AMC-400 was lower than that of AMC-200. This observation may be attributed to the excessive PAA dosage rendering the reaction solution acidic, leading to incomplete conversion of carboxyl groups to carboxylate groups and consequently abundant free PAA in AMC-400. This is supported by the earlier thermal degradation behavior of PAA in AMC-400 relative to AMC-200 and AMC-100. In fact, the actual effective PAA content in AMC did not increase, or even decreased, under this high-dosage condition. Accordingly, increasing the PAA dosage can indeed enhance the r1 of UAPNCs, but this effect follows a parabolic trend. Furthermore, sufficient PAA serves as a protective agent to inhibit Mn2+ oxidation, which also provides a critical basis for maintaining the structural stability of UAPNCs.

To further verify the above findings, a series of AMC and AGC samples with variable Na2CO3 dosages were also prepared. Evidently, the r1 of both AMC (Fig. 3g and Table S4) and AGC (Fig. S11a) increased with increasing Na2CO3 dosage, accompanied by a decrease in Mn content (Fig. 3h) and an augment in water content (Fig. 3i). This phenomenon can be attributed to the elevation of the actual effective PAA content in UAPNCs despite the unchanged total PAA dosage, which can be fully corroborated by the observation that AMC synthesized with 0.5 mmol Na2CO3 (AMC-0.5) exhibited a degradation trend similar to that of AMC-400, while AGC-0.5 displayed the same aggregation behavior as AGC-0.96 (Fig. S11b). Moreover, AMC samples with a fixed Mn2+ dosage (0.72 mmol) but varying PAA and Na2CO3 dosages were fabricated. As illustrated in Fig. 3j, the r1 of AMC increased with the simultaneous increase in PAA and Na2CO3 dosages, along with a decrease in Mn content (Fig. 3k), denoting an increase in the actual effective PAA content. Notably, when the PAA dosage was maintained at 600 mg and the Na2CO3 dosage was increased to 4.5 mmol, the r1 of AMC reached 29.51 ± 0.23 mM−1s−1, implying that large-scale synthesis of UAPNCs with high r1 should be feasible through the rational adjustment of PAA and Na2CO3 dosages. Essentially, the optimization of paramagnetic metal ion, PAA, and Na2CO3 dosages aims to ensure an appropriate effective PAA content and optimal τR, which requires both sufficient PAA and a neutral reaction environment.

In addition, other polycarboxylic acids were used for the synthesis of UAPNCs. As depicted in Fig. 3l and Table S5, when PAA-1800 in AMC was replaced with 200 mg of polyaspartic acid (PASP), PMA, PAA-3000, or PAA-5000, the resulting r1 were 18.77 ± 0.07, 20.31 ± 0.11, 27.91 ± 0.24, and 25.45 ± 0.31 mM−1s−1, respectively. Similar results were obtained for AGC (Fig. S12a). XRD analysis confirmed that all these samples exhibited an amorphous structure (Fig. S12b and S12c), and TEM imaging verified the ultrasmall size and good dispersity of PASP-modified AMC (AMC-PASP, Fig. S12d) and PMA-modified AMC (AMC-PMA, Fig. S12e) nanoclusters, verifying that other polycarboxylic acids beyond PAA can also enable the formation of UAPNCs. Notably, the r1 of AMC-PASP was lower than that of AMC, despite the similar morphology. This can be attributed to the lower carboxyl group density of PASP compared with PAA, which may reduce the q′. Consistent with this, TGA analysis revealed a decreased water content in AMC-PASP relative to AMC (Fig. 3m). In contrast, the lower r1 of AMC-PMA may be attributed to a shorter τR, which could be improved by increasing the PMA dosage (Fig. S12f). The r1 of AMC modified with different molecular weights of PAA exhibited only slight differences. Nevertheless, the r1 of these samples was governed by PAA dosage (Fig. S12g and S12h, and Table S6), with only the optimal PAA dosage varying among them. Therefore, the type of polycarboxylic acid does not affect the synthesis of UAPNCs, but the carboxyl group density and molecular weight of the polycarboxylic acids can influence r1 by altering q′ and τR, respectively, which can be effectively compensated by adjusting the polycarboxylic acid dosage.

Furthermore, the influence of reaction time was also examined. Surprisingly, no obvious differences in r1 were observed for AMC (Fig. 3n and Table S7) or AGC (Fig. S12i) over a time range of 1, 10, 30, and 120 min, suggesting that the reaction time has negligible impact on the relaxivity performance of UAPNCs under the current synthetic system. In addition, several common surface modifiers, including polyethylene glycol (PEG), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), were tested under identical synthetic procedures. Nevertheless, no well-defined UAPNCs were successfully obtained with any of these alternatives.

On the basis of the above systematic investigations, the optimal synthetic parameters were determined. The highest r1 of AMC reached 41.27 ± 0.33 mM−1s−1 (Fig. 3o), with a corresponding r2 of 106.58 ± 0.12 mM−1s−1 (Fig. 3p), achieved using 0.01 mmol of Mn2+, 200 mg of PAA-1800, 2 mmol of Na2CO3, and a reaction time of 1 min. However, considering practical yield and cost efficiency, the typical synthetic conditions are recommended as the preferred protocol for AMC preparation in this work. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. S13, the large-scale synthesized AMC prepared with 10 mmol Mn2+, 4 g PAA, and 20 mmol Na2CO3 exhibited a r1 of 24.73 ± 0.60 mM−1s−1 and a r2 of 53.53 ± 0.46 mM−1s−1, with a corresponding synthesis yield of approximately 70%. These results are similar to those of the representative AMC, thus confirming the reliability and feasibility of the large-scale synthesis route.

Overall, within the current synthetic system, only polycarboxylic acids can facilitate the formation of UAPNCs with excellent performance. Moreover, the type and molecular weight of polycarboxylic acids have little impact on the r1 of UAPNCs, and such minor effects can be effectively compensated by adjusting their dosage. Meanwhile, the variation in r1 of UAPNCs with the dosage of different components is most likely attributable to the change in the amount of effective PAA, which may further affect the τR of UAPNCs and thereby regulate their r1. Accordingly, the large-scale synthesis of high-performance UAPNCs is feasible, laying a solid foundation for their future biological application.

Nearby non-paramagnetic metal ions confer UAPNCs with a larger q′

Interestingly, the preceding text noted that the r1 of the mixture of Mn2+ and ACC was even higher than that of pure AMC, implying that the introduction of metal ions might modulate the relaxivity of UAPNCs. To investigate this effect, several common non-paramagnetic metal ions, including magnesium ions (Mg2+), calcium ion (Ca2+), and zinc ion (Zn2+), were further incorporated into UAPNCs in this study. Given its ionic radius similar to that of Mn2+ (0.72 vs 0.83) [45] and its ability to maintain the stability of amorphous structure [46], Mg2+ was first discussed. As illustrated in Fig. 4a and b, and Table S8, when the total molar amount of Mn2+ and Mg2+ was fixed at 0.24 mmol, the r1 of the resulting Mn-MgCO3 nanoclusters decreased from 39.56 ± 0.20 to 28.24 ± 0.24 mM−1s−1 as the dosage of Mn2+ increased from 0.01 to 0.22 mmol, while the r2 decreased from 108.54 ± 0.81 to 64.42 ± 0.44 mM−1s−1. Obviously, both the r1 and r2 of Mn-MgCO3 were almost consistently higher than those of typical AMC, indicating that Mg2+ incorporation can significantly enhance the relaxivity of UAPNCs, with a higher Mg2+ proportion corresponding to a higher r1. However, for Mn-MgCO3 synthesized with a fixed Mn2+ dosage (0.04 mmol), the r1 showed a slight parabolic trend with the gradual increase in Mg2+ dosage (Fig. 4c). Unlike pure AMC, this trend is likely governed by the balance between the increased Mg2+ proportion and the elevated total metal ion dosage. This is supported by the observation that the r1 of Mn-MgCO3 nanoclusters gradually decreased with an increase in the total molar amount of metal ions at a fixed Mn2+/Mg2+ molar ratio (1:5, Fig. 4d). Furthermore, homologous Mn-CaCO3 nanoclusters exhibited an identical trend, with their r1 decreasing from 41.05 ± 0.22 to 25.63 ± 0.21 mM−1s−1 (Fig. 4e). These results suggest that such incorporation can indeed enhance the relaxivity of UAPNCs, and this effect may not be unique to Mg2+ but rather a universal phenomenon for non-paramagnetic metal ions.

Thus, in order to further verify the universality of relaxivity enhancement via non-paramagnetic metal ion doping in UAPNCs, a series of controlled samples were prepared. First, as displayed in Fig. 4f and Table S9, Mn-CaCO3, Mn-MgCO3, and Mn-ZnCO3 nanoclusters were synthesized with a fixed Mn2+ dosage (0.04 mmol) and an identical non-paramagnetic metal ion dosage (0.2 mmol), yielding r1 of 37.13 ± 0.26, 36.74 ± 0.28, and 36.37 ± 0.16 mM−1s−1, respectively. Compared with pure AMC, the r1 of these doped nanoclusters increased by nearly 40%, with no significant differences observed among the three metal ion types, demonstrating that this relaxivity enhancing effect is independent of the non-paramagnetic metal ion species. Similar results were obtained for Gd-CaCO3, Gd-MgCO3, Gd-ZnCO3 nanoclusters (Fig. S14a). Next, to eliminate the influence of anionic species, Mn-CaSiO3, Mn-CaSO3, and Mn-Ca3(PO4)2 nanoclusters were fabricated (Fig. 4g). Although minor variations in r1 were observed among these samples, all exhibited a similar relaxivity-enhancing effect relative to their undoped nanoclusters. Similar results could be observed for Gd-CaSiO3, Gd-CaSO3, and Gd-Ca3(PO4)2 nanoclusters (Fig. S14b). In addition, this enhancing effect was also observed for other Mn-based nanoclusters (Fig. 4h). Importantly, XRD analysis further revealed that all these doped nanoclusters exhibited an amorphous structure (Fig. 4i and Fig. S14c), except for Gd-Ca3(PO4)2, just like GdPO4, which possessed a mixed crystalline-amorphous phase and a clearly lower r1 (Fig. S14d). Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. S15, the Mn-CaCO3, Mn-MgCO3, Mn-ZnCO3, Mn-CaSiO3, Mn-CaSO3, and Mn-Ca3(PO4)2 nanoclusters all maintained an ultrasmall size. Collectively, these results confirm that the strategy of non-paramagnetic metal ion doping to enhance the r1 of UAPNCs is universal, and this effect has no obvious correlation with the specific types of metal cations and anions employed in UAPNCs.

So, what exactly is the mechanism underlying this enhancement effect? As is well known, appropriate metal ion doping has emerged as an efficient strategy for optimizing crystalline nanoscale CAs [47], as it can effectively enhance the magnetization of nanoparticles and thus concurrently improve their r1 and r2 [28,48]. However, the magnetization of Mn-CaCO3, Mn-MgCO3, and Mn-ZnCO3 nanoclusters decreased significantly, reaching only 10% of that of AMC, while that of Gd-CaCO3 was 20% of AGC (Fig. 4j), indicating that the increase in their r1 and r2 cannot be attributed to enhanced magnetization. As discussed previously, hydration of paramagnetic nuclei is the dominant contributor to inner-sphere relaxation. But it must be noted that hydration is a common property shared by all metal ions, not unique to paramagnetic metal ions [49]. In other words, for Mn-CaCO3 nanoclusters, it can be inferred that water molecules bound to Ca2+ can interact with adjacent Mn2+, thereby increasing q' and facilitating second-sphere relaxation. More importantly, this effect may contribute more to the relaxation of the nanoclusters than the water molecules bound to the carboxyl groups of PAA, given the shorter spatial distance between Ca2+ and Mn2+. Accordingly, this non-specific enhancement effect is proposed to account for the higher r1 and r2 of the aforementioned non-paramagnetic metal ion-doped UAPNCs relative to pure Mn- and Gd-based nanoclusters. Additionally, since the hydration number of most common divalent metal ions is 6 [50], no significant differences in relaxivity were observed among the corresponding Mg2+-, Ca2+-, and Zn2+-doped nanoclusters in this study.

Furthermore, TGA measurements were performed to confirm this speculation. As illustrated in Fig. 4k, the water content of Mn-CaCO3, Mn-MgCO3, Mn-ZnCO3, Mn-CaSiO3, Mn-CaSO3, and Mn-Ca3(PO4)2 nanoclusters was all approximately 20%, consistent with that of AMC. Combined with the ICP results (Fig. S16a), which demonstrated that the Mn content in the corresponding nanoclusters was basically consistent with the feeding ratio, the molar amount of water molecules per mole of total metal ions in these nanoclusters was calculated to be close to that of AMC (Fig. S16b). On the contrary, the molar amount of water molecules per mole of Mn differed markedly from that of AMC (Fig. 4l). In other words, for Mn-CaCO3 nanoclusters, the number of water molecules capable of effective interaction with each manganese ion is higher than that in pure AMC owing to the additional water-absorbing effect of Ca2+, which may contribute more significantly to relaxivity and thereby endow the nanoclusters with a higher r1.

Admittedly, the actual values cannot be directly determined due to experimental limitations, but these results still indicate to a certain extent that the incorporation of non-specific non-paramagnetic metal ions may increase the q' of the UAPNCs, thereby causing a significant enhancement in r1 and r2.

Hepatocyte-specific MRI and CE-MRA performance of UAPNCs in mice

To verify the applicability of UAPNCs for in vivo hepatocyte-specific MRI and CE-MRA, contrast-enhanced MRI was performed in mice using a clinical 3.0 T scanner. As shown in Fig. 5a, whole-body coronal images were acquired with a three-dimensional fast field echo (3D FFE) sequence after tail vein injection of AMC at doses of 25, 10, and 5 μmol Mn/kg body weight. Markedly, the liver showed significant enhancement following AMC administration, with clear time- and dose-dependent responses, demonstrating the excellent suitability of AMC for hepatocyte-specific imaging. Dynamic contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) analysis revealed that liver CNR increased rapidly after AMC injection, peaked at 12 min, and then gradually declined (Fig. 5b). Moreover, the liver CNR in mice injected with AMC was not only comparable to that in mice administered an equivalent dose of Gd-EOB-DTPA, but also remained relatively high up to 2 h post-injection, thus meeting the demands for long-duration liver imaging. Surprisingly, unlike Gd-EOB-DTPA, obvious vascular enhancement was observed in mice with AMC at 25 μmol Mn/kg (Fig. S17), indicating that AMC is also a promising candidate for CE-MRA.

(i) Vascular reconstruction images of mice administered with AGC (20 μmol Gd/kg) and Gd-DTPA (200 μmol Gd/kg). (j) Blood concentration-time curve of AGC (20 μmol Gd/kg) in mice (n = 3). (k) EDS mapping images of AGC in mouse urine.

Furthermore, the bladder and gallbladder exhibited initial enhancement at 4 min post-injection, demonstrating that AMC, similar to Gd-EOB-DTPA, is metabolized rapidly via both hepatic and renal pathways (Fig. S18). Continuous monitoring for 7 days revealed that the enhancement in the liver and kidneys completely disappeared by 3 days after injection (Fig. S19). Given that endogenous Mn is mainly metabolized through the hepatic-biliary pathway [51], mouse urine was collected continuously for 3 days to evaluate the renal clearance of AMC. The renal clearance efficiency of each group is presented in Fig. S20a, showing that approximately 50% of AMC was excreted via the kidneys. More importantly, subsequent TEM (Fig. S20b) and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) mapping analysis (Fig. 5c) confirmed that AMC was excreted in its intact form. Owing to this excretion behavior, the blood concentration–time curve of AMC can be fitted to a two-compartment model (Fig. 5d), with a distribution half-life (t1/2, α) of 1.4 min and an elimination half-life (t1/2, β) of 18.9 min. Although the short half-life and dual metabolic pathway restrict its utility for long-term angiography, these features markedly improve its biosafety profile.

Hepatocellular carcinoma-bearing mouse models were then established and imaged using an enhanced-T1 high resolution isotropic volume examination (e-THRIVE) sequence after tail vein injection of AMC at 25 and 5 μmol Mn/kg. Transverse MRI images of the liver and corresponding H&E-stained pathological sections were displayed in Fig. 5e. Consistent with the findings in normal mice, normal liver tissue exhibited significant enhancement, whereas tumors displayed a distinct hypointense signal. Remarkably, even at a low AMC dose of 5 μmol Mn/kg, tumors were clearly visualized including those as small as 1 mm (Fig. S21). These results indicate that AMC holds great promise to serve as an alternative hepatocyte-specific CA for the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma.

Subsequently, a series of experiments were conducted to further explore the mechanism underlying the hepatocyte-specific imaging performance of AMC. First, similar in vivo MRI results were obtained for other Mn-based UAPNCs (Fig. S22), revealing that different anions do not interfere with hepatocyte-specific imaging. Next, whole-body coronal images were acquired after tail vein injection of AGC and Gd2O3-W at 20 μmol Gd/kg. As shown in Fig. S23, in contrast to AMC, the liver displayed only a typical perfusion enhancement pattern, with no obvious enhancement in the gallbladder. This confirms that the absence of Mn2+ abolishes specific hepatic enhancement. To further validate this conclusion, Mn-doped AGC nanoclusters (Mn-AGC) were synthesized. As expected, imaging results showed that specific hepatic enhancement was restored (Fig. S24). Meanwhile, consistent findings were obtained in cellular experiments. As seen in Fig. 5f, Mn uptake by AML-12 cells exhibited a clear time-dependence. Additionally, compared with AGC, Gd uptake by AML-12 cells was significantly increased after co-incubation with Mn-AGC (Fig. S25). This occurs because AML-12 cells not only take up Mn2+ but also passively internalize Gd3+ simultaneously, indirectly supporting that AML-12 cells phagocytose intact AMC nanoclusters rather than free Mn2+ released from AMC. Moreover, Gd-CaCO3 and Gd-MgCO3 nanoclusters were employed for in vivo imaging, and no obvious specific enhancement was observed in the liver, ruling out the involvement of other divalent metal ions in mediating hepatocyte-specific imaging (Fig. S26). Accordingly, it can be concluded that the presence of Mn2+ in AMC is likely the key factor enabling hepatocyte-specific MRI.

As an essential trace element in the human body, Mn is transported into hepatocytes mainly via solute carrier family 39 member 14 (SLC39A14), a transmembrane metal transporter [52,53]. Particularly, SLC39A14 can also mediate the transport of certain ultrasmall Mn-doped nanoparticles [54,55]. To clarify the role of SLC39A14 in AMC transport, western blot (WB) analysis was first performed to compare SLC39A14 protein expression among SLC39A14-knockout AML-12 cells (AML-KO), wild-type AML-12 cells, and Hepa 1-6 cells. As shown in Fig. 5g, SLC39A14 protein was significantly higher in AML-12 cells than in AML-KO and Hepa 1-6 cells. Interestingly, in all parallel experiments in this study, the SLC39A14 protein was not detected at its expected molecular weight in Hepa 1-6 cells (Fig. S27). Instead, a distinct band was observed at a slightly lower molecular weight, which may arise from the high expression of a specific SLC39A14 isoform in these cancer cells [56,57]. Consistent with the WB results, Mn uptake by AML-12 cells was significantly higher than that by the other two cell lines (Fig. 5h). Therefore, SLC39A14 may play an important role in the transport of AMC in hepatocytes, which also represents an important basis for AMC to achieve hepatocyte-specific imaging and hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis.

Finally, the CE-MRA performance of AGC was evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. S28a, unlike AMC or Gd-DTPA, mild vascular enhancement was still observed 2 h after injection of AGC at 20 μmol Gd/kg, which can be attributed to its prolonged blood circulation. Quantitative analysis of the vascular enhancement ratio further confirmed that AGC at 20 μmol Gd/kg achieved vascular enhancement comparable to that of Gd-DTPA at 200 μmol Gd/kg (Fig. S28b). However, owing to the blood-pool property of Gd-based nanoclusters, the background signal was substantially cleaner than that of Gd-DTPA, resulting in higher vessel-to-background contrast. Accordingly, vascular reconstruction images of AGC (Fig. 5i), Gd2(SiO3)3, and Gd2(SO3)3 (Fig. S29) demonstrated that, even at one-tenth the dose of Gd-DTPA, Gd-based nanoclusters produced markedly clearer vascular imaging with less background noise. Notably, the t1/2, α (5.9 min) and t1/2, β (127.3 min) of AGC were longer than those of AMC (Fig. 5j). Similarly, EDS mapping analysis confirmed that AGC was excreted via the kidneys in its intact form (Fig. 5k).

Overall, the in vivo imaging results of UAPNCs demonstrate their distinct and excellent MRI performance. Specifically, AMC exhibits dual potential as a hepatocyte-specific MRI and CE-MRA CA, characterized by prominent hepatic enhancement and clear visualization of small tumors at low doses. In contrast, AGC is an outstanding candidate for CE-MRA due to its prolonged circulation and superior low-dose vascular enhancement.

Stability and biocompatibility of UAPNCs

Excellent stability and biocompatibility are critical prerequisites for the biological application of UAPNCs. On the basis of the above results, a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments were carried out to systematically evaluate their biosafety profiles. Firstly, as presented in Fig. 6a, XRD analysis verified that AMC powder stored at room temperature exhibited excellent stability, maintaining an amorphous structure even at 6 and 15 months after synthesis. In addition, the r1 of AMC before and after freeze-drying was measured at 6 and 15 months (Fig. 6b and Table S10). No significant differences were observed between pre- and post-freeze-dried AMC, whereas slight decreases in r1 were detected at 6 and 15 months regardless of storage in solution or powder form. This minor reduction may be attributed to the slow oxidation of Mn2+ under ambient conditions, which can be readily avoided by sealed storage. In contrast, the r1 of AGC remained unchanged, and AGC retained its amorphous nature up to 16 months post-synthesis (Fig. S30).

The stability of AMC dispersed in various solutions was also evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. S31 and Table S11, both r1 and r2 remained nearly constant over 3 days irrespective of the dispersion medium, further verifying the great stability of AMC. Notably, the r1 of AMC dispersed in fetal bovine serum (FBS) was lower than that in other solutions, which can be attributed to protein adsorption onto the AMC surface. This adsorption generates an impermeable layer that reduces water exchange efficiency, as supported by the significantly increased r2, in good agreement with our previous results [36]. Of note, even in acetate buffers at pH 5.0, AMC showed no obvious degradation due to PAA modification, demonstrating their great stability. Regrettably, AMC does not function as a pH-responsive CA.

Subsequently, cytotoxicity was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay by measuring cell viability. AML-12 and Hepa 1-6 cells were incubated with AMC at Mn concentrations ranging from 0.03125 to 1 mM for 24 h (Fig. 6c and d). The results showed that cell viability remained above 80% for both cell lines at all tested concentrations, indicating low cytotoxicity and favorable biocompatibility of AMC. Analogously, AGC also displayed great biosafety (Fig. S32). It is worth noting that cell viability gradually decreased with increasing metal ion concentration, highlighting the importance of developing CAs with high relaxivity to allow lower clinical doses. Hemolysis assay was also performed by co-incubating fresh mouse red blood cells with AMC and AGC at 37 °C for 4 h. The hemolysis rate was below 2% at concentrations less than 2 mM (Fig. 6e and f), demonstrating their great biocompatibility.

Finally, the in vivo biosafety of AMC and AGC was evaluated using biodistribution analysis, routine blood tests, blood biochemistry tests, and histological examination. Considering the rapid metabolism of AMC, its biodistribution was only examined at 3 days post-injection with a dosage of 25 μmol Mn/kg (Fig. 6g). Interestingly, compared with the control group, Mn levels in most organs of AMC-injected mice were lower, except for a slight elevation in the liver, which may reflect compensatory excretion to maintain Mn homeostasis. In contrast, Gd residues were still observed in major organs at 3 days post-injection of AGC, with the exception of the brain (Fig. 6h). This suggests that, in the absence of a hepatic metabolic pathway, the clearance of Gd is slower than that of Mn. Meanwhile, routine blood and blood biochemistry tests indicated that both AMC and AGC exhibited favorable biosafety even at a dosage (100 μmol Mn or Gd/kg) four or five times higher than that used for imaging (Fig. 6i and Fig. S33), where the light blue shaded regions represent reference intervals according to the previous report [58]. Additionally, histological examination of major organs validated the in vivo biosafety of AMC and AGC at a dose of 100 μmol Mn or Gd/kg at 14 days post-injection (Fig. 6j). No obvious histological abnormalities, inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or pathological lesions were observed in these organs, indicating that neither AMC nor AGC induced obvious organ damage or toxicity at the tested dose.

Altogether, these results demonstrate that the representative AMC and AGC possess excellent stability and favorable biocompatibility in vitro and in vivo, despite the minor and readily resolvable disturbances, laying the foundation for further biological application.

Conclusion

In summary, this work systematically demonstrates the universality and superiority of UAPNCs as MRI CAs. By integrating the merits of ultrasmall amorphous structures and polycarboxylic acid, UAPNCs are proposed to simultaneously enhance q, q′ and τR, thereby delivering an exceptional r1 while maintaining low r2/r1 ratios. Importantly, the incorporation of non-paramagnetic metal ions into UAPNCs may further improve relaxivity by increasing q′. Promisingly, combined with their facile fabrication, tunable composition, excellent stability, and favorable biocompatibility, UAPNCs exhibit great potential as next-generation MRI CAs, especially AMC with dual capacity for hepatocyte-specific MRI and CE-MRA. Notably, composition-tunable non-paramagnetic metal ions-doped UAPNCs hold remarkable promise as personalized theranostic agents. Nonetheless, due to experimental limitations, nuclear magnetic relaxation dispersion (NMRD) profiles were not acquired, preventing direct quantification of the precise values of q, q′ and τR, and the mechanistic interpretations presented herein should therefore be regarded as correlational rather than definitive. Furthermore, the intrinsic characteristics of Fe3+ hampered the construction of corresponding Fe-based UAPNCs in the present work, which deserves further exploration. Overall, this study not only provides a class of high-performance MRI CAs but also offers a generalizable materials design paradigm for advanced translational nanomedicine.
