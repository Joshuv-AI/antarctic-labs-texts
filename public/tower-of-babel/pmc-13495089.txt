High-fat diet exposure, internal state, and reward value influence decision making during motivational conflict
Authors: Rodolfo J. Flores, Rosario B. Jaime-Lara, Miguel A. Arenivar, Monique Awanyai, Alice M. Graham, Héctor Bravo-Rivera, Claudia Colina-Prisco, Rodrigo S.E. Ortiz Figueroa, Mari Chiles, Hugo A. Tejeda, Paule V. Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Survival depends on adaptive decision-making wherein the benefits of seeking food are weighed against the risk of threats, a process influenced by internal states like hunger. Highly palatable, calorie-dense foods, such as a high-fat-containing diet (HFD), potently drive feeding even in the absence of hunger. However, it remains unclear whether approach-avoidance conflict is shaped by the interaction between hunger state, motivation for rewards of differing palatability, and the lasting effects of chronic high-fat diet exposure (cHFD). Here, we examined how hunger, reward value, and cHFD exposure affect behavioral strategies for resolving approach-avoidance conflict. We established a modified platform-mediated avoidance (PMA) task where mice resolve conflict between approaching a reward and avoiding a footshock. In this task, we systematically manipulated three key variables: (1) hunger state [via 6-hour (6h) vs. 23-hour (23h) food restriction], (2) reward type (sucrose vs. a high-fat liquid (HF-reward)), and (3) diet history (cHFD vs. control diet). We found that hunger, reward value, and diet pre-exposure shaped conflict resolution. Hunger increased risk-taking, with 23h food-restricted mice seeking more rewards than mice with limited 6h food restriction. Similarly, HF-reward increased approach behavior even under 6h food restriction. Importantly, cHFD pre-exposure robustly suppressed reward-seeking during conflict, overriding the motivational effects of both hunger and acute reward value. These findings highlight the interaction between internal states (e.g., hunger), reward value, and environmental factors (e.g., diet) in shaping behavior during conflict.

Introduction

Adaptive decision-making, where animals weigh the benefits of pursuing rewards against the risks of encountering threats, is essential for survival. This process involves integrating subjective internal states, such as hunger and fear, with external cues like food availability and predator presence (Aupperle et al., 2015; Aupperle and Paulus, 2010; Flavell et al., 2022; Mobbs et al., 2018; Moscarello and Penzo, 2022; Quartz, 2009; Tseng et al., 2023). Deficits in conflict resolution are associated with psychiatric conditions marked by maladaptive reward seeking or excessive avoidance (Loijen et al., 2020; Trew, 2011). Therefore, understanding the factors that influence approach-avoidance conflict behavior is critical for addressing decision-making deficits in stress-related disorders.

Hunger, a primary homeostatic drive, strongly influences approach-avoidance decisions. Animals must balance the need to obtain food against the risks associated with foraging (Flavell et al., 2022; McNamara and Houston, 1986; Mobbs et al., 2018; Moscarello and Penzo, 2022). These decisions reflect the integration of prior experience, threat prediction, and interoceptive state, which may be modulated by metabolic drive (Bach and Dayan, 2017a, Bach and Dayan, 2017b; Berridge, 2004; Burnett et al., 2016; Rossi and Stuber, 2018; Verharen et al., 2020).

Beyond internal states, the subjective value of rewards also plays a critical role in decision-making (Averbeck and Costa, 2017; Padoa-Schioppa, 2011; Pearson et al., 2014). Reward valuation, shaped by past experiences, can influence choice and risk-taking (Levy and Glimcher, 2012; Rangel et al., 2008). While the role of reward value on decision-making is well established, less is known about its influence in motivational conflict, where reward pursuit competes with threat avoidance (Aupperle and Paulus, 2010; Klaassen et al., 2021).

Chronic high-fat diet (cHFD) exposure has been implicated in altered reward processing, mood dysregulation, and increased vulnerability to stress-related disorders (Hryhorczuk et al., 2013; Carlin et al., 2013; Atar, 2026; Cohen et al., 2026). Diets high in fat can either enhance or blunt reward pursuit (Matikainen-Ankney et al., 2023; Mazzone et al., 2020; Oginsky et al., 2016; Sharma and Fulton, 2013), potentially leading to maladaptive decision-making under conflict. Understanding how cHFD exposure biases behavior in such contexts may shed light on mechanisms underlying impaired decision-making in psychiatric illness.

The present study examined how reward value and hunger state shape conflict resolution strategies in mice, and whether cHFD alters these behaviors. This question is especially relevant given links between obesity, cHFD, and depression, a disorder often characterized by impaired motivation and heightened avoidance (Hryhorczuk et al., 2013; Sharma and Fulton, 2013). To assess how hunger alters conflict behavior, we adapted the platform-mediated avoidance (PMA) task for mice (Bravo-Rivera et al., 2014, 2021). Using the PMA task in mice, we investigated how hunger state, reward value, and cHFD interact to influence approach-avoidance conflict behavior. Accordingly, we tested how different rewards (e.g., sucrose vs. HF) modulate conflict resolution. These findings offer insight into the behavioral manifestations of disrupted decision-making relevant to stress-related psychopathology.

Materials and methods

Eighty-two C57BL/6J mice (44 males, 38 females; Jackson Laboratory, Stock No: 000664), aged 8-10 weeks, were initially group-housed (4-5 same-sex littermates per cage) in standard polycarbonate cages (30 × 18 × 12 cm) with corncob bedding and nesting material. Housing was maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, on a 12:12 h reverse light/dark cycle (lights off at 08:00) with ad libitum water. Experiments were conducted during the animals’ dark phase. Unless noted, mice were on ad libitum standard chow (13% kcal fat, 28% protein, 57% carbohydrates; LabDiet 5001). For diet-pre-exposure experiments, mice were given 3 weeks of ad libitum access to chow or a cHFD (60% kcal fat, 20% protein, 20% carbohydrates; D12492, Research Diets Inc.). Mice were pair-housed with a perforated divider to allow food restriction while maintaining social contact. All procedures were approved by the National Institute of Mental Health Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to NIH guidelines.

Platform-mediated avoidance task

Reward training

Mice were trained to nosepoke during a 30-second (30s) light cue to obtain a reward (5 μl), delivered into the nosepoke-port via a mechanical dipper (MedAssociates: ENV-302M), which mice could subsequently retrieve. Each reward remained accessible for 4s or until retrieval. Nosepokes were detected via an infrared beam (MedAssociates: ENV-313M), and sessions consisted of 30 trials with 60s intertrial intervals (ITIs). Depending on the experiment, the reward was 10% (w/v) sucrose (0.4 kcal/mL), a liquid high-fat (HF) emulsion (1 kcal/mL), or undiluted Ensure Original (vanilla; Abbott Laboratories, Columbus, OH; ∼0.93 kcal/mL; ∼22% kcal from fat, ∼64% from carbohydrate, ∼14% from protein); the reward used in each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure (Ensure in Fig. 3). The liquid HF-reward was prepared by mixing 191 g of dry, suspendable high-fat diet powder (60% fat; D12492L, Research Diets) with 809 mL of water to yield a 1 kcal/mL emulsion, then heated to 38 °C prior to testing. The powder contained 200 g casein, 3 g L-cystine, 125 g maltodextrin-10, 68.8 g sucrose, 40 g cellulose, 10 g xanthan gum, 25 g soybean oil, 245 g lard, plus vitamins and minerals per 773.85 g of diet.

Conflict training

Following reward training, mice underwent conflict training. Here, reward conditions remained unchanged (30s light cue signaling reward availability), and conflict was introduced via a 30s auditory tone (2 kHz, 70 dB) that co-terminated with a 2s footshock (0.6 mA, grid floor). Reward trials were separated by 60 s ITIs. Tone-footshock stimuli overlapped with the light cue to generate the following trial types:1. Light-Only (Low Conflict Approach): 30s light cue without footshock or footshock-predictive tone.2. Tone-Only (Low Conflict Avoidance): 30s tone cue ending in a 2s footshock without light cue presentation.3. Co-Presentation (High Conflict Approach-Avoidance): Simultaneous 30s light and tone cues ending in a 2s footshock.4. Light→Tone (Intermediate Conflict Approach-Avoidance): Light presented alone for 15s, followed by 15s tone and light cue presentation overlap, followed by tone alone for 15s post-light offset, co-terminating with a 2s footshock.5. Tone→Light (Intermediate Conflict Avoidance-Approach): Tone presented alone for 15s, followed by 15s tone and light cue presentation overlap and co-terminating with a 2s footshock, followed by 15s light cue in isolation.

After training, satiety was induced by providing 2 g of chow 1 h before testing, and hunger was induced by 24h food restriction. Mice were returned to approximately 85% of free-feeding weight for three days between manipulations to reestablish baseline conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS and graphed in GraphPad Prism. Linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) or repeated-measures ANOVAs were used depending on the structure and completeness of each dataset. LMEs were used to accommodate repeated-measures datasets with missing observations; experimental factors (Restriction, Phase, Day, and Group where applicable) were included as fixed effects, with Mouse as a random intercept to account for repeated observations. When observations were complete, repeated-measures ANOVAs were used, with Restriction, Phase, and Day as within-subjects factors and Group as a between-subjects factor where appropriate. Shock outcomes were analyzed using LMEs with Restriction, Day, and Group (where applicable) as fixed effects. Sex was included as a between-subjects factor only in analyses of the experiment shown in Fig. 1, with the results presented in Fig. S1. The remaining experiments were not powered to test Sex × Group interactions because subdivision by sex and experimental condition resulted in 4-6 mice per cell, or because the experiment included only males, as in Fig. 3. Post hoc comparisons were performed where appropriate using Fisher's Least Significant Difference Test, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. For each analysis, the model type, relevant effect, and corresponding p-value are noted in the figure legend.

Results

Mice Display Adaptive Conflict Resolution: Pursuing Rewards While Avoiding Shocks.

Mice under 23h food restriction underwent reward training wherein a light cue in the reward/nosepoke port signaled sucrose availability. Over six days, mice increased cue-evoked nosepoking (i.e., nosepoking during the light phase) relative to anticipatory nosepoking (i.e., nosepoking during the ITI), indicating acquisition of the light-nosepoke-reward association (Fig. 1B–C). There was a small but significant decrease in time spent on the platform between the light and ITI phases (Fig. 1D), consistent with mice engaging in nosepoking during light presentation. Male and female mice nosepoked at similar rates (Fig. S1A), though females spent overall less time on the platform (Fig. S1B). Following reward training, mice were introduced to a 30s tone that co-terminated with a 2s footshock, generating the conflict trial types described in Section 2.2.2.

During Light-Only trials, nosepoking during the light-phase decreased on the first day of conflict relative to the final day of reward training (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, mice displayed increased nosepoking during the light-phase relative to the ITI (Fig. 1F–G, black lines), with females nosepoking less than males during both the ITI and the light-phase (Fig. S1D). During conflict, mice increased their time on platform during the ITI relative to reward training (Fig. 1D–H), which decreased during the light-phase as mice dismounted the platform to seek rewards (Fig. 1F–H, red lines), indicating that mice adapt their approach-avoidance strategies to maintain reward-seeking in the absence of an immediate threat. In contrast, Tone-Only trials revealed a rapid suppression of nosepoking, and increased time on platform during the tone-phase compared to ITI (Fig. 1F–H), suggesting rapid learning of the tone-shock association. Here, females nosepoked less than males during the ITI but not the tone-phase (Fig. S1D). During Co-Presentation trials, mice suppressed nosepoking and increased platform use (Fig. 1F–H), reflecting a strategy that prioritized avoidance over reward approach. Females again showed reduced nosepoking during the ITI but not during the co-presentation-phase (Fig. S1D). In Light→Tone trials, mice nosepoked during the initial light-phase, then disengaged and moved to the platform upon tone onset, showing flexible behavioral updating, with females nosepoking less than males during both the ITI and the light-phase (Fig. S1D). Conversely, in Tone→Light trials, mice initially suppressed nosepoking and increased platform use, but quickly resumed reward-seeking upon the light-phase. Females nosepoked less than males during the ITI and trended lower during the light-phase (Fig. S1D). Over time, mice adapted their behavior to maximize reward and avoid shocks, reflected by a decreasing proportion of shocks across days for all trial types (Fig. 1I). These findings demonstrate that mice integrate motivationally relevant cues to flexibly resolve approach-avoidance conflict. Female mice poked at lower rates than males during the inter-trial interval and the light cue, while time on platform and shock outcomes remained similar, suggesting that sex shapes reward-seeking vigor but not avoidance during conflict. Formal analyses of sex differences were limited to this cohort because the other experimental cohorts were not powered to test sex-dependent interactions.

Hunger promotes reward-seeking, while satiety promotes avoidance

Having established that mice can adaptively modulate approach and avoidance behaviors in response to cues signaling reward and threat, we next examined how hunger states shape conflict resolution in a separate cohort of mice. During reward training (under 6h food restriction), mice learned the light-reward association, showing increased nosepoking (Fig. 2B–C) and less platform time (Fig. S2A–B) during the light-phase versus the ITI.

During conflict (under 6h food restriction), nosepoking in Light-Only trials decreased relative to the final day of reward training but subsequently increased to reward training levels under 23h restriction (Fig. 2D). Thus, hunger and satiety can bi-directionally influence reward-seeking even during low-conflict contexts. In Tone-Only trials, mice showed similar avoidance behavior regardless of hunger state, receiving few shocks and spending similar platform time during 6h and 23h food restriction (Fig. 2G; Fig. S2C–D). This indicates that threat-avoidance is not affected by hunger state. Interestingly, under 23h restriction, mice spent less platform time during the ITI, suggesting increased risk tolerance. In Co-Presentation, nosepokes were slightly elevated under 23h restriction during ITI and the co-presentation-phase (Fig. 2E–F). Platform time also decreased during these phases under 23h restriction (Fig. S2C–D). In Light→Tone trials, mice under 23h restriction increased nosepoking during ITI, light, and co-presentation phases, but not during the final tone-phase (Fig. 2E–F). Platform use mirrored these patterns, decreasing during ITI, light, and co-presentation-phases but remaining unchanged during the tone-phase (Fig. S2C–D). In Tone→Light trials, heightened hunger (23h restriction) increased nosepoking during ITI, co-presentation, and light phases, but not during the tone-phase (Fig. 2E–F). Platform use decreased across all phases under 23h restriction, including the tone-phase (Fig. S2C–D). Despite modest increases in nosepoking and decreases in platform use under 23h restriction, shock outcomes remained consistent across hunger states (Fig. 2G). Thus, increased risk-taking behavior is not associated with increased shock outcomes. These findings demonstrate that hunger enhances reward motivation in a context-dependent manner without compromising threat assessment. Across all trial types, 23h restriction increased reward seeking and reduced platform use. Meanwhile, shock outcomes remained similar between hunger states (Fig. 2G). Thus, hunger state did not alter threat processing but promoted different strategies which preserved safety while maximizing reward opportunities.

Hunger state modulates reward-seeking and threat avoidance independent of learning

To address whether hunger state modulates approach/avoidance conflict resolution independently of learning, we tested the impact of acute satiety and hunger on reward-seeking behavior. During reward training, mice showed increased nosepoking during the light-phase relative to the ITI in Light-Only trials (Fig. 3B–C), confirming acquisition of the cue-nosepoke-reward contingency. Following five days of conflict training, this pattern persisted in Light-Only trials, with nosepoking remaining elevated during the light-phase relative to the ITI (Fig. 3D–E). In Tone-Only trials, nosepoking was suppressed during the tone to levels comparable to the ITI (Fig. 3D–E). In Co-Presentation trials, nosepoking increased during the co-presentation-phase relative to the ITI (Fig. 3D–E). In Light→Tone trials, nosepoking was elevated during the light and co-presentation-phases relative to the tone and ITI phases (Fig. 3D–E). In Tone→Light trials, nosepoking was elevated during the co-presentation-phase relative to the light, tone, and ITI phases (Fig. 3D–E). Collectively, mice approached during the reward-predictive light cue, suppressed responding during the aversive tone, and rapidly adjusted behavior when cues were presented sequentially or simultaneously.

We next tested whether acute hunger state changes altered conflict responses. When sated, nosepoking showed a trending decrease in Light-Only trials relative to the conflict training baseline (Fig. 3F). Tone-Only trials were unchanged across all phases. In Co-Presentation trials, nosepoking decreased regardless of phase, and in Light→Tone trials, nosepoking decreased across all phases. In Tone→Light trials, where animals first avoid, animals failed to re-engage in nosepoking following the onset of the light cue under both baseline and satiety conditions. Thus, under satiety, mice showed less approach for rewards during conflict.

Finally, we examined whether enhanced food restriction had the opposite effect (Fig. 3G). In Light-Only and Co-Presentation trials, nosepoking increased relative to the conflict training baseline across phases. In Light→Tone trials, nosepoking increased specifically during the light and co-presentation-phases, with no change during the tone-phase. Tone-Only and Tone→Light trials showed minimal change relative to baseline. Of note, suppression of reward seeking during conflict was not as pronounced as in our other experiments, which may be attributed to the use of highly palatable Ensure reward. Nonetheless, these findings demonstrate that hunger state bidirectionally modulates conflict resolution.

Reward value enhances reward-seeking in the face of threats under mild and enhanced food restriction

To assess how reward palatability and hunger state interact to influence approach-avoidance conflict resolution, chow-fed mice were tested in the PMA task with either sucrose or HF-reward, under 6h and 23h food restriction (Fig. 4A). During reward training, both groups increased nosepoking during the light-phase relative to the ITI (Fig. 4B–C), indicating learning of cue-nosepoke-reward contingency. Notably, mice receiving HF-reward displayed greater overall nosepoking, suggesting mice displayed higher motivational salience for HF-reward (Fig. 4B–C).

During conflict testing under 6h food restriction, both groups suppressed reward-seeking behavior relative to the last day of reward training (Fig. 4D), but mice receiving HF-reward showed higher nosepoking relative to sucrose-rewarded mice. In Light-Only trials, HF-rewarded mice exhibited greater nosepoking during the ITI and light-phase, and less platform time relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Thus, HF-reward retained its motivational value whereas the sucrose reward was devalued during conflict under mild food restriction. In Tone-Only trials, HF-rewarded mice nosepoked more and spent less platform time during ITIs relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). During the tone-phase, nosepoking decreased and platform time increased similarly in both groups. Thus, increased reward value enhanced anticipatory responding without impairing threat avoidance. During Co-Presentation trials, HF-rewarded mice exhibited reduced platform time during the ITI despite comparable nosepoking relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Nosepoking and platform time during the co-presentation-phase were similar between groups. This indicates that while HF-rewarded mice showed greater exploration of the reward zone during ITIs, overlapping reward and threat cues suppressed approach behavior irrespective of reward value. In Light→Tone trials, HF-rewarded mice displayed elevated nosepoking and reduced platform time during the light-phase relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Nosepoking and platform time were comparable between groups during the ITI, co-presentation, and tone phases, indicating that the enhanced approach was specific to low-threat periods. In Tone→Light trials, HF-rewarded mice exhibited increased nosepoking and reduced platform time during the ITI and light-phases relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Nosepoking was comparable between groups during the tone and co-presentation phases, while platform time was reduced across the ITI, tone, and light phases. Under 6h food restriction, total shocks did not differ between groups (Fig. 4G), suggesting that greater approach behavior in HF-rewarded mice did not broadly elevate shock outcomes. However, HF-rewarded mice received significantly more shocks in Tone-Only and Light→Tone trials in the first three days. Shocks were similar between groups in Co-Presentation and Tone→Light trials. Thus, HF-reward enhanced approach but not at the expense of threat avoidance and only incurred a modest cost early in training as animals adapted to conflict.

Under 23h food restriction, the influence of reward palatability was amplified. Consistent with HF-reward providing substantial caloric value within the task, mice receiving HF-reward lost less weight under 23h restriction than sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. S4), supporting the interpretation that HF-reward consumption partially compensated for restricted home-cage intake. HF-rewarded mice displayed even greater nosepoking during ITI and light phases and reduced platform time relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D), showing enhanced reward seeking under heightened hunger. In Tone-Only trials, HF-rewarded mice showed greater nosepoking and reduced platform time during the ITI relative to sucrose-rewarded mice (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Both groups showed similar decreases in nosepoking and increases in time on platform during the tone-phase, demonstrating that threat avoidance was preserved despite increased approach incentive and hunger drive. During Co-Presentation trials, HF-rewarded mice showed higher nosepoking and lower platform time during the ITI (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). However, there were no differences between the groups in nosepoking and time on platform during the co-presentation-phase. This suggests that under heightened hunger, HF-reward mice showed greater anticipatory responding that was suppressed during periods of high risk. In Light→Tone trials, HF-rewarded mice displayed elevated nosepoking during the ITI and light-phases, along with decreased time on platform (Fig. 4E–F; Fig. S3C–D). Both groups suppressed nosepoking and increased platform use during co-presentation and tone phases. In Tone→Light trials, HF-rewarded mice also showed more nosepoking during ITIs and light phases (Fig. 4E–F), with less platform use during ITI, tone, and light phases (Fig. S3C–D). Thus, despite showing high motivation for reward, HF-rewarded mice still minimized threat outcomes. Interestingly, under 23h food restriction, total shocks received were significantly higher in sucrose-rewarded mice versus HF-rewarded mice. Although HF-rewarded mice showed greater approach behavior overall, they did not incur more shocks, potentially due to approach-avoidance strategies acquired under 6h food restriction where they also showed increased approach behavior. Shock exposure was comparable between groups in Tone-Only, Light→Tone, and Co-Presentation trials, but not Tone→Light trials, where sucrose-rewarded mice received significantly more shocks than HF-rewarded mice. This suggests that group differences in shock outcomes under 23h restriction were selective rather than broadly distributed across trial types. Altogether, reward palatability promotes approach behavior even when energy needs are minimal, and hunger amplifies this effect without compromising threat sensitivity. These findings suggest that hedonic and homeostatic drives may interact synergistically to shape conflict behavior.

cHFD reduces motivation for sucrose reward

Having established that reward palatability shaped conflict resolution in chow-fed mice, we examined whether cHFD altered reward valuation and conflict resolution. Separate groups of mice received cHFD or regular chow for three weeks prior to PMA procedures using sucrose as the reward while remaining on their respective diets (Fig. 5A). As expected, cHFD-exposed mice gained significantly more weight than chow-fed mice across the diet exposure period (Fig. S4). During reward training, chow-fed mice displayed increased nosepoking during ITIs and light phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 5B–C), suggesting cHFD reduced motivational value for sucrose.

During conflict testing (under 6h food restriction), chow-fed mice displayed a decrease in nosepoking in the light-phase of Light-Only trials relative to the final day of reward training. In contrast, nosepoking in cHFD-exposed mice remained low (Fig. 5D). Under 6h food restriction, in Light-Only trials (Fig. 5E–F), both groups displayed similar ITI nosepoking. However, chow-fed mice showed a modest increase in nosepoking during the light-phase relative to cHFD mice. Time on platform did not differ between groups (Fig. S5C–D). Thus, during conflict, motivation to seek sucrose remained blunted in cHFD mice. In Tone-Only trials (Fig. 5E–F), chow- and cHFD-exposed mice displayed minimal nosepoking during tone and ITI phases along with similar time on platform (Fig. S5C–D), indicating intact threat avoidance in both groups under mild restriction. In Co-presentation trials (Fig. 5E–F, Fig. S5C–D), nosepoking and platform time did not differ between groups, suggesting cHFD did not alter reward pursuit or avoidance when reward and threat were co-presented. In Light→Tone (Fig. 5E–F) trials, chow-fed mice showed a modest increase in nosepoking during the light-phase relative to cHFD-exposed mice, with no overall differences in time on platform (Fig. S5C–D). In Tone→Light trials, nosepoking and platform occupation were similar across phases. There were no differences between groups in the proportion of shocks received in total or by trial-type (Fig. 5G). Altogether, at 6h restriction, chow-fed mice showed slightly more sucrose seeking during Light-Only and Light→Tone trials than cHFD-exposed mice. It is possible these marginal group differences are due to a floor effect since nosepoking is minimal in controls under 6h restriction.

Because animals under 6h food restriction continued to receive cHFD in their home cage during testing, one possibility was that sucrose was devalued relative to home cage cHFD or that cHFD resulted in greater fat stores or altered hunger state, reducing sucrose-seeking. To address these possibilities, we tested mice under 23h restriction. 23h restriction induced a measurable body-weight reduction across all groups (Fig. S4), confirming heightened metabolic demand. In Light-Only trials, controls nosepoked more in ITI and light-phases and spent less time on the platform compared to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 5E–F; Fig. S5C–D). Thus, heightened hunger promotes approach in chow-fed but not cHFD-exposed mice. In Tone-Only trials, both groups suppressed nosepoking during the tone, but chow-fed mice displayed more nosepoking during the ITI, along with a trending decrease in time on the platform (Fig. S5C–D). In Co-presentation trials, chow-fed mice showed an increase in nosepoking during the ITI and co-presentation-phase along with trending decreased in overall time on platform as compared to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 5E–F; Fig. S5C–D). Thus, threat processing remained intact across groups, but cHFD reduced reward seeking in the presence of threats. In Light→Tone and Tone→Light trials, controls nosepoked more during ITI, light, and co-presentation-phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 5E–F). cHFD-exposed mice spent more time on platform under heightened hunger (Fig. S5C–D). Importantly, shock exposure remained similar between groups (Fig. 5G), indicating differences were not due to altered threat sensitivity or avoidance learning, but rather a generalized reduction in reward motivation in cHFD-exposed mice. In summary, we observed that a previously palatable option (sucrose) becomes devalued by cHFD, resulting in animals reducing risky approach behavior in favor of behaviors that promote safety, even under strong homeostatic drive.

cHFD reduces motivation for HF-reward

Our sucrose-reward experiment demonstrated that cHFD mice showed attenuated reward-seeking during conflict, even under elevated homeostatic demand. However, because mice continued to consume cHFD in their home cage while receiving sucrose as the operant reinforcer, their decreased approach behavior could reflect lower reward preference to sucrose. Thus, we examined whether the effects of cHFD reduced the value of HF-reward as the reinforcer. As expected, cHFD-exposed mice gained significantly more weight than chow-fed mice across the diet exposure period (Fig. S4). During reward training under 6h restriction, chow-fed mice showed increased nosepoking during the light cue relative to the ITI, spending minimal time on the platform (Fig. 6B–C; Fig. S6A–B). In contrast, cHFD-exposed mice exhibited reduced nosepoking and greater platform occupation relative to chow-fed mice. During conflict training, in Light-Only trials (Fig. 6E–F; Fig. S6C–D), chow-fed mice nosepoked more and spent less time on the platform during the light-phase, but not the ITI, relative to cHFD-fed mice. This indicates that cHFD blunted incentive motivation for a HF-reward, prior to conflict and in the face of threats.

In Tone-Only trials, chow-fed mice showed increased nosepoking (Fig. 6E–F) and less time on platform during the ITI, relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. S6C–D). In contrast, during the tone-phase, chow-fed mice displayed similar reduced nosepoking and increased time on platform as cHFD-exposed mice. During Co-presentation trials, chow-fed mice showed more nosepoking during the ITI (Fig. 6E–F) and less time on the platform relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. S6C–D). During the co-presentation-phase both groups showed similar reductions in nosepoking for the HF-reward and increased time on the platform. Thus, in contrast to cHFD-exposed mice, chow-fed mice displayed greater risk tolerance, nosepoking in anticipation of reward but not when reward and threat co-occurred.

In Light→Tone and Tone→Light trials, chow-fed mice exhibited greater nosepoking (Fig. 6E–F) and reduced platform time (Fig. S6C–D) during the ITI and light-alone phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice. During the tone and co-presentation-phases, nosepoking and platform time were comparable between groups. Thus, chow-fed mice displayed greater risk tolerance in pursuit of HF-reward, rapidly adjusting their behavior to avoid threat during high-risk phases, whereas cHFD-exposed mice adopted a broadly avoidant strategy. Notably, shock exposure did not differ between groups (Fig. 6G), suggesting that behavioral differences cannot be attributed to deficits in avoidance learning or shock-induced suppression in cHFD-exposed mice. Together, these results indicate that cHFD decreases motivation for rewards under neutral and conflict conditions, even when the available reinforcer is a HF-reward. This suggests that cHFD induces a generalized reduction in approach behavior for hedonic and non-hedonic rewards.

Importantly, HFD-exposed mice still had access to cHFD in the home-cage during testing. Thus, the reduced motivation for HF-reward under 6h restriction could be due to differences in fat stores, hunger state and/or a strategy to obtain HF-reward in the home cage versus in the task. To address this, we increased food restriction to 23h. However, cHFD-exposed mice continued to display lower nosepoking in Light-Only trials (Fig. 6E–F), and greater platform occupation during both ITI and cue phases (Fig. S6C–D), relative to chow-fed controls. In Tone-Only trials, chow-fed mice displayed greater nosepoking and reduced platform time during the ITI relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 6E–F; Fig. S6C–D). During the tone-phase, nosepoking decreased and platform time increased similarly in both groups, indicating that threat avoidance was intact despite differences in anticipatory responding. During Co-Presentation trials, chow-fed mice exhibited greater nosepoking and reduced platform time during ITI and co-presentation-phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice (Fig. 6E–F; Fig. S6C–D). Thus, chow-fed mice maintained greater approach behavior even in periods of high conflict, whereas cHFD-exposed mice adopted an avoidant strategy irrespective of trial phase.

In Light→Tone trials, chow-fed mice exhibited greater nosepoking (Fig. 6E–F) and reduced platform time (Fig. S6C–D) during the ITI, light, and co-presentation-phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice. Nosepoking and platform time during the tone-phase were comparable between groups. In Tone→Light trials, chow-fed mice exhibited greater nosepoking during the ITI, tone, and light-phases relative to cHFD-exposed mice, while nosepoking during the co-presentation-phase was similar between groups (Fig. 6E–F). Platform time was similarly reduced in chow-fed mice during the ITI, tone, and light-phases, while no difference was observed during the co-presentation-phase (Fig. S6C–D). Therefore, in contrast to cHFD-exposed mice, chow-fed mice showed enhanced reward-seeking, but approach behavior was similarly suppressed in both groups during the high-risk phases. Shocks did not differ between groups across any trial type under 23h food restriction (Fig. 6G), indicating that the reduced approach behavior observed in cHFD-exposed mice did not reflect differences in shock sensitivity or avoidance learning. Altogether, these findings demonstrate that exposure to cHFD reduces incentive motivation for sucrose and HF-reward that persists during conflict and across food restriction conditions without affecting threat processing or avoidance learning.

Discussion

The present study revealed that mice can resolve motivational conflict by learning the value of cues and executing adaptive approach-avoidance strategies. Furthermore, the present study used the PMA task to investigate how mice resolve motivational conflicts under varying metabolic states, reward values, and cHFD exposure. We found that hunger and satiety significantly enhanced and decreased reward-seeking behavior in the face of threats, respectively. Further, HF-reward promotes risk-taking in the face of threats, even under conditions of limited homeostatic drive. Finally, cHFD exposure attenuates reward pursuit in the absence and presence of threats. Together, these results illustrate how physiological (hunger states), environmental (dietary exposure), and hedonic factors (reward palatability) shape adaptive or maladaptive behaviors under conflict.

Extending knowledge of motivational conflict resolution using a PMA task in mice

Mouse adaptations of PMA and related approach-avoidance paradigms have recently emerged, establishing that mice perform platform-mediated avoidance both under conflict (Manning et al., 2026; Laurent et al., 2025; Zeidler et al., 2025) and in purely avoidance-based variants (Halcomb et al., 2024). The present study extends this work, modifying the task to assess rapid adjustments in decision-making under conflict in mice. A key difference in the experimental procedure involved training mice to associate a light cue with reward availability, thus increasing reward salience prior to conflict. Furthermore, we incorporated distinct trial types that allowed behavioral assessment under varying levels of conflict (low conflict in Light-Only trials, high in Co-presentation trials, and avoidance in Tone-Only trials). In addition, by introducing partially overlapping sequences (Light→Tone and Tone→Light), we also probe behavioral flexibility in adapting to rapidly changing reward-punishment contingencies. The ability of mice to shift strategies in Light→Tone and Tone→Light trials demonstrate behavioral flexibility, an important cognitive function that is often impaired in stress- and reward-related disorders (e.g., anxiety, depression, addiction and obesity). Moreover, the present modified PMA task captures changes in behavioral flexibility across time. Specifically, mice progressively adapt their approach-avoidance strategies to maximize rewards and minimize shocks in response to cues across days. Importantly, while the PMA task provides strong experimental control over motivational conflict, it simplifies the complexity of real-world decision-making. For instance, while the use of a tone-shock allowed modeling of an aversive threat, future studies might test other stressor modalities (e.g., social or predator-based threats) to engage similar behavioral strategies. Altogether, we demonstrate that mice effectively resolve motivational conflict under complex trial structures, as in this modified PMA task.

Notably, the present PMA configuration revealed interesting sex differences. Before threat introduction, males and females nosepoked at comparable rates. Following threat introduction, nose poke rates declined more in females during phases without an explicit threat cue, specifically the intertrial interval and reward light cue. This difference was not present during the tone or trials in which reward and threat were presented together. Although we detected no sex differences in platform time or shock exposure, reward seeking was expressed differently across the session. Thus, females appeared to show a context dependent suppression of reward seeking during periods without an explicit threat cue, rather than a shift toward safety at the expense of reward.

Our findings partially align with previous work showing that female Long-Evans rats spend more time on the safety platform and engage in less reward seeking during conflict. This pattern was interpreted as a strategy that prioritized safety and was unrelated to reward motivation or estrous stage (Reimer et al., 2026). Female Sprague-Dawley rats have likewise shown greater avoidance and reduced food seeking, although these effects were abolished by social context (Ruble et al., 2025). In contrast, females in our study did not spend more time on the platform than males and did not differ in nose poking during phases in which reward and threat competed directly. The sex difference observed here therefore appears to involve reduced rates of reward seeking outside periods of direct competition between reward and threat, rather than a preference for safety. Differences in conflict task structure or between mice and rats may account for these divergent patterns.

However, the broader literature is mixed. Other work in Sprague-Dawley rats found no sex difference during acquisition and only subtle effects during extinction (López-Moraga et al., 2025), while work in Long-Evans rats found that the difference was confined to avoidance that persisted after extinction (Landin and Chandler, 2023). In mice, the only other PMA study involving an explicit appetitive option included both sexes but was not powered to detect sex differences (Zeidler et al., 2025). Other mouse PMA studies have examined avoidance without a reward option or competition between safety and spontaneous exploration. These studies therefore cannot directly inform the reduction in female reward seeking observed here (Halcomb et al., 2024; Gongwer et al., 2023; Gabriel et al., 2025). Nonetheless, our findings extend this literature and are consistent with the view that males and females can adopt distinct behavioral strategies in the face of threats (Shansky, 2018).

Because estrous cycle stage was not assessed in this study, we cannot determine whether ovarian hormone fluctuations contributed to the observed effect. Ovarian hormone fluctuations represent only one of several possible biological explanations, as sex differences may also reflect sex chromosome complement and the organizational effects of gonadal hormones during development (De Vries, 2004; McCarthy and Arnold, 2011; Ngun et al., 2011). Attributing the effect primarily to ovarian hormones while treating male behavior as the implicit reference would therefore overlook other possible biological mechanisms and the possibility that males and females use distinct behavioral strategies (Maney, 2016; Shansky, 2018). Future studies will be needed to distinguish the hormonal, chromosomal, and developmental contributions to this sex difference in both females and males.

Hunger state and risky decision-making

Our data show that prolonged food restriction (e.g., 23h) led mice to increase reward-seeking, even under risk of a mild footshock. This finding aligns with risk-sensitive foraging theory (Alcantara et al., 2025; Caraco, 1981; de Araujo Salgado et al., 2023; Houston and Rosenström, 2024; Nakagawa, 2009; Real and Caraco, 1986), which posits that animals facing energetic deficits become more willing to engage in risky behavior to secure vital resources. In our study, increased approach behavior under heightened hunger suggests that homeostatic signals (Rossi and Stuber, 2018) may override fear or avoidance circuits, enabling animals to pursue rewards in the presence of threats. This is consistent with previous findings that hunger can suppress fear responses and bias decision-making toward metabolic gains (Burnett et al., 2016; Koorneef et al., 2018; Padilla et al., 2016). This is also consistent with work showing that fasted or ghrelin-treated mice spend more time in food-baited, risky zones (Lockie et al., 2017). Our findings show that mice display increased risk-taking behavior but not at the cost of increased shock outcomes. This suggests that threat appraisal is not lost, but rather that alternative adaptive strategies are implemented. We note that deprivation level was the manipulated variable in this comparison (6 h vs. 23 h restriction), and deprivation duration therefore covaried with the broader physiological response to negative energy balance. Hunger is a homeostatic challenge that recruits coordinated endocrine and neural adaptations, including changes in leptin, ghrelin, and corticosterone signaling (Ahima et al., 1996; Toshinai et al., 2001; Heiderstadt et al., 2000; Rubio et al., 2023). These signals can act on reward systems and influence food motivation, learning, and behavioral allocation (Guarnieri et al., 2012; Skibicka et al., 2012). Because we did not collect physiological or behavioral measures of stress or arousal, we cannot formally dissociate a stress-related contribution from the motivational account. However, the observed increased reward approach without a rise in shock outcomes is consistent with preserved threat sensitivity. This is more readily accommodated by a motivational/homeostatic interpretation than by a generalized stress-induced disruption of behavior, which is often associated with more rigid or habitual responding rather than flexible, threat-sensitive behavior (Dias-Ferreira et al., 2009; Schwabe and Wolf, 2010). Notably, corticosterone signaling has itself been implicated in the enhanced motivation and learning produced by food restriction (Guarnieri et al., 2012), consistent with the interpretation that this neuroendocrine response forms part of an adaptive motivational state rather than a performance-degrading stressor.

Altogether, we show this task is sensitive to hunger state, highlighting the role of internal states in modulating decision-making during conflict. Future work using this task may help examine the dynamic processes governing motivational shifts in approach-avoidance conflict, including the mechanisms underlying hunger-mediated incentive salience.

Reward value and hedonic feeding in conflict

Beyond hunger, the intrinsic value of the reward emerged as a critical factor shaping approach-avoidance behavior, a finding that was evident across our experiments. The initial reward training established a clear preference where mice displayed significantly greater nosepoking for the HF-reward compared to sucrose, indicating its increased baseline incentive value. This is consistent with literature showing that rodents consume dietary fats or palatable foods in a hedonic manner (Bake et al., 2014; Furlong et al., 2014; Lardeux et al., 2015; Liu and Kanoski, 2018; Ritter, 2004; Wojnicki et al., 2008). This demonstrates the potent reinforcing properties of palatable rewards which may contribute to binge-like eating patterns and obesity (Nicola, 2016). Importantly, the heightened HF-reward value translates directly into risk-taking behavior even under 6h food restriction. In the present study, mice offered HF-reward increased their approach behavior in the face of threats (relative to mice offered sucrose reward under identical food-restriction conditions), demonstrating increased willingness to risk punishment for a highly desired reward. This finding supports the concept of hedonic feeding, where the incentive properties of palatable food can override homeostatic need and natural fear responses. Indeed, previous work in rodents has demonstrated that palatable foods and hedonic rewards can powerfully override natural fear responses and normal metabolic constraints through complex neural mechanisms (Egan et al., 2019; Friedman et al., 2015; Schultz et al., 2011; Ulrich-Lai et al., 2010). Finally, we found that this effect of reward value synergized with the impact of internal state. While hunger increased risk-taking for both sucrose and HF-reward, the approach behavior of mice seeking HF-reward remained significantly greater than the sucrose-reward group. Indeed, neuroadaptations in reward-related circuits, such as changes in dopamine signaling or hypothalamic pathways, can enhance motivation and craving for high-fat foods (Ferrario, 2020; Krashes et al., 2011; Matikainen-Ankney et al., 2023; Volkow et al., 2011). This suggests an interaction between internal state and external incentive value, consistent with models where these factors shape corticolimbic circuits to potentiate motivation (Lutter and Nestler, 2009; Moscarello et al., 2010). Taken together, these findings suggest that the high incentive value of HF-reward promotes risky decision-making, an effect that is present even with minimal physiological need and is enhanced by hunger. Of note, the increase in nosepokes following 23h food restriction may also reflect learned temporal anticipation of the highly palatable rewards, which has been reported in response to both control diets and highly palatable rewards in rodents (Mistlberger, 2011; Storch and Weitz, 2009; Hsu, 2010; Taufique et al., 2026). To further explore anticipatory behavior, future studies should examine whether increased nose poking persists in the PMA paradigm when less palatable rewards are presented, such as normal chow. However, previous studies have observed anticipatory behavior in response to normal chow/control diets in rodents (Luby et al., 2012; Petersen et al., 2022).

cHFD exposure and altered conflict resolution

Our results indicate that cHFD-exposure reduced incentive motivation that is evident even before conflict is introduced and becomes more pronounced under conflict. During reward training, cHFD-exposed mice showed attenuated, poorly differentiated approach for both sucrose-and HF-rewards (relative to chow controls). This suggests cHFD induced deficits in the ability or willingness of mice to work for these reinforcers. During conflict under 6h food-restriction, group differences were modest. This effect was particularly evident in mice offered sucrose-reward where even controls displayed low approach behavior. However, under 23h restriction and when the operant reinforcer was HF-reward, cHFD-exposed mice consistently nosepoked less and spent more time on the platform across trial-types. Importantly, during threat periods there were no differences in shocks received between groups. This pattern suggests that cHFD devalues both sucrose and HF-rewards and biases animals toward more avoidant strategies without altering threat sensitivity. Because cHFD-exposed and control mice were maintained on identical restriction schedules within each condition, the attenuated reward pursuit reflects dietary exposure rather than differences in deprivation or any associated stress response. While some studies report increased reward seeking after cHFD exposure (Figlewicz et al., 2013; Oginsky et al., 2016), our findings align strongly with studies showing reduced motivation for reinforcers after cHFD-exposure (Íbias et al., 2016; Mazzone et al., 2020; Rossi and Stuber, 2018; South et al., 2014; Steele et al., 2019; Tantot et al., 2017; Yamada et al., 2011). The underlying mechanism for this deficit is debated, but research points to adaptations in key limbic circuits that regulate motivation and decision-making (Felix-Ortiz and Tye, 2014; Lebow and Chen, 2016; Matikainen-Ankney et al., 2023; Mazzone et al., 2020). For example, some studies point to adaptations in brain reward systems, including dysfunction in dopamine and leptin signaling (Carlin et al., 2013; Ducrocq et al., 2019; Gazit Shimoni et al., 2025; South and Huang, 2008; Yamada et al., 2011). We also note that since cHFD exposure has been associated with anhedonia-like behaviors in rodents, the response to palatable-rewards may contribute or interact with these behavioral deficits, influencing their response to palatable rewards. Alternatively, a reduced motivation to seek rewards following cHFD exposure may be driven by decreased hunger and stronger postingestive negative-feedback mechanisms that may arise from increased fat stores. While our study cannot disentangle these potential mechanisms, our results demonstrate that cHFD exposure blunts motivation for rewards even under 23h food restriction. Additionally, although a 3-week cHFD exposure was sufficient to induce marked motivational deficits, both age and exposure duration may further modulate these behavioral effects. Lastly, pre-exposure to cHFD induces significant metabolic alterations, including changes in body weight and adiposity, leptin resistance, inflammation, impaired glucose metabolism and insulin resistance (Buttener et al., 2007; Dutheil et al., 2016; McDonald et al., 2011; Zemdegs et al., 2016). Indeed, we observed significant changes in body weight (Fig. S4). Future studies should probe other metabolic markers in relation to approach-avoidance behavior in the current PMA paradigm; This could help disentangle whether observed behavioral differences are induced by the diet or diet-induced metabolic alterations.

It is important to consider how cHFD exposure may impact anxiety-like behavior. Some studies show that cHFD can increase anxiety-like behavior, causing mice to spend less time in an open field or to exhibit risk-assessment behaviors (e.g., rearing in an elevated zero maze; Gainey et al., 2016; Dutheil et al., 2016; Zemdegs et al., 2016). However, other work has found that cHFD exposure lowered saccharin preference but did not affect auditory fear conditioning or anxiety-like behavior (Pierre et al., 2019). It is therefore possible that while cHFD can alter anxiety in some contexts, these may occur independently of its effects on reward motivation.

Thus, our study demonstrates that cHFD exposure induces a potent motivational deficit that shapes behavior in approach-avoidance conflict. While cHFD exposure may also alter how animals evaluate and respond to conflict, our data suggests that the primary deficit is reward devaluation. These findings are relevant to the link between obesity and depression (Hryhorczuk et al., 2013; Sharma and Fulton, 2013). Future work using conflict tasks may help to elucidate the nuances of reward-seeking that may remain hidden in simpler tasks (Aupperle et al., 2015; Nicola, 2016).

Conclusion

In summary, our study highlights how hunger states, reward value, and cHFD shape approach-avoidance conflict resolution in mice. We also identified sex differences in the expression of reward seeking, with females showing lower nosepoke rates during periods without an explicit threat cue, despite comparable avoidance and shock outcomes. Together, these findings highlight the dynamic interplay between metabolic signals, hedonic drives, sex, and threat processing in guiding adaptive decision-making under potential danger. Elucidating the mechanisms shaping approach-avoidance conflict will be essential for understanding the pathophysiological basis of maladaptive reward-seeking and avoidance observed in obesity, depression, and other stress-related disorders. Future studies should determine whether sex moderates the effects of hunger, reward value, and diet exposure in adequately powered cohorts and test the impact of psychosocial stress or other chronic stressors on approach-avoidance conflict (Mobbs et al., 2018; Verharen et al., 2020). Incorporating physiological or behavioral indices of stress and arousal may also help distinguish motivational, homeostatic, and stress-related contributions to deprivation-driven risk-taking. Such work may help identify mechanisms of maladaptive risk-taking or avoidance behaviors seen in obesity, depression, or eating disorders.
