Idiopathic mediastinal chyloma in a cat: a case report of a novel manifestation of chyle leakage
Authors: Myeong-Seon Jeong, Mun-Ik Lee, Hyeong-Seok Oh, Ju-Hyun An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChyloma is a rare pseudocystic collection of chyle resulting from leakage of the thoracic duct or its branches. While sporadically reported in humans, it has not been previously described in veterinary medicine. This report describes the first case of an idiopathic mediastinal chyloma in a cat.Case presentationA three-year-old, castrated male, domestic shorthair cat presented with acute dyspnea and lethargy. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) revealed a well-circumscribed cystic mass in the right caudal mediastinum causing compressed the adjacent lungs and heart. The lesion was surgically excised and contained chylous fluid. Histopathological examination confirmed a fibrous-walled pseudocyst lacking an epithelial lining, supporting the diagnosis of chyloma. Clinical signs resolved after surgery. Five months later, chylothorax developed at a different site, with CT lymphangiography demonstrating thoracic duct leakage. No underlying cause was identified, and both conditions were considered idiopathic. The cat was managed conservatively with no recurrence during the six-month follow-up period.ConclusionChyloma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of mediastinal cystic lesions in cats. Surgical excision may be effective for relieving mass effect, and long-term monitoring is recommended given the potential for recurrent chyle leakage.Keywords: Cat, Chyloma, Chylothorax, Mediastinal cyst, Thoracic duct, Case report, Lymphangiography

Introduction

Chylomas are defined as pseudocystic, circumscribed collections of chylous fluid arising from the obstruction or disruption of the thoracic duct or its branches [1]. Leaked chyle may accumulate within the mediastinum adjacent to the thoracic duct. Whether the fluid remains localized or extends into the pleural cavity depends on the volume of chyle and the structural integrity of the mediastinal pleura [2]. Local retention of chyle may subsequently result in the formation of a fibrous cystic capsule [3]. Previous imaging studies have demonstrated chyle flow from the thoracic duct to mediastinal chylomas and from chylomas to the pleural cavity [4]. Unlike loculated chylous effusions that can develop within the pleural space as a complication of chronic chylothorax [5], mediastinal chylomas arise from focal chyle leakage and form discrete encapsulated collections within the mediastinum [4].

Reports of cervical and mediastinal chylomas in humans are limited. Most cases have been described as postoperative [1, 3, 6–11], while others have been reported following trauma [12] or without an identifiable cause [13].

Diagnosis is typically established through a combination of imaging studies and fluid analysis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is useful for characterizing the lesion’s cystic nature and its relationship with surrounding structures. The chylous nature of the milky aspirate is confirmed by cytological and biochemical analyses. Histopathological examination further supports the diagnosis by demonstrating a fibrous cystic wall lacking an epithelial lining [7].

Management strategies include dietary management, needle aspiration, sclerotherapy, and surgical intervention [1]. For cervical chylomas, conservative therapy can be attempted initially, with sclerotherapy or surgical excision reserved for refractory cases [7]. In contrast, mediastinal chylomas often cause significant clinical signs due to compression of adjacent structures, including the trachea, esophagus, lungs, and heart, and frequently require surgical management [8–10].

To date, chyloma has not been reported in veterinary literature. Only a few lymphocele cases have been described, including a subcutaneous lymphocele in a cat [14] and a cervical lymphocele in a dog [15]. Herein, we describe the first documented case of an idiopathic mediastinal chyloma in a cat, demonstrating that chyle leakage may present as a localized mass rather than chylothorax.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old, castrated male, domestic shorthair cat was presented with a two-day history of acute dyspnea, exercise intolerance, anorexia, and lethargy. According to the owner’s history and available medical records, the cat had no known underlying disease and was kept strictly indoors with routine vaccinations and parasite prevention. There was no history of thoracic trauma, thoracic surgery, chest tube placement, or other thoracic procedures that could have resulted in secondary pleural fluid accumulation. The cat also had no history of chronic respiratory signs or veterinary evaluation suggestive of longstanding chylothorax.

On physical examination, the patient was in severe respiratory distress, characterized by open-mouth breathing, orthopnea, and tachypnea, despite oxygen supplementation. Pulse oximetry revealed an SpO₂ of 85%, indicating hypoxemia. The body condition score was 4/9, body temperature 39 °C, heart rate 180 beats per minute, and systolic blood pressure 180 mmHg. Cardiac auscultation was unremarkable.

Hematology (ProCyte, IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA) and serum biochemistry (Catalyst One, IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA) results were within normal limits, except for a markedly elevated feline serum amyloid A (FSAA) concentration of 427.75 µg/mL (Vet Chroma, Boditech Med Inc., Chuncheon, Republic of Korea). Hematological and biochemical parameters are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The feline pro-BNP test (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA) was negative. The SNAP Feline Triple Test (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA) was negative for FeLV antigen, FIV antibody, and heartworm antigen.

Thoracic radiographs (right lateral and ventrodorsal views) revealed a large soft-tissue opacity in the right caudal thoracic cavity (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the thorax demonstrated a 6.6 × 6.6 cm cystic mass in the right caudal mediastinum. The lesion exhibited homogeneous attenuation similar to water without post-contrast enhancement. It was well-encapsulated with no internal septations or evidence of local invasion. The mass displaced the heart and compressed the right cranial, middle, and caudal lung lobes, leading to partial atelectasis (Fig. 2). Multiple, mildly enlarged reactive lymph nodes were noted in the cranial mediastinum; however, no other abnormalities or identifiable causes for the lesion were identified.

The differential diagnoses for a caudal mediastinal mass in cats include granulomas, abscesses, and caudoventral diaphragmatic hernia or eventration [16]. Based on the cystic appearance and fluid attenuation on CT, the mass was initially suspected to represent an abscess. Given the concern for potential leakage of purulent material into the pleural cavity following percutaneous drainage, surgical removal was prioritized for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Premedication consisted of midazolam (0.2 mg/kg, IV; Myungmoon Pharm., Seoul, Republic of Korea) with flow-by preoxygenation. General anesthesia was induced with propofol (6 mg/kg, IV; Daewon Pharm., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and maintained with isoflurane in 100% oxygen. Mechanical ventilation (Comen AX 400; Comen Medical Instruments Co., Shenzhen, China) was provided. A fentanyl constant rate infusion (2.5 µg/kg/h, CRI; BC World Pharm., Seongnam, Republic of Korea) was administered intraoperatively following a loading dose (2 µg/kg). Cefazolin (20 mg/kg, IV; Chongkundang Pharm., Seoul, Republic of Korea) was administered prior to the surgical procedure and repeated every 90 min during surgery. A right-sided intercostal thoracotomy was performed at the ninth intercostal space. Upon incision of the intercostal muscles, a large, fluid-filled mass was identified. It was firmly adherent to the parietal pleura, hindering blunt dissection (Fig. 3A and B). Puncture of the thick capsule yielded approximately 50 mL of milky-pink fluid (Fig. 3C), after which the cyst wall was excised using a deroofing technique (Fig. 3D). Because of severe adhesions and distortion of the local anatomy, the thoracic duct could not be clearly identified intraoperatively. As the primary surgical objective was removal of the large mediastinal mass and no obvious chylous leakage was observed during exploration, thoracic duct ligation was not performed. A 24-French chest tube was placed, and the thoracic cavity was closed routinely.

The fluid obtained from the cystic mass was submitted for analysis. The triglyceride concentration was 424 mg/dL, higher than that of the serum (80 mg/dL). Cytological examination revealed numerous lipid droplets in the background, with a predominance of neutrophils and a small number of lymphocytes (Fig. 4). Bacterial and fungal cultures were negative (Table 3). Based on its milky appearance, markedly elevated triglyceride level, and cytologic results, the fluid was classified as chronic chyle.

Excised wall samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological examination revealed abundant necrotic and degenerate cellular debris with hemorrhage and fibrin, surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule containing reactive fibroblasts and infiltrated predominantly by neutrophils. No epithelial lining was observed (Fig. 5). PAS and GMS staining were negative, with no evidence of fungal organisms. Additional collagen-specific staining was not performed, as fibrous encapsulation was clearly identified on hematoxylin and eosin staining.

Postoperatively, the patient’s respiratory signs resolved rapidly. A low-fat diet (Hill’s Prescription Diet w/d; Hill’s Pet Nutrition, Topeka, KS, USA) was prescribed, and standard postoperative care was provided. Chest tube output remained minimal, with no evidence of chylous leakage. The tube was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged the following day in good condition.

Although a low-fat diet was recommended, the owner transitioned to a regular diet due to poor palatability. At the one-week recheck, thoracic radiographs showed re-expansion of the lung lobes with no effusion, and serum amyloid A concentration had normalized.

At a routine follow-up five months after surgery, thoracic radiographs revealed a right-sided pleural effusion. Thoracocentesis yielded 75 mL of milky fluid, which was confirmed to be chyle by biochemical and cytological analyses. Hematology, serum biochemistry, echocardiography and abdominal ultrasonography revealed no abnormalities. To localize the leakage, CT lymphangiography was performed via intra-metatarsal pad injection. Contrast extravasation was identified in the right cranial thorax, corresponding to the site of effusion (Fig. 6A). No leakage was detected in the right caudal mediastinum where the chyloma was previously located; however, the thoracic duct in this region was observed coursing along the right side of the midline (Fig. 6B).

Surgical options, including thoracic duct ligation, cisterna chyli ablation, subtotal pericardiectomy, or pleural access port placement were discussed with the owner. However, due to financial constraints, surgery was declined in favor of conservative management, which included a low-fat diet (Hill’s Prescription Diet w/d; Hill’s Pet Nutrition, Topeka, KS, USA) and oral rutin (250 mg PO every 8 h; NOW Foods, Bloomingdale, IL, USA). The patient remained clinically stable with no recurrence of chylothorax during a six-month follow-up period and was alive at the end of the follow-up. Discussion

The thoracic duct serves as the primary pathway for transporting chyle from intestinal lymphatics to the venous circulation. Disruption or obstruction of this duct can lead to chyle leakage, which may manifest as chylous fistulae, chylothorax, or rarely chyloma [7]. Chyloma can develop anywhere along the course of the thoracic duct, extending from the cisterna chyli to its termination in the venous system [6].

In the present case, the diagnosis of chyloma was supported by imaging findings demonstrating a cystic lesion within the mediastinum, biochemical confirmation of chylous fluid content, and histopathological identification of a pseudocystic structure lacking an epithelial lining.

Thoracic CT revealed a well-circumscribed, non-septated, low-attenuation cystic mass in the caudal mediastinum without post-contrast enhancement, consistent with previously reported findings [1, 3, 7–10]. CT is primarily used to characterize the cystic nature of the lesion and to localize the mass. In contrast, MRI findings have been rarely reported. When described, chylomas typically appear as circumscribed, non-enhancing fluid collections with T1 hypointensity and T2 hyperintensity [6]. In one study of patients with chylothorax, chylomas were detected in 6 of 7 cases (85.7%) using MRI. These lesions appeared as well-defined, cyst-like high-signal structures along the middle to lower thoracic duct, suggesting that MRI may serve as a useful adjunctive modality for detecting chylomas [4].

All reported cases demonstrated milky fluid within the cystic mass [1, 3, 6–10, 12]. Chylous effusions are typically characterized by elevated triglyceride concentrations and lower cholesterol concentrations than those of serum, and cytological examination commonly reveals a predominance of lymphocytes. Consistent with these features, the aspirated fluid in this case had a milky appearance with a triglyceride concentration of 424 mg/dL, exceeding the corresponding serum concentration (80 mg/dL) by more than fivefold. Although the cholesterol concentration in the fluid (290 mg/dL) was higher than the serum level (111 mg/dL), the markedly elevated triglyceride concentration (> 100 mg/dL) supports a chylous origin rather than a pseudochylous effusion. The neutrophilic shift observed on cytological examination was likely indicative of a chronic inflammatory response between the chyle and adjacent tissues [17, 18].

Histopathological examination further supported the diagnosis by confirming a thick fibrous capsule lacking an epithelial lining. A previous study described a circumscribed space lined by organizing fibrin and luminal lymphocytes [7]. In the present case, inflammatory cell infiltration of the fibrous capsule, predominantly composed of neutrophils, suggests ongoing inflammation associated with chyle leakage.

CT lymphangiography can aid in confirming the diagnosis and localizing the site of thoracic duct leakage [7, 19]. However, in this case, the procedure was not performed preoperatively and was instead conducted five months postoperatively following the development of chylothorax. Although no additional contrast leakage was observed at the site of the previous chyloma, the course of the thoracic duct was identified in the right caudal mediastinum. This right-sided duct location represents an anatomical variation, as the feline thoracic duct typically runs to the left of the midline [20]. Both the chyloma and the subsequent chylothorax were classified as idiopathic as no underlying etiology could be identified despite thorough investigation [18].

Treatment strategies for chyloma are tailored according to clinical presentation, lesion size, and anatomical location. Reported mediastinal cases have described compression of adjacent structures, including the left atrium, trachea, esophagus and stomach, resulting in pain, dysphagia and dyspnea [8, 9]. In the current case, the chyloma compressed the adjacent lung parenchyma and displaced the heart, leading to respiratory compromise that required prompt surgical intervention. Surgical management consists of excision of the cystic lesion and drainage of the chylous content. When the leakage site is identified, ligation of the thoracic duct is recommended [9]. If localization is not possible, ligation of the thoracic duct cranial to the diaphragm can be performed to block the upward flow of chyle and indirectly prevents reflux from the upper portion of the duct [10, 11]. Additional measures, such as application of fibrin glue along the posterior mediastinum or abrasive pleurodesis, may be employed as adjunctive therapies [8].

In this patient, the chyloma was excised without additional surgical interventions. Clinical signs resolved immediately after surgery, and thoracic radiographs obtained at the one-week follow-up demonstrated re-expansion of the previously collapsed lung lobes. The patient initially recovered well; however, a chylothorax developed at a different site five months later. This outcome suggests that excision of the chyloma alone was insufficient to prevent recurrence of thoracic duct leakage, even though no active chyle leakage was observed intraoperatively. Human case reports indicate that concurrent ligation is commonly performed during surgical management of mediastinal chyloma [9, 10].

This report has several limitations. First, lymphangiography was not performed preoperatively because chyle leakage was not suspected prior to surgery which limited our ability to localize the initial site of leakage. Second, the diagnoses of idiopathic chyloma and chylothorax were made by exclusion, and the presence of unrecognized predisposing factors cannot be completely ruled out. Third, further surgical options for the chylothorax were declined due to the owner’s financial constraints. However, no recurrence of chyle leakage was observed during the six-month follow-up.

Conclusion

This report describes the first case of a mediastinal chyloma in a cat, expanding the known manifestations of feline lymphatic disorders beyond chylothorax. Recognition of this condition is important when assessing mediastinal cystic lesions. Thoracic imaging plays a key role in characterizing such lesions and confirmation of chyle through fluid aspiration is essential. Surgical excision appears effective for alleviating respiratory compromise secondary to mass effect. Given the potential for recurrent leakage, long-term monitoring is advisable. Further studies are needed to establish standardized guidelines for diagnosis and management.
