Characteristics of Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinomas Based on Circumferential Localization
Authors: Natsuki Ishida, Satoshi Osawa, Kiichi Sugiura, Tomohiro Takebe, Kenichi Takahashi, Yusuke Asai, Satoshi Tamura, Tomoharu Matsuura, Mihoko Yamade, Moriya Iwaizumi, Yasushi Hamaya, Takanori Yamada, Ken Sugimoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimIn terms of their longitudinal positioning, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) commonly arises in the middle segment of the esophagus. However, limited attention has been given to the circumferential aspect. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and characteristics of ESCC lesions resected via endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), taking into consideration both longitudinal and circumferential positions.MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated 193 ESD‐resected ESCC lesions and compared the occurrence rate of ESCC development and the degree of histopathological invasion across various circumferential and longitudinal positions. We scrutinized lesion characteristics by location, with a particular focus on the proportion of early‐stage small lesions in each site.ResultsRegarding lesion location, 27 were situated on the anterior wall, 62 on the left, 66 on the posterior, and 38 on the right. Among the four circumferential positions, the anterior wall group had the smallest median tumor size. Additionally, the anterior wall group had the highest proportions of lesions with invasion depths limited to the epithelial layer and sizes < 10 mm, at 44.4% and 25.9%, respectively. Furthermore, a significant difference was noted among the four circumferential positions with respect to the proportion of ESCC lesions satisfying both criteria, with the highest proportion observed in the anterior wall group (p = 0.049).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that while ESCC occurrence on the anterior wall is less frequent, these lesions may present as small lesions with superficial invasion depths.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is globally ranked as the 8th most prevalent cancer and is the 6th leading cause of mortality, exhibiting varying epidemiological patterns [1, 2]. In East Asia, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) predominates and is strongly linked to external factors like smoking and alcohol consumption, which are known to contribute to its development [3, 4]. Notably, a Japanese study indicated the prevalence of ESCC in the middle thoracic esophagus (Mt), followed by the lower thoracic (Lt) and upper thoracic (Ut) regions [5]. However, while several studies have focused on the longitudinal distribution of ESCC lesions, limited research has explored their circumferential location [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Previously, in the surgical treatment of advanced esophageal cancer, the longitudinal location held greater importance over the circumferential location, and early detection of ESCC using white‐light endoscopy also posed challenges, particularly in determining the primary circumferential site in multifocal cases. However, the introduction of endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has revolutionized early‐stage esophageal cancer treatment [13, 14]. Recent developments in gastrointestinal endoscopy, specifically narrowband imaging (NBI), have simplified early ESCC detection [15, 16]. Moreover, magnification endoscopy with NBI has improved the early diagnosis of ESCC depth, enabling clinicians to tailor treatments based on the Japan Esophageal Society's classification system [17, 18, 19]. Consequently, these advancements have shifted the focus towards characterizing the circumferential location of ESCC for effective early detection.

While the exact mechanism remains unclear, ESCC development is more prevalent in the Mt region, with alcohol consumption and smoking recognized as significant risk factors. We hypothesized that ESCC characteristics, such as frequency and tumor progression, might vary based on circumferential location. In recent years, most early‐detected ESCC has been treated with ESD; however, there have been few reports examining the frequency of ESCC treated with ESD by circumferential location. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the relationship between circumferential location and ESCC development.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at a single center. We reviewed the medical records of 193 lesions in 143 patients diagnosed with early ESCC based on the Japanese classification of esophageal cancer, who were treated via ESD at our university hospital between March 2012 and December 2021 [20]. Patients with pT1a‐EP squamous cell carcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma in situ with sufficient cytologic and architectural atypia to suggest malignancy, as diagnosed according to the guidelines in Japan, were enrolled in this study. Patients with intraepithelial neoplasia was excluded from this study [20]. ESD procedures were performed following conventional protocols. The exclusion criteria encompassed cases of esophageal neoplasms histologically diagnosed as tumors other than squamous cell carcinomas, such as adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumor, basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, and adenosquamous carcinoma. At our institution, prior to performing ESD for the initial ESCC, detailed exploration of the ESCC was conducted using magnification endoscopy with NBI and iodine staining, provided there was no iodine allergy.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the identification of the carcinogenic site of the ESCC, particularly in terms of the circumferential location. Secondary endpoints encompassed the evaluation of invasion depth and tumor size at each specific site. All pathological diagnoses were performed according to the Japanese classification of esophageal cancer [20].

Circumferential Location

Tumor locations were categorized into four groups based on their circumferential orientation: anterior, left, posterior, and right walls. Orientation was determined based on the direction of the water surface in an endoscopic image. For instance, during esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) performed in the left‐lateral position, the direction of the water surface was designated along the left wall (Figure 1a). In cases where tumors spanned multiple walls, the location of the largest lesion was determined to be the primary lesion site (Figure 1b). Four expert gastrointestinal endoscopists (N.I., S.O., K.S., and T.Y.), blinded to patients' clinical data, determined the circumferential location. Any differences in scores among the experts were resolved through consensus.

Longitudinal Location

The longitudinal location was determined by measuring the distance from the incisor dentition to the lesion and from the incisor dentition to the esophagogastric junction. These locations were categorized into five regions: Ce, Ut, Mt, Lt, and Ae [20]. Due to the small number of lesions in the Ce and Ae regions, the following three groups were created: Ce‐Ut, Mt, and Lt‐Ae. Circumferential and longitudinal locations were determined based on the consensus of three or more gastroenterological endoscopy experts in ESD.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and the EZR plugin for R (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a modified version of the R commander designed to incorporate statistical functions commonly used in biostatistics [21]. Differences between median values were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, Fisher's exact test, or Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of all patients and early ESCC lesions. The median age of the patients was 70 years, comprising 170 men and 23 women. In terms of longitudinal location, lesions were distributed as follows: two in the cervical esophagus (Ce) (1.0%), 27 in the Ut (14.0%), 106 in the Mt (54.9%), 56 in the Lt (29.0%), and two in the abdominal esophagus (Ae) (1.0%). The median tumor size of all lesions was 19 mm. Regarding invasion depth, there were 74 lesions with epithelial (EP) invasion, 81 with lamina propria mucosae (LPM) invasion, 19 with muscularis mucosae (MM) invasion, 10 with submucosa 1 (SM1) invasion, and 9 with submucosa 2 (SM2) invasion.

Circumferential Location

Baseline characteristics of early ESCC lesions based on their circumferential location were examined (Table 2). Specifically, there were 27 lesions on the anterior wall (14.0%), 62 on the left wall (32.1%), 66 on the posterior wall (34.2%), and 38 on the right wall (19.7%). The posterior wall exhibited the highest prevalence of ESCC lesions, whereas the anterior wall showed the lowest. No significant differences were observed in patient clinical features, endoscopic findings, or longitudinal tumor location among the circumferential groups. Similarly, no significant variations were noted in characteristics, including circumferential location, among the longitudinal groups (Table 2).

Pathological Results

Table 3 presents the pathological findings of tumors post‐ESD across circumferential and longitudinal locations. Among the circumferential location groups, the highest percentage of invasion depth categorized as EP was observed in the anterior wall group (44.4%), although this difference was not statistically significant. Additionally, the tumor size exhibited no significant difference among the four groups; however, the anterior wall group had the smallest median size, as upon further analysis of tumor diameter, the anterior wall group had the highest proportion of lesions < 10 mm. There were no significant differences in invasion depth or venous/lymphatic invasion among the three longitudinal location groups (Table 3). Notably, the Lt‐Ae group exhibited the highest proportion of lesions < 10 mm in terms of tumor size.

Analysis for Tumor Invasion Depth and Size

Analysis focusing on early lesions with an invasion depth limited to the EP and a tumor size < 10 mm revealed a significant difference in the proportion of lesions meeting both criteria among the four circumferential location groups (p = 0.049) (Table 4). Additionally, a significant difference in this proportion was observed between anterior and left wall locations (p = 0.02) (Figure 2a). However, no significant difference was observed in the proportion of lesions satisfying both criteria among the three longitudinal groups (Table 4), nor when considering the proportion of lesions limited to the EP. The proportion of tumors < 10 mm significantly differed among the three longitudinal location groups (p = 0.020) (Table 4). Furthermore, a significant difference in this proportion was observed between the Ce‐Ut and Mt groups (p = 0.02) (Figure 2b).

Discussion

In this pilot study, we examined both the longitudinal and circumferential locations of ESD‐resected ESCC lesions to assess circumferential susceptibility. Key findings include the predominance of lesions were in the posterior wall, with the anterior wall showing the lowest frequency of lesions. Furthermore, the median tumor size was smallest in the anterior wall group, where a greater proportion of lesions exhibited an invasion depth limited to the EP and a tumor size < 10 mm. The anterior wall showed the highest percentage of lesions meeting both criteria. These findings suggest a lower carcinogenic susceptibility for ESCC in the anterior wall, while the posterior wall displayed a higher susceptibility.

To date, limited studies have explored circumferential tumor location [5]. One contributing factor could be the historical challenge in identifying early‐stage ESCC; however, the introduction of endoscopes utilizing NBI has significantly improved detection [15, 16]. Early ESCC lesions often present with a small circumference, facilitating the determination of their circumferential location. ESD for ESCC allows for esophageal preservation, enabling continued surveillance endoscopy post‐resection to detect multiple early ESCC recurrence lesions. Recent studies on ESD for ESCC by Hazama et al. [22] and Mitsui et al. [23] reported the proportion of lesions in the following order (highest to lowest): posterior, right, left, and anterior. These studies highlighted the impact of circumferential location on the difficulty of ESD procedures, drawing attention to this aspect. Importantly, their findings, indicating the posterior region as the most affected and the anterior region as the least affected, align with our present study results. This study revealed a lower incidence of lesions on the anterior wall compared with other circumferential locations. Notably, this location had the highest proportion of early lesions, defined by an invasion depth limited to the EP and tumor size < 10 mm.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has identified ethanol in alcoholic beverages, acetaldehyde resulting from alcohol metabolism, and smoking as prominent esophageal carcinogens [3]. These agents have been implicated in carcinogenesis through the induction of oxidative stress in the gastrointestinal epithelium, primarily triggered by ethanol itself [24]. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear, we considered that the variation in susceptibility to ESCC development across different sites may be attributed to differential exposure to carcinogens. Therefore, we hypothesized that susceptibility might differ among circumferential locations beyond just the middle esophagus.

Regarding posture, individuals typically spend the day upright or seated but assume a supine position during sleep, potentially leading to saliva accumulation in the esophagus. In a horizontal cross‐section of the esophagus, it is conceivable that fluids such as saliva tend to accumulate in the posterior wall due to gravitational effects, while the anterior wall is less exposed to such fluids (Figure 3). Thus, the observed lower incidence of ESCC lesions on the anterior wall and the higher proportion of lesions with shallow invasion depth and small size in this region could be attributed to reduced exposure of the anterior wall to the aforementioned carcinogenic fluids (alcohol and acetaldehyde). Additionally, due to the anatomical curvature of the vertebrae and surrounding organ positions, the middle esophagus is often dorsally located, making it more prone to fluid accumulation in the supine position. Consequently, the higher occurrence of ESCC lesions in the central esophagus, as observed from a longitudinal perspective, appears reasonable. Taken together, these findings suggest that caution should be exercised when examining the anterior wall via endoscopy, as ESCC lesions in this location may exhibit smaller sizes and shallower invasion depths. Furthermore, we believe that there is a relationship between the frequency of ESCC in each location of the esophagus and its depth and size. The results of this analysis show that ESCC in the anterior wall and Ut was small and shallow. The fact that it is rare in the anterior wall is consistent with the results of this analysis. However, as previously mentioned, ESCC in the Ut is the least frequent, as in previous reports. Additionally, to address concerns that secondary ESCC might have been more thoroughly examined by EGD compared with primary ESCC, we focused exclusively on 44 secondary ESCC lesions. Due to the limited number of cases, we could not conduct a detailed pathological investigation by location, nor could we provide in‐depth data; instead only the frequency of lesions by location was analyzed. Regarding the frequency of longitudinal locations, CE‐Ut 9 (20.5%), Mt 20 (45.5%), and Lt‐Ae 15 (34.1%) were the most common, as in previous reports. Regarding the frequency of circumferential locations, the anterior wall was the least common, as in all cases, with anterior 5 (11.4%), left 13 (29.5%), posterior 17 (38.6%), and right 9 (20.5%). Thus, the site‐specific frequency of secondary ESCC was similar for all cases. There are only a few reports evaluating circumferential location even in high‐stage ESCC. Nagasawa et al. [25] evaluated the prognosis by dividing the patients into left and anterior (L/A) and right and posterior (R/P) groups, reporting that the L/A group had a worse prognosis than the R/P group. Unfortunately, there were no studies dividing the patients into four groups based on circumferential location, so further evaluation of high‐stage ESCC cases is desirable.

This study has some limitations. First, the relatively small number of patients and the retrospective nature of this study, coupled with its single‐center setting, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the determination of longitudinal tumor location relied solely on endoscopy, as early ESCC lesions, suitable for ESD, were the main focus of the present study. These lesions are typically not detectable by esophagography or computed tomography. In cases where lesions spanned across multiple circumferential locations, the circumferential location was defined based on the segment with the longest extension in the longitudinal direction. While it would have been preferable to analyze only lesions localized to a single circumferential direction, this approach would have further reduced the sample size, potentially compromising the adequacy of the analysis. Third, we did not examine cases in which treatment other than ESD, such as surgery or CRT, was performed. For example, our study highlighted that ESCC lesions located in the anterior wall of the Ce‐Ut was rare. However, this may be due in part to the challenges in detecting early cancer at this location using a tangential view. As a result, early cancer in this area might go undetected, potentially leading to fewer instances where ESD is performed. These points, together with the small sample size and retrospective nature of the study, made it difficult to draw more substantial conclusions from this analysis. Last, while this study showed that ESCC lesions on the anterior wall were often infrequent, small, and at an early stage, it indicated a potential carcinogenic site in the posterior wall. This observation aligns with recent studies by Hazama et al. [22] and Mitsui et al. [23], which also highlighted the posterior wall as the most common site. However, direct observation of circumferential locations with high carcinogenesis was not feasible, given the focus on smaller early lesions. Thus, further research involving a larger number of cases and multicenter collaboration is needed to validate our findings.

In conclusion, ESCC lesions on the anterior wall are less frequent, exhibit shallow tumor invasion depth, and may be present in smaller sizes.
