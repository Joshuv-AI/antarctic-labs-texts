MRI-based glymphatic metrics correlate with caudate nucleus dopaminergic deficits and are independent of amyloid-β pathway in Parkinson's disease
Authors: Xinyi Xie, Ruolian Dai, Mei Xin, Yunlan Du, Zhongjiao Lu, Jianjun Liu, Hualong Wang, Chenpeng Zhang, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlymphatic dysfunction is thought to underlie neurodegenerative dementia; however, its interplay with dopaminergic degeneration and concurrent amyloid-β (Aβ) pathology in Parkinson's disease (PD) remains unresolved. This study aimed to evaluate MRI-based glymphatic metrics (the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space [DTI-ALPS] index and choroid plexus volume [CPV]) and their associations with dopaminergic degeneration, Aβ burden, and cognitive impairment (CI) in PD.Methods79 PD patients (48 with CI (PD-CI), and 31 cognitive normal (PD-N)) and 28 age-, gender-matched normal controls (NC) were included. Dopaminergic degeneration was measured by dopamine transporter (DAT) availability using 11C-CFT-PET in PD. Additionally, 46 out of 79 PD patients conducted 18F-Florbetapir-PET for quantifying global Aβ burden (Centiloid values) and regional Aβ burden (voxel-wise regression analysis).ResultsPD-CI showed a significantly reduced DTI-ALPS index (1.31 ± 0.12 vs. 1.40 ± 0.12, p = 0.014; and 1.41 ± 0.12, p = 0.004) and enlarged CPV (1.43 ± 0.31 vs. 1.07 ± 0.18; and 1.14 ± 0.24; p < 0.001 respectively) compared to NC and PD-N. Both the DTI-ALPS index and CPV were significantly correlated with age and caudate DAT availability (CAU_DAT), but not correlated with DAT availability in anterior / posterior putamen, Centiloid values or regional Aβ burden. Further mediation analyses indicated that CAU_DAT mediated the associations between glymphatic metrics and CI after adjustment for covariates, as assessed by the DTI-ALPS index (p = 0.026) and CPV (p = 0.042).ConclusionsThe caudate dopaminergic degeneration corelates with MRI-based glymphatic metrics and cognitive deterioration in PD.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, DTI-ALPS index, Choroid plexus volume, Dopaminergic degeneration, Cognitive impairment, Caudate nucleus

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disease, is characterized by α-synuclein pathology, and manifests with motor symptoms and non-motor symptoms. Cognitive decline in PD is recognized as a continuous process affecting nearly all patients over time (Aarsland et al., 2017). However, the mechanisms of cognitive impairment in PD remain incompletely understood.

The glymphatic (glial-lymphatic) system, a perivascular clearance pathway first identified by Iliff et al., facilitates the exchange of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid (ISF) to remove metabolic waste, including amyloid-β, from the brain (Iliff et al., 2012). Failure of the glymphatic system has been recognized to contribute to neurodegenerative dementia, as it leads to abnormal protein aggregation, activates neuroinflammation, and promotes a vicious positive feedback loop between abnormal protein accumulation and further glymphatic dysfunction, ultimately causing brain damage and cognitive decline(Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020).

Historically, the intrathecal administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents was employed in the assessment of glymphatic function. However, the invasive procedure and dose-related neurotoxicity limit its clinical application(Patel et al., 2020). In recent years, it has been found that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based metrics can partially reflect the function of the glymphatic system in non-invasively ways. The diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index has emerged as a valuable tool for comparing glymphatic function between neurological disorders and healthy controls(Costa et al., 2024). Consistent findings show reduced DTI-ALPS indices across various neurodegenerative diseases, including PD(Bae et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024); Alzheimer's disease (AD)(Huang et al., 2024b); frontotemporal dementia (FTD)(Jiang et al., 2023); and etc. Additionally, enlarged choroid plexus volume (CPV) has been recognized as another marker of glymphatic dysfunction, observed in PD(Yao et al., 2024) and other neurodegenerative diseases(Jeong et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023). The CPV enlargement was also associated with cognitive decline and decreased CSF Aβ1–42 levels in PD patients(He et al., 2024). These findings collectively suggested the involvement of glymphatic dysfunction in PD. Here, we hypothesize that PD patients with cognitive impairment (PD-CI) exhibit more significant glymphatic system abnormalities.

While amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition is a hallmark pathological feature of AD, it frequently co-occurs in PD(Aarsland et al., 2021; Compta et al., 2011; Jellinger and Korczyn, 2018). Postmortem studies reveal that 62–75% of PD patients exhibit mild to moderate AD neuropathological changes(Chen et al., 2024; Kotzbauer et al., 2012), with approximately half of those with PD dementia (PDD) showing moderate-to-severe cortical and subcortical Aβ deposits(Smith et al., 2019). The AD pathology may accelerate clinical progression and cognitive decline in PD(Tong and Chen, 2021). The co-occurrence of Aβ pathology in PD exacerbates α-synuclein accumulation in limbic and neocortical regions, and independently predicts progression to PDD (Toledo et al., 2016). Preclinical evidence suggested that impaired glymphatic system promoted Aβ accumulation (Simon et al., 2022). Notably, MRI-based markers of glymphatic function, such as the DTI-ALPS index (Hong et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024b) and CPV (Jeong et al., 2024), have been established as reliable correlates of whole-brain Aβ burden in AD, highlighting a potential mechanistic link between impaired clearance and protein pathology.

Limited studies reported that the DTI-ALPS index or CPV was in association with striatal subregional dopaminergic degeneration in PD(Jeong et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024), but the two metrics have not been tested simultaneously. Moreover, no studies to date have investigated the relationship between glymphatic metrics and Aβ co-pathology in PD. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate glymphatic metrics (the DTI-ALPS index and CPV) in PD: (1) to determine whether the two metrics are significantly altered in PD-CI, and (2) to explore their associations with dopaminergic degeneration, Aβ burden, and cognitive impairment (Fig. 1).

Methods

Participants and clinical assessment

All PD patients were from Renji Hospital Movement Disorder Clinic Cohort approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (LY2024-266-B), and informed consent was obtained. Patients were consecutively enrolled from the movement disorder clinic (Department of Neurology, Renji Hospital) starting in September 2024, and all participants with PD included in the present study were recruited up to October 2025. The study conformed with World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. PD was diagnosed according to the 2015 Movement Disorder Society (MDS) criteria(Postuma et al., 2015). The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) low-quality structural MRI; 2) secondary parkinsonism, such as drug-induced or vascular parkinsonism; 3) severe vascular pathology indicated by extensive white matter hyperintensities (WMH, Fazekas grade 3) or stroke history. The age/gender-matched cognitively normal controls (NCs) were recruited from a community-based cohort (the YuGarden Cohort, NCT06325527) during the same study period. The YuGarden Cohort underwent comprehensive clinical and research evaluations, which encompassed demographic data collection, standardized cognitive tests, biospecimen sampling, and multi-modal MRI.

Cognitive status was primarily assessed using the clinical dementia rating (CDR) scale and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Nasreddine et al., 2005). The CDR-global score (CDR-GS) and CDR-sum of box (CDR-SB) were recorded. Accordingly, patients with PD can be divided into PD with normal cognition (PD-N, CDR-GS = 0) and PD with cognitive impairment (PD-CI, CDR-GS ≥ 0.5). The PD patients additionally underwent motor evaluation using the Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) (Goetz et al., 2008). Hoehn and Yahr (H-Y) stage of each patient was also recorded. Levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated according to the 2023 updated conversion formula(Jost et al., 2023).

We additionally considered the body-first and brain-first subtypes of PD. According to the established criteria of previous publishments (Banwinkler et al., 2022), patients were classified as body-first PD (body-PD) or brain-first PD (brain-PD) based on the presence or absence of REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) preceding the onset of motor symptoms.

Imaging acquisition: MRI and PET-CT

The MRI images were scanned on a 3.0 T scanner of uPMR790 (United Imaging Healthcare). For diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), a single-shot echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with diffusion gradients was employed. Matrix: 128 × 118, voxel: 1.95 × 1.95 × 4.0 mm3, TR of 4699 ms, and TE of 84 ms. Two non-diffusion-weighted image (b = 0 s/mm2) and 32 diffusion-weighted images (b = 1000 s/mm2) were obtained. The three - dimensional T1-weighted structural MRI images (3D-T1 MRI) were acquired with the following parameters: TR = 7 ms, TE = 3 ms, flip angle = 10, slice thickness of 1 mm, and matrix = 256 * 256. WMH burden was assessed by Fazekas scale based on 2D-FLAIR MRI.

The Carbon 11 - labeled 2 - β - carbomethoxy - 3 - β - (4 - fluorophenyl) tropane (CFT) positron emission tomography (PET) images were conducted on OFF status of PD patients. About 60–70 min after injection, the patients were scanned on uEXPLORER (United Imaging Healthcare), to assess striatum dopamine transporter (DAT) availability. The PD participants received an intravenous injection of 18F-florbetapir and scanned 50 min after injection to assess amyloid burden. These PD patients with 18F-florbetapir- PET were stratified into Aβ-postive (A+) and Aβ-negative (A-) subgroups according to visual assessment. The tracers were generated in the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, under the standards of the Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) and the standards of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the quality control.

Imaging processing and indicator calculation

Diffusion tensor imaging-along the perivascular-space (DTI-ALPS) index

The DTI-ALPS calculation was conducted using the FMRIB Software Library version 6.0 (FSL; Oxford Centre for Functional MRI of the Brain, Oxford, UK; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/). The processing pipeline was validated with good consistency in a multicenter, cross-vendor, test-retest study (Liu et al., 2024), and the script is available (https://github.com/gbarisano/alps/). This pipeline included MP-PCA denoising and Gibbs correction in MRtrix3, followed by susceptibility distortion correction and eddy current/motion correction in FSL. FA maps and axial diffusivity maps were generated via dtifit command in FSL. Individual FA maps were linearly co-registered to the JHU-ICBM-FA template using flirt, with derived transformation matrices applied to diffusivity maps. ROIs were set as spheres with 5 mm diameter in the areas of bilateral superior corona radiata (SCR, projection fibers) and superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF, association fibers) at the level of the lateral ventricles on normalized FA maps by means of ITK-SNAP Software (M.X, blinded to the diagnosis). All ROIs were double checked without revision (XY.X, RL.D).

Diffusivity profiles (Dxx, Dyy, Dzz) from bilateral SCR and SLF ROIs were extracted for ALPS index computation. Specifically, the DTI-ALPS index was computed as the ratio of the mean diffusivity along the x-axis in the projection (Dxx_proj) and association areas (Dxx_assoc) to the mean diffusivity along the y-axis (Dyy_proj) and z-axis (Dzz_assoc) in the respective areas [DTI-ALPS index = mean (Dxx_proj, Dxx_assoc) / mean (Dyy_proj, Dzz_assoc)]. The final DTI-ALPS index was derived from the average values of the bilateral hemispheres.

Choroid plexus segmentation

The segment of choroid plexus (ChP) was based on automatic segmentation of FreeSurfer 7.0 platform (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) and subsequently Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) segmentation algorithm (https://github.com/EhsanTadayon/choroid-plexus-segmentation) (Tadayon et al., 2020). This method was validated to be more accurate than FreeSurfer and was adopted in prior studies. Briefly, the lateral ventricles and choroid plexus were segmented using FreeSurfer, and non-cerebrospinal fluid tissues were selected using GMM with two components, 3D Susan Smoothing, and a second Bayesian GMM with three components. The cluster with the highest average intensity value are chosen as the final ChP segmentation. Choroid plexus volume (CPV) was expressed as the ratio to total intracranial volume (TIV, mm3): CPV = ChP / TIV * 10^3. The TIV value was Z-score converted as one of the covariates.

PET imaging process

The processing of PET images was performed using MATLAB 2022b and the Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/), following a standardized pipeline: (1) All PET and 3D-T1 structural MRI images were manually reoriented; (2) Individual MRI images were coregistered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI)-152 T1-weighted template, and individual PET images was registered to its MRI images subsequently; (3) The unified segmentation method was applied to all T1-weighted images and the deformation fields derived from the T1 segmentation were applied to coregistered PET images (both 11C-CFT and 18F-florbetapir PET), to normalize into standard space (voxel size = 2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm).

For normalized CFT-PET images, we extracted the uptake values of caudate nucleus, anterior / posterior putamen, and occipital lobe. This extracting template was generated from the Anatomical Automatic Labeling Atlas 2 (AAL2) by means of Image calculator module of SPM12. The putamen was divided into the anterior and posterior putamen along the coronal anterior commissure plane. DAT availability, which represents the specific binding ratio, was computed as: (the uptake value of target region - uptake value of occipital lobe) / uptake value of occipital lobe. Subsequently, the bilateral average DAT availability values for the caudate nucleus, the anterior putamen, and the posterior putamen were computed.

After the normalization process of 18F-florbetapir PET images, the global cortical target (CTX) region, including the precuneus, frontal, temporal, parietal, insular cortices and the anterior striatum, acquired from GAAIN (https://www.gaain.org/centiloid-project) was utilized to estimated global Aβ burden. The reference region was set as the whole cerebellum. Then the 18F-florbetapir standard uptake value ratio (SUVr) was calculated and then converted to Centiloid (CL) scale following the formula: CL = 179.64*SUVr −186.95(Huang et al., 2024a).

For voxel-wise analysis, the normalized 18F-florbetapir PET images were smoothed using Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of [6 6 6], and followed by whole brain intensity normalization through cerebellum.

Statistical analysis and visualizing

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed; otherwise, they were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR). Group differences were examined using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons for normally distributed data, and non-parametric tests were applied otherwise. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square tests and reported as counts (percentages). Pearson correlations were computed between normally distributed continuous variables; Spearman correlations were used otherwise. Partial correlation analysis was additionally performed to control age, sex, WMH, and TIV.

Based on the results of correlation analysis, mediation analysis was employed to further explore the relationships among glymphatic metrics, dopamine degeneration / amyloid deposition, and cognitive impairment in PD. The mediation analysis was conducted based on the causal mediation framework and adjusted for age, sex, WMH, and TIV, using the mediation package on RStudio. The indirect effect was estimated via bootstrap with 1000 resamples, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated. p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Voxel-wise analysis of Florbetapir-PET maps were performed using SPM12 and incorporated age and sex as covariates. Multivariable regression Model was employed to assess the associations among DTI-ALPS index or CPV and standardized amyloid uptake value. A cluster-defining threshold of p < 0.001 at the voxel level was applied, and clusters with p < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

According to cognitive status, the PD patients were categorized as PD-N (n = 31) and PD-CI (n = 48). Subgroup comparisons indicated that PD-CI patients were older than those in the PD-N group (Table 1, p = 0.025) and had a higher proportion of males (p = 0.011). The PD patients were classified as early-to-medium stages (emPD, H-Y ≤ 2.5) and advanced stage (advancedPD, H-Y > 2.5). Comparisons of glymphatic metrics, DAT availability and Aβ burden

The DTI-ALPS index, ChP Segment, and CPV differed among the PD-N, PD-CI, and NC group (Fig. 2, p = 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). Post-hoc tests revealed no significant difference in the DTI-ALPS index between the PD-N group and the NC group (Fig. 2a, 1.41 ± 0.12 vs. 1.40 ± 0.12, p = 1.00), while the DTI-ALPS index values in the PD-CI group were significantly decreased (1.31 ± 0.12), p = 0.004 vs. PD-N, p = 0.014 vs. NC, respectively). The mean diffusivity (MD) along the x-axis (Dxx_proj, and Dxx_assoc), y-axis (Dyy_proj) and z-axis (Dzz_assoc) were also compared among PD-N, PD-CI, and NC, and significant differences were observed (all p < 0.001, Supplemental Table 1). Although no significant difference in the DTI-ALPS index was observed between the PD-N and the NC group, significant differences were identified in the MD along the x-axis, y-axis, and the z-axis (all p < 0.001, Bonferroni post-hoc test). Conversely, while a significant difference in the DTI-ALPS index was evident between the PD-N and the PD-CI, no significant differences were observed in the MD of Dxx_proj and Dxx_assoc. This divergence was primarily driven by significant alterations in the MD of Dyy_proj and Dzz_assoc (p = 0.044, and p = 0.036, Bonferroni post-hoc test).

Similarly, there were no significant differences in CPV (Fig. 2b, p = 0.920) between PD-N and NC. Remarkably enlarged CPV was observed in PD-CI (1.43 ± 0.31) compared to NC (1.07 ± 0.18, p < 0.001) and PD-N (1.14 ± 0.24, p < 0.001).

Dopamine transporter (DAT) availability in the caudate nucleus (CAU_DAT) was significantly lower in the PD-CI group compared to the PD-N group. (Fig. 2c, 0.24 ± 0.38 vs. 0.56 ± 0.41, p = 0.001). The DAT availability in putamen, either the anterior putamen (aPUT_DAT, p = 0.188) or the posterior putamen (pPUT_DAT, p = 0.501), did not exhibit significant differences between the PD-N and PD-CI. The Aβ burden in patients with PD-CI was similar to that in PD-N (Fig. 2d, −4.44 (24.84) vs. -1.87 (13.88), p = 0.699).

With respect to the body-first and brain-first subtypes of PD, 43 of 79 patients were successfully classified, including 17 body-first PD (body-PD) and 26 brain-first PD (brain-PD). The two subgroups were comparable in age, disease duration, motor symptom severity, and cognitive performance. No significant differences were observed in the DTI-ALPS index or CPV between the two subtypes. Similarly, neither striatal subregional dopaminergic degeneration nor Centiloid values differed significantly between body-PD and brain-PD (All information was listed in Supplemental Table 2).

Glymphatic metrics were associated with caudate dopaminergic degeneration and independent of Aβ burden

The correlation analysis was displayed in Fig. 3a. Overall, In PD patients, the DTI-ALPS index was negatively correlated with CPV (Fig. 3a, r = −0.39, p ≤0.001). The two metrics were in association with age, CDR-SB, and CAU_DAT. The CPV also correlated with MoCA. In addition, although PUT_DAT showed no association with either the DTI-ALPS index or CPV, both aPUT_DAT and pPUT_DAT were significantly correlated with disease duration, H-Y stage, and LEDD, as well as with CAU_DAT (Fig. 3a). The H-Y stage was associated with disease duration, LEDD, MoCA and CDR-SB scores, as well as dopaminergic degeneration across all striatal subregions (Fig. 3a), indicating dopaminergic deficits and cognitive deterioration progress with advances of PD.

After adjusting for age, sex, WMH, and TIV, both the DTI-ALPS index and CPV remained independently associated with CAU_DAT (Fig. 3b–e; r = 0.26, p = 0.020 and r = −0.24, p = 0.035, respectively). However, stratified analyses revealed distinct patterns. The DTI-ALPS index was associated with CAU_DAT only in PD-CI subgroup (Fig. 3b, r = 0.31, p = 0.032), whereas CPV has a tendency in association with CAU_DAT in PD-N subgroup (Fig. 3c, r = −0.34, p = 0.059). When stratified by disease stage, the DTI-ALPS index showed a positive trend with CAU_DAT in early-to-medium stages (emPD, H-Y ≤ 2.5) and rather than advanced stages (advancedPD, H-Y > 2.5) (Fig. 3d, p = 0.090 and p = 0.491, respectively). CPV was significantly associated with DAT_CAU in emPD (Fig. 3e, r = −0.33, p = 0.016).

Further, the DTI-ALPS index and CPV were unrelated to global Aβ burdens as quantified by the Centiloid value (p = 0.339, and p = 0.796, respectively). Centiloid values showed no significant correlation with duration, H-Y, LEDD, MoCA, CDR-SB, CAU_DAT, or PUT_DAT, even after age adjustment (Supplemental Table 3). We further performed voxel-wise regression analysis on standardized and intensity-normalized Florbetapir-PET maps, both in the PD group and its PD-N, PD-CI subgroups. The results revealed no significant voxel clusters associated with the DTI-ALPS index or CPV. Additionally, The PD patients with 18F-florbetapir- PET (n = 46) were stratified into Aβ-positive (A+, n = 11) and Aβ-negative (A-, n = 35) subgroups. No significant associations between DTI-ALPS index or CPV with CAU_DAT were observed in the A+ subgroup (p = 0.291, p = 0.268, respectively), likely due to the limited sample size (Supplemental Table 4). The associations were significant in the A- subgroup (p = 0.036, p = 0.037), whereas not after adjusted for age (p = 0.147, p = 0.163), potentially due to the reduced statistical power of this sub-cohort. Furthermore, multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated that the A+ status was not significantly associated with either the ALPS index (B = −0.03, p = 0.491) or the CPV (B = 0.135, p = 0.131) after adjusting for age, sex, WMH grade, and TIV.

Caudate dopaminergic degeneration mediated the link between glymphatic metrics and cognitive decline in PD

As noted previously, the glymphatic metrics (the DTI-ALPS and CPV) were associated with CAU_DAT and CDR-SB. The further mediation analysis was conducted and CAU_DAT was set as the mediator (Fig. 4). The results demonstrated that the glymphatic metrics indirectly affected cognitive impairment in PD via caudate nucleus dopaminergic deficits. The altered glymphatic metrics (either the DTI-ALPS index decreased or the CPV enlarged), the more deficits in caudate dopaminergic degeneration, and indirectly increased the CDR-SB, which indicating cognitive deterioration. After adjusting age, sex, WMH, and TIV, the indirect effects of CAU_DAT were significant (p = 0.026 and p = 0.042, respectively). CAU_DAT accounted for 39.8% of the total effect between the DTI-ALPS index and CDR-SB (Fig. 4a), and 34.7% of the total effect between CPV and CDR-SB (Fig. 4b). Notably, CAU_DAT fully mediated the relationship between glymphatic metrics and CDR-SB, as the direct effects were not significant (p = 0.172 for the DTI-ALPS index, and p = 0.206 for CPV).

Discussion

This study evaluated two MRI-derived glymphatic related metrics, the DTI-ALPS index and CPV, in PD. The relationships between these two metrics and dopaminergic neurodegeneration, clinical characteristics, and overall or regional amyloid burden were further examined. Key findings were: 1) PD-CI showed significantly decreased DTI-ALPS index and enlarged CPV compared to PD-N and NC, indicating impaired glymphatic function in PD-CI; 2) Both metrics correlated specifically with caudate nucleus dopaminergic degeneration, which mediated their associations with cognitive deterioration (CDR-SB) in PD; 3) The two metrics were independent of global or regional Aβ burden in PD.

First, this study simultaneously analyzed the DTI-ALPS index and CPV in PD patients and NC. Our findings demonstrate that the two MRI-based glymphatic metrics significantly altered in patients with PD-CI, aligning with prior literature. For example, Wood et al. reported that a lower DTI-ALPS index was observed in PD-MCI than in PD-NC, and a lower DTI-ALPS index at baseline was associated with longitudinal cognitive decline and worse progression (Wood et al., 2024). In addition, we found that the two metrics were independently associated with caudate dopaminergic deficits after adjusting age. Similarly, Yao et al. demonstrated that the DTI-ALPS index was strongly correlated with disease severity and striatal dopaminergic degeneration(Yao et al., 2024). Meanwhile, Jeong SH et al. uncovered the associations between CPV enlargement, dopaminergic degeneration of all striatal subregions and poorer outcomes in motor dysfunction progression in PD(Jeong et al., 2023). The link between glymphatic dysfunction and dopaminergic degeneration may help explain its predictive value for clinical progression in PD. For instance, a reduced DTI-ALPS index has been independently associated with an increased risk of recurrent falls, wheelchair dependence, and dementia(Zhou et al., 2024). It has also been shown to predict the progression of motor and non-motor symptoms in prodromal PD(Xing et al., 2025). An exception is a study by Bae et al., which did not find a significant association between the DTI-ALPS index and striatal dopaminergic loss (Bae et al., 2023), the discrepancy potentially attributable to methodological differences in dopamine transporter quantification or cohort characteristics. The severity of dopaminergic degeneration may reflect the burden of pathological proteins, such as α-synuclein (α-syn). Hence, it is inferred that glymphatic dysfunction is related to the burden of α-synuclein pathology. However, no α-syn-targeted radiotracer has been widely used in clinic to date.

Although the two metrics were significantly associated (r = −0.39), the correlation was moderate, which may reflect the functional complexity of the glymphatic system. Specifically, the DTI-ALPS index primarily captures regional interstitial fluid (ISF) dynamics by quantifying water diffusion along perivascular spaces between white matter tracts. Accumulating evidence indicates that it is also biased by white matter microstructural integrity and WMH(Li et al., 2025; Satpathi et al., 2025). In contrast, CPV reflects the structure of the choroid plexus—a key site for ISF and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production (Municio et al., 2023). Thus, while both metrics pertain to fluid dynamics within the brain, they likely measure complementary rather than overlapping aspects of glymphatic function, explaining their moderate rather than strong association. CPV might reflect pathological changes predominantly in early-stage PD or prior to the development of cognitive impairment (Fig. 3c, e).

Moving beyond the established correlation between glymphatic metrics and caudate dopaminergic degeneration, this study further demonstrated that caudate degeneration mediates the link between glymphatic impairment and cognitive decline in PD. Our findings highlight a central role for caudate dopaminergic deficits in driving cognitive deterioration in PD. This mechanistic pathway helps explain the elevated risk of cognitive impairment in older patients and those with longer disease duration or more advanced motor disability. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, caudate dopaminergic degeneration correlated not only with age but also with key indices of disease severity—including disease duration, H-Y stage, and putamina dopaminergic loss. These results suggest that the progressive spread of dopaminergic neurodegeneration from the putamen to the caudate may mark the transition from primarily motor symptoms to cognitive decline in PD. Supporting this view, prior research indicates that the caudate nucleus remains relatively spared in early-onset PD (Liu et al., 2015) and that dopaminergic denervation in the dorsal caudate is implicated during cognitive tasks (Aarsland et al., 2021). Furthermore, the caudate is a critical hub within frontal-striatal circuits that subserve executive functions such as attention, working memory, and cognitive flexibility(Grahn et al., 2008). In line with this, multiple studies have linked reduced caudate DAT availability to cognitive impairment in PD(Andersson et al., 2021; Palermo et al., 2024; Schrag et al., 2017; Yoshimura et al., 2025), reinforcing the importance of caudate dopaminergic integrity in PD-related cognitive impairment.

Interestingly, here this study proposes two important conceptual implications: 1) enhancing glymphatic function may be a viable therapeutic target for cognitive impairment in PD. Impaired glymphatic clearance appears to actively exacerbate PD pathology (Zhang et al., 2023). Preclinical studies have shown that suppressing glymphatic function—either through AQP4 deletion or cervical lymphatic ligation in α-synuclein-overexpressing mice—accelerates α-synuclein accumulation, dopaminergic neuron loss, and motor deficits(Zou et al., 2019). Conversely, recent work demonstrated that non-invasive mechanical stimulation of cervical lymph nodes can increase cerebrospinal fluid outflow and improve drainage in aged mice(Jin et al., 2025). This evidence suggests that augmenting glymphatic clearance could represent a promising non-pharmacological strategy for mitigating cognitive decline in neurodegenerative diseases. 2) our results further imply that alleviating caudate dopaminergic degeneration may also contribute to improving cognitive outcomes in PD.

In our cohort, neither the DTI-ALPS index nor CPV correlated with global Aβ burden (Centiloid values) or regional amyloid deposition, even when PD patients were stratified by cognitive status. This dissociation suggests that glymphatic dysfunction in PD may occur independently of, or prior to, significant Aβ accumulation. Consistent with this, we did not find cerebral Aβ burden differ between PD-N and PD-CI subgroups, and did not find cerebral Aβ burden link with clinical measurements in PD. These observations align with prior amyloid-PET studies and support the view that Aβ pathology is not the principal determinant of cognitive impairment in most PD patients (Melzer et al., 2019; Winer et al., 2018). Instead, Aβ accumulation may emerge secondarily as a consequence of compromised clearance mechanisms. Additionally, we observed substantial heterogeneity in Centiloid values within our PD cohort. As the cerebellum is widely adopted as the reference region in existing literature, this methodology requires further investigation and validation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Firstly, it should be emphasized that the two MRI-derived metrics reflect local information and cannot be considered fully equivalent to glymphatic function(Taoka et al., 2024). The primary significance of this study lies in revealing the concurrent involvement of caudate dopaminergic degeneration. Second, given the relatively small sample size in stratified analysis and the resulting limited statistical power, larger sample sizes are needed in further studies. Third, the manual placement of ROIs in DTI-ALPS processing might introduce measurement bias. In addition, the heterogeneity in PD have also been insufficiently addressed. While we found no significant differences in glymphatic metrics between body-first and brain-first subtypes, the heterogeneity of PD includes sex differences, onset laterality, clinical symptom subtypes, and etc. Moreover, cross-sectional design is employed, and the findings need further longitudinal validation.

Conclusions

The caudate nucleus dopaminergic degeneration significantly correlates with both glymphatic metrics and cognitive deterioration in PD, suggesting a coupled pathophysiology. The role of concurrent amyloid pathology requires further investigation.
