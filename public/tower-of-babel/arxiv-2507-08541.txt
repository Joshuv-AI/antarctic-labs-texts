Abstract
We introduce a series of graph decompositions based on the modulator/target scheme of modification problems that enable several algorithmic applications that parametrically extend the algorithmic potential of planarity. In the core of our approach is a polynomial time algorithm for computing planar ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulators. Given a graph class ℋ \mathcal{H} , a planar ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulator of a graph G G is a set X ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) X\subseteq V(G) such that the “torso” of X X is planar and all connected components of G − X G-X belong to ℋ \mathcal{H} . Here, the torso of X X is obtained from G ⁡ [ X ] G[X] if, for every connected component of G − X G-X , we form a clique out of its neighborhood on G ⁡ [ X ] G[X] . We introduce ℋ \mathcal{H} -Planarity as the problem of deciding whether a graph G G has a planar ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulator. We prove that, if ℋ \mathcal{H} is hereditary, CMSO-definable, and decidable in polynomial time, then ℋ \mathcal{H} -Planarity is solvable in polynomial time. The polynomial-time algorithm has several notable algorithmic consequences. First, we introduce two parametric extensions of ℋ \mathcal{H} -Planarity by defining the notions of ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treedepth and ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth , which generalize the concepts of elimination distance and tree decompositions to the class ℋ \mathcal{H} . For example, a graph is said to have ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth at most t t if it admits a tree decomposition such that every leaf-bag in the decomposition is contained in ℋ \mathcal{H} , and all other bags are either of size at most t + 1 t+1 or have a planar torso. By leveraging our polynomial-time algorithm for ℋ \mathcal{H} -Planarity , we prove the following theorem. Suppose that ℋ \mathcal{H} is a hereditary, CMSO-definable, and union-closed graph class, and consider the problem of determining whether a graph G G has an ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulator of size at most k k for some k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} , i.e., a set X ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) X\subseteq V(G) of size at most k k such that every connected component of G − X G-X lies in ℋ \mathcal{H} . If this problem is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) when parameterized by k k , then deciding whether a graph G G has ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treedepth or ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth at most k k is also FPT when parameterized by k k . By combining this result with existing FPT algorithms for various ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulator problems, we thereby obtain FPT algorithms parameterized by ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treedepth and ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth for numerous graph classes ℋ \mathcal{H} . Second, by combining the well-known algorithmic properties of planar graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth, our methods for computing ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treedepth and ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth lead to a variety of algorithmic applications. For instance, once we know that a given graph has bounded ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treedepth or bounded ℋ \mathcal{H} -planar treewidth, we can derive additive approximation algorithms for graph coloring and polynomial-time algorithms for counting (weighted) perfect matchings. Furthermore, we design Efficient Polynomial-Time Approximation Schemes (EPTAS-es) for several problems, including Maximum Independent Set . All previous (meta) algorithmic studies on ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulators and ℋ \mathcal{H} -treewidth deal with modulators of “low bidimensionality” in the sense that they cannot be spread along a grid-minor of the input graph [ Jansen, de Kroon, Włodarczyk, STOC 2021; Agrawal, Kanesh, Lokshtanov, Panolan, Ramanujan, Saurabh, and Zehavi, SODA 2022; Fomin, Golovach, Sau, Stamoulis, Thilikos, ACM Trans. Comp. Log. 2025 ]. Our results are the first to handle the situation where the ℋ \mathcal{H} -modulators act globally. For this, we introduce a new version of the “irrelevant vertex technique” that can deal with modulators that may act “everywhere” in the input graph. Our technique goes further than the current meta-algorithmic framework on the applicability of the irrelevant vertex technique [ Sau, Stamoulis, Thilikos, SODA 2025 ] that is conditioned by bidimensionality.

Introduction

Graph decompositions play a crucial role in graph theory and have a plethora of algorithmic applications. In our paper, we consider decompositions that historically stem out of graph modification problems. A typical problem of this type asks whether it is possible to apply a series of modifications to a graph in order to transform it into one that exhibits a desired target property. Many classical
NP
-complete problems can be described as the following graph modification problem to some class of
graphs
\mathcal{H}
. In the
\mathcal{H}
-Deletion
problem for an input graph
G
, we aim to find a vertex set
S
of the minimum size such that graph
G-S
is in the target class
\mathcal{H}
.
For example, if
\mathcal{H}
is the class of graphs without edges, then
\mathcal{H}
-Deletion
is
Vertex Cover
; and when
\mathcal{H}
is the class of bipartite graphs, then
\mathcal{H}
-Deletion
is
Odd Cycle Transversal
.
Most graph modification problems are known to be
NP
-complete
[
43
,
60
]
. However, there is a tremendous amount of study in parameterized algorithms establishing the fixed-parameter tractability of
\mathcal{H}
-Deletion
for
various graph classes
\mathcal{H}
when parameterized by the size of the set
S
, often referred to in the literature as the
\mathcal{H}
-modulator

(we refer to the book
[
15
]
for many examples of such algorithms).
The
\mathcal{H}
-Deletion
problem could be seen as a graph decomposition problem
where the task is to partition the set of vertices of a graph into a (small) modulator and
the set of vertices inducing a graph from
\mathcal{H}
. The main idea is to use the
constructed decomposition for algorithmic purposes. In particular, in the
parameterized complexity area, it is common to investigate problems under structural
parameterizations by the vertex cover number or the minimum size of a feedback vertex
set; we refer to
[
36
]
for basic examples.

Our results are motivated by the recent developments in the area.
The first step in these developments is due to Bulian and Dawar
[
5
,
6
]
, who noticed that many tractability results could
be extended when, instead of the size of the modulator, we parametrize by the
treedepth of the torso of the modulator and, that way, introduced the concept of
elimination distance
to
\mathcal{H}
(also called
\mathcal{H}
-treedepth
). These ideas were
generalized to the scenario where the parameter is the treewidth of the torso of the
modulator
S
by Eiben, Ganian, Hamm, and Kwon
[
18
]
. Equivalently, this
leads to the notion of
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth
, which is informally a tree decomposition with
leaf bags from
\mathcal{H}
and all other bags of bounded
size
[
18
,
37
]
. The attractiveness of the
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth is
that it combines the best algorithmic properties of both worlds: the nice properties of

\mathcal{H}
(e.g., being bipartite or chordal) with the nice properties of graphs of
bounded treewidth
[
1
,
18
,
37
]
. Research on

\mathcal{H}
-treewidth took an unexpected turn with the work of Agrawal, Kanesh,
Lokshtanov, Panolan, Ramanujan, Saurabh, and Zehavi
[
1
]
, who
demonstrated that, from the perspective of (non-uniform) fixed-parameter tractability
(
FPT
), the size of the modulator, the treedepth, and the treewidth of the
modulator’s torso offers equally powerful parameterizations for every hereditary graph
class
\mathcal{H}
, provided some mild additional conditions are met. We stress that in all aforementioned results, modulators’ torsos have always bounded treewidth.

Our motivation for introducing
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth
and
\mathcal{H}
-planar treewidth
stems from the well-known algorithmic properties of planar graphs, which have been exploited extensively in approximation, parameterized, and counting algorithms. In much the same way that
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth combines the best properties of treewidth and the class
\mathcal{H}
, we pose the following question:

How and when is it possible to combine the best of planarity, graph class
\mathcal{H}
, and treewidth or treedepth?

To arrive at a meaningful answer, it is essential to have efficient procedures for computing an

\mathcal{H}
-planar treewidth and an
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth.

Looking for planar modulators

In the core of our approach to

Irrelevant vertices for global modulators.

For the proof of
theorem

1

Next we prove that yes-instances of

[

an equivalence instance of the problem by removing an “irrelevant vertex”
in the middle of this wall. This is the general framework of

by a series of algorithmic meta-theorems that have been recently developed in
[

28

and, in its more powerful version, in

Unfortunately, none of these meta-algorithmic results can be applied in our case.
Typically, the applicability of the algorithmic meta-theorems in

24

require that the modifications are affecting only a

In technical terms, according to

28
]
in order to find
an irrelevant vertex. This initiates

part of the paper is devoted to this problem. Our approach applies
the irrelevant vertex technique beyond the meta-algorithmic framework of
[
54

and, we believe,
may have independent use for other problems involving global modification.

On the conditions on
\mathcal{H}
.

We proceed with a few remarks on the conditions in
theorem

1
.
First, it is important that we demand the torso of the modulator to be planar rather than to allow the whole modulator to be planar. Without this condition, the problem becomes
NP
-hard. Indeed, Farrugia
[
22
]
proved that for two graph classes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
that are hereditary and closed under disjoint-union, the problem of deciding whether a graph
G
admits a partition

of
V(G)
such that

and
G[B]\in\mathcal{Q}
is
NP
-hard, unless
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are both the class of edgeless graphs.
Hence, for any hereditary graph class
\mathcal{H}
closed under the disjoint union operation, the problem of deciding, given a graph
G

such that

is planar and that each connected component
C
of
G-S
belongs to
\mathcal{H}

-hard.

Second, the necessity of
\mathcal{H}
to be a polynomial-time decidable graph class is apparent—it is easy to show that

-hardness of deciding whether a graph is in
\mathcal{H}
implies

-hardness of
\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
as well. However, the necessity of

to be a hereditary property is less obvious. We prove in

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
becomes

-hard.

Finally, we do not know whether CMSO-definability of
\mathcal{H}
is necessary.
We require this condition because the initial step of our proof uses the meta-theorem of Lokshtanov, Ramanujan, Saurabh, and Zehavi from
[
46
]
on reducing CMSO-definable graph problems to problems on unbreakable graphs. Whether CMSO-definability could be replaced by a more general property remains an intriguing open question.

Using the meta-theorem of
[
46
]
comes with the following drawback:
theorem

1

[
46

In other words,

-planar graph

For example, the class of perfect graphs is hereditary, CMSO-definable, and polynomial-time decidable

Counting perfect matchings.

Our second example of applications of
Theorem

concerns counting perfect matchings. While counting perfect matchings on general graphs is #P-complete, on planar graphs, it is polynomial-time solvable by the celebrated Fisher–Kasteleyn–Temperley (FKT) algorithm
[

,
38
,
56

By making use of Valiant’s “matchgates”
[

\mathcal{H}
-planar graphs.

Let us remind that a

Let

The

in

where the sum is taken over all perfect matchings

If

is the number of perfect matchings in

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hereditary, CMSO-definable, and polynomial-time decidable graph class. Moreover, assume that the weighted (resp. unweighted) number of perfect matchings

is computable in polynomial time for graphs in
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}
-planar graph

{\sf pmm}(G)

Examples of classes of graphs
\mathcal{H}
where counting perfect matchings can be done in polynomial time are graphs
excluding a shallow-vortex as a minor

(see also

[

EPTAS’s.

Our third example is the extension of Baker’s technique on planar graphs used for approximation and
FPT
algorithms. While we provide an example for
Independent Set
,
similar results about efficient polynomial-time approximation schemes (EPTAS) could be obtained for many other graph optimization problems.

Let us remind that an independent set in a graph

is a set of pairwise nonadjacent vertices. We use
\alpha(G)
to denote the maximum size of an independent set of

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hereditary, CMSO-definable, and polynomial-time decidable graph class. We also assume that there is a polynomial-time algorithm computing a maximum independent set of graphs in
\mathcal{H}
.
Then, there is an algorithm that, given

and a
\mathcal{H}
-planar graph

, computes in time

an independent set of

Examples of graph classes where

is computable in polynomial time are perfect graphs
[
32
]
or graphs excluding

as an induced subgraph
[
33
]
.
Theorem

could also be modified for graph classes where computing a maximum independent set is quasi-polynomial or subexponential. In these cases, the approximation ratio will remain

, but the approximation algorithm’s running time will also become quasi-polynomial or subexponential.

Applications of
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth and
\mathcal{H}
-planar treewidth.

The above applications of

-planarity mentioned

\mathcal{H}\mbox{-}{\sf ptw}

3
with dynamic programming
on the tree decomposition, we may derive a polynomial time algorithm producing a proper coloring of

-planar graphs.
Exploring the full set of algorithmic applications of

-planarity,
\mathcal{H}

However, we expect that our results will appear to be useful in extending

The reason for this is that the modulator is a planar graph that might have big treewidth and might be

-planar treedepth

We sketch here
Lemma 3
, or more exactly, we explain the differences with
Theorem 6
.
Contrary to the
\mathcal{H}^{(a)}
-planarity case, a big apex grid is not an obstruction for a graph of bounded
\mathcal{H}^{(a)}
-planar treedepth.
Therefore, we cannot use the Flat Wall theorem variant of
Theorem 7
.
Instead, we use the classical Flat Wall theorem (
Proposition 5
).
If it reports that
G
has bounded treewidth, then we use Courcelle’s theorem (
Proposition 2
) to conclude.
If we obtain that
G
has a clique-minor of size
a+4k
, then this is a
no
-instance.
So we can assume that we find a small apex set
A
and a wall
W
that is flat in
G-A
and whose compass has bounded treewidth.
Let
A^{+}
be the set of vertices of
A
with many disjoint paths to
W
and
A^{-}=A\setminus A^{+}
.
Assuming
G
has a certifying elimination sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
, what we can essentially prove is that
W
is mostly part of
X_{i}
for some
i\in[k]
, and that the vertices of
A^{+}
must belong to
\bigcup_{j<i}X_{j}
and can hence be somewhat ignored.
Hence, we do the following. We find a subwall
W^{\prime}
of
W
that avoids the vertices of
A^{-}
, and thus is flat in
G-A^{+}
.
We divide
W^{\prime}
further in enough subwalls
W_{1},\dots,W_{r}
(which are flat in
G-A^{+}
) so that we are sure that one of them, say
W_{p}
, is totally contained in
X_{i}
(though we do not know which one).
Given that the compass of the walls
W_{\ell}
have bounded treewidth, we can use Courcelle’s theorem to compute the
\mathcal{H}^{(a)}
-planar treedepth
d_{\ell}
of each of them.
If, for each
\ell\in[r]
,
d_{\ell}>k
, then this is a
no
-instance.
Otherwise, we choose
\ell
such that
d_{\ell}
is minimum.
Then, given a central vertex
v
of
G-v
, we report a
yes
-instance if and only if
G-v
has
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth at most
k
.

The argument for the irrelevancy of
v
uses the core result as in the
\mathcal{H}^{(a)}
-planarity case, that is
Lemma 11
.
What we essentially prove is that, given a certifying elimination sequence
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
of
G-v
, most of
W_{\ell}
is in
Y_{i}
, and that, as such, we can replace
Y_{i},\dots,Y_{k}
according to the certifying elimination sequence we found for the compass of
W_{\ell}
, to obtain a certifying elimination sequence
Y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,Y_{k}^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
.

4
The
FPT
algorithm for
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
-Planarity

To prove
Theorem 6
, we need the notion of
sphere decompositions
.
In
Subsection 4.1
, we thus define sphere decompositions and related notions.
In
Subsection 4.2
, we define

and introduce the Flat Wall theorem.
In
Subsection 4.3
, we provide an obstruction set to
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
-Planarity
.
In

, we observe that a

-instance of

-Planarity
is essentially a graph with a special sphere decomposition, called

-compatible sphere decomposition

In

Finally, in

.

4.1
Drawing in a surface

We begin by defining drawings, sphere decompositions, vortices, and other related notions.

Drawing a graph in the sphere.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\}
be the sphere.
A
drawing
(with crossings) in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a triple
\Gamma=(U,V,E)
such that

•

V
is a finite set of point of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
E
is a finite family of subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
,

•

V\cup(\bigcup_{e\in E}e)=U
and
V\cap(\bigcup_{e\in E}e)=\emptyset,

•

for every
e\in E,

e=h((0,1)),
where
h\colon[0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}\to U
is a homeomorphism onto its image with
h(0),h(1)\in V,

and

•

if
e,e^{\prime}\in E
are distinct, then
|e\cap e^{\prime}|
is finite.

We call the elements of the set
V,
sometimes denoted by
V(\Gamma),
the
vertices of
\Gamma
and the set
E,
denoted by
E(\Gamma),
the set of
edges of
\Gamma
. We also denote
U(\Gamma)=U.

If
G
is a graph and
\Gamma=(U,V,E)
is a drawing with crossings in
\mathbb{S}^{2}

such that
V
and
E
naturally correspond to
V(G)
and
E(G)
respectively, we say that
\Gamma
is a
drawing of
G
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}

(possibly with crossings).

We remind that a
closed disk

D
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a set of points homeomorphic to the set
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\}
on the Euclidean plane. We use
{\sf bd}(D)
to denote the boundary of
D
.

Sphere decompositions.

A
sphere decomposition
of a graph
G
is a pair
\delta=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D}),
where
\Gamma
is a drawing of
G
in the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(with crossings), and
\mathcal{D}
is a collection of closed disks, each a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, such that

1.

the disks in
\mathcal{D}
have pairwise disjoint interiors,

2.

the boundary of each disk
D
in
\mathcal{D}
intersects
\Gamma
in vertices only, and
|{\sf bd}(D)\cap V(\Gamma)|\leq 3
,

3.

if
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
are distinct, then
\Delta_{1}\cap\Delta_{2}\subseteq V(\Gamma),
and

4.

every edge of
\Gamma
belongs to the interior of one of the disks in
\mathcal{D}.

The notion of a sphere decomposition has been introduced in
[
35
]
in the more general case where, instead
of a sphere, we have a surface
\Sigma
with a boundary, where it is called a
\Sigma
-decomposition. It has also been recently used in
[
57
]
.

Sphere embeddings.

A
sphere embedding
of a graph
G
is a sphere decomposition
\delta=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D})
where
\mathcal{D}
is a collection of closed disks such that, for any disk in
\mathcal{D},
only a single edge of
\Gamma
is drawn in its interior. In words,
\Gamma
is a drawing of
G
on the sphere without crossings.

Nodes, cells, and ground vertices.

For a sphere decomposition
\delta=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D})
,
let
N
be the set of all vertices of
\Gamma
that do not belong to the interior of the disks in
\mathcal{D}.

We refer to the elements of
N
as the
nodes
of
\delta.

If
\Delta\in\mathcal{D},
then we refer to the set
\Delta-N
as a
cell
of
\delta.

We denote the set of nodes of
\delta
by
N(\delta)
and the set of cells by
C(\delta).

For a cell
c\in C(\delta)
the set of nodes that belong to the closure of
c
is denoted by
\tilde{c}.
Given a cell
c\in C(\delta),
we define
its
disk
as

\Delta_{c}={\sf bd}(c)\cup c.

We define
\pi_{\delta}\colon N(\delta)\to V(G)
to be the mapping that assigns to every node in
N(\delta)
the corresponding vertex of
G.

We also define
ground vertices
in
\delta
as
{\sf ground}(\delta)=\textpi_{\delta}(N(\delta)).

For a cell
c\in C(\delta)
, we define the graph
\sigma_{\delta}(c),
or
\sigma(c)
if
\delta
is clear from the context, to be the subgraph of
G
consisting of all vertices and edges drawn in
\Delta_{c}.

Note that, for any cell
c\in C(\delta)
such that
\sigma_{\delta}(c)
is not connected, we can split
c
into
|{\sf cc}(\sigma_{\delta}(c))|
cells, and obtain a sphere decomposition
\delta^{\prime}
such that each cell
c\in C(\delta^{\prime})
is such that
\sigma_{\delta}(c)
is connected.
Hence, without loss of generality, when introducing a sphere rendition
\delta
, we assume
\sigma_{\delta}(c)
to be connected for each
c\in C(\delta)
.

Deleting a set.

Given a set
X\subseteq V(G),
we denote by
\delta-X
the sphere decomposition
\delta^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
of
G-X
where
\Gamma^{\prime}
is obtained by the drawing
\Gamma
after removing
all points in
X
and all drawings of edges with an endpoint in
X.
For every point
x\in\textpi_{\delta}^{-1}(X\cap N(\delta))
, we pick
\Delta_{x}
to be an open disk containing
x
and not containing any point of some remaining vertex or edge and such that no two such disks intersect.
We also
set
\Delta_{X}=\bigcup_{x\in\textpi_{\delta}^{-1}(X)}\Delta_{x}
and we define
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{D\setminus\Delta_{X}\mid D\in\mathcal{D}\}
.
Clearly, there is a one to one correspondence between the cells of
\delta
and the cells of
\delta^{\prime}.

If a cell
c
of
\delta
corresponds to a cell
c^{\prime}
of
\delta^{\prime},
then
we call
c^{\prime}
the
heir
of
c
in
\delta^{\prime}
and we call
c
the
precursor

of
c^{\prime}
in
\delta.

Grounded graphs.

Let
\textdelta
be a sphere decomposition of a graph
G
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}.

We say that a cycle
C
of
G
is
grounded in
\textdelta
if
C
uses edges of
\sigma(c_{1})
and
\sigma(c_{2})
for two distinct cells
c_{1},c_{2}\in C(\textdelta).

A
2
-connected subgraph
H
of
G
is said to be
grounded in
\textdelta
if every cycle in
H
is grounded in
\textdelta.

Tracks.

Let
\textdelta
be a sphere decomposition of a graph
G
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}.

For every cell
c\in C(\textdelta)
with
|\tilde{c}|=2
, we select one of the components of
{\sf bd}(c)-\tilde{c}.

This selection is called a
tie-breaker
in
\textdelta,
and we assume every
\textSigma
-decomposition to come equipped with a tie-breaker.
Let
C
be a cycle grounded in
\textdelta
.
We define the
track
of
C
as follows.
Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
be distinct maximal subpaths of
C
such that
P_{i}
is a subgraph of
\sigma(c)
for some cell
c.
Fix an index
i.

The maximality of
P_{i}
implies that its endpoints are
\textpi_{\textdelta}(n_{1})
and
\textpi_{\textdelta}(n_{2})
for distinct
\textdelta
-nodes
n_{1},n_{2}\in N(\textdelta).

If
|\tilde{c}|=2,
define
L_{i}
to be the component of
{\sf bd}(c)-\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
selected by the tie-breaker, and if
|\tilde{c}|=3,
define
L_{i}
to be the component of
{\sf bd}(c)-\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
that is disjoint from
\tilde{c}.

Finally, we define
L^{\prime}_{i}
by slightly pushing
L_{i}
to make it disjoint from all cells in
C(\textdelta).
We define such a curve
L^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i
while ensuring that the curves intersect only at a common endpoint.
The
track
of
C
is defined to be
\bigcup_{i\in[k]}L^{\prime}_{i}.

So the track of a cycle is the homeomorphic image of the unit circle.

Societies.

Let
\Omega
be a cyclic permutation of the elements of some set which we denote by
V(\Omega).

A
society
is a pair
(G,\Omega),
where
G
is a graph and
\Omega
is a cyclic permutation with
V(\Omega)\subseteq V(G).

Renditions.

Let
(G,\Omega)
be a society, and let
\Delta
be a closed disk in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
A
rendition
of
(G,\Omega)
is a sphere decomposition
\textrho=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D})
of
G
such that, for each
D\in\mathcal{D}
,
D\subseteq\Delta
, and
\pi_{\textrho}(N(\textrho)\cap{\sf bd}(\Delta))=V(\Omega)
,
mapping one of the two cyclic orders (clockwise or counter-clockwise) of
{\sf bd}(\Delta)
to the order of
\Omega.

\textdelta
-aligned disks.

We say a closed disk
\textDelta
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is
\textdelta
-aligned
if
its boundary intersects
\textGamma
only in nodes of
\textdelta
.
We denote by
\textOmega_{\textDelta}
one of the cyclic orderings of the vertices on the boundary of
\textDelta.

We define the
inner graph
of a
\textdelta
-aligned closed disk
\textDelta
as

\text{$\mathsf{inner}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{\textrm{$c\in C(\textdelta)$ and $c\subseteq\textDelta$}}\sigma(c)

and the
outer graph of
\textDelta
as

\text{$\mathsf{outer}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{\textrm{$c\in C(\textdelta)$ and $c\cap\textDelta\subseteq{\sf ground}(\textdelta)$}}\sigma(c).

If
\textDelta
is
\textdelta
-aligned, we define
\textGamma\cap\textDelta
to be the drawing of
\text{$\mathsf{inner}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta)
in
\textDelta
which is the restriction of
\textGamma
in
\textDelta.

If moreover
|V(\textOmega_{\textDelta})|\geq 4,
we denote by
\textdelta[\textDelta]
the rendition
(\textGamma\cap\textDelta,\{\textDelta_{c}\in\mathcal{D}\mid c\subseteq\textDelta\})
of
(\text{$\mathsf{inner}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta),\textOmega_{\textDelta})
in
\textDelta.

Let
\textdelta=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D})
be a sphere decomposition of a graph
G
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Let
C
be a cycle in
G
that is grounded in
\textdelta,
such that the track
T
of
C
bounds a closed disk
\textDelta_{C}
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}.

We define the
outer
(resp.
inner
)
graph
of
C
in
\textdelta
as the graph
\text{$\mathsf{outer}$}_{\textdelta}(C):=\text{$\mathsf{outer}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta_{C})
(resp.
\text{$\mathsf{inner}$}_{\textdelta}(C):=\text{$\mathsf{inner}$}_{\textdelta}(\textDelta_{C})
).

4.2
Flat walls

We will need to use an appropriate version of the Flat Wall theorem
[
51
,
40
]
.
Let us first define walls, flat walls, and the relevant vocabulary.

Walls.

Let
k,r\in\mathbb{N}
. The

(k\times r)
-grid
, denoted by
\Gamma_{k,r}
, is the
graph whose vertex set is
[k]\times[r]
and two vertices
(i,j)
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if
|i-i^{\prime}|+|j-j^{\prime}|=1
.
An
elementary
r
-wall
(see
Figure 4
), for some odd integer
r\geq 3
, is the graph obtained from a

(2r\times r)
-grid
with vertices
(x,y)\in[2r]\times[r]
,
after the removal of the
“vertical” edges
\{(x,y),(x,y+1)\}
for odd
x+y
, and then the removal of
all vertices of degree one.
Notice that, as
r\geq 3
, an elementary
r
-wall is a planar graph
that has a unique (up to topological isomorphism) embedding in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that all its finite faces are incident to exactly six
edges.
The
perimeter
of an elementary
r
-wall is the cycle bounding its infinite face,
while the cycles bounding its finite faces are called
bricks
.
Also, the vertices
in the perimeter of an elementary
r
-wall that have degree two are called
pegs
,
while the vertices
(1,1),(2,r),(2r-1,1),(2r,r)
are called
corners
(notice that the corners are also pegs).

An
r
-wall
is any graph
W
obtained from an elementary
r
-wall
\bar{W}

after subdividing edges. A graph
W
is a
wall
if it is an
r
-wall for some odd
r\geq 3

and we refer to
r
as the
height
of
W
. Given a graph
G
,
a
wall of

G
is a subgraph of
G
that is a wall.
We always assume that for every
r
-wall, the number
r
is odd.

We call the vertices of degree three of a wall
W

branch vertices
.
We use
D(W)
to denote the perimeter of the wall
W
.

Tilts.

The
interior
of a wall
W
is the graph obtained from
W
if we remove from it all edges of
D(W)
and all vertices of
D(W)
that have degree two in
W
.
Given two walls
W
and
\tilde{W}
of a graph
G
, we say that
\tilde{W}
is a
tilt
of
W
if
\tilde{W}
and
W
have identical interiors.

Layers.

The
layers
of an
r
-wall
W
are recursively defined as follows.
The first layer of
W
is its perimeter.
For
i=2,\ldots,(r-1)/2
, the
i
-th layer of
W
is the
(i-1)
-th layer of the subwall
W^{\prime}

obtained from
W
after removing from
W
its perimeter and
removing recursively all occurring vertices of degree one.
We refer to the
(r-1)/2
-th layer as the
inner layer
of
W
.
The
central vertices
of an
r
-wall are its two branch vertices that do not belong to any of its layers and that are connected by a path of
W
that does not intersect any layer.

Central walls.

Given an
r
-wall
W
and an odd
q\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
where
q\leq r
, we define the
central
q
-subwall

of
W
, denoted by
W^{(q)}
, to be the
q
-wall obtained from
W
after removing its first
(r-q)/2
layers
and all occurring vertices of degree one.

Flat walls.

Let
G
be a graph and let
W
be an
r
-wall of
G
, for some odd integer
r\geq 3
.
We say that a pair
(P,C)\subseteq V(D(W))\times V(D(W))
is a
choice of pegs and corners for
W
if
W
is a subdivision of an elementary
r
-wall
\bar{W}
where
P
and
C
are the pegs and the corners of
\bar{W}
, respectively (clearly,
C\subseteq P
).

We say that
W
is a
flat
r
-wall

of
G
if there is a separation
(X,Y)
of
G
and a choice
(P,C)

of pegs and corners for
W
such that:

•

V(W)\subseteq Y
,

•

P\subseteq X\cap Y\subseteq V(D(W))
, and

•

if
\Omega
is the cyclic ordering of the vertices
X\cap Y
as they appear in
D(W)
,
then there exists a rendition
\rho
of
(G[Y],\Omega)
.

We say that
W
is a
flat wall

of
G
if it is a flat
r
-wall for some odd integer
r\geq 3
.

Flatness pairs.

Given the above, we say that the choice of the 5-tuple
\mathfrak{R}=(X,Y,P,C,\rho)

certifies that
W
is a flat wall of
G
.
We call the pair
(W,\mathfrak{R})
a
flatness pair
of
G
and define
the
height
of the pair
(W,\mathfrak{R})
to be the height of
W
.

We call the graph
G[Y]
the
\mathfrak{R}
-compass
of
W
in
G
,
denoted by
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
.
We can assume that
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
is connected, updating
\mathfrak{R}
by removing from
Y
the vertices of all the connected components of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)

except for the one that contains
W
and including them in
X
(
\Gamma
can also be easily modified according to the removal of the aforementioned vertices from
Y
).
We define the
flaps
of the wall

in
\mathfrak{R}
as

{\sf flaps}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W):=\{\sigma(c)\mid c\in C(\rho)\}
.

Cell classification.

Given a cycle
C
of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
, we say that

C
is
\mathfrak{R}
-normal
if it is
not
a subgraph of a flap
F\in{\sf flaps}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
.
Given an
\mathfrak{R}
-normal cycle
C
of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
,
we call a cell

of
\mathfrak{R}

C
-perimetric
if

\sigma(c)
contains some edge of
C
.
Notice that if
c
is
C
-perimetric, then
\pi_{\rho}(\tilde{c})
contains two points

such that
\pi_{\rho}(p)
and
\pi_{\rho}(q)
are vertices of

where one,
say
P_{c}^{\rm in}
, of the two
(\pi_{\rho}(p),\pi_{\rho}(q))
-subpaths of

is a subgraph of
\sigma(c)
and the other,
denoted by

,
(\pi_{\rho}(p),\pi_{\rho}(q))
-subpath contains at most one internal vertex of
\sigma(c)
,
which should be the (unique) vertex

in
\pi_{\rho}(\tilde{c})\setminus\{\pi_{\rho}(p),\pi_{\rho}(q)\}
.
We pick a
(p,q)
-arc
A_{c}
in

such that
\pi_{\rho}^{-1}(z)\in A_{c}
if and only if
P_{c}^{\rm in}
contains
the vertex

as an internal vertex.

We consider the circle
K_{C}=\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\bigcup}}\{A_{c}\mid\mbox{$c$ is a $C$-perimetric cell of $\mathfrak{R}$}\}

and we denote by
\Delta_{C}
the closed disk bounded by

that is contained in
\Delta
.
A cell
c
of
\mathfrak{R}
is called
C
-internal
if

and is called
C
-external
if
\Delta_{C}\cap c=\emptyset
.
Notice that the cells of
\mathfrak{R}
are partitioned into
C
-internal,
C
-perimetric, and
C
-external cells.

Influence.

For every
\mathfrak{R}
-normal cycle
C
of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
, we define the set

{\sf influence}_{\mathfrak{R}}(C)=\{\sigma(c)\mid\mbox{$c$ is a cell of $\mathfrak{R}$ that is not $C$-external}\}
.

A wall
W^{\prime}
of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
is
\mathfrak{R}
-normal
if

is
\mathfrak{R}
-normal.
Notice that every wall of
W
(and hence every subwall of
W
) is an
\mathfrak{R}
-normal wall of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
. We denote by
{\cal S}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
the set of all
\mathfrak{R}
-normal walls of
{\sf compass}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
. Given a wall
W^{\prime}\in{\cal S}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W)
and a cell
c
of
\mathfrak{R}
,
we say that
c
is
W^{\prime}
-perimetric/internal/external
if
c
is
D(W^{\prime})
-perimetric/internal/external, respectively.
We also use
K_{W^{\prime}}
,
\Delta_{W^{\prime}}
,
{\sf influence}_{\mathfrak{R}}(W^{\prime})
as shortcuts
for
K_{D(W^{\prime})}
,
\Delta_{D(W^{\prime})}
,
{\sf influence}_{\mathfrak{R}}(D(W^{\prime}))
, respectively.

Tilts of flatness pairs.

Let

4.3

-Planarity

In this section, we show that apex grids are obstructions to the existence of planar

-modulators.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an arbitrary graph class and let

be a positive integer. Then any graph

containing the apex grid
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}

-modulator.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the class of all graphs. Because any graph class

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}

.

Claim 1
.

The apex grid
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}
does not admit a planar
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}
-modulator.

Proof of the claim.
The proof is by contradiction. Assume that
X\subseteq V(\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+})
is a planar
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}
-modulator.
Because
|V(\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+})|>k+5
, it implies that
X\neq\emptyset
, and because
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}
is not planar as
k^{\prime}\geq 3
, it implies that
V(\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+})\setminus X\neq\emptyset
.
Let
v
be the apex of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}
.
Denote by
B
the vertices of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}
of degree at most four,
and set
S:=V(\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+})\setminus N_{\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}}(B)
, that is

is the set of vertices of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}-v
that do not belong to the two outermost cycles of the grid.
It is straightforward to verify that for any two distinct nonadjacent vertices
x,y\in S
,
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}
has five internally vertex disjoint
x
-
y
-paths:
four paths in
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}-v
and one path with the middle vertex
v
.
Therefore, for any separation
(L,R)
of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}
of order at most four with

and

, either

or
S\subseteq R
.
Furthermore, because

is universal,
v\in L\cap R
.
For each connected component
C
of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}-X
,
(N_{\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}}[V(C)],V(G)\setminus V(C))
is a separation of order at most four.
This implies that either
S\subseteq N_{\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}}[V(C)]
for a connected component
C
of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}-X
or

.
However, because
|S|\geq k+4
and

contradicting that each connected component of
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}-X
has at most
k

Thus,
S\subseteq X
.
We also have that

.
Then because

,
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}[S]
contains

as a subgraph and, therefore,

This contradicts that the torso of

is planar and proves the claim.

\lhd

Given that
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}
is a minor-closed graph class, so is the class of
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}
-planar graphs.
Therefore, for any graph

containing
\Gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{+}

is not a
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}
-planar graph by

∎

Ground-maximal sphere decompositions.

Let

We say that a cell

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hereditary graph class and

be a graph.
Let

be a sphere decomposition of

-compatible.

-compatible.

Proof.

Let
\delta^{\prime}=(\Gamma^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
be a sphere decomposition that is more grounded than
\delta=(\Gamma,\mathcal{D})
.
Let

. Let us show that

is
\mathcal{H}
-compatible.
Given that
\delta^{\prime}
is more grounded than

, there is a cell

.

Let

.
Given that

is
\mathcal{H}
-compatible, there is a set

containing

such that
{\sf torso}(\sigma(c),S_{c})
has a planar embedding with the vertices of
\pi_{\delta}(\tilde{c})
on the outer face and such that, for each

Let

By heredity of

Additionally, we have

Quasi-4-connectivity.

Given

, a graph

is
k
-connected if for all separation
(L,R)
of order at most
k-1
of

L\subseteq R
or
R\subseteq L
.
A graph

is
quasi-4-connected
if it is 3-connected and that for all separation
(L,R)
of order three of
G
, either
|L\setminus R|\leq 1
or
|R\setminus L|\leq 1
.

Adhesions.

Let
(T,\beta)
be a tree decomposition.
The
adhesion
of two nodes

, denoted by

\beta(t)\cap\beta(t^{\prime})
.
The
adhesion
of
\mathcal{T}
is the maximum size of an adhesion over all pairs of adjacent nodes of

.

This section contains several algorithmic applications of

To combine nice algorithmic properties of planar graphs and graphs from
\mathcal{H}
,
we need a variant of
Theorem 1
that guarantees the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm computing a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator in a
\mathcal{H}
-planar graph. (Let us remind that

Theorem 1
only guarantees the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm deciding whether an input graph is
\mathcal{H}
-planar.) Thus, we start with the proof of the following theorem. The proof of the theorem combines self-reduction arguments with the hereditary properties of
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hereditary, CMSO-definable, and polynomial-time decidable graph class.
Then there exists a polynomial-time algorithm constructing for a given
\mathcal{H}
-planar graph
G
a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{H}
is trivial in the sense that
\mathcal{H}
includes every graph, then we choose a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator
X=\emptyset
of
G
. Otherwise, because
\mathcal{H}
is hereditary, there is a graph
F
of the minimum size such that
F\notin\mathcal{H}
. We say that
F
is a
minimum forbidden subgraph
. Because the inclusion in
\mathcal{H}
is decidable,
F
can be found in constant time by brute force checking all graphs of size
1,2,\ldots
where the constant depends on
\mathcal{H}
.

We consider two cases depending on whether
F
is connected or not.

Assume that
F
is connected. We pick an arbitrary vertex
r\in V(F)
and declare it to be the
root
of
F
. We construct five copies
F_{1},\ldots,F_{5}
of
F
rooted in
r_{1},\ldots,r_{5}
, respectively.
Then we define
F^{\prime}
to be the graph obtained from
F_{1},\ldots,F_{5}
by identifying their root into a single vertex
r
which is defined to be the root of
F^{\prime}
. Our self-reduction arguments are based on the following claim.

Claim 18
.

Let
v
be a vertex of a graph
G
. Then

\mathcal{H}
-modulator

with

obtained from

and

by identifying

and the root of

\mathcal{H}
-modulator.

Proof of the claim.
Suppose that

is a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator of

with

. Then each connected component of
G_{v}-X
is either a connected component of
G-X
or a connected component of one of the graphs
F_{1}-r_{1},\ldots,F_{5}-r_{5}
.
Because
F_{i}-r_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
for

, we have that each connected component of
G_{v}-X
is in
\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, the torsos of
X
with respect to
G
and
G_{v}
are the same because the root

of

is the unique vertex of

in

is a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator of

G_{v}
has a planar

-modulator

. We show that
r=v\in X

and

and

\lhd

\mathcal{H}
-planarity

Our second example is an extension of the celebrated FKT algorithm

7.3
EPTAS for Independent Set

Baker’s technique
[
4
]
provides PTAS and EPTAS for many optimization problems on planar graphs. This method is based on the fact that planar graphs have bounded
local treewidth
. In particular, for every planar graph
G
and every vertex
v\in V(G)
,
{\sf tw}(N_{G}^{r}[v])=\mathcal{O}(r)
, where
N_{G}^{r}[v]
is the
closed
r
-neighborhood
of
v
, that is, the set of vertices at distance at most
r
from
v

(see
[
49
]
).

Given that the torso of a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator
X
is planar, we extend Baker’s technique to
\mathcal{H}
-planar graphs, by replacing the role of treewidth with
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth
.

Definition 2

(
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth.)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a graph class and
G
be a graph.
A
tree
\mathcal{H}
-decomposition
of
G
is a triple
\mathcal{T}=(T,\beta,L)
where
(T,\beta)
is a tree decomposition of
{\sf torso}(G,X)
for some set
X\subseteq V(G)
such that each connected component in
L:={\sf cc}(G-X)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
.
The width of
\mathcal{T}
is the width of
(T,\beta)
.
The
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth of
G
is the minimum width of a tree
\mathcal{H}
-decomposition of
G
,
or,
in other words, the
minimum treewidth of the torso of a set
X\subseteq V(G)
such that each component of
G-X
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
.

As mentioned in the introduction,
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth has been defined by Eiben, Ganian, Hamm, and Kwon
[
18
]
. Its algorithmic properties have been studied
in
[
18
,
37
,
1
]
.

Let us explain how the EPTAS works on
Independent Set
.
The problem of
Independent Set
asks for an
independent set
of maximum size, that is a set
S
such that no edge of
G
has both endpoints in
S
.
The following is proved in
[
37
, Theorem 3.16]
(it is proven for the dual problem of
Vertex Cover
).

Proposition 11

(​​
[
37
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hereditary graph class on which
Independent Set
is polynomial-time solvable.
Then
Independent Set
can be solved in time
2^{k}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
when given a tree
\mathcal{H}
-decomposition of width at most
k-1
consisting of

nodes.

With
Theorem

8
on hands, the proof of the following theorem is almost identical to Baker’s style algorithms for planar graphs. The main observation is that the supergraph of an input
\mathcal{H}
-planar graph
G
obtained by adding to

all edges of the torso of its planar modulator, is of bounded local
\mathcal{H}
-treewidth.

See
5

Proof.

Let
G
be an
\mathcal{H}
-planar graph. We assume without loss of generality that
G
is a connected graph and
\varepsilon<1
.
By
Theorem

8
, there is a polynomial-time algorithm computing a planar
\mathcal{H}
-modulator

of
G
.
Let
v
be an arbitrary vertex in
X
.
For

, let

denote the set of vertices of

in
{\sf torso}(G,X)

Observe that the endpoints of an edge in

Given that, for

Let

For

Let

Therefore,

to find in time

of

.
Note that

.
We return as a solution the maximum size set

for

.

be a maximum independent set of

, of size

.
For

, let
\mathcal{D}_{i}:={\sf cc}(G-X)\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}

Observation 3

Let

-connected graph for an integer

\mathcal{H}
, a graph

if and only if the
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth of

Suppose that there is a set

of size at most

such that

. Because
|X|\leq 4k
,
G-X
is connected.
Also, since
|X|\leq 4k

\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{s}\}
of

such that

and
|X_{i}|\leq 4
for each

{\sf torso}(G_{i},X_{i})
, where

is planar. Then we decompose

by consecutively selecting

.

For the opposite direction, we use induction on
k
. We prove that if the
\mathcal{H}
-planar treedepth of a graph

is at most

then
a graph
G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
can be obtained by deleting at most
4k

as the deletion distance to
\mathcal{H}
is

. In particular, this proves the claim for

Conclusion

All our techniques heavily rely on the planarity of the torso of
X
.
In particular, this concerns our method for finding
an irrelevant vertex inside a flat wall no matter how the graph outside the modulator interacts with this flat wall. For this, we present our techniques for the
\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
problem and
later, in
section

5
and
section

6
we explain how these techniques can be extended
on graphs where
\mathcal{H}
-
ptd
or
\mathcal{H}
-

is bounded.
However,

and

are not the only minor-monotone parameters to plug in
eq.

1
, further than

{\sf td}
and
{\sf tw}
. The most general question one may ask in this direction is the following.

Given some target class
\mathcal{H}
and some minor-monotone parameter
{\sf p}
, which conditions on
\mathcal{H}
may imply an
FPT
-algorithm deciding
whether
\mathcal{H}
-
{\sf p}(G)\leq k
?

We conjecture that the conditions of
theorem

2
can be copied for
every
minor-monotone parameter
{\sf p}
, in other words we conjecture the following:
If
\mathcal{H}
is hereditary, CMSO-definable, and union-closed then an
FPT
-algorithm for checking
\mathcal{H}
-
{\sf size}(G)\leq k

implies an
FPT
-algorithm for checking
\mathcal{H}
-
{\sf p}(G)\leq k
, no matter the choice of
{\sf p}
.

The main obstacle for applying our technique to this general problem is that now the interaction of the modulator with the rest of the graph is not bounded by a fixed constant (that in our case is
\leq 4
for
\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
) but is bounded by some function of the parameter
k
. We believe that our techniques are a promising departure point for resolving this problem. However, this appears to be a quite challenging task.

Another open problem stems from the condition in
theorem

1
that
\mathcal{H}
is CMSO-definable. Clearly, we cannot drop the polynomial-time decidability condition for
\mathcal{H}
if we aim to obtain a polynomial-time algorithm for
\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
. Also we proved in
theorem

11
that the heredity condition is also crucial. However, the CMSO-definability condition is needed because we are using the meta-theorem of
[
46
]
. This meta-theorem is a powerful tool allowing us to reduce solving
\mathcal{H}
-Planarity
to solving
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
-Planarity
