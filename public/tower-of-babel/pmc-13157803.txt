Magnetic resonance imaging of hand involvement in polymyalgia rheumatica: a comparison with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Martin Fruth, Greta Kelmendi-Starova, Philipp Martin-Seidel, Philipp Sewerin, Lars Schimmöller, Xenofon Baraliakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvolvement of hands is a frequent finding in polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) and can lead to misclassification as late-onset rheumatoid arthritis (RA).ObjectiveTo compare the inflammatory pattern as shown by contrast-enhanced MRI (ceMRI) of hand involvement in PMR to seropositive RA (RA+).MethodsMRIs of patients diagnosed with PMR and clinically suspected hand involvement and with RA+, matched for age/sex, were analysed retrospectively. Synovitis, osteitis and erosions of metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints 2–5 plus tenosynovitis of flexor/extensor compartments were evaluated using the Rheumatoid Arthritis MRI Score. For assessment of periarticular inflammation around MCP joints, a semi-quantitative scoring was introduced.ResultsDistribution of synovitis scores differed significantly between the groups with mostly minor and occasionally moderate synovitis in PMR versus uniformly distributed total scores in RA+. Scores of periarticular inflammation were significantly higher in PMR. Tenosynovitis of extensors and flexors was prevalent in both groups, but flexor tenosynovitis was significantly more prevalent in PMR. Detection of erosions and osteitis was absent in PMR cases but prevalent in RA+ (all p<0.001). A combination of minor to moderate synovitis together with severe periarticular inflammation of an MCP joint represented a characteristic finding in PMR and, if evident in at least one MCP joint, differentiated the groups with a sensitivity and specificity of 78.4% and 78.4%, respectively. Specificity improved to 90.2% at the expense of sensitivity (70.6%) when this characteristic inflammatory pattern was present in more than one MCP joint per hand.ConclusionHand involvement in PMR could be clearly differentiated in ceMRI from RA+ by the intensity of periarticular inflammation of MCP joints.Keywords: Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Inflammation, Rheumatoid Arthritis, Polymyalgia Rheumatica

Introduction

Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is a common rheumatic condition of the elderly.1 Myalgic pain with proximal involvement of the shoulder and pelvic girdle, accompanied by increased inflammatory markers, is the main clinical symptom. However, in up to 50% of cases, there are also peripheral manifestations, mostly affecting the hands or knees.2 3 This can lead to misclassification of such cases as late-onset rheumatoid arthritis and ultimately to inadequate treatment regimes.

Recently, advances in [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG-PET/CT) and MRI have identified inflammation of the myotendinous unit as the hallmark in PMR.4 While the panoramic imaging capability of FDG-PET/CT outstandingly displays the stereotypic multilocular symmetric inflammation of shoulder and pelvic girdle, MRI allows a closer inspection of local pathomorphology, showing that the peritendineum, perimysium and the fibrous layer of joint capsules are the targets of inflammation in PMR.5,8 These characteristic inflammatory findings in the shoulder and pelvic girdle are highly diagnostic and have the potential to both confirm or rule out suspected clinical diagnoses. While these methods have demonstrated dominant non-synovial inflammation as the hallmark of PMR, the presence of non-synovial inflammation at peripheral sites has not yet been explored. Knowledge on this topic could allow for a distinction to be made against synovitis-centred RA.

Marzo-Ortega et al already identified significantly more extracapsular contrast enhancement around metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints in patients suffering from PMR as compared with RA, using dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI. While periarticular inflammation around MCP joints differed significantly between the groups, prevalence of articular and tenovaginal synovitis as well as osteitis and erosions did not.9 The authors therefore suggested a different anatomical basis for joint inflammation in PMR compared with RA.

Another frequently reported feature of hand involvement in PMR is tenosynovitis of flexor and extensor tendon sheaths. Owen et al highlighted tenosynovial inflammation of flexors at wrist level, while Cimmino et al described tenosynovitis as a frequent finding in PMR with a predilection for extensor tenosynovial inflammation.10 11

Based on our experience with MRI in PMR, it is also our impression that non-synovial inflammatory findings predominate within the inflammatory pattern observed in involvement of the hand, particularly in the MCP joints (figure 1). Tenosynovial inflammation, as well as peritendinitis of tendons without a synovial tendon sheath, is another frequent finding observed in PMR with hand involvement.

The objective of this study was to identify differences in the inflammatory pattern shown by contrast-enhanced MRI (ceMRI) in hands of patients with peripheral involvement of PMR compared with patients with seropositive RA (RA+).

Patients and methods

Study population and data collection

This is a retrospective study comparing imaging findings in hand ceMRI of consecutive cases suffering from PMR with clinically suspected hand involvement (PMR group) with findings in matched cases with RA+ group. All patients had undergone a ceMRI of the hand in our tertiary rheumatology university centre on indication by the treating rheumatologist for evaluation of inflammatory findings due to clinical evidence of inflammatory involvement. The hand chosen for MRI examination was identified by the treating rheumatologist on clinical consideration. All cases were examined in the period from January 2018 to May 2024.

In patients with PMR, active pelvic girdle inflammation due to PMR also had to be present at the same time. A PMR-compatible pelvic pattern was defined as bilateral peritendinitis of at least four tendons in the pelvic girdle including the rectus femoris or adductor longus origins. Cases with examinations overlayed by artefacts due to motion, insufficient fat saturation or metal-related as well as contrast-free examinations were excluded.

All eligible cases in the PMR group were matched for age and sex with cases of RA+, who had undergone a ceMRI of a hand within the same timeframe in our institution. Similar to the PMR group, to be included in the RA+ group, there had to be clinical evidence of arthritic activity in the examined hand. Cases with scans degraded by artefacts or with contrast-free scans were excluded. If more than one RA+ case was eligible for matching with its PMR counterpart, the one with the shortest time since diagnosis was chosen.

Clinical, laboratory and demographic information from the time point of the MRI examination were available from all patients. The cases were classified into groups based on the expert rheumatologist’s diagnosis. Flow chart of patient recruitment and case selection is shown in figure 2.

Magnetic resonance imaging

All investigated MRI scans were performed using a 1.5 T MRI scanner (Siemens AERA) with a dedicated 16-channel hand coil covering the hand from the fingertips to the distal forearm. The clinical routine protocol consisted of six sequences and the administration of the contrast agent in a constant order: coronal T1-weighted (T1w) turbo spin echo (TSE) 2.5 mm/20% (slice thickness/gap), transversal T1w TSE 3 mm/20%, transversal T2w turbo inversion recovery magnitude (TIRM) 3 mm/20%; weight-adapted bolus of gadoteric acid followed by a pusher bolus of 20 mL physiological saline solution, both at an injection rate of 1 mL/s; coronal proton densitiy-weighted (PDw) TSE with dixon fat saturation 2.5 mm/20%, transversal contrast-enhanced T1w TSE with dixon fat saturation 3 mm/20% and T1w isotropic volume interpolated breath hold examination 0.5 mm. Written informed consent for the ceMRI was provided in all cases. The pelvic girdle ceMRI in the PMR group followed a routine clinical protocol; sequence parameters for pelvic girdle MRI have been published previously.12 The combined MRI scans in the PMR group were performed in the following sequence: contrast-free sequences of the pelvic girdle in a supine position; hand scan including contrast-enhanced sequences in ‘superman’ position, completion of contrast-enhanced sequences of the pelvic girdle in supine position.

Investigated sites and image analyses

Based on our clinical experience with hand involvement in PMR and more recent findings,9 we focused on inflammatory findings in the MCP joints 2–5. Additionally, tenosynovitis of flexor tendons at the wrist and MCP level, as well as extensor tenosynovitis at the wrist level and non-synovial peritendinous inflammation of the flexor carpi ulnaris tendon (FCU), were assessed. Further investigation of synovial and non-synovial inflammation at the carpal and proximal interphalangeal levels was not performed.

MCP level

Articular synovitis, osteitis and erosive changes in MCP joints 2–5 were evaluated using the Rheumatoid Arthritis MRI Score (RAMRIS).13 14 The sum of synovitis scores for MCP joints 2–5 was calculated to provide an overall measure of articular synovitis burden in each case. Additionally, the number of inflamed MCP joints per case was calculated.

For the assessment of periarticular non-synovial inflammation, a semi-quantitative scoring system was introduced. The circumference of the MCP joint was assumed to be circular and divided into four equal 90° sectors on the transversal contrast-enhanced T1w images. The dividing lines of the sectors were rotated by 45° relative to a virtual line passing through the centres of the extensor and flexor tendons (figure 3). Each sector therefore contained an anatomically distinct part of the capsuloligamentous complex of the MCP joint. Sector 1 covered the extensor tendon and part of the sagittal ligament on each side; sector 2 covered the sagittal ligament with interwoven interosseous tendons on the ulnar side of the MCP joint; sector 3 covered the flexor tendon complex, including the lumbrical muscle and sector 4, similar to sector 2, covered the sagittal ligament including interosseous tendons on the radial side. Three consecutive transverse slices were investigated; the middle slice was defined as the one passing through the middle of the joint space or the one best fitting as middle of joint space. This enabled investigation of a segment of approximately 10 mm proximodistal length of each MCP joint using reproducible anatomical landmarks. The presence or absence of periarticular inflammation in each sector and slice was assessed. To be rated inflamed, there had to be contrast enhancement around the outer margin of the capsuloligamentous complex in >50% (ie, 45°) of the sector. Hence, assessment of each MCP joint consisted of four sectors in three consecutive transverse slices, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 12 per MCP. Scores were grouped into four categories of periarticular inflammation for each MCP: none (score 0), minor (score 1–4), moderate (score 5–8), severe (score 9–12).

Tenosynovitis

Extensor tenosynovitis as well as flexor tenosynovitis at wrist and MCP level were scored using the RAMRIS tenosynovitis scoring system.13 The resulting scores, 0–3, were aggregated as a sum score for all extensor compartments and for all flexors at wrist together with all flexors at MCP level and served as a metric to evaluate the tenosynovitic burden of extensors and flexors in each case. Furthermore, the number of tendon compartments affected by tenosynovitis of any score was calculated for every case. Predominance of extensor or flexor tenosynovitis per hand was evaluated by calculating the proportion of tenosynovitic burden of affected compartments of the seven flexors at the ratio of six extensor compartments. A ratio of 7/6 was considered a balanced tenosynovitic affection, a ratio of <7/6 indicated predominant extensor affection and a ratio of >7/6 indicated predominant flexor tenosynovitis.

Additionally, peritendinous inflammation of the FCU tendon was evaluated. In contrast to the other flexor tendons, the FCU tendon is not covered by a synovial tendon sheath but rather by loose connective tissue; thus, inflammation of this tendon represents non-synovial peritendinitis. Its portion near the pisiform bone was evaluated in a binary manner, inflammation was defined as circumferential peritendinous contrast enhancement in two consecutive slices.

Scoring was performed by two experienced radiologists (>10 years of experience in rheumatological imaging) blinded to clinical and demographic information in all cases in a consensus reading.

Statistical analyses

Clinical and demographic data for the PMR and RA+ group were analysed in a descriptive manner. Continuous variables are reported as arithmetic mean and SD and compared using the t-test. Ordinal data are reported as median and IQR and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as percentage and compared using the χ2 test. The discriminatory effect of imaging features was calculated using sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JASP (V.0.19.3).

Results

Patients

A total of 189 cases had undergone a combined ceMRI of the pelvic girdle and one hand in the aforementioned period. Of these, a total of 89 cases were diagnosed with PMR. Exclusion of non-eligible cases resulted in 51 cases for comparison with cases diagnosed with RA+ (figure 2). Data for rheumatoid factor (RF) and anticitrullinated peptide antibody (ACPA) were missing in one case in the PMR group. In the RA+ group, information on erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was missing in one case and time since diagnosis in two cases. One MCP 2 joint in the PMR group was excluded from evaluation due to a local metal-related artefact.

Mean levels of inflammatory markers, CRP and ESR, were lower in the PMR group, but not significantly (p=0.547 and p=0.246, respectively). RF and ACPA differed significantly between the groups: all cases in the RA+ group were RF-positive and only one case was ACPA-negative. In the PMR group, five cases were RF-positive, not exceeding 66 IU/mL, and two cases were ACPA-positive, not exceeding 52 IU/mL. No case in the PMR group was positive for both RF and ACPA. The number of patients who received glucocorticoid (GC) therapy due to high disease activity within 7 days before the hand MRI scan was significantly higher in the RA+ group (24/51, 47.0%) than in the PMR group (13/51, 25.5%; p=0.023) (table 1).

MRI-based findings

All 51 cases in the PMR group showed inflammatory activity in terms of tenosynovitis or arthritis of an MCP, whereas one case in the RA+ group showed no inflammatory activity at the investigated sites. A total of 203 and 204 MCP joints were evaluated in the PMR and RA+ groups, respectively.

MCP—articular synovitis

Articular synovitis of MCP joints was a frequent finding in both groups. Significantly more MCP joints per hand were affected by synovitis in the PMR group, whereas synovitis scores per individual MCP joint were significantly higher in the RA+ group. The sum of all MCP synovitis scores, as a measure of MCP synovitis burden per hand, was similar between the groups (table 2). Ιn the PMR group, MCP joints exhibited predominantly minor and moderate synovitis, whereas in the RA+ group, synovitis scores were more uniformly distributed (figure 4).

MCP—periarticular inflammation

Overall, periarticular inflammation scores for each evaluated MCP joint were significantly higher in the PMR group (table 2). In the PMR group, severe periarticular inflammation (score 9–12) was present in the majority of MCP joints, and only a few displayed no periarticular inflammation, whereas in the RA+ group, no or only minor periarticular inflammation (score 0–3) was most prevalent (figure 4).

In PMR, most MCP joints featured high periarticular inflammation scores, reflecting an involvement of the entire capsuloligamentous circumference across consecutive slices. In contrast, in RA+ cases, only the periarticular sector S1 around the extensor tendon of MCP joints 2 and 3 was moderately affected (figure 5).

Tenosynovitis

Detection of extensor tenosynovitis was similar between the PMR and RA+ groups, both in terms of the number of affected compartments and the tenosynovitic burden per hand. Flexor tenosynovitis was significantly more prevalent in PMR cases, both in terms of overall tenosynovitic burden and number of affected compartments. In the PMR group, significantly more cases showed predominant flexor tenosynovitis compared with RA+ group (table 2).

Peritendinitis of the FCU, a non-synovial inflammatory feature, was significantly more prevalent in the PMR group (table 2).

Osteitis in MCP joints

In both groups, the majority of bones showed no osteitis. In the PMR group, osteitis was significantly less prevalent compared with the RA+ group (table 2). All affected bones in the PMR group showed minor osteitis (score 1). In the RA+ group, severe osteitis was present in 13/408 (3.2%), moderate osteitis in 22/408 (5.4%) and minor osteitis in 81/408 (19.9%) of the evaluated bones.

Erosive damage in MCP joints

Similar to the prevalence of osteitis, erosive changes were significantly less prevalent in the PMR group compared with the RA+ group (table 2). All three affected bones in the PMR group showed only minimal erosive changes (score 1).

MCP—combination of articular synovitis and periarticular inflammation

Based on the different distribution of synovitis and periarticular inflammation in the PMR and RA+ groups, we defined the presence of minor to moderate synovitis (RAMRIS synovitis scores 1–2) together with severe periarticular inflammation (periarticular inflammation scores 9–12) as a characteristic feature for MCP inflammation in PMR. According to this definition, significantly more MCP joints showed this characteristic pattern in the PMR group (116/203, 57.1%) than in the RA+ group (16/204, 7.8%; p<0.001). At least one MCP joint with this characteristic pattern was found in 40/51 (78.4%) cases in the PMR group and in only 11/51 (21.6%) cases in the RA+ group (p<0.001). Based on this feature alone, the sensitivity and specificity for differentiation between the two groups were 78.4% and 78.4%, respectively, with an NPV and PPV of 78.4% and 78.4%, respectively. A higher threshold of at least two MCP joints with this characteristic inflammatory pattern improved specificity and PPV to 90.2% and 87.8%, respectively, at the expense of reduced sensitivity and NPV to 70.6% and 75.4%, respectively.

Discussion

This is the first study to analyse the similarities and differences between patients with PMR and RA+. We report that the distinction between the two diseases is attributable to the existence of non-synovial periarticular inflammation around the MCP joints. This finding was predominant in PMR, usually affecting the entire circumference, including the small interosseous tendons. Synovial inflammation of these MCP joints was usually minor to moderate in extent. Conversely, synovial inflammation was predominant in RA, being more often moderate to severe in extent, whereas periarticular non-synovial inflammation was significantly less common and, when present, was usually localised to mechanically stressed regions, such as the extensor tendon hood. This finding is consistent with those reported by Marzo-Ortega et al and McGonagle et al,9 14 who demonstrated significantly more extracapsular enhancement and periarticular oedema, respectively, in PMR compared with RA cases, on MRI examinations of the hands. They already postulated a different anatomic basis for joint disease in PMR compared with RA, namely a capsular-based inflammation.

At the pathomorphological level, the pattern of inflammation we observed around the MCP joints consisted of inflammation around the capsuloligamentous envelope of each MCP. At this level, the ligamentous and tendinous structures lack a synovial layer. Consequently, their involvement is indicative of a non-synovial inflammation and aligns with findings described in recent studies on the shoulder and pelvic girdle in PMR. In these regions, MRI-based and FDG-PET/CT-based imaging studies have already demonstrated that non-synovial inflammatory lesions predominate and define the pattern of inflammation in PMR.5 6 10 15 16 In MRI-based studies from our own group, we demonstrated that capsulitis and peritendinitis constitute the predominant pattern of inflammation in the pelvic and shoulder girdle in PMR and that this distinguishes it from other rheumatic diseases, particularly from synovitis-centred conditions, such as RA.5 6 Ultimately, the pattern of non-synovial inflammation observed around the MCP joints is identical to periarticular and peritendinous inflammations encountered in the pelvic and shoulder girdle but scaled down to the small capsuloligamentous complex of an MCP joint.

Interestingly, the presence of non-synovial interosseous tendon inflammation (ITI) around MCP joints has been described recently as an early feature in the RA disease continuum. Mankia et al reported ITI as a frequent finding in RA, both in early and established cases with similar prevalences in ACPA-positive and ACPA-negative cases of early RA.17 These findings were confirmed in a recent study of cases with early arthritis by van Dijk et al.18 Although we did not focus on ITI but rather on the overall circumferential periarticular inflammation of MCP joints in our study, it can be assumed that, in the lateral sectors of our scoring system, ITI contributed to positive scoring results in RA+ cases. Nevertheless, the extent of periarticular inflammation as demonstrated here predominates in PMR cases with hand involvement.

Different from the findings of Marzo-Ortega et al,9 erosive changes and osteitis were not frequent in our group of PMR cases with hand involvement. In that publication, the authors reported MRI-determined erosions and bone oedema being equally common in hand involvement in PMR and RA. The minor erosive changes and osteitis detected in a few cases in the PMR group are comparable with the expected prevalence of MRI-determined findings in symptom-free individuals from the general population in this age group.19 Hence, our data support the general assumption that PMR is a non-erosive disease and, conversely, the detection of erosive changes should put a diagnosis of PMR into question.

As demonstrated in earlier research by Cimmino et al and Owen et al, tenosynovitis in hand involvement in PMR was also prevalent in our study.10 11 In all PMR cases, tenosynovitis was detected in the flexors or extensors to a certain extent. However, given that tenosynovitis was also a common finding in nearly all RA+ cases, its value for distinguishing between the two conditions was deemed to be inadequate.

In the clinical setting, ultrasound (US) of the shoulder and pelvic girdle is the imaging modality of choice to support diagnostic decision-making in PMR, and its use in the hands for evaluation of distal involvement in PMR is therefore obvious. Detection of articular synovitis, tenosynovitis, erosive bony changes and crystal deposits is a core competence of US and allows for an objective assessment of inflammation and differential diagnostic consideration. For the detection of non-synovial inflammatory findings, which crystallise as the defining pathology in PMR,4 ceMRI seems to be superior to US at the moment. However, the ability of US to detect non-synovial inflammation in larger joints has been shown in a recent study. In that work, US-based detection of tendinitis and ligament inflammation as well as tenosynovitis discriminated better between a group of definite PMR cases and groups of PMR mimics and RA than the detection of synovitis or bursitis.20 To date, there are no published findings on US-based detection of non-synovial inflammation around small joints, such as the MCP joints, in distal involvement of PMR.

For clinical practice, our study outlines the additional value of sophisticated hand imaging in assessment of patients suffering from PMR since ceMRI strips down the different synovial and non-synovial inflammatory findings and provides supportive and reassuring features for classification as hand involvement in PMR. But it is important to stress that periarticular inflammation particularly around MCP joints is not an exclusive feature of hand involvement in PMR. Periarticular non-synovial inflammation, interpreted as inflammation of the synovio-enthesial complex, is also common in psoriatic arthritis (PsA). At the volar side of MCP joints, the annular ligaments (A1 and A2 pulley) are typical sites for enthesial-related periarticular inflammation in PsA, while on the dorsal side peritendinitis of the extensor tendon represents functional enthesitis.21,23 Even ITI was found in 14% of hand MRI scans of patients suffering from PsA in a large early arthritis cohort.24 Although there is only little likelihood of confusion between PsA and hand involvement in PMR on clinical grounds, it is imperative for all engaged in interpretation of hand MRI to be aware of these similarities.

Our study has some limitations. The requirement of a PMR-compatible inflammatory pattern of the pelvic girdle for inclusion in the PMR group may be considered both a limitation and a strength. On the one hand, we excluded cases that were diagnosed solely on clinical grounds. On the other hand, by tightening the inclusion criteria, we probably avoided inclusion of conditions that mimic hand involvement in PMR and thereby harmonised the group. Furthermore, we introduced a new scoring system for the evaluation of periarticular inflammation, which lacked, so far, profound validation. In addition, there was a disparity between the groups in terms of established diagnoses versus new diagnoses. However, we assume that the duration of the disease did not change the pattern of acute inflammation, so that these characteristics are still comparable, regardless of whether it is the first manifestation or a flare of an established disease. Lastly, the reduction of our evaluation to MCP joints and tenosynovitis is a simplification of what we consider to be a complex pattern of inflammation in hand involvement in PMR. From our experience, arthritis of the carpus and proximal interphalangeal joints as well as non-synovial myotendinous inflammation of the hand muscles are also common in hand involvement in PMR, but we considered their discriminatory potential to be lower than that of the MCP joints. Still, these features also exist and offer potential for future investigations.

Conclusion

The presence of extensive non-synovial periarticular inflammation of MCP joints together with disproportional minor synovitis represented the outstanding difference in the inflammatory pattern of hand involvement in PMR compared with RA+. Erosive damage and pre-erosive osteitis were virtually absent in PMR, and their detection should put a presumption of PMR into question. Extensive tenosynovitis of extensor and flexor compartments could be expected in both conditions, with a trend towards predominant flexor tenosynovitis in PMR.
