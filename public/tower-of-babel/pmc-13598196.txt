Patagonian Ice Sheet discharge enhanced by AMOC slowdown through thermal bipolar seesaw
Authors: Takuto Kasuya, Yuta Kuniyoshi, Kana Nagashima, Hitoshi Hasegawa, Ayako Abe-Ouchi, Julia R. Hagemann, Helge W. Arz, Carina B. Lange, Frank Lamy, Shinya Iwasaki, Wing-Le Chan, Naomi Harada, Masafumi Murayama, Fuyuki Saito, Yusuke Okazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), a major regulator of global climate, is weakening under ongoing warming, yet its far-field impacts remain uncertain. Past AMOC slowdown events during the last glacial period provide insights into how such changes propagate beyond the North Atlantic. By combining new marine sediment records from the South Pacific off Chile with long-term climate model simulations, we reveal that AMOC slowdowns enhanced precipitation along the Chilean margin by intensifying the Southern Hemisphere Westerly Winds and warming the South Pacific. These changes promoted sediment and meltwater discharge from the Patagonian Ice Sheet and may have strengthened Southern Ocean ventilation. Our findings demonstrate that AMOC weakening can trigger rapid, far-reaching hydroclimatic impacts extending into the Southern Hemisphere midlatitudes.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), a crucial driver of the Earth’s climate system, is currently at its weakest state in the last millennium (1). The future impact of the AMOC weakening is expected to extend far beyond the Atlantic Ocean, driving a large-scale reorganization of the global atmospheric circulation and hydroclimatic patterns (2, 3). Valuable insights into such far-field atmospheric and hydroclimatic responses to AMOC slowdowns can be gained from the last glacial period, when the AMOC has repeatedly weakened on millennial timescales (4, 5). Substantial AMOC slowdowns have occurred during Heinrich stadials (HSs) as a result of catastrophic iceberg discharges into the subpolar North Atlantic Ocean (known as Heinrich Events) (4, 6), which induced global-scale impacts via the thermal bipolar seesaw (7). In the tropics, these were evident as precipitation changes tied to southward migrations of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), reflecting Hadley circulation adjustments that partially compensated for the reduced northward ocean heat transport (5, 8). Furthermore, modeling studies suggest synchronous poleward shifts of the Southern Hemisphere Westerly Winds (SWW) (5, 9, 10). Such displacements are hypothesized to have enhanced wind-driven upwelling and CO2 outgassing from the Southern Ocean, thereby playing a central role in millennial-scale global climate change during the last glacial period (11–13). Proxy records support these latitudinal shifts of the SWW, as inferred from oceanic fronts migrations (14, 15) and Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) strength (16–18), although such signals could often be modulated by local sea surface temperature (SST) and sea-ice variability. Despite these advances, the hydroclimatic impacts (e.g., precipitation pattern) of AMOC slowdowns and associated SWW adjustments in the Southern midlatitudes under the SWW belt remain poorly constrained.

Western Patagonia is a key region for tracking past hydroclimatic variabilities within the SWW belt, as the Andean Cordillera blocks the prevailing SWW and receives substantial moisture from the South Pacific (19, 20) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). High orographic precipitation and snowfall support the growth of extensive ice masses on the southern Andes (19, 21, 22) and have contributed to the development of the Patagonian Ice Sheet (PIS), expanding from 38°S to 56°S during the last glacial period (23–26). Geomorphological records [e.g., moraines (23)] indicating glacier advances and retreats suggest a sensitivity of the PIS to millennial-scale SWW and its rain-belt migrations during the last deglaciation. For example, the PIS underwent pronounced thinning between ~18 and ~15 ka, corresponding to HS 1, likely driven by marked Southern Hemisphere warming and reduced precipitation associated with a poleward displacement of the SWW beyond southern Patagonia (23, 27, 28). Terrigenous sediments discharged from the western PIS were deposited offshore along the Chilean margin, where marine sediment cores provide continuous archives extending back to the last glacial period (14, 26, 29, 30). However, the relationship between PIS variability and AMOC slowdowns during HSs and their potential linkage to SWW changes remain unresolved.

To address this question, we present elemental and detrital records from hemipelagic sediment core MR16-09 PC3 (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2) recovered ~150 km offshore of the Golfo de Penas (~46.4°S), representative outlet for glaciomarine/glaciofluvial sediments from the western PIS in central Patagonia (46 to 49°S; hereafter, western-central PIS) into the eastern South Pacific (23, 31, 32). This well-dated sediment core (30) provides a high-resolution record of terrigenous detrital inputs, representing millennial-scale fluctuations in the western-central PIS glacier activity and local precipitation (snowfall), temporally linked to HSs. To explore interhemispheric connections via atmospheric-oceanic processes, we compared our results with key proxy records for AMOC variability (4), Southern Ocean SST (33), latitudinal ITCZ migration (8), and surface wind stress over the Southern Ocean (18, 34). We further conducted a 2,000-y-long coupled climate model simulation to evaluate mechanistically the hydroclimatic impacts of AMOC slowdowns and associated SWW belt changes on the precipitation patterns over western Patagonia. These long-term simulations also highlight the role of the thermal bipolar seesaw in shaping atmospheric and hydroclimatic responses in the southern midlatitudes.

Results and Discussion

Millennial-Scale Detrital Discharges from the PIS Temporally Associated with HSs.

To better constrain the western-central PIS activity, we analyzed detrital records from offshore western Patagonia over the last glacial period (Fig. 1 A–F). High-resolution titanium-to-bromine (Ti/Br) ratios, a proxy for terrigenous (37) vs. marine input (38), and relative bulk density obtained from X-ray transmission images identified 11 discrete layers in core MR16-09 PC3 (Fig. 1 A–C; Materials and Methods). These millennial-scale layers were characterized by concurrent increases in Ti/Br ratios and bulk density, both indicating increased terrigenous detrital inputs into the hemipelagic sediments. Additionally, we examined detrital grain-size distribution and applied numerical decomposing analysis to reconstruct changes in sediment transport regimes (Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3; Materials and Methods). The episodes of enhanced detrital input corresponded to poorly sorted detrital grain-size distributions with the appearance of coarser silt-size grains, suggesting pulsed delivery of terrigenous detrital materials within suspension range under energetic erosional and transport conditions.

We further analyzed detrital mineralogy reflecting bedrock compositions to trace migrations in major terrestrial erosion sources (Fig. 1 D and G and SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5; Materials and Methods). Detrital mineral assemblages show increased amphibole content simultaneously with the enhanced detrital discharges, demonstrating increased detritus sourcing from the Patagonian Batholith exposed along the Pacific coast south of ~47°S. This inference is supported by the reconstructed flowlines of the western PIS outlet glaciers toward the Pacific coast (23) (Fig. 1G), which suggest that westward incursions of the ice-front position promoted subglacial bedrock erosion and sediment export from the Batholith region. Since subglacial processes strongly amplify glaciomarine and glaciofluvial sediment discharges (30, 39), we attribute these detrital signals (Fig. 1 A–F) to millennial-scale ice-sheet expansions and associated marginal melts of the western-central PIS (30). Importantly, the absence of comparable detrital signals during early glacial termination (after ~18 ka; HS 1), when the PIS underwent a major deglaciation (23, 27, 28), suggests that substantial glacier retreat was unlikely to have been the primary cause of the enhanced detrital discharges (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Despite uncertainties in the age model (SI Appendix, Fig. S6; Materials and Methods), the timing of these episodic detrital discharges between 25 to 60 ka appears to be linked to HS 2 to 6 (Fig. 2). Most discharge onsets occurred during the latter half or immediately after HS 4 to 6 (orange stripes in Fig. 2), while others were nearly synchronous with HS 2 and 3 (blue stripes in Fig. 2). Regardless of these differences in timing, all episodes were abrupt and lasted for several thousand years, as evidenced by rapid shifts in bedrock erosion and sediment transport proxies (Fig. 1 A–F). They likely represent ice-contact glaciomarine sedimentation involving transport of suspended materials sourced from subglacial meltwater discharge at the PIS grounding line (42), accompanied by continued erosion and sediment export from the Batholith region. Once erosive outlet glaciers of the western PIS toward the Pacific coast (i.e., the Golfo de Penas) became more active, large volumes of suspended sediment could have been exported offshore via meltwater plumes over millennial timescales (23, 30) (Fig. 1G). Our data are broadly consistent with previous, primarily biomarker records from the same site (30) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These data show enhanced input of terrestrial biomarkers and meltwater indicators for most of the layers supporting our interpretation that the detrital layers represent enhanced discharges from the expanded PIS to Pacific shelf areas.

The observed detrital discharges from the western-central PIS require changes in the processes controlling glacier mass balance (30), i.e., precipitation (snowfall) and temperature-driven melting (43). While previous studies attributed millennial-scale sediment discharges to PIS growth driven by Southern Ocean cooling during Antarctic stadials (14, 15, 29, 30, 44), our records instead show that enhanced discharge from the western-central PIS coincided with Antarctic warming during most HSs (Fig. 2 C and G), without substantial glacier retreat. Notably, detrital discharge temporally linked to HS 4 to 6 align with local SST peaks derived from the alkenone UK’37-index in the same core (30) (Fig. 2 C and D), synchronous with Antarctic Isotope Maxima (AIM) marking HS terminations (5, 33, 40, 45) (Fig. 2G). Although a previous study interpreted SST cooling based on the UK37 index (30), these coincided with increases in alkenone C37:4 contents, suggesting that freshwater effects may bias those SST reconstructions during high-terrigenous intervals (30) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Here we instead adopt the UK’37 index, which excludes C37:4 in its formula and is generally less susceptible to such effects. This index indicates a warming trend during HSs, within age model uncertainty (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6; Materials and Methods). A nearby site unaffected by such terrigenous input (ODP Site 1233; ~41°S) also records warming synchronous with AIM (14, 15, 33), consistent with our interpretation. Although such increases in SST likely enhanced ice melt, the millennial-scale persistence of enhanced sediment and meltwater discharge without substantial glacier retreat suggests that precipitation (snowfall) also increased during HS 4 to 6.

The shorter lag between HS onsets and detrital discharges during HS 2 and 3, when cooling and PIS expansion were more pronounced than during HS 4 to 6, suggests that the timing of discharge may have reflected differences in the background climate state as well as PIS size and extent (26, 30). We also note that detrital input layers in core MR16-09 PC3 at ~18 to 24 ka and ~64 to 72 ka do not correspond to HSs but instead align with Antarctic cold periods (30) (Figs. 1 and 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These episodes may reflect a shift from a precipitation-driven to a temperature-controlled glacial system under full or near-full glacial conditions. The detailed dynamics behind these events remain to be explored in future studies.

Drivers of Enhanced Sediment Discharge from the Central PIS During AMOC Slowdowns.

The millennial-scale persistence of discharge events without substantial glacier retreat during intervals of elevated SSTs in HSs requires intensified snowfall over their accumulation areas (Figs. 1G and 2 C and D), driven by enhanced orographic precipitation (46), in addition to warming-induced melting. There are two possibilities accounting for this precipitation (snowfall) enhancement: one is stronger SWW, the other is greater atmospheric moisture supply. In essence, more intense or more moisture-laden SWW airflow from the South Pacific would directly amplify orographic precipitation. Similar processes operate in the modern climate, where positive glacier mass balance on the western Andean slope is strongly correlated with increased local precipitation and wind intensity (19, 21, 22). During the last glacial period, when the SWW core belt could have been weaker and displaced equatorward relative to today (45° to 60°S) (47), transient southward shifts or intensifications would have focused moisture-laden winds onto the accumulation areas feeding outlet glaciers near our study sites (~46.5° to 49°S) (23) (Fig. 1G), providing a robust link between climatic changes and the observed glacier activities. This interpretation is supported by proxy evidence from the Southern Ocean indicating near-synchronous enhancement of the surface wind stress, inferred from increased wind-driven deep-water upwelling (11, 34) (Fig. 2E) and intensification of the ACC and the Cape Horn Current (16–18) (Fig. 2F). Alternatively, because western Patagonia predominantly receives Pacific air masses via the prevailing westerlies (19, 20), the enhanced orographic precipitation may also be attributed to a temporary increase in sea-surface moisture content.

The inferred atmospheric changes are broadly consistent with previous model simulations proposing that AMOC slowdowns displace the SWW southward via a southward ITCZ shift and weakening of the Southern Hemisphere Hadley cell (5, 9, 10). Southward ITCZ shifts are well documented to largely coincide with AMOC slowdowns (e.g., refs. 8, 48, and 49) (Fig. 2 A and B), whereas our proxy records reveal a systematical lag in the SWW response (Fig. 2C). Although chronological uncertainties preclude fully ruling out synchrony (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), the inferred southward SWW changes at millennial timescales, marked by peak SSTs in the eastern South Pacific synchronous with Antarctic temperature maxima (14, 18, 29, 33), occurred roughly two millennia after the onset of AMOC slowdowns indicated by increased 231Pa/230Th ratios (4) during HS 4 to 6 (Fig. 2 A–C and G). To reconcile this lag, we infer that progressively intensifying SWW and/or associated moisture supply gradually increased accumulation over the central PIS, while concurrent warming enhanced ablation, together culminating in increased meltwater discharges during the latter half of each HS.

To mechanistically explain the timing discrepancies between AMOC slowdowns and hydroclimate responses, we conducted a freshwater hosing experiment with the coupled climate model MIROC4m (Materials and Methods). The experiment simulated a HS-like AMOC slowdown (approaching 0 Sv) by applying a 2,000-y freshwater anomaly in the subpolar North Atlantic during a weak phase (~10 Sv) of the AMOC’s self-sustained oscillation (Fig. 3A), consistent with paleoclimatic evidence (4) (Fig. 2A). Following the onset of this forcing (t = 0), the simulation shows enhanced precipitation along the Chilean coast south of ~45°S (Fig. 3G). These positive anomalies in precipitation, reaching up to ~100 mm/y, intensified progressively throughout the experiment, in line with our proxy-based interpretation. The precipitation increases expanded southward over time, corresponding to the southward shift of the westerly wind “anomalies” from north of 45°S to higher latitudes (Fig. 3 G and J). Notably, zonal wind anomalies were relatively modest near the SWW core at ~46°S (<2%; Fig. 3 H–J), indicating that these changes represent a southward intensification and contraction rather than a simple poleward displacement of the core position. The model further reveals a pronounced timescale contrast: the ITCZ shifted within a 100 y, as indicated by the rapid decline in the Northern Hemisphere low latitude precipitation (Fig. 3G), whereas the anomalies in the SWW and associated precipitation belt migrated southward more gradually, over 2,000 y after the onset of freshwater hosing (Fig. 3 G and J). This asynchronous atmospheric adjustment also agrees with our proxy evidence, implying that progressive SWW adjustments and the resulting precipitation response partially contributed to delayed response of the PIS to AMOC slowdowns during HSs (Figs. 2 and 3).

In addition, South Pacific warming seems to have further enhanced the moisture supply to the western-central PIS by increasing sea-surface evaporation. Our model simulates a progressive rise in the eastern South Pacific SST following AMOC slowdown (Fig. 3D), consistent with regional proxy records (14, 18, 29, 30, 33) and the thermal bipolar seesaw concept (7). This warming coincided with increased precipitation and southward intensifications of the SWW (Fig. 3 D, G, and J). Because enhanced orographic precipitation on the windward Andean slope requires strong winds and/or abundant atmospheric moisture (19, 20, 46), rising SSTs and resulting sea-surface evaporation presumably provided the necessary vapor to drive greater snowfall. This effect became particularly important during the latter half of the simulation, when elevated moisture content likely sustained high precipitation rates even as wind anomalies migrated farther south (Fig. 3 D and J).

On the other hand, gradual warming also has a more direct impact on glacier mass balance by enhancing ice melt. To examine the effects of increased precipitation and warming-induced melting on the PIS, we further analyzed the surface mass balance (SMB) response to AMOC slowdowns using downscaled MIROC4m outputs (Materials and Methods; SI Appendix, Supporting Information Text). The SMB estimates (precipitation minus ice-melt) reveal both temporal changes following freshwater hosing and pronounced spatial heterogeneity across the PIS. Relative to prehosing conditions, increased precipitation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8J) produces positive SMB anomalies in the interior region (blue in SI Appendix, Fig. S8L), including the accumulation areas feeding outlet glaciers near our study sites (Fig. 1G), 1,000 y after the onset of hosing. In contrast, enhanced ice melting in the low-elevation PIS margins (SI Appendix, Fig. S8K) generates negative SMB anomalies (red in SI Appendix, Fig. S8L). Consequently, AMOC slowdown enhances ice accumulation in the PIS interior and promotes ice melting along the thin marginal areas facing the Pacific coast (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 G, H, J, and L), potentially strengthening ice-mass flux from the interior toward the margins and enhancing meltwater discharge. Thus, these findings indicate that millennial-scale AMOC slowdowns influenced not only Patagonian hydroclimate induced by SWW adjustment but also ice-sheet dynamics through coupled atmospheric and oceanic processes, i.e., the thermal bipolar seesaw, providing a mechanistic basis for our proxy-based interpretation of increased detrital and meltwater discharge during HSs.

Furthermore, the SMB calculations support the idea that background PIS growth may have controlled the differing PIS responses to AMOC slowdowns during HS 4 to 6 and HS 2 to 3. We examined SMB responses for two PIS configurations representing a smaller PIS at 50 ka and a larger PIS at 30 ka (50), broadly corresponding to the ice-sheet states during HS 4 to 6 and HS 2 to 3, respectively. 1,000 y after hosing onset, both PIS configurations exhibit similar SMB patterns (SI Appendix, Figs. S8L and S9L), with positive anomalies in the interior accumulation areas and negative anomalies along the low-elevation margins. However, negative net SMB areas are more extensive in the larger 30-ka PIS (red in SI Appendix, Figs. S8H and S9H), indicating a more rapid onset of enhanced detrital and meltwater discharge than in the smaller 50-ka PIS. This likely reflects the larger extent of low-elevation Pacific-facing areas in the larger PIS, increasing sensitivity to warming-induced melting (SI Appendix, Figs. S8G and S9G) and shortening the response time to AMOC slowdowns. Alternatively, the larger extent of the 30-ka PIS toward the continental shelf margin may have increased the efficiency of meltwater discharge and sediment transport to the Pacific Ocean. Overall, while background climate state and PIS size likely modulated the sensitivity and timing of the PIS response, Patagonian hydroclimate and ice sheet may have remained influenced by AMOC slowdowns on millennial timescales throughout the glacial period.

Interhemispheric Teleconnections Via Coupled Atmospheric-Oceanic Processes.

Coupled atmospheric and oceanic processes may have affected more than just the moisture supply to western Patagonia. While previous studies have proposed that southward SWW shifts occurred in parallel with ITCZ migrations via fast-acting atmospheric teleconnections (5, 9, 10, 12, 51), our proxy and model results instead suggest gradual southward intensifications of the SWW in response to AMOC slowdowns during HS 4 to 6 (Figs. 2 and 3). We attribute this progressive SWW response to the influence of slower oceanic adjustment processes, as the migration of SWW anomalies over Patagonia coevolved with the thermal bipolar seesaw on multimillennial timescales (7) (Fig. 3 D and J).

We hypothesize that progressive warming in the Southern Hemisphere high latitudes (33) and the associated Antarctic sea-ice retreat (52) ultimately governed the timing of the SWW response. Following AMOC slowdowns, the thermal bipolar seesaw dictates delayed Southern Ocean warming on millennial timescales (7) (SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11). During the early phases of HSs, persistently cool SSTs and extensive Antarctic sea-ice would have kept the SWW in its initial equatorward-expanded state, counteracting the atmospheric forcing from rapid Hadley cell adjustments (5, 9, 10, 47, 53, 54) (SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S12, years 200 to 400). Subsequent Southern Ocean warming and the Antarctic sea-ice retreat strengthened the meridional temperature gradient at the sea-ice margin, providing the trigger for the southward SWW intensification during late HSs (SI Appendix, Fig. S10, years 800 to 1,000 and 1,800 to 2,000). Hence, a combination of atmospheric teleconnections and slower oceanic feedback provides a more comprehensive mechanism of the SWW response to millennial-scale AMOC slowdowns.

Our findings further indicate that millennial-scale SWW responses were pronounced during persistent AMOC slowdowns, such as HSs, rather than during all stadial intervals (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Throughout the last glacial period, weakened AMOC states recurred on millennial timescales, often aligning with Greenland stadial intervals but not exclusively with HSs (4). These episodes, regardless of their magnitude, modulated Northern Hemisphere and tropical atmospheric circulation via altered ocean heat transport (e.g., refs. 8, 48, and 49) and probably influenced the latitudinal position of Antarctic moisture sources (12, 51). In contrast, our sedimentary records indicate that detrital discharges from the central PIS consistently aligned with the more persistent and pronounced AMOC weakening (Fig. 2), suggesting the importance of event duration and magnitude in amplifying hydroclimatic impacts on Patagonia. This emphasizes that external forcing, i.e., freshwater inputs into the subpolar North Atlantic, generated more pronounced global hydroclimate responses during HS-related AMOC slowdowns than during shorter-lived Greenland stadials (55).

By integrating proxy and model evidence, we depict the spatiotemporal evolution of the southward intensification of the SWW in response to AMOC slowdowns in the last glacial period (Fig. 4). During active AMOC phases, such as Greenland interstadial intervals (4), a northerly ITCZ (8) corresponded to a northward-expanded SWW (Figs. 3 and 4A) if the Hadley cell width remained largely unchanged. This northward expansion is consistent with enhanced dust production from northeastern Patagonia, fueling increased export production in the sub-Antarctic South Atlantic (34, 56). In contrast, during HSs, abrupt AMOC slowdowns displaced the ITCZ southward (4, 8) (Fig. 4B), while the SWW contracted and strengthened poleward more gradually, tracking the thermal bipolar seesaw on multimillennial timescales (Fig. 3). Ultimately, positive SWW anomalies extended into the Antarctic Zone, likely promoting enhanced deep-water ventilation and CO2 outgassing to the atmosphere (11, 13, 34).

In summary, the high-resolution detrital records from hemipelagic sediment core MR16-09 PC3, recovered offshore of the western-central PIS, reveal millennial-scale sediment input in response to past AMOC slowdowns during the last glacial period (Figs. 1, 2, and 4). Despite differences in response sensitivity between HS 2 to 3 and HS 4 to 6, these records provide evidence that abrupt AMOC slowdowns during HSs triggered hydroclimate and ice-sheet changes in the Southern midlatitudes through the thermal bipolar seesaw and associated southward intensifications of the SWW. These changes potentially enhanced wind-driven upwelling and CO2 outgassing from the Southern Ocean (11, 13, 34) (Figs. 2 C, E, and H and 4B). Our results consistently suggest a gradual southward SWW response during HSs (Figs. 2 and 3), implying that atmospheric and oceanic processes have jointly modulated interhemispheric teleconnections, at least in the eastern South Pacific. The progressive SST rise along the thermal bipolar seesaw probably controlled moisture supply to western Patagonia and the timing of the SWW response, in addition to enhancing PIS marginal melt. Although glacial boundary conditions differ from the present, these findings can provide valuable insights into far-field climatological responses to future AMOC slowdowns. We highlight the tight coupling between interhemispheric atmospheric and oceanic circulation, Southern Hemisphere midlatitudes hydroclimate and cryosphere dynamics, and the global carbon cycle. In light of ongoing AMOC weakening (3), our findings raise the possibility of future abrupt shifts in climate and atmospheric CO2, driven by feedback analogous to those operating during past AMOC slowdowns.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Sediment Description.

Piston core MR16-09 PC3 was collected at 46.4053°S, 77.3242°W from a water depth of 3,082 m during R/V Mirai (Japan Agency for Marine Earth Science and Technology, JAMSTEC) cruise MR16-09 leg 2 in January 2017 (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). The recovered sediments consist mainly of dark olive gray to gray silt-, diatom-, and occasionally nannofossil-bearing clay to clayey nannofossil ooze (30, 57). Our X-ray radiographs (XRF Scan and X-ray Radiography) identified laminations within higher-density layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), indicating repeated increases in detrital input at submillennial timescales. This laminated structure is also evident in core MR16-09 PC2, which shows lithostratigraphy closely resembling that of core PC3 (58), despite being located more than 140 km apart from the site PC3 (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2). In addition to their positions above regional depressions, XRF-scanned ln(Zr/Rb) ratios, a grain-size indicator (59), show no evidence of upward fining within these laminated intervals (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), suggesting nonturbiditic deposition. At 904 to 919 cm below the seafloor (cmbsf) in core PC3, a prominent 15 cm thick brownish sandy turbiditic deposit with volcanic materials interrupts the normal deposition. Although this tephra layer was excluded from the age model (30), it produced residual geochemical and grain-size anomalies in the overlying sediments (red stars in Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Age Models.

The age model of core MR16-09 PC3 was established in a previous study (30) using a combination of radiocarbon dating, oxygen isotope stratigraphy, geomagnetic excursion, and tuning of XRF-scanned rubidium (Rb) record to the Antarctic (EPICA Dronning Maud Land: EDML) δ18O record (60) according to the assumption that grain-size changes linked to the climate (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). To assess the age uncertainty, we applied the Monte Carlo approach-based classical age-modeling (CLAM) technique using the R package “clam” (61), generating ±2 sigma CI (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Note that we followed the previous linear-interpolation concept since spline curves are unsuitable for core samples exhibiting highly variable sedimentation rates. Additionally, four Rb-EDML tie-points were excluded from the CI calculation, because Rb, which links to clay fraction variability, should not be tuned to Antarctic chronologies when examining the timing of grain-size changes. The onset (base) ages of the detrital discharge events fall within the ±2 sigma uncertainty ranges of HSs 2, 3, 4, 5, 5a, and 6, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These age model uncertainties, which largely originate from 14C-dating, also permit a previous interpretation that linked terrigenous discharges to Antarctic cooling based on UK37 SSTs (30). On the other hand, this study tested an alternative possibility that these discharge events are aligned with the timing of HSs and the corresponding Antarctic warmings based on UK’37 SSTs (see text).

The age model of core MR16-09 PC2 was constructed by stratigraphical tuning to core PC3 (58).

XRF Scan and X-ray Radiography.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) scans and radiography, an imaging technique using transmitted X-rays, of cores MR16-09 PC2 and PC3 were performed with an Itrax XRF Corescanner (Cox Analytical systems) at Marine Core Research Institute (MaCRI), Kochi University, Japan (30). The XRF scans were conducted using a Mo X-ray tube at 30 kV and 55 mA, with a down-core resolution of 5 mm and a measurement time of 15 s. The X-ray radiography was performed with a Mo X-ray tube at 60 kV and 50 mA, with a down-core resolution of 2 mm. For both scans, the Itrax X-ray beam was set to 0.2 mm × 20 mm. Variations of raw Titanium (Ti) and bromine (Br) counts are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7. Titanium is frequent in igneous rock-minerals, representing terrigenous refractory minerals (37). In contrast, bromine is exclusively associated with marine organic matter (38). Hence, logarithmic titanium to bromine ratio, ln(Ti/Br), represents terrigenous vs. marine inputs in the sediment (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S7). An intensity of X-ray radiographs (X-ray transmission images), indicating sediment density, is useful to identify sediment layers with higher-density terrigenous mineral contents.

Bead XRF Measurement.

XRF spectrometry analyses of core MR16-09 PC2 were conducted using a PANalytical Magix PRO PW2440 equipped with a Rh X-ray tube (50 kV and 60 mA) at MaCRI to quantify the major elements (SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, Fe2O3, MnO, MgO, CaO, Na2O, K2O, and P2O5), including Al, Mg, and Na, which are unavailable for XRF scanning (62). After igniting to 950 °C to decompose carbonates, a total of 63 discrete sediment samples from core MR16-09 PC2 were mixed with anhydrous lithium tetraborate flux and fused to form glass beads. The XRF data were calibrated by analyses of rock reference standards issued by the Geological Survey of Japan (GSJ; Geochemical Reference Sample Data Base). Data were reported on a volatile-free basis and used to infer detrital provenances.

Pretreatment for XRD and Grain-Size Analyses.

Prior to powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) and grain-size analyses, samples were treated for the isolation of the detritus. A split of 0.1 to 0.15 g of 209 freeze-dried discrete sediment samples from 10 to 80 ka intervals of core MR16-09 PC3 were used to treat in succession with dithionite-citrate solution buffered with sodium bicarbonate to remove Fe/Mn oxides, 20% acetic acid solution to remove carbonate, 10% hydrogen peroxide solution to remove organic matter, and 2 mol L−1 sodium carbonate solution to remove opal (63–65), thus isolating detrital components.

Powder XRD Analysis and Mineralogy.

Approximately 20 mg of isolated detrital samples were powdered using an agate mortar and used for analysis using a PANalytical X’Pert Pro X-ray diffractometer at JAMSTEC with a Cu-Kα source at a voltage of 45 kV and current of 40 mA. The scanning interval was 3° to 70° 2θ, scanning speed was 0.14° 2θ/s, and sampling step was 0.017° 2θ. We conducted background and Kα2 elimination based on the published protocols (66, 67). Then, we estimated mineral proportions (wt.%) by the Reference Intensity Ratio method with full pattern summation using the R package “powdR” (68). Based on the regional bedrock mineralogy, we selected reference patterns of seven mineralogical groups (plagioclase, quartz, mica, chlorite, amphibole, K-feldspar, and pyroxene) from the International Centre for Diffraction Data’s Powder Diffraction File (69) (SI Appendix, Table S2). To overcome the closed-sum problem for compositional data, mineral proportions were log-transformed with centered log ratios (clr) (70) based on the following equations:[1]clrx = lnx1gx,lnx2gx,⋯,lnxngx, [2]gx = ∏i=1nxi1n,

where xi (i = 1, 2, …, n) correspond to the concentration of individual minerals and gx represents the geometric mean.

Identification of the Detrital Provenance.

The geology of western part of central Patagonia (46 to 49°S) consists majorly of the margin parallel plutonic belt, the Patagonian Batholith (PB), and the inland and archipelagic metasedimentary belts, the Eastern Andean Metamorphic Complex (EAMC) and the Chonos Metamorphic Complex (CMC), respectively (Fig. 1G). The PB is composed of Cenozoic and Mesozoic granitoids and is flanked by the EAMC to the southeast and by the CMC to the northwest (71). The EAMC consists mainly of Paleozoic polydeformed turbidite successions and located under and around the present South Patagonian Icefield (SPI) (72). The CMC consists predominantly of Mesozoic metaturbidites and crops out throughout the Chonos Archipelago and the Taitao Peninsula (73). The major fjord systems (the Canal Baker, Canal Messier, and Canal Fallos; SI Appendix, Fig. S1), where the erosive outlet glaciers of the western PIS have flowed toward the Golfo de Penas and its southern margin, are distributed across the PB and EAMC (23). In contrast, subglacial erosion of the CMC appears to have been limited even during glacial periods due to thin ice cover in its fjord region (74).

Mineralogical properties allow us to identify the detrital provenance in our cores, particularly by contrasting amphibole contents. The PB is mainly in the form of hornblende-biotite granodiorites and tonalites as well as amphibole gabbro (e.g., refs. 71 and 75). Most tonalites contain both amphibole and biotite, a total of 20 to 30% (76). In contrast, metaturbidites of the EAMC and CMC (e.g., refs. 77 and 78) consist mainly of quartz, plagioclase, chlorite, and muscovite (with biotite in the EAMC), suggesting scarce amphibole contents. The EAMC and CMC also contain metabasites with amphibole, although limited to less than 5% of the basements (79, 80). Hence, amphibole content is a suitable indicator of the detrital provenance. In core MR16-09 PC3, amphibole contents (also their clr-transformed values) exhibit no significant relationship with detrital grain-size parameters (mean, median, and sortable silt mean grain sizes) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), indicating that relative provenance changes, rather than grain-size variations, were the primary driver of amphibole content changes.

To validate provenance identifications based on amphibole content, we further examined plagioclase mineralogy and elemental composition. Although plagioclase is present in all three major geological units, its composition varies: albite is dominant in the EAMC and CMC (77, 78), whereas the PB contains a higher proportion of anorthite content (75). Our results show that anorthite/albite [ICDD No. 00-041-1486 and 01-089-6424, respectively (69)] ratios covaried with amphibole content (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C), reinforcing the provenance inference. We also evaluated the potential contribution from the CMC, previously considered minor (74), using elemental compositions. This analysis draws on published datasets of major elemental compositions (Si, Ti, Al, Fe, Mn, Mg, Ca, Na, and K) in lake, fjord bottom, and river suspended sediments from western Patagonia (reference lists in SI Appendix, Table S3). Each dataset was classified into one of three distinct groups of catchment geology: the PB, EAMC, and CMC (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D and Table S3). Samples interpreted as mixtures of multiple sources were excluded based on geographic location. We then calculated log-ratios for all elemental pairs, excluding biologically influenced (Ca and Si) and redox sensitive (Fe and Mn) elements, to distinguish provenance geologies (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Among them, ln(Mg/Ti) and ln(Mg/K) effectively distinguished the CMC from the PB and EAMC (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B), likely representing low biotite contents in CMC-derived sediments, consistent with bedrock mineralogy. Since Mg and K are enriched in clay and fine-silt fractions, while Ti is associated with in coarser silts in western Patagonia (62), ln(Mg/K) serves as a grain size-independent provenance indicator. The bead-XRF-derived ln(Mg/K) values from core MR16-09 PC2 indicate constantly low CMC contributions throughout the last glacial period (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), supporting previous findings (74).

Grain-Size Analysis.

20 to 30 mg of isolated detrital materials was used for grain-size analysis using a laser-diffraction-scattering grain-size analyzer (Malvern, Mastersizer 3000) at JAMSTEC. 74 grain-size classes were determined in the diameter range of 0.0995 to 1,110 μm with the interval of each grain-size class being Δlog10 (μm) = ~0.055. The refractive index and absorption ratio were set as 1.65 and 1, respectively. A given polymodal grain-size distribution can be decomposed into several components with unimodal normal distributions on a logarithmic scale (81, 82). After converting grain-size to the log10 scale, the grain-size distribution was decomposed into several normal distributions by the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm using the R package “mixtools” [version 2.0.0 (83)] (82). The number of components were determined based on the mean square error (MSE) values (81):[3]MSE = 1m∑i=1myi - y^i2,

where yi and y^i are the ith measured and estimated grain-size percentages respectively, and m is the number of grain-size intervals. To prevent premature convergence before sufficient decomposition was achieved, the EM algorithm was applied twice: first, until the MSE fell below an empirical arbitrary value, 0.1; and second, until the MSE decreased below that obtained in the first iteration. The MSE values became smaller with increasing the number of fitted components and almost saturated around a specific number (three or four) of components (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), i.e., near the true number of components (82). We used a 99% range of MSE values for the four-component model across all data (x¯+3s=7.57×10-3) as a threshold to determine whether the data were better explained by three or four components. The finer components (EM1–3) were deposited throughout the entire periods, indicative of background sedimentation processes. In contrast, the coarser component (EM4), composed of medium-to-coarse silt-size grains and associated with poorly sorted detrital grain-size distributions, was deposited primarily during HSs.

Hosing Experiment with MIROC4m, a Fully Coupled Climate Model.

The model experiment is performed with the objective of simulating a “Heinrich stadial” assuming that it is triggered by the subsurface ocean warming in the North Atlantic due to the start of the cold phase after a Dansgaard–Oeschger (DO) event (84, 85).

A fully coupled atmosphere-ocean general circulation model (AOGCM), MIROC4m, developed in Japan (86), was used for this study. The atmospheric model, including the land surface component MATSIRO, was configured at T42 resolution (~2.8°), with 20 vertical layers. The ocean model COCO, which includes sea-ice dynamics formulated using viscous-elastic-plastic rheology, has a horizontal resolution of 1.4° to 0.56° in the tropics, with 43 uneven vertical layers. The model has already been used to investigate a range of paleoclimate phenomena, especially millennial-scale abrupt glacial climate changes such as DO events, HS, and deglacial climates, as well as glacials and interglacials (e.g., refs. 87–93).

In this study, we first simulated DO-like self-sustained AMOC oscillations with a millennial-scale periodicity under glacial conditions without external freshwater forcing. This experiment was conducted with the atmospheric CO2 level fixed at 200 ppm and with all other conditions identical to experiment P270 in Kuniyoshi et al. (87), which includes fixed LGM ice sheets [ICE-5G (94)] and a precessional phase of 270°, yielding strongest boreal and weakest austral seasonal insolation contrasts (87). Eccentricity and obliquity were set to 0.04 and 22.949°, respectively. Then, we performed the so-called “hosing experiment” partially by adding a freshwater flux of 0.2 Sv (0.2 × 106 m3 s−1) in the region 50°N to 70°N of the North Atlantic at timing 0, which is taken to be the beginning (at year 400) of the cold phase of the self-oscillatory mode, the so-called stadial condition. In order to simulate a long “Heinrich stadial,” 2,000 y of the freshwater release was continued as “hosing” as in Fig. 3. We confirm that the response to freshwater hosing does not depend on background states, such as precession and atmospheric CO2 level (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

SMB Calculations.

SMB for the PIS was estimated using MIROC4m outputs with two approaches. The first approach used precipitation and ice melt directly simulated by MIROC4m. In the model configuration, the horizontal resolution is ~2.8°, and the PIS region is represented by six grid cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). We used precipitation (assumed to occur predominantly as snow), ice melt (derived from the ice-sheet surface energy balance), and their difference (precipitation minus ice melt) as a first-order estimate of SMB (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 G–I). However, the smoothed model topography and coarse grid bias SMB toward excessive ice loss by underestimating precipitation and overestimating ice melt, respectively (see SI Appendix, Supporting Information Text for details).

To better resolve topographic effects on the ice melt, we performed a second SMB calculation using downscaled MIROC4m outputs and a positive degree-day (PDD) method (95, 96). Temperature and precipitation fields were downscaled from ~2.8° to ~0.25° resolution by bilinear interpolation using reconstructed PIS surface elevations at 50 ka (smaller PIS; SI Appendix, Fig. S8) and 30 ka (larger PIS; SI Appendix, Fig. S9) from Gowan et al. (50). Temperature was adjusted to the surface elevation using a lapse-rate of 6 °C km−1. Ice melt and a first-order SMB (precipitation minus ice melt) were then estimated using the PDD method applied to the downscaled climate fields.
