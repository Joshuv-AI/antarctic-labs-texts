Percutaneous closed reduction for locked pubic symphysis using a modified subcristal external fixator: A case report
Authors: Shuya Nohmi, Kazuki Oyama, Junichi Hamazaki, Takumi Hirabayashi, Kenji Kobayashi, Fumihiko Eto, Junichiro Morita, Takeshi Ogawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA locked pubic symphysis is a variant of lateral compression pelvic ring injury, characterized by the overlap of one pubis over the contralateral pubis. One challenge with locked pubic symphysis is the difficulty of achieving closed reduction. In this report, we present a case of a 17-year-old boy who sustained a locked pubic symphysis and was successfully treated with percutaneous reduction using a modified subcristal external fixator.After failed manual closed reduction, 5 mm pins were inserted 3 cm away from the anterior superior iliac spine to prevent injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. The pins were advanced through the iliac crest until bicortical fixation was achieved. For percutaneous closed reduction, the pins were used as joysticks to manipulate the hemipelvis, successfully achieving reduction.Percutaneous closed reduction using a modified subcristal external fixator for locked pubic symphysis is a less invasive alternative to conventional open reduction following failed closed reduction. It carries a lower risk of complications, such as nerve injury, and is worth attempting before resorting to open reduction.Keywords: Locked pubic symphysis, Percutaneous, Closed reduction, Subcristal external fixator

Introduction

A locked pubic symphysis is a rare variant of lateral compression pelvic ring injury, characterized by the overlap of one pubis over the contralateral pubis [1]. For initial management of the locked pubic symphysis, a closed reduction is typically attempted. However, it often fails, as locked pubic symphysis presents a significant challenge for closed reduction and frequently requires open reduction. Patients with locked pubic symphysis are often hemodynamically unstable because of associated injuries, making a less invasive reduction procedure preferable after failed closed reduction attempts.

Percutaneous closed reduction for a locked pubic symphysis using external fixators following failed manual closed reduction has been reported [[2], [3], [4]]. However, previously reported pin placement positions were anterosuperior, with pins inserted perpendicular to the iliac crest in a superior to inferior direction; anteroinferior, with supra-acetabular pins inserted in an anterior to posterior direction; and subcristal, with pins placed at the anterior superior iliac spine in a direction parallel to the iliac crest [5] has not been reported. We aimed to present the application of a modified subcristal external fixator to avoid complications and a case of a locked pubic symphysis percutaneously reduced using the subcristal external fixator.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old boy with no significant medical history was brought to the emergency department after a car accident. On admission, the patient was conscious and hemodynamically stable but complained of right inguinal and lumbosacral regional pain. Physical examination revealed internal rotation of the right leg without any open wounds. Vascular and neurological examination results were normal. A plain radiograph revealed an overlap of the pubic symphysis with the right pubis behind the contralateral pubis (Fig. 1). Computed tomography confirmed the findings in the pubis and revealed a displaced sacral fracture (Fig. 2A, B, and C) and hepatic injury. Hepatic injury was treated conservatively.

A plain radiograph of the pelvis after injury shows overlapping of the pubic symphysis.

Computed tomography (CT) images of the pelvis before reduction. (A and B) Axial views of the pelvis show the right pubis behind the contralateral pubis and a displaced sacral fracture. (C) A three-dimensional CT image shows the right pubis behind the contralateral pubis and a displaced sacral fracture.

First, we attempted closed reduction of the locked pubic symphysis under general anesthesia using previously reported maneuvers [1]. The right hip joint was flexed, abducted, and rotated externally, and the pelvis was manually stabilized by pushing it on the iliac wings. However, we did not achieve a reduction. The patient was sterilized and draped for surgical intervention.

Five-millimeter pins were inserted through the stab incisions under fluoroscopic guidance. Incisions were made 3 cm away from the anterior superior iliac spine to prevent lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) injury. The pins were inserted and advanced through the iliac crest until bicortical fixation was achieved. For percutaneous closed reduction, the pins were used as “joysticks” to manipulate the hemipelvis. By applying an external rotation force to the right hemipelvis and a counterforce to the contralateral hemipelvis, the locking of the pubic symphysis was reduced. Following percutaneous reduction, external fixation was performed by clamping the rods (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

A plain radiograph of the pelvis after reduction using a modified subcristal external fixator shows reduced pubic symphysis.

Photographs taken after reduction using a modified subcristal external fixator. White arrows indicate the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS); external fixation pins are inserted away from ASIS.

Three days after percutaneous closed reduction, percutaneous screw fixation of the sacrum and internal fixation of the pubic symphysis were performed (Fig. 5). Postoperatively, weight-bearing was allowed as tolerated, and the subsequent postoperative course was uneventful. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient was able to walk without pain. Plain radiographs revealed loosening of screws, but maintained reduction of the pubic symphysis (Fig. 6).

A plain radiograph of the pelvis after internal fixation shows that the pubic symphysis is fixed using a plate and the sacrum is fixed using a screw.

A plain radiograph of the pelvis one year after surgery shows loosening of screws, but maintained reduction of the pubic symphysis.

Discussion

Herein, we report a case of a locked pubic symphysis that was percutaneously reduced using a modified subcristal external fixator.

Manual closed reduction for a locked pubic symphysis is initially attempted but often fails [6]. Locked pubic symphysis is caused by high-energy trauma such as a road traffic accident or fall from height, and patients who sustain such injury are frequently hemodynamically unstable [3,7]. Therefore, a less invasive reduction procedure is preferred after failed attempts at closed reduction. Percutaneous closed reduction using an external fixator is less invasive than open reduction, making it a reasonable treatment strategy following a failed closed reduction attempt.

Three cases of percutaneous closed reduction for a locked pubic symphysis have been reported. Halıcı et al. [2] and Elbardesy et al. [3] used a tubular external fixator for percutaneous reduction and definitive fixation, whereas Cavazos et al. [4] used pedicle screws to reduce the locked symphysis percutaneously, which were then implanted subcutaneously and connected to rods to serve as internal fixation. The pin placement positions used in each report were anterosuperior, with pins inserted perpendicular to the iliac crest in a superior to inferior direction [2], and anteroinferior, with supra-acetabular pins inserted in an anterior to posterior direction [3,4]. In the present case, the pins were inserted using a modified subcristal external fixation and used only for reduction.

Complications related to external fixator pin placement have been reported [5,8], including incorrect pin placement, deviation from the ilium and penetration to the hip joint, neurovascular injury, and pin site infection. Subcristal external fixators were configured with pins placed at the anterior superior iliac spine parallel to the iliac crest [5]. Although not included in the original literature by Solomon, LFCN injuries can occur because of their anatomical location. Among the 205 LFCNs from cadaveric specimens obtained from Japanese individuals, 39.6 % coursed within 2 cm posterior to the anterior superior iliac spine, and only 2 % coursed more than 2 cm posterior [9]. In addition, Tomaszewski et al. [10] proposed maintaining a distance of 3 cm or more from the anterior superior iliac spine when operating to prevent injury to the LFCN. To prevent LFCN injury, external fixator pins were inserted 3 cm away from the anterior superior iliac spine.

A disadvantage of this technique is the possibility of cutting through of the ilium by pins used as joysticks because the ilium is thin. Russ et al. [8] reported a lateral posterior external fixator for pelvic fixation, and the pin insertion locations were similar to those used in our procedure. They proposed the use of pins as joysticks to manipulate the hemipelvis. Although this seems acceptable, we managed the problem by obtaining bicortical fixation under fluoroscopic control.

Conclusion

Herein, we reported a case of a percutaneously reduced locked pubic symphysis using a modified subcristal external fixator. This technique is less invasive than a conventional open reduction maneuver following failed closed reduction and carries a lower risk of complications, such as nerve injury, which is worth attempting before open reduction.
