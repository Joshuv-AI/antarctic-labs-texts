Effect of implementing clinical guidelines for treatment of symptoms in advanced cancer patients in specialist palliative care: a nationwide register-based study
Authors: Leslye Rojas-Concha, Maiken Bang Hansen, Mathilde Adsersen, Morten Aagaard Petersen, Henrik Larsen, Mogens Groenvold
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is limited evidence on whether the implementation of clinical guidelines has a positive effect on the symptomatology of cancer patients in palliative care. A large national project conducted 2017-2019 aimed to improve the quality of life of cancer patients by implementing clinical guidelines for severe pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression in Danish specialist palliative care. We investigated how pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression changed from the start of palliative care to 1-4 weeks later and whether changes were associated with timing of guideline implementation and with receipt of treatment according to guidelines.MethodsA prospective, national quality improvement study. Data for the project was obtained from the Danish Palliative Care Database. We included adult patients with advanced cancer who answered the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnire-Core-15-Palliative Care (EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL) questionnaire at the start of specialist palliative care and 1–4 weeks later. Multiple linear regression evaluated associations between changes in symptom severity and timing of guideline implementation and receiving treatment according to guidelines.ResultsOf 4,640 patients completing both EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL questionnaires, 3,879 (84%) qualified for one or more treatment guidelines. Pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression improved significantly. Timing of guideline implementation and receiving treatment according to guidelines were not associated with improvement, except for dyspnea, where small associations were observed.ConclusionsPatients reported improved pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression, but this improvement was not consistently associated with the implementation of the four guidelines in palliative care. The lack of impact may be attributed to a modest impact on clinical practice or the observational project design.

Background

Clinical guidelines summarize evidence-based recommendations that help physicians to make informed decisions about optimal treatment for patients [1]. Guidelines have the potential to enhance the quality of care and patient outcomes when effectively disseminated and implemented in routine care [2, 3]. Implementing clinical guidelines in palliative care may facilitate ongoing symptom management and monitoring of potential side effects due to the disease or treatment [4].

The growing interest in providing better palliative care has led to the development of several international clinical guidelines to improve the quality of life of cancer patients in palliative care [5–8]. Many countries have followed these initiatives and implemented their own national palliative care guidelines, which primarily include recommendations for symptom management, patient and caregiver education, and training of clinicians [9–14]. However, successful adoption of these clinical guidelines into routine care can only be achieved when healthcare providers perceive the content of the guidelines as clear, precise and based on high levels of evidence [15].

Ideally, the effectiveness of treatments recommended in palliative care guidelines can be evaluated by measuring the change in the level of patients’ symptoms from before to after implementation of these guidelines [4, 16]. While some studies have shown that pain, fatigue, anxiety, depression and quality of life improved after clinical guidelines were implemented in palliative care,[17, 18] other studies did not find increased symptom relief when clinicians followed clinical guidelines [19, 20]. Therefore, more evidence is needed to understand whether the implementation of clinical guidelines has a positive effect on the level of symptoms experienced by patients in palliative care.

A national improvement project was conducted in the 44 specialist palliative care services (palliative care teams/units and hospices) in Denmark between September 2017 and June 2019. The purpose of this project was to improve the quality of life of advanced cancer patients admitted to specialist palliative care by providing the best possible treatment according to evidence-based clinical guidelines [21]. In the project, four treatment guidelines were developed to relieve pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression. Clinicians had to implement the guidelines for patients reporting severe levels of the corresponding symptom on the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnire-Core-15-Palliative Care (EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL) questionnaire, i.e., patient reports of ‘quite a bit’ or ‘very much’ of the symptom [22].

The aim of this study was to investigate how the levels of pain, dyspnea, constipation, and depression changed from the start of specialist palliative care to 1–4 weeks later. Further, the study aimed to examine whether the change in the level of each symptom was associated with (1) the timing of guideline implementation (i.e., whether the patients were admitted to a palliative care service before or after the relevant guideline was implemented), and (2) whether patients received treatment according to guidelines.

Methods

Study design and patients

This national study evaluated compliance with guideline implementation in routine palliative care,[23] by evaluating the longitudinal effect on the level of pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression in advanced cancer patients, before and after physicians adopted and implemented a treatment guideline for each of the four symptoms in the 44 specialist palliative care services in Denmark.

This study included data for patients who: (1) were admitted to any of the 44 specialist palliative care services during the improvement project period i.e., between 7 September 2017 and 30 June 2019, (2) had a diagnosis of advanced cancer, (3) were at least 18 years old, (4) answered the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL at the first day of contact with the palliative service or up to three days before, (5) reported severe levels (i.e., scoring ‘quite a bit’ or ‘very much’) of pain, dyspnea, constipation and/or depression on the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL questionnaire at the start of palliative care, and (6) completed a second symptom assessment using the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL at follow-up (1–4 weeks later).

Data collection

All data from the improvement project i.e., information on patient sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, symptom scores reported on the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL (at the start of palliative care and follow-up), whether guidelines were followed (or not) and the type of interventions delivered to patients when physicians followed a guideline was registered in and collected from the Danish Palliative Care Database.

Reporting to this database is mandatory, and it contains relevant sociodemographic and clinical data, as well as patient-reported symptoms for around 60% of patients admitted to specialist palliative care since 2010 [24]. Symptom data is collected by the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL questionnaire at the start of palliative care and at follow-up. Clinicians in each palliative service collected patient information on paper, which was later registered in a web-based system (“Clinical Measurement System”) and delivered to the Danish Palliative Care Database [25].

Setting

Danish specialist palliative care is free of charge and is provided by physicians, nurses, and other professionals (e.g., psychologists) in hospital teams and hospices [26]. The majority of patients admitted to specialist palliative care have a diagnosis of advanced cancer (90%), suffer from multiple physical and psychological symptoms and their median survival from the time of referral to specialist palliative care is about 38 days [27]. In 2019, there was 44 specialist palliative care services divided into 25 palliative care teams in hospitals and 19 hospices [27]. Of the 25 palliative care teams, 5 provided inpatient and outpatient services and 20 provided only outpatient services [27].

Guideline implementation during the improvement project

The national improvement project method was introduced in the Danish Healthcare System in 2015, and its first initiative was the “Learning and Quality Teams Palliative Care”, which was implemented across all specialist palliative care services between 2017 and 2019 [28]. Before the project started, managers, physicians, nurses and secretaries from all palliative care services involved in the project developed technical competences through learning seminars, educational programs and national meetings [21].

During the project, physicians completed a paper survey for each patient, where they reported the symptom scores and whether they followed a treatment guideline (yes/no) when a patient qualified for it (e.g., a patient reporting severe level of dyspnea on the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL at the start of palliative care was eligible for the dyspnea guideline), as well as what type of interventions were provided to the patient.

The four treatment guidelines designed for the improvement project comprised a summary of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions recommended in the clinical guidelines already developed by the Danish Multidisciplinary Cancer Group for Palliative Care (DMCG-PAL) [29]. These guidelines were available in each palliative care service in print and online on the DMCG-PAL website.

Pharmacological interventions included, e.g., recommendations on the use of opioids for pain, [30] benzodiazepines for dyspnea, [31] and conventional laxatives to treat patients with constipation, [32] whereas non-pharmacological interventions included psychological therapy and collaborative care treatment recommended especially for patients with clinic depression [33]. In addition, all guidelines recommended ‘patient education’ on their disease and ‘caregiver training’ to relieve symptoms [34].

Further details on the type and frequency of interventions delivered during the improvement project have been reported [35].

Symptom assessment

The EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL questionnaire is an abbreviated version of the EORTC Core Quality of Life Questionnaire (QLQ-C30) developed for patients in palliative care [22]. This instrument measures the level of 9 symptoms/functions (including pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression) and overall quality of life. The symptoms/functions are scored 1 (not at all), 2 (a little), 3 (quite a bit) or 4 (very much) while overall quality of life is scored from 1 (very poor) to 7 (excellent). The systematic use of this questionnaire in specialist palliative care has been a Danish national quality standard since 2010 [26].

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to present the characteristics of patients completing the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL at both symptom assessments during the improvement project.

A patient qualified for treatment according to a guideline if reporting a severe level of a symptom on the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL, i.e., reporting ‘quite a bit’ (3) or ‘very much’ (4) to the corresponding item for pain, dyspnea, constipation, or depression. The EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL items for the four symptoms were linearly converted into 0-100 scales, where 100 represents the worst symptom score [36]. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess changes in the level of each symptom (mean scores) from the start of palliative care to follow-up.

Multiple linear regression analyses were used to evaluate significant associations between changes in symptom severity and two independent variables:

Timing of guideline implementation

This dichotomous variable was calculated by dividing patients into those who were admitted to a palliative care service before the start of the implementation of the corresponding guideline and those who were admitted afterwards. During the improvement project, each palliative care service was able to decide when to start implementing a treatment guideline i.e., when they felt that the staff was sufficiently trained and ready. We defined that a service had started the implementation of the corresponding guideline on the date the fifth patient received treatment according to the guideline [35].

Receiving treatment according to guidelines

We computed this variable by dividing patients into those who received treatment according to a guideline (i.e., when the physician reported that the corresponding guideline had been followed for a patient) and those who were reported not to receive treatment according to the guideline.

These multiple linear regressions were controlled for all available patient characteristics (i.e., sex, age, having children, residence, cohabitation status, cancer diagnosis, type of palliative service and out/inpatient status). The baseline score and the interaction between timing of implementation and receiving treatment according to guidelines were added to the model for each symptom. The interactions were removed if insignificant. The significance level used was p = 0.05.

Data analyses were performed using the statistical software SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1.

Results

Study population

Figure 1 shows the inclusion of the study population. From 7 September 2017 to 30 June 2019, 11,330 advanced cancer patients answered the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL at the start of Danish specialist palliative care and were included in the improvement project. Of these, 4,640 eligible patients also completed the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL 1–4 weeks later, and 3,879 (84%) of these qualified for one or more treatment guidelines (i.e., reported severe levels of pain, dyspnea, constipation, or depression on the initial EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL) and thus, were included in this study.

Inclusion of patients

Among the 3,879 included patients, 65% qualified for treatment according to the pain guideline, 34% to the dyspnea guideline, 28% to the constipation guideline, and 23% qualified for treatment according to the depression guideline (Fig. 1).

Around half of the included patients were females and a third were 70–79 years old. The majority of patients had adult children (81.1%) and lived in their private residence (96.9%) with their spouse or partner (54.4%). The most common cancer diagnoses were lung cancer (24.4%) and cancer in the digestive system (19.5%) (Table 1).

Change in the level of symptoms and associations

Figure 2 shows changes in the level of pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression from the start of palliative care to follow-up. Overall, there was significant improvement in the level of all four symptoms (p < 0.001) during the project. The changes in the mean scores differed from 20 for dyspnea to 43 for constipation.

We found no significant associations between the change in the level of pain, constipation and depression and whether patients received treatment according to the corresponding guideline, whereas dyspnea decreased significantly more among patients who did not receive treatment according to guidelines (Table 2). We also did not find significant interactions (i.e., interaction between timing of implementation and receiving treatment according to guidelines) for any of the symptoms.

Discussion

Main findings

In this study, we retrospectively evaluated guideline implementation in routine palliative care by investigating how the level of pain, dyspnea, constipation, and depression changed from the start of specialist palliative care to 1–4 weeks later and whether the change in each of the four symptoms was associated with the timing of guideline implementation and receiving treatment according to clinical guidelines.

We found that the levels of pain, dyspnea, constipation, and depression decreased significantly among the advanced cancer patients in specialist palliative care who qualified for the guidelines. The timing of guideline implementation and receiving treatment according to guidelines was associated with improvement of only one of the four symptoms, i.e., dyspnea, where a significantly larger improvement in dyspnea was observed after services started the implementation of the dyspnea guideline. To our surprise, dyspnea decreased more among those patients who did not receive treatment according to the corresponding guideline than among those who did.

Comparison with previous literature

The overall improvement in pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression observed during our project is line with a previous longitudinal study reporting that after 6 weeks of implementing the American Society of Clinical Oncology palliative care clinical guidelines in two palliative care clinics, 31 advanced cancer patients significantly improved pain, fatigue and anxiety measured by the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) [17]. Similarly, a randomized controlled trial found that lung cancer patients assigned to the early palliative care (n = 60), i.e., where physicians and nurses had to implement and follow ambulatory clinical guidelines for symptom control during 12 weeks, had better quality of life and reduced depression than patients assigned to the standard care group (n = 40).18

In the current study, the improvement observed in the levels of pain, constipation and depression during the project were not significantly associated with the timing of guideline implementation or receiving treatment according to guidelines. This is similar to what was reported in a randomized controlled trial, where no difference was seen after 1–4 weeks between 329 advanced cancer patients who received interventions recommended in the pain guidelines for moderate to severe pain and 359 patients who received usual care [20]. In our study, this may have happened because patients were treated equally well for pain and other symptoms regardless of whether physicians reported that they had followed the relevant guidelines for treating each symptom. Also, the clinical guidelines were known at the time and may not have revealed any new practices, so well-trained palliative care physicians’ knowledge and ability to help patients would be the same before and after their implementation. Furthermore, this lack of significant associations may also be related to the existing patient-centered care provided in the specialized palliative care services.

The small (4.9 on a 0-100 scale) but significant improvement observed for dyspnea scores after the services started the implementation of the dyspnea guideline and among those patients who did not receive treatment according to the dyspnea guideline may be random findings, which may be related to several pragmatic reasons, for example, whether physicians had sufficient time and motivation to complete the paper survey informing whether they followed or not a relevant guideline for each patient. However, the improvement related to implementation of guidelines may also reveal that a true improvement in care took place. As we reported in our previous study,35 qualitative data to understand the barriers (or facilitators) that clinicians may have had implementing the four guidelines and to what extent they viewed treatment according to the guidelines as different from their usual practice was not collected during the project.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is that it included a large sample of 3,879 cancer patients admitted to all specialist palliative care services in Denmark, who completed symptom assessments over time. Although we found significant improvement in the four symptoms during the project, there were some limitations that should be acknowledged. Our study did not use randomised allocation to a defined control group (receiving usual treatment) and an intervention group (receiving interventions clearly different treatment), as previous control trials did [18–20]. Instead we evaluated the impact of implementation based on the temporal pattern (before/after). Whether patients ‘received’ or ‘did not receive’ treatment according to guidelines was based on registrations from physicians on each patient and this registration may to some extent be subjective, and physicians may in some cases have forgotten to register when treatment was provided according to guidelines. It should also be noted that this project only included patients reporting high levels of pain, dyspnea, constipation or depression (i.e., scores 3–4 in the EORTC QLQ-C15-PAL) at admittance to palliative care, allowing more room for improvement, since the literature has shown that patients with high initial scores tend to show greatest improvement over time [37, 38]. This is important to be aware of when interpreting the magnitude of change in scores over time for each symptom. However, this concerns all patients in the study and ensures that the guidelines were studied in a relevant patient population.

Future studies investigating the effect of implementing clinical guidelines for the treatment of symptoms in patients in palliative care should ideally identify patients who do or do not receive treatment according to the guidelines via random allocation to intervention group versus control group and with more detailed registration of the impact on guidelines on the specific treatments and care delivered. Additionally, implementation strategies, such as team meetings, staff training and educational programs, should be maintained throughout the implementation period to ensure that clinicians improve their adherence to clinical guidelines [39].

Conclusions

In this project, patients with advanced cancer reported improved pain, dyspnea, constipation and depression. However, this symptom improvement was not associated with the implementation of the four guidelines in routine palliative care, maybe except for a small improvement in dyspnea.

We cannot conclude that the implementation of the guidelines did not have a positive effect on patients’ symptoms, since the lack of impact found in this study may be attributed to a modest impact on clinical practice or the observational project design.
