A Lyapunov–PINN framework for global stability of an SEIR epidemic model with educational interventions
Authors: Shahbaz Ahmad, Gunesh Kumar, Azhar Iqbal Kashif Butt, Muneerah Al Nuwairan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, the global dynamical behavior of an extended SEIR-type disease model is studied using Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). To capture the impact of educational interventions on disease transmission, the model includes two infectious classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_2$$\end{document}, representing individuals with and without education, respectively. The basic reproduction number, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document}, is derived to determine the existence and stability of disease-free and endemic equilibrium states. A Lyapunov-based loss function is incorporated into the PINN framework, allowing the network to enforce stability constraints while learning the system dynamics. The proposed framework utilizes structural symmetry within the compartmental dynamics to enhance the learning of Lyapunov functions and improve stability verification. This hybrid approach enables PINNs to validate global stability properties in a physics-consistent and data-efficient manner, in addition to approximating the solution trajectories of the disease model. The proposed framework builds upon conventional analytical methods in epidemiological modeling, highlighting the capability of Physics-Informed Neural Networks to accurately represent and analyze complex epidemic dynamics.

Introduction

The increasing complexity of infectious disease dynamics has stimulated the development of advanced computational approaches for epidemiological modeling. In recent years, artificial neural networks have emerged as a powerful alternative to classical analytical and numerical techniques. Unlike traditional approaches that often rely on simplifying assumptions or closed-form solutions, neural networks possess strong approximation capabilities, enabling them to capture highly nonlinear interactions inherent in disease transmission processes. This flexibility allows epidemiological systems to be modeled in a data-driven manner while accommodating complex relationships among state variables.

When trained on reliable epidemiological data, neural network models are capable of identifying latent dependencies, incorporating behavioral and environmental influences, and reconstructing transmission patterns that may not be easily detectable using standard compartmental frameworks. Consequently, these approaches have been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including outbreak prediction, estimation of disease burden, and risk-based population stratification1–3. Their effectiveness in capturing temporal dynamics and uncovering dominant transmission mechanisms has been demonstrated across diverse epidemiological settings, making them valuable tools for public health decision-making4,5.

Despite these advantages, purely data-driven models often lack consistency with established biological principles. To address this limitation, recent advances in scientific machine learning have focused on integrating physical laws and governing mechanisms directly into the learning process. Within this context, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have gained significant attention. PINNs incorporate the underlying system of differential equations into the training objective by embedding them into the loss function, thereby constraining the learned solution to satisfy known dynamical laws6,7. This hybrid formulation reduces reliance on large datasets while ensuring that the model remains physically and biologically consistent.

An important feature of PINNs is their mesh-free nature, which eliminates the need for discretization in time or space. This property enables efficient handling of high-dimensional and nonlinear dynamical systems, while maintaining numerical stability and computational efficiency8,9. As a result, PINNs provide a robust framework for solving inverse and forward problems in epidemiology.

Mathematical models remain fundamental tools for understanding the mechanisms of infectious disease transmission. Compartmental models, in particular, offer a systematic framework for analyzing the progression of epidemics and evaluating control strategies. A key component of these models is the incidence function, which governs the rate at which susceptible individuals become infected.

Classical epidemic models frequently employ the bilinear incidence function, as introduced in the foundational works of Anderson and May10 and further developed by Hethcote11. This formulation assumes that new infections occur through homogeneous mixing between susceptible and infectious populations, leading to a transmission rate proportional to the product SI. While this assumption simplifies analysis, it does not adequately reflect realistic contact structures in heterogeneous populations.

In practical scenarios, contact patterns are influenced by social structure, behavioral adaptation, and environmental factors, which violate the homogeneous mixing assumption. Furthermore, bilinear incidence fails to capture saturation effects that arise when the number of infectious individuals becomes large. In such cases, limitations in contact rates and behavioral responses tend to reduce effective transmission. These limitations have motivated the development of more general incidence functions that incorporate nonlinear and saturating effects, as supported by studies such as12,13.

To address these limitations, several generalized incidence formulations have been proposed in the literature. Korobeinikov and Maini14 introduced a class of incidence functions of the form f(S)h(I) and analyzed their stability properties in epidemic models. This framework was later extended to more general forms f(S, I), allowing joint dependence on both susceptible and infectious populations15,16. Similarly, Liu et al.17 proposed a nonlinear incidence function of the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta I^{p}S}{1 + \alpha I^{q}}$$\end{document}, which incorporates saturation effects and has been widely adopted in subsequent epidemiological studies18–20. In particular, saturated incidence functions such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta SI}{1+\alpha I}$$\end{document} and its generalized form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta I^{p}S}{1+\alpha I^{q}}$$\end{document} provide a biologically meaningful representation of transmission dynamics by accounting for reduced contact rates at higher infection levels due to crowding effects, behavioral changes, and limited interaction capacity. Such formulations have been shown to yield more realistic epidemic dynamics compared to the classical bilinear incidence assumption13,17. From a biological viewpoint, nonlinear incidence functions reflect the fact that disease transmission does not increase indefinitely with the number of infectious individuals. In realistic populations, effective contacts saturate due to behavioral changes, limited social mixing, and crowding effects. Thus, transmission tends to level off at high infection levels rather than grow linearly. Saturated forms such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta SI}{1+\alpha I}$$\end{document} and its generalization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\beta I^{p}S}{1+\alpha I^{q}}$$\end{document} capture this effect by reducing the marginal impact of infective, providing a more realistic description of disease spread compared to the classical bilinear incidence13,17.

More recent contributions include the work of Gómez et al.21, who investigated global stability in an SCIR framework, and Ghori et al.22, who developed a fractional-order SEIR model with saturated incidence. Naik et al.23,24 further explored discrete-time and nonlinear treatment effects in epidemic systems. These studies highlight the importance of incorporating realistic transmission mechanisms into mathematical models.

Motivated by these developments, the present study employs Physics-Informed Neural Networks to analyze the dynamics of an SEIR model with migration. A fully connected neural network is implemented within the PyTorch framework to approximate the solution of the governing system of ordinary differential equations. The training process is guided by a composite loss function that enforces agreement with initial conditions and ensures consistency with the governing equations through physics-based residuals. In addition, a Lyapunov-based constraint is incorporated into the loss function to enforce stability properties during training.

Through this formulation, the neural network simultaneously approximates the temporal evolution of the epidemiological states S(t), E(t), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_1(t)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_2(t)$$\end{document}, while also learning a Lyapunov function and its time derivative. By embedding structural and stability constraints into the learning process, the proposed framework enhances robustness and provides a reliable approach for analyzing nonlinear epidemic dynamics.

Despite the substantial progress in both classical epidemiological modeling and neural-network-based approaches, several important limitations remain. Traditional compartmental models often rely on simplified assumptions, such as homogeneous mixing and bilinear incidence, which may not adequately capture complex and nonlinear transmission dynamics observed in real-world epidemics. On the other hand, purely data-driven neural network models, while flexible, generally lack adherence to the underlying biological and dynamical principles governing disease spread, which can lead to physically inconsistent or unstable predictions, particularly in data-scarce scenarios. Although Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) provide a promising framework by incorporating governing equations into the learning process, existing studies have largely focused on standard epidemic models and do not sufficiently address the integration of migration effects, complex transmission structures, and stability constraints within a unified framework. In particular, the incorporation of Lyapunov-based stability conditions directly into the PINN training process remains relatively unexplored. These limitations highlight the need for a modeling approach that simultaneously ensures data-driven flexibility, dynamical consistency, and stability preservation in the analysis of nonlinear epidemic systems.

The novelty of this work lies in the integration of migration dynamics and Lyapunov-based stability constraints within a Physics-Informed Neural Network framework for an SEIR model. Unlike standard approaches, the proposed method simultaneously learns the epidemiological states and enforces stability properties during training, providing a unified and stability-aware data-driven modeling approach.

The aim of this study is to develop a robust and physically consistent computational framework for analyzing the dynamics of an SEIR epidemic model with migration using Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). In particular, the study seeks to approximate the solution of the governing system of differential equations through a fully connected neural network while simultaneously enforcing the intrinsic dynamical structure of the model. To achieve this, a composite loss function is constructed that incorporates initial conditions, differential equation residuals, and Lyapunov-based stability constraints, ensuring that the learned solution remains both accurate and dynamically consistent. Furthermore, the proposed framework is designed to simultaneously approximate the epidemiological state variables and an associated Lyapunov function, thereby integrating stability analysis directly into the learning process. The effectiveness and reliability of the proposed approach are demonstrated through numerical experiments and comparisons with classical numerical methods, highlighting its capability to handle nonlinear epidemic systems in a stable and efficient manner. The proposed framework is further designed to be robust with respect to variations in initial conditions and parameter settings, and the incorporation of physics-based and stability constraints promotes convergence toward physically consistent solutions.

Model without migration

This study investigates a modified SEIR model under the assumption of a closed population, with an emphasis on understanding the mechanisms governing disease transmission in the absence of migration. Employing this modeling framework allows for a systematic exploration of the primary drivers of disease transmission within a population. It facilitates the analysis of infection spread across spatial domains and provides a basis for assessing the projected effects of various intervention strategies. By introducing well-justified simplifying assumptions, the model can be expressed in a tractable form that retains the essential transmission mechanisms, thereby creating a foundational structure upon which more detailed or extended studies can be developed. Such an abstraction clarifies the most influential factors governing epidemic progression and establishes a conceptual basis for designing practical control measures and guiding public health decision-making. In the subsequent section, we analyze the qualitative dynamics of the system in detail, with emphasis on the case where migration between populations is absent.

Physics-informed neural network

Physics-Informed Neural Networks incorporate the system’s governing equations into the training process, guaranteeing that the resulting model adheres to the underlying physical laws. Specially created loss functions are used to incorporate this prior knowledge—such as basic physical principles or expert domain insights—into PINNs. Typically, such prior information is expressed through ordinary or partial differential equations (ODEs/PDEs). The network is trained by concurrently tuning its weights and biases along with the parameters governed by the underlying physical laws. Overall, the total loss function comprises two components: a data loss, representing the difference between network predictions and observed data, and a residual loss, which enforces adherence of the neural network to the system’s governing differential equations. SEIR model-informed machine learning

This section details how the SEIR model is incorporated into the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework as embedded prior knowledge. The central idea of the PINN methodology is to embed the governing differential equations of the SEIR epidemic model directly into the neural network training process. This approach allows the model to combine observed epidemiological data with the fundamental mechanisms governing disease spread, thereby improving both the efficiency of the training process and the network’s capacity to generalize to unseen scenarios. Purely data-driven neural network methods rely exclusively on the availability of observational data for training. In epidemiological applications, such data are frequently incomplete or contaminated by measurement errors. The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) approach addresses this challenge by incorporating the governing differential equations of the SEIR system directly into the loss function, treating them as dynamic constraints during training. This integration guides the network to generate outputs that adhere to the intrinsic biological principles of disease transmission, thereby maintaining robust and consistent predictions even in situations where observational data are limited or affected by noise. During the training process, the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) receives the temporal variable t as input and produces the compartmental states \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(S(t), E(t), I_1(t), I_2(t))^\top$$\end{document} as output. The corresponding time derivatives of these states are computed using automatic differentiation, a capability inherently supported by the PyTorch library. Discrepancies between these derivatives and those specified by the SEIR model define the physics-informed residuals, which provide structured guidance for the network during learning. By combining these residuals with the data-driven loss, which quantifies deviations from observed epidemiological measurements, the network optimizes a comprehensive objective that balances adherence to the underlying dynamics with fidelity to available data. Throughout training, the neural network simultaneously models the temporal dynamics of the SEIR system and infers the unknown epidemiological parameters. To incorporate global stability analysis within the PINN framework, a Lyapunov-based loss term is introduced to ensure that the derivative of the Lyapunov function along system trajectories remains negative definite. In this context, the derived solution remains compatible with the experimental observations, fulfills the constraints imposed by the governing ordinary differential equations, and maintains the stability properties of the equilibrium confirmed through analytical reasoning. The equilibrium state of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SEI_{1}I_{2}$$\end{document} system is represented by the vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(S^{*}, E^{*}, I_{1}^{*}, I_{2}^{*})^{\top }$$\end{document}. Consider the following candidate Lyapunov function V(t) for the system:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} V(t)&= S - S^* - S^* \ln \!\left( \frac{S}{S^*} \right) + E - E^* - E^* \ln \!\left( \frac{E}{E^*} \right) \\ &\quad + A \Bigl [ I_1 - I_1^* - I_1^* \ln \!\left( \frac{I_1}{I_1^*} \right) \Bigr ] + B \Bigl [ I_2 - I_2^* - I_2^* \ln \!\left( \frac{I_2}{I_2^*} \right) \Bigr ], \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the constants are defined as28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A = \frac{\mu + k}{k}, \qquad B = \frac{\beta \eta S^* I_2^*}{\sigma I_1^* + \alpha _2 \sigma I_1^* I_2^*}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The temporal derivative of the Lyapunov function V(t) can be expressed as:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \dot{V}(t)&= \left( 1 - \frac{S^*}{S} \right) \frac{dS}{dt} + \left( 1 - \frac{E^*}{E} \right) \frac{dE}{dt} \\ &\quad + A \left( 1 - \frac{I_1^*}{I_1} \right) \frac{dI_1}{dt} + B \left( 1 - \frac{I_2^*}{I_2} \right) \frac{dI_2}{dt}. \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

To guarantee the global stability criterion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t) \le 0$$\end{document}, a Lyapunov-inspired mean squared error term is defined as30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} MSE_{\textrm{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \Big [ \textrm{ReLU}\big ( \dot{V}(t_{i}) \big ) \Big ]^{2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the rectified linear unit function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ReLU}(x) = \max (0, x)$$\end{document}, ensures that only instances in which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document} is positive contribute to the loss, thereby penalizing violations of the stability condition. Within the PINN methodology, the total loss function is expressed as31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Total Loss} = \lambda _{1} MSE_{\textrm{IC}} + \lambda _{2} MSE_{\textrm{RES}} + \lambda _{3} MSE_{\textrm{Lyap}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{3}$$\end{document} determine the relative contributions of the initial condition term, the differential equation residual term, and the Lyapunov-based stability term to the overall optimization objective. During training, the neural network predicts the state variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(S(t), E(t), I_1(t), I_2(t))^\top$$\end{document}, computes their time derivatives via automatic differentiation, and evaluates the Lyapunov function V(t) along with its derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document} at the chosen collocation points. Whenever \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document} attains positive values, the Lyapunov-based loss term increases, prompting the optimizer to modify the network parameters so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document} remains non-positive, thereby incorporating the global stability property into the learned solution (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The schematic diagram of Lyapunov-informed PINN architecture, guiding the network toward globally stable solutions by penalizing positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document}.

The schematic diagram of Lyapunov-informed PINN architecture, guiding the network toward globally stable solutions by penalizing positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t)$$\end{document}.

The proposed Lyapunov-based loss term is motivated by Lyapunov’s direct method for stability analysis. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x(t) = (S(t), E(t), I_1(t), I_2(t))^\top$$\end{document} denote the state vector and let V(x) be a continuously differentiable, positive definite function with respect to the equilibrium point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^* = (S^*, E^*, I_1^*, I_2^*)^\top$$\end{document}. According to Lyapunov theory, if the time derivative of V along system trajectories satisfies32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \dot{V}(x(t)) \le 0 \quad \text {for all } t \ge 0, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}then the equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^*$$\end{document} is stable, and if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(x(t)) < 0$$\end{document} for all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x \ne x^*$$\end{document}, the equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable.

Within the proposed PINN framework, the neural network provides an approximation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}(t)$$\end{document} of the true solution. The Lyapunov function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V(\hat{x}(t))$$\end{document} and its time derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(\hat{x}(t))$$\end{document} are computed using automatic differentiation. The Lyapunov-informed loss term33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \left[ \max \left( 0, \dot{V}(t_i)\right) \right] ^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}penalizes any violation of the condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t) \le 0$$\end{document} at the collocation points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{t_i\}_{i=1}^N$$\end{document}.

Minimization of this loss enforces \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t_i) \le 0$$\end{document} over the training set. When combined with the residual loss enforcing the governing differential equations, the learned solution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}(t)$$\end{document} is constrained to follow system dynamics while simultaneously satisfying the Lyapunov stability condition. Under the standard assumptions of PINNs, namely sufficient network expressivity and dense sampling of the time domain, the learned solution converges (in an approximate sense) to trajectories for which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{V}(t) \le 0$$\end{document} almost everywhere. Consequently, the Lyapunov function becomes non-increasing along the learned trajectories, implying stability in the sense of Lyapunov.

It is important to note that the stability guarantee obtained through the Lyapunov-informed loss is approximate and depends on the quality of the neural network approximation and the distribution of collocation points. In the limit of vanishing training error and dense sampling, the proposed framework recovers the classical Lyapunov stability condition.

The baseline model achieves reasonable accuracy but exhibits a high stability violation percentage (18.7%), indicating frequent violations of the Lyapunov condition. Although the Lyapunov-only model trivially satisfies the stability condition, it fails to approximate the correct system dynamics due to the absence of governing equation constraints. This confirms that the Lyapunov-based loss term plays a critical complementary role when combined with the residual and initial condition losses, ensuring both accuracy and stability of the learned solution.

The computational performance of the proposed PINN framework depends primarily on the network architecture, the number of collocation points, and the training iterations required for convergence. Compared to classical numerical methods such as the Runge–Kutta scheme, the PINN approach generally incurs a higher computational cost due to the optimization process involved in training the neural network. However, this cost is offset by several advantages, including its mesh-free formulation, flexibility in handling high-dimensional systems, and its ability to incorporate data and physical constraints simultaneously.

In terms of scalability, the proposed framework remains applicable to more complex and higher-dimensional epidemic models, although increased model complexity typically requires deeper networks and longer training times. Despite this, PINNs offer a unified framework for solving forward and inverse problems, making them particularly suitable for large-scale and data-driven epidemiological applications where traditional methods may face limitations.

Sensitivity analysis and weight tuning strategy

The total loss function is defined as38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = \lambda _1 \textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{IC}} + \lambda _2 \textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{RES}} + \lambda _3 \textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{Lyap}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The performance of the proposed framework depends strongly on the selection of the weighting parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _3$$\end{document}. To analyze their influence, we performed a sensitivity study using different combinations of weights during training.

The weighting parameters were varied over a range of values motivated by preliminary numerical experiments:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _1 \in \{10^{-7},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}, \qquad \lambda _2 \in \{0.3,0.5,0.6,1.0\}, \qquad \lambda _3 \in \{0.001,0.01,0.1\}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For each configuration, the model performance was evaluated using the relative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _2$$\end{document} error, residual loss, and stability violation percentage (SVP).

The experiments showed that small values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _3$$\end{document} lead to accurate approximations of the system dynamics but may allow occasional violations of the Lyapunov stability condition. Conversely, excessively large values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _3$$\end{document} overemphasize the stability constraint and may reduce the accuracy of the learned dynamics.

The best trade-off between stability and accuracy was obtained for40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _1 = 0.01, \qquad \lambda _2 = 1.0, \qquad \lambda _3 = 0.01, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which were therefore selected in the final implementation.

The residual loss term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{RES}}$$\end{document} plays the dominant role in capturing the governing dynamics, while the Lyapunov-based loss term acts as a complementary regularization mechanism that enforces stability. The inclusion of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{MSE}_{\textrm{Lyap}}$$\end{document} significantly reduced the stability violation percentage without degrading the predictive accuracy of the PINN solution.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis confirms that a balanced choice of loss weights is essential for simultaneously achieving accurate dynamics and stable solutions.

Conclusions

This study analyzes an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SEI_1I_2R$$\end{document} epidemic model for global stability by introducing a Lyapunov–PINN framework. Classical Lyapunov methods were employed to establish conditions ensuring the global asymptotic stability of the disease-free and endemic equilibrium points. The PINN training procedure incorporated a loss term derived from Lyapunov theory to satisfy the stability requirements. To learn solution trajectories that naturally follow stability constraints, the network was trained to satisfy both the model equations and a decaying Lyapunov function. Numerical experiments with various values of the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0$$\end{document} confirmed the theoretical results. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0 < 1$$\end{document}, the infection dies out, and the system moves toward a disease-free state. However, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_0 > 1$$\end{document}, it converges towards an endemic state.

Moreover, the results obtained from the PINN framework exhibit strong concordance with those produced by classical RK4 simulations, indicating that the hybrid Lyapunov-PINN approach effectively captures the epidemic dynamics while reinforcing global stability. Taken together, these observations imply that the Lyapunov-constrained PINN not only replicates the behavior of the underlying mathematical model but also confirms analytical stability conditions in a data-efficient and physically consistent manner. This integrated methodology thus provides a robust computational tool for supporting policy decisions in infectious disease modeling, enhancing conventional analytical techniques through the incorporation of machine learning.

To capture heterogeneous mixing patterns, random perturbations, and incubation or reporting delays, the proposed Lyapunov–PINN framework can be further extended to spatially distributed, stochastic, or delay differential SEIR models in future studies. The applicability of this approach for real-time policy-driven epidemic modeling would also be strengthened by a thorough theoretical investigation of the convergence, error bounds, and stability-preserving characteristics of PINNs with time-varying epidemiological parameters.

Although the present study focuses on a deterministic formulation, the proposed PINN framework can be extended to incorporate stochastic dynamics and uncertainty quantification. In particular, stochastic epidemic models governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs), uncertain parameters, or noisy observations can be integrated into the PINN framework through stochastic residual formulations or ensemble-based training strategies. In addition, Bayesian PINNs and other probabilistic extensions may be employed to quantify predictive uncertainty and estimate confidence intervals. Although such extensions would increase the computational complexity of the training process, they would significantly improve the reliability and robustness of the model for realistic epidemiological applications involving noisy and incomplete data. These directions represent promising avenues for future research.
