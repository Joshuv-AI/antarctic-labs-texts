Constructive discretization and approximation in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
Abdellah Chkifa, Matthieu Dolbeault, David Krieg, Mario Ullrich

Abstract
We generalize the sparsification algorithm of Batson, Spielman and Srivastava, making one part of the result dimension-independent. In particular, we recover discretization inequalities in L 2 L_{2} - and sup-norms on general finite-dimensional subspaces, prove a suitable infinite-dimensional variant, and discuss the implications for the error of least-squares approximation based on samples. This gives a more constructive version of several recently established approximation bounds, some of which relied on the stronger and less constructive result of Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava. We also improve the constants and oversampling factors in these results.



1
Introduction

In recent years, much
progress has been made in norm discretization and sampling recovery, first
by considering concentration inequalities for random points,
then by applying a sample sparsification using
[
31
]
or
[
4
]
.
This follows the trend observed in other fields, including graph sparsification, frame discretization, matrix
sketching, subspace selection, and more generally randomized numerical linear algebra.

Here, we prove a generalization of the result from
[
4
]
by Batson, Spielman, and Srivastava (BSS), and expand on its implications
for the problems of norm discretization and sampling recovery.
The original result from
[
4
]
can be stated as follows:

Proposition 1

(
[
4
]
, Theorem 3.1)
.

Let
D
be a finite set and let
\mu
be the uniform distribution on
D
. Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}

be an orthonormal family in
L_{2}(D,\mu)
.
Then, for any
n>m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}>0}
such that

\lambda_{\min}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\right)\geq\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{2}

and

\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\right)\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{2}.

Here and in the following, we denote by

\lambda_{\min}(A)
and
\lambda_{\max}(A)
the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
A
.
The points and weights from Proposition
1
can be constructed in polynomial time.

In
[
4
]
, this theorem is applied to the problem of graph sparsification, see also
[
17
,
37
,
38
]
.
For our applications, we want to generalize the result in three ways: by allowing general measure spaces
(D,\mu)
; by decoupling the lower and upper frame bounds with two different
families of
L_{2}
-functions; and by making the upper frame bound independent of the dimension, thus allowing to consider very large and even infinite-dimensional families.

The first two kinds of generalizations are already available in the literature. General probability spaces are treated, for instance, in
[
13
, Theorem 6.4]
.
The second kind of generalization was achieved in
[
7
]
.
The result of
[
7
]
, called dual set spectral sparsification, can be reformulated as follows.

Proposition 2

(
[
7
]
, Lemma 13)
.

Let
D
be a finite set and let
\mu
be the uniform distribution on
D
. Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}
and
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{M})^{\top}

be orthonormal families in
L_{2}(D,\mu)
.
Then for any integer
n>m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}>0}
such that

\lambda_{\min}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\right)\geq\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)^{2}

and

\,\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}\right)\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{M}{n}}\right)^{2}.

Our main contribution, stated below, is the third kind of generalization. Namely, we replace the dimension
M
by an effective dimension in the
upper frame bound.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(D,\mu)
be a measure space, and let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}

and
b=(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{I}}^{\top}
be families of square-integrable functions on
D
,
where
\mathbb{I}
is at most countable.
Assume that
I:=\int_{D}a(x)a(x)^{*}d\mu(x)
and
J:=\int_{D}b(x)b(x)^{*}d\mu(x)
are positive definite, and that
J

has finite trace and effective dimension

\Tr(J)\,=\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}}\|b_{k}\|_{L_{2}(D,\mu)}^{2}\,<\,\infty\quad\text{and}\quad M:=\frac{\Tr(J)}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}.

Then, for any

n\geq m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}>0}
such that

\lambda_{\min}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\right)\geq\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n}}\right)^{2}\lambda_{\min}(I)

and

\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}\right)\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{M-1}{n}}\right)^{2}\lambda_{\max}(J).

One can retrieve Proposition
2
from Theorem
3
by taking
\mathbb{I}=\{1,\dots,M\}
and assuming that
a
and
b
are orthonormal families. Indeed, in that case, we see that
I
and
J
are the
m\times m
and
M\times M
identity matrices. In turn, Proposition
2
implies Proposition
1
by taking
M=m
and
b=a
.

Remark 4
.

The factor
m-1
instead of
m
in the lower bound is a minor improvement
already appearing in
[
8
]
. It allows to treat the case
n=m
, with the bound

1-\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{m}}>\frac{1}{2m}.

In the same way, the factor
M-1
instead of
M

in the upper bound is most interesting when
J
has rank one, where we recover
[
7
, Lemma 14]
.
This case will be used in Corollary
8
to obtain discretizations with equal weights. We will also leverage these improvements in Propositions
7
and Corollary
11
, when considering families
a
and
b
containing one more function.

.

Throughout the article,
L_{2}(D,\mu)
and its subspaces are assumed to consist of complex-valued functions, not equivalence classes, which is needed for discretization.
Moreover, it is possible to extend our results to Hilbert-valued functions, by combining them with

2
Norm discretization

One of our two applications is the discretization of norms. This problem has recently received much attention. For further reading, we refer to the comprehensive treatments of the topic in
[
16
,
20
,
30
,
41
]
.
The BSS theorem (Proposition
1
) can be stated
equivalently as a result on
L_{2}
-norm discretization,
where one aims to bound the
L_{2}
-norm of a function from a finite-dimensional space in terms of a discrete
\ell_{2}
-norm based on finitely many function evaluations.
Indeed, let
(D,\mu)
be as in Proposition
1
, and

V_{m}\subset L_{2}(D,\mu)
with
\dim(V_{m})=m
.
Then, Proposition
1
states that for every
n>m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)\,\|f\|_{2}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|f(x_{i})|^{2}}\;\leq\;\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)\,\|f\|_{2}

(1)

for all
f\in V_{m}
, where we denote
\|\cdot\|_{p}=\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}(D,\mu)}
.
A similar result for general probability spaces
(D,\mu)
is stated in
[
13
, Theorem 6.4]
.

Also our new result (Theorem
3
) can be restated equivalently in terms of norm discretization.
In the upper bound,
the finite-dimensional space
V_{m}

can be replaced with a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H
, i.e.,
a Hilbert space of functions such that function evaluations are continuous (although the functions themselves are not necessarily continuous).
We assume that
H
is
embedded
in
L_{2}(D,\mu)
continuously

\lambda:=\sup_{f\in H}\frac{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}{\|f\|_{H}^{2}}<\infty

(2)

and injectively

\|f\|_{2}\neq 0\quad\text{for all}\quad f\in H\setminus\{0\},

(3)

and that its kernel
K
has finite trace

\Tr(K):=\int K(x,x)\,d\mu(x)<\infty.

(4)

By
[
39
, Lemma 2.3]
, this last condition ensures that the embedding
H\hookrightarrow L_{2}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. In particular, (
4
) implies (
2
) with
\lambda\leq\Tr(K)
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
(D,\mu)
be a measure space,

V_{m}\subset L_{2}(D,\mu)
be an
m
-dimensional space of functions and let
H
be
a reproducing kernel Hilbert space satisfying conditions (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
).
For
n\geq m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}>0}
such that

\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n}}\right)\,\|f\|_{2}\,\leq\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|f(x_{i})|^{2}}\qquad\text{for all }f\in V_{m}

(5)

and

\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2}}\,\leq\,\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{M-1}{n}}\right)\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,\left\|g\right\|_{H}\qquad\text{for all }g\in H,

(6)

where
M=\Tr(K)/\lambda
is the “effective dimension” of
H
in
L_{2}(D,\mu)
.

Proof.

Let
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}
be an
L_{2}
-orthonormal basis of
V_{m}
,
and
b=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)^{\top}
be the basis of left singular vectors of the embedding
H\hookrightarrow L_{2}
.
Then
I=\int a\,a^{*}d\mu
is the identity, and

J=\int b\,b^{*}d\mu

is diagonal with trace
\Tr(J)=\Tr(K)
and largest entry
\lambda_{\max}(J)=\lambda
,
since
b
is orthonormal in
H
and orthogonal in
L_{2}
.

Let
x_{i}
and
w_{i}
be the points and weights from Theorem
3
, and denote

G=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}.

Then, any function
f\in V_{m}
can be written as
f=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}a_{k}

with
c\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Hence,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|f(x_{i})|^{2}=c\,G\,c^{*}\geq\lambda_{\min}(G)\,|c|^{2}=\lambda_{\min}(G)\,\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

In the same way, decomposing any
g\in H
as
g=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}}c_{k}b_{k}
with
c\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2}=c\,\Gamma\,c^{*}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Gamma)\,\|c\|_{\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})}^{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\Gamma)\,\|g\|_{H}^{2}.\qed

It is additionally possible to bound the sum of the weights as follows.

Proposition 7
.

Let the assumptions of Corollary
6
hold.
If
(D,\mu)
is a probability space and
\int f\,d\mu=0
for all
f\in H
,
then for
n\geq m
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
and weights
{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}>0}

such that (
5
) holds, (
6
) holds with
M
instead of
M-1
, and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\leq\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{M}{n}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Apply Corollary
6
to
\widetilde{H}:=H\oplus\Span\{1\}
, equipped with the norm

\|f+z\|_{\widetilde{H}}^{2}=\|f\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\lambda},\qquad f\in H,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Indeed,
\widetilde{H}
is also a reproducing kernel Hilbert space embedded in
L_{2}(D,\mu)
, with maximal eigenvalue

\tilde{\lambda}:=\sup_{f\in\widetilde{H}}\frac{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}{\|f\|_{\widetilde{H}}^{2}}=\lambda,

and its kernel
\widetilde{K}=K+\lambda
has finite trace

\Tr(\widetilde{K})=\Tr(K)+\lambda
.
This allows to translate (
5
) and (
6
) from
\widetilde{H}
to
H
, and we additionally control the weights through

\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}\leq\left(\sqrt{\lambda}+\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(K)}{n}}\right)\|1\|_{\widetilde{H}}=1+\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(K)}{n\lambda}}.\qed

The discretization inequality (
1
) is obtained for the special case where
H=V_{m}
, equipped with the
L_{2}
-norm, in which case

\lambda=1
and
\Tr(K)=m
.
For the problem of sampling recovery,
as discussed in Section
3
,
we will consider infinite-dimensional spaces
H
.

Another interesting application are one-sided
discretization inequalities with equal weights.
One-sided equal-weight discretization of the
L_{2}
-norm can be obtained in probability spaces
(D,\mu)
by choosing
H
as the one-dimensional space of constant functions.
In combination with a result of Kiefer and Wolfowitz
[
21
]
,
this can even be extended to the discretization of
L_{p}
-norms with
p>2
.
Let
B(D)
be the space of bounded functions on
D
.
In Section
4.3
, we prove the following.

Proposition 8
.

Let
(D,\mu)
be a probability space, and

V_{m}\subset L_{2}(D,\mu)\cap B(D)
with
\dim(V_{m})=m
.
For
n\geq m
, there exist points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n}\in D
such that,
for all
f\in V_{m}
and
2\leq p\leq\infty
,

\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}\right)\cdot\|f\|_{p}\;\leq\;\,m^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|f(x_{i})|^{2}}.

In the case
p\in\{2,\infty\}
, we can take
n
points instead of
2n
points.

In the case
n=m
, the factor
1-\sqrt{\frac{m}{n}}
can be replaced by
\frac{1}{2m}
.

We note that for
p=2
, the assumption
V_{m}\subset L_{2}
is sufficient,
while for
p=\infty
, the assumption
V_{m}\subset B(D)
is enough.
Up to constants, Corollary
8
has been first proven in
[
3
]
in the case
p=2
, and in
[
22
]
for
p>2
.
There, the authors also rely on BSS
[
4
]
,
but first generate a large enough random point set and then apply Proposition
1
.
With our direct approach,
which is based on basically the same algorithm,
see Section
5
,
we can minimize oversampling and improve the constants.

In particular, by putting
n=2m
and estimating the right hand side in terms of the maximum at the points, we obtain
that there are points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2m}\in D
such that, for all
f\in V_{m}
,

\|f\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;(2+\sqrt{2})\,\sqrt{m}\cdot\,\max_{1\leq i\leq 2m}\,\left|f(x_{i})\right|.

As the proof of Proposition
8
refers to the proof of Theorem
3
,
we will present it after the proof of Theorem
3
in Section
4
.

Example 9
.

For general spaces
V_{m}\subset L_{2}(D,\mu)
with a probability space
(D,\mu)
,
it is not possible to have a two-sided discretization with equal weights.
Indeed, consider
D=\{0,1\}
with
\mu(\{0\})=\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
and let
V_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}}
.
Assume that there are
n
points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in D
such that

\alpha\|f\|_{2}^{2}\leq w\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(x_{i})|^{2}\leq\beta\|f\|_{2}^{2},\quad f\in V_{2}

(7)

for some positive
\alpha
,
\beta
and
w
.
The case
f=1
shows that
\alpha\leq wn
.
Due to the lower bound, at least one of the points must be equal to 0, and the upper bound for the indicator function of
\{0\}
gives the condition
w\leq\beta\varepsilon
, hence
n\geq\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\varepsilon^{-1}
.
As
\varepsilon
is arbitrarily small,
there are no fixed values of
\alpha,\beta
and
n

such that (
7
) can be obtained
for every 2-dimensional subspace of every
L_{2}
probability space.

3
Sampling Recovery

Based on the discretization inequalities above,
we easily obtain error bounds for corresponding
weighted least-squares algorithms
on general approximation spaces
V_{m}
.
Assume we are given points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in D

and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0
.
The data is assumed to be of the form
y_{i}=f(x_{i})

for some unknown function
f
, which we want to learn.
We consider the weighted least squares approximation

\tilde{f}:=\argmin_{g\in V_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,|y_{i}-g(x_{i})|^{2}.

(8)

In the following, the points and weights will always be chosen such that the minimizer is uniquely defined.
As before, the points and weights can be constructed by Algorithm
1
that will be discussed in Section
5
.
Via Corollary
6
,
we obtain the following.

Corollary 10
.

Let
(D,\mu)
be a measure space.
Let
H
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space satisfying conditions (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
).
Let
V_{m}\subset H
be an
m
-dimensional subspace and
let
n\geq m
.
Then, there are points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in D

and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0

such that, for any
f\in H
, the weighted least squares approximation (
8
) with
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
satisfies

\big\|f-\tilde{f}\big\|_{2}\,\leq\,\left(1+\frac{1+s}{1-r}\right)\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}\,\big\|f-P_{m}f\big\|_{H},

where

\lambda_{m}:=\sup_{f\in H_{m}}\frac{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}{\|f\|_{H}^{2}},\qquad r:=\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad s:=\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(K_{m})-\lambda_{m}}{n\,\lambda_{m}}}.

Here,
H_{m}
is the
H
-orthogonal complement of
V_{m}
,

P_{m}
is the
H
-orthogonal projection onto
V_{m}
,
and
K_{m}
is the reproducing kernel of
(H_{m},\|\cdot\|_{H})
.

Proof.

Applying Corollary
6
to the spaces
V_{m}
and
H_{m}
gives points and weights such that

(1-r)\|\tilde{g}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|\tilde{g}(x_{i})|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|g(x_{i})|^{2}}\leq(1+s)\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}\,\|g\|_{H},

where
\tilde{g}\in V_{m}
is the weighted least-squares projection of
{g=f-P_{m}f\in H_{m}}
.
As this projection is linear and sends
P_{m}f\in V_{m}
to itself,
\tilde{g}=\tilde{f}-P_{m}f
. Hence

\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}=\|g-\tilde{g}\|_{2}\leq\|g\|_{2}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{1+s}{1-r}\right)\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}\,\|g\|_{H}.\qed

In the case of noisy measurements
y_{i}=f(x_{i})+e_{i}
for arbitrary
e_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, the following extension holds under additional assumptions.

Corollary 11
.

Assume that
(D,\mu)
is a probability space, that
H
satisfies conditions (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
), and that
V_{m}\subset H
has dimension
m
.
Moreover, assume that

V_{m}
contains the Riesz representer of the integral, i.e.,

h=\int K(x,\cdot)\,d\mu(x)\,\in V_{m}.

Then, for
n\geq m
,
there are points

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in D

and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0

such that, for any
f\in H
and
e\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the least squares approximation (
8
) on
V_{m}
with noisy measurements
y_{i}=f(x_{i})+e_{i}
satisfies

\big\|f-\tilde{f}\big\|_{2}\,\leq\,\frac{1+\tilde{s}}{1-r}\,\Big(2\,\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}\,\big\|f-P_{m}f\big\|_{H}+\,\|e\|_{\infty}\Big),

(9)

with
\lambda_{m}
,
K_{m}
,
P_{m}
and
r
as in Corollary
10
,
and
\tilde{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(K_{m})}{n\,\lambda_{m}}}
.

Proof.

Observe that
h\in H
with
\|h\|_{H}\leq\sqrt{\Tr(K)}
.
Moreover, for any function
f
in
H_{m}
, the
H
-orthogonal complement of
V_{m}
, it holds

0=\langle f,h\rangle_{H}=\int\langle f,K(x,\cdot)\rangle_{H}\,d\mu(x)=\int f(x)\,d\mu(x).

Replacing Corollary
6
by Proposition
7
in the proof of Corollary
10
,
the weighted least squares projection
\tilde{f}
of the noisy measurements
y_{i}=f(x_{i})+e_{i}
onto
V_{m}
satisfies (
9
).
Indeed, letting
\hat{f}
be the solution for exact data
f(x_{i})
, we bound

\|f-\hat{f}\|_{2}
as in Corollary
11

(with
s
replaced by
\tilde{s}
) and control the norm of

\hat{f}-\tilde{f}\in V_{m}
via

(1-r)\|\hat{f}-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|(\hat{f}-\tilde{f})(x_{i})|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|e_{i}|^{2}}\leq(1+\tilde{s})\|e\|_{\infty},

where the second inequality comes from the fact that

\hat{f}-\tilde{f}=\argmin\limits_{g\in V_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|e_{i}-g(x_{i})|^{2},

which holds by linearity of (

.

Corollary 16

Proof.

For all

4.1
Recap on linear algebra in infinite dimension

For convenience, before we start with the proof of the main result,
we collect a few properties for the computation with bounded operators on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
, which we see as potentially infinite matrices
A=(A_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{I}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{I}}
.
We refer to
[
35
, Chapter VI]
for a comprehensive treatment.
Consider the class of
positive definite invertible operators

\mathcal{P}=\Big\{A\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{I}}\colon A^{*}=A,\;\sup_{\|x\|_{\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})}=1}x^{*}Ax<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\|x\|_{\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})}=1}x^{*}Ax>0\Big\}.

In finite dimension, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{m}
the set of positive definite matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, and recall that elements of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
or
\mathcal{P}
have an inverse in
\mathcal{P}_{m}
or
\mathcal{P}
.

For self-adjoint operators
A
and
B
, we write
A\preccurlyeq B
(or
B\succcurlyeq A
) if
B-A
is positive semi-definite
and
A\prec B
(or
B\succ A
) if
B-A\in\mathcal{P}
. Clearly,
A\prec B
implies
A\preccurlyeq B
.
Then, the following properties hold for bounded operators
A
and
B
:

(i)

If
A
is self-adjoint and
B\succcurlyeq 0
, then
ABA\succcurlyeq 0
.

(ii)

If
A,B\succcurlyeq 0
and
A
has finite trace, then
AB
has finite trace
\Tr(AB)\geq 0
.

Moreover, if
A
and
B
are positive definite,
\Tr(AB)>0
.

(iii)

Shermann-Morrison formula:
If
A\in\mathcal{P}
,
a\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
and
w\in\mathbb{R}
are such that
1+wa^{*}A^{-1}a>0
,
then
A+waa^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
with

(A+waa^{*})^{-1}=A^{-1}-\frac{wA^{-1}aa^{*}A^{-1}}{1+wa^{*}A^{-1}a}.

(iv)

If
a,b\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
, then
\Tr(ab^{*})=b^{*}a
.

(v)

Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: If
A\succcurlyeq 0
and
B\succcurlyeq 0
have finite trace, then

|\Tr(AB)|^{2}\,\leq\,\Tr(A^{2})\cdot\Tr(B^{2})<\infty.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
3

We first note that we may assume without loss of generality
that
I
is the identity matrix, as explained in
[
4
, Proof of Theorem 1.2]
.
Indeed, if
I
is not the identity matrix,
we apply the theorem for
\tilde{a}:=I^{-1/2}a
in place of
a
.
Then
\widetilde{I}:=\int\tilde{a}(x)\tilde{a}(x)^{*}d\mu(x)
is the identity matrix.
We get points
x_{i}\in D
and weights
w_{i}>0
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tilde{a}(x_{i})\tilde{a}(x_{i})^{*}\succcurlyeq\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{n}}\right)^{2}\widetilde{I}.

Multiplying from the left and right by
I^{1/2}
,
we retrieve the statement by property (
i
).

The basic idea of the proof is to select points
x
and weights
w
one by one,
in such a way that adding the rank-one updates
wa(x)a(x)^{*}
and
wb(x)b(x)^{*}
to matrices
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
B\in\mathcal{P}

modifies their eigenvalues in a controlled way.
This change is quantified via the
lower
and
upper potentials

\Phi(A):=\Tr(A^{-1})\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi(B):=\Tr(JB^{-1}).

Note that
\Phi(A)
and
\Psi(B)
,
for
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
B\in\mathcal{P}
,
are bounded and positive by property (
ii
) since
the positive definite
J
has finite trace,

Y:=A^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad W:=B^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}.

In fact, we even have

A\succcurlyeq\Phi(A)^{-1}I\quad\text{and}\quad B\succcurlyeq\Psi(B)^{-1}J

for all
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
B\in\mathcal{P}
.
To see the second inequality, note that
B^{-1/2}JB^{-1/2}\succcurlyeq 0
by property (
i
) and its norm is hence smaller than its trace, which is equal to
\Psi(B)
, and therefore
\Psi(B)I-B^{-1/2}JB^{-1/2}\succcurlyeq 0
;
multiplying with
B^{1/2}
from both sides gives the inequality.

Before each step, we update
A
and
B
by
increments

\delta,\zeta>0
:
assuming that
A\succ\delta I
, we denote

Z:=(A-\delta I)^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad X:=(B+\zeta J)^{-1}\in\mathcal{P},

which satisfy
Z\succ Y
and
X\prec W
. Taking traces, we obtain

\Tr(Z)>\Tr(Y)=\Phi(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\Tr(JX)<\Tr(JW)=\Psi(B)

(13)

by applying again property (
ii
).
With this, we can define the
lower verifier

L_{A}^{\delta}(a)\,:=\,\frac{a^{*}Z^{2}a}{\Tr(Z-Y)}-a^{*}Za

and
upper verifier

U_{B}^{\zeta}(b):=\frac{b^{*}XJXb}{\Tr(JW-JX)}+b^{*}Xb\geq 0,

which will be used to check
whether the potentials of
A
and
B
increase when adding rank-one updates of the form
waa^{*}
and
-wbb^{*}
and increments
-\delta I
and
\zeta J
.
This is expressed in the following lemma.

Lemma 17
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
,
B\in\mathcal{P}
,

I\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
be the identity,
and
J\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{I}}
be positive definite with finite trace.
For
a\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
,
b\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
and
w>0
,
and for
0<\delta<\Phi(A)^{-1}
and
\zeta>0
,
consider the updated operators

A^{\prime}=A-\delta I+waa^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\prime}=B+\zeta J-wbb^{*}.

a)

If
L_{A}^{\delta}(a)\geq\frac{1}{w}
,
then
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
with
\Phi(A^{\prime})\leq\Phi(A)
.

b)

If
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b)\leq\frac{1}{w}
, then
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
with
\Psi(B^{\prime})\leq\Psi(B)
.

Proof.

We follow
[
4
, Lemmas 3.3–3.4]
.
We first note that

\lambda_{\min}(A)\geq\Phi(A)^{-1}>\delta.

Hence
A\succ\delta I
and
Z=(A-\delta I)^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
.
Since
1+wa^{*}Za>0
for any vector
a\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and weight
w>0
,
the Shermann-Morrison formula shows that
A^{\prime}=Z^{-1}+waa^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
with

\Phi(A^{\prime})\overset{\eqref{property:SM}}{=}\Tr\left(Z-\frac{wZaa^{*}Z}{1+wa^{*}Za}\right)\overset{\eqref{property:circ}}{=}\Tr(Z)-\frac{a^{*}Z^{2}a}{\frac{1}{w}+a^{*}Za}.

(14)

Rearranging the terms, we see that
\Phi(A^{\prime})\leq\Phi(A)

if and only if the lower verifier satisfies
L_{A}^{\delta}(a)\geq\frac{1}{w}
.

We turn to the upper potential.
Since
B\in\mathcal{P}
,
X:=(B+\zeta J)^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Let
b\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
and
w>0
such that
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b)\leq\frac{1}{w}
. Observing that either
Xb=0
or

\frac{1}{w}-b^{*}Xb\geq\frac{b^{*}XJXb}{\Tr(JW-JX)}>0

is positive definite, we see that
1-wb^{*}Xb>0

The Sherman-Morrison formula then shows that the operator

B^{\prime}=X^{-1}-wbb^{*}

\mathcal{P}
, and yields

\Psi(B^{\prime})\overset{\eqref{property:SM}}{=}\Tr(JX)+\frac{\Tr(JXbb^{*}X)}{\frac{1}{w}-b^{*}Xb}\overset{\eqref{property:circ}}{=}\Tr(JX)+\frac{b^{*}XJXb}{\frac{1}{w}-b^{*}Xb}.

Thus, if the upper verifier
satisfies
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b)\leq\frac{1}{w}
, we get
\Psi(B^{\prime})\leq\Psi(B)
.

We now turn to the

For any

We follow

Cleary, the ‘In particular’-part follows from the first part and Lemma

Recalling that

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in D

and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0

such that for
a(x_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
b(x_{i})\in\ell_{2}(\mathbb{I})
,
the operators

A_{n}:=A_{0}-n\delta I+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}

and

B_{n}:=B_{0}+n\zeta J-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}

belong to
\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
\mathcal{P}
, respectively, and their potentials are bounded by

\Phi(A_{n})\leq\Phi(A_{0})=\frac{r}{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi(B_{n})\leq\Psi(B_{0})=\frac{s}{\zeta}.

As

A_{n}\succcurlyeq\Phi(A_{n})^{-1}I\succcurlyeq\frac{\delta}{r}\,I
, this gives

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\succcurlyeq\Big(n\delta+\frac{\delta}{r}-\frac{m\delta}{r}\,\Big)I=(1-r)^{2}I.

(20)

In the same way, as
B_{n}\succcurlyeq\Psi(B_{n})^{-1}J\succcurlyeq\frac{\zeta}{s}\,J
, we use the assumption
s\geq\frac{1}{n}
and the bound
J\preccurlyeq\lambda_{\max}(J)I
to obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}\preccurlyeq\Big(n\zeta-\frac{\zeta}{s}\Big)J+\frac{\zeta}{s}\Tr(J)\,I\preccurlyeq(1+s)^{2}\lambda_{\max}(J)\,I,

(21)

which is the desired result.

In the case
m=1
, we have
r=0
, hence
A_{0}
is not defined and the above proof does not apply. However, we can replace the lower verifier
L_{A}^{\delta}(a(x))
by
L(a(x))=n|a(x)|^{2}
,
where we identify
a=(a_{1})
and
a_{1}
,
since it holds

\int_{D}L(a(x))\,d\mu(x)=n.

Hence,
\int L(a(x))\geq\int U_{B_{i-1}}(b(x))
for
i=1
,
and inductively choosing
x_{i}
and
w_{i}
with
L(a(x_{i}))\geq\frac{1}{w_{i}}\geq U_{B_{i}}^{\zeta}(b(x_{i}))
,
also for
i=2,...,n
.
This results in points and weights that satisfy the upper bound (
21
) and
w_{i}\geq 1/L(a(x_{i}))
. Noting that the
a(x_{i})
are scalar,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}|a(x_{i})|^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}=\lambda_{\min}(I).

Similarly, if
M<1+\frac{1}{n}
,
we replace the upper verifier
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))
by

U(b(x))=\frac{n}{\Tr(J)}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}}|b_{k}(x)|^{2},

which satisfies

\int_{D}U(b(x))\,d\mu(x)=n.

Hence,
\int L^{\delta}_{A_{i-1}}(a(x))\geq\int U(b(x))
for
i=1
,
and inductively choosing
x_{i}
and
w_{i}
with
L^{\delta}_{A_{i-1}}(a(x_{i}))\geq\frac{1}{w_{i}}\geq U(b(x_{i}))
,
also for
i=2,...,n
.
This results in points and weights that satisfy the lower bound (
20
) and
w_{i}\leq 1/U(b(x_{i}))
.
We conclude with

\begin{split}\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}\right)&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}}|b_{k}(x_{i})|^{2}\leq\Tr(J)=M\lambda_{\max}(J)\\
&\leq(1+s)^{2}\lambda_{\max}(J),\end{split}

where for the last inequality
s<\frac{1}{n}
implies
M=1+ns^{2}\leq 1+s
.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Proposition 20

(

)

Let

.

Regarding the first issue, one option is to find a finite subset of
D
in which at least one point satisfies the condition of line
5
.
This
candidate set
could be a rank-1 lattice
[
2
]
, an admissible mesh
[
6
]
, or a large initial sample
[
9
]
.
Constructing points by searching within a larger
finite set is often referred to as
subsampling
,
and has been performed in
[
2
,
3
]

for algorithms based on
[
4
]
in the case
b=a
.
Note that we do not have to know a priori whether the candidate set is suitable, we can simply test our favorite set.

Another way of dealing with this issue is to propose a point
x_{i}
at random.
Similarly to
[
8
]
,
we show in Section
5.1
below that a random sample from the so-called
Christoffel density

will pass the criterion from line
5
, and hence will be accepted, with a probability of order
1/m
.

The second issue is addressed in Section
5.2
, where we analyse the error from truncating the infinite basis
b
at some index
N
. In practice, we do not restrict ourselves to the RKHS setting, and consider functions
f
in the span of an orthonormal system
(\eta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{I}}
in

L_{2}(D,\mu)
such that, for any
k\in\mathbb{I}
and
x\in D
, the point value
\eta_{k}(x)
can be computed in time
O(1)
.
Given a function
f\in L_{2}(D,\mu)
and a finite set
I\subset\mathbb{I}
, we define
the best approximation of
f
in
\Span\{\eta_{k},\;k\in I\}
as

f_{I}:=\sum_{k\in I}\hat{f}(k)\,\eta_{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{f}(k):=\int_{D}f\,\overline{\eta_{k}}\,d\mu.

In order to bound the truncation error
f-f_{I}
in uniform norm, we assume that the functions
\eta_{k}
are bounded in
L_{\infty}
, with a bound of the form

\sum_{k\in I_{\ell}}|\eta_{k}(x)|^{2}\,\leq\,C_{\eta}^{2}\,\ell^{2\theta},\qquad x\in D,\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}

(22)

for some finite
\theta\geq 1/2
and
C_{\eta}>0
, and where
(I_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is a nested sequence of subsets of
\mathbb{I}
with
|I_{\ell}|\leq\ell
. Since the integral of the left hand side in (
22
) equals
|I_{\ell}|
,
the value
\theta=1/2
is the best possible value (unless
|I_{\ell}|=o(\ell)
).
Examples where this best possible behavior occurs are given by the Fourier, Walsh, Haar, or Chebyshev system with their corresponding orthogonality measure
\mu
.
However, note that the value of
\theta
will not appear in our error bound;
it will appear in the result by restricting the class of functions for which the error bound is applicable.
Also, condition (
22
) can be avoided if another way of bounding
\|f-f_{I}\|_{\infty}
is available, see Remark
27
.

Combining the above condition on
(\eta_{k})
with a decay assumption on the coefficients of
f
, we prove that the truncation error can be controlled for a set
I_{N}
of large cardinality
N
. As this integer can be much larger than
m
and
n
, we present in Section
5.3
an implementation whose cost is polynomial in
n
but linear in
N
. We note that, as the algorithm produces deterministic guarantees from randomly proposed points, its computational complexity is necessarily a random variable.
In the end, we obtain our constructive result.

Theorem 21
.

Let
(D,\mu)
be a probability space,
(\eta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{I}}
an
L_{2}
-orthonormal system and
(I_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
nested index sets with

that satisfy condition (
22
),

,

,
\alpha_{0}>\theta
and

.
Assume that the constant function
1
belongs to
\{\eta_{k},\;k\in I_{m}\}
. Algorithm
1
can be implemented, with

and

defined in (
23
), in time

O\big((c+Nn)n^{2}\mathcal{G}\big),

where

is the cost of drawing one point from the Christoffel density, and
{\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{N}}
is a shifted geometric random variable of parameter

. It yields points

and weights

which satisfy the following.
If

with

for some

and

for

from these values satisfies

,

In summary, given (

we can construct

.

5.1
Christoffel sampling

One possible implementation of line
4
in the algorithm consists of drawing candidate points for
x_{i}
according to the Christoffel density associated to
V_{m}
,

d\rho(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(x).

For some
V_{m}
, this density can be given explicitly, and even sampled from.
However, by assumption (
22
) of Theorem
21
,
sampling from
\rho

can be done via rejection sampling from the underlying measure with an acceptance probability of order at least
m^{-(2\theta-1)}
.
See e.g.
[
9
, Sec. 5]
and
[
18
]
for alternative strategies
to generate samples from the Christoffel density.

Recall that a suggested point
x
will be accepted if
L_{A}^{\delta}(a(x))\geq U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))
.
By using a slightly smaller increment
\delta
for the lower barrier in the proof of Theorem
3
, a good acceptance probability is achieved in exchange for a slightly worse lower frame bound.

Lemma 22
.

If
x
is drawn according to the Christoffel measure
\rho
and the increment
\delta=\frac{1-r}{n}
is replaced by

\delta_{\epsilon}=\frac{1-r-\epsilon}{n}

for some parameter
\epsilon<(1-r)^{2}
in the computation of
L_{A}^{\delta}
and the updates
A\leftarrow A^{\prime}
(but not in the initialization
A_{0}
),
then the acceptance probability satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(L_{A}^{\delta_{\varepsilon}}(a(x))\geq U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))\right)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{m}.

In addition, any points
x_{1},\dots x_{n}
returned by our algorithm for the modified value of
\delta
satisfy the upper bound (
21
) while the lower bound (
20
) becomes

\lambda_{\min}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}a(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}\right)\geq\Big(n\delta_{\epsilon}+\frac{\delta}{r}-\frac{m\delta}{r}\,\Big)=(1-r)^{2}-\epsilon.

In the sequel, we will take
\epsilon=\frac{(1-r)^{2}}{2}
, which changes (
20
) by a factor 2.

Proof.

We start with
A\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
B\in\mathcal{P}
such that (
15
) holds for the original
\delta=\frac{1-r}{n}
.
Let

\Delta(x):=\frac{L_{A}^{\delta_{\epsilon}}(a(x))-U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}(x)|^{2}}.

We now consider a random variable
x\sim\rho
.
According to Lemma
18
,
the expectation of
\Delta(x)
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Delta(x))=\int_{D}\frac{L_{A}^{\delta_{\epsilon}}(a(x))-U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))}{m}\,d\mu(x)\geq\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}-\Phi(A)-\frac{1}{\zeta}-\Psi(B)\right).

Thanks to condition (
15
),
we get

\mathbb{E}(\Delta(x))\geq\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)=\frac{\epsilon}{m\,\delta_{\epsilon}(1-r)}.

On the other hand,
\Delta(x)
is almost surely bounded by

\displaystyle\Delta(x)\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\end{subarray}}\frac{L_{A}^{\delta_{\varepsilon}}(a)}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}|^{2}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(Z^{2})}{\Tr(Z)-\Tr(Y)}\leq\frac{\Tr(Z^{2})}{\delta_{\epsilon}\Tr(YZ)}\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{\epsilon}(1-r)},

where
Y
and
Z
are from Section
4.2
with
\delta_{\varepsilon}
in place of
\delta
. Here,
the third inequality follows from
Z-Y=\delta_{\epsilon}YZ

and the last inequality comes from the fact that

\Tr(Z^{2})-\Tr(YZ)=\delta_{\epsilon}\Tr(YZ^{2})\leq\delta_{\epsilon}\Tr(Y)\Tr(Z^{2})\leq r\Tr(Z^{2}),

by a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
v
) between
Y^{1/2}
and
Y^{1/2}Z
and
since
\Tr(Y)=\Phi(A)\leq r/\delta_{\epsilon}
. In the end, we obtain an acceptance probability

\mathbb{P}(\Delta(x)\geq 0)\geq\frac{\int_{D}\Delta\,d\rho}{\sup_{x\in D}\Delta(x)}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{m}.

Note that, by Lemma
17
, choosing
x_{i}
with
\Delta(x_{i})\geq 0
and
w_{i}
accordingly, we obtain that the potentials do not increase
when switching to the updates

A^{\prime}:=A-\delta_{\varepsilon}I+wa(x_{i})a(x_{i})^{*}
and

B^{\prime}:=B+\zeta J-wb(x_{i})b(x_{i})^{*}
and
so
condition (
15
) remains true with the original
\delta
also for the updated operators.
∎

In total, when
n=O(m)
, we therefore need
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
candidate points drawn according to the Christoffel functions, in order to implement the algorithm. More precisely, the number of draws is a geometric random variable of expectation
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
.
We cannot hope to use less Christoffel points, as illustrated by the following example.

Example 23
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
D=[0,m]
with the Lebesgue measure
\mu

and define a RKHS
H
on
D
by its kernel
K(x,y)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}}\sigma_{k}^{2}b_{k}(x)b_{k}(y)
, where the functions

b_{k}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathbbm{1}_{[k-1,k]}&\text{ for }1\leq k\leq m,\\
\cos(2\pi(k-m)x)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}&\text{ for }k>m,\\
\sin(2\pi(m-k)x)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}&\text{ for }k<-m,\\
\end{array}\right.

are orthonormal in
L_{2}(D)
,
and
\sigma_{k}:=\frac{1}{|k|}
for
k\in\mathbb{I}:=\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-m,\dots,0\}
. Let also
V_{m}=\Span\{b_{k}\colon 1\leq k\leq m\}
. From (
11
), we get

g_{2m}^{\rm lin}(B_{H},L_{2})\,\leq\,\frac{10}{m}.

On the other hand,
let
A_{n}
be an approximation that uses information from at
most
n
samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Let
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n^{\prime}}\}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\cap[0,1]

be the samples in
[0,1]
. Observing that
A_{n}(-f)=A_{n}(f)
if
f(x_{1})=\dots f(x_{n})=0
,

\sup_{\|f\|_{H}\leq 1}\|f-A_{n}(f)\|_{L_{2}([0,m])}\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|f\|_{H}\leq 1\\
f(x_{i})=0\end{subarray}}\|f\|_{L_{2}([0,m])}\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|f\|_{H}\leq 1\\
f(y_{i})=0\end{subarray}}\|f\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}.

As
\|f\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}\geq\big|\int_{0}^{1}f\big|
, the lower bound from
[
28
, Corollary 2]
with
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{k}=\sigma_{|k|+m}^{2}
for
k\neq 0
gives

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|f\|_{H}\leq 1\\
f(y_{i})=0\end{subarray}}\|f\|_{L_{2}([0,1])}\geq\sqrt{\min\bigg\{\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2},\ \frac{1}{8n^{\prime}}\sum_{k>4n^{\prime}}\lambda_{k}\bigg\}}\geq\frac{1}{8\max(n^{\prime},\sqrt{mn^{\prime}})}.

As a consequence, the approximation
A_{n}
can only attain an optimal error if
n^{\prime}\gtrsim m
.
As the Christoffel density of
V_{m}
is uniform on
[0,m]
,
if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are drawn i.i.d. from that density,
we must have
n\gtrsim m^{2}
with high probability. In summary, any
algorithm achieving an error of optimal order
must use
\Omega(m^{2})
Christoffel samples.

Remark 24
.

We remark that sampling from the
adaptive quasi-density
a(x)^{*}Z^{2}a(x)

would instead lead to a constant acceptance probability. However, we do not see an efficient way for sampling from it
directly, see also
[
8
]
.
A different sampling density has been introduced in
[
26
]
,
namely,

d\tilde{\varrho}(x)=\frac{1}{2}d\rho(x)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{K_{m}(x,x)}{\Tr(K_{m})}d\mu(x),

where
K_{m}
is the kernel of
H_{m}
, the orthogonal complement of
V_{m}
.
With this density (and weights
w_{i}=1/(n\tilde{\rho}(x_{i}))
),
it is proven that

n=\mathcal{O}(m\log m)
i.i.d. samples suffice with high probability.
In the particular example above,
the density results in choosing approximately
1/2
of the points in
[0,1]

instead of a ratio
1/m
.

Remark 25
.

In a different setting,
where we consider instead the expected error
\mathbb{E}\,\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}^{2}

of random reconstructions,
it is known that already
\mathcal{O}(m\log m)
i.i.d. Christoffel samples
give optimal results, see
[
11
]
.
In that setting, better constants can also be attained using determinantal point processes
[
5
]
.
In particular, in the interpolation regime
n=m
, the factor
m
in (
10
) can be replaced by
\sqrt{m}
when considering randomized sampling numbers.

We add that it would be
possible to keep the value
\delta=(1-r)/n
in Lemma
22
, with a smaller acceptance probability.
Taking
w_{i}=1/L_{A}^{\delta}(a(x_{i}))
for the weights, we get
\Phi(A^{\prime})=\Phi(A)
by (
14
), thus the lower potential remains equal to its initial value

\Phi(A)=\Tr(Y)=\frac{r}{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\delta}-\Tr(Y)=n.

Recall that we also have
\frac{1}{\zeta}-\Psi(B)\leq n
, and observe that

\Tr(Z)-\Tr(Y)=\delta\Tr(YZ)\overset{\eqref{property:trace-positive}}{\geq}\delta\Tr(Y^{2})\overset{\eqref{property:CS}}{\geq}\frac{\delta}{m}\big(\Tr Y\big)^{2}=\frac{r}{m}\Tr(Y).

Then, according to (
17
) and (
18
),
the expectation of
\Delta(x_{i})
w.r.t.
x_{i}\sim d\rho
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Delta(x_{i}))=\int_{D}\frac{L_{A}^{\delta}(a(x))-U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x))}{m}\,d\mu(x)\geq\frac{\Tr(Z)}{\Tr(Y)}\,\frac{n}{m}-\frac{n}{m}\geq\frac{rn}{m^{2}}.

Combining this calculation with the uniform bound on
\Delta
, the acceptance probability is

\mathbb{P}(\Delta(x)\geq 0)\geq\frac{\int_{D}\Delta d\rho}{\sup_{x\in D}\Delta(x)}\geq\frac{rn\delta(1-r)}{m^{2}}=\frac{r(1-r)^{2}}{m^{2}},

which is smaller than in Lemma
22
by a factor
m
.

Remark 26
.

Example
23
suggests that the inequality
\lambda_{\max}(Z^{2})\leq\Tr(Z^{2})
in the proof of Lemma
22
is sharp, while the above Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\Tr(Y^{2})\geq\frac{1}{m}(\Tr Y)^{2}
is not.
In other words, the matrix
A
only has a few small eigenvalues, which explains why the potential
\Phi(A)
gives a tight control on
\lambda_{\min}(A)
in the BSS approach.

5.2
Constructive results via truncation

For the sake of simplicity, in this subsection, we assume that
\mathbb{I}=\mathbb{N}
and that
I_{\ell}=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, which only amounts to reordering the indices, and can therefore be done without loss of generality. We also denote
f_{m}=f_{I_{m}}
.

Additionally, all
\log
functions are meant in base
2
, which does not influence the statement of Theorem
21
.

Proof of the error bound of Theorem
21
.

We first construct the RKHS
H=\Span\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}\}
with kernel

K(x,y):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}k^{-2t}\eta_{k}(x)\overline{\eta_{k}(y)},\quad\text{where}\quad t=\frac{\alpha_{0}+\theta}{2}.

This allows to apply Corollary
11
to the function
f_{N}\in H
and the space
V_{m}=\Span\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m}\}
. More precisely, we take

a=(\eta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq m}\quad\text{and}\quad b=(k^{-t}\eta_{k})_{m<k\leq N}.

(23)

Then
\lambda_{m}=(m+1)^{-2t}
,
P_{m}f_{N}=f_{m}
and

\Tr(K_{m})=\sum_{m<k\leq N}k^{-2t}\leq\lambda_{m}+\int_{m+1}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{2t}}\leq\lambda_{m}\frac{m+2t}{2t-1},

which is finite since
t>\theta\geq 1/2
. With the choice
n=2m
, we get

\frac{1}{1-r}\leq 4\quad\text{and}\quad 1+\tilde{s}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2t}{2t-1}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}-\theta}}.

Therefore, Corollary
11
, with measurements
y_{i}=f(x_{i})=f_{N}(x_{i})+e_{i}
, and the modification of the increment
\delta
from Lemma
22
, gives

\|f_{N}-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\leq 16\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}-\theta}}\,\Big(2\,m^{-t}\,\big\|f_{N}-f_{m}\big\|_{H}+\,\|f-f_{N}\|_{\infty}\Big).

(24)

We then control the two terms on the RHS by dyadic decompositions. We have

\displaystyle\|f_{N}-f_{m}\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k>m}k^{2t}\,|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\;\sum_{k=2^{\ell}m+1}^{2^{\ell+1}m}\;k^{2t}\,|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\,(2^{\ell+1}m)^{2t}\,\|f-f_{2^{\ell}m}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{f}^{2}\,C_{H}^{2}\,m^{2t-2\alpha}\,\log^{2\beta}(m),

where

C_{H}:=2^{t+\beta}\Big(1+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}2^{\ell(2t-2\alpha)}\ell^{2\beta}\Big)^{1/2}

is finite since
\alpha>t
.

We control
\|f-f_{N}\|_{\infty}
in the same way.
For all
x\in D
,

\displaystyle|f(x)-f_{N}(x)|\,

\displaystyle\leq\,\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\;\sum_{k=2^{\ell}N+1}^{2^{\ell+1}N}\;|\hat{f}(k)|\,|\eta_{k}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\,\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\|f-f_{2^{\ell}N}\|_{2}\,\Big(\sum_{k\leq 2^{\ell+1}N}|\eta_{k}(x)|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\,C_{f}\,C_{\infty}N^{\theta-\alpha}\,\log^{\beta}N,

where

C_{\infty}:=C_{\eta}2^{\theta}\Big(1+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}2^{\ell(\theta-\alpha)}\ell^{\beta}\Big)

is finite since
\alpha>\theta
. By the choice of
N
, we have

N^{\theta-\alpha}\leq m^{-\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\log^{\beta}(N)\leq\left(\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}-\theta}\right)^{\beta}\log^{\beta}(m),

and therefore

\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\leq\|f-f_{N}\|_{2}+\|f_{N}-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\leq C_{f}\,C\,m^{-\alpha}\log^{\beta}(m),

where

C=(1+32C_{H}+16C_{\infty})\left(\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}-\theta}\right)^{\beta+1/2}.\qed

Remark 27
.

As can be seen from
the proof, see (
24
), the uniform bound (
22
) on the basis can be avoided and it may be possible to choose a smaller truncation index
N
if there is other knowledge about
\|f-f_{N}\|_{\infty}
,
possibly via smoothness assumptions.
For example, if we fix parameters
t>1/2
,
p>1
and truncate at
N=\lceil m^{p}\rceil
, a bound
\|f-f_{m}\|_{2}\in O(m^{-\alpha})
with
\alpha>t
will result in a corresponding bound
\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\in O(m^{-\alpha})
under the additional assumption that
\|f-f_{N}\|_{\infty}\in O(N^{-\alpha/p})
.

Remark 28
.

Given a function
f
, the decay of the coefficients of
f
ensures that its expansion in the basis
(\eta_{k})
converges almost everywhere. As the distribution of
(x_{i})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu^{\otimes n}
, the assumption
f(x_{i})=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}f_{I_{\ell}}(x_{i})
is almost surely true for each
f
.

Example 29
.

An example, where Theorem
21
could be applied
is the approximation on Sobolev spaces
H^{\alpha}_{\rm mix}

of
d
-variate periodic functions with mixed smoothness
\alpha>1/2
.
These can be defined by

H^{\alpha}_{\rm mix}=\left\{f\in C([0,1]^{d})\colon\|f\|_{\alpha}^{2}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{k}^{2\alpha}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}<\infty\right\},

where
\hat{f}(k)
are the usual Fourier coefficients and
w_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\{1,|k_{i}|\}
.
For integer
\alpha
, they can also be defined as spaces of functions possessing (weak) derivatives
in
L_{2}
up to mixed order
\alpha
,
see, e.g.,
[
29
, Sec. 2.1]
for a few details,
and
[
15
]
for a comprehensive treatment of dominating mixed smoothness.
If we choose the functions
\eta_{k}
as the trigonometric monomials
with the frequencies
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
ordered according to their hyperbolic distance
w_{k}
to the origin
and
\alpha_{0}>1/2
,
the algorithm from Theorem
21
satisfies

\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{2}\,\lesssim\,m^{-\alpha}(\log m)^{\alpha(d-1)}\cdot\|f\|_{\alpha},

(25)

for any smoothness
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
.

We present this example since
for the class
H^{\alpha}_{\rm mix}
,
no (polynomial time) construction was previously known to give the optimal order (
25
).
Only the existence of such algorithms was known due to
[
14
]
.
Previous constructions that come close to the optimal order include sparse grids (Smolyak’s algorithm), see
[
36
]
,
random points
[
26
]
,
subsamples of random points
[
3
]

and generated sets (lattices with a non-integer generating vector), see
[
12
]
.

5.3
Fast computations

If implemented naively, Algorithm
1
would have a cost
O(N^{3})
per iteration due to the inversion of
N\times N
matrices when computing
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x_{i}))
on line
5
. This cost becomes prohibitive for large values of
N
, which occur in Theorem
21
when
\alpha
is close to
\theta
, or when
p
is large in Remark
27
.

However, it can be exploited that the matrices to be inverted after
i
iterations are of the form

W=(D-MM^{*})^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad X=(D^{\prime}-MM^{*})^{-1},

where
D=B_{0}+i\zeta J
and
D^{\prime}=D+\zeta J
are diagonal by orthogonality of the basis
b
, and

M=[\sqrt{w_{1}}b_{1}|\dots|\sqrt{w_{i}}b_{i}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times i}

is thin and tall since
i\leq n\ll N
for the choice
n=2m
. By the Woodbury matrix identity, we get

W=D^{-1}+D^{-1}M(I-M^{*}D^{-1}M)^{-1}M^{*}D^{-1},

and similarly for
X
. The matrix
I-M^{*}D^{-1}M
is now of more reasonable size
i\times i
, it can be assembled in time
O(Nn^{2})
and inverted in time
O(n^{3})
.

In the end, we never explicitly construct matrix
B
. The computation of

\Tr(JW)=\Tr(JD^{-1})+\Tr((I-M^{*}D^{-1}M)^{-1}\cdot M^{*}D^{-1}JD^{-1}M),

as well as that of
\Tr(JX)
, takes
O(Nn^{2})
since
M^{*}D^{-1}JD^{-1}M
can also be assembled in time
O(Nn^{2})
. Moreover, once
I-M^{*}{D^{\prime}}^{-1}M
is known, evaluating
Xb
for some vector
b\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
takes
O(Nn)
by successive matrix-vector products.
With this strategy, we are ready finish the proof of Theorem
21
.

Proof of the computational complexity in Theorem
21
.

At each iteration, we compute
Y
and
Z
in time
O(n^{3})
, and the inverses of
I-M^{*}D^{-1}M
and
I-M^{*}{D^{\prime}}^{-1}M
in
O(Nn^{2})
, which allows to access
\Tr(JW-JX)
at the same cost.

We then sample a point
x_{i}
from the Christoffel measure
\rho
at a cost
c
, evaluate
L_{A}^{\delta}(a(x_{i}))
in
O(n^{2})
and
Xb(x_{i})
in
O(Nn)
, from which we evaluate
b(x_{i})^{*}Xb(x_{i})
,
(Xb(x_{i}))^{*}JXb(x_{i})
, and hence of
U_{B}^{\zeta}(b(x_{i}))
, in
O(N)
.

In conclusion, recalling that
n\ll N
, the cost of all matrix inversions and trace estimations over the
n
iterations is
O(Nn^{3})
. Proposing and testing samples costs
c+O(Nn)
, and the total number of proposed points is described by a negative binomial distribution with
n
successes and a success probability
\Omega(1/n)
by Lemma
22
. We conclude by bounding this random number in law by
O(n^{2})\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathcal{G}\geq 1
is a shifted geometric variable, satisfying
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}=k)=2^{-k}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

6
Numerical results

We now discuss some numerical examples
for the sampling points and weights
from Theorem
21
.
Figures
2
–
5
show the sampling points in
D=[0,1]^{2}

for different choices of basis functions and weights.
In all cases, the points in the algorithm are suggested randomly and uniformly distributed,
and the weight in line
6
is chosen minimal.
In addition, in each step,
all previous points are tested again before new points are proposed.
If one of the previous points is accepted, we only have to increase its weight,
thus reducing the size of the final point set.

For a given univariate orthonormal system
(\eta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
we consider the tensor product bases

a=\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{11.0pt}\eta_{j}\otimes\eta_{k}\colon\;j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;\text{ with }\;\sigma_{j,k}\leq R\right)

and

b=\!\left(\rule{0.0pt}{11.0pt}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j,k}}\cdot\eta_{j}\otimes\eta_{k}\colon\;j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;\text{ with }\;R<\sigma_{j,k}\leq R^{\prime}\;\text{ or }\;(j,k)=(0,0)\right).

Here, the weights describe either

•

isotropic smoothness, i.e.,
\sigma_{j,k}=\max\{1,2\pi(j^{2}+k^{2})\}
, or

•

mixed smoothness, i.e.,
\sigma_{j,k}=\max\{1,2\pi|j|\}\cdot\max\{1,2\pi|k|\}
.

As for univariate basis functions, we consider

•

trigonometric polynomials
:
\eta_{k}^{T}(x):=e^{2\pi{\rm i}kx}
, or

•

Legendre polynomials
:

\displaystyle\eta_{k}^{L}(x):=\sqrt{2k+1}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\left[\,x^{k}(1-x)^{k}\,\right]
, or

•

Chebyshev polynomials
:
\eta_{0}^{C}=1
and

\eta^{C}_{k}(x):=\sqrt{2}\,\cos(k\arccos(2x-1)),\qquad k\geq 1.

The first two are orthonormal
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure,
while the latter is orthonormal w.r.t. the
arcsine density

\nu(x)=(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)})^{-1}
.

For the trigonometric system, condition (
22
) is satisfied with
\theta=1/2
.
In this case, the Christoffel density gives the uniform distribution.
Also for the Chebyshev system, condition (
22
) is satisfied with
\theta=1/2
.
For the Legendre system, we can choose
\theta=1
.
If we choose the truncation parameter
R^{\prime}
large enough
(as can be computed via Theorem
21
),
for any previously chosen threshold
\alpha_{0}>\theta
,
the resulting least squares approximation hence gives
optimal
L_{2}
-approximation rates
if the rate of best
L_{2}
-approximation in this basis
(ordered according to
\sigma_{j,k}
)
is at least
\alpha_{0}
.

We add that the true acceptance rate of the suggested points was a lot higher than the theoretical lower bounds from Section
5.1
;
usually every second to tenth randomly generated point was accepted by the algorithm.
For this reason, we also did not have to decrease the increment
\delta
to
\delta_{\varepsilon}

as done in Section
5.1

to increase the theoretical acceptance rate;
we used precisely the parameters as given in Algorithm
1
.

In all pictures, the size of the points is drawn proportional to the size of the corresponding weight.
In addition to the points and weights,
we also plot the area of all points that would be rejected by the algorithm
in the following iteration.
That is, the blue area can be interpreted as the area
that is ‘blocked’ by the already chosen sampling points.
Two observations that we find interesting are:

•

The blocked area is roughly ball-shaped in the isotropic cases
and roughly hyperbolically shaped in the hyperbolic case, see Figure
2
.
This was not clear a priori, since the corresponding shape is a priori given only on the Fourier side,
not on the sampling domain.
Towards the end of the construction,
the area of acceptable points takes roughly this shape, see Figure
3
.
This fits to the knowledge that, in the isotropic case,
the error is dominated by the size of the largest empty ball
(see, e.g.,
[
24
]
).
In the hyperbolic case, there is no theoretical result undermining this observation.

As one would expect and hope,
for the trigonometric system, the points are evenly spread over the domain.
The points for the Legendre and Chebyshev systems have a much higher concentration towards the boundary. The weights of the points at the boundary, however, are chosen larger for Chebychev polynomials, but smaller for Legendre polynomials,
see Figures

and

.
