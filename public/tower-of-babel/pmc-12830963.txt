Digital image correlation technique for the kinematic assessment of laser system optomechanical mounts
Authors: Javier Pisonero, Marta Olivar, J D Pisonero, Roberto García-Martín, C Méndez, Diego González-Aguilera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA study of the Digital Image Correlation (DIC) technique has been made for its use in optomechanical components, such as mounts in high power lasers. An analysis has been executed on the submicro-positioning of these systems with a view to stabilising the beampointing, critical in lasers for their correct operation in different applications. DIC measurements may add distinct advantages in comparison with traditional systems to the setup, including a superior sensitivity and responsiveness, as well as lower dependence of beam sampling optomechanical components. Besides this, DIC excels in capturing detailed surface displacements with high precision, offering a more comprehensive analysis of the entire optomechanical surface that can be very valuable for posterior advanced beampointing control included in laser stabilisation setups. Discussion about its use as a high accuracy movement monitoring sensor inside laser chains is included. Finally, accuracies of around a tenth of a micron have been achieved comparable to the precision typically obtained with PSD-based systems which have an accuracy of around one micron or one tenth of a micron.Keywords: Digital image correlation, Optomechanical components, Submicro-positioning, BeampointingSubject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Precise control and monitoring of mirror positions play a pivotal role in numerous fields such as astronomy1,2, medical analysis3, and optics4–10. Many mechanical systems use high accuracy position feedback in conjunction with servo electronics and mechanical actuators to obtain precision positioning of mechanical components11. The accuracy of the position detector is one of the main factors that limits the system performance12.

In laser systems, where mirrors are employed to direct laser beams both inside laser cavities and beam transport components, precise positioning is crucial for achieving desired outcomes since slight misalignments can lead to energy losses and compromised functionality.

Beampointing Stability Degradation (BSD) is a problem that affects almost every laser system13–16, this issue can strongly degrade the performance of the beamline for different applications. As an example, in manufacturing processes, deviation of laser can produce unexpected defects in the final product. It can also lead to losing signal or efficiency in communication applications.

This laser BSD can be caused by many disturbance sources, some of them are high frequency ones such as air disturbance due to airflow cooling and vibrations coming from electric motors and cooling components. On the other hand, low frequency ones include changes in temperature distribution inside laser cavities and surrounding media, humidity changes leading to transmission medium variations and elastic deformations caused by forces on mechanical components.

In order to reduce these problems, laser beam pointing control systems are included into laser chains. These systems can be passive or active. Passive ones are devoted to include elements able to minimise spurious movements of optomechanical mounts such as temperature-controlled breadboards, proper isolating breadboard supports and low thermal expansion materials elections. Active control systems, usually have two key elements: Steering Mirrors (SM) and Position Sensitive Devices (PSD)17,18. Typical examples of applications that can benefit from these systems are the control of optomechanical component movement inside a large high-power laser chain subjected to different drift sources as well as the thermal behaviour of optical mounts in space applications. In the active stabilisation case, high accuracy position sensing is the first step to enable real-time adjustments to ensure that mirrors are aligned precisely, maximising the effectiveness of optical systems19.

Digital Image Correlation is a non-contact optical technique for measuring strain and displacement. High speed full-field experimental data can be obtained being also good for flexible materials20,21. This technique is widely adopted by the solid mechanics community and applied to measure strain fields22–24. The technique involves the use of digital cameras that register a series of images of a surface on which a randomised speckle pattern is applied. The speckle pattern is mapped to calculate the deformed shape, allowing the derivation of the deflections and strains of the investigated object.

Currently, DIC has become a standardised method to track displacements25–27. The benefit of using DIC over other standard methods is that it is contact-free, has high accuracy in a large range and only requires camera. Moreover, the displacement can be tracked over the entire image, which is a great advantage compared to the limitations of standard sensors that only measure the deformation at a specific point. In standard DIC, the displacements are directly extracted from the images. Hence, the position of the camera must be fixed during the entire test.

This work presents an analysis of a well-established position sensing technique such as DIC, as a tracking solution for characterising the movement of optomechanical components, highlighting the various advantages offered by the method.

Materials and methods

DIC technique

This technique is based on the capture of successive images in which a reference image where the specimen has not suffered any displacement or deformation is compared with successive images captured during the test. This image will be the basis for the comparison to be able to make a direct measurement of displacements suffered during the test. Thanks to the methods developed by different authors, as presented in the work by Blaber28, this comparison can be carried out, obtaining results also within the subpixel range. When combining this with a proper camera-objective, it is now possible to measure displacements close to submicron resolution29–31.

DIC algorithms rely heavily on the accuracy of the initial values provided by whole-pixel search algorithms for displacement monitoring. When the measured displacement is too large or exceeds the search domain, the calculation time and memory consumption of the DIC algorithm will increase greatly, and even fail to obtain the correct result32.

The precision of the measurement from the DIC technique depends also on the position of the camera and light panels, the colour temperature of light, size of the Region of Interest (RoI) and type of camera used in experiments.

The methods start by choosing a RoI where these comparisons will be made. It is essential that the speckle pattern fully cover the RoI. Once the RoI is defined, the image is divided into square subsets that will be directly compared to obtain the displacement.

Comparison of two subsets of consecutive images (image i and i + 1) begins by determining the centroid in image i. A correlation coefficient is used in order to get the subset belonging to Image i + 1 most similar to Image i. Different types of correlation has been developed33,34, but the one employed in this work is Zero-Mean Normalized Sum of Squared Differences (ZNSSD) as defined in Eq. (1). This method has been selected due to its because it provides greater robustness under non-uniform illumination conditions. All of this, both the algorithm and the use of the technique in other applications, has been validated in previous work35–37.

The motion vector between two consecutive images, I and I + 1, can be determined with one pixel precision. In order to achieve sub-pixel precision, grayscale interpolation is used. The Quintic B-spline method is the one that has been used in previous works and has given best results35–37. As for the optimization problem, the Inverse-Compositional Gauss-Newton (IC-GN) algorithm has been used38. This entire computational strategy has been used throughout the RoI. To minimise the error propagation and optimise the computational cost, the reliability-guided digital image correlation (RG-DIC) algorithm has been used26. This algorithm searches for the minimum Correlation Coefficient (CLS), calculated by Eq. (2), which is a variation of Eq. (1). Convergence analysis

It is possible to obtain displacements by calculating the centroid of the RoI, but Multithreaded Reliability Guided DIC permits dividing the entire RoI in subregions and selecting the centre of the subset by the definition of seeds. Thanks to this algorithm, bad data points are computed the last, and the separation of the regions allows the selection of different seeds, which means more points of the initial guess of displacements. A balance of the number of regions, computing time and precision is important for these applications, because of the low range of displacements. For this reason, a convergence analysis has been carried out.

In this case, an analysis of 1 to 8 seeds have been used for these three different trials to check if there is any behaviour pattern on the variation of acquisition rate and range of movements. The placement of those seed points will automatically define the regions. To ensure repeatability and a correct comparison, all processing has been carried out with the same RoI, and with the virtual strain gauges placed in the same position, in addition, the conditions of each trial have been replicated to obtain a total of nine similar tests. Image acquisition system

The acquisition system is configured by a CCD camera, a microcontroller as synchronization source and a PC managing all the data and elements. The camera model used is the Manta® G-504. This camera has a Sony ICX 665, Progressive CCD Monochrome sensor type, with a resolution of 2452 (H) x 2056 (V) pixels, with a square size of 3.45 × 3.45 μm and a maximum Frame Rate with full resolution of 9.2 images per second. The focal length of the attached lens is 50 mm. To minimize potential distortions (i.e., radial and tangential), the software uses a self-calibration approach based on the Brown-Conrady model.

A laser printed speckle pattern has been used. In previous works, it was possible to pre-impregnate the piece with white matte paint, but not in this case since this mount is specifically manufactured for optics usage within a clean environment. This is not a problem, because the main objective of this work is to measure the displacements, and not the strains, which will not appear for the proper geometry of the tests. A piece of Polylactic Acid (PLA) with dimensions of 70 × 20 × 6 mm was additively manufactured using a Prusa® MK3S printer to serve as the base for applying the speckle pattern. The PLA material used exhibits a Young’s modulus of approximately 3.2 GPa and a coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of about 68 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. Note that temperature variations during all tests were minimal, as they were conducted in climate-controlled environments, so thermal expansion is not expected to influence the measurements obtained. Subsequently, the part was affixed to the mount using M3® double-sided tape, specifically designed for optical applications. To carry out these measurements, a speckle pattern was printed on paper using a laser printer, concretely a conventional laser printer with high quality, 1200 dpi. This pattern has been transferred to the surface of the PLA piece thanks to the use of a conventional gel transfer, concretely Medium gel 190 Vallejo ©, leaving the piece impregnated by the pattern. This speckle pattern has been designed for this application taking in consideration the following facts: (i) pattern randomness; (ii) circular spots and (iii) covering among the 40–70% of the printed piece surface. In order to reduce homogeneity, the Mean Intensity Gradient (MIG) was used for assessing the quality of the speckle pattern. Considering those, a speckle pattern has been designed with a regular circular specks mesh. The principal parameters of this mesh are the diameter of the spots (d) and the separation step (s) among centres. The diameter of the spots was 0.324 mm and the step was 0.432 mm, which are parameters used previously for this configuration36,37. To guarantee a random pattern, a Gaussian random factor has been applied. The MIG obtained in this pattern had a value of 5639. All parameters shown have been guaranteed thanks to the generation of a controlled pattern based on a Matlab © script, which guarantees these characteristics. The appearance of this pattern is shown in Fig. 4. To optimise the Ground Sampling Distance (GSD) or distance covered by a pixel, the distance between the pattern surface and the camera is the minimum possible for proper focusing. This distance is 20 cm. With this configuration, camera and lenses, the GSD obtained was 0.012 mm/px.

Test configuration

In this work an analysis of different aspects have been carried out, both mechanical and of the DIC technique. These are (i) accuracy of the technique for displacements in the micrometric range, under theoretically linear and controlled displacements; (ii) study the best ratio between processing time and accuracy of the result according to the number of seeds in the processing; (iii) displacements from combined movements and with out-of-plane movements.

Linear displacement

As it is said in the previous section, the whole mount was attached to the motorised translation stage Thorlabs® MTS25//MZ-8. Placing the camera in a perpendicular way to the pattern as it is shown in Fig. 2, a theoretical linear displacement through x direction can be registered. To ensure orthogonality, specific bases were used for the different components. These bases maintain alignment by being securely screwed into the calibrated table, as shown in Fig. 3. Thanks to the mechanical positioning and secure fixing of the base where the mount is placed, the use of levels for the positioning ensures the perpendicularity of the camera relative to the plane under study. A frame has been designed to place an LED strip to ensure uniform illumination of the pattern. It has been designed so that the light falls on the entire surface with the correct orientation. A PC where the stage motion is programmed and an Arduino® Uno, which provides the trigger for the camera acquisition, control the whole system. In this way, it is possible to synchronise the acquisition rate of the camera, one frame per second, with the motor movement. This acquisition frequency is enough to follow low frequency disturbance sources such as thermal movements affecting the mount. The test was performed with a linear displacement of 30 μm with a speed of 0.5 μm/sec. This test will be named TH_Test.

Three different tests have been carried out with a linear displacement character through the previously presented Thorlabs® MTS25//MZ-8 motorized linear stage. The first two tests have a small longitudinal displacement of 32 μm at different acquisition rates. The noise measured while the target is not moving has also been studied. Finally, the third test has a larger displacement of 20 mm in order to make a comparison between displacement acquisitions in different orders of magnitude. Discussion

First of all, it has been demonstrated that DIC techniques permit measurement displacements in the entire pattern surface of the mirror mount captured by the camera, which also means that reprocessing is possible. In addition, it should be noted that the DIC needs to be processed to obtain the sub-pixel precision, and in this case, within the desired order of magnitude.

Compared to other techniques such as Laser Doppler Vibrometry or Position Sensitive Detectors, Table 11 shows a comparison of the main characteristics of each technique, thanks to the results obtained in40–46. It should be noted that this study was conducted using the DIC technique due to the expertise of the team and the extremely limited space available inside the laser, with mounts that have special treatments that cannot be modified. Future on-site tests will validate performance against high-frequency vibrations and thermal drifts under real conditions.

It should be noted that this work has been carried out with the aim of obtaining small-scale displacement measurements over a long period of time. It does not seek to measure vibrations caused over short periods, so the frequency of displacement acquisition is not a critical parameter.

Under controlled conditions in TH Test that are very favourable for the DIC technique, the accuracy achieved throughout the RoI is within a tenth of a micron. As the Fig. 8 shows, at all times there is a very high degree of correlation between the data obtained by DIC and the theoretical motion proposed, Where it can be seen that the displacements recorded by the strain gauges are adapted to the theoretical movement proposed. In addition, as shown in Fig. 9, the distribution of the error, which is the difference between the measured value and the theoretical value in each strain gauge, is less than half a micron, its mean being around one tenth of a micron. This tenth of a micron of error can also be influenced by the mechanical system itself, since, as the Table 3 shows, the Min. Repeatable Incremental Movement oscillates in the order of magnitude of the error. It is possible that this deviation between the theoretical and measured displacement is a consequence of the uncertainty of the movement, and not of the DIC technique.

The convergence analysis mainly shows that the smaller the number of seeds used the shorter the processing time of this part, but the longer the processing time of the individual images. Looking at the Fig. 10, these times are usually balanced from one to four seeds, but from this number of seeds onwards, the total time increases. This may be because the more seeds are placed the more complex geometries appear in the sub-regions, complicating the processing algorithm. This may also be one of the reasons why the errors respect to theoretical movement increase, as shown in the Fig. 10; Table 7. Where, the lower number of seeds, the higher precision achieved. In future work, full days of laser use will be recorded, so reducing the processing time is a key part of obtaining consistent results from day to day. This is why this convergence analysis is of great importance for future processing work.

The noise observed in the convergence analysis exhibits a similar trend to the error obtained in the TH Test, as shown by the comparison between Tables 6 and 8. Therefore, the error observed in a fully controlled environment may also be attributed to this factor.

The analysis of combined movements provides readings that may be of interest for applying this technique in a real environment. The vertical calibration displacements exhibit an almost linear behaviour. However, it is observed that the error increases when the recorded plane is not perfectly perpendicular to the camera, as shown by comparing the test configuration in Fig. 7 with the errors recorded in Fig. 13. Additionally, these errors are greater than those obtained in the TH test, as indicated by the comparison between violin plots of the errors shown in Figs. 9 and 13, with errors approximately three times larger, though still around a tenth of a micron. This indicates that if out-of-plane movements are expected, in this study an uncertainty of approximately 0.1 microns per micron of measured displacement should be expected, but this should be studied individually for each system, taking into account the type of mechanical dependence between its components. In the context of binocular vision-based 3D DIC, it would be valuable to capture these different planes simultaneously. However, the space around the laser is extremely limited, and any obstruction of the laser must be avoided. As a result, proper positioning of the equipment becomes nearly impossible, which is why this approach was not considered in the present work.

Conclusions

Optomechanical mirror mount displacements due to in-mount installed linear actuators have been presented for DIC sensors. For different movements, theoretical and real displacements have been analysed. The DIC technique is sufficiently accurate and robust to be used in high precision environments such as laser systems. In such applications, this technique is within the same accuracy range as dedicated mechanical systems designed specifically for this type of application.

As for processing, if the RoI is geometrically simple, it is not necessary to use Multithreated processing, as it has been shown that with a single seed, the processing time is acceptable and the accuracy is the best, avoiding errors and problems in the different subregions generated. Also, it is necessary to place enough virtual extensometers to allow analysis over as much surface area as possible, being able to analyse the entire RoI if necessary.

The loss of linearity observed in the displacements is due to rotational movement. However, as can be seen in Fig. 12; Table 9, error increases significantly when out-of-plane movements appear, which can be corrected by Eq. (4). In comparison with the results obtained in the TH Test, the error is 3 times greater, being always less than a micron. Therefore, if out-of-plane movements are foreseen and movements of this order of magnitude are recorded, a correction must be made according to this calculated error. This is important because in real environments on laser systems, space is very limited and it is not possible to place cameras in all possible planes.

The calculation of the angle of deflection is sufficiently precise to be used in laser applications and can be decisive in the correction of beampointing. It can therefore be concluded that this technique is perfectly adjustable to the different particularities of laser systems.

Thanks to the work presented, which investigates both the feasibility and precision of the technique, it will be possible to analyse displacements in frames within a real laser environment. Furthermore, given the larger number of images to be processed, the most efficient method for performing this processing without compromising precision has been identified through an analysis of the number of seeds.
