Development and validation of a pathomics model to predict SOX11 expression and prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Shuna Liu, Yuhui Cai, Boping Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSOX11 is overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), in which it promotes tumorigenesis and is associated with poor prognosis. This study aims to assess the prognostic value of SOX11 expression in HCC patients and to develop a predictive pathomics model based on this expression.MethodsWe analyzed data from 335 HCC patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, including pathological images and gene expression profiles. Patients were stratified into high and low SOX11 expression groups based on RNA sequencing data for prognostic and survival analysis. A pathomics model was constructed using the light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) algorithm. Its predictive performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and interpretability. Potential pathological mechanisms were investigated through differential gene expression, enrichment analysis, immune cell infiltration profiling, and gene mutation analysis.ResultsSOX11 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. High SOX11 expression was significantly associated with reduced overall survival (OS) in the HCC population, and this association remained significant after adjusting for multiple clinical covariates. Nine pathomics features were selected to construct a pathomics signature (PS) for predicting SOX11 expression. The PS demonstrated strong discriminative ability, with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.928 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.895–0.959] in the training cohort, the mean AUC of the model after 5-fold cross-validation was 0.869 (95% CI: 0.824–0.895), and 0.830 (95% CI: 0.699–0.879) in the validation cohort. It also showed good calibration across all cohorts, and decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed its clinical utility. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that high PS expression was associated with poorer OS.ConclusionsSOX11 expression may influence the prognosis of HCC patients. The PS can accurately and robustly predict both SOX11 expression and patient prognosis, making it a potentially valuable tool for clinical application.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pathomics, SOX11, survival, predictive model

Key findings

• High SOX11 expression was significantly associated with reduced overall survival in the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) population, the pathomics signature can accurately and robustly predict both SOX11 expression and patient prognosis, making it a potentially valuable tool for clinical application.

What is known and what is new?

• SOX11 is closely associated with tumorigenesis, progression, and migration.

• The innovation of this study is that a pathomic model was developed based on routine diagnostic pathology sections to predict the expression of SOX11. These findings may provide new insights into prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for HCC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Pathomics conducts in-depth analysis of digital pathological images, which can reveal subtle changes in the tissue microenvironment, cellular characteristics, and morphological features, and provide unique insights for biomarker discovery. This not only enhances the speed and accuracy of biomarker discovery, but also significantly improves the precision and effectiveness of clinical treatment, and promotes the transformation from empirical medicine to precision medicine.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes about 90% of primary liver cancers and is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Projections indicate that the HCC mortality rate will rise by 50% over the next two decades (1). Although effective treatments, including surgical resection and liver transplantation, are available, postoperative recurrence remains common. Furthermore, traditional prognostic indicators—such as clinical and pathological features, biomarkers like alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and imaging techniques—are insufficient to meet the demands of precision medicine (2). Therefore, improved prognostic markers are urgently needed to guide clinical decision-making and identify high-risk patient subgroups.

In humans, SOX11 is located on chromosome 2p25.2. It binds to open chromatin regions, promoting nucleosome remodeling and subsequent transcription. Upregulation of SOX11 has been confirmed in several cancers, including glioma, breast cancer, and ovarian cancer (3). SOX11 has been proposed as a potential tumor suppressor gene in HCC. Overexpression of SOX11 restores the inhibitory effects of miR-221 on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and promotes apoptosis (4,5). Kaplan-Meier analysis further showed that SOX11 messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression is negatively correlated with overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in HCC patients (6). SOX11-related gene signature represents a potential novel biomarker for predicting survival in HCC patients. Therefore, investigating SOX11 expression as a prognostic marker may offer valuable insights into HCC biology and inform future therapeutic strategies.

Currently, there are several methods available to evaluate SOX11 expression, but these techniques have drawbacks in terms of accuracy, cost, and clinical feasibility. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining is widely employed in clinical practice, yielding readily accessible imaging data. Furthermore, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have revolutionized the field of pathology, culminating in the emergence of pathomics (7,8). This AI-based approach converts histopathological images into high-throughput data, enabling predictions of treatment responses, disease outcomes, and molecular expression in various cancers (9-12). In the case of HCC, pathomic analyses have shown promising results by uncovering prognostic markers and genetic changes (13,14). Furthermore, pathomic models have shown the capability to predict RNA sequencing expression levels based on whole-slide images (15,16). These results indicate that AI-driven analysis of H&E-stained slides could aid in predicting SOX11 expression. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1260/rc).

Methods

Data collection and processing

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study utilized data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) for HCC patients with comprehensive data, including publicly accessible gene expression profile and clinical parameters such as follow-up information, treatments like ablation or embolization, AFP levels, age, gender, hepatic inflammation, histologic grade, pathologic stage, pharmaceutical therapy, residual tumor status, and vascular invasion, as well as whole-slide images. The criteria for inclusion were: (I) a histologically confirmed diagnosis of HCC; (II) complete clinical and follow-up information; and (III) availability of digital pathology sections. The exclusion criteria were: (I) low-quality whole-slide images (blurry or significantly discolored); and (II) OS of less than 30 days. In total, 335 HCC patients with accessible whole-slide images and complete clinical data were randomly divided into a training set (n=200) and a validation set (n=115) for prognostic analysis, as illustrated in Figure 1.

SOX11 expression and prognostic analysis

Differences in SOX11 expression between tumor and normal tissues were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The R package “survminer” was used to determine the optimal cutoff value for SOX11 expression, set at 4, enabling the classification of patients into high- and low-expression groups. Survival differences were assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test, with a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To evaluate the effect of these variables on OS, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed, calculating the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the strength and direction of the associations. Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to adjust for potential confounders.

Images acquisition and processing

All H&E slide images were obtained from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded or fresh-frozen surgical resection specimens collected prior to systemic therapy. Slides with no identifiable tumor tissue, low resolution, or significant artifacts were excluded. Remaining slides were reviewed by pathologists to confirm the presence of tumor tissue and sufficient image quality for analysis. Two experienced pathologists annotated tumor cells and tumor stroma using QuPath (version 0.4.4) (17). A third senior pathologist independently reviewed the annotated regions of interest (ROIs) to ensure annotation accuracy. Due to the large size of digitized images, all ROIs were divided into non-overlapping patches (1,000×1,000 pixels) at 20× magnification. One pathologist manually selected regions containing nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic figures, cancer infiltration and invasion, tumor differentiation, and grading features. Ten representative 1,000×1,000-pixel image patches were then extracted and verified by another pathologist. In cases of disagreement, a third pathologist was consulted to make the final decision.

Pathomics feature extraction

We used the open-source image analysis software CellProfiler (version 4.0.7), developed by the Broad Institute (Cambridge, MA, USA), to extract pathomics tumor nucleus features from the selected screenshots (18). We used the “Unmix Colors” module to separate H&E-stained images and convert them into grayscale images for H&E, and the “ColorToGray” module to convert H&E-stained images into grayscale. We performed two measurements on the images. In the first measurement, we assessed the overall image quality on grayscale H&E, hematoxylin, and eosin images. This process yielded 296 original features. In the second measurement, we carefully explored the hematoxylin images. First, we segmented the stained images with the nucleus as the center using the “IdentifyPrimaryObjects” and “IdentifySecondaryObjects” modules. We then extracted and measured quantitative features such as object shape, size, texture, and pixel intensity distribution using multiple modules. Due to the large number of recognized objects, we calculated the mean, median, and standard deviation of each object feature, resulting in 844 pathological features. Features from both overall and object measurements were summarized by averaging every 10 screenshots for each patient.

Pathomics feature selection

At the beginning, all variables were normalized, and a U-test was performed on each feature to initially remove redundant features. A P value cutoff of 0.05 was used to effectively identify significant distinguishing features. Next, to consider the relationships between features, a correlation analysis was conducted. If the correlation coefficient between any pair of features was greater than 0.9, one of the features was eliminated. Then, this study uses two algorithms for feature selection. The first method is the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm based on linear computation, the goal of feature selection is to select the feature set that can make the current model area under the curve (AUC) the highest, and 5-fold cross validation is used to select the best Lambda value to determine the number of subsets and feature names. The second method is the nonlinear algorithm Boruta to accurately select a subset of features for modeling. The Boruta algorithm works by shuffling the original features to create shadow features, then combining these shadow features with the original ones into a single feature matrix for training. It ultimately identifies the original features that are genuinely related to the dependent variable by comparing their importance scores to those of the shadow features. The common features selected by both algorithms are then used for building the subsequent model.

Pathomics signature (PS) construction and validation

Using the optimal features identified, we developed a prediction model utilizing the light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) algorithm. We chose LightGBM for its robustness and effectiveness in handling various data types, including non-linear relationships and complex feature interactions (19). Additionally, LightGBM has shown strong performance with imbalanced datasets, which is crucial in biomedical research where certain data classes may be underrepresented. The model’s predicted values were used to create a signature known as the PS.

To comprehensively assess the PS, we employed several approaches, including receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), to evaluate its ability to distinguish outcomes. We also assessed the model’s calibration using calibration curves and applied the Hosmer-Lemeshow test to evaluate the models’ goodness-of-fit. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed on all models to quantify the net benefit for patients across different threshold probabilities, allowing us to evaluate the clinical usefulness of our predictive models. However, explaining the precise predictions made by LightGBM for patients can be challenging. To address this, we introduced the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm (20), which is a unified framework for explaining machine learning predictions proposed by Lundberg and Lee. SHAP provides global and individual explanations for the prediction model, and ranks the features in the model and identifies their importance.

Molecular validation of PS

To explore the biological underpinnings of the PS, we performed an in-depth pathogenomic analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high- and low-PS subgroups were identified using the “DESeq2” package in R. Multiple testing correction was applied using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with a significance threshold of FDR <0.01. Genes were further filtered using the criteria: |log2 fold change (log2FC)| >2 and FDR <0.01. Functional enrichment analysis based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package. The association between PS and immune-related features was also investigated using gene expression data. Stromal score, immune score, ESTIMATE score, and tumor purity were calculated using the ESTIMATE package in R and visualized according to their distributions across different PS subgroups. Additionally, somatic mutation profiles and copy number variation (CNV) data of HCC patients were obtained from the TCGA and visualized using the “maftools” and “RCircos” packages in R.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 4.1.1; R Studio, http://www.R-project.org) and Jupyter Notebook (version 6.4.11). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the “survival” package in R to show survival rate changes across different groups for each variable, with survival differences between groups assessed using the log-rank test. Risk factors affecting OS were identified using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SOX11 is up-regulated in HCC and is negatively correlated with patients’ prognosis

To investigate SOX11 expression in HCC, publicly available RNA-sequencing data from TCGA database were analyzed. SOX11 expression was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal liver tissues (Figure 2A). Based on SOX11 RNA expression levels and a cutoff value of 4, patients were stratified into high-expression (n=110) and low-expression (n=225) groups (Figure 2B). Higher SOX11 expression was associated with shorter OS (Figure 2C). The baseline characteristics of all patients are presented in Table 1, and no significant differences were detected across all cohorts (P>0.05). To determine whether SOX11 expression was independently associated with survival, Cox regression analyses were performed. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that SOX11 expression was an independent predictor of OS in HCC patients. Significant HRs were observed in both univariate analysis (HR =1.82; 95% CI: 1.28–2.58; P<0.001) and multivariate analysis (HR =1.66; 95% CI: 1.14–2.41; P=0.008) (Figure 3).

Pathomics feature selection and signature construction

Initially, redundant features were removed using the Mann-Whitney U test and Spearman correlation analysis, resulting in 40 retained pathomics features (Figure 4A,4B). Subsequently, the LASSO and Boruta algorithms were applied to further select relevant features. For LASSO, features were selected based on the one-standard-error rule, yielding 10 features (Figure 4C,4D). The Boruta algorithm identified 9 relevant features (Figure 4E). To identify the most robust and informative feature set, the intersection of the two subsets was selected. A total of nine features were retained, all of which demonstrated consistent importance across both methods (Figure 4F). A prediction model for SOX11 expression was constructed using LightGBM based on the selected optimal features. The model outputs the probability (ranging from 0% to 100%) of high SOX11 expression for each patient, based on image-derived feature values. This probability was defined as the PS. Histograms of predicted PS values in the training and validation cohorts are shown in Figure 4G,4H. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that high PS was significantly associated with worse OS in both cohorts (P<0.05) (Figure 4I,4J). The raincloud plot (Figure 4K) illustrates that PS values were significantly higher in the SOX11 high-expression group compared to the low-expression group in both cohorts (P<0.05).

PS validation

PS demonstrated strong performance in predicting SOX11 expression, AUC values of 0.928 (95% CI: 0.895–0.959) in the training cohort (Figure 5A), the mean AUC of the model after 5-fold cross-validation was 0.869 (95% CI: 0.824–0.895). And 0.830 (95% CI: 0.699–0.879) in the validation cohort (Figure 5B), indicating high predictive accuracy. DCA further confirmed the clinical utility of the PS-based model in both cohorts (Figure 5C,5D). Calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted probabilities and actual SOX11 expression status in all three datasets (Figure 5E,5F). To enhance model interpretability and clinical applicability, overall and individual Shapley values were calculated for the LightGBM model. In the global SHAP summary plot (Figure 5G), the contributions of the nine most important pathomics features are visualized, with Mean_IdentifyPrimaryObjects_AreaShape_Zernike_4_2 contributing the most. Individual-level SHAP plots (Figure 5H,5I) illustrate two representative examples of correctly predicted high and low SOX11 expression cases.

Molecular alteration behind PS

To investigate transcriptional differences between high- and low-PS subgroups, DEGs were identified using RNA-seq data (Figure 6A,6B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in metabolism-related pathways (Figure 6C,6D). The relationship between PS and immune-related features was subsequently explored. As shown in Figure 6E, stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score were all lower in the low-PS group compared to the high-PS group, suggesting a more immunosuppressive microenvironment and reduced stromal cell content. Somatic mutation data from TCGA were analyzed to assess differences in mutational profiles between PS subgroups. Missense mutations represented the most common variant type across both groups. Frequently mutated genes included TP53, CTNNB1, and TTN, each with mutation frequencies exceeding 20% in both subgroups (Figure 6F).

Discussion

HCC is an aggressive malignancy that is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to poor prognosis in many patients. In this study, we explored the clinical implications of SOX11 expression in HCC and developed a pathomics model to predict SOX11 expression from histopathological images. Our findings demonstrated that both SOX11 expression and the derived PS were independent risk factors for OS in HCC patients. Moreover, the PS demonstrated favorable performance in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. It was also associated with immune microenvironment characteristics and metabolic pathways in HCC. These findings may offer new insights into prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets in HCC.

The SOX family was initially identified based on its highly conserved high-mobility group DNA-binding domains, and early studies primarily focused on its role in embryonic development and tissue differentiation (21,22). SOX11 is known to regulate neural differentiation, bone formation, and other critical processes during embryogenesis (23). More recent studies have expanded our understanding of SOX11 by revealing its involvement in tumorigenesis. SOX11 has been extensively studied in oncology and is highly expressed in nodal lymphomas, where it serves as a reliable diagnostic biomarker. Elevated SOX11 expression has also been reported in epithelial ovarian cancer. Moreover, SOX11 may function as an oncogenic factor in certain contexts. For example, it has been shown to promote metastasis and invasiveness in gastric cancer cells in both in vivo and in vitro models, and may serve as a prognostic biomarker for specific gastric cancer subtypes (24). In neuroblastoma, SOX11 promotes cell survival by regulating the transcription of BNIP3 and TANK (25). Collectively, these studies indicate that SOX11 plays tumor-associated roles across multiple cancer types. However, the role of SOX11 in HCC remains poorly understood. In our study, elevated SOX11 expression was significantly associated with reduced OS in HCC patients. This association remained significant even after adjustment for multiple clinical variables, indicating that SOX11 expression may serve as an independent predictor of patient outcomes. These results are consistent with prior studies reporting SOX11 as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in various cancers.

In traditional pathology, experienced pathologists use microscopes with various magnifications to assess tissue morphology, distinguish between benign and malignant tumors, evaluate tumor cell growth patterns, and determine the degree of differentiation. However, due to limitations in field of view and magnification, as well as the vast complexity of histopathological data, it is not feasible to routinely characterize all substructural details of tissue sections, let alone perform comprehensive analyses of the tumor microenvironment (TME). With the advent of digital pathology, there has been increasing interest in quantitatively analyzing histopathological features using computational methods. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of combining digital pathology with machine learning algorithms to predict clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses (26,27). Pathomics, an emerging discipline, enables the extraction and quantification of high-dimensional features from whole-slide images, offering a novel in vivo approach to study tumor heterogeneity. The microscopic features of tumor cells vary significantly among individuals and can reflect disease progression, clinical outcomes, treatment responses, and underlying changes in the TME. Conventional histopathological evaluation often fails to fully capture these subtle variations, whereas pathomics provides a scalable and objective solution to complement traditional pathology. Several studies have demonstrated that pathomics features are predictive of prognosis in gastric cancer and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, as well as the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in rectal and breast cancers (28,29). In the present study, we extracted pathological whole slide images (WSIs) of HCC from TCGA, selected 9 robust features using the LASSO and Boruta algorithms, and constructed a predictive model based on the LightGBM algorithm. The resulting model achieved high predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.928 (95% CI: 0.895–0.959) in the training cohort and 0.830 (95% CI: 0.699–0.879) in the validation cohort. This study demonstrates that pathomics enables objective, quantitative, and accurate prediction of SOX11 expression, and may serve as a non-invasive strategy to identify patient subgroups with potential benefit from immunotherapy in the future.

We further investigated the potential pathomechanisms underlying differences between high and low PS groups. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs between the two groups were significantly associated with metabolic pathways, which are well-documented contributors to HCC progression and are considered promising therapeutic targets (30). Gene mutation analysis revealed that the mutation rates for TP53, TTN, and CTNNB1 exceeded 20% in both the high and low PS groups. These three genes have been investigated as potential diagnostic and prognostic markers for various cancers (31). Hu et al. proposed that mutations in CTNNB1 could influence the metabolic characteristics that impact HCC prognosis (32). Additionally, the mutation rates of TP53 and PCLO were found to be higher in the high PS group compared to the low PS group, suggesting that these genes may be risk factors for tumor development. Long et al. (33) showed that liver cancer associated with TP53 could be utilized to create an immune prognostic model. Conversely, Moul identified CTNNB1, TTN, TP53, ALB, MUC16, and PCLO as the most prevalent somatic mutations linked to lipid metabolism in liver cancer, which aligns with our research findings (34).

Although this study offers valuable insights into the prognostic significance of SOX11 expression and the PS in HCC, several limitations should be considered. First, the analysis relied on publicly available datasets, which could introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader clinical population. Second, the retrospective nature of the study prevents us from establishing causal relationships between SOX11 expression, PS, and patient outcomes. Therefore, prospective studies with larger, independent cohorts are needed to validate these results and further assess the clinical utility of SOX11 expression and PS as prognostic markers in HCC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we established a PS to accurately and robustly predict both SOX11 expression and patient prognosis, making it a potentially valuable tool for clinical application in HCC patients.
