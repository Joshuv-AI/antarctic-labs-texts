Characteristics of refractive errors in children with intermittent exotropia compared with the general population
Authors: Dae Hee Kim, Seung Ah Chung, Jin Choi, Sei Yeul Oh, Mirae Kim, Young-Woo Suh, Nam Yeo Kang, Hyun Taek Lim, Korean Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus (KAPOS)
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compared refractive errors in children with intermittent exotropia (IXT) against the general population using two large nationwide datasets: the Korean Intermittent Exotropia Multicenter Study (KIEMS), involving 2,744 patients aged 5–18 years, and the Korean National Health Examination Survey (KNHANES), involving 1,224 participants. Age-specific spherical equivalent (SE) distributions in IXT patients were generally similar to the general population. While the non-dominant eye in IXT patients tended to be more myopic than the dominant eye, it did not exceed the myopia levels observed in the more myopic eyes of the general population. However, IXT patients exhibited a significantly higher rate of anisometropia, particularly in those older than 9 years (P<0.001). Linear regression analysis revealed that refractive profiles, including SE and interocular differences, had a very weak linear relationship with exotropia angles ($R^2=0.000–0.005$). Additionally, convergence insufficiency type IXT was more myopic than other types, while poor distance control was associated with less myopia (P<0.05). In conclusion, children with IXT show a higher prevalence of anisometropia but not a significantly higher incidence of myopia compared to the general population. Furthermore, refractive profiles in IXT are not strongly correlated with the magnitude of exotropia angle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53576-3.

Introduction

Intermittent exotropia (IXT) is a divergent form of strabismus with intermittent fusional disruption. Myopia has been suggested as a possible pathophysiological mechanism in IXT, which may reduce the need for accommodation, leading to divergence, just as hyperopia induces accommodative convergence and plays a crucial role in esotropia.1 In addition, considering that IXT is one of the most prevalent forms of strabismus in Asian countries,2,3 where the prevalence of myopia is also notably high,4 the geographical overlap is enough to raise suspicion of the association between IXT and myopia.

The prevalence of myopia varies depending on ethnicity and the social and geographic environments.4 Myopia, as the axial elongation of the eyeball during childhood, is a growth process whose severity is largely dependent on age.5 Thus, statistics on refractive errors, especially in children, need to consider ethnicity, environment, and age. Previous studies have investigated the association between IXT and myopia, in which simple comparisons were conducted only with epidemiological summary reports, which may not be sufficient to reveal the associations.6,7

IXT and myopia are multifactorial diseases, and a large number of observations are needed to investigate both conditions. The Korean Intermittent Exotropia Multicenter Study (KIEMS), initiated by the Korean Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus (KAPOS), investigates the clinical characteristics of patients with IXT. KIEMS was conducted in a single ethnic group using a standardized protocol on a large scale; therefore, it is suitable for analyzing IXT and refractive errors. In this study, we investigated the refractive error distributions of Korean children with IXT using the dataset from KIEMS for age-specific comparisons with the general population data from the Korean National Health Examination Survey (KNHANES). We also investigated the association between IXT and refractive errors.

Results

In total, 2744 KIEMS patients were included in this study. Mean age of the patients was 8.24 ± 2.68 years. Female was 1328 and accounted for 48.4%. Mean SEs for more and less myopic eye were -1.07 ± 1.95 D and -0.57 ± 1.82 D. In the more myopic eye, the distribution of patients was as follows: hyperopia (SE > +0.50 D) in 446 patients (16.3%), emmetropia (-0.50 D ≤ SE ≤ +0.50 D) in 875 patients (31.9%), and myopia (SE < -0.50 D) in 1423 patients (51.9%). In the less myopic eye, hyperopia was observed in 651 patients (23.7%), emmetropia in 963 patients (35.1%), and myopia in 1130 patients (41.2%). The mean exotropia angles were 21.96 ± 7.80 and 24.27 ± 8.48 prism diopters (PD) under distant and near viewing conditions each. Participants from the KNHANES (1224 participants) were older and, accordingly, more myopic than the KIEMS patients (P < 0.001). Linear regression analysis of the association between SE and the exotropia angle showed weak linearity. No definite linearity was noted between SE in more and less myopic eyes and the distant exotropia angle (R2 = 0.000, P = 0.349 for more myopic eyes; R2 = 0.001, P = 0.199 for less myopic eyes, test for the coefficient of SE in linear regression analysis). For the near exotropia angle, weak linearity was noted in both more and less myopic SE (R2 = 0.005, P < 0.001 for more myopic eyes; R2 = 0.004, P = 0.001 for less myopic eyes, test for coefficient of SE in linear regression analysis) (Fig. 4). Linear regression analysis between the interocular SE difference and exotropia angle showed no linearity (R2 = 0.000, P = 0.485 for distant angle; R2 = 0.001, P = 0.105 for near angle; R2 = 0.001, P = 0.160 for distant-near angle, each test for the coefficient of interocular SE difference in linear regression analysis) (Supplement Figure 1). Patients with basic, convergence insufficiency (CI), and divergence excess (DE) types consisted of 2316 (84.4%), 328 (12.0%), and 100 (3.6%) patients. CI-type exotropia showed more myopic SE in more and less myopic eyes (P < 0.001 for both) than in basic (P < 0.001 for both more and less myopic eyes in post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test) or DE-type exotropia (P < 0.001 for both more and P = 0.001 for less myopic eyes in post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test). A comparison between basic and DE-type exotropia showed no differences in SE in both more (P = 0.913, post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test) and less myopic eyes (P = 0.870 in the post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test) (Supplement Figure 3).

Patients with poor distant control showed less myopia in more myopic eyes (P < 0.001, ANOVA) than those with good distant control (P < 0.001, post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test), and also in less myopic eyes (P < 0.001, ANOVA) than those with good distant control (P < 0.001, post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test). For near control, no SE differences were noted between patients with good, fair, and poor control (P = 0.954 and 0.665 for more and less myopic eyes, respectively, ANOVA) (Supplement Figure 4).

Discussion

This study investigated the correlation between IXT and refractive errors using a large dataset from a nationwide multicenter study conducted in Korea. Many patients with exotropia exhibited myopia in both eyes; however, the SE distributions according to age were similar to those in the general population in Korea. Anisometropia is more prevalent in older patients than in the general population. The SE, interocular SE difference, and cylindrical errors showed weak or no linear correlation with the exotropia angle. CI-type IXT showed more myopia than any other type of IXT. Patients with poor distant control showed less myopia in both more and less myopic eyes than those with good distant control.

Previous studies have described the association between myopia and IXT. Ekdawi et al. reported in a population-based cohort study that patients with IXT tended to have myopia compared to other studies investigating the prevalence of refractive errors in the U.S.8 Wen et al. also showed myopic preponderance in patients with IXT when compared with reports about the prevalence of refractive errors in China.9 However, it is necessary to compare refractive errors with age-matched controls, especially in childhood. However, these studies performed a simple comparison with previously published data. Unlike these studies, we compared patients with IXT with general population in an age-specific manner, and KIEMS patients showed no definite difference in age-specific SE from KNHANES participants, in both more and less myopic eyes. In this study, the non-dominant eye was more myopic than the dominant eye, which is consistent with a previous study that showed a similar trend in myopic adults.10 Still, it is noteworthy that the non-dominant eye was also less myopic than the more myopic eye of general population in Korea in an age-specific comparison.

Association between the progression of myopia and IXT remains controversial. Oruz et al. showed that myopic progression in patients with IXT was far more rapid than that in patients without IXT.11 However, Shin et al. reported that patients with IXT showed no significant differences from patients without IXT.12 Moon et al. reported that the non-dominant eye showed more rapid myopic progression in a small cohort study.13 They reported that patients with large angles and poor controls exhibited greater myopic progression in the non-dominant eye, suggesting that the loss of dominance in IXT may induce retinal blurring and exacerbate myopic progression.

In our study, non-dominant eyes showed a similar SE distribution to dominant eyes in patients until 8 years of age and then became significantly more myopic than dominant eyes as age increased in patients after 9 years of age. These tendencies were shown in this study, as the anisometropia rate and interocular SE differences tended to increase in older patients compared with the general population. Thus, IXT may affect more myopic progression, insidiously in the non-dominant eye. Given that small differences in SE at an early age can lead to significant differences in myopic progression later on,5,14 the younger (especially those aged 5–8 years) the patients with IXT, the greater the myopic progression in the non-dominant eye. Nevertheless, it should not be overlooked that if the effects of IXT on myopic progression were considerable, patients would become more myopic than the general population with age. However, these tendencies were not observed in this study. Collectively, these results suggest that IXT may affect asymmetric myopic progression, especially in the non-dominant eye. However, effects of IXT on anisometropia development and association between myopia progression and IXT are yet to be revealed in a large cohort study. Moreover, it is worth investigating the effects of IXT surgery on anisometropia development, even though previous studies with small sample sizes have reported that IXT surgery may not affect myopic progression rate.11,12

SE, interocular SE difference, and cylindrical errors showed weak or no linear correlations with distant, near, and distant-near angles in this study. Wen et al. also reported little correlation between SE and exotropia angle.9 Cotter et al. reported in a population-based study of children aged 6–72 months that risk factors for exotropia were a higher degree of astigmatism and anisometropia.15 They investigated astigmatism and anisometropia as causes of IXT, whereas current study investigated the quantitative relationship between these factors and exotropia severity. Based on these findings, it can be assumed that the occurrence of IXT may be slightly affected by SE, anisometropia, and cylindrical errors. However, the magnitude of IXT may not be affected by these factors.

Patients with CI-type exotropia showed more myopia in both more and less myopic eyes. This finding confirms previous reports that compared refractive errors.9,16 Myopic patients may require less accommodation at near fixation, thus reducing the convergent efforts for fusion and resulting in a larger exotropic angle at near fixation than at distant fixation. In addition, a previous study reported that accommodation lag in the non-dominant eye, which may play a greater role at near fixation, may hinder fusional convergence in patients with IXT.17 However, it is of note that patients with CI-type IXT accounted for only 12.0 % in spite of over 40 to 50 % of our patients with myopia. This indicates that mechanisms other than fusional accommodation may play a role in the pathogenesis of IXT.

In the present study, patients with poorly controlled distant fixation tended to be less myopic. In contrast, near-control status was not associated with the severity of myopia. This finding was already confirmed in another study using the KIEMS dataset. They speculated that myopic patients with low myopia in Korea, where myopia in childhood reaches up to 60% of the population18,19 would be visually immature and result in poor fusional convergence, considering myopia got severe with age. Patients with low myopia showed low age, and they would have low visual maturity and fusional potency. Considering that age-specific refractive error distributions vary according to ethnicity or environment,4 this finding may differ from those of other populations.

This study has some limitations. First, there was a difference in refraction methods between the two datasets. KNHANES used non-cycloplegic autorefraction, whereas KIEMS employed standard cycloplegic refraction. The KIEMS data seemed to show less myopia than the KNAHNES data than it really did. Although the difference may be within -0.5 to -0.75 diopters according to a previous study investigating the effects of cycloplegia on autorefraction measurements,20 this systematic difference may still have underestimated the true difference in refractive error distribution between the two groups. Second, potential selection bias may arise from the retrospective design. The KIEMS data collected from multiple referral centers, which might not fully represent the entire pediatric IXT population. The impact of unintended missing cases could introduce potential selection bias. Third, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes the establishment of temporal causality. Regarding dynamic questions—such as whether IXT promotes myopic progression in the non-dominant eye or if anisometropia is a causal factor for IXT—only hypotheses can be proposed. Therefore, our conclusions should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than etiological evidence, and prospective cohort studies are urgently needed for verification.

In conclusion, patients with IXT showed refractive error distributions that were generally comparable to those of the general population in Korea. However, our observations suggest that the non-dominant eye tends to manifest a higher degree of myopia compared to the dominant eye yet remains significantly less myopic than the average observed in the general population. The frequency of anisometropia was also found to increase with advancing age in patients with IXT. The magnitudes of the SE, cylindrical error, and interocular SE difference were weakly correlated with the severity of exotropia. CI-type IXT exhibited a higher degree of myopia compared to other IXT subtypes. Furthermore, patients with poor distance control were notably less myopic than those with good distance control.

Methods

KIEMS is a nationwide, retrospective, observational, cross-sectional multicenter study. Protocol and analysis of the entire KIEMS dataset have been described previously.21,22 In short, the study was conducted in collaboration with 65 strabismus specialists from 53 institutions in the KAPOS. The study included medical records of first-visit patients with IXT between March 2019 and February 2020. The patients consisted of those with a strabismus angle of > 8 prism diopters (PD) of exotropia at 6 m or 33 cm using prism and alternate cover test, regardless of age. Informed consents from the participants were waived by IRB, considering the retrospective nature of the study. The KIEMS protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of all participating institutions for data collection, and comprehensive ethical approval was granted by the IRB of Kim’s Eye Hospital.

We included patients aged 5–18 years from the KIEMS dataset without amblyopia which was defined as 0.2 or more interocular difference of LogMAR visual acuity. We collected cycloplegic refraction (CR) data using cycloplegic refraction with 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride (Cyclogyl; Alcon Lab. Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA) and 1% tropicamide (Mydriacyl, Alcon Lab. Inc.). For age-specific comparison of refractive errors between the patients and the normal population in Korea, we collected refractive error data of patients aged 5 to 18 years from the 7th KNHANES (2016), the most recently available dataset. The KNHANES protocol was approved by IRB of the Korean Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Sampling design and size of the KNHANES were designed to represent the entire Korean population using stratified, multistage, clustered sampling methods. Specific KNHANES protocol has been described previously.23,24 Noncycloplegic refractive errors were measured using an autorefractor (KR-8800; Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Spherical equivalents (SE) were calculated as spherical errors + 0.5 × cylindrical errors. Anisometropia was defined as > 1.5 D an interocular difference.

Exotropia angles measured at distant and near fixations were collected. Exotropia types were categorized into three types: basic (difference between distant and near angle less than 10 PD), convergence insufficiency (near – distant angle ≥ 10 PD) and divergence excess (distant – near angle ≥ 10 PD) types. Ocular dominance data at distant fixation were also investigated, and only dominance data from patients who showed monocular dominance were included. Fusional control under distant and near viewing conditions was defined as follows: good, when ocular fusion was disrupted only after the cover test at distance fixation and was rapidly regained without blinking or fixating ocular movements; fair, when ocular fusion was regained only after blinking or fixating movements after disruption with cover testing at distance fixation; and poor when ocular fusion was spontaneously broken without fusion disruption or not regained despite blinking or re-fixation.25 We excluded data with missing values for the strabismus angle and CR.

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.7; https://www.python.org/), SciPy (version 1.13.1; https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/index.html), Statsmodel (version 0.14.4; https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/index.html), and scikit-posthocs package (version 0.11.2; https://scikit-posthocs.readthedocs.io/). For categorical variables, such as sex and number of anisometropias, Fisher’s exact test was conducted for ratio differences. For age-specific refractive error comparisons between patients and KNHANES data, an independent t-test was used for each age group from 5 to 18 years. A paired t-test was used to compare SE between the dominant and non-dominant eyes. Linearity between exotropia angle and SE was evaluated using linear regression analysis. Multiple comparisons of SE according to exotropia type or fusional control groups were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests and post-hoc Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test for differences. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
