Association of Postoperative Slip Reduction With Patient-Reported Outcomes at Two Years After Fusion Surgery for Low-Grade Degenerative Spondylolisthesis: A Single-Center Study
Authors: Tom Folkerts, Julia Wimmer, Lukas Schönnagel, Anna-Maria Mielke, Bruno Verna, Pedro Rocha Torres, Paul Köhli, Jiaqi Zhu, Jennifer Shue, Rolando Duculan, Andrew A Sama, Federico P Girardi, Frank P Cammisa, Carol A Mancuso, Marco D Burkhard, Alexander P Hughes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective cohort study of prospectively enrolled patients.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association between postoperative slip reduction and improvement in disability and low back pain (LBP) after lumbar fusion for low-grade degenerative spondylolisthesis (DLS), and to determine whether outcomes are independently associated with slip reduction or with sagittal alignment changes.Methods160 patients undergoing lumbar fusion for low-grade DLS were analyzed. Slip reduction was quantified on lateral radiographs as absolute change in relative slippage normalized to vertebral body dimensions. Disability and LBP were assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and numeric rating scale (NRS) preoperatively and at two years. Associations between slip reduction and improvement in ODI and LBP were analyzed using multivariable linear regression adjusted for demographics, baseline symptoms, fusion length, and preoperative slippage.ResultsSlip reduction was independently associated with greater improvement in ODI and LBP (both p<0.001) at two years. Although slip reduction correlated with changes in segmental slip angle, lumbar lordosis, and PI–LL (all p≤0.001), these alignment parameters were not independently associated with clinical outcomes (all p>0.05).ConclusionsPostoperative slip reduction was independently associated with greater improvement in disability and LBP among patients undergoing lumbar fusion with attempted reduction for low-grade DLS, whereas associated changes in sagittal alignment were not. However, given the moderate explanatory power of the regression models, slip reduction should be interpreted as one of several factors associated with postoperative outcomes. As this was an observational analysis, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded, and prospective validation is warranted to further define the role of slip reduction in surgical decision-making.Keywords: degenerative spondylolisthesis, lumbar fusion, slip reduction, patient-reported outcome measures, sagittal alignment

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) is a common cause of spinal stenosis and neurogenic claudication in the aging population. The typical surgical treatment for DLS is decompression with or without fusion.1,2 When fusion is performed, the role of intraoperative slippage reduction remains a subject of ongoing debate, with uncertainty regarding whether achieving greater slip reduction is associated with improved clinical outcomes.3-7

In high-grade DLS, the available evidence tends to favor slippage reduction. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that reduction provides significantly greater improvement in disability scores and superior correction of spinopelvic parameters compared to in situ fusion. 3 Reduction in high-grade cases achieves better radiographic correction while maintaining neurologic complication rates comparable to those of in situ fusion, leading to growing acceptance of reduction techniques in this population, in which the biomechanical rationale for restoring alignment is more evident. 4 The proposed mechanism underlying these benefits is the restoration of sagittal balance through correction of local kyphotic deformity and improvement of spinopelvic parameters, particularly pelvic tilt and slip angle.3,4,8

In low-grade DLS, however, the clinical relevance of slip reduction remains highly debated. Multiple recent meta-analyses comparing reduction with in situ fusion have not demonstrated superior clinical outcomes with reduction, despite consistently greater radiographic correction.5,7,9 The current North American Spine Society guidelines state that there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against reduction with fusion in DLS, highlighting the lack of consensus in the existing literature. 10 However, these studies primarily addressed whether reduction should be attempted at all, rather than whether the degree of slip reduction influences outcome. Within cohorts undergoing reduction, the magnitude of postoperative slip correction has received comparatively little attention and has either not been separately analyzed or has been evaluated using heterogeneous measurement methods and different predefined thresholds.5,6,9,11 As a result, the relationship between the extent of slip reduction achieved and postoperative clinical improvement remains insufficiently characterized.

Given the ongoing controversy regarding the relationship between slip reduction and clinical outcomes in DLS, the present study analyzed a prospectively enrolled cohort of patients with low-grade DLS in whom slip reduction was consistently attempted during fusion surgery. The study aimed to address two objectives. First, we evaluated whether the extent of postoperative slip reduction achieved was associated with improvements in disability and low back pain (LBP) at two-year follow-up. Second, we examined whether postoperative clinical outcomes were independently associated with slip reduction or with the associated changes in sagittal alignment parameters.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This single-center investigation retrospectively analyzed a prospectively maintained database of patients who underwent fusion surgery for symptomatic DLS at a tertiary academic spine center between January 2016 and December 2018. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board at our institution (IRB# 2015-237), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.12,13

All surgical procedures were performed by board-certified spine surgeons with at least 10 years of clinical experience. Posterior decompression with pedicle screw instrumentation was performed in all cases, with interbody fusion achieved via a transforaminal (TLIF), anterior (ALIF), or lateral (LLIF) approach using either polyetheretherketone (PEEK) or titanium cages, based on surgeon preference and patient anatomy. Intraoperatively, reduction of spondylolisthesis was consistently attempted through screw-based reduction maneuvers and interbody cage placement. Postoperative management followed standardized institutional protocols and best practice guidelines. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for LBP, were prospectively collected preoperatively and at two-year follow-up.

Data Collection and Population

The cohort included adult patients (≥18 years) with DLS who underwent lumbar fusion surgery. Patients were required to provide written informed consent. Inclusion further required complete preoperative and postoperative lumbar spine imaging, including upright standing lateral radiographs obtained preoperatively and within one week postoperatively, as well as complete pre- and postoperative PROMs. Exclusion criteria included prior lumbar surgery, incomplete or poor-quality preoperative or postoperative imaging, and missing pre- or postoperative PROMs. Demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index) and clinical variables, including osteoporosis (documented diagnosis), diabetes mellitus (documented diagnosis requiring pharmacological treatment), smoking status (never, former, current), and preoperative motor deficit (documented motor weakness on clinical examination), were recorded for all patients.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

PROMs included preoperative and two-year postoperative assessments of functional disability using the ODI and LBP intensity measured with the NRS. The ODI is a validated 10-item instrument designed to evaluate disability associated with spinal conditions across domains such as pain, mobility, and activities of daily living. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 to 5, with scores summed and expressed as a total percentage ranging from 0% to 100%.

Absolute (ΔODI_abs) and relative (ΔODI_rel) improvements in functional disability were calculated from preoperative and postoperative ODI scores, with minimal clinically important difference (MCID) achievement defined as a relative improvement of at least 30% from baseline.14,15 Absolute improvement represents the difference in ODI percentage points, whereas relative improvement reflects the proportional change relative to the preoperative ODI value. Calculations were performed using the following formulas:

I. Absolute ODI improvement (ΔODI_abs) [percentage points] = preoperative ODI − postoperative ODI.

I. Absolute ODI improvement (ΔODI_abs) [percentage points] = preoperative ODI − postoperative ODI.

II. Relative ODI improvement (ΔODI_rel) [%] = (ΔODI_abs/preoperative ODI) × 100.

II. Relative ODI improvement (ΔODI_rel) [%] = (ΔODI_abs/preoperative ODI) × 100.

LBP intensity was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), an 11-point validated scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable). Preoperative and two-year postoperative values were recorded. Absolute and relative changes in LBP were calculated, with MCID achievement defined as a relative improvement of at least 30% from baseline.14,16 The corresponding calculations were performed as follows:

III. Absolute LBP improvement (ΔLBP_abs) [NRS points] = preoperative LBP − postoperative LBP.

III. Absolute LBP improvement (ΔLBP_abs) [NRS points] = preoperative LBP − postoperative LBP.

IV. Relative LBP improvement (ΔLBP_rel) [%] = (ΔLBP_abs/preoperative LBP) × 100.

IV. Relative LBP improvement (ΔLBP_rel) [%] = (ΔLBP_abs/preoperative LBP) × 100.

Radiographic Assessment of Segmental Slippage

Segmental slippage was quantified on upright standing lateral lumbar radiographs obtained preoperatively and within one week postoperatively. To account for interindividual differences in vertebral body size, segmental slippage was expressed as relative slippage (RS) rather than absolute slippage (Figure 1). RS was assessed by measuring the absolute sagittal slippage (d1) of the superior vertebral body relative to the inferior vertebral body and the anteroposterior length of the superior endplate of the caudal vertebra (d2). To determine d1, a reference line (l1) was placed along the posterior margin of the inferior vertebral body, followed by a parallel line (l2) along the posterior margin of the superior vertebral body; the perpendicular distance between these lines represented d1 in millimeters. In the same image, the anteroposterior length of the superior endplate of the caudal vertebra (d2) was measured (Figure 1A).17-19 Additionally, the degree of preoperative slippage was classified according to the Meyerding system, distinguishing between low-grade (grade I or II) and high-grade (grade III or higher) spondylolisthesis.20-22

Postoperative slip reduction (ΔSlippage) was assessed on immediate postoperative radiographs obtained within the first week after surgery, as this time point most directly reflects the surgically achieved reduction. Additionally, the most recent available follow-up radiograph obtained during postoperative clinical follow-up related to the index procedure was used for each patient to assess whether the achieved reduction was maintained over time. ΔSlippage was defined as the absolute difference in RS between preoperative and postoperative imaging and is therefore expressed in percentage points, with higher values indicating a greater reduction in segmental slippage.

Dynamic instability was quantified on preoperative flexion–extension radiographs as the relative translation between flexion and extension positions, calculated as the ratio of sagittal translation to the anteroposterior endplate length of the caudal vertebra.18,19,23

Sagittal Alignment Measurements

Segmental and global sagittal alignment parameters were assessed on upright standing lateral radiographs obtained pre- and postoperatively. Measurements included segmental slip angle (SA) and spinopelvic alignment parameters. The intervertebral SA was determined by drawing a line along the inferior endplate of the cranial vertebra and a second line along the superior endplate of the caudal vertebra at the affected segment. The angle formed between these lines represents segmental alignment, with positive values indicating kyphosis and negative values indicating lordosis. 24 Spinopelvic alignment was evaluated by measuring pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), PI–LL difference, pelvic tilt (PT), and sacral slope (SS). Definitions and measurement details for these parameters are provided in Supplemental Table 1.25,26 For all sagittal alignment parameters, both preoperative and postoperative values were recorded, and changes between time points were calculated and expressed as delta values (ΔSA, ΔPI, ΔLL, ΔPI–LL, ΔPT, and ΔSS).

Statistical Analysis

Prior to analysis, continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using histogram visualization. Continuous variables are reported as medians (interquartile range [IQR]), whereas categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers (=N). All analyses were performed using a complete-case approach, and no imputation of missing data was undertaken. All available observations were included in the statistical analyses, and no observations were excluded on the basis of extreme values or apparent outliers. Between-group differences were evaluated with the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as all consecutive eligible patients treated during the study period were included. The primary multivariable regression models included six adjustment variables in addition to the primary predictor, corresponding to approximately 23 subjects per variable (SPV), which exceeds recommended minimum thresholds for multivariable regression modelling.27,28 Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between postoperative slip reduction (ΔSlippage) and relative postoperative improvement in functional disability (ΔODI_rel) and LBP (ΔLBP_rel). Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), number of fused levels, preoperative slippage, and baseline symptom severity (preoperative ODI or LBP, respectively). The primary analytical models were intentionally restricted to a limited set of adjustment variables reflecting the principal demographic, disease-specific, and surgical characteristics of the cohort, whereas all additional candidate covariates were evaluated uniformly in separate sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings. These analyses additionally adjusted for osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, preoperative motor deficit, cage type, surgical approach, dynamic instability, and operating surgeon. Because all procedures were performed at a single tertiary academic center by experienced board-certified spine surgeons following standardized treatment principles, with slip reduction consistently attempted in all patients, surgeon-related variability was expected to be limited. The inclusion of operating surgeon in these sensitivity analyses allowed this assumption to be formally evaluated. To evaluate whether the observed associations were robust to the choice of outcome metric, the primary models were additionally fitted using absolute postoperative improvement (ΔODI_abs, ΔLBP_abs) as outcomes. To assess the temporal stability of the achieved reduction, Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate the association between immediate postoperative and follow-up slip reduction values. The primary regression analyses were subsequently repeated using follow-up slip reduction values to evaluate the robustness of the observed associations. To examine the relationship between postoperative slip reduction and changes in sagittal alignment, separate multivariable linear regression models were fitted with changes in segmental slip angle (ΔSA) and spinopelvic alignment parameters (ΔLL, ΔPI, ΔPI–LL, ΔSS, ΔPT) as dependent variables. These models were adjusted for age, BMI, sex, and number of fused levels. To assess whether sagittal alignment parameters mediated the association between postoperative slip reduction and clinical outcomes, additional multivariable models were constructed including postoperative slip reduction and sagittal alignment parameters that demonstrated significant associations with slip reduction in prior analyses. Adjusted R2 values are reported to quantify the proportion of variance explained by each model, accounting for the number of included covariates. Radiographic images were independently evaluated by two spine surgeons, each blinded to the other’s measurements. Interrater reliability for radiographic measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) in a randomly selected subset of 40 patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the discriminative ability of postoperative slip reduction for predicting achievement of the MCID in ODI and LBP. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and an exploratory cohort-specific cutoff value was identified using the Youden index. 29 Bootstrap resampling with 2,000 iterations was performed to derive 95% confidence intervals for AUC values. To evaluate the robustness of the ROC-derived threshold, additional multivariable sensitivity analyses were performed using binary slip reduction thresholds of 6%, 8%, and 10%. The primary regression models were repeated using achievement of the respective threshold as the predictor while retaining all covariates included in the primary models. These analyses were performed using both immediate postoperative and follow-up slip reduction values. The primary analyses were hypothesis-driven and focused on the association between postoperative slip reduction and relative improvement in ODI and LBP. Covariates included in the regression models served as adjustment variables rather than independent hypotheses. Secondary analyses, including ROC-derived threshold identification, group comparisons, sagittal alignment analyses, and sensitivity analyses evaluating model robustness, were considered exploratory. Accordingly, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as these exploratory analyses were intended to generate hypotheses and to provide complementary analyses rather than to support the primary study conclusions. All analyses were performed using RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 175 patients who underwent fusion surgery for DLS were identified from the prospectively maintained cohort. Complete two-year PROM data were available for 173 patients (98.9%). Two patients without complete two-year PROMs were also among those excluded for insufficient imaging. Overall, 15 patients were excluded because preoperative or postoperative radiographic imaging was missing or insufficient for assessment of slip reduction, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 160 patients (Figure 2). Of these, 108 patients (67.5%) were female. The median age was 68.0 years (IQR 61.0 to 72.4), and the median body mass index was 29.0 kg/m2 (IQR 24.9 to 32.3). All patients had low-grade DLS according to the Meyerding classification, with 74.4% classified as grade I and 25.6% as grade II. The most frequently affected level was L4–L5, accounting for 79.7% of cases. Osteoporosis was documented in 33 patients (20.6%), diabetes mellitus in 9 (5.6%), and preoperative motor deficit in 5 (3.1%). Five patients (3.1%) were current smokers and 70 (43.8%) were former smokers. Dynamic instability, quantified as relative translation between flexion and extension radiographs, had a median value of 4.0% (IQR 2.4–6.2). Interbody fusion was performed via a transforaminal approach (TLIF) in 128 patients (80.0%), an anterior approach (ALIF) in 14 (8.8%), and a lateral approach (LLIF) in 18 (11.3%). PEEK cages were used in 144 patients (90.0%) and titanium cages in 16 (10.0%).

Association Between Postoperative Slip Reduction and Improvements in ODI and LBP

Interobserver reliability was excellent for preoperative RS (ICC 0.944, 95% CI 0.896–0.970), postoperative RS (ICC 0.955, 95% CI 0.914–0.976), and postoperative slip reduction (ICC 0.905, 95% CI 0.827–0.949). In multivariable linear regression analyses, postoperative slip reduction was independently associated with relative improvements in both ODI and LBP (Table 1, Figure 3). Slip reduction showed a positive association with ΔODI_rel (β = 4.51, p < 0.001) and ΔLBP_rel (β = 3.76, p < 0.001). Specifically, each 1-percentage-point increase in postoperative slip reduction was associated with a 4.51-percentage-point greater relative improvement in ODI and a 3.76-percentage-point greater relative improvement in LBP. Across the observed IQR of postoperative slip reduction in this cohort (5.97 to 14.65 percentage points), corresponding to an 8.68-percentage-point difference in slip reduction, the estimated association translates into an approximately 39-percentage-point difference in relative ODI improvement and a 33-percentage-point difference in relative LBP improvement, assuming all other covariates remain constant. These associations were consistent when absolute postoperative improvement was used as the outcome measure (ΔODI_abs: β = 1.95, p < 0.001; ΔLBP_abs: β = 0.21, p < 0.001; Supplemental Table 2). Preoperative slippage was inversely associated with relative improvement in ODI (p = 0.001) and LBP (p = 0.045). Baseline symptom severity was significantly associated with postoperative improvement, with higher preoperative ODI and LBP scores corresponding to greater relative postoperative improvement (both p < 0.001).

Exploratory ROC-Derived Threshold of Postoperative Slip Reduction and Clinical Improvement

Exploratory ROC analysis identified a cutoff of approximately 8% postoperative slip reduction that best discriminated patients achieving the MCID in functional disability and LBP within this cohort (Table 2, Supplemental Figure 1). The ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI 0.83–0.95) for ODI and 0.70 (95% CI 0.58–0.82) for LBP. Sensitivity and specificity values corresponding to this cutoff are summarized in Table 2. Consistent associations were observed across alternative slip reduction thresholds of 6%, 8%, and 10%, as well as when follow-up slip reduction values were used (Supplemental Table 3).

Preoperative slippage differed between groups (p < 0.001), whereas preoperative ODI (p = 0.525), preoperative LBP (p = 0.429), and follow-up duration (p = 0.329) were comparable. Association Between Postoperative Slip Reduction and Sagittal Alignment

Interobserver reliability for sagittal alignment measurements ranged from 0.878 to 0.957, with ICC values of 0.953 (95% CI 0.858–0.980) for SA, 0.957 (95% CI 0.919–0.977) for LL, 0.936 (95% CI 0.882–0.965) for SS, 0.912 (95% CI 0.840–0.952) for PI, and 0.878 (95% CI 0.781–0.933) for PT. Multivariable linear regression analyses, adjusted for age, BMI, sex, and number of fused levels, demonstrated significant associations between postoperative slip reduction and changes in segmental SA (p < 0.001), LL (p < 0.001), and PI–LL difference (p = 0.001; Table 4). No significant associations were observed between postoperative slip reduction and changes in PI (p = 0.848), SS (p = 0.365), or PT (p = 0.153). Complementing the adjusted multivariable regression analyses presented in Table 4, descriptive preoperative and postoperative sagittal alignment parameters stratified by the exploratory postoperative slip reduction groups are provided in Supplemental Table 4.

Association Between Spinopelvic Alignment and Postoperative Outcomes

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for demographic factors, surgical characteristics, and sagittal alignment parameters previously shown to be associated with postoperative slip reduction (Table 4), postoperative slip reduction remained independently associated with relative improvement in both LBP (p < 0.001) and functional disability (p < 0.001). None of the included sagittal alignment parameters showed independent associations with postoperative outcomes (Table 5).

Discussion

This study investigated postoperative slip reduction in DLS and its association with clinical outcomes and radiographic parameters, resulting in the following main findings: 1) Postoperative slip reduction was independently associated with improved two-year outcomes in both functional disability and LBP; 2) Slip reduction correlated significantly with changes in segmental SA, LL, and PI-LL difference; 3) However, none of these sagittal alignment parameters demonstrated independent associations with clinical outcomes.

The existing literature on slip reduction in low-grade DLS has focused primarily on comparing outcomes between patients undergoing attempted reduction and those treated with in situ fusion. In the most recent meta-analysis, which included five studies with 704 patients, Wang et al reported no significant differences between reduction and in situ fusion in measures of LBP and disability, as well as in complication rates, fusion rates, or reoperation rates. 5 Similar conclusions have been drawn in other contemporary meta-analyses and cohort studies, which reported superior radiographic correction with reduction but no corresponding advantage in patient-reported outcomes.6,9,11,30,31 Importantly, these investigations addressed a different clinical question: whether attempting slip reduction provides additional benefit over in situ fusion. The present study instead investigated whether the degree of postoperative slip reduction achieved was associated with clinical improvement among patients undergoing attempted reduction.

Evidence evaluating the association between the magnitude of slip reduction and clinical outcomes remains limited, and the available studies have reported conflicting findings. Chan et al analyzed 206 patients with Meyerding grade I DLS from the Quality Outcomes Database and found no association between the magnitude of slip reduction and patient-reported outcomes at 24 months, using a dichotomized threshold of ≥3 mm versus <3 mm reduction. 30 Similarly, Hagenmaier et al found no association between the extent of slip reduction and clinical outcomes in a prospective cohort of patients with low-grade spondylolisthesis treated with instrumented posterolateral fusion. Although their cohort included both DLS and isthmic spondylolisthesis, no association was observed in either the overall cohort or subgroup analyses according to spondylolisthesis etiology. 11 By contrast, Wegmann et al reported a significant association between the magnitude of slip reduction and clinical improvement. In a prospective cohort of 40 patients with DLS undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion, of whom approximately 15% had high-grade slippage (Meyerding grade III–IV), greater reductions in anterior slippage and sagittal rotation were positively correlated with improved outcomes at 12 and 24 months. 32 While differences in patient populations and analytical methodology should be considered when comparing these studies, the finding that greater slip reduction was associated with improved clinical outcomes in both studies supports this association.

Several methodological factors may help explain the heterogeneity of findings among studies examining the relationship between slip reduction and clinical outcomes. A key source of variability relates to the quantification of postoperative slippage and slip reduction. Prior studies have not consistently assessed slip reduction using relative measures. Instead, absolute millimeter-based cutoffs such as ≥3 mm have been applied, which do not account for interindividual variation in vertebral body size and may therefore misrepresent the proportional magnitude of slip reduction.30,31 As demonstrated by Bourassa-Moreau et al, various radiographic measurement techniques can yield substantially different estimates of slippage, with patients shifting between low- and high-grade classifications depending on the method used. 33 The present study applied a relative slippage measure normalized to vertebral body dimensions. This approach offers greater reliability and resolution for detecting clinically meaningful changes and enables a more accurate, graded assessment of how varying degrees of slip reduction relate to clinical improvement.33,34

Beyond measurement methodology, the present study’s analytical approach also differed from previous investigations. Rather than relying on predefined slip reduction thresholds or comparisons of mean outcome scores, this study evaluated relative improvement from baseline and additionally explored a cohort-specific slip reduction threshold for achieving MCID. Recent evidence demonstrates that percentage-based MCID thresholds outperform absolute point-change values, particularly in patients with low or high baseline scores. Asher et al, analyzing 23,280 patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery, found that a 30% reduction in disability and LBP predicted satisfaction with greater accuracy than absolute point-change MCID values across all procedure categories, with the largest differences favoring percentage-based thresholds in patients with the lowest (ODI 0-20%) and highest (ODI 81-100%) disability categories. 14 Similarly, Power et al demonstrated that for patients with relatively low or high presurgical scores, MCIDs based on percentage change rather than absolute change yielded higher correct classification rates. 35 Applying percentage-based outcome definitions, postoperative slip reduction discriminated MCID achievement well within the present cohort. This relationship may be less apparent when mean score differences are analyzed across heterogeneous populations without stratification by baseline severity or degree of reduction.

In addition to the choice of outcome metric, the use of multivariable regression models adjusting for preoperative slippage and baseline symptom severity allowed for a graded analysis of the relationship between slip reduction and clinical improvement. The observed inverse association between preoperative slippage and relative improvement suggests differential treatment responses in patients with more severe baseline spondylolisthesis, a relationship that may be obscured when baseline slip severity is not considered. Importantly, the degree of intraoperative slip reduction achieved may itself reflect underlying disease characteristics rather than representing a purely modifiable surgical variable. Patients with more rigid, chronically degenerated segments may achieve less intraoperative reduction and simultaneously have a poorer intrinsic prognosis, independent of surgical technique. Although the present analyses adjusted for preoperative slippage, baseline symptom severity, and dynamic instability, unmeasured aspects of segmental rigidity and chronicity of degeneration may still confound the observed association between slip reduction and clinical improvement. Accordingly, the degree of reduction achieved may partly reflect underlying disease severity rather than a purely modifiable surgical target, which warrants consideration when interpreting the observed associations. These observations are consistent with established evidence that baseline characteristics modify treatment response in DLS. Singh et al identified high slip percentage as a predictor of non-improvement at long-term follow-up. 36 Similarly, Pearson et al, analyzing the SPORT trial cohort, identified neurogenic claudication, reflex asymmetry, and dissatisfaction with symptoms as independent predictors of greater treatment effect, with differences ranging from 6-9 ODI points depending on baseline characteristics. 37 These findings highlight the importance of accounting for baseline severity when examining treatment-outcome relationships. This relationship, however, differed between the two outcome measures in the present cohort: preoperative LBP was significantly associated with relative improvement in LBP, whereas preoperative ODI was not significantly associated with relative improvement in ODI. As this finding was not a prespecified hypothesis, the following interpretation is offered as a post hoc, hypothesis-generating explanation. This asymmetry likely reflects fundamental differences in the measurement characteristics and construct coverage of the two instruments. The NRS captures pain intensity as a unidimensional construct on a narrow 11-point scale (0–10), in which higher baseline severity may translate more directly into greater proportional improvement. In contrast, the ODI assesses disability across ten heterogeneous functional domains on a broader scale (0–100%), encompassing activities such as personal care, walking, sitting, and social life. Patients with similar total ODI scores may therefore present with markedly different domain-level impairment profiles and may improve in different domains regardless of their overall baseline score, potentially attenuating the statistical relationship between baseline severity and relative improvement. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that pain and disability represent distinct constructs with only moderate correlation after spine surgery, suggesting that the determinants of improvement differ between these outcome dimensions. 38 Accordingly, baseline ODI may be a less direct predictor of proportional postoperative improvement than baseline pain intensity, despite remaining an important adjustment variable within the regression model.

Furthermore, several prior investigations examining slip reduction in spondylolisthesis included heterogeneous patient populations, combining isthmic and degenerative etiologies or pooling low- and high-grade cases. For example, 71% of patients had isthmic spondylolisthesis in Hagenmaier et al's cohort, while Wegmann et al's cohort included approximately 15% of patients with high-grade slippage.11,32 By focusing exclusively on low-grade DLS, the present study avoided these sources of patient heterogeneity.

The present study further demonstrated that sagittal alignment parameters, while significantly associated with slip reduction, did not independently predict clinical outcomes. This observation challenges prior investigations that reported associations between restoration of spinopelvic alignment and clinical outcomes after fusion for DLS.39,40 However, these associations are not undisputed, and emerging evidence suggests a more nuanced relationship. Mohanty et al demonstrated that fusion improved outcomes only in patients with high PI-LL difference, whereas patients with low PI-LL difference had worse outcomes with fusion compared with decompression alone. 41 Most recently, Sheidae Mehne and Motlagh Pirooz reported a clinical-radiographic paradox in which patients achieved substantial symptom relief after lumbar fusion despite the lack of overall sagittal alignment correction, with only individualized lordosis restoration correlating with pain improvement. 42 These findings align with the present study’s results and suggest that sagittal alignment parameters may serve as markers of slip severity and reducibility rather than independent determinants of outcome. Accordingly, the association between postoperative slip reduction and clinical improvement observed in the present study may reflect mechanisms other than sagittal alignment restoration alone.

Several mechanisms may explain the observed association between slip reduction and improved outcomes independent of global sagittal alignment parameters. Although spinal canal and foraminal dimensions were not measured in the present cohort, indirect neural decompression represents a plausible explanatory mechanism. Accordingly, the following mechanistic rationale is based on existing anatomical, imaging, cadaveric, and clinical evidence rather than on findings from the present study. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that the degree of vertebral slippage directly influences neural space dimensions. Imaging studies report a significant inverse correlation between slippage percentage and spinal canal volume, while cadaveric models show that incremental increases in slip percentage significantly reduce foraminal area and volume.43-46 A clinical study further reported significant increases in spinal canal cross-sectional area (+19%) and intervertebral foramen areas (+21–39%) following vertebral reduction in DLS, with concurrent improvements in pain and disability. 47

In the present cohort, patients achieving ≥8% slip reduction demonstrated not only greater correction but also lower absolute postoperative slippage despite higher preoperative slip values, suggesting more complete restoration of segmental anatomy that may contribute to sustained indirect neural decompression through increased foraminal and canal dimensions. Additionally, slip reduction may reduce abnormal loading patterns on facet joints and adjacent segments by normalizing stress distribution at the fused level and reducing compensatory hypermobility at adjacent levels.48-50 While the present study did not assess adjacent segment degeneration, this biomechanical optimization may contribute to sustained pain relief beyond the immediate postoperative period.

From a clinical and research perspective, the present findings may carry implications for surgical decision-making in patients with low-grade DLS. Among patients in whom reduction is attempted, greater slip reduction was independently associated with more favorable outcomes across the continuous range of achieved reduction, and this association remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses. These findings support the use of continuous percentage-based measures when evaluating postoperative slip reduction in future studies. Exploratory ROC analysis also identified a cohort-specific cutoff of approximately 8% that discriminated patients achieving MCID across the evaluated outcome measures. However, as this cutoff was both derived and evaluated within the same dataset, it should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and should not be used to guide clinical decision-making until externally validated in independent cohorts. Importantly, the moderate explanatory power of the multivariable models (adjusted R2 values of 0.21–0.28) indicates that postoperative slip reduction represents only one of several contributors to postoperative clinical outcomes, and that additional patient-, disease-, and treatment-related factors account for a substantial proportion of outcome variability. In addition, restoration of sagittal alignment parameters remains relevant for overall spinal biomechanics and may mitigate the risk of adjacent segment disease. However, the present findings suggest that improvements in LBP and function may be more closely associated with slip reduction than with attainment of specific alignment targets. Furthermore, the inverse association between preoperative slippage and relative improvement suggests that patients with more severe baseline spondylolisthesis may benefit from achieving greater degrees of slip reduction, although this requires prospective validation. Finally, the magnitude of slip reduction remained independently associated with clinical improvement, whereas the number of fused levels did not, suggesting that postoperative improvement may be more closely related to the degree of slip reduction achieved than to the extent of fusion.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Although the analysis was retrospective, the data were derived from a prospectively collected cohort with standardized PROM assessment, which reduces but does not fully eliminate the risk of bias. Nevertheless, the observational design precludes definitive causal inference. Furthermore, despite adjustment for measured covariates, the degree of postoperative slip reduction may have been influenced by surgeon- and case-specific factors that could not be fully captured in a retrospective analysis. In addition, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Patients with more severe or more rigid baseline spondylolisthesis may achieve less intraoperative slip reduction and have a poorer intrinsic prognosis, independent of the degree of reduction achieved. Although the analyses adjusted for preoperative slippage, baseline symptom severity, and dynamic instability, unmeasured aspects of disease severity may still have contributed to the observed association. The multivariable models also demonstrated only moderate explanatory power (adjusted R2 ranging from 0.21 to 0.28 across analyses), indicating that a substantial proportion of the variability in postoperative outcomes remains unexplained. Additional patient-specific, psychosocial, radiographic, and surgical factors not captured in the present study may also contribute to postoperative disability and pain outcomes. Moreover, given the moderate sample size, the identified association and the proposed slip reduction threshold require external validation in larger, independent cohorts to confirm their robustness and generalizability. Further, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the exploratory correlation analyses between slip reduction and the six sagittal alignment parameters (Table 4). Nevertheless, the primary conclusions of the present study are based on the multivariable analyses rather than these individual correlations, as none of the alignment parameters demonstrated independent associations with clinical outcomes. As the ROC-derived 8% threshold was both derived and evaluated within the same dataset, it should be regarded as exploratory and should not inform clinical decision-making until prospectively validated in independent cohorts. The analysis was also limited to a mid-term follow-up of two years, which may not fully capture longer-term outcomes. In addition, a temporal discrepancy exists between radiographic assessment of slip reduction (immediate postoperative) and clinical outcome measurement (two years). Sensitivity analyses using the most recent available follow-up radiograph (median 9.1 months) demonstrated a strong correlation with immediate postoperative values, and all primary results remained unchanged. While previous evidence indicates that slip reduction is maintained after successful fusion, the degree of reduction at the exact time of PROM assessment was not measured.31,51,52 Additionally, as a single-center study, the external validity of these findings may be influenced by center-specific surgical decision-making, imaging protocols, and patient characteristics, which may limit direct extrapolation to other clinical settings. Of note, as the present cohort included only patients in whom reduction was attempted, the findings do not address whether attempting reduction confers benefit over in situ fusion, a question that requires comparative study designs. Finally, future studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying the association between slip reduction and improved postoperative outcomes.

Conclusion

The present study found that postoperative slip reduction was independently associated with greater improvement in disability and LBP in patients undergoing fusion for low-grade DLS in whom slip reduction was consistently attempted. While slip reduction was associated with changes in sagittal alignment parameters, these parameters were not independently associated with clinical outcomes, suggesting that alignment correction may primarily reflect slip reducibility rather than being independently associated with clinical improvement. However, as this was an observational analysis, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded, and postoperative slip reduction should be interpreted as one of several factors associated with postoperative clinical outcomes, given the moderate explanatory power of the regression models. Prospective validation is therefore warranted before these findings can inform surgical decision-making.
