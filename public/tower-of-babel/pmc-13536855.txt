Pathogens in rheumatoid arthritis: epidemiological, mechanistic, and clinical insights
Authors: Futai Feng, Ziyan Wu, Honglin Xu, Zhan Li, Hongzheng Wu, Jingdi Zhang, Zhixin Xu, Shulan Zhang, Yongzhe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by synovial inflammation, joint destruction, and systemic comorbidities. Emerging evidence highlights the critical involvement of the microbiome in disease pathogenesis, encompassing bacteria, viruses, fungi, and mycoplasmas. This review synthesizes recent findings on microbiome alterations, summarizing epidemiological, molecular, and mechanistic data. Among the evaluated pathogens, Porphyromonas gingivalis and Prevotella copri currently demonstrate the strongest causal evidence for initiating autoimmunity. Porphyromonas gingivalis directly catalyzes host protein citrullination via its unique peptidylarginine deiminase, driving anti-citrullinated protein antibody production, while Prevotella copri expands during the preclinical phase to drive T helper 17 cell polarization. Furthermore, chronic Hepatitis C virus infection presents compelling causal links, as continuous viral stimulation triggers robust autoantibody production in atypical memory B cells. Other infectious agents, including Epstein-Barr virus and Proteus mirabilis, act as environmental triggers through molecular mimicry, whereas opportunistic pathogens like Pneumocystis jirovecii emerge as severe secondary complications resulting from profound pharmacological immunosuppression. In terms of immediate clinical translation, several microbiome-targeted strategies are poised for routine practice. These include non-surgical periodontal therapy and oral hygiene optimization to reduce systemic disease activity, the utilization of direct-acting antivirals for Hepatitis C virus to concurrently resolve joint inflammation, and targeted prophylaxis using sulfasalazine to prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in highly immunosuppressed populations. Additionally, the immunomodulatory application of Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides for symptomatic pain relief and the preclinical development of bacterial virulence factor inhibitors represent promising therapeutic avenues. Together, these findings confirm that the microbiome acts as a critical modifier of the inflammatory milieu. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by symmetric polyarthritis, synovial hyperplasia, and progressive joint destruction [1]. The pathogenesis of RA is driven by a complex interplay of genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Among environmental factors, pathogens are increasingly recognized as prominent environmental contributors, with their roles classified into three categories: (1) disease initiation, by triggering autoimmunity; (2) disease modification, by exacerbating pre-existing inflammation; and (3) complication-related effects, by inducing secondary infections or lymphoproliferative disorders [2, 3]. Mechanistic studies suggest that microbial influences in RA are multifaceted, involving the modulation of immune cell differentiation, the production of autoantibodies, and direct contributions to inflammatory cascades [4]. These interactions underscore the microbiota’s role as both a mediator of immune balance and a potential amplifier of autoimmune responses.

This review specifically examines pathogens that are implicated in the onset and progression of RA. It aims to provide a comprehensive summary of the epidemiological associations, pathogenic mechanisms, and clinical implications of viruses, bacteria, fungi, and mycoplasmas in RA, thereby offering valuable insights for both basic research and clinical practice.

Viruses and rheumatoid arthritis

Viral infections have long been proposed as potential environmental triggers for RA. A growing body of evidence suggests that several viruses—most notably Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, parvovirus B19, chikungunya virus, and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1—interact with genetic and immunological risk factors to influence RA initiation, progression, and clinical outcomes. These viruses may drive disease through mechanisms including molecular mimicry, chronic immune activation, and dysregulation of T and B cell responses. Understanding virus-specific effects provides valuable insight into RA heterogeneity and may guide the development of precision prevention strategies and targeted antiviral-immunomodulatory therapies.

Epstein-Barr virus

Epidemiological and mechanistic studies position Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) as a prominent candidate among environmental factors associated with the initiation and progression of RA, supported by epidemiological, histopathological, and mechanistic evidence [5, 6].

Epidemiologically, RA patients exhibit distinct serological and molecular signatures of EBV exposure, including elevated anti-EBV antibody titers, a tenfold higher EBV load compared to healthy controls, and impaired EBV-specific suppressor T cell function—early findings that positioned EBV as a candidate trigger for chronic immune complex–mediated RA [7]. Subsequent studies refine this association. RA patients show higher positivity for anti-RA-associated nuclear antigen (anti-RANA, 71% vs. 6%), elevated anti-viral capsid antigen (anti-VCA) titers, and preferential reactivity to the diffuse component of anti-early antigen (anti-EA) [8]. Notably, antibodies against a citrullinated EBV nuclear antigen 2 peptide (EBNA-2, strain AG876) exhibit 77% sensitivity and 95% specificity for RA, linking EBV-driven post-translational modification to autoantibody generation [9]. And salivary EBV DNA is elevated relative to healthy individuals and patients with other rheumatic diseases [10].

Tissue-level evidence, though variable, supports EBV presence in inflamed RA synovium. While one study detected no EBV in 37 RA synovial biopsies [11], others identified EBV-encoded small RNA1 (EBER1) in 100% of RA synovia with non-resolving inflammation [12], and latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) and EBER1 localized to synovial lining cells in 23.5% of RA patients [13]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in disease activity, biopsy sites, or detection methods but collectively support EBV localization in inflamed RA synovium.

Mechanistically, EBV contributes to RA pathogenesis through multiple pathways. In mice, pre-exposure to EBV DNA increases both incidence and severity of collagen-induced arthritis (CIA), enhancing anti-collagen IgG production and IL-17 A+IFNγ + joint-infiltrating cells [14]. In humans, RA patients show defective EBV-specific immune regulation, including absent late B-cell suppression and impaired cytotoxic T-cell responses, which correlate with disease activity and can be restored by sulphasalazine treatment [15, 16]. EBER1, present in the synovium of patients with persistent inflammation, directly induces IL-6 secretion from synovial fibroblasts, promoting synovial inflammation [12]. Molecular mimicry further links EBV to RA: the EBV antigen BOLF1 shares homology with MAP_4027 and IRF5, a transcription factor that drives inflammatory cytokine expression in macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells [17]. In murine models, IRF5 deficiency reduces arthritis severity, highlighting its role in MyD88-dependent cytokine production (IL-1β, TNFα, IL-6) and neutrophil recruitment [18]. EBV-induced IRF5 overexpression promotes autoantibody production, plasmacytoid dendritic cell expansion, and spontaneous NETosis, thereby linking innate and adaptive immunity and driving the generation of anti-citrullinated/carbamylated protein antibodies [19, 20].

EBV is also implicated in RA-related complications, especially methotrexate (MTX)-associated lymphoproliferative disorders (MTX-LPD). EBV is detectable in 24–50% of RA-MTX-LPD cases, and peripheral EBV load predicts spontaneous regression following MTX withdrawal [21, 22]. Notably, tocilizumab does not increase EBV load and may reduce EBV positivity [23].

Meta-analyses corroborate the epidemiological association of EBV with RA susceptibility and related lymphoproliferative disorders, representing Level II evidence [24–26]. However, while robust in vitro and murine mechanistic data highlight pathways such as IRF5 activation and T-cell dysfunction, definitive human causal links remain elusive. Furthermore, conflicting synovial biopsy data suggest that EBV localization in the joint may not be a universal feature, but rather restricted to specific dynamic inflammatory states. Therefore, current evidence best positions EBV as a significant disease modifier and amplifier of the inflammatory cascade, rather than a definitively proven solitary initiator of RA immunopathogenesis.

Cytomegalovirus

The association between cytomegalovirus (CMV) and RA is less well established than that of EBV. Current evidence suggests that CMV is more likely to contribute to disease progression rather than initiation, and its pathogenic influence may be limited to specific patient subsets rather than exerting a universal effect. In this context, CMV is better characterized as a disease modifier in CMV-seropositive patients with chronic inflammation or immunosuppression.

Studies show inconsistent results regarding CMV’s role in RA. Most serological investigations report no significant difference in CMV IgG titers between RA patients and healthy controls. Although 20% of RA patients test positive for CMV IgM, this is largely attributed to rheumatoid factor (RF) interference rather than acute infection. Similarly, the higher prevalence of anti-CMV antibodies in RF-positive RA patients is likely due to RF-mediated cross-reactivity rather than a true pathogen-specific immune response [27, 28]. Modern epidemiological studies confirm the absence of a causal link. In a cross-sectional NHANES cohort (n = 5133), the initial association between CMV IgG seropositivity and RA (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.21–3.12) lost statistical significance after covariate adjustment, and Mendelian randomization studies found no genetic correlation between CMV antibody responses and RA [29]. A systematic review of 48 studies further confirmed no significant association between CMV infection and RA risk (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.78–1.09), differentiating CMV from pathogens such as parvovirus B19 or hepatitis C virus that show consistent links with RA [30].

CMV is detectable in synovial tissues of only a small subset of RA patients. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses report CMV DNA in 6–13.3% of RA synovial samples—higher than the 0–3.1% detection rate in non-RA joint disorders. CMV-positive RA patients often display higher CMV-specific antibody titers in synovial fluid than in serum, suggesting localized immune activation [31, 32]. In contrast, CMV DNA is not detectable in peripheral blood mononuclear cells or serum, arguing against systemic viral reactivation as a common feature of RA [33]. Collectively, these data support the concept of localized CMV persistence in a minority of RA patients rather than a widespread pathogenic role.

CMV infection is strongly linked to T-cell dysregulation, particularly the expansion of cytotoxic CD4 + CD28null and CD8 + CD28null T-cell subsets, which are enriched in RA. In early RA, CMV-seropositive patients have higher frequencies of CD8 + CD28null T cells than seronegative patients, and these cells correlate with C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, suggesting CMV shapes T-cell immunity early in disease [34]. These CD28null T cells are associated with cardiovascular mortality in RA. CD4 + CD28null T cells directly damage blood vessels, and their expansion beyond 1–2% is exclusive to CMV-infected individuals. A 10-year follow-up study showed that CMV IgG positivity correlated with increased carotid intima-media thickness (IMT), a marker of atherosclerosis, mediated by CD4 + CD28null and CD8 + CD28null T cells expressing the vascular homing receptor CX3CR1 [35, 36]. CMV-positive RA patients also show increased frequencies of LIR-1 + CD8+ T cells, which exhibit both CMV-specific cytotoxicity and autoreactive potential. Normally, LIR-1 restrains T-cell activation, but in RA, insufficient soluble HLA-G fails to suppress these cells, whose frequencies correlate with disease activity [37]. Interestingly, TNFα inhibitors enhance CMV-specific T-cell immunity in RA, increasing IFNγ-producing CD8 + memory T cells—suggesting that immunotherapy may partially restore CMV immune surveillance, though the clinical implications of this finding remain unclear [38].

CMV seropositivity is associated with more severe RA outcomes, including accelerated radiographic joint destruction and higher rates of joint replacement surgery. These patients also exhibit elevated serum monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1) levels and increased CMV-specific IFNγ-secreting CD4 + T cells [39]. CMV-related complications occur mainly in immunosuppressed RA patients, with case reports describing splenic infarction, ileocolitis, and lupus-like syndrome during methotrexate or TNFα inhibitor therapy [40–42]. Not all therapies increase CMV risk, however: in a cohort of 22 RA patients treated with tocilizumab, CMV viral load did not increase, and one CMV-positive patient converted to negative after 9.2 months of follow-up [23].

Overall, the relationship between CMV and RA rests on Level II evidence. Consistent with its localized presence and absence of systemic genetic correlation, CMV primarily acts as a disease modifier in seropositive, immunosuppressed patients. Rather than initiating autoimmunity, its pathogenic contribution is largely confined to shaping T-cell dysregulation (e.g., CD28null T cell expansion) and exacerbating vascular or joint destruction in specific patient subsets.

Parvovirus B19

The relationship between parvovirus B19 (B19V) and RA is characterized by subgroup-specific associations and mechanistic evidence of proinflammatory effects, but lacks universal support as a causal trigger.

Serological and pathogen detection studies reveal conflicting but clinically relevant patterns. In a case–control study of 118 RA patients and 49 healthy controls, B19V DNA was more frequently detected in RA patients (25.4% vs. 18.4%), with a significantly higher prevalence of cell-free plasma B19V DNA (13.6% vs. 2%) [43]. RA patients with circulating B19V DNA (B19 + p) exhibited higher disease activity, increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate, anemia, and elevated plasma IL-6 levels, particularly in those with high CRP [43]. Notably, most B19 + p RA patients lacked neutralizing antibodies to B19V VP-1 S (VP1u) and VP-N antigens, suggesting impaired viral clearance that may perpetuate inflammation [43]. In contrast, other studies failed to confirm B19V involvement in RA pathogenesis: a study of 142 early RA patients detected no B19V DNA in sera, synovial fluids, or synovial fluid granulocytes, with only 3% showing serological evidence of recent B19V infection [44]. A twin study of 155 discordant pairs found no overall association between B19V IgG seropositivity and RA, though subgroup analyses suggested possible links in RF-seronegative RA and female-affected opposite-sex dizygotic pairs [45].

Mechanistically, B19V promotes RA-relevant inflammation primarily via its non-structural protein 1 (NS1), which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages. In vitro studies using human monocytic cell lines show NS1 upregulates NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1, leading to increased IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α secretion as well as enhanced matrix metalloproteinase 9 activity [46, 47]. Celastrol treatment suppresses these effects, reducing inflammasome activation and cytokine release, providing direct evidence of B19V’s pathogenic potential and identifying possible therapeutic targets [46].

Clinically, B19V infection has been linked to the progression of acute arthritis to chronic RA-like disease in case reports. Patients with B19V-associated mono- or oligoarthritis can develop persistent inflammatory arthropathy fulfilling ACR/EULAR criteria for RA or spondyloarthritis, particularly when viral replication persists [47, 48]. These observations align with subgroup findings from the twin study, suggesting B19V may act as a trigger in genetically susceptible patients rather than as a universal cause of RA [45].

Collectively, the current literature provides Level III evidence for the involvement of Parvovirus B19 in RA. While in vitro macrophage models robustly demonstrate NS1-driven inflammasome activation and subsequent pro-inflammatory cytokine release, human epidemiological data remain subgroup-specific and occasionally conflicting. Therefore, B19V is best characterized not as a universal causal trigger, but rather as a potential disease modifier that may unmask or amplify preexisting autoimmunity in genetically susceptible individuals.

Chikungunya Virus

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), an arthritogenic alphavirus transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, has a strong epidemiological and mechanistic link to RA, largely by triggering chronic inflammatory arthritis that mimics RA and, in some cases, facilitating de novo RA development.

Clinically, CHIKV infection progresses through acute and chronic phases, with 32.3–50% of infected individuals developing persistent joint symptoms for > 3 months (chronic chikungunya arthritis, CCA) [49, 50]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies (2879 patients) confirmed that 13.7% (95% CI: 6.12–27.87%) of CHIKV-infected patients are subsequently diagnosed with RA. CCA frequently fulfills ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria for seronegative RA, presenting with symmetric small joint involvement, morning stiffness, and elevated disease activity scores [51, 52]. Notably, CCA patients often show low positivity for RF and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP, 13.5–20.3%) antibodies, distinguishing them from classic seropositive RA but overlapping with seronegative RA phenotypes [53, 54]. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that CCA can progress to erosive joint damage for up to 13 years post-infection, mirroring RA’s destructive features [55].

Mechanistically, CHIKV and RA share overlapping immunopathogenic pathways. CHIKV infection induces elevated serum IL-6, IL-17 A, TNF-α, and HMGB1, all central cytokines in RA pathogenesis, with IL-6 and IL-17 A correlating with joint inflammation severity and chronicity [56, 57]. Flow cytometry and CyTOF studies reveal expansion of effector CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and reduced regulatory T cells—similar to RA immunophenotypes [52, 58]. Molecular mimicry further links CHIKV to RA. Peptide motif analysis shows CHIKV capsid proteins share sequence homology with collagen epitopes that bind RA-susceptible HLA-DR4 alleles, potentially triggering cross-reactive T cell responses and autoimmunity [59]. CHIKV-infected fibroblast-like synoviocytes secrete RANKL, IL-6, and MCP-1, promoting macrophage recruitment and osteoclastogenesis—key drivers of bone erosion [60]. Mouse models of CHIKV arthritis show transcriptomic overlap with RA and CIA, including T cell- and IFN-γ-related signatures [61].

CHIKV also impacts patients with pre-existing RA, exacerbating disease activity. A case series of 6 RA patients in clinical remission showed severe RA reactivation following CHIKV infection, with increased Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) requiring intensified therapy [62]. Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) such as methotrexate, alone or in combination with hydroxychloroquine/sulfasalazine, reduce disease activity and improve functional outcomes in CCA patients without impairing antiviral responses [63, 64]. Biologics targeting IL-1β and TNF-α also alleviate CCA symptoms and reduce bone loss in murine models, suggesting potential therapeutic repurposing [65, 66].

In summary, there is robust Level II evidence linking CHIKV to chronic arthropathy, but it is crucial to clinically distinguish Chronic Chikungunya Arthritis (CCA) from true autoimmune RA. Although mechanistic overlaps exist in murine models and in vitro transcriptomics, clinical evidence suggests CHIKV acts primarily to mimic RA phenotypes or occasionally unmask latent autoimmunity, rather than serving as a universal trigger for bona fide ACPA-positive RA.

Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1

Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1), a retrovirus primarily associated with adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) and HTLV-1-associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP), also acts as a disease modifier influencing RA susceptibility, pathogenesis, and outcomes—particularly in endemic regions [67].

Epidemiological evidence consistently links HTLV-1 infection to increased RA risk and severity. A cross-sectional study of 2091 Nagasaki cohort demonstrated that HTLV-1 seropositivity was significantly more prevalent in RA patients than in non-RA controls (27.3% vs. 10.1%), and this association remained significant after adjusting for age, sex, and hepatitis C virus infection [68]. A meta-analysis confirmed a 2.8-fold increased RA incidence in HTLV-1 carriers [69]. Notably, HTLV-1-positive RA patients have higher global assessment scores and HAQ scores and may face an increased risk of serious infections requiring hospitalization [70].

The molecular mechanisms underlying HTLV-I-associated RA primarily center on the viral transactivating gene tax, which drives synovial cell dysregulation and inflammatory responses. In vitro studies show Tax activates the IL-6 gene in RA synovial cells via NF-κB signaling, driving synovial hyperplasia and inflammation [71]. Transfection of tax into synovial cells enhances proliferation and cytokine secretion, recapitulating RA-like synovial overgrowth [72]. Tax transgenic mice develop chronic inflammatory arthropathy resembling RA, with synovial proliferation, cartilage erosion, and RF production, and in some cases exhibit ankylosis, suggesting contributions to both inflammatory and degenerative processes [72, 73].

HTLV-I infection significantly impacts therapeutic responses in RA patients. TNF inhibitor efficacy is reduced in HTLV-1-positive RA patients, with lower EULAR response rates and higher DAS28-ESR scores compared with HTLV-1-negative patients [74]. Conversely, IL-6 blockade with tocilizumab has been effective without exacerbating HAM/TSP symptoms [75]. HTLV-1 proviral load (PVL) serves as a prognostic marker: patients with comorbidities (e.g., bronchiectasis, malignancies) have significantly higher PVL, indicating elevated ATL risk [76]. Flow cytometric profiling of HTLV-1-infected cells is emerging as a tool for ATL risk prediction in RA patients receiving antirheumatic therapy [77]. Additionally, longitudinal analysis showed that HTLV-I PVL does not change significantly over 2 years in most HTLV-I-positive RA patients, but individual PVL increases correlate with infection incidence, highlighting the need for regular PVL monitoring [78].

Taken together, Level II epidemiological and mechanistic evidence supports HTLV-1 as a regional disease modifier. While transgenic mouse models demonstrate the direct arthritogenic potential of the tax gene, human data predominantly associate the virus with amplified disease severity and altered therapeutic responses. This positions HTLV-1 primarily as an exacerbating factor in endemic populations rather than a global initiator of RA.

Hepatitis C virus

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is increasingly recognized as a potent environmental trigger for the development of RA [79]. Population based epidemiological studies demonstrate that HCV infection significantly increases the risk of incident RA [80]. The fundamental pathogenetic mechanism relies on the profound lymphotropism of the HCV, which establishes a state of chronic immune system stimulation [81, 82]. Specifically, the activation of TLR9 within HCV-associated atypical memory B cells induces a distinct transcriptional signature characterized by the overexpression of TNFα [83]. This sustained viral stimulation promotes type 1 effector T-cell activation and drives the robust production of RF and other autoantibodies [83].

The intricate relationship between these two conditions is further highlighted during clinical management. Immunosuppressive therapies required for RA can complicate viral control; for instance, biologic agents including rituximab have been shown to exacerbate hepatitis C viremia by decreasing the transmission of host protective exosomal microRNA 155 [84]. However, the therapeutic landscape has been revolutionized by the introduction of direct-acting antivirals [81]. Clinical evidence confirms that successful viral eradication utilizing direct-acting antivirals not only resolves hepatic pathology but also significantly improves RA disease activity scores [85]. Furthermore, studies demonstrate that successful viral eradication not only reverses hepatic pathology but also substantially attenuates synovial inflammation and autoantibody titers in certain patients [85, 86]. Additionally, large cohort studies reveal that patients receiving antiviral therapies exhibit a significantly reduced longitudinal risk of developing RA compared to untreated cohorts, providing Level II evidence that HCV infection directly contributes to the immunopathogenesis of RA [87].

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2

The emergence of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 has underscored the critical role of acute viral infections as potential environmental triggers and disease modifiers in systemic autoimmunity. Large population based epidemiological studies reveal a significantly increased incidence of inflammatory arthritis, specifically including rheumatoid arthritis, following coronavirus disease 2019 infection [88]. The pathogenetic crosstalk between these conditions involves profound immune hyperactivation characterized by a massive release of proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNFα, which closely mirrors the synovial cytokine cascade observed in RA [89, 90]. Mechanistically, the viral infection drives macrophage-mediated pathways and shares multiple genetic susceptibility loci with RA, particularly those regulating immune function and coagulation [91]. Furthermore, the virus can induce the production of novel polyreactive RF that cross-react with viral spike proteins and host immunoglobulin G epitopes, providing crucial insights into how transient viral infections precipitate the loss of peripheral immune tolerance [92].

In the clinical context, the bidirectional relationship between the virus and host autoimmunity presents significant challenges. Patients with RA exhibit a persistently higher risk of viral infection, intensive care admission, and mortality compared to the general population [93, 94]. The management of these patients is further complicated by baseline immunosuppressive therapies. Extensive clinical data demonstrate that the use of systemic glucocorticoids and B-cell depleting therapies, such as rituximab, significantly predisposes patients to severe viral outcomes and impairs vaccine immunogenicity. Conversely, targeted treatments such as tumor necrosis factor inhibitors appear to exert a protective effect against severe respiratory complications, highlighting the intricate therapeutic interplay during acute viral outbreaks [95–97]. Overall, large population-based registries provide Level II evidence defining SARS-CoV-2 as a potent environmental trigger and clinical modifier of RA.

Bacteria and rheumatoid arthritis

Oral and gut bacteria play crucial roles in the initiation and progression of RA, with protein citrullination serving as a shared mechanistic axis. Among these, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is the most extensively studied pathogen, with strong epidemiological and mechanistic links to RA. Prevotella copri contributes to disease initiation through gut dysbiosis and Th17 activation, whereas Helicobacter pylori and Staphylococcus aureus primarily modulate disease severity and risk of complications. Periodontal intervention, modulation of gut microbiota, and inhibition of key virulence factors represent promising therapeutic strategies.

Porphyromonas gingivalis

RA exhibits a robust epidemiological association with P. gingivalis, the major pathogen of chronic periodontitis. A meta-analysis of 28 studies demonstrated that P. gingivalis exposure significantly increases RA risk (OR = 1.86; 95% CI: 1.43–2.43), with stronger effects in European (OR = 2.17) and North American (OR = 2.50) cohorts compared with Asian populations (OR = 1.11), highlighting geographical variability [98]. Another meta-analysis reported significantly elevated anti-P. gingivalis antibody titers in RA patients compared to both healthy controls and periodontitis patients without systemic disease [99]. Detection of P. gingivalis DNA in synovial tissue, particularly in HLA-DRB1*04-positive patients, suggests genotype-dependent tissue tropism [100]. Furthermore, high serum anti-Porphyromonas gingivalis peptidylarginine deiminase (PPAD) IgG levels correlate with poor response to biological DMARDs, suggesting a potential role of P. gingivalis immunity as a prognostic biomarker [101].

Mechanistically, P. gingivalis promotes RA primarily via two virulence factors: PPAD and gingipains (Rgp and Kgp). PPAD uniquely catalyzes protein citrullination at neutral pH, generating neoantigens that drive anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA) production [102]. Incubation of P. gingivalis with host proteins such as fibrinogen or α-enolase results in gingipain-mediated proteolysis, followed by PPAD-dependent citrullination, yielding immunogenic epitopes that trigger RA-specific autoimmunity [103]. In CIA models, P. gingivalis dysbiosis induces systemic citrullination and ACPA production, whereas PPAD-deficient mutants fail to exacerbate arthritis, confirming PPAD’s essential role [104].

Gingipains further exacerbate synovial inflammation by degrading immune effector molecules, activating proinflammatory cytokines, and impairing macrophage phagocytosis through CD14 cleavage [105, 106]. Gingipains also generate RF antigens by cleaving IgG Fc regions, thereby promoting immune complex formation and joint inflammation [106].

Beyond direct citrullination, P. gingivalis modulates host immunity by expanding Th17 cell populations and increasing IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-23 production, which collectively drive synovial hyperplasia and bone erosion [104]. P. gingivalis also induces neutrophil extracellular trap (NETs) formation, releasing citrullinated proteins and peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) into the extracellular milieu—effects amplified in individuals carrying PAD4 risk polymorphisms [107]. Additionally, oral P. gingivalis dysbiosis disrupts the gut-oral microbiome axis, altering intestinal microbiota composition, impairing barrier integrity, and promoting systemic inflammation [108].

Animal studies support a causal role for P. gingivalis in RA. In Lewis rats, oral exposure leads to periodontitis followed by ACPA-positive arthritis with histological evidence of synovitis and bone erosion within 8 months [109]. In C3H mice, combined P. gingivalis dysbiosis and collagen immunization induce severe, ACPA-positive arthritis with germinal center B cell and T follicular helper cell expansion [110]. Notably, arthritogenicity is strain-dependent: fimbriae-expressing strains (e.g., W83) can initiate arthritis even without prior autoimmune priming, whereas non-fimbriated strains mainly exacerbate preexisting arthritis [111].

Future directions include the development of gingipain inhibitors (e.g., COR388, COR286), which block citrullination and inflammation [112], and PPAD inhibitors, which suppress ACPA generation in preclinical models [113]. Identifying highly arthritogenic P. gingivalis strains and dissecting gut-oral microbiome interactions will advance microbiota-targeted approaches for RA prevention.

Supported by highly consistent epidemiological meta-analyses and robust in vivo murine models (Level I evidence), P. gingivalis currently stands as one of the most compelling microbial candidates in RA pathogenesis. Notably, its unique direct enzymatic capacity (PPAD) distinguishes it mechanistically from other pathogens. Nevertheless, while it is a potent driver of disease exacerbation in animal models, its role in human RA is likely synergistic within the oral-gut axis rather than solely causative. Future directions must prioritize identifying highly arthritogenic strains and developing targeted PPAD/gingipain inhibitors to translate these mechanisms into clinical prevention.

Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans

Growing evidence implicates Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa) in RA initiation and progression through its unique ability to induce hypercitrullination in neutrophils and bridge local periodontal inflammation to systemic autoimmunity [114, 115].

Clinical associations between Aa and RA are supported by several cohort studies, though findings are not universally consistent. A Chinese cohort reported significantly higher Aa prevalence and abundance in RA patients with periodontitis, correlating with DAS28, RF, anti-CCP antibodies, and ACPA levels [116]. Similarly, a Swedish study found elevated anti-leukotoxin A (LtxA) IgM levels in both early and established RA patients, suggesting that exposure may precede clinical disease onset [117]. However, some studies failed to detect significant associations between Aa carriage and RA parameters, emphasizing the need for large-scale, multiethnic studies to clarify population-specific effects [115, 118].

LtxA, a pore-forming toxin targeting neutrophils, is the principal virulence factor linking Aa to RA [115]. LtxA promotes massive calcium influx into neutrophils, triggering peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs) activation, widespread protein citrullination, and generation of ACPA [119, 120]. Additionally, LtxA induces neutrophil cell death and NETosis, releasing hypercitrullinated proteins into the extracellular space, further amplifying the immune response [121].

Additional mechanisms include inflammasome activation: systemic Aa dysbiosis activates caspase-11–dependent inflammasomes in macrophages, enhancing IL-1β and IL-18 production and exacerbating arthritis severity in CIA models [122]. Aa induced IL-1β production and AIM2 inflammasome activation [123]. Moreover, Aa lipopolysaccharide stimulates toll-like receptor 4 signaling, intensifying systemic inflammation and synovial immune activation [124].

Periodontal therapy targeting Aa can improve RA outcomes. Nonsurgical periodontal treatment (NSPT) significantly reduces Aa abundance and DAS28 scores in RA patients with concomitant periodontitis [125]. Adjunctive antimicrobial strategies, including photodynamic therapy and diode laser treatment, effectively eliminate Aa in vitro even in the presence of immunosuppressive drugs [126]. Case reports further suggest that systemic eradication of Aa can lead to resolution of arthritis symptoms and normalization of anti-CCP titers in patients with Aa-related dysbiosis [127].

Overall, the implication of Aa in RA pathogenesis is supported by Level II evidence. Although its unique LtxA-mediated hypercitrullination pathway provides a highly plausible mechanistic link in vitro and in animal models, clinical associations remain heterogeneous across different ethnic cohorts. Thus, Aa is best viewed as a critical amplifier of the autoimmune repertoire in a specific subset of periodontitis-associated RA patients, rather than a universal driver.

Helicobacter pylori

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a common gastric pathogen, has been proposed as a potential environmental trigger for RA via mechanisms involving protein citrullination, immune dysregulation, and molecular mimicry [128].

The potential link between H. pylori infection and RA has been widely studied, though epidemiological evidence remains inconsistent. Some studies report a positive association. H. pylori-positive RA patients show higher DAS28 and ACPA levels, indicating more severe disease [129, 130]. A 17-year follow-up cohort found that H. pylori infection was associated with an increased risk of RA, particularly in women under 30 [131]. H. pylori infection also correlates with methotrexate discontinuation due to gastrointestinal intolerance [132]. Conversely, large Danish and Japanese cohorts found no significant association between H. pylori and RA risk [133, 134], suggesting heterogeneity due to strain virulence, host genetics, or confounders.

Mechanistically, H. pylori upregulate PAD4 expression through hypoxia-inducible factor 1α stabilization, promoting protein citrullination and ACPA generation [129]. Chronic infection sustains IL-6 and IL-8 production, driving Th1/Th2 skewing, loss of tolerance, and autoantibody production [135]. Molecular mimicry between H. pylori heat-shock protein 60 and its human homolog may induce cross-reactive immune responses against joint tissue [136, 137]. In addition, H. pylori urease selectively activates B-1 cells, leading to rheumatoid factor and anti-ssDNA antibody production [138]. Infection with virulent strains, particularly cagA-positive H. pylori, amplifies immune dysregulation, elevates mucosal cytokines, and increases the risk of autoimmune disease, including RA [136, 139].

Clinically, eradication therapy has been associated with improvement in DAS28, ESR, and fibrinogen levels over 24 months [140], and may reduce cardiovascular risk in RA patients [141].

In conclusion, Level II evidence regarding H. pylori and RA remains conflicting. While in vitro studies suggest potential mechanisms like PAD4 upregulation and molecular mimicry, large-scale clinical cohorts fail to show a uniform epidemiological association. H. pylori may therefore act as a systemic inflammatory modifier or an aggravator of therapy-related complications rather than a direct causative agent of joint autoimmunity. Controlled trials are ultimately required to clarify its clinical relevance in routine RA management.

Prevotella copri

Prevotella copri (P. copri) is one of the most prominent gut commensals associated with RA. Its abundance is enriched in fecal samples from patients with new-onset untreated RA and in individuals at preclinical stages of disease [142, 143], suggesting a role in early pathogenesis.

In CIA models, RA-derived P. copri strains exacerbate joint inflammation, an effect potentiated by high-fiber diets that promote fermentation and succinate overproduction [144]. Succinate acts as a pro-inflammatory metabolite, activating macrophages and worsening arthritis. Fecal microbiota transplantation from RA patients into SKG mice induces Th17 expansion and more severe arthritis, supporting a causal role of dysbiosis in promoting autoreactive T cell activation [145].

RA patients exhibit humoral and cellular immune responses to P. copri, including elevated IgA and IgG anti-PC-p27 antibodies and Th1/Th17 responses that correlate with ACPA levels [146–148]. Pangenome analyses reveal that RA-derived P. copri strains possess unique genomic elements, including conjugative transposons, which may confer enhanced immunostimulatory capacity [149].

Clinically, reductions in P. copri abundance correlate with improved DAS28-CRP scores in patients treated with TNF inhibitors, suggesting that P. copri may serve as a biomarker for treatment response [150].

Collectively, Level II evidence positions P. copri as a key player in the gut-joint axis. While fecal microbiota transplantation in SKG mice elegantly demonstrates its arthritogenic potential in vivo, human data are currently predominantly associative. P. copri expansion likely represents a critical dysbiotic event that promotes Th17-driven systemic inflammation, thereby modifying the immune milieu to favor RA onset and progression.

Staphylococcus aureus

RA patients are at substantially increased risk of Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (SAB) and subsequent osteoarticular infection (OAI), with a 90-day cumulative incidence nearly twice that of non-RA patients (HR 1.93, 95% CI: 1.54–2.41) [151]. Risk is further elevated in patients with orthopedic implants or those receiving TNF inhibitors [151].

Nasal colonization with S. aureus, a major risk factor for invasive disease, is more common in RA patients, particularly those with active disease or receiving biologic DMARDs [152, 153]. SAB in RA patients is associated with higher rates of disseminated infection and increased mortality [154].

RA therapies have complex effects on S. aureus outcomes. In murine models, tofacitinib worsens S. aureus-induced septic arthritis but improves survival in systemic sepsis and toxic shock by suppressing TNF-α and IFN-γ [155]. Interestingly, auranofin, an oral gold compound approved for RA, exhibits potent in vitro and in vivo activity against methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), suggesting its potential for therapeutic repurposing [156].

In summary, the relationship between S. aureus and RA is primarily unidirectional, supported by Level III epidemiological evidence highlighting the pathogen as a severe opportunistic complication rather than an autoimmune trigger. While RA therapies (e.g., TNFi and JAK inhibitors) alter host susceptibility, S. aureus acts as a major driver of secondary morbidity and mortality—such as septic arthritis and bacteremia—rather than an initiator of the underlying RA immunopathogenesis.

Proteus mirabilis

Proteus mirabilis has been identified as a primary microbial culprit in the etiopathogenesis of RA [157]. Extensive epidemiological studies spanning diverse geographic populations consistently demonstrate significantly elevated titers of P. mirabilis-specific antibodies in the sera of RA patients compared to healthy controls or individuals with inactive disease [158, 159]. These elevated antibody levels positively correlate with biochemical markers of disease activity, including CRP and ESR [160, 161]. Furthermore, urinalysis frequently reveals asymptomatic P. mirabilis bacteriuria in RA cohorts, suggesting that subclinical upper urinary tract infections may serve as a crucial environmental trigger for this systemic autoimmune condition [162, 163].

Mechanistically, molecular mimicry serves as the central pathway through which P. mirabilis drives RA pathogenesis. The P. mirabilis haemolysin sequence (ESRRAL) exhibits profound stereochemical homology with the shared epitope (EQRRAA/EQKRAA) found in RA-susceptibility HLA-DR1/4 alleles [164]. Concurrently, the bacterial urease sequence (IRRET) structurally mimics the LRREI motif of human type XI collagen, a major constituent of hyaline cartilage [159, 164]. Autoantibodies generated against these bacterial antigens cross-react with self-peptides, exerting cytotoxic effects on articular cartilage and synovial tissues [165]. Notably, both the ESRRAL and IRRET sequences contain arginine doublets that function as substrates for PAD, facilitating citrullination and elucidating the early generation of ACPA in RA [159]. Additionally, antibodies targeting the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of P. mirabilis strain S1959 demonstrate cross-reactivity with host type I collagen [166]. Interestingly, regarding bone metabolism, P. mirabilis-derived outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have been shown to induce mitochondria-dependent apoptosis in osteoclasts, paradoxically attenuating bone loss in experimental models [167].

Translating these mechanistic insights offers significant therapeutic potential for microbiome-targeted RA interventions. Clinical studies indicate that short-term fasting followed by a prolonged vegetarian diet significantly reduces anti-Proteus antibody titers; this immunological decline parallels improvements in disease activity and a reduction in urinary P. mirabilis isolation [168, 169]. Consequently, integrating targeted antimicrobial measures, including specific antibiotics, dietary modifications, and natural bacteriostatic agents, with conventional early RA therapies has been proposed to arrest irreversible joint destruction [170]. Novel antimicrobial strategies further support this approach; the combination of quercetin and chondroitin sulfate exerts synergistic antibacterial effects against P. mirabilis while simultaneously downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [171]. Furthermore, specific plant extracts and sulfapyridine, the intestinal azo-reduction metabolite of sulphasalazine, demonstrate potent inhibitory activity against P. mirabilis growth, highlighting the viability of microbiome-modulating strategies in RA management [172–174]. Collectively, extensive cross-sectional data provide Level III evidence supporting the role of P. mirabilis as a driver of RA via molecular mimicry.

Fungi and rheumatoid arthritis

Fungi play dual roles in RA, functioning as both opportunistic pathogens and potential immunomodulators. Opportunistic infections such as PCP, candidiasis, aspergillosis, and cryptococcosis are particularly relevant in patients undergoing immunosuppression and require early recognition and prophylaxis. Conversely, fungal-derived polysaccharides such as GLPs and S. boulardii components demonstrate therapeutic potential by modulating immune responses, inhibiting angiogenesis, and protecting bone integrity. A deeper understanding of the fungal–host interaction network may open new avenues for biomarker discovery, infection risk stratification, and microbiome-targeted therapies in RA.

Fungi as potential disease modifiers of RA

Candida

Epidemiological studies indicate that RA is the most frequent autoimmune comorbidity among candidemia patients, with C. albicans accounting for two-thirds of cases [175]. ELISA data have further shown that RA patients exhibit elevated anti-Candida enolase IgG antibody levels compared to healthy controls [176].

Immunological studies suggest impaired Candida-specific host defenses in RA. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse models, Candida cell-wall components exacerbated disease severity and increased IFN-γ and anti-type II collagen antibody production [177]. Although phagocytosis of Candida by peripheral blood PMNs appears intact [178], synovial fluid PMNs from RA patients frequently display defective C3b receptor function, reducing opsonophagocytic capacity [179]. Interestingly, Candida killing remains normal, suggesting that impaired phagocytosis—rather than candidacidal dysfunction—may contribute to infection susceptibility. Moreover, RA patients exhibit impaired oral immune responses to Candida albicans despite elevated IL-17 A levels, indicating a paradoxical Th17 dysfunction [180]. Lymphocyte responses to Candida antigens are blunted but may normalize following RA treatment, suggesting reversibility of immune defects [181].

Immunosuppressive agents can further exacerbate susceptibility. Leflunomide alters late-phase immune responses by increasing anti-Candida IgM and reducing IgG production, correlating with impaired resistance to reinfection in CIA models [182, 183]. Rituximab profoundly reduces IL-17 and IL-22 production, impairing Th17-mediated mucosal immunity [184]. Tofacitinib, a JAK inhibitor, decreases survival in Candida-infected mice by inhibiting chemokine expression and neutrophil infiltration, thereby increasing infection risk [185].

Collectively, current data provide Level III evidence for Candida as both an opportunistic pathogen and a potential disease modifier. While in vivo murine models demonstrate that Candida cell-wall components can exacerbate arthritis, human data primarily highlight a cyclical relationship: RA-associated Th17 dysfunction and immunosuppressive therapies predispose patients to infection, which may in turn transiently amplify systemic inflammatory cascades rather than initiate de novo autoimmunity.

Other fungi

Other fungal species have also been implicated in RA pathogenesis or therapy. Sporidiobolus pararoseus polysaccharides modulate arachidonic acid metabolism and bone remodeling pathways, while their extracellular polysaccharides improve gut barrier function and microbiota composition [186, 187]. Fungal metabolite flavipin has been identified as an inhibitor of RA-associated signaling molecules [188]. Saccharomyces boulardii alleviates CIA by restoring intestinal mucosal integrity via TLR2/MYD88/NF-κB pathway inhibition [189]. Fungal β-glucans activate Dectin-1 and induce autoimmune arthritis in genetically susceptible mice [214]. Clinical cases have reported disseminated Histoplasma capsulatum infection and coccidioidomycosis involving the cranium in RA patients [190–194], as well as septic arthritis caused by Saccharomyces species [195–197].

Fungi as opportunistic pathogens in RA

Pneumocystis jirovecii

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) is a severe opportunistic infection in RA patients, particularly among those receiving immunosuppressive therapy such as biologic DMARDs, JAK inhibitors, or MTX [198–200]. Clinical evidence indicates that patients treated with infliximab or etanercept are especially susceptible to early-onset PCP, often occurring within the first six months of therapy and presenting with rapid clinical progression [201–203]. Adalimumab has also been implicated, highlighting the need for close monitoring during treatment initiation [204].

A retrospective analysis demonstrated that biologic DMARDs increase the risk of P. jirovecii colonization by up to eightfold, particularly in older patients and those receiving long-term therapy [200]. Interestingly, RA patients developing PCP while on MTX may present with relatively preserved lymphocyte counts and distinct biomarker patterns—such as elevated β-D-glucan and soluble IL-2 receptor levels—suggesting a unique pathophysiology compared with PCP in other immunosuppressed populations [205–207].

Multiple risk factors have been identified, including advanced age, high corticosteroid or MTX doses, prolonged treatment duration, hypoalbuminemia, and the concurrent use of multiple immunosuppressive agents [208–210]. High baseline prednisolone dosage is associated with worse outcomes and increased mortality [210]. Notably, RA-associated PCP differs from AIDS-related PCP in both clinical presentation and radiology, often showing intense pulmonary inflammation despite lower organism burden [207].

Preventive strategies are crucial. Sulfasalazine use has been associated with reduced colonization and decreased PCP incidence [211, 212]. Additionally, PCR-based screening for asymptomatic P. jirovecii colonization has been proposed as a tool for identifying high-risk patients and guiding prophylactic therapy [199, 213].

Aspergillus

Opportunistic aspergillosis has been reported in RA patients, especially those receiving long-term immunosuppressive therapy [214–218]. Clinical data suggest that female and middle-aged RA patients are disproportionately affected. Immunological studies have shown elevated IgG and IgA antibody levels against Aspergillus fumigatus antigens in RA patients compared with controls, indicating sensitization [219]. Elevated serum galactomannan antigen levels (≥ 0.5) were detected in 18.2% of RA patients and correlated with abnormal serum protein profiles and hemoglobin levels [220]. These findings suggest that RA patients may be more prone to subclinical colonization or sensitization, which could predispose them to invasive disease under immunosuppression.

Cryptococcus

Cryptococcosis has been described in RA patients, particularly those treated with corticosteroids, MTX, TNF inhibitors, or JAK inhibitors [221–227]. Clinical manifestations range from cutaneous cryptococcosis and pulmonary nodules to disseminated infections and cryptococcal meningitis [228, 229]. Some cases mimic bacterial cellulitis or malignancy, delaying diagnosis [230, 231]. Epidemiological studies indicate that RA patients with chronic kidney disease and those receiving adalimumab are at higher risk [232]. Early recognition and antifungal therapy are critical for preventing poor outcomes.

Taken together, the clinical evidence surrounding Pneumocystis jirovecii, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus in RA is defined by Level III epidemiological data (primarily retrospective cohorts and case-control studies) identifying them as opportunistic complications. These fungal pathogens do not initiate or drive RA autoimmunity; rather, their clinical relevance lies entirely in their emergence as severe, sometimes life-threatening secondary infections resulting from the profound immunosuppression induced by biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs.

Ganoderma lucidum: fungal immunomodulators for RA therapy

Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides (GLPs) exhibit multiple protective effects in RA, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, anti-angiogenic, and osteoprotective activities [233]. Mechanistically, GLPs inhibit the proliferation and migration of rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs) and restore the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby reducing synovial inflammation [234, 235]. GLPs regulate antigen-presenting cell function, suppress macrophage and natural killer cell activity, and shift macrophage polarization by modulating the M1/M2 ratio [236–239]. These effects collectively attenuate cytokine production and promote immune homeostasis. GLPs also influence both humoral and cellular immunity by regulating immunoglobulin production and modulating T- and B-lymphocyte proliferation [240–243]. Beyond immunomodulation, GLPs inhibit angiogenesis by reducing vascular endothelial cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression [244–247]. They also exert osteoprotective effects by inhibiting matrix metalloproteinases and stimulating osteoblast differentiation, thereby preserving bone and cartilage integrity [235, 248–250].

GLP/GLPP demonstrates compelling preclinical efficacy as a natural anti-RA agent, but rigorous pharmacokinetic characterization and Phase I/II RA trials are essential to advance translational development. Strategic formulation approaches, such as nanogels, may enhance bioactivity and facilitate future clinical applications.

Mycoplasmas and rheumatoid arthritis

Mycoplasmas have long been implicated in arthritic diseases, with at least 13 species demonstrated to induce arthritis in animal models [251]. Epidemiological evidence supports a potential link between mycoplasma infections and RA susceptibility. A population-based cohort study involving 116,053 patients with Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) and 464,212 matched controls reported a significantly higher risk of RA development in MP patients, particularly within the first two years of follow-up. The adjusted hazard ratio (HR) was 3.19 for individuals aged ≤ 19 years and 4.14 for those aged ≥ 65 years [252]. Similarly, a case-control study found that RA patients had 2.34-fold increased odds of testing positive for IgG antibodies against MP compared to controls [253].

At the molecular level, Mycoplasma fermentans is among the most frequently detected species in RA patients. Nested PCR analyses identified M. fermentans DNA in 88% of RA synovial fluid samples, revealing four distinct strains, while it was absent in osteoarthritis samples [254]. Additionally, 23% of RA patients exhibited bacteremia, and IgM/IgG antibodies against M. fermentans PG18 were significantly more prevalent in RA sera than in healthy controls [255]. Mycoplasma hominis is another relevant species, as multicenter studies in Italy and Vietnam reported significantly higher seroprevalence of anti-M. hominis lipid-associated membrane protein (LAMP) antibodies in RA patients compared to controls [256]. Notably, M. hominis has been isolated from the synovial fluid of immunocompromised RA patients, including those receiving rituximab who developed septic arthritis, highlighting its pathogenic potential in immune-deficient populations [257].

Mechanistically, mycoplasmal components contribute to RA-related inflammation. LAMPs from Mycoplasma species stimulate proliferation of RASF and induce secretion of proinflammatory cytokines. LAMPs from Ureaplasma urealyticum (UuLAMPs) induce dose-dependent RASF proliferation and elevate TNF-α and IL-1β production in THP-1 macrophages, with a 25 kDa UuLAMP protein identified as a RA-specific cross-reactive antigen [258]. Mitogen (MAM), a superantigen, activates T cells bearing Vβ T cell receptors enriched in human RA synovium, thereby triggering CIA in murine models. MAM exacerbates established CIA and can induce arthritis in mice suboptimally immunized with type II collagen [259]. Correspondingly, RA sera exhibit elevated anti-MAM antibodies compared to sera from other rheumatic diseases or healthy controls [260]. Additionally, the glycolipid GGPL-III, detected in 38% of RA synovial tissues but absent in osteoarthritis or normal tissues, promotes TNF-α and IL-6 production and stimulates RASF proliferation, indicating its role in sustaining synovial inflammation [261].

Therapeutically, tetracyclines such as minocycline have demonstrated potential benefits in RA treatment. Controlled studies indicate that tetracyclines may provide clinical improvement as both monotherapy and adjunctive therapy, potentially through antimicrobial activity against mycoplasmas or via immunomodulatory effects, including inhibition of metalloproteases [262]. However, the precise contribution of mycoplasma eradication to the therapeutic efficacy of tetracyclines remains to be clarified.

In summary, while large population-based cohorts provide Level II evidence for M. pneumoniae, the implication of other mycoplasmas (such as M. fermentans and M. hominis) currently rests on Level III evidence. Although epidemiological associations and PCR detection in synovial fluid exist, mechanistic insights rely heavily on in vitro synoviocyte stimulation assays (e.g., via LAMPs) rather than definitive in vivo human pathogenesis. Consequently, while mycoplasmas exhibit clear pro-inflammatory potential, their precise clinical role—whether as opportunistic colonizers, secondary amplifiers, or genuine disease modifiers—remains unproven and warrants cautious interpretation.

Polymicrobial interactions in RA pathogenesis

Although individual microbial taxa have been associated with RA, increasing evidence from human cohorts and mechanistic studies suggests that polymicrobial interactions within and across mucosal niches contribute more significantly to disease pathogenesis than single microbial species acting in isolation. Dysbiosis in complex microbial ecosystems—particularly within the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract—can disrupt mucosal barriers, shape systemic immune responses, and influence autoantigen generation. As visually summarized in Fig. 1, these cross-niche dynamics support an integrated oral-gut-joint axis model of RA [263, 264], serving as a continuous conduit for disease propagation that ultimately drives profound structural destruction within the synovial microenvironment. Microbial communities exist as dynamic ecological networks in which both synergistic and antagonistic interactions modulate immune homeostasis and inflammatory potential. These interactions can influence the abundance, virulence, and immunogenicity of individual taxa, thereby altering host outcomes in ways that are not explained by single-organism effects [265, 266].

Oral polymicrobial synergy

Co-colonization with P. gingivalis and Aa has been proposed as a particularly important pathogenic combination. P. gingivalis expresses PPAD, an enzyme capable of catalyzing bacterial protein citrullination [103], whereas Aa produces LtxA, which induces neutrophil hypercitrullination through calcium influx and activation of host PAD enzymes [115]. Together, these mechanisms may generate a large pool of citrullinated autoantigens capable of triggering ACPA responses in genetically susceptible individuals. This synergy increases oral barrier permeability, promoting bacterial translocation to the gut and synovium [108].

Cross-kingdom interactions within the oral microbiome may further enhance inflammatory signaling. For example, periodontal infection with P. gingivalis can disrupt epithelial tight junctions through gingipain-mediated degradation of barrier proteins, facilitating colonization by opportunistic fungi such as Candida albicans [177]. Through mitochondrial oxygen scavenging, C. albicans creates anaerobic microniches that enable obligate anaerobes like P. gingivalis to proliferate even under aerobic conditions, reinforcing bacterial persistence in the oral cavity [267, 268]. Fungal β-glucans and other cell-wall components can activate dectin-1 signaling and amplify Th17 responses, thereby potentiating the inflammatory environment initiated by periodontal bacteria [180].

Oral–gut microbial crosstalk

Increasing evidence indicates that microbial dysbiosis in the oral cavity may influence intestinal microbial communities and immune responses. Oral bacteria can translocate to the gastrointestinal tract through saliva swallowing, particularly under conditions of periodontal disease, where barrier disruption facilitates microbial dissemination.

Oral P. gingivalis infection disrupts gut microbiome composition, promoting P. copri enrichment and succinate production [108]. P. gingivalis DNA is detected in the RA gut, where it induces intestinal barrier damage and systemic translocation of P. copri metabolites (e.g., succinate) [108]. This synergy amplifies Th17 cell expansion in both the gut and synovium [144].

Viral infections may further modulate these microbial networks. EBV infection impairs gut mucosal immune function, reducing the abundance of protective commensals (e.g., Lactobacillus) and promoting P. copri dysbiosis [24]. EBV-induced IRF5 activation in the gut further amplifies P. copri-mediated proinflammatory cytokine production [19].

Antagonistic polymicrobial interactions

Not all microbial interactions promote inflammation. Certain commensal organisms exert protective or antagonistic effects, counteracting dysbiosis and limiting inflammatory responses. For example, Lactobacillus rhamnosus can inhibit the growth of P. copri through competition for nutrients and the production of short-chain fatty acids, particularly butyrate [150]. These metabolites strengthen epithelial barrier integrity and suppress inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB activation. Similarly, Bifidobacterium longum has been reported to restore intestinal barrier function and reduce fungal colonization by limiting the adhesion and translocation of Candida species [269–271]. Experimental studies in murine models of inflammatory arthritis suggest that supplementation with Bifidobacterium species can partially reverse Th17-driven immune dysregulation and attenuate joint inflammation [272]. These findings highlight the potential role of beneficial microbial networks in maintaining mucosal immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmune activation.

Clinical implications of polymicrobial networks

Recognition of polymicrobial interactions provides important insight into why therapeutic strategies targeting single pathogens have shown limited success in RA. For example, periodontal treatment or antibiotic therapy directed solely against P. gingivalis has produced inconsistent effects on RA disease activity, suggesting that broader microbial ecosystems must be considered. Future therapeutic approaches may therefore require microbiome-level interventions that target polymicrobial communities rather than individual organisms. Potential strategies include combined periodontal therapy and probiotic supplementation to restore oral–gut microbial balance, as well as microbiome-modulating therapies designed to enhance beneficial commensals and suppress pro-inflammatory microbial networks.

Molecular mimicry: cross-reactive immune responses triggered by pathogen-host protein homology

Molecular mimicry occurs when pathogen antigens share amino acid sequences with host proteins, leading to cross-recognition of host tissues by immune cells. This process is crucial for understanding how pathogens may act as environmental triggers in RA. In molecular mimicry, pathogens can induce autoimmune responses by generating cross-reactive antibodies or T cells that mistakenly target host tissues.

EBV and CHIKV both use molecular mimicry to trigger T-cell cross-recognition, with EBV inducing an immune response against the host transcription factor IRF5, while CHIKV targets collagen epitopes found in joint tissues. H. pylori can induce a cross-reactive B-cell response against human HSP60, which is found in synoviocytes, thereby promoting the production of autoantibodies that damage joint tissue. Mycoplasma fermentans likely triggers T-cell cross-reactivity through an uncharacterized glycolipid antigen (GGPL-III), which may stimulate synovial fibroblasts and sustain inflammation. (Table 2)

Host inflammasome activation: the innate immune inflammation amplification pathway triggered by pathogens

Inflammasome activation, particularly NLRP3, caspase-11, and AIM2, plays a crucial role in the excessive activation of innate immunity in RA. Pathogens can trigger inflammasome activation, leading to the release of IL-1β and IL-18, key mediators of inflammation in RA.

Parvovirus B19 induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation via its non-structural protein NS1, leading to increased IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MMP-9, all of which contribute to joint damage. Aa triggers the non-canonical caspase-11 inflammasome pathway through systemic infection, leading to IL-1β and IL-18 release, which exacerbates CIA. Mycoplasma hominis is presumed to activate inflammasomes via its LAMP, though this pathway requires further validation (Table 4).

Summary of commonalities and differences in core mechanisms

The key mechanisms through which pathogens contribute to RA share certain commonalities while also exhibiting notable differences. All pathogens disrupt immune homeostasis by either breaking immune tolerance or promoting inflammatory pathways, often involving the interplay between innate and adaptive immunity. For instance, citrullination leads to ACPA production, and inflammasome activation drives Th17 polarization, both of which are critical in RA pathogenesis. However, pathogens differ in the specific mechanisms they employ. Viruses such as EBV and CHIKV primarily exploit molecular mimicry or viral protein-mediated regulation of host immune molecules. In contrast, bacterial pathogens like P. gingivalis and Aa use enzyme catalysis, metabolite regulation, or toxin induction to influence immune responses. Mycoplasmas, on the other hand, tend to stimulate synoviocytes directly through their membrane proteins, like LAMP. These differences are closely tied to the pathogen’s colonization sites, infection types (acute or chronic), and interactions with the host immune system, highlighting the complexity of RA pathogenesis and the diverse ways in which infections can drive disease progression.

Discussion

RA is a chronic autoimmune disease shaped by complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors. Increasingly, the microbiome—comprising bacteria, viruses, fungi, and mycoplasmas—emerges as a key player in RA pathogenesis, rather than a mere bystander. Epidemiological, molecular, and mechanistic studies reveal how microbial dysbiosis and pathogen exposure contribute to RA, from triggering autoimmunity through protein citrullination or molecular mimicry to exacerbating joint destruction via chronic inflammation. This section explores the clinical implications of these insights, focusing on pathogen-related biomarkers for diagnosis, microbe-targeted therapies for specific patient subgroups, and critical unresolved questions regarding the role of pathogens in RA.

Diagnosis and prediction: pathogen-associated biomarkers for early RA detection

Early detection of RA, ideally before irreversible joint damage occurs, remains a clinical challenge. Pathogen-related biomarkers have shown promise in addressing this gap. In particular, pathogen-specific antibodies offer valuable tools for identifying preclinical RA and stratifying patients by disease subtype. For instance, antibodies against a citrullinated EBV EBNA-2 peptide exhibit 77% sensitivity and 95% specificity for RA, linking EBV-induced post-translational modification to autoantibody generation. Bacterial antibodies, such as those against PPAD or Aa LtxA, also hold diagnostic value. Elevated IgG levels against PPAD predict poor responses to bDMARDs, while anti-LtxA IgM antibodies are elevated in early RA, often preceding clinical symptoms. However, challenges such as geographic variability—where the association between P. gingivalis exposure and RA risk is stronger in European and North American cohorts than in Asian populations—and potential cross-reactivity with RF complicate the interpretation of these markers.

In seronegative patients, microbial changes in the gut and oral microbiome provide non-invasive signals for early RA. P. copri is enriched in fecal samples from treatment-naive new-onset RA patients and individuals at preclinical stages. Its abundance correlates with Th17 cell expansion and DAS28, and reduced P. copri levels following TNF inhibitor treatment are associated with improved clinical outcomes. In the oral cavity, P. gingivalis and Aa are more prevalent in RA patients than in healthy controls, with their abundance linked to subgingival plaque severity, ACPA titers, and joint erosion in patients with comorbid periodontitis.

However, translating microbial abundance into clinical practice requires addressing challenges such as the disconnect between abundance and function. Metatranscriptomic or metabolomic analyses are needed to confirm microbial activity. Additionally, factors like diet, which can alter P. copri levels, must be accounted for. To overcome the limitations of single biomarkers, combinatorial panels have shown high sensitivity for early RA detection. Integrating host genetic factors, such as the HLA-DRB1*04 genotype, further enhances specificity for precision risk stratification.

Therapeutic intervention: microbe-targeted strategies for RA subgroups

Therapies targeting microbial factors in RA have shifted from broad antimicrobial treatments to more precise interventions that modulate host-microbe interactions. To bridge the critical gap between basic microbiological mechanisms and routine clinical management, we highlight several pathogen-targeted therapeutic and prophylactic strategies that are rapidly approaching clinical reality (Fig. 3). By directly targeting specific ecological niches, ranging from oral optimization to antiviral interventions and targeted prophylaxis, these tailored approaches are increasingly effective depending on specific microbial exposures, disease subtypes, and the immunosuppressive histories of patients.

Emerging pathogen-targeted therapeutic and prophylactic strategies in rheumatoid arthritis. This schematic outlines four distinct clinical translation avenues mapped to their respective anatomical niches and therapeutic outcomes. (A) Oral niche optimization: Non-surgical periodontal therapy combined with advanced oral interdental cleaners effectively eradicates local pathogens, leading to reduced systemic DAS28 scores and attenuated autoantibody levels. (B) Direct antiviral intervention: For patients with concurrent Hepatitis C virus infection, successful viral clearance via direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) promotes the resolution of persistent rheumatoid synovitis and drives the attenuation of autoantibody titers. (C) Targeted prophylaxis: Medicinal interventions provide critical defensive protection against opportunistic infections, such as preventing Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in heavily immunosuppressed patients. (D) Immunomodulatory supplementation: The administration of Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides (GLP) targets rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs), resulting in significant adjunctive symptomatic pain relief and improved patient global assessments

For P. gingivalis, gingipain inhibitors (e.g., COR388, COR286) block protein citrullination and ACPA production, making them promising for ACPA-positive RA patients with comorbid periodontitis. PPAD inhibitors, currently in preclinical stages, aim to suppress autoantigen generation. Non-surgical periodontal treatments have shown efficacy in reducing P. gingivalis abundance and lowering DAS28 in RA patients with periodontitis. Photodynamic therapy or diode laser treatment can eliminate Aa even in immunosuppressed patients. In the gut, probiotics like Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG reduce P. copri levels and improve gut barrier function, while fructo-oligosaccharides inhibit P. copri-mediated succinate production, benefiting patients with high-fiber diet intolerance. For H. pylori, quadruple therapy lowers DAS28 and CRP in cagA-positive RA patients and reduces MTX-associated gastrointestinal intolerance, an important option for young women with MTX non-adherence.

Viruses such as EBV and HTLV-1 require therapies that balance viral control with immune homeostasis. Sulfasalazine restores EBV-specific T-cell function, while tocilizumab does not increase EBV load and may even reduce EBV positivity, positioning it as a preferred bDMARD for EBV-associated RA. For HTLV-1-positive RA patients, tocilizumab controls disease activity without exacerbating HTLV-1-associated myelopathy, a key advantage over TNF inhibitors.

Fungi, including Ganoderma lucidum and Saccharomyces boulardii, also offer therapeutic potential. Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides inhibit RASF proliferation and protect bone integrity, offering a potential treatment for mild-to-moderate RA patients intolerant to bDMARDs. S. boulardii helps repair gut barrier function, benefiting patients with gut fungal dysbiosis. Prophylactic strategies, such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for PCP in immunosuppressed patients and fluconazole for Candida infections, are critical for preventing infections in high-risk individuals.

Mycoplasmas, such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae and M. fermentans, can be targeted with tetracyclines like minocycline, which suppresses mycoplasma LAMP-mediated RASF activation, improving joint swelling in early RA, especially in patients with mycoplasma antibody positivity.

Unresolved problems: barriers to translating microbial insights into clinical practice

Despite significant advances in understanding the role of pathogens in RA, several critical gaps remain. First, establishing causality rather than mere correlation is essential. Most current evidence is observational, and definitive proof requires longitudinal cohort studies to track microbial colonization and its progression to disease onset. Interventional trials, such as those testing pathogen clearance to reduce autoimmune risk, and the validation of host microbe interactions are necessary for meaningful progress.

This challenge of establishing causality is particularly evident when determining whether mucosal dysbiosis serves as the primary environmental trigger or arises as a secondary consequence of systemic inflammation and pharmacological treatments. Although emerging longitudinal analyses of individuals at risk for RA suggest that specific microbial alterations, notably the expansion of Prevotella copri, occur during the preclinical phase [144], completely isolating this primary effect from therapeutic confounding factors remains problematic. The chronic administration of conventional disease modifying antirheumatic drugs, such as methotrexate, profoundly alters the gut microenvironment and frequently results in therapy induced dysbiosis [273]. Consequently, definitively disentangling preexisting dysbiosis from disease or drug induced microbial shifts remains a major unresolved challenge that complicates early diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Another significant challenge is understanding why pathogens exhibit tissue and patient subgroup specificity. Epstein-Barr virus detection in synovial tissues varies widely, and the mechanisms driving Porphyromonas gingivalis translocation from the oral cavity to the synovium remain unclear. Subgroup specificity is equally puzzling, including why only some cytomegalovirus seropositive patients develop joint destruction, or why the pathogenic role of human T lymphotropic virus type 1 is confined to certain geographic regions, which requires profound further investigation.

Furthermore, translating these microbial insights into clinical practice introduces a critical therapeutic dilemma regarding antimicrobial resistance, particularly concerning Mycoplasma species and periodontal pathogen eradication. While eliminating arthritogenic microbes presents a logical strategy to halt disease progression, the current reliance on prolonged conventional antibiotics inevitably exerts severe selective pressure and drives the development of multidrug resistant strains. The unresolved problem lies in how to achieve targeted pathogen clearance without causing profound collateral damage to the beneficial commensal flora. Such untargeted pharmacological approaches paradoxically disrupt immune homeostasis and significantly increase the long-term risk of developing RA [274, 275]. Developing precision interventions, including targeted enzymatic inhibitors or microbiome modulating therapies that can restore mucosal homeostasis without fueling the global antimicrobial resistance crisis, remains a formidable barrier to clinical application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pathogens serve as critical environmental modifiers in the RA immune landscape, offering significant opportunities for early diagnosis through pathogen-specific antibodies and microbiome abundance markers, as well as for precision treatments targeting microbial factors. However, establishing causality, resolving tissue and subgroup specificity, and standardizing biomarkers remain key challenges. Future research leveraging longitudinal cohorts, multi-omics approaches, and interventional trials will bridge these gaps, advancing our understanding of RA and paving the way for more effective, targeted therapies.
