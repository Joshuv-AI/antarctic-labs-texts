Exploring the regulatory role of Lagenaria siceraria seed in enhancing blood health, growth, antioxidant capacity, gut integrity, immune function, and resistance to Vibrio alginolyticus in European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax)
Authors: Roshmon Thomas Mathew, Mohamed Ashour, Ehab El-Haroun, Yousef Ahmed Alkhamis, Ahmed Saud Alsaqufi, Abdallah Tageldein Mansour, Hailah M. Almohaimeed, Nehal A. Younis, Moaheda E.H. Eissa, El-Sayed Hemdan Eissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The intensification of aquaculture, along with global bans on antibiotic growth promoters, has created an urgent need for sustainable immunomodulatory strategies to enhance fish health and welfare. Hence, this study evaluated, for the first time, the immunomodulatory effects of Lagenaria siceraria seed (LS) supplementation in European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax) by assessing its impact on gut integrity, blood health markers, growth attributes, digestive enzyme activity, antioxidant status, immune-related gene expression, and resistance to Vibrio alginolyticus infection. A total of 204 fish (19.07 ± 0.06 g) were randomly distributed into four dietary treatment groups including a control basal diet (LS0) and three experimental diets supplemented with LS at concentrations of 5 (LS5), 10 (LS10), and 15 g/kg (LS15). Fish in the LS groups exhibited higher growth indices (averaged daily gain (ADG), final body weight, fish biomass, specific growth rate (SGR), and feed conversion ratio (FCR)), and digestive enzymes activity (lipase, amylase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin) compared to the LS0 group (P < 0.05), with the best results observed in the LS10 and LS15 groups. Dietary LS15 supplementation significantly optimized body composition, leading to maximum crude protein and dry matter levels while minimizing crude lipid and moisture content compared to the LS5 and LS0 treatments (P < 0.05). LS inclusion improved antioxidant status including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and lysosome activity, while significantly reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations compared to the free-LS diet (P < 0.05). The LS15 diet notably enhanced blood health and protein profiles, while decreasing hepatic enzyme markers compared to the LS5 and control groups (P < 0.05); the LS10 group showed an intermediate response. Dietary LS supplementation upregulated the mRNA expression of complement component 3 (C3), SOD, and GPx genes significantly, while downregulating the expression of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) gene, indicating potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (P < 0.05). Intestinal morphometric analysis revealed that LS-supplemented groups exhibited superior gut integrity, with significant improvements in villus length, width, and overall absorptive capacity compared to the LS0 group. A decreasing trend in cumulative mortality following V. alginolyticus challenge was observed in fish fed LS-supplemented diets, particularly in the LS15 group, although the effect did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). Overall, these findings demonstrate that Lagenaria siceraria seed supplementation significantly enhances growth performance, disease resistance, metabolic health, and intestinal integrity in European seabass due to its potent immunomodulatory and antioxidant properties, especially at the 15 g/kg inclusion level. The results suggest that L. siceraria seed meal may be a promising functional feed additive for seabass culture.

Introduction

Aquaculture is a rapidly growing global food production sector, currently supplying nearly half of the world’s seafood demand1, significantly contributing to meeting demand, ensuring food security, and driving economic growth2. However, its expansion faces obstacles from environmental stressors like water quality issues and temperature shifts, as well as the constant risk of disease outbreaks3, which can severely impact productivity and result in significant economic losses. The intensification of aquaculture necessary for sustaining high production has created challenging environments that increase stress on aquatic animals4. These intensive practices expose fish to environmental stressors such as overcrowding, suboptimal water quality, and handling, which can lead to immune dysfunction and increase susceptibility to infectious diseases5. Due to the complications and potential risks to human health associated with the reliance on antibiotic compounds, it is now critical and urgent to find natural, growth-promoting alternatives for maintaining aquatic animal health6,7.

The European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax L.) is a commercially vital marine species in Mediterranean aquaculture. Given its significant economic value, research efforts must focus on optimizing production yields and enhancing disease resistance to ensure sustainable industry growth8. Vibriosis remains a significant threat to global aquaculture, causing severe mortality and economic disruptions across numerous fish species8,9. Although many Vibrio species are benign, certain strains, including V. parahaemolyticus, V. harveyi, and V. alginolyticus, have recently emerged as highly destructive agents8. Given the growing scrutiny over chemical usage in food production, there is an urgent need to develop sustainable, safe, applicable, and bio-based interventions to safeguard aquatic health5,7,10.

Phytogenic feed additives act as effective immunostimulatory agents, boosting immune competence and enhancing resilience against pathogens in aquaculture11,12. This has led to an increasing interest in dietary immunostimulants that can specifically prime the innate immune system to improve pathogen resilience. Adding natural immunostimulants like herbs or their bioactive phytogenics to aquatic diets provides a sustainable and safe alternative to traditional chemotherapeutics, enhancing the immune profile, stress tolerance, and growth performance of cultured fish5,10,13–15. One such candidate is the bottle gourd, Lagenaria siceraria, a large, pubescent annual climber belonging to the Cucurbitaceae family. Indigenous to Africa and Asia, it serves as a staple vegetable in these regions. The fruit of L. siceraria (LS) exhibits a high edible yield of approximately 94.17% and is characterized by a dense nutritional profile, including dietary fiber (0.6%), carbohydrates (2.5%), lipids (0.1%), minerals (0.5%), and essential vitamins such as the B-complex and vitamin C15,16. Furthermore, it possesses a diverse amino acid composition, notably aspartic acid (1.9%), phenylalanine (0.9%), and leucine (0.8%). Beyond its nutritional value, the fruit of L. siceraria demonstrates significant pharmacological potential17, empirical studies have confirmed its antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antineoplastic, and cardioprotective properties16,18. Historically, L. siceraria has been utilized in ethnomedicine to manage various clinical conditions, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hepatic disorders19,20. Existing literature indicates that dietary fortification with LS significantly improves growth performance and physiological health in tilapia and shrimp by stimulating immune repsones and improved antioxidant capability. Despite these findings, the dietary application of LS in European seabass (D. labrax) remains unexplored, particularly concerning its impact on growth performance, antioxidant status, and overall physiological health. Hence, this study hypothesizes that the inclusion of LS in Dicentrarchus labrax diets would enhance growth, hemato-biochemical profiles, and intestinal morphological integrity, while simultaneously upregulating genes associated with systemic immune function. Consequently, this study aims to evaluate the effects of dietary LS administration on growth attributes, digestive enzyme activity, antioxidant status, and the expression of immune-related genes, as well as the subsequent resistance of D. labrax to Vibrio alginolyticus infection.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and material sources

All experimental procedures and protocols were conducted in strict accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and the Council regarding the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The study was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Agriculture, Arish University, Egypt (Ethical code: Arish -IACUC/6/12/1/2024/01). Furthermore, all animal handling and reporting followed the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0.

Lagenaria siceraria seeds (LS) were sourced from a private farm in the Diarb Negm District, Sharqia Governorate, Egypt. The botanical identity of the seeds was authenticated by Prof. El-Sayed El-Desoky, Professor of Botany, Faculty of Agriculture, Zagazig University. To prepare the samples, the seeds were oven-dried at 65 °C for 3 h, then weighed and pulverized into a fine powder. The resulting samples were allowed to cool to room temperature, packed in airtight plastic bags, and stored at 4 °C until further analysis or use.

Fish rearing and trial specifications

In a 77-day feeding trial conducted at a private farm in Damietta Governorate, Egypt, 204 European seabass (D. labrax) with an initial average weight of 19.07 ± 0.06 g were evaluated. Following a one-week acclimation period, the fish were randomly distributed into four experimental groups across 12 hapas (1 m³ each), submerged within a 3-acre earthen pond. Each hapa was stocked with 17 individuals. The feeding trial was conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD), with hapas assigned to experimental treatments to minimize location bias within the earthen pond. The daily feeding allowance was established at 3% of body weight, partitioned into three equal rations provided at 08:00, 13:00, and 19:00 h. This rate was dynamically adjusted according to fluctuations in water temperature throughout the experimental period. Water quality parameters were monitored using a multi-parameter probe (Aquacombo, China), yielding the following mean values: dissolved oxygen (DO) 5.7 mg/L, temperature 24.1 °C, pH 8.5, salinity 37.3 ppt, and ammonia nitrogen (NH3) 0.0160 mg/L. Throughout the experimental period, fish were maintained under a natural photoperiod (14 h L:8 h D). For all physiological assessments, including histological and biochemical assays, samples were coded and analyzed by investigators blinded to the treatment groups to ensure objective results.

All fish were weighed using a high-precision digital balance (0.01 g sensitivity). Sampling and Hematology determination

At the end of the trial, nine fish were randomly selected from each experimental group for hematological and biochemical analysis. Prior to sampling, fish were fasted for 12 h to ensure metabolic stability. The fish were anesthetized using 25 mg/L of MS-222 (Syncaine®, USA). Blood specimens were then collected from the caudal vasculature of three fish per replicate (totaling nine fish per treatment group), following the procedure described by21. Blood samples were collected into EDTA-precoated tubes for hematological profiling, and other samples were collected into non-heparinized tubes for serum separation. The blood was collected into EDTA-containing tubes for the evaluation of hematological parameters.

In accordance with the methods of22, six samples per group were analyzed for red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, packed cell volume (PCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), and white blood cell (WBC) count. For biochemical analysis, serum was harvested from non-heparinized blood allowed to clot, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C; the resulting supernatant was stored at − 20 °C. Following blood collection, fish were rinsed with deionized water and dissected to obtain mid-gut segments for histopathological examination, which were preserved at 4 °C. Additionally, liver samples were stabilized in 500 µL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA isolation and gene expression analysis.

Assessing whole-body composition

For the determination of whole-body proximate composition, three fish were randomly selected from each experimental group. Samples were dried in a Memmert Universal Oven (UN30) at 105 °C until a constant weight was achieved for moisture determination. Crude protein content was quantified using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 981.10), and crude lipid content was determined via the ether extraction method using a Soxtec system (Tacator System 1046, Höganäs, Sweden). Ash content was measured gravimetrically after incinerating the samples in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for six hours.

Blood health markers

For the assessment of biochemical health markers, six serum samples per experimental group were analyzed for liver enzyme activity and protein fractions. Total Protein (TP) and Albumin (ALB) concentrations were determined via spectrophotometric methods using specialized diagnostic reagents. Finally, globulin (GLO) levels were calculated by subtracting the albumin concentration from the total protein value (GLO = TP - ALB). Serum activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) were quantified using commercial Quantitative Sandwich ELISA kits (MyBioSource, San Diego, USA; Cat. Nos. MBS1601734 and MBS2800393, respectively), following the methods of23 and24, respectively. These assays employed a Quantitative Sandwich Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay method. The detection range was 0.31 ng/mL to 20ng/mL for ALT and 0.2ng/mL to 60 ng/mL for AST. Similarly, Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) was measured using a quantitative sandwich ELISA (MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS033204; detection range: 15.6U/L-500U/L, as described by25.

Antioxidant status

The serum levels of antioxidant and oxidative indicators were measured in fish (n = 6 per group). Catalase (CAT) activity was assessed following the method described by26, which measures the degradation of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). One unit of enzyme activity is defined as the amount of enzyme needed to degrade 1 mmol of H₂O₂ within one minute. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) was determined using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600D, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), following the protocol outlined by27. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels were evaluated using the method described by28, focusing on the formation of the WST-1 formazan complex. The protein content of the enzyme extract was determined using Bradford reagent to calculate SOD activity in units of U per mg of protein. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured based on the system outlined by29. All assay kits used in this study were provided by BioAssay Systems (Hayward, CA, USA). Lysozyme (LYZ) activity in the serum was measured using a turbidimetric assay30 with Micrococcus luteus as the bacterial substrate in a pH 6.2 phosphate buffer.

Gut digestive enzymes

For the assessment of intestinal digestive enzyme activities, gut specimens (n = 6 per treatment) were homogenized in a buffer (0.02 M Tris, 0.01 M phosphate, and 50% v/v glycerol; pH 7.5) at a 1:20 (w/v) ratio. The homogenates were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used for enzymatic assays. Activities of amylase (Cat. No. C016-1-1), trypsin (A080-2-2), lipase (A054-2-1), and chymotrypsin were determined using commercial diagnostic kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) following the methodologies of31–33 and34, respectively. Absorbance was recorded at 540 nm using a spectrophotometer. All digestive enzyme activities were normalized and expressed as U/mg protein.

Histological study

Intestinal specimens from Dicentrarchus labrax (n = 3 per group) were collected and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 72 h, with the fixative replaced daily. Following fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Longitudinal Sect. (5 μm thickness) were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2155, UK). The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for micromorphological examination. Morphometric analysis was performed on 50 well-oriented villi per intestinal segment for each fish. Villus height (VH) was measured from the tip to the base, and villus width (VW) was recorded at the midpoint. To ensure data robustness, ten villi were randomly selected and measured from the mid-intestine of each fish. To prevent measurement bias, the samples were coded, and the analysis was performed by an investigator who was unaware of the experimental groupings. All slides were examined under a light microscope equipped with a digital imaging system and specialized software. The absorptive surface area (ASA) was calculated using the following formula:

ASA (mm2) = villus height x villus width.

Gene expression

Whole RNA was extracted from 50 mg of hepatic specimens of fish using the RNeasy Mini Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Inc., South Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and quality were then determined using a Nanodrop UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Q5000/Quawell, Quawell Technology, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) at OD260/280. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription kit (Qiagen, Heidelberg, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis, such as qRT-PCR. The specific primer sequences for the complement component 3 (C3), interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) genes used in RT-qPCR are listed in Table 2. The qPCR analysis was performed using a Rotor-Gene Q apparatus with a QuantiTect® SYBR® Green PCR kit (SensiFast™ SYBR Lo-Rox kit, Bioline) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s. The relative fold change in the expression levels of the target genes was calculated using the comparative 2−∆∆Ct method, with GAPDH as the housekeeping gene (internal control) for standardization35. To ensure the stability and reproducibility of the gene expression data, all analyses were performed using three biological replicates per group.

Challenge assay

Following the feeding trial, a bacterial challenge was conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary Lagenaria siceraria seed meal on the immunological response and disease resistance of hapa-reared European seabass. The pathogenic strain, Vibrio alginolyticus, was originally isolated from morbid fish and characterized through biochemical assays and molecular diagnostics (PCR) for virulence genes. The isolate was provided by the Department of Poultry and Fish Diseases, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Zagazig University. For the challenge, the bacterium was incubated overnight in nutrient broth at 28 °C, centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min at 25 °C minutes and the resulting pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The bacterial density was confirmed via the agar plate count method. At the end of the growth trial, 20 fish per treatment (LS0, LS5, LS10, and LS15) were randomly selected and moved to challenge tanks where they continued to receive their respective diets. Each fish received an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 200 µL of the bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/fish), according to the protocol of36. Mortality was monitored daily for 14 days, and clinical specificity was confirmed by re-isolating the pathogen from the liver and kidneys of deceased individuals.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) using completely randomized design (CRD). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Quantitative results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between experimental groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test as a post-hoc analysis to identify specific significant differences. For parameters involving repeated measurements, such as growth performance attributes and feed efficiency recorded over time, the data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure. For the bacterial challenge, cumulative survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between survival curves were determined using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

In contrast, dietary LS had no significant effect on carcass ash content (Fig. 1E), which remained consistent across all groups (P > 0.05).

Liver function and blood proteins

Dietary supplementation with LS (5, 10, and 15 g/kg) significantly reduced the activities of liver enzymes, including ALT, AST, and ALP (P < 0.05; Table 5). Regarding serum proteins, total protein levels increased dose-dependently with higher LS concentrations (P < 0.05). Furthermore, LS supplementation enhanced albumin and globulin concentrations, with peak values observed in the LS15 group (P < 0.05).

Fish fed LS-supplemented diets showed a numerical reduction in cumulative mortality following Vibrio alginolyticus challenge compared to the control group (Fig. 6); however, these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Discussion

In modern aquaculture, the strategic application of immunomodulatory agents has emerged as a cornerstone for enhancing aquatic animal welfare and zootechnical performance by fortifying innate defenses against pathogenic insult. This work offers substantial practical advantages, including the attenuation of antibiotic dependency, accelerated growth, and optimized nutrient utilization. As a non-pharmacological intervention, nutritional immunomodulation provides a resilient framework for mitigating the deleterious effects of infectious outbreaks and environmental stressors. The present study evaluates Lagenaria siceraria seed meal (LS) as a candidate functional feed additive for European seabass (D. labrax).

Our findings demonstrate that the dietary inclusion of LS seed meal at 10–15 g/kg significantly improved zootechnical performance, as evidenced by enhanced growth metrics including final body weight (FBW), weight gain (WG), and average daily gain (ADG) alongside a superior feed conversion ratio (FCR) and increased survival rates. Furthermore, LS supplementation optimized hematological indices and serum protein fractions while concurrently attenuating leakage enzyme activities in the liver, indicating improved hepatocyte integrity. At the physiological level, LS inclusion stimulated intestinal enzymes activity, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and fortified gut mucosal barrier function, while refining whole-body proximate composition. Ultimately, LS upregulated the transcriptional profiles of key immunoregulatory genes and augmented lysozyme activity, validating its efficacy in strengthening both the systemic and mucosal immunocompetence of European seabass (D. labrax).

Optimizing growth parameters is essential to maximizing the profit margins and production efficiency of fish farms. The present study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with LS significantly promotes growth performance in European seabass, as evidenced by increased final body weight and average daily gain. This growth promotion was accompanied by a reduction in the feed conversion ratio (FCR), indicating enhanced nutrient utilization efficiency. These findings align with previous reports in other species, such as Nile tilapia and shrimp15,37, where LS inclusion improved digestive enzyme activity and intestinal health. The growth-promoting effects of LS can be attributed to its dense nutritional profile and the presence of diverse bioactive compounds. LS seeds are rich in essential amino acids, particularly lysine and leucine, which are critical for protein synthesis and the optimization of other amino acid metabolic pathways38. Beyond basic nutrition, the presence of saponins, triterpenoid glycosides (cucurbitacins), and phytosterols (fucosterol and campesterol)19, likely played a synergistic role in modulating the fish’s physiology. Furthermore, the inclusion of LS may function as a prebiotic-like substrate, fostering beneficial gut microbiota that enhance intestinal metabolism and the production of health-promoting biomolecules16. These phytochemicals, along with identified flavonoids and alkaloids39, possess potent antimicrobial17,39, and antioxidant properties40. Specifically37, reported that the inclusion of Lagenaria siceraria seed powder in Nile tilapia diets (at a rate of 2.5 g/kg) significantly enhanced growth performance, feed utilization efficiency, and digestive enzyme activity. The addition of L. siceraria effectively modulates the intestinal environment by stimulating the proliferation of beneficial gut microbiota. This change in microbial populations enhances nutrient digestibility and absorption, which serves as a key factor in the growth promotion and improved feed efficiency observed in this study17,38. Collectively, these elements work in concert to inhibit potential pathogens while stimulating beneficial microflora, thereby improving feed digestibility and driving the overall enhancement in growth and feed efficiency observed in the treated seabass.

Dietary fortification with LS significantly enhanced the whole-body proximate composition of European seabass, specifically increasing crude protein and lipid content while reducing moisture levels. The LS15 (15 g/kg) group exhibited the most pronounced improvements, likely due to the seed meal’s ability to optimize the FCR and overall growth. These physiological improvements facilitate superior nutrient absorption and subsequent protein and lipid deposition within the muscle tissue. Our findings are consistent with reports by37, and15 in Litopenaeus vannamei, where LS inclusion significantly boosted whole-body crude protein and ash contents. Collectively, these outcomes underscore the capacity of LS seed meal to enhance fish meat quality. This effect is likely driven by the presence of cucurbitacins, dietary fibers, polyphenols, campesterol, and sitosterol, which are known to promote digestive efficiency and metabolic health38.

Digestive enzyme activity is a fundamental regulator of fish growth and development, as it directly dictates the efficiency of nutrient hydrolysis and subsequent absorption41. In the present study, dietary inclusion of LS significantly stimulated the activities of key intestinal enzymes, including lipase, amylase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin. This enzymatic upregulation appears to be a primary physiological driver behind the enhanced growth performance observed in the LS-supplemented groups. Monitoring these enzymes provides a critical diagnostic tool for evaluating intestinal functional integrity and the metabolic impact of novel feed ingredients42,43. The elevated levels of these enzymes in seabass fed the LS seed meal confirm a state of optimized feed digestibility and nutrient bioavailability, which correlates with the accelerated growth trajectories reported in similar studies on Nile tilapia and shrimp15,37.

Assessment of hematological parameters provides critical insights into the physiological health, nutritional status, and immunocompetence of fish. In the present study, European seabass fed diets supplemented with 10 and 15 g/kg of LS exhibited significant improvements in key erythrogram indices (hemoglobin, RBC count, PCV, and MCV) and WBC count compared to the control group. These findings align with37, who reported similar enhancements in the hematological profiles of Nile tilapia receiving LS-supplemented diets. These phytochemicals are well-documented to function as immunostimulants, promoting hematopoiesis and stabilizing erythrocyte membranes16,19. This enhancement is likely due to the presence of bioactive substances such as saponins, glycosides, flavonoids, terpenoids, tannins, and phenols, which are well-documented immunostimulants known to improve hematological parameters in fish15,16,20. Furthermore, the positive shift in hematological markers (Hb and WBCs) mirrors effects observed in seabass supplemented with other botanicals, such as ginseng, which contains a comparable profile of bioactive molecules17,44. Collectively, these results suggest that LS inclusion supports a robust cellular system and enhances the non-specific immune capacity of the fish.

Serum proteins are essential mediators of physiological homeostasis and humoral immunity in fish. In the present study, dietary inclusion of LS significantly enhanced key protein markers, including total protein, albumin, and globulin fractions. This elevation in serum proteins, particularly globulins, suggests a potent immunomodulatory effect15,21,37, driven by the bioactive constituents of the LS supplement. These findings corroborate previous reports, where LS supplementation improved overall health status by boosting protein biosynthesis and modulating systemic immunity in Nile tilapia37 and shrimp15. Furthermore, LS demonstrated a significant hepatoprotective effect, as evidenced by the reduction in serum ALT and AST activities. These enzymes are primary indicators of hepatocellular integrity, and their release into the bloodstream typically signifies membrane damage or necrosis. The stabilization of these enzyme levels in LS-fed seabass likely results from the high concentrations of phenolic and flavonoid groups, which function as potent antioxidants and membrane-stabilizing agents16,45.This protective capacity aligns with recent evidence demonstrating that LS can mitigate dyslipidemia44, and neutralize chemical-induced toxicities, such as those caused by Betamethasone46 and furan45. Collectively, these results indicate that LS not only promotes protein metabolic efficiency but also safeguards hepatic function against physiological and environmental stressors.

The antioxidant defense system is a critical homeostatic mechanism in aquatic organisms, particularly given their high sensitivity to environmental fluctuations10. In the present study, dietary supplementation with LS significantly upregulated the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, catalase, and GPx, while concurrently reducing MDA levels. These results underscore the potent antioxidant capacity of LS in neutralizing oxidative stress and fortifying the cellular defense systems of European seabass. Such findings align with the established role of plant-derived bioactive molecules, including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolics, and terpenoids, in modulating biological activities such as phagocytosis, complement system activation, and systemic anti-stress responses38,40,45. Botanical supplements mitigate oxidative damage by either directly scavenging free radicals or by activating endogenous antioxidant pathways47. This is consistent with recent reports by20, who observed that L. siceraria oil effectively reduced MDA concentrations and enhanced antioxidant indices (SOD, GPx) in biological samples. Collectively, these data suggest that LS functions as a robust nutritional antioxidant, protecting cellular membranes from oxidative degradation and improving the overall physiological resilience of the fish. In clinical models of dyslipidemia, LS has been demonstrated to fortify the antioxidant defense system by significantly elevating GSH, CAT, and SOD levels44. These potent antioxidative effects are directly attributable to the rich profile of bioactive phytochemicals, specifically phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, and terpenoids, which exhibit significant anti-lipid peroxidation potential18. Mechanistically, these compounds function as highly efficient reducing agents, hydrogen donors, singlet oxygen quenchers, and metal chelators48. They neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) by donating electrons or hydrogen atoms, thereby converting unstable free radicals into stable, non-radical molecules and terminating the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation49.

Lysozyme is a vital cationic enzyme that exhibits potent phagocytic and antibacterial properties. In the present study, the significant elevation of serum lysozyme activity suggests that the dietary inclusion of LS bolsters the non-specific defense mechanisms of European seabass against potential bacterial pathogens. These results align with a growing body of research demonstrating the capacity of plant-derived phytochemicals to enhance the immunocompetence of cultured fish. Furthermore, our findings are consistent with the immunomodulatory effects reported by40 who observed that L. siceraria extracts stimulated the proliferation of white blood cells and lymphocytes, thereby strengthening the systemic immune response. Collectively, the upregulation of lysozyme activity in this study confirms that LS functions as a functional immunostimulant, improving the primary line of defense in D. labrax.

The intestine is the primary site for nutrient assimilation in teleost fish, facilitate the uptake of essential nutrients. In the present study, dietary inclusion of LS significantly enhanced the ASA and the villus height-to-width ratio in the mid-intestine of European seabass. Given that VH and VW are key determinants of mucosal topography, an increase in these morphometric indices significantly expands the available surface area for nutrient-epithelial interaction. This structural optimization directly improves the intestine’s capacity for digestion and absorption50. Collectively, these histomorphometric improvements indicate that LS seed meal exerts a positive impact on gastrointestinal architecture and functional health, which correlates with the superior growth trajectories observed in the treated seabass51,52. Enhancing the integrity of the intestinal mucosa is well-documented to facilitate more efficient nutrient uptake, thereby supporting optimized feed utilization and growth performance across various commercially important fish species51.

The current study demonstrates that the inclusion of LS seed meal significantly upregulates the hepatic mRNA transcription of key antioxidant enzymes (GPx and SOD) and immunoregulatory genes (IL-1β and C3) in European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax). These findings underscore the potential of LS to bolster antioxidant capacity, modulate the pro-inflammatory response, and enhance systemic immunocompetence. Specifically, the coordinated modulation of C3, IL-1β, SOD, and GPx reflects a robust, multifaceted improvement in both innate immunity and redox homeostasis, suggesting that LS serves as an effective functional feed additive. As a cornerstone of the complement cascade, Complement C3 plays a pivotal role in pathogen opsonization and subsequent clearance53. The elevated transcriptional expression of C3 observed in this study aligns with its fundamental role in the teleost innate immune response following pathogenic insult, further validating the immunostimulatory potential of LS supplementation54. These molecular benefits are likely driven by the targeted modulation of essential immunoregulatory and antioxidant pathways. Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), a primary pro-inflammatory cytokine, is critical in the early-phase immune response, orchestrating leukocyte activation and the induction of secondary cytokine cascades. Its rapid upregulation within key teleost lymphoid tissues specifically the spleen and head kidney underscores its vital role in early immunological signaling and the efficient initiation of the inflammatory cascade in response to infection53,55.

Regarding oxidative stress mitigation, superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) are pivotal antioxidant enzymes that preserve cellular integrity by neutralizing ROS generated during metabolic flux and immune activation56. In the current study, the upregulation of these genes indicates that supplementation with LS significantly fortifies the endogenous antioxidant defense system. This enhancement not only attenuates oxidative damage to lipid membranes but also maintains the functional viability of leucocytes. These multifaceted improvements spanning both innate immunity and redox homeostasis are likely driven by LS-derived bioactive phytochemicals, specifically flavonoids and saponins. These compounds are well-documented modulators of pro-inflammatory signaling and redox-sensitive pathways (e.g., Nrf2/Keap1)57. Nevertheless, optimizing the dietary inclusion level remains critical; while 10–15 g/kg proved effective here, excessive supplementation may induce metabolic imbalances or diminish feed palatability, potentially offsetting the observed growth and health benefits. However, the precision of dietary inclusion levels is paramount. While 10–15 g/kg provided optimal efficacy in this trial, supra-optimal concentrations may trigger metabolic disturbances or reduce feed palatability, potentially compromising the growth performance and health benefits observed at lower doses.

In the present study, while the LS15 group exhibited the lowest cumulative mortality, no significant differences in resistance to V. alginolyticus were observed between the LS-supplemented treatments and the control (P > 0.05). These results demonstrate that LS inclusion significantly enhances the disease resistance of European seabass, corroborating similar findings in Litopenaeus vannamei15 and Nile tilapia37. This enhanced immunity parameters are likely driven by the potent antimicrobial and therapeutic properties of the LS plant and its bioactive extracts15,20,38. Mechanistically, these protective effects are attributed to the synergistic action of a rich phytochemical profile, including cucurbitacins (B, D, G, and H), phytosterols (fucosterol and campesterol), di- and triterpenes, and essential micronutrients such as ascorbic acid and B-complex vitamins16,18. These compounds are believed to inhibit pathogenic colonization and mitigate systemic infection through direct antibacterial activity and immunomodulatory signaling58,59. Nevertheless the positive outcomes observed for seabass health and growth, certain limitations of the current study should be noted. Future research incorporating high-throughput intestinal microbiota analysis (16 S rRNA sequencing), a broader array of immune-related gene expression assays, and detailed assessments of phagocytic activity would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms. This study is limited by the lack of qualitative and quantitative analysis of the LS seed batch. Future research should include HPLC/LC-MS profiling and assays for total phenolics, flavonoids, and saponins to better correlate specific bioactive compounds with the observed biological effects. Furthermore, the absence of a vehicle control group (e.g., PBS-injected fish) represents a limitation in accounting for procedural stress.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementation with Lagenaria siceraria seed meal (10 and 15 g/kg diet) has demonstrated significant improvements in several health and growth indicators in European seabass. Mechanistically, LS inclusion improved intestinal morphology and mucosal integrity leading to significantly better growth performance, feed efficiency, and carcass quality. At the molecular level, LS strengthened immunocompetence and oxidative stress resilience, evidenced by the upregulation of antioxidant and immune-related genes and a corresponding increase in enzymatic activity. Fish receiving LS-supplemented diets (particularly LS15) exhibited a non-significant trend toward reduced cumulative mortality following V. alginolyticus challenge (P > 0.05). These findings underscore the potential of L. siceraria seed meal as a sustainable functional feed ingredient that concurrently optimizes growth, physiological health, and disease resistance in intensive European seabass aquaculture.
