The rolling tangent space a forgotten vision on geodesics and parallel transport?
Constant Pinteaux, Gijs M. Tuynman

Abstract
. Given a submanifold M ⊂ 𝐑 ν M\subset\mathbf{R}^{\nu} , a curve γ : I → M \gamma:I\to M and tangent vectors v v along γ \gamma , we roll the tangent space along γ \gamma . In doing so, we get an imprint of γ \gamma on the tangent space , as well as an imprint of tangent vectors. We show that γ \gamma is a geodesic on M M if and only if this trace/imprint on the (affine) tangent space is a straight line and that v v is a set of parallel vectors if and only if their imprint on the tangent space is constant. In other words, in the view of the imprint on the rolling tangent space, a geodesic is a straight line, parallel transport is indeed that: parallel transport, and the covariant derivative becomes the ordinary derivative.



1.
Introduction

One of the guiding ideas behind the definition of a geodesic on a differentiable submanifold
M
of
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
is that it generalises the notion of a straight line in the sense that it is a curve realising the shortest distance between two points.
In this paper we show that it also generalises the notion of “being straight:” for a short-sighted inhabitant of
M
, a geodesic really is a straight line.
The best way to visualise this is the game of a person inside a (big) ball walking on the earth, rolling the ball over the ground.
When we see the ball/submanifold as being fixed, it becomes the tangent space/ground that rolls around
M
.
In this way we obtain a map that starts with a curve on
M
and produces a trace curve on the (rolling) tangent space.
We will show that the original curve is a geodesic if and only if this trace curve on the tangent space is a straight line.
This vision of geodesics goes back at least to W.T. van Est in his lectures on differential geometry of surfaces in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
given in the late 1970’s at the University of Amsterdam with the second author in attendance. Whether he developed this vision or if he used other sources is unknown to us, but we have been unable to find this description in the literature.
As it seems unlikely that this vision was never known, we conclude that it is forgotten, hence a (modern) revival in this paper.

A “collateral” benefit of the rolling tangent space is that it “explains” the notion of parallel transport of a tangent vector along a curve.
By definition a tangent vector
v
to
M
at a point
\gamma(t)
(for a curve
\gamma
on
M
) lies in the tangent space and as such it leaves an imprint on the rolling tangent space.
We show that parallel transport of
v
along
\gamma
is the actual parallel transport (picking up the vector and parallel translating it to a new point) in the rolling tangent space along the trace curve.

3.
The rolling tangent space

The idea behind the rolling tangent space is the game of a person inside a ball.
Imagine a big transparent ball (in the form of a rugby ball to make it more interesting), with inside it a person that can walk.
At each moment this ball touches the ground at a single point: the point where our person inside has its feet.
And when this person makes a step, he has to “push” the ball on the ground at a new point, the point where he puts his feet after the step.
In this way the ball rolls over the ground (without slipping) in the direction taken by the person inside.

Now suppose that we have painted a curve on this (transparent) ball and suppose the person inside follows this curve: at each step he puts his feet a bit farther on this curve.
In this way he will follow this curve, the ball will roll over te ground and he will trace a new curve on the ground: the points where his feet are at every step.

For instance, if the ball is a (perfect) sphere and the painted curve is a great circle (equator), then this trace on the ground will be a straight line.
But if the painted curve is a (small) circle, then the trace curve on the ground will also be a circle.
Another example is a cone (Chinese hat).
If the painted curve is a circle around the tip of the cone, then (as for a small circle on a sphere) the trace curve on the ground will also be a circle.
And the movement of the cone on the ground is described by a rotation around the tip of the cone.

When we want to transcribe this game in mathematical terms, we change our point of view; instead of being a spectator that sees a ball rolling on the ground we become someone living on the (surface of the) ball that sees the ground (the tangent space) turning around him.
Said differently, we no longer see the ball rolling on the ground, we see the ground (i.e., the tangent space) rolling on the ball.
An ant on the ball who thinks the ball is the center of the universe and thus (necessarily) fixed, sees the ground moving around him.
This is the movement of the rolling tangent space.

And that is exactly what happens to us on Earth: we think the Earth is flat and that straight lines are the shortest curves, because that is what we see!
And when walking, this impression does not change.

A rather different way to imagine the movement of the rolling tangent space uses the idea of polishing a surface.
Imagine we have a surface
M
in
\mathbf{R}^{3}

For that we take a piece of sandpaper (a plane/tangent space), we apply it to the wood and we make movements to polish the wood.
For these movements there are several different ways: we can move the sandpaper parallel to itself (translations in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
), which is the standard way, but we can also rotate the sandpaper around its point of contact, for instance when we want to polish a small disc around this point.
But as the piece of wood will not be flat (in general), we also have to change the plane of the sandpaper in order to follow the curvature of the piece of wood.
In general, when polishing, one will make a combination of all three types of moves (parallel translating the sand paper, rotating it, both without changing the plane containing the sand paper, and changing this plane).

But what should we do when we don’t want to leave any trace on the wood, but simply want to “move” the piece of sandpaper along the wood without polishing?
Obviously we should not make parallel translations, nor rotations.
When not making that kind of movement, (almost) all points of the sandpaper will move and their trajectories will be orthogonal to the plane containing the piece of sandpaper, as else the movement would have a component of parallel translation or rotation, which would leave a trace of polishing.
It will be this way to visualise the rolling tangent space that will be the basis of our mathematical formulation of this movement: curves that belong at all times to the (affine) tangent space, but whose velocity vector is orthogonal to these (affine) tangent spaces.

sends (transports) the two points
x,y\in H_{s}
to two new points
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in H_{t}
, and these two points can be interpreted as a vector
y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}=v^{\prime}\in T_{\gamma(t)}M
(the vector space underlying the affine space
H_{t}
) attached to the (initial) point
x^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(t,s)(x)
.
Knowing that
\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(t,s)
is an affine map whose underlying linear map is
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)
, this new vector
v^{\prime}
attached to
x^{\prime}
is given by
v^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)(v)
.
Taking
x=\gamma(s)
, which is quite natural as
v
belongs to the tangent space to
M
at the point
\gamma(s)
, the image vector
v^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)(v)\in T_{\gamma(t)}M
will be attached to the image point
x^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(t,s)\bigl(\gamma(s)\bigr)={\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}(s)
, a point of the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
in
H_{t}
.
But
H_{t}
is an affine space, so we can “attach” the vector
v^{\prime}
also to the point
\gamma(t)\in H_{t}
.
In this way we have “transported” the vector
v^{\prime}
from (being attached to)
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}(s)
to (being attached to)
\gamma(t)
.
To summarise: starting with a tangent vector
v\in T_{\gamma(s)}M
at
\gamma(s)
, we apply the movement of the rolling tangent space to obtain a vector in
H_{t}
attached to the point
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}(s)
, which we then “parallel transport” to attach it to the point
\gamma(t)\in H_{t}
.
It is this procedure that is called the
parallel transport of
v\in T_{\gamma(s)}M
to
T_{\gamma(t)}M
along the curve
\gamma
.
In this general situation it is important to add “along
\gamma

v\in T_{\gamma(s)}M
to

will depend upon the curve.
But beware: the two tangent spaces
T_{\gamma(s)}M
and
T_{\gamma(t)}M
are different and there is no reason to think that the vectors
v
and

are parallel, and even less that they are equal.
We can only be sure that they have the same length: as
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)
is an isometry, the vectors
v
and
v^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)(v)
have the same length.

The preceding discussion leads us to the following definitions.
A

\gamma
is a map

such that for each

we have

.
Note that we do not require that

is a tangent vector to the curve, only that it is tangent to the submanifold
M
.
A vector field of tangent vectors along
\gamma
is said to be
parallel
(one also speaks of a vector field of

) if it satisfies the condition

\forall s,t\in I\quad:\quad v(t)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(v(s)\bigr)\kern 3.99994pt.

Now let
v
be a vector field of tangent vectors along
\gamma
.
Then we have shown above that
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(v(s)\bigr)

{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}(s)

In this way we obtain, for fixed

along the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
given by

(4.4)

{\widehat{v}}_{t}(s)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(v(s)\bigr)\kern 3.99994pt.

According to the definition, it is immediate that the vector field
v

\gamma

along the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
is constant.
Said differently, interpreting

as a vector attached to
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}(s)

) are parallel (and have the same length).

Once we have this interpretation/vision of a parallel vector field of tangent vectors along a curve, we can go backwards.
For a fixed
s\in I
, the curve

is the unique curve

satisfying the conditions RTS
1
and RTS
2
, as well as the initial condition
x_{s,x_{o}}(s)=x_{o}
.
It then follows easily that a vector field
v
of tangent vectors along
\gamma
is parallel if and only if the curve

seen in the surrounding vector space
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
satisfies the following two conditions, analogues of RTS
1
and RTS

:

(PT 1)

\forall t\in I:v(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}M
et

(PT 2)

\forall t\in I:\langle\mskip 1.5muv^{\prime}(t),T_{\gamma(t)}M\mskip 1.5mu\rangle=0
, i.e.,
v^{\prime}(t)
is orthogonal to
T_{\gamma(t)}M
.

In this way we see that parallel transport along a curve is intimately related to the movement of the rolling tangent space.

By way of conclusion.

In Riemannian geometry one introduces the notion of a geodesic as a generalisation of a shortest curve (at least locally) and one introduces the notions of parallel transport of a (tangent) vector along a curve and of a vector field of parallel (tangent) vectors (in most cases without a real geometric interpretation).
Imagine now a short-sighted inhabitant of a submanifold
M\subset\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
who thinks he is living in an affine euclidean space
H_{t}
and who does not realise that, when walking, this affine space changes.
When this inhabitant notes down his walks in his affine space (the rolling tangent space), he
sees
that the shortest distance between two points is a straight line and that he can translate a vector (in his affine space) from one point (of attachment) to another, which allows him to define a vector field of parallel vectors as one that is constant.
The origin of this “simplified vision” lies in the fact that our inhabitant does not see the real curve
\gamma
, but only the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
in his affine space, as well as the trace vector field

{\widehat{v}}_{t}
along the trace curve.
The person inside the transparent ball who walks on the earth only sees the trace he leaves on the ground.
Of course, having such a simplified vision of the surrounding world does not imply that our inhabitant will be unable to note that his world is not an affine space, as he will be able to observe anomalies.
For instance that the sum of the angles in a triangle does not add up to
\pi
, or that the ratio between diameter and circumference of a circle is different from
\pi
(see
[
Pet80
]
for an amusing account of these phenomena).
Nevertheless, for our short-sighted inhabitant geodesics really are straight lines and parallel transport is just attaching the same vector to different points.

\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)\bigl({\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\bigr)\mathbin{\smash{\overset{{\text{{\ (\ref{TransportParallelParDifferenceEspTanBas})\ }}}}{=}}}\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(s,t)\bigl(x_{o}+{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\bigr)-\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(s,t)(x_{o})\kern 3.99994pt.

But
\gamma^{\prime}(s)\in T_{\gamma(s)}M
, hence (with

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(s,t)\bigl(x_{o}+{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\bigr)-\operatorname{\mathrm{R}\mkern-4.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{S}}(s,t)(x_{o})\kern 3.99994pt.

\displaystyle\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)\bigl({\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr)\kern 3.99994pt.

\scriptstyle QED

5.11

. Corollary.

\pi\big(k_{\gamma}(s)\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)\bigl(k_{{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}}(s)\bigr)
.
In particular,
k_{\gamma}(s)
is orthogonal to
T_{\gamma(s)}M
if and only if
k_{{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}}(s)=0
.
Said differently,
\gamma
is a geodesic if and only if
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
is a straight line.

Proof.
The linear map
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)
being an isometry, we use [
5.10
] to obtain the equalities

\|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\|=\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\bigr\rangle=\langle\mskip 1.5mu{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(s),{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle\kern 3.99994pt.

By definition of orthogonal projection we have
\bigl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)-\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\bigr\rangle=0
, hence
\bigl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\bigr\rangle=\langle\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle
.
Using the definition of the curvature vector, linearity of
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)
and [
5.10
] we then find

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)\bigl(k_{{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}}(s)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(s,t)\biggl(\frac{1}{\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|^{2}}\cdot\Bigl(\,{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(s)-\frac{\langle\mskip 1.5mu{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}(s),{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle}{\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|^{2}}\cdot{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\,\Bigr)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\|^{2}}\cdot\Bigl(\,\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr)-\frac{\bigl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\pi\bigl(\gamma^{\prime\prime}(s)\bigr),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\bigr\rangle}{\|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\|^{2}}\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(s)\,\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\pi\bigl(k_{\gamma}(s)\bigr)\kern 3.99994pt.

\scriptstyle QED

6.
Postlude: the equations in a local chart

In the previous sections we have considered the curve
\gamma:I\to M
essentially as a curve in the surrounding vector space
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
and we have used, without moderation, the standard scalar product of
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
in our computations.
However, in differential geometry one likes to express things in terms of (local) charts (which allows a generalisation to an arbitrary manifold, not only submanifolds of some
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
).
In this section we will do exactly that, we will translate the equations for the rolling tangent space and for parallel transport in terms of local coordinates on a chart.
This will show in particular that these equations really are equivalent to the usual definitions of geodesics and parallel transport.

6.1

. Definition.

A
chart
for the submanifold
M\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
(of class
C^{2}
and dimension
n
) is an injective map
\psi:U\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}\to M\subset\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
of class
C^{2}
defined on an open subset
U
of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that its Jacobian
(D\psi)(x)
is everywhere injective.
And for simplicity we take it as a definition that the tangent space
T_{\psi(x)}M
has the vectors
(\partial_{1}\psi)(x),\dots,(\partial_{n}\psi)(x)
as basis.
For us the important properties of a chart are the following.

(i)

The image
\psi(U)
is open in
M
for the induced topology by
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
.

(ii)

They are “everywhere:”
for any point
m\in M
there exists a chart
\psi:U\to M
and
x\in U
such that
m=\psi(x)
.

(iii)

For any curve
\gamma:J\to M
of class
C^{2}
such that
\gamma(J)\subset\psi(U)
there exists a (unique) map
c:J\to U
of class
C^{2}
such that
\gamma=\psi\,{\raise 0.8pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}\,c
.
If in addition
v:J\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
is a map of class
C^{1}
such that
v(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}M
, then there exists a (unique) map
w:J\to\mathbf{R}^{n}
of class
C^{1}
such that
v(t)=(D\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot w(t)
.

Proof of [
5.2
].
The proof is subdivided into several steps.
In the first step we prove that there exist everywhere small subintervals
J\subset I
on which a moving frame can be defined.
In the second step we prove that if two such intervals overlap, then we can define a moving frame on their union.
And in the third step we prove that there exists “arbitrary large” intervals
\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a,b\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}\subset I
on which a moving frame can be defined.
And we conclude in the fourth step.

\bullet

Step 1.

Choose
t_{o}\in I
, then there exists a chart
\psi:U\to M
such that
\gamma(t_{o})=\psi(x_{o})
for some
x_{o}\in U
.
By continuity of
\gamma
and the fact that
\psi(U)
is open in
M
[
6.1
.
i
], there exists an interval
t_{o}\subset J\subset I
such that
\gamma(J)\subset\psi(U)
.
Let
c:J\to U
be such that
\gamma=\psi\,{\raise 0.8pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}\,c
[
6.1
.
iii
], then the maps
e_{i}:J\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
defined as

e_{i}(t)=(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)

are (by definition) a moving frame on
J\subset I
.

\bullet

Step 2.

Let
e_{i}:J\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
and
e_{i}^{\prime}:J^{\prime}\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
be two moving frames on
J
and
J^{\prime}
respectively and suppose
J\cap J^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, say
t_{o}\in J\cap J^{\prime}
.
Without loss of generality we may assume that we have
J=\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a,b\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}
and
J^{\prime}=\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}
with
a<a^{\prime}<t_{o}<b<b^{\prime}
(in all other cases the result would be trivial).
By definition of a (moving) frame, for all
t\in J\cap J^{\prime}
the vectors
e_{i}(t)
and
e^{\prime}_{i}(t)
form a basis of
T_{\gamma(t)}M
, hence there exists a (matrix valued) map
P:J\cap J^{\prime}\to\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbf{R})\subset\mathrm{M}(n\times n,\mathbf{R})
of class
C^{1}
such that

\forall t\in J\cap J^{\prime}\quad:\quad e^{\prime}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(t)\cdot P_{ji}(t)\kern 3.99994pt.

As
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbf{R})
is an open subset of the vector space
\mathrm{M}(n\times n,\mathbf{R})
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}\bigl(P(t_{o})\bigr)
, the ball of radius
\delta
in
\mathrm{M}(n\times n,\mathbf{R})
with center at
P(t_{o})
is contained in
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbf{R})
.
By continuity of
P
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}t_{o}-\varepsilon,t_{o}+\varepsilon\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}\subset J\cap J^{\prime}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall|t-t_{o}|<\varepsilon:P(t)\in B_{\delta}\bigl(P(t_{o})\bigr)\kern 3.99994pt.

Now let
\rho:\mathbf{R}\to\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu[\mskip 2.0mu}0,1\mathclose{\mskip 2.5mu]\mskip 0.5mu}
be a smooth function such that

\forall t\leq t_{o}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon:\rho(t)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall t\geq t_{o}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon:\rho(t)=1

and use it to define the maps
e^{\prime\prime}_{i}:J\cup J^{\prime}\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
by

\forall t\in J\cup J^{\prime}:e^{\prime\prime}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(t)\cdot\raise 0.6pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\Bigl($}\bigl(1-\rho(t)\bigr)\cdot P_{ji}(t_{o})+\rho(t)\cdot P_{ji}(t)\raise 0.6pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\Bigr)$}\kern 3.99994pt.

To prove that this is a moving frame on
J\cup J^{\prime}
of class
C^{1}
we distinguish three cases.

—

For
t<t_{o}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon
we have
e^{\prime\prime}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(t)\cdot P_{ji}(t_{o})
, which is obviously of class
C^{1}
and a basis of
T_{\gamma(t)}M
(a constant change of basis from
e_{i}(t)
).

—

For
t>t_{o}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon
we have
e^{\prime\prime}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(t)\cdot P_{ji}(t)=e_{i}^{\prime}(t)
, which is, by definition, of class
C^{1}
and a basis of
T_{\gamma(t)}M
.

—

For
|t-t_{o}|<\varepsilon
the expression obviously is of class
C^{1}
.
Moreover the convex combination
\bigl(1-\rho(t)\bigr)\cdot P_{ji}(t_{o})+\rho(t)\cdot P_{ji}(t)
belongs to the convex ball
B_{\delta}\bigl(P(t_{o})\bigr)\subset\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbf{R})
and thus
e^{\prime\prime}_{i}(t)
is a basis of
T_{\gamma(t)}M
.

In all cases it forms a basis of the tangent space and is of class
C^{1}
on all three open subsets (covering
J\cup J^{\prime}
), hence of class
C^{1}
on
J\cup J^{\prime}
as wanted.

\bullet

Step 3.

Chose
t_{o}\in I
arbitrary and define
\mathcal{F}
as

\quad\mathcal{F}=\{\,t\in I\mid\exists t_{o},t\in J\subset I\text{ an open interval and }\exists e_{i}:J\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}\text{ of class $C^{1}$: }\\
\forall s\in J:e_{1}(s),\dots,e_{n}(s)\text{ basis of }T_{\gamma(s)}M\,\}\kern 3.99994pt.\quad

Roughly speaking,
\mathcal{F}
is the set of those
t\in I
such that there exists a moving frame on the interval
\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a,t\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}
containing
t_{o}
.
By step 1
\mathcal{F}
is not empty and contains certainly
t_{o}
.
We thus can define
a_{o}=\inf\mathcal{F}
and
b_{o}=\sup\mathcal{F}
and we claim that
a_{o},b_{o}\notin I
, i.e.,
I=\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a_{o},b_{o}\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}
(
a_{o}=-\infty
and
b_{o}=+\infty
allowed).

So suppose
a_{o}\in I
.
By step 1 there exists an interval
J
containing
a_{o}
on which there exists a moving frame.
By definition of the infimum, there exists
t\in J\cap\mathcal{F}
, hence there exists an interval
J^{\prime}
containing
t
and
t_{o}
on which there exists a moving frame.
As we have
t\in J\cap J^{\prime}
, we can apply step 2 to conclude that there exists a moving frame on
J\cup J^{\prime}
.
But
J
is open, hence there exists
t^{\prime}\in J
with
t^{\prime}<a_{o}
.
It follows that
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, contradicting the definition of
a_{o}
.
Hence
a_{o}\notin I
.
The argument to prove
b_{o}\notin I
is similar and left to the reader.

\bullet

Step 4.

To finish the proof, let
a<t_{o}<b\in I
be arbitrary.
By step 3 there exist
a^{\prime}<a
and
b^{\prime}>b
with
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.
By definition there exist intervals
J,J^{\prime}\subset I
with
a^{\prime},t_{o}\in J
and
b^{\prime},t_{o}\in J^{\prime}
on which there exist moving frames.
By step 2 there then exists a moving frame on
J\cup J^{\prime}\supset\mathopen{\mskip 0.5mu(\mskip 1.0mu}a,b\mathclose{\mskip 1.0mu)\mskip 0.5mu}
.

\scriptstyle QED

Once we have taken care of the proof of [
5.2
], we can attack the expression of our equations in a (local) chart.
We thus suppose that we have a chart
\psi:U\to M
and a curve
\gamma=I\to\psi(U)\subset M
.
According to [

.
iii
] there exists a curve
c:I\to U
such that

We now start with the notion of a vector field of parallel tangent vectors along the curve
\gamma
.
According to the definition given in §

this is a map
v:I\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
satisfying the conditions

2
.
We then can use [

.

to obtain a map

such that

v(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot w_{i}(t)\kern 3.99994pt.

Said differently, the vector

with respect to the basis (moving frame)

In order to obtain the equations for

6.3

. Definition.

Associated to a chart
\psi:U\to M

\mathbf{g}_{ij}(x)=\langle\mskip 1.5mu(\partial_{i}\psi)(x),(\partial_{j}\psi)(x)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle\kern 3.99994pt.

Its inverse matrix is denoted by
\mathbf{g}^{ij}(x)
, i.e., we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}_{ij}(x)\cdot\mathbf{g}^{jk}(x)=\delta_{i}^{k}\text{ the identity matrix.}

And we finish with the
Christoffel symbols

\mathbf{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{jk}:U\to\mathbf{R}
defined as

\mathbf{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{jk}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}^{i\ell}(x)\cdot\langle\mskip 1.5mu(\partial_{\ell}\psi)(x),(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi)(x)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle\kern 3.99994pt.

These definitions are closely related to the definitions in [
5.3

, and thus we have the equalities

g_{ij}(t)=\mathbf{g}_{ij}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\ ,\qquad\hat{g}_{ij}(t)=\mathbf{g}^{ij}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\ ,\qquad B_{ik}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{jk}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c^{\prime}_{j}(t)\kern 3.99994pt.

. Lemma.

The map

\forall 1\leq i\leq n\quad:\quad w^{\prime}_{i}(t)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{k\ell}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}(t)\cdot w_{\ell}(t)=0\kern 3.99994pt.

v
is parallel if and only if it is orthogonal to

, which gives the equations for

(because the vectors

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\mskip 1.5muv^{\prime}(t),(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle=\biggl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot w_{j}(t)\biggr)^{\prime},(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\mskip 1.5mu\biggr\rangle

gives us the equations for

\displaystyle 0=w_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\ell}{}_{kj}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c^{\prime}_{k}(t)\cdot w_{j}(t)\kern 3.99994pt.

\scriptstyle QED

The knowledgeable reader will have recognised in the left hand side of (

and in the equation (

This covariant derivative is alternatively denoted as (see for instance
[

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot\Bigl(w^{\prime}_{i}(t)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{i}{}_{k\ell}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}(t)\cdot w_{\ell}(t)\Bigr)

6.7

. Lemma.

Let
v:I\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
be a vector field of tangent vectors along
\gamma

be the trace vector field along the trace curve

Then we have the equality (note the change/switch in names between
t
and

:
t
is fixed and

denotes the variable in

{\widehat{v}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\biggl(\frac{Dv}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s)\biggr)\kern 3.99994pt.

Proof.
According to (

] with

\displaystyle{\widehat{v}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\biggl(\,\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}(t)\cdot A_{ij}(t,s)\cdot w_{j}(s)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}(t)\cdot\bigl(A_{ij}(t,s)\cdot w^{\prime}_{j}(s)+(\partial_{s}{A_{ij}})(t,s)\cdot w_{j}(s)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}(t)\cdot A_{ij}(t,s)\cdot\Bigl(w^{\prime}_{j}(s)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{jk}(s)\cdot w_{k}(s)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e_{i}(t)\cdot A_{ij}(t,s)\cdot\Bigl(w^{\prime}_{j}(s)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{j}{}_{\ell k}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c^{\prime}_{\ell}(t)\cdot w_{k}(s)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\biggl(\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(t)\cdot\Bigl(w^{\prime}_{j}(s)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{j}{}_{\ell k}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c^{\prime}_{\ell}(t)\cdot w_{k}(s)\Bigr)\biggr)

According to [
6.7
], the covariant derivative (of a vector field of tangent vectors along a curve) is equal to the ordinary derivative of the trace field along the trace curve in a fixed affine tangent space,
up to the isometry
\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)
that maps the tangent space
T_{\gamma(s)}M
to the tangent space
T_{\gamma(t)}M
.
That we need such an isometry is obvious as there is no reason that tangent spaces at different points are equal.
We thus have shown that geodesics on a submanifold correspond to trace curves that are straight lines in a (fixed) affine tangent space and that the notion of the covariant derivative on the submanifold correspond to the ordinary derivative of the trace field.
We are now going to use these facts to obtain the equations of a geodesic in terms of local coordinates, and that without the (additional ) condition that the curve should be parametrised by arc length.

6.8

. Proposition.

The curve
\gamma
is a geodesic on

if and only if it satisfies the equation

(6.9)

\dfrac{D\gamma^{\prime}}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s)-\frac{\biggl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\dfrac{D\gamma^{\prime}}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\biggr\rangle}{\langle\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\prime}(s),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle}\cdot\gamma^{\prime}(s)=0\kern 3.99994pt,

\gamma^{\prime}:I\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}

\dfrac{D\gamma^{\prime}}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s)=0\kern 3.99994pt,

which in terms of the coefficients

of the curve

Proof.
We have shown that
\gamma
is a geodesic on
M
if and only if the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
is a straight line, which is the case if and only if the normalised tangent vector is constant:

(6.12)

\forall s\in I\quad:\quad 0=\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|\cdot\left(\,\frac{{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)}{\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|}\,\right)^{\prime}={\widehat{\gamma}}^{\prime\prime}_{t}(s)-\frac{\langle\mskip 1.5mu{\widehat{\gamma}}^{\prime}_{t},{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime\prime}\mskip 1.5mu\rangle}{\|{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\|^{2}}\cdot{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}(s)\kern 3.99994pt.

We now interpret the map
\gamma^{\prime}:I\to\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
as a vector field of tangent vectors along
\gamma
whose associated trace field along the trace curve
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}
is given by
{\widehat{\gamma}}_{t}^{\prime}:I\to T_{\gamma(t)}M
(use (
4.4
) and [
5.10
]).
As we have
\gamma(s)=\psi\bigl(c(s)\bigr)
and thus
\gamma^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c^{\prime}_{i}(t)
, it follows that the coefficients
w_{i}(s)
(
6.2
) of the vector field
v=\gamma^{\prime}
of tangent vectors along
\gamma
are given by
w_{i}(s)=c_{i}^{\prime}(s)
.
We thus can use [
6.7
] and [
5.10
] to rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\biggl(\dfrac{D\gamma^{\prime}}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s)\biggr)-\frac{\biggl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\biggl(\dfrac{D\gamma^{\prime}}{{\operatorname{d}}s}(s)\biggr),\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr)\mskip 1.5mu\biggr\rangle}{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=11.62494pt\bigl\langle\mskip 1.5mu\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr),\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr)\mskip 1.5mu\bigr\rangle}\cdot\operatorname{\mathrm{P}\mkern-3.0mu\raise 3.01385pt\hbox{\footnotesize$\mathrm{T}$}}(t,s)\bigl(\gamma^{\prime}(s)\bigr)

Remarks.

\bullet
The knowledgeable reader will have recognised in (
6.10
) the description that a geodesic (of constant velocity) is a curve for which the tangent vectors form a parallel vector field along
\gamma
.
He will also have recognised in (
6.11
) the standard set of equations for a geodesic in terms of local coordinates (see for instance
[
GHL04
, (2.78) p81]
,
[
Pre10
, 9.2.3 p223]
or
[
DC92
, (1) p62]
).
These equations are usually obtained by translating the condition
k_{\gamma}(t)\perp T_{\gamma(t)}M
(for constant velocity curves) in terms of the local coordinates
c_{i}(t)
.

\bullet
The scalar product
\langle\mskip 1.5mu\cdot,\cdot\mskip 1.5mu\rangle
used in [
6.8
] is the standard scalar product of
\mathbf{R}^{\nu}
.
Using the metric tensor this can be transformed in terms of the local coordinates as follows.
For
v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}(s)\cdot w_{i}
and
v^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}(s)\cdot w^{\prime}_{i}
with
e_{i}(s)=(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(s)\bigr)
we have

\langle\mskip 1.5muv,v^{\prime}\mskip 1.5mu\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot w^{\prime}_{j}\cdot\langle\mskip 1.5mu(\partial_{i}\psi)\bigl(c(s)\bigr),(\partial_{j}\psi)\bigl(c(s)\bigr)\mskip 1.5mu\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}w_{i}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{ij}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot w^{\prime}_{i}\kern 3.99994pt.

As both
\gamma^{\prime}(s)
and
(D\gamma^{\prime}/{\operatorname{d}}s)(s)
are of this form, (
6.9
) can be expressed completely in local objects, translating in the set of equations

\displaystyle\kern-50.00008ptc_{j}^{\prime\prime}(s)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\Gamma^{j}{}_{\ell k}\bigl(c(s)\bigr)\cdot c_{\ell}^{\prime}(s)\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\ \displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\prime}(s)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{ij}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot\Bigl(c_{j}^{\prime\prime}(s)+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}\Gamma^{j}{}_{\ell k}\bigl(c(s)\bigr)\cdot c_{\ell}^{\prime}(s)\cdot c_{k}^{\prime}(s)\Bigr)\ }{\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c^{\prime}_{i}(s)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{ij}\bigl(c(t)\bigr)\cdot c_{j}^{\prime}(s)}\kern 3.99994pt.

These are the equations that a general (regular) curve given in local coordinates
c_{i}(s)
has to satisfy in order that it is a geodesic, whether it is parametrised by arc length or not.
