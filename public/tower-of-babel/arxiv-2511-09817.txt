Introduction

The Euler operator

xD,\qquad D=\frac{d}{dx},

plays a central role in combinatorics, special functions, and symbolic calculus.
Its powers admit elegant symbolic expansions expressed by the classical Grunert formula
[
1
,
4
,
6
,
3
,
10
]
:

(xD)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k)\,x^{k}D^{k},

(1)

where
S(n,k)
are the Stirling numbers of the second kind.
This formula explicitly reveals the combinatorial structure underlying powers of
xD
and has been extensively studied in both symbolic and analytic settings.

Although the symbolic expansion of the differential operator
(xD)^{n}
is classical and well understood, its
integral analogue
has received comparatively little attention. The operator

x\mathrm{I},\qquad\mathrm{I}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt,

naturally arises in the study of Volterra equations, iterated integration, and combinatorial operator theory.
Nevertheless, despite its fundamental role, no direct analogue of the Grunert formula is known for powers of
x\mathrm{I}
.

This naturally raises the following question:

Can one obtain a Grunert-type symbolic expansion for the integral operator
x\mathrm{I}
?

That is, can we express

(x\mathrm{I})^{n}=\sum_{k}c(n,k)\,x^{\alpha(n,k)}\mathrm{I}^{\beta(n,k)},

(2)

for suitable coefficients
c(n,k)
and exponents
\alpha(n,k),\beta(n,k)
?

However, by Cauchy’s formula for repeated integration,

\mathrm{I}^{m}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,f(t)\,dt\quad\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Levy1945}{}{}]}},

(3)

it follows that the operator
(x\mathrm{I})^{n}
can be written in the Volterra form

(x\mathrm{I})^{n}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}K_{n}(x,t)\,f(t)\,dt,

where the kernel
K_{n}(x,t)
is determined by the coefficients
c(n,k)
in (
2
).
Historically, this may explain why greater attention was given to the analysis of integral kernels rather than to symbolic operator expansions. Nevertheless, the operator
(x\mathrm{I})^{n}
can be defined recursively as

(x\mathrm{I})^{0}f(x):=f(x),\qquad(x\mathrm{I})^{n}f(x):=x\cdot\mathrm{I}\!\big((t\mathrm{I})^{\,n-1}f(t)\big)(x),\quad n\geq 1,

(4)

mirroring the iterative structure familiar from differential operators and motivating the search for an explicit closed expansion.

Motivation and significance.

Establishing a symbolic expansion for
(x\mathrm{I})^{n}
is significant for several reasons:

•

It enables the
direct computation
of iterated integrals without performing repeated integration.

•

It reveals
combinatorial connections
between integral operators and integer sequences.

•

It unifies the symbolic calculus of differentiation and integration under a single operator framework.

Main contribution.

In this paper, we derive an explicit closed formula for the powers of
x\mathrm{I}
:

(x\mathrm{I})^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x^{\,n-k}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k},

(5)

where
a(n,k)
are the Bessel numbers (OEIS
A001498
)
[
2
]
.
This result provides the integral analogue of the Grunert expansion (
1
), offering a unified symbolic framework for iterated integration and yielding new identities involving classical functions and integer sequences
[
9
]
.

Bessel Numbers

The Bessel polynomials
y_{n}(x)
are defined by
[
5
]
:

y_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(n+k)!}{k!(n-k)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{k}.

(6)

The coefficients of these polynomials, taken in increasing order of the exponent, are called the Bessel numbers
a(n,k)
, forming the sequence
A001498
in the OEIS
[
9
]
:

a(n,k)=\frac{(n+k)!}{2^{k}\,k!\,(n-k)!},\qquad 0\leq k\leq n.

(7)

These coefficients satisfy the recurrence relation
[
2
]
:

a(n,k)=a(n-1,k)+(n-k+1)\,a(n,k-1),\qquad a(0,0)=1,

(8)

with
a(n,k)=0
for
k<0
or
k>n
.

3
A new symbolic formula

Theorem 1
.

For
n\geq 1
,

(x\mathrm{I})^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x^{n-k}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k}.

(9)

Proof.

We prove (
9
) by induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is immediate.
Assume now that (
9
) holds for some
n\geq 1
. Then

(x\mathrm{I})^{\,n+1}f(x)=x\mathrm{I}\Big((t\mathrm{I})^{n}f(t)\Big)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x\mathrm{I}\big(t^{\,n-k}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k}f(t)\big)(x).

For each term, we apply integration by parts:

\mathrm{I}\big(t^{\,n-k}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k}f(t)\big)(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\,n-k}(-1)^{j}(n-k)_{j}\,x^{\,n-k+1-j}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k+j+1}f(x),

where
(n-k)_{j}=(n-k)(n-k-1)\cdots(n-k-j+1)
denotes the falling factorial.

Substituting this expansion yields a double sum:

(x\mathrm{I})^{\,n+1}f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{k+j}(n-k)_{j}\,a(n-1,k)\,x^{\,n+1-k-j}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+k+j+1}f(x).

Reindexing with
i=k+j
produces a single sum:

(x\mathrm{I})^{\,n+1}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}a(n,i)\,x^{\,n+1-i}\mathrm{I}^{\,n+1+i}f(x),

where the coefficients satisfy the combinatorial identity

\displaystyle a(n,i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(n-1,i)}(n-k)_{\,i-k}\,a(n-1,k)

(10)

It is straightforward to verify that this recurrence follows from (
8
); hence the coefficients are exactly the Bessel numbers
a(n,k)
(OEIS
A001498
).

∎

Corollary 2
.

From (
3
) and (
7
), it follows that:

(x\mathrm{I})^{n}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{x\bigl(x^{2}-t^{2}\bigr)^{n-1}}{(2(n-1))!!}\,f(t)\,dt.

(11)

Applications

Power functions

For
\alpha>0
:

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n}(t^{\alpha-1})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{x(x^{2}-t^{2})^{n-1}}{2^{\,n-1}(n-1)!}t^{\alpha-1}\,dt

\displaystyle\text{let }t

\displaystyle=x\sqrt{u},\quad dt=\frac{x}{2}u^{-1/2}\,du,\quad u\in[0,1]

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2^{\,n-1}(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}\big(x^{2}(1-u)\big)^{\,n-1}\big(xu^{1/2}\big)^{\alpha-1}\frac{x}{2}u^{-1/2}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{2^{\,n-1}(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{x}{2}\,x^{2(n-1)}x^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{n-1}u^{\alpha/2-1}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\alpha+2n-1}}{2^{n}(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{B}\Big(n,\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\alpha+2n-1}\Gamma(\alpha/2)}{2^{n}\,\Gamma(n+\alpha/2)}.

Series expansion gives

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n}(t^{\alpha-1})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x^{n-k}\,\mathrm{I}^{n+k}(t^{\alpha-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x^{n-k}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{n-1+k}}{(n-1+k)!}t^{\alpha-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{(n-1+k)!}\,x^{n-k}\int_{0}^{x}x^{n-1+k}(1-u)^{n-1+k}\,x^{\alpha-1}u^{\alpha-1}\,x\,du\qquad(t=xu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{(n-1+k)!}\,x^{n-k+(n-1+k)+(\alpha-1)+1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{n-1+k}u^{\alpha-1}\,du

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{(n-1+k)!}\,x^{2n+\alpha-1}B(\alpha,n+k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{(n-1+k)!}\,x^{2n+\alpha-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(n+k)}{\Gamma(\alpha+n+k)}

\displaystyle=x^{\alpha+2n-1}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{\Gamma(\alpha+n+k)}.

which leads to

\frac{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}{2^{n}\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(n+\alpha/2)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)}{\Gamma(\alpha+n+k)}.

(12)

Exponential function

For
f(t)=e^{t}
:

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n+1}(e^{t})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}a(n-1,k)\,x^{n-k}\,\mathrm{I}^{n+k}(e^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{n+1-k}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{n+k}}{(n+k)!}e^{t}\,dt.

The integral evaluates as

\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)^{n+k}e^{t}\,dt=e^{x}\,\gamma(n+k+1,x),

where
\gamma
is the lower incomplete gamma function. Hence,

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n+1}(e^{t})(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}a(n,k)}{(n+k)!}\,x^{n+1-k}\,\gamma(n+k+1,x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{n+1-k}\left(1-e^{-x}\sum_{j=0}^{n+k}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{\,n+1-k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{\,n+1-k}\sum_{j=0}^{n+k}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}.

It is shown in
[
11
]
that:

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{\,n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{n+k}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\,n-k}\frac{(2(n-k)-1)!!}{(2k)!!}\,x^{2k}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n+1}(e^{t})(x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a(n,k)\,x^{\,n+1-k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\,n-k}\frac{(2(n-k)-1)!!}{(2k)!!}\,x^{2k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{\,n+1}e^{x}\,y_{n}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{x}\Bigr)-\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\,n-k}\frac{(2(n-k)-1)!!}{(2k)!!}\,x^{\,2k+1}

(13)

Evaluating at
x=1

yields a Dobinski–type identity for the sequences

a(n)=\hrefhttps://oeis.org/A000806
:

a(n)=y_{n}(-1)=\frac{1}{e}\left((x\mathrm{I})^{n+1}(e^{t})(1)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{\,n-k}\frac{(2(n-k)-1)!!}{(2k)!!}\right).

(14)

The function
\ln(1+t)

From
[
8
]
,

\mathrm{I}^{m}(\ln(1+t))(x)=\frac{1}{m!}\left[(1+x)^{m}\ln(1+x)-A_{m}(x)\right],

where

A_{m}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(H_{m}-H_{m-k})\,x^{k},

and
H_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k}
denotes the
m
th harmonic number.

We get that

\displaystyle(x\mathrm{I})^{n}(\ln(1+t))(x)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(n-k-1)!\,2^{k}\,k!(n+k)}\,x^{\,n-k}(1+x)^{\,n+k}\right]\ln(1+x)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(n-k-1)!\,2^{k}\,k!(n+k)}\,x^{\,n-k}A_{\,n+k}(x).

(15)
