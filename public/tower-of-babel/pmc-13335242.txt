The curious case of aplastic anemia: a fatal triple co-infection with Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia
Authors: Sevda Sadeghi Borkehim, Hamed Azhdari Tehrani, Farid Javandoust Gharehbagh, Mohammad Kord, Mahdi Azimi, Ilad Alavi Darazam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcurrent opportunistic infections are rare but can lead to life-threatening complications in patients with hematologic disorders. Immunosuppression, particularly severe neutropenia, significantly increases the risk of invasive fungal and bacterial infections.Case summaryWe report a 34-year-old female with chronic hepatitis B initially presented with pancytopenia. Subsequent bone marrow evaluation revealed aplastic anemia, and she subsequently received immunosuppressive therapy. During hospitalization, she developed multiple infectious episodes, including fever, pulmonary nodular lesions, and oral mucosal lesions. PCR assays and histopathology confirmed a rare triple co-infection involving Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia species. Although multimodal therapy combining antifungal and antibacterial agents and surgical intervention was initiated, persistent neutropenia prevented complete infection resolution.ConclusionCo-infection with Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with malignancy or receiving corticosteroid therapy, requires early recognition and appropriate therapy to reduce complications and mortality. This case underscores that persistent, profound neutropenia remains an insurmountable barrier to recovery, highlighting that immune reconstitution is as critical as antimicrobial therapy in such scenarios.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Co-occurring opportunistic infections caused by the species of Nocardia, Aspergillus and Mucorales are rare but have significant clinical importance in immunocompromised patients. These infections are often linked to high mortality rates and are very challenging to diagnose. These are some of the most critical opportunistic pathogens that affect this vulnerable population. The incidence rate of nocardial infection has been estimated to be 500–1000 cases annually in the United States and 90–130 cases in Italy [1, 2]. Invasive aspergillosis on a global scale causes approximately 2.1 million cases each year [3], but mucormycosis is considered one of the most virulent angioinvasive fungal diseases, with a mortality rate ranging between 30 and 69%, depending on the underlying comorbidities and the location of the invasion [4].

The nocardial disease may present with isolated pulmonary, cutaneous, or central nervous system; or as a disseminated systemic infection [5–8]. Aspergillus infections have a continuum between noninvasive lesions, to invasive pulmonary or cerebral disease, and are most frequently seen in immunocompromised patients [9]. Mucorales infections most usually appear as rhino-orbito cerebral mucormycosis, followed by pulmonary and cutaneous manifestations [10].

The common predisposing factors of such opportunistic infections include hematologic malignancies, organ transplantation, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, long-term corticosteroid use, established immunosuppressive conditions or therapies (e.g., neutropenia or immunosuppressive drug use) and chronic pulmonary disorders [11–15].

Although each of these infections has been extensively studied, their concomitant occurrence in a single patient is extremely rare. The co-existence of several opportunistic pathogens poses severe diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, owing to the overlap of clinical manifestations and the complexity of the treatment of this type of infection.

Herein, we describe a rare case of concurrent Nocardia, Aspergillus, and Mucorales infections in an immunocompromised patient. This case highlights the importance of early recognition, prompt microbiological diagnosis, and timely, and targeted management to improve outcomes in complex opportunistic coinfection.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old female with a history of chronic hepatitis B was presented following an incidental finding of pancytopenia during routine testing (Table 1). Physical examination, besides the presence of malaise, showed small purpuric lesions on the chest and ecchymosis on the thigh. At the time of initial presentation, the patient was neutropenic, with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of 112/μL. Initial computed tomography (CT) scans of the paranasal sinuses and lungs were unremarkable (Figure1). Bone marrow aspiration revealed significant hypocellularity and diffuse hematopoietic residues. Biopsy and immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed a mixed infiltration of T-and B-lymphoid cells (CD3+ and CD20+), negative for CD34, with no evidence of blast proliferation. The findings supported a diagnosis of aplastic anemia. Additionally, she had a 12-year history of chronic hepatitis B following a needlestick injury, had not received prior antiviral therapy, and her previous viral load was unavailable; Serology tests showed negative HBeAg and positive HBsAg, suggestive of chronic hepatitis B infection. Due to the patient’s immunosuppression and neutropenia, prophylactic therapy with oral acyclovir (400 mg BD) and fluconazole (100 mg BD) was initiated on the first day of hospitalization due to prevent fungal and viral opportunistic infections. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis with cotrimoxazole was withheld on initial admission due to the underlying aplastic anemia and concerns regarding further bone marrow suppression. This decision was a deliberate risk-benefit assessment, as the primary therapeutic goal was to stabilize hematological condition and prevent life-threatening complications associated with severe pancytopenia.

The patient was treated for aplastic anemia with immunosuppressive treatment, including prednisolone 50 mg daily, cyclosporine 50 mg BD, and eltrombopag 50 mg TDS, was administered. Cyclosporine has the T-cell-depleting effect and considering the history of hepatitis B and the fact that the patient is taking cyclosporine, tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) 25 mg daily was started as prophylaxis. Viral load at baseline was 989 IU/mL and liver functions tests were normal. Broad-spectrum antibiotics of empirical nature, meropenem (2000 mg stat, then 1000 mg TDS) and vancomycin (1000 mg stat, then 1000 mg BD) were also initiated. She remained stable and afebrile during this period.

On day 9, the patient developed fever. Chest CT scans revealed nodular lesions with a halo sign (Fig. 2), suggesting an invasive fungal infection. Serum galactomannan (GM) was positive on three consecutive days (0.7, 0.7, 0.8) (Table 2). On day 13, Liposomal amphotericin B (LAMB) at a daily dose of 250 mg was introduced, along with other broad-spectrum antibiotics, including linezolid (600 mg BD), imipenem (500 mg QID), and amikacin (1000 mg daily). A necrotic nodule appeared on the anterior chest wall, initially suspected to be ecthyma gangrenosum (Fig. 3). A biopsy of the lesion was performed, including histopathologic examination and special stains. Histopathology demonstrated moderate perivascular and perifollicular lymphoplasmacytic inflammation, consistent with chronic folliculitis. Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative for acid-fast bacilli, and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining showed no fungal elements, effectively excluding bacterial and fungal infection.

The patient remained neutropenic, and the diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis was classified as a proven invasive fungal disease according to the criteria of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) and the Mycoses Study Group (MSG), based on histopathologic demonstration of septate, acute-angle branching hyphae consistent with Aspergillus spp. in a tissue biopsy specimen (Fig. 4A(, along with compatible clinical and radiologic findings. On day 17, LAMB was subsequently replaced with voriconazole (300 mg BD), and broad-spectrum antibiotics were continued.

On day 27, the patient developed a new episode of fever accompanied by hypoesthesia of the hard palate. She also reported blurred vision, more prominent in the left eye. Fundoscopic examination revealed pre-retinal and sub-hyaloid hemorrhages. The following day, a white plaque appeared on the posterior hard palate (Fig. 5A). ENT and dermatology evaluations suggested oral candidiasis, and nystatin drops were prescribed.

Subsequently, bilateral nasal endoscopy and biopsy were performed due to profound neutropenia, radiologic evidence of sinus involvement on CT, and the presence of necrotic lesions (Figs. 6C and 7A and B). The procedure was intended to obtain tissue for definitive diagnosis and to guide clinical decision-making regarding the need for potential surgical intervention. Endoscopic evaluation revealed a bleeding lesion on the palate with mucosal inflammation. Histopathologic examination of the palatal lesion confirmed mucormycosis with angioinvasion (Fig. 4B). Voriconazole was discontinued, and high-dose LAMB (400 mg daily) was restarted. Surgical debridement was performed on day 37 with no progression observed during the second-look procedure.

Persistent fever and progression of pulmonary abnormalities by day 55 raised suspicion of an additional infectious process. On day 58, bronchoscopy revealed copious secretions and necrotic mucosa. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) analysis demonstrated both septate and non-septate hyphae. Serum GM reported galactomannan was elevated (4.0). BAL cultures were positive for Aspergillus flavus and Mucorales, whereas bacterial cultures showed only normal flora (Fig. 8A-C), and Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative for acid-fast bacilli. Multiplex real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) performed on BAL fluid detected Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia, while results for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacteria were negative. A final diagnosis of dual invasive fungal infection (aspergillosis and mucormycosis) with concurrent nocardiosis was established. Table 3 summarizes the infectious episodes.

Despite 11 days of high-dose LAMB, the patient continued to deteriorate (Figs. 6E and F and 5B). High-dose LAMB was maintained, and intravenous posaconazole (300 mg BD, then 300 mg daily) was added. The antibacterial regimen was adjusted to include imipenem (500 mg QID), linezolid (600 mg BD), and amikacin (1000 mg daily) for broader coverage. Surgical debridement was planned but postponed due to thrombocytopenia. The patient remained febrile and neutropenic, and repeat imaging confirmed persistent sinonasal and pulmonary involvement.

On day 74, she reported new-onset pulsatile left-sided headache. CT scans of the brain, paranasal sinuses, and lungs, along with a lumbar puncture (Fig. 6G), were performed. Surgical exploration revealed necrosis of the middle and inferior turbinates. However, debridement was deferred due to persistent neutropenia. Persistent fever suggested an uncontrolled source of infection.

The antimicrobial regimen was adjusted several times and expanded to include cotrimoxazole (800 mg QID), vancomycin (1100 mg stat, then 750 mg BD), colistin (9 MIU stat, then 4.5 MIU BD), and high-dose meropenem (2000 mg QID) due to ongoing fever. On day 86, her femoral Central venous line (CV line) was removed and cultured. The organism’s antibiogram was concerning for a Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, showing intrinsic resistance to imipenem, cefepime, and ceftazidime. It demonstrated variable sensitivity: sensitive to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone in both central and peripheral samples, and showed discordant sensitivity to amikacin, gentamicin, and cotrimoxazole (sensitive in the central line culture but resistant in the peripheral culture). Based on this profile, ciprofloxacin (400 mg TDS) and amikacin (500 mg daily) were added to her regimen. Furthermore, given the patient’s prolonged fever, neutropenia, and new-onset headache, PCR testing for Toxoplasma gondii was also requested.

Despite maximal antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, and supportive therapy, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate (Fig. 6H). On day 98, she developed signs of multiorgan dysfunction and unfortunately passed away on February 14 due to cardiac arrest. Discussion

The occurrence of concurrent invasive infections in patients with hematological disorders is rare but highly life-threatening. In this report, we describe a patient with aplastic anemia who developed multiple opportunistic infections, including Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing simultaneous infections in immunosuppressed patients. Patients with aplastic anemia are at increased risk of invasive fungal infections due to prolonged and profound neutropenia, and unfortunately, their response to antifungal therapy is often suboptimal [16]. Therefore, any delay in identifying the causative pathogens may result in late initiation of appropriate treatment, adverse clinical outcomes, and even death [17].

Diagnosing Aspergillus and Mucorales infections is difficult, as biopsy is often not feasible, while available serological and molecular tests lack standardized protocols [18, 19]. In a case report, diagnostic efforts such as BAL, GM testing, and imaging, did not identify the infection, and a definitive diagnosis was made only by postmortem autopsy [20]. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of PCR-based assays in immunocompromised patients with unexplained pulmonary infiltrates. In a large cohort of patients with hematologic malignancies and hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, PCR assays targeting Mucorales were shown to facilitate earlier diagnosis of invasive pulmonary infections in culture-negative cases, whereas Nocardia PCR supported early detection in conjunction with conventional microbiological methods [17]. Moreover, molecular analysis of BAL specimens has been reported to enable detection of Nocardia spp. in culture-negative cases, with species-level confirmation, supporting the potential diagnostic role of BAL-based PCR in selected clinical settings [21]. In our patient, serial GM testing supported the diagnosis of invasive aspergillosis, while PCR confirmed the presence of triple infection with Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia. However, it should be noted that detection of Nocardia DNA in respiratory specimens does not definitively indicate active infection, as colonization can occur, particularly in patients with underlying chronic pulmonary conditions [22].

Neutropenia, immunosuppressive therapy, and profound lymphopenia with marked CD4 T-cell depletion are the principal risk factors for these opportunistic infections; corticosteroid exposure may also facilitate dissemination to the central nervous system [23]. Similarly, a case report described a patient with rheumatoid arthritis who developed concurrent Aspergillus and Nocardia infections, identifying major risk factors such as T-cell immunodeficiency (e.g., after transplantation), prolonged corticosteroid therapy, graft-versus-host disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic granulomatous disease, and pulmonary alveolar proteinosis [24]. Even short courses of corticosteroid therapy may predispose patients to nocardiosis [25]. Risk factors for aspergillosis overlap considerably; prolonged corticosteroid exposure, T-cell suppression, neutropenia, malignancy and transplantation are most significant [24, 26]. In this patient, immunosuppression, neutropenia, and corticosteroid exposure represented the major predisposing factors for her multiple infections.

Because of the rarity of such co-infections, no consensus exists regarding optimal initial therapy or treatment duration [23]. In this patient, LAMB was initiated for antifungal prophylaxis against Mucorales in patients with prolonged neutropenia [27]. After invasive aspergillosis was confirmed, LAMB was replaced with voriconazole (300 mg twice daily) in accordance with the 2016 IDSA guidelines, which recommend voriconazole as the first-line therapy for invasive aspergillosis and reserve LAMB for cases where voriconazole is contraindicated or ineffective. Similarly, a meta-analysis assessing antifungal resistance among Aspergillus species in Iran from 2010 to 2024 supported these recommendations [9].

Following histopathological confirmation of mucormycosis, voriconazole was discontinued due to its lack of efficacy, as reported in previous studies [28]. In our case, a combination of LAMB and surgical debridement was employed. Similarly, Moshayedi et al. [29]. demonstrated that this therapeutic approach significantly reduced morbidity and mortality in a patient with aplastic anemia and angioinvasive mucormycosis.

Guidelines recommend a minimum daily dose of 5 mg/kg of LAMB for mucormycosis without Central Nervous System (CNS) involvement, and 10 mg/kg/day when CNS involvement is present [30, 31]. A prospective pilot study demonstrated that high-dose LAMB (10 mg/kg) was associated with improved survival in patients with CNS or disseminated mucormycosis, compared with standard dosing [32]. A favorable response was observed in 45% of patients with underlying malignancies treated with high dose LAMB and surgery [33]. In this case, the dosage of LAMB was escalated from 7 mg/kg to 10 mg/kg due to the patient’s deterioration.

Evidence regarding combination therapy with amphotericin B and azoles remains inconclusive. Several studies have demonstrated no significant improvement in survival with combination therapy compared to monotherapy [32, 34, 35]. Conversely, some studies have recommended the use of LAMB in combination with posaconazole in progressive cases [36]. In two experimental murine models, mice receiving combination therapy demonstrated improved survival [37, 38]. In our case, LAMB was administered in combination with parenteral posaconazole after deterioration was observed.

In a case series from 2002 to 2010, 21 patients with confirmed nocardiosis were evaluated; 17 cases presented with pulmonary nocardiosis, while four had disseminated disease. All patients were treated with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole)TMP-SMX(, and complete recovery was achieved in 19 patients [13]. Another report described a case of a Nocardia farcinica multiloculated pontine abscess with neutrophilic meningitis. The infection was initially treated with linezolid and meropenem, followed by TMP-SMX and ceftriaxone after microbiological confirmation. Complete resolution was achieved after nine months [39]. Another report described a patient with lupus and concurrent Nocardia and Aspergillus infection who responded to six weeks of combination therapy with imipenem and TMP-SMX [23]. Based on these studies, TMP-SMX is considered the first-line therapy for Nocardia infections, either alone or in combination with amikacin, imipenem, or third-generation cephalosporins [24]. Similarly, in this case, the patient was managed with TMP-SMX, vancomycin, colistin, and high-dose meropenem following clinical deterioration and the onset of multiorgan dysfunction.

Deterioration and therapeutic failure in this patient were likely multifactorial, attributable to profound neutropenia predisposing to uncontrolled infection, as well as persistent thrombocytopenia, which precluded surgical debridement and resulted in inadequate source control.

In contrast to previously reported cases of dual opportunistic infections, our patient presented with a rare and severe triple infection caused by Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia in the setting of aplastic anemia. This report emphasizes the need for a high index of clinical suspicion and comprehensive diagnostic strategies in immunocompromised patients. Our experience highlights that effective management of such complex infections requires a multimodal approach, combining targeted antifungal and antibacterial therapy with surgical debridement whenever clinically feasible.

Given the rarity of these co-infections, evidence-based treatment guidelines are lacking. Prospective multicenter studies and the establishment of patient registries are crucial to define optimal diagnostic algorithms, therapeutic strategies, and outcome predictors in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

This fatal case of triple co-infection with Aspergillus, Mucorales, and Nocardia underscores a critical limitation in the management of profoundly immunocompromised patients. While early and accurate diagnosis using advanced molecular techniques and prompt initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy are essential pillars of management, they remain insufficient in the face of irreversible bone marrow failure. This case highlights the need for a paradigm shift—one in which efforts to achieve immune reconstitution— through granulocyte transfusions, hematopoietic growth factors, or emerging immunotherapies—are pursued with the same urgency as antimicrobial treatment. For patients with persistent neutropenia, survival ultimately depends on overcoming this fundamental immunologic deficit.
