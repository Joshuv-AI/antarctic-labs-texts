An Advection-Difusion Model Incorporating Investor Inertia for the Dynamics of Financial Asset Prices
Diego da Silva Santos, Luiz Gustavo Bastos Pinho

Abstract
Standard models of asset price dynamics, such as geometric Brownian motion ( Osborne 1959 ; Samuelson 2016 ) , do not formally incorporate investor inertia. This paper introduces a novel framework for modelling stock price dynamics that incorporates the concept of investor inertia, inspired by diffusion with retention models ( Bevilacqua et al. 2011 ) . The asset’s log-price is modelled as a three-state discrete random walk, allowing for movements in any of three directions: up, down, or neutral. We demonstrate that this framework naturally leads to an advection–diffusion partial differential equation, in which the advection (drift) term arises directly from the asymmetry between buying, selling, and holding decisions. Remarkably, the model implies that log-prices follow a normal distribution — a finding of great practical interest due to its analytical tractability. The applicability of the model is confirmed through simulation and an empirical application using Brazilian PETR4.SA data.

1
Introduction

The idea that asset price evolution can be described by stochastic processes comes from the pioneering work of
Bachelier 1900
. In his thesis Théorie de la Spéculation, he was the first to model market fluctuations using Brownian motion, drawing a direct analogy with the random motion of particles studied in physics.

Despite its originality, Bachelier’s work remained largely unknown for over half a century. The idea was independently rediscovered and refined. The physicist
Osborne 1959
reinforced the analogy with physics and introduced a crucial correction by observing that it is the log-price of assets, rather than their nominal price, that follows a random path. Shortly thereafter,
Samuelson 2016
synthesised these ideas, providing them with a rigorous economic theoretical foundation and establishing Geometric Brownian Motion as the standard model for capturing the unpredictable nature of markets.

However, these models generally rely on the simplifying assumption that, at each instant, the asset price undergoes a displacement, either positive or negative. This binary view does not explicitly incorporate a fundamental behaviour observed in the market: investor inertia
Barber and Odean 2000
, whereby periods of collective indecision result in the predominant choice being not to trade, thereby maintaining prices at their current levels.

To address this, this paper introduces a novel model for price dynamics inspired by the work of
(
Bevilacqua et al. 2011
)
. We propose an analogy whereby, just as particles in a medium can be temporarily retained, market participants may choose to ’retain’ their positions and quotes. We model this idea through a three-state random walk for the asset’s log-price. Unlike the standard model, our framework assumes that market agents can make one of three decisions at each moment: to trade at a higher (buy), lower (sell) or equal (retain) value to the current moment, as described in
(
Davis and Norman 1990
)
.

The main theoretical contribution of this work is to demonstrate that, in the continuous limit, this discrete three-state model converges to an advection-diffusion equation. We demonstrate that the advection (or drift) term, which is often added ad hoc to many models to represent a market trend, emerges naturally here. This is a direct consequence of the asymmetry between buy, sell and hold decisions. Volatility arises as a direct consequence of total trading activity. Markets with high inertia exhibit low activity and therefore low volatility.

To validate the model and explore its properties, this paper presents a numerical simulation study that elucidates the role of the diffusion and advection parameters. This is followed by an empirical application that calibrates the model using historical data from the Brazilian stock market (PETR4.SA). Therefore, the work proposes a new dynamic for prices, offering a fresh perspective on how investor inertia can influence the collective behaviour of the market.

2
Theoretical Foundations

In this section, we will review the fundamental concepts that underpin modern asset pricing modelling. We start with the intuitive idea of a random walk, show how it converges to the diffusion equation and trace the mathematical developments that led to the standard model in finance. This foundation is essential for providing context for the introduction of our diffusion model with retention.

2.1
The Diffusion Equation and Asset Price Dynamics

A random walk is a process that generates random values for a variable representing a given position. In a discrete model, the probability of being at point
x
at time
t
, denoted by
p(x,t)
, evolves from adjacent positions. Suppose we have the positions
x
,
x-a
and
x+a
. We want to calculate the probability of being at position
x
at time
t+\Delta t
. There are two possible outcomes: it was either at
x-a
or at
x+a
. Then:

p(x,t+\Delta t)=\alpha p(x-a,t)+\beta p(x+a,t).

That is, if we know the probability of being at the current time, then we also know the probabilities of being at a certain future time. This is because it is a Markovian process. For a symmetric walk, where the probability of taking a step up or down by a distance l is the same (
\alpha=\beta=1/2
), the relationship is:

p(x,t+\Delta t)=\frac{p(x-a,t)+p(x+a,t)}{2}.

In the continuous limit, where the time
\Delta t
and space
a
intervals become infinitesimal, this recurrence relation converges to the diffusion equation:

\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}=k\frac{\partial^{2}p(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}},

(1)

where
k=\lim\frac{a^{2}}{2\Delta t}
, is the diffusion coefficient. In physics, this equation is used to model the dissipation process of particles through a medium. For example, it can be used to describe the temporal evolution of a function
p(x,t)
that represents the temperature at a given position at a given time. For instance, the flow of a fluid through a pipe causes particles to move from areas of higher concentration to areas of lower concentration, and this movement can be explained by considering the flow in small time increments along the pipe.

The first to apply this formalism to financial markets was
(
Bachelier 1900
)
. In his thesis, he postulated that the price of an asset
S
in a “fair game” follows a driftless random walk. He concluded that the probability density of prices,
p(S,t)
, should satisfy the pure heat equation:

\frac{\partial p(S,t)}{\partial t}=k\frac{\partial^{2}p(S,t)}{\partial S^{2}}.

(2)

By analogy, the equation describes how the “certainty” about today’s price “diffuses” into a cloud of probabilities over future prices, driven by the random “shocks” of trading.

2.2
The Wiener Process

The path taken by a single ’particle’ (i.e. the price) in a diffusion process can be modelled mathematically using the Wiener process, also known as Brownian motion. The increment
dX_{t}
of a standard Wiener process over an infinitesimal time interval
dt
exhibits the following properties:

i.

Null mean:
E(dX_{t})=0
;

ii.

Variance proportional to time:
E(\{dX_{t}\}^{2})=dt
, which implies that its standard deviation is proportional to
\sqrt{dt}
;

iii.

Independent increments: the value of
dX_{t}
in an interval is independent of the values in previous intervals (Markov property).

The Generalised Wiener Process is used to model systems that have an inherent tendency, which adds a “drift” term:

dx_{t}=\alpha dt+\sigma dX_{t}.

(3)

In this stochastic differential equation (SDE),
\alpha
is the drift rate (the deterministic trend per unit time) and
\sigma
is the volatility (the magnitude of stochastic uncertainty). The increments of this process, denoted by
\Delta x
, follow a normal distribution, i.e.
\Delta x\sim N(\alpha\Delta t,\sigma^{2}\Delta t)
.

2.3
The Dynamics of Asset Log-Returns

Although pioneering, Bachelier’s model (Equation
2
) implied a non-zero probability of negative prices. Theoretical developments driven by the work of
(
Osborne 1959
)
and
(
Samuelson 2016
)
established that the diffusion model should be applied to the logarithm of the asset price rather than the price itself.

In financial practice, the analysis of asset time series focuses on returns, normalising price variations. The logarithmic (or continuously compounded) return between time periods
t-1
and
t
is defined as follows:

r_{t}=\ln(S_{t})-\ln(S_{t-1})=\ln\left(\frac{S_{t}}{S_{t-1}}\right).

(4)

This definition is mathematically convenient and, for small variations,
r_{t}
is approximately equal to the simple return
R_{t}=(S_{t}-S_{t-1})/S_{t-1}
. The main advantage is that, while the price
S_{t}
s constrained to be positive, its logarithm
x_{t}=\ln(S_{t})
can take any real value, making it an ideal candidate for modelling by a generalised Wiener process.

Therefore, the fundamental postulate of quantitative finance is that the dynamics of the logarithm of an asset’s price,
x_{t}=\ln(S_{t})
, is described by:

d\ln(S_{t})=vdt+\sigma dX_{t}.

(5)

This model, known as geometric Brownian motion, captures the two essential characteristics of an asset’s dynamics: a continuous expected rate of return, represented by drift
v
and a random shock term
\sigma X_{t}
, representing market uncertainty or risk. It forms the basis of the Black–Scholes–Merton model
(
Merton et al. 1971
;
Black and Scholes 1973
)
and is the starting point for most pricing models. Against this backdrop, we will present our advection-diffusion model.

2.4
Diffusion with Retention

Consider the process of fluid displacement through a certain pipeline. While the dynamics of particle displacement are well modelled by the diffusion process, any obstruction to their movement is disregarded in classical diffusion modelling. Diffusion with retention takes into account external factors that may cause retention of the fluid particles during displacement, such as collision with the pipeline structure, which induces retention of the fluid.

To address the temporary retention effect, additional terms are introduced into the fundamental diffusion equation or the diffusion coefficient is expanded to incorporate higher-order terms
(
Bevilacqua et al. 2011
)
.

3
Dynamic of the Distribution of Asset Prices Based on Investor Inertia

In this section, we develop our model from first principles. We start with a three-state random walk to model an investor’s decision to buy, sell or hold an asset. We show that this discrete process converges to an advection–diffusion equation in the continuous limit. The solution to this equation provides a behavioural foundation for the drift and volatility parameters of the canonical geometric Brownian motion.

It is widely accepted that the logarithm of stock prices follows Brownian motion, meaning that the difference in prices over two discrete time intervals is normally distributed, with the variance proportional to the time interval. The evolution of the probability distribution of prices can be investigated in a manner similar to that employed in
(
Bevilacqua et al. 2011
)
.

Let
S
be a random variable representing the value of a asset, and let
p(x,t)
be the density of
x=\log S
at the point
s
and at the time
t
. Consider that an individual is willing to pay
S_{0}
for a given stock at time
t_{0}
. At each moment, this individual’s preference may remain the same (with probability
\gamma
) or change. If the preference indeed changes, they may be willing to pay
S_{t-1}\Delta s
with probability
\alpha
or
S_{t-1}/\Delta s
with probability
\beta
, such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
.

If we consider a large number of individuals and define
p_{N}(x,t)
as the proportion of individuals, among
N
, who are willing to pay
x
at time
t
, it is reasonable to asset that
p^{*}(x,t)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}p_{N}(x,t)
represents the probability density for the price. Let
p(x,t)
be the probability density function of the logarithm of the price,
\Delta x=\log\Delta s
,
x_{i}=x_{0}+i\Delta s
and
t_{i}=i\Delta t
. Consider a random walk over the positive reals observed at the times
t_{i}
such that

x_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rc}x_{i-1}+\Delta x,\mbox{with probability }\beta,\\
x_{i-1},\mbox{with probability }\gamma,\\
x_{i-1}-\Delta x,\mbox{with probability }\alpha.\end{array}\right.

Thus, by setting
p^{t}_{n}=p(x_{n},t)
, the fundamental rule of contents redistribution, similar to the process in the Figure
1
, reads:

\displaystyle p_{n}^{t}=\gamma p_{n}^{t}+\alpha p_{n-1}^{t-1}+\beta p_{n+1}^{t-1}.

(13)

Subtracting
p_{n}^{t-1}
in both sides in Equation (
13
), we have that

\displaystyle p_{n}^{t}-p_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\gamma-1)p_{n}^{t-1}+\alpha p_{n-1}^{t-1}+\beta p_{n+1}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha p_{n-1}^{t-1}+\beta p_{n+1}^{t-1}-(\alpha+\beta)p_{n}^{t-1},

where
\gamma=1-\alpha-\beta
. Having the equalities
\alpha=\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\right)
and
\beta=\left(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\right)
, it follows that

\displaystyle p_{n}^{t}-p_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}p_{n-1}^{t-1}-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}p_{n-1}^{t-1}+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}p_{n+1}^{t-1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}p_{n+1}^{t-1}-(\alpha+\beta)p_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha+\beta)(p_{n-1}^{t-1}-2p_{n}^{t-1}+p_{n-1}^{t-1})+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}(p_{n+1}^{t-1}-p_{n-1}^{t-1}).

Since the function
p
is sufficiently smooth, the second derivate is finite. Thus, we have

\displaystyle p_{n}^{t}-p_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha+\beta)\Delta^{2}p_{n}^{t-1}+(\beta-\alpha)\Delta p_{n}^{t-1}+O(\Delta x)^{2}.

Multiplying both sides by (
1/\Delta t
), having

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}^{t}-p_{n}^{t-1}}{\Delta t}=\frac{(\Delta x)^{2}}{\Delta t}(\alpha+\beta)\frac{\Delta^{2}p_{n}^{t-1}}{(\Delta x)^{2}}+\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}(\beta-\alpha)\frac{\Delta p_{n}^{t-1}}{\Delta x}+O(\Delta x)^{2},

and taking
K_{2}=(\Delta x)^{2}/\Delta t
and
K_{1}=\Delta x/\Delta t
, in the continous limit,
\Delta x\rightarrow 0
and
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{2}(\alpha+\beta)\frac{\partial^{2}p}{x^{2}}+K_{1}(\beta-\alpha)\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{2}(1-\gamma)\frac{\partial^{2}p}{x^{2}}+K_{1}(\beta-\alpha)\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}.

Note that if the inertia probability
\gamma
is high, volatility decreases,
(1-\gamma)
, that in terms of total trading probability
(\alpha+\beta)
, market volatility is driven by total trading activity. Note that the drift term does not directly depend on
\gamma
. This means that a market with high inertia (many investors sitting idle) can still have a strong trend if the small volume of trading that occurs is strongly imbalanced in one direction.

Now, we solve the PDE to find
p(x,t)
. The previus equation, by taking term relationed of volatility
\mathcal{V}=K_{2}(\alpha+\beta)
and the term relationed of the drift
\mathcal{D}=K_{1}(\alpha-\beta)
, is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x^{2}}-\mathcal{D}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}.

To solve this problem, we use a transformation of variables designed to eliminate the first-order term (advection). Let us set
p=ve^{ax+bt}
. We have that the partial derivatives of
p(x,t)
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}e^{ax+bt}+bve^{ax+bt},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}e^{ax+bt}+ave^{ax+bt},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}e^{ax+bt}+2a\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}e^{ax+bt}+a^{2}ve^{ax+bt}.

Thus, substituting the values, the equation takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}e^{ax+bt}+bve^{ax+bt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}e^{ax+bt}+2\mathcal{V}a\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}e^{ax+bt}+\mathcal{V}a^{2}ve^{ax+bt}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}e^{ax+bt}-\mathcal{D}ave^{ax+bt},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+bv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}+2\mathcal{V}a\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\mathcal{V}a^{2}v-\mathcal{D}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\mathcal{D}av

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}+(2\mathcal{V}a-\mathcal{D})\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+(\mathcal{V}a^{2}-\mathcal{D}a-b)v.

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}},

with the initial condition

, where
\delta(x)
is the Dirac delta function, meaning that initially the asset is valued at

. However, it is known that the solution to this equation is

\displaystyle v(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathcal{V}t}}exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4\mathcal{V}t}\right).

This provides us with

\displaystyle p(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi A^{2}t}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4\mathcal{V}t}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}x}{2\mathcal{V}}-\frac{\mathcal{D}^{2}t}{4\mathcal{V}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathcal{V}t}}\exp\left(\frac{-x^{2}+2\mathcal{D}tx-\mathcal{D}^{2}t^{2}}{4\mathcal{V}t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mathcal{V}t}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mathcal{D}t)^{2}}{4\mathcal{V}t}\right).

This is the probability density function of a normal distribution,
N(\mathcal{D}t,2\mathcal{V}t)
. The parameter D directly governs the mean of the distribution,
E[X_{t}]=\mathcal{D}_{t}
. This establishes it as the drift rate, or the continuous expected return of the logarithm of the price. This parameter is a direct consequence of the asymmetry between trading probabilities. If the probability of
\alpha
exceeds that of
\beta
, then
\mathcal{D}
is positive, generating a downward trend. If
\beta>\alpha
, the trend is upward. Thus, the model provides a behavioural foundation for market drift.

The parameter
\mathcal{V}
determines the variance of the distribution,
Var[X_{t}]=2\mathcal{V}t
. It represents the diffusion coefficient and is the fundamental source of uncertainty and risk in the model. A higher value of
\mathcal{V}
implies greater dispersion of possible price outcomes, which broadens the distribution curve and increases the probability of extreme price movements. The variance grows linearly with time, which is a characteristic of Brownian motion that is also present in our model.

The result found allows a direct bridge with the standard model of finance, where the log-price also follows a Generalized Brownian Motion,
dx_{t}=vdt+\sigma dX_{t}
, whose solution is
N(vt,\sigma^{2}t)
. By comparing the two solutions, we can establish a direct correspondence between the parameters:

i.

Mean:
\mathcal{D}t=vt\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}=v
;

ii.

Variance:
2\mathcal{V}t=\sigma^{2}t\Rightarrow\sigma^{2}=2\mathcal{V}
.

The most important relationship here is
\sigma=\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}}
. This means that volatility
\sigma
, one of the most crucial parameters in finance, is directly linked to our diffusion coefficient
A
. Our model therefore offers both a physical and a microeconomic interpretation for the origin of market volatility.

4
Simulation and Empirical Application for Model Validation with Market Data

A simulation exercise was conducted to validate the robustness and applicability of the derived advection–diffusion model, and the results were compared against historical data from a real traded asset. This procedure assesses whether, once calibrated with parameters extracted from market data, the model can generate price trajectories that are statistically consistent with observed market dynamics.

The asset selected for analysis was the preferred stock of Petrobras (PETR4.SA), one of the most liquid securities traded on the Brazilian stock exchange, B3. Adjusted closing prices were collected for the period from January 1, 2020, to August 27, 2025, using the Yahoo Finance platform. Based on these prices, daily log-returns were computed as
r_{t}=\ln(S_{t}/S_{t-1})
, which serve as the empirical counterpart to the infinitesimal increments
dx
of our continuous-time process.

The model parameters,
\mathcal{D}
(drift) and
\mathcal{V}
(diffusion), were estimated directly from the log-return series. Assuming a time step
\Delta t=1
for daily data, the parameters were obtained as follows:

•

The drift parameter
\mathcal{D}
was set equal to the sample mean
\bar{x}
of the log-returns, aligning the expected trend of the model with the historical daily average return of the asset;

•

The diffusion parameter
\mathcal{V}
was computed as half the sample variance of the log-returns,
\mathcal{V}=s^{2}/2
.

These are the maximum likelihood estimators of normal distribution. With
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{D}
estimated, a synthetic price trajectory was simulated starting from an initial price of 100. For each trading day, a random increment was drawn from a normal distribution with mean
\mathcal{D}\Delta t
and variance
2\mathcal{V}\Delta t
. The log price was updated additively and then exponentiated to recover the price level. To enable a direct visual comparison, the historical price series was normalised to start at the same initial value of 100.

\mathcal{D}

(\alpha+\beta)

Both trajectories exhibit an overall upward trend throughout the analysed period. Although the simulated trajectory diverged at certain points, reaching higher peaks, its direction and growth magnitude remained consistent with the historical trend. This confirms that the parameter

The main difference between the model and the data concerns the shape of the distribution. The histogram of the actual data shows a much steeper and narrower peak around the mean than the theoretical normal curve, indicating leptokurtosis. This suggests that a larger proportion of daily returns are actually concentrated at values close to zero than the Gaussian model predicts. The direct consequence of this is the presence of ’heavier tails’. While not dramatically apparent in this graph, the greater concentration of data at the centre implies that extreme events (large daily losses or gains) are more likely in reality than the normal model suggests. 5
Discussion

Geometric Brownian motion is a fundamental postulate used to model asset price dynamics in quantitative finance. However, this classical model describes market behaviour in terms of macroscopic parameters (drift and volatility) without providing a clear microeconomic basis for them. This study addresses this issue by proposing and solving a model for price dynamics.

This research primarily demonstrates that the classical advection–diffusion equation can be rigorously derived from a discrete three-state random walk that represents an investor’s fundamental decisions: to buy (with probability
\beta
), to sell (with probability
\alpha
), or to refrain from trading (with probability
\gamma
). The latter - investor inertia - is a realistic market behaviour that is often left unformalised in standard models. The derivation reveals the distinct roles played by each parameter in shaping market dynamics.

The resulting PDE was shown to have a diffusion coefficient that is directly proportional to either
(\alpha+\beta)
or
(1-\gamma)
. This result provides a clear and intuitive financial interpretation of volatility, showing that it arises as a direct consequence of total trading activity. Markets with high inertia (large
\gamma
) exhibit low activity and therefore low volatility. Conversely, markets with low inertia (small
\gamma
) are characterised by high activity and high volatility.

The advection coefficient (drift) was found to be proportional to the difference (
\beta-\alpha
). This shows that market trends are not an abstract rate of return, but rather the overall result of the imbalance between buying and selling forces among active market participants. A market with net buying sentiment (
\beta>\alpha
) will exhibit positive drift, while a market with net selling sentiment (
\alpha>\beta
) will exhibit negative drift.

The model was validated by applying it to historical data from asset PETR4.SA. This showed that its parameters could be estimated from real-time series, yielding stochastic trajectories that were both plausible and consistent with observed market behaviour.

Although the simplest solution to the PDE yields a normal distribution for log-returns, which is a useful first-order approximation, it shares the well-known limitation of the Black–Scholes model in that it fails to capture extreme events. A more detailed investigation employing methodologies that are sensitive to higher-order effects (see, for example,
(
Bevilacqua et al. 2011
)
) could explore how the inertia parameter
\gamma
may introduce fourth-order differential terms. This could potentially offer an endogenous explanation for the heavy tails (leptokurtosis) observed in empirical log-return distributions.
