Chain mediation model of consultation empathy, resilience and resignation coping on depression: a cross-sectional study among patients with COVID-19 in China
Authors: Lujie Wang, Shuling Huang, Zhiyi Feng, Yuhui Lin, Yuan Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the mediating role of resilience and resignation coping in the relationship between consultation empathy and depression in patients with COVID-19.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingParticipants were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, Guangdong province.ParticipantsA total of 215 patients were recruited for this study.Outcome measuresA total of 215 patients completed the Consultation and Relational Empathy Measure, Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. PROCESS 4.1 model 6 was used to analyse the moderated mediating effects.ResultsConsultation empathy had a positive correlation with resilience (r=0.34, p<0.001), and a negative correlation with resignation (r=−0.288, p<0.001) and depression (r=−0.379, p<0.001). Resilience had a negative correlation with resignation (r=−0.463, p<0.001) and depression (r=−0.605, p<0.001). Resignation had a positive correlation (r=0.547, p<0.001) with depression. In the moderated mediating model, consultation empathy had significant indirect predictive effects on depression through resilience (95% CI −0.093 to –0.030) or resignation (95% CI −0.043 to –0.005). Consultation empathy had significant indirect predictive effects on depression through both resilience and resignation (95% CI −0.030 to –0.008).ConclusionsConsultation empathy not only predicted depression directly, but also indirectly predicted depression through the chain mediating effects of resilience and resignation coping.

Introduction

Since the emergence of the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2 in 2019, designated as COVID-19, it has posed a serious threat to public health around the world. The WHO estimates that as of 10 August 2023, there were more than 769 million confirmed illnesses and more than 6.9 million documented deaths globally.1 COVID-19 can be divided into asymptomatic infection, mild disease, normal type and severe type.2 There is an increasing consensus that this pandemic can significantly burden the psychological health of affected patients.3 4 The numerous asymptomatic and mild patients, with hidden physical symptoms, are the main transmission route. They generally do not require special physical treatment, but isolation measures and various uncertainties caused by symptom concealment bring psychological pressure to these patients. It is quite important to pay attention to their psychological state.5 Depression is a common mental disorder observed in patients with COVID-19.5

It is well-established that persistently low mood and anhedonia are characteristics of depression, although cognitive impairment is often observed.6 Meta-analyses have reported prevalence rates of 29.3%–46.2% for depression among patients with COVID-19,7 with no significant gender difference.8 About 12.7% of patients continued to be depressed 1 year after discharge,9 while the 12-month prevalence of major depressive disorder is approximately 6% overall,10 which persistently affected their quality of life.11 Overwhelming evidence substantiates that depression is associated with immune disorders,12 13 thus may cause adverse effects on outcomes and even cause secondary COVID-19 infection. Therefore, it is important to investigate the characteristics and mechanisms underlying depression in patients with COVID-19.

Empathy is the capacity to share and understand the emotions of other people.14 It is considered an essential element of all therapeutic relationships and a crucial factor in the definition of quality of care.15 16 In the clinical context, empathy warrants the medical staff to understand the subject’s feelings and act (after confirming with the patient that this understanding is correct).17 It has been established that patient perception of empathy of the medical staff (consultation empathy) positively affects COVID-19 patients’ psychological status by promoting their self-confidence and reducing their stress.18 Interestingly, behavioural studies have demonstrated that empathic listening can significantly relieve patients’ depressive emotions.19 In the present study, we hypothesised that empathy-based consultation could improve COVID-19 patients’ depression.

Resilience refers to positive adaptation to adversities,20 21 which includes leveraging resilience resources to overcome difficulties and transforming the negative impacts of serious threats to healthy and developmental outcomes.21 A previous study documented resilience could protect against depression in mildly symptomatic COVID-19 patients.22 Resilience is the outcome of the interplay between internal and environmental factors.23 Given that resilience is influenced by interactive environments, it is crucial to explore the impact of healthcare professionals, who are the most frequent and direct sources of interaction for hospitalised COVID-19 patients. Therefore, investigating the influence of empathetic consultation on the resilience of patients with COVID-19 becomes vital. However, the potential impact of empathy-based consultation on the resilience of patients with COVID-19 has not been investigated yet, and further research is needed to examine this relationship.

The coping mechanism is a series of cognitive or behavioural strategies developed to counter psychological stress.24 During sickness, individuals generally exhibit three coping mechanisms: confrontation, avoidance and resignation.25 An increasing body of evidence suggests that resignation is positively associated with depression.26–28 Recent studies have emphasised the role of positive interpersonal support in shaping constructive coping styles.29 30 However, despite the existing research suggesting that positive interpersonal interactions can reduce resignation, there is limited evidence on the potential impact of empathy-based consultation in reducing resignation.

Therefore, this study explored the relationships between consultation empathy, resilience, resignation and depression in patients with COVID-19. This study aimed to identify the mediating effects of resilience and resignation in the relationship between consultation empathy and depression in patients with COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients were included based on the following criteria: Chinese; age ≥18 years; patients in isolation in a mobile cabin hospital managed by the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University from 1 December to 10 December 2022; COVID-19 infection defined by a positive RT-PCR essay according to the diagnostic criteria of the Novel Coronavirus Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment Programme (eighth edition)2; diagnosis of COVID-19-related pneumonia at discharge; and absence of psychiatric or highly disabling pathologies.

Questionnaires were distributed through the online platform Wenjuanxing. A total of 268 questionnaires were distributed and 217 were collected, of which 215 were valid questionnaires, for a valid questionnaire rate of 99.2%. Among the participants, 66% were male, with an average age of 34.71±9.90. The diagnosis of asymptomatic, mild and normal types was 50.7%, 33.5% and 15.8%, respectively. The average hospitalisation length was 11.04±7.12.

Measures

Consultation and Relational Empathy Measure

Patient perception of relational empathy and communication in the consultation was assessed by the Consultation and Relational Empathy (CARE) Measure, developed by Mercer in 2004,31 consisting of 10 items rated on a Likert-type 5-point scale ranging from 1 ‘poor’ to 5 ‘excellent’; the higher the total score, the higher the staff’s empathy to patients. The Cronbach’s α value of CARE is 0.920.31 In the present study, Cronbach’s α value was 0.980, consistent with a previous study by Fung et al who adapted CARE into a Chinese version, yielding a Cronbach’s α value of 0.962.32

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale

Patient resilience was assessed by the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), which is composed of 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 ‘not true at all’ to 4 ‘true nearly all of the time’, with higher scores reﬂecting greater resilience.33 This scale is based on ﬁve factors: personal competence, high standards and tenacity; trust in one’s instincts, tolerance of negative aﬀect and strengthening eﬀects of stress; positive acceptance of change and secure relationships; control; and spiritual inﬂuences.34 Growing evidence suggests that the Chinese version had good reliability and validity.35 36 Cronbach’s α for the full scale was 0.89,33 and in this study was 0.949.

Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire

This study used the Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ) to investigate how patients cope with COVID-19 infection. Feifel et al conceived the MCMQ to assess how patients use the three cognitive–behavioural coping strategies (Confrontation, Avoidance and Resignation) in dealing with their illness.37 The Chinese version of MCMQ is composed of 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘all the time’ or ‘very little’ to ‘very much’, which has good reliability and validity.38 The resignation subscale includes five items, which reflect the level of the participants’ resignation. The Cronbach’s α value of Chinese version is 0.760,38 and in this study, the value was 0.766.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale

Levels of depression were assessed by the depression subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-D).39 The HADS-D includes seven items rated on a 4-point Likert scale and ranges from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The HADS-D has been translated into Chinese and validated in the Chinese population.40 The Cronbach’s α value of the Chinese version of the scale is 0.77,40 and in this study was 0.803.

Data collection and data analyses

Data collection and ethical consideration

The present study used the online platform Wenjuanxing for data collection since conducting face-to-face surveys during isolation is challenging. Wenjuanxing is widely acknowledged as a relatively secure platform with minimal risk of data breach and leakage by a third party. Data collection was conducted in a mobile cabin hospital managed by the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University from 1 December to 10 December 2022. We sent the questionnaire link to patients who met the inclusion criteria, and they could choose to fill out the questionnaire or not.

Statistical analyses

SPSS V.27.0 was used to analyse the data. The normality distribution of the measures was assessed before analysis. CARE, CD-RISC and the HADS-D yielded skewness and kurtosis values between −2 and +2.41 Since the kurtosis of MCMQ was greater than 2, square root transformation was applied before subsequent analysis. Continuous variables were described as means and SD, and categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Harman’s single-factor test was used to detect all factor collinearity issues. The difference was assessed by an independent t-test or analysis of variance test. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between each variable. The chain mediation effect was analysed using Serial mediation Hayes Process 4.1 model 6 with SPSS.42 The benefit of using this program is its capability to analyse both the direct and indirect effects concurrently through either ordinary least squares method or maximum likelihood estimation. The bootstrapping method was used to extract 50 000 samples repeatedly, and a 95% CI was calculated; if 95% CI did not include ‘0’, the mediating effect was statistically signiﬁcant.

Patient and public involvement

The development of the research question and outcome measures in this study was informed by patients’ priorities, experiences and preferences through a thorough literature review and consultation with healthcare professionals. We also conducted pilot interviews to gain insights into the psychological aspects that are most relevant to hospitalised patients with COVID-19.

To involve patients in the design of this study, we communicated with them online beforehand. Patients provided valuable input regarding the selection of appropriate psychological questionnaires and the inclusion of relevant measures that accurately reflect their experiences and priorities. In terms of recruitment and conduct of the study, patients were given the freedom to decide whether they wanted to participate. We assured them that their decision would not affect their medical care or treatment in any way. This approach ensured that only willing participants were included and respected their autonomy. The results of the study will be disseminated to the study participants through a comprehensive report. We will provide a summary of the findings along with recommendations for healthcare professionals to address the psychological needs of patients with COVID-19. As this study did not involve a randomised controlled trial or any specific intervention, the burden of the intervention was not assessed by patients themselves. However, we made efforts to minimise any potential burden or discomfort by keeping the online questionnaire concise and ensuring the voluntary nature of participation. Overall, patient involvement was integral in shaping the research question, outcome measures and study design, ensuring that the study addressed their priorities and experiences while respecting their autonomy and minimising any potential burden.

Results

Common method bias test

Using Harman’s single-factor test for common method deviation, seven factors with eigenvalues >1 were identified. The interpretation rate of the ﬁrst factor was 36.07%, which was <40% of the reference value.43 Therefore, there was no common method deviation in the data of this study.

Differences in research variables according to general characteristics

The mean age of the study subjects was 34.71±9.90 (19–59 years old), with male predominance (66%, 142/215) and a mean hospitalisation duration of 11.04±7.12 (2–27 days). According to the Novel Coronavirus Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment Programme (eighth edition),2 patients were classified as asymptomatic type (n=109), mild type (n=72) and common type (n=34). More details on the included study participants are provided in online supplemental table 1.

The differences in consultation and relational empathy, patient resilience, resignation and depression according to general characteristics were analysed (table 1). The patients’ resilience includes gender (t=2.887, p<0.01) and age (F=3.561, p<0.05). The resignation includes gender (t=−2.701, p<0.01), age (F=2.687, p<0.05) and education status (F=23.318, p<0.05). Empathy during the consultation and depression of patients did not differ significantly (p>0.05). Therefore, gender, age and educational status were controlled during the mediation analysis. The numerical value of ‘t’ and ‘F’ represents the significance test value for regression parameters, and when ‘p’ is less than 0.05, it indicates a statistical difference.

The mean CARE score was 41.40 points (±8.83), 70.56 points (±18.22) for resilience, 2.57 points (±0.43) for resignation (after square root conversion) and 3.96 points (±3.60) for depression. The skewness and kurtosis values of the study variables ranged from −2 to +2, satisfying the assumption for normal distribution (table 1).

As for the correlation between the study variables, CARE was positively correlated with resilience (r=0.340, p<0.001) and negatively with resignation (r=−0.288, p<0.001) and depression (r=−0.379, p<0.001). Resilience had a negative correlation with resignation (r=−0.463, p<0.001) and depression (r=−0.605, p<0.001). Finally, resignation had a positive correlation with depression (r=0.547, p<0.001) (table 1).

Significance and effect analysis of the mediation effect of resilience and resignation

Bootstrap analysis was used to test the significance of the mediating effect of the model. After adjusting for potential covariates (gender, age, education status), resilience and resignation were the mediator variables of the influence of the consultation and relational empathy on depression of the patients infected with COVID-19, and the total effect size (95% CI) of the mediating effect was −0.097 (-0.141 to –0.059), accounting for 64.67% of the total effect. Indeed, our findings suggest that CARE can directly affect the depression of patients and indirectly affect depression through resilience and resignation (figure 1). The mediating effect of resilience and resignation can be achieved through the following three paths (table 2): path 1 (−0.058): CARE→resilience→depression; path 2 (−0.022): CARE→resignation→depression; path 3 (−0.017): CARE→resilience→resignation→depression, which accounted for 38.67%, 14.67% and 11.33% of the total effect, respectively.

Discussion

Here, we sought to assess the relationships among empathy-based consultation, resilience, resignation and depression in patients with COVID-19. In addition, we assessed the mediation effects of resilience and resignation in the process of consultation empathy affecting depression. Although the consultation empathy negatively correlated with depression, its direct effects were not signiﬁcant when resilience or resignation was introduced.

Moreover, we substantiated that empathy-based consultation positively affected resilience and resilience was a mediating pathway that negatively affected depression. It is widely thought that a supportive environment can contribute to the development of resilience. In this respect, Sutherland et al demonstrated that family, peer and social support could stimulate the development of resilience in adolescence and early adulthood.44 An integrative review by Llistosella et al revealed that support from the environment, individuals’ perceived support and self-efficacy were protective factors for resilience.45 The perception of clinician–patient empathy provides a supportive environment for patients with COVID-19. On the one hand, through empathy, clinicians provided companionship and understanding besides treatment.46 On the other hand, clinical empathy made patients feel respected, enhancing their self-efficacy.47 Studies in different populations have substantiated that self-efficacy could positively affect resilience in different populations.48 49

We found that resilience was a mediator that negatively affected depression in patients with COVID-19, consistent with a previous study that confirmed resilience as a protective factor for depression in Chinese patients with mild COVID-19 symptoms.22 There is an increasing consensus that resilience mitigates the development of depression.50 51 Based on the theory of resilience, different strategies have been postulated to reduce depression, including providing social support, a rearing environment, cognitive interventions and neurobiological interventions.52 53 Our study further validated that consultation empathy could alleviate depression by improving resilience in isolated patients during COVID-19.

This study also confirmed that consultation empathy negatively affected resignation, and resignation was a mediating pathway that positively affected depression. As a negative medical coping mode, resignation reflects a sense of powerlessness and hopelessness toward diseases.37 A recent study substantiated that resignation was positively associated with stigma.54 On the contrary, perceived social support contributes to the formation of positive coping styles and develops post-traumatic growth.55 Therefore, providing good interpersonal support and consultation empathy helps to decrease the use of the medical coping style of resignation.

Our findings substantiated that resignation positively affected depression in patients with COVID-19, consistent with a previous study.26 Resignation is a passive avoidant coping style. Growing evidence suggests that unlike approach coping, avoidant coping is related to psychiatric diseases, such as major depression, anxiety, panic attacks and post-traumatic stress disorder.56–58 Some researchers divided avoidance into active avoidant coping (ie, diversion) and passive avoidant coping (ie, resignation and withdrawal).59 60 Researchers further confirmed that perceived stress could be predicted by resignation rather than diversion avoidance coping.61 It is well-established that perceived stress is a factor that influences depression based on multiple studies conducted in the context of COVID-19.62–64 Another research provided direct evidence that resignation was positively correlated with depression and could positively affect depression.65 Our results may provide the foothold for future studies on therapies that reduce avoidance to relieve depression. In recent years, many types of psychotherapies have been reported to reduce avoidance thoughts and reactions, mainly cognitive therapies. Nevertheless, our findings corroborated that consultation empathy was another practical way to diminish avoidance coping, thus alleviating depression in patients with COVID-19.

This study showed that resilience and resignation had a chain mediating effect between consultation empathy and depression. In this respect, good consultation empathy enhanced COVID-19 patients’ resilience, and patients with high resilience were less likely to resign, reducing depressive symptoms. According to the cognitive behavioural theory (a therapy widely proven to treat depression), core beliefs influence automatic thinking, affecting behaviour.66 Resilience refers to the capacity to adapt positively to adversity20 21 and the core belief in one’s own ability to overcome difficulties. Resignation, as one of the coping modes, includes a series of cognitive and behavioural strategies when individuals face illness.24 25 This result further validated the cognitive behavioural theory and confirmed the value of consultation empathy for altering the cognition and behaviour of patients with COVID-19, which in turn can help treat depression.

Our results are helpful to remind medical staff to strengthen empathy for patients with COVID-19, and try to change their resignation attitude to disease to protect their mental health. At the same time, it can remind medical decision-makers to develop medical policies that place greater emphasis on doctor–patient empathy, and provide training on empathy skills for relevant medical staff.

Limitations

Limitations found in the present study should be acknowledged. First, the convenience sampling technique may have led to selection bias, emphasising the importance of conducting randomised controlled trials in future studies. Besides, a cross-sectional study design cannot prove causality between predictor and outcome variables. In the future, cohort studies or Mendelian studies are needed to clarify causal relationships. Moreover, the study sample consisted of a relatively small number of volunteers. Indeed, this part of the population may have felt good consultation empathy, causing bias in the findings. More large sample studies using randomised controls are warranted. Finally, data in the present study were collected from a single centre. Indeed, healthcare workers in the same hospital may have similar doctor–patient communication and empathic skills. And considering the high homogeneity of single centre patients (in this study, most of them were asymptomatic or mild patients, and faced the same medical staff, mainly male and young people). Accordingly, a multicentre study is essential to increase the robustness of our findings, since multicentre studies can obtain more sample sizes in a short period of time and reduce the homogeneity of participants.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this is the first study to assess the relationship between empathy-based consultation, resilience, resignation coping and depression. Our findings indicated that resilience and resignation had a chain mediating effect between consultation and depression. Therefore, the medical institution should value the cultivation of empathic ability in healthcare workers. Furthermore, healthcare workers could encourage patients to change their coping styles using empathy, disease education and other psychological supports.
