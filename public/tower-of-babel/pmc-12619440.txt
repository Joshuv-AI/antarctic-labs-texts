Factors associated with ulcerative colitis with non-anemic iron deficiency
Authors: Shogo Kitahata, Mai Fukumoto, Kana Matsuoka, Takuya Matsuda, Taisei Murakami, Kei Onishi, Hirofumi Izumoto, Kozue Kanemitsu, Tomoe Kawamura, Taira Kuroda, Junko Matsuoka, Fujimasa Tada, Hideki Miyata, Atsushi Hiraoka, Kazuhiro Tange, Yasunori Yamamoto, Eiji Takeshita, Yoshiou Ikeda, Shinya Furukawa, Eiji Tsubouchi, Tomoyuki Ninomiya, Yoichi Hiasa

Background

The most common cause of anemia in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) is iron deficiency (ID) resulting from intestinal disease [1]. Anemia is a late symptom of ID, and recent studies have demonstrated a high prevalence of non-anemic ID among patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [2, 3]. Non-anemic ID is characterized by normal hemoglobin (Hb) levels and a reduction in the body’s total iron content. Serum ferritin levels reflect the iron stores [4]. Iron deficiency can be categorized into absolute and functional IDs. In UC, anemia of chronic disease (ACD), which is the cause of functional ID, usually overlaps with ID anemia (IDA), making the diagnosis of ID challenging [5–8]. Inflammation causes high serum ferritin levels despite empty iron stores [8]. Therefore, the diagnostic criteria for ID in patients with IBD depend on the degree of inflammation. According to the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organization (ECCO) recommendations, serum ferritin levels < 30 ng/mL in patients with IBD in remission (no clinical, endoscopic, or biochemical evidence of active disease) and < 100 ng/mL in those with active IBD are indicators of ID [8]. The ECCO guidelines advocate regular screening for Hb and iron store levels [8]. Non-anemic ID may be underestimated and untreated despite causing symptoms, such as fatigue, which can negatively impact the quality of life [2, 9–11]. Therefore, elucidating the characteristics of patients with UC with non-anemic ID is important. Limited and inconsistent reports exist regarding patients with UC with non-anemic ID. Additionally, among-study differences exist in the demographic, geographic, and socioeconomic characteristics, which could restrict the generalizability of findings regarding ID in patients with UC [8]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine factors associated with non-anemic ID in Japanese patients with UC.

Methods

Participants

Blood tests, including ferritin tests, were performed with colonoscopy in 152 patients with UC who visited the Ehime Prefectural Central Hospital (Ehime, Japan) between April 2021 and March 2023. The maximum interval between blood tests and colonoscopy was 1 month; however, only patients who were clinically stable during this period were included if they were not tested on the same day. UC was diagnosed based on the established clinical, radiographic, endoscopic, and histopathological criteria [12]. The exclusion criteria included a history of ileostomy or colostomy; the use of oral or intravenous iron supplements within 3 months; and comorbid hematological disorders, malignancies, or anemia. Finally, we included 112 patients in this analysis (Fig. 1).

Patient flow chart of the study

Evaluation of UC disease activity

Clinical remission (CR) was indicated by a partial Mayo score ≤ 1. A gastroenterologist evaluated endoscopic activity using the Mayo Endoscopic Subscore (MES) [13]. Mucosal healing (MH) was defined as MES ≤ 1. Based on the medical history and endoscopic findings upon inclusion, patients with UC were classified as E1 (proctitis), E2 (left-sided colitis), or E3 (pancolitis) based on the Montreal Classification according to the extent of the lesions [14].

Definition of anemia and iron deficiency

Anemia was defined as Hb levels < 13.0 and < 12.0 g/dL in males and females, respectively [8]. Iron deficiency was defined as ferritin levels < 100 ng/mL in patients with disease activity (defined as MES ≥ 2) and < 30 ng/mL in those without disease activity [8].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Easy-R (EZR) version 1.37 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Fisher’s exact and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare nominal and continuous variables, respectively. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to estimate the crude odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the association of non-anemic ID with sex, MH, CR, use of prednisolone, C-reactive protein (CRP) level, and transferrin saturation (TSAT) level. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify the independent predictors of non-anemic ID, with the inclusion of all significantly (p < 0.05) associated variables from univariate analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study protocol was developed in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent ethical guidelines and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ehime Central Hospital (Approval No. 03–57). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Results

To identify factors associated with non-anemic ID, patients were classified as having ID (n = 38) and normal iron status (n = 74). The prevalence of non-anemic ID was 33.9%. Table 1 summarizes the patients’ clinical characteristics. No significant between-group differences were found in age. The prevalence of non-anemic ID was significantly higher in females than in males (p = 0.001). A significantly higher prevalence of non-anemic ID was found in the non-CR (p < 0.001), non-MH (p < 0.001), high-CRP (p = 0.017), and low-TSAT (p < 0.001) groups than in the corresponding groups. The prevalence of non-anemic ID in prednisolone users was significantly higher than in non-prednisolone users (p = 0.037). Multivariate analysis, which included age, sex, CR, MH, use of prednisolone, CRP level, and TSAT level as variables, revealed that female sex (adjusted OR: 15.40, 95% CI: 2.66–89.00) and non-MH status (adjusted OR: 18.10, 95% CI: 2.97–110.00) were independently associated with non-anemic ID (Table 2). After adjusting for covariates, non-CR, the use of prednisolone, CRP level, and TSAT level did not significantly correlate with non-anemic ID (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, female sex and non-MH status were independently and significantly associated with the prevalence of non-anemic ID among patients with UC.

Here, the prevalence of ID among patients without anemia was 33.9%. Previous studies have reported a prevalence of ID ranging from 26 to 62% [15–19]. The inconsistency in the reported prevalence of ID could be attributed to among-study differences in the sample size, age, sex, and ID definition.

Some studies have reported an association between sex and anemia or ID among patients with IBD [15, 20]. A Greek retrospective study on 687 patients with IBD reported that sex was significantly associated with anemia (p < 0.001) [20]. Moreover, a Dutch cross-sectional study on 926 patients with UC, which included those with anemia, reported that the prevalence of ID was 29.7% (73/434) and 55.2% (160/492) in males and females, respectively [15]. However, these studies did not report the prevalence of ID according to sex among patients with UC without anemia. Healthy adults lose approximately 1–2 mg of iron daily from the intestinal mucosa, urinary tract, skin, and biliary tract [21]. During menstruation, this loss increases to approximately 2 mg daily [21]. Even in patients with UC without anemia, the high prevalence of ID among females may be attributed to menstrual bleeding in addition to IBD-induced bleeding.

Evidence regarding the association between disease activity and ID or anemia exists [15, 17]. A Dutch IBD study, which included patients with anemia, found that ID was significantly more prevalent among patients with high CRP levels (p < 0.001) [15]. Another French cross-sectional study on 1036 participants with IBD reported that the prevalence of ID (including patients with anemia) was higher among patients with disease activity than among those without [17]. In the present study, multivariate analysis revealed that CR and CRP levels were unassociated with non-anemic ID, whereas MH was associated with non-anemic ID. The inconsistency in the results of this study and previous reports may be attributed to the definitions of disease activity and ID based on endoscopic findings according to the ECCO recommendations, exclusion of patients with anemia, differences in CRP given the median, or the possibility that CRP is not an accurate marker of IBD activity. Most previous studies on ID among patients with IBD established the diagnostic criteria for ID based on biochemical evidence, including CRP levels [15, 17]. However, accurate assessment of disease activity is routinely achieved through endoscopic evaluation [22] since patients with IBD may not exhibit clinical symptoms or elevated levels of biochemical markers, including CRP, regardless of the presence of inflammation [23]. Our study has several strengths. First, the definition of ID was based on an accurate assessment of disease activity using endoscopic findings according to the ECCO recommendations. Second, we could elucidate the characteristics of patients with UC without non-anemic ID after excluding patients with anemia.

This study has some limitations. First, we did not include a control group and only included patients. Second, ferritin—which can be elevated in the setting of inflammation—was the only marker of ID used. However, defining ID using ferritin cutoff values according to endoscopic disease activity following the ECCO guidelines minimized this effect. Third, IDA may coexist with ACD. However, this effect was minimized in the present study since we excluded patients with anemia. Fourth, this was a single-center study with a small sample size and was not representative of Japanese patients with UC. However, the median age, sex ratio, and frequency of drug use were similar to those in previous Japanese national surveys on UC [24]. Finally, we did not consider data regarding soluble transferrin receptor, which have been reportedly associated with iron dynamics.

Conclusions

Female sex and non-MH status were associated with the prevalence of non-anemic ID among patients with UC. Therefore, monitoring iron dynamics among patients with UC who have these characteristics is recommended even in the absence of anemia.
