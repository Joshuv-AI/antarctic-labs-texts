Nursing faculty perceptions of soft skills teaching in undergraduate nursing education: a qualitative study
Authors: Sheron Penha Serrano, Edison Luiz Devos Barlem, Emanuelli Mancio Ferreira da Luz, Kênia Lara Silva, Vitória Maria dos Santos Mendes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoft skills correspond to intrapersonal and interpersonal abilities related to how individuals interact, make decisions, and manage their activities. In the context of undergraduate nursing education, their development is fundamental to the preparation of professionals capable of acting in an ethical, critical, and relational manner, making it relevant to understand how these competencies are incorporated into the teaching and learning process. In this context, the objective of this study is to understand how faculty members in undergraduate nursing programs incorporate soft skills into their pedagogical approaches and practices, identifying the competencies considered essential and the challenges to their implementation.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted with 26 nursing faculty members from four federal public universities in southern Brazil. Data were collected between June and September 2025 through semi-structured interviews, following the criteria of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist. The interviews were processed using IRaMuTeQ software and analyzed in light of Discursive Textual Analysis.ResultsThree analytical categories emerged: faculty understanding of soft skills in nursing education; pedagogical approaches and strategies for the development of these competencies; and perceived difficulties in their promotion within teaching. The faculty members recognize the relevance of soft skills and report the use of active methodologies and reflective strategies for their development. However, most had not received specific training, and the teaching of these competencies occurs predominantly in an implicit manner.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that, although soft skills are widely valued in nursing education, their promotion still lacks pedagogical systematization and institutional support. Challenges such as the subjectivity of these competencies, the prioritization of technical skills by students, and distractions associated with the use of technologies limit their intentional development. These results contribute to the international literature in nursing education by highlighting the need for structured institutional strategies for faculty development and for the explicit integration of soft skills into nursing curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09339-9.

Background

In the 1960s, the term soft skills was introduced by the United States Army to refer to non-technical skills considered essential for the full performance of soldiers. Since then, this terminology has been used to describe aspects of human behavior related to emotional, social, and cognitive dimensions, such as empathy, creativity, and resilience [1].

Soft skills comprise a set of abilities related to how individuals interact with one another and how they face and manage their tasks. According to the literature, these competencies can be organized into two broad groups: intrapersonal skills, associated with self-management and self-regulation, and interpersonal skills, related to communication, teamwork, and social relationships [2, 3]. They are therefore multidimensional constructs that go beyond technical domains, articulating different competencies essential to professional practice [2].

In the context of higher education, the development of soft skills has gained increasing relevance, especially in light of the demands of an increasingly complex and dynamic labor market. Thus, it becomes necessary to adopt pedagogical strategies that encompass not only the mastery of hard skills but also the development of transversal competencies capable of sustaining critical, ethical, and relational professional practice [3–5].

In the health field, and particularly in nursing, the literature indicates that incorporating learning strategies aimed at the development of interpersonal skills contributes to the preparation of professionals better equipped to deal with complex situations in the workplace, positively impacting the quality of care provided [3, 6]. Recognizing soft skills as competencies that can be taught and enhanced reinforces the understanding that their development can be promoted through intentionally structured educational programs.

From this perspective, soft skills are understood not as innate abilities, but as competencies constructed throughout social and professional experiences. This understanding supports both the possibility and the need for their teaching within nursing education, particularly through pedagogical practices that value interaction, dialogue, and experiential learning.

However, the literature also highlights challenges to the effective implementation of soft skills teaching. Among these are the predominance of traditional pedagogical models, which limit the development of critical thinking [7], and insufficient faculty training to address these competencies in a systematic manner. Considering that many faculty members received their own education grounded in traditional frameworks, the adoption of pedagogical approaches aimed at developing soft skills requires the acquisition of new educational competencies, with a focus on interaction, dialogue, and the stimulation of creativity [7, 8].

Despite the recognition of the importance of soft skills in nursing education, gaps remain regarding how these competencies are incorporated into faculty pedagogical practices, as well as the challenges faced in their effective promotion in teaching. In this context, the objective of this study is to understand how faculty members in undergraduate nursing programs incorporate soft skills into their pedagogical approaches and practices, identifying the competencies considered essential and the challenges to their implementation.

Methods

Study design

This was a qualitative, exploratory, and descriptive study whose objective was to understand how faculty members in undergraduate nursing programs incorporate soft skills into their pedagogical approaches and practices, identifying the competencies considered essential and the challenges to their implementation. The research was conducted through semi-structured interviews, with data processing performed using the software Interface de R pour les Analyses Multidimensionnelles de Textes et de Questionnaires (IRaMuTeQ) and analysis grounded in Discursive Textual Analysis (DTA).

The study was carried out in five undergraduate nursing programs at four federal public universities located in the state of Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, identified as institutions “A,” “B,” “C,” and “D.”

Participants and inclusion criteria

Participant selection was carried out using the snowball sampling technique, characterized as a non-probabilistic sample based on referral networks [9]. At each university, initial contact was made with the coordinator of the undergraduate nursing program, who indicated faculty members potentially eligible according to the study topic and inclusion criteria.

After the interviews were conducted, participants were invited to indicate other faculty members who met the same criteria. In cases where there was no response from the program coordination, faculty members were indicated by professors from other participating institutions, based on previously established professional connections.

Permanent faculty members with at least one year of experience in higher education who taught specific courses within the undergraduate nursing programs of the participating universities were included. Faculty members who worked exclusively in graduate programs, substitute faculty, those on leave during the data collection period, and faculty members affiliated only with basic courses within the program were excluded.

The four universities included in the study comprised five undergraduate nursing programs and 204 faculty members, according to information available on their institutional websites. A total of 26 nursing faculty members participated in the study, five from institution “A” and seven from each of institutions “B,” “C,” and “D.” At institution “B,” four participants were affiliated with one campus and three with another.

The use of snowball sampling proved appropriate to the objective of the study, as it enabled access to faculty members with relevant and heterogeneous experiences regarding the investigated phenomenon, favoring analytical depth and diversity of perspectives among the participating institutions.

Data collection

In accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [10], data collection was carried out between June and September 2025 through recorded semi-structured interviews guided by an interview guide developed by the research team (Additional file 1). Considering that the study involved universities located in different regions of the state, the interviews were conducted remotely using the digital platforms Zoom and Google Meet.

Prior to the beginning of data collection, a pilot test was conducted with a faculty member from an undergraduate nursing program at a university not included in the study, in order to assess the clarity and relevance of the instrument. Based on this procedure, an additional question was included in the interview guide.

The guiding question was: “Are you aware of any pedagogical approach or strategy that may stimulate the development of intrapersonal and interpersonal skills among students?” In addition, specific questions were included related to the soft skills of communication, teamwork, critical thinking, problem-solving, emotional intelligence, empathy, and leadership.

Invitations to participate were sent individually to the referred faculty members, accompanied by information about the study and the Informed Consent Form. After agreement was obtained, interviews were scheduled according to participants’ availability, lasted an average of one hour, and were conducted until theoretical saturation was reached.

All interviews were recorded, transcribed with the assistance of the online platform TurboScribe, and returned to participants for validation. Of the 26 faculty members interviewed, 25 returned the validated transcripts; among these, one faculty member requested minor adjustments to the text, which were made according to her indications. One participant did not respond after three contact attempts, and the original version of the transcript was therefore maintained.

Data analysis

The data were initially processed using IRaMuTeQ software, applying Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC), a method that organizes textual segments based on lexical similarity, facilitating the identification of discursive patterns within the analyzed corpus [11, 12].

Subsequently, data analysis was conducted in light of Discursive Textual Analysis (DTA), a qualitative method aimed at constructing new understandings of the investigated discourses and phenomena. DTA comprises three interrelated analytical focuses: text deconstruction, categorization, and the capture of the emerging new [13, 14].

The combination of lexical processing through IRaMuTeQ and the interpretative analysis proposed by DTA enabled both the systematic organization of the corpus and the in-depth analytical exploration of the meanings produced in the discourses, ensuring methodological rigor and analytical triangulation.

The analysis began with the reading and rereading of the transcripts of the 26 interviews, followed by the identification of units of meaning. The use of DHC contributed to organizing the corpus into classes with similar content, supporting the construction of intermediate categories. Finally, the capture of the emerging new resulted from the recursive articulation among the analytical focuses, allowing for the development of an expanded interpretative understanding of the studied phenomenon.

To preserve participants’ anonymity, faculty members were identified by the acronym “IND” (individual), followed by the interview number and the letter corresponding to their affiliated institution (“A,” “B,” “C,” or “D”).

Data processing using IRaMuTeQ software

Through the Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC) method, it was possible to identify that the overall corpus consisted of 26 texts/interviews, comprising 4,328 text segments (TS), of which 3,988 TS were retained (92.14%). The corpus presented 151,743 word occurrences, including 6,988 distinct forms, of which 2,984 corresponded to hapax, that is, words that appear only once.

Thus, the analyzed corpus was organized by the software into five emerging classes: class 1, composed of 561 TS (13.05%); class 2, with 1,045 TS (24.31%); class 3, with 1,089 TS (25.34%); class 4, with 1,155 TS (26.87%); and class 5, with 448 TS (10.42%), as presented in Fig. 1.

In view of this, based on the reading of the text segments (TS) processed by IRaMuTeQ and derived from the textual corpus, it was possible to identify units of meaning (corpus excerpts with relevant meaning) and to name the classes, which are considered intermediate categories in the DTA process [15]. In addition, the software generated a dendrogram (Fig. 2), which also contributed to naming the intermediate categories, as it highlights the words with the highest frequency within each class.

Therefore, although the DHC generated five classes, only three were considered in the analysis. After reading the text segments of class 1, it was found that its content did not address the objective of the study, as it primarily referred to aspects of clinical practice, without mentioning pedagogical approaches or strategies. Likewise, class 5 was not further explored, as its TS indicated elements already encompassed within the other analyzed classes. Thus, classes (intermediate categories) 2, 3, and 4 were analyzed.

Category 2: Faculty understanding of soft skills in nursing education

According to the dendrogram presented in Fig. 2, the most frequent word was “soft skills,” followed by the words “faculty,” “training,” “skill,” among others. Therefore, this category illustrates faculty members’ perceptions of soft skills, considerations regarding training related to these competencies, the skills investigated in the data collection instrument, as well as soft skills indicated beyond these as essential to nursing education, presented in Subcategories 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

Subcategory 1: Faculty perceptions of soft skills

Participating faculty members describe soft skills as fundamental in nursing education, emphasizing that such competencies underpin professional practice and should receive greater attention from institutions. According to some participants, they are considered even more determinant than technical skills, as illustrated in the excerpts below.

I think soft skills are extremely important, and they should be discussed more within institutions so that they can be more effectively implemented. (IND25-D)

I think soft skills are extremely important, and they should be discussed more within institutions so that they can be more effectively implemented. (IND25-D)

I think they are the main skills we need to have […] technical aspects we can develop without difficulty […] the structured course sequence is there for that […] But without these skills, the soft skills, you cannot fully develop the proposal, the profile of what it means to be a nurse. (IND20-C)

I think they are the main skills we need to have […] technical aspects we can develop without difficulty […] the structured course sequence is there for that […] But without these skills, the soft skills, you cannot fully develop the proposal, the profile of what it means to be a nurse. (IND20-C)

I believe that soft skills are more important than hard skills for professional success. (IND11-C)

I believe that soft skills are more important than hard skills for professional success. (IND11-C)

Subcategory 2: Training on soft skills

Participating nursing faculty members reported the importance of training initiatives on this topic, as they understand that these skills, in addition to being essential, tend to be the most difficult to develop in nursing education. Although none of the participants reported having attended specific training using the terminology “soft skills,” some mentioned having participated in more focused training activities, such as those related to communication.

The more I reflect on it, the more I realize that we need to work on this type of competency and skill within nursing, because it is the most difficult part. (IND16-C)

The more I reflect on it, the more I realize that we need to work on this type of competency and skill within nursing, because it is the most difficult part. (IND16-C)

I have never received any type of training regarding this [soft skills], but I consider them extremely important, because it is impossible to talk about nursing without talking about these skills. (IND07-A)

I have never received any type of training regarding this [soft skills], but I consider them extremely important, because it is impossible to talk about nursing without talking about these skills. (IND07-A)

I have taken training courses focused on communication […] one was offered by the university […] and others I pursued on my own, seeking them out independently; they were not institutionalized. (IND05-B)

Subcategory 3: Soft skills investigated in the instrument

With regard to the soft skills addressed in the data collection instrument, participating faculty members commented on interpersonal skills, highlighting communication and teamwork as fundamental in the teaching–learning process in nursing education. They emphasized that communication influences decision-making, interpersonal relationships, and work organization.

Furthermore, teamwork was also described by participants as fundamental, since nurses do not work in isolation and depend on their relationship with the team to ensure the best possible care for patients.

Communication is the foundation of the entire process […] the way it is conveyed, the content, shapes your decision-making, shapes your interpersonal relationships, shapes the organization of your work […] I would say it is fundamental for education and for students’ development. (IND05-B)

Communication is the foundation of the entire process […] the way it is conveyed, the content, shapes your decision-making, shapes your interpersonal relationships, shapes the organization of your work […] I would say it is fundamental for education and for students’ development. (IND05-B)

Communication is extremely important — what to say, how to say it, to whom to say it, the right moment to speak, and even when not to speak — it is an essential skill in the teaching–learning process. (IND07-A)

Communication is extremely important — what to say, how to say it, to whom to say it, the right moment to speak, and even when not to speak — it is an essential skill in the teaching–learning process. (IND07-A)

Teamwork may be the second most important soft skill in nursing practice, because, after all, nurses do not work alone. If we do not maintain good relationships with the team, which is our support base, we will not be able to provide adequate care. (IND26-D)

Teamwork may be the second most important soft skill in nursing practice, because, after all, nurses do not work alone. If we do not maintain good relationships with the team, which is our support base, we will not be able to provide adequate care. (IND26-D)

Still related to interpersonal skills, the soft skills of leadership and empathy were also highlighted. In participants’ accounts, leadership is emphasized as a competency established in the National Curricular Guidelines and as fundamental to nurses’ performance as team leaders. Empathy, in turn, was described as being associated with the ability to understand others and as a foundation for the development of various soft skills.

Leadership is of utmost importance; it is a competency established in the curricular guidelines, so nurses do need to develop leadership, they are the leaders of the team. (IND11-C)

Leadership is of utmost importance; it is a competency established in the curricular guidelines, so nurses do need to develop leadership, they are the leaders of the team. (IND11-C)

The need for empathy […] is necessarily centered on the possibility of understanding others. And in order to develop all those soft skills, or most of them, you really need to recognize the other person. (IND05-B)

The need for empathy […] is necessarily centered on the possibility of understanding others. And in order to develop all those soft skills, or most of them, you really need to recognize the other person. (IND05-B)

With regard to intrapersonal skills, participating faculty members emphasized critical thinking, problem-solving, and emotional intelligence. They highlighted that critical thinking distinguishes nurses in professional practice, that problem-solving is part of the daily routine of the profession, and that emotional intelligence supports the development of other soft skills.

Critical thinking is essential […] nurses are trained to have a distinguishing feature, and that distinguishing feature is not the technical skills they are able to perform because they are nurses, but rather the ability to think critically about situations […] it is necessary for them to develop this critical perspective regarding everything they experience. (IND16-C)

(IND16-C)

Problem-solving, I believe, is a very important skill, not only for professional practice but for life. In nursing, we are constantly dealing with and trying to resolve problems and conflicts, whatever they may be; this skill is extremely important. (IND08-A)

I think emotional intelligence is the foundation for all other soft skills […] At some point, a soft skill will be hindered depending on your emotional intelligence; your leadership will be hindered if you do not have emotional intelligence. (IND01-C)

In participants’ accounts, communication and relational skills were frequently prioritized and directly associated with decision-making and the organization of nursing work.

Subcategory 4: Soft skills considered essential by faculty members

In addition to the soft skills directly addressed in the data collection instrument, participating faculty members identified other skills they described as essential for students’ development in undergraduate nursing education. Various skills were mentioned, including socioemotional, relational, organizational, and professional aspects.

Among the socioemotional aspects, self-awareness, resilience, flexibility, tolerance, availability, compassion, situational awareness, and sincerity were highlighted by participants. Selected excerpts are presented below, in which faculty members emphasize that self-awareness is the foundation for the development of other soft skills and that emotional resilience is necessary in light of the emotionally demanding situations experienced in the profession.

I think self-awareness may be what stands out the most, because without self-awareness we are not truly able to develop all the other competencies. (IND26-D)

I think self-awareness may be what stands out the most, because without self-awareness we are not truly able to develop all the other competencies. (IND26-D)

I think working on emotional resilience is also very important, considering that we are in unhealthy environments, with people who are, most of the time, ill, sick, or in the process of dying in many situations. (IND08-A)

I think working on emotional resilience is also very important, considering that we are in unhealthy environments, with people who are, most of the time, ill, sick, or in the process of dying in many situations. (IND08-A)

Regarding relational aspects, active listening, bonding, negotiation, respect, solidarity, and conflict resolution were highlighted by participants. As an example of these skills, faculty members emphasized the importance of developing the soft skill of conflict resolution during training, describing the presence of reactive behaviors in practice settings. Another participant further described conflict resolution as being associated with the management of dilemmas present in everyday professional practice, as evidenced in the excerpts below.

I think conflict resolution […] people tend to be quite reactive, and I believe this has greatly affected the settings we are part of, such as hospitals. I think learning how to resolve conflicts, how to mediate conflicts, is a very important skill that needs to be developed. (IND11-C)

I think conflict resolution […] people tend to be quite reactive, and I believe this has greatly affected the settings we are part of, such as hospitals. I think learning how to resolve conflicts, how to mediate conflicts, is a very important skill that needs to be developed. (IND11-C)

I think [another essential soft skill for development in undergraduate nursing education] is the issue of conflicts and dilemmas; we will always have to resolve something in a way that may be less harmful, and conflict is similar and operates within that same context. I believe conflict resolution, and the management of dilemmas, are essential. (IND02-B)

I think [another essential soft skill for development in undergraduate nursing education] is the issue of conflicts and dilemmas; we will always have to resolve something in a way that may be less harmful, and conflict is similar and operates within that same context. I believe conflict resolution, and the management of dilemmas, are essential. (IND02-B)

In addition, organizational and professional aspects were mentioned that some participating faculty members consider essential for development in undergraduate nursing education, such as responsibility, organization, commitment, time management, autonomy, decision-making, and ethical issues. As exemplified below, participants mention time management as necessary for the performance of future nursing professionals, considering the responsibilities they will assume in different settings. Furthermore, one participant highlights the importance of ethical and moral issues in the development of soft skills within the health context.

I think time management […] because soon the students will be graduating, they will be taking responsibility for primary care units, inpatient units, and they need to learn how to manage their time. (IND08-A)

I think time management […] because soon the students will be graduating, they will be taking responsibility for primary care units, inpatient units, and they need to learn how to manage their time. (IND08-A)

I do not know if I could call it a soft skill, but I think so; what permeates all of them are ethical and moral issues […] I think that when we work in the health field, reflecting on ethical and moral issues is a major contribution to integrating all the other soft skills. (IND01-C)

I do not know if I could call it a soft skill, but I think so; what permeates all of them are ethical and moral issues […] I think that when we work in the health field, reflecting on ethical and moral issues is a major contribution to integrating all the other soft skills. (IND01-C)

Overall, participants identified soft skills that extend beyond those traditionally outlined in curricula, encompassing socioemotional, relational, organizational, and ethical–moral dimensions.

Category 3: Pedagogical approaches and strategies for the development of soft skills

The most frequent word in this category was “practical,” followed by the words “assessment,” “instrument,” and “course,” as well as other slightly less frequent terms such as “approach,” “strategy,” “simulation,” and “case study.” Thus, throughout this category, the pedagogical approaches and strategies reported by participants to promote the development of soft skills are presented.

Active methodologies were mentioned several times by faculty members as an approach to facilitate the development of soft skills, and were described as promoting closer engagement with students and their active role in the teaching–learning process.

I use active methodologies […] I try, in every class I design, to start from lived experiences so that I can discuss with the students based on reality. And we can also go beyond the technical aspect and discuss the broader context. (IND16-C)

I use active methodologies […] I try, in every class I design, to start from lived experiences so that I can discuss with the students based on reality. And we can also go beyond the technical aspect and discuss the broader context. (IND16-C)

I think that when we work with active methodologies, we have the possibility of being a bit closer to the students. Listening more, allowing them to bring their own experiences and backgrounds. I think that through more active methodologies […] we are able to work on these skills, the soft skills. (IND19-D)

I think that when we work with active methodologies, we have the possibility of being a bit closer to the students. Listening more, allowing them to bring their own experiences and backgrounds. I think that through more active methodologies […] we are able to work on these skills, the soft skills. (IND19-D)

I think that any approach based on active methodologies will support the development of soft skills, because it ultimately reinforces the role of the student as the protagonist of their own learning process. (IND11-C)

I think that any approach based on active methodologies will support the development of soft skills, because it ultimately reinforces the role of the student as the protagonist of their own learning process. (IND11-C)

Participants report that they encourage students’ reflection and autonomy through examples from practice, as well as by returning questions in the form of new questions, which was described as an approach related to the soft skills of communication and problem-solving.

We encourage communication all the time […] in theoretical classes, I usually use examples from my own practice, of course. And I try to take a reflective approach so that they think about how communication should be, how it actually is, and what its importance is for health promotion. (IND21-C)

As for an approach to communication, I do not have a specific framework to tell you about […] in the past, when students asked me a question, I would immediately answer it. Now, when they ask me a question, I return the question to them, so that they can research it, seek out the knowledge, and then bring it back for us to discuss. (IND23-C)

As for an approach to communication, I do not have a specific framework to tell you about […] in the past, when students asked me a question, I would immediately answer it. Participating faculty members emphasize that emotional intelligence and empathy are also addressed mainly through examples and conversations with students. Likewise, they described the soft skill of teamwork is developed based on examples from their professional practice.

For emotional intelligence and empathy, I bring examples from my professional experience, situations I have already gone through, and I say, look, in this situation, if you take this path, it will not turn out well […] But if you choose a different approach, to minimize a conflict or some difficulty, you could handle it this way, perhaps. It is about approaching it through conversation, holding a meeting, trying to clarify a misunderstanding with a patient or a team member. (IND18-D)

For emotional intelligence and empathy, I bring examples from my professional experience, situations I have already gone through, and I say, look, in this situation, if you take this path, it will not turn out well […] But if you choose a different approach, to minimize a conflict or some difficulty, you could handle it this way, perhaps. It is about approaching it through conversation, holding a meeting, trying to clarify a misunderstanding with a patient or a team member. (IND18-D)

I think that when it comes to emotional intelligence and empathy, we rely a lot on examples […] what we have been doing is talking one-on-one about situations that we see require attention, because many times people do not come forward on their own. (IND20-C)

I think that when it comes to emotional intelligence and empathy, we rely a lot on examples […] what we have been doing is talking one-on-one about situations that we see require attention, because many times people do not come forward on their own. (IND20-C)

As for a pedagogical approach to teamwork, both in theory and in practice, we always try to bring examples from our own work when engaging with students. Transversally across the three analytical categories, the results indicate that, although faculty members widely recognize the relevance of soft skills in nursing education, their promotion occurs predominantly in an implicit, non-systematized, and pedagogically unintentional manner.

Soft skills emerge primarily as indirect outcomes of teaching practices, especially associated with the use of active methodologies, reflective strategies, and practical experiences, rather than as explicitly planned, assessed, or institutionally guided objectives. Overall, the findings suggest that, although soft skills are widely recognized as important, their incorporation into pedagogical practices is not consistently structured.

Discussion

The results indicate that faculty members in this study recognize the importance of soft skills in nursing education, attributing to these competencies a central role in enhancing professional practice. Based on participants’ self-reports, these competencies were described as essential to teaching and to the development of professional practice. This perception converges with international findings that identify the teaching of soft skills as a fundamental element for strengthening interpersonal interactions and improving health care delivery, as observed among nurse educators in South Africa [16]. Thus, the results suggest that recognition of the formative value of these competencies is shared among the participants and aligns with findings from other educational contexts. However, this interpretation should be considered with caution, as it is based on self-reported data and may reflect perspectives aligned with socially or professionally desirable expectations.

However, although participants acknowledge the relevance of soft skills, the findings suggest a significant gap regarding formal training for their teaching. None of the participants reported having received institutionally structured training aimed at the pedagogical development of these competencies, which may reflect the specific institutional context of the participating universities. This result suggests that the teaching of these competencies tends to rely on individual faculty initiative, rather than structured institutional strategies. This pattern observed among participants is consistent with what has been described in the international literature, which shows that nursing faculty, despite recognizing the importance of soft skills, often assume individual responsibility for teaching them, highlighting the fragility of institutional strategies for faculty development [17].

This mismatch between recognition and formative support, as reported by participants, indicates that the teaching of soft skills remains largely dependent on individual initiative and insufficiently structured within the curriculum, often being understood as something that develops through practice rather than through intentional pedagogical planning.

With regard to the soft skills investigated in the data collection instrument, participants highlighted, based on their reports, both interpersonal and intrapersonal skills as essential to the teaching–learning process in nursing education. Communication emerged as a structuring competency in training, being associated with decision-making, interpersonal relationships, and work organization. This centrality attributed to communication in participants’ accounts is supported by the educational literature, which recognizes communication skills as fundamental tools in the relationship between nursing professionals and patients, with a direct impact on care outcomes [18]. Thus, the findings suggest that communication is a central competency within the context of this study, reinforcing its relevance in the investigated setting.

Similarly, teamwork was mentioned by participants as one of the main competencies for nursing practice, considering that nurses work in integration with other health professionals. Collaboration among team members was associated with the promotion of more person-centered care and improved care outcomes, both for patients and for health services, as highlighted by McLaney et al. [19]. Therefore, the findings indicate that teamwork is understood by the faculty members in this study as a soft skill inseparable from professional nursing practice.

Other interpersonal skills, such as leadership and empathy, were also emphasized by participants. Leadership was recognized as a fundamental competency for nurses’ performance as team leaders, as reported by participants, reinforcing the need for its development beginning in undergraduate education in order to prepare future professionals to work in different care settings [20]. Empathy, in turn, was understood as a transversal skill capable of supporting the development of various soft skills. This understanding, as expressed in participants’ accounts, is supported by the literature, which identifies empathy as a factor contributing to increased leadership effectiveness and the strengthening of professional relationships [21, 22].

Within the domain of intrapersonal skills, participating faculty members highlighted critical thinking, problem-solving, and emotional intelligence as relevant competencies for nursing education. Critical thinking was understood as a distinguishing feature of nurses in professional practice, as reported, particularly in light of the complexity of care contexts. This finding converges with the literature pointing to the need for transformations in health education in order to prepare professionals capable of critically analyzing situations and responding reflectively to contemporary challenges [23]. Complementarily, studies indicate that the development of problem-solving skills is closely related to critical thinking, since pedagogical strategies based on problematization foster the analytical reasoning of nursing students [24].

Emotional intelligence was also identified by participating faculty members as a foundation supporting the development of other soft skills, a perception supported by studies that highlight this competency as essential to professional nursing practice, with implications for ethical aspects, crisis management, and quality of care [25]. Furthermore, evidence from a systematic review indicates that training in emotional intelligence contributes to stress reduction and the enhancement of communication skills, reinforcing its relevance within the educational context [26].

Beyond the skills initially investigated, participating faculty members expanded the set of soft skills considered essential to nursing education, incorporating socioemotional, relational, organizational, and professional competencies. Among the socioemotional aspects, self-awareness was identified as a foundation for the development of other soft skills, as it fosters critical reflection and the construction of professional identity throughout the educational process. This understanding is aligned with the literature that recognizes self-awareness as a component of emotional intelligence and as a factor associated with nurses’ professional performance [27, 28].

Participating nursing faculty members also emphasized the importance of emotional resilience, considering the emotionally demanding situations experienced in professional practice. Studies indicate that resilience contributes to reducing emotional strain and burnout, as professionals with higher levels of this competency demonstrate greater adaptability to stressors and lower levels of emotional exhaustion [29, 30]. These findings suggest the importance of educational strategies to support the development of resilience in nursing education, although this interpretation is grounded in participants’ accounts within a specific educational, institutional and regional context.

With regard to relational aspects, conflict resolution was highlighted by participating faculty members as a skill that needs to be developed during training. Evidence from studies involving nurses working in intensive care units indicates moderate levels of conflict, even in atypical contexts such as the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasizing collaboration as the primary strategy for resolution [31]. These findings suggest that conflict management is a relevant competency for nursing practice and point to the importance of its intentional inclusion in the educational process.

The soft skills considered essential by participating faculty members also encompassed organizational and professional aspects, such as time management and ethical–moral issues. Time management was associated, in participants’ accounts, with the ability to handle simultaneous demands, balance personal and professional life, and reduce stress related to work overload, as evidenced in the literature [32]. Likewise, the development of ethical–moral competencies was recognized as fundamental for analyzing complex situations and making well-grounded ethical decisions, contributing to improvements in the quality of health care [33].

The results also indicate that participants use different pedagogical approaches and strategies to promote the development of soft skills in nursing education, with emphasis on the use of active methodologies. These approaches were described by participants as facilitating closer interaction between faculty and students, as well as strengthening students’ active role in the teaching–learning process. This finding converges with the literature pointing to a progressive transition from traditional pedagogical models to student-centered approaches, which favor the development of transversal competencies such as communication, teamwork, and autonomy [34, 35]. However, as these findings are based on participants’ reports, the absence of direct observation limits the ability to assess how these strategies are implemented in actual teaching practice.

In the present study, active methodologies do not emerge merely as didactic techniques, but as pedagogical devices that enable greater student engagement with real or simulated professional practice situations. Based on participants’ reports of using strategies that stimulate reflection, problematization, and active participation, faculty members indicate a broadened understanding of soft skills teaching, aligned with pedagogical proposals that foster the development of critical thinking and student autonomy. These findings are consistent with evidence that the use of active methodologies contributes to more positive student attitudes toward communication, teamwork, and stress management [34].

It is important to consider that the adoption of active methodologies, although relevant, may not be sufficient in itself to ensure the development of soft skills, which may indicate the need for greater attention to pedagogical intentionality in their implementation. It is pertinent to acknowledge that the effects of these strategies may vary according to how they are planned and conducted, particularly with regard to the explicit articulation of objectives related to soft skills.

Among the pedagogical strategies mentioned, case studies were widely used by participating faculty members as a means of articulating theory and practice. This strategy was associated with the development of various soft skills, such as communication, teamwork, critical thinking, and problem-solving. The literature indicates that case-based learning promotes the problematization of complex situations and stimulates students’ critical reflection, in addition to contributing to greater self-confidence and satisfaction in the learning process when compared to traditional lecture-based methods [36, 37]. Thus, the findings suggest the potential of case studies as a pedagogical strategy for the integrated development of technical and non-technical competencies.

Complementarily, participating faculty members reported the use of paper-based cases as a pedagogical resource to deepen classroom discussions, particularly when students were not engaged in practice settings. This strategy was associated with the stimulation of critical thinking and problem-solving, as it requires analysis, information seeking, and decision-making. Evidence indicates that the use of paper-based cases fosters reflection on real professional practice situations, contributing to the development of these soft skills within the context of nursing education [37, 38].

Clinical simulation stood out as one of the most relevant pedagogical strategies for the development of the investigated soft skills. Participating faculty members reported that simulation enables the prior definition of pedagogical objectives, the observation of students’ behaviors, and the integration of multiple competencies within a safe and controlled environment. This finding is consistent with studies identifying simulation as an effective strategy for strengthening communication, leadership, teamwork, and critical thinking, in addition to contributing to increased student self-confidence and satisfaction [39, 40]. In this way, clinical simulation can be understood as an intentional and structured approach to the development of soft skills in nursing education.

The use of portfolios was also mentioned as a pedagogical strategy that fosters reflection on experiences lived in different learning settings. Participating faculty members associated this strategy with the development of soft skills such as emotional intelligence, empathy, and responsibility, since the portfolio allows students to express feelings, perceptions, and learning derived from their experiences. The literature highlights the portfolio as a resource capable of integrating teaching, learning, and assessment, stimulating critical-reflective reasoning, autonomy, and decision-making in nursing education [41, 42].

In addition, the flipped classroom and problem-based learning were identified as pedagogical strategies used by some participating faculty members to promote the development of soft skills. From a conceptual perspective, the flipped classroom presupposes students’ prior study of the content, so that in-person time can be dedicated to discussion, problematization, and deeper exploration, rather than being limited to group presentations, with which it is sometimes mistakenly associated. In the reports, the flipped classroom was linked to the expansion of space for reflective discussions in class, while problem-based learning was associated with the stimulation of critical thinking and problem-solving. Evidence indicates that these strategies foster student engagement and the development of competencies such as communication, cooperation, autonomy, and time management, particularly when combined within the educational process [43, 44].

Overall, the results indicate that participating faculty members draw on different pedagogical strategies to promote the development of soft skills, with emphasis on approaches that stimulate reflection, active participation, and engagement with professional practice situations. However, the findings suggest that these strategies are often adopted sporadically and depend largely on individual faculty initiative, which may indicate the need for greater pedagogical systematization and institutional support to consolidate the teaching of soft skills in nursing education. In this regard, mechanisms such as faculty performance evaluation and career progression criteria may contribute to encouraging and valuing pedagogical practices aimed at the development of these competencies.

The results also indicate that, although participants recognize the importance of soft skills in nursing education, their promotion in teaching is permeated by various pedagogical, institutional, and relational difficulties. Among the main challenges identified in participants’ reports are the subjectivity of these competencies, students’ emotional immaturity, the prioritization of technical skills, and the impact of the intensive use of technologies on the teaching and learning process.

Although some of the challenges reported by faculty members are related to the specific nature of soft skills, the findings suggest that certain difficulties may also reflect broader aspects of the contemporary educational context. Elements such as the intensive use of digital technologies, classroom distraction, and issues related to students’ emotional maturity are not exclusive to the teaching of soft skills but permeate the educational process as a whole. Such factors may intensify the perceived difficulties, indicating the need for pedagogical strategies that take into account transformations in contemporary educational dynamics.

The subjectivity associated with soft skills was mentioned as a factor that hinders both their teaching and evaluation. Participating faculty members reported the absence of clear protocols, instruments, or frameworks to guide the approach to these competencies, which contributes to their being addressed in an implicit and transversal manner, without explicit pedagogical intentionality. This perception is supported by the literature, which identifies the lack of conceptual consensus and standardization as one of the main barriers to the systematic incorporation of soft skills into education, particularly when compared to hard skills, which are traditionally associated with more objective assessment criteria [45].

Associated with this subjectivity, students’ emotional immaturity was identified as an additional difficulty in the development of soft skills. Participating faculty members reported that many students demonstrate resistance or limited openness to reflecting on socioemotional and relational aspects, which restricts engagement in activities aimed at these competencies. This finding is consistent with studies indicating that students’ recognition of the importance of soft skills tends to occur later, particularly when they begin to experience clinical practice settings more intensively [46]. Thus, the appreciation of these competencies appears to be conditioned by practical experience, which makes their continuous promotion throughout the educational process more challenging.

Another relevant challenge identified by participants refers to students’ preference for the teaching of hard skills to the detriment of socioemotional and relational competencies. The reports indicate that activities aimed at developing soft skills are sometimes perceived by students as secondary or less relevant when compared to technical and procedural skills. This perception is corroborated by the literature, which points out that nursing students frequently associate professional competence with the mastery of psychomotor skills, contributing to the devaluation of soft skills in the educational process [46].

This preference may also be interpreted as reflecting the reproduction of a traditional educational model centered on memorization and content transmission, experienced throughout students’ prior educational trajectories. In this context, more problematizing pedagogical proposals oriented toward autonomy may generate resistance, which may, in turn, affect the recognition and appreciation of soft skills within the educational process.

In addition, participating faculty members highlighted the intensive use of digital technologies as a factor that negatively interferes with the development of soft skills. The constant presence of electronic devices in the classroom was associated with difficulties in concentration, reduced depth of reading, and diminished reflective capacity among students. Evidence suggests that frequent use of technologies, especially for purposes unrelated to the educational process, may compromise students’ attention and active participation, interfering with activities that require listening, reflection, and interaction, which are fundamental aspects for the development of soft skills [47, 48].

Taken together, the identified challenges suggest that the difficulties in promoting soft skills in teaching are not limited to individual factors related to students or faculty members but reflect broader structural and pedagogical limitations. The absence of clear guidelines, insufficient faculty training, a training culture centered on technical skills, and the impacts of digital technologies may create a scenario that hinders the intentional and systematic incorporation of these competencies into nursing education.

Thus, the findings point to the need for institutional strategies that recognize soft skills as essential components of professional education, promoting their explicit integration into curricula, faculty pedagogical development, and the creation of learning environments that foster reflection, interaction, and student engagement. Nevertheless, these considerations should be interpreted with caution, considering the context-specific nature of the study and the use of self-reported data, which may influence how soft skills are perceived and described, and therefore limit the transferability of the findings to other educational contexts.

Limitations

This study presents some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the data were obtained exclusively through interviews with faculty members and are therefore based on self-reported accounts. This characteristic may influence the perceptions presented, as the responses reflect how participants interpret and describe their own pedagogical practices. Furthermore, although the interviews were conducted with cameras on, the virtual format may have influenced the dynamics of the dialogue and limited aspects of interaction that might have been more broadly perceived in face-to-face encounters.

In addition, the possibility of social desirability bias cannot be ruled out, considering that faculty members may have emphasized aspects perceived as socially or professionally desirable, particularly regarding the value attributed to soft skills and the use of pedagogical strategies aligned with contemporary approaches. Another limitation concerns the absence of direct observation of pedagogical practices in teaching settings. The inclusion of observational methods could have expanded the understanding of how soft skills are effectively promoted in everyday teaching activities, allowing for triangulation between discourse and practice.

Moreover, participants were affiliated with federal public universities in a single region of Brazil, which delimits the findings to this institutional and regional context. Although the inclusion of multiple institutional settings contributes to the diversity of perspectives, the results should not be generalized to other educational contexts without due caution. Despite these limitations, the study offers relevant contributions by deepening the understanding of the incorporation of soft skills into nursing education and providing support for future reflections and investigations that may expand and complement the findings presented here.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that, among the participating faculty members, there is recognition of the relevance of soft skills in the context of nursing education, as well as reports of the use of different pedagogical approaches and strategies to promote their development, particularly through active methodologies and learning environments that stimulate critical reflection, autonomy, and students’ active role. However, the absence of specific training for teaching these competencies, reported by a significant proportion of faculty members, reveals a formative gap that calls for investment in continuing education processes.

The findings also indicate that faculty members expand the set of soft skills initially addressed in the data collection instrument, incorporating competencies of a socioemotional, relational, organizational, and ethical nature. Despite this broadened recognition, relevant challenges persist in promoting these competencies in teaching, such as the subjectivity associated with soft skills, students’ emotional immaturity, the predominant appreciation of technical skills, and the impacts of the intensive use of technologies on concentration and reflective capacity.

In this sense, the results suggest that, although technical mastery is indispensable in nursing education, the intentional and systematic development of soft skills is equally essential for building a more comprehensive professional education. It is evident, however, that the absence of structured theoretical-pedagogical frameworks to guide the teaching of these competencies contributes to their unsystematic and predominantly implicit approach. Thus, the adoption of structured institutional strategies aimed at integrating these competencies into curricula and promoting faculty pedagogical development is fundamental to strengthening the educational process.

Therefore, investing in faculty training for the teaching of soft skills may strengthen professional nursing education and contribute to improving the quality of health care. This study points to the need for intentional and institutionally supported strategies to integrate soft skills into nursing curricula, promoting a more comprehensive education aligned with the contemporary demands of professional practice.
