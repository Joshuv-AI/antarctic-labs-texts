Relative hyperglycemia and new-onset atrial fibrillation after coronary artery bypass grafting: a focus on stress hyperglycemia ratio
Authors: Heshan Cao, Long Gui, Junying Wei, Min Zheng, Mingjin Cheng, Chengdong Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNew-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), a novel biomarker reflecting relative hyperglycemia, has an undetermined role in NOAF risk assessment. This study aimed to evaluate the association between SHR and NOAF after CABG, and to further develop an interpretable machine learning (ML) model to validate its potential value in clinical risk stratification.MethodsCABG patients were retrospectively identified from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database. Baseline characteristics from the first postoperative ICU day were extracted. The association between SHR and NOAF was assessed using logistic regression and restricted cubic spline methods. Feature selection was performed via multivariable logistic regression, the Boruta algorithm, and LASSO regression, with the intersection of selected variables used to construct 15 ML prediction models. Finally, the SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method was applied for interpretability analysis of the best-performing model, which was subsequently deployed as an online tool.ResultsA total of 3,498 eligible CABG patients were enrolled, with an NOAF incidence of approximately 18.6% (651 patients). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that, after adjusting for confounding factors, SHR was independently associated with the risk of NOAF after CABG (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.04–1.85, P = 0.027). Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between SHR and NOAF risk, with no significant non-linear relationship observed (P for non-linearity > 0.05). The intersection of the three feature selection approaches identified eight key features, with SHR consistently emerging as an important predictor of NOAF. Among the 15 ML models developed, the XGBoost model exhibited the best performance with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.813, while removing SHR reduced AUC to 0.779. SHAP analysis further confirmed the positive contribution of elevated SHR levels to NOAF risk.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that SHR is significantly associated with NOAF after CABG, with a higher SHR serving as an independent risk factor. As a novel serological biomarker, SHR shows substantial clinical potential for early risk stratification and management optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05231-4.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is one of the most prevalent cardiovascular disorders worldwide and remains a principal cause of mortality [1]. For patients with severe coronary occlusive lesions, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has become an essential revascularization strategy widely adopted in clinical practice, substantially improving survival and overall cardiovascular outcomes [2, 3]. New-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) is the most common arrhythmic complication following CABG, with reported incidences ranging from approximately 5% to 40% [4, 5]. Numerous studies have shown that NOAF is associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased mortality, and an elevated risk of other adverse events, making it a major concern in the perioperative period [6–9]. The pathogenesis of NOAF is multifactorial, involving factors such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and autonomic nervous system activation, which are all heightened during the profound physiological stress of cardiac surgery [10, 11]. This acute stress response is also a key driver of metabolic disturbances, notably stress hyperglycemia (SH) [12].

SH refers to the acute elevation of blood glucose levels in response to severe physiological stress. In the context of CABG, surgical trauma and inflammatory responses can markedly activate the body’s stress mechanisms, leading to SH. Previous research has demonstrated a significant association between postoperative hyperglycemia and the occurrence of NOAF [13]. However, a critical limitation of relying on a single postoperative blood glucose measurement is its inherent confounding by the patient’s pre-existing diabetic status or chronic glycemic control. This makes it difficult to ascertain whether the hyperglycemia truly reflects the acute surgical stress or is merely a manifestation of underlying dysglycemia. To overcome this limitation, Roberts et al. [14] introduced the concept of the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), which relates the acute glucose level to the chronic glycemic background, offering a more precise quantification of the relative hyperglycemia under stress conditions. This metric is therefore postulated to be a purer indicator of acute dysmetabolic stress. Notably, among non-diabetic patients with acute myocardial infarction, SHR has been validated as an independent predictor of in‐hospital NOAF, underscoring its potential role in the pathogenesis of atrial fibrillation (AF) and its utility in risk stratification [15].

While previous studies on risk factors for NOAF after CABG have extensively examined clinical characteristics and surgical variables, the predictive value of objective metrics of acute metabolic stress, such as SHR, remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between SHR and the occurrence of NOAF after CABG, as well as to develop an interpretable machine learning (ML) prediction model incorporating SHR. By elucidating the intrinsic link between SHR and NOAF risk, we hope to provide novel theoretical insights and practical tools for the early identification and personalized management of high-risk patients after CABG, ultimately improving clinical outcomes.

Data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the publicly available, large-scale critical care database, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) [16, 17]. The database comprises detailed electronic medical records of over 300,000 patients treated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2022. The use of these data was approved by the institutional review board, and as all patient information is de-identified, informed consent was not required. Author Heshan Cao has completed the necessary training and obtained authorization to access the database (Certification No. 63137030).

Study population

Patients who underwent CABG and were subsequently admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) Procedure Coding (Table S1). The study endpoint was the occurrence of NOAF after the first day of ICU admission [18]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age younger than 18 years; (2) No record of ICU admission or non-first ICU admission; (3) An ICU stay of less than 24 h, or an interval between the surgery date and ICU admission exceeding 24 h; (4) Missing blood glucose data within 24 h of ICU admission or missing HbA1c data from admission to 24 h after ICU admission; (5) A prior history of AF or any record of NOAF prior to or within 24 h of ICU admission. The inclusion and exclusion flowchart is detailed in Fig. S1.

Data extraction and SHR calculation

Structured Query Language (SQL) was used to extract the following variables from the MIMIC-IV database. The variable selection was based on established clinical knowledge and prior studies utilizing this database that investigated risk factors for adverse outcomes in critically ill patients [19–22]. (1) Demographics: age, gender, race, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI); (2) Vital signs: heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean blood pressure (MBP), respiratory rate, temperature, and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2); (3) Laboratory tests: hematocrit, hemoglobin, platelet, white blood cell count (WBC), sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, anion gap, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), international normalized ratio (INR), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), pH, and lactate; (4) Comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, old myocardial infarction (OMI), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD); (5) Medication Usage: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB), β-blockers, loop diuretics, insulin, glucocorticoids, vasoactive agents, statins, antiplatelet agents, and calcium channel blockers (CCB).

Vital signs and laboratory tests were extracted from the records obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission, and when multiple recordings existed for the same parameter, the average value was used. Medication usage was determined by assigning binary dummy variables based on whether the patient received the medication within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Details on variable type and description are provided in Table S2. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded to minimize potential bias, a common threshold employed in clinical research [23, 24]. For variables with a missing rate below 20%, multiple imputation was performed using the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) approach implemented in the miceforest package [25]. Details on missing data are provided in Fig. S2. SHR was calculated using the following formula: SHR = Maximum blood glucose (mg/dL)/(28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7) [14].

Sensitivity analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis was initially performed to identify potential confounding factors, and multivariate logistic regression was then used to evaluate the association between SHR and NOAF, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Patients were divided into three groups based on SHR tertiles. To assess the robustness of the association between SHR and NOAF, SHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable (with T1 serving as the reference group). Three stepwise logistic regression models were constructed: Model I was unadjusted; Model II was adjusted for demographics, vital signs, and laboratory tests; and Model III was further adjusted for comorbidities and medications on top of Model II. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to explore the dose-response relationship between SHR and NOAF. Additionally, subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, race, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, OMI, COPD, CKD, β-blocker usage, and statin usage were conducted to investigate intergroup heterogeneity and potential interaction effects.

Feature selection

In addition to including variables significantly associated with NOAF identified via multivariate logistic regression, the study employed the Boruta algorithm and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to evaluate feature importance. For the Boruta algorithm, a confidence level of 0.01 and a maximum iteration limit of 100 were set, and features that were rejected were excluded. LASSO regression employed 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty coefficient (lambda), with variable selection based on the lambda.1se criterion. The final set of key features was determined by taking the intersection of the variables selected by the three methods, thereby maximizing the identification of factors associated with NOAF while minimizing the number of features to enhance clinical applicability.

Model development and explanation

Data were randomly split using stratified sampling, with 80% allocated for training and 20% for validation, ensuring similar proportions of positive cases in both sets to reduce overfitting risk. A total of 15 ML models were developed, including Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Category Boosting (CatBoost), Decision Tree (DT), Extra Trees (ET), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Logistic Regression (LR), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Naive Bayes (NB), Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network (TabPFN), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). To enhance model performance, the optimal hyperparameters were determined via a combination of 5-fold cross-validation and Bayesian optimization during the training phase (Table S3). Model evaluation was conducted using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The classification threshold was optimized by maximizing Youden’s index, defined as (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Calibration curve and decision curve were used to assess model calibration and clinical decision-making value. To elucidate the contribution of each input feature to the predictions, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method [26] was applied for both global and local interpretability, thereby providing a visual representation of the impact of each feature on the model’s decisions.

Webpage deployment

To facilitate clinical application, the final predictive model was deployed as a web application using the Streamlit Python library. Upon entering the required variable values, the system follows a two-step process: first, it automatically calculates the SHR from the provided blood glucose and HbA1c values; subsequently, the system loads the pre-trained model weights and evaluates the online risk of NOAF for an individual patient based on the calculated SHR and seven additional input features. The model outputs the predicted probability and visually presents the contribution of each feature to the prediction using SHAP force plot.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). Differences in continuous variables between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical comparisons were conducted using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

Correlation analysis was performed to examine the interrelationships among variables, with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.7 indicating a high degree of correlation (Fig. S3). Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF >10 considered indicative of substantial collinearity (Table S4). The minimum sample size required for this study was estimated according to the clinical prediction model sample size estimation method proposed by Riley et al. [27].

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1) and Python (version 3.9.18), and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

According to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 3,498 patients undergoing CABG surgery were ultimately enrolled, with a mean age of 68.2 years and 75.9% male (n = 2,654). Among these patients, 651 developed NOAF postoperatively, corresponding to an overall incidence rate of approximately 18.6%. Table 1 presents a comparison of the baseline characteristics between the NOAF and non-NOAF groups. The results indicate that patients in the NOAF group were older and had a higher proportion of white race. Furthermore, the NOAF group exhibited significantly higher values in respiratory rate, SpO2, WBC, serum potassium, anion gap, creatinine, BUN, PT, PTT, INR, lactate, and blood glucose levels compared to the non‐NOAF group. In addition, the proportions of patients receiving glucocorticoids and vasoactive agents were higher in the NOAF group, whereas the proportions using β‐blockers, loop diuretics, and statins were lower. The NOAF group also had a significantly increased prevalence of comorbid COPD and CKD. Moreover, patients in the NOAF group demonstrated lower SBP, DBP, MBP, hematocrit, hemoglobin, platelet count, bicarbonate, PO2, PCO2, and pH compared with those in the non‐NOAF group. Notably, the SHR was significantly higher in the NOAF group [0.97 (0.77, 1.15) vs. 0.93 (0.74, 1.11); P = 0.002].

Correlation analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified age, race, BMI, SBP, DBP, respiratory rate, hemoglobin, platelet count, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, anion gap, creatinine, BUN, PTT, INR, PO2, PCO2, pH, lactate, hypertension, COPD, CKD, β-blocker use, loop diuretic use, glucocorticoid use, vasoactive agent use, and statin use as potential confounding factors (Table S6). In the sequentially adjusted logistic regression models, after adjusting for all confounders, SHR as a continuous variable remained independently associated with the risk of NOAF after CABG (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.04–1.85, P = 0.027). Furthermore, when SHR was entered as a categorical variable (using T1 as the reference), patients in the T2 and T3 groups exhibited a significantly increased adjusted risk of NOAF (Table 2). RCS analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between SHR and NOAF risk, with no significant non-linear relationship observed (P for non-linear = 0.392) (Fig. 2B).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, race, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, OMI, COPD, CKD, β-blocker use, and statin use revealed that the effect of SHR on the outcome was statistically significant in patients aged ≥ 65 years, male, non-white, those with a BMI < 25 kg/m2, individuals with diabetes and COPD, patients without hyperlipidemia, and in the subgroup of patients receiving postoperative β‐blockers. However, no significant interaction was observed among these subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3). Although some subgroups (e.g., patients with COPD) had a limited sample size and wider CIs, the overall pattern of association remained consistent.

Feature selection

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the following variables as independently associated with NOAF: age, β-blocker use, BMI, BUN, hemoglobin, INR, PO2, SBP, SHR, and statin use. The Boruta algorithm selected features including age, β‐blocker use, BMI, BUN, hemoglobin, PTT, PO2, SBP, SHR, DBP, and lactate. LASSO regression further identified age, β‐blocker use, BMI, BUN, hemoglobin, respiratory rate, PO2, SBP, SHR, anion gap, creatinine, PTT, INR, lactate, CKD, and statin use. The intersection of these three methods yielded eight key features: SHR, age, BMI, SBP, hemoglobin, BUN, PO2, and β‐blocker use (Fig. 4).

Model performance

Based on the eight selected predictive variables and an NOAF incidence of approximately 18.6%, sample size estimation using the pmsampsize package developed by Riley et al. [27] indicated that a minimum of 737 subjects, including at least 138 NOAF events, were required. The actual sample size of 3,498 patients (with 651 NOAF events) far exceeded this minimum requirement, thereby satisfying the sample size needs for subsequent ML model development (Table S7).

Fifteen ML models were constructed based on the eight key features. Among these, XGBoost, GBDT, and LightGBM exhibited the highest performance, with XGBoost performing best on the validation set (AUC = 0.813, 95% CI: 0.772–0.849; accuracy = 0.756; sensitivity = 0.738; specificity = 0.760) (Fig. 5 and Table S8). Calibration and decision curve analyses further confirmed that the model exhibited satisfactory calibration and clinical benefit. To evaluate the contribution of SHR to the model’s performance, the XGBoost model was re-evaluated without SHR (while keeping other parameters constant), resulting in a decrease of the AUC to 0.779.

Model interpretation

Figure 6 illustrates the global explanation of the XGBoost model’s decision process using the SHAP methodology. The SHAP summary plots revealed that the eight key features contributed to the prediction of NOAF in the following order: age, BUN, β-blocker use, PO2, hemoglobin, SBP, SHR, and BMI. SHAP dependence plots demonstrated that, across the range of each feature, SHAP values greater than zero contributed positively to the prediction of NOAF risk, whereas values less than zero contributed negatively. Notably, the dependence plot for SHR showed a clear positive correlation, corroborating the findings of the RCS analysis.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we investigated the association between SHR and NOAF after CABG. Our study revealed several key findings: elevated SHR levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of NOAF, and this association remained robust after adjustment for multiple potential confounders. Feature selection and SHAP-based interpretability analysis further identified SHR as one of the most influential predictors of NOAF, thereby strengthening the robustness of our findings.

SHR, a metric reflecting the extent of SH, has attracted growing clinical attention in recent years [28, 29]. Unlike absolute blood glucose or HbA1c levels, SHR effectively differentiates acute SH from chronic diabetic hyperglycemia, providing a more sensitive measure of the acute stress response. Numerous studies have demonstrated that SH is closely linked to adverse outcomes in cardiovascular disease. For instance, Esdaile et al. [28] reported in a meta-analysis that patients in the highest SHR group (including those with acute myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke) had significantly increased risks of both short-term and long-term all-cause mortality compared with lower SHR groups. Similarly, Qiao et al. [29] found that in patients with coronary three-vessel disease undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, elevated SHR nearly doubled the risk of long-term cardiovascular events, regardless of diabetes status. Other evidence further extends these findings beyond obstructive coronary artery disease. A large-scale study in patients with ischemia and nonobstructive coronary arteries demonstrated that elevated SHR independently predicted a higher risk of rehospitalization for chest pain, even after rigorous adjustment for confounders [30]. Collectively, these findings underscore SHR as a valuable prognostic marker across diverse coronary artery disease contexts. Our study extends this line of evidence by applying SHR to the prediction of postoperative AF in CABG patients, thereby introducing a novel perspective.

We observed a clear dose-response relationship between SHR and NOAF risk. With increasing SHR quartiles, the incidence of NOAF rose progressively, and multivariable models consistently showed adjusted ORs >1.3 for higher SHR groups compared to reference. RCS analysis and SHAP dependence plot further confirmed the graded association. In other cardiovascular contexts, similar dose-response patterns have been reported. For example, Wei et al. [31] observed a J-shaped association between SHR and mortality in ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients, with a risk inflection point around 1.2, while Li et al. [32] showed that SHR was linearly associated with in-hospital cardiac arrest in acute coronary syndrome patients. Although these studies investigated different outcomes, they consistently support the notion that elevated SHR confers worse cardiovascular prognosis. Notably, several studies have reported non-linear relationships, such as Huang et al. [19] demonstrating a U-shaped association between SHR and mortality among acute myocardial infarction patients with AF, and Lai et al. [33] describing a U-shaped association between SHR and CKD severity in diabetic patients. These findings suggest that both extremely low and excessively high SHR levels may be detrimental. In our study, however, we did not observe an excess risk at the lowest SHR range, likely due to differences in patient population and outcome measures.

The relationship between SHR and NOAF may involve complex pathophysiological mechanisms. Following CABG, patients experience a pronounced stress response characterized by increased catecholamine release and elevated inflammatory cytokine levels, leading to acute SH. Elevated blood glucose levels can induce structural and electrophysiological remodeling of atrial myocardium—including atrial fibrosis and ion channel dysfunction—that leads to reduced atrial conduction velocity and prolonged action potential duration, thereby establishing an electrophysiological substrate for AF [34]. Furthermore, pronounced fluctuations in blood glucose levels during and after surgery can evoke oxidative stress and further inflammatory cytokine release, exacerbating myocardial injury and electrophysiological instability, which, in turn, heightens the risk of AF [35]. In addition, excessive sympathetic nervous system activation not only precipitates SH but also directly contributes to AF by increasing atrial ectopic activity and shortening the effective refractory period [36]. Consequently, patients with elevated SHR may experience more pronounced neurohormonal activation and inflammatory responses, which ultimately heightens their risk of developing AF. Recent work has even implicated immunometabolic pathways, such as altered NKT cell and PD-1 expression in HFpEF patients with high SHR, which may influence cardio-cerebral interactions [37]. Whether such mechanisms contribute to AF development after CABG warrants further investigation.

Considering that NOAF after CABG may result from the interplay of multiple factors, we further explored the feasibility of incorporating SHR into a ML model to improve risk prediction performance. Although traditional logistic regression models are useful for quantifying the independent effect of SHR, they are less adept at handling complex, non-linear relationships. In this study, we constructed a streamlined and high-performing ML prediction model by integrating features selected via multiple screening methods, achieving an AUC exceeding 0.8 in validation. To overcome the “black box” issue commonly associated with ML models, SHAP analysis was employed to elucidate the contribution of individual features [26]. The high contribution of SHR to the model’s predictions further underscores its significant impact on NOAF risk stratification and enhances the clinical credibility and utility of our predictive model.

In addition to SHR, SHAP analysis also identified age, BUN, and postoperative β-blocker use as important factors of NOAF. Advanced age has been widely recognized as an independent predictor of NOAF after CABG [38]. For instance, Mathew et al. [39] demonstrated that a 10-year increase in age is associated with a 75% rise in AF incidence, with patients over 70 years representing a particularly high-risk group for postoperative AF. Moreover, BUN is an important indicator of renal function, and its elevated levels typically suggest renal dysfunction and overall systemic deterioration. Multiple studies have confirmed that renal dysfunction is a significant risk factor for postoperative NOAF [40, 41]. In addition, numerous studies have shown that the use of β‐blockers can effectively reduce the risk of NOAF after cardiac surgery [39, 42, 43]. These findings further corroborate the clinical relevance and validity of our predictive model. To facilitate clinical adoption, we have also developed an online prediction tool based on SHR, which provides individualized AF risk assessments and clearly delineates the main contributing risk factors to inform targeted preventive strategies.

Traditional risk scores for AF following cardiac surgery, such as the CHA₂DS₂-VASc [44] and POAF [45] scores, primarily rely on demographic data and baseline comorbidities and lack consideration of metabolic stress indicators. The AUC of these models has been validated to be generally around 0.6 to 0.7 [46], indicating only moderate discriminative ability. By incorporating SHR, our study provides a valuable complement to these existing models, capturing additional risk information that conventional predictors may miss. In our data, the addition of SHR resulted in a marked improvement in AUC. This suggests that models excluding SH factors might underestimate the risk in patients with significant stress responses yet without apparent chronic risk factors. Therefore, SHR represents an innovative biomarker that improves discriminative ability in risk prediction and offers new biological insights by emphasizing the role of stress-induced metabolic factors.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias, as institutional practice patterns and patient characteristics may not fully represent those of other populations, potentially limiting generalizability. Despite this inherent limitation, the relatively large sample size in our study helped improve the stability of statistical estimates and reduce random error. Nevertheless, external validation in multicenter cohorts is warranted. Second, certain important cardiac biomarkers, such as ejection fraction (EF) and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), were excluded due to high missing rates, which may have resulted in residual confounding. To mitigate this, we incorporated comprehensive clinical variables and adjusted for a wide range of recognized confounders to enhance the validity of our findings. The consistent findings across multiple models and subgroup analyses suggest that the magnitude of such bias is unlikely to be large. Third, because exposure variables were collected only within the first 24 h after ICU admission, the dynamic changes in glucose levels and other physiological parameters over time were not captured, which might have attenuated the observed associations. To overcome this limitation, future prospective, multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations, along with longitudinal data collection, are needed to confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that an elevated SHR is significantly associated with an increased risk of NOAF after CABG. The integration of ML with SHAP-based interpretability further confirms the potential value of SHR in clinical risk stratification. Our findings offer a novel perspective for perioperative management and lay the groundwork for future targeted intervention strategies. Integrating SHR with conventional risk indicators may facilitate the development of more comprehensive predictive models, ultimately advancing personalized intervention strategies to improve postoperative outcomes.
