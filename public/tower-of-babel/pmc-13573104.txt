Spatial and Temporal Heterogeneity of Macrophage Efferocytosis in Tumors: Emerging Implications for Immunotherapy Biomarkers and Therapeutic Timing
Authors: Shanyi Li, Jilei Li, Zhenyu Zhang, Sizhe Wang, Chunzheng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) remains a major barrier in cancer therapy, reflecting the fact that tumor immunity is governed not only by cell type abundance but also by spatial organization and temporal dynamics within the tumor microenvironment (TME). In this review, we examine macrophage efferocytosis as a biologically and clinically actionable process that conditions immunosuppression or immune activation depending on niche context and treatment timing. The central question is whether apoptotic cell clearance by tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) can serve as a biomarker‐relevant regulator of therapeutic response, and more broadly, why conventional TAMs and efferocytosis reviews have been insufficient to explain spatial and temporal heterogeneity. Synthesizing evidence from spatial transcriptomics, single‐cell atlas studies, multiplexed tissue imaging, and in vivo lineage tracing, we show that efferocytic macrophages concentrate in defined microanatomical compartments, including tumor margins, perivascular regions, and therapy‐induced apoptotic zones, where they promote tolerogenic signaling and suppress antigen presentation. However, when efferocytosis is modulated in a delayed or spatially restricted manner, antigen persistence is prolonged and adaptive antitumor immunity is enhanced. Organ‐specific differences in macrophage lineage, stromal architecture, and apoptotic burden further shape the functional meaning of efferocytosis across tumor types. On this basis, we propose a working model of efferocytosis‐associated “hotspots” and “deserts” that may support biomarker‐driven patient stratification: these spatial patterns are conceptualized as a model proposed in this review rather than a settled biological category. Overall, this work reframes macrophage efferocytosis as a spatially and temporally constrained determinant of immune contexture with important implications for precision immunotherapy, combination design, and trial optimization.

Introduction

Tumor immunology has been reshaped by the clinical success of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which have yielded durable responses in subsets of patients across a broad spectrum of malignancies [1]. Nevertheless, a large proportion of individuals exhibit either primary refractoriness or acquire resistance over the course of treatment, indicating the need to identify additional regulatory layers within the tumor immune microenvironment that govern therapeutic responsiveness [2]. Although biomarkers such as PD‐L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and global measures of T‐cell infiltration have improved patient stratification, they predominantly reflect static or bulk characteristics of the immune landscape [3]. Increasing evidence indicates that immune regulation in tumors is intrinsically dynamic and spatially organized, with functional consequences that cannot be fully captured by conventional endpoint‐based or dissociative assays [4]. This realization has prompted a conceptual shift toward understanding immune populations together with their spatial distribution within the tumor architecture and how their functional states evolve over time in response to therapeutic intervention [5]. Emerging evidence indicates that responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) are coordinated by multiple innate immune cell populations, including macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK) cells, which can either promote durable antitumor immunity or contribute to immune evasion depending on their activation state and interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [6, 7, 8]. This broader immunological framework highlights macrophages not as isolated regulators but as integral components of dynamic innate immune networks that shape therapeutic responsiveness, thereby providing an appropriate context for understanding the spatial and temporal regulation of macrophage efferocytosis. Traditional tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) and efferocytosis reviews have largely emphasized molecular determinants or generalized immunosuppression, while giving comparatively little attention to the spatial distribution of efferocytic macrophages, the temporal evolution of their states, the evidence base for biomarkerization, or the most realistic path to prospective validation. Accordingly, this review is organized around five linked questions: why existing TAMs and efferocytosis reviews are insufficient, what the spatial dimension adds, what the temporal dimension adds, which evidence currently supports biomarker development, and what validation strategy is most feasible.

Among the diverse cellular constituents of the TME, TAMs have emerged as particularly potent regulators of both immune activation and immunosuppression [9, 10]. Often representing the most abundant immune population in solid tumors, TAMs display pronounced phenotypic and functional plasticity, enabling them to either support cytotoxic antitumor responses or facilitate tumor progression depending on their local microenvironment [11, 12]. A central yet incompletely resolved function of TAMs is efferocytosis, the phagocytic clearance of apoptotic cells, which in physiological settings promotes tissue homeostasis and the resolution of inflammation [13, 14]. Within tumors, however, this same process is frequently co‐opted to reinforce immune tolerance through sustained production of immunosuppressive cytokines, attenuation of antigen presentation, and stabilization of regulatory immune circuits [15].

Notably, recent experimental and translational studies suggest that efferocytosis is not a uniform biological event, but rather encompasses qualitatively distinct pathways, such as LC3‐associated phagocytosis (LAP) versus canonical phagosome maturation, that can differentially shape antigen cross‐presentation and T‐cell priming [16, 17]. These findings establish macrophage efferocytosis as a dual‐edged regulator of tumor immunity whose impact is dictated by both molecular context and microenvironmental localization. In this framework, spatial localization defines where candidate efferocytic states occur, whereas longitudinal sampling, lineage tracing, and treatment‐perturbation models are required to determine whether those states are stable, transitional, or therapy‐induced.

Despite growing recognition of efferocytosis as a pivotal immunoregulatory axis, most existing reviews have emphasized its molecular determinants or its generalized contribution to immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance, with comparatively limited consideration of its spatial distribution within tumors or its temporal evolution during disease progression and treatment [18, 19]. To address this unresolved issue, this review examines: whether macrophage efferocytosis is best understood as a uniform pathway‐level event or as a spatially and temporally constrained state that varies by anatomical niche, disease stage, and treatment context. To answer this question, we synthesize evidence across five levels—direct mechanistic evidence, spatial association evidence, temporal inference, biomarker discovery evidence, and early translational evidence—and use this evidence hierarchy to distinguish established mechanisms from emerging hypotheses, as summarized in Table 1. This approach addresses a substantive conceptual gap, as accumulating evidence from single‐cell, spatial, and in vivo lineage‐tracing studies demonstrates that macrophage subsets occupy distinct anatomical niches and undergo fate‐dependent functional transitions over time, particularly in response to ICB and cytotoxic therapies [20, 21, 22]. Spatially confined TAM populations, for example at tumor–stromal interfaces, perivascular regions, or sites of therapy‐induced cell death, may exhibit disproportionately high efferocytic activity and exert localized immunosuppressive effects that shape regional immune exclusion or T‐cell dysfunction [23, 24]. Similarly, temporal fluctuations in efferocytosis following chemotherapy or radiotherapy can generate transient windows of enhanced antigen availability or, conversely, periods of heightened immune tolerance [25, 26]. Accordingly, this review further provides a niche‐based model for interpreting efferocytosis “hotspots” and “deserts,” clarifies when efferocytosis is likely to be biomarker‐relevant, and identifies the most realistic translational route for testing whether spatially restricted or time‐dependent modulation of efferocytosis can improve immunotherapy response. Integrating these spatial and temporal dimensions is therefore essential for a mechanistic and translational understanding of how efferocytosis influences clinical outcomes.

This review defines a clear conceptual scope supported by an explicit hierarchy of evidence. We distinguish five complementary levels: (i) direct mechanistic evidence, derived from genetic perturbation, in vitro systems, and in vivo functional studies that establish causal roles for efferocytosis; (ii) spatial association evidence, obtained from spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed imaging platforms that localize efferocytic macrophages within defined anatomical niches and characterize their cellular interactions; (iii) temporal inference, inferred from longitudinal sampling, lineage‐tracing approaches, and treatment‐perturbation models that capture dynamic changes during tumor progression and therapy; (iv) biomarker discovery evidence, encompassing transcriptomic signatures and spatially resolved molecular programs associated with clinical outcomes; and (v) early translational evidence, including preclinical targeting strategies and emerging clinical correlations that suggest, but do not yet establish, biomarker utility. By organizing current evidence according to this hierarchy, we aim to distinguish causally supported mechanisms from spatial correlations and hypothesis‐generating observations, thereby providing a rigorous framework for interpreting the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of macrophage efferocytosis and its potential relevance to biomarker‐driven immunotherapy.

Molecular and Cellular Basis of Efferocytosis

Macrophage efferocytosis constitutes a highly coordinated molecular and cellular program that integrates apoptotic cell recognition, cytoskeletal reorganization, and transcriptional reprogramming to achieve immunologically silent clearance [14], as summarized in Figure 1. Before receptor‐mediated recognition, apoptotic cells also emit soluble “find‐me” signals that recruit professional phagocytes to sites of cell death. Early apoptotic cells release nucleotides, especially ATP and UTP, through caspase‐activated pannexin 1 channels, thereby promoting P2Y2‐dependent migration of monocytes and macrophages toward dying cells [33]. As apoptotic‐cell clearance proceeds, exposed phosphatidylserine (PS) then serves as a major “eat‐me” signal that facilitates recognition and engulfment by phagocytes [34]. PS on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane is detected either directly by PS‐binding receptors, including members of the TIM family and Stabilin‐2, or indirectly through bridging molecules such as Gas6, Protein S, and Milk fat globule‐EGF factor 8 (MFG‐E8) [35]. Gas6 and Protein S couple PS exposure to TAM family receptor tyrosine kinases, including MerTK, Axl, and Tyro3, whereas MFG‐E8 primarily bridges PS to integrins such as αvβ3 and αvβ5 on phagocytes. Engagement of these receptors not only initiates engulfment but also activates immunoregulatory signaling cascades, including PI3K/AKT and STAT3, that drive macrophages toward an anti‐inflammatory, tissue‐repair phenotype characterized by sustained production of IL‐10 and TGF‐β [36, 37]. Within the TME, these canonical signaling modules are further rewired by tumor‐derived cues‐including hypoxia, lactate accumulation, and oncogenic cytokine gradients‐thereby giving rise to distinct transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming that reinforces immune tolerance beyond physiological efferocytosis. For example, TAM receptor signaling in tumors is frequently coupled to sustained STAT3 activation, lipid metabolic adaptation, and PD‐L1 upregulation, collectively indicating that efferocytosis in cancer represents a quantitatively and qualitatively distinct state rather than a mere extension of homeostatic clearance [38]. Recent comprehensive reviews further emphasize that macrophage‐mediated immunosuppression extends beyond efferocytosis alone and involves coordinated interactions among myeloid‐cell subsets, metabolic remodeling, and immune checkpoint signaling that together shape resistance to cancer immunotherapy [6, 39]. Counterbalancing this process, “don't‐eat‐me” signals such as CD47 expressed on viable or stressed tumor cells bind to SIRPα on macrophages, imposing an inhibitory checkpoint that limits inappropriate phagocytosis [40]. The dynamic interplay between PS‐mediated activation and CD47‐dependent inhibition therefore calibrates efferocytic efficiency within distinct tumor niches and exerts a decisive influence on the immunological tone of the TME. In parallel, accumulating studies have highlighted the CD47–SIRPα axis as a central macrophage immune checkpoint and an important therapeutic target whose clinical development is increasingly focused on improving efficacy while minimizing hematologic toxicity [41, 42].

Following receptor engagement, efferocytosis proceeds through cytoskeletal remodeling mechanisms that translate ligand binding into physical engulfment. The ELMO–DOCK180–Rac1 signaling axis serves as a central effector, promoting actin polymerization and phagocytic cup formation, thereby enabling internalization of apoptotic cargo into specialized efferosomes [43]. These vesicles subsequently mature and fuse with lysosomes, ensuring efficient degradation and metabolic recycling of cellular constituents. A defining feature distinguishing efferocytosis from canonical phagocytosis is the involvement of LAP, a noncanonical autophagy‐related pathway in which LC3 is conjugated to the phagosomal membrane, accelerating vesicle maturation while restraining pro‐inflammatory signaling [44, 45]. In contrast to classical phagocytosis, which may promote inflammasome activation and immunogenic antigen presentation, LAP‐dependent efferocytosis limits the release of danger‐associated molecular patterns and suppresses type I interferon responses, thereby favoring immune tolerance [36]. Notably, experimental ablation of LAP components, including Rubicon or ATG5, converts efferocytosis into an immunostimulatory process that enhances tumor antigen cross‐presentation and potentiates CD8+ T‐cell priming in vivo, underscoring the qualitative nature of efferocytic pathways in shaping antitumor immunity [46]. Thus, LAP‐dependent and classical phagocytic pathways should be considered functionally distinct because they differentially regulate antigen processing, interferon signaling, and cross‐presentation. In the TME, emerging evidence further suggests that LAP‐dominant efferocytosis preferentially enforces immune silencing, whereas disruption of LAP or engagement of alternative phagocytic routes can reprogram macrophages toward a more immunogenic phenotype, highlighting the importance of distinguishing canonical versus noncanonical efferocytic programs in tumor contexts [47].

Although efferocytosis predominantly promotes immune tolerance through the induction of anti‐inflammatory macrophage programs, it can also contribute to immunogenic responses under specific therapeutic and molecular contexts. In many tumor settings, efferocytic clearance establishes an immunosuppressive state characterized by increased production of IL‐10 and TGF‐β, reduced major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II expression, and enhanced PD‐L1 expression, collectively resulting in impaired T‐cell activation and reinforcement of immune escape [13, 38]. However, when apoptotic tumor cells arise from immunogenic cell death‐inducing therapies, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, the uptake and processing of tumor‐derived material by professional antigen‐presenting cells, particularly conventional dendritic cells, may promote MHC class I–mediated cross‐presentation and activation of tumor‐reactive CD8+ T cells [48]. This immunogenic outcome is frequently associated with type I interferon signaling and induction of T‐cell‐recruiting chemokines, including CXCL9 and CXCL10, which promote cytotoxic lymphocyte recruitment and expansion [49]. Thus, the consequences of tumor cell efferocytosis vary substantially across biological settings and may range from immunological tolerance, characterized by IL‐10, TGF‐β, MHC class II suppression, and PD‐L1 induction, to immune activation when antigen cross‐presentation and inflammatory signaling pathways are appropriately engaged.

Molecular, imaging, and transcriptomic approaches have improved the detection and functional characterization of efferocytosis across experimental systems. These integrated strategies have begun to reveal that efferocytic signatures in tumors diverge substantially from those observed under physiological conditions, reflecting tumor‐specific reprogramming rather than conserved baseline pathways. In vitro systems frequently employ fluorescently labeled apoptotic targets and PS‐binding probes such as annexin V to quantify engulfment dynamics and delineate receptor dependencies, while genetic or pharmacological perturbation of TAM receptors or CD47–SIRPα interactions provides mechanistic insight [50, 51, 52]. In vivo, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplexed imaging modalities, including imaging mass cytometry (IMC) and high‐resolution confocal microscopy, enable spatial localization of efferocytic macrophages through co‐detection of lineage markers (CD68, F4/80), TAM receptors, and LC3 recruitment to phagosomal membranes [53, 54, 55]. These approaches have revealed preferential accumulation of efferocytic macrophages at tumor–stromal boundaries and within regions of therapy‐induced apoptosis, highlighting pronounced spatial heterogeneity with direct implications for localized immunosuppression. In human malignancies, coordinated transcriptional programs encompassing MERTK, GAS6, MFGE8, IL‐10, and STAT3 target genes define efferocytosis macrophage subsets that correlate with diminished cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration and poor responsiveness to ICB [56, 57]. The convergence of single‐cell RNA sequencing with spatial transcriptomics now enables resolution of efferocytic macrophage states within defined anatomical niches, establishing a quantitative framework for biomarker‐driven patient stratification and rational design of immunotherapeutic combinations [58, 59]. Spatial Heterogeneity—Evidence and Methodologies

Macrophage efferocytosis is increasingly recognized as a spatially organized and context‐dependent process within the TME, rather than a uniformly distributed clearance mechanism [15]. Recent advances in spatial transcriptomics and high‐dimensional tissue imaging have revealed that TAMs occupy discrete anatomical niches, including tumor–stromal interfaces, perivascular regions, hypoxic cores, and sites of therapy‐induced apoptosis, where efferocytic activity and immunoregulatory programs are differentially enriched [23, 69]. Spatial transcriptomics platforms such as 10× Genomics Visium and Visium HD enable the mapping of gene expression patterns across intact tissue sections. A comparison of currently available spatial platforms, including their resolutions, molecular readouts, strengths, limitations, and potential applications for studying efferocytosis, is summarized in Table 3. Standard Visium provides spot‐level transcriptomic profiles containing multiple cells per spot, whereas Visium HD approaches single‐cell–scale mapping but still requires computational strategies such as binning, deconvolution, or segmentation for accurate transcript assignment. In contrast, IMC and co‐detection by indexing (CODEX) are protein‐based multiplexed imaging approaches, whereas Spatial Molecular Imager (CosMx) spatial molecular imaging is a high‐plex spatial platform primarily centered on RNA detection, with optional same‐cell protein readouts. These platforms allow simultaneous detection of multiple protein markers at subcellular resolution, enabling direct visualization of TAM subsets, apoptotic targets, and their spatial relationships with T lymphocytes and stromal elements [70, 71]. While these imaging‐based approaches offer superior spatial fidelity and allow detailed interrogation of cell–cell interactions, they are limited by antibody panel design, constrained whole‐transcriptome coverage (for RNA‐based platforms), and high technical complexity [72, 73]. Spatial proteomics and cyclic immunofluorescence further complement these techniques by enabling quantitative assessment of efferocytic receptors and downstream signaling mediators, such as phosphorylated AKT and STAT3, within defined microanatomical contexts, collectively providing a multimodal framework to capture the spatial heterogeneity of efferocytosis across tumors [74, 75]. Such spatially resolved analyses further facilitate the discrimination of distinct efferocytic programs, including canonical receptor‐mediated clearance and noncanonical pathways such as LAP, which may be preferentially enriched within specific anatomical niches. Taken together, these spatial data explain where candidate efferocytic states occur, but they do not by themselves establish whether those states are stable, transitional, or therapy‐induced. These observations are consistent with recent reviews emphasizing that spatial immune remodeling emerges from coordinated interactions among immune, stromal, and malignant cell populations and therefore requires integrative spatial profiling to resolve functionally distinct immune niches rather than isolated cell states [76].

Integration of these technologies across preclinical and human studies has begun to define conserved spatial patterns of efferocytic TAM enrichment and their functional consequences for local immune regulation. In murine tumor models, TAM populations expressing high levels of MerTK and Gas6 preferentially accumulate in perivascular and necrotic regions, where apoptotic burden is elevated and access to circulating monocytes and growth factors is enhanced [77, 78]. These niches exhibit coordinated upregulation of immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL‐10, and reduced expression of antigen presentation machinery, resulting in localized exclusion or dysfunction of cytotoxic T cells [79]. In human malignancies, spatial transcriptomic analyses of lung, breast, gastric, and pancreatic cancers have revealed clustering of efferocytosis‐associated gene signatures at tumor margins and within tertiary lymphoid structure–adjacent regions, where macrophages frequently co‐localize with fibroblasts and endothelial cells and engage in extensive ligand–receptor communication networks [80, 81]. IMC studies further reveal that TAM subsets expressing STAB1, MerTK, and MFG‐E8 are enriched at tumor interfaces, forming immune‐exclusion barriers that impair CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and contribute to a cold immune phenotype, correlating with poor clinical outcomes [82, 83, 84].

Clinically, these spatial configurations have been linked to adverse outcomes, including diminished responsiveness to ICB and increased likelihood of disease progression, suggesting that the anatomical positioning of efferocytic macrophages may serve as a biomarker of regional immune competence [85]. Conversely, in responder cohorts, macrophage populations with lower efferocytic signatures and higher expression of antigen‐presenting and interferon‐responsive genes are enriched within intratumoral niches that support sustained T‐cell–macrophage interactions, underscoring the prognostic and predictive value of spatially resolved efferocytosis profiling [86, 87].

These recent data support a conceptual model in which tumors are organized into “efferocytosis hotspots” and “efferocytosis deserts,” reflecting localized zones of high or low apoptotic clearance activity with distinct immunological consequences [88], as proposed in the conceptual framework illustrated in Figure 2. Importantly, these terms are introduced here as a hypothesis‐generating conceptual framework to facilitate interpretation of spatial heterogeneity rather than as discrete biological entities. Current evidence primarily derives from spatial association studies and preclinical models, whereas direct functional validation and prospective clinical confirmation remain limited. While these spatial patterns define where candidate efferocytic states occur, complementary longitudinal sampling, lineage‐tracing approaches, and treatment‐perturbation models are required to determine whether such states are stable, transitional, or therapy‐induced.

Proposed spatial and temporal organization of efferocytosis‐associated hotspots and immune niches in the TME. Tumor cell death generates apoptotic substrates that accumulate in hypoxic, necrotic, and treatment‐induced regions, where efferocytic TAMs expressing MerTK and STAB1 preferentially localize. Following chemotherapy or radiotherapy, increased apoptotic burden transiently enhances macrophage efferocytic activity, whereas sustained efferocytosis may promote regulatory TAM states associated with TGF‐β signaling and immunosuppression. In contrast, regions with preserved antigen presentation, dendritic cell activation, and CD8+ T‐cell recruitment represent immune‐active niches. The model illustrates that the functional consequences of tumor efferocytosis depend on the spatial localization and temporal context of apoptotic cell clearance.

These high‐clearance regions are characterized by dense accumulation of MerTK+, Axl+, and STAB1+ TAMs, elevated expression of bridging molecules such as Gas6 and MFG‐E8, and activation of downstream PI3K/AKT and STAT3 signaling pathways that reinforce immune tolerance [62, 89]. Within these regions, rapid clearance of apoptotic cells limits the release of damage‐associated molecular patterns and suppresses type I interferon signaling, thereby constraining dendritic cell licensing and attenuating local T‐cell priming [64]. In contrast, efferocytosis deserts exhibit reduced TAM density or altered efferocytic programming, frequently coinciding with enhanced antigen persistence, increased expression of MHC molecules, and heightened cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration [90]. Experimental disruption of LAP or pharmacological inhibition of TAM receptors has been shown to reprogram tolerogenic efferocytic responses toward inflammatory and antigen‐presenting states, enhancing type I interferon signaling, CD8+ T‐cell activation, and responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade in preclinical tumor models [91, 92]. Translationally, this model provides a rationale for biomarker‐driven immunotherapy strategies that target efferocytosis selectively within defined tumor niches, rather than globally suppressing macrophage function. By integrating spatial transcriptomic and proteomic signatures with clinical outcome data, it may be possible to stratify patients based on the distribution and functional state of efferocytic macrophages, identify windows of therapeutic vulnerability following cytotoxic or radiation‐induced tumor cell death, and design temporally optimized combination regimens that reprogram local immune landscapes to favor durable antitumor immunity.

Temporal Heterogeneity and Its Evolution Following Therapeutic Intervention

In the treatment‐naive setting, efferocytosis is embedded within a highly organized and spatially constrained macrophage network that preferentially enforces immune tolerance rather than immune activation [103]. High‐dimensional single‐cell and spatial profiling across multiple solid tumor types consistently shows that TAMs enriched for PS‐recognition receptors, TAM family kinases, and downstream anti‐inflammatory programs are concentrated in perivascular zones and tumor–stromal interfaces, where apoptotic tumor cells and stromal debris accumulate under hypoxic and metabolic stress [13, 104]. Within these niches, efferocytic macrophages display coordinated upregulation of IL‐10, TGF‐β1, SELENOP, and lipid‐handling pathways, coupled to repression of MHC class II and antigen‐presentation machinery, thereby constraining cytotoxic T‐cell priming and stabilizing regulatory immune circuits [105]. In vivo fate‐mapping further reveals that newly recruited monocyte‐derived macrophages initially acquire transient inflammatory features upon tumor entry but rapidly transition toward an efferocytic, PD‐L1–high, and metabolically adapted state as their residence time within the TME increases [106, 107]. This baseline configuration establishes a permissive equilibrium in which apoptotic cell clearance proceeds efficiently yet silently, preventing the release of damage‐associated molecular patterns and limiting the availability of tumor antigens for cross‐presentation by dendritic cells [37, 108]. Across the natural trajectory of tumor progression, accumulating evidence indicates that the functional role of efferocytosis is dynamically reprogrammed in a stage‐dependent manner: in early tumorigenesis and pre‐malignant lesions, efferocytosis primarily contributes to tissue homeostasis and inflammatory resolution; as tumors advance to carcinoma in situ and invasive stages, escalating apoptotic burden and stromal remodeling reinforce immunosuppressive efferocytic programs; in advanced and metastatic disease, efferocytosis is further co‐opted to promote immune evasion, metastatic niche conditioning, and systemic immune tolerance [89]. This temporal axis adds a disease‐course explanation that spatial data alone cannot provide: the same process can appear homeostatic, suppressive, or metastasis‐promoting depending on when it is observed.

Cytotoxic and immune‐based therapies markedly alter this baseline state by acutely increasing tumor cell death and generating a surge of apoptotic and stressed cells that must be processed by the phagocytic compartment [36, 109]. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are associated with a short‐lived efferocytic peak characterized by macrophage clustering at necrotic–apoptotic boundaries and upregulation of MerTK‐dependent phagocytic programs and LC3‐associated processing pathways [25, 110]. Spatial transcriptomic analyses of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in non‐small cell lung cancer illustrate that responder tumors develop localized microdomains in which efferocytosis‐associated macrophage subsets co‐localize with antigen‐presenting fibroblasts and lymphocyte aggregates, coinciding with enhanced chemokine gradients and increased CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell infiltration [27]. By contrast, non‐responder lesions are dominated by matrix‐associated, TGF‐β–driven macrophage phenotypes that spatially segregate efferocytic activity from T‐cell–rich regions, effectively uncoupling apoptotic cell clearance from adaptive immune engagement [85, 111]. Over extended time scales, sustained high‐volume efferocytosis promotes immunometabolic remodeling of macrophages, including lipid accumulation, mitochondrial reprogramming, and epigenetic stabilization of anti‐inflammatory transcriptional states, reinforcing PD‐L1 expression and attenuating interferon‐driven signaling pathways [112, 113].

A further manifestation of efferocytosis‐associated metabolic adaptation is the emergence of lipid‐associated macrophage (LAM)‐like states within the TME. Continuous acquisition of apoptotic material provides macrophages with abundant lipid substrates, promoting intracellular lipid accumulation, cholesterol handling programs, and transcriptional remodeling associated with tissue adaptation and immune regulation [15]. Recent single‐cell studies have identified TREM2‐expressing macrophage populations as a conserved lipid‐handling macrophage state across pathological contexts, characterized by increased expression of genes involved in lipid uptake, cholesterol metabolism, and phagocytic function, including TREM2, APOE, LPL, and LGALS3 [114, 115]. In tumors, TREM2+ LAM‐like macrophages have been associated with immunosuppressive microenvironmental features, reduced T‐cell activity, and resistance to immunotherapy [116]. Mechanistically, the TREM2–APOE axis regulates macrophage metabolic fitness and survival under lipid‐rich conditions, enabling persistent macrophage adaptation within tumor niches [115, 117]. These findings suggest that efferocytosis‐driven lipid remodeling does not merely represent a metabolic consequence of corpse clearance but may actively reinforce macrophage states that support immune escape and therapeutic resistance. Together, these immunometabolic adaptations provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how initial therapeutic cytotoxicity can evolve into an immune‐refractory microenvironment. Accordingly, the temporal dimension identifies when biomarker candidates are most likely to be informative and when prospective validation is required to distinguish stable programs from therapy‐induced states.

Together, these data emphasize that the immunological impact of efferocytosis is critically dependent on treatment timing and spatial context rather than on pathway activity alone. Immediate and indiscriminate inhibition of apoptotic cell clearance following radiotherapy or intensive chemotherapy can precipitate secondary necrosis and excessive inflammatory remodeling, thereby disrupting antigen trafficking and impairing lymphocyte infiltration [118]. In contrast, delayed or spatially restricted modulation of MerTK, Axl, or CD47–SIRPα signaling during the resolution phase of tumor cell death can extend the window of antigen availability for cross‐presentation and potentiate dendritic cell activation in concert with ICB [119]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that temporally staged combinations of PD‐1 or PD‐L1 inhibitors with efferocytosis‐targeted interventions sustain interferon‐driven chemokine feedback loops and prolong intratumoral T‐cell expansion, whereas continuous phagocytosis inhibition compromises therapeutic selectivity and increases systemic toxicity [120, 121]. Early translational analyses further indicate that baseline efferocytosis‐related gene signatures and the spatial co‐localization of efferocytic macrophages with lymphocyte niches correlate with clinical responsiveness to ICIs in lung and gastric cancers [32, 122]. Framing efferocytosis as a temporally gated and spatially constrained immunoregulatory axis therefore establishes a clinically actionable therapeutic window in which modulation of apoptotic cell clearance can amplify, rather than suppress, adaptive antitumor immunity when integrated with rational treatment sequencing and biomarker‐guided patient stratification [61, 123]. Current conceptual frameworks suggest that immune ecosystem dynamics are governed by continuous reciprocal interactions among macrophages, lymphocytes, stromal cells, and therapy‐induced environmental perturbations, reinforcing the need to interpret efferocytosis within a dynamically evolving immune ecosystem rather than as an isolated macrophage program [124].

Organ‐Specific Heterogeneity in the Context of Tumors

Macrophage efferocytosis varies across tumor sites according to tissue‐resident macrophage lineages, stromal architecture, and predominant modes of cell death [125, 126]. In the liver, the sinusoidal network and the presence of embryonically seeded Kupffer cells establish a clearance system that is intrinsically tolerogenic, favoring rapid PS‐dependent uptake of apoptotic tumor cells and circulating debris. Spatial and lineage‐tracing studies in hepatocellular carcinoma and liver metastasis models show that Kupffer‐like and monocyte‐derived macrophages preferentially occupy perivascular and peri‐sinusoidal niches, where MerTK‐ and Axl‐driven efferocytosis is coupled to sustained IL‐10 and TGF‐β production and repression of antigen presentation pathways, thereby limiting cytotoxic T‐cell recruitment and reinforcing immune privilege [127, 128, 129]. By contrast, the lung microenvironment, shaped by continuous antigenic exposure and a dense network of interstitial macrophages, exhibits a more heterogeneous efferocytic landscape [130]. High‐dimensional single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses of non‐small cell lung cancer identify discrete macrophage subsets enriched for SELENOP and lipid‐handling programs that accumulate at tumor margins and within tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), where efferocytosis coincides with antigen‐presenting fibroblasts and lymphocyte aggregates, indicating that apoptotic cell clearance can, in this context, facilitate adaptive immune engagement rather than uniformly enforce tolerance [27]. These divergent baseline states indicate that organ‐specific macrophage ontogeny and niche organization shape the immunological consequences of efferocytosis before therapeutic intervention.

Further distinctions emerge in tissues characterized by extreme stromal density or immune restriction. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the desmoplastic matrix and hypovascular architecture impose spatial confinement on both dying tumor cells and phagocytes, favoring prolonged corpse persistence and chronic engagement of TAM receptor signaling in infiltrating monocyte‐derived macrophages [15, 131]. Functional studies link this sustained efferocytic state to immunometabolic remodeling, lipid accumulation, and epigenetic stabilization of anti‐inflammatory transcriptional programs, together reinforcing T‐cell exclusion and resistance to ICB [132, 133]. In the mammary gland, by contrast, macrophage populations display pronounced plasticity and dynamic interactions with epithelial and stromal compartments [134]. Single‐cell trajectory and network analyses in breast cancer segregate efferocytosis‐related gene programs among macrophage clusters with divergent differentiation states and signaling axes, including SPP1–CD44 and LGALS9–CD44 pathways, which modulate both tumor cell survival and patterns of immune infiltration [135]. The central nervous system represents a further extreme, where resident microglia and the blood–brain barrier impose stringent control over immune cell entry. In gliomas and brain metastases, microglial efferocytosis is closely coupled to tissue repair and synaptic pruning programs and is frequently uncoupled from efficient antigen cross‐presentation, thereby sustaining an immunologically “cold” milieu despite substantial tumor cell death [31, 136]. These organ‐specific configurations illustrate how the balance between tolerogenic repair and immunogenic clearance is tuned by local macrophage lineage and stromal context.

The divergent effects of efferocytosis across tumor types indicate that apoptotic cell clearance should not be interpreted as an intrinsically immunosuppressive or immunogenic process. Rather, its functional consequence is determined by the interaction between macrophage lineage, tissue architecture, apoptotic context, and the degree of coupling between clearance and antigen presentation. In pancreatic and liver cancers, where stromal restriction, tissue‐resident macrophage programs, and chronic apoptotic stress predominate, persistent efferocytosis is frequently associated with IL‐10/TGF‐β‐dominant macrophage states, impaired antigen presentation, and T‐cell exclusion [137, 138]. Conversely, in subsets of non‐small cell lung cancer, efferocytic macrophage states located near tertiary lymphoid structures may coexist with antigen‐presenting niches and enhanced lymphocyte recruitment, suggesting that clearance activity can be compatible with immune activation when apoptotic material is efficiently coupled to antigen presentation pathways [27, 139]. These findings reveal that the biological meaning of efferocytosis is context‐dependent: the same clearance program may reinforce immune tolerance when spatially separated from antigen‐presenting circuits but may contribute to antitumor immunity when integrated within immune‐active niches.

These biological distinctions have direct implications for therapeutic targeting of efferocytosis in cancer. In pancreatic cancer models, selective disruption of macrophage‐derived signals associated with apoptotic cell clearance restores CD8+ T‐cell function and significantly reduces hepatic metastatic burden, highlighting heightened sensitivity of pancreatic and liver niches to modulation of this pathway [15]. Conversely, in lung tumors, spatial analyses indicate that efferocytosis‐associated macrophages co‐localizing with lymphoid structures correlate with favorable responses to chemoimmunotherapy, suggesting that indiscriminate inhibition of apoptotic cell uptake in this setting may inadvertently blunt beneficial antigen presentation loops [25, 140]. Therapeutic strategies directed at the CD47–SIRPα axis further emphasize the need for organ‐aware deployment, as systemic blockade can overcome phagocytic checkpoints but is constrained by hematologic toxicity and variable tissue efficacy, driving the development of bispecific and conditionally active agents designed to restrict activity to the TME [141, 142, 143]. Integrating organ specificity into biomarker development, including spatial mapping of efferocytic macrophage subsets and efferocytosis‐related gene signatures, therefore provides a rational approach for patient stratification and for optimizing the timing and anatomical targeting of efferocytosis‐modulating interventions across distinct tumor types.

Candidate Biomarker Panels and Multi‐Omic Signatures

Biomarker development for efferocytosis‐centered immunotherapy requires moving beyond single‐analyte measurements toward integrated multimodal panels capable of capturing the molecular, metabolic, spatial, and systemic dimensions of apoptotic cell clearance. At the tissue level, IHC and multiplexed imaging platforms can be leveraged to operationalize core efferocytic receptors and bridging molecules, including MerTK, Axl, STAB1, MFG‐E8, and Gas6, in parallel with surrogate indicators of PS exposure such as Annexin V staining, thereby defining a baseline index of “efferocytic competence” within tumor‐associated macrophage populations [13, 144]. These protein‐level readouts gain substantial interpretive value when combined with transcriptional signatures derived from curated efferocytosis gene sets encompassing regulators of LAP, Rubicon‐dependent trafficking, and phagosome maturation, which have been shown to stratify prognosis and immune infiltration patterns in gastric and breast cancers [30, 67]. Combining phenotypic and transcriptomic features allows identification of macrophage subsets that are not only actively engaged in apoptotic cell uptake but also transcriptionally stabilized in either tolerogenic or antigen‐processing states, supporting their deployment as screening biomarkers for identifying patients whose TME are predisposed toward immunosuppression or immune activation before the initiation of ICB therapy. Growing evidence from the immunotherapy biomarker field indicates that integrating spatial context, immune‐cell states, and multimodal molecular signatures is likely to improve patient stratification and therapeutic decision‐making beyond conventional single‐analyte biomarkers [145].

Metabolic and spatial parameters provide complementary biomarker information relevant to therapeutic sequencing and combination design. Tumor‐associated lactate accumulation and polyamine profiles, measurable in interstitial fluid or peripheral blood, reflect hypoxia‐driven immunometabolic remodeling that has been linked to macrophage polarization and attenuation of interferon‐dependent signaling within the tumor immune microenvironment [146, 147, 148]. These metabolic indicators can be systematically integrated with spatial metrics derived from spatial transcriptomics and IMC to generate an “efferocytosis hotspot score,” defined by the density and anatomical co‐localization of efferocytic macrophages with antigen‐presenting fibroblasts, vascular niches, and lymphocyte aggregates. In non‐small cell lung cancer, for instance, SELENOP‐positive macrophages enriched at tumor margins and within TLSs have been shown to spatially coincide with enhanced T‐cell recruitment and favorable responses to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy [27]. Such spatially resolved signatures provide a mechanistic rationale for guiding therapeutic timing, as tumors characterized by compartmentalized efferocytosis in proximity to lymphoid niches may preferentially benefit from delayed or regionally constrained modulation of MerTK, Axl, or CD47–SIRPα signaling to prolong antigen availability and potentiate immune checkpoint inhibition [38, 149], whereas diffuse, matrix‐associated efferocytosis may predict primary resistance and justify early integration of stroma‐targeting or metabolic interventions [103].

To achieve clinical translatability, this biomarker architecture must extend beyond tissue‐based assays to incorporate circulating readouts that enable longitudinal monitoring and adaptive trial integration. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and extracellular vesicles bearing PS‐positive membrane fragments provide minimally invasive surrogates of systemic apoptotic burden and efferocytic flux, while plasma Gas6 concentrations offer a dynamic indicator of TAM receptor pathway engagement and pharmacodynamic modulation [63, 150]. The validation trajectory for such composite panels should follow a staged and rigorous framework, beginning with model derivation in publicly available single‐cell and spatial datasets, proceeding through internal validation in independent retrospective cohorts, and culminating in prospective, biomarker‐enriched clinical trial arms in which candidate signatures are embedded as predefined secondary endpoints. The translational feasibility of this approach is supported by convergent evidence that efferocytosis‐related gene signatures and the spatial co‐localization of macrophages with lymphocyte niches correlate with clinical responsiveness to ICB across lung, gastric, and breast malignancies [23, 139, 151]. By integrating tissue, metabolic, spatial, and circulating dimensions into a unified analytical pipeline, this strategy elevates efferocytosis from a mechanistic hallmark of immune regulation to a quantifiable and clinically actionable axis for patient stratification, rational combination design, and adaptive governance in precision immuno‐oncology.

Clinically, these biomarker layers could serve distinct but complementary functions across the treatment pathway. Pretreatment tissue‐based and spatial signatures could be used to enrich for tumors with high efferocytic activity or immunosuppressive macrophage niches, thereby defining patient populations most likely to benefit from MerTK‐, Axl‐, or phagocytic checkpoint‐directed interventions. Longitudinal circulating or on‐treatment tissue readouts could subsequently capture therapy‐induced changes in apoptotic burden and macrophage state, providing a rationale for timing efferocytosis modulation relative to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or ICB rather than relying on fixed concurrent administration. In combination immunotherapy, efferocytosis‐associated biomarkers could help distinguish tumors in which suppressive apoptotic‐cell clearance is likely to constrain antigen presentation and T‐cell recruitment, thereby prioritizing combinations with ICB or other macrophage‐reprogramming strategies. Clinically, these hypotheses should be tested in biomarker‐enriched, window‐of‐opportunity or adaptive trials that incorporate pretreatment and on‐treatment sampling and prospectively defined spatial, pharmacodynamic, and immune‐response endpoints, allowing candidate biomarkers to be evaluated simultaneously for patient selection, treatment sequencing, and combination refinement.

Translational Trial Designs and Combination Strategies

Recognition that macrophage efferocytosis is spatially and temporally regulated within the TME supports clinical trial designs that move beyond biomarker‐agnostic randomization toward mechanism‐based patient selection. A biomarker‐enriched randomized design can operationalize this concept by limiting eligibility to patients whose pretreatment biopsies exhibit a high efferocytosis hotspot score, defined by the spatial co‐localization of efferocytic macrophage subsets with apoptotic tumor cells and lymphoid or stromal niches, as resolved by spatial transcriptomics or multiplexed tissue imaging platforms. Within such a design, participants may be randomized to receive ICB alone or in combination with a MerTK or Axl inhibitor, allowing direct evaluation of whether selective modulation of apoptotic cell clearance amplifies adaptive immune engagement in a biologically primed microenvironment. This strategy is consistent with spatial and temporal lineage‐tracing studies demonstrating that macrophage subsets residing at tumor–stromal interfaces or within perivascular niches display heightened sensitivity to PD‐L1 blockade and undergo distinct differentiation trajectories, indicating that anatomical context conditions therapeutic responsiveness [20]. Incorporating spatial metrics and efferocytosis‐related transcriptional signatures as stratification variables or secondary endpoints further strengthens mechanistic interpretation by linking pathway modulation to immune redistribution and clinical outcomes. Recent literature on macrophage‐targeted immunotherapy supports the concept that therapeutic reprogramming of TAMs, rather than indiscriminate macrophage depletion, may provide superior efficacy while preserving tissue homeostasis [9]. Window‐of‐opportunity studies provide a complementary approach to interrogate efferocytosis as a pharmacodynamic axis in human tumors. In resectable disease settings, short‐term preoperative administration of MerTK or Axl inhibitors, either alone or in combination with ICB, can be paired with serial tumor sampling to quantify changes in macrophage phagocytic programs, antigen presentation machinery, and T‐cell infiltration at both histological and transcriptional resolution [152, 153]. The feasibility of this paradigm is supported by neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy studies in non‐small cell lung cancer, in which spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed the therapy‐induced accumulation of SELENOP‐positive macrophages within TLSs and enhanced interactions with antigen‐presenting fibroblasts and T‐cell populations in clinical responders [27]. Applying a similar framework to efferocytosis‐focused interventions enables the dissection of whether transient interference with apoptotic cell clearance reprograms macrophage–lymphocyte crosstalk toward immunogenicity or instead triggers compensatory tolerogenic circuits. Incorporating high‐dimensional imaging and immune profiling as predefined endpoints allows early‐phase trials to generate mechanistic insights that can be directly translated into rational dose, schedule, and combination refinement.

Temporal sequencing and route of delivery represent additional design dimensions with substantial implications for safety and efficacy. Preclinical evidence indicates that immediate, systemic inhibition of efferocytosis following radiotherapy or chemotherapy can exacerbate secondary necrosis and off‐tumor toxicity, whereas delayed or spatially restricted modulation can prolong antigen availability and enhance CD8+ T‐cell priming, particularly in the context of ICB [154, 155]. These observations motivate randomized sequencing trials in which efferocytosis‐targeted agents are administered either concurrently with cytotoxic therapy or during a defined post‐treatment resolution phase, with integrated immunological and clinical endpoints, including immune infiltration, interferon‐driven chemokine gradients, and pathological response, used to evaluate therapeutic impact. Parallel evaluation of local versus systemic delivery strategies, such as intratumoral administration or nanoparticle‐based carriers, directly addresses the hematologic and on‐target toxicities that have limited the clinical development of phagocytosis‐modulating therapies, including CD47–SIRPα blockade [143, 156]. Tumor‐restricted delivery approaches offer the prospect of concentrating efferocytosis modulation within immunologically active niches while preserving homeostatic clearance in normal tissues.

Beyond conventional systemic administration, tumor‐restricted delivery strategies represent one of the most promising approaches for improving the therapeutic index of efferocytosis‐targeted therapies by minimizing off‐target immune perturbation while preserving physiological efferocytosis in healthy tissues. Local intratumoral administration, injectable biomaterial depots, and sustained‐release formulations have demonstrated the capacity to concentrate immunomodulatory agents within the TME, thereby enhancing local efficacy while reducing systemic exposure and treatment‐related toxicity [157, 158]. Nanoparticle‐based delivery systems further improve tumor selectivity by exploiting enhanced permeability and retention effects, abnormal tumor vasculature, and TAM‐selective targeting strategies to preferentially deliver therapeutic cargos to TAMs [159, 160]. In parallel, antibody–drug conjugates and macrophage‐targeted bispecific antibodies provide complementary strategies that spatially restrict target engagement to malignant tissues or specific macrophage subsets, thereby reducing systemic inhibition of physiological phagocytic pathways [161, 162]. Incorporation of spatial biomarkers, including the density and localization of MerTK+, Axl+, and STAB1+ efferocytic macrophages, may further facilitate patient selection for localized interventions and identify tumors most likely to benefit from spatially confined modulation of efferocytosis [22, 23]. Collectively, these advances support a precision immunotherapy framework in which efferocytosis is modulated through biomarker‐guided and spatially restricted intervention rather than indiscriminate systemic blockade. This perspective is further supported by recent reviews proposing that precision immunotherapy should integrate spatial organization, dynamic immune‐state transitions, and biomarker‐guided patient stratification to optimize therapeutic timing and combination strategies across tumor types [163]. However, predictive biomarkers that reliably identify patients suitable for spatially confined modulation remain insufficiently validated. In addition, it remains unresolved whether spatially defined efferocytic macrophage states represent stable biological programs, transient therapy‐induced adaptations, or dynamic transitions between functional phenotypes. Resolving these questions will require longitudinal sampling combined with spatially resolved functional analyses in prospective clinical cohorts.

Challenges, Limitations, and Knowledge Gaps

The clinical implementation of efferocytosis‐centered biomarkers and therapeutic strategies is limited by persistent challenges in sample acquisition and temporal alignment, as most spatial and single‐cell profiling efforts remain anchored to single‐time‐point biopsies that only partially capture the dynamic remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [36, 164, 165]. Serial tumor sampling is inherently invasive and ethically constrained, and is therefore largely confined to resectable disease or window‐of‐opportunity trial designs, resulting in the systematic underrepresentation of advanced and metastatic settings. Even when longitudinal material can be obtained, variability in pre‐analytical handling, ischemia time, fixation, and preservation protocols introduces technical noise that can obscure true biological signal, particularly for labile readouts such as PS exposure, TAM receptor activation states, and metabolite profiles. Spatial profiling technologies introduce additional layers of complexity, as platform‐specific chemistries, antibody panels, image segmentation strategies, and computational pipelines can yield divergent cell‐state annotations and neighborhood metrics across IMC, CODEX, and spatial transcriptomics [166, 167]. These batch effects and methodological inconsistencies impede cross‐cohort comparability and limit the transferability of efferocytosis‐related gene signatures and spatial “hotspot” scores between institutions and trial networks. Although statistical harmonization approaches, including empirical Bayes correction, mutual nearest‐neighbor alignment, and joint multimodal embedding, have demonstrated the capacity to mitigate such biases, their successful deployment depends on standardized metadata, harmonized sampling protocols, and consensus quality‐control benchmarks to ensure reproducibility and regulatory credibility [168, 169].

A complementary set of limitations arises from biological safety considerations and the incomplete translatability of preclinical models to human disease. Efferocytosis pathways are integral to physiological tissue homeostasis, and systemic perturbation—particularly of the CD47–SIRPα axis or TAM family kinases—has been associated with clinically significant on‐target, off‐tumor toxicities, including anemia and thrombocytopenia, thereby constraining the therapeutic index of these interventions [170]. Tumor‐restricted delivery strategies and conditionally active or bispecific formats offer a conceptual route to widening the safety window, yet robust, prospectively validated biomarkers capable of identifying patients in whom spatially confined modulation can be achieved remain insufficiently developed. In addition, it remains unresolved whether spatially defined efferocytic macrophage states represent stable biological programs, transient therapy‐induced adaptations, or dynamic transitions between functional phenotypes. Resolving these questions will require longitudinal sampling combined with spatially resolved functional analyses in prospective clinical cohorts. Furthermore, commonly used murine tumor models reproduce only a subset of the ontogenetic diversity, stromal architecture, and immune conditioning that shape macrophage efferocytosis in human cancers, limiting the predictive value of preclinical efficacy and safety signals [171]. Human tumors display substantially greater inter‐patient heterogeneity in macrophage lineage composition, metabolic state, and spatial organization, complicating the direct transfer of efferocytosis‐related transcriptional signatures and therapeutic sequencing paradigms derived from animal studies [172]. Bridging this gap will require rigorously standardized sampling standard operating procedures and multi‐institutional reference datasets that integrate tissue‐based, circulating, and imaging‐derived biomarkers within a unified analytical framework. From a statistical standpoint, hierarchical modeling and Bayesian adaptive trial designs that leverage priors derived from public single‐cell and spatial atlases can enhance power in biomarker‐enriched cohorts and support the iterative refinement of candidate signatures as evidence accumulates [173, 174].

Beyond these technical considerations, three practical issues remain major obstacles to clinical implementation. First, assay standardization remains insufficient across current efferocytosis studies. Different investigations employ distinct combinations of apoptotic‐cell substrates, receptor panels, imaging modalities, transcriptomic platforms, and computational algorithms, resulting in substantial variability in efferocytosis quantification and limiting cross‐study comparability [175]. Establishing consensus operating procedures, standardized marker panels, unified quality‐control criteria, and reproducible analytical pipelines will therefore be essential before efferocytosis‐related biomarkers can be incorporated into multicenter clinical studies. Second, spatial sampling bias represents an inherent challenge because efferocytosis preferentially occurs within discrete tumor niches rather than uniformly throughout the TME [15]. Single‐core biopsies or limited tissue sections may fail to capture immune‐active margins, hypoxic regions, tertiary lymphoid structures, or therapy‐induced apoptotic hotspots, thereby underestimating spatial heterogeneity and reducing biomarker reproducibility. Integrating multiregion sampling, image‐guided biopsy strategies, and spatially resolved molecular profiling may help mitigate these limitations. Third, biomarker validation should progress through a rigorous translational framework that includes independent external validation, prospective multicenter cohorts, standardized analytical workflows, and incorporation into biomarker‐enriched clinical trials. Importantly, future studies should evaluate not only analytical reproducibility but also clinical utility, including predictive performance for patient selection, treatment sequencing, and combination immunotherapy. Collectively, addressing assay standardization, spatial sampling bias, and prospective biomarker validation represents a prerequisite for translating efferocytosis from an experimentally observable biological process into a robust clinical biomarker platform. Together, these methodological and conceptual advances are critical for transforming efferocytosis from a compelling mechanistic construct into a rigorously validated and clinically actionable axis in precision immuno‐oncology.

Conclusions and Translational Outlook

Across diverse solid tumors, convergent evidence indicates that efferocytic programs are not intrinsically tolerogenic or immunogenic, but instead acquire functional meaning within defined anatomical niches and temporal windows that govern antigen availability, lymphocyte recruitment, and sensitivity to ICB. A critical near‐term objective is therefore the establishment of coordinated spatial–temporal patient cohorts in which pretreatment, on‐treatment, and post‐treatment samples are collected under harmonized standard operating procedures and interrogated using matched spatial transcriptomic and multiplexed imaging platforms. These efforts will enable the development of a standardized biomarker panel that integrates tissue‐based markers, efferocytosis‐related transcriptional modules linked to LC3‐associated processing and phagosome maturation, and circulating readouts such as extracellular vesicle‐associated PS and plasma Gas6, thereby promoting cross‐study comparability and facilitating regulatory alignment. In other words, the evidence currently supports biomarker discovery, but not yet routine biomarker deployment.

A pragmatic translational roadmap can therefore focus on two attainable milestones. First, the community should construct and publicly release an open, version‐controlled efferocytosis spatial signature derived from curated single‐cell and spatial reference atlases across multiple tumor indications, accompanied by standardized computational workflows for batch correction, joint embedding, and clinical‐grade quality control. Such a resource would establish a shared analytical framework for biomarker development and accelerate independent validation across institutions. Second, biomarker‐driven window‐of‐opportunity trials in at least two anatomically and immunologically distinct malignancies, such as non‐small cell lung cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, should be initiated to prospectively test whether transient, spatially informed modulation of TAM receptor signaling or phagocytic checkpoints can reprogram macrophage–lymphocyte crosstalk and improve pathological or molecular response endpoints. The incorporation of hierarchical and Bayesian adaptive designs within these trials will support the iterative refinement of candidate signatures and therapeutic schedules as mechanistic and clinical data accrue. This is the most realistic next step for converting spatial and temporal hypotheses into validated clinical tools.

Together, this action‐oriented strategy delineates a feasible pathway for translating efferocytosis from a compelling mechanistic construct into a rigorously validated, biomarker‐guided component of precision immuno‐oncology. What is already established is that efferocytosis is niche‐dependent and treatment‐sensitive; what looks most promising is spatially anchored biomarker development; and what still must be shown prospectively is whether the proposed hotspot–desert framework, introduced here as a hypothesis‐generating model, can be validated as a biologically generalizable and clinically actionable framework across diverse tumor types.
