Unipolar electrogram-guided vs. ablation index-guided high-power short-duration pulmonary vein isolation in patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation: a single-center randomized controlled trial
Authors: Wei-Wei Wang, Si-Qi Li, Hui-Zhong Lin, Qiong Jiang, Xu-Dong Sun, Hua Chen, Zhao-Yang Chen, Jian-Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone of catheter ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF). The ablation index (AI) provides an estimate of lesion size and depth, but does not definitively confirm transmurality. Complete elimination of the negative component of the unipolar electrogram (UPE) has been proposed as a marker of transmural lesion formation. High-power short-duration (HPSD) radiofrequency ablation (RFA) produces more homogeneous lesions with clearer boundaries and thus may be more suitable for PVI. This study aimed to assess the AI values achieved during PVI using UPE-guided HPSD RFA and to compare the efficacy and safety of UPE-guided vs. fixed AI-guided HPSD RFA in patients with PAF.MethodsA total of 276 patients with PAF were randomly assigned to two groups: the UPE group and the AI group. In the UPE group (n=136), the ablation endpoint for each lesion was defined as continuation of ablation for 2 seconds after conversion of the UPE morphology from RS to R. In the AI group (n=140), the ablation endpoint for each lesion was achievement of a predefined target AI value (400–450 for the anterior wall and 360–380 for the posterior wall). The pulmonary vein (PV) first-pass isolation rate, acute PV reconnection (APVR) rate, and atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence rate were compared between groups.ResultsThe AI values were significantly lower in the UPE group than in the AI group (P<0.001). The inter-lesion distance (ILD) was significantly greater in the UPE group than in the AI group (P<0.001). The left and right PV first-pass isolation rates were higher in the UPE group (93.4% vs. 85.0%, P=0.03; and 95.6% vs. 89.3%, P=0.048, respectively). APVR occurred less frequently in the UPE group than in the AI group (11.0% vs. 20.0%, P=0.040). At 12 months post-procedure, AF recurrence was observed in 13 patients (9.6%) in the UPE group and 14 patients (10.0%) in the AI group (P=0.902). Three patients in the UPE group and four patients in the AI group developed inguinal hematoma (2.2% vs. 2.9%, P>0.99).ConclusionsBoth UPE-guided and AI-guided HPSD RFA were effective and safe in patients with PAF. UPE-guided ablation was associated with lower AI values, higher PV first-pass isolation rates, and a lower incidence of APVR. These findings suggest that UPE-guided HPSD RFA may provide a more individualized approach to PVI than fixed AI-guided ablation alone.Keywords: Ablation index (AI), atrial fibrillation (AF), pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), unipolar potential, wall permeability

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone of catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) (1). Pulmonary vein reconnection (PVR) with the left atrium is a major cause of AF recurrence following PVI in patients with paroxysmal AF (PAF) (2). In clinical practice, achieving safe, transmural, continuous, and durable ablation lesions to ensure sustained PVI remains a significant challenge.

The ablation index (AI) has been shown to correlate reliably with lesion size and depth (3-5). When comparable AI values are achieved, lesion characteristics, including lesion diameter and depth, are generally similar (6). However, AI represents an estimated parameter rather than a direct measurement of lesion depth and therefore cannot definitively confirm the attainment of transmurality ablation.

Complete elimination of the negative component of the unipolar potential during radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has been reported to indicate transmural lesion formation (7-9). Consequently, PVI guided by elimination of the negative unipolar potential may more accurately reflect lesion transmurality than PVI guided by AI.

Previous studies have demonstrated that, compared with low-power long-duration (LPLD) ablation, high-power short-duration (HPSD) ablation produces lesions with more consistent lesion widths, clearer demarcation, and greater homogeneity. HPSD ablation has also been associated with a higher likelihood of achieving transmural lesions while reducing the risk of injury to the epicardium and adjacent structures. Therefore, HPSD ablation may be more suitable for PVI (10).

This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of HPSD RFA guided by complete elimination of the negative component of the unipolar potential compared with AI-guided HPSD RFA in Chinese patients with PAF. We present this article in accordance with the CONSORT reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0593/rc).

Methods

Participants

A total of 308 patients with PAF treated at the Department of Cardiology at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between August 1, 2022, and June 30, 2024 were enrolled in the study. All patients had demonstrated inadequate response to at least one antiarrhythmic drug and were scheduled to undergo catheter ablation. A prospective randomized study design was adopted. The study cutoff date was July 1, 2024. Of the 308 patients, 276 met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age ≥16 and <90 years; (II) a diagnosis of symptomatic PAF that was refractory to at least one antiarrhythmic drug and for which catheter RFA was scheduled; and (III) a preoperative New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class of I, II, or III.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age <16 or ≥90 years; (II) a preoperative NYHA functional class of IV; (III) persistent or long-standing persistent AF; (IV) contraindications to RFA; (V) intraoperative complications that precluded achievement of the predefined procedural endpoints; and/or (VI) inability to complete follow-up at the designated postoperative time points.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (approval No. 2023KJT068). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For participants younger than 18 years, informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. All patients agreed to undergo follow-up assessments in accordance with the study protocol.

RFA strategy and method

Transthoracic echocardiography was routinely conducted preoperatively in all patients. Cardiac thrombus was excluded by transesophageal echocardiography or cardiac computed tomography. Local anesthesia was administered, with moderate sedation and analgesia provided as required. A steerable decapolar coronary sinus electrode catheter (Triguy, Huitai, Shenzhen, China) was inserted via the left femoral vein. Double transseptal puncture was performed via the right femoral vein, and two 8-F PREFACE™ sheaths (Biosense Webster Inc., Irvine, CA, USA) were advanced into the left atrium. A multi-electrode mapping catheter (PentaRay Nav Catheter; Biosense Webster Inc.) and a 3.5-mm contact force (CF)-sensing saline-irrigated ablation catheter (THERMOCOOL SMARTTOUCH; Biosense Webster Inc.) were introduced into the left atrium through the two sheaths, respectively.

Three-dimensional reconstruction of the left atrium was performed using the multi-electrode mapping catheter under the guidance of the electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO 3; Biosense Webster Inc.). Bipolar and unipolar pulmonary vein (PV) potentials were then recorded. The bipolar filter was set at 30–500 Hz, and the unipolar filter was set at 0.5–180 Hz. RFA was delivered using the SmartAblate RFA system (Biosense Webster Inc.) with a power setting of 45 W, a temperature limit of 43 ℃, and a saline irrigation rate of 25 mL/min during ablation.

Point-by-point ablation was performed circumferentially around the ipsilateral PVs at 5–10 mm outside the PV ostia. The CF between the ablation catheter and myocardial tissue was maintained between 5 and 20 g at each lesion site. Impedance, temperature, power, AI, and other procedural parameters were continuously monitored in real time. Ablation sites were automatically annotated in real time using the Visitag module (Visitag OR, Biosense Webster Inc.). The Visitag settings were configured as follows: catheter stability was defined as a movement range of <3 mm within 3 seconds; a minimum CF of 5 g was required for >30% of the ablation duration; and each ablation site was annotated with a radius of 5 mm.

In the unipolar electrogram (UPE) group, the ablation endpoint for each lesion was defined as continuation of ablation for 2 seconds after the UPE morphology at that site changed from RS to R (Figure 1), and the corresponding AI value was recorded. After the ablation endpoint for a given lesion was reached, the ablation catheter was advanced along the predefined ablation line until an RS morphology reappeared on the UPE recorded by the ablation catheter. Additional ablation was then delivered. The ILD between adjacent ablation sites was recorded.

In the AI group, the ablation endpoint for each lesion was defined as attainment of the target AI values (400–450 for the anterior wall and 360–380 for the posterior wall), with an ILD <6 mm. Point-by-point ablation was performed in the antral regions of the bilateral PVs to create continuous circumferential ablation lines. Upon completion of the ablation lines, disappearance of PV potentials recorded by the PentaRay catheter was confirmed. Pacing at the PV ostia was subsequently performed using the ablation catheter at an output of 8 V and a pulse width of 2 ms to verify bidirectional conduction block between the PVs and the atrium, thereby confirming the initial PVI.

Following confirmation of the initial PVI, patients were observed for 30 minutes. After this interval, PV potentials were reassessed using the PentaRay catheter positioned within the PVs, and pacing was repeated to reconfirm bidirectional conduction block between the PVs and the atrium. Recovery of PV potential during this period was defined as acute PV reconnection (APVR). In such cases, additional ablation was delivered at the site of PVR until PVI was re-established. If bidirectional conduction block was maintained throughout the 30-minute observation period, this was defined as the procedural endpoint for PVI.

For patients who presented with AF at the beginning of the procedure, synchronized external direct-current cardioversion was performed to restore sinus rhythm before initiation of the above-described protocol.

After achievement of the PVI endpoint, a routine intracardiac electrophysiological study was performed. If atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT), atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia, atrial tachycardia, atrial flutter (AFL), or other arrhythmias were inducible, additional ablation was conducted according to the underlying electrophysiological mechanisms of each arrhythmia.

Study grouping

Patients who presented in sinus rhythm were eligible for randomization. Patients who presented with AF underwent synchronized external direct-current cardioversion at 150 J. Patients who successfully converted to and maintained sinus rhythm after cardioversion were subsequently eligible for randomization.

The sample size was calculated based on previously published AI targets for PVI, with a minimum of 75 patients required in each group to detect a 10-point difference in AI values. Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence to either the AI-guided HPSD ablation group (AI group, n=140) or the UPE-guided HPSD ablation group (UPE group, n=136).

Postoperative follow-up

All patients underwent routine outpatient follow-up during the first postoperative week and subsequently at intervals of 1–4 weeks. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was routinely performed at each follow-up visit. At 3 and 12 months after the operation, 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring was routinely performed. During the intervening follow-up periods, 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring was performed based on the patient’s symptoms and preference. Patients were instructed to contact their treating physicians by telephone, messaging, or WeChat if symptoms occurred during follow-up. Timely ECG and/or 24-hour Holter monitoring was arranged as indicated. Procedural success was defined as the absence of documented AF, AFL, or atrial tachycardia lasting >30 seconds during the 3–12-month postoperative follow-up period.

Postoperative medication

Routine anticoagulation therapy was administered for 3 months after the procedure. Beyond 3 months, continuation of anticoagulation was determined based on the patient’s CHA2DS2-VASc score [congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years (2 points), diabetes mellitus, prior stroke/transient ischemic attack/systemic thromboembolism (2 points), vascular disease, age 65–74 years, and sex category (female)], the presence or absence of AF recurrence, the clinical assessment of the treating physician, and patient preference.

Postoperatively, antiarrhythmic drug therapy with propafenone or amiodarone was either withheld or administered for 3 months based on the patient’s condition. At 3 months after the procedure, 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring was routinely performed. In patients without documented AF or AFL lasting >30 seconds, antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued. Documentation of AF or AFL lasting >30 seconds was considered post-procedural recurrence. Patients with recurrence could elect to undergo repeat catheter ablation or to continue to receive oral antiarrhythmic drug therapy.

During the 3–12-month follow-up period, any episode of AF or AFL lasting >30 seconds confirmed by ECG or 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring was considered post-procedural recurrence. Again, patients with documented recurrence could elect to undergo repeat catheter ablation or to continue to receive oral antiarrhythmic drug therapy. Post-procedural antiarrhythmic drug use was compared between groups (Table 1).

When clinically indicated, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, as well as β-blockers, statins, spironolactone, and other therapies, were prescribed based on the patient’s clinical condition.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were between-group differences in mean AI values and inter-lesion distances (ILDs) at the anterior and posterior walls of the left and right PVs, the first-pass isolation rates of the left and right PVs, the incidence of APVR, and AF recurrence within 12 months after the index ablation procedure. The secondary outcomes were procedural duration, fluoroscopy time, fluoroscopy dose, and the incidence of procedure-related adverse events. The composite safety endpoint was defined as the occurrence of any of the following adverse events: puncture-site bleeding, arteriovenous fistula, pseudoaneurysm, acute cardiac tamponade, phrenic nerve injury, PV stenosis, atrioesophageal fistula, or symptomatic stroke, including hemorrhagic stroke.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. Categorical variables were expressed as the number of cases (percentages), and intergroup differences were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared between groups using non-parametric tests. Postoperative AF-free survival time was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. A two-tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The K-S test showed that, among the continuous variables, only body mass index (BMI) was normally distributed, whereas all other continuous variables were non-normally distributed.

Baseline data of the two groups of patients

A total of 276 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, underwent the assigned procedures according to the randomization protocol, and completed follow-up (Figure 2). During the 12-month postoperative follow-up period, no patients were lost to follow-up or withdrew from the study.

The AI group comprised 140 patients, of whom 80 were male (57.1%), with a median age of 62 years (range, 54–69 years). The UPE group comprised 136 patients, of whom 73 were male (53.7%), with a median age of 61 years (range, 55–71 years). The prevalence of hypertension was significantly higher in the AI group than in the UPE group (57.9% vs. 44.1%, P=0.022). BMI was also significantly higher in the AI group than in the UPE group (24.8±3.4 vs. 23.8±3.0 kg/m2, P=0.01). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of the remaining baseline characteristics (Table 1).

Comparison of surgical conditions between the two groups

Compared with the AI group, the UPE group demonstrated a lower AI value at the anterior wall of the left PV {404 [395–408] vs. 430 [430–430], P<0.001} and a greater ILD between adjacent ablation sites {4.9 [4.9–5.1] vs. 4.5 [4.4–4.6] mm, P<0.001}. The first-pass isolation rate of the left PV was higher in the UPE group than in the AI group (93.4% vs. 85.0%, P=0.03) (Table 2).

Compared with the AI group, the UPE group demonstrated a lower AI value at the anterior wall of the right PV {410 [405–415] vs. 430 [430–430], P<0.001} and a greater ILD between adjacent ablation sites (4.9 vs. 4.5 mm, P<0.001). Similarly, the UPE group demonstrated a lower AI value at the posterior wall of the right PV {359 [355–365] vs. 383 [382–386], P<0.001} and a greater ILD between adjacent ablation sites {4.9 vs. 4.5 mm, P<0.001}. The first-pass isolation rate of the right PV was higher in the UPE group than in the AI group (95.6% vs. 89.3%, P=0.048) (Table 2).

After the initial PVI, patients were observed for 30 minutes. The incidence of APVR was significantly lower in the UPE group than in the AI group (11.0% vs. 20.0%, P=0.040). The number of PVs exhibiting APVR was also lower in the UPE group (17 vs. 38 branches, P=0.040) (Table 2).

No significant difference was observed between the two groups in the proportion of patients who underwent electrical cardioversion before ablation (8.8% vs. 8.6%, P=0.941). Electrophysiological examinations were performed after PVI. In the AI group, 4 patients (2.9%) successfully underwent ablation of AVNRT, compared with 5 patients in the UPE group (3.7%). One patient in each group was found to have a latent accessory pathway in the left anterior wall accompanied by AF and underwent ablation of the left anterior wall latent accessory pathway.

A total of 35 patients had inducible AFL, and high-density activation mapping was performed. In the AI group, 10 patients (7.1%) were confirmed to have AFL at the mitral annular isthmus, and linear ablation along the isthmus and/or ablation within the coronary sinus was successful. In the UPE group, 7 patients (5.1%) were confirmed to have AFL at the mitral annular isthmus, and linear ablation along the isthmus was successful. In the AI group, 8 patients (5.7%) were confirmed to have AFL at the tricuspid annular isthmus, and linear ablation along the isthmus was successful. In the UPE group, 10 patients (7.4%) were confirmed to have AFL at the tricuspid annular isthmus, and linear ablation along the isthmus was successful.

Six patients (4.3%) in the AI group and 13 patients (9.6%) in the UPE group were induced to have AF, which was related to the superior vena cava (SVC). After SVC isolation (SVCI), AF was terminated, and normal sinus rhythm (NSR) was restored (Table 2).

Compared with the AI group, the UPE group had a significantly shorter procedural duration {85 [70–105] vs. 100 [95–105] min, P<0.001}. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of fluoroscopy time (P=0.637) or fluoroscopy dose (P=0.896) (Table 2).

After 30 minutes of observation after the initial PVI, the sites of APVR were the superior or anteroinferior aspect of the left superior PV; the anterosuperior aspect of the left inferior PV; the superior or posteroinferior aspect of the right superior PV; and the posterosuperior aspect of the right inferior PV. After point-by-point ablation at these sites, PVI was re- performed (Table 3).

This study used a 3.5-mm CF-sensing saline-irrigated ablation catheter manufactured by Johnson & Johnson (THERMOCOOL SMARTTOUCH; Biosense Webster Inc.). The surgical operation was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended parameters for the device, including ablation power, temperature limit, ablation time, saline infusion speed, and contact pressure. Comparison of surgical safety between the two groups

No cases of acute cardiac tamponade occurred in either group. In the AI group, four patients developed varying degrees of bruising at the groin vascular puncture site, compared with three patients in the UPE group. Pseudoaneurysm and arteriovenous fistula were excluded by local ultrasonography. The bruising resolved gradually following local compression and bandaging. No significant difference was observed between the two groups in the incidence of procedural complications (2.9% vs. 2.2%, P>0.99) (Table 4). During the procedure and throughout the follow-up period, no procedure-related complications, including left atrial-esophageal fistula, phrenic nerve injury, symptomatic stroke, or hemorrhagic stroke, were reported.

Comparison of surgical efficacy between the two groups

At the end of the 12-month postoperative follow-up period, AF recurrence occurred in 14 patients (10.0%) in the AI group and 13 patients (9.6%) in the UPE group. Among the patients with recurrence, 7 patients (5.0%) in the AI group and 6 patients (4.4%) in the UPE group underwent repeat RFA, with no significant difference observed between the two groups (P=0.818).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that: (I) during HPSD RF ablation for PVI, the AI required to achieve PVI was lower in the UPE group than in the AI group; (II) the ILD was greater in the UPE group than in the AI group; (III) the first-pass isolation rates of both the left and right PVs were higher in the UPE group than in the AI group; (IV) the total procedural duration was shorter in the UPE group than in the AI group; and (V) no significant difference was observed between the two groups in the 1-year AF recurrence rate.

The AI integrates key procedural parameters, including ablation power, CF between the ablation catheter and myocardial tissue, and the duration for which adequate CF is maintained, thereby providing a reliable estimate of lesion extent and depth (3-5). According to Taghji et al., durable PVI requires an AI ≥400 on the posterior wall of the left atrium and ≥550 on the anterior wall, with an ILD ≤6 mm between adjacent ablation sites. AI-guided PVI for the treatment of PAF has been associated with a single-procedure success rate of up to 91.3% (3). Philips et al. reported that PVI guided by the CLOSE protocol achieved a single-procedure success rate of up to 94% at 12 months in patients with PAF. Further, regardless of ablation power, catheter CF, or ablation duration, comparable AI values have been associated with similar lesion characteristics in terms of diameter and depth (6).

Tissue injury induced by RFA consists of two components: resistive heating and conductive heating. LPLD ablation (25–30 W) predominantly produces conductive heating, which may increase the risk of injury to the epicardium and adjacent structures, thereby contributing to complications. In contrast, HPSD ablation (40–50 W) predominantly generates resistive heating, resulting in a relatively consistent lesion width, clearer lesion boundaries, and greater lesion homogeneity. This approach facilitates the creation of transmural lesions while reducing the risk of injury to the epicardium and surrounding structures. Therefore, HPSD ablation has been considered more suitable for PVI (10).

Previous studies have demonstrated that, compared with LPLD ablation, HPSD ablation is associated with higher PV first-pass isolation rates, lower rates of APVR, a reduced risk of esophageal injury, and shorter procedural duration (11-13). Bunch et al. reported that HPSD ablation (50 W) resulted in shorter procedural and fluoroscopy times than LPLD ablation (30 W), with no significant difference in the 1-year AF recurrence rate (14). Liu et al. found that high-power ablation (>45 W) was associated with a lower rate of atrial arrhythmia recurrence, higher first-pass isolation rates, and shorter ablation duration (15). Jin et al. reported no significant difference in 1-year ablation success rates between conventional ablation and HPSD ablation using a low AI target (16). Liu et al. conducted a meta-analysis and found that AI- or lesion size index-guided HPSD ablation was associated with higher PV first-pass isolation rates, lower APVR rates, higher procedural success rates, and shorter procedural times, with no significant difference in major complications (17).

However, the AI represents an estimated parameter rather than a direct measurement of lesion depth. During AI-guided ablation, attainment of a preset AI value at a given ablation site indicates that a certain estimated lesion depth has been achieved. However, it does not definitively confirm transmurality. Myocardial thickness varies across different atrial regions within the same individual. Interindividual variability in myocardial thickness may also occur at the same anatomical location. Consequently, when a specific ablation site reaches the preset AI value, three potential scenarios may arise: (I) the lesion has achieved transmurality; (II) transmurality has not been achieved, potentially resulting in acute or delayed PVR; or (III) a transmural lesion has already formed before the preset AI value is reached, but ablation continues until the target AI is reached, potentially increasing the risk of epicardial injury or steam pops. Therefore, the CLOSE protocol may not consistently provide an accurate assessment of lesion transmurality or long-term durability.

Previous studies have demonstrated that complete elimination of the negative component of the unipolar potential is indicative of transmural ablation lesion formation (7-9). In normally conducting tissue, electrical signals recorded by the UPE typically exhibit a biphasic RS morphology. When transmural injury occurs, loss of tissue excitability and conductivity results in a monophasic R morphology on the UPE. Accordingly, conversion of the local UPE morphology from biphasic RS to monophasic R reflects the development of a transmural ablation lesion within the myocardial tissue (18). Bortone et al. conducted PVI for PAF using a CF-sensing ablation catheter, and reported that 87% of patients maintained sinus rhythm without antiarrhythmic drug therapy at 2-year follow-up (19). Ejima et al. reported that unipolar electrical signal-guided ablation was associated with a higher 12-month procedural success rate than CF-sensing ablation (20), and further demonstrated that high-power ablation resulted in fewer APVRs and a shorter procedural duration than conventional-power ablation under UPE guidance (21).

Pambrun et al. enrolled 50 patients with PAF and performed PVI using high-power ablation guided by elimination of the negative component of the unipolar signal (10). Compared with standard power ablation, high-power ablation required less time to achieve equivalent ablation energy, produced greater lesion depth and width, and was associated with higher single-shot PVI rates and lower PVR. Therefore, high-power ablation (45 W) was used in all patients in this study.

The control group (AI group) was treated using relatively moderate AI targets of 400–450 for the anterior wall of the PVs and 360–380 for the posterior wall. In the AI group, the first-pass isolation rates and 1-year AF recurrence rate were comparable to those reported in previous studies. These findings support the effectiveness of combining CF-sensing saline-irrigated ablation catheters with high-power delivery and moderate AI targets for PVI.

In the UPE group, the endpoint for each ablation site was defined as continuation of ablation for 2 seconds after the UPE morphology changed from RS to R. The AI values in the UPE group were significantly lower than those in the AI group (all P<0.001). These findings indicate that the AI values required to achieve transmural ablation at the PV antrum may be lower than those reported in previous studies, consistent with the findings of Sun et al. (22). This approach accounts for variations in atrial wall thickness, potentially facilitating the formation of durable transmural lesions while minimizing excessive energy delivery.

The CLOSE protocol recommends an ILD of ≤6 mm; however, the optimal ILD has not been definitively established (6). Previous studies have reported that smaller ILDs are associated with higher first-pass isolation rates (23-25). In the present study, the ILD was significantly greater in the UPE group than in the AI group (median 4.9 vs. 4.5 mm; all P<0.001). Despite the larger ILD, the first-pass isolation rates of both the left and right PVs were higher in the UPE group than in the AI group. These findings indicate that the optimal ILD may be determined according to the electrophysiological characteristics of the specific ablation site rather than solely on the basis of a preset ILD value, consistent with current guideline recommendations (26).

First-pass isolation is considered an indicator of high-quality lesion formation (26). Previous studies have shown that first-pass isolation is a significant predictor of favorable outcomes after AF ablation (27,28). Achievement of first-pass isolation also reduces the likelihood of APVR. In the present study, first-pass isolation rates were higher and APVR incidence was lower in the UPE group than in the AI group, suggesting that the UPE-guided ablation strategy was effective and appropriate.

The procedural duration was significantly shorter in the UPE group than in the AI group (median 85 vs. 100 minutes, P<0.001). Several factors may account for this difference: (I) the AI required to achieve the ablation endpoint was lower in the UPE group; (II) the greater ILD reduced the number of ablation sites; and (III) higher first-pass isolation rates and fewer APVR cases reduced the need for repeat ablation.

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in fluoroscopy time, fluoroscopy dose, or the incidence of perioperative complications. During the 1-year postoperative follow-up period, AF recurrence rates and sinus rhythm survival did not differ significantly between the two groups. These findings suggest that the UPE-guided ablation strategy was both effective and safe.

Clinical implications

AI provides a reliable estimate of lesion size and depth. However, different patients or ablation sites may require different AI values. The UPE-guided ablation approach enables tailored lesion delivery based on local electrophysiological responses, facilitating durable transmural lesion formation while minimizing excessive energy delivery.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Second, the proportion of patients with AF recurrence who underwent repeat ablation was low. Third, insertable cardiac monitors were not used, which may have resulted in underestimation of AF recurrence. Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months; therefore, longer-term outcomes require further evaluation.

Conclusions

Our study showed that both UPE-guided and AI-guided HPSD RFA were effective and safe in patients with PAF. The UPE-guided ablation approach, which enabled tailored lesion delivery, facilitated durable transmural lesion formation while minimizing excessive energy delivery, and was associated with lower AI values, higher PV first-pass isolation rates, and a lower incidence of APVR. These findings suggest that UPE-guided HPSD RFA may provide a more individualized approach to PVI than fixed AI-guided ablation alone. Further multicenter randomized studies with larger sample sizes and longer-term follow-up are needed to confirm the safety of UPE-guided HPSD RFA.
