Keyhole Foraminotomy Combined With Unilateral Laminotomy for Bilateral Decompression Under Biportal Endoscopic Spinal Surgery for the Treatment of Mixed‐Type Cervical Spondylosis
Authors: Lejian Jiang, Xiaowei Jing, Xiaowen Qiu, Tianxin Wu, Danlei Zheng, Zhuolin Zhong, Yongzhi Jian, Qingfeng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMixed‐type cervical spondylosis (MCS) refers to a degenerative disease in which multiple structures of the cervical spine are affected. Achieving adequate multi‐site decompression with minimal muscle dissection is technically demanding in cervical spine surgery. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of one‐stage decompression for MCS with multi‐site lesions utilizing biportal endoscopic spinal surgery (BESS). A combined approach involving keyhole foraminotomy and unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (Keyhole‐ULBD) was proposed and applied in clinical practice.MethodsThis study was designed as a technical descriptive study. Patients diagnosed with MCS who underwent Keyhole‐ULBD using the BESS technique between October 2022 and June 2024 were enrolled. Demographic characteristics and baseline clinical data were collected. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and modified Japanese Orthopedic Association (mJOA) scores at 1, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Radiological parameters were measured to assess the extent of decompression and cervical stability. Paired t‐test and Wilcoxon signed‐rank test were applied to compare preoperative and postoperative measurements, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 12 MCS patients were enrolled. Compared with preoperative values, there was a significant increase in foraminal area (from 0.19 ± 0.06 to 0.25 ± 0.06 cm2) and spinal canal area (from 1.36 ± 0.21 to 2.75 ± 0.48 cm2), as well as a marked improvement in VAS‐neck scores (from 5.00 ± 1.67 to 1.50 ± 0.55) and mJOA scores (from 12.01 ± 2.19 to 15.83 ± 1.83) at 1 month postoperatively. At the 1‐year follow‐up, both radiological decompression and improvements in pain and functional outcomes were sustained, demonstrating stable long‐term clinical efficacy.ConclusionThe Keyhole‐ULBD technique offers a safe and effective approach for one‐stage decompression in MCS.Keywords: biportal endoscopic spinal surgery, keyhole foraminotomy, mixed‐type cervical spondylosis, ULBD

Introduction

Mixed‐type cervical spondylosis (MCS) is a degenerative condition of the cervical spine characterized by the simultaneous presence of multiple structural abnormalities. The most common form involves concurrent compression of both the spinal cord and nerve root(s), primarily resulting from multi‐site stenosis of the spinal canal, lateral recesses, and intervertebral foramina [1]. For MCS patients exhibiting clinical manifestations of spinal cord compression, surgical treatment is typically warranted [2]. Traditionally, the surgical approach for isolated cervical spondylotic myelopathy is determined by the number of affected segments and the pathological characteristics of the lesions [3]. In MCS patients involving more than two spinal segments, traditional surgical management often consisted of cervical laminoplasty in combination with foraminotomy [4]. Despite its effectiveness, this approach is relatively invasive and may lead to complications such as axial neck pain and postoperative cervical kyphosis. For MCS patients caused by cervical disc herniation, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is commonly recommended [5]. However, the coexistence of foraminal stenosis with cervical canal stenosis, often caused by developmental narrowing, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, or posterior vertebral osteophyte formation, poses a significant challenge in selecting the optimal surgical approach (Figure 1a–e). Achieving effective multi‐site decompression while preserving cervical stability necessitates advanced surgical proficiency and a high degree of intraoperative adaptability.

Biportal endoscopic spinal surgery (BESS) enables precise and visualized manipulation within the cervical spinal canal. It allows minimal resection of pathological tissues and serves as an effective alternative to open surgery. By leveraging the anatomical features of the cervical facet joint, posterior keyhole foraminotomy facilitates efficient decompression of the affected nerve root via foraminal enlargement [6]. Nerve root impingement resulting from cervical disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte formation can be effectively alleviated through keyhole foraminotomy. Compared to ACDF, this approach preserves segmental mobility and reduces the risk of adjacent segment degeneration [7]. For lesions causing spinal canal stenosis, BESS also offers an effective treatment strategy. Unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD) involves decompression extending from the ipsilateral spinolaminar junction across to the contralateral pedicle's inner cortex. This technique allows targeted removal of compressive lesions, such as osteophytes and thickened ligamentum flavum, thereby expanding the dorsal spinal canal and relieving spinal cord compression symptoms [8]. The similar surgical corridors of keyhole foraminotomy and ULBD enable feasible one‐stage decompression for MCS with both foraminal stenosis and spinal canal stenosis.

This retrospective study investigated the clinical efficacy and technical characteristics of the combined keyhole foraminotomy and ULBD under BESS in the management of MCS characterized by concurrent radiculopathy and myelopathy.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This study, conducted between October 2022 and June 2024, was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of authors' hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a detailed explanation of the study protocol. Patients were included if they were diagnosed with degenerative MCS disease, characterized by concomitant cervical myelopathy and radiculopathy with imaging evidence of simultaneous central canal and foraminal stenosis, involving one or two cervical levels between C3 and C7, and refractory to at least 3 months of conservative treatment. Eligible patients were required to present with clinical symptoms consistent with radiological findings and have complete perioperative and follow‐up data. Patients with congenital cervical spine structural abnormalities (including congenital spinal canal stenosis or vertebral segmentation anomalies), cervical instability or deformity, multi‐segment (≥ 3 levels) cervical involvement, prior cervical spine surgery, traumatic, infectious, inflammatory, or neoplastic spinal pathology, or insufficient follow‐up were excluded from this study.

Surgical Procedures

Positioning and Surgical Ports Establishment

A detailed preoperative evaluation was performed to determine the patient's chief complaint and affected cervical segment(s). Patients who had both cervical radiculopathy and myelopathy, which were corresponding to radiological findings (taking C5/6 segment as an example), were diagnosed with MCS. Imaging revealed narrowing of the C5/6 cervical spinal stenosis with osteophyte formation at the posterior vertebral edge (Figure 1a,b) and narrowing of the left intervertebral foramen (Figure 1b,c). In addition, sagittal magnetic resonance (MR) imaging revealed spinal cord compression with associated intramedullary signal changes (Figure 1d). The left foraminal stenosis on the patient's axial MR image was considered to be responsible for his left upper limb numbness (Figure 1e).

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone on the operating table with slight cervical flexion and a mild head‐up, foot‐down tilt, taking care to keep the cervical spine approximately parallel to the horizontal plane. The vertical cranial skin incision was positioned at the intersection of the line connecting the upper margin of the pedicles of the cranial vertebra and the medial border of the pedicle on the symptomatic side. Similarly, the caudal skin incision was located at the intersection of the line connecting the lower margin of the pedicles of the caudal vertebra and the medial border of the pedicle on the symptomatic side (Figure 1f). Subsequently, initial endoscopic corridors were established with the assistance of two Kirschner wires. The Kirschner wires were introduced through the incisions and used to probe the bony surface of the lateral masses at the lateral edge of the V‐point in a vertical direction, which was confirmed under fluoroscopy. Following the direction of Kirschner wires, sequential dilators were used to gradually split the cervical muscles (Figure 1g). When the endoscopic sheaths reached the bony surface, a ring saw was utilized to detach the soft tissue on the bony surface. The use of the ring saw minimized thermal injury to muscle tissue caused by electrocautery. Finally, the viewing portal and working portal were established (Figure 1h).

Posterior Cervical Keyhole Foraminotomy

By clearing the soft tissue, the V‐point of the affected segment was fully exposed (Figure 2a–c). The keyhole procedure primarily targeted decompression of the ipsilateral lateral recess and foramen. First, a keyhole decompression window was created by sequential drilling of the superior lamina, the inferior lamina, partial ligamentum flavum and the V‐point (Figure 2d–f). This minor endoscopic laminotomy was performed to enlarge the decompression window and improve the mobility of the dural sac, thereby facilitating full exposure of herniated disc material and/or posterior vertebral osteophytes. Then, a partial medial facetectomy was conducted for cervical nerve root exposure, as described previously (Figure 2g) [9]. During this step, the high‐speed burr was used only to remove the dorsal bony portion of the superior facet joint. The remaining ventral cortical portion of the facet joint was then excised using Kerrison rongeurs to minimize potential injury to the nerve root. To preserve the stability of the facet joint, resection of the medial margin of the facet was limited to the lateral border of the pedicle. After dissection of the anatomical shoulder and axillar area of the nerve root, herniated disc tissue and vertebral osteophytes can be removed. Following meticulous inspection for residual fragments and adequate hemostasis, preliminary decompression of the nerve root pathway was achieved (Figure 2h).

Unilateral Laminotomy for Bilateral Decompression

For spinal stenosis with folding or hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum, complete excision of the bilateral ligamentum flavum was required for decompression. After the keyhole foraminotomy step, exposure of the medial portions of lateral masses and the adjacent segments of laminae was gradually expanded via the V‐point with the high‐speed burr. Then, laminotomy at the affected segment was performed with a high‐speed burr from the symptomatic side toward the midline, and in a cranio‐caudal direction. The specific sequence for laminar removal and ligamentum flavum resection was illustrated in Figure 3a. Starting from the symptomatic side, the inferior portion of the superior lamina was drilled in a clockwise direction (Figure 3d), followed by drilling of the superior portion of the inferior lamina in a counterclockwise direction (Figure 3e). Notably, the attachment of the ligamentum flavum to the lamina typically defines the superior and inferior boundaries of decompression, which is particularly important for contralateral decompression (Figure 3b). For the contralateral approach, the base of the spinous process was drilled (Over‐the‐top technique) (Figure 3c,f). The region and sequence of laminar resection on the contralateral side were similar to those on the ipsilateral side (Figure 3g). A large segment of the ligamentum flavum, once detached from its laminar attachment, can provide temporary protection to the dural sac from drill‐related injury and is therefore recommended to be removed at the end of the laminectomy procedure. It should be noted that the site of foraminal stenosis is typically located near the medial margin and vicinity of the pedicle. Osteophyte formation and facet joint hypertrophy rarely occur at the lateral margin of the pedicle. Therefore, when performing ULBD for contralateral decompression, it is recommended that the distal extent of decompression reach only as far as the lateral margin of the pedicle. Finally, hemostasis was thoroughly achieved, and the working and viewing portals were withdrawn, with adequate decompression of the posterior spinal canal and intervertebral foramen confirmed (Figure 3h,i).

For patients with multi‐segment stenosis, an additional working channel can be established for adjacent segment decompression (Figure 4a–h). In such cases, a Shift Technique can be selectively employed to facilitate two‐segment ligamentum flavum resection and complete removal of the intermediate lamina. The floating spinous process provides sufficient space for the dural sac to drift posteriorly following decompression.

Postoperative Cervical Immobilization

Postoperatively, a standardized cervical immobilization protocol was implemented. All patients were required to wear a cervical collar for 4 weeks to limit cervical motion and support early postoperative stabilization.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, affected cervical segments, laterality of involvement, and concomitant symptoms were collected. Perioperative parameters such as estimated blood loss and operative time were recorded. Functional recovery (modified Japanese Orthopedic Association, mJOA) and symptom improvement (Visual Analog Scale for neck and arm, VAS) at different time points (preoperative and 1, 6, and 12 months postoperative) were assessed and compared for statistical significance [10, 11]. Postoperative and follow‐up complications were systematically recorded during the in‐hospital period and throughout the entire follow‐up duration, including spinal cord injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, postoperative hematoma and surgical site infection, nerve root injury, mechanical neck pain, recurrence, and cervical instability. Axial computed tomography (CT) images were used to quantify changes in the maximum foraminal area and spinal canal area before and after surgery [12]. Foraminal height (FH) and width (FW) were measured on the oblique sagittal CT images (Figure 5a,b). FH was measured as the vertical distance between osseous boundaries on a sagittal plane intersecting the midpoint of the foramen (Figure 5c). FW was defined as the maximal transverse dimension traversing the foramen (Figure 5d) [13]. Foraminal area was determined as the largest enclosed region bordered by the pedicle, vertebral body, and intervertebral disc structures on the sagittal reconstruction (Figure 5e). The spinal canal area was measured on axial CT at the most stenotic level, bounded anteriorly by the vertebral body (including osteophytes) and posteriorly by the lamina, with ligamentum flavum thickness cross‐validated on MR images (Figure 5f). In addition, flexion and extension views of cervical radiographs obtained at 12 months postoperatively were collected to evaluate cervical stability, with particular attention to segmental angulation and anteroposterior vertebral translation of the affected segment [14, 15]. Cervical instability was evaluated based on combined angular and translational criteria, primarily defined as anteroposterior vertebral translation > 3.5 mm or > 20% of the vertebral body width on dynamic flexion–extension radiographs, rather than angular change alone [15]. To evaluate the adequacy of bony decompression and cervical stability, quantitative imaging analyses primarily focused on longitudinal radiographic data, with particular comparison of preoperative, immediate postoperative, and 12‐month postoperative measurements. Imaging measurements were independently performed by two surgeons, and interobserver reliability was evaluated by comparing their measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Paired t‐test and Wilcoxon signed‐rank test were employed to compare preoperative and postoperative outcomes, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Radiographic measurements, including spinal canal and foraminal dimensions, were analyzed using the paired t‐test after confirming that the differences between paired observations followed a normal distribution. Clinical outcomes, including pain and functional scores, were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 19.0, StataCorp).

Results

Demographic Data

A total of 12 patients (10 males and 2 females) diagnosed with MCS characterized by concurrent radiculopathy and myelopathy were enrolled in this study. The mean age at the time of surgery was 60.17 ± 10.38 years. Imaging findings and physical examination revealed that the C5/6 segment was most commonly affected (Table 1). The laterality of the affected segments was evenly distributed, with a left‐to‐right ratio of 1:1. The interval between onset of radicular pain and myelopathic symptoms varied widely among patients, ranging from 2 weeks to 10 years and 8 days to 1 year, respectively.

The mean operative time was 130.33 ± 30.35 min, with an average blood loss of 24.17 ± 10.68 mL and hospital stay of 6.33 ± 1.03 days (Table 2). Among the 12 patients, one intraoperative dural tear was observed in the presence of severe ligamentum flavum adhesion, and two patients developed postoperative neurological symptoms, including numbness and sensory hypoesthesia. No cases of spinal cord injury, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, epidural hematoma, surgical site infection, recurrence, or cervical instability were observed.

Radiological measurements revealed significant postoperative increases in both foraminal and spinal canal areas. Oblique sagittal CT images demonstrated that keyhole surgery significantly increased foraminal height from 7.59 ± 1.23 to 8.45 ± 1.00 mm (p < 0.05), width from 3.04 ± 1.06 to 4.60 ± 1.21 mm (p < 0.05), and area from 0.19 ± 0.06 to 0.25 ± 0.06 cm2 (p < 0.05). Additionally, axial CT images showed that ULBD significantly expanded the spinal canal area from 1.36 ± 0.21 to 2.75 ± 0.48 cm2 (p < 0.05). Radiological evaluation at 12 months demonstrated that foraminal height, width, area, and spinal canal area remained stable, with no significant changes compared with the immediate postoperative values (all p > 0.05).

Flexion and extension cervical radiographs were also obtained beyond 12 months postoperatively to assess mid‐term cervical mobility and stability. The affected segments demonstrated an angulation of 16.5° ± 5.9° and vertebral translation of 1.91 ± 0.55 mm, with no evidence of significant cervical instability, compared with previously reported studies [15, 16].

Clinical Outcomes

Pain intensity and neurological function were evaluated using the VAS and mJOA scores at predefined time points. At 1 month postoperatively, VAS scores for neck and arm pain significantly decreased from 5.00 ± 1.67 to 1.50 ± 0.55 and from 4.50 ± 1.20 to 1.81 ± 0.43, respectively (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, mJOA scores significantly improved from 12.01 ± 2.19 to 15.83 ± 1.83 (p < 0.05) (Table 3, Figure 6). Continued improvements in both pain and functional outcomes were observed at the 1‐month follow‐up, with particularly marked recovery in younger patients presenting with acute myelopathy due to short‐duration spinal cord compression. Patients' pain intensity and neurological function scores showed slight improvement at mid‐term follow‐up (6 months and 1 year, Table 3, Figure 6).

Discussion

Due to the multi‐site degeneration and marked morphological variability of MCS, optimal surgical strategies are relatively underreported [17, 18]. In most cases, spinal cord compression constitutes the primary surgical indication [19]. Conventional posterior approaches, including laminoplasty, laminectomy, and unilateral open‐door laminoplasty, are widely adopted for the treatment of cervical spondylotic myelopathy [20, 21]. However, these procedures may not simultaneously address concomitant foraminal stenosis and may involve extensive posterior structural disruption. Against this background, the present study underscores the advantages of the combined keyhole and ULBD technique. It demonstrates that one‐stage decompression of both central canal and foraminal stenosis is feasible, thereby avoiding staged procedures and reducing surgical burden. By leveraging shared surgical corridors and the precision of the BESS system, adequate decompression can be achieved while preserving posterior structures and maintaining segmental stability. Compared with conventional approaches, this technique allows simultaneous foraminal decompression without additional exposure, is technically less demanding than unilateral open‐door laminoplasty, and may lower the risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury. The bone‐preserving nature of ULBD further reduces the potential for postoperative cervical instability, supporting its role as a safe and minimally invasive option for selected patients with MCS involving three or fewer segments. Nevertheless, appropriate patient selection remains essential. This technique is not recommended for patients with segmental cervical instability requiring fusion, vertebral artery anatomical variations, or central‐type disc herniation. In addition, when foraminal stenosis coexists with severe congenital spinal canal stenosis or canal hypoplasia, more extensive posterior decompression procedures, such as unilateral open‐door laminoplasty or total laminectomy, may be more appropriate surgical options [22].

This study highlights several critical surgical strategies aimed at reducing intraoperative complications and optimizing outcomes in spinal decompression procedures. The findings reinforce the importance of meticulous technique to prevent iatrogenic injury and preserve spinal stability and neural integrity.

Avoidance of Iatrogenic Cervical Instability

One of the primary concerns in posterior cervical decompression is the risk of iatrogenic cervical instability [23]. This can be significantly minimized by cautious preservation of anatomical structures during both surgical corridor establishment and decompression [24]. Specifically, maintaining facet joint resection below 50% is essential for preserving biomechanical stability. Furthermore, while exposing the contralateral ligamentum flavum through undercutting of the spinous process base, careful protection of the posterior ligamentous complex is crucial [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that disruption of these stabilizing structures may adversely affect postoperative recovery and long‐term spinal alignment, underscoring the need for their preservation during decompressive surgery [26, 27].

Minimizing Dural Compression and Injury During ULBD

Our findings also suggest technical refinements to reduce the risk of dural compression or tearing, particularly during ULBD. To prevent inadvertent compression of the dural sac by surgical instruments during contralateral over‐the‐top decompression, we recommend resecting the spinous process base from the operative side in a posterior‐superior to anterior‐inferior direction. This directional approach facilitates safer instrument navigation through the corridor and improves visual access to the contralateral side. Additionally, it is recommended that the lamina be drilled along the surface of the ligamentum flavum, avoiding deep advancement of the burr into the spinal canal and thereby preventing contact with or injury to the dural sac. In cases of severe spinal stenosis, adhesions between the dura mater, ligamentum flavum, and lamina are often encountered. Dissection in such scenarios requires heightened caution to prevent dural tears or cerebrospinal fluid leakage [28]. For ipsilateral decompression, the use of a hook dissector is advised to gently separate adhesions. During final removal of the contralateral ligamentum flavum, a 2‐mm nerve dissector should be employed to identify adhesion planes and facilitate delicate dissection in order to prevent dural tears caused by adhesions. After the ligamentum flavum is adequately mobilized, it is removed piecemeal using pituitary rongeurs. The distal limit of ligamentum flavum resection is defined by the medial border of the contralateral lateral mass. Additionally, maintaining adequate ipsilateral working space is critical to ensure safe contralateral access and to further reduce the risk of dural injury.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which is mainly attributable to the low incidence of MCS. Second, this study was designed as a single‐center retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, although short‐ and mid‐term clinical and radiological outcomes were available, the duration of follow‐up was still limited, and longer‐term outcomes are required to further evaluate the durability of decompression, the potential impact on cervical biomechanical stability, and the risk of late‐onset complications. Finally, the absence of a control group or direct comparison with other surgical approaches precludes definitive conclusions regarding the relative efficacy of this technique. Therefore, the results of the present study should be interpreted as preliminary evidence supporting the technical feasibility and initial clinical effectiveness of biportal endoscopic decompression for degenerative MCS. Future multicenter, prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up periods are warranted.

Overall, our clinical experience of Keyhole‐ULBD for MCS underscores the importance of meticulous surgical technique in preserving key anatomical structures and minimizing complications, including cervical instability, dural injury, and excessive intraoperative bleeding. Among patients with MCS, the application of this surgical strategy allowed for successful multi‐site decompression, with marked improvement in both myelopathic and radicular symptoms. No recurrence or radiographic signs of cervical instability were noted over a 1‐year follow‐up. Keyhole‐ULBD under BESS can significantly enhance the safety, precision, and overall outcomes of cervical decompression procedures.
