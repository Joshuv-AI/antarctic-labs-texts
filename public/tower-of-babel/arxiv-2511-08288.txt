The central heat trace on large compact classical groups
Thibaut Lemoine, Mylène Maïda

Abstract
We establish the large- N N asymptotic expansion of the (central) trace of the heat kernel on any compact classical group G N ⊂ GL N ​ ( ℂ ) G_{N}\subset\mathrm{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C}) , which extends a previous result known only for U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) [ 28 ] . It admits two new interpretations of the trace: in terms of ramified coverings of the torus, and Gromov–Witten invariants on elliptic curves. These connections allow us to explore several aspects of the gauge/string duality in two dimensions: a Yang–Mills/Hurwitz duality, and Yang–Mills/Gromov–Witten duality.

2.1
Heat kernel on compact classical groups

The complex classical groups are special linear groups over the spaces of real, complex or quaternionic numbers. Their compact real forms, called

2.2
Random partitions

An integer partition is a finite family
\alpha=(\alpha_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\alpha_{r})
of nonincreasing positive integers. Its size is
|\alpha|=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}
, its length is
\ell(\alpha)=r
, and its parts are the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
. By convention, we denote by
\varnothing
the empty partition, which has zero size and length. If
n\geqslant 0
is an integer, we write
\alpha\vdash n
if
\alpha
is a partition such that
|\alpha|=n
. Besides, we denote by
\mathscr{P}_{n}
the set of partitions
\alpha\vdash n
, and
\mathscr{P}=\bigsqcup_{n\geqslant 0}\mathscr{P}_{n}
the set of all integer partitions. The generating function of the numbers
p(n)
of partitions of
n
is given by

\sum_{n\geqslant 0}p(n)q^{n}=\prod_{m\geqslant 1}(1-q^{m})^{-1},

which is absolutely convergent for
|q|<1
and corresponds to the inverse of Euler’s function
\phi:q\mapsto\prod_{m\geqslant 1}(1-q^{m})
(see
[
5
, §14.3]
).

Partitions are in bijection with Young diagrams, which are left-justified tables, such that the
i
-th line of the Young diagram of
\alpha
contains
\alpha_{i}
cells. See Fig.
1
for an illustration.

Figure 1:
The Young diagram representing the partition
\alpha=(8,6,5,5,4,2,1)
.

Integer partitions are ubiquitous in mathematics, and random partitions have been used to solve various problems ranging from the distribution of the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation
[
9
]
to the asymptotics of volumes of moduli spaces of holomorphic differentials
[
15
]
. Let
\mathscr{U}(q)
be the
q
-
uniform measure
on
\mathscr{P}
for
q\in(0,1)
, which is defined by

(7)

\mathbb{P}(\alpha)=\phi(q)q^{|\alpha|},\quad\forall\alpha\in\mathscr{P}.

A partition
\alpha\in\mathscr{P}
will follow this distribution if its size
|\alpha|
follows a geometric distribution of parameter
1-q
, and, conditionally to
|\alpha|=n
,
\alpha
follows the uniform distribution on
\mathscr{P}_{n}
. Its construction is analogous to the Poissonized Plancherel measure
[
9
]
, where the size of the partition is also randomized, but via a Poisson distribution.

Given any partition
\lambda\vdash n
, we assign to any cell
\square\in\lambda
of its Young diagram a
content

c(\square)=j(\square)-i(\square)
, which is the difference of its coordinates in the diagram. The
total content
is defined by

K(\lambda)=\sum_{\square\in\lambda}c(\square).

It can be expressed explicitely as a function of the length of the rows of the diagram:

(8)

K(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\lambda_{i}\left(\lambda_{i}+1-2i\right).

The typical observables that we will consider in the present paper are polynomials of
K(\lambda)
and
|\lambda|
.
It will be useful to compare the total content to the size and length of a given partition.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathscr{P}
,

(9)

|K(\alpha)|\leqslant|\alpha|^{2},

and

(10)

-\ell(\alpha)|\alpha|\leqslant 2K(\alpha)\leqslant\alpha_{1}|\alpha|.

Proof.

On the one hand, by (
8
),

2K(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\alpha)}\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{i}+1-2i)=\alpha_{1}\sum_{i}(\alpha_{i}+1-2i)\leqslant\alpha_{1}|\alpha|.

On the other hand, the sum of contents of the column
j
of
\alpha
is

(j-1)+(j-2)+\ldots+(j-n_{j})\geqslant n_{j}\left(j-\frac{1}{2}(n_{j}+1)\right),

where
n_{j}\leqslant\ell(\alpha)
is the height of the column
j
. It follows that

K(\alpha)=\sum_{j}n_{j}\left(j-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}n_{j}\right)\geqslant-\sum_{j}\frac{n_{j}}{2}\ell(\alpha)\geqslant-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|\ell(\alpha),

which proves (
10
). Then, (
9
) can be deduced from the fact that
\ell(\alpha)\leqslant|\alpha|
and
\alpha_{1}\leqslant|\alpha|
.
∎

The final result of this subsection is that only even powers of the total content contribute to the expectations of the observables.

Proposition 2.2

Let
\alpha,\beta
two independent
q

By symmetry of the roles of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[K(\alpha)^{k_{1}}K(\beta)^{k_{2}}

\displaystyle P(|\alpha|,|\beta|)]=\phi(q)^{2}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant 0}q^{n_{1}+n_{2}}P(n_{1},n_{2})\sum_{\alpha\vdash n_{1}}K(\alpha)^{k_{1}}\sum_{\beta\vdash n_{2}}K(\beta)^{k_{2}}.

However, for any

2.3
Highest weights/partitions correspondence

For any compact classical group
G_{N}
, we want to describe
\widehat{G}_{N}
in terms of partitions instead of highest weights, because it is more adapted to the study of the large-
N
asymptotics. Indeed, the expression of

for
\lambda\in\widehat{G}_{N}
has a nontrivial dependence on
N
that becomes fully explicit in this new description, and it is the key of the asymptotic expansion of
\mathrm{Tr}(e^{\frac{t}{2}\Delta})
. As we will see below, the relationship between highest weights of special orthogonal and symplectic groups is quite straightforward, but we shall explain more carefully the procedure for unitary groups. There is a well-established construction
[

25

that produces a highest weight of

from a couple of partitions. Namely, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are two arbitrary partitions, for any

one can construct the highest weight

[\alpha,\beta]_{N}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)},0,\ldots,0,-\beta_{\ell(\beta)},\ldots,-\beta_{1}).

It corresponds to a rational representation of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
but also an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
. It has the convenience to be well-defined for any
N
large enough, and it is suitable for large-
N
asymptotics, hence it is named
stable representation
. What we shall consider is a slightly modified version of this construction, because the map
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto[\alpha,\beta]_{N}
does not produce all highest weights: we will rather consider a shift of these rational representations by an arbitrary integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and introduce the map
\lambda_{N}:\mathscr{P}\times\mathscr{P}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
defined by

\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)=(\alpha_{1}+n,\ldots,\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}+n,n,\ldots,n,n-\beta_{\ell(\beta)},\ldots,n-\beta_{1}).

Now this map is surjective for any fixed
N
, but not injective, as explained in
[
29
, §2.2]
. In order to get injectivity, we have to force some kind of “discontinuity” between both partitions, by setting
\lambda_{k}=n
for a particular
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
, and imposing that
\ell(\alpha)\leqslant k-1
and
\ell(\beta)\leqslant N-k
. The choice of this
k
is not unique, but not completely arbitrary either: as long as
k
is of order
N/2
everything will work fine. We choose
k=\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor
, so that the cutoffs for lengths of
\alpha
and
\beta
are respectively

A_{N}=\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor-1,\quad B_{N}=N-\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
G_{N}
be a compact classical group of type
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A,B,C,D\}
. Its dual
\widehat{G}_{N}
can be described in terms of integer partitions. More precisely,

\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\simeq\Lambda_{N}^{(1)}:=\{(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\mathscr{P}\times\mathscr{P}\times{\mathbb{Z}}:\ell(\alpha)\leqslant A_{N},\ell(\beta)\leqslant B_{N}\},

\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\simeq\Lambda_{N}^{(2)}:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathscr{P}\times\mathscr{P}:\ell(\alpha)\leqslant A_{N},\ell(\beta)\leqslant B_{N}\},

\widehat{{\mathrm{SO}}}(2N+1)\simeq\widehat{{\mathrm{Sp}}}(N)\simeq\Lambda_{N}^{(3)}:=\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}:\ell(\mu)\leqslant N\},

\widehat{{\mathrm{SO}}}(2N)\simeq\Lambda_{N}^{(4)}:=\{(\mu,m,n)\in\mathscr{P}\times{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}:\ell(\mu)\leqslant N-2,|n|\leqslant m\}.

Proof.

The cases of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
are treated in
[
29
,
28
]
, and are respectively described by bijections
\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}:\Lambda_{N}^{(1)}\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
,
\lambda_{N}^{A}:\Lambda_{N}^{(2)}\to\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
given by

\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n)=(\alpha_{1}+n,\ldots,\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}+n,n,\ldots,n,n-\beta_{\ell(\beta)},\ldots,n-\beta_{1}),

\lambda_{N}^{A}(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell(\alpha)}+\beta_{1},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{1},\beta_{1}-\beta_{\ell(\beta)},\ldots,\beta_{1}-\beta_{2},0).

The parameters
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
ensure that
\lambda_{A_{N}+1}=n
is independent of
\alpha
and
\beta
, and that
N\geqslant\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\beta)+1
, so that there is a unique way to recover
\alpha,\beta,n
in the case of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, or
\alpha,\beta
in the case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
. For
{\mathrm{SO}}(2N+1)
, there is a much more straightforward bijection
\lambda_{N}^{B}:\Lambda_{N}^{(3)}\to\widehat{{\mathrm{SO}}}(2N+1)
given by

\lambda_{N}^{B}(\mu)=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\ell(\mu)},0,\ldots,0),

and the same happens for
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
:
\lambda_{N}^{C}:\Lambda_{N}^{(3)}\to{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
. Finally, in the case of
{\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
, the bijection
\lambda_{N}^{D}:\Lambda_{N}^{(4)}\to{\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
is defined by

\lambda_{N}^{D}(\mu,m,n)=(\mu_{1}+m,\ldots,\mu_{N-2}+m,m,n).

In the formula above, we have set
\mu_{i}=0
for all
i>\ell(\mu)
by convention. From this, it is clear that we have a bijection, whose inverse is given by
n_{\lambda}=\lambda_{N}
,
m_{\lambda}=\lambda_{N-1}
and
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i}-m
for all
i\leqslant N-1
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The Casimir numbers of the compact classical groups can be rewritten in terms of integer partitions:

(i)

If
G_{N}={\mathrm{U}}(N)
,

c_{2}^{A^{\prime}}(\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+n^{2}+\frac{2}{N}F^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n),

where

F^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n)=K(\alpha)+K(\beta)+n(|\alpha|-|\beta|).

(ii)

If
G_{N}={\mathrm{SU}}(N)
,

c_{2}^{A}(\lambda_{N}^{A}(\alpha,\beta))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+\frac{2}{N}F_{1}^{A}(\alpha,\beta)-\frac{1}{N^{2}}F_{2}^{A}(\alpha,\beta),

where

F_{1}^{A}(\alpha,\beta)=K(\alpha)+K(\beta),\quad F_{2}^{A}(\alpha,\beta)=(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2}.

(iii)

If
G_{N}={\mathrm{SO}}(2N+1)
,

c_{2}^{B}(\lambda_{N}^{B}(\mu))=|\mu|+\frac{2}{2N+1}F^{B}(\mu),

where

F^{B}(\mu)=K(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}|\mu|.

(iv)

If
G_{N}={\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
,

c_{2}^{C}(\lambda_{N}^{C}(\mu))=|\mu|+\frac{2}{2N}F^{C}(\mu),

where

F^{C}(\mu)=K(\mu)+\frac{1}{2}|\mu|.

(v)

If
G_{N}={\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
,

c_{2}^{D}(\lambda_{N}^{D}(\mu,m,n))=|\mu|+\frac{2}{2N}F_{1}^{D}(\mu)+F_{2,N}^{D}(\mu,m,n),

where

F_{1}^{D}(\mu)=K(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}|\mu|,\quad F_{2,N}^{D}(\mu,m,n)=\frac{N-1}{2}m+\frac{N-1}{2N}m^{2}+\frac{|\mu|}{N}m+\frac{n^{2}}{2N}.

Proof.

The proofs in the cases of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
can be found in
[
29
]
. Let
\mu\in\Lambda_{N}^{(3)}
be a highest weight. We have

c_{2}^{D}(\lambda_{N}^{B}(\mu))=\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}\mu_{i}(\mu_{i}+2N+1-2i)=|\mu|+\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}\mu_{i}(\mu_{i}-2i),

and a routine computation shows that

2K(\mu)-|\mu|=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\mu)}\mu_{i}(\mu_{i}-2i),

so that the result follows. The computation for
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
is exactly the same, now let us treat the case of
{\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
. Let
(\mu,m,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{(4)}
. Recall that

\lambda_{N}^{D}(\mu,m,n)=(\mu_{1}+m,\ldots,\mu_{N-2}+m,m,n),

so that

c_{2}^{D}(\lambda_{N}^{D}(\mu,m,n))=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\mu_{i}+m)(\mu_{i}+m+2N-2i)+\frac{1}{2N}n^{2}.

The result follows from simplifications.
∎

This result makes completely explicit the dependence of the Casimir on the parameter
N
. An interesting consequence of this lemma is the following estimate of the spectral gap for the negative Laplacian on
G_{N}
. It is not a particularly difficult result, but we believe it is worth mentioning by analogy with the current active research around spectral gaps of large random geometric objects (see for instance the recent breakthroughs
[
22
,
4
,
3
,
20
,
21
]
for spectral gaps of random surfaces). Recall that the lowest eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{G_{N}}
is always 0 and the only highest weight that contributes to this eigenvalue is the trivial highest weight
(0,\ldots,0)
. The spectral gap is by definition the second smallest eigenvalue, that is, the smallest possible Casimir
c_{2}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)
.

Theorem 2.5

(Asymptotic spectral gap)
.

Let
(G_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of compact classical groups of type
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A,B,C,D\}
.
As
N
tends to infinity, the Casimir numbers of all highest weights concentrate around integer values (or half-integer values if
G_{N}={\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
). More specifically, let
\alpha,\beta,\mu
be integer partitions and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be integers, then for
N
large enough,

c_{2}^{A^{\prime}}(\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+n^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

c_{2}^{A}(\lambda_{N}^{A}(\alpha,\beta))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

c_{2}^{B}(\lambda_{N}^{B}(\mu))=|\mu|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

c_{2}^{C}(\lambda_{N}^{C}(\mu))=|\mu|+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

c_{2}^{D}(\lambda_{N}^{D}(\mu,m,n))=|\mu|+\frac{1}{2}m(N+m-1)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

In particular, the negative Laplacian
-\Delta_{G_{N}}
has an asymptotic spectral gap of 1 as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

The expressions of Casimirs follow from Lemma
2.4
, by isolating the dependence in
N
in each formula. For the spectral gap, we need to use the bijections from Proposition
2.3
and express the highest weights in terms of partitions, then let
N
tend to infinity. We see that in this regime, the lowest nonzero Casimirs are given by the partitions with smallest sizes: for
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
we have either
(\alpha,\beta,n)=((1),\varnothing,0)
or
(\varnothing,(1),0)
, for
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
we have
(\alpha,\beta)=((1),\varnothing)
or
(\varnothing,(1))
, and for the other cases we have
\mu=(1)
, with
m=n=0
for the case of
{\mathrm{SO}}(2N)
. In all these cases, the Casimir converges to 1, so that asymptotically there is no eigenvalue of
-\Delta
in
(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma 2.7

Altogether,

Let us expand the powers of

in order to extract the right powers of
N

\mathbb{E}[F_{N}(\alpha,\beta)^{2k+1}]=0.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{2p+1}\frac{(-t)^{k}\mathbb{E}[F_{N}(\alpha,\beta)^{k}]}{k!N^{k}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}\leqslant 4p+2}\frac{1}{k_{1}!k_{2}!}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}}t^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{2^{k_{2}}N^{k_{1}+2k_{2}}}\mathbb{E}[(K(\alpha)+K(\beta))^{k_{1}}(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2k_{2}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}\leqslant 2p+1}\frac{1}{k_{1}!k_{2}!}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}}t^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{2^{k_{2}}N^{k_{1}+2k_{2}}}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}[(K(\alpha)+K(\beta))^{k_{1}}(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2k_{2}}]+O_{t}(N^{-2p-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+2k_{2}\leqslant 2p+1}\frac{(-1)^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+k_{2}}}{\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!k_{2}!2^{k_{2}}N^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+2k_{2}}}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}[K(\alpha)^{\ell_{1}}K(\beta)^{\ell_{2}}(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2k_{2}}]+O_{t}(N^{-2p-2}).

In the second equality, we used the fact that the terms such that

are absorbed in the

, the terms vanish unless both

with

∎

3
Random surface representation

In this section, we recall the basics of integration over Hurwitz spaces, which is equivalent to the enumeration of ramified coverings of surfaces, then we state and prove two versions of Theorem
1.2
.

3.1
Integration over Hurwitz spaces

Let
\Sigma_{g}
be a compact connected Riemann surface of genus
g\geqslant 0
. A
ramified covering
of
\Sigma_{g}
is the data of a Riemann surface
X
together with a non-constant holomorphic map
\pi:X\to\Sigma_{g}
. By standard results of complex analysis,
\pi^{-1}(x)\subset X
is discrete for any
x\in\Sigma_{g}
. The surface
\Sigma_{g}
is called the
base space
, or the
target space
. Locally around a point
p\in X
,
\pi
is conjugated (using holomorphic coordinate changes) to a monomial
z\mapsto z^{k}
on
\mathbb{C}
, with
k\geqslant 1
, and
e_{p}=k
is called the
ramification index
of
X
at
p
. The point
p\in X
is said to be a
ramification point
if
e_{p}>1
, and we denote by
R=\{\pi(p),p\in X\ \text{s.t.}\ e_{p}>1\}\subset\Sigma_{g}
the
ramification locus
of
X\to\Sigma_{g}
, which is also a discrete set. There is an integer
n\geqslant 1
such that for any
x\in\Sigma_{g}\setminus R
, its preimage
\pi^{-1}(x)
is a finite set of cardinal
n
, and the integer
n
is denoted by
\deg(X)
and called the
degree
of the covering. Note that for any
x\in\Sigma_{g}
we have the identity

n=\deg(X)=\sum_{p\in\pi^{-1}(x)}e_{p},

so that the preimage
\pi^{-1}(x)
has less than
n
points for
x\in R
. The Riemann–Hurwitz formula states that

(16)

\chi(X)=\deg(X)\chi(\Sigma_{g})+\sum_{p\in X}(e_{p}-1),

where
\chi(X)=2-2g(X)
is the Euler characteristic of the surface
X
.

Thanks to the monodromy map, for any fixed
x\in\Sigma_{g}\setminus R
, ramified coverings
\pi:X\to\Sigma_{g}
of degree
n
with ramification locus
R
are in bijection with the set
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g}\setminus R,x),S_{n})
of homomorphisms from the fundamental group of
\Sigma_{g}\setminus R
based in
x
to the permutation group
S_{n}.
Equivalence classes of ramified coverings of
\Sigma_{g}
are therefore in bijection with

\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g}\setminus R,x),S_{n})/S_{n}.

We denote by
[X]
the equivalence class of a ramified covering
X.
Note that the degree and Euler characteristic of a ramified covering only depends on its equivalence class : we can denote it by
\deg(X)=\deg([X])
and
\chi(X)=\chi([X])
respectively.
If
R
is empty, then it is the space of representations of surface groups, and it corresponds to the set of equivalence classes of
unramified coverings
. Random models in this setting have been extensively studied for compact surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 2
by Magee, Naud and Puder
[
36
,
35
,
34
]
.

In this work, we consider the case of a complex torus
\mathbb{T}
, i.e.
g=1
. Given conjugacy classes
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
of
S_{n}
, the subset of ramified coverings
X\to\mathbb{T}
with ramification locus
R=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
and with monodromy in the class
C_{i}
around
x_{i}
is the set

\{(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k})\in S_{n}^{2}\times C_{1}\times\ldots\times C_{k}\ |\ [\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}]\tau_{1}\ldots\tau_{k}=1\}.

Note that
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
are assumed to be nontrivial conjugacy classes, otherwise there would be no ramification. We say that there is a
generic ramification
over
x\in R
if the corresponding monodromy is conjugated to a transposition. Let
\mathbb{X}_{1}(n,k)
be the set of ramified coverings
X\to\mathbb{T}
with degree
n
and with
k
generic ramifications. Its quotient by the action of
S_{n}
is the
Hurwitz space

\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,k)
, whose cardinal is the
Hurwitz number

H_{1}(n,k)
.

The Riemann–Hurwitz formula (
16
) gives

\chi(X)=-2k,\quad\forall[X]\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,2k),

therefore the Euler characteristic of
X
does not depend on its degree, which is specific of the case where the target surface is a torus.

In general, Hurwitz numbers can be expressed in terms of irreducible representations of the symmetric group
[
27
]
, and it is in particular the case of those we care about:

(17)

H_{1}(n,k)=\sum_{\alpha\vdash n}K(\alpha)^{k}.

Using Proposition
2.2
we find that
H_{1}(n,k)=0
for all odd
k
. The asymptotic behaviour of the number
H^{\prime}_{1}(n,k)
of
connected
7
7

7

For a correspondence between enumeration of connected and disconnected coverings, see
[
15
, §2.2]
for instance.
ramified coverings has been investigated in the groundbreaking paper
[
15
]
by Eskin and Okounkov (recently generalized by Aggarwal
[
1
]
), who used them to compute asymptotics of Masur–Veech volumes of strata of moduli spaces of abelian differentials, or translation surfaces.

As opposed to the aforementioned works, we do not exactly consider a uniform counting of equivalence classes of ramified coverings, because we also randomize the degree and number of ramification points of the covering: for any real parameter
t>0
, let
\rho_{t}
be the measure on the countably infinite space

\mathcal{R}=\bigsqcup_{n\geqslant 1}\bigsqcup_{k\geqslant 0}\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,2k),

defined by

d\rho_{t}([X])=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{t}^{n}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\sum_{[Y]\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,2k)}\delta_{[Y]}([X]).

The measure
\rho_{t}
can be interpreted as a randomization of the counting measure on
\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,2k)
, when
n
is taken to follow a geometric distribution of parameter
1-q_{t}
and
m=2k
a Poisson distribution of parameter
t
conditioned to take even values. It is the measure-theoretic equivalent of (a specialization of) the two-parameter generating series
\mathcal{F}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}[[q,X]]
of Hurwitz numbers on the torus with varying degrees and generic ramifications
[
27
]
:

\mathcal{F}_{1}(q,X)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}q^{n}\frac{X^{2k}}{(2k)!}H_{1}(n,2k).

In particular, we will be interested in integrals of functions of the degree and Euler characteristic of the coverings: for any function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
we have the identity

(18)

\int_{\mathcal{R}}f(\deg(X),\chi(X))d\rho_{t}([X])=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{t}^{n}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}f(n,-2k)H_{1}(n,2k).

Remark 3.1
.

The measure
\rho_{t}
has infinite mass (see Lemma
A.2
for more details), so it cannot be normalized into a probability measure. In that sense, it is a slight language abuse to speak about “random surfaces”. However, if we replace the counting measure on
\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,k)
by the uniform measure and we apply the proper normalization to the distributions of
\deg(X)
and
\chi(X)
we obtain a true probability measure

d\mu_{t}([X])=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{q_{t}^{n}}{1-q_{t}}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!\cosh(t)}\sum_{[Y]\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(n,2k)}\frac{1}{H_{1}(n,2k)}\delta_{[Y]}([X]).

This normalization is inessential here, and using it would rather complicate the formulas, therefore we will stick with the initial measure
\rho_{t}
, while keeping the terminology of random surfaces.

Remark 3.2
.

Our definition of the measure on the space of ramified coverings is slightly different from the one we considered in
[
28
]
. The main difference in the approach of the present paper is that the ramification locus is not specified, only the number of ramification points; this enables a simpler definition, and the measures considered are in fact equivalent, because the measure
\rho_{t}
only depends on the number of ramification points, not on their positions. We believe that it avoids superfluous – yet interesting – topological and measure-theoretic developments. The interested readers might find such developments in the last chapter of
[
33
]
.

As
\rho_{t}
has infinite mass, any integral representation with respect to
\rho_{t}
needs a priori to have a cutoff, be it on the Euler characteristic (cutoff on
k
) or on the degree (cutoff on
n
). As pointed out above, in the case of the torus, both quantities are independent, therefore we have to make a choice. We will explore both approaches in this paper, because we believe that both have their merits and demerits: imposing a cutoff on the Euler characteristic of on the degree will lead us to Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
respectively.

3.1.1

Proof of Theorem

for

\displaystyle\sum_{2k_{1}+k_{2}\leqslant p}\frac{t^{2k_{1}+k_{2}}}{N^{2k_{1}+k_{2}}2^{k_{2}}(2k_{1})!k_{2}!}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{t}^{n}n^{k_{2}}H_{1}(n,2k_{1})+O_{t}(N^{-p-1}).

\displaystyle\sum_{2k_{1}\leqslant p}\frac{t^{p+1}}{N^{p+1}(2k_{1})!(p-2k_{1}+1)!}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{t}^{n(1-1/N)}p(n)n^{4k_{1}+p+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{t^{p+1}}{N^{p+1}(p+1)!}\sum_{2k_{1}\leqslant p}\binom{p+1}{2k_{1}}\sum_{n\geqslant 1}n^{3p+1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}n(1-1/N)+\pi\sqrt{2n/3}},

Proof of Theorem
3.4
for
{\mathrm{U}}(N)

By Corollary

For fixed
\gamma\in(0,1)

be a new subset with a cutoff on the size of partitions instead of their lengths. For

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(e^{\frac{t}{2}\Delta_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}})=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta(q_{t})}{\phi(q_{t})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\frac{t}{N}F^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n)}\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}^{(1)}(\gamma)}(\alpha,\beta,n)\right]+\sum_{(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{(1)}\setminus\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}^{(1)}(\gamma)}e^{-\frac{t}{2}c_{2}^{A^{\prime}}(\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n))}.

2.7

\displaystyle\sum_{(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{(1)}\setminus\tilde{\Lambda}_{N}^{(1)}(\gamma)}e^{-\frac{t}{2}\left(c_{2}^{A^{\prime}}(\lambda_{N}^{A^{\prime}}(\alpha,\beta,n))\right)}\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|>N^{\gamma}}\sum_{\beta\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|+\frac{1}{2}|\beta|+\left(n+\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{N}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{|\beta|>N^{\gamma}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|+\frac{1}{2}|\beta|+\left(n+\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{N}\right)^{2}}.

On the one hand, for any

and the end of the proof consists in expanding the exponentials of contents in power series and identifying the measures.

4
Applications to gauge/string duality

In this section, which is quite independent of the previous ones, we translate the main results of the present paper in terms of gauge/string duality for the two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory.

4.1
Yang–Mills/Hurwitz duality

Euclidean quantum Yang–Mills theory on a manifold
M
endowed with a volume form corresponds to path integrals with respect to the formal measure

(32)

d\mu_{M,G}(A)=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}S_{\mathrm{YM}}(A)}\mathcal{D}A,

defined on the space
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}
of connections modulo gauge transformations of a
G
-principal bundle over
M
, where
G
is a compact Lie group named the structure group. The group of gauge transformations is
\mathcal{G}=\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(\Sigma,G)
and acts on the space of connections by

g\cdot A=g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg,\quad\forall g\in\mathcal{G},\ \forall A\in\mathcal{A}.

Let us describe the different parts of (
32
):

–

The measure
\mathcal{D}A
is the push-forward of a formal Lebesgue measure
8
8

8

It is formal because there exists no such measure in the sense of Lebesgue integration.
on
\mathcal{A}
by the quotient map
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}
,

–

The functional
S_{\mathrm{YM}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is a gauge-invariant action called the
Yang–Mills action
, defined by

S_{\mathrm{YM}}(A)=\langle F_{A}\wedge\star F_{A}\rangle,

where
F_{A}=dA+A\wedge A
is the curvature of the connection,

–

T>0
is a real parameter called the
coupling constant
,

–

The quantity
Z
is a normalization constant called the
partition function
, ensuring that the total mass of
\mu_{M,G}
is 1.

The partition function on a closed oriented surface of genus
g\geqslant 0
and total area
t>0
admits the following Fourier expansion
[
47
,
50
]
:

(33)

Z_{G}(g,t)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{G}}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}e^{-\frac{t}{2}c_{2}(\lambda)}.

From this formula, it appears that Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
describe the asymptotic behavior of the partition function
Z_{G_{N}}(1,t)
with structure group
G_{N}
. See
[
31
]
for a recent review of the construction of the Yang–Mills measure and the computation of the partition function.

Since the breakthrough work by ’t Hooft
[

]
and further developments by Gross and Taylor

,

]
in two dimensions, the observables of
\mathrm{YM}_{2}
are conjectured to be related to string theory; although many descriptions have been given in physics for this duality in the last 30 years (this two-dimensional gauge/string duality remains an active topic in physics as of today

7
]
), essentially formal results have been obtained. Many other gauge/string dualities are conjectured: the most famous might be the AdS/CFT correspondence proposed by Maldacena
[
39
]
, and more recent dualities seem to be related to the geometric Langlands conjecture
[
24
]
, but most dualities in dimension higher than 2 are assuming supersymmetry (as far as we know). It is worth mentioning that the techniques of random partitions that we apply have also been helpful for a particular case of four-dimensional supersymmetric gauge theory called
Seiberg–Witten theory

[
40
]
. In the case of two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, from the perspective of Gross and Taylor, the dual string theory corresponds to a theory of random surfaces, more specifically random ramified coverings of the base space (Hurwitz theory). Most of the literature treats the case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
, but other classical groups also attracted a bit of interest
[
46
]
; in the case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
, a coupling of random ramified coverings appears in the expressions and is interpreted as a coupling of two
chiral sectors
of the theory. We shall not elaborate on this notion of chirality, but mention that the
chiral partition function
is obtained by replacing the sum over couples of partitions by a sum over a single partition
\lambda
such that
\ell(\lambda)\leqslant N
. The idea is that the chiral partition function is easier to understand in the large-
N
limit, both in terms of asymptotic expansion and in terms of string representation (see
[
41
]
), but it fails to capture the complete asymptotic behavior of the full partition function in general. This is why we developed the tools described in §
2.3
in order to find the correct correspondence between partitions and highest weights.

A first step towards a mathematical proof of gauge/string duality on compact surfaces has been achieved for
g=1
and
G={\mathrm{U}}(N)
by the authors in
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
: they proved that
Z_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}(1,t)
admits an asymptotic expansion which can be described in terms of Hurwitz numbers
H_{1}(n,2k)
. Theorem
1.1
extends this result to all compact classical groups, and in particular for
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
we recover the asymptotic expansion, in genus 1, corresponding to the so-called
Gross–Taylor asymptotic series

[
11
, Eq. (2.2)]
. A random surface interpretation in the spirit of Gross–Taylor was found in
[
28
, Corollary 6.2]
for
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
for the chiral partition function, but the case of the full partition function remained unsolved – and supposedly harder. Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
fill this gap, and not only bring an answer for the full partition with gauge group
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, but also for the other groups. In particular, in the large-
N
limit, their statements imply
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, and Equation (
23
) is a mathematically rigorous equivalent of
[
17
, Eq. (3.6)]
in genus 1.

In the cases of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
the couplings of random coverings from Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
correspond to the couplings of chiral sectors mentioned earlier, and the absence of coupling in the cases of
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
can be interpreted as an intrinsic chirality of the theory for these gauge groups. The factor
N^{\chi(X)}
in the random surface representation already appeared in the foundational paper
[
52
]
and can be found in other mathematical works more or less related to gauge/string duality
[
45
,
10
]
.

4.2
Yang–Mills/Gromov–Witten duality

As an alternative to the theory of random ramified coverings initiated by Gross and Taylor, we shall now introduce a new gauge/string duality, where the dual string model is a topological sigma-model coupled to gravity. Initially developed by Witten in the late ’80s
[
49
,
51
]
, it is based on similar path integrals from conformal field theory. Given a Riemannian manifold
X
(playing the role of spacetime), one considers path integrals over the space
\mathcal{M}_{X}
of maps
\phi:\Sigma\to X
, where
\Sigma
is a closed Riemann surface, with respect to a measure

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}^{X}}e^{-S(\phi)}\mathcal{D}\phi.

The measure
\mathcal{D}\phi
is the formal Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{M}_{X}
, which is ill-defined as in the case of Yang–Mills theory, and the functional
S
is a conformally invariant Lagrangian. The partition function
\mathcal{Z}^{X}
(we use a calligraphic
\mathcal{Z}
to avoid confusion with the Yang–Mills partition function
Z
) is defined by

\mathcal{Z}^{X}=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{X}}e^{-S(\phi)}\mathcal{D}\phi.

This formal definition of path integrals can made rigorous under further assumptions on
X
, with the help of Gromov–Witten invariants

\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\omega)\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}(\omega)\rangle_{d}^{X}.

These invariants correspond to integrals of some cohomology classes
\tau_{k_{i}}(\omega)
, called
\tau
-classes, with respect to the moduli space of stable maps
\pi:C\to X
of degree
d
. We defer to Appendix
B
the precise definition of these invariants and the associated moduli space, as we do not need them directly. The
n
-point correlation function of the physical model, which also corresponds to the partition function with
n
fixed boundary conditions, is defined as the generating function

(34)

\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{X}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\sum_{d\geqslant 0}q^{d}\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\omega)\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}(\omega)\rangle_{d}^{X}z_{1}^{k_{1}+1}\ldots z_{n}^{k_{n}+1},

where
q\in(0,1)
is a coupling parameter and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are arbitrary coupling constants.

Okounkov and Pandharipande developed a Gromov–Witten/Hurwitz correspondence, which can be summarized in the following statement: Gromov–Witten invariants with target a nonsingular algebraic curve can be expressed combinatorially through shifted symmetric functions

\mathbf{p}_{k}:\lambda\in\mathscr{P}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left((\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i)^{k}-(\frac{1}{2}-i)^{k}\right)+(1-2^{-k})\zeta(-k).

The functions
\mathbf{p}_{k}
are zeta-regularizations
9
9

9

An enlightening explanation of the necessity of this regularization can be found in
[
42
, §0.4.3]
.
of the power sums

p_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\left(\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i\right)^{k},

and they can be described by the means of exponential generating functions

(35)

\mathbf{e}(\lambda,z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{z(\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathbf{p}_{k}(\lambda)z^{k}.

The precise formula of the correspondence for a curve
X
of genus
g
is
[
42
, eq. (0.25)]
:

(36)

\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\omega)\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}(\omega)\rangle_{d}^{X}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\left(\frac{f_{\lambda}}{d!}\right)^{2-2g}\frac{\mathbf{p}_{k_{1}+1}(\lambda)}{(k_{1}+1)!}\ldots\frac{\mathbf{p}_{k_{n}+1}(\lambda)}{(k_{n}+1)!},

where
f_{\lambda}
is the dimension of the representation of the symmetric group
S_{d}
indexed by
\lambda.

If the target is
\mathbb{T}
, which is an elliptic curve, it has genus
g=1
and the factor involving
f_{\lambda}/d!
vanishes. Moreover, for any
\lambda\in\mathscr{P}
,

\mathbf{p_{2}}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left((\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i)^{2}-(\frac{1}{2}-i)^{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\lambda_{i}(\lambda_{i}+1-2i)=2K(\lambda),

thus it is easy to check that the Hurwitz numbers enumerating ramified coverings on the torus with simple ramifications are particular Gromov–Witten invariants:

\left\langle\tau_{1}(\omega)^{2m}\right\rangle_{n}^{\mathbb{T}}=H_{1}(n,2m).

The new gauge/string duality we propose is the following: instead of writing the partition function as an integral over a Hurwitz space (Theorem
1.2
), we show that its asymptotic expansion obtained in Theorem
1.1
can be expressed in terms of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{q_{t}}^{\mathbb{T}}
.

Corollary 4.1

(Gauge/string duality for YM on a torus)
.

Let
(G_{N})
be a sequence of compact classical groups of the same type
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A,B,C,D\}
with
G_{N}\subset{\mathrm{GL}}_{N}(\mathbb{C})

, there are explicit functionals
\{\Psi_{k,t}^{\tau},k\geqslant 0\}
of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{q_{t}}^{\mathbb{T}}
such that for any

(37)

Z_{G_{N}}(1,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\frac{\Psi_{k,t}^{\tau}(\mathcal{Z}_{q_{t}}^{\mathbb{T}})}{N^{k}}+O_{t}(N^{-p-1}).

The duality is summarized in the following diagram, where the horizontal arrows describe approximate correspondences up to

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z_{G_{N}}(1,t)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Tr}(e^{\frac{t}{2}\Delta_{G_{N}}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sum_{k=0}^{p}\frac{a_{k}^{\tau}(t)}{N^{k}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Cor.~\ref{cor:gauge_string}}

If we notice that (

\mathrm{Tr}(e^{\frac{t}{2}\Delta_{G_{N}}})

4.1

1.3

. We conjecture that it admits a generalization to any genus
g\geqslant 2
; however, it is no longer only related to the expansion of the trace of the heat kernel, but also on the expansion of the Witten zeta function
\zeta_{G_{N}}

[
50
]
, for which only two partial results have been obtained:

–

an asymptotic expansion up to any order (for
g\geqslant 2
and
t=0

[

–

an estimation of the first nontrivial coefficient of the expansion

To conclude, let us note that physicists also conjectured that this duality goes beyond the partition function, and holds for Wilson loop expectations, which are integrals of traces of holonomies along loops in the base surface with respect to the Yang–Mills measure
[
18
,
17
,
11
]
. Up to date, the best rigorous results on Wilson loops have been obtained for the continuous Yang–Mills measure in the plane
[
45
]
, or the discrete measure in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

[
10
]
. The gauge/string duality is much less explicit, because the string side is described in terms of “combinatorial” surfaces (i.e. maps) rather than ramified coverings or stable maps. The correspondence between the approaches of these works and ours has yet to be explored, but it is quite unlikely that these techniques of random maps can be easily extended to the continuous Yang–Mills measure on surfaces other than the plane, mainly because of nontrivial topological effects of the compact surfaces on all observables.

4.3
Proof of the duality

The generating function of Hurwitz numbers of base
\mathbb{T}
with
k
generic ramifications is the function
\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
defined by

(38)

\mathcal{F}_{1,k}(q)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}H_{1}(n,k)q^{n}.

Let
\mathcal{D}=q\frac{d}{dq}
be a homogeneous first-order differential operator on power series in
q
. Many observables of
q
-uniform random partitions can be rewritten as derivatives of this generating function: if
\alpha
is a
q
-uniform random partition, for any
k\geqslant 0
and any
m\geqslant 1
,

(39)

\phi(q)^{-1}\mathbb{E}[K(\alpha)^{k}|\alpha|^{m}]=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}n^{m}H_{1}(n,k)q^{n}=\mathcal{D}^{m}\mathcal{F}_{1,k}(q).

In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{1,0}(q)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}p(n)q^{n}=\phi(q)^{-1}
. The key observation to get the gauge/string duality is that the coefficients of (
2
) can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
. As we shall see, the proof of Theorem
1.3
will follow.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any type
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A,B,C,D\}
and any
t>0
, the coefficients
(a_{k}^{\tau}(t))
of the expansion of the central heat trace can be expressed in terms of generating functions of Hurwitz numbers:

(i)

For
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A\}
,

(40)

a_{2k}^{\tau}(t)=\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+2k_{3}+2k_{4}=2k}\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}}^{\tau}(t)\mathcal{D}^{k_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{1,2k_{3}}(q_{t})\mathcal{D}^{k_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{1,2k_{4}}(q_{t}),

(ii)

For
\tau\in\{B,C,D\}
,

(41)

a_{k}^{\tau}(t)=\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}=k}\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{\tau}(t)\mathcal{D}^{k_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{1,2k_{2}}(q_{t}).

The coefficients
\kappa
are explicit:

(42)

\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}}^{A^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{k_{2}}t^{k_{1}+k_{2}+2k_{3}+2k_{4}}\mathcal{D}^{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}}\theta(q_{t})}{2^{k_{1}+k_{2}}k_{1}!k_{2}!(2k_{3})!(2k_{4})!},

(43)

\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}}^{A}(t)=\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!(-1)^{k_{2}}t^{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}+2k_{3}+2k_{4}}}{2^{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}}\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}!k_{1}!k_{2}!(2k_{3})!(2k_{4})!},

(44)

\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{B}(t)=\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{D}(t)=\frac{t^{k}}{2^{k_{1}}k_{1}!(2k_{2})!},

(45)

\kappa_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{C}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}}t^{k}}{2^{k_{1}}k_{1}!(2k_{2})!}.

Proof.

The results follows from the expression of the coefficients obtained in the proof of Theorem
2.9
, combined with (
39
). Let us detail the proof for
a_{k}^{B}(t)
and leave the others to the reader. By the binomial theorem,

\displaystyle a_{k}^{B}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}\frac{(-t)^{k}}{2^{k}k_{1}!k_{2}!}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{t}^{n}(-n)^{k_{1}}\sum_{\mu\vdash n}(2K(\mu))^{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}\frac{(-1)^{k_{2}}t^{k}}{2^{k_{1}}k_{1}!k_{2}!}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{t}^{n}n^{k_{1}}H_{1}(n,k_{2}).

As Hurwitz numbers
H_{1}(n,k_{2})
vanish for odd values of
k_{2}
, we can even restrict to
k_{1}+2k_{2}=k
and replace
k_{2}
by
2k_{2}
in the expression. Then, using (
39
), we finally obtain

a_{k}^{B}(t)=\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}=k}\frac{t^{k}}{2^{k_{1}}k_{1}!(2k_{2})!}\mathcal{D}^{k_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{1,2k_{2}}(q_{t}),

which is the expected formula.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Lemma
4.2
we know that the coefficients are explicit functionals of generating functions of Hurwitz numbers. We conclude by using the fact that the generating function
\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
can be easily expressed in terms of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{\mathbb{T}}
:

(46)

\mathcal{F}_{1,k}(q)=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\frac{d}{dz_{1}}\right)^{2}\ldots\left(\frac{d}{dz_{k}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{\mathbb{T}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})|_{z_{1}=\ldots=z_{k}=0}.

∎

Appendix A
Estimates on Hurwitz numbers and applications

In this appendix, we give simple yet useful bounds on Hurwitz numbers
H_{1}(n,k)
and their consequences in terms of integration, in particular the fact that
\rho_{t}
has infinite mass and that the functions
\Phi_{t,N}^{A}
and
\Phi_{t,N}^{A^{\prime}}
are not integrable with respect to
\rho_{t}
.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
n\geqslant 1
and
k\geqslant 0
,

(47)

4^{-k}(n-1)^{4k}\leqslant H_{1}(n,2k)\leqslant p(n)n^{4k}.

Proof.

Let us fix
n\geqslant 1
. For any
k\geqslant 0
,

H_{1}(n,2k)=\sum_{\alpha\vdash n}K(\alpha)^{2k},

and we apply (
9
) to get the upper bound. If we denote by
(n)
the partition corresponding to a Young diagram with only one row of length
n
, we have
K((n))=n(n-1)/2,
and we find that

H_{1}(n,2k)\geqslant K((n))^{2k}=\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\right)^{2k},

which implies the lower bound.
∎

The following lemma states that the measure
\rho_{t}
has infinite mass:

Lemma A.2
.

For any
p\geqslant 1
and any
t>0
, the following sums are finite:

\sum_{n=1}^{p}q_{t}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}H_{1}(n,2k),\quad\sum_{k=0}^{p}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{t}^{n}H_{1}(n,2k).

However, the double series

\sum_{n\geqslant 1,k\geqslant 0}q_{t}^{n}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}H_{1}(n,2k)

diverges.

Proof.

The first assertion boils down to prove that if we fix
n
or
k
, the sum over the other variable converges. For any
n\geqslant 1
, by the upper bound of Lemma
A.1
,

A_{n}:=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}H_{1}(n,2k)\leqslant p(n)\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(tn^{2})^{2k}}{(2k)!}=p(n)\cosh(tn^{2})<\infty.

In fact, by using (
17
) and Fubini’s theorem, we can even compute the exact value of
A_{n}
:

A_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}K(\lambda)^{2k}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\cosh(tK(\lambda)).

Likewise, if we fix
k\geqslant 0
, then

B_{k}:=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q_{t}^{n}H_{1}(n,2k)\leqslant\sum_{n\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}n}p(n)n^{4k},

and the RHS also converges because
p(n)\leqslant e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}
for
n
large enough (see
[
5
]
for instance).

However, as
n
tends to infinity, we see that
q_{t}^{n}A_{n}
diverges. By the lower bound of Proposition
A.1
,

A_{n}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}H_{1}(n,2k)\geqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{1}{(2k)!}\left(\frac{t}{4}\left(n-1\right)^{2}\right)^{2k}=\cosh\left(\frac{t}{4}(n-1)^{2}\right),

and

e^{-\frac{t}{2}n}A_{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{t}{2}n}\left(e^{\frac{t}{4}(n-1)^{2}}+e^{-\frac{t}{4}(n-1)^{2}}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{t}{4}(n^{2}-4n+1)}.

The RHS does not converge to zero (it even diverges as
n\to\infty
), therefore it is trivially not summable.
∎

We now wonder about integrability the coupling functions appearing in the random surface representation in the unitary and special unitary cases:

Proposition A.3

(Non-integrability of
\Phi_{t,N}^{A^{\prime}}
and
\Phi_{t,N}^{A}
)
.

For any
p,N\geqslant 1
and any
\tau\in\{A^{\prime},A\}
, with
\Phi_{t,N}^{\tau}
defined in (
25
) and (
26
), the following integral diverges:

\displaystyle I_{p}^{\tau}(N)=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\chi(X_{1})+\chi(X_{2})\geqslant-2p\}}\Phi_{t,N}^{\tau}(X_{1},X_{2})N^{\chi(X_{1})+\chi(X_{2})}d\rho_{t}^{\otimes 2}([X_{1}],[X_{2}]).

Proof.

Case of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
.
Let us fix
p\geqslant 1
. Using (
47
), we get

\displaystyle I_{p}^{A^{\prime}}(N)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}\leqslant p}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}n^{2}}\left(e^{\frac{tn}{N}(n_{1}-n_{2})}+e^{-\frac{tn}{N}(n_{1}-n_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\times N^{-2k_{1}-2k_{2}}H_{1}(n_{1},2k_{1})H_{1}(n_{2},2k_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}\leqslant p}\sum_{n_{1}\geqslant n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}e^{-\frac{tN^{2}}{2}+t(n_{1}-n_{2})}

\displaystyle\times N^{-2k_{1}-2k_{2}}4^{-k_{1}-k_{2}}(n_{1}-1)^{4k_{1}}(n_{2}-1)^{4k_{2}}.

and it is clear that for any fixed
k_{1},k_{2},N
,

\sum_{n_{1}\geqslant n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})}e^{-\frac{tN^{2}}{2}+t(n_{1}-n_{2})}\times N^{-2k_{1}-2k_{2}}4^{-k_{1}-k_{2}}(n_{1}-1)^{4k_{1}}(n_{2}-1)^{4k_{2}}

diverges, so that it is obviously the case for
I_{p}^{A^{\prime}}(N)
.

Case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
.
Let us fix
p\geqslant 1
. Similarly, using (
47
), we get

\displaystyle I_{p}^{A}(N)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}\leqslant p}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})+\frac{t^{2}}{2N^{2}}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\times N^{-2k_{1}-2k_{2}}H_{1}(n_{1},2k_{1})H_{1}(n_{2},2k_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}\leqslant p}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})+\frac{t^{2}}{2N^{2}}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\times N^{-2k_{1}-2k_{2}}4^{-k_{1}-k_{2}}(n_{1}-1)^{4k_{1}}(n_{2}-1)^{4k_{2}}.

However, it is clear that for any fixed
k_{1},k_{2},N
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\geqslant 1}e^{-\frac{t}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2})+\frac{t^{2}}{2N^{2}}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}}(n_{1}-1)^{4k_{1}}(n_{2}-1)^{4k_{2}}

diverges, so that it is obviously the case for
I_{p}^{A}(N)
.
∎

Appendix B
Gromov–Witten invariants

This appendix is aimed to provide a self-contained definition of Gromov–Witten invariants, which are well-known objects for algebraic geometers but not necessarily to other readers who might be interested in the paper. We will keep it short, and hope that it could serve as a reading guide for the already overwhelming literature on the subject.

Definition B.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve, and
g,n\geqslant 0
and
d>0
be fixed integers. The
moduli space of stable maps

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,d)
is the set of isomorphism classes of pointed algebraic maps
\pi:C\to X
of degree
d
, where
C
is a nonsingular curve of genus
g
with
n
distinct marked points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
.

This moduli space has been introduced by Kontsevich
[
26
]
, and we refer to
[
23
]
for a general overview in the context of mirror symmetry. In the setting we care about,
X
is a compact Riemann surface,
\pi:C\to X
is a ramified covering of
X
of degree
d
with ramification locus
\{\pi(p_{1}),\ldots,\pi(p_{n})\}
, and the isomorphism is described by conjugation of monodromies around the points
\pi(p_{i})
. We will denote the stable map by
(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},\pi)
. There are natural morphisms

{\mathrm{ev}}_{i}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,d)&\longrightarrow&X\\
(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},\pi)&\longmapsto&\pi(p_{i}),\end{array}\right.

which are the evaluations of the map at the marked points. In the setting of ramified coverings, they correspond to the projections onto the points of the ramification locus of
X
. The cotangent line bundles
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,d)
are line bundles over the moduli space where the fiber of
L_{i}
above the point
(C,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},\pi)
is the cotangent line
T_{p_{i}}C
. The most important cohomology classes in Gromov–Witten theory are
\psi
-classes and
\tau
-classes:

–

Appendix C
Generating functions of integer partitions and the Bloch–Okounkov theorem

For any formal parameter
q
, let
F_{q}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
be the following generating function, called
n
-
point function
:

(49)

F_{q}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}q^{n}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t_{k}^{\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i}.

It converges if
1<|t_{i}|
and
|t_{1}\ldots t_{n}|<\frac{1}{q}
, and admits a meromorphic extension on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. A first easy result is a correspondence between
F_{q}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{\mathbb{T}}
.

Proposition C.1
.

For any
q\in(0,1)
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(50)

F_{q}(e^{z_{1}},\ldots,e^{z_{n}})=\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{\mathbb{T}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}).

Proof.

Fix
q\in(0,1)
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

F_{q_{t}}(e^{z_{1}},\ldots,e^{z_{n}})=\sum_{d\geqslant 0}q_{t}^{d}\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\mathbf{e}(\lambda,z_{1})\ldots\mathbf{e}(\lambda,z_{n}).

For any
d\geqslant 0
, combining (
36
) and (
35
) gives

\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\omega)\ldots\tau_{k_{n}}(\omega)\rangle_{d}^{\mathbb{T}}z_{1}^{k_{1}+1}\ldots z_{n}^{k_{n}+1}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash d}\mathbf{e}(\lambda,z_{1})\ldots\mathbf{e}(\lambda,z_{n}),

and the result follows.
∎

The generating function
\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
, which is expressed in terms of
\mathcal{Z}_{q}^{\mathbb{T}}

\mathbf{p}_{k}(\lambda)-(2^{-k}-1)\zeta(-k)=\left(t\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{k}\left.\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i}-\frac{1}{\log t}\right)\right|_{t=1}

In particular,

\mathbf{p}_{2}(\lambda)=\left(t\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{2}\left.\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i}-\frac{1}{\log t}\right)\right|_{t=1},

because

\displaystyle H_{1}(n,k)=

\displaystyle 2^{-k}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\mathbf{p}_{2}(\lambda)\ldots\mathbf{p}_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-k}\left(t_{1}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\right)^{2}\ldots\left(t_{m}\frac{d}{dt_{k}}\right)^{2}\left.\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t_{\ell}^{\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-i}-\frac{1}{\log t_{\ell}}\right)\right|_{t_{1}=\ldots=t_{k}=1}.
