Association between EEG microstate and cognitive function in depressed patients with and without suicidal ideation
Authors: Yuhang He, Fengchun Wu, Zhendong Zhang, Yun Yi, Shixuan Feng, Kunrong Lin, Wanting Xu, Hehua Li, Kai Wu, Mei Fong Lam, Yuanyuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is closely linked to suicidal ideation (SI), which has been associated with cognitive dysfunction and abnormal brain network connectivity. However, the relationships among SI, cognitive functioning, and large-scale brain dynamics in MDD remain unclear. Resting-state EEG microstate analysis provides a novel method for exploring these associations by capturing transient global brain activity patterns.MethodsEighty-eight drug-naïve, first-episode MDD patients were divided into SI (n = 54) and noSI (n = 34) groups, with 34 age- and gender-matched healthy controls. SI was assessed using the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI), and cognition was evaluated with the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB). Resting-state EEG was recorded, and microstate parameters were analyzed. Group differences were tested with ANOVA and ANCOVA, correlations examined with Pearson and partial analyses, and multiple comparisons controlled by FDR correction.ResultsThe noSI group showed greater microstate D coverage (p = 0.037) and higher transition probability from microstate A to D (p = 0.011) than the SI group; these effects remained after controlling for depressive and anxiety symptoms and agitation and FDR correction. In the SI group, both measures correlated positively with SDMT scores, and SI severity was negatively related to microstate C coverage and transition probability from microstate B to C; however, none of these correlations survived adjustment.ConclusionMDD patients with SI show alterations in EEG microstate dynamics that are associated with reduced processing speed. These findings indicate that large-scale brain network instability may contribute to neurocognitive dysfunction related to suicidality. EEG microstates may provide preliminary insights into its neural basis and represent exploratory biomarkers that require replication and longitudinal validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable. This study is not a clinical trial. It is an observational study designed to investigate the relationship between EEG microstates and cognitive function in depressed patients with and without suicidal ideation. Therefore, no clinical trial registration number is applicable to this research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07617-2.

Introduction

Suicide is a serious global public health concern and remains one of the leading causes of disability and premature mortality [1, 2]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the strongest risk factors for suicide, associated with nearly a twentyfold increase in suicide-related outcomes [3]. Among individuals with MDD, the prevalence of suicidal ideation (SI) is as high as 53.1% [4]. Importantly, those experiencing SI are nearly four times more likely to attempt suicide compared to those without SI [5]. Beyond being a critical predictor of suicidal behavior, SI is increasingly recognized as being associated with cognitive dysfunction [6], including deficits in executive function, cognitive flexibility, information processing, and emotion regulation [7]. Given these associations, investigating the cognitive and neurophysiological correlates of SI in MDD may provide valuable insights into suicide vulnerability [8, 9].

Electroencephalography (EEG), due to its high temporal resolution and accessibility, has been widely used in suicide research among individuals with MDD. Previous studies have reported that MDD demonstrates increased spectral power in the alpha2 (9–11 Hz) and delta (2–4 Hz) bands, particularly in brain regions associated with suicide severity [10]. Event-related potential (ERP) studies have yielded relevant neurophysiological markers: for example, reward positivity in response to positive outcomes is negatively correlated with SI severity, while responses to negative outcomes are positively correlated [11]. Additionally, P3 components elicited by punishment-related cues and feedback can differentiate individuals with SI from those with a history of suicide attempts [12]. However, these EEG findings have provided limited insight into the temporal dynamics of large-scale brain network organization [13, 14].

EEG microstate analysis, which captures transient, quasi-stable configurations of brain activity, has emerged as a promising approach for assessing the brain’s fast-changing functional dynamics [15, 16]. Microstates typically last 80–120 milliseconds and are categorized into four canonical classes (A–D), each associated with distinct neural networks [17]. For instance, microstate C has been linked to the default mode network (DMN), and microstate D to the executive control network (ECN) [18, 19]. In patients with MDD, alterations in microstate D—such as reduced duration and frequency—have been associated with impairments in executive functioning and cognitive control [20]. More recently, He et al. [21] reported that adolescents with MDD and SI exhibit distinct microstate alterations compared to their non-SI counterparts, including a decreased occurrence and coverage of microstate B—linked to perceptual processing—and increased transition probabilities from microstates D and C to microstate A. These findings imply that SI may involve specific disruptions in the dynamic organization of large-scale brain networks, which can be captured through microstate analysis [22, 23].

Emerging evidence also indicates that microstate parameters are correlated with various cognitive functions, including attention, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [24–26]. Therefore, microstate analysis not only reflects dynamic brain organization but also serves as a potential neurophysiological window into cognitive dysfunction associated with SI. Despite these advances, no study has systematically investigated the combined relationship among SI, cognitive performance, and microstate dynamics in MDD. This gap limits our understanding of suicide-related brain function and the development of objective biomarkers for clinical assessment [27, 28]. Based on the above, we put forward two hypotheses: [1] individuals with MDD and SI would exhibit distinct EEG microstate patterns compared to those without SI; [2] these microstate parameters would be significantly associated with cognitive performance in the SI group. We recruited drug-naïve, first-episode MDD patients grouped based on the presence or absence of SI, as well as healthy controls. All participants underwent standardized cognitive assessment and resting-state EEG recording. Our aim was to investigate the neurophysiological correlates of SI and and evaluate whether EEG microstate parameters may reflect neurophysiological alterations associated with suicidal ideation in depression.

Methods

Participants

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version). Between June 2020 and September 2023, 88 medication-naïve first-episode MDD patients and 34 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited through the Department of Psychiatry at the Brain Hospital and via community advertisements. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. Inclusion criteria were: [1] right-handedness; [2] Chinese Han ethnicity; [3] for MDD patients, a score ≥ 17 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17); [4] first-episode MDD with no prior psychotropic medication use or irregular use for less than two weeks. For HCs, absence of any DSM-5 Axis I psychiatric disorder in the individual or in first-degree relatives within three generations. Exclusion criteria included: [1] current or past diagnosis of alcohol or substance use disorder (DSM-5); [2] intellectual disability, dementia, or other cognitive disorders; [3] severe physical illness (e.g., cardiovascular or thyroid diseases); [4] history of traumatic brain injury or epilepsy; [5] current or past diagnosis of neuroleptic malignant syndrome or severe tardive dyskinesia; [6] electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) within the past six months; [7] pregnancy or breastfeeding; [8] any contraindication to EEG assessment. Based on the presence of SI—defined as a score >0 on items 4 or 5 of the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI)—MDD patients were divided into two groups: those with SI (SI group, n = 54) and those without SI (noSI group, n = 34). An additional HC group (n = 34) was included, yielding three final study groups.

Clinical and cognitive assessments

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the HAMD-17 [29], and SI was measured with the BSSI [30, 31], based on the participants’ self-report over the past week. Cognitive performance was evaluated using the Chinese version of the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) [32–35], which has been validated in Chinese populations [32]. Based on previous research [34, 36], five cognitive domains were assessed: processing speed (SOP), attention/vigilance (AV), working memory (WM), verbal learning (VRB), and visual learning (VIS). Sop was measured with the Trail Making Test Part A (TMT-A), the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), and the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia–Symbol Coding (BACS-SC). Attention/vigilance was assessed using the Continuous Performance Test–Identical Pairs version (CPT-IP), and visual learning was assessed with the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test–Revised (BVMT-R). All assessments were administered by trained clinicians or graduate-level psychology students. Inter-rater reliability was maintained at an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) above 0.80.

EEG data collection

EEG recordings were obtained during the same clinical visit, corresponding to the patients’ current clinical state. EEG data were collected using a 32-channel scalp electrode system (Neuroscan), sampled at 1000 Hz, with the online reference electrode located at the Cz position. Scalp electrodes were positioned according to the international 10–20 system, and electrode impedance was maintained below 10 kΩ throughout the recording. Participants were seated in a dimly lit, quiet room, instructed to remain still with their eyes closed and maintain a relaxed state during the 6-minute resting-state session. Trained personnel continuously monitored the recordings to ensure data quality.

EEG preprocessing

EEG preprocessing was conducted in MATLAB 2022b using the EEGLAB toolbox. A bandpass filter ranging from 0.1 to 80 Hz was applied, along with a notch filter at 48–52 Hz to eliminate power line interference. Each channel was visually inspected, bad channels were interpolated and segments with significant signal drift were excluded. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was performed to identify and remove components related to eye blinks, eye movements, and muscle artifacts. After the electrodes A1 and A2 (located outside the scalp) were excluded, a whole-brain average reference was applied.

EEG microstate analysis

To enhance computational efficiency for subsequent analyses, the EEG data were downsampled to 500 Hz. Based on previous studies [21], data were further band-pass filtered between 1 and 40 Hz. Microstate segmentation was conducted using the Microstate 0.3 plugin in EEGLAB. Global Field Power (GFP) was calculated, and 1,000 GFP peaks(with high signal-to-noise ratios) were randomly extracted from each participant for microstate clustering. A minimum peak distance of 10 ms was set, and a polarity-ignored k-means clustering approach was employed, with 100 random restarts and set k = 4 to construct a group-level template, as this solution provided high global explained variance (GEV = 0.752) and matched canonical topographies [37]. Based on the group-level template, individual EEG data were re-fitted using a 30 ms window smoothing to compute the following microstate metrics: global explained variance (GEV, the extent to which the group-level template explains the individual data), duration (the mean occurrence time of each microstate), occurrence rate (the frequency of microstate occurrences per second), coverage (the percentage of total recording time occupied by the microstate), and transition probabilities between microstates. To further evaluate the robustness of the microstate segmentation, clustering solutions with k = 5 and k = 6 were also computed as sensitivity analyses, which showed minimal GEV gain and lower stability compared with k = 4 [38].

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0). Group differences in demographic, clinical, cognitive, and EEG microstate variables were examined using one-way ANOVA, independent-sample t-tests, or chi-square tests, as appropriate, with Bonferroni correction applied for post-hoc comparisons. Given that depressive severity, anxiety, and psychomotor agitation may confound the relationship between SI and neural measures [4], additional ANCOVA analyses were performed to compare EEG microstate parameters between the SI and noSI groups, controlling for HAMD-17, HAMA, and agitation scores. Pearson correlations were conducted to examine the relationships between microstate parameters and SI severity (total BSSI scores). Subsequently, partial correlations were performed while controlling for HAMD-17 total scores (excluding agitation items), HAMA scores, and agitation ratings. Within the SI group, additional Pearson correlations were used to explore associations between microstate parameters and cognitive performance. To account for multiple testing across microstate parameters and correlations, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and both uncorrected and FDR-adjusted p-values reported.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant group differences were observed in age (F = 2.047, p = 0.134) or gender distribution (χ² = 4.493, p = 0.106). Educational attainment demonstrated a trend toward group differences (F = 2.788, p = 0.066). In terms of depression severity, the SI group showed significantly higher HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items) scores than the noSI group (t = 3.508, p = 0.001), indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed in agitation scores (t = 1.681, p = 0.758) or HAMA scores (t = -0.310, p = 0.096). In terms of cognitive performance, the HC group demonstrated significantly better overall functioning (p < 0.05) than both the SI and noSI groups.

Intergroup differences in EEG microstates

GEV across k = 4–6 is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1; the values increase only marginally with higher k, supporting the selection of k = 4 for all main analyses. Results of EEG microstate analyses are summarized in Tables 2 and 3; Figs. 1 and 2. Regarding coverage, One-way ANOVA with post hoc comparisons revealed that noSI groups showed significantly higher microstate D coverage than both SI and HC (F = 4.250, p < 0.05, η² = 0.067), while no difference was observed between SI and HC groups. In contrast, no significant group differences were found in microstate occurrence frequency. For transition probabilities, the SI group exhibited a significantly lower probability of transitioning from microstate A to D compared with the noSI group (F = 4.433, p < 0.05, η² = 0.069). Additionally, the noSI group demonstrated a significantly higher probability of transitioning from microstate C to D than the HC (F = 4.096, p < 0.05, η² = 0.064). Further ANCOVA controlling for HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items), HAMA, and agitation scores yielded similar patterns. Specifically, the SI group showed lower microstate D coverage (t = 2.08, p = 0.040, η² = 0.050) and reduced transition from microstate A to D (t = 2.82, p = 0.006, η² = 0.087). These effects remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (adjusted p = 0.040 and 0.012, respectively). Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Correlations between EEG microstate characteristics, cognitive function, and SI severity in the SI

To examine associations of EEG microstate dynamics with cognitive performance and SI within the SI group, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted (Fig. 3). Microstate D coverage was positively correlated with processing speed, as indexed by SDMT T-scores (r = 0.269, p = 0.049), and the transition probability from microstate A to D showed a similar positive correlation (r = 0.278, p = 0.042). SI severity was negatively associated with microstate C coverage (r = -0.285, p = 0.042) and with the transition probability from microstate B to C (r = -0.331, p = 0.018). Partial correlations controlling for HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items), HAMA, and agitation scores showed similar associations (microstate C coverage: r = -0.305, p = 0.035; transition probability from microstate B to C: r = -0.338, p = 0.019). However, none of the correlations survived FDR correction. Detailed results are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate associations among SI, cognitive performance, and large-scale brain network dynamics in patients with MDD using resting-state EEG microstate analysis. The main findings can be summarized as follows: [1] patients with MDD exhibited significant impairments across multiple cognitive domains compared with HC, and the SI group showed higher HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items) scores than the noSI group; [2] the noSI group exhibited higher microstate D coverage compared with both HC and SI groups, and also showed a higher probability of transitioning from microstate C to D relative to HC; [3] the SI group showed a lower transition probability from microstate A to D compared with the noSI group, and this association remained significant after adjusting for HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items), HAMA, and agitation scores with FDR correction; [4] within the SI group, both the coverage of microstate D and the transition probability from microstate A to D were positively correlated with processing speed (SDMT T-scores). Higher SI severity was associated with reduced microstate C coverage and lower transition probability from B to C, although these associations did not survive FDR correction.

In this study, patients with MDD exhibited significantly lower cognitive performance across attention, executive function, and processing speed compared with HCs, consistent with prior evidence of widespread cognitive deficits in depression [39]. The SI group also showed higher HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items) scores than the noSI group, indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Regarding EEG microstate dynamics, we found that the noSI group exhibited greater microstate D coverage compared with both HCs and the SI group, as well as a higher probability of transitioning from microstate C to D relative to HCs. Importantly, the SI group showed a significantly lower transition probability from microstate A to D compared with the noSI group, and this effect remained significant after adjusting for depressive and anxiety symptoms with FDR correction. In addition, exploratory analyses suggested that within the SI group, microstate D coverage and transition probability from microstate A to D were positively associated with processing speed, while higher SI severity was related to reduced microstate C coverage and lower transition probability from microstate B to C; however, these latter associations did not survive correction. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence for altered large-scale brain network dynamics in MDD, particularly in relation to suicide vulnerability and cognitive control. Nevertheless, the exploratory nature of several associations warrants cautious interpretation, and replication in larger samples will be necessary to clarify these effects [40, 41].

Compared to the HC group, individuals in the noSI group exhibited increased coverage of microstate D and more frequent transitions from microstate C to D, pointing to possible enhanced engagement of attention and cognitive control networks [42]. Microstate D has been linked to the frontoparietal control network and dorsal attention network (DAN), which support externally directed attention and goal-oriented processing [43]. Such increases may reflect a potential compensatory mechanism to counteract default mode network (DMN) overactivity—a pattern commonly observed in MDD and associated with rumination and self-referential thought [44–46]. In this context, greater microstate D activity could indicate strengthened cognitive control processes that help suppress intrusive negative thoughts [47], potentially serving as a protective factor that distinguishes noSI individuals from those with suicidal ideation. Microstate C, on the other hand, has been associated with the anterior cingulate cortex and prefrontal regions, and is involved in conflict monitoring and emotional regulation [48]. This ability to shift across functional networks could facilitate adaptive responses to external demands and reduce the cognitive burden associated with depressive symptoms. In contrast, reduced microstate D engagement or less flexible transitions from C to D in the SI group may reflect weaker compensatory control, potentially contributing to vulnerability to suicidal ideation [49]. While these interpretations remain speculative and require validation with multimodal neuroimaging, they provide a plausible neural explanation for why some individuals with depression may not develop suicidal ideation despite significant emotional distress.

In contrast to the noSI group, individuals in the SI group exhibited significantly reduced microstate D coverage and lower transition probability from microstate A to D, indicating impaired dynamic coordination between sensory input and executive control networks. While noSI individuals may benefit from enhanced microstate D engagement and more frequent transitions from C to D as a compensatory mechanism to regulate internal rumination, SI patients appear to lack such protective network dynamics. Previous fMRI studies have reported that SI is associated with decreased functional connectivity between the DMN and DAN, and that this disconnection is positively correlated with suicide risk [50]. This disrupted network integration may hinder the redirection of attention from internal rumination to external stimuli, thereby reinforcing maladaptive cognitive loops. Microstate A has been linked to bilateral superior and middle temporal gyri, part of the auditory network involved in early sensory processing, whereas microstate D reflects the activation of attentional control systems. Reduced transition probability from microstate A to D in the SI group may therefore reflect a breakdown in the pathway through which external inputs trigger appropriate cognitive engagement. This failure to allocate cognitive resources toward executive function in a timely manner could underlie difficulties in emotion regulation and goal-directed behavior [51]. Notably, prior research has associated active SI with DMN dysfunction [52]. Our findings further suggest that insufficient activation and integration of control networks—as indexed by diminished microstate D coverage and abnormal transitions from C to D—may constitute a neural vulnerability for SI. These insights highlight the potential of interventions aimed at enhancing sensory-to-control transitions and attentional flexibility to reduce suicide risk.

Further analysis revealed that, within the SI group, both the coverage of microstate D and the transition probability from microstate A to D were positively correlated with SDMT T-scores, suggesting a potential link between microstate D dynamics and cognitive performance, particularly in processing speed and executive control [22]. Individuals with higher coverage and more efficient transitions of microstate D may potentially shift more effectively from sensory processing to cognitive control states, supporting adaptive task performance and emotional regulation. These findings highlight the potential role of microstate D dynamics in supporting cognitive function in individuals with SI [39]. Separately, higher SI severity was negatively associated with microstate C coverage and the transition probability from B to C. Although these associations were assessed using partial correlations controlling for HAMD-17 (excluding agitation items), HAMA, and agitation scores, they did not survive FDR correction, indicating that these trends should be interpreted cautiously [21]. Microstate B is linked to occipital regions involved in visual processing and autobiographical memory [53, 54], and its dysfunction may potentially influence affect regulation via external cues or personal memories [55]. Similarly, disruptions in microstate C may reflect preliminary alterations in DMN functional integration, leading to subtle difficulties in attentional allocation and modulation of internally focused negative content [56]. Taken together, these findings are broadly consistent with prior reports linking SI to large-scale network dysfunction, including the DMN, attentional control networks, and visual/autobiographical memory systems [41, 57], suggesting that suicidal ideation in MDD may involve specific disruptions in dynamic brain network integration that warrant further investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest and the cross-sectional design limits statistical power and causal inference, highlighting the need for larger, longitudinal studies. Second, the exclusive use of resting-state EEG restricts the capture of dynamic cognitive processes. Third, SI is heterogeneous, encompassing both passive and active forms, and potential confounders such as hopelessness or psychosocial factors were not controlled, which may influence EEG patterns and suicide risk. Fourth, we did not separate periodic and aperiodic EEG components, which may underlie microstate alterations; future work should incorporate both to clarify whether observed changes reflect oscillatory or non-oscillatory dynamics. Finally, although average referencing was applied, different schemes may affect microstate topography. Future studies could compare average reference with alternatives such as the reference electrode standardization techniqueto assess robustness.

Conclusion

Patients with MDD exhibit EEG microstate alterations associated with cognitive performance and differences between SI and noSI groups. The noSI group showed patterns suggestive of potential compensatory network mechanisms, whereas the SI group exhibited signs of network vulnerability. These findings indicate that EEG microstate features represent exploratory biomarkers requiring replication and longitudinal validation. Future studies with larger and longitudinal samples are needed to confirm these associations and assess their predictive value.
