Association of cognitive impairment with adverse cardiovascular outcomes: the mediating role of modifiable risk factors in a prospective cohort study
Authors: Le Zhou, Zejun Yang, Lan Ren, Gaifeng Hu, Jue Wang, Sitong Li, Xiaodong Peng, Manlin Zhao, Qifan Li, Zixu Zhao, Mingxiao Li, Meiqi Zhao, Ting Shen, Zhixian Wang, Enze Li, Yize Zhao, Ning Zhou, Caihua Sang, Chang-Sheng Ma, Jianzeng Dong, Yiwei Lai, Liu He, Jingrui Zhang, Wen-Yu Wang, Xin Du, Ribo Tang, Deyong Long
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate, in a prospective cohort study, the association between cognitive impairment and cardiovascular disease (CVD), to quantify the extent to which uncontrolled risk factors mediate this association, and to explore whether the mediation effect varies across sex and age groups.DesignProspective cohort study.SettingUK Biobank, a large population-based cohort study in the UK.ParticipantsA total of 152 155 participants without prevalent CVD or dementia at baseline were included. The mean age was 56.3±8.2 years, and 44.0% were male.Primary outcomesCardiovascular death and composite cardiovascular outcomes, assessed using Cox proportional-hazards models and mediation analyses.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.03–13.87 years, 1474 cardiovascular deaths and 21 518 composite cardiovascular outcomes were recorded. Participants with cognitive impairment (n=23 146; 15.2%) exhibited higher proportions of lifestyle, metabolic and psychological risks (p<0.001), except alcohol consumption. Cognitive impairment was associated with a 21% increased risk of cardiovascular death (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.37) and a 5% higher risk of composite cardiovascular outcomes (HR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.09). Mediation analyses indicated that 11.30%, 9.10% and 8.25% of the association between cognitive impairment and cardiovascular death were mediated by uncontrolled glucose, alcohol consumption and depression, respectively. Similarly, 17.50%, 12.39% and 11.98% of the association with composite cardiovascular outcomes were mediated by depression, anxiety and alcohol consumption, respectively. These mediating effects varied by sex and were more pronounced in females.ConclusionsCognitive impairment is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular death and composite cardiovascular outcomes. Uncontrolled lifestyle, cardiometabolic and psychological risk factors partially mediate this association, highlighting the importance of comprehensive management to improve cardiovascular prognosis in this population.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are responsible for approximately 19.8 million deaths globally, resulting in a loss of 396 million years of life and 44.9 million years lived with disability.1 Given this, reducing the burden of CVD remains a critical global public health priority. Cognitive impairment and CVD are increasingly prevalent among the ageing population, often co-occurring and sharing common risk factors such as hypertension, obesity and dyslipidaemia.2 Cognitive impairment encompasses a broad range of deficits in cognitive functions, including memory, language, logic, attention, problem-solving, visuospatial skills and executive function.3 These cognitive deficits may hinder the ability to effectively manage key cardiovascular risk factors, such as maintaining healthy lipid and glucose levels.4 5 Poor management of these risk factors, in turn, is likely to increase the risk of CVD.68 While previous studies have shown that cognitive impairment can accelerate the onset of CVD,9 10 it remains uncertain to what extent uncontrolled cardiovascular risk factors mediate this relationship.

In this study, we investigate the association between cognitive impairment and the incidence of CVD in adult participants from the UK Biobank and explore to the extent to which uncontrolled risk factors may mediate this relationship. Additionally, we examine how these mediating effects vary by sex and age, aiming to provide insights for the development of personalised interventions.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank represents a comprehensive, prospective cohort study designed to provide an extensive repository of genetic and health-related information. Detailed documentation regarding the UK Biobank’s design and its participant demographics is available in previous literature.11 To summarise, over 500 000 participants aged 37–73 were enrolled between 2006 and 2010 at 23 assessment centres across England, Scotland and Wales, and were subsequently followed for cardiovascular outcomes through 30 November 2022 for mortality data and 31 October 2023 for morbidity data. These participants contributed a broad spectrum of health data via touchscreen questionnaires, physical examinations and biological samples. For our analysis, participants with complete cognitive assessments and no prior history of atherosclerotic CVD, atrial fibrillation, heart failure or dementia were included. Finally, a total of 152 155 participants were selected for this study. A detailed description of the selection process for the analytic sample is provided in online supplemental figure 1.

Cognitive function assessment

To evaluate cognitive impairment and its potential associations with health outcomes, we used a comprehensive battery of UK Biobank cognitive tests, assessing four domains via standardised touchscreen tasks: processing speed, reasoning, memory and prospective memory. Processing speed was measured with a ‘Snap’—style game requiring participants to press a button with their dominant hand when matching symbols appeared; the mean response time (ms) across four trials was analysed, with longer times indicating poorer performance. Reasoning was assessed with a 13-item fluid intelligence test, scored as the number of correct answers (0–13), with higher scores reflecting better ability. Memory was evaluated with a card-matching task; the six-pair trial (less prone to ceiling effects than the three-pair trial) was used, with higher error counts denoting worse performance. Prospective memory was tested by asking participants to recall and execute an instruction (touching an orange circle instead of a blue square at the end of the session); impairment was defined as failure on the first attempt.

Participants included in this study had complete assessments in all four cognitive domains. Cognitive impairment was defined as scoring below the fifth percentile (or the nearest feasible proportion) in any one of the four domains, consistent with previous studies.12

Study outcomes

The study outcomes are CVD death and a composite of CVD outcomes. Incidence of study outcomes was obtained by the following sources, including self-reports, inpatient and primary care records. Information on death and the date of death was obtained by reviewing death certificates from the National Health Service Information Centre (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register (Scotland). Data on disease diagnosis and diagnosis date were sourced from hospital inpatient records, including the Hospital Episode Statistics (England), Scottish Morbidity Record data (Scotland) and the Patient Episode Database (Wales). Cardiovascular deaths were defined as those with CVD listed as the primary cause. Composite CVD outcomes comprised fatal or non-fatal coronary heart disease, ischaemic stroke, heart failure and atrial fibrillation. This variable was coded as binary, with participants classified as having a composite CVD outcome if any of these events occurred during follow-up. The International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes were used to accurately categorise these CVD outcomes (as detailed in the online supplemental table 2). CVD outcomes were identified using ICD-10 codes harmonised from both ICD-9 and ICD-10 records via the official WHO cross-walk as implemented in the UK Biobank data-field mapping. Because ICD-10 is more detailed than ICD-9, non-one-to-one matches were grouped at the broader disease-category level to ensure consistent classification. Participants who were diagnosed with CVD during follow-up were considered to have had incident events. The date of diagnosis was defined as the earliest diagnosis date for any CVD outcome.

Statistical analysis

The mean±SD or median (IQR) was employed to describe continuous variables as appropriate, while frequency and proportions were utilised for categorical variables. For the comparison of baseline characteristics, we exclusively used available, non-imputed data to maintain the integrity and accuracy of these comparisons. Detailed information on baseline definitions is provided in the online supplemental tables 1 and 3. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, depending on the distribution of the data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and histogram inspection; variables with approximately normal distributions were compared using the Student’s t-test, while skewed variables were analysed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. Incidences were calculated as the number of CVD deaths or composite CVD outcomes divided by the total person-years of follow-up, with person-years defined as the time from baseline assessment to the date of event occurrence, death or end of follow-up, whichever came first. Because UK Biobank follow-up is conducted through national registries, left truncation was not an issue. Poisson models were used to estimate their 95% CIs, and the Poisson assumption (equal mean and variance) was assessed by comparing observed means and variances of event counts. No substantial overdispersion was observed; results were also consistent when checked with negative binomial models, supporting robustness. Cox proportional hazard models with follow-up time (calendar time) as the time scale were applied to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the association between cognitive impairment and the occurrence of CVD. In addition, variance inflation factors were calculated for all covariates, and all values were <5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. In the analysis of incident CVD, we excluded participants who reported prevalent CVD and dementia at baseline. Data were censored at the date of death or diagnosis (for incident CVD analysis), or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. Follow-up in the UK Biobank is conducted through linkage with national health and death registries, which ensures near-complete follow-up and minimises loss to follow-up. Accordingly, participants were censored only at the date of outcome occurrence or at the end of follow-up (30 November 2022 for mortality and 31 October 2023 for morbidity), and no additional censoring for loss to follow-up was required. To minimise the influence of reverse causation, events within 1 month after enrolment were excluded. Factors known to be associated with cognitive impairment and CVD were included in the analyses as confounders. These variables encompassed self-reported age, sex (female vs male), race/ethnicity (recoded as white and other), education (recoded as college or university degree and other education), body mass index (BMI), family history of CVD, family history of dementia, alcohol intake, smoking status, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, depression and nervous. Missing values for the adjusted covariates parameters were imputed with mean values for continuous variables (details were shown in online supplemental table 4). Three models were set up sequentially to demonstrate the robustness of the association. Model 1 included variables such as age, sex, education level and race. Model 2 expanded to include family history of CVD and dementia. Model 3 incorporated additional factors like nervousness, depression, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analysis using model 3, stratified in five distinct ways: by gender (male or female), age (<65 years or ≥65 years), family history of CVD (yes or no), family history of dementia (yes or no), and education level (college or university degree vs other levels of education). Meanwhile, propensity score-based overlap weighting was performed as a sensitivity analysis to compare participants with and without cognitive impairment. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression including age, sex, education, race, family history of dementia, family history of CVD, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, nervousness and depression as covariates (online supplemental figure 2). Overlap weighting was implemented with the R package ‘WeightIt’,13 and covariate balance was assessed using standardised mean differences, which confirmed adequate balance after weighting. The weighted data were analysed using the cmprsk package14 to account for competing risk of non-CV death. Specifically, we estimated subdistribution HRs rather than cause-specific HRs, as this approach incorporates the influence of competing events and allows direct interpretation of the relative incidence of CVD outcomes in the presence of competing risks.

Mediation analysis comprehensively evaluated the potential mediation effect of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors in associations of cognitive impairment with cardiovascular risk. Modifiable cardiovascular risk factors in this study were classified into three distinct aspects: lifestyle risk factors, cardiometabolic risk factors and psychological risk factors. Lifestyle risks included cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption and lower Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension scores. Metabolic risks encompassed uncontrolled blood pressure, uncontrolled glucose levels, uncontrolled lipid levels and obesity. Psychological risks involved symptoms of nervousness and depression. Further details are provided in the online supplemental tables 5 and 6. Mediation analyses were performed with the R package ‘mediation’15 adjusting for potential confounders including age, education level, sex, race, family history of dementia and family history of CVD. Standard errors and percentile bootstrap CIs for effects estimates were computed based on 100 bootstrap resamples. Additionally, we conducted mediation analyses across different age and gender subgroups.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R, V.4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All statistical tests were two-sided, and we considered p<0.05 to be statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

We have not involved patients or members of the general public in the design or conduct of this study.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Baseline characteristics categorised by cognitive impairment status are presented in table 1. Among the 152 155 participants included in the analysis, 85 205 (55.9%) were women, with a mean (SD) age of 56.3 (8.2) years. Of these, 23 146 (15.2%) participants were identified as having cognitive impairment, while 129 009 (84.8%) were cognitively normal. compared with cognitively normal participants, those with cognitive impairment were older, more likely to be non-white, had lower levels of education, a family history of dementia, higher levels of low-density lipoprotein and total cholesterol, and higher BMI, Townsend Deprivation Index, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, glycosylated haemoglobin and usage of lipid and antihypertensive medications. Additionally, individuals with cognitive impairment had a higher prevalence of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and diabetes.

Associations between cognitive impairment and CVD

During a median follow-up of 13.03 years (Q1–Q3: 12.73–13.34), the UK Biobank recorded 1,474 CVD deaths, representing an incidence rate of 0.97%. The incidence of CVD death was notably higher in the cognitive impairment group (1.14 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.27), compared with the cognitively normal group (0.69 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI 0.65 to 0.73). Participants with cognitive impairment demonstrated 1.21 times higher risk of CVD death compared with those without cognitive impairment during the follow-up period (table 3 and online supplemental table 8).

Over a median follow-up of 13.87 years (Q1–Q3: 13.58–14.24), the UK Biobank recorded 21 518 composite CVD outcomes (14.10%), including 11 409 cases of coronary heart disease (7.50%), 2210 cases of ischaemic stroke (1.45%), 3698 cases of heart failure (2.43%) and 8867 cases of atrial fibrillation (5.83%). The incidence of these composite CVD events was notably higher in the cognitive impairment group (13.90 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI 13.48 to 14.34) compared with those without cognitive impairment (10.47 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI 10.31 to 10.62). After adjusting for potential confounders, cognitive impairment was associated with an increased risk of composite CVD events (adjusted HR, 1.05 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.09); p=0.007), coronary heart disease (adjusted HR, 1.07 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.13); p=0.004), ischaemic stroke (adjusted HR, 1.13 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.26); p=0.020) and heart failure (adjusted HR, 1.10 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.19); p=0.024) (table 3). However, no significant associations were observed between CI and atrial fibrillation in any adjusted models (p>0.05 in all models) (table 3). Sensitivity analyses using propensity score-based overlap weighting (model 4) confirmed the stability and consistency of these associations and effect sizes. Furthermore, the relationship between different cognitive domains and CVD is presented in online supplemental table 7.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated low populational heterogeneity in the association between cognitive impairment and CVD death, composite CVD events and their components. However, potential interactions were observed between age and cognitive impairment in relation to CVD death (P for interaction=0.042) and heart failure (P for interaction<0.001), as well as between sex and cognitive impairment in relation to heart failure (P for interaction=0.033) and atrial fibrillation (P for interaction=0.006). These findings are illustrated in figures1 2 and online supplemental figures 3–6.

For the association between cognitive impairment and CVD death, the proportions mediated by uncontrolled glucose, alcohol consumption and depression were 11.30%, 9.10% and 8.25%, respectively. In the case of composite CVD events, the mediation proportions were 17.50% for depression, 12.39% for anxiety and 11.98% for alcohol consumption. Notably, uncontrolled blood pressure did not mediate the association between cognitive impairment and either CVD death or composite CVD events.

Discussion

Main findings

This prospective cohort study revealed that participants with cognitive impairment exhibited poorer management of risk factors, including lifestyle, metabolic and psychological risks, compared with those without cognitive impairment. Cognitive impairment was significantly associated with an elevated risk of CVD, and our findings further indicate that this increased risk is partially attributable to inadequate management of these risk factors. Mediation analyses showed that 11.30%, 9.10% and 8.25% of the association between cognitive impairment and CVD death were mediated by uncontrolled glucose, alcohol consumption and depression, respectively. Similarly, 17.50%, 12.39% and 11.98% of the association between cognitive impairment and composite CVD outcomes were mediated by depression, anxiety and alcohol consumption, respectively.

Comparison with previous literature

The relationship between cognitive impairment and adverse CVD outcomes has garnered increasing attention in recent years.101618 Retrospective and longitudinal observational studies have consistently highlighted an association between cognitive function and an elevated risk of CVD and mortality,91820 which is consistent with our study. O’Donnell et al21 have demonstrated that cognitive impairment impacts the incidence of CVD in a large-scale study. This study included 30 959 patients (accounting for 98.2% of the ONTARGET and TRANSCEND populations) with a history of cardiovascular events or diabetes mellitus. Cognitive impairment, assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), was linked to an increased risk of future cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, stroke, myocardial infarction and hospitalisation for heart failure. Notably, a lower MMSE score was associated with a 35% higher risk of cardiovascular events. Participants with MMSE scores that dropped by two or more points over 2 years experienced a higher incidence of cardiovascular events compared with those without such a decline (12.1% vs 8.8%). Importantly, the study population of O’Donnell et al consisted of individuals with established vascular disease or diabetes (a high-risk group), whereas our analysis was based on the UK Biobank, which is a community-based cohort initially free of CVD. This distinction may partially explain why the magnitude of risk increase observed in our study (HR range 1.1–1.13) was smaller compared with the more pronounced risk increase observed by O’Donnell et al. In addition, our study did not find a significant association between cognitive impairment and AF. This lack of association may be due to differences in underlying mechanisms, the relatively younger baseline age of the UK Biobank population, or possible underdetection of asymptomatic AF in this cohort. Similarly, another study found that a faster decline in cognitive function was associated with increased mortality, independent of baseline cognitive status.22 However, the pathological pathways linking cognitive decline to cardiovascular events remain unclear, and effective measures to improve prognosis require further investigation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that the relationship between cognitive impairment and CVD may be partially mediated through unhealthy lifestyles, impaired cardiometabolic status and psychological distress. This mediation model provides an explanatory framework for the observed association and offers insights into the potential mechanisms connecting cognitive impairment to CVD. Our findings suggest that individuals with cognitive impairment are more likely to adopt unhealthy lifestyle behaviours and experience poor cardiometabolic and psychological status, possibly due to factors such as social isolation, poor treatment adherence and inadequate care. Over time, these factors may contribute to an increased risk of adverse CVD outcomes.

Explanations of findings

Cognitive impairment and CVD often co-occur, creating a self-reinforcing vicious cycle. On one hand, these conditions share several common risk factors. It is estimated that nearly one-quarter of dementia cases can be attributed to cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes, smoking, obesity, hypertension and physical inactivity.23 24 Furthermore, CVD contributes to cognitive decline through various mechanisms, including reduced cerebral perfusion, thromboembolism, low-grade inflammation, neurohumoral dysregulation and ischaemic brain injury.25 26 On the other hand, cognitive impairment can negatively impact key aspects of non-clinical management,27 further exacerbating cardiovascular health outcomes and perpetuating the cycle between the two conditions. Salzwedel et al28 noted that cognitive impairment complicates secondary prevention, lifestyle modifications and self-management in patients with coronary heart disease. Medication adherence, which is essential for preventing CVD recurrence, is often compromised in elderly individuals with cognitive impairment.29 Additionally, declines in language, memory and social cognitive functions frequently contribute to social isolation, anxiety and psychological loneliness in this population.30 In the present study, impairments across various cognitive domains were found to differentially impact patients’ ability to manage risk factors (online supplemental tables 7–10). It is worth noting that our definition of cognitive impairment—scoring below the fifth percentile in any one of the four domains—classified approximately 15% of participants as impaired. This approach may have introduced heterogeneity, as some individuals might have isolated deficits in a single domain while others exhibited more global impairments. We chose this cut-off in order to broadly capture mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and to remain consistent with prior studies, but we acknowledge that this definition may group together individuals with differing clinical profiles. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting our findings and represents a limitation of the present study.

Among the mediators identified in our study, psychological factors—particularly depression and anxiety—were found to be especially detrimental. These factors, along with lifestyle and biochemical risk factors, showed a stronger association with both cognitive impairment and CVD. A bidirectional relationship likely exists between cognitive function and psychological well-being, highlighting a complex interplay between the two.31 On the one hand, several longitudinal studies have consistently demonstrated that lower levels of psychological well-being are associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in older adults, possibly due to the detrimental effects of chronic stress, depression or reduced engagement in cognitively stimulating activities.3234 On the other hand, rapid cognitive decline and the onset of dementia have been shown to significantly impair psychological well-being.35 This is likely because individuals with dementia often face challenges in adapting to changes in their abilities, setting new goals and maintaining meaningful relationships, which exacerbate feelings of isolation, frustration and diminished life satisfaction.36 Guo et al revealed that the trajectory of psychological well-being begins to decline even before a formal diagnosis of MCI, suggesting that early cognitive changes already impact emotional health. This reduction in psychological well-being prior to the onset of MCI may further accelerate cognitive decline, creating a reinforcing cycle between cognitive function and psychological well-being.37 Moreover, the psychological changes secondary to cognitive impairment, such as the misuse of alcohol as a coping mechanism, can also contribute to the development of CVD.38 For instance, a prospective cohort study in England involving 1681 young adults found a positive association between negative cognitive styles and subsequent alcohol consumption as a coping strategy.39 Notably, uncontrolled blood pressure did not significantly mediate these associations, and the mediating effects displayed variations by sex. This finding may be explained by the relatively similar blood pressure management between individuals with and without cognitive impairment, likely due to the widespread availability of antihypertensive medications and routine clinical monitoring in primary care. As a result, differences in blood pressure control between groups may have been attenuated, reducing its mediating effect compared with other lifestyle, metabolic and psychological factors.

Future implications

Cognitive impairment is associated with an elevated risk of CVD-related mortality and a higher incidence of CVD events, mediated through various contributing factors. This highlights the critical need for early, individualised management strategies for patients with declining cognitive function and comorbid conditions to prevent adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, as current guidelines suggest, pharmacological treatments for dementia are generally not recommended for patients with cognitive impairment patients due to their limited efficacy in preventing disease progression.40 Instead, non-pharmacological interventions including physical exercise, vascular risk factor management, cognitive training and stimulation have been extensively studied and shown potential in delaying cognitive deterioration.4143 Du et al evaluated social participation levels in 3201 individuals across 20 domains, including volunteer work, physical activities and social events. Their findings revealed that lower levels of early education were associated with cognitive decline, with social participation acting as a mediating factor in this relationship.44 Similarly, Mao et al demonstrated that nurse-led interventions, such as home telephone support or home visits, effectively improved postoperative cognitive function in patients undergoing carotid artery revascularisation surgery.45 Moreover, personalised lifestyle interventions—encompassing modifications in physical activity, diet, stress management, nutraceutical support and medications—have shown promise in improving cognitive impairment.46 These findings underscore the need for aggressive vascular risk factor management and comprehensive care for patients with cognitive impairment, comparable to other secondary prevention populations. However, whether these interventions also confer benefits for cardiovascular outcomes remains unclear. This study, through mediation analysis, sheds light on the potential pathways linking cognitive impairment to cardiovascular outcomes, emphasising the importance of comprehensive management strategies. Future research is needed to explore whether active interventions—such as addressing lifestyle, cardiometabolic and psychosocial risk factors, alongside enhancing social support and family care—could effectively reduce the incidence of CVD in this population. Additionally, optimising patients’ overall medical and functional status, coupled with counselling on key issues such as vascular risk factor control, fulfilling social needs, promoting healthy lifestyles and ensuring periodic follow-ups, can maximise patient and caregiver well-being and reduce risk for negative outcomes.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, most patients were not assessed dynamically for the mediators, as both cognitive function and mediators were collected only at baseline. This limitation restricts our ability to establish temporality between the exposure and mediators, thereby affecting the validity of the mediation analysis. Second, the generalisability of our findings is limited, as a large proportion of the study population consisted of White British individuals. Differences in lifestyle and dietary habits across populations may influence the relative importance and ranking of mediators in other cultural and ethnic groups. Finally, our mediation analysis rests on the assumption of no unmeasured confounding. Although we adjusted for a wide range of demographic, lifestyle, metabolic and psychological factors, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies incorporating sensitivity analyses, such as mediation E-values, will be valuable to further assess the robustness of our findings.

Conclusions

Cognitive impairment is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular death and a higher incidence of CVDs. Our study further reveals that this relationship is partly mediated by unfavourable lifestyle, cardiometabolic and psychological factors, with notable sex differences in the contribution of these mediators. These findings highlight the practical importance of developing targeted interventions—such as improving lifestyle behaviours, optimising metabolic control and strengthening mental health and social support—to reduce cardiovascular events and improve outcomes in cognitively impaired individuals.
