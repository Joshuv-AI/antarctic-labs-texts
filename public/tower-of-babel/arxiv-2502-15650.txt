List Decoding Quotient Reed-Muller Codes
Omri Gotlib, Tali Kaufman, Shachar Lovett

Abstract
Reed-Muller codes consist of evaluations of n n -variate polynomials over a finite field 𝔽 \mathbbm{F} with degree at most d d . Much like every linear code, Reed-Muller codes can be characterized by constraints, where a codeword is valid if and only if it satisfies all degree- d d constraints. For a subset X ~ ⊆ 𝔽 n {\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n} , we introduce the notion of X ~ {\tilde{X}} -quotient Reed-Muller code. A function F : X ~ → 𝔽 F:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F} is a valid codeword in the quotient code if it satisfies all the constraints of degree- d d polynomials lying in X ~ {\tilde{X}} . This gives rise to a novel phenomenon: a quotient codeword may have many extensions to original codewords. This weakens the connection between original codewords and quotient codewords which introduces a richer range of behaviors along with substantial new challenges. Our goal is to answer the following question: what properties of X ~ {\tilde{X}} will imply that the quotient code inherits its distance and list-decoding radius from the original code? We address this question using techniques developed by Bhowmick and Lovett [ BL14 ] , identifying key properties of 𝔽 n \mathbbm{F}^{n} used in their proof and extending them to general subsets X ~ ⊆ 𝔽 n {\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n} . By introducing a new tool, we overcome the novel challenge in analyzing the quotient code that arises from the weak connection between original and quotient codewords. This enables us to apply known results from additive combinatorics and algebraic geometry [ KZ18 , KZ19 , LZ21 ] to show that when X ~ {\tilde{X}} is a high rank variety , X ~ {\tilde{X}} -quotient Reed-Muller codes inherit the distance and list-decoding parameters from the original Reed-Muller codes.

Our Question

Our goal is to answer the following question:
what properties of
{\tilde{X}}
will imply that the quotient code inherits its distance and list-decoding radius from the original code?

This question is analogous to the study of quotients of expander graphs—just as not all quotients of an expander necessarily preserve expansion,
not all subsets
{\tilde{X}}
necessarily yield a well-behaved quotient code.
Understanding the conditions under which expansion is preserved has been a fundamental problem in the study of expanders,
and similarly, identifying the conditions under which a quotient code retains key properties of the original code is a central challenge in our work.
Given this parallel, we believe our question may have broader implications for future research in both coding theory and theoretical computer science.

We answer this question in the context of

Notably, our approach does

address the case of where there are multiple lifts,
but also introduces a novel framework for analyzing unique-lift (puncturing) setting when the field size is constant-a scenario that is typically considered more challenging.

Main Technical Challenge

We achieve these results by combining the two black-box properties of subsets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
we presented.
Analysis of the polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
raises a new challenge, as previous techniques that were used to analyze low-degree polynomials,
both regarding
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

[
GT07a
]
and regarding subsets
{\tilde{X}}

[
LZ21
]
,
were focused on maintaining the behavior of polynomials
in the set they work on
(
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
and
{\tilde{X}}
accordingly).

The novelty of our new technique is that it uses a similar approach to analyze polynomials
{\tilde{X}}
as commonly used in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,

while simultaneously maintaining a connection
between polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
to polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
This connection allows us to deduce that polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
behave similarly to polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Informally, given a question regarding a polynomial in
{\tilde{X}}
, our new technique allows us to
associate it with a “correct” lift of it, and answer the question
using properties of its lift
.
We emphasize that the correct lift (the one we later choose to use)
depend
on the question,
thus we cannot pick a single canonical lift to
generally
describe each polynomial in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Next we describe this challenge in more detail.

Analyses of polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
were commonly based on the structure-randomness connection of polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
To use this connection, a procedure introduced by
[
GT07a
]
, which is called the
regularization process
, is often used
[
KL08
,
TZ11
,
HL11
,
BFH+13
,
BHT13
,
BL15
]
.
This procedure takes any collection of polynomials, and constructs from it another collection of polynomials that has
equidistriubtion in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

and “captures” all functions “captured” by the previous collection.
This notion of “capturing” is formulated by a definition called
measurable
,
and thus it is required that every function measurable by the old collection will be measurable by the new collection.

We note that the regularization procedure achieves random behavior in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
by requiring the collection to have an
extremely low algebraic structure
:
This implies the new collection has random behavior (equidistributed) as it is a property of
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
The notion of structure is captured by a definition called
rank
, where a polynomial with high rank has extremely low structure.
Additionally, the notion of lack of random behavior is captured by a definition called
bias
, where a polynomial with low bias behaves randomly (equidistributed).
Therefore, the equidistribution is achieved in the regularization process by constructing a collection with high rank, as
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
high rank implies low bias
.

To generalize these ideas to
{\tilde{X}}
, one must achieve a similar result in
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
:
Given any collection of polynomials, construct a new collection of polynomials that is both equidistributed in
{\tilde{X}}

and captures every function in
{\tilde{X}}
that was previously captured.
In our case, however, we must also ensure that the new collection also captures all functions that were previously-captured
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
as in our case we wish to use the connection of polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
to polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
This can be summarized by 3 requirements:

1.

The polynomials in the new collection will behave random in
{\tilde{X}}
.

2.

Every function that was measurable in
{\tilde{X}}
by the old collection will be measurable by the new collection in
{\tilde{X}}
.

3.

Every function that was measurable
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
by the old collection will be measurable by the new collection
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Alas, this third-requirement is incompatible with the way we achieve the first requirement.
Achieving the first requirement, which is the random behavior in
{\tilde{X}}
, is done by requiring an extremely low algebraic structure
according to relative rank
.
This requires one to consider all possible lifts of polynomials in the collection to avoid any structure.

More accurately

11

11

\mathbbm{F}^{n}

-equivalent polynomial for

.

Typically (in

For

Introducing New Tools

We overcome this challenge by presenting a new definition that relaxes the notion of
measurable
we required for functions in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
which we call
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable
.
This enables us to describe a relaxed version of the regularization process,
in which we require that every function in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that was
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable by the old collection will still be
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable by the new collection.
In contrast to the original regularization process, which mandated that functions that were measurable by the old collection will be measurable by the collection,
this relaxed definition only requires such functions to be
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable
by the new collection.

Even though we no longer need to capture all previously captured functions in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
it is important that the new relaxed-definition is strict enough to keep the connection between polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
and in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Therefore, maintaining the
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable functions throughout the regularization process cannot be done trivially,
and this is handled in a procedure we call
the
{\tilde{X}}
-relative regularization process
which is a stronger-version of the regularization process that is used in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
This new definition and procedure are thoroughly described in Section
5
.

We note that these new definition and procedure are a novel contribution of this work, and we believe they can
be useful in future research of the quotient Reed-Muller code.

1.2.1
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable and The
{\tilde{X}}
-Relative Regularization Process

In this subsection we discuss the generalization of the regularization process to subsets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
using the equivalent of rank-bias relation in
{\tilde{X}}
.
We name this tool
the relative regularization process
.

Practically, we use this tool to show that given a specific question in mind, every
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
has some polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
that behave “similarly” in respect to this question.
This allows us to pull properties of
P
to better understand
p
.
The perfect candidate for such
P
is a
lift
of
p
.

In order to use
P
to deduce properties of
p
, we use the well-studied properties of polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
to acheive properties of
P
, and relate these to properties of
p
.
More specifically, assume that
p
and
P
are measurable in respect of a collection of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
(each in its domain).
Our strategy is to use
P
to deduce properties of
\Gamma_{P}
, and then use the properties of
\Gamma_{P}
to deduce properties of
p
.

Now let us describe the extra challenge.
We start by following the ideas of the regularization process we described for
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Assuming the collection is not a collection of
{\tilde{X}}
-relative high rank, then there must exist a polynomial in the collection that has low
relative
rank, which we denote by
P^{\star}

21
21

21

More precisely, some linear combination of polynomials has low relative rank.

.
Note that in relative rank, this does not necessarily mean that
P^{\star}
is of low rank, but that there exists another
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent polynomial that has a low rank.
Thus, even if we remove the low-rank
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent polynomial and add to the collection all the polynomials that decomposed it,
we cannot require that every function that was measurable by the old collection will still be measurable by the new collection:
even the polynomial we removed is not necessarily measurable by the new collection!

To allow such regularization process to still apply, we note that while
P
might not be measurable in respect of the new collection, a
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent polynomial of
P

is
measurable with respect of it.
Therefore, we relax the notion of being measurable to being
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable
.

We say a function
F
is
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable in respect of
\mathcal{P}
if it can be determined by the polynomials of
\mathcal{P}

up to a valid
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder
.
We first describe an incomplete definition, then present the challenge that rises with it, and finally present its resolution.

Definition 1.7

(
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable, Incomplete Definition)
.
22
22

22

This incomplete definition lacks the requirement of the
validity
of the
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder

We say a function

is
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable
if there exists a function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

and a
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder, i.e a function
\overline{F}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
with
\overline{F}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0

such that:

\forall a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}:F(a)=\Gamma(P_{1}(a),...,P_{c}(a))+\overline{F}(a)

Previous works analyzing polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
were able to deduce two things from
F
being measurable by
\mathcal{P}
:
that the structure of
\Gamma
is similar to the structure of
F
, and that a random input of
\Gamma

.

To study polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
, we wish to connect

to
P
(which is a lift of

).
Thus, we think of

, and require two similar things.
Firstly, we want the structure of
\Gamma

(in this case,

), which we understand as

\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Secondly, we want a random input of

The latter is easily achieved using the fact high
{\tilde{X}}
-relative rank implies equidistribution in
{\tilde{X}}
.
The former, however, might be damaged by the remainder as we defined it: we can only learn the structure of
\Gamma

.
However, the structure of

can be very different from the structure of
F
,
as we did not require any structure of the
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder
\overline{F}
.
Thus, we can not deduce the structure of
\Gamma

To handle this issue, we add one more requirement regarding the
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder,

\deg(F-\overline{F})\leq\deg(F)

-remainder for

.
This can be summarized by the following (complete) definition:

Definition 1.8

(
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable)
.

We say a function
F
is
{\tilde{X}}
-measurable
if there exists a function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

and a
valid

{\tilde{X}}
-remainder, i.e a function
\overline{F}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

with
\overline{F}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0
and
\deg(F-\overline{F})\leq\deg(F)

such that:

\forall a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}:F(a)=\Gamma(P_{1}(a),...,P_{c}(a))+\overline{F}(a)

We use this new definition the following way:
Instead of using
F
to understand
\Gamma
, we use
F-\overline{F}
to do so.
We choose
F-\overline{F}
as it has the same structure as
F
, but it is “closer” to the function
\Gamma
as
F-\overline{F}=\Gamma(P_{1},...,P_{c})

23
23

23

One can think of this step as ”taking the right
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent” in respect of
\mathcal{P}
.

.
Finally, as
\Gamma

p

\Gamma
to deduce properties regarding

.

With this in hand, let us finish describing the relative-regularization process.
The requirement on the validity of the
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder raises a new challenge in the
{\tilde{X}}
-relative regularization process:
we need to somehow control the structure of the
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder, even though this “error”

We address this challenge using a Lemma proved in

Therefore, we add to each step of the relative-regularization process a (regular) regularization, which ensures

This strong structure of

Theorem 1.9

(Relative Regularization Process, Informal, See Theorem

)
.

\leq d
,
such that:

1.

Every function that is
{\tilde{X}}

{\tilde{X}}
-measurable in respect to the new collection.

The new collection is of
{\tilde{X}}

in
{\tilde{X}}

More specifically, we wish to show that there is a constant number of words that are

Let

Thereby, every polynomial-specific measurable function can be thought of as a

Next, we lift each polynomial from
\mathfrak{h}
and apply the

.
This yields a new collection of polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that is constant sized and randomly-behaving (in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

Denote this new collection by
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

24

24

We use the same notations as the original proof for clearannce.

.
Thereby, the question of list decoding is reduced to the following question:
We have a specific constant-sized randomly-behaving collection of polynomials

.
We need to bound the amount of polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
that are

-close to be measurable by this collection in
{\tilde{X}}
.
Note that the randomly-behaving property was achieved using the

if
it is of the following form:

P(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\sum_{0\leq d_{1},...,d_{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}{d_{i}}\leq d}{c_{d_{1},...,d_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{d_{i}}}

We denote the set of all polynomials of degree
\leq d
by
Poly_{\leq d}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})
.
The value
d
in the definition above is called the
global degree
of the function
P
, shorthand by the
degree
of
P
,
and it is denoted by
\deg(P)=d
.

Additionally, the set of all polynomials from
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
to
\mathbbm{F}
of degree
\leq d
is denoted by:

Poly_{\leq d}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})

Next, we present a known equivalent definition for a polynomial using derivatives.
To do so, we first define a derivative in the case of finite fields.

Definition 2.2

(Derivative)
.

Given a function
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
and
a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
we define the derivative of
F
in direction
a
as a function
D_{a}F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

defined as follows:

D_{a}F(x)\coloneqq F(x+a)-F(x)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
A function
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is a polynomial of degree
\leq d

if and only if
D_{a}F
is a polynomial of degree
\leq d-1
for all
a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

This leads us to a natural definition of a
degree
of a function using derivatives.

Definition 2.4

(Local Degree)
.

For a function
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
, we define its
local degree
,
to be the least integer
d\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
for all
a_{1},...,a_{d+1},x\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}
:

D_{a_{d+1}}...D_{a_{1}}F(x)=0

In
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, the two definitions of degree coincide, and we get a single definition of a degree:

Lemma 2.5

(Equivalance of definitions of a degree)
.

Let
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
be a function, and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer.
Then, the global degree of a
F

equals
its local degree.

Remark 2.6
.

We sometimes refer to the requirement that the local degree of a function is
\leq d
,
as the
local criteria
of degree
\leq d
polynomials.

2.3
Rank-bias in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

We start by defining the notion of
bias
, which is a measure of how the function is far from being equidistributed (see Appendix
A
for the exact details).

Definition 2.7

(Bias)
.

Let
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
The
bias
of the function
F
is defined in the following way:

bias({F})\coloneqq 1/\left|\mathbbm{F}^{n}\right|\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}}{e\left[F(x)\right]}

Moreover, for a subset
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, we define the
bias of
F
in
{\tilde{X}}
to be:

bias_{{\tilde{X}}}{({F})}\coloneqq 1/\left|{\tilde{X}}\right|\cdot\sum_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}{e\left[F(x)\right]}

Next, we present a standard definition of rank of a polynomial, which is a notion that measures how
structured
is the function.
Note that low rank implies the polynomial is highly structured.
Formally we have the following definition:

Definition 2.8

(Rank of a Polynomial)
.

Given a constant
d\in\mathbbm{N}
and a polynomial
P
,
the
d
-rank
of
P
, denoted as
rank_{d}\left(P\right)
is defined to be
the smallest integer
{{r}}
such that
P
can be computed given
{{r}}
polynomials of degree
<d
.
In other wards, we say
rank_{d}\left(P\right)={{r}}
if
{{r}}
is the smallest integer such that
there exists
{{r}}
polynomials
Q_{1},...,Q_{{r}}\in Poly_{\leq d-1}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})

and a function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
such that:

P(x)=\Gamma\left(Q_{1}(x),...,Q_{{r}}(x)\right)

If
d=1
, then
1
-rank is defined to be
\infty
if
P
is non constant, and
0
otherwise.

Moreover, for a polynomial
P
of degree
\deg(P)=d
we denote
rank\left(P\right)\coloneqq rank_{d}\left(P\right)
.

We call such function
\Gamma
a
decomposition
or a
computation
of
P
using lower-degree polynomials.

Let us now define a factor.
Note that we focus our discussion to factors in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
but define the basic definitions over a general set
U
so they will apply for factors over a general sets.
This is necessary as we will later use them also for other sets such as
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Definition 2.9

(Factor)
.

Let
U
be a set.
A
factor over
U
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}
, is simply a partition of the set
U
.
Each subset in the partition is called an
atom
.

A collection of function
F_{1},...,F_{c}:U\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
defines a factor
\mathcal{B}_{F_{1},...,F_{c}}
over
U

with atoms:

\left\{u\in U\middle|f_{1}(u)=b_{1},...,f_{c}(u)=b_{c}\right\}

for all
b_{1},...,b_{c}\in\mathbbm{F}
.

Additionally, we use
\mathcal{B}
to also denote the map
\mathcal{B}(u)\rightarrow(F_{1}(u),...,F_{c}(u))
.

Notation
.

Let
F_{1},...,F_{c}:U\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
be a collection of functions.
For a factor
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{F_{1},...,F_{c}}
, we denote by
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
the amount of functions that define it, i.e.
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|=c
.
Moreover, we denote
\left\lVert{\mathcal{B}}\right\rVert\coloneqq\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c}
, which is the maximal amount of (possibly empty) atoms.
Additionally, the rank of the factor is defined to be the rank of the polynomials that generate it.

Definition 2.10

(Polynomial Factor)
.

We say a factor
\mathcal{B}
over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
is a
polynomial factor
if it is defined by a collection of polynomials
P_{1},...,P_{c}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
,
i.e.
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{P_{1},...,P_{c}}
.
The degree of the factor, denote as
\deg(\mathcal{B})
is the maximal degree of the polynomials
P_{1},...,P_{c}
.

Note that the notion of degree (and polynomial) are defined only for functions over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
therefore this definition is well-defined only for
U=\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Definition 2.11

(Rank of a Factor)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of polynomials
P_{1},...,P_{c}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
The rank of the polynomial collection is defined as:

rank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\coloneqq\min\left\{rank_{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}\right)\middle|0\neq\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbbm{F}^{c},d=\max_{i\in\left[c\right]}{\deg(\lambda_{i}P_{i})}\right\}

For a factor
\mathcal{B}
defined by a collection of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
, we define its rank to be the rank of the collection of polynomials defining it.
For a non-decreasing function
{{{r}}}:\mathbbm{N}\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
, a factor
\mathcal{B}
is called
{{{r}}}
-regular if its rank is at least
{{{r}}}(\left|\mathcal{B}\right|)
.

Note
.

Note that in the definition above, the rank of each linear combination is calculated as the
d
-rank,
where
d
is the maximal degree of a polynomial that participates in the linear combination non-trivially.
This is crucial as it ensures that a high rank factor do not have linear dependence in the largest-degree homogenous component of any of its polynomials.

We now present a fundamental property of high rank polynomials, that was first proved by
[
GT07a
]
when
d<\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|
,
later extended to general fields by
[
KL08
]
, and further extended also to large fields by
[
BL15
]
.
This property of high rank polynomials is that they they have low bias:

Theorem 2.12

(Rank-bias in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
)
.

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field.
Let
\epsilon>0
and
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
There exists
r_{\ref{high-rank-implies-low-bias}}\coloneqq r_{\ref{high-rank-implies-low-bias}}(\mathbbm{F},d,\epsilon)
,
such that for every degree-
d
polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
:
if
rank\left(P\right)\geq r_{\ref{high-rank-implies-low-bias}}
then
bias({P})<\epsilon
.

Remark 2.13
.

This property implies that a collection of polynomials that have high rank is
equidistributed
.
See Appendix
A
for more details in this regard.

2.4
Regularization in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

In this subsection we define the regularization process in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Before doing so, let us present some definitions in this regard.
Note that we define the basic definitions over a general set
U
so they will apply for factors over a general sets,
as this is necessary as we will later use them also for other sets such as
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Definition 2.14

(Measureable)
.

Let
U
be a set, and let
A\subseteq U
.
Let
\mathfrak{F}=\left\{F_{1},...,F_{c}\right\}
be a collection of functions
F_{i}:U\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
We say a function
G:U\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is
measurable in respect of
\mathfrak{F}
in
A
, shorthand by
\mathfrak{F}
-measurable in
A
,
if there exists a function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
such that:

\forall a\in A:g(a)=\Gamma(F_{1}(a),...,F_{c}(a))

When discussing the factor over
A
defined by
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{F_{1},...,F_{c}}
,
we also say
G
is
measurable in resepct of
\mathcal{B}
.
The function
\Gamma
will be denoted as the
measurement function
of
G
in respect of
\mathfrak{F}
.
Additionally, when
A=U
, we sometimes omit the specification of the domain, and say
G
is measurable in respect of
\mathfrak{F}
.

Note that in this paper, we usually think of
U=\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, and
A
is either
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
or
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Remark 2.15
.

If
G
is
\mathfrak{F}
-measurable in
A
, then every value of
G
in
A

can be determined by the values of
F_{1},...,F_{c}
.
In other words, the function
G
is constant inside every atom of the factor defined by
\mathfrak{F}
.

Definition 2.16

(Syntactic Refinement)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be polynomial factors over
U
.
We say a factor
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a
syntactic refinement
of the factor
\mathcal{B}
, if the collection of functions defining
\mathcal{B}
is a subset of the set
of functions defining
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.
We denote this property of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
by
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\mathcal{B}
.

We now present a standard generalized definition of refinement, where we only require the atoms induced by the refined factors are sub-atoms of those that are induced by the original factor.
Note that in this refinement, we allow the refined factor to include completely different polynomials than the original factor.

Definition 2.17

(Semantic Refinement)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be polynomial factors on
U
defined by
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
respectively.
We say the factor
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a
semantic refinement
of the factor
\mathcal{B}
in
A\subseteq U
,
if
x,y\in A
with
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(y)
implies that
\mathcal{B}(x)=\mathcal{B}(y)
.
We denote this property of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
by
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}|A}\mathcal{B}
.
When
A=U
, we sometimes omit
A
from the syntax and denote it with
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}

Note that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\mathcal{B}
implies
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}|A}\mathcal{B}
for every
A\subseteq U
.

Remark 2.18
.

A handy property of semantic refinement is that if
F:A\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is
\mathcal{P}
-measurable,
then it is also
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable in
A
.
Moreover, the other direction is also true:
If every
\mathcal{P}
-measurable function
F:A\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
in
A
is also
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable in
A
, then
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}|A}\mathcal{B}
.

Next, we recall a lemma that was presented in
[
BFH+13
, Theorem 4.1]
, that allows us, given a polynomial that is measurable by a high rank factor in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
to replace the polynomials in the measurement function to any collection of polynomials with smaller or equal degree, and preserve the degree of the original polynomial.
Note that we state the lemma under the constraint that
d<\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|
, but it is also valid for when
d\geq\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|
with proper generalization of definitions to this case (See
[
BFH+13
, Theorem 4.1]
for the exact statement).

Lemma 2.19

(Preserving Degree in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
)
.

Let
d>0
an integer such that
d<\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|
, and let
P_{1},...,P_{c}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
be polynomials of degree at most
d
, that form a factor of rank
\geq{{{r}}}^{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(\mathbbm{F},d,c)
.
Assume that for
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
, the function
\gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
defined as
\gamma(a)\coloneqq\Gamma(P_{1}(a),...,P_{c}(a))
is of
\deg(\gamma)=d^{\prime}
.

Then, for every collection of polynomials
Q_{1},...,Q_{c}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
that satisfy
\deg(Q_{i})\leq\deg(P_{i})
,
the function
\gamma^{\prime}
defined as
\gamma^{\prime}(a)=\Gamma(Q_{1}(a),...,Q_{c}(a))
is a polynomial of
\deg(\gamma^{\prime})\leq d^{\prime}
.

Next, we restate a useful lemma from
[
BL15
, Lemma 4.17]
that shows that under the conditions
above,
\Gamma
is as a low-degree polynomial (with even stronger conditions).
Formally, they showed:

Lemma 2.20

(Faithful Composition)
.

In the case discussed above, the structure of
\Gamma
is as follows:

\Gamma(z_{1},...,z_{c_{1}})=\sum_{\alpha\in\left[p-1\right]^{c}}{{C_{\alpha}}\cdot{\prod_{i=1}^{c}}{z_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}}

where
C_{\alpha}=0
whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{c_{1}}(\alpha_{i}\cdot\deg(P_{i}))>d^{\prime}
.

In other words, this means that
\Gamma
as a function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
,
is a polynomial of degree
\leq d^{\prime}
, even when substituting its
i
-th input by any polynomial of degree
\leq\deg(P_{i})
.

Finally, we restate the regularization process, that was first presented by
[
GT07a
, Lemma 2.3]
.
The regularization process shows that every factor have a high-rank factor that semantically refines it,
without increasing the size of the factor too much (its new size is independent of
n
).

Lemma 2.21

(Regularization in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
)
.

Let
{{{r}}}:\mathbbm{N}\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
be a non-decreasing function and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
There exists
C_{{{{r}}},d}^{\ref{regularization-in-Fn-lemma}}:\mathbbm{N}\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
such that the following holds:
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a factor on
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
defined by polynomials
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},...,P_{c})
where for all
i\in[c]
:
P_{i}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
and
\deg(P_{i})\leq d
.
Then, there is an
{{{r}}}
-regular factor
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
defined by polynomials
\mathcal{Q}=(Q_{1},...,Q_{c^{\prime}})
where
for all
i\in[c]
:
Q_{i}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
and
\deg(Q_{i})\leq d
such that

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}
and
c^{\prime}\leq C_{{{{r}}},d}^{\ref{regularization-in-Fn-lemma}}(c)
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{B}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}
for some polynomial factor
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
with rank at least
{{{r}}}(c^{\prime})+c^{\prime}+1
,
then we can require that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}
.

3
Polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}

In this section we wish to generalize the definition of degree-
d
polynomials for functions
f:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
Note that we wish to define it using a property of
f
that is intrinsic to
{\tilde{X}}
: given a function
f:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
,
we wish be able to determine its degree only using values of
{\tilde{X}}
, without considering any value outside of
{\tilde{X}}
(such as values of
\mathbbm{F}^{n}\setminus{\tilde{X}}
).

To define such property, we generalize the local definition of a degree that is defined for polynomials in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
We remind the reader that in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, we said a function over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
is a polynomial of degree
\leq d
if and only if its
(d+1)
-derivative in every direction is
\equiv 0
.
Thus, in order to determine the
(d+1)
-derivative of a function in directions
y_{1},...,y_{d+1}
, one needs to evaluate the function over all the points of the cube generated by
x,y_{1},...,y_{d+1}
,
which is the set of points
\left\{x+\sum_{i\in S}{y_{i}}\right\}_{S\subseteq[d+1]}
.
This raises a challnge in extending this definition for functions defined over
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
:
depending on
{\tilde{X}}
, the function
f:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is not be defined to all points in all the cubes of
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, because some of those points do not lie in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Therefore, to generalize the definition of a polynomial to
{\tilde{X}}
, we start by giving the formal definition and notation of the set of cubes in
{\tilde{X}}
:

Definition 3.1

(Cubes)
.

Let
k\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer and let
x,y_{1},...,y_{k}\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
We define the cube
(x|y_{1},...y_{k})
as follows:

(x|y_{1},...,y_{k})\coloneqq\left\{x+\sum_{i\in S}{y_{i}}\right\}_{S\subseteq[k]}

We refer to
x
as the
offset
of the cube, and
y_{1},...,y_{k}
as the
directions
of the cube.

Moreover, Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be a subset.
We define the
set of cubes of
{\tilde{X}}
of size
k
as follows:

C_{k}({\tilde{X}})\coloneqq\left\{(x|y_{1},...,y_{k})\middle|\forall S\subseteq[k]:(x+\sum_{i\in S}{y_{i}})\in{\tilde{X}}\right\}

Using this definition, we can define a polynomial of degree
\leq d
for subsets of
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
:

Definition 3.2

(Polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
)
.

Let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer, and let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
We say the degree of a function
f:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is
d

if
d
is the smallest integer such that
f
vanishes over all cubes of size
(d+1)
, i.e:

\forall{(x|y_{1},...,y_{d+1})\in C_{d+1}({\tilde{X}})}:D_{y_{d+1}}...D_{y_{1}}p(x)=0

A function over
{\tilde{X}}
of degree
\leq d
is also called a
polynomial of degree
\leq d
.
We denote the set of polynomials of degree
\leq d
over
{\tilde{X}}
by
Poly_{\leq d}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})
.

Note
.

For
{\tilde{X}}=\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, the definition above coincides with the local definition of polynomials.

3.1
Lifting Polynomials

Our goal to achieve good properties for polynomials over
{\tilde{X}}
.
To do so, we wish to connect the desired properties of polynomials defined over
{\tilde{X}}
, to properties known for polynomials over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Following such strategy raises a question: given a polynomial
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
, which polynomial over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
should we consider to deduce properties of
p
?
To find such a polynomial over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, it would have been useful that all polynomials over
{\tilde{X}}
actually ”came from” polynomials over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
More formally, it would have been useful that all polynomials
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
would be equal to a restriction of some polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
of degree
\leq d
, to the set
{\tilde{X}}
.
This would give us a ”good candidate” (or candidates) to polynomials over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, that using their known properties, we could achieve the properties we desire for polynomials over
{\tilde{X}}
.

Generally speaking, the existence of such polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is not trivial by itself, and it mapy depend on the polynomial
p
and the set
{\tilde{X}}
.
In this subsection, we discuss sets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that have this property for every polynomial
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
Before formulating the notion above, we start by a simple remark:

Remark 3.3
.

By the local criteria for
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, we have that a restriction of a polynomial of degree
\leq d
over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
to
{\tilde{X}}
is a polynomial of degree
\leq d
over
{\tilde{X}}
.
Therefore, the other direction is true: every restriction of a polynomial over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
to
{\tilde{X}}
is a polynomial over
{\tilde{X}}
.

Next, let us define subsets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that have the desired property, which we call
d
-lift-enabler variety
.

Definition 3.4

(
d
-lift-enabler Subset)
.

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a field, and
n>0
be an integer.
For an integer
d>0
, we say a subset
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
is
d
-lift-enabler
if for every
d^{\prime}\leq d
,
for every polynomial
p\in Poly_{d^{\prime}}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})

there exist a polynomial
\widehat{p}\in Poly_{d^{\prime}}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})
such that
p|_{{\tilde{X}}}=\widehat{p}|_{{\tilde{X}}}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Using the local criterion of polynomials and the fact that that
C_{d+1}({\tilde{X}})\subseteq C_{d+1}(\mathbbm{F}^{n})
,
one can see that for a polynomial
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
with
\deg(p)=d
,
every extension
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
with
p=P|_{{\tilde{X}}}

holds the bound
\deg(\widehat{p})\geq d
.
The other direction is not true in the general case, but it is specifically promised when the variety is
d
-lift-enabler.

This definition naturally raises the following definition:

Definition 3.6

(The Lift Operator)
.

Let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer.
Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be a
d
-lift-enabler subset.
We define
the
d
-lift operator
to be an operator
\widehat{\square}:Poly_{\leq d}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})\rightarrow Poly_{\leq d}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})
the following way:

Let
d^{\prime}\leq d
.
Given a polynomial
p:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
of degree
d^{\prime}
, the operator
\widehat{\square}
returns a polynomial
\widehat{p}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
of degree
d^{\prime}

such that
p=\widehat{p}|_{{\tilde{X}}}
.
Note that we did not require the lift to be unique.
Thus, in case there are multiple valid lifts for a polynomial

, the lift operator picks a single (consistent) one of them.
Moreover, the lift always exists because the subset
{\tilde{X}}
is
d
-lift-enabler.

In addition, for a collection
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{1},...,p_{c})
of polynomials

,
we denote
\widehat{\mathfrak{p}}\coloneqq(\widehat{p_{1}},...,\widehat{p_{c}})

In the following subsections, we give example to two concrete sets

that are
d
-lift-enablers.
Before doing so, we define an algebraic variety:

Definition 3.7

(Algebraic Variety)
.

For a collection of functions
\mathfrak{F}\coloneqq\left\{F_{1},...F_{c}\right\}
such that

,
we denote
Z\left(\mathfrak{F}\right)\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}\middle|\forall i:F_{i}(x)=0\right\}

If the collection is a collection of polynomials, we call

, shorthand by

The degree of the variety is defined to be the maximal degree of polynomials in the collection that defines it.
The rank of the variety is defined to be the rank of the collection that defines the variety (as a collection).

4
Relative Rank-Bias Property

In this section, we generalize the relation between rank and bias that is known for
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
also for
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Specifically, in Lemma
2.12
, it was shown that high rank factors have low bias in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
We wish to define an alternative definition of rank for
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, called
{\tilde{X}}
-relative rank
,
such that high
{\tilde{X}}
-relative rank implies low bias in
{\tilde{X}}
.
This type of relation (and definition) was shown previously to a few sets;
in
[
LZ21
, Theorem 1.8]
for sets
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)
where
\mathcal{Q}
is a collection of polynomials of high rank;
and in
[
GK22b
, Theorem 1.4]
for sets
{\tilde{X}}=S^{n}
for
S\subset\mathbbm{F}
.

To understand this notion, we first introduce a simple example that demonstrates the need for a different definition of rank to achieve equidistribution properties in subsets of
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Example 4.1
.

Let
{\tilde{X}}=\left\{x\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}\middle|x_{1}=0\right\}
.
Define
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
by
P(x)\coloneqq x_{1}
.

In
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
P
has rank
\infty
as it can not be decomposed polynomials of degree
<1
(constants).
Additionally, it is perfectly equidistributed.
This is the simplest example of the rank-bias relation in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

However, when restricting
P
to
{\tilde{X}}
, we get
P|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0
.
As
0
is a constant function, it is the
least
equidistributed possible in
{\tilde{X}}
.
Therefore, we see that the way we defined rank in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
does not imply the desired equidistribution in
{\tilde{X}}
:
we found a polynomial with high rank (infinity) that has a very high bias
in
{\tilde{X}}
(the maximal).

The reason the definition of rank in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
fails to capture equidistribution even on subsets that are really similar to
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
(isomorphic to
\mathbbm{F}^{k}
), is because of the following reason:
Even though our polynomial
P
does
not
have a decomposition to a few lower-degree polynomials by itself,
there
exists
a
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent
polynomial that has such structured decomposition.
Here, by
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent we mean a polynomial in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that is bounded by the same degree bound, and is equal to
P
in
{\tilde{X}}
.
In the example described above, this equivalent polynomial is the constant function
0
, and its decomposition is the trivial one (any function decomposes a constant function).
An alternative perspective which we use throughout this paper to
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalence is that both polynomials are equal up to a
valid
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder
: a bounded degree polynomial that is
\equiv 0
in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Generally speaking, high
{\tilde{X}}
-relative rank may not imply low bias in
{\tilde{X}}
.
Therefore, this structure-randomness relation is not true for a general subset
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
but is a
property
of the subset
{\tilde{X}}
.
Thus, we say that a subset has the
relative rank-bias property
if this relation holds,
i.e. if high
{\tilde{X}}
-relative rank implies equidistribution in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Let us now formally define our definition for relative rank, inspired by the two different definitions of relative rank presented in
[
LZ21
, Definition 1.6]

and in
[
GK22b
, Definition 1.3]
:

Definition 4.2

(Relative Rank of a Polynomial)
.

Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
For an integer
d\in\mathbbm{N}
and a polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
, we define its
d
-relative rank
in respect of
{\tilde{X}}
as:

rank_{{d},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(P\right)\coloneqq\min\left\{rank_{d}\left(P-\overline{P}\right)\middle|\overline{P}\in Poly_{\leq\deg(P)}(\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}),\overline{P}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0\right\}

For a polynomial
P
of degree
\deg(P)=d
we denote
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(P\right)\coloneqq rank_{{d},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(P\right)
.

Definition 4.3

(
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent and
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder)
.

Moreover, we say a polynomial is
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent
to
P
if its restriction to
{\tilde{X}}
is
\equiv P|_{{\tilde{X}}}
.
We say it is
valid
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent to
P
if it is
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalent to
P
and its of the
same
degree of
P
.

Similarly, we say a polynomial is
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder
of
P
if its restriction to
{\tilde{X}}
is
\equiv 0
.
We say it is
valid
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder
if it is
{\tilde{X}}
-remainder of
P
and its of degree
smaller or equal
of the degree of
P
.

We typically denote such polynomial as
\overline{P}
.

In other words, the
d
-relative rank of a polynomial
P
is the smallest
d
-rank of all valid
{\tilde{X}}
-equivalents of
P
.

Note 4.4
.

Note that
[
GK22b
, Definition 1.3]
defines rank in a substantially different way than our definition,
and consequentially our results will not apply to the sets they presented.
One of the main differences in the definition of rank occurs for
d=1
.
In the definition we use for rank, the rank of every (non-constant) degree-
1
polynomial is
\infty
, where in the definition used in
[
GK22b
]
it is a finite number (which is possibly very small).
This difference is crucial, as for example, it makes regularization according to their definition not-trivially possible,
where it is known to be possible when rank is defined by the definition we use (Lemma
2.21
).

Definition 4.5

(Relative Rank of a Factor)
.

Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{1},...,P_{c}\right\}
.
The rank of the polynomial set
\mathcal{P}
relative to the subset
{\tilde{X}}
is defined as:

rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\coloneqq\min\left\{rank_{{d},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}\right)\middle|0\neq\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbbm{F}^{c},d=\max_{i\in\left[c\right]}{\deg(\lambda_{i}P_{i})}\right\}

For a factor
\mathcal{B}
defined by a collection of polynomials, we define its relative rank relative to
{\tilde{X}}
to be the relative rank of the collection of polynomials defining it, relative to the set
{\tilde{X}}
.

For a non-decreasing function
{{{r}}}:\mathbbm{N}\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
, a factor
\mathcal{B}
is called
{{{r}}}
-
{\tilde{X}}
-regular if its relative rank in respect to
{\tilde{X}}
is at least
{{{r}}}(\left|\mathcal{B}\right|)
.

4.1
Relative Rank-Bias Property

Definition 4.6

(Relative Rank-Bias property)
.

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer.
Let

\leq d

As an immediate corollary of Lemma

that shows that high rank implies low bias, we have that

Corollary 4.7

(
\mathbbm{F}^{n}

.

For every finite field
\mathbbm{F}
and

This is a simple usage of Lemma

Now, if

∎

4.2
Limited-Relative Rank-Bias Property

Sometimes, however, we can not request
{\tilde{X}}
to be such that high relative rank implies low bias for every
\epsilon>0
,
but only for
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon
for some constant
\epsilon>0
.
This leads to defining the
limited relative rank-bias property
, which will be used to discuss such sets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}

As we will later see, this definition raises naturally where
{\tilde{X}}
is a high rank variety,
in which for the relative rank-bias property to hold for some
\epsilon>0

\epsilon
.
Thus, to have the relative rank-bias property for a high rank variety but without requiring an infinitely large rank, we must limit the relative rank-bias property for
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon

We formulate the definition of this property as follows:

Definition 4.8

(Limited Relative Rank-bias property)
.

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer, and let
\epsilon>0

We say a set
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
has the
({\tilde{r}},\mathbbm{F},d,\epsilon)
-limited-relative-rank-bias property
if
for every
\epsilon^{\prime}\geq\epsilon
,
for every polynomial
P
of degree
\leq d
with
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(P\right)\geq{\tilde{r}}(\epsilon^{\prime})
we have:

bias_{{\tilde{X}}}{({P})}<\epsilon^{\prime}

As a convention, we denote by
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
the
\epsilon
such that the limited-relative-rank-bias property holds for
{\tilde{X}}
.

-relative measurement function

Note
.

Note that if

then the same bound also bounds the degree of the remainder, i.e.
\deg(\overline{G})\leq\deg(G)
.
Therefore
\overline{G}
is a valid

-remainder of

Moreover, this requirement is equivalent to the definition above,
as if
\deg(\overline{G})\leq\deg(G)
, then we also have
\deg(G-\overline{G})\leq\deg(G)
.

Note
.

Also note that without the bound on the degree of the remainder,
being measurable relative to
{\tilde{X}}
is in fact equivalent for being a measurable in
A={\tilde{X}}
.
This is true because under these conditions, the remainder
\overline{G}
has no constraints but
\overline{G}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0
,
thus the condition left on the measurement is just being a measurement to
G
in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Remark 5.2
.

If
G
is a function that it is
\mathfrak{F}
-measurable relative to
{\tilde{X}}
,
then every value of
G
can be determined by the values of

up to a remainder
\overline{G}
of degree
\leq d
.
Thus, perhaps we do not know that the function

is constant inside every atom of
\mathfrak{F}
as in a regular semantic refinement,
but we do know that there exists a function
(G-\overline{G})
that equals to
G
on
{\tilde{X}}
, is constant on every atom of
\mathfrak{F}
and it is a function with a bounded degree i.e.
\deg(G-\overline{G})\leq\deg(G)
.

Next, we present a new type of refinement, which is a relaxation of semantic refinement.
This relaxation will allow us to discuss the corresponding claim of the polynomial regularity lemma (Lemma
2.21
) for relative rank (instead of rank).

Definition 5.3

(Semantic Refinement Relative to
{\tilde{X}}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be polynomial factors on
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, defined by sets of polynomials
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
respectively,
and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
.
We say a factor
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is a
semantic refinement relative to
{\tilde{X}}
of the factor
\mathcal{B}
,
or
{\tilde{X}}
-relative semantic refinement
,
if the following holds:
Every function
F:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
that is
\mathcal{P}
-measurable relative to
{\tilde{X}}
,
is also
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable relative to
{\tilde{X}}
.
If the definition above holds, we denote
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}
.

Note
.

It is easy to see that this relation is transitive, i.e. if

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}
and

\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
,
then
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}
.

Remark 5.4
.

In
{\tilde{X}}
, semantic refinements relative to
{\tilde{X}}
behave the same as regular semantic refinements in the perspective of being measurable:
every function that is
\mathcal{P}
-measurable in
{\tilde{X}}
is also
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable in
{\tilde{X}}
.
However, the two definitions behave differently in the perspective of being measurable in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Specifically, in relative semantic refinements,
if
G
is a
\mathcal{P}
-measurable function it is not necessarily
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable.
However, it is measurable up to a remainder
\overline{G}
of degree
\leq\deg(G)
such that
\overline{G}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0
.

Corollary 5.5
.

If
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}
, then in
{\tilde{X}}
it is a regular semantic refinement, i.e.
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{sem}|{\tilde{X}}}\mathcal{B}
.

\leq\alpha_{i^{\star}}\cdot d^{\star}+d_{F}-\alpha_{i^{\star}}\cdot d^{\star}=d_{F}
.
This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

Now, it remains to prove the second part of the Theorem

Claim 5.9

If
\mathcal{B}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}

Proof.

We will show claim step-by-step.
We denote by
\mathcal{P},\bar{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}

\mathcal{B},\bar{\mathcal{B}},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}

\mathcal{B}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}

with

{{{{r}}}_{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(\mathbbm{F},d,c^{\prime})}+c^{\prime}+1

then we can require that
\mathcal{B}_{1}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}
, and also that
\mathcal{B}_{2}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}
.

For the first part, we have
\mathcal{B}_{1}\succeq_{\text{syn}}\bar{\mathcal{B}}

,

rank\left(\bar{\mathcal{P}}\right)>{{{r}}}_{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(\mathbbm{F},d,c^{\prime})+c^{\prime}+1\geq{{{r}}}_{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(\mathbbm{F},d,c_{1})+c_{1}+1

P^{1}_{i^{\star}}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}

P^{1}_{i^{\star}}\notin\bar{\mathcal{P}}
.
Note that this is possible whenever
\mathcal{P}^{\star}\cap\bar{\mathcal{P}}\neq\emptyset

i^{\star}

{{{r}}}
-
{\tilde{X}}

\vec{P}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{c_{1}}{\lambda_{i}P^{1}_{i}(x)}

6
Radius of Reed-Muller over
{\tilde{X}}

We recall that the normalized distances of Reed-Muller codes over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
and over
{\tilde{X}}

are denoted by
\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)
and
\delta_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)
respectively.
We present a theorem that shows that Reed-Muller codes over a subset
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that is
d
-lift-enabler and has the (limited) relative rank-bias property,
has (approximately) an
equal
normalized distance as Reed-Muller codes over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

There exist a function
\epsilon_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
such that the following holds:
Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer that represents a degree.
Let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}>0
, and let
{\tilde{r}}:[{\tilde{\epsilon}},\infty]\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
be a limited-relative-rank-bias function.

Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be a set with the following properties

1.

{\tilde{X}}
is
d
-lift-enabler with a lift operator
\widehat{\square}
.

2.

{\tilde{X}}
has the
({\tilde{r}},\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{\epsilon}})
-relative-rank-bias property.

Then, for
\epsilon_{1}\coloneqq\epsilon_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
we have that for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}
:

\delta_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)\geq\ \delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon_{1}

Proof.

We wish to do a reduction of our question regarding the radius of Reed-Muller in
{\tilde{X}}
to the same question about Reed-Muller in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.
Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, and let
d\in\mathbbm{N}
be an integer that represents a degree.
Let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}>0
, and let
{\tilde{r}}:[{\tilde{\epsilon}},\infty]\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}
be a limited-rank-relative-bias function.
Let
\epsilon_{1}\coloneqq\epsilon_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
be a function we will specify later.
Let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be a set with the properties defined above.

Moreover, let
\epsilon>\epsilon_{1}
be some positive value.
We will show that:

\delta_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)>\ \delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon

This will be enough as if the above holds for every
\epsilon>\epsilon_{1}
, we get that in fact

\delta_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)\geq\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon_{1}
.

For start, we note a simple observation: as Reed-Muller over
{\tilde{X}}
is a linear code, we have

\delta_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)=\min\left\{\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)\neq 0\right]\middle|p\in Poly_{\leq d}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})\right\}

Now, let
p\in Poly_{\leq d}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})
be a polynomial over
{\tilde{X}}
,
and denote
d_{p}\coloneqq\deg(p)
.
We wish to lower-bound the value of
\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)\neq 0\right]
.
To do so, we will equivalently upper-bound the value of
\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)=0\right]
.
Precisely, to complete the proof all we need to show is:

\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)=0\right]\leq 1-\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)+\epsilon

Now we begin the proof itself.
First, we lift the polynomial
p
and get a polynomial
\widehat{p}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

such that
\widehat{p}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv p
and
\deg(\widehat{p})=d_{p}
.
Next, denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\widehat{p}}
the factor defined by the set of single polynomial
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\widehat{p}\right\}
.
Trivially, the polynomial
\widehat{p}
is measurable in respect of
\mathcal{P}
.

We define the rank function:

{{{r}}}(m)\coloneqq\max\left\{{\tilde{r}}\left(\dfrac{\epsilon/2}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{m}}\right),{{{r}}}_{\ref{high-rank-implies-low-bias}}\left(\mathbbm{F},d,\dfrac{\epsilon/2}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{m}}\right)\right\}

Then, we
{{{r}}}
-
{\tilde{X}}
-regularize
\mathcal{P}
using Lemma
5.6
.
This gives us a
{{{r}}}
-
{\tilde{X}}
-regular factor
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, which is defined by a set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\coloneqq\left\{P^{\prime}_{1},...,P^{\prime}_{c^{\prime}}\right\}

of degree
\leq d
such that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\succeq^{{\tilde{X}}}_{\text{sem}}\mathcal{B}_{\widehat{p}}
with
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right)\geq{{{r}}}

and with bounded amount of polynomials defining it i.e,
c^{\prime}\leq C_{{{{r}}},d}^{\ref{theorem:regularization-in-X}}(1)
.
Therefore, from definition we have that
\widehat{p}
is
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-measurable relative to
{\tilde{X}}
.
Thus, there exists a measurement function
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{c^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

and a remainder
\overline{\Gamma}:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
with
\overline{\Gamma}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv 0

and degree bounded by
d_{p}
, such that:

\forall a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}:\widehat{p}(a)=\Gamma(P^{\prime}_{1}(a),...,P^{\prime}_{c^{\prime}}(a))+\overline{\Gamma}(a)

Next, we denote
P^{\prime}\coloneqq\widehat{p}-\overline{\Gamma}
.
By definition of remainder function, we have that
P^{\prime}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv p
.
Additionally, note that
P^{\prime}
is a polynomial over
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
of degree
\deg(P^{\prime})=d_{p}\leq d
,
and hence by the definition of
\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)
:

\Pr_{a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}}\left[P^{\prime}(a)=0\right]\leq 1-\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)

(6)

For the next step, we claim that
P^{\prime}
equals
0
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
approximately with the same probability it equals
0
in
{\tilde{X}}
.
Note that this is the heart of the proof: it allows use properties known in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
to new properties in
{\tilde{X}}
.
This is formulated as follows:

Claim 6.2
.

We have:

\left|\Pr_{a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}}\left[P^{\prime}(a)=0\right]-\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[P^{\prime}(x)=0\right]\right|\leq\epsilon

Proof.

Denote
S\coloneqq\mathbbm{F}^{c^{\prime}}
, and for all
s\in S
, denote:

p_{1}(s)\coloneqq\Pr_{a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}}\left[(P^{\prime}_{1}(a),...,P^{\prime}_{c^{\prime}}(a))=s\right]

As of our choice of
{{{r}}}
, we have
rank\left(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right)\geq{{{r}}}_{\ref{high-rank-implies-low-bias}}\left(\mathbbm{F},d,\dfrac{\epsilon/2}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c^{\prime}}}\right)
.
By combining Lemma
2.12
with Lemma
A.2
,
we have that
p_{1}
is (
\epsilon/2\left|S\right|
)-equidistributed, i.e:

p_{1}(s)=\dfrac{1\pm\epsilon/2}{\left|S\right|}

Similarly, denote:

p_{2}(s)\coloneqq\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[(P^{\prime}_{1}(x),...,P^{\prime}_{c^{\prime}}(x))=s\right]

As of our choice of
{{{r}}}
, we have
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right)\geq{\tilde{r}}(\epsilon/2\left|S\right|)
.
Now, we wish to use the relative rank-bias relation with Lemma
A.2

to conclude similarly that
p_{2}
is (
\epsilon/2\left|S\right|
)-equidistributed, i.e:

p_{2}(s)=\dfrac{1\pm\epsilon/2}{\left|S\right|}

However, in order to so, we must first ensure that
(\epsilon/2\left|S\right|)\geq{\tilde{\epsilon}}
.
This is done by choosing a correct
\epsilon_{1}
, and formulated in the following claim:

Claim 6.3
.

One can choose
\epsilon_{1}\coloneqq\epsilon_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
such that if
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{1}
we have that
\epsilon/2\left|S\right|\geq\epsilon_{1}
.

Proof.

We need that:

\dfrac{\epsilon}{2\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c^{\prime}}}\geq{\tilde{\epsilon}}

As
c^{\prime}\leq C^{\ref{theorem:regularization-in-X}}_{{{{r}}},d}(1)
,
for the term above to hold it is enough that the following will be true:

\epsilon\geq{\tilde{\epsilon}}\cdot 2\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{C^{\ref{theorem:regularization-in-X}}_{{{{r}}},d}(1)}

and as
{{{r}}}
and thus also
C^{\ref{theorem:regularization-in-X}}_{{{{r}}},d}(1)
are independent of
n
,
we can pick
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
and get what we aimed for.
∎

Now, under that assumption of
\epsilon_{1}
written above, we have that
p_{2}
is (
\epsilon/2\left|S\right|
)-equidistributed.
This allows us to use the similar distributions of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
and in
{\tilde{X}}
to conclude
that
P^{\prime}
behaves similar in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
and in
{\tilde{X}}
:

\displaystyle\Pr_{a\in\mathbbm{F}^{n}}\left[P^{\prime}(a)=0\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S}{p_{1}(s)\cdot 1_{\Gamma(s)=0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S}{p_{2}(s)\cdot 1_{\Gamma(s)=0}}\pm\epsilon

\displaystyle=\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[P^{\prime}(x)=0\right]\pm\epsilon

which concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

Finally, as
P^{\prime}|_{{\tilde{X}}}\equiv p
, we have that
\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[P^{\prime}(x)=0\right]=\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)=0\right]
.
Thus, the claim above combining with (
6
)
shows that the probability we wished to bound is bounded as we aimed for:

\Pr_{x\in{\tilde{X}}}\left[p(x)=0\right]\leq 1-\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)+\epsilon

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

.

Under the same conditions,

with the

.

p

7
List Decoding Reed Muller Over
{\tilde{X}}

In this section, we prove our main theorem:
we prove the list decoding radius of Reed-Muller codes in is
{\tilde{X}}

at least

the list decoding radius of Reed-Muller codes in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
assuming
{\tilde{X}}
is lift-enabler and has the relative rank-bias property.
We start by presenting formally the list decoding radius in
{\tilde{X}}
.

Definition 7.1

(List Decoding in
{\tilde{X}}
)
.

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field.
Let
d,n\in\mathbbm{N}
, and let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

We define the reed muller list-decoding count in
{\tilde{X}}
at distance
\tau
as follows:

\ell_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d,\tau)\coloneqq\max_{F:{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}}{\left|\left\{P\in Poly_{\leq d}({\tilde{X}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F})\middle|{dist\left(P,F\right)\leq\tau}\right\}\right|}

Additionally, we define
LDR_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d)
to be the
list decoding radius
, which is
the maximum
\tau
for which
\ell_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d,\tau-\epsilon)
is bounded by a
constant
depending only on
\epsilon,\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|,d
.

We recall that it was shown in
[
BL14
, Theorem 1]
that the list decoding radius of Reed Muller is
\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)
.
To be more precise, it was shown that for every
\epsilon>0
, the list-decoding count is constant (independent of
n
) in distance
\tau=\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon
.
Formally, they have shown the following theorem:

Theorem 7.2

(List Decoding RM in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
)
.

There exists a function
c(\mathbbm{F},d,\epsilon)
such that the following holds:
Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, let
\epsilon>0
, and let
d,n\in\mathbbm{N}
.
Then, we have:

\ell_{\mathbbm{F},\mathbbm{F}^{n}}(d,\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon)\leq c(\mathbbm{F},d,\epsilon)

Additionally, we recall a lemma that was presented in
[
BL14
, Corollary 3.3]
, and was used in the analysis of the list decoding radius of Reed-Muller codes in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
:

Lemma 7.3

(Low Complexity Approximation)
.

[
BL14
, Corollary 3.3]

Let
G:A\rightarrow B
, and let
\epsilon>0
.
Let
\mathfrak{F}\subseteq B^{A}
be a collection of functions from
A
to
B
.
Then there exists
c\leq 1/\epsilon^{2}
functions
F_{1},...,F_{c}\in\mathfrak{F}
such that
for every
F\in\mathfrak{F}
, there is a function
\Gamma_{F}:B^{c}\rightarrow B
such that:

\Pr_{x\in A}\left[\Gamma_{F}(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))=F(x)\right]\geq\Pr_{x\in A}\left[G(x)=F(x)\right]-\epsilon

The lemma shows that
G
can “estimated” by a only a few functions from
\mathfrak{F}
.
Note that the estimation is close to
G
in compare to every
F\in\mathfrak{F}
and not necessarily close to
G
itself.

Finally, we present our main theorem, which shows that under assumptions on the subset
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
, the list decoding radius of polynomials in
{\tilde{X}}
will be similar to the list decoding radius in
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

In more details (and informally), we show that if
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
is lift-enabler, has the limited-relative-rank-bias-property,
the list-decoding count is constant (independent of
n
) for every valid
\epsilon
in distance
\tau=\delta_{\mathbbm{F}}(d)-\epsilon
.
Note that not every
\epsilon>0
will be valid: the valid values of
\epsilon
will depend on the limitations of the rank-bias property.
Formally, we show the following:

Theorem 7.4

(List Decoding RM in
{\tilde{X}}
)
.

There exist functions
c_{1}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},{\tilde{\epsilon}})
and

Let
\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, and let

be an integer that represents a degree.
Let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}>0
, and let

Let

{\tilde{X}}

\ell_{\mathbbm{F},{\tilde{X}}}(d,\delta_{\mathbbm{F},\mathbbm{F}^{n}}(d)-\epsilon)\leq c_{2}(\mathbbm{F},d,{\tilde{r}},\epsilon)

Proof.

We follow the lines of the proof of

\mathbbm{F}
be a finite field, and let

be an integer that represents a degree.

{\tilde{\epsilon}}>0

We wish to bound the amount of polynomials in

Apply Lemma

to obtain

{{{r}}}_{1},{{{r}}}_{2}:\mathbbm{N}\rightarrow\mathbbm{N}

For

{{{r}}}_{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(c)\coloneqq{{{r}}}_{\ref{preserving-degree-starting-field}}(\mathbbm{F},d,c)

Specifically, if we start with

Appendix A
Equidistribution of Functions

Assume we have a collection of functions
(F_{1},...,F_{c}
), where
F_{i}:A\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
for some finite set
A
.
We are interested in showing that the functions are equidistributed, which means that their values behave close to independent random variables.
We begin by formulating this definition:

Definition A.1

(Equidistribution of Functions)
.

Given
\epsilon>0
and
A\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
,
we say a collection of functions
\mathfrak{F}=(F_{1},...,F_{c})
where
F_{i}:A\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
is
\epsilon
-equidistributed in
A
if for all
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{c})\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}
we have:

\Pr_{x\in A}\left[(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))=\vec{\alpha}\right]=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c}}\pm\epsilon

The following is a standard lemma that shows that if every linear combination of a collection of functions has low bias, the collection is equidistributed.
We repeat the steps of the proof of
[
HHL19
, Lemma 7.24]
, but here, we think of
A
as any finite set (and not particularly
\mathbbm{F}^{n}
):

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\epsilon>0
, and let
A
be a finite set.
Let
\mathfrak{F}=(F_{1},...,F_{c})
be a collection of functions defined over
A
, i.e.
F_{i}:A\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
.
Assume each linear combination of the collection has low bias, i.e for each
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{c})\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}
such that
\lambda\neq\vec{0}
we have:

bias_{x\in A}{({\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}F_{i}}})}<\epsilon

Then, the collection
\mathfrak{F}
is
\epsilon
-equidistributed over
A
.

In particular, for
\epsilon<\frac{1}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c}}
, the lemma shows that each atom of
\mathfrak{F}
is not empty i.e for all
\vec{\alpha}
there is some
x\in A
such that
(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))=\vec{\alpha}
.

Proof.

We wish to show that for each
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}
we have:

\Pr_{x\in A}\left[(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))=\vec{\alpha}\right]=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|}\pm\epsilon

We express the fraction of inputs that are in the atom
\vec{\alpha}
the following way:

\Pr_{x\in A}\left[(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))\right]=\mathbbm{E}_{x\in A}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{c}{1_{[F_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}]}}\right]

We use the fact that for every
0\neq x\in\mathbbm{F}
, we have
\sum_{\lambda=0}^{p-1}e\left[\lambda x\right]=0
,
and if
x=0
we have
\sum_{\lambda=0}^{p-1}e\left[\lambda x\right]=p
.
Therefore, the expression above equals:

=\mathbbm{E}_{x\in A}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{c}\left({\frac{1}{p}\cdot\sum_{\lambda_{i}=0}^{p-1}{e\left[\lambda_{i}(F_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i})\right]}}\right)\right]=\\
\frac{1}{p^{c}}\cdot{\mathbbm{E}_{x\in A}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{c}\sum_{\lambda_{i}=0}^{p-1}{e\left[\lambda_{i}(F_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i})\right]}\right]}

By the definition of character functions, we have that
e\left[a+b\right]=e\left[a\right]\cdot e\left[b\right]
, and therefore the expression above equals:

\frac{1}{p^{c}}\cdot\sum_{(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{c})\in\prod_{i=1}^{c}[0,p-1]}\left(\mathbbm{E}_{x\in A}\left[e\left[\sum_{i=0}^{c}{\lambda_{i}(F_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i})}\right]\right]\right)

Now, we use the fact that:

bias_{x\in A}{({\sum_{i=1}^{c}(\lambda_{i}(F_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i})})}=bias_{x\in A}{({\sum_{i=1}^{c}(\lambda_{i}F_{i}(x))})}<\epsilon

and get that:

\Pr_{x\in A}\left[(F_{1}(x),...,F_{c}(x))=\vec{\alpha}\right]=\frac{1}{p^{c}}\cdot\left(1\pm\epsilon\prod_{i=1}^{c}{p}\right)=\frac{1}{\left|\mathbbm{F}\right|^{c}}\pm\epsilon

∎

Appendix B
Comparing Ranks

In this section, we compare the definition of rank we used in this paper to another definition of rank used implicitly throughout this paper.
This comparison is crucial, as there is no universally accepted definition of rank;
different theorems presented throughout this paper employ distinct definitions.
We demonstrate that our definition is sufficiently comprehensive, in that a polynomial (or a factor) classified as having high rank according to our criteria
also exhibits high rank according to the second implicitly-used definition.
While in many cases the comparison may appear straightforward, we include it for the sake of completeness.

Specifically, we compare our definition of rank with the definition established in
[
LZ21
]
.
The paper
[
LZ21
]
extended the original definition of rank that was presented in
[
Sch85
]
,
to include also the concept of relative rank.
It is important to note that this definition is specifically defined to subsets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that can be expressed as sets in the form
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)
for some set of polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
,
and not to a general set
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

First, we present a useful notation that is used in the definition presented in
[
LZ21
]
:

Notation

(Largest Degree Homogenous Part)
.

For a polynomial
P
of degree
d
, we denote by
\mathrm{h}({P})
its degree-
d
homogenous component.
In other words,
\mathrm{h}({P})
is the sum of all the monomials of
P
of degree exactly
d
.
For a set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{1},...,P_{c}\right\}
, we define
\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})\coloneqq\left\{\mathrm{h}({P_{i}})\middle|i=1,...,c\right\}
.

Next, we present the exact definition of rank for a polynomial:

Definition B.1

(Schmidt Rank of a Polynomial)
.

The schmidt rank of a homogenous polynomial
P:\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
, noted as
schmrank\left(P\right)
, is the minimal
r
such that there exist
(Q_{i},H_{i})_{i\in[r]}

with
\deg{Q_{i}},\deg{H_{i}}<\deg{P}
such that:

P(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(Q(x)\cdot H(x))

For a general polynomial
P
of degree
d
, we set its rank to be the rank of its degree-
d
homogenous component, i.e.
schmrank\left(P\right)\coloneqq schmrank\left(\mathrm{h}({P})\right)
.

Remark B.2

(High rank implies high schmidt rank)
.

If
rank\left(P\right)\geq 2\cdot r+1
for some constant
r\in\mathbbm{N}
, then
schmrank\left(P\right)\geq r
.

Proof.

For homogenous polynomial
P
, assume
schmrank\left(P\right)<r
.
Then, there exist
r^{\prime}<r
such that there exist
(Q_{i},H_{i})_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}
with
\deg{Q_{i}},\deg{H}_{i}<\deg P
such that:

P(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}(Q(x)\cdot H(x))

Then we can choose
\Gamma:\mathbbm{F}^{2r^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}
to be a sum of multiples of each two consecutive variables to get that

P(x)=\Gamma(Q_{1}(x),H_{1}(x),...,Q_{r^{\prime}}(x),H_{r^{\prime}}(x))
, where the polynomials are from a degree
<\deg(P)
.
This means that
rank\left(P\right)\leq 2r^{\prime}<2r
as we requested.

If we do not assume
P
is homogenous, by adding
P-\mathrm{h}({P})
as an input to
\Gamma
,
one can create a
\Gamma^{\prime}:\mathbbm{F}^{2r^{\prime}+1}\rightarrow\mathbbm{F}

which equals to
P
when substituting the inputs with some polynomials with degree
<\deg{P}
,
which concludes the proof in a similar way.
∎

Next, we present the definition of Schmidt rank of a factor as defined in
[
LZ21
]
.

Definition B.3

(Schmidt Rank of a Factor)
.

For a factor of homogenous polynomials
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},...,P_{c})
, the schmidt rank of the factor is defined as:

schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\coloneqq\min\left(schmrank\left(\sum_{i=1}^{c}\lambda_{i}P_{i}\right)\middle|0\neq(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{c})\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}\right)

Similarly, for a factor of general polynomials
\mathcal{P}
, we set its rank to be the rank of its matching homogenous-factor,
i.e.
schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\coloneqq schmrank\left(\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})\right)

For a factor
\mathcal{B}
generated by
\mathcal{P}
, we define
schmrank\left(\mathcal{B}\right)\coloneqq schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
.

To establish the equivalence of this definition with the one employed throughout the paper, we must first acknowledge two key distinctions between the definitions.
The first distinction is that this definition focuses on the largest-degree homogeneous components of the polynomials involved in the factor, rather than considering linear combinations of polynomials from the factor.
The second distinction pertains to the treatment of
d
in the computation of
d
-rank of each linear combination.
This definition uses the degree of the linear combination directly to calculate the rank that participates in the minimum, in contrast to our definition which uses
\max_{i}{\deg(\lambda_{i}P_{i})}
.
Despite these differences, we will demonstrate that both definitions ultimately yield a similar rank assessment, thereby affirming their equivalence.

Remark B.4

(High Rank Implies High Schmidt Rank for Factors)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\left(P_{1},...,P_{c}\right)
be a set of polynomials and let
{{r}}\in\mathbbm{N}
be a positive integer, i.e.
{{r}}>0
.
If
rank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\geq 2\cdot{{r}}+1
, then
schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\geq{{r}}
.

Proof.

Assume that
schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\leq{{r}}
for
{{r}}>0
.
We will show that
rank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\leq 2{{r}}+1
.
By definition, there exists a linear combination of polynomials in
\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})
with rank
\leq{{r}}
.
In other words, there exists
\vec{0}\neq\lambda\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}
such that
schmrank\left(\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}\mathrm{h}({P_{i}})}\right)\leq{{r}}
.
Denote
\vec{P_{h}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}\mathrm{h}({P_{i}})}
.
As was shown in a previous remark, a rank of a polynomial is smaller than its schmidt rank up to a constant factor,
thus
rank\left(\vec{P_{h}}\right)\leq 2{{r}}+1
(see Remark
B.2
).

Next, we denote
\vec{P}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}
, and
d_{M}\coloneqq\max_{i\in\left[c\right]}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}
.
Note that
\deg(\vec{P})\leq d_{M}
.
We wish to show that
rank_{d_{M}}\left(\vec{P}\right)\leq 2{{r}}+1
.
First, we observe that the
d_{M}
-degree homogenous component of
\vec{P_{h}}
equals the
d_{M}
-degree homogenous component of
\vec{P}
.
This is true because every highest-degree component of polynomials in the linear combination that generated
\vec{P}
,
also exists in the linear combination that generates
\vec{P_{h}}
.
In particular, all homogenous components of degree
d_{M}
exists in both linear combinations
\vec{P_{h}}
and
\vec{P}
.
Therefore, if the degree of
\vec{P}
equals
d_{M}
, we have that
rank_{d_{M}}\left(\vec{P}\right)=rank\left(\vec{P}\right)\geq 2{{r}}+1
.
Otherwise, if
\deg(\vec{P})<d_{M}
, then
rank_{d_{M}}\left(\vec{P}\right)=1\leq 2{{r}}+1
.
This completes the proof.

∎

Note
.

In the case discussed above, if
\deg(\vec{P})<d_{M}
, then
schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)=0
.

Proof.

Assume that
\deg(\vec{P})<d_{M}
.
Therefore, the degree of the linear combination
\vec{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}
is strictly smaller than the degree of at least one of the polynomials participating in it.
Denote by
\vec{\lambda}^{\star}
the sub-combination of
\vec{\lambda}
that consists only the polynomials that participated in
\vec{P}
that are of degree
=d_{M}
.
Trivially,
\vec{\lambda}^{\star}\neq\vec{0}
.
Additionally, we have
\deg(\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}^{\star}P_{i}})<d_{M}
.
Now, we use the following observation: the linear combination above, when summing only the homogenous components of each polynomial, equals
0
, i.e.
\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}^{\star}\mathrm{h}({P_{i}})}\equiv 0
.
By this, we found a linear combination of
\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})
that is
\equiv 0
.
Thus by definition, we have
schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)=0
.
∎

Note
.

This shows that if we compare only the differences in the definition of rank of a factor, i.e. the focus on linear combinations of the largest-degree homogenous components in contrast to the use of the maximal degree
d
-rank,
the two definitions for a rank of a factor are equal up to
\pm 1

(in case we use the same definition of rank for a single polynomial).
To avoid confusion, we omit the exact definitions and respective proof.

We now present the definition of relative rank as stated in
[
LZ21
, Definition 1.6]
:
We remind the reader that this definition is specifically defined to subsets
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
that can be expressed by
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)
for some set of polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
, and not to a general set
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
.

Definition B.5

(Relative Schmidt Rank of a Polynomial)
.

The relative schmidt rank of a homogeneous polynomial
P
relative to a collection of homogeneous polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{{\tilde{c}}})
is

shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)\coloneqq\min\left\{schmrank\left(P+\sum_{i=1}^{{\tilde{c}}}{R_{i}L_{i}}\right)\middle|\deg(L_{i})+\deg(R_{i})\leq\deg(P),\forall i\in[{\tilde{c}}]\right\}

Note that whenever
\deg{L_{i}}>\deg{P}
, this implies
R_{i}=0
.

For general polynomial
P
and general collection of polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
, we define
the schmidt rank of the former in respect to the latter by the relative rank of their largest-degree homogenous component,
i.e.
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)\coloneqq shcmrank_{\mathrm{h}({\tilde{\mathcal{L}}})}\left({\mathrm{h}({P})}\right)
.

Remark B.6

(High Relative Rank
\Rightarrow
High Relative Schmidt Rank)
.

Let
P
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{L_{1},...,L_{{\tilde{c}}}\right\}
be polynomials,
and let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be defined as
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)
.

If
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(P\right)\geq 2\cdot{{r}}+2
for some constant
{{r}}\in\mathbbm{N}
,
then
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)\geq r
.

Proof.

Let
P
and
L_{1},...,L_{{\tilde{c}}}
be polynomials.
Assume that
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)\leq{{r}}
.
Then, there exists
R_{1},...,R_{\tilde{c}}
with

\deg(L_{i})+\deg(R_{i})\leq\deg(P)
for all
i\in\left[{\tilde{c}}\right]

such that:

schmrank\left(\mathrm{h}({P})+\sum_{i=1}^{{\tilde{c}}}{R_{i}\mathrm{h}({L_{i}})}\right)\leq{{r}}

Denote
\overline{P_{h}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{{\tilde{c}}}{R_{i}\mathrm{h}({L_{i}})}
.
As we have shown earlier, a rank of a polynomial is smaller than its schmidt rank up to a constant factor (See Remark
B.2
).
Thus:

rank\left(\mathrm{h}({P})+\overline{P_{h}}\right)\leq 2\cdot schmrank\left(\mathrm{h}({P})+\overline{P_{h}}\right)+1\leq 2\cdot shcmrank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left({P}\right)+1=2{{r}}+1

Next, we denote the respective remainder polynomial for the non-homogenous analogue, i.e
\overline{P}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{{\tilde{c}}}{R_{i}L_{i}}
.
By observing the highest degree homogenous component of each summand, one can see that
\mathrm{h}({P+\overline{P}})=\mathrm{h}({\mathrm{h}({P})+\overline{P_{h}}})
.
Therefore, by adding to the decomposition the non higest-degree-homogenous-component, one can see that:

rank\left(P+\overline{P}\right)\leq rank\left(\mathrm{h}({P})+\overline{P_{h}}\right)+1\leq 2{{r}}+2

This completes the proof as
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(P\right)\leq rank\left(P+\overline{P}\right)\leq 2{{r}}+2
.
∎

Remark B.7

(Relative Schmidt Rank over Varieties of High Degree)
.

If the polynomials defining the variety
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=(L_{1},...,L_{{\tilde{c}}})
are of degree
>\deg(P)
,
then,
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)=schmrank\left(P\right)
.
This is true because in this case, in the calculation of the minimum in the definition of relative schmidt rank, we must have
R_{i}=1
for all
i\in[{\tilde{c}}]
and therefore the minimum above is simply
rank\left(P\right)
.

Note that a similar statement holds for factors aswell.
If
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},...,P_{c})
is a factor of degree
d
, then if all the polynomials in
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
have degree
>d
, then the statement above is also true i.e
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}\right)=schmrank\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
.
This is true because for every linear combination of
\mathcal{P}
has degree
\leq d
and therefore its relative schimdt rank equals its rank.

Finally, we present the extension of the definition of relative rank for polynomials factors:

Definition B.8

(Relative Schmidt Rank of a Factor)
.

The relative rank of a set of homogenous polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{1},...,P_{c}\right\}

relative to another collection of polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{L_{1},...,L_{\tilde{c}}\right\}
is defined as:

shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}\right)\coloneqq\min\left\{shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}}\right)\middle|\vec{0}\neq(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{c})\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}\right\}

If
\mathcal{P}
is a general collection of polynomials, then
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}\right)\coloneqq shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})}\right)
.

For a factor
\mathcal{B}
generated by a set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
, we define its schmidt rank relative to
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)

to be
shcmrank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left({\mathcal{B}}\right)\coloneqq shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}\right)
.

Remark B.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\left\{P_{1},...,P_{c}\right\}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{L_{1},...,L_{{\tilde{c}}}\right\}
be sets of polynomials,
and let
{\tilde{X}}\subseteq\mathbbm{F}^{n}
be defined as
{\tilde{X}}=Z\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\right)
.
Additionally, let
{{r}}\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
{{r}}>0
.
If
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\geq 2\cdot{{r}}+2
for some constant
{{r}}\in\mathbbm{N}
, then
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({P}\right)\geq r
.

Proof.

Assume that
shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}\right)\leq{{r}}
.
We will show that
rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\leq 2{{r}}+2
.
Let
\vec{0}\neq\vec{\lambda}\in\mathbbm{F}^{c}
be some vector of coefficients.
Let
\vec{P}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}P_{i}}
and
\vec{P_{h}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{c}{\lambda_{i}\mathrm{h}({P_{i}})}

be the linear combinations of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathrm{h}({\mathcal{P}})
with coefficients
\vec{\lambda}
respectively,
and let
d_{M}\coloneqq\max_{i\in\left[c\right]}{\deg(\lambda_{i}P_{i})}
.
Additionally, denote
\hat{{{r}}}\coloneqq shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\vec{P_{h}}}\right)\leq{{r}}
.
It is enough to show that
rank_{{d_{M}},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(\vec{P}\right)\leq 2\hat{{{r}}}+2
,
If
\deg(\vec{P})<d_{M}
, then
rank_{{d_{M}},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(\vec{P}\right)=1\leq 2{{r}}+2
.
Otherwise, if
\deg(\vec{P})=d_{M}
, then the remark follows from Remark
B.6

as:

rank_{{d_{M}},{{\tilde{X}}}}\left(\vec{P}\right)=rank_{{\tilde{X}}}\left(\vec{P}\right)\leq 2\cdot shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\vec{P}}\right)+2

Where:

shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\vec{P}}\right)shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathrm{h}({\vec{P}})}\right)=shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\mathrm{h}({\vec{P_{h}}})}\right)=shcmrank_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}\left({\vec{P_{h}}}\right)=\hat{{{r}}}

∎
