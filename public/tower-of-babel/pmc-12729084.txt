Enhancing the antenatal care quality through incentivized routine health information system in Northwestern Ethiopia: a quasi-experimental study (2020–2021)
Authors: Biniam Kefiyalew Taye, Lemma Derseh Gezie, Asmamaw Atnafu, Shegaw Anagaw Mengiste, Jens Johan Kaasbøll, Monika Knudsen Gullslett, Binyam Tilahun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) is essential for maternal and newborn health, yet its utilization and quality remain suboptimal in many low-income settings. Evidence on the effectiveness of non-financial incentives to improve ANC is limited. This study examined the effect of a performance-based non-financial incentive (PBNI) intervention on the quality and optimal utilization of ANC in northwestern Ethiopia.MethodsA quasi-experimental pre-post study with a comparator group was conducted in 2020–2021 involving 1,927 women. The intervention provided non-financial incentives such as smartphones, power banks, scholarships, and certificates, which were distributed at the facility, departmental, and individual levels. Incentives were tied to the performance of the Routine Health Information System(RHIS), focusing on data quality and utilization, including maternal care indicators among the evaluation criteria. Quality ANC was defined as receiving at least 8 of 9 essential components, while optimal ANC was defined as having four or more visits, in accordance with 2020–2021 national guidelines. The analysis used difference-in-differences robust Poisson regression, reporting incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe study revealed a significant 1.44-fold improvement in ANC quality through a PBNI (IRR = 1.44, 95% CI [1.07–1.96]). However, no statistically significant association was found for optimal ANC (IRR = 0.85, 95% CI [0.68, 1.05]). The adjusted analysis identified rural residence (IRR = 1.43, 95% CI [1.12, 1.83]), husbands with primary education (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.09, 1.55]), and birth intervals of 24 to 33 months (IRR = 1.59, 95% CI [1.16, 2.19]) as factors associated with quality ANC. Besides, women with higher education (IRR = 1.98, 95% CI [1.55, 2.52]), engagement in housework (IRR = 0.63, 95% CI [0.52, 0.77]), and awareness of at least two danger signs (IRR = 1.15, 95% CI [1.02, 1.29]) were linked to optimal ANC.ConclusionPBNI significantly enhances ANC quality but lacks statistical significance for optimal utilization. PBNI can motivate providers and enhance service quality when integrated with RHIS-strengthening efforts; however, longer implementation and complementary demand-side strategies may be necessary for sustained optimal ANC improvements.

Background

Antenatal care (ANC) remains a key element of maternal and newborn health, providing a platform for preventive, diagnostic, and counseling interventions that lower morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Despite progress, significant disparities still exist in both the quality and coverage of ANC, especially in low-resource settings like Ethiopia [3, 4]. Many women start ANC late, attend fewer than the recommended visits, or receive incomplete service packages [5, 6]. Barriers go beyond access and infrastructure to include health-system responsiveness, data-driven decision-making, and provider motivation [7, 8]. Strengthening the performance of the Routine Health Information System (RHIS), which records, monitors, and enhances ANC services, has therefore become crucial to ensuring that every contact results in high-quality care [9, 10].

In Ethiopia, the RHIS underpins the Ministry of Health’s(MOH) Information Revolution agenda within the Health Sector Transformation Programs (HSTP), promoting data-driven decision-making at all levels [11, 12]. However, ongoing challenges such as incomplete documentation and inaccurate records at frontline facilities weaken the effective use of data for quality patient care [13–15]. In response, national initiatives like Connected Woreda and the Capacity Building & Mentorship Programme (CBMP) are being implemented to strengthen RHIS functions [16, 17]. In this context, we developed a Performance-Based Non-Financial Incentive (PBNI) model in northwestern Ethiopia to motivate providers, improve RHIS practices, and ultimately enhance ANC services.

Incentive-based strategies are widely employed to influence provider’s behavioral change [18–20]. To date, system-level programs have mainly depended on financial models, such as pay-for-performance (P4P), which have had mixed and context-dependent effects on ANC quality and coverage [21–29]. Non-financial incentives, such as recognition, professional development opportunities, and better working conditions, offer a complementary approach and have shown promise in several settings [30–38]. The PBNI model tested here introduces a non-financial, system-level alignment with RHIS’s core purpose: generating and utilizing quality information to promote service improvement [39, 40].

Conceptually, the incentive-based intervention contrasts performance-based with unconditional incentives: the former ties rewards to predefined targets, reinforcing desired behaviors through feedback and accountability [41, 42]. Our study is based on the Performance of Routine Information System Management (PRISM) framework, which posits that behavioral, organizational, and technical factors interact to influence RHIS performance (specifically improving data quality and use) and, ultimately, health service outcomes (i.e., quality and coverage) [43]. From a behavioral science perspective, Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) emphasizes vicarious learning when staff observe colleagues being recognized for good performance [44], and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) indicates that incentives can strengthen attitudes, perceived control, and intentions toward accurate documentation and high-quality care [45]. Therefore, PBNI was designed primarily to target the behavioral domain of the PRISM framework, to encourage better quality recording and evidence-based service provision across facilities and departments.

This paper is part of a larger research series on PBNI. Earlier analyses demonstrated improvements in RHIS outputs, such as data quality and utilization in participating facilities [46, 47]. Here, we expand the pathway by investigating the subsequent effect on service outcomes, evaluating whether implementing a PBNI scheme within routine health facility processes enhances the quality and optimal delivery of ANC services. We hypothesized that facilities exposed to PBNI would achieve higher ANC quality and greater optimal attendance compared to comparison sites, reflecting improvements in provider motivation and information-use behaviors aligned with PRISM effect pathways.

Materials and methods

Study area, design, and period

The present study was conducted in the districts of “Wegera” and “Tach-armachiho” in the Central Gondar Zone, located in the Amhara National Regional State in Northwest Ethiopia. “Wegera” district served as an intervention district, whereas “Tach Armachiho” served as a comparator group (Fig. 1). As of 2021, the combined population of these two districts was 398,350, with 93,214 women in the reproductive age group (23.4% of the population) [48, 49]. The districts hosted 2 primary hospitals, 13 health centers, and 72 health posts, collectively delivering comprehensive healthcare services to the local population. A pre-post quasi-experimental with a nonequivalent comparator group design was employed. A retrospective cross-sectional household survey was conducted at baseline and end-line in both intervention and comparison districts. The PBNI intervention was implemented from October 2020 to July 2021 [50].

Study participants and sample size determination

The study population in this study were women who gave birth within six months before the intervention (April to September 2020) and following the intervention (February to July 2021), in the 11 health centers of the two districts. The required sample size was calculated to detect differences in proportions between the two groups. Previous studies from the nearby study areas estimated optimal ANC (33% to 40.9%) [51–53] and quality ANC (32.7% and 24.5%) [54, 55]. These proportions were considered with additional assumptions: a 1:1 intervention to comparator district ratio, a 95% confidence level, a 90% statistical power, and an expected 10% change in the intervention district. These assumptions were entered into the Epi-Info statistical package V.7.0’s STATCALC program to compute the separate sample sizes. Additionally, we considered a design effect of 2 and a 10% non-response rate. The resulting minimum sample size was approximately 1927 participants, computed based on the assumption of 24.5% proportion of quality of ANC [55].

Sampling procedure

Six of the health centers were in the intervention district, and five were in the comparator district. The source population for the study sample was the list of women in the birth records at these health centers. A total of 2,426 women who had given birth were identified from the birth record across the pre- and post-study periods. Of 2,426, 1,181 were from the intervention district, and 1,245 were from the comparator district. To be included in the study, women needed readable identification information, such as names and addresses, in the birth record. This readability was important for matching the sampled women during the household survey. Consequently, out of the 2,426 women in the birth record, 2,344(1,131 from the intervention district and 1,213 from the comparator district) met the inclusion criteria. From this total of 2,344 women, 1,927 samples were selected using stratified random sampling techniques (Fig. 2).

Intervention

Design of the intervention

The PBNI was developed through a collaboration among the University of Gondar, the MOH, the Amhara Regional Health Bureau, as well as the Central Gondar Zone Health Office Department and the “Wegera” District Health Office Departments. The design of the PBNI was informed by pre-intervention study findings [56].

Intervention components and target areas

The non-financial incentives package included smartphones, flash drives, power banks, certificates of recognition, desktop computers, and scholarship opportunities. A PBNI intervention was implemented across healthcare facilities, departments, and individual providers’ levels. Departments in the health facilities enrolled for these incentives include: Maternal and Child Health (MCH), Adult Outpatient Disease Department (OPD), Under-5 OPD, Health Management Information Systems (HMIS), and Medical Records Units (MRU). The pool of individual providers included a range of professionals, such as medical record personnel, health information technology (HIT) personnel, health officers, midwives, nurses, laboratory technicians, and pharmacy personnel.

Two key considerations guided the selection of departments included in the PBNI intervention. First, from a programmatic standpoint, the implementing partners identified these units as critical to the overall performance of the RHIS. Although the PBNI was designed as a system-wide initiative to enhance RHIS performance, this study specifically examined its effects on ANC. The inclusion of these departments was therefore strategic, as each contributes distinct functions in data generation, verification, reporting, and archiving. For example, the MCH unit documents ANC, OPDs generate disease-specific indicators, the MRUs maintain standard archival and HMIS units, ensuring oversight of data quality and reporting of indicators across departments. Incentivizing these units was intended to foster shared accountability for data quality and its effective use throughout the facility.

Second, the inclusion strategy was informed by motivational theory principles, which emphasize peer observation and social reinforcement as drivers of sustained performance. Recognizing high-performing departments was expected to produce positive spillover effects: staff witnessing their peers being rewarded for accurate documentation and data use would be encouraged to emulate these behaviors. In this way, the intervention leveraged both structural and behavioral pathways to strengthen engagement in RHIS-related tasks and, ultimately, to improve the quality of care delivered through ANC services [57, 58].

Best performers selection procedures

The selection of the best performers in this study followed two distinct phases: a subjective and an objective approach.

Subjective approach

The subjective approach was chosen due to practical considerations, acknowledging the resource constraints that made it unfeasible to quantitatively assess the performance of all healthcare providers in the district. Thus, management authorities in the intervention district were tasked with identifying high-performing healthcare providers whom they deemed high-performing in terms of data quality and usage. The subjective approach was deployed in two phases. Initially, district-level managers nominated the two best performers regarding data use and quality from each of the six participating health centers. Accordingly, a total of 12 health professionals were nominated at the district level. In the second phase, each health center’s directors identified top performers within their centers, resulting in another 12 nominees. Consequently, the number of potential awardees was reduced to a more manageable size, consisting of 24 nominees, facilitating a more concentrated objective assessment.

Objective approach

Having three entities to incentivize – health centers, case teams, and individual healthcare providers, we underwent a flexible approach to employ objective measures for identifying top performers. For health centers, we employed 14 quantitative performance measures (Table 1). Each performance measure established a specific target and the points to be earned. The allocation of point values and performance targets followed the consideration of the priority areas for RHIS activities defined by the MOH, Ethiopia [59].

We evaluated the performance of case teams and the 24 individuals chosen during the subjective phases, aligning the 14 quantitative performance measures with their pertinent roles and job descriptions. Details regarding performance measures and the assessment process for individuals, departments, and facilities were described elsewhere in prior studies [50, 60–62].

Implementation and phases of PBNI award

Before the PBNI intervention, an announcement was made in the intervention district. A University of Gondar team visited district health offices and centers to brief their leadership on the forthcoming PBNI program. In this phase, banners depicting non-financial rewards were displayed at each health center.

The PBNI award ceremonial program took place bimonthly three times. Representatives from the University of Gondar, MOH, the Amhara Regional Health Bureau, and the Zone Health Department attended these ceremonies. During these bi-monthly events, officials from the MOH and the University of Gondar rewarded the top-performing healthcare providers, departments, and health centers, with the top three recipients receiving incentives. Additionally, recipients of non-financial incentives in each round were presented with certificates of recognition. Professionals from the University of Gondar also gave presentations during the award programs, explaining details of the performance measurement and selection procedures.

Variables of the study

Outcome variable

Optimal ANC

coded as 1 (“Yes”) if a woman received at least four ANC visits and 0 (“No”) otherwise [63]. For operational clarity, “optimal ANC” in this study follows Ethiopia’s national indicator in effect during the intervention period (four or more ANC visits) – under the HSTP-II (2020/21–2024/25) [11]; later, Ethiopia adopted the WHO’s eight-contact model nationally within the Health Sector Medium-Term Development and Investment Plan (HSDIP 2023/24–2025/26) [64].

Quality ANC

Quality ANC was defined as an ANC experience in which a woman received at least eight of the nine recommended basic care components [52, 63]. The nine components, covering basic assessments, diagnostic tests, preventive services, and counselling, were selected based on prior research that evaluated the quality of ANC [65–67]. Data for each component were obtained through face-to-face interviews with women. Each item was coded as 1 = “Yes” if the procedure was reported at least once during pregnancy and 0 = “No” otherwise. The ≥ 8-of-9 threshold was chosen to represent a high standard of care while maintaining consistency with previous studies that applied similar composite measures to assess ANC quality. Although all nine components are recommended, occasional omissions may happen without significantly affecting the overall quality of care; therefore, the ≥ 8-of-9 criterion provides a balanced, evidence-based operational definition [52, 63]. Independent variables

To disentangle the intervention effect from other factors assumed to be associated with this study’s outcomes, a variety of independent variables were measured, based on established evidence from previous studies. The independent variables gathered comprised aspects related to both women and household characteristics, such as age, religion, residential location, wealth index, educational status of women and their partners, and the occupations of both partners. Additionally, obstetric variables such as birth order, history of pregnancy-related complications, knowledge of danger signs, history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, birth interval from last child in months, and the pregnancy’s intendedness were measured.

The household wealth index was created using the standard method employed in the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). Asset ownership and housing features, such as the type of flooring, roofing, and wall materials, source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and ownership of durable goods, were coded as binary indicators and combined through principal component analysis (PCA) [68].

Data collection, quality control, and data collectors

A household survey linked with health facility data collection was conducted for this study. Health facility data were needed for obtaining participant identity details, as samples were selected from medical records (delivery registers) at health facilities. Data collection began at the health centers level by reviewing the birth record to collect the women’s identity details. Having the identity details, data collectors visited households where the sampled women belonged to conduct the household-level survey. The data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from the previous study [52].

Eleven data collectors and two supervisors were involved in the data collection. The recruited data collectors included professionals with expertise in health officers, midwives, nurses and Health Information Technicians (HIT). The data collectors were trained for three days regarding methodologies, the study objectives, and ethical considerations. The principal investigator and supervisors closely overseen the entire data collection process.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered into Epi-Data v-3.1 software and analyzed using Stata − 17. Categorical women’s sociodemographic variables were expressed as frequencies and proportions. The mean and standard deviation were computed for continuous variables. The baseline comparisons of women’s sociodemographic variables between the intervention and comparator districts was done, utilizing Pearson’s chi-squared tests for categorical variables and a two-sample independent t-test for continuous variables. The proportion of optimal and quality ANC was calculated, and comparisons were made between the intervention and comparator groups across the pre-post period.

A robust Poisson regression-based Difference-in-Differences (DID) model was employed to compare outcome variable differences (optimal ANC and quality ANC) between intervention and comparator districts before and after the PBNI intervention [69–72]. A robust Poisson model, considered an alternative for estimating risk ratios for binary outcomes [73, 74], has been cited for providing less biased estimates compared to other models for binary outcomes [75]. Most of the women were Orthodox Christians (97.3%), married (91.7%), and were resided in rural areas (84.4%). Baseline comparison between the study groups by sociodemographic characteristics

When we compared the baseline distribution of women’s characteristics between a comparator and an intervention group using the Pearson Chi-square, we found no significant differences between the two groups for the following variables: age of women, marital status, place of residence, occupation for both women and their husbands, wealth quantile, and birth interval.

However, the Pearson Chi-square test showed significant baseline differences between the two groups for the remaining variables, including women’s education status, husbands’ education status, women’s religion, birth order, pregnancy intendedness, previous adverse pregnancy outcomes, previous pregnancy-related complications, and knowledge of danger signs.

Regarding this study’s outcome variables, while optimal ANC distribution was similar between the intervention and comparator groups at the baseline phase, a statistically significant difference was observed for quality ANC utilization. Approximately 22.5% of women in the intervention group attended quality ANC compared to 30.0% in the comparator group (Table 4).

The magnitude of optimal and quality ANC visits across the baseline and endline study period

The study revealed that during the baseline period, the percentage of women with optimal ANC visits was higher in the comparator district 44.4%(95%CI, 39.8, 49.1) compared to the intervention district 41.5%(95%CI, 37. 46.1). At the endline period, the intervention district showed a decrease in attendance of optimal ANC visits 38.9%(95%CI,34.4, 43.5), while the comparator group showed an increase 46.0%(95%CI, 41.3, 50.7).

Regarding quality ANC visits, the comparator group had higher proportion during baseline 30.3%(95%CI, 26.1,34.7), compared to the intervention group 22.5%(95%CI, 18.9, 26.6.) Both groups showed an increase in the proportion of quality ANC visits from baseline to end line, with the intervention group showing a higher percentage 36.4% (95%CI,32.0, 41.0), compared to 32% (95%CI, 27.5,36.3) in the comparator group (Fig. 3).

Effect of PBNI on attendance of quality ANC

In the multivariable Poisson regression, women from the intervention group showed a 27% lower rate of quality ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58–0.91) compared to women in the comparator district. There was no significant difference between the baseline and end-line periods observed in the rate of quality ANC utilization (adjusted IRR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.84–1.26). However, the estimated effect of the PBNI intervention by the interaction term revealed that the PBNI intervention significantly increased the rate of quality ANC attendance by 1.44 times (adjusted IRR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.07–1.96).

Other than the intervention, the rate of quality ANC visits was statistically significantly associated with the women’s place of residence, birth interval, and husbands’ education. Women living in rural areas have a 1.43 times higher rate of quality ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.12–1.83) than those living in urban areas. Likewise, women whose husbands have primary education have a 1.30 times higher likelihood of quality ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.55) compared to women whose husbands have no education. Women who had birth intervals ranging from 24 to 33 months were found to have a 1.59-fold higher chance of utilizing quality ANC (adjusted IRR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.16–2.19), compared to those whose birth intervals were less than 24 months (Table 5).

Effect of PBNI on attendance of optimal ANC

Based on the results of a multivariable Poisson regression, women in the intervention district did not show a statistically significant difference in the rate of optimal ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 0.97 [0.84, 1.14]) when compared to women from the comparator district. Similarly, when comparing the endline phase to the baseline, no statistically significant difference was found in the rate of optimal ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 1.07 [0.92, 1.29]). Regarding the PBNI intervention’s effect, a multivariable Poisson regression model showed no significant effect on optimal ANC attendance rates (adjusted IRR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.68–1.05).

Among women’s characteristics analyzed in the adjusted poison regression model, women’s age, education, occupation, Previous Pregnancy complications, and knowledge on danger signs were statistically significantly associated with the rate of optimal ANC attendance. Women with primary education showed near twofold higher rate of ANC visits (adjusted IRR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.54–2.03) compared to women with no education, controlling for other variables. Likewise, women with higher education showed a 1.98 times higher rate of optimal ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 1.98, 95% CI: 1.55–2.52) compared to women with no education. Regarding occupation, women engaged in housework showed a 37% lower rate of optimal ANC attendance (adjusted IRR: 0.63,95% CI: 0.52–0.77) than employed women. Similarly, farmer women (adjusted IRR:0.72,95% CI: 0.58–0.88) and women in other occupations (adjusted IRR:0.58,95% CI: 0.44–0.76) were found to have significantly lower rates of optimal ANC attendance, compared to employed women. The rate of optimal ANC visits increased by 1.21 times among women who previously experienced at least one pregnancy-related complication (adjusted IRR:1.21,95% CI: 1.04–1.39) compared to women who did not experience it. Discussions

In the current study, the PBNI intervention, primarily deployed to improve health facilities’ data quality and use, was evaluated for effectiveness in improving quality and optimal ANC. The findings showed that the PBNI intervention led to a significant increase in the quality of ANC visits at healthcare facilities, as indicated by statistically significant associations in adjusted analyses. However, there was no statistically significant difference in optimal ANC attendance rates.

The PBNI intervention was found to be particularly effective in improving the provision of recommended basic ANC care procedures. Some previous research that explored comparable aspects of ANC quality (measured by adherence to recommended care during ANC) also reported positive outcomes associated with using non-financial incentives. For instance, a study conducted in El Salvador showed that nonfinancial incentives improved adherence to recommended care during ANC [35]. Likewise, an Indian study found significant improvements in recommended pregnancy care associated with non-financial incentives [36]. Non-financial incentives were effective in improving the quality of ANC despite variations in the target groups among the studies. While the Salvadoran and Indian studies focused on incentivizing community health workers, our study offered incentives to healthcare providers at health facilities. The consistency of findings across the studies indicates the adaptability of non-financial incentives to various healthcare settings. It demonstrates non-financial incentives’ capability to effectively motivate healthcare providers at different healthcare system levels to deliver quality care during ANC. However, our findings differ from a previous study conducted in African countries (specifically, Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Tanzania), where it was reported that non-financial incentives were not significantly linked to the quality of ANC [76]. The differences in the components of the incentive packages used in our study compared to the African research may explain these differing results. In contrast to the current study, which provided various non-cash incentives such as flash disks, smartphones, desktop computers, power banks, scholarship opportunity and certificates of recognition, the African study employed non-financial incentives comprise provision of regular performance feedback alongside the Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) [76, 77]. Existing evidence indicates that a combination of non-cash incentives can effectively motivate healthcare providers and improve team coordination, leading to better healthcare quality [31, 35, 36, 78–81]. Therefore, organizations and policymakers can consider designing the mix of non-cash incentive items in their non-financial incentive programs design.

The PBNI in the current study effectively improved the quality of ANC without direct ties to performance indicators, as it was primarily implemented to improve the data quality and information use in the intervention district. The use of performance indicators has been a topic of debate in healthcare. Performance measures, considered vital for rewarding best performers, can also have unintended consequences [82–84]. According to some research, performance measures may encourage healthcare providers on specific quality dimensions while neglecting other essential elements [85, 86]. Our current study’s findings challenge the conventional belief that PBI are the primary drivers of healthcare provider performance. Some empirical evidence exists that supports the effectiveness of incentives that are not tied to specific performance measures. In a Tanzanian study, healthcare providers’ performance was significantly improved in terms of adhering to protocols when unconditional non-financial incentives were compared to PBI [87]. Besides, some experts in the field of medical ethics endorse unconditional non-financial incentives, emphasizing healthcare professionals’ ability to deliver quality healthcare independently of external conditions [88, 89]. For healthcare organizations and policymakers, the finding of our study suggests the importance of considering unconditioned non-financial incentives as a viable means to strengthen healthcare providers’ intrinsic motivation for improving healthcare quality. Nonetheless, it remains essential for future research to recognize the necessity of further empirical investigations to assess unconditioned non-financial effectiveness across a range of healthcare contexts.

As per this study’s literature review, non-financial incentives such as PBNI, aimed at improving optimal and quality ANC, have been scarcely explored in prior research. In resource-limited settings, prior efforts to enhance ANC quality and coverage mainly focused on P4P. The literature contains contradictory results concerning the effects of P4P. Regarding quality ANC visits, some previous studies have documented positive results of P4P, which coincide with our current research findings [90–93]. However, according to some other studies, the P4P did not improve the quality of recommended elements of ANC [94–96]. The current study’s design may account for the observed difference. Our research introduced incentives at multiple levels (individual, team, and healthcare facility), different from the predominantly facility-focused incentive approach employed in previous studies. Some researchers in the field emphasize the influence of incentive design on its effectiveness, encouraging comprehensive schemes encompassing different structural levels of healthcare organizations [97–100]. Such an approach not only motivates individual performance but also recognized to fosters better care coordination among teams and aligns with the broader priorities of the organization [101, 102].

In this study, the PBNI intervention was found to be not statistically significantly associated with the rate of optimal ANC attendance. This result aligns with previous research papers revealing the limited effectiveness of incentive schemes in increasing the chance of women receiving optimal ANC visits. According to studies from Afghanistan, Malawi, Cameroon, and Rwanda no significant differences in ANC coverage were observed linked to incentive intervention [29, 103–105]. However, the finding of the present study contradicts some prior studies in Burkina Faso, Cambodia, and Nigeria, indicating significant increases in ANC visit rates attributable to incentives interventions [106–108]. The relatively short intervention period in the current study may account for the PBNI’s failure to positively influence the rate of optimal ANC attendance. In contrast, previous studies that reported a statistically significant effect of incentives on optimal ANC4 coverage had longer intervention periods, such as three years in a study conducted in Burkina Faso, one year in a Cambodian study, and five years in a Nigerian study. Besides, the data source among the studies could have influenced the discrepancies in findings between our study and prior research. While the present study relied on data reported by mothers in household surveys, previous studies used administrative data. To enrich the understanding of non-financial incentives’ effectiveness, future research needs to consider triangulating administrative and population-based data sources [10, 109–113].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study used a nonequivalent control-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design to evaluate the effect of PBNI on ANC quality and optimal visits. Although not as rigorous as a randomized controlled trial, this design provides stronger internal validity than other quasi-experimental approaches that lack comparator groups or baseline data [114, 115]. Comparing changes over time between the intervention and comparison districts allowed the effect of the PBNI to be distinguished from wider trends in service performance.

To minimize selection bias, the comparison district (Tach-Armachiho) was selected for its close similarity to the intervention district (Wegera) in terms of context, service organization, and participation in the Ministry of Health’s CBMP for RHIS strengthening. The study was sufficiently powered: sample-size assumptions were set at a 90% power level to detect even small effects; 1766 women were enrolled through random sampling. The analysis employed robust Poisson regression within a DiD framework, which adjusted for measured covariates and accounted for clustering effects (using robust standard errors in the model). These features reduce bias from time-invariant unobserved factors and increase confidence in the estimated intervention effect [69, 74].

Several steps were taken to ensure reliable data collection. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face surveys with mothers, and field supervisors, including the principal investigator, monitored implementation daily to maintain data quality. The study was implemented under routine program conditions, increasing its real-world relevance and policy value. By focusing on non-financial, performance-based incentives, it contributes new evidence to a field dominated by financial incentive models such as P4P.

Some limitations should be noted. Because the intervention was delivered through government structures at the facility level, randomization was not feasible. Although the DiD approach controls unchanging differences, unmeasured factors could still exist. The study measured pertinent variables feasible within its scope and budget. Security problems in nearby regions limited the participation of two planned facilities, and the reliance on mothers’ recall may have introduced recall biases, though most recent births were prioritized to minimize this concern.

While statistical inference applies to the study population, the findings are also relevant to other primary-health-care settings in Ethiopia that are implementing the CBMP initiatives as part of improving health service quality through strengthening RHIS. Conducting the intervention through existing government systems strengthens its credibility and potential for wider adoption. Future studies should test similar approaches using a more robust design, such as cluster-randomized designs, to assess scalability and long-term sustainability.

Implications of the study

The finding that the PBNI intervention improved the quality of ANC but did not significantly change the optimal number of ANC visits carries several important implications.

First, system-level behavioral interventions such as PBNI can strengthen provider motivation and reinforce adherence to evidence-based service standards, thereby improving the quality of maternal health care delivered through existing structures. Consistent with theoretical and prior empirical evidence [31, 44, 45], interventions that reinforce recognition, teamwork, and self-efficacy among providers can help overcome motivational barriers associated with heavy workload and limited resources. PBNI can strengthen health workers’ self-esteem and confidence in maintaining quality documentation and service delivery, even in busy environments where staff shortages and competing demands are common across LMICs [116].

However, improving coverage and continuity of care requires more than provider-side incentives. Women’s early initiation and sustained attendance across pregnancy depend on socio-cultural and accessibility factors that lie beyond the influence of supply-side interventions alone [7, 8]. The absence of an effect on optimal visits, therefore, underscores the need to complement PBNI with demand-side strategies and to sustain implementation over a longer period to achieve lasting behavioral changes among clients.

Second, these results highlight the value of aligning incentive-based interventions with the broader RHIS agenda. Integrating PBNI within RHIS-strengthening initiatives, such as leadership engagement, supportive supervision, and community-linked data-use platforms, can translate motivational gains into improved service outcomes [117]. The study contributes to the growing recognition that non-financial incentives, when properly designed and linked to system performance, can be feasible and impactful schemes in motivating health-care providers and improving quality in resource-limited settings [118].

Finally, this research extends the evidence base beyond earlier PBNI studies that focused primarily on RHIS outputs (e.g., data quality and use) [46, 119], by assessing the downstream effects on service-delivery outcomes such as ANC quality. This step forward illustrates the potential of aligning RHIS investments with outcome-level goals to ensure that data-driven accountability mechanisms contribute tangibly to maternal and newborn health gains in Ethiopia and similar low-resource settings.

Conclusions

This quasi-experimental study demonstrated that PBNI significantly improved the quality of ANC care provided in health centers, even though it did not increase the optimal number of ANC visits. These findings indicate that non-financial incentives can motivate providers and help them follow care standards, thereby enhancing maternal health services within current systems. The results add new evidence from Ethiopia to a literature mainly focused on financial incentive models, showing that strengthening the RHIS through system-level behavioral interventions can effectively promote high-quality maternal care. The observed improvements in ANC quality suggest that a substantial program effect is achievable in similar resource-limited settings with low ANC quality. The findings emphasize the potential of incorporating structured, non-financial incentives into Ethiopia’s ongoing health system initiatives, such as the information revolution and the CBMP, to maintain motivation among frontline providers and ensure the delivery of high-quality, evidence-based care. Future research should include longer follow-up periods and more rigorous experimental designs to evaluate the sustainability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness of non-financial incentive models across wider maternal and child health settings.
