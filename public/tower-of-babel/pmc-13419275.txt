High‐Temporal‐Resolution Monitoring of Creatine and Phosphocreatine Dynamics in Skeletal Muscle Using Ultrafast Z‐Spectroscopy (UFZ) CEST MRI at 3T
Authors: Licheng Ju, T Jake Samuel, Michael Schär, Kexin Wang, Yihan Wu, Robert G Weiss, Jiadi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo evaluate the feasibility of High‐Temporal‐Resolution (HTR) Ultrafast Z‐spectrum (UFZ) CEST MRI for monitoring the dynamic fluctuations of phosphocreatine (PCr) and creatine (Cr) in human skeletal muscle (SM) at 3 T during and after exercise.MethodsHTR UFZ‐CEST MRI of SM was achieved using a 1D spatial and spectral encoding with a 2D turbo spin‐echo readout. Five healthy subjects performed in‐magnet plantar flexion exercise, and dynamic changes in Amide, PCr, and partial PCr‐mixed creatine (Cr+) CEST signals were quantified using the Polynomial and Lorentzian Line‐shape Fitting (PLOF) method. This approach enables monitoring of PCr/Cr CEST dynamics throughout exercise and recovery.ResultsDynamic CEST contrasts with 7.6 s temporal resolution revealed PCr depletion (−76.9% ± 17.7%) and concomitant Cr elevation (469.0% ± 116.3%) during exercise, followed by exponential PCr recovery and Cr decay during recovery. The post‐exercise recovery constants for Cr and PCr CEST were 61.5 ± 45.6 s and 73.3 ± 66.1 s, respectively. Using two‐step Bloch‐McConnell simulation based on the experimental lineshape and CEST changes, we estimated an exchange rate of 150 ± 70 s−1 for CrCEST and 185 ± 105 s−1 for PCrCEST in the resting state.ConclusionThese results establish UFZ‐CEST as a rapid, noninvasive method for mapping Cr/PCr dynamics in human SM and provide a foundation for clinical assessment of mitochondrial function.Keywords: chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST), creatine (Cr), muscle, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), phosphocreatine (PCr), polynomial and Lorentzian line‐shape fitting (PLOF), ultrafast Z‐spectroscopy (UFZ)

Introduction

The creatine (Cr) and phosphocreatine (PCr) shuttle system plays a critical role in skeletal muscle by serving as a rapid energy buffer to support muscle contraction. The dynamic reversible interconversion between Cr and PCr during and post exercise provides valuable insight into mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and energy metabolism [1]. Phosphorus‐31 magnetic resonance spectroscopy (31P MRS) is a well‐established and highly specific technique for evaluating high‐energy phosphate metabolites such as ATP and PCr [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite its inherent strengths, 31P MRS is rarely implemented in routine clinical practice. This is primarily due to its relatively low sensitivity compared to proton‐based MRI techniques and the relatively limited number of MR systems with specialized 31P RF coils and dedicated transmit‐receive hardware worldwide. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H MRS), on the other hand, is more widely accessible and can measure total creatine (tCr), which comprises both Cr and PCr. However, 1H MRS lacks the spectral resolution necessary to reliably separate Cr from PCr in muscle at clinical field strengths, making it challenging to quantify dynamic changes between these two metabolites individually [11, 12]. As a result, there remains a strong need for high‐resolution, non‐invasive, and clinically translatable MRI method for the assessment of Cr and PCr dynamics in skeletal muscle.

Chemical Exchange Saturation Transfer (CEST) MRI has emerged as a highly sensitive technique for detecting Cr [13, 14, 15, 16, 17] and PCr [13, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22] in vivo without the need for specialized 31P MRS hardware. However, isolating and quantifying Cr and PCr CEST signals remains challenging due to the crowded nature of the in vivo Z‐spectrum [23]. Early CrCEST studies relied heavily on the magnetization transfer ratio asymmetry (MTRasym) approach, which failed to eliminate strong asymmetric background contributions, including magnetization transfer contrast (MTC) and relayed Nuclear Overhauser Effects (rNOEs) [24, 25], as well as separating PCr and Cr CEST. To overcome these limitations, the Polynomial and Lorentzian Line‐shape Fitting (PLOF) method was developed [19, 20, 26, 27]. The PLOF approach can be considered a form of baseline correction similar to those used in NMR spectroscopy; by combining polynomial and Lorentzian functions, the algorithm removes broad background components from the CEST spectra, including MTC, NOE, and fast‐exchanging CEST, while preserving sharp, slow‐exchanging peaks [28, 29, 30]. These include the amide signal at 3.5 ppm, 2.6 ppm associated with PCr and slow‐exchanging amines, and the 2.0 ppm peak, which comprises Cr/PCr CEST and protein arginine residues (ArgCEST). PLOF enables specific quantification of Cr and PCr CEST signals [19, 20, 26, 27]. Conventionally, CrCEST was not considered feasible for extraction with PLOF at 3 T due to the rapid exchange rate (˜1000 s−1) observed in Cr phantom studies [13, 31]. However, recent research indicates that the in vivo CrCEST exchange rate is approximately 270 s−1 in the brain [27, 32, 33] and less than 150 s−1 in skeletal muscle [34]. Based on these findings, the PLOF method has been applied to simultaneously extract PCr/CrCEST signals in skeletal muscle at 3 T [34, 35].

Despite improvements in spectral quantification, temporal resolution remains a critical limitation. The PCr‐Cr exchange reaction is highly dynamic, particularly in response to exercise [36, 37, 38, 39]. Capturing these rapid changes requires fast and continuous imaging, which traditional CEST approaches struggle to achieve. To address the time constraint, Ultrafast Z‐Spectroscopy (UFZ) CEST MRI was introduced [40, 41, 42]. UFZ achieves rapid Z‐spectrum acquisition by spatially encoding the spectral dimension using a gradient applied simultaneously with the saturation pulse. The 2D UFZ method on muscle can acquire a 2D CEST map in approximately 50 s using a TSE readout [35]. However, this is still insufficient to capture the full dynamics of PCr/Cr changes during and immediately after exercise. To overcome these limitations, our objective is to further accelerate the acquisition speed by implementing a 1D UFZ‐CEST approach to achieve a temporal resolution of less than 10 s. This fast acquisition will enable us to observe both the depletion phase of PCr during exercise and its recovery post‐exercise, offering a more detailed picture of muscle bioenergetics. The Z‐spectra dynamics captured by the 1D UFZ‐CEST method also enable the extraction of key CEST properties, including the exchange rates of PCr and Cr in skeletal muscle.

Methods

MRI Experiments

All human studies were approved by the Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board and all participants (three male and two female healthy subjects, aged between 23 and 27 years old) provided written informed consent. A 3 T Philips Achieva MRI system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) and a two‐piece Sense XL Torso coil (Philips Healthcare) were used for all CEST experiments.

An optimized continuous wave saturation pulse with 0.6 μT amplitude and 2 s duration was applied according to a previous study [34]. Strength of the gradient during the saturation pulse of the UFZ module was set to 1.0 mT/m to achieve a Z‐spectral range of about 13 ppm (−3 to 10 ppm relative to the water frequency) across 40 mm. In 1D UFZ, the UFZ module gradient is oriented along the readout direction of a single slice acquisition. The single‐slice field of view (FOV, 30 × 40 mm2, slice thickness 12 mm) was positioned within the medial gastrocnemius (MG). The acquisition matrix was set as 32 × 100 to achieve 100 offsets for the Z‐spectrum. Two sets of dynamic scans with two acquisitions each (center frequencies: [200, −3] ppm, [200, 3] ppm) were acquired before the in‐magnet plantar flexion exercise (PFE) study under identical gradient and FOV specifications for the offset calibration [35]. Unsaturated spectra applying off‐resonance saturation centred at 200 ppm (S0) and saturated spectra with saturation frequency centred at 3 ppm (S) were alternately acquired during the exercise scan. The single‐shot TSE readout parameters were TR = 3.8 s, TE = 7.3 ms, flip angle 90o and refocusing angle 120o, spectral presaturation with inversion recovery (SPIR) fat suppression was used. Fold‐over suppression was realized via reduced FOV in phase‐encoding direction (X direction) by applying the excitation pulse (X direction) orthogonal to the slice selective refocusing pulses (Y direction) [43]. Total time for each dynamic was 3.8 s.

All subjects underwent graded PFE to volitional fatigue followed by post exercise recovery. Subjects were positioned half seated, feet first in the MRI system with the calf muscle of one leg sandwiched in the two‐piece coil. To minimize motion and exercise contributions from muscle other than the calf muscle, the exercising leg was fixed to the table using soft straps. The pulley system is a custom‐built MR compatible device consisting of a foot pedal to lift an adjustable weight [37]. The graded PFE protocol starts with lifting a 0.9 kg weight for 60.8 s (16 dynamic scans), then pauses for 15.2 s (4 dynamic scans) acquiring CEST data without motion. This process is repeated by adding weight in the later stage until self‐reported fatigue. Post‐exercise recovery is monitored for 15.8 min (250 dynamics). Detailed procedures can be found (Table S1). 1D UFZ sequence is running throughout the process by dynamic scans. The PFE exercise requires a fixed motion range of 5.08 cm at a rate of 1 Hz.

Additionally, conventional CEST contrasts in a single slice were acquired with single‐shot TSE readout and 2 s long 0.6 μT presaturation. 54 offsets (−2.2 to 8 ppm) were swept within 2 min before and post exercise. High‐resolution T 2 weighted images were collected for anatomical referencing using TSE sequence with a resolution of 0.44 × 0.44 × 4.0 mm3. T 1 maps on human legs were acquired during rest using the Dual Flip Angle (DFA) method as detailed previously [34].

Data Analysis

All MRI results were analyzed using in‐house developed MATLAB scripts (The MathWorks, R2023a, Natick, MA). Prior to additional processing, Rician noise corrections were applied [33, 34]. A B0 correction was implemented through water saturation shift referencing (WASSR), under the assumption that the entire tissue slice was homogeneous [44]. The Z‐spectrum underwent linear interpolation at increments of 0.01 ppm. The CEST signal was extracted using the PLOF method [23] across the Z‐spectrum from 0.5 to 7.5 ppm. Background fitting was carried out for the ranges of 0.5–1.5 ppm and 5–7.5 ppm. The extracted CEST signal, ΔZ, was determined by subtracting the fitted background from the observed Z value. Then, Lorentzian functions were utilized for the AmideCEST, PCrCEST, and PCr mixed CrCEST (Cr+CEST) peaks located at 3.5 ppm, 2.6 ppm, and 2.0 ppm, respectively.

Paired t‐tests were used to evaluate the significance of CEST contrast differences between rest state and initial recovery point. A significance level threshold of p = 0.05 was used.

PCr and Cr CEST Exchange Rate Estimation

A two‐step Bloch‐McConnell (BM) fitting procedure was used to evaluate the exchange rates of PCr and Cr in muscle [27, 33, 34]. To minimize interference from protein signals in the measured CEST contrasts, we aimed to align the BM simulated CEST differences with the observed CEST changes between the initial recovery point and the resting state that is, ∆CrCEST and ∆PCrCEST, to estimate the exchange rate. In the first step, we included the water and magnetization transfer contrast (MTC) pools to model the background, with the MTC pool representing a combination of macromolecules and other CEST components. For this study, a Super‐Lorentzian line shape was used to describe the mixed MTC and CEST background. During background fitting, T1water was fixed as the mean T 1 value for the medial gastrocnemius from the T 1 map, MTC offset was set at −3.5 ppm, while T2water (30–100 ms), T2MTC (10–30 μs), and fMTC (1%–100%) were optimized to align with the averaged experimental Z‐spectrum (n = 5). The CEST acquisition parameters were established with B1 at 0.6 μT and a saturation time of 2 s. Two sets of background parameters were obtained for the resting state and the initial recovery point, respectively.

In the second step, we incorporated the PCr or Cr pool for determing the ∆CrCEST and ∆PCrCEST, while maintaining the water and MTC parameters. At rest, the PCr concentration is assumed as 25 mM, and 10 mM for Cr [37]. At the initial recovery point, based on the 31P MRS research using the same exercise protocol [37], it is assumed that 20 mM PCr has converted to Cr, that is, PCr is 5 mM, and Cr is 30 mM. During simulation, the exchange rate of Cr (kexCr) or PCr (kexPCr) was varied within a range of 20–1000 s−1 at 10 s−1 interval, while T1Cr or T1PCr was fixed at 1.8 s, and T2Cr or T2PCr was fixed at 60 ms, values which approximate the T 1/T 2 of water. The same 31P MRS study indicated that the current PFE protocol induces a pH reduction of 0.2 in healthy subjects [37]. Results

Dynamic Changes of CEST Contrasts Before, During and Post Exercise

In the 1D UFZ‐CEST approach, each pair of unsaturated (S0) and saturated (S) spectra acquired over two dynamics was used to construct a single Z‐spectrum for extracting CEST contrasts, resulting in a temporal resolution of 7.6 s. This continuous scan enabled the extraction of dynamic changes in AmideCEST, PCrCEST, and Cr+CEST during the resting state, during the pause in physical exercise, and during the post‐exercise recovery phase. The FOV was positioned within the MG muscle, as illustrated in Figure 1A. The mean T 1 value of the MG region was employed for CEST extraction using the PLOF method. The extracted CEST contrasts were averaged over the entire FOV, assuming the tissues were homogeneous.

Figure 1B–D display representative dynamics of Cr, Amide, and PCr CEST from a typical subject. In contrast to the relatively constant levels of AmideCEST throughout the entire process, both Cr and PCr CEST show significant changes. During exercise, PCr is converted to Cr, leading to a depletion of PCrCEST and an increase in CrCEST, a trend clearly illustrated in Figure 1B,D. In the post‐exercise recovery phase, the exponential decay of CrCEST and the recovery of PCrCEST can also be observed in the dynamic results. To validate the ability of the PLOF approach to reliably extract ∆CrCEST and ∆PCrCEST, we compared BM simulations with PLOF processing (Figure S6). The results indicate that PLOF can accurately determine ∆CrCEST and ∆PCrCEST for exchange rates below 300 s−1. At higher exchange rates, PLOF tends to underestimate the CEST signal. Because using ∆CrCEST alone is insufficient to distinguish between exchange rates above 500 s−1 and those below 200 s−1, we performed Z‐spectrum lineshape simulations across various exchange rates for both Cr and PCr (Figure S7). At exchange rates exceeding 500 s−1, the predicted lineshapes broaden significantly; as this degree of broadening is inconsistent with our experimental observations (Figure 2B), exchange rates in that range were excluded. This further explains why the PLOF method underestimates the signal at high exchange rates: as a lineshape‐fitting approach, it tends to truncate CEST peaks that have undergone significant exchange‐related broadening.

Mitochondrial Function Evaluation

Figure 3A shows AmideCEST, Cr+CEST, CrCEST, and PCrCEST at rest and at the initial recovery point, revealing significant post‐exercise changes for Cr+/Cr/PCr CEST with p values of 0.012, 0.0045 and 0.0002, respectively. AmideCEST shows non‐significant change with p value of 0.77. The differences between initial recovery and rest are summarized in Figure 3B. Cr+CEST and CrCEST increased markedly after exercise (0.0129 ± 0.0066 and 0.0173 ± 0.0067, respectively), while PCrCEST decreased (−0.0145 ± 0.0024). AmideCEST remains relatively constant (−0.0008 ± 0.0060).

Post‐exercise recovery time constants have been well established as indicators of OXPHOS [38, 50]. These recovery time constants were derived from various CEST contrasts using a second‐order exponential equation [35]. All contrasts were normalized before the recovery time fitting. As shown in Figure 3C, the time constants for Cr+, Cr, and PCr CEST were 56.7 ± 33.0 s, 61.5 ± 45.6 s, and 73.3 ± 66.1 s, respectively. Discussion

This study demonstrates that 1D UFZ CEST can achieve 7.6 s temporal resolution in skeletal muscle CEST imaging by sacrificing one spatial readout dimension, enabling continuous monitoring of Cr and PCr dynamics during and after exercise. Compared with conventional high spectrum resolution CEST, which typically requires about 5 min per Z‐spectrum, UFZ significantly reduces acquisition time while preserving sensitivity to dynamic metabolic changes. The observed PCr depletion and Cr accumulation during PFE, followed by their reciprocal recovery kinetics, are consistent with the prior 31P MRS findings [37, 38, 50].

Investigations modulating PCr and Cr concentrations via euthanasia estimated CrCEST exchange rates between 45.5 and 150.7 s−1 in mice [34]. However, the exchange rate in human skeletal muscle has remained elusive until now. The primary obstacle in determining this rate is that the CEST peak at 2 ppm is a composite signal, containing contributions from both PCr and Cr. In this study, our ability to capture rapid Z‐spectra during PFE, a state where PCr is rapidly converted to Cr, enabled the direct and simultaneous estimation of both PCr and CrCEST exchange rates. We observed an unexpectedly low CrCEST exchange rate of 150 ± 70 s−1 at resting state. This finding provides a physiological explanation for why the CrCEST signal remains persistently weak despite the substantial increase in Cr concentration (20 mM increment) during PFE [37]. For comparison, PCrCEST exchange rates are 185 ± 105 s−1 at 2.6 ppm, which is lower than 260–366 s−1 determined in phantoms with pH 7.3 [18, 20]. The values are consistent with established skeletal muscle studies in animals (134 s−1) [51] and humans (160 s−1) [18].

Recent literature has suggested a higher rate of ˜500 s−1 for CrCEST in muscle [52]. However, our simulations indicated that such a fast exchange would produce a CrCEST signal substantially broader than what we observed (Figure S7). In contrast, our data consistently yielded a narrow 2 ppm peak (Figure 2A,B), which is incompatible with higher rate predictions. While the exact reasons for such elevated rate estimations require further examination, the limitations of previous multi‐pool fitting models offer a potential explanation. The validation studies in mice reveal that the CrCEST peak is modest, with the 2 ppm signal being heavily influenced by protein aromatic NOE contributions [20, 34]. The inability of standard multi‐pool fitting to remove these NOE signals results in a systematic overestimation of CrCEST magnitude and exchange rates.

Post‐exercise recovery times for Cr and PCr were slightly different (61.5 ± 45.6 s vs. 73.3 ± 66.1 s, respectively). This variation may stem from the fact that T 1 and T 2 changes during the post‐exercise period affect CrCEST and PCrCEST differently, particularly as the Cr signal resides closer to the water resonance. The precise cause remains to be determined through further study.

The UFZ method requires a degree of tissue homogeneity. While this condition is satisfied in the muscle using 40 mm FOV, it may be violated when using a larger FOV or imaging other tissue. UFZ‐CEST enables high temporal resolution with full Z‐spectrum acquisition, significantly outperforming traditional CEST methods in speed. However, this is achieved by sacrificing spatial resolution in one dimension and necessitates tissue homogeneity along the spectral encoding direction. Other approaches have sought high temporal resolution by sampling fewer offsets—essentially trading spectral resolution for speed—as seen in average saturation efficiency filter (ASEF) CEST [53] and double saturation power (DSP) CEST [54]. Due to the limited number of offsets in these methods, lineshape fitting (such as PLOF) cannot be utilized, making it difficult to resolve overlapping amide and PCr/Cr CEST signals.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a 1D UFZ CEST MRI method at 3 T that enables HTR monitoring of Cr and PCr dynamics in skeletal muscle. This technique captures rapid Z‐spectral changes during exercise and recovery, providing in vivo exchange rate estimates for PCr and Cr CEST. Furthermore, it yields recovery rates post PFE similar to 31P MRS. Consequently, UFZ‐CEST offers a clinically translatable tool for assessing muscle bioenergetics on standard clinical MRI scanners.
