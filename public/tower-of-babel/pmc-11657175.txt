Difficulty in financing fracture care in south-western Nigeria: a prospective cohort study of predictors and recommendations for improvement
Authors: Stephen Adesope Adesina, Isaac Olusayo Amole, Adepeju Olatayo Adegoke, Akinsola Idowu Akinwumi, Imri Goodness Adefokun, Ehimen Oluwadamilare Odekhiran, Olusola Tunde Ekunnrin, Simeon Ayorinde Ojo, Adewumi Ojeniyi Durodola, Olufemi Timothy Awotunde, Innocent Chiedu Ikem, Samuel Uwale Eyesan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone fractures remain a significant global public health issue despite preventive measures, leading to substantial health and economic consequences. Effective treatment options are difficult to access in most sub-Saharan African countries, leading to reliance on unqualified practitioners and resulting in serious complications that worsen poverty. In Nigeria, the main challenge is the need for out-of-pocket payment for healthcare, which is the primary method of health financing. However, anecdotal evidence suggests that some patients find it easier to finance their fracture care than others. This study aimed to identify the factors that predict difficult fracture-care financing among patients treated operatively for long-bone fractures (LBFs) in southwestern Nigeria.MethodsThe study collected socio-demographic and clinical data on 690 consecutive adult patients with LBFs treated with SIGN nails between July 2014 and June 2024 at a mission teaching hospital in southwestern Nigeria. A literature review and preliminary interviews were conducted with patients, caregivers, and hospital staff to understand potential risk factors for difficult fracture-care financing. The patients were divided into two cohorts – easy fracture-care financing (EF) and difficult fracture-care financing (DF) – based on their promptness in paying the in-patient care bills. Potential risk factors for DF identified in the initial univariate analysis were entered as covariates into a binary logistic regression to determine the significant predictors of DF.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 45.1 years (SD = 17.52). The DF cohort comprised 321 (46.5%) patients. Significant predictors of DF included younger age (p = 0.018), male gender (p = 0.002), lack of formal education or low level of education (p < 0.001), residence in the hospital city (p < 0.001), absence of health insurance (p = 0.013), lack of extended family support (p < 0.001), previous traditional bone setting treatment (p = 0.005), concomitant injury (p = 0.017), and a post-operative hospital stay longer than one week (p = 0.036).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that improving people’s socioeconomic status through job creation, women’s empowerment, reduction of gender inequality, higher education, comprehensive health insurance coverage, and stronger family bonds, as well as preventive strategies to reduce the occurrence and severity of injuries, can improve fracture-care financing in low-resource settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20991-y.

Background

Bone fractures are a significant global public health issue, leading to substantial health and economic consequences for individuals, families, and societies [1]. Long-bone fractures (LBFs) have reached epidemic levels worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due mainly to road traffic crashes (RTCs) [2]. These countries bear 80–90% of the global trauma burden, with an estimated 80% of severe fractures and 90% of global deaths from road traffic injuries [3–5]. Various factors contribute to this, including the increasing use of secondhand, inadequately maintained vehicles, poorly constructed road networks, alcohol use, and ineffective traffic regulations and enforcement [3, 5].

Injuries occur regularly, irrespective of an organised and accessible health system to handle them [6]. Primary preventive measures, such as building robust road networks and reducing drunk driving, as well as secondary prevention through crash helmets, safety belts, and better vehicle maintenance, are being promoted. However, despite these preventive measures, some fractures will still occur, necessitating curative injury management [7]. Therefore, tertiary prevention, such as improved emergency services, well-equipped health facilities, and a larger number of trained personnel, is crucial. Unfortunately, many sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries struggle to effectively provide fracture prevention at a pace that matches the rate of orthopaedic injuries [3–5].

A large portion of the population in SSA countries cannot access or afford modern fracture care [8, 9]. As a result, many people turn to traditional bone setters (TBS) and unqualified practitioners, leading to high mortality rates and long-term health issues [10–12], exacerbating poverty in these areas [6, 13]. On the other hand, high-income countries have made significant progress in treating fractures through advanced medical, surgical, anaesthesia, and rehabilitative services [14]. One of the main obstacles to accessing modern fracture care in LMICs is the financial burden, including costs for transportation, medical implants, medications, surgical procedures, and rehabilitation services [8, 15, 16]. The quality of healthcare financing is a measure of a country’s overall health system functioning. A sustainable health system financing is essential for achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC) as it improves effective service coverage and financial protection [17]. UHC, which is Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target 3.8 in turn plays a pivotal role in economic growth and job creation (SDG 8), gender equality (SDG 5), education (SDG 4), nutrition (SDG 2) and poverty reduction (SDG 1) [18].

Health financing involves mobilising resources, pooling them and using them strategically to purchase health services [18]. In Nigeria, health financing comes from various sources such as government funding from tax revenue, direct out-of-pocket payments (OOP), donor funding, and health insurance, including the Formal Sector Social Health Insurance Programme by the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), community-based health insurance, private health insurance, and voluntary health insurance [19, 20]. Unfortunately, most of these financing mechanisms are ineffective, and OOP remains the dominant method of health financing in Nigeria [19, 21]. The government’s budget allocation to health is inadequate, not based on evidence, and predominantly used for personnel costs instead of improving health services [19, 21].

The NHIS, which aimed to ensure access to quality health services for all Nigerians and move towards UHC, has fallen short of its objectives [20]. According to the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey, only 3% of people aged 15–49 have health insurance coverage [22]. Other health insurance mechanisms have been ineffective due to poor coordination, small and unsustainable participant pools, and reliance on external support for purchasing health services rather than building more sustainable community structures [19]. None of the available health insurance provides comprehensive coverage for surgical care of orthopaedic injuries. Therefore, some experts believe that Nigeria has not made adequate progress towards achieving universal health coverage in the context of its changing health needs and funding landscape [21].

Furthermore, the current state of donor funding shows a significant difference in attention and resources between infectious diseases and traumatic injuries [23]. While diseases like HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria receive substantial international support and funding for prevention and treatment, traumatic injuries do not receive the same level of aid. Trauma is the leading cause of death and disability in the global working population, ages 5 to 29, and the 12th leading cause of death when all ages are considered [24]. However, trauma received less than 1.0% of the total development assistance for health. In contrast, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria received 36.0% in 2017 [23, 25]. Even though charitable organisations like SIGN Fracture Care International provide training for trauma specialists and donate implants to treat fractures in LMICs [26, 27], many other costs associated with trauma care are not covered.

The above discourse suggests that OOP for fracture care may remain the primary source of financing for several more years. In our practice, we have observed that some patients find it easier to finance fracture care, while others face significant challenges that result in catastrophic spending, further impoverishing them. This indicates a knowledge gap in the overall discourse on healthcare financing, particularly in the context of fracture care financing in low-resource settings. To support health advocacy and improve access to high-quality fracture care, it is important to understand the factors contributing to difficulty in financing fracture care in low-resource settings. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the sociodemographic and clinical factors that predict difficult financing of fracture care among patients who underwent locked intramedullary (IM) nailing for LBFs in a teaching hospital in southwestern Nigeria.

Methods

Background of the study centre

The study was undertaken at a mission teaching hospital in a semi-urban city in southwestern Nigeria. The hospital offers subsidised surgical and non-surgical treatments to a large number of trauma victims who visit daily with varying degrees and durations of injuries. It serves as a referral centre for health facilities within the city and from villages and towns in the region. Since SIGN Fracture Care International began providing implants to our centre in 2014, there has also been a significant increase in patients from distant cities seeking fracture care. The patient population includes artisans, taxi drivers, motorcyclists, government workers, small-scale farmers, and traders, most of whom do not have health insurance, except for a few civil servants with limited coverage for certain surgical conditions. For the treatment of LBFs, the hospital receives charitable donations of SIGN implants, which include SIGN locked IM nails, interlocking screws, and Fx plates from SIGN Fracture Care International (Richland, WA, USA).

SIGN implants are the only modern implants that patients can easily access free of charge. Patients requiring other types of implants obtained them from distant cities at high costs. Apart from SIGN implants and occasional donations of operating room consumables and equipment from Western friends, the hospital does not receive any other aid or grants to assist patients with the cost of care. Therefore, every patient must pay a hospital bill to cover the cost of medications, surgical consumables not obtained through donations, bed fees, and subsidised charges for surgical and rehabilitative procedures. Emergency packs are available for resuscitation and emergency procedures for acute injuries, but a portion of the bill must be paid before patients undergo elective surgical operations. However, acutely injured patients experiencing severe financial difficulties and unable to pay after 48 h or more receive support from hospital social workers who facilitate pay-after-service arrangements with hospital management. This support mitigates financial problems to prevent protracted delays in surgery and complications.

Study design

This study was a prospective cohort analysis of data from 690 patients aged 18 and older who underwent surgical fixation of humerus, femur, or tibia fractures using SIGN nails between July 2014 and June 2024. The inclusion criteria focused on patients who were admitted for treatment. Patients treated non-operatively, by other surgical methods, or as outpatients were excluded from the study. Our Institutional Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocols, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data on patients were collected post-operatively and updated at discharge (usually 5–7 days post-operatively) using a questionnaire designed for this study (See Supplementary file). The study involved patients with fractures, categorised into two groups: (1) Patients with fresh fractures ((≤ 3 weeks old) were first resuscitated and stabilised in the Accident and Emergency unit before being admitted to the orthopaedic ward. Before surgery, these patients were required to pay 50% of the surgery fees and procure the necessary surgical consumables. (2) Patients with old fractures (> 3 weeks old, mostly nonunions and malunions) were evaluated and prepared for surgery at the weekly Orthopaedic outpatient clinic. They were admitted to the hospital the day before surgery, provided they had deposited 80% of the surgery fees and bought the required surgical consumables. Each patient was responsible for procuring all necessary medications for each day of their post-operative recovery.

To identify potential risk factors contributing to difficulties in financing fracture care and to inform the selection of variables for which data would be collected, we conducted a literature review and preliminary qualitative interviews with patients, caregivers, and hospital staff, including nurses and ward orderlies at the outset of the study. We asked these informants to share their insights on why some patients struggle to pay their hospital bills while others find it manageable. The recurring reasons mentioned were cross-verified with existing literature to identify potential risk factors associated with challenges in financing fracture care. These factors encompass both sociodemographic characteristics and clinical variables related to the injuries. Socio-demographic variables included were age, gender, marital status, education level, occupation, city of residence, health insurance, and extended family support (EFS). Clinical variables included the cause of fracture, fracture age (fresh/old), fracture type (open/closed), previous treatment, concomitant injury, comorbidity, number of fractures fixed with IM nails, and post-operative length of hospital stay (PLOS).

Statistical analysis and data management

The data were stored on the lead author’s laptop in SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp, New York, USA) and later analysed. As depicted in the flow chart (Fig. 1), patients were classified into two cohorts based on how promptly they paid their bills for in-patient care:

Easy fracture-care Financing (EF) - paying promptly. This cohort included (i) fresh fracture patients who paid for surgical materials and fees within 24 h of notification, and (ii) old fracture patients who completed pre-operative tests and paid for surgery during their initial visit or within one week. Patients in this cohort also procured the prescribed medications for each post-operative day.Difficult fracture-care Financing (DF) - requesting time to gather funds. This cohort consisted of (i) fresh fracture patients who took longer than 24 h to gather funds for surgical materials and deposit or inconsistently purchased the prescribed post-operative medications for each day, and (ii) old fracture patients who returned home to gather funds after being informed of costs and delayed admission for over one week due to financial constraints.

Based on age, patients were categorised as youth (≤ 24 years), young adults (25–44 years), middle age (45–59 years) and older adults (≥ 60 years). Patients were also categorised into three occupational groups: (1) daily-income earners (artisans, commercial drivers/riders, farmers, and traders), who must go daily to work to earn income from manual labour or sales, (2) salary-earning professionals, who could be granted paid sick leave, and (3) dependants i.e. participants, including students, artisanal apprentices and older adults without a pension, who did not earn income and relied on family members for financial support. Patients who received aid in cash or kind from extended family members (parents, uncles, aunts, siblings, cousins, etc.) were categorised as having a supportive extended family.

The socio-demographic and clinical variables were tested for significant associations with fracture-care financing. Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test was used for categorical variables, and the independent sample t-test was used for numerical variables. The normality of the data was assessed visually with histograms to determine the appropriateness of parametric tests. Variables that demonstrated significance in the univariate analyses were then entered as covariates into a binary logistic regression analysis to determine the significant independent predictors of difficult fracture-care financing. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Six hundred and ninety patients were enrolled for the study. Their mean age was 45.1 years (SD = 17.52, range 18–99 years). Figure 2 shows that nearly half (44.3%) were young adults (25–44 years). A statistically significant difference (t = 3.784, p < 0.001) was found between the mean ages of EF (47.4 years, SD = 18.11) and DF (42.4 years, SD = 16.46) cohorts. There were 408 (59.1%) daily-income earners, 152 (22.0%) salary-earning professionals and 130 (18.8%) dependants. One-way analysis of variance revealed the mean age of daily-income earners (42.0 years) was significantly lower than that of dependants (55.1 years, p < 0.001) and salary-earning professionals (45.0 years, p = 0.024). Only 56 (8.1%) had health insurance. Figure 3 shows that a significantly higher proportion of those who lived outside the hospital city had received failed TBS or orthodox treatment (p < 0.001). In Fig. 4, a significantly higher proportion of males than females sustained concomitant injury.

In Table 1, Pearson’s chi-square analysis showed that more males than females had DF (p < 0.001). Additionally, a higher proportion of single and separated/divorced individuals experienced DF compared to the married and widowed (p = 0.012). Patients with tertiary education were less likely to have DF than those with lower education levels (p < 0.001). Significantly more of the daily-income earners experienced DF (p < 0.001) than salary earners and dependents. A higher proportion of patients residing in the city where the hospital is located had DF compared to those from outside (p < 0.001). Moreover, patients without health insurance experienced DF more than those with insurance (p < 0.001). Lack of EFS was significantly associated with DF compared to having one (p < 0.001). Religion showed no statistically significant association with fracture-care financing (p = 0.616).

Table 3 presents the results of the binary logistic regression used to identify the independent predictors of DF. The overall model showed statistical significance compared to the null model (χ2 (19) = 183.4, p < 0.001), explained 31.2% of the variance in DF (Nagelkerke R2), and accurately predicted 72.0% of cases. Significant predictors of DF included patients’ age (p = 0.018), gender (p = 0.002), level of education (p < 0.001), city of residence (p < 0.001), health insurance status (p = 0.013), EFS (p < 0.001), nature of previous care (p = 0.005), concomitant injury (p = 0.017), and PLOS (p = 0.036). Marital status (p = 0.777), occupational class (p = 0.383), cause of fracture (p = 0.504), and comorbidity (p = 0.413) did not have a significant independent impact.

For every one-year decrease in age, the odds of DF increased by 0.98 (95% CI = 0.964–0.997). Males had 1.96 times the odds of DF of females. Patients without formal education had odds of DF that were 6.83 times more than those of tertiary-educated patients (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 2.879–16.199). Primary-educated and secondary-educated patients had 5.38 times (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 2.738–10.582) and 3.19 times (p < 0.001, 95% CI = 1.837–5.541) the odds of tertiary-educated patients, respectively. Patients who resided in the hospital city were 2.47 times more likely to have DF than those outside (95% CI = 1.721–3.592). Patients without health insurance had 2.74 times the odds of DF compared to those with health insurance (95% CI = 1.236–6.057). Patients without EFS were 4.27 times more likely to experience DF than those with it (95% CI = 2.504–7.274). Patients who received a prior TBS treatment were 2.13 times more likely to experience DF than those without previous treatment (p = 0.001, 95% CI = 1.348–3.368). Patients with a concomitant injury had 1.60 times the odds of DF compared to those without (95% CI = 1.089–2.351). Patients discharged after one week post-operatively were 1.91 times more likely to have DF than those discharged within one week (95% CI = 1.044–3.500).

Discussion

Almost half of the patients (46.5%) in our study requested time to gather funds (DF cohort, Fig. 1) before they could pay their hospital bills. This meant that the treatment was delayed due to financial constraints. Qualitative inquiry from some patients in the DF cohort revealed that sourcing funds involved obtaining a loan, selling a property, or engaging in manual labour to raise money to treat the injured family member. As a result, these patients became poorer after discharge than they were before they suffered a bone fracture. Studies in Nigeria have found that most Nigerians pay for health services out-of-pocket [19, 20]. When people use up their life savings, sell assets, or borrow to pay for services and treatments, they plunge deeper into poverty, affecting their futures and often their children’s [13]. On the contrary, UHC implies that everyone can access the full range of quality health services they need, when and where they need them, without facing catastrophic health spending due to healthcare costs. Increasing health financing substantially in developing countries is part of SDG target 3.c [28].

In 2023, the World Health Organisation noted that the world is not progressing significantly towards UHC [13]. Our data show that none of the three objectives of UHC – equity in access, quality of health services, and financial risk protection – is being realised in our Nigerian setting. We found that lack of health insurance was an independent predictor of DF, meaning that having insurance, albeit with partial coverage, protects against DF. Unfortunately, only 56 of our 690 patients (8.1%) had health insurance. Moreover, our study suggests that patients lack proximate access to quality healthcare since only 47.5% (328/690) live within the city where the hospital is located. The remaining patients had to travel varying distances to the hospital. Additionally, a significantly higher proportion of those who lived outside the hospital city had received failed TBS or orthodox treatment (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that most people may continue to pay for healthcare services out-of-pocket beyond 2030. Therefore, seeking ways to enhance their ability to afford high-quality health services in the interim is crucial, with the hope that UHC will ultimately be achieved.

Several socio-demographic and clinical variables influenced our patients’ ability to finance their fracture care. Many of these are modifiable factors that can be adjusted to improve people’s purchasing power for healthcare services. Except for marital status and occupational class, all other socio-demographic variables that demonstrated significant associations with fracture-care financing in univariate analysis (Table 1) were also independent predictors of DF in the logistic regression analysis (Table 3). Increasing age was found to be protective, possibly due to the greater financial independence associated with older ages in our setting. Our data also shows that daily-income earners were younger and significantly experienced DF more than the other occupational classes. Due to high unemployment and underemployment rates in Nigeria [29], many youths and young adults lack the financial stability to navigate an unexpected expense presented by a fracture. Therefore, reducing unemployment can make it easier for people to finance their fracture care.

The likelihood of DF is higher in males than in females, possibly because men typically serve as the primary breadwinners in Nigerian families. If a man is injured, the woman often lacks independent means to arrange for his care unless there is family savings she can access. On the other hand, the man can independently decide on his wife’s care and provide funding to support his decision [30]. The higher likelihood of DF in males may also be because they are prone to more severe injuries than females, which require higher costs of care. Our data showed a significantly higher proportion of males than females sustained concomitant injuries (Fig. 4). Studies have shown that male trauma victims incur higher hospital costs than females, perhaps due to more serious accidents causing more physical injuries [31, 32]. Although the univariate analysis demonstrated that a higher proportion of single and separated/divorced individuals experienced DF compared to the married and widowed, the income inequality between genders may explain why marriage was not an independent predictor of DF. Being married to a woman without robust financial autonomy is unlikely to offer any protection against DF.

As the level of education increased, the likelihood of DF significantly decreased, suggesting that higher education is protective against DF. Education influences other factors that can positively impact health financing, such as financial literacy, knowledge acquisition on financial preparedness for contingencies, obtaining salary-earning jobs that provide paid sick leave so that income flow is not impaired by absence from work, and enrollment in health insurance schemes. Research has shown that education correlates positively with financial literacy and fiscal well-being [33]. Education drives economic growth and improves living standards due to its potential to increase productivity and income [34]. In Nigeria, access to enrollment and the uptake of health insurance have significant direct relationships with the level of education [35, 36]. The location of patients’ residences was also an independent predictor of DF. Hospital-city dwellers were 2.47 times more likely to have DF than those outside. This finding probably reflects the poorer economic situation of hospital-city dwellers compared to patients from other towns and cities. As stated earlier in the Methods section and reflected in our data (Table 1), patronage by patients from outside has greatly increased because of the versatility of SIGN nails and faster recovery from LBFs following SIGN IM nailing compared to other treatment modalities in our environment [37]. These patients appear to be richer and better prepared financially than the hospital-city dwellers before they embark on long-distance journeys to seek care in our centre.

Another independent predictor of DF in this study is the lack of EFS. Some patients interviewed by the authors narrated how extended family relations overseas sent money to finance their care. For others, a less busy relative relieved a vibrant, economically productive informal caregiver of their duty to wait on an injured spouse so that they could go to work and ensure income flow continued during hospital stay and recovery. Extended families have played important roles in African societies, where social safety nets are often non-existent. Individuals and nuclear families cope with stressful life events through a network of reciprocal assistance provided by members of a larger, extended family group. The system can afford effective caregiving arrangements and defray care costs using the various income streams of different family members, such that a member is not impoverished by healthcare costs. Although rapid urbanisation and westernisation are causing a disintegration of the African extended family system, they have not destroyed the traditional pattern of extended family networks. In settings where the system is preserved, people care for their relatives deeply and assist in various ways whether they live geographically close or apart [38].

Independent clinical predictors of DF included previous TBS treatment, concomitant injury, and PLOS. These factors indicate a serious injury or a complex treatment process, leading to higher treatment costs. Patients who had received TBS treatment before seeking care at our centre had old fractures and had spent a significant amount of money on ineffective treatments over a long period. Previous studies have also reported that treating old fractures is costlier than treating fresh fractures [39, 40]. Similarly, research has demonstrated a direct connection between multiple injuries and longer hospital stays with higher treatment costs. A study in a similar setting found that patients with multiple injuries incurred higher care costs across various expense categories, including laboratory and radiological investigations, medications, consumables, procedures, and admission costs [15]. Similarly, a cost-analysis study in Spain noted that patients with multiple injuries represent a significant health cost, primarily due to prolonged hospital stays [41]. Another study across 12 Level 1 trauma centres in Australia revealed that the total treatment costs increased by $225 for each additional body region injured and by $1500 for every extra day in the hospital [32].

Our findings have important practical implications and recommendations for healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organisations in low-resource settings to address the disparities in financing for fracture care. Healthcare providers can play a crucial role by offering flexible payment plans and sliding scale fees to lessen the financial burden on patients. They can also encourage enrollment in health insurance and collaborate with social services to assist patients with limited financial resources. Policymakers are critical in shaping the healthcare financing landscape. They must increase funding for healthcare infrastructure and human resources, implement policies that promote UHC, expand health insurance programmes—especially for underprivileged populations—and monitor and evaluate healthcare financing systems to identify areas for improvement. Community organisations can enhance the accessibility and affordability of fracture care by establishing community-based health insurance schemes, providing financial assistance for healthcare costs, organising health education programmes, facilitating access to services for underserved populations, and advocating for healthcare policy changes that support UHC. By implementing these recommendations, stakeholders can collaboratively address disparities in fracture care financing, promote UHC, and ensure that quality healthcare is affordable for everyone.

Limitations of the study

Our research has limitations that may impact the interpretation of the findings. The study focused primarily on factors that increase the cost of care, such as concomitant injuries, comorbid conditions, and length of stay. However, it did not include the exact treatment costs for each patient, which could have revealed the impact of bill size on patients’ ability to pay. Additionally, some patients who paid promptly may have secured loans or sold assets before hospitalisation, potentially masking their financial difficulties. The study also could not objectively estimate indirect care costs, such as lost workdays for patients and caregivers, which contribute to the difficulty experienced in financing fracture care. Furthermore, the study did not fully capture some confounding factors that may influence patients’ ability to pay promptly for healthcare, including socioeconomic status, cultural influences, transportation difficulties, and other social determinants—for example, needing approval from a key family figure before making financial decisions or transportation challenges delaying payment. The study also excluded patients who discharged themselves against medical advice due to financial constraints, potentially underestimating the prevalence of DF.

Despite these limitations, our study contributes valuable insights. It is the first to focus specifically on predictors of difficulty in fracture-care financing in Nigeria. The large and diverse population sample, spanning multiple years, accurately represents Nigeria’s broader population. The study evaluated occupation and education level, crucial indicators of socioeconomic status, while other indicators like income, wealth, family structure and access to resources were indirectly assessed through sociodemographic variables. The variable “City of residence” indirectly assessed transportation challenges. Clarifications obtained during patient interviews reduced bias by ensuring accurate classification of DF cases. The statistical analysis identified independent predictors of DF, enhancing the study’s power. This study provides a foundation for planning larger research on fracture care financing in low-resource settings, focusing on analysing the effects of socioeconomic and other factors on healthcare access, the development and evaluation of innovative healthcare financing solutions, etc.

Conclusion

The study highlights significant predictors of difficulty in fracture care financing in a setting where out-of-pocket payments remain the primary source of healthcare financing and UHC seems distant. Significant predictors of DF included socioeconomic factors (younger age, male gender, lack/low formal education, residence in the hospital city, lack of health insurance, and lack of EFS) and clinical factors (previous TBS treatment, concomitant injury, and PLOS longer than one week). The findings suggest that improving people’s socioeconomic status through entrepreneurship and job creation to reduce unemployment, women’s empowerment, reduction of gender inequality, higher education, comprehensive health insurance coverage, and stronger extended family networks, as well as preventive strategies to reduce the occurrence and severity of injuries, can improve fracture-care financing in low-resource settings. Empowering people to finance their healthcare while waiting for UHC can lead to positive outcomes, as any extra income gained can be redirected towards human development. The findings can inform healthcare policy and practice to improve accessibility and affordability of fracture care.
