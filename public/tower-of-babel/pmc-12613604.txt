Integrated transcriptomic and WGCNA identify drought-responsive TFs in Larix olgensis
Authors: Wenna Zhao, Hao Dong, Chengpeng Cui, Chen Wang, Yu Liu, Yajing Ning, Hanguo Zhang, Meng Li, Shujuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal forests face increasingly severe drought events, which suppress the growth of larch. Transcription factors (TFs), acting as regulators, can increase plant stress tolerance by modulating gene expression networks. This study integrated RNA-seq, WGCNA and physiology to systematically analyze the adaptation mechanisms of Larix olgensis under drought stress. Among the 50,110 unigenes identified via RNA-seq, 1,391 unigenes were annotated as TFs. These TFs were clustered into four distinct clusters based on expression profiles. TFs within Cluster 1 exhibited significant differential expression during visible wilting of L. olgensis (72 h and 96 h). Ten TFs were selected as candidate TFs from Cluster 1 based on fold-change rank (p < 0.05). Subsequent WGCNA clustered the 50,110 unigenes into 19 co-expression modules. Notably, the firebrick4 module displayed a strong positive correlation with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents, whereas the darkolivegreen module showed a strong positive correlation with peroxidase (POD) activity. Three TFs in the firebrick4 module (TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2, TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1 and TRINITY_DN1230_c0_g1) and two TFs in the darkolivegreen module (TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g3 and TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g1) were identified by co-expression network analysis. The findings of this study contribute to the knowledge of drought-responsive TFs in L. olgensis, establishing a foundational resource for future functional studies and molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07604-5.Keywords: RNA-seq, Gene co-expression network, Physiological characteristics, Module-trait relationships, Conifer

Introduction

Drought stress, exacerbated by escalating global climate change, severely restricts tree growth and even leads to mortality, thereby disrupting the ecological balance [1–3]. Larix olgensis, an ecologically and economically vital conifer species in northeastern China, plays a critical role in regional forest ecosystems [4]. However, the increasing frequency of drought events has resulted in a significant decline in the drought resistance of L. olgensis in arid regions, which threatens their survival and ecological functions [5]. Understanding the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance in L. olgensis is essential for sustainable forest management. Plants responses to drought stress primarily involve osmotic adjustment, redox homeostasis, hormone signal transduction and morphological adaptations [6]. Drought triggers oxidative damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, leading to membrane lipid peroxidation and chlorophyll degradation. Concurrently, plants activate enzymatic antioxidants (superoxide dismutase, SOD, EC: 1.15.1.1; peroxidase, POD, EC: 1.11.1.7; etc.) and osmotic regulators (proline, soluble sugars, etc.) to mitigate stress. Limited studies have reported that under drought stress, L. olgensis exhibits significantly increased POD activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, accompanied by reduced plant height [7].

Transcription factors (TFs) serve as central regulators that orchestrate complex gene networks to mitigate cellular damage and increase stress tolerance under drought stress. TFs typically consist of a DNA-binding domain (a structural motif for binding DNA), a transcriptional regulatory domain (for activating or repressing transcription), a nuclear localization signal and oligomerization sites (for protein-protein interaction) [8]. Current evidence indicates that the WRKY, NAC, bZIP, AP2/ERF and MYB families play prominent roles in plant responses to drought stress. WRKY TFs are named after their conserved WRKYGQK domain, and this gene family plays a pivotal role in regulating the transcriptional reprogramming associated with plant stress responses [9]. For instance, MaWRKYIIc7 is involved in modulating drought stress tolerance in mulberry (Morus spp.), and the overexpression of MaWRKYIIc7 reduced the stomatal aperture, decreased MDA content and enhanced ROS scavenging capacity [10]. NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC) TFs act as regulators in the plant drought response, involving complex mechanisms that include hormone signal integration, ROS homeostasis, gene expression regulation and self-activity regulation [11]. For example, the overexpression of five NAC genes from maize has been shown to enhance drought tolerance in Arabidopsis, and among them, ZmNAP can interact with ZmNAC19 to activate the drought stress response [12]. Furthermore, NAC family genes enhance drought tolerance in rice by activating the promoters of abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis genes (NCED1, NCED2 and NCED5) [13]. TFs belonging to the bZIP family contribute significantly to drought response. As demonstrated by GmTRAB1 in soybean, which enhances osmotic stress tolerance through proline accumulation, antioxidant enzyme activation and ROS accumulation [14]. The AP2/ERF family proteins are primarily classified into five subfamilies (AP2, dehydration-responsive element binding (DREB), ethylene-responsive element binding factor (ERF), ABI3/VP1 and the unclassified Soloist subgroup), with the DREB subfamily being particularly important for drought stress [15]. GmDREB1 confers drought tolerance in transgenic soybean through interaction with GmERF008 and GmERF116 [16]. The MYB family modulates biological processes such as ABA signal transduction to resist drought stress, with phylogenetically related species exhibiting higher conservation in MYB-mediated regulatory pathways during drought adaptation [17]. Furthermore, MYB TFs function as regulatory hubs that coordinate plant drought responses through hormonal signaling pathways, antioxidant enzyme systems, osmotic adjustment compounds and morphological modifications [18]. Recent studies have identified several TF genes involved in drought response in Larix. For example, LoMYB8 [19], LoNAC3 [20] and LoNAC5 [21] have been shown to positively or negatively regulate drought tolerance. However, research on TFs in L. olgensis remains limited, primarily due to the lack of a reference genome and comprehensive functional annotation. Therefore, functional characterization of individual genes represents a practical and strategic approach under current resource constraints. The systematic identification of key TFs will establish an essential foundation for understanding the molecular mechanisms of drought adaptation in this species.

Recent advances in RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) have enabled genome-wide identification of stress-responsive modules in non-model plants [22, 23]. The WGCNA method enables the analysis of associations between co-expression networks and plant traits, facilitating the identification of key genes closely linked to specific phenotypic characteristics. In sweet potato, WGCNA analysis from two drought-resistant cultivars identified cluster modules associated with drought resistance, and further analysis of the co-expression network identified 20 hub genes critical for drought adaptation [24]. This approach also allows targeted screening of genes related to specific metabolites. By analyzing the correlations between co-expression modules and flavonoid content, researchers can identify eigengenes significantly associated with flavonoid accumulation. Subsequently, constructing a scale-free network topology centered on flavonoid-related regulatory genes enables the screening of key TFs and hub genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis [25].

This study aims to elucidate the response mechanisms of L. olgensis under polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG6000)-induced drought stress by integrating physiological indices, RNA-seq and WGCNA. The research objectives include: (1) Elucidating physiological changes at distinct stress time points; (2) Identifying key TFs and predicting their functional networks. This research seeks to provide a theoretical foundation and candidate TFs for breeding drought-resistant varieties, thereby supporting the sustainable management of forest ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Materials and drought treatment

The seeds used in this study were collected from a hybrid maternal tree (family line 73 − 14 × 73 − 1, female parent × male parent) in the Qingshan Seed Orchard, Linkou County, Heilongjiang Province, China. This hybrid was produced by controlled pollination between L. olgensis family lines 73 − 14 and 73 − 1. Prior to sowing, the seeds were rinsed under running water for 1–2 days, surface-sterilized with 2% (w/v) potassium permanganate (KMnO4) for 20 min, and thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove residual KMnO₄. Seeds were then sown in sterilized soil (garden soil: vermiculite = 1:1, v/v; autoclaved at 121 °C for 30 min, twice). Germination occurred approximately 10 days after sowing. Seedlings were transplanted into individual pots (two seedlings per pot) one month later. After five months of growth, six-month-old seedlings were subjected to simulated drought stress via irrigation with 35% (w/v) PEG6000 solution.

The drought stress treatment comprised five time points: 0 h (hour) (control, normal watering), 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h. To ensure progressive stress, a reverse-order irrigation protocol was applied. Initially, 100 mL of 35% PEG6000 solution was applied to each pot designated for the 96 h treatment. Excess solution draining from the bottom of the pots was discarded. Subsequently, 50 mL of 35% PEG6000 solution was replenished every 12 h, with drainage similarly removed. This procedure was repeated sequentially for the remaining time points, and all samples were collected simultaneously at the end of the treatment period. Each time point included three biological replicates, with each replicate comprising three pots (two seedlings per pot, for a total of six seedlings per replicate).

Analysis of the photosynthetic and physiological parameters of L. olgensis under drought stress

Determination of chlorophyll content

The measurement of chlorophyll content was performed following the method described by Song [26]. Fresh needles (0.2 g) were sliced and immersed in 10 mL of 95% ethanol, followed by incubation in darkness until the tissues became bleached. The mixture was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance values of the extracts were measured at 470 nm, 649 nm and 665 nm with 95% ethanol used as the blank control. Chlorophyll concentrations were calculated as follows: chlorophyll a (Ca, mg∙L−1) = 13.95 × OD665 − 6.88 × OD649; chlorophyll b (Cb, mg∙L−1) = 24.96 × OD649 − 7.32 × OD665; total chlorophyll (Ct, mg∙L−1) = Ca + Cb; chlorophyll content (mg∙g−1 FW) = (C × Vt)/FW, where C is the chlorophyll concentration, Vt is the extract volume (mL), and FW is the fresh weight (g) of the needles.

Quantification of the MDA content

MDA content was measured following the method as described in the book [27]. Needles (0.2 g) were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized with 5 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min, 2 mL of supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of the 0.67% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) and boiled for 30 min. The cooled mixture was centrifuged again. The absorbance of the final supernatant was measured at 450 nm, 532 nm and 600 nm. The MDA concentration and content were calculated using the following formula: MDA concentration (C, µmol∙L−1) = 6.45 × (A532 - A600) − 0.56 × A450; MDA content (µmol∙g−1 FW) = (C × Vt)/FW, where Vt is the extract volume (mL) and FW is the fresh weight (g).

Determination of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content

The H2O2 concentration in the needle extracts was quantified via a standard curve established according to the method described in Song’s doctoral thesis [26]. The H2O2 content (µmol∙g−1 FW) was calculated as follows: H2O2 = (C × Vt)/FW, where C is the extract concentration (µmol∙L−1), Vt is the extract volume (mL), and FW is the fresh weight (g).

SOD activity assay

SOD activity was determined via the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction method as described in the book [27]. The reaction solution consisted of 0.05 M phosphate buffer (PB) (pH 7.8), 130 mM L-methionine, 750 µM NBT, 100 µM EDTA disodium salt and 20 µM riboflavin. Needle samples (0.2 g) were ground to a powder in liquid nitrogen, homogenized in 0.05 M phosphate buffer to a final volume of 5 mL, and centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected as the enzyme extract. In the assay, reaction mixtures containing the enzyme extract were exposed to light to initiate NBT reduction. A dark control tube served as the blank. The absorbance was measured at 560 nm. The total SOD activity was calculated refer to the methods described in the book.

POD activity assay

The measurement of POD activity was performed referred to the method described by Zhang [28]. Needles (0.1 g) were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized with 1.5 mL of 0.01 M PB (pH 7.8). After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min, 0.5 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mL of 0.05 M PB (pH 7.8), 0.5 mL of 0.1 M guaiacol, and 0.5 mL of 0.8% H2O2. The reaction mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 10 min, and the absorbance at 470 nm was recorded. A control was prepared by replacing the enzyme extract with PB. The POD activity (U∙g−1/FW∙min) was calculated as follows: POD activity = (N × ΔA470)/(W × T), where N is the dilution factor, ΔA470 is the absorbance change of the sample, W is the fresh weight (g), and T is the reaction time (min).

RNA-seq and analysis

RNA-seq

Needle samples from 15 individuals (3 biological replicates × 5 time points) were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and immediately transported on dry ice to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) for de novo transcriptome sequencing. Total RNA was extracted from each sample and subjected to quality control. Strand-specific libraries were prepared for transcriptome sequencing [29]. Eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using Oligo (dT) - attached magnetic beads. The purified mRNA was fragmented into short segments using Fragmentation Buffer, and first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with random hexamer primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized using buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA Polymerase I. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with AMPure XP beads, and blunt ends were generated via T4 DNA polymerase and Klenow DNA polymerase. After 3’-adenylation and adapter ligation, fragments were size-selected with AMPure XP beads. Following the dUTP-based strand-specific protocol, the second cDNA strand containing uracil (U) was degraded using USER enzyme, and the library was amplified via PCR. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform with paired-end 150 base pairs (bp) (PE150) reads. The raw reads from all samples were pooled and assembled de novo using Trinity. Unigenes were obtained after normalization. Expression levels of unigenes across samples were quantified using Salmon, and transcript abundance was normalized as transcripts per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (TPM).

Identification of differentially expressed unigenes (DEGs)

DEGs were screened using thresholds of a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 (|log2FC| ≥ 1) and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Volcano plots, UpSet plot, and GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment were generated using the cloud-based analytical tools on the LC-Bio OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Gene annotation and enrichment analysis

Unigenes were annotated against four databases (National Center for Biotechnology Information-NCBI_NR, GO, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes-KEGG and Plant Transcription Factor Database-PlantTFDB). For GO and KEGG, all annotations meeting the E-value threshold (< 0.00001) were included. Unigenes were annotated using the NR database via OmicShare tools. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using LC-Bio’s cloud platform, with background gene sets derived from RNA-seq data. TFs were annotated using the Arabidopsis thaliana reference in the PlantTFDB database (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php) based on sequence similarity, followed by statistical analysis of the family distribution of the differentially expressed TFs.

WGCNA analysis

A co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA R package, following the methods of Wang et al. [30]. Modifications included estimating Pearson correlations between genes based on TPM values and setting clustering parameters to a minimum module size of 30 genes, a sensitivity of 3.0 and a soft thresholding power of 20 (R2 = 0.88) (The scale-free topology model fit plot (R²) can be found in Figure S1 of Supplementary Material 1). Modules were correlated with phenotypic traits (MDA content, H2O2 content, POD activity and SOD activity) to calculate gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM).

qRT-PCR analysis

To validate the reliability of the RNA-seq results, total RNA was extracted using the Omega Plant RNA Kit (R6827), and cDNA was synthesized with the TaKaRa PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR047A) with gDNA Eraser. We selected ten of the top 20 differentially expressed TFs for validation. Gene-specific primers were designed using SnapGene software (Table S1 in Supplementary Material 1). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 system with SGExcel SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s (pre-denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 30 s. α-tubulin served as the internal reference gene [31]. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with three biological replicates [32].

Phylogenetic and conserved domain analysis

The protein sequences of the key TFs were used as queries to retrieve homologous sequences via BLASTX against the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Multiple sequence alignment was performed with ClustalW, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA 7.0 using the neighbor-joining method with a p-distance model (bootstrap = 1000 replicates) [22]. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using the ITOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/). Conserved domains of these proteins were identified through Batch CD-Search at NCBI and subsequently visualized with TBtools [33].

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, experimental data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, USA). Multiple comparisons were performed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method. All measurements were based on three biological replicates as described in section 2.1, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Column graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). Co-expressed networks were carried out by the cytoscape_v3.10.0. The cluster analysis, polar area diagrams of the TFs and heatmaps were generated by LC-Bio OmicStudio platform.

Results

The response of L. olgensis to drought stress varied over time

Six-month-old wild type (WT) seedlings were treated with PEG6000, and the growth status of the plants was continuously observed. There was no significant phenotypic change observed in L. olgensis seedlings at 24 h (Fig. 1A). However, slight wilting of the apical needles was observed after 48 h, and pronounced wilting of the apical needles was observed at 72 h. Most needles had wilted at 96 h. The total chlorophyll content gradually decreased under progressive stress (Fig. 1B), with a significant reduction compared with that in the control group (0 h) at 72 h and 96 h. The results indicated that the response of L. olgensis to water deficit was evident at 72 h. MDA is a terminal product of membrane lipid peroxidation [34], gradually accumulated with chronic stress (Fig. 1C). Compared with that of the control (0 h), the MDA content exhibited significant increase at 48 h. H2O2 content can reflect an imbalance in ROS levels under stress conditions [35]. With the extension of drought stress treatment time, the H2O2 content progressively increased. A significant difference was observed at 24 h compared with 0 h (Fig. 1D). SOD and POD play important defensive roles in ROS scavenging [36]. SOD acts as the first line of defense, specifically eliminating superoxide anions (O₂−), POD detoxifies the downstream product H2O2 generated by SOD, for preventing cytotoxic accumulation [37]. This study revealed that SOD activity increased at 24 h, but that POD activity increased significantly at 72 h under drought stress in L. olgensis (Fig. 1E and F). These results showed that increasing levels of drought stress progressively reduced the chlorophyll content and increased the contents of MDA and H2O2, as well as the activities of SOD and POD at different treatment times.

Identification and function prediction of unigenes

RNA-seq and assembly

RNA-seq analysis was performed on 15 samples. Sequencing adapters were removed using Cutadapt, followed by quality filtering with fqtrim, yielding 87.1 Gb of clean data (Q30 > 94%, efficiency > 95%). Each sample containing ≥ 5 Gb of valid data (Table S2 in Supplementary Material 1). A mixed-sample assembly strategy was adopted using Trinity. After normalization, 181,749 transcripts and 50,110 unigenes were obtained. Subsequently, assembly quality was assessed, such as unigene length and GC content. Unigene length distribution was as follows: 200–300 bp (29.4%),300-1,000 bp (36.8%), 1,000–2,000 bp (16.9%), and ≥ 2,000 bp (16.9%) (Figure S2 in Supplementary Material 1). Among the 50,110 unigenes, 68% presented a GC content between 40% and 60% (Figure S3 in Supplementary Material 1).

Functional annotation of unigenes

A total of 50,110 unigenes were annotated by the NR, GO, KEGG and PlantTFDB databases. Among these, the NR database had the highest annotation rate at 54.37% (27,245 unigenes). Statistical analysis of the species distribution of homologous proteins showed that the highest proportion (40.04%) of annotated unigenes matched to Picea sitchensis, indicating the closest phylogenetic relationship with this species. In contrast, only 1.74% of unigenes matched to Pinus tabuliformis among the Pinaceae species (Fig. 2A).

A total of 26,978 unigenes were successfully annotated into three major categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) (Fig. 2B). Within the BP category, a large number of unigenes were annotated to stress-related terms, including ‘protein phosphorylation’ (GO: 0006468), ‘oxidation-reduction process’ (GO: 0055114), ‘defense response’ (GO: 0006952) and ‘signal transduction’ (GO: 0007165). A total of 293, 277 and 231 unigenes were annotated to ‘response to abscisic acid’ (GO: 0009737), ‘response to oxidative stress’ (GO: 0006979) and ‘response to water deprivation’ (GO: 0009414) respectively, suggesting their potential importance in the drought stress. Among CC category, ‘nucleus’ (GO: 0005634) contained the highest number of annotated unigenes (6,504), followed by ‘cytoplasm’ (GO: 0005737), ‘plasma membrane’ (GO: 0005886), ‘integral component of membrane’ (GO: 0016021), and ‘cytosol’ (GO: 0005829). In MF category, 4,288 unigenes were in ‘protein binding’ (GO: 0005515). There were more genes in ‘ATP binding’ (GO: 0005524) and ‘metal ion binding’ (GO: 0046872) than with the other GO terms.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed 9,051 unigenes distributed across six major metabolic pathways (Fig. 2C). Within the metabolism category (5,999 unigenes), the largest proportion (23.85%) was associated with ‘carbohydrate metabolism’. The fewest unigenes were found in ‘Glycan biosynthesis and metabolism’. In genetic information processing pathways, ‘translation’ pathways contained the most annotated unigenes (1,248). Notably, a substantial number of unigenes were enriched in several stress-responsive pathways: ‘environmental adaptation under organismal systems’, ‘signal transduction’ in environmental information processing, and ‘transport and catabolism’ pathways in cellular processes. These results provided potential genes for mechanisms of drought stress response in L. olgensis.

TFs were annotated based on sequence similarity against A. thaliana data from PlantTFDB, and their family distribution was analyzed (Fig. 2D). The data showed that the WRKY family had the most members, followed by MYB/MYB_related, AP2/ERF, C2H2, bHLH and NAC families.

Identification and analysis of DEGs under drought stress

The two drought treatment groups (24 h and 48 h) and the 0 h control group were compared by pairwise comparisons. A limited number of DEGs were identified in the 24 h vs. 0 h and 48 h vs. 0 h groups, with 98 DEGs (33 upregulated and 65 downregulated) found in the 24 h vs. 0 h group (Fig. 3A), and 371 DEGs (145 upregulated and 226 downregulated) detected in the 48 h vs. 0 h group (Fig. 3B). However, more DEGs were found in the 72 h vs. 0 h and 96 h vs. 0 h groups. A total of 4,992 DEGs were identified in the 72 h vs. 0 h group, including 2,607 upregulated and 2,385 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 3C). A total of 6,952 DEGs were obtained in the 96 h vs. 0 h group, comprising 2,608 upregulated and 4,344 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 3D). The UpSet plot showed that 4,231 DEGs were shared between the 72 h vs. 0 h and 96 h vs. 0 h groups, whereas 35 common DEGs were detected in the four comparison groups (Fig. 3E). However, no TF was differentially expressed for all four comparison groups. Notably, a total of 209 TFs were commonly differentially expressed in the 72 h vs. 0 h and 96 h vs. 0 h groups (Fig. 3F).

Genes in the four groups were classified through GO enrichment analysis. After 24 h of drought stress treatment, rapid stress response pathways were activated. Unigenes were significantly enriched in BP category, including ‘obsolete activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) activity’ (GO: 0000187) and ‘signal transduction’. CC category such as ‘cell wall’ (GO: 0005618), ‘Golgi membrane’ (GO: 0000139) and ‘transport vesicle membrane’ (GO: 0030658) were significantly enriched. MF category exhibiting the most abundant significantly enriched terms were ‘protein binding’, ‘chromatin binding’ (GO: 0003682) and ‘calcium ion binding’ (GO: 0005509) (Fig. 3G). After 48 h of drought stress, DEGs in the BP category were significantly enriched in ‘negative regulation of apoptotic process’ (GO: 0043066), ‘cell adhesion’ (GO: 0007155), ‘response to hypoxia’ (GO: 0001666) and ‘gluconeogenesis’ (GO: 0006094). In the CC category, ‘cytoplasm’, ‘membrane’ (GO: 0016020) and ‘plasma membrane’ began to abundant significantly enrichment. However, no significant change in enrichment was detected in the MF category (Fig. 3H). After 72 h of drought stress treatment, the plants presented severe physiological changes. DEGs in the BP category were significantly enriched in ‘response to oxidative stress’, ‘flavonoid biosynthetic process’ (GO: 0009813), ‘lipid metabolic process’ (GO: 0006629), ‘transmembrane transport’ (GO: 0055085), ‘cell wall organization’ (GO: 0071555), ‘flavonoid glucuronidation’ (GO: 0052696), ‘response to water deprivation’ and ‘response to salicylic acid’ (GO: 0009751) (Fig. 3I). At 96 h, the BP and CC enrichment patterns were consistent with the GO terms at 72 h (Fig. 3J). In the MF category, kinase-related terms emerged at both 72 h and 96 h, specifically ‘protein kinase activity’ (GO: 0019901) and ‘protein kinase binding’ (GO: 0019901). These results indicated that the response mechanism of Larix to drought was similar at 72 h and 96 h. The differential gene analysis across drought stress time points provided a detailed dataset for subsequent research on TFs. The significant enrichment of DEGs in the aforementioned GO terms suggested that L. olgensis may respond to drought stress through these related pathways.

TFs analysis

In the 24 h vs. 0 h group, only one differentially expressed TF was identified, which belongs to the SBP TF family (Fig. 4A). In the group of 48 h vs. 0 h, the number of annotated TF families increased, with a total of 16 unigenes annotated across the following families: MYB (four), AP2/ERF (three), ARR-B (three), WRKY (two), YABBY (one), HD-ZIP (one), NAC (one) and C3H (one) (Fig. 4B). In the 72 h vs. 0 h group, a total of 235 TFs were significantly differentially expressed, of which 159 were upregulated and 76 were downregulated, and they belonged to 32 families (Fig. 4C). The WRKY family had the highest number of genes (45), followed by the MYB/MYB related (45), AP2/ERF (28), NAC (21), C2H2 (14) and bHLH (13). In the 96 h vs. 0 h group, 290 TFs (including 170 upregulated and 120 downregulated) were annotated, belonging to 35 families (Fig. 4D). Among these, 56 unigenes were annotated to the WRKY family, followed by MYB/MYB related (52), AP2/ERF (33), NAC (21), C2H2 (20), and bHLH (19).

Clusters analysis of TFs and qRT-PCR

To elucidate the expression patterns of TFs, we performed cluster analysis on the 1,391 annotated TFs, dividing them into four distinct clusters (Fig. 5A). Clusters 1 and 3 presented similar expression patterns; however, the expression levels in Cluster 1 increased significantly after 72 h, whereas those in Cluster 3 showed a significant increase after 48 h of drought stress. In contrast, Clusters 2 and 4 presented similar expression patterns, with an overall decreasing trend in relative expression levels under drought stress. The expression in Cluster 2 declined significantly at 24 h, whereas that in Cluster 4 exhibited a significant decrease at 72 h. Integrated analysis with physiological phenotypes and DEGs revealed that L. olgensis initiated a pronounced response to drought stress after 72 h. Consequently, the differentially expressed TFs within Cluster 1 were selected for further analysis.

To identify key drought-responsive TFs, after excluding several TFs with incomplete coding sequences (CDS) sequences, we selected the top 10 TFs exhibiting higher FC values at both 72 h and 96 h of drought stress (Table S3 in Supplementary Material 1). The expression patterns of these 10 candidate TFs were subsequently validated across qRT-PCR analysis. As shown in Fig. 5B, their expression patterns under stress closely aligned with the RNA-seq results, confirming the high reliability of the RNA-seq data. The significance analysis of the qRT-PCR results can be found in Table S4 of Supplementary Material 1.

WGCNA analysis of L. olgensis under drought stress

WGCNA analysis revealed gene modules associated with the response of L. olgensis to drought stress

To identify unigenes associated with drought stress responses in L. olgensis, we performed WGCNA on 50,110 genes expressed under different drought stress time points. The genes were clustered into 19 co-expression modules (Fig. 6A). Pairwise correlation analysis revealed high topological overlap among genes within the same module (Fig. 6B). Among these modules, the darkorange2 exhibited the highest number of eigengenes (6,807), followed by firebrick4 (5,107 eigengenes), while the smallest modules were the thistle (49 eigengenes) and the blue2 (66 eigengenes) (Fig. 6C). To further investigate co-expression patterns associated with drought stress responses, we analyzed correlations between these modules and physiological indices (H2O2 content, MDA content, POD activity and SOD activity) under drought stress. The darkorange2 module showed a significant negative correlation with the H2O2 content (r = −0.89, p = 7.4e-6), whereas the firebrick4 module exhibited the strongest positive correlation (r = 0.79, p = 4.8e-4) (Fig. 6D). The MDA content displayed similar correlation trends with the darkorange2 module and firebrick4 module. Notably, the darkolivegreen module showed a strong positive correlation with POD activity (r = 0.85, p = 7.3e-5), while no module was significantly associated with SOD activity. These results highlight distinct regulatory networks that control oxidative stress and antioxidant responses in L. olgensis under drought conditions. The firebrick4 and darkolivegreen were selected as the key modules.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of module eigengenes associated with drought stress responses in L. olgensis

To elucidate the functional roles of eigengenes in the firebrick4 (5,107 genes) and darkolivegreen (371 genes) modules, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO analysis revealed that eigengenes in drought-responsive modules were predominantly enriched in the BP category such as ‘protein phosphorylation’, ‘defense response’ and ‘oxidation-reduction process’; CC category including the ‘nucleus’, ‘plasma membrane’ and ‘cytoplasm’; and MF category such as ‘protein binding’, ‘ATP binding’ and ‘protein serine/threonine phosphatase activity’ (GO:0004722) (Fig. 6E). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment of these eigengenes in stress-responsive pathways, notably ‘MAPK signaling pathway-plant’ (ko04016), ‘phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’ (ko00940) and ‘flavonoid biosynthesis’ (ko00941) (Fig. 6F). These enriched terms and pathways are associated with drought stress adaptation mechanisms, underscoring the pivotal roles of the firebrick4 and darkolivegreen modules in regulating oxidative stress mitigation and secondary metabolite biosynthesis during drought stress in L. olgensis.

Co-expression networks of module eigengenes related to drought stress

To identify hub genes within the co-expression networks, we analyzed eigengene pairs from the firebrick4 module (associated with H2O2 and MDA content) and the darkolivegreen module (positively correlated with POD activity), with weight ≥ 0.3. From the firebrick4 module, we obtained 3,036 pairs of associated paths derived from 558 eigengenes (Fig. 7A). Within this module, 47 eigengenes presented degree values exceeding 50, indicating their core significance. Among these, the unknown gene TRINITY_DN2095_c0_g1 displayed the highest degree (170), followed by TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2, TRINITY_DN307_c0_g1, TRINITY_DN231_c0_g1 and TRINITY_DN6710_c0_g1, with degrees of 162, 140, 120, and 103, respectively. This module identified 34 TFs, among which three TFs exhibited high connectivity and were named ERF3 (TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2) which occupies a central position, an unknown MYB-related TF (TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1) and NAC68 (TRINITY_DN1230_c0_g1). In the darkolivegreen module, we constructed a co-expression network comprising 1,230 pairs of associated paths derived from 87 eigengenes (Fig. 7B). This network included seven TFs and 80 functional genes. Fourteen eigengenes within this network achieved degree values above 50. Among them, TRINITY_DN2263_c0_g1, named HAK5, possessed the highest degree value, interacting with 64 eigengenes, demonstrating its pivotal role within this network. Notably, the network identified two highly connected (degree ≥ 30) TFs belonging to the WRKY family, named WRKY42 (TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g3) and WRKY6 (TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g1). Therefore, ERF3, an unknown MYB-related TF and NAC68 from the firebrick4 module, along with WRKY42 and WRKY6 from the darkolivegreen module, were identified as key TFs.

Expression levels of key TFs under drought stress

Based on the co-expression network analysis, a total of 34 TFs from the firebrick4 module and seven TFs from the darkolivegreen module were identified. The TPM values of these TFs were extracted from the RNA-seq data and used to construct expression heatmaps. ERF3 (TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2) and the unknown MYB-related TF (TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1) were significantly induced at 72 h and 96 h, which coincided with the pronounced drought symptoms observed in L. olgensis (Fig. 8A). Discussion

De novo RNA-seq of L. olgensis

Owing to the vast size and complex architecture of conifer genomes, reference genomes are currently available for only a limited number of coniferous species (P. abies, P. taeda, P. lambertiana, Pseudotsuga menziesii var. menziesii, P. tabuliformis, L. gmelinii, Sequoia sempervirens and L. kaempferi [38]. Although a reference genome for L. kaempferi was released in 2022, its incomplete assembly has led most subsequent studies to continue employing de novo transcriptome assembly approaches [39]. Notably, single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing has recently been applied to larch transcriptomics. For instance, Li et al. integrated PacBio full-length cDNA sequencing with de novo RNA-seq to characterize 35,414 genes [40]. Compared with conventional RNA-seq, full-length transcript sequencing can capture three times as many open reading frames (ORFs). However, this approach results in lower sequence coverage than RNA-seq-based estimates [38]. Given its lower sequencing cost, RNA-seq remains the preferred approach for molecular breeding applications. In our study, de novo RNA-seq generated 50,110 unigenes as the foundational transcriptomic resource.

Phased physiological and molecular responses of L. olgensis to drought stress

Through the determination of physiological parameters, this study revealed a stage-specific adaptation mechanism in L. olgensis under progressive drought stress. In this study, L. olgensis exhibited rapid accumulation of MDA and H2O2, accompanied by the swift activation of SOD at 24 h (Fig. 1C-E). This drought stress pattern is consistent with observations in L. gmelinii under natural drought conditions, where MDA content progressively increased while SOD activity showed an initial rise followed by a decline under progressive stress [41]. This pattern suggests that antioxidant defense mechanisms are preferentially initiated to mitigate oxidative damage in the mild drought stages. The response at the molecular level corresponds to the morphological changes: DEGs increased significantly at 72 h and 96 h compared with those at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 3A-D). However, a previous PEG-induced drought study on one-year-old L. olgensis seedlings reported a large number of DEGs (4,118; 2,046 and 5,114) at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h, respectively [42]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in stress intensity resulting from variations in experimental setups. Previous studies have shown that interactions between PEG concentration and growth substrate can significantly alter osmotic potential and substrate water retention [43]. Furthermore, plant age influences drought sensitivity in larch. Older larch is generally more responsive to temperature and moisture changes than young seedlings [44]. These factors may contribute to divergent transcriptomic responses under similar treatments. Conversely, studies in sorghum demonstrated no significant variation in the numbers of DEGs under different drought stress severities, but notable changes at transcriptomic level occurred during rehydration [45]. Such disparities highlight species-specific drought response mechanisms and inherent variations in drought tolerance. The preliminary analysis describes physiological and molecular changes under different degrees of drought in L. olgensis, but key early indicators of stress (including rapid stomatal closure, ABA accumulation [46] and water use efficiency dynamics [47]) were not monitored. Future studies should focus on these parameters to elucidate stage-specific adaptation strategies in L. olgensis under drought stress.

Integrated RNA-seq and WGCNA analyses revealed key TFs that respond to drought stress in L. olgensis

TFs are essential regulators of plant biological processes, including development and the response to environmental stress [48]. Through cluster analysis of the TFs, we identified four distinct clusters (Fig. 5A). TFs within cluster 1 exhibited differential expression in L. olgensis under severe water deficit (72 h and 96 h). Based on FC values and p-values, we selected 10 key TFs from cluster 1, including one MYB family TF, three NAC family TFs, two C2H2 family TFs, two LBD family TFs, and two ERF/AP2 family TFs. These drought-responsive TFs were similarly induced in the transcriptomic data from other plant species [49, 50]. In addition, TFs differentially expressed at earlier drought stages (24 h and 48 h) may contribute to drought avoidance strategies by modulating developmental processes to mitigate stress damage. A differentially expressed SBP family gene was identified at 24 h (Fig. 4A). A point mutation in the OsSPL3 gene impairs OsmiR156-directed cleavage of OsSPL3 transcripts in the lcrn1, resulting in reduced crown root number in Oryza sativa [51]. The root is the most sensitive organ to drought stress [52]. Abiotic stresses often inhibit plant biomass accumulation, and the expression of the SBP family gene may ensure the normal growth of conifers in early response to drought.

WGCNA is widely employed to identify associations between phenotypic traits and gene expression patterns, thereby identifying biologically relevant modules and target genes [53]. WGCNA revealed two key modules: the firebrick4 module and the darkolivegreen module (Fig. 6D). Within the co-expression network constructed with eigengenes in the firebrick4 module (positive correlation with H2O2 and MDA content), this study identified three key TFs with the highest degrees (Fig. 7A). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1 was closely related to an uncharacterized protein from P. sitchensis (GAA0153378.1) as well as to AtDIV1, AtDIV2, and AtMYBS1 from Arabidopsis (Fig. 9A). It has been reported that AtDIV1 and AtDIV2 function as key regulators in salt stress responses [54]. Conserved domain analysis showed that TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1 lacks the N-terminal domain present in its Arabidopsis homologs, while retaining the more conserved C-terminal domain [55]. Given that PEG-induced drought and salt stress both trigger similar physiological effects, such as osmotic and oxidative stress, TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1 may play a role similar to that of AtDIV1 and AtDIV2. TRINITY_DN1230_c0_g1 was closely related to two proteins from P. sitchensis and also exhibited high sequence similarity to several known stress-associated NAC TFs (Fig. 9B). These include RD26 and ATAF1 from Arabidopsis, as well as NAC2 and NAC11 from P. wilsonii. These NAC TFs have been reported to regulate drought tolerance positively or negatively in plants [56–58]. These findings suggest that TRINITY_DN1230_c0_g1 may act as a potential regulator in the drought response. Within the darkolivegreen module, the most connected key TFs were TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g3 and TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g1 (Fig. 7B). Phylogenetic analysis indicates that TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g1 was closely related to P. massoniana WRKY164 (Fig. 9C). Despite their high connectivity in the co-expression network, neither exhibited significant differential expression in L. olgensis under drought treatment. This suggests their potential role in fine-tuning POD activity during drought stress. Such regulation may occur through protein-protein interactions or post-transcriptional mechanisms. The identification of these non-DEGs with high connectivity highlights the advantage of WGCNA over conventional DEG analysis and expands our understanding of the drought response regulatory network. Functional annotation based on their closest homologs suggested that WRKY42 and WRKY6 may participate in low-phosphate (Pi) stress responses [59–61], implying potential conservation of this role. It is important to note that TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2 and TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g3 do not contain CDS. This is a common characteristic of de novo transcriptome assemblies in non-model species without a reference genome. The causes include low transcript abundance, the inherent challenges of accurately assembling long transcripts from short-read sequencing data, and the complexity introduced by alternative splicing [62, 63]. Nevertheless, these partial sequences remain a valuable resource for putative gene identification and expression profiling.

However, this study has certain limitations. The selected physiological indices (including H2O2 content, MDA concentration, POD activity, and SOD activity) represented general oxidative stress markers rather than drought-specific indicators. While PEG-induced drought enables precise control of stress intensity, it cannot fully replicate field conditions such as soil-root-microbiome interactions. Therefore, the key TFs in this study may enhance drought tolerance primarily by modulating antioxidant capacity rather than acting as specific drought-response regulators. Their biological roles require further functional validation via transgenic or CRISPR-based approaches. This study represents the first application of WGCNA to analyze the time-course transcriptome of L. olgensis under drought stress. By integrating physiological indicators, we constructed co-expression networks of potential drought-responsive genes, establishing a foundational resource for future functional studies and molecular breeding.

Conclusion

This study integrated RNA-seq and physiological analyses with WGCNA to construct a drought-responsive co-expression network in L. olgensis. Based on FC values (p < 0.05), we preliminarily identified 10 TFs responsive to drought stress. These TFs belong to the MYB, NAC, C2H2, AP2/ERF and LBD families. WGCNA further revealed two key modules: the firebrick4 module and the darkolivegreen module. Subsequent co-expression network analysis detected three key TFs from the firebrick4 module and two from the darkolivegreen module. These key regulators include TRINITY_DN13923_c0_g2, TRINITY_DN3004_c0_g1, TRINITY_DN1230_c0_g1, TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g3 and TRINITY_DN1156_c1_g1. These TFs likely play important regulatory roles in the drought stress response of L. olgensis. Overall, these findings contribute to the understanding of drought-responsive TFs in L. olgensis, and establish a foundational resource for future functional studies and molecular breeding.
