Clinical characteristics and predictors of disease progression in parkinson’s disease in a cohort of patients in the ashanti region, Ghana
Authors: Vida Obese, Berrnard Nkum, John Akassi, Manolo Agbenorku, Sheila Adamu, Priscilla Abrafi Opare-Addo, Andrew Jacobs Bilson, Hanson Ababio, Mie Rizig, Fred Stephen Sarfo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with motor and non-motor symptoms. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) with its rapidly rising ageing population is experiencing a surge in the burden of PD in clinical care. Delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation, coupled with socioeconomic and healthcare barriers, may uniquely contribute to the population of PD with advanced clinical disease in SSA. This study aimed to clinically characterize Ghanaian PD patients, and identify predictors of mid-to-advanced disease.Methods:A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital, Ghana (May 2023 - March 2024). Consecutive adults meeting UK Brain Bank criteria for PD diagnosis were neurologist-confirmed and evaluated using structured interviews including Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging, and MDS-UPDRS. Data were analyzed using Graph Pad Prism, with logistic regression identifying predictors of mid-to-advanced PD defined by H&Y stages 3–5.Results:Of 161 participants (mean age 64.0 ± 9.1 years; 114 males), 98 (60.9%) were in early-stage PD (H&Y stages 1–2). Mid-to-advanced stages (H&Y stages 3–5) had higher motor scores (19.9 vs 15.4; p = 0.003) but lower non-motor scores (p < 0.001). Medication use was universal in mid-to-advanced stages vs 42.9% in early stages (p = 0.001). Male patients, those who had lost employment due to PD, and those with moderate UPDRS severity were more likely to have mid-to-advanced disease, whereas higher income and urinary symptoms were less likely.Conclusion:Mixes of socio-demographic and clinical features are associated with mid-to-advanced stages of PD among this Ghanaian sample of PD patients. Integrating socioeconomic determinants into prognostic models may enable earlier identification of patients with mid-to-advanced PD and guide targeted interventions such as social support systems to improve their quality of life in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is an age-related degenerative disease of the central nervous system that evolves slowly and progressively and is caused by the selective loss of dopamine-producing cells and the inappropriate accumulation of alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies in the brain and spinal cord [1]. First described in 1817, PD is now recognized as the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease, with an estimated prevalence of 1.5 to 26 per 100,000 in the ageing population [2].

In Africa, with its rapidly ageing population, the burden of PD is growing, yet the region remains severely underrepresented in research. As a result, most available prevalence estimates are derived largely from populations of European ancestry, which may not accurately reflect disease burden, presentation, or progression in African settings. Studies conducted in Black populations, particularly within Sub-Saharan Africa, account for less than 5% of the global Parkinson’s disease literature, whereas studies conducted in European populations constitute approximately 80% [3]. This could be due to substantial underdiagnosis, limited access to specialist care, and the scarcity of population-based studies in Africa. Limited funding for neurological research is also a major contributor [2]. Moreover, Africa faces high burdens of communicable diseases such as tuberculosis, HIV, and Ebola [4]. Scarce resources are therefore directed toward neurological conditions, leaving less investment in neuroscience [5]. This underrepresentation creates uncertainty about how demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors shape disease progression in African populations.

Evidence suggests that PD progression is heterogeneous: age, sex, and socioeconomic status influence severity, with men generally at higher risk and individuals of lower socioeconomic status often presenting with more advanced disease [6]. Motor and non-motor symptoms, including depression, sleep disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction, strongly affect quality of life and may predict progression [7]. Yet these features are often under-recognized in routine care, particularly in low-resource settings [8].

In Ghana, PD care is fragmented and delayed. Access to levodopa and other dopaminergic therapies is inconsistent, and treatment is often initiated late compared with high-income settings [9,10]. This exacerbates functional decline and reduces opportunities for early intervention.

Given these gaps, context-specific data are essential. This study, therefore, seeks to (1) compare the demographic and clinical characteristics of individuals with PD symptoms by Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage (1–2 vs. 3–5); (2) assess differences in Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) domain scores, motor and non-motor features, and treatment patterns by stage; and (3) identify predictors of mid-to-advanced PD using logistic regression modelling. The study also aims to contribute to a growing body of literature on PD in low-resource settings and provide new insights into the interplay between clinical presentation and structural inequities in Ghana.

Methods

Study area

This hospital-based, analytical, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), Kumasi, Ghana. KATH is a 1000-bed tertiary facility with a specialty outpatient neurology clinic. The adult neurology clinic, established in 2012, runs once weekly and manages approximately 80–100 adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) per week with neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s disease.

Study design

This was a hospital-based, analytical, cross-sectional study.

Study population

All PD patients receiving treatment from the KATH neurology clinic were eligible.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology Institutional Review Board (Ref No. CHRPE/AP/206/20). Information sheets and consent forms were provided in participants’ native languages and explained in the presence of caregivers or family members. Participants gave written informed consent, witnessed by caregivers, and could withdraw at any time. Data were password-protected to prevent unauthorized access.

Participant recruitment, diagnostic ascertainment and clinical evaluation

Patients meeting the UK Brain Bank criteria for PD, aged ≥ 18 years, were recruited between May 2023 and March 2024. Each case was reviewed by a neurologist, and all individuals were unrelated. Diagnosis of a Parkinsonian Syndrome was based on bradykinesia plus at least one of: muscular rigidity, rest tremor (4–6 Hz), or postural instability unrelated to other causes. Patients with secondary causes or red flags for other Parkinsonism syndromes were excluded. Demographic characteristics, age at onset, family history, clinical history, Hoehn and Yahr stage, and MDS-UPDRS scores were documented. Age at onset was defined as the age at earliest motor symptom. Data from case records were logged in a secure, de-identified database with unique study codes.

Sampling technique

Given the limited pool of PD patients, consecutive sampling was used to recruit all eligible clinic attendees. Identifiers were added to patient records to prevent duplicate assessment.

Data collection methods

Structured questionnaire interviews and complete neurological examinations, including Hoehn and Yahr and MDS-UPDRS assessments, were conducted on the same day of recruitment.

Clinical scales

Movement disorder society – unified parkinson’s disease rating scale (MDS-UPDRS)

The MDS-UPDRS evaluates non-motor and motor experiences of daily living and motor complications. It has four parts: I (mentation, behaviour, mood; 6 items), II (activities of daily living; 13 items), III (motor examination; 27 items), and IV (complications of therapy; 11 items). Each item is scored 0–4, with higher scores indicating greater severity. The maximum total score is 199, reflecting the worst disability.

Hoehn and yahr scale

The Hoehn and Yahr scale classifies PD severity from Stage 1 (unilateral involvement) to Stage 5 (wheelchair-bound or bedridden). Originally published in 1967, it remains a widely used system to assess disease progression.

Data management and analysis

Each participant was assigned a unique ID to protect confidentiality and prevent duplicate entries. Clinical characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using Graph Pad Prism (version 10.2.0, Boston, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test assessed normality of continuous variables. A 5% margin of error was used for all inferential analyses. Variables with sparse data or high multi-collinearity were excluded to improve model stability. In the multivariable logistic regression, some predictors were removed when their inclusion yielded excessively wide confidence intervals, indicating overfitting or instability.

Results

Socio-demographic and Clinical Characteristics of Study Participants:

Of 181 patients approached, 20 were excluded (13 declined, 7 did not have idiopathic PD). Thus, 161 were recruited and analyzed. As shown in Table 1, males constituted 70.8% (M:F ratio 2.4:1). Mean age was 65.2 ± 4.96 years for men and 65.5 ± 5.01 years for women. Early-onset PD (≤ 50 years) accounted for 20.3%. A positive family history of PD was reported by 9.9%.

Additional demographics are summarized in Table S1. Most participants had secondary education (55.9%), while 52.2% were unemployed. Income was expressed in Ghana Cedis (GHS), as a dollar equivalent, and was mainly ≤ 600 Ghana Cedis ($60) (78.9%). Almost 89% obtained medication from a non-profit organization (NPO). Comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes mellitus, occurred in 46 (28.6%) patients. Non-motor complaints included constipation (60.2%), sexual problems (39.8%), and sleep disturbances (33.3%).

Clinical features of PD including motor, non-motor, and hoehn and yahr stages

Among the study participants, the MDS-UPDRS assessment found a mean non-motor score 41.8 ± 21.6 and motor score 37.1 ± 20.5. The most burdensome non-motor symptoms were depressed mood, fatigue, hallucinations, constipation, apathy, and pain (Table S2). High motor impairment was seen in hobbies/activities, handwriting, and tremor (Table S3).

Hoehn and yahr staging

Using the Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging system, participants were distributed across Stages 1 to 4, with Stage 3 being the most common (31.7%). Earlier stages were less frequent, including Stage 1.5, which accounted for 8.1% of the cohort (Fig. 1).

Correlation between non-motor severity and H&Y staging

Non-motor severity showed a moderate positive correlation with advancing H&Y stage (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2 ; however, stage-wise comparisons demonstrated lower mean reported non-motor scores in mid-to-advanced stages.

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics by Stages (early versus mid-advanced) of PD:

Of the 161 participants, 60.9% were in Stages 1–2 (early stages) and 39.1% in Stages 3–5 (mid-to-advanced stages). Most were aged ≥ 65 (62.1%), with no significant age difference between stages. The Akan ethnic group predominated (81.4%). Males were significantly more frequent in mid-to-advanced stages (p = 0.009). Educational attainment differed significantly between the stages of PD with 73.5% of early-stage patients having attained secondary education, compared with 28.6% of mid-to-advanced-stage patients (p < 0.001). Employment status also differed, with unemployment more common in mid-to-advanced stages (63.5%, p < 0.001). Loss of employment due to PD was higher in mid-to-advanced stages (58.7% vs. 22.5%, p < 0.001). Only 88.9% received support from the non-profit organization (NPO), more often in H&Y stages 1–2 (p < 0.001). Levodopa use differed significantly by Hoehn and Yahr stage (p < 0.001). All participants with mid-to-advanced disease were receiving levodopa therapy, compared with 42.9% of those with early-stage disease (p < 0.001). Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were analyzed as comorbidities. Hypertension was presented separately due to its high prevalence in the cohort and the distinction was not intended to imply differing clinical relevance (Table 2).

UPDRS domain scores and treatment patterns

UPDRS Motor scores were significantly higher in mid-to-advanced stages (mean = 19.9 vs. 15.4, p = 0.003). Interestingly, non-motor scores were higher in early-stage patients (mean = 17.6 vs. 12.4, p < 0.001). Regarding UPDRS-based disease severity classification, early stages were mostly mild (45.9%), while moderate disease was more common in mid-to-advanced stages (53.9%, p < 0.001). Medication use was near-universal in mid-to-advanced stages (100% vs. 85.7% in early stages, p = 0.001) (Table S4).

Factors Associated with mid-to-advanced PD:

Multivariable logistic regression identified several independent predictors. Male sex was strongly associated with mid-to-advanced PD (aOR = 20.51; 95% CI: 3.18–132.21; p = 0.001). Higher Income was protective: monthly income ≥ GHS 600 ($60) (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.01–0.51; p = 0.007), and GHS 300–600 ($30-$60) (aOR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.06–0.98; p = 0.046). Loss of employment due to PD increased risk (aOR = 10.95; 95% CI: 1.33–90.22; p = 0.026). Urinary problems were associated with lower odds (aOR = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.01–0.92; p = 0.042). Moderate UPDRS severity predicted mid-to-advanced PD compared to mild (aOR = 4.52; 95% CI: 1.03–19.89; p = 0.046).

Other sociodemographic variables were not significant after adjustment. Full model details are provided in Table 3.

Discussion

In this study, disease stage refers to Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging, with early-stage Parkinson’s disease defined as H&Y stages 1–2 and mid-to-advanced disease defined as stages 3–5. Disease severity refers to Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)-based categories (mild, moderate, severe), derived from total UPDRS scores.

Demographic and general characteristics

The mean ages for men and women were 65.2 and 65.5 years respectively, consistent with a Ghanaian study and a systematic review reporting 65 and 66.7 years [11]. Nearly three-quarters of participants were male, similar to an African study reporting male predominance [12].

Dotchin and colleagues [13] reported mean onset at 69.4 years in Tanzania, later than ours at 59 years. Early-onset cases accounted for 20.3%, and family history was reported in 9.9%. This contrasts with South African data where Early-Onset PD (EOPD) and familial PD were more frequent [14]. Furthermore, a study in Nigeria revealed only 1% reported family history though 16% had early-onset cases [15].

Socioeconomic profiles revealed two-thirds had education beyond no formal/primary level, 20% were employed, and less than a quarter earned ≥ GHS 600 ($60) monthly. About two-thirds lost their jobs, largely due to disability or stigma, echoing global reports of PD-related income loss [16]. It is worth noting that most participants, ∼89%, received medication support from a non-profit organization, providing some relief.

Levodopa remains the cornerstone of PD therapy, yet about one in five patients had not received it. This is troubling, given the robust evidence for levodopa’s efficacy in improving motor symptoms and quality of life. Those not given levodopa were consequently at risk for prolonged disability and potential disease progression. The implications of non-treatment extend beyond symptom control. Patients not given levodopa may face increased caregiver dependency, reduced functional independence, and poorer clinical outcomes, particularly where alternatives are scarce or prohibitively expensive.

Demographic and clinical characteristics by hoehn & yahr stage

Sex, educational level, employment status and support from non-profit organization were significantly associated with H&Y stage. Male sex predominated in mid-to-advanced stages (p = 0.009), consistent with global reports linking male sex to faster progression [6]. Biological mechanisms, including estrogen-mediated neuroprotection and oxidative stress, have been proposed as possible reasons [17]. Women have also been reported to have better motor outcomes [18], possibly explaining their lower representation in mid-to-advanced stages.

Educational attainment was lower in mid-to-advanced-stage participants (p < 0.001). Low education is linked to delayed care-seeking, reduced adherence, and poor recognition of symptoms [19]. Unlike some high-income settings where higher education correlates with PD risk [20], in low-resource settings, low education likely exacerbates barriers to care.

Employment loss was more common in mid-to-advanced stages (p < 0.001). This supports the bidirectional relationship between socioeconomic disadvantage and disease severity: disability causes unemployment, which limits access to care, accelerating progression [21]. Although the majority of participants reported receiving support from a non-profit organization, reliance on external assistance was significantly higher among individuals with mid-to-advanced disease (p < 0.001). This suggests that available support, while important, may be insufficient to offset the financial and functional demands of advanced PD. This mirrors findings from Ghana and Nigeria, where most PD patients rely on out-of-pocket payments, leaving advanced-stage patients at high risk of catastrophic health expenditure [19].

Interestingly, while age, body mass index (BMI), and income were not statistically significant predictors of H&Y stage in our analysis, socioeconomic factors likely outweigh biological determinants. European studies also suggest age may not independently predict progression when other factors are controlled [22], although others have found faster progression in older patients [23].

UPDRS scores and treatment history by H&Y stage

UPDRS motor scores were significantly higher in mid-to-advanced stages, reflecting worsening bradykinesia, rigidity, and instability. This aligns with findings by Raval and colleagues [24], confirming faster motor decline in mid-to-advanced stages (H&Y stages 3–5).

Although mean non-motor scores were lower in mid-to-advanced H&Y stages, correlation analysis demonstrated a moderately strong positive association between non-motor UPDRS-based severity and advancing disease stage (r = 0.52, p < 0.001), indicating that non-motor burden increases across the disease continuum. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects differences between aggregate stage-wise comparisons and continuous analyses [25]. In advanced PD, non-motor symptoms may be under-reported due to reduced patient insight, cognitive impairment, caregiver-mediated responses, or clinical prioritization of motor disability [8]. Similar patterns have been described in other cohorts, where non-motor symptoms peak earlier or plateau despite on-going disease progression [7]. These findings emphasize the complexity of non-motor symptom assessment and suggest that cross-sectional comparisons may underestimate true non-motor burden in advanced disease.

Though contradictory, the lower prevalence of urinary symptoms in mid-to-advanced disease has been reported in some cohorts and may reflect complex autonomic nervous system involvement [29]. It also may not necessarily worsen in parallel with motor disability. Another possible explanation could be that patients with severe autonomic dysfunction may experience higher morbidity and mortality, reducing their representation in mid-to-advanced-stage clinic populations. That notwithstanding, longitudinal studies with systematic non-motor screening are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between motor and non-motor symptom progression in Sub-Saharan African populations.

Severity classification differed significantly, with mild disease pre-dominating in early stages and moderate in mid-to-advanced stages. Medication use also rose with stage, with universal use in mid-to-advanced PD versus 42.9% in early stages. This is consistent with a report from Tanzania, where treatment is often delayed until disability impedes daily function [13]. In high-income settings, therapy typically starts earlier to preserve independence [9]. In low-resource settings, initiation may be delayed by cost, availability, and shortage of trained specialists [10].

The observed stage-related differences in both symptom burden and medication uptake sheds more light on the dual role of clinical and health system factors in shaping PD progression in resource-limited settings. Without structural interventions to improve drug availability and affordability [26], patients remain at risk for avoidable progression.

Predictors of mid-to-advanced PD in ghana

In our study, male sex was the strongest predictor of mid-to-advanced disease (aOR = 20.51; p = 0.001). This is consistent with a study associating male sex with faster progression, increased dementia risk, and greater functional decline [27]. As stated earlier, reasons behind this proposition include biological differences, environmental exposure, and delayed healthcare-seeking in men.

Employment loss due to PD increased odds of mid-to-advanced disease nearly eleven-fold (aOR = 10.95; p = 0.026). This reflects both the direct disability burden and indirect socioeconomic impact, where income loss restricts treatment access and worsens progression [28].

Conversely, higher income was protective, with individuals earning ≥ GHS 600 ($60) per month showing markedly reduced odds of advanced disease (aOR = 0.08; p = 0.007). This supports evidence that socioeconomic advantage improves access to diagnosis, medication adherence, and specialist care [26]. In Ghana’s out-of-pocket system, income directly influences disease trajectory.

Interestingly, urinary problems were associated with reduced odds of mid-to-advanced PD (aOR = 0.09; p = 0.042). Although counterintuitive, similar findings have been noted in some cohorts [29], suggesting that urinary symptoms may occur earlier in the disease course or be influenced by dopaminergic therapy patterns. Survival bias could also partly explain this observation, as patients with more severe autonomic dysfunction may have increased morbidity and mortality before reaching later stages.

Moderate UPDRS severity was a significant predictor of mid-to-advanced PD (aOR = 4.52; p = 0.046), emphasizing the value of UPDRS-based severity classification in prognostic modeling [30]. This association is consistent with literature demonstrating that UPDRS progression predicts both motor and non-motor decline and can serve as a robust staging tool in diverse clinical settings [30].

Future directions

The interplay between socioeconomic and clinical determinants is particularly salient in Ghana, where financial barriers, healthcare access, and employment loss may amplify disease burden. Future prognostic models in low-resource settings should therefore integrate both biomedical and socioeconomic indicators, as this multidimensional approach could enable earlier intervention and more tailored disease management [26].

Furthermore, beyond clinical predictors, our findings highlight the urgent need to strengthen local capacity for Parkinson’s disease (PD) care in Ghana. Scalable, locally adapted technological solutions (such as mobile health platforms for symptom monitoring, tele-neurology consultations, and wearable devices for tracking motor symptoms) may help reduce gaps in specialist access, particularly in underserved regions [31, 32]. Given the increasing penetration of mobile technology in Ghana, such tools could support earlier detection, longitudinal monitoring, and patient-focused care.

In parallel, targeted educational initiatives are essential to build sustainable expertise. Well-organized training programs, international exchange collaborations, and continuous professional development for healthcare workers could enhance early diagnosis and multidisciplinary management of PD [33, 34]. Incorporating PD-centered education into existing medical and allied health curricula, alongside community awareness campaigns, may also reduce diagnostic delays and stigma. Together, these strategies offer practical pathways to address both clinical and systemic drivers of disease progression in resource-limited settings such as Ghana.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its comprehensive evaluation of both clinical and socioeconomic factors in relation to PD stage, aiding a multidimensional understanding of disease progression in a resource-limited setting. The use of standardized UPDRS scoring and H&Y staging supports comparability with other cohorts, while inclusion of non-motor symptoms allows for a more complete disease profile. Additionally, the focus on an African context addresses a critical gap in the PD literature, where region-specific data remain scarce.

However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between identified predictors and disease stage. Symptom data, particularly for non-motor features, were partly reliant on patient and caregiver recall, which may introduce bias. The single-center, specialist clinic setting may limit generalizability to community-based PD populations or those without access to tertiary care. Income and employment data were self-reported and may not fully capture the complexity of socioeconomic status. Finally, unmeasured variables such as environmental exposures and medication adherence over time could influence observed associations.

Conclusion

In this Ghanaian PD cohort, male sex, loss of employment due to PD, and moderate UPDRS severity were strong predictors of mid-to-advanced disease, while higher income was protective. The unexpected protective association of urinary problems highlights the complex nature of non-motor symptoms in PD progression. These findings highlight the need for prognostic models in resource-limited settings to integrate both socioeconomic and clinical variables. Early identification of at-risk individuals, coupled with targeted interventions addressing both biomedical and structural barriers, may help slow disease progression and preserve quality of life in low- and middle-income contexts.
