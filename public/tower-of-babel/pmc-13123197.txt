In vitro evaluation of gamma irradiation versus autoclaving on the morphology and regenerative potential of Allo-demineralized dentin matrix
Authors: Marwa Ahmed Abozed, Iman Fathy, Mariem Osama Wassel, Wael Hamdy El shater, Nagwa Mohamed Ali Khattab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDemineralized dentin matrix (DDM) can serve as a novel scaffold for dental tissue regeneration. This study aimed to assess the impact of two distinct sterilizing methods on morphology of Allo-DDM as well as on the viability, metabolic activity, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs).MethodsAllo-DDM scaffolds were prepared from human-extracted premolars and sterilized using two different techniques. These scaffolds were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to assess their morphology and by Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDAX) to assess the scaffolds’ mineral content. BMMSCs were grouped into four groups: [1] Collagen group (positive control): Stem cells received a collagen scaffold; [2] Gamma-ray group(G-DDM): Stem cells received gamma-sterilized DDM scaffold; [3] Autoclave group(A-DDM): Stem cells received autoclave-sterilized DDM scaffold), and [4] Negative Control group: Stem cells were provided with culture media only. Cell viability and metabolic activity were assessed using trypan blue and MTT assays, while cell differentiation was evaluated using the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) assay.ResultsSEM micrographs revealed that G-DDM had a higher number of dentinal tubules than the A-DDM group, while there were no significant differences between G-DDM and A-DDM in the EDAX analysis. Cell viability and metabolic activity assessment showed that the collagen group had significantly higher viability, followed by G-DDM, while A-DDM and negative control groups did not improve BMMSC viability metabolic activity after 48 h. The ALP assessment revealed that cells significantly differentiated in collagen and G-DDM groups than in A-DMM and control groups.ConclusionGamma sterilization more effectively preserved the structural, chemical, and biological characteristics of Allo-DDM scaffolds compared to autoclaving. Gamma-treated scaffolds outperformed autoclaved scaffolds in terms of cell survival, metabolic activity, and ALP activity.Keywords: Autoclaving, Biomimetic scaffold, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, Collagen, Demineralized dentin matrix, Gamma irradiation, Regenerative scaffold

Introduction

Regenerative endodontic treatments can change endodontics and the entire field of dentistry. These novel treatments seek to reduce dental procedures’ invasiveness, significantly increasing patients’ overall quality of life [1]. One of the critical elements of this emerging technique is a greater emphasis on keeping natural teeth. Three important regenerative medicine components, scaffolds, cells, and signaling molecules, are the basis of advanced regeneration procedures [2]. Integrating these three components in infected root canals is a significant issue [3].

In tissue engineering, a scaffold material serves as a three-dimensional framework upon which new regeneration of tissues can take place [4]. Some scaffolds also have the capability of slowly releasing growth factors and other bioactive substances, which further improves cellular activity and tissue repair [5]. Several scaffold materials capable of pulp regeneration have been discovered. For example, collagen, chitosan, or a mix of the two can be used as scaffolds [6]. Nevertheless, the search for an ideal scaffold is still ongoing.

Demineralized dentin matrix (DDM) is a biocompatible scaffold that consists of dentin’s organic elements and growth factors responsible for tissue regeneration and tooth preservation. In contrast to the typical collagen membranes, DDM offers the possibility of increasing patient outcomes considerably, as it creates an optimal environment for stem cells’ proliferation and differentiation, which is of special importance in the case of immature permanent teeth with necrotic pulps and open apexes [7].

Processed dentin is a composite of collagen and growth factors, the major organic components that are the basis for its structure and functions [8]. After the elimination of the mineral component of dentin, the organic matrix becomes a niche that either supports stem cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation into odontoblast-like cells [9] or activates inflammation that creates an environment that is beneficial for healing of the injured tissues. A variety of bioactive stimuli precisely regulates odontoblast differentiation, each performing a different function. For an instance, transforming growth factors promote cell growth and proliferation, epidermal growth factors enhance tissue regeneration, fibroblast growth factors help tissue development, and bone morphogenic proteins (BMPs) are needed to form bone and dental tissues [10]. Collagen type I (COL-I) provides the structural support for the dentin matrix [10]. Once the matrix is formed, bioactive components incorporate into the new dentin, initiating a complex signaling dialogue that maintains and regulates dentinogenesis [10].

Given its rich content of natural proteins and bioactive substances, DDM serves as an important resource for studies on bone and dentin regeneration [10]. This allows for the bio-recycling of teeth, which are otherwise considered dental waste [11]. Previous research has already used DDM to create unique regeneration scaffolds and bone grafts to improve healing capabilities [11]. The quantity and quality of tissue available for autogenous demineralized dentin matrix (Auto-DDM) application is limited by dental cavities or restorations present in the harvested teeth [12]. Thus, allogeneic demineralized dentin matrix (Allo-DDM), which is obtained from a different donor, has emerged as an alternative to the patient-derived Auto-DDM. This technique effectively overcomes the limitations of using autogenous tissue and allows for broader clinical application [13].

Allo-DDM is considered an effective and safe substitute for standard regeneration materials used for various dental surgical and regenerative procedures [13, 14]. As Allo-DDM scaffolds are derived from human tissue, they require efficient sterilization and processing to ensure their benefits are maintained without the potential hazard of disease transmission [15, 16]. Yet, there is little research evaluating the effect of sterilizing processes on the metabolic activity and osteogenic capabilities of Allo-DDM. Therefore, the current study aims to address this knowledge gap by thoroughly investigating the impact of two different sterilizing methods (gamma irradiation and autoclaving) on the morphology, mineral content and some biological properties of Allo-DDM; the null hypothesis was that there were no significant differences between Allo-DDM sterilized by either gamma irradiation or autoclaving compared to typical collagen membrane. This study could provide valuable insights into optimizing sterilization practices for Allo-DDM, thereby enhancing its application in clinical settings [17].

Methods

Study design

This in-vitro study adhered to the PRILE guidelines for reporting laboratory studies in endodontology and the MIBO guidelines for reporting essential information regarding studies on mesenchymal stem cells [18, 19]. The present study was conducted in full accordance with the World Medical Declaration of Helsinki, and the institutional research ethics committee at Ain Shams University ethically approved the collection and storage of teeth, animal handling, and all experimental protocols (ID: FDASU-Rec-IR092204).

Recipient details

According to previous research [20], bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) were isolated from white albino rats. Rats were obtained from the institutional experimental research center at Ain Shams University, and the isolation process was carried out in the Stem Cells and Biomaterials Central Laboratory at Ain Shams University.

Study groups

For vitality and ALP assessment, four distinct BMMSC groups were used, as detailed below:

Collagen Group (positive control): In this group, stem cells received a collagen scaffold (Buk-gu, Busan, Republic of Korea), which offered a natural support matrix critical for cellular attachment and growth.

Control Group: stem cells were cultured in a culture medium without any scaffold (i.e., negative control).

Fabrication of Allo-DDM

To our knowledge, there is no standard demineralization approach for human DDM. Thus, the technique must optimize demineralizing agent concentration, pH, and exposure duration. Before demineralization, dentin matrix must be prepared to eliminate mechanical instrument debris.

Collection of dentin tissue

Five freshly extracted premolars, collected for orthodontic treatment, were used to produce Allogeneic Demineralized Dentin Matrix (Allo-DDM) [9]. The guardians of children undergoing orthodontic treatment provided written informed consent to use these teeth for research. These extracted teeth were stored in 250 ml of normal saline solution (0.5%) at a regulated temperature of 4 °C for a maximum of one month according to the Guidelines of Good Practice for Tooth Handling Institution from the Korea Ministry of Health and Welfare (KMHW) [21].

During the preparation phase, the periodontal ligaments and cementum were carefully scraped from the root surfaces using a low-speed diamond grinding stone (Diamond bur, Rodent AG, Seoul, Korea), and a flow of water cooling was maintained to minimize heat damage. The crowns of the teeth were removed with a low-speed diamond disk (Isomet 1000; Buehler International, Inc., Lake Bluff, IL, USA), underwater cooling, the radicular pulp tissue was meticulously extirpated with a size 25 K-file and saline irrigation. To create the necessary samples, dentin tissue was harvested from both the coronal and middle thirds of the root structure and cut into thin disks of 1 mm in thickness. Likewise, the slicing operation was carried out with a low-speed diamond disk and continuous water cooling to preserve tissue integrity [9, 22].

Decalcification

The dentin disks were carefully immersed in a decalcifying solution containing 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 min, followed by a second exposure in a 10% EDTA solution for an additional 5 min. Each disk was subsequently immersed in a 5% EDTA solution for 5 min to facilitate effective decalcification [9, 14, 22].

Following decalcification, each dentin specimen underwent two washes with 20 ml of distilled water for 5 h and was ultrasonically cleaned every hour for 5 min in an ultrasonic cleanser (Kudos, Shanghai, China) at 53 kHz to remove any residual decalcifying agents and ensure the complete elimination of the EDTA solution. After washing, the specimens were air-dried overnight in a laminar airflow fume hood (Standard Fume Hood BFSD-201), ensuring a sterile environment and preserving the integrity of each sample [9, 22].

Grinding and sieving

The grinding process adopted in the present study produced granules with a target size range of 200 to 400 microns. A particular grinding device with a homogenizer (HG-15D; Wise Tis, Wonju, Korea) was employed. After 3 to 5 s, the grinding cycle was repeated ten times. The granules were then grounded and stirred thoroughly for 20 s with sieves of various mesh within the specified range of 200 to 400 microns [23] (Central Laboratory - Faculty of Science - Ain Shams University – Egypt).

This procedure resulted in a fine powder that was subsequently divided into two specimens of 10 mg DDM powder. The first specimen was divided by weight, and each 5 mg powder was put in an Autoclave Sterilization self-sealing 3.5 × 10 inches pouch (AMZ Medical Supplies Ltd., London, UK) for autoclave sterilization. For the second specimen, a 60 × 60 + 5 cm pouch with 50 microns (Silver Dragon Industrial Ltd., Guangdong, China) thickness was used to obtain the best protection during the gamma sterilization process [23, 24].

Sterilization

After passage through a rigorous autoclaving process consisting of exposure to a high temperature of 121 °C and an impressive pressure of 9.6 MPa, the first sealed specimen was sterilized for 25 min. It was then allowed to acclimate and stabilize at ambient temperature [25, 26].

One was divided into two specimens of 5 mg each, and the other specimen was divided by weight into two specimens of 5 mg each. Gamma Radiation (Co-60) was used for each specimen. With 25 kGy, a multifunctional irradiator (CTR/IPEN/CNEN-SP) was used in a room temperature and pressure at a dose rate of 1 kGy per unit of time (National Research Centre - Giza, Dokki, Egypt) The specimens are immediately stored in a refrigerator at 4°C to preserve their integrity [25].

Isolation and characterization of bone marrow stem cells

Isolation of bone marrow stem cells

Two healthy albino rats (6 weeks of age) were used for the extraction of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs). Following euthanasia, the intact femurs and tibiae were carefully dissected under sterile conditions. To preserve cell viability and prevent contamination, the freshly isolated bones were immediately immersed in a sterile transfer medium consisting of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., MA, USA) supplemented with antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) and an antifungal agent (fungizone). Within a laminar flow cabinet, the epiphyses of each bone were excised aseptically using sterile scissors. The bone marrow was then flushed out by inserting a sterile needle and gently aspirating the marrow cavity. The collected marrow was carefully resuspended in a complete culture medium composed of 80 ml DMEM, 15 ml fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., MA, USA), and 2–3 ml of the antibiotic-antifungal mixture. The resulting cell suspension was transferred into T75 culture flasks and incubated under optimal conditions to allow for cell adhesion, proliferation, and subsequent experimental assessment [27].

Cell culturing

The final supplemented culture medium containing the needed nutrients for growth was used to establish cell cultures. They placed the culture flasks into a humidified incubator (Thermo Scientific HERAcell VIOS 160i CO2 incubator) with an infrared sensor set to 37 °C under the atmosphere of 5% CO2, 95% air by volume. Accordingly, they created the best conditions for the multiplication of the cells. The health and development of the cultured cells were evaluated daily via an inverted light microscope (Leica DMi8-Automated Inverted Fluorescence Microscope). This method allowed a complete cell morphology analysis and early potential bacterial or fungal contamination detection in the cultures [22].

The non-adherent cells were then incubated for 48 h, following which the supernatant was taken away carefully by sterile pipette aspiration to avoid contamination. To further cleanse the environment, the adherent cells of the culture flasks were again washed twice with sterile PBS. After, 10 ml of fresh complete media was added to each dish to properly maintain the cultured cells for the continuation, which would need to grow in a set of environments suitable for their growth and maintenance [28].

Characterization of BMMSCs

On the third culture passage, BMMSC was identified as cells with characteristic morphology, and BMMSC had a distinct spindle-shaped morphology. A flow cytometric analysis with distinct immunophenotypic characteristics of the isolated cells was then carried out. Further, we found that CD34 and CD45 were not expressed, but that extensive expression of CD90 and CD105 was observed. Immunophenotypic assessment confirmed a stemness of MSCs, validating their identification and potential research use [29].

Expanding the cell culture

Subcultures were maintained by cells reaching 70–80% confluence. The first step consisted of carefully washing the cells with PBS to wash off the residual media and detach weakly adhering cells. Then, 1 ml of 0.05% trypsin/EDTA solution (Lonza®) was added to the culture flask, and the cell detachment process was observed for 2 to 5 min to attend to the detached cells. The cells remained rounded and detached from the flask surface during this period. To inactivate trypsin and cease its activity, 5 ml of DMEM-based media was added to the flask to prevent cell damage. The cell suspension was transferred to a 10 ml test tube for further processing, consisting of trypsin and detached cells in a medium. Centrifugation of the tube was done for 5 min at 600 rpm with Thermo Scientific Heraeus Fresco 21 microcentrifuge. After this, the supernatant was gently removed, and a pellet formed at the bottom of the tube. Afterward, the pellet was resuspended in a 3 ml complete medium to uniform cell distribution and was ready for further growth. Subsequently, the resuspended cell solution was divided into T-75 flasks at a 1:2 ratio for further growth until cells had about 80% confluence [27, 28].

Outcomes evaluation

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

For SEM analysis, one pouch of each sterilization method was used. The analysis was performed in an advanced Environmental SEM field emission gun (FEG ESEM), the ThermoFisher (USA) Quattro S Field Emission Gun, Environmental SEM and used the APEX™ Standard Version (V1.3; AMETEK Inc., Pennsylvania USA). The advanced arrangement in this case included the Low-Vacuum Secondary Electron Detector (LVD) ranging from 20 to 200 Pa. SEM stubs were used to position scaffolds as they provided stability and good morphological conditions for assessment [30]. The levels of calcium (Ca) and phosphate (P) in the scaffold were quantified using Energy Dispersive X ray Spectroscopy (EDAX-APEX), and this is an essential parameter of assessing the efficiency of the sterilization process as well as the alterations suffered by the material [11, 30]. The collagen membrane and one pouch were analyzed using SEM analysis to assess topical morphology.

Cytotoxicity evaluation (cell viability test)

BMMSCs of the third passage were carefully implanted on different testing materials with an initial concentration of 103 cells/ml and incubated for 48 h in a humidified incubator at 37 ˚C and 5% CO2 with 95% air. After the incubation, the cells were counted on a hemocytometer by mixing 25 𝜇L of the collected cell suspension and 25 𝜇L of Trypan Blue (T.B.) solution in the buffer. This combined reagent was then analyzed with the Invitrogen™ Countess™ Automated Cell Counter because the Trypan Blue stain was apparent due to the good definition of live and non-live cells since trypan blue does not go to the dead cells. Hence, the living and dead cells are distinguishable. Following the automated counting process, the data was used to calculate the percentage of viable cells using the formula: . The total number of viable cells per milliliter of culture was estimated by calculating . This process was so rigorous that the correct measurement of cell viability was performed, and valuable data for continuing the investigation was provided [31].

The metabolic activity, after the plating of BMMSCs onto the different tested scaffolds for 48 h, was determined by the MTT assay on bone marrow cells. Then, we incubated our culture medium, replenishing 100 µL of it with fresh media for keeping conditions in optimal conditions, and each well received 20 µL of 1 mg/mL MTT solution to start the MTT reduction process. After the plates had been incubated in a cultured atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. The MTT solution was removed, and 100 µL of SDS with HCl was added to each well to suspend the formazan crystals. By performing this process, we ensured that all the formazans had dissolved and could be measured accurately. Cell survival was determined using a spectrophotometer by following the optical density of cells at 570 nm and giving valuable insight into the efficacy of the different scaffolds tested [11, 31].

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the ALP activity assay was performed using the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (Fluorometric) ab83371 (Abcam, MA, USA). After a 48-hour treatment at a 400 µg/mL concentration, supernatant was collected from bone marrow cells. It compared the impact of the treatment to the results obtained from the untreated control cells. ALP’s enzymatic activity was used in the assay as it cleaves the phosphate group off the nonfluorescent substrate 4-methylumbelliferyl phosphate disodium salt (MUP). The enzymatic process produces a fluorescent signal from the dephosphorylated product measured at 360/440 nm excitation/emission. The fluorescence intensity was quantified using the FLUOSTAR OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH GmbH, Ortenberg, Germany) [11].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. Normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For data that met both normality and homogeneity assumptions, comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For data that did not meet these assumptions, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons was used. All tests were two-tailed and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23 (IBM. Armonk, USA).

Discussion

Scaffolds are considered a key element in regenerative endodontics. In clinical applications, scaffold biomaterials are required to exhibit nontoxicity, non-infectiousness, and biocompatibility [32]. Among various scaffold materials, Allo-DDM, derived from human teeth, has emerged as a promising candidate in tissue engineering, particularly for its capacity to promote osteoconduction and foster dentin regeneration [6, 7, 16]. However, the risk of infection remains a significant concern [33]. Therefore, the objective of this study was to thoroughly evaluate how different sterilization techniques, either Gamma radiation or steam autoclaving, could impact the physical, chemical, and biological attributes of Allo-DDM scaffolds.

In preparing DDM, sequential exposure of dentin pieces to chelating agents like EDTA at varying concentrations and durations is needed. The implemented protocol of the present study [22] (17% EDTA for 10 min, followed by 10% EDTA for 5 min, and then 5% EDTA for another 5 min) leverages EDTA’s chelating ability to demineralize dentin while preserving its organic matrix, especially collagen [25]. Studies showed that higher EDTA concentrations and longer exposure times increase the demineralizing effect but may also induce a more degraded and detached collagen even when applied for a few minutes [11, 14, 22]. On the other hand, the application of lower EDTA concentrations, such as 5% EDTA, was shown to cause minimal collagen modification yet a diminished extent of dentin demineralization, suggesting that a balance between effective demineralization and preservation of the organic matrix is crucial [34]. It is essential to note that the demineralization process removes hydroxyapatite but retains type I collagen and non-collagenous proteins, including growth factors like BMPs and TGF-β, which are essential for osteoinduction and odontogenic differentiation [15, 35].

The European Association of Tissue Banks (EATB) and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) accept a gamma radiation dose of 25 kGy as the standard sterilizing level for biological tissues, ensuring a sterility assurance level (SAL) of 10⁻⁶ [36]. The American Association of Tissue Banks (AATB) recommends a perhaps less harmful dose of 15 kGy to minimize structural changes of live tissues such as bone matrix [37]. Dentin matrix is distinct from bone matrix due to absence of vascular and cellular components, rendering it less susceptible to viral transmission [3]. Nonetheless, guaranteeing viral inactivation is crucial for allogeneic applications. In that context, research has indicated that both 15 and 25 kGy effectively inactivate viruses like HBV without substantially compromising the characteristics of tissues such as bone or dentin matrix [38, 39].

In the present investigation, SEM analysis revealed that autoclaved A-DDM had fewer, narrow and corrugated dentinal tubules. This finding supports earlier suggestions that high temperatures and pressures during autoclaving can compromise the dentin matrix’s integrity and structural characteristics [26, 36]. For instance, Parsell et al., 1998 [40] demonstrated how heat and pressure can break the ionic bonds between collagen and hydroxyapatite, causing denaturation of the organic matrix components. Such denaturation can severely affect the material’s performance in dental applications. Likewise, Silva et al., 2018 [41] found that high-temperature sterilization of autoclaving considerably modified the physical and morphological features of both enamel and dentin. These findings align with our observations, which revealed corrugation of collagen fibers, collapse, and narrowing of dentinal tubule lumens after DDM autoclaving. Such alterations led Soares et al., 2011 [42] to conclude that autoclaving is inappropriate for maintaining the demineralized dentin’s architectural integrity necessary for cell attachment. Collectively, these morphological changes may impair mechanical stability in load-bearing applications and limit cellular infiltration and vascularization needed for tissue regeneration [34].

Conversely, in the present study, SEM analysis revealed that gamma sterilization at 25 kGy effectively preserved dentinal tubule openings, suggesting the preservation of the collagen matrix’s integrity. This aligns with previous investigations reporting minimal morphological changes in DDM after gamma sterilization [26, 27, 41]. Ku et al., 2022 [27] demonstrated the efficacy of gamma sterilization in preserving the original composition and biological functionality of DDM, which is essential for encouraging stem cell migration, adhesion, and proliferation within tissue engineering scaffolds such as those used for pulp tissue regeneration [43]. Furthermore, it was reported that while gamma rays can break down collagen’s covalent bonds, they may also promote new inter- or intramolecular cross-links, which could help preserve the fibril structure and scaffold characteristics. This minimal degradation can also enhance the release of growth factors [44–46]. Supporting this, Sultan et al., 2023 [11] and Wong et al., 2014 [47] found that gamma-sterilized scaffolds successfully retained essential components like collagen, which provides greater surface area and interconnectivity necessary for cell attachment and vascular ingrowth, along with other bioactive compounds vital for cell survival and growth. These findings demonstrate that gamma sterilization is an effective way to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the DDM.

EDAX elemental analysis revealed significant differences among groups in the levels of key mineral components such as calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), and carbon (C) as well as Ca/P ratio. The Ca/P ratio is closely associated with the osteoconductive potential of the scaffold [23, 48]. In the current study, a lower Ca/P ratio and a higher C content in G-DDM compared to A-DDM indicates an enhanced organic content, supporting the preservation of the protein component which includes growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) in G-DDM. BMPs are known to play a crucial role in osteoinduction. Conversely, the Ca/P ratio was higher in A-DDM than in G-DDM. This could be due to the loss of collagen integrity which was observed in A-DDM through SEM analysis. This loss would reduce the collagen surface area relative to the inorganic content, thereby increasing the Ca/P ratio per surface area. Notably, the collagen membrane group had the highest carbon content, suggesting a primary organic composition due to the quantity of collagen in the material.

The present investigation utilized rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) to evaluate cell viability and ALP activity owing to their multipotent differentiation capacity, established osteogenic potential, ease of isolation and culturing, and cost-effectiveness, which collectively provides a reliable cell source for research [20, 32]. The Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was assessed as an essential biomarker for osteogenic differentiation [9].

Results showed that both G-DDM and A-DDM promoted the ALP activity of BMMSCs, with G-DDM showing significantly higher mean values. This observation is consistent with the findings of de Sousa et al., 2022 [49], Jiang et al., 2019 [26], who noted that gamma sterilization preserved the native dentin architecture and bioactive molecules vital for promoting cellular infiltration, and nutrient exchange and thereby osteoinduction. Interestingly, research conducted by Han et al., 2008 [50], revealed that exposure to high irradiation doses, specifically in the 0 to 25 kGy range, enhanced the bone formation potential rather than its reduction. Additionally, evidence suggests that gamma rays can cause minimal denaturation of collagen, leading to a quicker release of growth factors from demineralized bone matrix and a shorter time required for osteoinductivity [32].

In contrast, the A-DDM showed considerably lower metabolic activity and ALP levels, suggesting that the observed collagen structural changes and the reported reduction of growth factors [36], associated with the high autoclaving temperature may substantially degrade the scaffold’s osteoinductive capacities [39], Han et al., 2008 similarly observed that autoclaving bone transplant materials significantly decreased their osteoinductive ability [50].

Interestingly, results also revealed that the collagen group had the maximum metabolic activity and ALP activity compared to G-DD and A-DDM. This is consistent with previous studies, which suggest that culturing stem cells on collagen helps to achieve high metabolic activity, proliferation, and differentiation in comparison to other matrices [44]. Consequently, collagen can be used as a vehicle to introduce DDM into in vivo application [42]. To act as a carrier for DDM particles [49].

It is worth noting that while in vitro studies show promising in vitro osteoinduction, the in vivo environment introduces additional variables such as immune responses, vascularization, and the presence of competing cell types. These factors can influence the scaffold’s integration and regenerative performance, making outcomes less predictable than in controlled laboratory settings [35, 51]. Although DDM is biocompatible and has been used safely in clinical studies, the potential for immune reactions, especially with allogeneic or xenogeneic sources remain a concern. The immune system’s response to residual proteins or incomplete decellularization can affect scaffold survival and bone or dentin regeneration.

It is also crucial to emphasize that achieving predictable revascularization and integration of DDM in clinical settings is difficult. The scaffold’s porosity, particle size, and surface characteristics must support rapid vascular ingrowth and cell colonization which are essential for successful regeneration but are still not fully replicated in vitro [35, 50, 51]. Additionally, there is currently no standardized vehicle or delivery system for DDM inside root canals. Therefore, future research should focus on optimizing these parameters and developing well characterized DDM products tailored for specific clinical indications.

Study limitations

While SEM-EDAX provided useful insights into scaffold surface morphology and elemental composition, it cannot differentiate between crystalline and amorphous phases, limiting its ability to comprehensively assess dentin mineralization. Additionally, although qualitative microstructural observations were made, the resolution of some SEM images was insufficient for detailed visualization of dentinal tubules or particle-level features. Quantitative analysis of particle size and porosity was not performed, as the study was designed to compare the biological impact of sterilization methods rather than providing full microstructural characterization.

Additionally, the gamma irradiation dose gradient within the scaffolds was not precisely measured. This means it was not possible to quantify or control the exact radiation dose at every point in the complex structure. Consequently, different sections of the scaffold may have received slightly different sterilization doses, potentially compromising the material’s integrity or affecting cell response. There was a lack of direct quantification of key growth factors. Only the ALP activity was measured, which is just a preliminary biomarker for bone activity, while the actual signaling molecules (growth factors) crucial for robust tissue regeneration were not assessed. Finally, the experiment lacked complementary cellular morphological data, such as live/dead or cytoskeletal imaging. This absence prevented a direct assessment of cell viability, adherence, and proper cellular spreading, which are essential indicators of the scaffolds’ biological efficacy. These areas represent critical avenues for future research using advanced imaging and analytical techniques.

Conclusion

This study showed that gamma-sterilized scaffolds at 25 kGy exhibited better performance than autoclaved scaffolds in terms of dentin surface morphology, cell viability, metabolic activity, and ALP activity. These results suggest that gamma sterilization may be more suitable than autoclaving for preserving the structural and biological properties of Allo-DDM scaffolds. However, further studies, including molecular and in vivo evaluations, are required to confirm these findings and to validate the potential of gamma-sterilized DDM in bone and dental tissue engineering applications.
