Incentivizing In-depth Reasoning over Long Contexts with Process Advantage Shaping
Miao Peng, Weizhou Shen, Nuo Chen, Chenliang Li, Ming Yan, Jia Li

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has proven effective in enhancing LLMs short-context reasoning, but its performance degrades in long‑context scenarios that require both precise grounding and robust long‑range reasoning. We identify the “almost-there” phenomenon—trajectories that are largely correct but fail at the final step—in long-context reasoning RL and attribute this failure to two factors: (1) the lack of high reasoning density in long-context QA data that push LLMs beyond mere grounding toward sophisticated multi-hop reasoning; and (2) the loss of valuable learning signals during long-context RL training due to the indiscriminate penalization of partially correct trajectories with incorrect outcomes. To overcome this bottleneck, we propose DeepReasonQA , a KG-driven synthesis framework that controllably constructing high-difficulty, multi-hop long-context QA pairs with inherent reasoning chains. Building on this, we introduce Long-context Process Advantage Shaping ( LongPAS ), a simple yet effective method that performs fine-grained credit assignment by evaluating reasoning steps along Validity and Relevance dimensions, which captures critical learning signals from “almost-there” trajectories. Experiments on three long-context reasoning benchmarks show that our approach substantially outperforms RLVR baselines and matches frontier LLMs while using far fewer parameters. Further analysis confirms the effectiveness of our methods in strengthening long-context reasoning while maintaining stable RL training.

Introduction

Reasoning over long contexts is a critical capability for modern large language models (LLMs), as many real‑world tasks—such as document understanding
(
6

7
)
or agentic deep research
(

—require grounding information and perform complex reasoning across millions of tokens

;
23
;
18
. While advanced LLMs have successfully employ RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) to enhance short-context complex reasoning skills
14
;
45
, performance still degrades significantly when confronted with long-context scenarios. Recent work has explored improving long‑context capability in RL by progressively scaling the context window
(
39
)
, or by using data‑driven RL approaches to learn plan–retrieve–reason patterns
(
40
)
. However, being primarily focused on improving information grounding, these methods still struggle with in-depth reasoning over long-context documents.

A key reason is that they suffer from the limitation of current outcome‑based RLVR algorithms

;
32
;
9
: These methods uniformly rely on sparse, outcome‑level rewards, making it inherently difficult to distinguish the common “almost‑there” cases—instances where most reasoning steps are correct but the final answer is wrong. Such reward sparsity causes RLVR to ignore valuable learning signals contained in these partially correct trajectories, a problem that becomes especially severe in complex long‑context tasks where these cases are frequent. To empirically validate this, we examined Entity Coverage and Triple Coverage across varying uncertainty levels in Figure
1
.
It reveals that in high-uncertainty samples (accuracy around 50%), the model successfully anchors most critical entities but falters during information integration and final reasoning (Further analysis in Section
3.1
). Despite their potential to provide valuable learning signals
(
4
)
, these “almost-there” trajectories are effectively penalized as failures under sparse reward regimes.

While integrating process-level supervision appears to be a direct remedy for this sparsity, the unique characteristics of long-context tasks impose significant challenges: (1)
The Sparsity of Reasoning Density
: Unlike logic-heavy short-context tasks, key information in long documents is highly scattered. This “reasoning-grounding imbalance” makes it difficult for LLMs to incentivize high-quality reasoning patterns without being overwhelmed by contextual noise. Besides, the vast search space of long-form text hinders effective construction of high-quality, step-level data with explicit dependencies. (2)
The Indeterminacy of Credit Assignment
: Many steps involve mere context grounding or document browsing, making it impractical to directly pinpoint which specific step or reasoning hop contributed to an ”almost-there” failure, resulting in difficult and noisy fine-grained supervision signal assignment.
These limitations collectively raise the following key questions:

1.

How to construct long-context QA data that possesses both “high reasoning density” and “explicit step dependencies,” along with reliable step-level supervision signals?

2.

How can these step-level supervision signals be utilized to achieve fine-grained credit assignment and mitigate reward sparsity within the RL training process?

To resolve these problems, we propose to shift the paradigm from mining supervision in noisy natural data to constructing supervision via controlled synthesis. We first propose KG-driven long-range multi-hop QA synthesis framework: We automatically extract and construct high-difficulty, complex multi-hop QA dataset
DeepReasonQA
from sparse long documents at scale, covering critical multi-hop reasoning scenarios. Besides, the controllable construction process inherently produces explicit reasoning chains alongside QA pair, which offers fine-grained step-level supervision. To enable effective RL training, we introduce Long-context Process Advantage Shaping (

) to improve credit assignment in long-context RL.
LongPAS

Validity
and

Our comprehensive evaluation on both in-domain and out-of-domain long-context multi-hop reasoning benchmarks demonstrates the high quality of
DeepReasonQA
and the effectiveness of
LongPAS
, surpassing strong RLVR baselines across multiple LLM series and achieving performance comparable to frontier LLMs with substantially larger parameters. Critically, beyond achieving superior accuracy,
LongPAS
also induces more effective reasoning policies that exhibit more precise and accurate information grounding, as well as more complex reasoning over long-context documents. Further analyses validate that our core algorithms support stable RL training and improve step-wise reasoning validity in challenging long-context reasoning scenarios.

2
Preliminary

Long-context RLVR

Given a question
Q
and a long context
C
including a set of documents
c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n}
, the standard long-context RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward) framework aims to optimize a policy
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid C,Q)
that generates the answer
A
to the question. The objective is to maximize the expected verifiable reward
r_{\text{ans}}(y)
, which typically evaluates the correctness of the final answer through verifiable rules or preference reward model:

J_{\text{ans}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(C,Q)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y\mid C,Q)}[r_{\text{ans}}(y)]\right],

where
D
denotes the training set.

Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
31
)
is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
30
)
. GRPO maintains the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and a reference “old” policy
\pi_{old}
. Given a prompt
(C,Q)\sim\mathcal{D}
, GRPO first samples a group of
N
candidate answers
\bm{y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{G}\}
from the old policy
\pi_{old}(y\mid C,Q)
. For each answer
y_{i}\in\bm{y}
, the verifiable reward
r_{i}=r_{\text{ans}}(y_{i})
is computed. GRPO estimates the advantage using the relative quality of answers within this group.
The normalized advantage
\hat{A}_{i}
for the
i
-th answer is then defined as:

\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{n}\})}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{n}\})}.

The policy
\pi_{\theta}
is optimized by maximizing the following objective function:

J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{(C,Q),\bm{y}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\epsilon}\left(\rho_{i}(\theta),\hat{A}_{i}\right)\right]-\beta\cdot\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{(C,Q)}\left[\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid C,Q)\|\pi_{old}(\cdot\mid C,Q)]\right],

where
\hat{\mathbb{E}}
denotes the empirical expectation over samples from
\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{y}\sim\pi_{old}^{N}
. The term
\rho_{i}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid C,Q)}{\pi_{old}(y_{i}\mid C,Q)}
represents the probability ratio.
f_{\epsilon}(x,A)=\min(xA,\text{clip}(x,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A)
is the standard PPO clipping function, and
\beta
is a hyper-parameter controlling the KL divergence penalty.

3
Methodology

3.1
The Challenge of Almost-there Phenomenon in Long-Context Reasoning

Sparse reward signals often hinder RLVR when tackling complex long-context tasks. Given that these long trajectories consist of essential reasoning components like retrieval and information synthesis, they frequently harbor correct sub-steps even when the final output is wrong. Therefore, indiscriminate penalization based solely on final failures can be counterproductive, as it risks suppressing valid reasoning behaviors and undermining overall performance.

To quantify the prevalence of accurate reasoning steps within the trajectories of long-context multi-hop problems, we conduct an empirical analysis on FRAMES dataset
(
18
)
using Qwen3-4B model. Specifically, we leverage Gemini-2.5-Pro to identify the key ground-truth reasoning chains
P
for each QA pair
(Q,A)
in FRAMES. They are formally structured as chained triplets
(s_{i},r_{i},o_{i})
, such as (DeMar DeRozan)
-
[played for]
\to
(San Antonio Spurs)
-
[competed in the]
\to
(Western Conference)… For each question, we perform N rollouts
{T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{N}}
and categorize them based on the ratio of positive outcomes. Subsequently, we calculate the entity coverage and triplet coverage between each negative rollout (trajectories with false outcome), denoted as
T_{f}
, and its corresponding ground-truth reasoning chain
P
using the following formulas:

Entity Coverage
measures the proportion of correct entities in
P
that are present in
T_{f}
, where
\mathcal{E}(T_{f})
and
\mathcal{E}(P)
are the sets of entities in
T_{f}
and
P
, respectively:

\text{Entity Coverage}(T_{f},P)=\frac{|\mathcal{E}(T_{f})\cap\mathcal{E}(P)|}{|\mathcal{E}(P)|},

Triplet Coverage
assessment relies on an LLM (LLM-as-a-judge) to evaluate the correctness of each step in
T_{f}
. We define
\mathcal{R}(T_{f})
as the set of triplets in
T_{f}
, and
\mathbb{I}_{Judge}(R,P)
as an indicator function that equals 1 if the LLM judge deems the triplet
R\in\mathcal{R}(T_{f})
to be a valid and correct reasoning step present in
P
, and 0 otherwise. The Triplet Coverage is then calculated as:

\text{Triplet Coverage}(T_{f},P)=\frac{\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}(T_{f})}\mathbb{I}_{Judge}(R,P)}{|\mathcal{R}(P)|}.

We report the distributions of Entity & Triplet Coverage between negative rollouts and ground-truth reasoning chains in Figure
1
, and the empirical results reveal that the model’s “errors” are not uniform. As the Group Positive Ratio increases, suggesting decreased question difficulty, both Entity and Triplet Coverage of negative rollouts consistently improve, indicating that even failed trajectories are often containing high-ratio true reasoning steps.
Furthermore, the significantly higher Entity Coverage compared to Triplet Coverage demonstrates that while the model has a strong ability of information grounding, its capability to organize a strict logical chain remains a greater challenge.
Crucially, we observe that the coverage scores peak and remain above the overall average when the Group Positive Ratio is around 50%. These samples represent questions where the model is on the boundary of success and failure, making them particularly valuable for RL training
(
4
)
. This finding underscores the necessity of implementing reward mechanisms that avoid incorrectly penalizing the correct sub-steps present within these “almost-there” trajectories.

3.2
Multi-hop Reasoning QA Synthesis

To address the scarcity of high-reasoning-density QA pairs that necessitate long-range reasoning over long-context documents, we propose a Knowledge-Guided Long-Context Multi-hop QA Synthesis Framework. As shown in Figure
2
. This framework automates the extraction and construction of highly complex, multi-hop QA pairs from sparse, noisy, and long documents, thereby bypassing the need for manual data labeling. Crucially, it generates high-quality reasoning chains to accompany these complex questions, offering explicit dependency paths for stepwise supervision signals.

KG Construction

The data collection process involves aggregating Wikipedia page documents according to the hierarchical catalog, spanning diverse knowledge domains and subcategories including Sports, History, Science, Health, Biology, etc. We then perform triplet extraction from single documents and consolidate these into an initial Knowledge Graph
G

(
26
)
. A more complex, cross-document knowledge graph
G_{d}
is subsequently formed via domain-level aggregation. To enhance the quality and cohesion of the graph, the cross-document knowledge graph
G_{d}
is further refined by methods including entity and relation clustering.

Reasoning Path Sampling

To generate challenging multi-hop paths, we first sample relation-relevant subgraphs centered on target entities within the domain-specific knowledge graph. Long-range reasoning paths are subsequently derived using sampling strategies such as Random Walk and BFS. A key constraint is the sparse distribution of information, where crucial path nodes must be distributed across a wide range of documents to mandate rigorous cross-document information grounding and retrieval. The sampled reasoning paths are further enhanced via approaches including information perturbation and the obfuscation of entities and concepts in
G_{d}
. For instance, we perform Temporal Obfuscation (”the year end with 5 in late 20th century”), Location Obfuscation (”a country with a population of over 1.4 billion”), Institutional Obfuscation (”a prestigious science and engineering university located in Beijing”), etc.

Question Generation

We identify that high-reasoning-density questions necessitate the ability to synthesize deep and widely scattered contextual information for long-range reasoning. To this end, we categorize deep reasoning in long-context scenarios into following distinct paradigms:

•

In-depth Reasoning
: It includes
Multi-hop Reasoning
and
Causal Analysis
, which focus on tracking intricate logical chains across massive contexts to identify non-obvious dependencies and root causes.

•

Temporal Reasoning
: It requires aggregating discrete quantitative data spread throughout the text to perform precise calculations and model dynamic shifts over time.

•

Hypothetical Scenario
: It evaluates the ability of counterfactual reasoning by mapping existing logic onto new, speculative frameworks.

Based on the source documents and the extracted reasoning paths as ground truth, we employ a strong teacher LLM as the generator to synthesize high-quality multi-hop QA pairs. The generation process integrates the aforementioned paradigms and incorporates question expression obfuscation. We achieved precise control over question complexity by regulating the length of the sampled paths, and ensured the variety of the dataset by using paths derived from various sampling methods.

Quality Control

Ideally, the constructed long-context multi-hop QAs should be well-grounded in supporting documents, with concise answers and high-quality reasoning paths. With QA pairs obtained, we further employ a four-stage quality control pipeline to obtain a high-quality dataset: (1)
Answer Alignment Check
: teacher LLMs are used as generator, responder, and verifier to ensure consistency between the generated questions and answers; (2)
Knowledge Grounding Check
: questions that can be answered correctly without the source documents are removed to avoid the potential influence from LLMs’ inherent knowledge; (3)
Complex Answer Filtering
: we retain only QA pairs whose answers are under 20 words to ensure stable and reliable validation; (4)
Contextual Robustness Check
: samples that are easily perturbed by irrelevant documents are filtered out. More detailed quality control pipeline can be referred at Appendix
A.2
.

3.3
Long-Context Reinforcement Learning with Process Advantage Shaping

Analysis in Section
3.1
unveils the common “almost-there” phenomenon in existing RLVR methods, highlighting the inherent limitations of relying on sparse, outcome-based rewards tied exclusively to final correctness. Therefore, we introduce
LongPAS
, which incorporates process advantage shaping guided by on-policy reference trajectories to facilitate fine-grained credit assignment. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure
3
.

On-policy Supervision from GT-guided Trajectory

Given a question
Q
and long context
C
, in addition to regular group sampling rollouts
T
, we perform auxiliary sampling by incorporating the ground-truth reasoning chain into the prompt. This process transforms condensed entity-relation phrases into a natural language trajectory
\tau_{p}
, ensuring it exhibits reasoning patterns and thought processes consistent with the policy rollouts. Consequently,
\tau_{p}
can serve as an on-policy reference supervision signal.
To extract intermediate steps from reasoning trajectories, we craft a simple prompt template that instructs the LLM to structure its response into distinct steps. This template explicitly mandates a step-by-step format to facilitate parsing. The detailed prompt templates can be found in Appendix
B
.

Hybrid Reward Design

Unlike tasks such as Math and Coding in short-context scenarios, which allow for verification via rule-based functions, the long-context reasoning tasks (e.g., open-ended QA) present unique challenges due to inherent answer diversity. Therefore, we adopt a hybrid reward mechanism
(
39
)
to balance precision and recall. Specifically, we first perform exact string matching between the model prediction
y_{pred}
and the ground truth
y_{gt}
as
R_{rule}=\mathbb{I}(y_{pred}=y_{gt})
. To mitigate potential false negatives and handle answer diversity, we additionally employ an LLM-based evaluator to assess the semantic equivalence between
y_{pred}
and
y_{gt}
as
R_{LLM}=\text{LLM}_{Judge}(Q,y_{pred},y_{gt})
, where
Q
denotes the input question. We then obtain the final reward through maximum selection as follows:

R(Q,y_{pred},y_{gt})=\max\left\{\mathbb{I}(y_{pred}=y_{gt}),\text{LLM}_{Judge}(Q,y_{pred},y_{gt})\right\}.

Overview of Long-Context Reinforcement Learning with Process Advantage Shaping.

Process Advantage Shaping

We posit that the reference GT-guided trajectory
\tau_{p}
encapsulates the grounded information scope and essential reasoning steps. This allows us to mitigate the limitation of “erroneous penalization of correct sub-steps” via stepwise advantage shaping.
Specifically, for group sampling rollouts
T=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},...,\tau_{n}\}
, we evaluate each sub-step
s_{j}
of rollout
\tau_{i}
in two dimensions:
validity
and
relevance
.
Validity
is evaluated with an LLM-as-a-Judge, denoted as
\mathbb{I}_{\text{valid}}(s_{i,j})=\text{LLM}_{Judge}(s_{j},\tau_{p})
. The judge assesses whether
\tau_{i}
aligns with the necessary entities and reasoning logic of the reference
\tau_{p}
.
Relevance
is quantified by semantic similarity
\text{sim}(s_{i,j},\tau_{p})
and reflects the extent to which the sub-step
s_{j}
is semantically aligned with its rollout
\tau_{i}
. To balance exploration and exploitation, we utilize these signals to perform granular stepwise advantage reweighting specifically on rollouts with negative outcomes, whereas positive rollouts remain intact to foster exploration of diverse reasoning paths. The reweighted stepwise advantage
\hat{A}_{i,j}
for the
j
-th step
s_{i,j}
in the
i
-th rollout
\tau_{i}
is calculated as:

\hat{A}_{i,j}=\underbrace{\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{n}\})}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{n}\})}}_{\text{Group Relative Advantage}}\cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\mathbb{I}(\tau_{i}\in T_{f})\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\text{valid}}(s_{i,j})\cdot\text{sim}(s_{i,j},\tau_{p})\right)}_{\text{Step-wise Reweighting Coefficient}},

where
\mathbb{I}(\tau_{i}\in T_{f})
is an indicator function for negative rollouts, and
\text{sim}(s_{i,j},\tau_{p})
denotes the semantic similarity between step
s_{i,j}
and
\tau_{p}
. Consequently, when a sub-step in a negative rollout is deemed correct, the penalty vanishes (
\hat{A}_{i,j}\to 0
), whereas erroneous steps retain the full negative signal. For Thinking models, since their outputs contain “thinking trajectories” enclosed by “
<think>
” and “
</think>
” tokens, we assign the average advantage of the “response” part uniformly to each token within this thinking segment.

We opt to mitigate penalties rather than assign positive rewards to avoid optimization ambiguity. Assigning positive advantages within negative rollouts risks incentivizing ”plausible but ineffective” reasoning paths that do not lead to correct outcomes. Instead, our approach acts as a conservative credit assignment mechanism: it signals that correct sub-steps were not the cause of failure—preventing them from being “unlearned”.

Overall Training Objective

Following the defined Process Advantage Estimation algorithm, we now formalize the final training objective, which incorporates the Step-wise Re-weighted Advantage (
\hat{A}_{i,j}
). The policy
\pi_{\theta}
is optimized by maximizing the following objective function:

J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{(C,Q),\bm{y}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{L_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{i}}f_{\epsilon}\left(\rho_{i,j}(\theta),\hat{A}_{i,j}\right)\right]-\beta\cdot\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{(C,Q)}\left[\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid C,Q)\|\pi_{old}(\cdot\mid C,Q)]\right],

where
\rho_{i,j}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(s_{i,j}\mid s_{i,<j},C,Q)}{\pi_{old}(s_{i,j}\mid s_{i,<j},C,Q)}
denotes the probability ratio of
j
-th step in
i
-th trajectory.

4
Experimental Setup

Data Construction

During the multi-hop QA synthesis pipeline in Section
3.2
, we employ KGGen
(
26
)
to extract entities and triplets from plain texts. We sample reasoning paths from 2 to 30 hops to control the difficulties of corresponding questions.

As a result, we construct the raw
DeepReasonQA
dataset containing 14,577 QA samples with documents up to 288K tokens in length. Prior to training LLMs, we filter the training questions to exclude trivial and insurmountable instances. Specifically, for each question, we perform 8 full-context rollouts with Qwen3-4B-Thinking and retain only those whose empirical success rate lies in the interval [0.25, 0.75]. This yields a filtered RL training dataset of 2,012 QA samples with documents up to 60K tokens. Detailed dataset statistics and data filtering pipeline are provided in Appendix
A.1
and
A.2
.

Training Settings

We conduct experiments on different LLM backbones: (1) Instruct Models: LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct; (2) Thinking Models: Qwen3-4B-Thinking and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking. We include RLVR as the baseline method implemented by naive GRPO
(
31
)
, and trained on the same dataset. Our RL framework is implemented upon VeRL
(
33
)
. For the RL training, we use the AdamW optimizer with a constant learning rate of 2e-6 and a 5-step linear warmup. The maximum input length is set to 60K tokens, the maximum output length is set to 30K tokens for Thinking models and 10K for Instruct models. We conduct purely on-policy training, use a prompt batch size 128 and set group size
N
to 8, sampling temperature to 0.7 and top-
p
to 0.95. As for GT path guided sampling, temperature is set to 0 and top-
p
is set to 1. We adopt GPT-OSS-120B as the LLM-as-Judge for answer evaluator and provide validity signals in Process Advantage Estimation. We use Qwen3-8B-Embedding to calculate semantic similarity between trajectory steps.

Evaluation Configurations

We evaluate all LLMs on three widely-used and challenging long-context QA benchmarks: (1) FRAMES
(
18
)
, which comprises questions requiring 2-15 Wikipedia articles to answer. (2) LongBench V2
(
7
)
, a realistic multi-choice QA benchmark that assess the ability of LLMs to handle long-context problems requiring deep understanding and reasoning, with contexts ranging from 8K to 2M words. (3) Multi-Hop QA: We adopt three subsets including 2WikiMultiHopQA, HotpotQA and MusiQue from Longbench
(
6
)
, which cover 3-5 hop questions with corresponding documents. To contextualize performance, we also evaluate frontier LLMs in different sizes including GPT5-Nano
(
27
)
, Gemini2.5-Flash-Thinking
(
10
)
and GPT-OSS (20B and 120B)
(
1
)
. More detailed evaluation configurations can be found in Appendix
B
.

In this section, we conduct ablation studies to investigate the key components in
LongPAS
that achieve long-context in-depth reasoning, including (1) the training data length; (2) process signals in advantage estimation; and (3) the role of on-policy trajectory supervision.

Training Data Length

We investigate the impact of training data length on the performance of
LongPAS
. Specifically, we train Qwen3-4B with varying maximum input lengths—20K, 40K, and 60K tokens—aligned with different context window sizes. As shown in Figure
5
, the performance of the
LongPAS
model, across both Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Thinking variants, exhibits a monotonic increase with the expansion of the maximum input length during training, utilizing 20K, 40K, and 60K token context windows. This observation underscores the critical role of long-sequence training in improving the model’s long-context grounding and reasoning. Specifically, the FRAMES and Multi-Hop QA tasks register the most substantial performance gains. This indicates that training with extended context windows significantly enhances the model’s capability for complex information grounding and long-range dependency modeling, which are prerequisites for multi-step reasoning. While performance gains on LongBench V2 benchmark are less pronounced, the consistent positive correlation confirms that greater exposure to longer contexts during training systematically elevates the model’s overall proficiency across diverse long-context tasks.

Process Signal of Advantage Shaping

In Section
3.3
, we estimate the stepwise advantage shaping signals along two dimensions:
Validity
and
Relevance
. We perform an ablation by removing the
Validity
signal
\mathbb{I}_{\text{valid}}(s_{i,j})=\text{LLM}_{Judge}(s_{j},\tau_{p})
, and retaining only
Relevance
as the indicator of the necessity of sub-steps. As reported in Table
3
, removing the
Validity
signal causes a clear performance decline across all benchmarks, especially on long-context multi-hop reasoning tasks. On FRAMES, performance decreases from 64.90 to 62.68 (-2.28%), and on LongBench V2 it drops from 42.94 to 41.15 (-1.79%). We also evaluate an ablation that removes
Relevance
signal while keeping only
Validity
signal, which binarizes the continuous reweighting coefficient. A similar performance degradation is observed: -1.73% on FRAMES and -2.04% on Multi-Hop QA.
These reductions indicate that both the
Validity
and
Relevance
signal play an essential role in filtering out incorrect or noisy intermediate steps, thereby ensuring that advantage shaping emphasizes only logically sound reasoning transitions.

On-policy Trajectory Supervision

During Process Advantage Shaping, we sample GT-guided reference trajectory via an on-policy strategy. To explore the role of on-policy supervision in Process Advantage Shaping, we conduct an ablation comparison by replacing it with off-policy supervision from teacher LLMs. Specifically, we sample GT-guided reference trajectories
\tau_{d}
from Gemini2.5-Pro by incorporating the ground-truth reasoning chain into the prompt. As shown in Table
3
, we can also observe a performance drop across all benchmarks, especially on multi-hop reasoning benchmarks (-2.01% on FRAMES). We conclude this phenomenon for the following reasons: (1) Strategy-Data Distribution Mismatch: While
\tau_{p}
provides a stable, efficient process advantage signal (expecially
Relevance
signal) by aligning with the current policy’s capability range
P_{\pi}(\tau)
, the off-policy
\tau_{d}
comes from a powerful Teacher LLM (
P_{\text{Teacher}}(\tau)
) that is out-of-distribution for the student model. This causes the training model to struggle with accurately estimating the advantage of paths it rarely explores, leading to unstable optimization; (2) Teacher LLM’s reasoning steps may be too complex or rely on knowledge organization the training model
\pi
has not yet learned, and because
\tau_{d}
is static, it cannot dynamically adapt to the policy’s learning progress like
\tau_{p}
.

Related Works

6.1
Long-Context Data Synthesis

Most existing approaches to long-context data synthesis primarily focused on superficially extending input length by augmenting existing datasets with filler content. Specifically, methods such as those by
19
;
21
;
22
involved padding questions with additional irrelevant documents, for example, by adding unrelated passages to MusiQue
(
38
)
or using document-filling on HotpotQA
(
43
)
and SQuAD
(
29
)
. Although these strategies effectively increase the sheer volume of context, they are fundamentally limited as they fail to generate the high-quality, challenging training data necessary to develop models capable of deep reasoning.

In contrast, recent advancements in long-context instruction data generation introduce several distinct approaches aimed at creating more complex and faithful contexts. LongFaith
(
42
)
focuses on synthesizing faithful reasoning datasets by incorporating ground truth and citation-based prompting. To achieve diversity and realism, Wildlong
(
20
)
uses a multi-pronged approach involving meta-information extraction, graph-based modeling, and adaptive generation. Other methods concentrate on generating the context itself:
(
49
)
utilizes off-the-shelf LLMs to create extended background contexts for existing pairs, while Quest
(
12
)
employs a query-centric method to aggregate diverse, relevant documents. Re3Syn
(
46
)
provides a structured pipeline using semantic retrieval, dependency recognition, and a reorder algorithm to transform short documents into coherent long-context data. SPELL
(
44
)
proposes a multi-role self-play reinforcement learning framework to construct high-quality long-context QA data at scale.

6.2
Long-Context Reasoning

While high-quality, human-like chains of thought (CoT) have significantly driven recent advances in LLM reasoning, most existing studies have primarily focused on short-context tasks, such as mathematics
(
14
;
13
;
2
)
and code
(
25
;
3
)
. Consequently, the exploration of advanced long-context reasoning patterns remains notably limited. Although previous works successfully extended model context windows during pre- and mid-training stages
(
41
;
10
;
36
)
using methods like RoPE
(
34
;
28
)
, the resulting models often fail to exhibit strong reasoning capabilities or reliably utilize information across the extended context.

Yet, there are limited researches explored the role of post-training in unlocking LLMs’ abilities of robust long-context reasoning. Long-context SFT
(
5
)
and DPO
(
8
)
tend to introduce non-generalizable bias and noise. More recent efforts like QwenLong-L1
(
39
)
, SoLoPO
(
35
)
and E-GRPO
(
47
)
employ standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to incentivize long-context reasoning. However, these existing works predominantly concentrate on optimizing RL training strategies themselves, often overlooking the crucial role of synthesizing high-quality long-context reasoning data. While methods like LoongRL
(
40
)
attempt to address the data gap by proposing a data-driven RL approach—transforming standard multi-hop questions into high-difficulty long-context tasks through the inclusion of distracting documents—they remain susceptible to an inherent RL challenge: the ubiquitous “almost-there” phenomenon during training. This issue frequently discards valuable learning signals, which ultimately leads to unstable training dynamics when applied to complex reasoning models.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we systematically investigate the “almost-there” phenomenon within long-context RLVR, highlighting how sparse, outcome-based rewards often overlook pivotal learning signals in partially correct trajectories. To address this, we propose a KG-driven synthesis framework to construct
DeepReasonQA
, a high-quality multi-hop QA dataset featuring explicit reasoning chains. Building on this, we introduce
LongPAS
, which enables fine-grained credit assignment via process advantage shaping. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach significantly enhances the long-context reasoning capabilities of LLMs, consistently outperforming established baselines and allowing smaller models to achieve parity with frontier LLMs. Further analysis reveals that
LongPAS
not only stabilizes reinforcement learning but also fosters robust information grounding and complex logical integration. This paradigm—integrating structured data synthesis with granular process supervision—establishes a scalable path toward next-generation reasoning agents capable of navigating increasingly intricate, large-scale information environments.

Limitations

This paper aims to incentivize in-depth reasoning of long-context LLMs on long-range multi-hop QA tasks, and offers a recipe combining a KG-guided multi-hop QA synthesis framework with a process advantage shaping strategy. While it makes substantial progress in long-context reasoning, several limitations remain:

Data Domain and Source

The synthesis framework currently relies primarily on Wikipedia as the underlying knowledge source. While
DeepReasonQA
already yields substantial improvements and effectively incentivizes deep long-context reasoning patterns, incorporating long documents from domains such as law, finance, and medicine could introduce richer stylistic and structural diversity, potentially improving robustness and transferability to real-world long-context reasoning tasks.

Coupling of Synthesis Framework and Process Supervision

A potential limitation is the perceived coupling between LONGPAS and the KG-driven synthesis framework, as the algorithm currently utilizes the specific reasoning chains generated during synthesis as the primary source of process signals. While this might appear to restrict the scalability of LONGPAS to datasets where explicit ground-truth reasoning paths are available , we argue that the core contribution of LONGPAS lies in its generalizable mechanism for Process Advantage Shaping. Specifically, the method provides a robust framework for effectively leveraging any form of auxiliary supervision—whether they are KG-derived reasoning chains or standard reasoning trajectories distilled from teacher LLMs—to stabilize long-context RL training. By transforming these signals into fine-grained credit assignment, LONGPAS addresses the fundamental ”almost-there” bottleneck in a way that aligns with contemporary efforts
(
11
;
50
)
to integrate SFT-level supervision into the RL stage. Thus, rather than being a restricted implementation, our approach offers a novel and versatile perspective on mitigating reward sparsity in complex, long-context scenarios.

Sophistication of the Reward Model

Our current implementation uses a hybrid reward function that combines simple rule-based checks with an LLM-as-a-judge to balance precision and recall. This works well for tasks with clear correctness criteria, such as factoid multi-hop questions, but may be less effective for open-ended or subjective tasks where correctness is multifaceted. A promising direction is to develop more advanced, rubric-based LLM reward models that score responses along dimensions such as logical rigor, citation accuracy, and coherence, enabling our framework to better handle complex, agentic scenarios.

A.2
Detailed Quality Control Pipeline

Ideally, the constructed long-context multi-hop QAs should be well-grounded in supporting documents, with concise answer and high-quality reasoning paths. Thus, we applied a four‑stage filtering pipeline to ensure the final
DeepReasonQA
is of high quality:

Answer Alignment Check:

During question generation, we use Gemini‑2.5‑Pro
(
10
)
to produce QA pairs based on task‑specific prompts, documents, and reasoning paths. DeepSeek-V3‑2508
(
24
)
is then used to answer the generated questions, and GPT‑OSS‑120B
(
1
)
serves as a verifier to assess whether the two answers are consistent. Samples with misaligned answers are removed.

Knowledge Grounding Check:

To reduce the potential bias from internal inherent knowledge, we temporarily remove the source documents and check whether the model can still answer correctly. Samples that remain answerable without the documents are filtered out to ensure the dataset genuinely tests contextual reasoning.

Complex Answer Filtering:

QA pairs whose answers exceed 20 words are discarded, as overly complex answers are unstable and difficult to verify reliably.

Contextual Robustness Check:

We augment each context with irrelevant documents and re‑evaluate the model’s answer. Samples whose answer accuracy (pass@k) drops to zero are removed, ensuring that each question–answer pair is robust rather than brittle under context perturbations.

Benchmark Details

In our experiments, we evaluate the long-context capabilities of our model and baselines on several widely-used benchmarks:

•

FRAMES

(

To align with our long-context setting, we equip each question with corresponding wikipedia pages to minimize the effect of information retrieval.

•

(

It comprises 503 challenging multiple‑choice questions with context lengths ranging from 8K to 2M words, covering six key task categories: single‑document QA, multi‑document QA, long in‑context learning, long‑dialogue understanding, code repository comprehension, and long structured‑data understanding.

•

(
6
)

(

)

SFT Configurations

To construct the SFT dataset, we adopt
DeepReasonQA
(2k QA pairs) and employ DeepSeek-V3-0528 and DeepSeek-R1-0528 to generate teacher trajectories under the guidance of ground-truth reasoning chains, for the Instruct model and Thinking model respectively. We filter out samples with incorrect final answers and retain 1,536 instances. The input length in the SFT stage is set to 60K. Training is conducted for 4 epochs with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of 1e-5.

Evaluation Configurations

For all LLM backbones, we conduct evaluation under a 128K context window. Specifically, LLaMA3.1‑8B‑Instruct and Qwen2.5‑7B‑Instruct (with YaRN enabled) are evaluated with a maximum input length of 120K and an output limit of 10K. For all frontier models and the Qwen3 series, the input length is also set to 120K, with output limits of 10K for Instruct models and 30K for reasoning models.
For each question, we generate
N=4
candidate responses and report the average score (Pass@1) in our main experiments, as well as Pass@k for test‑time scaling analyses. The Pass@k metric provides an unbiased estimate of the probability that at least one of the
k
sampled responses is correct, given
n
candidate solutions per problem. For multiple-choice tasks, we report standard accuracy. For open-end multi-hop QA tasks, we use the Hybrid Reward mentioned in Section
3.3
and use GPT-OSS-120B as LLM-as-a-judge
(
48
)
to evaluate semantic equivalence between a model’s prediction and the ground-truth answer.

Training Prompt

Here we list the training prompt template we used during Training.

System Prompt during training

You are a helpful assistant. Please read the provided text and answer the question below. Please structure your response into two main sections: Thought and Solution. In the Thought section, detail your reasoning process using the specified format:
<begin_of_thought>
thought with steps start with ‘Step N:’
<end_of_thought>
Each step should include detailed considerations such as analyzing questions, summarizing relevant findings, brainstorming new ideas, verifying the accuracy of the current steps, refining any errors, and revisiting previous steps. In the Solution section, based on various attempts, explorations, and reflections from the Thought section, systematically present the final solution that you deem correct. The solution should remain a logical, accurate, concise expression style and detail necessary step needed to reach the conclusion, formatted as follows:
<begin_of_solution>
Therefore, the answer is {insert answer here}
<end_of_solution>
.

LLM-as-judge Prompt

Here we list the prompts of LLM-as-Judge (1) when used as Outcome Reward Model to judge whether predicted answer is aligned with ground-truth answer; (2) when used as Sub-step Validity Signal to judge whether a sub-step in rollout trajectories aligns with the necessary entities and reasoning logic of the reference trajectory.

Prompt for LLM-as-Judge as Reward Model

You are an expert in verifying if two answers are the same.

Your input is a problem and two answers, Answer 1 and Answer 2. You need to check if they are equivalent.

Your task is to determine if two answers are equivalent, without attempting to solve the original problem.

Compare the answers to verify they represent identical values or meaning, even when written in different forms or notations.

Your output must follow the following format:

1) Provide an explanation for why the answers are equivalent or not.

2) Then provide your final answer in the form of: [[YES]] or [[NO]]

Problem:
{question}

Answer 1:
{predicted answer}

Answer 2:
{golden answer}

Prompt for LLM-as-Judge as Sub-step Validity Signal

You are an expert in analyzing reasoning traces. Your task is to determine if a “given reasoning substep from a model’s output” is contained or reflected within the “Ground Truth reasoning solution”.

In your assessment, you must strictly adhere to the following special rules:

1. Ignore Step Order: You need to check if the logical content or core reasoning expressed by the substep is covered by the Ground Truth path, regardless of its position in either path.

2. Accept Varied Granularity: Differences in reasoning granularity are allowed. If the model substep is a logical combination (merger) of multiple steps in the Ground Truth, or if it is only a part (subset) of a single Ground Truth step, it should still be considered a match, as long as its core logic is clearly included or reflected in the Ground Truth path.

Specifically, you should check:

1. Does the substep text or its semantic equivalent appear in the Ground Truth solution?

2. Is the substep’s core logic or reasoning step reflected or contained within the Ground Truth solution?

To illustrate the qualitative differences in reasoning, we present a comparative case study using trajectories generated by Qwen3-4B-Thinking and

for the same question.

As shown in the examples below, the task involves multi-hop fact retrieval and date-based quantitative reasoning. The reasoning trajectory of
LongPAS
exhibits more advanced critical reasoning and temporal logic, covering stages such as Information Grounding, Information Extraction, Strategy Adjustment, Temporal Calculation, Self-Correction, and Answer Confirmation.

Comparing the two reasoning trajectories, we observe a shift from “simple pattern matching and computation” to “logical reasoning based on state judgment”. The reasoning trajectory of
LongPAS
exhibits several richer reasoning patterns: (1)

Constraint Consciousness
– It shows a stronger awareness of task boundaries and constraints throughout the thinking process; (3)
Robust Verification Loop
– It adopts a more divergent validation strategy, revisiting candidate answers, cross-validating them with multiple pieces of evidence, and rejecting inconsistent hypotheses before committing to a final conclusion.

We also observe that both models share an initial segment of correct reasoning steps, but the vanilla model later diverges into incorrect deductions and calculations. This phenomenon further demonstrates that
LongPAS
can better learn from “almost-there” samples during RL training, leading to more accurate and precise reasoning.

TASK: As a specialist in complex problem design, you will be provided with {k_context} Wikipedia articles and a set of “Multi-hop Reasoning Paths” derived from a knowledge graph. Your goal is to generate one challenging factoid “Multihop Reasoning” question that meets the following criteria.

Your design principles are to maximize reasoning steps, maximize information lookup dependencies, and minimize direct clues while ensuring uniqueness and verifiability of the answer.

1. Core Requirements

* Standalone & Context-Independent: Questions must not reference “Article 1,” “Article 2,” etc. They should be fully understandable without any additional context.

* Unambiguous & Verifiable Answer: The question should have a single, clear, and factual answer.

* Chain-of-Thought Reasoning: The question must be structured to force a rigorous, step-by-step reasoning process. Each logical step must build upon the previous one, following the provided multi-hop reasoning path.

2. Advanced Reasoning Requirements

* Multi-hop Reasoning via Knowledge Graph Paths: Each question must be constructed by tracing and combining information along the provided multi-hop reasoning path. The path acts as a logical blueprint, connecting entities and concepts from different articles. It’s important to note that the path may only involve a subset of the {k_context} articles.

* Multi-hop Reasoning Path Format: (Subject 1)-[Relation 1]-(Subject 2)-[Relation 2]-(Subject 3)-[Relation 3]-(Subject 4)…

* Generate a problem that requires reasoning through multiple entities and relationships. The problem should call for starting from one entity, reaching another through multiple chains of relationships, and analyzing the significance of this connection.

[[Question]]:
