Intention to use future contraception after abortion and associated factors among women visiting health facilities in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia, 2024
Authors: Mamaru Tilahun Afework, Setarg Ayenew Birhanie, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Mulatu Kassahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntention to use future contraception after abortion refers to a woman’s expressed plan to use a contraceptive method after abortion. Inadequate family planning counselling and limited access to contraceptive services among post-abortion women may increase the risk of repeated unintended pregnancies and induced abortions. Although the Ethiopian Ministry of Health has integrated safe abortion care with post-abortion family planning services, abortion rates remain high and contraceptive utilization remains low in many areas, including the study setting. Limited evidence exists regarding women’s intention to use future contraception after abortion in Debre Markos town. This study assessed the intention to use future contraception after abortion and its associated factors among women receiving abortion services in health facilities of Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 326 women selected by consecutive sampling. Data were collected using a structured, pretested interviewer-administered questionnaire, entered into EpiData 4.6, and analysed using Stata 17. Binary logistic regression was performed. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.53), and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 2).ResultsThe proportion intending to use future contraception after abortion was 70.2% (95% CI: 65.0%–75.2%). Higher educational status (AOR = 4.42; 95% CI: 1.34–14.48), unintended pregnancy (AOR = 5.00; 95% CI: 1.29–19.36), desire to avoid pregnancy for at least six months (AOR = 8.14; 95% CI: 3.10–21.38), favourable attitude (AOR = 6.25; 95% CI: 2.31–16.89), and good knowledge (AOR = 7.99; 95% CI: 3.00–21.30) were positively associated with intention. Spontaneous abortion (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.02–0.52) and not receiving the preferred contraceptive method (AOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.05–0.34) were negatively associated.ConclusionSeven in ten women intended to use future contraception after abortion. Educational status, unintended pregnancy, desire to delay pregnancy, favourable attitude, and good knowledge increased intention, whereas spontaneous abortion and not receiving the preferred contraceptive method reduced intention.Keywords: future contraception, post-abortion care, induced abortion, family planning, Ethiopia

Introduction

Intention to use future contraception after abortion refers to a woman’s expressed plan or willingness to use a contraceptive method following an abortion. 1 It represents a behavioral intention rather than actual contraceptive uptake. Women’s beliefs, knowledge, and attitudes toward contraception may influence their intention to use family planning methods after abortion. 2 A woman’s specific beliefs about contraceptives influence her engagement and adherence. 3

Post-abortion care (PAC) is a comprehensive approach that includes management of abortion-related complications, provision of family planning counselling and services, and other reproductive health interventions as needed. 4 Post-abortion family planning (PAFP) refers to the initiation of contraceptive methods immediately after abortion or before the return of fertility to prevent subsequent unintended pregnancies. Effective provision of PAFP can reduce unintended pregnancies and repeat abortions, particularly among women with unmet family planning needs.5,6 The post-abortion period provides an important opportunity to counsel women and their partners about family planning and to inform them about the potential benefits of contraceptive use, including preventing unintended pregnancies and reducing associated physical, emotional, and financial burdens.7,8

The world health organization (WHO) guideline recommends that a woman wait at least six months after an abortion before getting pregnant to allow her body to regain strength and prepare for a healthy pregnancy. 9 In most women, fertility returns on average about two weeks after an abortion; however, ovulation can occur as early as 11 days post-abortion. 4 Therefore, timely provision of PAFP is critical. Providing these services helps meet the reproductive intentions of women who most clearly demonstrate an unmet need for FP, reduces unintended pregnancies, and prevents repeat abortions, thus reducing maternal deaths. 10

Globally an estimated 55.9 million abortions occur each year and the majority (49.3 million) abortions occur in developing countries. 11 Unsafe abortion is preventable but it still causes 13% of maternal deaths and 20% of the overall burden of maternal death and long-term disability. 11 Every year, more than 44 million women have been complicated with induced abortions, and of these, around 20 million women accounted for unsafe abortions. Unsafe abortion contributes 37 deaths per 100,000 live births in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). 12 Numbers of unsafe abortions have increased from time to time and the overall unsafe abortion rates remain unchanged at about 14 unsafe abortions per 1000 women aged 15-44 year. 13 In Africa out of all pregnancies, 15% end in abortion. The 2010–2014 estimate shows 8.2 million induced abortions occurred each year. Most abortions which done in Africa are unsafe, the majority fell into the least-safe category. Africa is the world region with the highest number of abortion-related deaths. 14

In Ethiopia, an estimated 620,300 induced abortions were performed in 2014, with an annual abortion rate of 28 per 1,000 women. 11 And it is one of the SSA countries having high maternal and newborn mortality. In the country 412 maternal death per 100,000 live births were occurred annually. 15 It is estimated that as many as 95% of unintended pregnancies in Ethiopia occur among women who do not practice contraception at all. 16 In Debre Markos, 34.8% of pregnancies were unintended and 24.8% were mistimed. 17 The estimated abortion rate for Debre Markos in 2021 was 100 abortions per 1,000 women of reproductive age (annual woreda report), three times higher than the national rate.17,18 There is evidence on the health risks of repeat abortions, including reduced fertility, complications, and poor outcomes in future pregnancies, in addition to potential psychological impacts. 19 Post-abortion contraception is a key strategy to reduce unintended pregnancy, repeat induced abortions, and lower morbidity and mortality among women, neonates, infants, and children. 12 If contraception were accessible and used consistently and correctly by women wanting to avoid pregnancy, maternal deaths would decline by an estimated 25–35%. 20

Some studies on intention to use post-abortion contraception in Ethiopia have concluded that women had intention to use contraception, either immediately or with a future plan. 21 However, in our study, we defined intention as planning to use contraception immediately or within two weeks after abortion. The National Demographic and Health Survey lacks information on significant factors associated with intention to use PAFP. In the study area, the prevalence of post-abortion contraceptive uptake was (59.2%), 22 while 34.9% of women had experienced a repeat induced abortion. These findings suggest an unmet need for post-abortion family planning and highlight the importance of ensuring that women have access to contraceptive counselling and methods following abortion, in accordance with WHO recommendations. 12 Therefore, this study aimed to assess intention to use post-abortion contraception and associated factors among women receiving abortion services in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design, area and period

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Debre Markos town administration, East Gojjam Zone, Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia, from 1 January to 29 March 2024. Debre Markos town is 300 km from Addis Ababa (the capital) and 265 km from Bahir Dar (the regional capital). 23 The town has four sub-cities and 20 kebeles, with a total population of 146,153. 23 There are six health institutions providing legal abortion services: one governmental referral hospital, three health centers, and two non-governmental (NGO) clinics (town health department office). In the study area, post-abortion contraception coverage in 2023 was 59.2%, 24 and the abortion rate in 2021 was 100 per 1,000 live births (annual town health department report.

Source and study population

The source population comprised all women who visited health institutions for abortion services and were diagnosed with abortion in Debre Markos town. All women who visited the selected health institutions for abortion services during the study period constituted the study population.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion: Women who visited health institutions for abortion services during their current pregnancy.

Exclusion: Women who were critically ill and unable to respond, or mentally unstable.

Sample size determination

Sample size for the first objective was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming an intention to use post-abortion contraception of 73.5% from a study in Gondar. 25 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, and 10% non-response rate. The calculated sample size was 329.

For the second objective, sample sizes based on various factors were smaller; therefore, the maximum (329) was taken as the final sample size.

Sampling procedure

Although systematic random sampling was initially planned, the sampling interval (448/329 ≈ 1.3) was approximated to 1. Therefore, a consecutive sampling approach was used, and 329 women were interviewed after proportional allocation to each health institution.

Based on a three-month report from the six health institutions preceding the survey, there were 165, 10, 8, 9, 170 and 86 women who received safe abortion services in Debre Markos Referral Hospital, Debre Markos Health Centre, Wuseta Health Centre, Hedasie Health Centre, Maristops International Clinic and Family Guidance Clinic respectively (total 448). The formula ‘ni = (n/N) × Ni’ was used to allocate sample to each institution (Figure 2).

Data collection technique

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered pre-tested structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated to Amharic and back-translated to English to ensure consistency. It included sections on socio-demographic characteristics, maternal health and obstetric factors, intention, knowledge (15 yes/no questions), and attitude (10 Likert scale items).

Data collection procedure

Six trained midwives and nurses collected data under the supervision of a senior public health officer. The objective was explained to participants; if a participant did not meet the criteria, the next eligible woman was interviewed. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity was maintained by using codes instead of names.

Data quality assurance

Training was given to data collectors and supervisors for half a day. A pre-test was conducted on 5% of respondents at Amanuale Health Centre (not included in the main study). The questionnaire was modified accordingly. Data were checked daily for completeness, clarity and consistency by the principal investigator.

Data management and data analysis

Data were entered using EpiData version 4.6 and exported to Stata version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages) were used to summarize variables. Binary logistic regression was performed. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in bivariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression using a backward elimination procedure. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.53). Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factor (VIF < 2 for all variables). Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported; statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of Debre Markos University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Approval No: CMHS/R/C/TT/D/123/11/15). Permission letters were obtained from all selected health facilities. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from their legally authorized representative, in addition to assent from the minor participants themselves. For participants unable to read or write, the consent form was read aloud in the local language, and a thumbprint was obtained in the presence of an independent witness. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without affecting their care. Confidentiality was ensured by using codes and interviewing participants alone in a private room. Completed questionnaires and electronic data were stored securely in password protected files accessible only to the research team.

Magnitude of intention to use future contraception after abortion

The proportion of women intending to use future contraception after abortion was 70.2% (229 women; 95% CI: 65.0–75.2%). Among the 229 women who had intention, 203 (88.6%) intended to use contraception immediately after the abortion, while 26(11.4%) intended to use contraception within two weeks. The remaining 97 women (29.8%) had no intention to use contraception after abortion.

Factors associated with intention to use future contraception after abortion

A total of 25 independent variables were assessed in the bivariable analysis. Variables with a p-value ≤0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model; consequently, 19 variables were entered into the multivariable model using backward elimination.

In the bivariable analysis, intention was significantly associated with age, marital status, residence, occupation, education, gravidity, desire of pregnancy, number of abortions, type of abortion, reason for abortion, future wanted children, time for next pregnancy, decision-maker on the number of children, period free from pregnancy after abortion, attitude towards contraception, knowledge of contraception, information after abortion about contraception, choice availability of contraception, and number of live children.

Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using a backward elimination procedure. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.53), indicating good model fit. Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factor (VIF < 2 for all variables).

In the multivariable analysis, seven variables remained significantly associated with intention to use future contraception after abortion (p < 0.05): educational status, desire of pregnancy, type of abortion, minimum period free from pregnancy, method choice availability, attitude towards contraception, and knowledge of contraception.

Women who were educated were 4.42 times more likely to intend to use future contraception after abortion compared to uneducated women (AOR = 4.42; 95% CI: 1.34–14.48). Women who aborted an unintended pregnancy were 5.00 times more likely to intend to use future contraception compared to those who aborted a wanted pregnancy (AOR = 5.00; 95% CI: 1.29–19.36). Women who had a spontaneous abortion had 85% lower odds of intention compared to those with induced abortion (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.02–0.52). Women who wanted to avoid pregnancy for 6 months or more after abortion were 8.14 times more likely to intend to use contraception compared to those who wanted pregnancy immediately (AOR = 8.14; 95% CI: 3.10–21.38). Women who did not receive their preferred contraceptive method had 87% lower odds of intention compared to those who received their method of choice (AOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.05–0.34). Women with a favourable attitude towards contraception were 6.25 times more likely to intend to use contraception compared to those with an unfavourable attitude (AOR = 6.25; 95% CI: 2.31–16.89). Women with good knowledge of contraception were 7.99 times more likely to intend to use contraception compared to those with poor knowledge (AOR = 7.99; 95% CI: 3.00–21.30) (Table 7).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to assess intention to use future contraception after abortion and associated factors among women in Debre Markos town, Northwest Ethiopia. The proportion of women intending to use future contraception after abortion was 70.2%. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Gambela (74.4%), 30 Gondar (73.5%), 25 and Harari (74.7%) 1 in Ethiopia. The finding was higher than studies conducted in urban Ghana 50.2%, 31 sub-Saharan Africa (45.76%), 32 Nepal (61.5%), 7 China (42.5%), 33 and India (41%). 34 This may be due to differences in socio-demographic characteristics: 85% of participants had primary education or above. Additionally, this study assessed intention towards general contraception, whereas the Chinese study focused on long-acting methods and the Nepali study focused on married women only. However, the finding was lower than a study conducted in Addis Ababa (90.6%), 26 which may be due to differences in socio-demographic characteristics in Addis Ababa, 86.6% of respondents were urban residents, which is associated with greater awareness about contraception.

Women with primary education or above were more likely to intend to use future contraception after abortion than uneducated women. This was supported by studies conducted in North Carolina 35 and Gambella. 30 Educational attainment may enhance health literacy, enabling women to understand reproductive health information, access contraceptive services, and make informed decisions about their fertility. 36 Educated women may also be more aware of the consequences of unintended pregnancy and more motivated to plan their families. 37

Women who aborted an unintended pregnancy were more likely to intend to use future contraception compared to those who aborted a wanted pregnancy. This is consistent with studies conducted in Gondar 25 and Addis Ababa. 26 This may be because women who had a wanted pregnancy had a plan to have a baby and may wish to regain fertility quickly, whereas women with an unintended pregnancy are more motivated to avoid another pregnancy in the future.

Women who did not receive their preferred contraceptive method had decreased odds of intention to use future contraception compared to those who received their method of choice. This is in line with studies conducted in Rwanda, 38 Guinea 39 and Nigeria. 40 This suggests that availability of the preferred method increases the likelihood of intention to use post-abortion contraception. When women are offered a range of methods and allowed to choose, they are more likely to adopt and continue using contraception. 41

Women who had a favorable attitude toward contraception were more likely to intend to use future contraception than those with an unfavorable attitude. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Nepal. 42 The theory of planned behavior suggests that attitudes are a key determinant of intention and subsequent behavior. 43 A favorable attitude may create internal motivation to perform the behavior, reduce perceived barriers, and increase self-efficacy 44

Women who had good knowledge of contraception were more likely to intend to use future contraception than those with poor knowledge. This finding is similar to studies conducted in Uganda. 45 Knowledge dispels myths and misconceptions about contraceptive methods, their side effects, and their benefits. Women with accurate knowledge are better equipped to make informed choices and are more likely to adopt and adhere to contraceptive methods.

Type of abortion was significantly associated with intention. Women who had a spontaneous abortion had 85% lower odds of intending to use future contraception compared to those who had an induced abortion (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.02–0.52). This finding is consistent with the observation that women with spontaneous abortion may plan to conceive soon and may therefore not intend to use contraception. 1

Although age, marital status, residence, occupation, gravidity, number of abortions, reason for abortion, future wanted children, decision maker on the number of children, information after abortion about contraception, and number of live children were associated with contraceptive intention in the bivariable analysis, they were not independently associated after adjustment for potential confounders. This suggests that their apparent effects may be explained by other sociodemographic and reproductive factors, such as educational status, knowledge, and attitude, which were more directly associated with intention. These findings highlight the importance of addressing multiple levels of influence individual, interpersonal, and health system when designing interventions to promote post-abortion contraceptive intention.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths: This study has several strengths. It used a facility-based cross-sectional design with a large sample size (n = 326) and a high response rate (99%). The use of a validated questionnaire, interviewer-administered data collection, and systematic random sampling enhanced the reliability and validity of the findings. The study also included both induced and spontaneous abortion cases, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of intention across different types of abortion.

Limitations: Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the associations reported are correlational, not causal. Second, the study relied on self-reported measures of intention, knowledge, attitude, and contraceptive use, which may be subject to social desirability and recall bias. Third, the study was conducted in a single town (Debre Markos), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings in Ethiopia. Fourth, the study did not assess long-term contraceptive use; intention may not always translate into actual behavior. Fifth, the study did not include qualitative data to explore the reasons behind low intention among certain subgroups. Finally, the study did not assess the role of partner involvement, community norms, or religious beliefs, which may influence contraceptive intention and use.

Clinical and policy implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for clinical practice and health policy. First, healthcare providers should provide comprehensive contraceptive counselling to all women seeking abortion services, including information on the full range of methods, their benefits, and their side effects. Second, health facilities should ensure that a wide range of contraceptive methods is available and that women receive their preferred method. Third, interventions should address not only knowledge but also attitudes and beliefs about contraception, as these are important determinants of intention. Fourth, targeted interventions are needed for women with lower educational levels, women with spontaneous abortion, and women who do not receive their preferred method. Fifth, policy makers should strengthen post-abortion care services and ensure that contraceptive services are integrated into abortion care. Finally, future research should use longitudinal designs to examine the relationship between intention and actual contraceptive use, and qualitative studies to explore the reasons behind low intention.

Conclusion

The proportion of women intending to use future contraception after abortion was 70.2%. Maternal education, unintended pregnancy, type of abortion, desire to avoid pregnancy for at least six months, method choice availability, attitude toward contraception, and knowledge of contraception were independent factors associated with intention. Improving women’s intention toward contraception will result in increased utilization and adherence in the future.

Recommendation

For health providers: All methods of choice should be available in each health institution. Health professionals should provide adequate information to women who had spontaneous or induced abortion to avoid pregnancy for at least six months for a good outcome of the next pregnancy.

For hospitals and health centers: Good monitoring and follow-up of contraceptive use after abortion should be implemented, including tracing clients. Periodic training should be provided for health care providers on national guidelines and effective counseling.

For researchers: Further investigation using different study designs should be conducted to identify other factors.

This study involves human data and all research methods and procedures were performed following the declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethical Review Board of Debre Markos University. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of Debre Markos University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Approval No: CMHS/R/C/TT/D/123/11/15). Consent to participate

Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from their legally authorized representative, in addition to assent from the minor participants themselves. For participants unable to read or write, the consent form was read aloud in the local language, and a thumbprint was obtained in the presence of an independent witness. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without affecting their care. Confidentiality was ensured by using codes and interviewing participants alone in a private room. Completed questionnaires and electronic data were stored securely in password protected files accessible only to the research team.
