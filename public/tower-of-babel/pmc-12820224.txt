Hyperglycemia exacerbates osteoarthritis by impairing macrophage efferocytosis through modulation of CD11b lactylation
Authors: Hao Zhou, Yong Xiao, Xinchen Xue, Jiuxiang Liu, Hongtao Xu, Jinwen Wang, Jinchun Zhou, Qiang Zuo, Wenwei Liang, Huanghe Song, Zhefeng Chen, Feng Liu, Xiao Li, Kai Shen, Weimin Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) and diabetes predominantly affect middle-aged and elderly populations, with OA patients frequently presenting with comorbid diabetes. Despite evidence that diabetes aggravates OA progression, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Here, we demonstrate that hyperglycaemia exacerbates OA progression through CD11b lactylation-mediated impairment of macrophage efferocytosis. Mechanistically, hyperglycemia enhances glycolysis in synovial macrophages, leading to lactate accumulation and CD11b lactylation at K575. This post-translational modification alters the conformation of the CD11b protein, impairing recognition of apoptotic cells and compromising synovial macrophage efferocytosis, thereby exacerbating inflammation and accelerating OA progression. Additionally, we identify CBP as a lactyl-transferase whose targeted knockdown in synovial macrophages effectively reduces lactylation levels, restores efferocytosis function, and delays hyperglycemia-associated OA progression. Collectively, our findings represent an important molecular mechanism by which hyperglycemia aggravates OA and suggest that targeting CBP could be utilized as therapeutic strategy for mitigating hyperglycemia-associated OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disease that predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly populations1–3. With the accelerating global demographic shift toward an aging population, the worldwide prevalence of OA continues to rise annually, imposing a substantial burden on both patients and society4. The pathogenesis of OA is complex, with established mechanisms including abnormal mechanical stress, cellular metabolic dysregulation, cellular senescence, and synovial inflammation2,5,6. Among these, the inflammatory theory posits that synovial inflammation damages chondrocytes through excessive release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby accelerating OA progression. Diabetes mellitus (DM) represents a group of metabolic disorders characterized primarily by hyperglycemia, also prevalent in middle-aged and elderly individuals7. As a systemic metabolic syndrome, diabetes can induce secondary pathological alterations across multiple tissues and organs8. Studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia can stimulate the production of tissue inflammatory mediators, causing persistent inflammation and resulting in continuous tissue and organ damage9,10. Both OA and diabetes are common conditions in older adults, and frequently occur concomitantly11. Some literature even reports that OA presents with increased severity in diabetic patients11,12. In recent years, researchers have proposed that diabetes constitutes a risk factor for OA development and progression12,13. However, the mechanisms by which hyperglycemia exacerbates OA progression remain unclear. Therefore, elucidating these mechanisms is of critical importance.

In contrast to the avascular articular cartilage, the richly vascularized synovium is more susceptible to hyperglycemic insult14. Synovial macrophages, the predominant immune cells within the joint synovium, play critical roles in maintaining the synovial microenvironment and joint homeostasis5,15. Under physiological conditions, synovial macrophages clear apoptotic cells through efferocytosis, thereby preserving synovial microenvironment stability. Impairment of macrophage efferocytosis leads to inadequate clearance of apoptotic cells, resulting in secondary necrosis that exacerbates synovial inflammation and promotes OA progression16,17. Our clinical sample analysis reveals that hyperglycemic patients exhibit more severe synovial inflammation and significantly compromised synovial macrophage efferocytosis. However, the molecular mechanisms by which hyperglycemia influences macrophage efferocytosis require further investigation.

Lactate, a metabolite of glycolysis, was previously considered merely an intermediate metabolite. Recent studies have revealed that lactate can regulate protein function, localization, and intracellular signaling pathways through lactylation18–20. Lactylation represents a recently discovered epigenetic regulatory mechanism that involves the covalent attachment of lactate to lysine residues on histones and non-histone proteins, participating in pathological processes including inflammation, metabolism, and tumorigenesis21. Fan et al. reported that lactate accumulation following myocardial ischemia increases Snail1 lactylation, promoting endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition and consequently exacerbating post-ischemic cardiac dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis22. Fan et al. demonstrated that Ikzf1 lactylation enhances Th17 cell differentiation, aggravating inflammatory responses in autoimmune uveitis23. Evidently, protein lactylation plays a crucial role in disease pathogenesis and progression. The macrophage efferocytosis process encompasses three phases: recognition, engulfment, and digestion of apoptotic cells, with dysfunction at any stage compromising efferocytosis24–26. Macrophages recognize phosphatidylserine (PS) on apoptotic cell surfaces through cell surface receptors, including MerTK, LRP1, and CD11b, subsequently engulfing and digesting these cells to complete the efferocytosis process. CD11b, a macrophage surface antigen, participates in macrophage adhesion, migration, and phagocytic clearance of pathogens27. However, the impact of CD11b lactylation on macrophage efferocytosis remains unexplored. Therefore, investigating whether CD11b lactylation affects macrophage efferocytosis and its implications for hyperglycemia-associated OA holds significant importance.

Epigenetic research indicates that alterations in protein conformation significantly impact protein function28–30. Extensive literature demonstrates that post-translational modifications can change protein conformation, resulting in functional alterations that contribute to disease pathogenesis and progression. Gan et al. reported that phosphorylation of PINK1 induces conformational changes and activates PINK1, thereby regulating mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production31. Eshun-Wilson et al. demonstrated that acetylation of K40 in α-tubulin alters protein conformation, affecting microtubule structure and function32. However, whether lactylation influences protein conformation and function, particularly its effects on CD11b, remains unexplored.

In the present study, we demonstrate that hyperglycemia enhances glycolysis in synovial macrophages, resulting in intracellular lactate accumulation and increased CD11b lactylation. This alters CD11b protein conformation, ultimately impairing macrophage recognition of apoptotic cells. The consequent dysfunction in synovial macrophage efferocytosis exacerbates synovial inflammation and accelerates OA progression. Furthermore, we identify CBP as a lactylation transferase and show that targeted knockdown of CBP in synovial macrophages effectively reduces lactylation levels, restores efferocytosis function, and attenuates the progression of hyperglycemia-associated OA.

Results

Hyperglycemia aggravates synovial inflammation and promotes OA progression

To determine whether hyperglycemia exacerbates OA, we analyzed clinical data from patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for end-stage primary OA (all classified as K-L grade IV). We divided the patients into the OA-only group and the diabetic OA group based on the presence of diabetes, and compared the ages of the two groups. The results showed that the average age of the OA-only group was (70.60 ± 6.73) years, while the average age of the diabetic OA group was (65.29 ± 6.97) years. The diabetic OA group was significantly younger than the simple OA group (P < 0.001), while there was no significant difference in BMI between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1a). This indicates that, even in cases of severe end-stage OA, the diabetic OA group is younger, suggesting that hyperglycemia may exacerbate the condition of OA. To evaluate the impact of hyperglycemia on synovial inflammation, we collected synovial tissue samples from healthy controls, OA-only patients, and diabetic OA patients. H&E staining demonstrated that compared to OA-only patients, diabetic OA patients exhibited more severe synovial hyperplasia, greater inflammatory cell infiltration, and significantly higher synovial inflammation scores (Fig. 1b). The immunohistochemical results of human synovial tissue show that pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and NLRP3 are significantly upregulated in the synovium of diabetic OA patients compared to those with OA-only, while the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 is downregulated (Fig. 1c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed increased macrophage infiltration in diabetic OA synovium, with a higher proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages and a lower proportion of anti-inflammatory macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). In addition, we co-cultured synovial tissue explants with chondrocytes to assess their effect on chondrocyte function. Western blot analysis revealed that, compared to the OA-only synovial co-culture group, the diabetic OA synovial co-culture group showed a significant decrease in extracellular proteoglycans (ACAN) and collagen type II (COL2A) in chondrocytes. In contrast, the levels of extracellular matrix-degrading enzymes ADAMTS5 and MMP13 were significantly increased (Fig. 1d). To further validate that hyperglycemia accelerates OA progression, we established a hyperglycemic OA mice model. Streptozotocin (STZ) was administered intraperitoneally to destroy pancreatic β-cells, and tail vein blood glucose levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L indicated successful diabetes induction. Destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) was performed to induce OA. Results showed that hyperglycemic mice developed more severe synovial inflammation (Fig. 1f) with higher synovial inflammation scores (Fig. 1g). H&E, Safranin O-Fast Green, and Toluidine Blue staining revealed significantly increased articular cartilage erosion and proteoglycan loss in hyperglycemic OA mice compared to OA-only mice (Fig. 1f, i and Supplementary Fig. 1a), with corresponding higher OARSI scores (Fig. 1h). Micro-CT analysis demonstrated significantly increased osteophyte formation and subchondral bone sclerosis in hyperglycemic OA mice compared to OA-only mice (Fig. 1f, j, k, l). Immunohistochemical results revealed that hyperglycemia significantly upregulates the expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and NLRP3 while markedly downregulating the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 in the synovium of OA mice (Fig. 1m). Immunofluorescence staining also showed increased macrophage infiltration in hyperglycemic OA mouse synovium, with a higher proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages and a lower proportion of anti-inflammatory macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hyperglycemia exacerbates synovial inflammation and accelerates OA progression.

Hyperglycemia impairs synovial macrophage efferocytosis

To investigate the mechanism by which hyperglycemia exacerbates synovial inflammation and promotes OA progression, we isolated peripheral blood monocytes from OA-only patients and diabetic OA patients. Following macrophage differentiation and exposure to normoglycemic or hyperglycemic conditions, RNA-Seq analysis was performed. Results revealed significant differential mRNA expression patterns in macrophages exposed to hyperglycemic conditions compared to normoglycemic conditions (Fig. 2a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis identified efferocytosis as significantly affected by hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 2b). We subsequently validated the impact of hyperglycemia on macrophage efferocytosis at both cellular and tissue levels. Immunofluorescence staining of synovial tissue demonstrated significantly higher levels of residual apoptotic cells in diabetic OA patients and hyperglycemic mice compared to OA-only controls (Fig. 2c, d). Quantification of cleaved-caspase3 (CC3) positive cells in synovial tissue further confirmed these findings (Fig. 2e, f). These results indicate that hyperglycemia impairs synovial macrophage efferocytosis, resulting in ineffective clearance and accumulation of apoptotic cells within the synovium. To further validate these observations, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments using induced apoptotic Jurkat cells and macrophages cultured under normoglycemic or hyperglycemic conditions. Flow cytometry analysis revealed significantly reduced efferocytosis in macrophages cultured under hyperglycemic conditions compared to normoglycemic controls (Fig. 2g). Hyperglycemia impairs macrophage efferocytosis by enhancing glycolysis

To elucidate the mechanism by which hyperglycemia impairs macrophage efferocytosis, we further analyzed the macrophage RNA-Seq data. Results revealed significant differences in glucose metabolism-related mRNA expression between macrophages cultured under normoglycemic and hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 3a). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that hyperglycemia significantly enhances macrophage glycolysis (Fig. 3a). To validate these sequencing results, we conducted Seahorse experiments to assess glucose metabolism in macrophages. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements indicated that hyperglycemia had no significant effect on macrophage oxidative phosphorylation, while proton efflux rate (PER) measurements confirmed that hyperglycemia significantly enhanced macrophage glycolysis (Fig. 3b–e). To verify whether hyperglycemia impairs macrophage efferocytosis through glycolysis, we treated hyperglycemia-exposed macrophages with dichloroacetate (DCA) to inhibit glycolysis or rotenone to promote glycolysis, and subsequently assessed efferocytosis rates. Results demonstrated that inhibiting glycolysis significantly restored macrophage efferocytosis under hyperglycemic conditions, whereas promoting glycolysis further impaired macrophage efferocytosis (Fig. 3f). To further validate whether inhibiting glycolysis could restore macrophage efferocytosis, alleviate synovial inflammation, and delay hyperglycemia-associated OA progression, we conducted in vivo experiments by intraperitoneally administering 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) to inhibit glycolysis in hyperglycemic OA mice. Results demonstrated that 2-DG administration effectively attenuated synovial inflammation and significantly reduced OA progression. H&E staining revealed that 2-DG administration significantly reduced synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration in hyperglycemic OA mice (Fig. 3g, h). Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated that 2-DG administration significantly alleviated synovial inflammation in hyperglycemic mice (Fig. 3k). H&E, Safranin O-Fast Green, and Toluidine Blue staining demonstrated that glycolysis inhibition effectively reduced cartilage erosion and proteoglycan loss (Fig. 3g, i, j and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Micro-CT analysis showed that glycolysis inhibition significantly reduced osteophyte formation and subchondral bone sclerosis in hyperglycemic OA mice knee joints (Fig. 3l–o). Immunofluorescence results demonstrated that glycolysis inhibition significantly suppressed synovial macrophage infiltration in hyperglycemic OA mice, reducing the proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages while increasing the proportion of anti-inflammatory macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Immunofluorescence analysis of synovial apoptotic cells revealed that 2-DG administration effectively reduced apoptotic cell content in the synovium of hyperglycemic OA mice, indicating that glycolysis inhibition effectively restored macrophage efferocytosis under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 3p, q). In conclusion, hyperglycemia impairs macrophage efferocytosis by enhancing glycolysis, and inhibiting glycolysis effectively restores macrophage efferocytosis, reduces synovial inflammation, and delays the progression of hyperglycemia-associated OA.

Lactate accumulation enhances macrophage protein lactylation and impairs efferocytosis

We next investigated the mechanisms by which enhanced glycolysis impairs macrophage efferocytosis. Lactate, previously regarded merely as an intermediate metabolite of glycolysis, has recently been identified as a signaling molecule capable of covalently binding to lysine residues on histones and non-histone proteins. This post-translational modification, termed lactylation, represents a novel epigenetic regulatory mechanism implicated in inflammation, metabolism, and tumorigenesis. While hyperglycemia enhances macrophage glycolysis, the intracellular lactate levels and extent of protein lactylation remained unclear. Using a lactate detection assay, we found significantly elevated intracellular lactate concentrations in macrophages cultured under hyperglycemic conditions compared to normoglycemic controls (Fig. 4a). Western blot analysis revealed significantly increased protein lactylation levels in hyperglycemia-exposed macrophages (Fig. 4b). Cellular immunofluorescence results corroborated these findings, demonstrating markedly elevated protein lactylation levels in hyperglycemia-cultured macrophages (Fig. 4c, d). We further assessed protein lactylation levels in human and mice synovial macrophages, finding significantly elevated lactylation in synovial macrophages from hyperglycemic patients and mice (Fig. 4e–h). To verify whether lactate production directly increases protein lactylation, we treated normoglycemia-cultured macrophages with exogenous lactate. Intracellular lactate measurements demonstrated that exogenous lactate stimulation significantly elevated lactate levels in macrophages, reaching magnitudes comparable to those observed under hyperglycemic culture conditions (Fig. 4i). Western blot analysis demonstrated that lactate stimulation, similar to hyperglycemic culture, significantly increased macrophage protein lactylation levels (Fig. 4j). Immunofluorescence results confirmed these findings, showing significantly increased protein lactylation following lactate stimulation (Fig. 4k, l). To determine whether increased lactylation affects macrophage efferocytosis, we assessed the efferocytosis rate of lactate-stimulated macrophages. Results demonstrated that lactate stimulation significantly reduced macrophage efferocytosis capacity (Fig. 4m). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that lactate significantly enhanced synovial macrophage infiltration and increased the proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Lactate administration elevated lactylation levels in synovial macrophages (Fig. 5i, j). Immunofluorescence analysis of synovial apoptotic cells revealed significant accumulation following lactate administration, indicating impaired synovial macrophage efferocytosis (Fig. 5k, l). Increased CD11b K575 lactylation impairs macrophage efferocytosis function

To identify specific lactylated proteins in macrophages, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and mass spectrometry analysis. Results revealed lactylation of CD11b at the K575 site (Fig. 6a). To verify whether CD11b is involved in the process of efferocytosis, we constructed CD11b knockout macrophages. The results revealed that knocking down CD11b significantly impaired the efferocytosis function of macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 4a). This indicates that CD11b plays an important role in macrophage efferocytosis. Co-IP confirmed increased CD11b lactylation levels in macrophages cultured under hyperglycemic conditions (Fig. 6b). To verify specific lactylation at the CD11b K575 site, we constructed a point mutation replacing lysine (K) with arginine (R) at position 575. Results demonstrated that hyperglycemia significantly increased lactylation of wild-type CD11b (K575), while having no notable effect on mutated CD11b (R575) lactylation levels (Fig. 6c). For exogenous validation, we transfected Flag-CD11b K575 and Flag-CD11b R575 into HEK293T cells. Co-IP results confirmed that hyperglycemia had no significant effect on R575 CD11b lactylation levels (Fig. 6d). These findings verify that hyperglycemia specifically enhances CD11b lactylation at the K575 site. To determine whether CD11b K575 lactylation affects macrophage efferocytosis function, we assessed efferocytosis rates in mutated macrophages by flow cytometry. Results revealed that hyperglycemia had no impact on efferocytosis rates in CD11b R575 macrophages (Fig. 6e). CD11b K575 lactylation alters protein conformation and reduces macrophage recognition of apoptotic cells

Epigenetic research indicates that protein conformational changes significantly impact protein localization, stability, and particularly function. Extensive literature demonstrates that post-translational modifications can alter protein conformation, resulting in functional changes that contribute to disease pathogenesis. However, whether CD11b K575 lactylation affects CD11b protein conformation and function remains unexplored. We therefore employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to evaluate the impact of CD11b K575 lactylation on CD11b conformation and its binding capacity to PS, a key apoptotic cell surface marker. Initially, we constructed comprehensive models of wild-type and lactylated CD11b-CD18 complexes based on human CD11b-CD18 crystal structures (PDB codes: 7USL, 7P2D). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4b, the root-mean-square-deviation (RMSD) of CD11b-CD18-PS and CD11bK575-Lac-CD18-PS complexes stabilized over time, indicating both models reached equilibrium states during the 300 ns simulation period. The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values for most residues in the αI domain of CD11b were lower than corresponding values in the CD11bK575-Lac αI domain (Supplementary Fig. 4c), suggesting smaller conformational changes in the wild-type CD11b αI domain compared to the lactylated variant. Post-MD simulation structural representations of CD11b and CD11bK575-Lac proteins are shown in Fig. 6f, g. To analyze conformational differences, we superimposed the CD11b and CD11bK575-Lac structures (Fig. 6h). Results revealed that the αI domain of CD11bK575-Lac was positioned closer to the Calf-2 domain compared to wild-type CD11b, confirming that lactylation at the K575 site induces conformational changes in the CD11b protein.

To determine whether these conformational changes affect CD11b’s ability to recognize apoptotic cells, we further simulated the binding capacity of wild-type and lactylated CD11b-CD18 complexes with PS. Post-MD simulation results demonstrated distinct binding modes between the proteins and the PS ligand. In the wild-type complex, CD11b residues Leu180, Lys182, and Thr185 formed hydrogen bonds with PS, while Lys182 additionally participated in electrostatic interactions. Furthermore, CD11b residues Phe187, Met169, and Ile256 engaged in hydrophobic interactions with PS (Fig. 6i). In the CD11bK575-Lac-CD18-PS complex, CD11bK575-Lac formed a hydrogen bond network with PS involving residues Arg142, Pro145, Gln146, Lys182, and Ser344. Residues Arg142 and Lys182 of CD11bK575-Lac also participated in electrostatic interactions with the ligand. In addition, CD11bK575-Lac residues Met169, Phe318, and Leu321 formed hydrophobic interactions with PS (Fig. 6j). Binding free energy calculations revealed values of − 102.01 and − 94.90 kcal/mol for CD11b-CD18 and CD11bK575-Lac-CD18 with PS, respectively (Fig. 6k), indicating stronger binding between PS and wild-type CD11b-CD18 compared to its lactylated counterpart. Specifically, binding energy for PS with CD11b-CD18 was predominantly determined by ΔEvdw (van der Waals energy), ΔEele (electrostatic potential energy), and ΔGnonpolar (nonpolar solvation energy); whereas binding energy for PS with CD11bK575-Lac-CD18 was primarily governed by ΔEvdw and ΔGnonpolar (Fig. 6k). Supplementary Fig. 4d presents energy decomposition analysis for key residues contributing ΔGbind values less than − 2.0 kcal/mol in both complexes. Notably, seven residues—Phe172, Leu180, Lys182, Val254, Tyr283, Ile285, and Phe313—contributed to protein-PS ligand binding in both complex models. These results demonstrate that CD11b K575 lactylation alters CD11b conformation, reducing its recognition capacity for apoptotic cells and consequently impairing macrophage efferocytosis function.

CBP acts as a lactylation transferase binding to CD11b and catalyzing its lactylation

Lactylation transferases can covalently attach lactate to lysine residues on histone or non-histone proteins. We further sought to identify the lactylation transferase that catalyzes CD11b lactylation. Since both lactylation and acetylation modifications occur on lysine residues, and most currently identified lactylation transferases are acetyltransferases, we employed Co-IP coupled with mass spectrometry analysis to identify proteins interacting with CD11b in macrophages cultured under high glucose conditions, and intersected these with acetyltransferases. Results revealed that the acetyltransferase CBP interacts with CD11b (Fig. 7a). Endogenous Co-IP experiments confirmed the interaction between CD11b and CBP (Fig. 7b, c). When exogenous Flag-CD11b and His-CBP were transfected into HEK293T cells, Co-IP results also demonstrated the interaction between CD11b and CBP (Fig. 7g). To determine whether CBP functions as a lactylation transferase for CD11b, we constructed lentivirus for CBP knockdown (Fig. 7d). Results showed that CBP knockdown significantly reduced lactylation levels in macrophages following high glucose and lactate stimulation (Fig. 7e). Co-IP results also indicated that CD11b lactylation levels were significantly decreased in macrophages after CBP knockdown (Fig. 7f). In addition, we treated macrophages with the CBP inhibitor A-485. The results showed that following the administration of A-485, the levels of lactylation modification of global proteins in macrophages were significantly reduced after high glucose culture and exogenous lactate stimulation. Co-IP results also confirmed that the lactylation of CD11b was decreased (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). This is consistent with the findings from the endogenous CBP knockout experiments. Efferocytosis function assays demonstrated that knockdown of CBP significantly restored the efferocytosis rates of macrophages cultured under high-glucose conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Targeted knockdown of CBP in synovial macrophages delays OA progression in hyperglycemic mice

To evaluate the potential of targeting knockdown CBP in synovial macrophages to reduce protein lactylation levels, restore macrophage efferocytosis function, and alleviate synovial inflammation, we conducted further in vivo validation in mice. We constructed rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP for targeted knockdown of CBP in synovial macrophages. First, we conducted the safety assessment of the intra-articular injection of rAAV-shCBP. The results showed that all mice survived six weeks after the injection. Since rAAV carries ZsGreen fluorescence, we found that after the intra-articular injection, ZsGreen was only enriched in the knee joint, with no infection observed in distant organs (Supplementary Fig. 6a). In addition, we collected samples from the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of the mice for sectioning. The results also indicated that there was no rAAV-shCBP infection in these vital organs (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Furthermore, HE staining of the tissues revealed that, compared to normal control mice, there were no significant structural changes in the important organs of mice injected with rAAV-shCBP (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Therefore, the intra-articular injection of targeted synovial macrophages with rAAV-shCBP showed no significant toxicity in mice. After intra-articular injection of rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP into mouse knee joints, we collected tissues 3 weeks later to observe ZsGreen-positive cells in the joints. Results showed significantly higher ZsGreen expression in the synovium compared to cartilage (Supplementary Fig. 6d), demonstrating satisfactory synovial targeting efficiency of rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP. We then performed intra-articular injections of rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP or the negative control rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shNC into the knee joints of hyperglycemic OA mice. Results showed that intra-articular injection of rAAV-shCBP significantly reduced CBP content in the synovium (Fig. 8a). H&E staining revealed that knockdown of CBP in synovial macrophages significantly reduced synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration, alleviating synovial inflammation in hyperglycemic OA mice (Fig. 8b, c, f and Supplementary Fig. 6f, g). H&E staining, Safranin O-Fast Green staining, and Toluidine Blue staining demonstrated that rAAV-shCBP significantly delayed cartilage wear and proteoglycan loss in hyperglycemic OA mice (Fig. 8b, d, e and Supplementary Fig. 6e). Micro-CT results showed that targeted knockdown of CBP in synovial macrophages significantly reduced osteophyte formation and subchondral bone sclerosis in hyperglycemic OA mice (Fig. 8g–i). Discussion

The pathogenesis of OA involves a comprehensive joint pathology, primarily characterized by the imbalance of articular cartilage, abnormal subchondral bone remodeling, and chronic synovial inflammation33,34. Due to the complexity of its pathogenesis, there are currently no effective treatment methods available. Existing intervention strategies include the stabilization of HIF-1α to maintain cartilage function35; the use of nanocarriers (such as liposomes and exosomes) to deliver nanoenzymes, mitochondria, or gene-editing tools to eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS), restore metabolic functions, and promote regeneration36; and the application of novel models, such as organ chips, to accelerate the clinical translation of targeted drugs and biological agents37. Diabetes, as a systemic metabolic syndrome, can cause secondary pathological changes in multiple tissues and organs throughout the body8,38. However, the relationship between diabetes and OA remains controversial. Some literature reports indicate that diabetes could increase the risk of OA, but when adjusted for body mass index, the relationship between diabetes and OA weakens and becomes non-significant3,39. This suggests that diabetes may promote OA development due to patient weight rather than diabetes itself. Other literature reports, however, identify diabetes as an independent risk factor for OA12,13. In our study, we collected data from patients undergoing TKA and analyzed the age differences between OA-only patients and diabetic OA patients. We found that diabetic OA patients were significantly younger when undergoing TKA compared to patients with OA alone. Since all patients undergoing TKA had stage IV OA, the younger age of diabetic OA patients indicates that diabetes can exacerbate OA progression. Histological staining of clinical samples revealed that, compared to OA-only patients, diabetic OA patients exhibited more severe synovial hyperplasia and higher synovial inflammation scores. This further demonstrates that OA is more severe in diabetic patients. In addition, our animal experiments showed that hyperglycemic mice developed more severe synovial inflammation and faster OA progression. Therefore, through our current research, we can confirm that hyperglycemia aggravates OA progression, suggesting that hyperglycemia is indeed one of the risk factors for OA.

OA and diabetes are common conditions in middle-aged and elderly populations, and they frequently occur together40,41. Notably, patients with hyperglycemia often experience more severe OA. Despite blood glucose control measures, the incidence of OA in diabetic patients remains high. Literature suggests that diabetes is a risk factor for OA development and progression. However, the mechanisms by which hyperglycemia promotes OA progression have remained unclear. This comprehensive study, integrating clinical samples, cellular experiments, and animal models, reveals a novel mechanism: hyperglycemia promotes glycolysis in synovial macrophages, leading to intracellular lactate accumulation. This results in increased lactylation modification at the K575 site of CD11b, altering its protein conformation. Consequently, macrophages exhibit reduced ability to recognize apoptotic cells, impairing their efferocytosis function. This impairment exacerbates synovial inflammation and promotes OA progression. Furthermore, we identified CBP as a lactylation transferase. By targeting and knocking down CBP in synovial macrophages, we effectively reduced lactylation modification levels in these cells, restored efferocytosis function, and delayed the progression of hyperglycemia-associated OA.

It is well established that articular cartilage primarily receives nutrition from the synovium. Therefore, pathological changes in synovial cells inevitably lead to cartilage cell dysfunction, promoting cartilage degeneration42. Synovial inflammation is a typical characteristic and important regulatory factor in OA development, damaging chondrocytes through the secretion of inflammatory factors and accelerating OA progression43–45. Synovial tissue is rich in blood vessels with abundant blood supply, whereas articular cartilage lacks blood supply46. As a result, synovial tissue is more susceptible to hyperglycemic effects, which is why we selected synovial tissue as our research focus. Synovial macrophages are the most important immune cells in the joint synovium, playing crucial roles in maintaining the synovial microenvironment and joint homeostasis. Therefore, in this study, we primarily focused on the effects of hyperglycemia on synovial macrophages.

Macrophage efferocytosis plays a critical role in maintaining synovial microenvironment homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, apoptotic cells in the synovium are cleared by macrophages through efferocytosis, thereby maintaining stability in the synovial microenvironment24,25. When macrophage efferocytosis is impaired, apoptotic cells cannot be cleared in a timely manner. These uncleared apoptotic cells eventually undergo secondary necrosis, exacerbating synovial inflammation and promoting OA progression16. Macrophages recognize apoptotic cells through surface receptors, including MerTK, LRP1, and CD11b, which identify PS and other antigens on the apoptotic cell surface. This recognition initiates the subsequent phagocytosis and digestion processes, ultimately completing efferocytosis25. In our study, we found that hyperglycemia promotes glycolysis in macrophages, impairing their efferocytosis function. Literature has reported that after completing efferocytosis, macrophages utilize breakdown products from apoptotic cells to induce a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, thereby promoting continuous efferocytosis47. This appears to contradict our finding that glycolysis impairs efferocytosis function. However, this discrepancy may be explained by the level of glycolysis: moderate increases in glycolysis may provide energy support for macrophage efferocytosis, but hyperglycemia-induced excessive glycolysis leads to intracellular lactate accumulation that cannot be cleared in a timely manner, resulting in elevated protein lactylation modification levels. This explanation is consistent with our results.

Lactylation is a novel post-translational modification where lactate forms a covalent bond with lysine residues on proteins through the action of lactylation transferases21. However, the impact of lactylation on protein function remains controversial. Zhang et al. reported that histone lactylation promotes the conversion of pro-inflammatory macrophages to anti-inflammatory macrophages, suggesting that lactylation has anti-inflammatory effects21. In contrast, Fan et al. reported that Ikzf1 lactylation activates microglia, aggravating inflammatory responses in autoimmune uveitis23. This “double-edged sword” effect of lactylation in inflammation may be determined by cell type, modification target, or disease stage, requiring more spatiotemporally specific studies for validation.

Our Co-IP/MS identified 68 lactylated proteins in macrophages. Among these, the five proteins with the highest abundance of lactylation were ASTE1, CEP162, CD11b, DENND4A, and AGA. Moreover, previous studies have reported that CD11b serves as a surface antigen on macrophages, participating in myeloid cell adhesion, migration, inflammatory induction, and phagocytic clearance of pathogens—functions directly relevant to our investigation of efferocytosis27. And our results also showed the loss of CD11b significantly impaired macrophage efferocytic function. These factors led us to select CD11b as our primary research target. The impact of other lactylation-modified proteins identified in this study on efferocytosis remains to be investigated further.

In this study, we found that high glucose stimulation promotes glycolysis in macrophages without significantly affecting oxidative phosphorylation, which aligns with findings from other researchers48. Theoretically, when cells are provided with abundant glucose, both oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis should increase; however, our observations indicate otherwise. This discrepancy remains uninvestigated to date and warrants further in-depth research. Similarly, the mechanisms by which hyperglycemia enhances cellular glycolysis require additional investigation. Based on transcriptomic sequencing of synovial macrophages, we found that high glucose enhances glycolysis and impairs macrophage efferocytosis. Subsequent experimental validation confirmed that high glucose-induced glycolytic activation directly compromises efferocytosis. The transcriptome data also revealed differentially expressed genes in macrophages under high glucose conditions. The mechanisms governing macrophage efferocytosis remain incompletely understood. It is plausible that hyperglycemia may additionally impair efferocytic capacity through alternative pathways, such as modulating the expression of specific genes in macrophages. This potential regulatory dimension warrants further investigation. In our in vitro experiments, inhibiting glycolysis restored the efferocytosis rate of macrophages in high glucose environments. Furthermore, injecting 2-DG into hyperglycemic mice alleviated synovial inflammation and delayed OA progression. Previous studies have shown that increased glycolysis in chondrocytes can exacerbate OA, while glycolysis inhibition can delay OA development49. When we administered intraperitoneal 2-DG to inhibit glycolysis, we think it primarily affected synovial macrophages rather than chondrocytes. This differential impact occurs because synovial tissue is richly vascularized with an abundant blood supply, whereas articular cartilage lacks blood supply, making synovial macrophages more susceptible to systemic medications than chondrocytes.

In summary, our research provides comprehensive evidence from clinical samples, cellular experiments, and animal models demonstrating that hyperglycemia exacerbates synovial inflammation and accelerates OA progression. Through mechanistic investigation, we discovered that hyperglycemia promotes glycolysis in synovial macrophages, leading to intracellular lactate accumulation. This metabolic alteration results in increased lactylation modification at the K575 site of CD11b, altering its protein conformation. These structural changes ultimately compromise the macrophage’s ability to recognize apoptotic cells, impairing efferocytosis function in synovial macrophages. This functional impairment intensifies synovial inflammation and promotes OA development. Furthermore, we identified CBP as a lactylation transferase. By targeting and knocking down CBP in synovial macrophages, we effectively reduced lactylation modification levels within these cells, restored efferocytosis function, and successfully delayed the progression of hyperglycemia-associated OA.

Methods

Clinical data

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (Approval No: 2024-SR-387). All patients were informed prior to data collection and provided written informed consent. In this study, we collected data from patients who underwent TKA for primary OA at our hospital from 2019 to 2024. All included patients reported pain in the affected knee, which worsened with movement and walking, and did not improve with conservative treatment, severely affecting their quality of life and work. The inclusion criteria for the study were: (1) patients with end-stage knee OA (Kellgren–Lawrence grade IV); (2) primary unilateral TKA; (3) knee pain that significantly impacted the patient’s quality of life, unrelieved by conservative treatment. The exclusion criteria were: (1) diabetes duration of less than 5 years; (2) diabetic patients with well-controlled blood glucose over a long period (HbA1c < 6%); (3) patients with other metabolic diseases, such as hyperlipidemia and hyperuricemia; (4) patients with rheumatoid arthritis or other non-primary osteoarthritis conditions. After screening based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1547 patients were included in the study, comprising 628 males and 919 females, with an average age of (69.16 ± 6.77) years and an average BMI of (26.78 ± 6.77) kg/m². Based on the presence of diabetes, patients were divided into the OA-only group and the diabetic OA group (HbA1c > 6%). The OA-only group included 1046 patients, while the diabetic OA group had 501 patients. In the simple OA group, there were 804 females and 242 males, with an average age of (70.60 ± 6.73) years and an average BMI of (26.59 ± 5.01) kg/m². In the diabetic OA group, there were 400 females and 101 males, with an average age of (65.29 ± 6.97) years and an average BMI of (26.97 ± 3.75) kg/m².

Synovial tissue and cartilage were collected from both primary OA and diabetic OA patients during their TKA surgeries. For normal control synovial tissue, we collected synovial tissue excised during knee arthroscopy from patients with meniscus or ligament injuries whose imaging studies showed no significant OA manifestations.

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells and chondrocytes

Peripheral blood was collected from both primary OA patients and diabetic OA patients. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using a PBMC extraction kit (Beyotime, China) via centrifugation method. The isolated PBMCs were then cultured in either normal glucose (5 mM) or high glucose (25 mM) culture medium. Macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to induce PBMCs to differentiate into macrophages for subsequent experiments.

Cartilage from non-damaged areas of OA patients was collected for chondrocyte extraction. The chondrocyte extraction method was the same as previously described50. Extracted chondrocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). When chondrocyte density reached 80-90%, cells were passaged. Third and fourth passage chondrocytes were used for subsequent experiments.

Co-culture of synovial explants with chondrocytes

Synovial tissue explants collected during surgery were co-cultured with the previously extracted chondrocytes. The co-culture system was established using Transwell chambers with a pore size of 0.4 μm, allowing the exchange of soluble substances such as cytokines. The collected synovial tissue was cut into pieces of approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm and placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell insert, while chondrocytes were seeded in the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells. The co-culture was maintained for 3 days. Following the co-culture period, chondrocytes were collected from the lower chamber, and proteins were extracted. Western blot analysis was performed to examine the status of cartilage extracellular matrix components, including Aggrecan, COL2A, MMP13, and ADAMTS5, in the chondrocytes.

Micromass assay

Chondrocytes were digested and counted for use in the micromass experiment. The procedure was performed as previously described by our group51. Briefly, 20 μL of culture medium containing 4 × 105 chondrocytes were spotted in the center of each well of a 24-well plate and incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, synovial explants from different experimental groups were placed in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts and co-cultured with the chondrocyte micromass for 7 days. After the 7-day culture period, the micromass were fixed and stained with Alcian blue (Solar-bio, China).

Establishment of Hyperglycemic and OA Mice Models

C57BL/6 J mice used in experiments were purchased from Nanjing Medical University and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with no more than 5 mice per cage. The mice were maintained at constant temperature and humidity with a controlled 12 h light/dark cycle. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum. Considering the influence of sex and age on the stability of the experiment, 6-week-old male mice were used in this study. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No: IACUC-2408036).

The hyperglycemic mice model was established through intraperitoneal injection of Streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma, S0130). C57BL/6 J mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of STZ (200 mg/kg) for 5 consecutive days. Blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein one week after the final injection, and mice with levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/L were considered successfully modeled.

The OA mice model was established using the DMM method. Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and after knee joint exposure, the medial meniscotibial ligament of the right knee was transected. For the sham group, only the joint capsule was opened without transection of the medial meniscotibial ligament, followed by layer-by-layer suturing of the joint capsule and skin. All mice were euthanized with CO2 and tissues were harvested 6 weeks post-surgery for subsequent analyses.

Micro-CT

Following fixation, mouse knee joints were scanned using a SkyScan 1176 micro-CT system (Burker, Germany) at a resolution of 8 µm. The acquired data were reconstructed into 3D images, and quantitative analyses were performed using SkyScan analysis software to determine the number of osteophytes, trabecular bone volume per total volume (BV/TV), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in the knee joints.

Histological and Immunofluorescence Staining

For histological staining, human synovial tissues and decalcified mice knee joint tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Following deparaffinization, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Safranin O-Fast Green (SO-FG), and Toluidine Blue (TB) according to standard protocols. Synovial inflammation was evaluated using the Krenn synovitis scoring system (0–9)52. The severity of cartilage degeneration was assessed using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system (grades 0–6)53.

For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were first deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate antigen retrieval solution (Beyotime, China). Tissues were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 5% BSA (Biosharp, China) for 1 hour. Sections were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, sections were incubated with corresponding Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594-labeled secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI solution for 10 min. Finally, tissues were mounted with mounting medium. All images were captured using a fluorescence microscope or confocal microscope (Leica, Germany). Fluorescence intensity and proportion of positive cells were analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA-Seq Analysis

Total RNA from macrophages cultured under normal and high glucose conditions was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA quality was assessed, and RNA purity was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). After quality verification, 1.5 μg of RNA from each sample was submitted to Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. for library construction and sequencing. Briefly, cDNA was synthesized using mRNA as a template. Following cDNA purification, end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation were performed. Fragment size selection (200–250 bp) of double-stranded cDNA was conducted using AMPure XP beads, followed by PCR amplification to construct the cDNA library. After quality control of the libraries, pooling was performed according to effective library concentration and required data output. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina Hiseq4000 platform using a PE150 sequencing strategy. HTSeq software (v 0.5.4 p3) was used to analyze gene expression levels in each sample, followed by differential expression analysis and GO and GSEA pathway enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes.

Efferocytosis assay

Efferocytosis capacity of macrophages was assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, Jurkat cells were induced to undergo apoptosis by ultraviolet irradiation (254 nm, UVP) and subsequently labeled with CFSE (Thermo Scientific, USA). The labeled apoptotic Jurkat cells were co-cultured with macrophages for 4 h. Following co-culture, unphagocytosed Jurkat cells were removed by washing, and the macrophages were collected and stained with CD68- CoraLite. Flow cytometry was then performed to determine the efferocytosis rate of macrophages.

Seahorse assay

Mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis in macrophages were assessed using the Seahorse XF96 Mitochondrial Stress Test and Proton Efflux Rate assay (Agilent Technologies, USA). Briefly, macrophages cultured under different conditions were seeded in Seahorse XF96 microplates at a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The Seahorse probe plate was hydrated overnight in a 37 °C non-CO₂ incubator. The following day, culture medium in the 96-well plate was replaced with Seahorse assay medium, and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator. For the mitochondrial stress test, baseline measurements were recorded, followed by sequential injections of 2 μM oligomycin, 3 μM FCCP, and 1 μM rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA) to assess mitochondrial function. For glycolysis rate determination, following baseline glycolysis measurements, macrophages were treated with 0.5 μM Rot/AA and 50 mM 2-DG for continued glycolysis assessment. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and proton efflux rate (PER) data were normalized to cell number and analyzed using WAVE software (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Animal treatment

To investigate the impact of glycolysis on synovial macrophage efferocytosis and OA progression, we inhibited glycolysis in hyperglycemic OA mice through intraperitoneal administration of 2-DG (MCE, HY-13966). Mice received weekly intraperitoneal injections of 2-DG at 0.5 g/kg. Assessments were conducted after 6 weeks of treatment.

To examine the effect of lactylation modifications on synovial macrophage efferocytosis, we administered lactate (MCE, HY-B2227) to OA mice via intraperitoneal injection. Following OA induction, mice received weekly intraperitoneal injections of lactate at 0.5 g/kg. After 6 weeks, tissues were harvested to assess synovial lactylation modifications, synovial inflammation, macrophage efferocytosis function, and OA progression.

Lactate content determination

Lactate content was measured using the CheKine Micro Lactate Detection Kit (Abbkine, China). Briefly, cells were lysed in Lactate Assay Buffer at a ratio of 1 mL per 5 × 106 cells and subjected to ultrasonic disruption on ice for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and lactate concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Western blotting and immunoprecipitation

Western blotting was performed to determine intracellular protein levels. Protein from tissue and cell samples was collected using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). After determining protein concentration with a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime, China), protein supernatants were mixed with loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. The denatured protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Following blocking in 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h, PVDF membranes were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and documented with a Tanon 5200 imaging system. The primary antibodies were listed in Supplementary Table.1. Uncropped gels have been provided in the Source Data file.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed according to the protocol of the immunoprecipitation kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, protein A/G magnetic beads were first washed with TBST and then incubated with the required primary antibodies or IgG antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The antibody-bound beads were then incubated with protein samples overnight at 4 °C on a rotary mixer. After incubation, beads were separated and washed with lysis buffer. Proteins were then eluted using SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer via heat denaturation. For tagged proteins, elution was performed using 3 × Flag peptide or 6×His peptide (Beyotime, China) through peptide competition. The eluted proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE, membrane transfer, and visualization as described above.

IP-Mass spectrometry assay

Total protein was extracted from macrophages cultured under different conditions and subjected to immunoprecipitation using protein A/G magnetic beads and Pan-Kla antibody to isolate all lactylated proteins, as described above. Proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis, and protein-containing gel bands from different positions were excised for in-gel digestion and peptide extraction. The extracted peptides were ionized through a nanoESI source and analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) in Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode to identify lactylated proteins. The same methodology was applied to identify proteins interacting with CD11b in macrophages cultured under high glucose conditions.

Construction of CD11b Knockout THP1 cell lines

CD11b gene knockout THP1 cells was achieved using CRISPR-Cas9 RNP containing humanized hSpCas9 expression cassette and chimeric guide RNA (Haixing Bioscience, China). Two specific guide RNA sequences targeting exons 6-12 of the CD11b gene were selected using the http://crispr.mit.edu website: GTCCGGGTGTTCTCCCATTGAGG and TATGTGGGAACTAGCGAGTGAGG. Following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), RNP complexes were delivered into cells using the Neon electroporation system. After 48 hours post-transfection, single-cell clones were transferred to 96-well plates for culture. To identify insertion/deletion mutations (indels) in CD11b-targeted clones, genomic DNA was isolated using the Quick-DNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research) and PCR amplification of exon-flanking sequences was performed using 2 × Taq Master Mix (Dye Plus; Vazyme, P112) with primers: forward 5’-AGAAGGAGGTCTGCTTTGGG-3’, reverse 5’-GTCGTAGGGTGGAGGGAGAG-3’. Sanger sequencing (GENEWIZ, China) was conducted to analyze genomic DNA from single clones. Double allelic mutant clones were selected for subsequent studies. Cell lines constructed using this strategy included: wild-type (+/+), heterozygous (+/−), two heterozygous subtypes (+/−A and +/−B), and homozygous knockout (−/−) cells. All clones were maintained under the same culture conditions as the parental cells.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The crystal structure of the CD11b protein with sequence Phe17- Phe766 (PDB ID: 7P2D) was superimposed with the crystal structure of the CD11b-CD18 complex (PDB ID: 7USL) to add the missing residues in CD11b (PDB ID: 7USL), and a relatively complete complex of the CD11b protein (residues Phe17-Pro1099) and CD18 protein was constructed. The 3D structure of PS was generated from PubChem (Compound ID: 9547096). For the molecular docking of CD11b and PS, we employed AutoDock-GPU on the Wecomput platform (https://wemol.wecomput.com/ui/#/) to obtain the possible conformations and orientations of the ligand at the binding sites. The αI domain of CD11b was used as the binding site. The 3D structure of PS used as the ligand and was minimized using the Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF). The docking simulation used the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm and empirical free energy scoring function. During the docking process, the CD11b protein remained fixed while PS was allowed certain flexibility. A total of 100 genetic algorithms (GA) runs were performed for the ligand. The most probable complex was selected based on the binding site and docking score. In addition, the CD11bK575-Lac-CD18-PS model was modeled by introducing K575 lactylation in the CD11b protein.

The MD simulations were performed with Amber22 software for the CD11b-CD18-PS and CD11bK575-Lac-CD18-PS complex models. AMBER19SB Force Field and General AMBER Force Field (GAFF) Force Field parameters were used for the protein and ligand, respectively. The partial atomic charges of epicatechin were calculated by the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charge following the molecule optimization of lactated K575 and PS at B3LYP/6-31 G(d) level by the ORCA package. Then each of the models were neutralized and solvated in a box with TIP3P water molecules. The solvent layers between the box edges and solute surface were set to 1.0 nm.

During the MD simulations, First, the steepest descent (SD) and CG methods were employed to remove the bad contact of initial structures. Second, each system was heated gradually from 0 to 310 K within 1.0 ns, with a positional restraint of 20.0 kcal mol-1 Å−2 on protein. Third, the systems were further equilibrated in a canonical ensemble (NVT) for 1.0 ns at 310 K with 10 kcal mol-1 Å−2 harmonic position restraints applied to the protein atoms. Subsequently, equilibration of the systems was carried out in an isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT, T = 310 K and P = 1 atm). Finally, the 300 ns MD simulations were carried out at 310 K and 1 atm and MD snapshots were collected every 10 ps for post-MD analysis. All MD simulations were performed with periodic boundary conditions and the Newton equations were integrated with a 2.0 fs time step using the Verlet algorithm. A cut-off distance of 10 Å was used for the van der Waals and electrostatic interactions. Based on the result of the production run, the binding free energy of CD11b-CD18 protein and PS was calculated using the molecular mechanics generalized born surface area (MM/GBSA) method by employing the MMPBSA.py program from AmberTools22 suit.

The molecular visualizations of the dynamic movie were carried out using the UCSF Chimera program, and 3D interaction diagrams of the complex were created with PyMOL software.

Lentiviral infection

CBP knockdown (sh-CBP1: 5’-GCACAGAAUGUUGGAGUAUUU-3’, sh-CBP2: 5’-GGAUCUACUUCUACGACAUUU-3’, sh-CBP3: 5’-GCTGGAACAAGACCATCATAA-3’) and control lentiviruses were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were infected with lentiviruses in the presence of 5 μg/mL polybrene (MOI of 50 for macrophages). At 48 h post-infection, stable cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 1 week. The stably infected cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Molecular docking

The structure of the catalytic core of human CBP was constructed through homology modeling using the MODELER program, with the crystal structure (PDB ID: 5U7G) serving as a template. For docking of the CD11b protein with the human CBP catalytic core, the HDOCK program on the Wecomput platform (https://wemol.wecomput.com/ui/#/) was utilized to generate the CD11b-CBP complex model based on binding sites and docking scores. The molecular visualizations of 3D interaction diagrams of complex were created with PyMOL software.

Construction and transfection of plasmid fragments

Human Flag-tagged CD11b(K575), Flag-tagged CD11b(R575), His-tagged CBP FL(1-2442aa), His-tagged CBP MUT1(1-682aa), His-tagged CBP MUT2(683-1311aa), and His-tagged CBP MUT3(1312-2442aa) plasmids were all constructed by SYNBIO Biotech (Suzhou, China). All plasmids were transfected into macrophages and HEK 293 T cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Construction and Intra-articular Injection of Recombinant Adeno-associated Virus

To target and knock down CBP in synovial macrophages, we constructed recombinant adeno-associated virus rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP-WPRE (rAAV-shCBP). Both rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shNC-WPRE and rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP-WPRE were purchased from Obio Technology (Shanghai, China).

To evaluate the synovial targeting specificity of rAAV-F4/80-ZsGreen-shCBP-WPRE, 10 μL of rAAV-shCBP was injected into the mouse joint cavity. Three weeks post-injection, mice were euthanized with CO2 and knee joints were collected. ZsGreen expression in synovial tissue was observed using a fluorescence microscope.

To target and knockdown CBP in mouse synovial macrophages, 10 μL of rAAV-shCBP (7 × 10¹² vg/mL) or control rAAV-shNC was injected into the joint cavity on days 7, 21, and 35 after OA induction. The same injection protocol was applied to the sham group mice. All mice were euthanized with CO2 6 weeks after model establishment, and knee joints were harvested.

Immunohistochemical staining

For immunohistochemical staining, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval as described above. Subsequently, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 15 min at room temperature to inactivate endogenous enzymes, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were then incubated with CBP primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The following day, after washing with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO, China) for 1 hour at room temperature. Visualization was performed using a Diaminobenzidine kit (DAB). Finally, nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and sections were mounted. All images were observed and captured using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software was used for data visualization and statistical analysis. Differences between the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparison tests was employed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
