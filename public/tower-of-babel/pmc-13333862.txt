Preparation and performance of alkali-activated synchronous grouting material incorporating shield muck
Authors: Huifang Zhang, Lei Wang, Yingxue Wang, Chengqian Qu, Zheying Liu, Xiaoli Chai, Haifang Zhang, Peng Wang, Jie Chen, Zheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid development of urban underground space, the large volume of muck generated from shield tunneling has posed significant environmental and engineering challenges in terms of disposal and stockpiling. To promote the resource utilization of shield tunneling muck and reduce the consumption of natural sand, this study employs tunneling muck as a fine aggregate to partially or fully replace natural river sand, while fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, steel slag, and silica fume are used as cementitious components to prepare an alkali-activated synchronous grouting material. The effects of muck replacement ratio (0%–100%) on the workability, mechanical properties, durability, and microstructural characteristics of the grouting slurry were systematically investigated.The results indicate that as the muck replacement ratio increases from 0% to 100%, the flowability of the slurry decreases from 385 mm to 144 mm, while the setting time is shortened from 118 min to 30 min; notably, the bleeding rate remains zero throughout the entire replacement range. In terms of mechanical performance, both 3-day and 28-day compressive strengths exhibit a trend of initial increase followed by a decline with increasing replacement ratio, reaching peak values of 6.98 MPa and 11.54 MPa, respectively, at a replacement ratio of 40%. Durability tests reveal that resistance to salt attack and freeze–thaw cycles gradually deteriorates with increasing muck content. The mixture with a 20% replacement ratio (M1) demonstrates the best durability performance, exhibiting corrosion resistance coefficients of 0.916 and 0.905 after immersion in 5% NaCl and 5% MgSO₄ solutions for 56 days, respectively, and a compressive strength loss of only 8.76% after 20 freeze–thaw cycles.However, when the replacement ratio exceeds 40%, the slurry flowability deteriorates sharply, accompanied by pronounced reductions in mechanical properties and water-dispersion resistance. SEM and XRD analyses reveal that at muck contents of 40% or lower, muck particles are effectively encapsulated by C–A–S–H gels, forming a dense microstructure; at higher replacement levels, insufficient cementitious phases lead to weakened interfacial bonding and increased porosity. Considering the overall performance and resource utilization efficiency, it is recommended that the muck replacement ratio be controlled within 40% under the investigated mix design to achieve an optimal balance between engineering performance and sustainable resource utilization.Keywords: shield tunneling muck, synchronous grouting material, alkali activation, steel slagSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

With the continuous expansion of urban underground space, shield tunneling has been extensively adopted in metro and tunnel construction due to its high efficiency, safety, and environmental friendliness. However, this construction method inevitably generates a large quantity of shield muck. Conventional disposal practices, which mainly rely on off-site transportation and landfilling, not only consume substantial land resources but also pose potential risks of environmental pollution. Consequently, the scientific management and resource-oriented utilization of shield muck have become critical issues in contemporary underground engineering.

In recent years, considerable efforts have been devoted to exploring the resource-oriented utilization of shield muck in the production of construction materials, roadbed fillers, and synchronous grouting materials. Among these applications, the use of tunneling muck as a raw material for synchronous grouting can effectively reduce the volume of muck transported off-site, lower disposal costs, and promote the reduction and harmless treatment of tunneling waste. Nevertheless, due to its inherently high water content, high compressibility, and complex composition, shield muck cannot directly satisfy engineering performance requirements, making mix proportion optimization and material modification essential to enhance its overall properties.Alkali-activation technology has attracted increasing attention in the development of tunneling muck-based grouting materials owing to its capability to improve mechanical performance and durability. Under alkaline conditions, reactive aluminosilicate components in fly ash and slag dissolve and subsequently participate in gel formation and structural build-up of the binder matrix. In fly ash-based alkali-activated systems, the reaction process and setting behavior are strongly influenced by the activator composition. Liu et al.1 reported that the use of sodium hydroxide and anhydrous borax as a composite activator could effectively regulate the setting behavior of Class F fly ash systems, indicating that the early-stage reaction and structural evolution of alkali-activated fly ash are highly sensitive to activator chemistry. Therefore, the performance of alkali-activated grouting materials depends not only on precursor composition but also on the chemical characteristics of the activator system. As the primary strength-contributing phase in alkali-activated systems, the morphology and quantity of C–A–S–H gels play a decisive role in governing the macroscopic performance of the material. Moreover, the synergistic utilization of fly ash, slag, and steel slag further contributes to the enhancement of grouting material performance.A number of studies have systematically investigated this topic. Wu et al.2 successfully developed a novel synchronous grouting material through the on-site resource utilization of shield muck. Du et al.3 employed slag and fly ash as solid precursors to produce a high-performance grouting system. Zhou et al.4 prepared a ternary alkali-activated synchronous grouting material and reported that the incorporation of slag facilitated the formation of a denser microstructure, thereby improving impermeability and durability. Zhang et al.5 established regression models based on response surface methodology to systematically analyze the effects of multiple factors on the workability and mechanical properties of grouting materials.From an engineering practice perspective, Luo et al.6, based on a Zhengzhou Metro project, evaluated the feasibility of using shield muck as synchronous grouting material and obtained optimal mix proportions through multi-objective optimization, achieving significant cost reductions while effectively controlling ground settlement and segment uplift. Liu et al.7 comprehensively elucidated resource utilization pathways for shield muck in road engineering and demonstrated that solidification treatment markedly improved its strength and durability. Zhou et al.8, relying on a Suzhou rail transit project, successfully prepared synchronous grouting slurries from shield muck that met engineering requirements and realized their application in overlapping tunnel construction.

From the perspectives of environmental impact and economic performance, Li et al.9 employed life cycle assessment (LCA) to evaluate the carbon emission intensity associated with the resource utilization of shield muck, demonstrating that this approach can substantially reduce carbon emissions while delivering notable economic benefits. Ren10 prepared synchronous grouting material incorporating shield muck by partially replacing cement with rice husk ash, achieving cost reduction alongside improved slurry performance. Cao et al.11 further optimized the mix proportion of fly ash–shield muck–based concrete using response surface methodology, resulting in enhanced overall material performance.Considering varying geological conditions, Wang et al.12 investigated the feasibility of producing synchronous grouting slurry from earth-pressure-balance shield muck in silty clay strata. Wu et al.13 effectively addressed challenges related to muck discharge and slurry blowout in water-rich sandy formations by regulating the flow plasticity of tunneling muck through the incorporation of modifiers. Ni et al.14 achieved efficient resource utilization of silty clay shield muck by completely replacing cement with geopolymer binders for solidification.

With respect to material performance optimization and microstructural mechanisms, Wu et al.15 investigated the feasibility of preparing grouting materials from silty clay shield muck through orthogonal experimental design. Zhang et al.5, focusing on the geological conditions of Nanjing Metro Line 7, optimized grouting material mix proportions using uniform design and multi-objective optimization methods. Wang et al.16 examined the mechanical properties and stabilization mechanisms of shield muck solidified with carbide slag and fly ash. Yang et al.17 further confirmed the enhancement of mechanical performance in stabilized muck materials through combined experimental testing and microstructural analysis. Wang et al.18 proposed a composite activation strategy to enhance the cementitious reactivity of shield muck. Additionally, Zhang et al.19 and Jian et al.20 improved the performance and economic efficiency of shield muck applied in backfill grouting through mix proportion optimization and multi-objective planning, respectively.In recent years, international studies have also conducted systematic investigations into the high-value utilization of shield muck. For instance, Wang et al.21 developed high-performance unfired bricks primarily composed of shield muck and crushed stone, achieving a 28-day compressive strength of 40.1 MPa, a water absorption of 5.4%, and a post-freeze strength loss of 11.5% under optimal mix proportions. Wei Wang et al.22 systematically studied eco-grouting materials derived from shield muck and reported that the optimized slurry exhibited superior performance in terms of apparent density, setting characteristics, impermeability, and water-to-land strength ratio. Field applications further demonstrated that these eco-grouting materials provided significantly improved ground settlement control compared with conventional grouting materials.

Although substantial progress has been achieved in the use of alkali-activated technology for converting industrial solid wastes into grouting materials, existing studies on shield muck reutilization have mainly focused on its stabilization treatment, its use as a low-value filling material, or its incorporation as a supplementary cementitious component. By comparison, studies that employ shield muck directly as a fine aggregate substitute for natural river sand in alkali-activated synchronous grouting materials remain relatively limited. Moreover, the coupled influence of shield muck replacement ratio on fresh properties, mechanical performance, durability, and microstructural evolution has not yet been sufficiently clarified within a unified experimental framework.In this context, the present study investigates the feasibility of using shield muck as a partial or full replacement for natural river sand in an alkali-activated solid-waste-based synchronous grouting material. Compared with previous studies, the novelty of this work lies mainly in three aspects. First, shield muck is introduced as a fine aggregate substitute rather than being treated merely as a material to be stabilized or as an auxiliary cementitious precursor. Second, a relatively systematic replacement series ranging from 0% to 100% is designed to clarify the transition in engineering performance with increasing muck content. Third, the effects of replacement ratio are evaluated from the combined perspectives of workability, compressive strength, durability, and SEM/XRD-based microstructural characteristics, so as to better reveal the relationship between material composition, microstructure, and macroscopic behavior. The findings are expected to provide useful experimental evidence for the resource utilization of shield muck in synchronous grouting applications.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

The materials used in this study consisted of the primary raw materials for the preparation of alkali-activated solid-waste–based grouting materials, as well as the reference materials employed to produce the conventional cement-based control mixture (denoted as the ST group).

(1)Raw Materials for Alkali-Activated Solid-Waste Grouting Material.

The cementitious materials mainly consisted of fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBS), steel slag, and silica fume. The fly ash was a Class F fly ash (Grade I) supplied by Aotai Mineral Products Processing Plant (Lingshou County, China), with a density of 2.40 g/cm³ and a mean particle size of 43 μm. Both GGBS and steel slag were provided by Wuhu Environmental Protection Technology Co., Ltd., among which the S95-grade GGBS had a density of 3.10 g/cm³ and a specific surface area of 430 cm²/kg. The measured activity index of the steel slag was 95.7, indicating that it possessed a certain level of potential cementitious reactivity. Silica fume, also supplied by Aotai Mineral Products Processing Plant, contained more than 90% SiO₂, with an average particle size of approximately 196 nm.The alkali activator was composed of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium silicate solution. NaOH was purchased from Suqian Yiyuefeng Trading Co., Ltd., with a purity of 96% (by mass) and in flake form. The sodium silicate solution was produced by Jiashan Yourui Refractory Materials Co., Ltd., with a silicate modulus of 3.3 and mass fractions of 26.98% SiO₂ and 8.53% Na₂O; the solid content was 35.5%, the Baumé degree was 38.5°Be, and the pH ranged from 11 to 13. A modified sodium silicate solution with a silicate modulus of 1.6 was prepared by dissolving NaOH into the original sodium silicate solution.Natural river sand supplied by Zhangjiakou Yiyuan Building Materials Co., Ltd. was used as the fine aggregate. After sieving, the particle size distribution was optimized to a fineness modulus of 1.65, and the grading curve is shown in Fig. 1. The shield muck (denoted as SS) used in this study was collected from a construction section of Beijing Metro Line 22. The measured natural water content of the as-received muck was 48.3%, indicating a pronounced water-retaining characteristic. Prior to use, the collected muck was air-dried under laboratory conditions, manually crushed to break down agglomerates, and sieved to remove oversized particles and visible impurities. Only the fraction meeting the grading requirement for fine aggregate replacement was used in the experiments.The mineralogical composition of the shield muck is presented in Fig. 2, with quartz (20 wt%), plagioclase (47 wt%), and expansive clay minerals accounting for approximately 14.0 wt% of the total content. These clay minerals provide the fundamental basis for the strong water absorption capacity of the shield muck and can significantly influence the fresh and hardened properties of the grout.A polycarboxylate-based high-range water-reducing admixture was supplied by Shanxi Feike New Materials Technology Co., Ltd., providing a water-reduction efficiency of 25%.

Experimental methods

Material mixing and proportioning

All raw materials were accurately weighed according to the designed mix proportions. The primary focus of this study was the alkali-activated solid-waste-based synchronous grouting material. To provide an engineering reference for comparison, a conventional cement-based synchronous grouting mixture was prepared and denoted as the ST group. The ST mixture was selected as a representative cement-fly ash-bentonite-sand synchronous grouting formulation based on the composition framework commonly reported for conventional shield-tunneling synchronous grouts in engineering practice and the literature23,24. In the present study, the ST group was mainly used as an engineering benchmark for representative hardened-property and durability-related evaluations, whereas the main systematic investigation of fresh-property evolution under different shield muck replacement ratios was carried out in the alkali-activated series. The purpose of introducing the ST group was to provide a practical reference to conventional cement-based synchronous grouting materials, rather than to serve as a full one-to-one control for every test item included in the alkali-activated system. The detailed mix proportions of the ST group are presented in Table 2.The alkali-activated solid-waste experimental groups (M0–M5) employed fly ash, GGBS, steel slag, and silica fume as the cementitious components. The baseline mix design was defined by a liquid-to-solid ratio (L/S) of 0.8, an activator-to-binder ratio of 0.48, and an aggregate-to-binder ratio of 1.5. Here, the liquid-to-solid ratio refers to the mass ratio of the total liquid phase (alkali activator solution plus additional water) to the total cementitious materials, while the activator-to-binder ratio refers to the mass ratio of sodium silicate solution and NaOH to the total cementitious materials. The commercial sodium silicate solution had an initial silicate modulus of 3.3, which was adjusted to 1.6 by adding NaOH. The dosages of sodium silicate solution and NaOH adopted in each mixture are listed in Table 3. The proportions of fly ash, GGBS, steel slag, and silica fume in the binder system were kept constant throughout the experimental program to ensure that the replacement ratio of shield muck was the primary variable under investigation. By fixing the baseline binder formulation, the interference caused by binder-composition variation was minimized, so that the observed changes in fresh properties, compressive strength, durability, and microstructural evolution could be more directly associated with the replacement of natural river sand by shield muck. The selected binder composition was used as a unified reference matrix for the present study, rather than as an optimized formulation for all engineering conditions.Within the binder system, the fly ash content was fixed at 60%, while GGBS and steel slag were incorporated at a mass ratio of 2:1, accounting for a combined proportion of 36%; silica fume was added at a dosage of 4%.To investigate the resource utilization efficiency of shield muck, the replacement ratio of natural river sand with tunneling muck was systematically varied based on the optimized binder system. Six replacement levels (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%) were designed, corresponding to six experimental groups (M0–M5). The detailed mix proportions of these mixtures are summarized in Table 3.

After weighing, all materials were placed beside the mixer for use. Prior to formal mixing, the mixer was operated under no-load conditions to check the rotation direction of the mixing drum and the operational status of all components. The mixing procedure was initiated only after confirming normal operation.For the alkali-activated solid-waste mixtures (M0–M5), the mixing process was conducted as follows. First, the dry mixture of fly ash, GGBS, steel slag, and silica fume was introduced into the mixing bowl and mixed at low speed for 30 s to achieve preliminary homogenization. Subsequently, the particle size–optimized natural river sand and shield muck were added, followed by low-speed mixing for an additional 60 s. After a total mixing time of 90 s, the alkali activator was introduced and mixed at low speed for 60 s to ensure sufficient contact with the solid components. At 150 s, the pre-mixed water and superplasticizer were added; the mixture was then mixed at low speed for 60 s and subsequently at high speed for 30 s. The total mixing duration was 240 s, ensuring uniform dispersion of all constituents and the formation of a homogeneous slurry.For the ST group, mixing was performed following standard procedures commonly adopted for cement-based grouting materials. Upon completion of mixing, the mixing blades and the inner wall of the mixing bowl were immediately and thoroughly cleaned to prevent residual materials from affecting subsequent experiments.

Specimen casting and curing

Immediately after mixing, specimen casting was carried out. The fresh slurry was poured into molds, and a combination of layered vibration and rodding was employed to ensure adequate compaction. Specifically, rodding was first performed sequentially from the mold edges toward the center, followed by vibration to eliminate entrapped air and prevent voids or segregation. After compaction, excess slurry on the surface was removed, and the specimens were carefully leveled with a trowel to achieve a smooth surface flush with the top of the mold.The molded specimens, together with the molds, were then transferred to a standard curing chamber (temperature of 20 ± 2 °C and relative humidity > 95%) and kept undisturbed for 24 h prior to demolding. After demolding, the specimens were continuously cured under the same conditions until the designated testing ages (3 and 28 days) to systematically evaluate strength development. For the flowability, compressive strength, and anti-washout-related strength data, the corresponding error bars in the figures represent the standard deviation (SD) of the parallel measurements. When abnormal dispersion was observed, the data were checked against the specimen condition and test record to ensure result consistency.

Durability performance: The salt resistance test was conducted with reference to Test Method for Sulfate Resistance of Cement (GB/T 749–2008)28 and Standard for Test Methods of Long-term Performance and Durability of Ordinary Concrete (GB/T 50082–2009)29. After standard curing, the specimens were immersed in 5 wt% NaCl solution or 5 wt% MgSO₄ solution under laboratory conditions. During the immersion period, the solution was kept sufficient to fully cover the specimens, and the specimens were taken out at the designated ages for strength testing. The corrosion resistance coefficient, defined as the ratio of compressive strength after exposure to that of the reference specimens, was measured to evaluate the salt resistance. The specimens were subjected to 10 and 20 freeze-thaw cycles between − 20 ℃ and 20 ℃. After the designated number of cycles, the compressive strength was measured and the strength loss ratio was calculated to assess freeze-thaw durability. For each freeze-thaw condition, at least three parallel specimens were tested, and the reported values represent the arithmetic mean of the parallel results.

The micro-morphology and structural features of the samples were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Merlin Compact). The phase composition and its evolution were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab SE), with a scanning range of 5°–70° (2θ) and a scanning rate of 10°/min.

Results and discussion

Workability

Flowability

To systematically evaluate the feasibility of shield muck reutilization, six replacement levels of shield muck (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%) were designed, corresponding to mixtures M0–M5. The flowability results of the grouting slurries are summarized in Table 4.As shown in Fig. 4, the flowability of the grouting materials exhibits a clear monotonic decreasing trend with increasing shield muck replacement ratio. Compared with the reference mixture M0, the flowability decreased by 11.95%, 18.18%, 39.22%, 50.65%, and 62.60% for M1, M2, M3, M4, and M5, respectively. Under constant conditions of binder composition, liquid-to-solid ratio, and admixture dosage, the increase in shield muck content is therefore identified as the dominant factor responsible for the deterioration of slurry flowability.

When no shield muck was incorporated (M0), the grout exhibited the highest flowability of 385 mm, indicating that the binder-rich system contained sufficient free water to ensure effective lubrication and free spreading. Under this condition, the slurry was relatively homogeneous, and interparticle friction was negligible, which contributed to its excellent spreading ability.

With the introduction of shield muck, the flowability decreased continuously, dropping to 339 mm at 20% replacement and 315 mm at 40% replacement. Within this low-to-moderate replacement range, although part of the free water was adsorbed by muck particles and internal particle interaction increased, the slurry still maintained acceptable workability for grouting applications. This suggests that, at replacement ratios up to approximately 40%, the binder phase still provides sufficient gel products to compensate for the additional water demand and to maintain effective particle coating and lubrication.Once the replacement ratio reached 60% or above, the deterioration in flowability became much more pronounced. The flowability decreased sharply to 234 mm at 60% replacement, representing a 39.22% reduction relative to M0, and further declined to 190 mm and 144 mm at 80% and 100% replacement, corresponding to total reductions of 50.65% and 62.60%, respectively. This result indicates that the slurry had entered a regime in which the binder phase could no longer effectively compensate for the increased water demand and particle interaction introduced by the shield muck, in contrast to the behavior observed at lower replacement ratios. A large number of muck particles accumulated within the slurry, leading to stronger interparticle contact and a less favorable packing state, which markedly restricted free spreading under self-weight. Under these conditions, the system gradually shifted from a binder-dominated slurry to a particle-dominated mixture, in which reduced free-water availability, intensified particle interaction, and ineffective particle packing jointly resulted in a substantial loss of flowability.

Overall, the results demonstrate a clear negative correlation between shield muck replacement ratio and grout flowability. Within the investigated range, controlling the replacement ratio at no more than 40% is beneficial for maintaining acceptable workability while improving resource utilization efficiency.

Setting time

To systematically investigate the feasibility of shield muck for resource utilization, six replacement levels (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%) were designed to elucidate its influence on the setting time of synchronous grouting materials. The measured setting times of the slurries under different mix proportions are summarized in Table 5, while the corresponding variation trends and fitted curves are illustrated in Fig. 5.An increase in the replacement ratio of shield muck markedly shortened the setting time of the grouting materials. To further describe this effect, the reported mean values were fitted using a second-order polynomial model. The fitted relationship can be expressed as T = 116.75–1.598R + 0.00746R², where T is the setting time (min) and R is the shield muck replacement ratio (%). The fitted curve is presented in Fig. 5, and the model showed a high goodness of fit (R² = 0.998). Specifically, the setting time decreased from 118 min at a replacement ratio of 0% to 30 min at 100%, corresponding to a total reduction of 74.58%.

The pronounced shortening of setting time indicates that the incorporation of shield muck influenced the early-stage reaction process and structural build-up of the slurry system. This effect may be associated with two factors. First, the introduction of shield muck may alter the local reaction environment of the slurry through soluble ions and clay-containing fine particles, thereby affecting its early stiffening behavior. Second, compared with natural river sand, shield muck particles exhibit different surface characteristics and water-absorption behavior, which may further influence the distribution of free water, particle packing, and the formation of an early solid skeleton. Based on the observed variation in macroscopic setting behavior, it is reasonable to infer that the incorporation of shield muck promoted the early structural formation of the slurry system. This analysis provides a plausible explanation for its role in the setting process.

Bleeding Rate

Bleeding rate is a critical indicator for evaluating the stability and water-retention capacity of grouting materials. As shown in Fig. 6, the bleeding rate of the grouting slurry remained zero across all replacement ratios of shield muck (0%–100%), indicating excellent system stability without water exudation or particle segregation during static conditions. This outstanding performance can be primarily attributed to the dual role of shield muck. First, shield muck particles generally exhibit a high specific surface area and a porous microstructure, which endow them with a strong capacity to adsorb and retain free water, effectively preventing upward water migration and bleeding. Second, the abundant network-like C–A–S–H gels generated through alkali-activation reactions firmly immobilize water within a three-dimensional gel framework, further enhancing the water-retention capability of the system. The synergistic effect of physical water adsorption by shield muck and chemical encapsulation by cementitious gel products ensures that the slurry maintains homogeneity and stability throughout the entire resting period.

Bleeding Rate of Grouting Material at Different shield muck Replacement Rates (All Zero).

The zero-bleeding characteristic is of particular significance for synchronous grouting applications. It implies that the slurry is unlikely to cause pipeline blockage during pumping and filling due to water separation, while also preventing the formation of preferential water channels within the grouting layer caused by moisture loss. Consequently, intimate contact between the grouting material, tunnel lining segments, and surrounding ground can be maintained, thereby improving waterproofing performance and load-bearing uniformity. Although the flowability decreases at higher replacement ratios, the overall stability of the grouting system remains consistently preserved.

The bleeding rate of all mixtures remained 0 within the investigated test range. This result may be associated with the relatively high powder content of the binder system, the presence of fine particles in shield muck, and the water-retaining effect induced by clay-containing components, all of which may contribute to suppressing free-water separation in the slurry. However, it should be noted that the present study only reports the observed bleeding-test results under the adopted mix design and test conditions. Therefore, this zero-bleeding behavior should be interpreted cautiously rather than generalized beyond the present experimental system.

Anti-washout resistance

When the replacement ratio of shield muck exceeded 60%, the flowability of the grouting slurry decreased markedly, and stable underwater specimen preparation could not be achieved. During the casting process, the slurry exhibited severe consistency loss, poor continuity during discharge, and a pronounced tendency toward particle accumulation and blockage, which prevented effective mold filling under submerged conditions. As a result, the underwater anti-washout test could not be completed for these mixtures, and the corresponding water-to-air strength ratios were not obtained. This behavior indicates that, at excessively high replacement ratios, the combined effects of reduced free-water availability, intensified particle interaction, and particle agglomeration significantly impair the castability and underwater placement adaptability of the grout.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the anti-washout performance of the grouting materials generally exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing replacement ratio of shield muck, with a particularly pronounced decline observed when the replacement ratio exceeded 40%. This behavior is mainly associated with the mineralogical composition and particle morphology of shield muck. At relatively low replacement ratios, the amount of shield muck introduced into the system is limited, and the generated cementitious gels are still sufficient to coat the particles and maintain slurry integrity. Under such conditions, the adverse effects of expansive clay minerals on water absorption and structural stability remain relatively moderate. However, as the replacement ratio increases, the cumulative effects of clay-related water adsorption, particle agglomeration, and irregular particle packing become progressively more pronounced. Once the replacement ratio exceeds a certain level, the available cementitious phases are no longer sufficient to effectively bind and encapsulate the excessive muck particles, resulting in reduced slurry homogeneity, weaker structural stability, and increased susceptibility to water-induced dispersion. Therefore, the deterioration observed at high replacement ratios can be understood as the result of a transition from a binder-dominated system to a particle-dominated system.

Air-cured strength, underwater-cast strength, and water-to-air strength ratio of grouting materials at different shield muck replacement ratios (strength data shown as mean ± SD, n = 3).

Although all grouting slurries incorporating shield muck exhibited lower anti-washout resistance than the reference mixture without muck, the water-to-air strength ratio remained above the minimum requirement of 65% specified by relevant standards when the replacement ratio did not exceed 40%. This indicates that the partial substitution of fine aggregate with shield muck is feasible in practical engineering applications. By controlling the replacement ratio within 40%, the grouting material can maintain acceptable workability and underwater stability within the investigated test range, while simultaneously enabling the partial utilization of shield muck. Engineering properties such as impermeability and bond behavior with surrounding media or tunnel segments were not evaluated in the present study and require further investigation.

Mechanical properties

To systematically evaluate the influence of shield muck replacement ratio on the mechanical performance of the grouting material, this study focused on the aggregate component after determining the optimal binder system. Six replacement levels ranging from 0% to 100% were designed, and the compressive strengths at curing ages of 3 and 28 days were measured. The test results are summarized in Table 6.The variation of compressive strength with shield muck replacement ratio is clearly illustrated in Fig. 8. Overall, the incorporation of shield muck exhibits a pronounced dosage-dependent effect on strength development. At an early curing age of 3 days, the compressive strength increased slightly from 6.86 MPa for M0 to 6.98 MPa for M2, corresponding to an increase of 1.75%, and then decreased markedly to 2.80 MPa for M5 at higher replacement ratios, representing a reduction of 59.18% relative to M0. In contrast, the 28-day compressive strength showed an overall decreasing trend with increasing replacement ratio, declining from 13.68 MPa for M0 to 11.54 MPa for M2 and 7.33 MPa for M5, corresponding to reductions of 15.64% and 46.42%, respectively. These results indicate that, within an appropriate replacement range, shield muck can contribute to the early-stage structural optimization of the grout system. A possible explanation is that a moderate amount of fine muck particles improves particle packing and filling efficiency, while the alkali-activated binder still provides sufficient gel phases to encapsulate the particles and maintain structural continuity. However, as the replacement ratio increases further, the available cementitious phases become insufficient to effectively bind the increasing amount of muck particles, and the negative effects of additional water demand, particle agglomeration, and weak interfacial regions gradually become dominant, leading to a pronounced reduction in strength.

Microstructural mechanism

SEM analysis

The microstructural morphology of the specimens at curing ages of 3 and 28 days was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Figs. 9 and 10. In general, curing age and shield muck replacement ratio jointly affected the microstructural evolution of the grout system. Compared with the 3-day specimens, the 28-day specimens generally exhibited a denser matrix, more continuous gel coverage, and improved structural integrity, indicating the progressive development of hydration and alkali-activation products over time. However, the beneficial effect of prolonged curing depended strongly on the shield muck replacement ratio.

SEM images of grouting material at 3-day age for different shield muck replacement rates.

SEM images of grouting material at 28-day age for different shield muck replacement rates.

For mixtures with no or low-to-moderate shield muck content (M0–M2), relatively compact microstructures were already formed at 3 days. In particular, M0 showed a dense gel-rich matrix, while M1 and M2 exhibited effective encapsulation of muck particles by the surrounding reaction products. In mixture M2, a relatively distinct core-shell-type particle–matrix structure could still be observed at 3 days, which is consistent with its relatively favorable early-age strength. After 28 days of curing, these mixtures became further densified, with improved continuity of the reaction products and fewer visible pores, suggesting that prolonged curing effectively strengthened the matrix structure in the low-replacement regime.In contrast, mixtures with high shield muck replacement ratios (M3–M5) exhibited much looser and more heterogeneous microstructures at 3 days, characterized by insufficient gel coverage, weak particle–matrix bonding, and more visible pores. Although some local densification could still be observed after 28 days, the overall structural defects remained evident. In particular, for M4 and M5, the matrix was still dominated by loosely packed muck particles, interparticle voids, and discontinuous bonding regions, indicating that the microstructural deficiencies induced by excessive shield muck content could not be fundamentally compensated for by extended curing alone.

Overall, the SEM observations indicate that curing age mainly enhances the compactness of systems with low or moderate shield muck replacement, whereas excessive replacement leads to early-formed structural defects that remain difficult to eliminate at later ages. This difference in age-dependent microstructural evolution provides a clearer explanation for the corresponding changes in macroscopic mechanical properties and durability.

XRD analysis

To further examine the influence of shield muck replacement ratio on the phase characteristics of the alkali-activated system, XRD analyses were conducted on representative specimens with low (20%, M1), moderate (40%, M2), and high (100%, M5) replacement ratios. The diffraction patterns obtained at curing ages of 3 days and 28 days are shown in Fig. 11.

Effect of shield muck replacement ratio on phase composition: XRD patterns at (a) 3-day and (b) 28-day Ages.

At 3 days, the diffraction patterns of M1 and M2 showed a certain degree of diffuse background feature in the 2θ range of approximately 20°–35°, which is commonly associated with the presence of amorphous or poorly crystalline reaction products in alkali-activated systems. Compared with the high-replacement mixture M5, this feature was relatively more discernible in M1 and M2, suggesting that the contribution of reaction products in these mixtures may be comparatively greater within the investigated range. By contrast, the diffraction pattern of M5 was more strongly dominated by crystalline mineral peaks such as quartz and feldspar originating from the shield muck, indicating a relatively lower contribution of amorphous reaction products.

At 28 days, M1 and M2 still exhibited similar diffuse-background characteristics in the same diffraction range, indicating that reaction-product development continued with curing age. This observation is qualitatively consistent with the progressively denser microstructural features observed in the SEM images and with the comparatively better macroscopic performance of these mixtures. In contrast, M5 did not show an obvious enhancement of this diffuse feature at 28 days, and its diffraction pattern remained mainly characterized by crystalline phases from the shield muck, suggesting that the microstructural improvement achieved during curing under this condition was relatively limited.

Overall, the XRD results suggest that the shield muck replacement ratio had a significant influence on the phase characteristics of the alkali-activated grout system. Within the investigated range, mixtures with low to moderate replacement ratios exhibited relatively stronger amorphous-feature characteristics, whereas the high-replacement mixture showed a more crystal-dominated pattern. These phase-characteristic differences are qualitatively consistent with the corresponding variations in mechanical properties and microstructural compactness.

Microstructural mechanism analysis

A schematic interpretation of the influence of shield muck content on the microstructure and performance of the grouting material is presented in Fig. 12. As shown, an evident transition of the system can be observed with increasing shield muck replacement ratio, evolving from a stage of “optimization and enhancement” to one of “deterioration and degradation.”

Schematic diagram of the mechanism of shield muck content on microstructure of grouting material.

In the low-replacement regime (≤ 40%), an appropriate amount of shield muck particles can be uniformly dispersed within the cementitious matrix as micro-aggregates under alkali-activated conditions. Industrial solid wastes, including fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, and steel slag, react under alkaline conditions and generate binding gel phases that contribute to particle encapsulation and matrix densification. In the present study, these products are interpreted mainly as calcium-aluminosilicate-hydrate-type gels based on the combined SEM and XRD results.The gel phases effectively encapsulate the shield muck particles, giving rise to a dense three-dimensional network characterized by a typical “core–shell” structure. This microstructural configuration enables efficient micro-aggregate filling and strong interfacial bonding, resulting in a low-porosity matrix at the microscopic scale. Correspondingly, such a refined microstructure translates into favorable macroscopic performance, including appropriate flowability, zero bleeding, and superior mechanical properties and durability.

Once the replacement ratio exceeds the critical threshold of 40%, the system enters the high-replacement regime (> 40%). In this stage, the excessive incorporation of shield muck causes the physical packing effect to become dominant, leading to the formation of a loose spatial skeleton structure. Under such conditions, the relative content of cementitious components per unit volume becomes insufficient to effectively encapsulate all muck particles, resulting in extensive particle exposure and agglomeration.

Meanwhile, the excessive shield muck may competitively adsorb alkaline activators and water molecules, thereby mildly suppressing the alkali-activation and hydration reactions and causing a sharp reduction in the generation of gel products. Consequently, the microstructure evolves into a loose and poorly bonded configuration characterized by an insufficient cementitious phase, abundant weak interfacial zones, and a high volume of pores.The degradation of this microstructure directly translates into pronounced deterioration of macroscopic performance, manifested by a drastic loss of flowability, a substantial reduction in compressive strength, and a weakened resistance to water-induced dispersion.

From the perspective of system balance, the effect of shield muck depends on the competition between the binding capacity of the alkali-activated matrix and the water-demand/packing effect introduced by the muck particles. At low replacement ratios, the binder system still provides sufficient gel phases to compensate for the additional water demand and to maintain effective particle encapsulation. As a result, the negative influence of expansive clay minerals does not dominate the overall performance. In contrast, at high replacement ratios, the increase in water adsorption, particle interaction, and ineffective packing progressively exceeds the compensating capacity of the binder system, leading to marked deterioration in workability, anti-washout resistance, and strength development.

Overall, the proposed schematic provides an interpretation of the possible microstructural origin of the “benefit–drawback” transition of shield muck in grouting materials based on the combined macroscopic test results and the qualitative SEM/XRD observations. It provides solid theoretical support for limiting the replacement ratio of shield muck to within 40% in practical applications, thereby achieving a balance between large-scale solid-waste utilization and engineering performance requirements.

Durability performance

Salt attack resistance analysis

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of the fresh properties and mechanical performance discussed above, four representative mixtures were selected for the durability-related tests, including the reference group (ST) and mixtures with shield muck replacement ratios of 0% (M0), 20% (M1), and 40% (M2). It should be noted that the present durability evaluation was carried out on selected representative mixtures rather than on the full replacement series, and therefore the corresponding discussion is limited to the investigated compositions and test conditions.Figure 13 illustrates the compressive strength evolution of the specimens after immersion in 5% NaCl solution and 5% MgSO₄ solution for 28 and 56 days, in comparison with the strength development under standard curing conditions.

Compressive Strength under Different Erosion Conditions.

The results show that the specimens cured under standard conditions exhibited a steady increase in compressive strength with curing age, which can be attributed to the continuous hydration process. In contrast, under corrosive environments, except for the M2 group, the compressive strength of the other mixtures still increased slightly at 28 days, indicating that the enhanced hydration of the cementitious system could, to some extent, offset the detrimental effects of corrosive ions at the early stage. However, as the immersion period was prolonged to 56 days, a noticeable reduction in compressive strength was observed for all specimens in both corrosive media, demonstrating that the cumulative effects of salt erosion gradually became dominant.

The resistance of the materials to salt erosion was evaluated using the corrosion resistance coefficient, defined as the ratio of compressive strength under corrosive conditions to that under standard curing. The corrosion resistance coefficients of all mixtures at curing ages of 28 and 56 days are summarized in Table 7, and their variation trends are illustrated in Fig. 14.In the NaCl solution, the corrosion resistance coefficient generally decreased with increasing shield muck replacement ratio, and the rate of deterioration became more pronounced with prolonged exposure time. At 28 days, although the reactive components in shield muck could participate in alkali activation and contribute to the formation of C–A–S–H gel, the presence of expansive clay minerals, such as montmorillonite, led to water absorption and swelling, inducing internal stresses and reducing the compactness of the specimens, thereby weakening their resistance to chloride attack. Nevertheless, at this stage, all shield muck–containing mixtures (M0, M1, and M2) still exhibited higher corrosion resistance coefficients than the reference ST mixture.

Freeze–thaw resistance analysis

To evaluate freeze-thaw durability and to compare the alkali-activated system with a conventional cement-based engineering reference, freeze-thaw cycling tests with 10 and 20 cycles were conducted on four groups of specimens, namely ST, M0, M1, and M2.The compressive strength loss ratios are summarized in Table 8, while the variations in compressive strength and strength loss ratio are illustrated in Fig. 15.The results show that all specimens incorporating shield muck (M0, M1, and M2) exhibited higher compressive strengths after freeze–thaw cycling than the reference group (ST), indicating that the incorporation of shield muck can enhance the resistance of the matrix to freeze–thaw damage to a certain extent. With an increasing number of freeze–thaw cycles, the compressive strength of all groups gradually decreased, following a trend characterized by a rapid initial decline followed by a slower reduction.

This behavior can be attributed to the freeze–thaw damage mechanism. During the early cycles, larger capillary pores within the specimens become saturated with water, and the expansion stress induced by ice formation promotes rapid initiation and propagation of microcracks, leading to a pronounced loss in strength. As the number of cycles increases, these weak pores are progressively damaged, and subsequent freeze–thaw action mainly affects denser gel pores, resulting in a relatively reduced damage rate.In terms of compressive strength loss ratio, the M1 group (20% replacement ratio) exhibited loss ratios of 5.96% and 8.76% after 10 and 20 freeze–thaw cycles, respectively, which are comparable to those of the ST group (5.01% and 9.67%). This indicates that M1 possesses good freeze–thaw stability. However, with a further increase in shield muck replacement ratio, the M2 group (40% replacement ratio) showed a pronounced increase in strength loss, reaching 20.16% after 20 freeze–thaw cycles, demonstrating that an excessively high replacement ratio significantly weakens the freeze–thaw resistance of the material.

Overall, the results suggest that an appropriate incorporation of shield muck (e.g., the M1 group) enables the grouting material to maintain relatively favorable freeze–thaw resistance within the investigated test range. After 20 freeze–thaw cycles, the compressive strength loss ratio of M1 was 8.76%, which was close to that of the ST group (9.67%) and markedly lower than that of M2 (20.16%). These results indicate that a replacement ratio of approximately 20% provides a reasonable balance between solid-waste utilization and freeze–thaw durability. However, the present study did not include direct microscopic characterization of freeze–thaw damage evolution, such as crack propagation or pore-structure change, and therefore the mechanistic interpretation remains primarily based on macroscopic strength loss.

From a broader literature perspective, the present findings can also be compared with previous studies on alkali-activated cementitious systems and grout penetration behavior. For example, previous research on oil-well cementing materials has shown that changes in formulation can significantly influence compressive strength, setting behavior, and sealing performance of alkali-activated or modified cementitious systems30,31. In addition, grout diffusion studies have demonstrated that penetration behavior is strongly affected by the structural characteristics of the porous medium, such as dry density and flow-path complexity32. Although the application scenarios differ from those in the present study, these findings support the view that both intrinsic material design and external transport conditions jointly govern the engineering performance of grouting materials. In comparison with these studies, the present work specifically focuses on the direct use of shield muck as a fine aggregate substitute in an alkali-activated synchronous grouting system and demonstrates that the replacement ratio governs the transition from acceptable engineering performance to pronounced deterioration under the investigated mix design.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the feasibility of utilizing shield muck as a partial replacement for natural river sand in alkali-activated solid-waste-based synchronous grouting materials. Based on comprehensive evaluations of fresh properties, mechanical performance, durability, and microstructural characteristics, the following conclusions can be drawn:

(1)The replacement ratio of shield muck plays a decisive role in governing the overall performance of the grouting material, and approximately 40% can be regarded as a critical transition level under the investigated mix design.When the replacement ratio does not exceed 40%, the grouting material simultaneously satisfies workability requirements (flowability ≥ 315 mm and zero bleeding) and mechanical performance demands, achieving compressive strengths of 6.98 MPa and 11.54 MPa at 3 and 28 days, respectively. However, when the replacement ratio exceeds 60%, all performance indicators deteriorate markedly.

(2) An appropriate incorporation of shield muck contributes to relatively favorable durability performance within the investigated test range.Among the tested mixtures, specimens with a 20% replacement ratio (M1) showed comparatively good durability-related performance, maintaining corrosion resistance coefficients of 0.916 and 0.905 after 56 days of immersion in 5% NaCl and 5% MgSO₄ solutions, respectively. Moreover, after 20 freeze–thaw cycles, the compressive strength loss was 8.76%, indicating a reasonable balance between shield muck utilization and durability under the present test conditions.

(3) The evolution of microstructure is the fundamental mechanism underlying the observed macroscopic performance variations.SEM and XRD analyses revealed that at replacement ratios of 40% or lower, sufficient C–A–S–H gel is generated to effectively encapsulate shield muck particles, forming a dense core–shell microstructure. In contrast, when the replacement ratio exceeds this threshold, an insufficient amount of binding gel leads to weakened interfacial bonding, increased porosity, and a loose microstructure, thereby causing pronounced degradation in mechanical and durability performance.

(4) The utilization of shield muck in synchronous grouting materials offers significant economic and environmental benefits.Replacing natural river sand with shield muck not only reduces raw material procurement costs but also substantially lowers expenses associated with muck transportation and disposal. This approach provides an attractive solution for promoting the recycling of construction waste and advancing green construction practices.

Overall, this study demonstrates that incorporating shield muck as a functional component into alkali-activated solid-waste systems represents an effective strategy for its high-value utilization. This pathway not only offers a practical solution for managing underground construction waste but also opens new avenues for the development of low-cost, low-environmental-impact, and high-performance grouting materials, thereby contributing to the sustainable development of tunnel engineering.
