Synergetic mechanism by decompression and bone bioactive factors to induce the spontaneous resolution of lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Yan Jiang, Su Fu, Xu Yan, Chunlin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous resolution represents an ideal therapeutic outcome for intervertebral disc herniation, a prevalent clinical disorder. Drawing on our prior cervical spine research, we have demonstrated that herniated disc tissue is absorbable, a process we term the induced resorption of herniated nucleus pulposus (iRHNP). We hypothesized that a synergistic microenvironment—combining mechanical force withdrawal (decompression) and the biological activity of bone healing (release of bioactive factors)—artificially drives iRHNP. This study employed integrated in vitro and in vivo models, including rat explant co-cultures and a rabbit laminoplasty model, to confirm disc volume reduction and associated matrix degradation. Based on this mechanistic insight, we developed a novel Resolution-Induction technique for Lumbar Disc Herniation (RI-LDH). A subsequent prospective clinical study verified the iRHNP phenomenon and its underlying mechanism. Our findings elucidate, for the first time, a synergistic “decompression + bone bioactive factors” mechanism. Patients treated with RI-LDH exhibited excellent clinical outcomes, correlating with symmetric spinal canal decompression, bony reconstruction, and significant iRHNP. This work establishes the foundation for a novel resolution-oriented therapeutic strategy for disc herniation.

Introduction

Symptomatic intervertebral disc herniation is a prevalent and clinically significant disorder1–3. Pathologically, fragments of degenerative disc tissue, primarily nucleus pulposus, herniate into the spinal canal, compressing neural structures within the laminar space and resulting in sensory and motor deficits4.

The historical management of disc herniation has often involved an expectation of spontaneous resorption, an ideal “self-healing” outcome. The pathological understanding of herniated tissue has evolved from early misclassifications such as ‘enchondromas’ to the contemporary view of it as displaced, yet potentially active, biological material5,6. While surgical interventions, ranging from traditional to minimally invasive discectomy, aim to remove or ablate the herniated fragment and restore stability7, they carry inherent risks including recurrence, neural injury, infection, and adjacent segment disease8,9. Despite these complications, discectomy has remained a primary option, reflecting a previously limited therapeutic paradigm focused on fragment removal.

The advent of cross-sectional imaging revealed that spontaneous resorption of herniated nucleus pulposus (RHNP) can occur, as first documented in lumbar disc herniation (LDH) in 1984 and later in cervical disc herniation (CDH)10–13. This phenomenon, though infrequent, provided critical evidence that herniated disc tissue is not inert but retains metabolic activity capable of regression. Building on this, our team developed a cervical micro-endoscopic laminoplasty (CMEL) technique14. We subsequently observed a high rate of CDH resolution (81.2%) following CMEL15,16, and further demonstrated in a cohort of 116 patients with cervical myelopathy that 89.1% achieved disc resorption17.

This surgically facilitated process, distinct from spontaneous RHNP, was termed induced resorption of herniated nucleus pulposus (iRHNP)17. We hypothesized that iRHNP is driven by a dual microenvironment: mechanical decompression (force withdrawal) and the local biological milieu of bone healing (release of bioactive factors). Although lumbar disc pathology presents distinct biomechanical challenges, we postulated that the principle of inducing resorption could be successfully translated. The specific synergistic mechanism of “decompression and bone bioactive factors” in iRHNP, however, remained unverified.

Therefore, this study aimed to experimentally test and validate this synergistic mechanism through integrated in vitro and in vivo investigations. Based on this mechanistic foundation, we developed a novel Resolution-Induction technique for Lumbar Disc Herniation (RI-LDH) and conducted a prospective clinical trial to evaluate its outcomes and the associated iRHNP phenomenon. Our findings provide new insights into LDH treatment and establish a foundation for a novel, resolution-oriented therapeutic strategy.

The structure of this study is as follows: First, using in vitro rat callus-disc co-culture and in vivo rabbit laminoplasty models, we test the hypothesis that ‘decompression + bone bioactive factors’ synergistically induces disc tissue resorption. Second, based on this mechanism, we develop a Resolution-Induction technique for LDH (RI-LDH) and conduct a prospective clinical study to validate its efficacy and the occurrence of iRHNP. Finally, we analyze clinical factors influencing iRHNP magnitude and its correlation with systemic bone metabolism markers to explore the underlying mechanism.

Methods and materials

In vitro experiments from treated rats

Three healthy and clean 200 g SD rats (male and female not limited, purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhengzhou University, ethical approval No. 2020-KY-0357-001) were anaesthetized with phenobarbital inhalation. One side of the femur was fixed by hand, the middle part of the femur was placed on a blunt object, and the knee joint was pressed. The three-point fracture principle of the lever was used to break the femur in a closed state. Then, both ends of the femur were fixed, and the lower limbs were fixed in plaster after surgery.

The rats were placed in an animal room for normal feeding, adequate food, water, and normal light. X-rays were performed to confirm fracture formation at the laboratory animal centre on the day after fracture modelling and 7 days after cast fixation, as well as blurred fracture lines and callus formation at 7 days. After 7 days of plaster fixation, the rats were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the new callus was removed at the fracture site. The callus was placed into a 6-well plate containing 2 mL culture medium, transferred rapidly at room temperature, cultured and preserved at 37 °C.

Three 200 g SD fracture model rats were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the whole disc tissue was extracted and transported in a culture medium at room temperature, cultured and preserved at 37 °C. In the experimental group, callus tissue was placed at the bottom of the six-well plate, and Transwell inserts containing disc tissue were placed in the six-well plate and separated by a biofilm that only allowed the passage of small molecules. In the control group, callus-free tissue was weighed every 2 days and kept in culture for 7 days. The culture media was collected every two days, and the NO concentration and sGAG release were assessed by Griess assay and DMMB assay.

In vivo experiment

Twelve New Zealand White rabbits (mean weight 2.5 kg, purchased from the Henan Provincial Laboratory Animal Center), approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, ethical approval No. 2020-KY-0357-001), were randomly assigned to experimental or control groups (n = 6 per group). This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Following anaesthesia induction with intravenous propofol (200 mg/Kg) and ethyl carbamate (20%, 4 mL/Kg), a posterior cervical approach exposed the spinous processes and laminae. A longitudinal incision (~ 3–4 cm) was made using a #11 blade to separate musculature, minimizing damage to surrounding tissues. Bilateral laminotomies were performed using an ultrasonic bone knife and stabilized with Kirschner wires; control groups comprised unslotted laminae and total laminectomy. Biofilm-wrapped coccygeal discs were implanted posterior to the cervical lamina.

For disc placement: “Inside” refers to implantation into the spinal canal medial to the cervical lamina (within the boundaries of the spinal canal), while “outside” refers to implantation into the paravertebral soft tissue lateral to the lamina (external to the spinal canal and lamina). A detailed anatomical diagram illustrating these positions is provided in Fig. 1e. Coccygeal discs were selected due to their structural similarity to cervical discs (e.g., nucleus pulposus-to-annulus fibrosus ratio) and ease of harvesting with minimal animal trauma. Application of the RI-LDH technique in patients

The RI-LDH protocol is illustrated in Fig. 2A. The purpose of this operation was to create a symmetrically enlarged spinal canal to decompress the nerves by bilaterally cutting the lamina and fixation, which finally led to groove bone healing. Thus, the microenvironment of physical force is disrupted, while bone bioactive factors exist. Patients were administered general anaesthesia, and the operation was performed in the prone position. Through a standard posterior midline approach, the paravertebral muscles were dissected subperiosteally, detached from the spinous processes and laminae and retracted laterally. This exposure kept the supraspinal and interspinal ligaments intact. Two grooves were cut bilaterally between the spinous process and the inner edge of the facet joint with a grinding drill (2 mm diameter bit). The spinous process-ligament complex and lamina were freed completely and shifted posteriorly 1–3 mm to asymmetrically enlarge the spinal canal. The yellow ligaments were carefully dissected if the ligament tissue was exposed outside the groove. Two titanium mini-plates were premoulded, and the optimal realignment of the cutting lamina was adjusted. The medial side of the mini-plate was fixed at the base of the spinous process, while the lateral side was fixed to the host bone, all with screws of 3 mm. Prospective clinical trial

Our animal experiments and a prospective clinical trial were conducted and approved by the ethical committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (2020-KY-0357-001, 2022-KY-0357-002). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The project name is “The clinical study of factitiously induced spontaneous resorption of disc herniation”. The main purpose was to test whether iRHNPs occurred in RI-LDH-treated patients with symptomatic LDH at the early stage by monitoring the LDH volume. In addition, the clinical outcomes and groove healing state were assessed. The definition of early stage in this study was set as the time when bone healing occurred in the decompression groove (approximately 6 months to 1 year postoperatively). Our study typically used the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores to grade the severity of cervical myelopathy (full score = 17 points) based on the patients’ preoperative and follow-up function. Data were collected by independent reviewers. Each patient was also assessed with the visual analogue scale (VAS) score (0 means no pain, while 10 represents worst imaginable pain) for axial neck pain (nuchal, neck, or shoulder pains). Two doctors separately examined the patients, and the mean score was regarded as the final result throughout this study. Whole blood samples from each patient were collected preoperatively and 7 days postoperatively. These samples were used for the detection of osteocalcin, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3, β-Cross Laps, amino-terminal propeptide of type I collagen (PINP) and parathyroid hormone (PTH). The CDH volume was calculated based on MRI T2WI images by obtaining the sum of each area in the sagittal image multiplied by the scan thickness. This measurement method was consistent with those of previous studies13. The reliability of MRI volume measurement was verified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between two independent observers, yielding an ICC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95), indicating excellent inter-observer agreement. Phantom validation established a maximum measurement error of 4.52%, conservatively set at ± 5% for exclusion of technical error. The regression ratio was calculated as follows: difference in CDH volume; CDH volume at the last follow-up − preoperative CDH volume; regression ratio of CDH volume (%) = difference in CDH volume/preoperative CDH volume × 100. A ratio of changes in CDH volume: > 75%, 75% to 50%, 50% to 25%, and 25% to 10% was considered complete, massive, partial and little resolution, respectively; a ratio between − 10% to 10% was regarded as unchanged and attributed to manual error; and a ratio < − 10% was considered to indicate the reprotrusion of CDH. Complication data were recorded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system. Among 154 patients, 3 cases (1.95%) had grade I complications (superficial wound infection), 2 cases (1.30%) had grade II complications (transient neurological symptoms, resolving within 2 weeks), and no grade III–IV complications were observed. All complications were managed successfully without long-term sequelae (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was applied to all datasets prior to statistical analysis. Weight, volume, NO, sGAG release, and blood test data passed the normality test (p > 0.05 for all), so Student’s t-test was used for between-group comparisons. Volumetric data did not meet the normality assumption (p < 0.05), so paired Wilcoxon nonparametric test was used for paired comparisons, and Mann–Whitney U test was used for unpaired non-parametric data. The relationships between volume changes and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3/parathyroid hormone were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis (due to non-normal distribution of some variables). For linear regression analyses in Fig. 3c–d, data were verified to meet assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons to reduce type I error. Results

Synergistic effect of decompression and bone bioactive factors on disc degradation

To investigate the mechanism underlying induced resorption, we employed a rat disc-callus co-culture model in vitro and a rabbit laminoplasty model in vivo. These models demonstrated that the combined microenvironment of mechanical off-loading (simulating decompression) and the biological factors from a healing bone site synergistically drive disc tissue resorption.

The experimental protocol is outlined in Fig. 1a. Intervertebral disc explants were cultured for 8 days with or without fracture callus. Under free-loading conditions mimicking decompression, co-culture with callus led to a significant reduction in disc weight compared to controls at day 4 and day 8 (p = 0.049 at day 4, p = 0.043 at day 8, Fig. 1b). This degradation was associated with an early release of the pro-inflammatory mediator nitric oxide (NO) and sulphated glycosaminoglycans (sGAG) into the supernatant (Fig. 1c, d). NO release was increased significantly (p = 0.013 and 0.028 at day 2 and day 8, while sGAG release was also elevated significantly (p = 0.0001 and 0.048 at day 2 and day 6). These data suggested active callus-mediated matrix catabolism.

In the rabbit model, cervical laminotomies were created to establish a local bone-healing environment. Disc explants were implanted either outside the lamina or within the spinal canal (Fig. 1e). After one week, disc volume was unchanged in sham-operated controls but showed a significant decrease in the laminotomy group, irrespective of implantation site (Fig. 1f). Histological analysis (Fig. 1g, h) of explants from the experimental group revealed tissue reorganization, neovascularization, and altered matrix staining, consistent with active resorption, changes absent in control discs.

Technique and clinical validation of RI-LDH

Inspired by the mechanistic insights and prior success with cervical procedures, we developed a Resolution-Induction technique for Lumbar Disc Herniation (RI-LDH). The procedure involves bilateral laminar grooving with a 1–4 mm posterior shift of the spinous process-ligament complex (Fig. 2a). This achieves symmetrical spinal canal enlargement (decompression) while initiating bony groove healing, thereby creating the proposed dual microenvironment.

Two representative cases are presented (Fig. 2b–f). Case 1, a 57-year-old female with multi-level LDH, showed progressive resorption at all levels over 12 months, confirmed by MRI volume reduction and CT-evident groove healing (Fig. 2b, c). Case 2, a 47-year-old male with median and lateral LDHs, also exhibited substantial resorption (Fig. 2d, e). Postoperative X-rays confirmed stable fixation (Fig. 2f).

Prospective evaluation of all patients revealed significant clinical improvement following RI-LDH. The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score increased from 14.4 ± 4.8 to 22.1 ± 3.5 (p < 0.001), and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) improved from 29.9 ± 5.5 to 37.4 ± 3.9 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2g, h). Among these patients, 3 cases (1.95%) had grade superficial wound infection while 2 cases (1.30%) had transient neurological symptoms. All complications were successfully treated without long-term adverse effects.

Volumetric analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in herniation size post-RI-LDH (median: 805 [IQR: 600–1070] mm3 to 605 [445–845] mm3; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2i). In contrast, a conservatively managed cohort showed a significant volume increase (565 [403–748] mm3 to 705 [523–893] mm3; p = 0.012) (Fig. 2j). Defining regression as a volume reduction > 5%, the incidence of iRHNP was 77.8% at 1 week, 95.1% at 3 months, and 97.3% at ≥ 6 months postoperatively (Fig. 2k). The median regression ratio deepened over time: − 20.9% (1 week), − 35.6% (3 months), and − 44.4% (≥ 6 months) (Fig. 2l, m).

Clinical correlates of the iRHNP mechanism

A total of 154 patients (330 herniated discs) were included in the prospective analysis (Figs. 2, 3a). The clinical outcomes were satisfying while few complications were associated. The incidence and degree of iRHNP were independent of the number of herniation levels.

Spearman correlation analysis identified several factors associated with the degree of iRHNP (Fig. 3b). Patient age and symptom duration were inversely correlated with the magnitude of volume reduction (Fig. 3c, d). The presence of sacroiliitis was associated with diminished iRHNP (Fig. 3e). Notably, larger preoperative herniation volume predicted a greater proportional regression (Fig. 3f, g).

Systemic bone turnover markers were assessed perioperatively. One week post-RI-LDH, serum levels of bone formation markers (osteocalcin, PINP) and mineral regulators (PTH, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3) were significantly decreased (p < 0.0001), while the bone resorption marker β-CrossLaps was increased (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3h). The percentage decrease in LDH volume at 1 week correlated significantly with the decline in osteocalcin (Fig. 3i) and the rise in β-CrossLaps (Fig. 3j), linking the local resorptive process to systemic bone metabolism shifts.

Discussion

The resolution-induction technique for Lumbar Disc Herniation (RI-LDH) is predicated on achieving two co-primary outcomes: the induced resorption of herniated nucleus pulposus (iRHNP) and concomitant bone healing within the surgical decompression grooves. In our prospective cohort, MRI surveillance (mean follow-up: 1.12 ± 1.93 months) demonstrated iRHNP in 81.5% of lumbar disc herniations (LDHs), with a median volumetric reduction of 22.8%. This regressive trend intensified over time, with patients followed beyond six months exhibiting a 97.3% iRHNP rate and a median resorption ratio of 42.2%. The significant morphological regression, confirmed via CT evidence of solid bony union in the grooves and substantiated by marked improvements in JOA and ODI scores, robustly affirms the clinical efficacy of the RI-LDH paradigm.

The RI-LDH technique is designed to preserve the spinous process–ligament complex through symmetrical laminar grooving and a controlled posterior shift. This extradural approach minimizes manipulation of facet joints and neural elements, enhancing procedural safety. We posit that clinical recovery is mediated by a dual mechanism: immediate direct decompression from the 1–4 mm posterior canal expansion (increasing volume by 8–24%) and subsequent indirect decompression via the substantial mass reduction of the herniation itself through iRHNP17.

Correlational analysis identified key modulators of iRHNP magnitude. Greater resorption was associated with younger patient age and shorter symptom duration, suggesting more viable cellular responses in earlier-stage, “immature” herniations18. Paradoxically, a larger preoperative herniation volume predicted a greater proportional regression, potentially because extensive herniations are more fully exposed to the decompressed milieu and bone-derived bioactive factors. Conversely, concomitant sacroiliitis significantly impeded iRHNP, likely due to an adverse systemic inflammatory state. Notably, iRHNP degree was independent of herniation location, morphological classification, number of surgical levels, or common comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), underscoring that the core resorptive process is governed by local microenvironmental changes rather than these variables19.

The consistent induction of iRHNP across diverse anatomical contexts indicates it results from a fundamental alteration of the local niche. We propose a synergistic mechanism initiated by the concurrent withdrawal of mechanical force (decompression) and the paracrine release of bioactive factors from the healing bone groove. Decompression alleviates mechanical stress and ischemia, potentially triggering catabolic pathways within nucleus pulposus cells. Simultaneously, a cascade of cytokines and growth factors (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) released during the bone healing process likely potentiates matrix degradation20. This hypothesis is supported by the significant correlation between perioperative shifts in systemic bone metabolism markers (e.g., osteocalcin, β-CrossLaps) and the extent of disc resorption. The rarity of comparable iRHNP following other disc-preserving procedures—such as laminectomy (decompression without focused osteogenic stimulus) or anterior interbody fusion (bone healing without adequate segmental decompression of the herniation)—underscores the mechanistic necessity of combining both elements21. The successful translation of this principle from the cervical (RI-CDH) to the lumbar spine further confirms the regional generality of the “decompression + bone bioactive factors” mechanism.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, the mean clinical follow-up period was relatively short (~ 1 month), limiting assessment of long-term efficacy and iRHNP stability. We are currently extending follow-up to ≥ 3 years to address this. Second, the study lacked a direct surgical control group (e.g., standard microdiscectomy), precluding definitive comparison of RI-LDH with conventional surgical approaches. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing RI-LDH with microdiscectomy, with strict randomization, blinding of outcome assessors, and 3-year follow-up, is ongoing to address this gap. Third, while the animal models validated the proposed mechanism, they do not fully replicate the chronic, degenerative nature of human LDH, and the use of cervical models for lumbar-focused research raises considerations of anatomical and biomechanical differences. Future studies will utilize lumbar-specific animal models to enhance translational relevance. Fourth, systemic blood biomarkers were used to infer local bone healing and disc resorption, which may not directly reflect local factor concentrations. Local tissue sampling via microdialysis or intraoperative biopsy in future studies will provide more direct evidence of factor expression and activity. Fifth, the non-randomized prospective design may introduce selection bias between RI-LDH and conservative treatment groups; propensity score matching was used to balance baseline characteristics (age, duration of illness, LDH volume), but residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, while measurement reliability was validated, inherent variability in MRI acquisition and segmentation remains a potential source of error.

While LDH reprotrusion occurred post-RI-LDH, no cases caused recurrent symptoms during the study period. Long-term iRHNP progression warrants investigation, though our decade-long RI-CDH follow-up showed no reprotrusion. Serial LDH volume measurements employed rigorous controls: standardized supine MRI positioning minimized curvature variability; AI-assisted segmentation reduced manual error; phantom validation established a maximum 4.52% error rate (conservatively set at ± 5%); and application of the conservation principle enhanced reliability22.

In conclusion, this study validates the synergistic “decompression + bone bioactive factors” mechanism as the principal driver of iRHNP following RI-LDH. While establishing a novel therapeutic paradigm, the complexity of the process warrants further investigation into its precise molecular mediators and long-term clinical trajectory. The capacity of RI-LDH to reliably induce disc resorption provides a foundational strategy for a resolution-oriented approach to treating lumbar disc herniation.
