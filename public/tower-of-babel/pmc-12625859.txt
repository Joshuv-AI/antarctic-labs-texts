Association between late access to care and mortality for PWH: insights from the French Hospital Database on HIV (ANRS CO4-FHDH) from 2002 to 2016
Authors: Valérie Potard, Fabienne Caby, Malamine Gassama, Emilie Lanoy, Sylvie Abel, Firouzé Bani-Sadr, Agathe Becker, Sylvie Bregigeon, Blandine Denis, Pierre de Truchis, Claudine Duvivier, Christine Katlama, Isabelle Lamaury, Guillaume Martin-Blondel, Jean-Luc Meynard, Lionel Piroth, Axel Ursenbach, Olivier Robineau, Pierre Tattevin, Dominique Costagliola, Sophie Grabar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUsing three categories of stages of HIV disease at access to care (advanced, intermediate, early HIV disease), we explored the impact of delayed access on the risk of death at up to 5 years and whether progress in antiretroviral regimens has mitigated this impact.MethodsAdults from the French Hospital Database on HIV (ANRS CO4-FHDH) cohort with HIV-1 infection and first access to care during 2002–2021 were included. To study the impact of the stage of HIV disease at first access to care on the risk of death, only participants included from 2002 to 2016 were analysed to allow for at least 5 years of follow-up until 31 December 2021. Fine and Gray competing risk models considering lost to follow-up as a competing event were used adjusting for age, gender, mode of acquisition, region of origin, time between diagnosis and access to care, period of access to care (2002–2013 vs 2014–2016).ResultsAmong the 64 400 people living with HIV included, 18 305 (28.4%) had advanced and 13 042 (20.3%) intermediate HIV disease. The 5-year cumulative incidence of death was estimated as 1.8% (95% CI 1.7% to 1.9%) overall, from 0.9% (0.8% to 1.0%) for those with early HIV disease to 6.0% (5.4% to 6.7%) for those with AIDS. People with AIDS had a much higher risk of death than those with early HIV disease, with a sub-distribution HR (sHR) of 18.4 (95% CI 12.0 to 28.4) in the first 6 months of follow-up, which remained significant at 48–60 months: sHR=2.1 (1.3 to 3.3). The risk of death was higher for the other categories of advanced HIV disease but to a smaller extent. The risk of death was not statistically different depending on the calendar period.ConclusionsDelayed access to care remains associated with an elevated risk of death, even after 48 months. There was no significant improvement in the risk of death after 2014 when immediate initiation of combined antiretroviral therapy was recommended and integrase strand transfer inhibitor-based regimens became the preferred first-line.

Introduction

Access to care only when HIV disease is advanced is associated with a poor prognosis.15 People living with HIV (PWH) starting combined antiretroviral therapy (cART) with a low CD4+ cell count have a shorter life expectancy.1 Early diagnosis of HIV reduces both AIDS-related and non-AIDS-related deaths.5

Early diagnosis of HIV and immediate initiation of cART are key factors in the fight against HIV. This is a major public health paradigm to not only enable PWH to live longer and in better health, but also to reduce HIV transmission and control the epidemic. The INSIGHT START and TEMPRANO trials showed that immediate initiation of cART for PWH reduces severe morbidity and mortality relative to deferred initiation of cART after CD4+ cell counts have already declined to <350/mm3.6 7 These results have prompted the WHO to recommend universal treatment of PWH since 2016.8 In France, this was recommended at the end of 2013.9

The IeDEA and COHERE cohort studies found median CD4 counts among PWH starting cART to still be <350/mm3 in 2015 in all low-income, lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income and high-income countries.10 Data from the European cohort collaborations COHERE and EuroSIDA showed that between 2010 and 2016 the proportion of PWH first accessing care late, defined by CD4 counts ≤350/mm3 or an AIDS-defining event, was 48.4%.11 The proportion varied depending on the country, ranging from 36.9% in Estonia to 64.2% in Poland, but there was no significant change over time.11

Few studies have analysed the impact of advanced HIV disease at first access to care on mortality,2412 but most were carried out before universal cART was recommended and before the consensus definition of late access to care was updated.24 This updated definition reclassifies individuals with primary infection as ‘not late’13 in order to more accurately define late-stage disease. These studies showed a deleterious effect both of advanced HIV disease in the short and long term.24

In parallel with universal cART expansion,8 8 2013 in France,9 the preferred treatment for initiation also evolved towards integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based regimens (2014 in France14). Only two recent studies on this topic conducted in Spain (PISCIS cohort) showed that INSTI-based first-line treatment was associated with reduced 2-year mortality for PWH initiating cART15 and for those with advanced HIV disease who started therapy.16

The objectives of the present study were to describe the stages of HIV disease at first access to care, using the new definition of late-stage disease, over the 2002–2021 period in France, to identify factors associated with intermediate or advanced HIV disease and assess the impact of first access to care for patients with intermediate or advanced HIV disease on the risk of death up to 5 years after the first access to care. In addition, we assessed whether the impact was still the same after 2014 when immediate initiation of cART was recommended and INSTI-based regimens became the preferred first-line treatment in France.

Methods

The French Hospital Database on HIV (ANRS CO4-FHDH)

The ANRS CO4-FHDH is a hospital-based open multicentre cohort in which inclusions have been ongoing since 1989.17 Individuals are eligible if they are 18 years of age or older, have documented HIV-1 or HIV-2 infection and have given their written informed consent to participate. Data are collected prospectively on standardised forms, which include demographic characteristics, the date and type of clinical events recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases, antiretroviral therapy and biological markers.18

Study population

Antiretroviral-naïve people living with HIV-1 enrolled in the ANRS CO4-FHDH between 1 January 2002 and 31 December 2021 with available CD4 counts within 15 days from enrolment if initiating cART, or 30 days if not, were included in the present analysis. PWH with an AIDS-defining event of an unknown type at the time of diagnosis were not eligible. Knowledge of the CD4 count and AIDS status was necessary to classify the stage at first access to care. The type of AIDS-defining event is associated with the prognosis,19 20 which is why we excluded participants with an unknown type of AIDS-defining event.

Statistical analysis

We used the updated consensus definition of late HIV diagnosis to define the stage at enrolment.13 The advanced HIV disease stage, defined as AIDS or a CD4 count ≤200/mm3 and no primary infection, was divided into three categories: (1) AIDS, (2) CD4 count≤50/mm3 without AIDS and (3) CD4 count=50–200/mm3 without AIDS. The intermediate HIV disease stage was defined as a CD4 count between 200 and 350/mm3 without AIDS and no primary infection and the early HIV disease stage as either primary infection or a CD4 count>350/mm3 without AIDS.

Continuous variables are expressed as medians with IQRs and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Associations between characteristics and the stage of HIV disease at first access to care were explored using multivariable multinomial logistic regression. The following potential confounding factors were accounted for: age, gender, mode of acquisition of HIV infection and geographic origin, time interval between diagnosis and access to care, calendar period of access to care and region of the healthcare centre.

To study the impact of the stage of HIV disease at first access to care on the risk of death, the analysis was restricted to participants with at least one follow-up, included from 2002 to 2016 to allow for at least 5 years of follow-up until 31 December 2021 (last ANRS CO4-FHDH update at the time of this analysis). The baseline for the analyses was the date of enrolment in the ANRS CO4-FHDH cohort. The causes of death were coded using the ART-CC cause of death coding rules.21 The cumulative incidence of death was assessed considering loss to follow-up as a competing event using the Fine and Gray model.22 Individuals were considered lost to follow-up if there was an interval of more than 18 months between the last follow-up visit and the last database update for the centre in which they were followed. Analyses were censored at 5 years. Multivariable competing-risk regression models, yielding sub-distribution HRs (sHRs), were used to assess the impact of the stage of HIV disease at first access to care on mortality. As the risk of death was not proportional over the 60 months of follow-up, the time was divided into five intervals: 0–6, 6–12, 12–24, 24–48 and 48–60 months. Multivariable competing-risk regression models were adjusted for the same variables as the multinomial logistic regression. SAS software (V.9.4; SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

Patient and public involvement

This project, like all projects conducted in the ANRS CO4-FHDH, has been reviewed and discussed by the ANRS CO4-FHDH Scientific Committee, which includes clinicians from the study sites, two members of the coordination and analysis team, two representatives of PWH associations and three representatives of ANRS-MIE.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Among the 85 910 antiretroviral-naïve participants with HIV-1 infection aged 18 years or above who were enrolled in the ANRS CO4-FHDH between 1 January 2002 and 31 December 2021 (online supplemental figure S1), 5792 were excluded from the analysis (no available CD4+ T-cell count for 4778 and unknown type of AIDS-defining illness at enrolment for 1014). Excluded PWH were older, less likely to be men who have sex with men (MSM) or women originating from sub-Saharan Africa, had a longer time between diagnosis and access to care and were more likely to have been followed in the Paris area (seeonline supplemental table S1).

Among the 80 118 PWH included in the present analysis, 29% first accessed care with advanced HIV disease (9% with AIDS, 5% with CD4≤50/mm3, 15% with CD4=50–200/mm3) and 20% first accessed care with intermediate HIV disease. The characteristics of PWH and their association with the stage of HIV disease at the time of first access to care are presented in table 1. The proportion of PWH first accessing care with advanced HIV disease was 33% in the 2002–2004 period and then slightly decreased from the 2005–2007 (29%) to 2017–2019 (27%) periods, before increasing to 31% for the 2020–2021 period (table 1 and figure 1). PWH who first accessed care in the 2002–2004 period were more likely to have accessed care with advanced or intermediate HIV disease than those who first accessed care in the 2005–2007 period, whereas PWH first accessing care between 2008 and 2019 were less likely to have accessed care with advanced or intermediate HIV disease. There was no significant difference in the risk of first accessing care with advanced or intermediate HIV disease between PWH who first accessed care in the 2020–2021 period and those who first accessed care in the 2005–2007 period.

Mortality

Among the 65 572 participants enrolled up to 31 December 2016, 1172 (1.8%) had no follow-up after enrolment. These 1172 PWH were older, less likely to be MSM or women originating from sub-Saharan Africa, more likely to be injection drug users, had a longer time between diagnosis and access to care, and were more likely to have been followed in a French overseas territory (online supplemental table S2). The baseline characteristics of the 64 400 PWH included in this analysis are presented in online supplemental table S3.

During a median follow-up of 60 months, 1164 PWH died, including 708 who first accessed care with advanced HIV disease and 173 with intermediate HIV disease. The overall 5-year cumulative incidence of death was estimated to be 1.8% (95% CI 1.7% to 1.9%) (figure 2), ranging from 6.0% of PWH with AIDS down to 0.9% of those with early HIV disease.

Cumulative incidence of death among people living with HIV first accessing care between 2002 and 2016.

The adjusted sHR of death for each time interval is shown in table 2 according to the stage of HIV disease at first access to care and calendar period. PWH with one of the three stages of advanced HIV disease, when they first accessed care, had a much higher risk of death in the first 6 months than those with early HIV disease. In particular, the sHR was 18.4 for those with AIDS. The risk of death for the three stages of advanced HIV disease was still significantly higher 24–48 months after access to care, with an sHR of 2.7 for those with AIDS, 2.6 for those with a CD4 count≤50 without AIDS and 1.7 for those with a CD4 count of 50–200 without AIDS. The risk of death for PWH with AIDS was still significant 48–60 months after access to care, with an sHR of 2.1. The death rate at 60 months did not differ between PWH who first accessed care between 2002 and 2013 and those who first accessed care between 2014 and 2016 (figure 1). After adjustment, there was no difference in the risk of death for PWH newly accessing care between 2002 and 2013 and those newly accessing care between 2014 and 2016 (table 2).

Discussion

This study shows that the proportion of PWH who first accessed care at an advanced stage was still high in France in 2020–2021 (31%) and has varied little between 2002 and 2021 (29% overall). Factors associated with first access to care at an advanced stage of HIV disease were being older, belonging to groups other than MSM originating from France and being cared for in French Guiana or Guadeloupe.

Moreover, we showed that the risk of death was higher for PWH who first accessed care at an advanced stage of HIV disease than those who first accessed care at an early stage; 11.2-fold higher during the first 6 months and 1.7-fold higher between 48 and 60 months. There was no significant improvement in the risk of death after 2014, when immediate cART initiation and INSTI-based treatments were recommended as the first-line regimen, regardless of the stage at first access to care.

When comparing with the French National Health data system, which contains data on all reimbursed health expenditures in France, the ANRS CO4-FHDH includes 66% of PWH in care with similar age, sex and geographical distributions. In addition, the main strength of this study was its large size, with more than 64 000 PWH enrolled between 2002 and 2016, which allowed a robust analysis of the impact of first access to care with advanced HIV disease on the risk of death. The large size of the study allowed us to explore the evolution of the risk of death in the short, middle and long term according to the stage of HIV disease at first access to care. Loss to follow-up was considered as a competing risk for the risk of death so as not to underestimate the risk of death due to an unknown death status among those lost to follow-up,23 which was not accounted for in most previously published papers on this topic.2 3 12

PWH without follow-up after enrolment (n=1172) constituted a limitation of the study. The factors associated with the risk of no follow-up after enrolment were the same as those associated with the risk of death, that is, older PWH, injection drug users originating from France and PWH cared for in France overseas. Therefore, this issue may have led to a slight underestimation of the risk of death. Of note, the proportion of PWH without follow-up was higher in the 2002–2013 than in the 2014–2016 period (2.0% vs 1.2%), although still small.

We found that factors associated with intermediate HIV disease were similar to those associated with advanced HIV disease. A higher risk of first access to care at an advanced stage among older individuals has already been reported in Europe, as well as in other parts of the world.2427 Several explanations can be considered: (1) as the CD4 count declines with age, older PWH are more likely to present with a lower CD4 count at diagnosis; (2) older individuals are less targeted for test-and-treat strategies; (3) older individuals do not see themselves as being at risk for HIV and (4) symptoms of HIV disease may be attributed to an age-related disease by physicians. The risk of first accessing care with advanced HIV disease was lower among MSM originating from France, who probably have a greater awareness of the risk of HIV infection and have more frequent testing.28 In addition, it is still more difficult for migrants to access healthcare because of language, cultural and financial barriers, as well as less knowledge of the healthcare system. Several studies have reported similar results, showing heterosexual transmission and migration status as risk factors for first accessing care with advanced HIV disease122426 28 29 and, strikingly, these factors have not changed over time, illustrating the difficulty of targeting screening programmes to the population at the highest risk of first accessing care at an advanced stage.

The risk of death among PWH who first accessed care with an AIDS-defining event was 18.4-fold higher (95% CI 12.0 to 28.4) during the first 6 months and 11.0-fold higher (95% CI 6.7 to 18.0) between 6 and 12 months than those who first accessed care at an early stage of the disease. In the CoRIS cohort in Spain, covering the period 2004–2013, similar results were observed: PWH who first accessed care with an AIDS-defining event had a 22.6-fold higher risk of death (95% CI 11.5 to 44.6) during the first year.4

Surprisingly, the risk of death did not decrease for participants who first accessed care in 2014 or later when immediate treatment initiation was recommended and INSTI-based treatments became the preferred first-line regimen. In the Spanish PISCIS cohort, INSTI-based first-line treatments were associated with a reduction in mortality relative to non-INSTI-based treatments for PWH with advanced HIV disease who started therapy between 2010 and 2020 after accounting for the differential access of INSTI before and after 2016.16 This is not necessarily in contrast with our results, as we compared the period before 2014 and the period 2014–2016, instead of before and after 2016 and the uptake of INSTI-based first-line treatment was gradual in our study from 27.0% in 2014 to 49.4% in 2016. It remains to be seen whether an improvement will be observed over a more recent period associated with higher uptake of INSTI-based first-line regimen. Similarly, we did not observe a reduction in the risk of death after 2014, regardless of the stage of first access to care. Given the large size of our study, this is unlikely to be explained by a lack of power, but rather by the absence of a clinically meaningful improvement over these years. These sobering findings might suggest that advanced HIV disease may be associated with damage to the immune system that treatment cannot reverse.30

Given that the 5-year cumulative risk of death was 0.9% among PWH who first accessed care with an early stage of the disease, 584 deaths could have been avoided if all participants had accessed care at an early stage, and the number of deaths 5 years after the first access to care would have been reduced by half. Almost a third of PWH first accessed care at an advanced stage in 2020–2021 in France.31 This is an important issue and indicates long time intervals between infection, diagnosis and care, in particular during the COVID-19 pandemic, not only in France.32 This situation leads to higher mortality, as confirmed in this study. It also induces higher healthcare costs during the years following ART initiation33 and contributes to fuelling the HIV epidemic.

Conclusion

From 2002 to 2021, in France, an average of 29% of PWH first accessed care at an advanced stage of HIV infection, the figure being 31% in 2020–2021. First access to care at an advanced stage is associated with an increased risk of death, even after 48 months of follow-up, with no improvement since immediate cART initiation was recommended and INSTI-based regimens became the preferred first-line regimen. Early diagnosis remains a major objective to diminish the burden of death among newly diagnosed PWH and to control the HIV pandemic.
