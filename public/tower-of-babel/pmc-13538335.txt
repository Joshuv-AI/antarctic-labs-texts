Integration of network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation to elucidate the mechanism of Psychotria rubra in depression treatment
Authors: Li Yin, Chengshu Lu, Ju Ye, Yewen Zhang, Shirui Mo, Huakun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression is a chronic mental disorder with high disability and mortality rates, affecting approximately 95 million individuals in China alone. The limitations of current antidepressant therapies, including restricted efficacy, delayed onset, and significant adverse effects, have motivated the search for alternative treatments from traditional Chinese medicine. Psychotria rubra (PR), a widely distributed medicinal plant in southern China with reported antidepressant properties, represents a promising candidate; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying its antidepressant effects remain largely unexplored. This study aims to elucidate the mechanistic basis of PR in depression treatment by integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vivo experimental validation. We integrated two depression-related transcriptome datasets [GSE76826 (training set: 10 depressed vs. 12 healthy blood samples) and GSE98793 (validation set: 64 depressed vs. 64 healthy blood samples)] to identify differentially expressed genes in depression. Ferroptosis-related genes were retrieved from GeneCards (score > 8) and FerrDb databases, and eight machine learning models with five-fold cross-validation were employed to screen key diagnostic genes. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations validated interactions between PR's active ingredients and target genes. In vivo, eighty male C57BL/6 mice (6-week-old) were randomly allocated into eight groups (n = 10 per group): control, chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model, deacetyl asperulosidic acid (10 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, intraperitoneal), deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester (10 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, intraperitoneal), Procyanidin B1 (20 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, intraperitoneal), Procyanidin B2 (20 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, intraperitoneal), Procyanidin C1 (20 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, intraperitoneal), and fluoxetine (10 mg·kg⁻1·d⁻1, oral gavage, positive control). Following 21 days of CUMS induction, drug treatment was administered for 14 consecutive days. Behavioral assessments (sucrose preference test, open field test, tail suspension test, and forced swimming test), inflammatory factors (serotonin, interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α), oxidative stress markers (superoxide dismutase, malondialdehyde), and ferroptosis indicators (reactive oxygen species, glutathione, Fe2⁺ levels) were measured. Machine learning analysis identified EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGF-A as candidate diagnostic genes associated with depression and ferroptosis. Molecular docking revealed favorable binding affinities between multiple PR active ingredients (including asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester) and EPAS1, with deacetyl asperulosidic acid showing the most favorable docking score (− 8.737 kcal/mol) and asperuloside and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester exhibiting comparable binding free energies (− 35.22 and − 35.26 kcal/mol, respectively) in molecular dynamics simulations. In vivo, PR's main active ingredients significantly improved depressive-like behaviors and reduced neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis-related markers in CUMS-induced mice, with Procyanidin B1 demonstrating the most pronounced antidepressant-like effect. PR alleviated depression potentially through modulating EPAS1 expression. Procyanidin B1 might be the key active ingredients in PR to alleviate the progression of depression and ferroptosis. This study offers a promising direction for developing new antidepressants from traditional Chinese medicine with multi-component, multi-target effects.Keywords: Psychotria rubra, Depression, Ferroptosis, EPAS1, Procyanidin B1

Introduction

Depression is a chronic mental illness, characterized by continuous and long-term lack of pleasure or depressive emotions, and is associated with high disability and mortality rates (Kwiatkowska et al. 2025; Monroe and Harkness 2022). The number of individuals with depression in China is as high as 95 million, and about one-third of the total number of these patients are under the age of 18 years, demonstrating a continuous increase in the incidence rate and younger patient population, which seriously affects the social and economic development and the quality of life of patients (Lu et al. 2024; Nisar et al. 2020). Currently, monoamine oxidase inhibitors and monoamine neurotransmitter reuptake inhibitors are used to treat depression (Birkenhager and Heijnen 2024; Noori et al. 2022). However, these drugs often have side effects, such as liver toxicity, single effects, slow onset, and significant individual differences (Eynde et al. 2023). In contrast, traditional Chinese medicine is characterized by multiple components, multiple effects, and multiple targets (Xi et al. 2025). Therefore, efforts are increasingly being made to identify antidepressants from natural products, such as traditional Chinese medicines (Huang et al. 2024; Tang and Tian 2024). Among them, Psychotria rubra (PR) belonging to the genus Rubia in the family Rubiaceae is widely distributed in areas such as Qiannan, Guizhou Province, and the Daxiaoshan region in the Guangxi Province, China. PR possesses the effects of clearing and detoxifying heat, reducing swelling, removing toxins, and dispelling wind and dampness (A et al. 1998). In addition, it has a good antidepressant-like effect in preclinical behavioral despair models, as evidenced by reduced immobility time in forced swimming and tail suspension tests (Jl et al. 2011). Preliminary phytochemical investigations have identified several bioactive constituents in PR, including iridoid glycosides (such as asperuloside and deacetyl asperulosidic acid) and Procyanidins (Procyanidin B1, B2, and C1), which may contribute to its neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties. Notably, Procyanidins have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects in various central nervous system disorder models(Liu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a, b), supporting their potential relevance in depression intervention. However, the mechanisms underlying the role of PR in depression remain unclear.

Ferroptosis, a novel form of cell death, has fundamentally different characteristics from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy (Jiang et al. 2021). It primarily involves lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids triggered by reactive oxygen species and Fe2+ on the cell membrane, consequently disrupting the membrane structure and ultimately inducing cell death (Liang et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2021). Ferroptosis has been implicated in depression development. For instance, Shen et al. reported that acupuncture could attenuate chronic, unpredictable, and mild stress-induced depression-like behaviors by mediating ferroptosis (Shen et al. 2024). Similarly, Li et al. demonstrated that the suppression of ferroptosis ameliorated depression through tsRNA-3029b (Li et al. 2023). Emerging evidence suggests that natural polyphenolic compounds, particularly Procyanidins, can modulate ferroptosis through their potent antioxidant and iron-chelating properties. For example, Procyanidin B1 has been reported to attenuate oxidative stress-induced neuronal injury by scavenging free radicals and chelating transition metal ions, mechanisms that directly counteract the lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation central to ferroptosis (Liu et al. 2026). Furthermore, iridoid glycosides such as asperuloside have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in depression models via pathways intersecting with oxidative stress regulation (Yin et al. 2025a). These preliminary findings provide a biological rationale for hypothesizing that PR may exert antidepressant effects, at least in part, through ferroptosis modulation. Therefore, ferroptosis may serve as a novel target for the prevention and treatment of depression. However, it remains unclear whether PR improves depression by mediating ferroptosis.

The present study was designed to address these gaps through an integrated multi-omics and experimental approach. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) identify potential ferroptosis-related therapeutic targets of PR in depression using network pharmacology and bioinformatics analysis of publicly available transcriptome datasets; (2) screen and prioritize key diagnostic genes through machine learning algorithms; (3) evaluate the binding interactions between PR active ingredients and candidate target proteins using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations; and (4) validate the antidepressant effects of PR main active components and their impacts on neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis markers in a CUMS-induced mouse model of depression. Here, we elucidated the potential mechanisms of PR in depression using an integrative approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vivo experiments. In addition, we further established a depression mouse model to analyze the regulatory effects of the main active ingredients of PR on disease progression, inflammation levels, and ferroptosis progression in the depression mouse model.

Materials and methods

Data source

Two depression-related transcriptome datasets (GSE76826 and GSE98793) were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The data for 10 depressed and 12 healthy blood samples were obtained from the GSE76826 dataset after removing samples from the remission group. The GSE98793 dataset included 64 depression and 64 healthy blood samples, excluding 64 patients who simultaneously suffered from both generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder. The GSE76826 and GSE98793 datasets were used as the training and validation sets, respectively. While the training set sample size is modest, we employed five-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling (100 iterations) to mitigate overfitting risks and enhance model robustness. In addition, nine ferroptosis-related genes were acquired from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using the keyword "ferroptosis" with relevance score > 8 (a stringent threshold selected to ensure high-confidence gene-disease associations, capturing only the most strongly curated ferroptosis-related genes). Next, 621 regulators of ferroptosis genes, including drivers, inhibitors, markers, and unclassified regulatory factors, were obtained from the FerrDb V2 database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/). Finally, 565 ferroptosis-related genes were obtained after deduplication and merging of the genes obtained from the two databases. Subsequently, the "GSVA" package (Wang et al. 2014) was utilized to calculate the ferroptosis score in each sample by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to compare the ferroptosis score between depression and healthy groups.

Screening of active ingredients of PR and potential targets

The active ingredients of PR were explored using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC/Q-TOF MS) analysis. Approximately 200 mg of the PR extract from sections S1 to S8 was extracted, and 20 mL of 50% methanol was added. Next, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane. For control samples, 5 mg each of deacetyl asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester, Procyanidin B1, asperulosidic acid, Procyanidin B2, and Procyanidin C1 were added to 20 mL of pure water. Afterward, the mixture was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane. The ACQUITY Premier Oligonucleotide BEH C18 (1.7 μm × 2.1 × 100 mm) was used for MS, the injection volume was 1 μL, the mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile, and the column temperature was set to 35 °C. Kromasil 100–5-C18 (4.6 μm × 250 mm) was used for liquid chromatography, the injection volume was 5 μL, the mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile, and the column temperature was set to 30 °C.

Collection of target genes of PR in treating depression

The DEGs between depression and healthy groups were identified employing the “limma” package (Smyth 2005) with criteria set to |log2FC|> 0 and P.adj < 0.05. The P-value was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method. Fisher’s exact test was applied to explore the correlation between DEGs and potential targets of the active ingredients of PR, and overlapping genes were identified.

Weighted gene co-expression analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed to identify the module genes that highly correlated with the phenotype. All genes in the dataset were ranked in descending order of variance, and those with the top 25% of genes ranked by variance across samples were selected for subsequent WGCNA (this percentile-based filtering strategy retains the most variable genes that are likely to drive phenotypic differences while reducing noise from low-variance, stably expressed housekeeping genes) (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). Firstly, the “power” value was employed to analyze the square of the association coefficient between average connectivity, connectivity (k), and p(k). Clustering and dynamic pruning techniques, parameters such as minModuleSize = 70 were utilized to group genes with high correlations into modules, with the MEDissThres parameter set to 0.3. Next, modules closely associated with phenotype traits were selected with a cut-off value of P < 0.05 and r ≥ 0.6. Subsequently, the overlapped genes between DEGs, module genes, ferroptosis-related genes, and potential targets of active PR ingredients were ascertained using “VennDiagram” (Chen 2022), which were considered ferroptosis-related genes against depression. Next, the enriched analysis was applied to these ferroptosis-related genes against depression using the “clusterProfiler” package (Liaw and Wiener 2002) with P < 0.05.

Machine learning

Based on the obtained ferroptosis-related genes against depression, eight machine learning model algorithms were employed to screen feature genes. To prevent overfitting given the limited training set size, five-fold cross-validation with 100 bootstrap resampling iterations was implemented for each algorithm using "glmnet," "randomForestcaret," "gbm," "xgboost," and "pls," (Chen and Guestrin 2016; Chin 2010; Hengl et al. 2004; Liaw and Wiener 2002; Meinshausen 2007; Van Essen 2012; Yu et al. 2012), “LASSO,” “ridge regression,” “Elastic Net regression,” “SVM-RFE,” “random forest (RF),” “gradient boosting machine, GBM,” “extreme gradient boosting, XGBoost,” and “partial least squares logistic regression, and PLSLR.” Next, the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated using “caret” and “pROC” packages to evaluate the performance of machine learning models (Hanley and Mcneil 1982). Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, with calibration assessed through five-fold cross-validation repeated 100 times using bootstrap resampling. Afterward, the mean AUC values were sorted in descending order and the machine learning model with the highest AUC value was selected as the optimal machine learning model; the feature genes in the optimal machine learning model were obtained as the candidate diagnostic genes. Spearman's method was employed to assess the correlation between the candidate diagnostic genes and the depression/ferroptosis score. Moreover, the diagnostic performance of the candidate diagnostic genes was analyzed using the ROC curves, and the key diagnostic genes were identified. In addition, the expression of key diagnostic genes was confirmed in the validation set.

Molecular docking

The protein structures of the key diagnostic genes were acquired from the PDB database. The crystal structure of EPAS1 (HIF-2α) was retrieved using PDB ID 6 × 28 (resolution: 2.39 Å), which contains the PAS-B domain where small-molecule ligands are known to bind. The biological assembly (chain A) was used for docking, and all crystallographic water molecules were removed prior to receptor preparation. For MAPK14 and VEGFA, structures with the highest resolution available in the PDB were selected (PDB IDs to be specified upon revision). The three-dimensional molecular structure of SDF files of active ingredients was obtained from PubChem, and energy minimization was performed under the MMFF94 force field. “AutoDock Vina 1.2.3” (Eberhardt et al. 2021) software was used for molecular docking. Water molecules, salt ions, and small molecules were removed using PyMOL 2.5.5″ (Seeliger and de Groot 2010). The binding site was defined by a search box centered on the co-crystallized ligand binding pocket (center coordinates: x = 12.3, y = 45.6, z = 78.9) with dimensions of 25 × 25 × 25 Å3. Prior to virtual screening, the docking protocol was validated by redocking the co-crystallized ligand to the receptor, yielding a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.85 Å between the predicted and experimental poses, confirming the reliability of the docking parameters. "ADFRSuite 1.0″ (Ravindranath et al. 2015) was used to convert all the processed small molecules and receptor proteins into the PDBQT format. The maximum number of binding modes was set to 32, and the remaining parameters were maintained at default settings. The docking results were subsequently illustrated utilizing the “PyMol 2.5.2.”

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

All simulations and analyses were conducted utilizing “AMBER22” (Lee et al. 2020). Charges of small molecules were detected through the “antechamber” package and “Hartree–Fock (HF) SCF/6-31G*” (Wang et al. 2006) in the “Gaussian 09” software (Frisch et al. 2009). The ff14SB force field (Maier et al. 2015) was employed for proteins, and GAFF (Wang et al. 2004) was applied for small-molecule parameterization. The structures were solvated in a 10 Å box of TIP3P water (Mark and Nilsson 2001), and the LEaP module was utilized to add hydrogen atoms, and existing charges were neutralized by adding s Na+/Cl−. For molecular dynamics simulation, the system was briefly minimized by utilizing 2,500 steepest descent and 2,500 conjugate gradient cycles. A fixed volume and constant heating rate were used and the system was heated for 200 ps to slowly increase the temperature from 0 K to 298.15 K, and subsequently equilibrated for 100 ns without restraints using NPT. Long-range interactions were simulated using the “particle mesh Ewald (PME)” method (Sagui and Darden 1999) with a parameter of 10 Å. The “SHAKE” method (Kr Utler et al. 2015) was used for limiting the bond length of hydrogen atoms, and the “Langevin algorithm” (Larini et al. 2007) was utilized for temperature control, where the collision frequency γ was set to 2 ps−1. The system pressure was 1 atm, the integration step size was 2 fs, and the trajectory was recorded every 10 ps. Behavioral assessments

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

The 4-day SPT protocol included: Day 1: Two bottles of 1% sucrose solution provided for 24 h adaptation; Day 2: One bottle replaced with distilled water; bottle positions swapped every 12 h to eliminate lateral bias; Day 3: Water deprivation for 24 h; Day 4: Simultaneous access to 1% sucrose solution and distilled water for 24hSucrose preference was calculated as: (sucrose consumption/total fluid intake) × 100%.

Open field test (OFT)

Locomotor activity was assessed in a 50 × 50 × 40 cm apparatus with a central 25 × 25 cm zone defined as the center area. Mice were placed in the center and recorded for 5-min locomotor activity. Total distance traveled and time spent in center zone were analyzed during the last 4 min using EthoVision XT tracking software.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Mice were suspended 1 cm from the tail tip in a sound-attenuating chamber. Immobility duration was recorded during the last 4 min of the 6-min test session using a digital timer. Immobility was defined as the absence of all movements except those required for respiration.

Forced swimming test (FST)

The test was conducted in a 30 cm deep cylindrical tank with water temperature maintained at 22–25 °C. Immobility time was recorded during the last 4 min of the 6-min test period. Immobility was defined as floating without struggling, with only minimal movements to keep the head above water.

Sample collection

After behavioral testing, mice were anesthetized in a closed chamber saturated with ether vapor and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation. Cardiac perfusion was performed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood. The brain tissues were rapidly harvested on ice, and hippocampal regions were dissected and immediately placed into sterile cryotubes. Samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

ROS measurement in hippocampus by flow cytometry

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantified using the ROS Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Hippocampal tissues were digested with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by filtration through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. The DCFH-DA probe was diluted according to the manufacturer's instructions (negative control without probe, positive control treated with 50 μM H₂O₂). Cells were incubated with the probe at 37 °C for 30 min, washed with PBS, and analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. The complete gating strategy was as follows: (1) a forward scatter-area (FSC-A) versus side scatter-area (SSC-A) plot was used to identify the main cell population and exclude debris; (2) a forward scatter-height (FSC-H) versus FSC-A plot was applied to exclude doublets and select single cells; (3) fluorescent events were then gated on a histogram of FITC fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission at 488/525 nm), with the negative control sample used to establish the fluorescence threshold; (4) the positive gate was set at the upper 2% of the negative control distribution, and the percentage of DCF-positive cells as well as the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) were recorded for each sample. A minimum of 10,000 single cells were acquired per sample. Fluorescence intensity was quantified to determine intracellular ROS levels.

Inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and Fe2⁺ analysis in hippocampus

Hippocampal tissues were weighed and homogenized in ice-cold 0.7% PBS (1:9 w/v) using a tissue homogenizer on ice. Homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for analysis. Levels of 5-HT, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, GSH, MDA, and SOD activity were measured using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Fe2⁺ concentrations were quantified with the Iron Assay Kit (Beyotime, Nantong, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All procedures were performed strictly following kit instructions.

Western blot

Hippocampal tissues were rapidly dissected on ice and homogenized in ice‑cold RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Homogenates were sonicated for 15 s on ice, incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore) via a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% non‑fat dry milk in Tris‑buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‑20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ACSL4 (1:1000, ab155282, Abcam), SLC7A11 (1:1000, ab175186, Abcam), GPX4 (1:2000, ab125066, Abcam), EPAS1/HIF‑2α (1:1000, ab199729, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:5000, #5174, Cell Signaling Technology) as the loading control. After thorough washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (ECL, Millipore) and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio‑Rad).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA) and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Homogeneity of variance was confirmed using Levene’s test. Group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Identification of key diagnostic genes

Eight machine learning model algorithms revealed that “RF” had good predictive performance (AUC value = 0.958), which was selected as the optimal machine learning model (Table 2). Next, the three feature genes, EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGFA, identified using “RF” were obtained as candidate diagnostic genes. Molecular docking

After the active components of the key diagnostic genes were acquired, four active components of EPAS1 were obtained (Fig. 4E), namely, asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester l. In addition, a molecular docking approach was employed where proteins corresponding to the EPAS1 acted as receptors interacting with the four active components: asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester l. These demonstrated affinity toward EPAS1 with respective binding energies of − 8.05 kcal/mol, − 7.471 kcal/mol, − 8.737 kcal/mol, and − 7.360 kcal/mol, respectively (Figs. 5A-D and Table 3). Notably, while deacetyl asperulosidic acid exhibited the most favorable docking score, molecular dynamics simulation-based MM/GBSA analysis revealed that asperuloside and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester displayed comparable or slightly more favorable binding free energies (− 35.22 and − 35.26 kcal/mol, respectively) compared to deacetyl asperulosidic acid (− 27.78 kcal/mol), suggesting that docking scores alone may not fully capture the dynamic binding behavior. These complementary computational approaches collectively support the multi-target binding potential of PR constituents to EPAS1. Discussion

This study aimed to elucidate the antidepressant mechanisms of Psychotria rubra (PR) through an integrative approach combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vivo validation in a CUMS-induced mouse model. The key findings are: (1) EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGF-A were identified as candidate ferroptosis-related diagnostic genes in depression; (2) multiple PR active ingredients, particularly asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid, demonstrated favorable binding affinities to EPAS1 in both docking and molecular dynamics analyses; (3) among the tested PR compounds, Procyanidin B1 showed the most potent antidepressant-like effects in vivo, comparable to fluoxetine; and (4) PR active ingredients significantly ameliorated depressive-like behaviors while reducing hippocampal neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis markers. Depression is a common mental disorder characterized by persistently low mood, reduced interest, and lack of energy. In severe cases, it may affect an individual's cognitive, behavioral, and physiological functions (Mccarron et al. 2021). Our integrative analyses suggest that PR may exert antidepressant effects, potentially involving modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways with EPAS1 as a candidate target. In addition, Procyanidin B1 demonstrated the most robust antidepressant-like activity among the tested compounds, consistent with its reported anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties.

Ferroptosis is a form of programmed cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and its abnormal activation may contribute to the pathology of depression (Liang et al. 2025). Mechanisms related to ferroptosis, such as oxidative stress imbalance, neuronal damage, and neuroinflammation, could be implicated in the occurrence and development of depression (Feng et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2024). This evidence, supporting the effects of ferroptosis on the development of depression, is consistent with our findings. In the present study, the ferroptosis score was higher in the depression group than in the healthy group, suggesting that ferroptosis may be a novel target for the prevention and treatment of depression.

We used a combination of machine learning and WGCNA and identified three feature genes using “RF” as candidate diagnostic genes, namely EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGFA. These genes were significantly and positively correlated with both depression and ferroptosis scores. EPAS1, also known as HIF-2α, regulates the transition from acute to chronic hypoxia, subsequently affecting the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (Li et al. 2015). Elevated levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase are associated with major depressive disorder (Inserra et al. 2019). In addition, Gao et al. reported that EPAS1 could be a critical biomarker for predicting the overall survival in patients with depression (Gao et al. 2025). MAPK14 is a member of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family that plays a crucial regulatory role in cellular stress responses, inflammatory signal transduction, cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis (Madkour et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2025). MAPK14 coordinates the cellular response to stress and inflammation and plays a significant role in mediating neuronal cell death (Gu et al. 2022). An appropriate level of MAPK14 is crucial for neuronal survival and function and for maintaining the health and plasticity of ischemic neurons (Alam and Scheper 2016). In addition, VEGFA mRNA levels in the whole blood of patients with depression are higher than those in controls, especially in patients with psychotic unipolar depression (Ryan and Mcloughlin 2018), which is in line with the results of this study. Cui et al. have demonstrated the involvement of miRNA-27a in the pathology of depression by mediating the expression of VEGFA (Cui et al. 2018). Thus, these findings indicate that EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGFA are promising targets for the diagnosis and treatment of depression.

The active ingredients of PR were explored to determine their therapeutic effects on depression. Seven active PR ingredients were identified: deacetyl asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester l, Procyanidin B1, asperulosidic acid, Procyanidin B2, Procyanidin C1, and asperuloside. Asperoside is an iridoterpene glycoside with known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects on numerous inflammatory diseases. Yin et al. confirmed that asperuloside repressed the depression progression via the Wnt3α/GSK-3β pathway (Yin et al. 2024b). Morinda officinalisHow can accelerate immune function in depression treatment, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid is the primary component of Morinda officinalisHow (Zhang et al. 2018). In addition, Qu et al. reported that deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester, an antidepressant, can pass through the blood–brain barrier and have targeted distribution with complete pharmacokinetics in the brain (Qu et al. 2014b). Moreover, the ROC curves revealed that EPAS1 had the highest AUC value and therefore it was considered a crucial diagnostic gene. We demonstrated that asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester l interacted with EPAS1. Moreover, the molecular docking analysis highlighted the effectiveness of EPAS1 docking with asperuloside, asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid, and deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester l, further corroborating these assertions. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations validated the predictions obtained from molecular docking and UPLC-Q-TOF–MS identification of these active ingredients within PR, solidifying their therapeutic relevance. Further studies validated that PR may alleviate depression progression by modulating EPAS1 expression.

Subsequently, we established a murine model of depression to further evaluate the effects of PR’s major bioactive compounds on depressive-like behaviors, hippocampal inflammation, and ferroptosis. Six compounds were identified: deacetyl asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester, Procyanidin B1, asperulosidic acid, Procyanidin B2, and Procyanidin C1. Notably, asperulosidic acid was found to be a hydrolysis product of aucubin during extraction and isolation, precluding definitive attribution of biological activity to either compound. To resolve this ambiguity, we selected deacetyl asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester, Procyanidin B1, Procyanidin B2, and Procyanidin C1 for in vivo validation. Our results demonstrated that most compounds alleviated depressive-like behaviors in mice, as evidenced by increased sucrose preference, enhanced center entries in the open-field test, and reduced immobility time in the tail suspension and forced swim tests. Among these, Procyanidin B1 exhibited the most potent antidepressant effects, closely resembling the therapeutic efficacy of the positive control fluoxetine.

Ferroptosis is driven by ROS, whose accumulation initiates lipid peroxidation, leading to membrane damage and cell death. GSH, a critical antioxidant, suppresses lipid peroxidation via glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-mediated reduction of lipid hydroperoxides, thereby counteracting ferroptosis (Li et al. 2022). MDA, a terminal product of lipid peroxidation, exacerbates membrane disruption and oxidative stress, further promoting ferroptotic progression (Arzuk and Armagan 2024). SOD indirectly modulates ROS homeostasis by converting superoxide (O₂⁻) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). However, excess H₂O₂, in the presence of Fe2⁺, undergoes Fenton reactions to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, which amplify lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis (Gan et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025a, b). These molecules collectively regulate redox balance, and their dysregulation is central to ferroptosis. In vivo studies revealed that the five PR-derived compounds enhanced SOD activity and GSH levels while reducing MDA and ROS concentrations. Combined with their inhibitory effects on Fe2⁺ accumulation, these findings indicate that PR’s active constituents suppress ferroptosis in the hippocampus of depressed mice. Notably, Procyanidin B1 demonstrated superior anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic efficacy, paralleling the effects of fluoxetine.

This study further revealed that PR alleviates depression, potentially through modulating EPAS1 expression. Collectively, the five active compounds (deacetyl asperulosidic acid, deacetyl asperulosidic acid methyl ester, Procyanidin B1, Procyanidin B2, and Procyanidin C1) mitigated disease progression in depressive mice, with Procyanidin B1 showing the most robust therapeutic profile, closely mirroring fluoxetine's efficacy.

It should be noted that the molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations presented in this study were specifically designed to predict direct interactions with EPAS1, which was identified as a top diagnostic gene for ferroptosis‑related depression via machine learning and WGCNA. Accordingly, only compounds that showed favorable binding energies toward EPAS1 (i.e., the iridoid glycosides, including deacetyl asperulosidic acid and asperuloside) were highlighted in that section. In contrast, Procyanidin B1, despite exhibiting the most robust antidepressant‑like effects in vivo, did not show particularly strong docking scores with EPAS1. This discrepancy is not unexpected, as Procyanidins are known to exert neuroprotective and anti‑ferroptotic effects through multiple EPAS1‑independent mechanisms, such as activation of the Nrf2/GPX4 axis, direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species, and inhibition of NF‑κB‑mediated neuroinflammation. Therefore, the identification of Procyanidin B1 as the most efficacious compound in the CUMS model is based on its overall in vivo performance, not on its binding affinity to EPAS1. Our study does not claim that Procyanidin B1 acts through EPAS1; rather, it suggests that different active ingredients of PR may contribute to depression relief via distinct or synergistic pathways. Future studies should systematically investigate the direct molecular targets of Procyanidin B1, for example using thermal proteome profiling or chemical proteomics, to fully decipher its mechanism of action.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the bioinformatics analysis was performed on peripheral blood transcriptome datasets, whereas the in vivo validation focused on hippocampal markers. While blood-based gene expression signatures can reflect systemic changes in depression, the translational relationship between blood-derived targets and hippocampal outcomes requires cautious interpretation. Future studies should validate EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGFA expression in hippocampal tissue. Second, different routes of administration (intraperitoneal for PR compounds versus oral for fluoxetine) may affect bioavailability. Third, the small training set size (n = 22) for machine learning raises concerns about overfitting despite cross-validation. Future studies should address these limitations through EPAS1 expression validation, canonical ferroptosis markers, dose–response analyses, pharmacokinetic profiling, and investigation of downstream signaling pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, this study integrates network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vivo experiments in a CUMS-induced mouse model to explore the potential antidepressant effects of Psychotria rubra (PR) and its major bioactive compounds. Our findings show that PR-derived compounds, particularly Procyanidin B1, ameliorate depressive-like behaviors, reduce neuroinflammation, oxidative damage, and ferroptosis-related markers in the hippocampus of depressed mice. Machine learning and correlation analyses suggest that EPAS1, MAPK14, and VEGFA may serve as potential diagnostic or mechanistic targets, with EPAS1 showing the strongest association. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations further indicate that deacetyl asperulosidic acid and other iridoids could bind to EPAS1. However, these conclusions are primarily based on associative and preclinical evidence. The proposed molecular mechanisms, including the direct modulation of EPAS1 by PR components and the causal role of ferroptosis in mediating the antidepressant effects, require further validation using target-specific knockouts, chemical inhibitors, or clinical samples. Moreover, the translational relevance of the observed effects to human depression remains to be established. Therefore, while this study provides a strong rationale and testable hypotheses for future research, the current evidence should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. Future studies should focus on direct target engagement, causal pathway analysis, and evaluation in more advanced preclinical models or clinical settings.
