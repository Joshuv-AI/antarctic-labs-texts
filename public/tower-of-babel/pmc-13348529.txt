Diagnostic value of miR-148b-3p in left ventricular hypertrophy patients with hypertension
Authors: Kai Xie, Chunjin Lin, Yu Wang, Weiling Huang, Xianwei Tian, Minna Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore the expression characteristics, diagnostic value, and regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in hypertensive patients with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), providing preliminary evidence for mechanism research and clinical intervention.Methods108 patients with hypertension, 105 patients with hypertension-related LVH, and 112 healthy controls were enrolled. Serum miR-148b-3p levels were detected by RT-qPCR. ROC curve analysis evaluated its diagnostic value, and logistic regression identified risk factors for hypertension with LVH. H9c2 cell experiments and Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy models were used to verify miR-148b-3p’s functional role via CCK-8 (cell viability) and ELISA (ANP/BNP levels).ResultsSerum miR-148b-3p was higher in the hypertension with LVH group than in the hypertension and healthy controls. miR-148b-3p could effectively distinguish hypertensive patients with LVH (AUC = 0.821, 95%CI: 0.761–0.882). Additionally, elevated miR-148b-3p expression emerged as an independent risk factor for LVH and was strongly positively correlated with LVMI. In cellular experiments, overexpressing miR-148b-3p led to reduced cell proliferation and increased ANP and BNP expression; in contrast, the miR-148b-3p inhibitor reversed the above effects and alleviated Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy. Mechanistically, miR-148b-3p promotes myocardial hypertrophy by directly targeting KLF4.ConclusionsmiR-148b-3p is highly expressed in hypertension with LVH, has diagnostic potential, and promotes myocardial hypertrophy by targeting KLF4 and regulating cell function, serving as a novel molecular marker and therapeutic target.

Background

Hypertension, as a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases characterized by persistent abnormal elevation of blood pressure, can lead to chronic damage of target organs such as myocardial hypertrophy [1, 2]. In response to physiological and pathological stress, the heart exhibits myocardial hypertrophy as a form of adaptation [3]. During the progression of heart failure, myocardial hypertrophy is a crucial pathological change [4]. In the early stage of increased load, myocardial hypertrophy helps maintain cardiac output; however, as myocardial hypertrophy progresses, it will eventually enter a decompensated stage, triggering severe cardiovascular diseases and even death [5, 6]. Research has indicated that myocardial hypertrophy is a risk factor for the occurrence and death of cardiovascular diseases, and the severity of its condition plays an important predictive role in progressive heart diseases [6]. Although multiple signaling pathways regulating myocardial hypertrophy have been identified in clinical research, their molecular mechanisms still require in-depth exploration. Therefore, exploring new regulatory genes and therapeutic targets is of great significance for the prophylaxis and intervention of heart diseases.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are widely recognized to be capable of mediating myocardial hypertrophy when their expression or function is abnormal [7]. For example: miR-212 is significantly upregulated in animal and cellular models of myocardial hypertrophy, which can directly promote cardiomyocyte hypertrophy [8]; the miR-27a family (including miR-27a-3p) is upregulated in Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy models, and its inhibitors can alleviate myocardial hypertrophy and improve electrical remodeling [9, 10]; let-7a exerts an anti-hypertrophic effect, whose expression is decreased in Ang II-induced models [11]. These studies indicate that miRNAs play a bidirectional regulatory role in the pathological process of myocardial hypertrophy, either as “pro-hypertrophic” or “anti-hypertrophic” factors, and thus are potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Among the numerous miRNAs associated with myocardial hypertrophy, the research clues of miR-148b-3p are particularly noteworthy. Earlier studies that employed miRNA microarray analysis observed a notable elevation in the level of miR-148b-3p in the myocardial tissue of patients diagnosed with HCM [12]. Further verification using the public GSE60292 dataset showed that the level of this miRNA in myocardial hypertrophy-related samples was 3.98 times that in the normal control group [13]. In animal experiments, miR-148b-3p was also identified as a significantly upregulated differential miRNA in rats with swimming exercise-induced myocardial hypertrophy [14]. Moreover miR-148b-3p is also related to other pathological processes of cardiovascular diseases. For instance, it is positively correlated with the stroke risk score in patients with atrial fibrillation [15], and its overexpression can reduce the viability of cardiomyocytes treated with ischemia-reperfusion (H/R) and promote cell apoptosis [16]. These observations imply that miR-148b-3p may be involved in pathological cardiac remodeling by regulating cardiomyocyte survival and function. However, current studies on the expression characteristics, clinical diagnostic value, and specific regulatory mechanism of miR-148b-3p in hypertensive patients with myocardial hypertrophy are still relatively insufficient, which provides a clear direction for this study.

This study focuses on the expression and diagnostic value of miR-148b-3p in hypertension with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). Furthermore, combined with H9c2 cell experiments and the Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy model, it explores the regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in myocardial hypertrophy, so as to provide a preliminary basis for mechanism research and clinical intervention.

Methods

Participants

From January 2023 to March 2024, 108 patients with hypertension and 105 patients diagnosed with hypertension with LVH, all admitted to Yichang Central People’s Hospital, were selected as the study participants. Inclusion Criteria: (1) Diagnosis conforms to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension [17]; (2) Left ventricular mass index (LVMI) must meet the following requirements: > 115 g/m² for male and > 95 g/m² for female; (3) Blood glucose and electrolytes levels are within normal ranges. Notably, lipid profiles (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C) were not used as exclusion criteria, as these parameters were analyzed as potential metabolic indicators associated with disease progression. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Patients with restrictive cardiomyopathy or secondary hypertension; (2) Patients with complicated moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease or abnormal thyroid function; (3) Patients with complicated malignant arrhythmia or congenital heart disease. Furthermore, 112 healthy participants in the corresponding period were chosen to serve as the controls. The enrollment criteria for the control group were: no underlying diseases such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, or diabetes; normal liver and kidney function; and LVMI within the normal range as shown by echocardiography.

A 5 mL sample of fasting venous blood was collected from each subject. The blood samples were placed in vacuum blood collection tubes, and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm. Serum was obtained by centrifugation and stored at -80 °C. Besides, general information of the participants (age, gender, body mass index) was collected through questionnaires and medical records. Relevant biochemical indicators (TC, TG, HDL-C, etc.), blood pressure indicators (SBP, DBP), and ultrasonic parameters (LVMI, LVEDD, LVESD) were detected using an automatic biochemical analyzer, an electronic sphygmomanometer, and a cardiac ultrasound machine, respectively. Echocardiographic examinations were performed using a Philips EPIQ 7 C ultrasound system with a 2.5-5 MHz phased-array transducer. LVMI was calculated in accordance with the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE): LV mass was derived using the cube function formula (LV mass = 0.8 × 1.04 × [(LVIDd + IVSTd + PWTd)³ - LVIDd³] + 0.6), and then normalized to body surface area (BSA) to obtain LVMI. Intra-observer and inter-observer variability assessments were performed to ensure measurement reliability: 30 randomly selected echocardiographic images were reanalyzed by the same observer (intra-observer) and a second independent observer (inter-observer) who were blinded to the study groups. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for LVMI was 0.92 (95%CI: 0.85–0.96) for intra-observer variability and 0.89 (95%CI: 0.81–0.94) for inter-observer variability, indicating good reproducibility of the measurements. All participants underwent seated right upper arm blood pressure measurements using a standard electronic sphygmomanometer after at least 15 min of quiet rest. Three consecutive measurements were taken at 5-minute intervals, with the average value serving as the final blood pressure reading (SBP, DBP).

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (No. 2023051, Date:2023.01.09). Written informed consent has been obtained from the participants involved.

Cell culture and transfected

The rat cardiomyocyte cell line H9c2, purchased from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, was cultured in DMEM medium, and maintained in an incubator under routine culture conditions. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected for experiments.

Using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent, miR-148b-3p mimic, miR-148b-3p inhibitor, and the negative control group were transfected into H9c2 cells. After transfection, the cells were cultured for 48 h. Brief procedures: First, H9c2 cells were seeded into each well of the culture plate. Then, the prepared transfection mixture was added to each well. After 48 h of culture, the cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced myocardial hypertrophy model

For the transfected H9c2 cells, except for the blank control group, 1 µM Ang II was added to the other groups, and the cells were further cultured for 24 h to establish the cardiomyocyte hypertrophy model. After model establishment, miR-148b-3p inhibitor and NC inhibitor were transfected following the above-mentioned method.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

A total of 200 µL serum was used as input volume. Total RNA was extracted strictly following the TRIzol reagent protocol: 1 mL TRIzol LS reagent was added to serum samples, vortexed vigorously for 15 s, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min; 200 µL chloroform was added, shaken vigorously for 15 s, and incubated at room temperature for 3 min; centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C; the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol, and incubated at -20 °C overnight; centrifuged at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol (prepared with RNase-free water); the RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in 20 µL RNase-free water.

The RNA concentration and purity of were detected with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 and A260/A230 ratios ≥ 1.5 considered qualified. RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, with clear 18 S and 28 S rRNA bands indicating no significant degradation. Qualified total RNA was converted into cDNA via reverse transcription, with the PrimeScript Reverse Transcription Kit employed for the process. Subsequently, RT-qPCR detection was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix Kit. The 20 µL reaction system consisted of 10 µL SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µmol/L), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µmol/L), 2 µL cDNA template, and 7.2 µL RNase-free water. With U6 as the internal reference gene, the relative expression of miR-148b-3p was calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Cell viability assay

H9c2 cells after transfection or Ang II treatment were seeded into a 96-well plate. After 24 h of culture, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by further incubation for 2 h. The OD value was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

ELISA

The levels of ANP and BNP in H9c2 cells were detected according to the instructions of the ELISA kit. The brief procedures are as follows: First, centrifuge to collect the cell supernatant and perform appropriate dilution. Subsequently, add the primary antibody and enzyme-labeled secondary antibody to the standard wells and sample wells sequentially. Terminate the experiment by adding the substrate solution and stop solution in sequence, and finally measure the OD value.

Bioinformatics analysis

Potential target genes of miR-148b-3p were predicted using multiple databases (miRDB, EvmiRNA, miRmap, miRWalk, and TarBase). The predicted target genes were subjected to intersection analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis via the STRING database to construct a PPI network, with the confidence score threshold set at > 0.4. The network was visualized, and node degrees were calculated to identify hub genes.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT) 3’-UTR sequence of KLF4 containing the predicted binding site of miR-148b-3p and the mutant (MUT) sequence with the binding site mutated were synthesized and cloned into the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector. H9c2 cells were seeded into 24-well plates and co-transfected with miR-148b-3p mimic (or NC mimic) and WT-KLF4 (or MUT-KLF4). Finally, the luciferase activity was detected.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 23.0 statistical software and GraphPad 9.0 software were used for data processing and graphing. Two-group comparisons used independent samples t-test; multiple-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA. Diagnostic value was determined through ROC curve analysis; Pearson correlation analysis was applied for correlation analysis; and logistic regression analysis was performed to assess relevant risk factors. P-value < 0.05.

Expression and diagnostic value of miR-148b-3p

The results showed that the level of serum miR-148b-3p in the hypertension with LVH group was higher than that in the hypertension and the healthy controls (P < 0.000001, Fig. 1A). ROC curve analysis revealed that the AUC of miR-148b-3p for distinguishing patients with simple hypertension from healthy controls was 0.870 (95%CI: 0.818–0.922), with a sensitivity of 80.56% (95%CI: 72.10%-86.92%), a specificity of 89.29% (95%CI: 82.20%-93.76%), a positive predictive value (PPV) of 87.90%, a negative predictive value (NPV) of 82.80%, and a corresponding cut‑off value of 1.126 (P < 0.000001, Fig. 1B). In addition, the AUC of miR-148b-3p for distinguishing hypertension with LVH from simple hypertension was 0.821 (95%CI: 0.761–0.882), with a sensitivity of 76.19% (95% CI: 67.21%-83.32%), and a specificity of 88.89% (95% CI: 81.58%-93.53%), a PPV of 86.90%, a NPV of 79.30%, and a corresponding cut‑off value of 1.77 (P < 0.000001, Fig. 1C). Results of Pearson correlation analysis indicated that in patients with hypertensive LVH, a higher level of miR-148b-3p was associated with a larger LVMI (r = 0.686, P < 0.000001, Fig. 1D).

Effect of miR-148b-3p on the expression and function of H9c2 cells

The level of miR-148b-3p in H9c2 cells of the miR-148b-3p mimic group was increased, while that in the miR-148b-3p inhibitor group was decreased (P < 0.000001, Fig. 2A), indicating successful transfection. CCK-8 assay indicated that miR-148b-3p reduced the OD value (P < 0.000001, Fig. 2B). ELISA results demonstrated that the miR-148b-3p mimic effectively increased the levels of ANP and BNP, while the miR-148b-3p inhibitor exerted the opposite effect (P < 0.000001, Fig. 2C-D).

Regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in the Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy model

The level of miR-148b-3p increased with the prolongation of Ang II treatment time (P < 0.004417, Fig. 3A). After transfection with miR-148b-3p inhibitor, the relative expression of miR-148b-3p in Ang II-treated cells was decreased (P < 0.000001, Fig. 3B). The OD value of H9c2 cells in the Ang II treatment group was lower than that in control group, while after transfection with miR-148b-3p inhibitor, the OD value of cells in the Ang II treatment group was increased (P < 0.000001, Fig. 3C). Moreover, the levels of ANP and BNP in H9c2 cells treated with Ang II were increased; in contrast, after transfection with miR-148b-3p inhibitor, the levels of ANP and BNP were decreased (P < 0.000001, Fig. 3D-E).

miR-148b-3p regulates Ang II-induced myocardial hypertrophy by targeting KLF4

To clarify the molecular mechanism by which miR-148b-3p regulates myocardial hypertrophy, potential target genes of miR-148b-3p were first predicted using multiple databases, yielding a total of 188 intersecting genes (Fig. 4A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these genes were enriched in signaling pathways closely associated with myocardial hypertrophy, such as the PI3K-Akt, mTOR, and FoxO signaling pathways (Fig. 4B), suggesting that miR-148b-3p may be involved in the progression of myocardial hypertrophy by regulating these pathways. PPI network analysis was performed on the intersecting genes, leading to the identification of 14 core hub genes with high node degrees. Combined with previous studies, KLF4 was selected as a key candidate target gene (Fig. 4C). The binding site between miR-148b-3p and KLF4 was predicted using the ENCORI database (Fig. 4D) and verified by dual-luciferase reporter assay. Compared with the control group, the luciferase activity was significantly reduced in the group co-transfected with KLF4-WT and miR-148b-3p mimic (P < 0.000001, Fig. 4E).

In Ang II-treated H9c2 cells, the relative mRNA expression of KLF4 was significantly downregulated compared with the control group; however, transfection with miR-148b-3p inhibitor significantly reversed the Ang II-induced inhibition of KLF4 expression (P < 0.000001, Fig. 4F). To verify the role of KLF4 in miR-148b-3p-mediated cardiac hypertrophy, si-KLF4 was transfected into cells. In Ang II-treated cells, co-transfection with si-KLF4 partially abrogated the promoting effect of miR-148b-3p inhibitor on cell viability (P < 0.000001, Fig. 4G). Furthermore, silencing KLF4 significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of miR-148b-3p inhibitor on Ang II-induced ANP and BNP secretion (P < 0.000001, Fig. 4H-I).

Discussion

As one of the most typical target organ complications of hypertension [18], myocardial hypertrophy can occur in all age groups. Clinically, patients with myocardial hypertrophy present with symptoms such as chest tightness, dizziness, and precordial discomfort; some patients develop severe complications including heart failure and atrial fibrillation, and in severe cases, even death, which is detrimental to individual health [19, 20]. Focusing on miR-148b-3p, this study conducted clinical and cellular experiments to further verify its expression characteristics, diagnostic potential, and regulatory role in hypertensive patients with myocardial hypertrophy, thereby providing new evidence for the mechanism research and clinical intervention of this disease.

In recent years, miRNAs have been validated to perform a key role in cardiovascular diseases, providing a new direction for mechanism research and marker screening of myocardial hypertrophy [21, 22]. For example, studies have focused on miR-208a [23], miR-483-3p [24], and miR-4454 [25]. Regarding miR-148b-3p, the focus of this study, it has also been reported that its expression is upregulated in samples from patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [12, 13]. In the study, experimental results confirmed that miR-148b-3p level is upregulated in patients with hypertension-related LVH, and this trend is consistent with previous reports. As typical blood pressure indicators, SBP and DBP that remain at high levels for a long time can stimulate cardiomyocyte enlargement and myocardial fiber thickening [26]. LDL-C is the primary lipid risk factor for atherosclerosis, and it exerts the most direct and critical impact on myocardial hypertrophy and cardiovascular diseases [27]. TC serves as a barometer reflecting the overall metabolism of all cholesterol in the blood; by indirectly indicating abnormal components, it provides an important reference for cardiovascular disease risk assessment [28, 29]. High levels of TG can exacerbate vascular damage and thrombus risk [30]. In contrast, high levels of HDL-C, known as the “scavenger” of blood vessels, act as a protective factor against cardiovascular diseases [31]. In this study, it was found that there were significant differences in the above indicators among healthy participants, hypertensive patients, and patients with hypertension-related LVH. As a key clinical marker for evaluating LVH [32], LVMI can objectively reflect the presence, severity, and progression of LVH. Meanwhile, it provides critical evidence for risk assessment and therapeutic efficacy monitoring of LVH [33, 34]. The present study revealed that miR-148b-3p not only had a significant positive correlation with LVMI, but also that high miR-148b-3p expression was confirmed to be an independent risk factor for LVH in patients with hypertension. This indicates that miR-148b-3p is not merely a “bystander” of the pathological state, but more likely participates in the development of LVH.

To investigate the biological function and molecular mechanism of miR-148b-3p, we first performed bioinformatics analysis of its potential target genes. PPI network analysis identified KLF4 as a hub gene, and in vitro experiments further confirmed that KLF4 is a direct target gene of miR-148b-3p. As a member of the Krüppel-like transcription factor family, KLF4 has been well-documented as a key anti-hypertrophic factor in multiple studies: overexpression of KLF4 in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes inhibits fetal gene expression, protein synthesis, and cell enlargement—three core features of myocardial hypertrophy [35]; its anti-hypertrophic effect is primarily mediated by suppressing the expression and activity of Myocardin, thereby blocking the activation of the fetal gene program that drives pathological cardiac remodeling [36, 37]. Beyond its direct inhibition of hypertrophic signaling, KLF4 also functions as an endogenous anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory factor in hypertensive heart disease, further contributing to the maintenance of cardiac homeostasis [38]. Additionally, OTUD7B has been reported to stabilize KLF4 expression through deubiquitination, which in turn activates downstream signaling pathways to inhibit myocardial hypertrophy [39], highlighting the importance of KLF4 stability in its anti-hypertrophic function. Consistent with these previous findings, our cellular experiments confirmed that overexpression of miR-148b-3p downregulates KLF4 expression, inhibits H9c2 cell proliferation, and upregulates the expression of ANP and BNP—marker genes of myocardial hypertrophy [40]. Silencing KLF4 abrogates the protective effects of the miR-148b-3p inhibitor, indicating that the regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in myocardial hypertrophy is dependent on KLF4. Collectively, these results demonstrate that miR-148b-3p disrupts the endogenous anti-hypertrophic barrier formed by KLF4, thereby promoting the development and progression of myocardial hypertrophy. The mechanistic experiments in H9c2 cells confirmed that miR-148b-3p promotes the expression of hypertrophy markers (ANP/BNP) and regulates cell viability, which supports the proposed miR-148b-3p/KLF4 regulatory pathway. However, it should be noted that H9c2 cells are a rat cardiomyoblast cell line that exhibits phenotypic differences from primary adult human cardiomyocytes (e.g., incomplete differentiation), and thus represent a simplified in vitro model. Additionally, reduced cell viability/proliferation is not a classical endpoint of myocardial hypertrophy; the observed changes in H9c2 cell proliferation mainly reflect the functional regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in cardiomyocyte-related cells, rather than a direct hallmark of hypertrophy. Therefore, these in vitro results should be interpreted as mechanistic support for the pro-hypertrophic role of miR-148b-3p, rather than direct evidence of clinical causality.

The findings of this study hold several important clinical implications. First, serum miR-148b-3p demonstrates significant diagnostic potential for LVH in hypertensive patients, with an AUC of 0.821 (95%CI: 0.761–0.882), sensitivity of 76.19%, and specificity of 88.89%. This suggests that miR-148b-3p could serve as a novel non-invasive biomarker to facilitate detection of high-risk patients. Second, the strong positive correlation between miR-148b-3p and LVMI (r = 0.686, P < 0.0001), combined with its status as an independent risk factor (OR = 4.698, 95%CI: 2.315–9.535), indicates its utility in risk stratification for hypertensive patients. Third, our cellular experiments revealing that miR-148b-3p promotes cardiomyocyte hypertrophy by targeting KLF4 suggest that inhibiting miR-148b-3p may represent a promising therapeutic strategy, providing preliminary experimental evidence for targeted interventions against myocardial hypertrophy. Finally, given miR-148b-3p’s involvement in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation and hypertrophy marker expression (ANP and BNP), longitudinal monitoring of serum miR-148b-3p levels may help assess disease progression and therapeutic response in clinical practice.

Notably, in light of the aforementioned clinical implications, the potential regulatory effect of antihypertensive drugs on serum miR-148b-3p levels has attracted considerable attention, which is crucial for confirming the causal association between hypertension and the upregulation of miR-148b-3p. Existing studies have indirectly indicated that antihypertensive agents (e.g., renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors and calcium channel blockers) can modulate the expression of various cardiovascular-related microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby reversing pathological myocardial remodeling and reducing target organ damage. For example, RAAS inhibitors (losartan and enalapril) have been demonstrated to downregulate the expression of pro-hypertrophic miRNAs (e.g., miR-27a-3p and miR-212) [9, 41] and upregulate that of anti-hypertrophic miRNAs (e.g., let-7a) [11] in hypertensive patients and animal models. Given that our study has confirmed that Ang II, the core effector molecule of the RAAS, can upregulate miR-148b-3p expression in a time-dependent manner in H9c2 cells, it is reasonable to speculate that RAAS inhibitors, as first-line antihypertensive drugs, may reduce serum miR-148b-3p levels in hypertensive patients by inhibiting the Ang II/miR-148b-3p/KLF4 axis, yet direct evidence for the regulatory effect of such drugs on miR-148b-3p is still lacking at present.

Despite the valuable findings of this study, several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional design only identifies the association between miR-148b-3p and existing LVH, precluding causal inferences, validation of its diagnostic value, and analysis of dynamic changes in miR-148b-3p during disease progression or its response to antihypertensive treatment; further verification via longitudinal follow-up is required. Second, the use of rat-derived H9c2 cells (which exhibit phenotypic differences from primary cardiomyocytes) limits the extrapolation of in vitro results to clinical settings. Future studies should employ primary cardiomyocytes (e.g., neonatal rat ventricular myocytes, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes) to more accurately validate the regulatory role of miR-148b-3p in myocardial hypertrophy. Additionally, reduced cell viability/proliferation is not a classical hypertrophy endpoint, and the functional changes observed in H9c2 cells should be interpreted as mechanistic support rather than direct clinical relevance. Third, direct data on the regulatory effect of antihypertensive drugs on miR-148b-3p are currently lacking, and future prospective clinical intervention studies (e.g., dynamic detection of serum miR-148b-3p levels pre- and post-antihypertensive treatment) and in vivo animal experiments are needed to verify such effects and underlying mechanisms—confirmation that drugs reduce miR-148b-3p associated with LVMI improvement would further validate the causal link and provide a novel pharmacodynamic index. Fourth, U6 may not be the optimal reference gene for serum miRNA quantification; future research should adopt multiple reference genes or exogenous spike-in controls to achieve more robust standardization. Fifth, this study did not directly measure cell size or protein synthesis. Future studies should supplement experiments such as phalloidin staining for cell surface area quantification and ³H-leucine incorporation assay for protein synthesis detection to further enhance the reliability of the results. Sixth, the single-center design and limited sample size restrict the generalizability of the findings, necessitating multicenter studies with larger cohorts to validate the diagnostic value and regulatory role of miR-148b-3p. Seventh, important clinical confounders, including hypertension duration, detailed antihypertensive therapy history, smoking status, and comprehensive renal function data, were not systematically collected or reported during patient enrollment. These factors may potentially affect miR-148b-3p expression and the development of LVH, and their absence limits the completeness of our risk factor analysis. Future studies should incorporate these variables to better control potential confounding effects.

Conclusions

In summary, through dual verification by clinical and cellular experiments, this study confirms that miR-148b-3p is highly expressed in hypertensive patients with LVH and exhibits favorable diagnostic potential. Moreover, miR-148b-3p promotes LVH progression by directly targeting KLF4 and regulating hypertrophy marker gene expression and cell proliferation. Therefore, it shows potential as a novel molecular marker and provides a preliminary basis for exploring therapeutic targets.
