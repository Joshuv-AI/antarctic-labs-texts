Roles of polysaccharide charge and viscosity in soy protein–polysaccharide stabilized high internal phase emulsions for plant-based patties
Authors: Ying Chen, Jiamei Yu, Lin Zhang, Qingqing Xu, Yu Du, Yongjun Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soy protein isolate (SPI)–polysaccharide-stabilized high internal phase emulsions (HIPEs) are promising fat alternatives for meat analogs. However, the distinct contributions of polysaccharide charge and viscosity to emulsion formation, stabilization and meat analog quality remain unclear. This study systematically investigated five polysaccharides with different charges and viscosities, namely cationic chitosan (CS), neutral locust bean gum (LBG) and guar gum (GG), and anionic xanthan gum (XG) and high methoxyl pectin (HP), in modulating SPI structure, HIPE performance, and plant-based patty quality. Results showed that polysaccharide charge dominated SPI-polysaccharide interactions and dictated SPI conformational remodeling. Neutral polysaccharides interacted with SPI through hydrogen bonding/hydrophobic interactions, significantly increasing the β-sheet content (GG: from 35.91% to 43.23%), and achieving the smallest initial droplet size through a high interfacial adsorption rate (GG: 6.81 μm). During emulsion stabilization and flavor retention, viscosity was the predominant factor. High-viscosity XG and GG excelled in inhibiting droplet aggregation, reducing gravitational separation, and maximizing volatile flavor retention under both heating and storage. At the application stage, viscosity predominantly influenced the hardness of patties. This study reveals the division of labor between polysaccharide charge and viscosity across different performance dimensions of HIPEs, guiding polysaccharide selection for tailoring HIPEs in meat analogs.

Introduction

Meat is a primary source of high-quality protein, but livestock production is resource-intensive and environmentally unsustainable (Wen et al., 2023). To address sustainability concerns, plant-based meat analogs have attracted increasing interest in recent years. Lipids are a key component of meat analogs, contributing to unique sensory properties (e.g., aroma, smoothness and juiciness) (Chen et al., 2023). However, the direct application of traditional vegetable oils tends to induce texture defects in meat analogs. In response, high internal phase emulsions (HIPEs) are regarded as an ideal fat alternative to conventional vegetable oils.

HIPEs are typically formulated with an oil phase volume fraction of 0.74 or higher. In such systems, the densely packed droplets create a highly viscous and self-supporting gel-like structure (Hong et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that HIPEs offer multiple benefits to meat products, including enhanced water holding capacity, improved texture, and higher acceptability, owing to the increased mechanical robustness of the gel network resulting from interactions between the emulsion droplet interfaces and the surrounding protein matrix (Chen et al., 2024). Generally, HIPE stabilization relies on amphiphilic biopolymers, with soy protein being widely used due to its high nutritional value and low cost. However, pure protein-stabilized HIPEs are susceptible to droplet coalescence and structural breakdown under thermal and mechanical stresses, limiting their practical application in meat analogs (Li et al., 2025). To overcome these limitations, numerous studies have employed a combination of proteins and polysaccharides, wherein proteins adsorb at the oil–water interface to reduce surface tension, while polysaccharides modulate the interfacial structure, thereby preventing droplet coalescence and significantly enhancing system stability (Zhang et al., 2026). This finding is corroborated by Zhu et al., who reported that emulsions co-stabilized by soy protein isolate (SPI) and cellulose nanofibers exhibited markedly superior stability under high-temperature, low-pH, and high-salt conditions compared to those stabilized by protein alone (Zhu et al., 2025). Despite these promising results, the efficacy of protein–polysaccharide co-stabilization varies considerably with polysaccharide type. Among the various physicochemical properties of polysaccharides, charge characteristics and viscosity are widely recognized as the two core factors governing the performance of such HIPEs.

Polysaccharides are classified as cationic, neutral or anionic based on their charge distribution and density, and these charge properties fundamentally determine their modes of interaction with proteins. Specifically, cationic (positively charged) polysaccharides mainly associate with SPI through electrostatic attraction, neutral polysaccharides primarily through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, whereas anionic polysaccharides experience electrostatic repulsion (Han et al., 2024). These intermolecular interactions dictate the formation of SPI–polysaccharide complexes and subsequently regulate protein conformation and interfacial adsorption behavior (Zhang et al., 2026). Meanwhile, high-viscosity polysaccharide solutions can generate a macromolecular crowding effect that restricts molecular diffusion and shifts protein folding equilibria toward more compact conformations (Hu et al., 2020). However, unlike direct binding interactions, the crowding effect influences protein behavior indirectly by modifying the local physicochemical environment, rather than through specific polysaccharide–protein association. This mechanistic distinction led us to hypothesize that polysaccharide charge plays a dominant role in SPI–polysaccharide interactions. In parallel to crowding effect, polysaccharide viscosity also governs the rheological properties of the continuous phase, which critically affects droplet mobility, coalescence resistance and molecular diffusion. To date, most existing studies have treated these two factors (charge and viscosity) as intertwined variables, leaving a critical gap in understanding which factor plays the dominant role in different performance dimensions, such as emulsion formation, resistance to environmental stresses including heating and storage and flavor retention. This ambiguity impedes the rational selection of polysaccharides for targeted applications.

Based on the above considerations, five polysaccharides were selected as representative structural models with distinct charge characteristics and viscosity properties. Chitosan (CS), locust bean gum (LBG), guar gum (GG), xanthan gum (XG) and high-methoxy pectin (HP) were chosen because they represent three major charge categories, including cationic, neutral, and anionic polysaccharides, while exhibiting different viscosity characteristics. Specifically, CS represents cationic polysaccharides, LBG and GG represent neutral polysaccharides, whereas XG and HP represent anionic polysaccharides containing negatively charged groups. Notably, although high-methoxy pectin has a high degree of methyl esterification, the remaining unesterified carboxyl groups confer a net negative charge, classifying it as an anionic polysaccharide. Detailed structural information of these polysaccharides is summarized in Table S1. Using these model polysaccharides, this study aimed to determine which factor (charge and viscosity) is predominant in governing: (1) HIPE formation; (2) the multi-dimensional stability of HIPEs (thermal stability, storage stability and flavor retention), and (3) the quality attributes of meat analogs incorporating these HIPEs. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical basis for the rational selection of polysaccharides for HIPE formulations in plant-based meat applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Soy protein isolate (SPI, protein content >90%) was obtained from Wilmar R&D Centre (Shanghai, China). Textured soy protein was sourced from Bodhisu Food Co., Ltd. (Hongkong, China). Chitosan (CS), locust bean gum (LBG), guar gum (GG), xanthan gum (XG) and high methoxyl pectin (HP, DE > 74%) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rapeseed oil was obtained from a local supermarket. Transglutaminase (TGase, 120 U/g) was provided by Taixing dongsheng Bio-tech Co., Ltd. (Taixing, China). Fat-soluble liquid flavor (beef-type aroma) was obtained from Givaudan Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of SPI-polysaccharide complexes

The concentrations of SPI (4%, w/w) and polysaccharides (0.4%, w/w) were selected according to previous studies (Wang et al., 2024) and preliminary experiments, which demonstrated that this formulation produced stable HIPEs with suitable viscosity for homogenization and subsequent characterization. Specifically, SPI (4%, w/w) and polysaccharide (0.4%, w/w) were separately dissolved in deionized water with overnight stirring at 25°C. The resulting solutions were then combined and stirred for 2 h to form complexes. Finally, the SPI-polysaccharide complex was freeze-dried for 48 h to obtain the powder.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectra of the proteins and protein-polysaccharide complexes were collected using a Nicolet IS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Specifically, the sample was mixed with KBr at a 1:100 (w/w) ratio. The mixture was then compressed into flakes for FTIR analysis. The spectral data were acquired over a wavenumber range of 4000 to 500 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Determination of protein secondary structure

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was employed to assess the influence of polysaccharides on the secondary structure of SPI. CD spectra in the far-UV region (190-260 nm) were acquired on a spectropolarimeter (Applied Photophysics Ltd., UK) using a 0.1 cm quartz cuvette, with PBS buffer used for baseline subtraction. The secondary structure composition (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn and random coil) were quantified from the spectra using the CDNN algorithm.

Ultraviolet (UV) spectrum measurement

First, sample solutions with a protein concentration of 0.4 mg/mL were prepared in deionized water. Subsequently, spectral measurements were performed in the wavelength range of 200–400 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV-1900, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with the slit width set to 1 nm. Baseline correction was performed with deionized water prior to measurements.

Fluorescence spectroscopy measurement

Fluorescence spectra of SPI and its polysaccharide complexes were acquired on a Fluoromax-4 spectrofluorometer (HORIBA, Japan) using a 285 nm excitation wavelength, a 290–500 nm emission range, a 5 nm slit width for both excitation and emission, and a sample concentration of 0.4 mg/mL.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity (H0)

Surface hydrophobicity of the samples was determined using the method of Xue et al. (2025), and fluorescence spectra were recorded on a spectrophotometer. Briefly, 20 μL of ANS (8 mmol/L) was mixed into 4 mL of protein-polysaccharide sample solution (0.4 mg/mL), followed by dark incubation for 10 min before analysis. An ANS-ultrapure water solution prepared in the same manner served as the blank. The excitation wavelength and emission scanning wavelength were set at 390 nm and 400–600 nm, respectively, and the maximum fluorescence intensity was expressed as H0.

Particle size, zeta potential and viscosity of protein-polysaccharide solutions

The mean particle size and zeta potential of the SPI–polysaccharide solution were recorded by a ZEN3600 laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Co., Ltd., UK) and a zeta potential analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK), respectively. Prior to measurements, the samples were diluted with deionized water to 0.2 mg/mL for particle size analysis and to 0.1 mg/mL for zeta potential determination. The viscosity was measured at 25°C using an NDJ-5S rotational viscometer (Youyi, Shanghai, China) at a sample concentration of 20 mg/mL.

Preparation of high internal phase emulsion

High internal phase emulsions (HIPEs) were prepared by homogenizing a 25% (v/v) aqueous solution containing 2% protein-polysaccharide complex with 75% (v/v) rapeseed oil at 13000 rpm for 3 min using a high-speed homogenizer (FSH-2A, Jtliangyou, Changzhou, China). Additionally, HIPEs stabilized by SPI were prepared using the identical method to serve as a blank control for evaluating the effect of the polysaccharide on HIPE formation.

Particle size and microstructure of emulsion

The mean particle size of the emulsions was determined using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (BT-2600, Better, Dandong, China). Prior to measurement, the samples were pre-diluted 10-fold with deionized water to facilitate sample introduction. During analysis, the pre-diluted samples were introduced into the circulating dispersion unit of the instrument, where they were further dispersed and diluted with deionized water until the obscuration reached 10%–20%. For microstructural characterization, a B302 optical microscope (Conptec, Chongqing, China) was employed to acquire images at 25°C.

The measurement of the interfacial film thickness

The interfacial film thickness was determined following the method described by Zhao et al. (2026). First, a 0.1 wt% polystyrene latex suspension was prepared using deionized water. Subsequently, 900 μL of protein or protein–polysaccharide complex solution (0.1 mg/mL) was mixed with 100 μL of the latex suspension (0.1 wt%), and the mixture was allowed to equilibrate under a quiescent condition for 30 min to ensure homogeneous adsorption of the components onto the latex particle surfaces. Finally, the particle size of each sample was measured using a laser particle size analyzer, and the interfacial film thickness was calculated according to the following equation.d=da−d02where d represents the interfacial film thickness, da represents the particle size after adsorption, and d0 represents the particle size of the polystyrene latex.

Percentage of adsorbed proteins

The percentage of adsorbed protein was calculated according to the method of Zhang et al., with minor modifications (Zhang et al., 2026). Specifically, the protein-polysaccharide stabilized emulsion was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min. The aqueous phase was then collected using a syringe and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. Subsequently, the protein concentration in the aqueous phase was analyzed using the biuret method. The percentage of adsorbed protein (AP%) was calculated according to the following formula.AP%=C0−CfC0×100Where C0 is the total protein concentration, and Cf is the protein concentration in the aqueous phase after centrifugation.

Stability evaluation of emulsions

The stability of the emulsions was evaluated following the procedure of Ma et al., with minor modifications (Ma et al., 2025). Thermal stability was evaluated by heating the emulsions at 100°C (30 min). For the storage stability test, 20 mL of each emulsion was placed in a sealed glass vial and stored at 25°C for 18 days. After exposure to these stress conditions, the particle size of the samples was measured before and after treatment, and the emulsion stability index (ESI) was calculated using the following equation (Choi et al., 2025). Stability of volatile flavor compounds

Flavor compounds must be incorporated into the oil phase of high internal phase emulsions when used as fat replacers in plant-based meat products to enhance aroma and consumer acceptance. However, these compounds are prone to volatilization loss during cooking or storage. Therefore, the effect of polysaccharide type on the retention of flavor compounds was evaluated. First, the flavor compounds were incorporated into the oil phase, and the emulsion was prepared according to the method described in Section 2.9. The emulsions were then either heat-treated at 100°C for 30 min or stored at 25°C for 18 days. The changes in compound concentrations before and after treatment were subsequently analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME, SPME fiber: 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS) coupled with comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC×GC-MS, QP2020NX, Shimadzu, Japan), with 1,2-dichlorobenzene as the internal standard. The column set consisted of a DB-WAX (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm) in the first dimension and a lower polar DB-17ms (1.2 m × 0.18 mm× 0.18 μm) in the second dimension. Helium (99.999% purity) was applied as carrier gas (1.5 mL/min). The temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature 35°C, held for 2 min, then increased to 160°C at a rate of 4°C/min, and further increased to 240°C at a rate of 6°C/min. The EI ionization mode was employed. The interface temperature was 250°C, the ion source temperature was 230°C, and data were acquired in full scan mode. The modulator cold jet temperature was maintained at 50°C, and the modulation period was 4 s. Preparation of plant-based patties

The preparation process of the patties was as follows: hydrated textured soy protein (TSP, 60% moisture content) was initially ground by a grinder. A mixture comprising the ground TSP (60 g), emulsion [10 mL, approximately 13% (w/w) of the formulation, within the typical fat range (0%–15%) for meat analogs], SPI (6 g), Transglutaminase (0.30 g) and beet-derived plant colorant. (0.20 g) was then thoroughly mixed. The above mixtures were formed into patties by a molding machine, sealed in polyethylene film, and held at 45°C (6 h) to induce enzyme-mediated crosslinking (Chen et al., 2025). Upon completion of the crosslinking, the samples were immediately analyzed.

Quality evaluation of patties

Color of patties

A colorimeter (WF32, FRU, Shenzhen, China) was employed to measure the color of raw patties at 25°C. Five random surface measurements were performed on each sample to record the values for lightness (L*), redness (a*) and yellowness (b*).

Texture of patties

A TA-XT Plus texture analyzer (Lotun Science, Xiamen, China) was used to determine the texture properties (hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess and chewiness) of the patties. The analyzer was fitted with a 36 mm diameter cylindrical probe. The test was performed at a speed of 1 mm/s with 50% compression strain.

Water distribution of patties

The transverse relaxation time (T2) was measured using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analyzer (Niumag Analytical Instruments, China) following the method of Chen et al. (Chen et al., 2023). Approximately 5 g of sample was weighed into an 18 mm NMR tube for measurement. The acquisition parameters were configured as follows: spectral width (SW) of 100 kHz, 90° pulse (P1) of 10.00 μs, 180° pulse (P2) of 20.48 μs, number of scans (NS) of 4, and echo time (TE) of 0.4 ms.

Cooking loss of patties

The patties were roasted in a preheated oven (KX32-V2171-E, Joyoung, China) at 180°C for 15 min. During roasting, the samples were placed in roasting trays lined with aluminum foil. After cooling to 25°C, the samples were immediately analyzed. Cooking loss was calculated based on the weight difference before and after roasting, using the formula: Cooking loss (%) = [(M1 - M2)/M1] × 100, where M1 and M2 are the weights (g) of the patty before and after cooking, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out using a minimum of three independent sample batches, and each batch consisted of at least two technical replicates. The data were expressed as means ± standard deviations and analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS version 13.0. Duncan's multiple range test served as the post-hoc method for comparing group means. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05. Shapiro-Wilk tests and Levene's tests were applied to assess normality and homogeneity of variances, respectively. All datasets passed the Shapiro–Wilk (p > 0.05) and Levene's (p > 0.05) tests, fulfilling the prerequisite assumptions for one-way ANOVA (Table S2).

Results and discussion

FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy allows for the evaluation of protein structural changes and their interactions with polysaccharides. As shown in Fig. 1A, SPI displayed a broad and intense stretching band at 3285 cm−1, corresponding to the O-H stretching vibration (Zhang et al., 2020). Upon the addition of polysaccharides, this band consistently exhibited a slight blue shift, regardless of polysaccharide type. This spectral shift points to a change in the hydrogen-bonding environment around the protein, which could result either from direct hydrogen-bonding interactions between SPI and the polysaccharides, or from polysaccharide-induced conformational changes in SPI that rearrange its intramolecular hydrogen-bond network (Wang et al., 2026). Additionally, three characteristic amide bands of SPI were observed at 1661 cm−1 (amide I, C=O stretching), 1534 cm−1 (amide II, N–H bending and C–N stretching) and 1240 cm−1 (amide III, N–H deformation and C–N stretching), which are established markers of protein secondary structure (Wang et al., 2024). Secondary structure analysis of proteins

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy is regarded as a powerful tool for analyzing protein structure. The technique exploits the differential absorption of circularly polarized light by the intrinsic chiral chromophores of a protein, thereby providing insights into its secondary structure composition, such as α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil (Yan et al., 2025). The secondary structure composition of SPI and its polysaccharide complexes is summarized in Table 1. Native SPI comprised 8.43% α-helix, 35.91% β-sheet, 22.73% β-turn and 32.93% random coil. With the exception of SPI-CS, all complexes exhibited a significant increase in β-sheet content, accompanied by a decrease in α-helix, β-turn and random coil contents. Among the four polysaccharides, the neutral polysaccharides, guar gum (GG) and locust bean gum (LBG), induced the most pronounced structural alterations in SPI. Specifically, the addition of guar gum (SPI-GG) increased the β-sheet content from 35.91% to 43.23%, with corresponding decreases in α-helix, β-turn and random coil to 7.11%, 19.04% and 30.62%, respectively. A similar trend was observed for locust bean gum (SPI-LBG), which increased the β-sheet content to 41.27% and decreased the α-helix, β-turn and random coil to 7.53%, 20.17% and 31.03%, respectively. Although hydrogen bonding is an important interaction involved in SPI–polysaccharide association, the conformational changes of SPI observed in this study cannot be attributed solely to hydrogen bonding. For neutral polysaccharides (GG and LBG), hydrogen bonding may serve as a dominant interaction due to the abundance of hydroxyl groups and the absence of charged functional groups, while hydrophobic interactions may also contribute to protein rearrangement. For ionic polysaccharides (CS, XG and HP), electrostatic interactions arising from charged groups may play a more prominent role in regulating SPI conformation. However, hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions may simultaneously participate in the association process. UV spectra analysis

When UV light is absorbed by protein molecules, electrons are excited to higher energy levels, and the accompanying energy transition produces a characteristic UV absorption spectrum. As shown in Fig. 1B, the UV spectra of SPI exhibit two characteristic peaks: one at ∼210 nm, corresponding to the n→π* transition of the protein backbone (C=O group), and the other at ∼265 nm, which is attributed to the π→π* transition of aromatic amino acid residues (Trp, Tyr and Phe) (Zhang et al., 2026). It was observed that the introduction of polysaccharides induced a shift in the peak near 210 nm, likely attributable to binding between carbonyl oxygen atoms in SPI and the polysaccharides (Yuan et al., 2019). Moreover, the addition of polysaccharides resulted in a notable decrease in the maximum absorption (265 nm) intensity, following the order: SPI > SPI-HP > SPI-CS > SPI-XG > SPI-LBG > SPI-GG. This trend suggests that the polysaccharides induced structural rearrangements in the protein, altering the microenvironments of Trp, Tyr, and Phe residues, among which SPI-GG and SPI-LBG are likely to have undergone the most significant conformational remodeling. It should be noted that the changes in UV absorbance upon polysaccharide addition may not be solely attributable to alterations in protein conformation, but may also be associated with light scattering effects arising from the formation of SPI-polysaccharide complexes. Nevertheless, the consistency of the UV spectral trends with CD, fluorescence and surface hydrophobicity data (Sections 3.2, 3.4, 3.5) suggests that the absorbance variations predominantly reflect genuine structural rearrangements, rather than scattering artifacts.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectrum analysis

The intrinsic fluorescence of proteins, arising from their aromatic amino acid residues (tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine) upon excitation at specific wavelengths, can be utilized to characterize alterations in the amino acid microenvironment. The fluorescence properties of SPI in response to polysaccharides with varying charge characteristics are shown in Fig. 1C. A significant quenching of intrinsic fluorescence was observed upon polysaccharide addition, following the order: SPI > SPI-CS > SPI-HP > SPI-XG > SPI-LBG > SPI-GG. This trend suggests that SPI-polysaccharide interactions shift aromatic amino acid residues to a more hydrophilic microenvironment, thereby inducing a fluorescence quenching effect (Liu et al., 2023). This finding aligns with the work of Yang et al., who reported similar quenching of SPI by oxidized fucoidan (Yang et al., 2025). Additionally, the reduction in fluorescence intensity may also be attributed to the formation of protein–polysaccharide complexes, wherein aromatic amino acids are shielded by polysaccharide chains, resulting in the quenching of intrinsic protein fluorescence (Xie et al., 2026). Notably, neutral polysaccharides (SPI-GG and SPI-LBG) induced the most pronounced fluorescence quenching. While both conformational changes and shielding effects may contribute, these two groups also exhibited the greatest β-sheet increase, UV absorbance decrease, and surface hydrophobicity reduction, suggesting that structural rearrangement plays a predominant role.

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) analysis

H0 serves as an indicator of the surface distribution of hydrophobic amino acid residues, and its variation profoundly determines the interfacial properties of the protein, ultimately affecting the stability of the colloidal system. Similar to the findings from intrinsic fluorescence analysis, the incorporation of polysaccharides significantly reduced the surface hydrophobicity of SPI, with the effect being more notable upon the addition of neutral polysaccharides (Fig. 1D). This effect can be attributed to two factors: (1) non-covalent interactions with polysaccharides induce a structural rearrangement in the proteins that masks internal hydrophobic groups; (2) surface hydrophobic sites are directly buried due to the formation of a polysaccharide layer (Chai et al., 2025). Furthermore, this phenomenon highlights that neutral and charged polysaccharides elicit distinct structural alterations in SPI by means of different binding modes. The extensive hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions established between neutral polysaccharides (guar gum and locust bean gum) and SPI give rise to more significant structural rearrangements. In contrast, electrostatic interactions (chitosan, xanthan gum and pectin) lead to relatively minor modifications to the protein structure.

Particle size, zeta-potential and viscosity of SPI-polysaccharide solution

The effect of polysaccharides on SPI particle size is presented in Fig. 2A. The addition of CS, XG or HP led to an increase in protein particle size. This observation aligns with the findings of Hu et al., who reported that sodium alginate, chitosan and carrageenan similarly increased particle size of soy-whey protein solutions, an effect attributed to protein-polysaccharide aggregation (Hu et al., 2024). Notably, the addition of GG resulted in a decrease in protein particle size. In general, the size of protein particles is governed not only by aggregation behavior, but also by surface hydrophobicity. We have previously demonstrated that GG effectively reduced the surface hydrophobicity of SPI (Section 3.5). The reduced exposure of hydrophobic groups weakens hydrophobic attraction between protein molecules, thereby suppressing protein aggregation and facilitating protein hydration (Zhang et al., 2024). As a result, SPI particles become more dispersed in the aqueous phase, leading to a smaller apparent particle size. Colloidal stability is governed by interparticle electrostatic repulsion, with a higher absolute zeta potential value corresponding to stronger repulsive forces that suppress aggregation and enhance stability. As shown in Fig. 2B, the introduction of cationic CS led to a slight decrease in the absolute zeta potential of SPI-CS compared to native SPI. Meanwhile, a similar decrease was observed upon the addition of neutral polysaccharides (LBG and GG). As mentioned earlier, polysaccharide incorporation induces significant protein conformational alterations, potentially leading to the burial of charged surface groups and consequent zeta potential changes. Moreover, the intrinsic positive charge of chitosan may directly neutralize the inherent negative charge of SPI, contributing to this change. Unlike cationic or neutral polysaccharides, anionic polysaccharides (XG and HP) markedly increased the system's absolute zeta potential, primarily due to the introduction of supplemental negative charges. Notably, a significantly larger absolute zeta potential was recorded for the SPI-XG compared to SPI-HP. Although Ji et al. attributed the higher absolute zeta-potential of XG-containing systems mainly to its higher charge density (Ji et al., 2024), the difference in molecular weight between XG and HP should also be considered. XG possesses a substantially higher molecular weight than HP, which may produce a thicker hydrated adsorption layer and alter the position of the shear plane. Since zeta-potential is measured at the shear plane rather than at the particle surface, the observed difference is likely the combined result of charge density and molecular-weight-dependent interfacial structure, rather than charge density alone (Barany, 2015).

The viscosity of the polysaccharide-containing solutions was measured, and the results are shown in Fig. 2C. The presence of polysaccharides significantly enhanced the viscosity of the system, in the descending order SPI-XG (845.46 mPa s) > SPI-GG (22.94 mPa s) > SPI-LBG (5.74 mPa s) > SPI-HP (4.63 mPa s) > SPI-CS (2.02 mPa s) > SPI (0.39 mPa s). These viscosity and charge differences are expected to play distinct roles. However, it remains unclear whether charge properties or viscosity exerts a dominant influence on HIPE stability and flavor retention, and how these factors ultimately translate into the quality attributes of plant-based patties.

Morphological characteristics and particle size of the emulsions

Microscopically, all samples were composed of well-defined spherical droplets of varying sizes, consistent with typical oil-in-water emulsion structures (Fig. 3A). Notably, the emulsions stabilized by SPI-polysaccharide complexes exhibited a more uniform droplet distribution compared to those stabilized by SPI alone, suggesting an enhancement in the emulsifying properties of SPI and the formation of more stable emulsions. Macroscopically, although all samples exhibited an orange-yellow hue, those stabilized by SPI-LBG, SPI-GG and SPI-XG displayed increased whiteness (Fig. S1), indicating more effective encapsulation of the oil phase. Droplet size measurements also provided quantitative support for these morphological observations (Fig. 3B). The incorporation of polysaccharides significantly decreased the mean droplet diameter, with the most pronounced decrease observed for the SPI-GG, SPI-LBG and SPI-XG complexes, which yielded the smallest droplet sizes of 6.81 μm, 8.25 μm and 8.26 μm, respectively. Interfacial loading

The effect of polysaccharide type on the thickness of the emulsion interfacial film was systematically investigated using polystyrene latex beads as model adsorption surfaces, with the results presented in Fig. 3C. It was observed that the presence of polysaccharides increases the interfacial film thickness of emulsion droplets to varying extents, a finding that aligns with the report by Zhao et al., who demonstrated that chitosan significantly increases the interfacial film thickness of myofibrillar protein emulsions (Zhao et al., 2026). Notably, XG is the most efficient at increasing interfacial film thickness, followed by HP, GG, and LBG, whereas CS exhibits the lowest efficiency. The superior thickening effect of XG can be attributed to the combined influence of its molecular conformation and charge characteristics (Lei Ji, Leonardo Cornacchia, Guido Sala and Elke Scholten, 2024). On the one hand, XG adopts a rigid rod-like conformation in aqueous solution, resulting in a large hydrodynamic volume that favors the formation of a more spatially extended interfacial layer, thereby increasing the apparent film thickness. On the other hand, as an anionic polysaccharide, XG can promote interfacial accumulation through electrostatic interactions with oppositely charged regions of proteins during the initial adsorption process. Therefore, the pronounced increase in interfacial film thickness induced by XG reflects not only enhanced interfacial accumulation, but also the formation of a more spatially extended interfacial architecture. Further, the percentage of interfacial protein adsorption in the emulsion was analyzed, and the results are shown in Fig. 3D. In line with the interfacial film thickness results, the presence of polysaccharides significantly increased the percentage of protein adsorption, with SPI-XG showing the highest value at 52.73%. In particular, although SPI-HP exhibited a greater interfacial film thickness than SPI-GG, the opposite trend was observed for the percentage of interfacial protein adsorption. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the fact that interfacial film thickness reflects the entire adsorbed layer (protein and polysaccharide), whereas protein adsorption percentage quantifies only the protein fraction at the interface. For anionic HP, highly hydrated HP chains generate a thicker film but with looser protein packing. In contrast, SPI-GG forms a denser protein layer through hydrogen-bonding-induced conformational ordering, as evidenced by a significant increase in β-sheet content, which enables higher protein adsorption efficiency.

Stability evaluation of emulsion

Thermal stability

Considering that high internal phase emulsions used as fat replacers in plant-based analogs are subjected to high-temperature cooking with internal temperatures above 80°C, we evaluated the thermal stability of the emulsion after treatment at 100°C for 30 min. Although this test temperature exceeds the internal temperature reached during actual cooking, it serves as an accelerated stress test that rapidly differentiates heat tolerance among emulsions, offering valuable insight into the relative stability of the SPI-polysaccharide system under severe conditions. From a macroscopic perspective, all samples demonstrated high thermal stability with no observable phase separation following the heat treatment (Fig. 4A). However, quantitative analysis of the emulsion stability index (ESI) revealed distinct differences among the sample groups (Fig. 4B). Native SPI-stabilized emulsion exhibited the largest ESI (far from 1.0), indicating substantial droplet size growth upon heating. In contrast, all SPI-polysaccharide complexes improved thermal stability to varying degrees, with ESI values ranking as follows: SPI-XG ≈ SPI-GG ≈ SPI-LBG < SPI-HP ≈ SPI-CS < SPI. Under high temperatures, interfacial proteins typically denature, thereby enhancing the attractive forces between oil droplets and inducing aggregation. In contrast, polysaccharides can reduce the solvent accessibility of proteins via the crowding effect, promoting the adoption of compact and stable conformations in protein molecules. As a result, polysaccharides effectively suppress variations in droplet size and inhibit aggregation in protein-based emulsions during thermal treatment. This mechanism explains the superior stability observed in emulsions stabilized by the SPI-polysaccharide complex compared to those stabilized by SPI alone (Nooshkam et al., 2023).Fig. 4A: Macroscopic images of soy protein isolate-polysaccharide complex-stabilized emulsions after treatment at 100°C for 30 min. Microscopic observation further supported these quantitative findings (Fig. 4C). Heat-induced droplet aggregation was obvious in all samples, but the extent varied considerably. The SPI-stabilized emulsion displayed severe aggregation, with numerous large, irregularly clustered droplets. In comparison, polysaccharide-containing emulsions showed significantly greater resistance to aggregation, with the SPI-LBG, SPI-GG and SPI-XG groups exhibiting the most well-dispersed and least aggregated droplets. Notably, neutral (LBG, GG) and anionic polysaccharides (XG, HP) exhibited greater protective effects than cationic chitosan (CS). This difference is attributed to two factors: the mechanism for regulating the interfacial properties of the emulsion, and the system viscosity. For neutral polysaccharides (LBG and GG), their strong hydrogen-bonding interactions with SPI induce a significant increase in β-sheet content (as shown in Section 3.2), which promotes the formation of a more rigid, ordered and densely protein film at the oil-water interface. Such a reinforced interfacial layer is more resistant to thermal disruption, thereby effectively suppressing droplet coalescence during heating. In contrast, anionic polysaccharides (XG and HP) primarily enhance thermal stability through strong electrostatic repulsion due to their negative charges, which persists at elevated temperatures and prevents droplet approach and fusion. Furthermore, the increased interfacial film thickness and protein adsorption observed in the SPI-XG group may also contribute to its improved stability. Chitosan (CS), being cationic, partially neutralizes the native negative charge of SPI, weakening electrostatic repulsion and compromising the thermal stability of the interfacial layer. System viscosity also plays a crucial role in stability. High viscosity can enhance the thermal stability of emulsions by inhibiting droplet coalescence and creaming during heat treatment. More importantly, the thermal stability of the emulsion showed a strong consistency with its viscosity. From this perspective, the thermal stability of the emulsion is primarily governed by viscosity rather than by the charge characteristics of the polysaccharide.

Storage stability

The changes in the appearance, ESI and emulsion microstructure after 18 days of storage are shown in Fig. 5. Visually, the emulsions stabilized with different emulsifiers exhibited distinct macroscopic states (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the SPI-stabilized emulsion showed severe delamination over time. Previous research attributes this instability primarily to electrostatic repulsion between free and adsorbed proteins at the oil-water interface. This repulsion ultimately drives the separation of the external aqueous phase from the emulsion layer (Li et al., 2024). The incorporation of polysaccharides markedly mitigated phase separation, with the SPI-XG, SPI-LBG and SPI-GG groups showing the least pronounced phase separation. To quantitatively compare stability across sample groups, the ESI was analyzed (Fig. 5B). After 18 days of storage, the ESI values ranked as follows: SPI-XG < SPI-GG ≈ SPI-LBG < SPI-HP < SPI-CS < SPI. At the microstructural level, the lower degree of aggregation and slower increase in particle size observed in the SPI-XG, SPI-LBG and SPI-GG groups further support these findings (Fig. 5C). Notably, SPI-XG and SPI-GG exhibited comparable stability during heat treatment, whereas the former demonstrated significantly better stability than the latter during storage. During storage at room temperature, the dominant destabilization process is mainly associated with droplet movement, collision and gravitational separation. Therefore, the exceptionally high viscosity of XG plays a predominant role by reducing droplet mobility and suppressing aggregation (Boostani et al., 2017). In contrast, during thermal treatment, the contribution of interfacial layer strength becomes increasingly important because protein unfolding and interfacial disruption are accelerated. Under these conditions, GG may provide additional protection through its stronger interaction with SPI and the formation of a more ordered protein structure with increased β-sheet content. Retention rate of aroma compounds during processing

The retention of characteristic aroma compounds is critical for the sensory quality of plant-based analogs, particularly during thermal processing and storage. In this work, a beef-flavored flavoring was incorporated into the oil phase to evaluate the effect of polysaccharide type on flavor compound retention. As shown in Tables S3 and 37 volatile flavor compounds were detected in the beef-flavored flavoring, predominantly comprising esters (13), furans (9), acids (6), alcohols (3), ketones (3), pyrazines (2) and others (1). However, when the flavoring was added to the oil phase at 0.04 g/g (4%, w/w based on the oil phase) and emulsified, only a few compounds, such as 4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone and 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one, showed relatively high response values under the tested conditions (Table S4). This observation was attributed to the dilution effect. Given that 4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone and 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one are characteristic aroma compounds in cooked meat, they were selected as model compounds to investigate the influence of the protein–polysaccharide interface on flavor stability. As shown in Fig. 6A and B, the retention rates of the two flavor compounds varied with the type of polysaccharide following heat treatment at 100°C for 30 min. Specifically, SPI-XG and SPI-GG exhibited the highest retention rates, followed by SPI-LBG and SPI-HP, whereas SPI-CS showed only a modest improvement compared to native SPI. Generally, aroma release entails a stepwise process: the aroma first transfers within oil droplets, then crosses the oil–water interface, diffuses through the aqueous phase, and finally permeates into the air across the water–air boundary (Mao et al., 2014). From this perspective, the varying protective effects of emulsions on aroma compounds are primarily attributable to differences in the oil-water interface and the aqueous phase system. The high retention of aroma compounds during heat treatment for SPI-XG can be attributed to two key properties of xanthan gum. First, the greater interfacial film thickness prevented droplet coalescence and rupture during heat treatment, thereby maintaining the integrity of the interfacial film. Second, its highest viscosity among the tested polysaccharides effectively reduced molecular mobility and suppressed the diffusion of volatile compounds. For SPI-GG, although guar gum is neutral and does not enhance electrostatic repulsion, its interaction with SPI in solution induces a more compact and ordered protein conformation, as evidenced by the increased β-sheet content. This likely results in an interfacial layer with fewer defects, increasing resistance to volatile permeation. Meanwhile, the viscosity of guar gum, second only to xanthan gum among the tested polysaccharides, works together with the compact interfacial film to restrict volatile diffusion. Given that the retention rates of volatiles were consistent with the viscosity profiles of the systems, it can be inferred that under thermal treatment, viscosity exerts a stronger influence on flavor retention in emulsions than does polysaccharide charge. This conclusion also holds for flavor storage stability, with the highest retention rates observed for the high-viscosity XG and GG (Fig. 6C and D), further confirming that flavor volatility depends on system viscosity rather than charge. Although enhanced flavor retention was observed for the SPI–polysaccharide-stabilized emulsions, the present HS-SPME-GC×GC-MS analysis reflects the overall headspace concentration after treatment and does not distinguish whether the improved retention originates from reduced mass-transfer kinetics (e.g., diffusion barriers imposed by the interfacial layer and viscous continuous phase) or changes in thermodynamic partitioning between the oil, aqueous and headspace phases. Given the strong correlation between flavor retention and system viscosity observed in this study, diffusion limitation is likely to be a major contributing factor. Texture properties

Texture profile analysis offers a quantitative description of food texture characteristics and serves as a predictor of product sensory quality (Zhao et al., 2024). Among the various parameters, hardness reflects the maximum resistance of patties to biting force, whereas springiness denotes their ability to recover from deformation. Chewiness, defined as the energy required to masticate the patty, is governed by both springiness and gumminess. For cohesiveness, it reflects the ability of the product to maintain the stability of its internal structure. Table 2 presents the texture profiles of plant-based patties prepared with different high internal phase emulsion formulations. Compared to the control group, the incorporation of polysaccharides generally enhanced the mechanical properties of the patties, with the extent of improvement varying by polysaccharide type. Specifically, the ranking of hardness followed the order: SPI-XG (13037.47 g) ≈ SPI-GG (12745.08 g) > SPI-LBG (12218.23 g) > SPI-HP (11583.33 g) > SPI-CS (11085.46 g) > SPI (10393.44 g). This enhancement can be attributed to the increased system viscosity and the retarded formation of protein aggregate networks induced by polysaccharides, which together promote a more organized and reinforced network structure (Chen et al., 2025). Moreover, the charge characteristics of polysaccharides play a critical role in modulating protein network formation through distinct mechanisms (Li et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Cationic polysaccharides (e.g., CS) tend to form rigid macromolecular networks via charge neutralization, intermolecular entanglement and physical crosslinking. Neutral polysaccharides (e.g., LBG and GG) primarily associate with proteins through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, thereby influencing network progression. Anionic polysaccharides (e.g., XG and HP) promote ordered protein aggregation through electrostatic repulsion, leading to a more compact network. It is worth noting that the order of patty hardness correlated consistently with polysaccharide viscosity, reinforcing the critical role of viscosity properties in determining product quality.

Water distribution and cooking loss of patties

Given that polysaccharides may alter water state through their impact on the protein network formation in patties, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was used to characterize the water distribution of the samples. As illustrated in Fig. S3, two characteristic peaks, T2b (0–10 ms) and T21 (10–100 ms), were identified in the plant-based patty samples, representing bound water and immobilized water, respectively. Bound water is defined as water tightly associated with macromolecules such as proteins, whereas immobilized water refers to water entrapped within the protein network (Xu et al., 2025). In particular, the addition of polysaccharides transitioned the sample structure from a bimodal-like to a unimodal pattern, indicating a more uniform water distribution that was more effectively trapped within the protein network. This enhanced water-holding capacity was also reflected in the cooking loss rate, as the polysaccharide-containing sample groups showed a lower cooking loss (4.86 ∼ 5.69%) compared to the control group (5.76%) (Table 2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SPI-polysaccharide complex-stabilized emulsions are more effective than SPI-stabilized emulsions alone at stabilizing the aqueous phase within plant-based patties and mitigating moisture loss during cooking, although the LF-NMR technique used here did not resolve differences among polysaccharide types.

Conclusions

This study revealed the distinct roles of polysaccharide charge and viscosity in HIPEs. The charge characteristics of polysaccharides were identified as the primary factor associated with SPI conformational remodeling through different non-covalent interaction modes. Neutral polysaccharides (GG and LBG), mainly through hydrogen bonding together with hydrophobic interactions, induced the greatest increase in β-sheet content, whereas ionic polysaccharides (CS, XG and HP) primarily affected SPI conformation through electrostatic interactions, although multiple interactions may coexist simultaneously. Viscosity governed the resistance of HIPEs to thermal and storage stresses as well as the texture of plant-based patties, with high-viscosity XG and GG providing excellent emulsion stability and product hardness. Nevertheless, considering that the two model aroma compounds selected in this study, namely furanone and 2-methyltetrahydrofuran-3-one, are both relatively polar heterocycles, the retention behavior of nonpolar or weakly polar flavor compounds, such as hydrocarbons and aldehydes, may be governed by interfacial properties via distinctly different mechanisms. Accordingly, future investigations should incorporate aroma compounds with a broader range of physicochemical properties under realistic processing conditions to confirm the general validity of the proposed mechanism. In parallel, advanced microscopic techniques could be employed in future work to directly visualize the interfacial architecture and microstructural organization of SPI–polysaccharide-stabilized HIPEs, thereby providing complementary insights into the structural basis of emulsion stability. Overall, this work provided a mechanistic framework for the selection of polysaccharides based on charge and viscosity to tailor HIPEs for premium plant-based meat analogs.
