A novel recombinant ALV-K carrying ALV-J-derived LTR, pol, and 3’UTR from Chinese indigenous chicken breeds exhibits enhanced replication and increased hemangioma-associated pathogenicity
Authors: Jinqun Li, Wanyi Zhou, Hongmei Wang, Xinjie Luo, Huilin Chen, Xueli Shu, Yilin Yuan, Ming Liao, Weisheng Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian leukosis virus (ALV) is a prototypical alpharetrovirus that can induce various tumors and cause serious immunosuppression, posing severe threats to the poultry industry. ALVs isolated from chickens are classified into seven subgroups (A to E, J, and K). In 2024, a novel ALV-K strain designated GD24LHB130 was isolated from Qingyuan Partridge chickens in Guangdong Province, China. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that GD24LHB130 is a recombinant ALV-K strain harboring the LTR, pol, and 3’UTR regions of ALV-J. In vitro infection assays and LTR promoter activity analysis demonstrated that GD24LHB130 exhibits significantly enhanced replicative capacity compared to classical ALV-K strains, with the recombination of the ALV-J LTR region strongly associated with this replication advantage. In vivo pathogenicity experiments confirmed that GD24LHB130 causes significant growth retardation, immune organ damage, persistent viremia and viral shedding, and hemangioma-like lesion formation in infected chickens. This is the first report of a recombinant ALV-K strain capable of inducing hemangioma-like lesions, which exhibits enhanced replication capacity and pathogenic potential. These findings not only expand the epidemiological knowledge of ALV but also provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying ALV-K pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05530-8.

Introduction

Avian leukosis virus (ALV), a member of the family Retroviridae and genus Alpharetrovirus, primarily causes multiple neoplastic diseases and immunosuppression, posing significant economic losses to the poultry industry [1, 2]. The ALV genome encodes three core genes—gag, pol, and env—as well as the non-coding 5’UTR and 3’UTR regions at both terminals [3, 4]. Based on envelope interference pattern, host range specificity, and serological cross-neutralization, ALVs from chickens are classified into seven subgroups (A, B, C, D, E, J, and K) [5]. Among them, ALV-A, ALV-B, and ALV-J are common exogenous viruses that can induce tumors in clinical practice, while ALV-E is an endogenous virus with no or weak pathogenicity [6–8]. In particular, ALV-K, as a novel exogenous virus first discovered in Chinese native Luhua chickens in 2012, exhibiting more than 90% homology in the gp85 region with several Japanese fowl glioma-inducing viruses (FGV) and the ALV strain TW-3593 isolated from Taiwan Province, was classified into a new ALV subgroup [8, 9].

In recent years, ALV-K has been widely disseminated in commercial broilers, laying hens, and local chicken breeds in China [10–13]. Although initially considered low pathogenic due to its relatively weak replication capacity, ALV-K complicates diagnostic efforts by interfering with the detection of exogenous ALV, thereby hindering the national eradication of ALV [14]. Moreover, increasing evidence has suggested that ALV-K carries the risk of mutation and recombination. Most of the recombinant ALV-K strains reported previously, which had high similarity with ALV-E in the 3’UTR and LTR regions, generally had weaker replication capacity and pathogenicity [11]. However, studies have shown that artificially recombining ALV-K env into the ALV-J backbone could significantly enhance the replication capacity and pathogenicity of ALV-K [15]. Moreover, some naturally occurring recombinant ALV-K strains containing ALV-K env and gene fragments from other subgroups of ALV were reported to be highly pathogenic [10, 16, 17]. These studies indicated that ALV-K could enhance its pathogenicity and replication capacity through recombination, thereby enhancing its threat to chickens.

In 2024, a recombinant ALV-K strain containing the pol, LTR, and 3’UTR regions of ALV-J was isolated from a local chicken flock in Guangdong Province, China. This study further conducted genome sequence analysis, in vitro infection assays, and in vivo pathogenicity experiments, aiming to provide new insights into the epidemic characteristics, replication dynamics, and pathogenicity mechanisms of the novel recombinant ALV-K strain.

Materials and methods

Virus isolation and identification

A total of 500 anticoagulated blood samples were aseptically collected from clinically healthy roosters of a grandparent stock flock of Qingyuan Partridge chickens undergoing an exogenous ALV eradication program in Guangdong Province, China. These anticoagulated blood samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, 100 µL plasma was inoculated into DF-1 cells (from ATCC, kept in our lab) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Australia) at 39 °C and 5% CO2 for 9 days. Exogenous ALV was detected via ALV p27 antigen ELISA test kit (IDvet, France). Positive cells were fixed in cold acetone for 20 min, incubated with mouse anti-ALV p27 mAb (Qianxun Biological, Guangzhou) at 4 °C overnight, washed with PBS three times, stained with 1:200 Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime, Shanghai), and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Genomic DNA or RNA was extracted from positive samples using Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China) or RNAfast200 Total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Fastagen, Shanghai, China). PCR identification of ALV-A, ALV-B, ALV-J, ALV-K, Reticuloendotheliosis virus (REV), Chicken infectious anemia virus (CIAV), and Marek’s disease virus (MDV) was performed with primers in Table 1 [12, 18, 19]. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) of ALV-A, ALV-B, ALV-J, ALV-K, REV, and MDV was conducted using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) and primers in Supplementary Table 1 [18–20].

Genome amplification, cloning, and sequencing

PCR was performed with specific primers (Table 2) to amplify the LTR, gag, pol, and env regions of ALV-K. All PCR reactions were performed using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) under the following conditions: 3 min at 94 °C followed by 30 cycles of 15 s at 94 °C, 15 s at 56 °C, and 2 min at 72 °C, and a final elongation for 5 min at 72 °C. The positive products were purified using a DNA Gel Extraction Kit (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China), ligated into TA/Blunt-Zero Cloning Vector (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Positive clones were identified via bacterial PCR and submitted to Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for sequencing.

Sequence analysis

Genomic fragments were spliced using SnapGene software. Multiple sequence alignment of the complete genome with other ALV strains was performed using the NCBI BLAST program. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree of the full-length genome, gp85 gene, and LTR region of the ALV-K isolate was constructed with MEGA 11.0 software, using 1000 bootstrap replicates for statistical support. Multiple sequence alignment, similarity analysis, and mutation analysis of the ALV-K isolate were conducted via the Clustal W algorithm implemented in the MegAlign program of DNASTAR software. The GenBank accession numbers and background information of the reference ALV strains are provided in Table 3. GD24NH05 and GD24NH3635 are ALV-K strains isolated in 2024 from different local chicken breeds in Guangdong Province during the same sampling period.

In vitro replication capacity analysis

DF-1 cells seeded in 24-well plates were inoculated with 250 µL of 103.5 TCID50 ALV-J SCAU-HN06, ALV-K GD24LHB130, or ALV-K GD24NH3635. Supernatants were collected at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 post-infection (dpi) for ALV p27 ELISA, and growth kinetic curves were plotted using S/P values.

Similar to the previously described method [21], fragments containing env of these three isolates were PCR-amplified and cloned into RCASBP(A)-EGFP to construct the RCASBP-SCAU-HN06-EGFP, RCASBP-GD24LHB130-EGFP, and RCASBP-GD24NH3635-EGFP recombinant vectors. DF-1 cells seeded in 6-well plates were transfected with 1 µg recombinant vector using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Shanghai, China). At 3 or 6 days post-transfection (dpt), fluorescence observation, ALV p27 ELISA detection, and GFP-positive cell percentage analysis (LSRII flow cytometer; Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) were performed.

Proviral genomes of GD24LHB130 and GD24NH3635 were PCR-amplified (Supplementary Table 2) and cloned into pBlueScript II KS(+) vector (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) via Kpn I and EcoR I restriction sites to construct infectious clones rGD24LHB130 and rGD24NH3635. GD24LHB130-derived LTR, pol, env, or 3’UTR was amplified and inserted into rGD24NH3635 linearized by reverse PCR to generate recombinant clones: rNH3635-LTR(LHB130), rNH3635-pol(LHB130), rNH3635-env(LHB130), and rNH3635-3’UTR(LHB130). All clone plasmids were transfected into DF-1 cells to harvest viral supernatants. DF-1 cells seeded in 24-well plates were inoculated with 100 µL of 103 TCID50 viral supernatant, and ALV p27 ELISA was performed at 7 dpi.

LTR promoter activity analysis

The LTR of ALV-J SCAU-HN06, ALV-K GD24LHB130, and ALV-K GD24NH3635 were amplified by PCR using specific primers (Supplementary Table 3) and were inserted into the pGL3-basic vector through the Kpn I and Bgl Ⅱ restriction sites to construct luciferase reporter plasmids pGL3-SCAU-HN06-LTR, pGL3-GD24LHB130-LTR, and pGL3-GD24NH3635-LTR. DF-1 cells seeded in 24-well plates were co-transfected with 100 ng of the luciferase reporter plasmids or the empty vector pGL3-basic plasmid and 10 ng pRL-TK internal reference plasmid using lipofectamine 3000. After 48 h, the cells were lysed and the fluorescence was detected according to the instructions of the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Pathogenicity experiments

150 one-day-old Qingyuan Partridge chicken embryo eggs without exogenous ALV were purchased from a commercial chicken breeding farm in Guangdong Province, which holds legal qualifications for avian embryo production and operation. At 5 days of incubation, the chicken embryo eggs were randomly divided into two groups. The infected group (n = 100) was inoculated with 100 µL of 5000 TCID50 of GD24LHB130 via yolk sac, while the control group (n = 50) was injected with an equal volume of DMEM medium. After hatching of embryo eggs, 15 chickens from each group were housed in a negative-pressure filtered-air isolator with free access to commercial feed and drinking water. At the end of the animal experiment, chickens were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation combined with cervical dislocation, with reference to the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Tissue DNA was extracted from infected and control chickens for PCR and qPCR detection to further rule out infections with MDV, REV, and other exogenous ALVs. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of South China Agricultural University (Ethics No.: 2025C046).

Viral shedding and viremia detection

Cloacal swabs and anticoagulated blood samples were collected from the two groups of chickens at weekly intervals from 1 to 56 days after hatching (dph). Plasma was separated from anticoagulated blood and inoculated into DF-1 cells, which were then maintained for 9 days. The ALVs in the cell supernatants and cloacal swabs were detected using ALV p27 antigen test kit.

Clinical autopsy and histopathological examination

After 56 days of rearing, clinical necropsy was performed on the surviving chickens to observe the lesions. The liver, spleen, and kidney of infected and control chickens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, cut into 4–5 μm sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The histopathological lesions were examined using an optical microscope.

Viral load testing of organs

At the end of the experiment, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, glandular stomachs, thymus, bursa of Fabricius, and brains of the 6 chickens in the infection group were randomly collected. RT-qPCR assays were performed to analyze the viral load of GD24LHB130 in different organs [19].

Body weight and immune organ index determination

From 1 to 56 dph, the body weights of chickens were measured weekly. At the end of the experiment, the spleens, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius were weighed, and the immune organ index (immune organ index = immune organ weight (g)/body weight (kg)) was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences were evaluated using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) by the Prism 6.0 software package (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Data are expressed as mean ± SD. A P value of < 0.05 was considered significant. ∗, ∗∗, ∗∗∗, and ∗∗∗∗ indicate P values less than 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001, respectively.

Results

Virus isolation and identification

A total of 500 anticoagulated blood samples collected from clinically healthy roosters of a grandparent stock flock of Qingyuan Partridge chickens in Guangdong Province were inoculated into DF-1 cells for 9 days. The results of ALV p27 ELISA (Supplementary Fig. 1) and ALV subgroup-specific PCR assays (Supplementary Fig. 2) indicated that 5 chickens were infected with ALV-J and 1 with ALV-K. The exogenous ALV infection rate in this breeding flock was 1.2% (6/500). Subsequently, DF-1 cells were infected with the ALV-K virus suspension, and ALV p27 detection was performed via ELISA (Fig. 1A) and IFA (Fig. 1B). The results revalidated the presence of ALV p27 antigen, corroborating the initial positive result. PCR detection of this ALV-K isolate for ALV-A, ALV-B, ALV-J, ALV-K, MDV, and REV showed a specific fragment of about 1200 bp of ALV-K, whereas no bands corresponding to other viruses were observed (Fig. 1C). qPCR detection for ALV-A, ALV-B, ALV-J, ALV-K, MDV, and REV also showed only specific amplification of ALV-K (Fig. 1D). The ALV-K isolate was named GD24LHB130.

Molecular characterization analysis of the complete genome

The proviral genome of GD24LHB130 (7705 bp) consists of a gag-pol-env sequence and two LTRs at both ends. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that GD24LHB130 shared the highest homology with ALV-K strains and clustered within the same major clade (Fig. 2A). The gag gene (2103 bp) shared over 98% homology with that of other ALV subgroups. The pol gene (2622 bp) exhibited over 98% homology with that of ALV-J and contained a premature stop codon (unique to ALV-J pol) that results in a 22-amino-acid C-terminal deletion (Supplementary Fig. 3). The gp37 gene (609 bp) had more than 98% homology with ALV-K. The gp85 gene (1002 bp) shared the highest similarity (over 99%) with ALV-K isolates GX22NN02K and TW-3593, as well as the FGV variant KmN_119. The 5’UTR shared over 96% homology with that of ALV-K, while the 3’UTR and LTR regions exhibited a maximum homology of over 99% with those of ALV-J (Fig. 2B). Collectively, GD24LHB130 is a novel recombinant ALV-K strain harboring the LTR, pol, and 3’UTR regions of ALV-J.

Sequence analysis of gp85 gene

The sequence homology between the gp85 of GD24LHB130 and the ALV-K reference strains ranged from 94.2% to 99.6%. In contrast, its similarity with ALV-A, ALV-B, ALV-C, ALV-D, ALV-E, and ALV-J was only 83.8%-85.7%, 82.9%-83.2%, 87.9%, 84.0%, 88.3%-88.4%, and 49.4%-50.6%, respectively. Notably, the gp85 of GD24LHB130 shared 95.8% and 96.0% homology with those of ALV-K isolates GD24NH05 and GD24NH3635—isolated from different local chicken breeds in Guangdong Province during the same period—and the three strains clustered into three distinct evolutionary clades. Specifically, the gp85 of GD24LHB130, which exhibits higher identity with that of ALV-K strains (e.g., GD1701, TW-3593) and certain FGV variants (e.g., Sp-40), was grouped into a clade designated Group Ⅲ (Fig. 2C).

Sequence analysis of LTR and 3’UTR region

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the LTR of GD24LHB130 shared the highest similarity (87.3%-94.1%) with ALV-J reference strains, whereas its homology to other ALV-K reference strains was relatively low (73.4% with GD24NH05 and 72.3% with GD24NH3635; Fig. 3A). The 3’UTR of GD24LHB130 contains the rTM region, direct repeat 1 (DR1), and E element. Phylogenetic analysis clustered it with ALV-J reference strains (66.7%-90.7% homology; Fig. 3B). Sequence analysis confirmed that the DR1 and E elements are intact, but a 175 bp deletion was identified in the rTM region compared to the ALV-J prototype strain HPRS-103—a feature consistent with hemangioma-inducing ALV-J strains (e.g., SCAU-HN06; Fig. 3C). Further sequence analysis revealed the U3 region of GD24LHB130 harbors one CAAT LTR enhancer, one CAAT box, one PRE box, one TATA box, one poly(A) signal, two CArG boxes, and two Y boxes—one fewer PRE box than ALV-J prototype HPRS-103—with high homology to ALV-J (Fig. 4). The U3 region of GD24LHB130 harbors ALV-J-like transcriptional regulatory elements distinct from classical ALV-K strains.

Analysis of in vitro replication capacity

DF-1 cells were seeded with equal titers of ALV-J SCAU-HN06, ALV-K GD24LHB130, ALV-K GD24NH3635, and ALV-K GD24NH05, respectively. ALV p27 antigen ELISA results showed that GD24LHB130 replicated significantly more efficiently than GD24NH3635 and GD24NH05 (P < 0.001) and exhibited comparable replication efficiency to SCAU-HN06 at 7 and 9 dpi (P > 0.05; Fig. 5A).

Fluorescence observation (Fig. 5B) and ALV p27 ELISA (Fig. 5C) at 3 and 6 dpt revealed that the replication capacity of RCASBP-GD24LHB130-EGFP was significantly weaker than that of RCASBP-SCAU-HN06-EGFP (P < 0.001) but comparable to that of RCASBP-GD24NH3635-EGFP (P > 0.05), indicating env is not the key determinant of the enhanced replication capacity of GD24LHB130.

To identify the critical region responsible for the enhanced replication, recombinant infectious clones were constructed and used to infect DF-1 cells (Fig. 5D). ALV p27 ELISA at 7 dpi (Fig. 5E) showed that rGD24NH3635 replicated significantly less efficiently than rGD24LHB130 (P < 0.001), whereas replacement of its LTR with that of GD24LHB130 significantly enhanced viral replication (P < 0.01). In contrast, replacement of the pol, env, or 3’UTR region had no significant effect (P > 0.05), indicating that the ALV-J-derived LTR is a major contributor to the replication advantage of GD24LHB130.

Analysis of LTR promoter activity

DF-1 cells were co-transfected with either pGL3-SCAU-HN06-LTR, pGL3-GD24LHB130-LTR, pGL3-GD24NH3635-LTR, or the pGL3-basic empty vector, together with the pRL-TK internal control plasmid. Relative luciferase activity assays showed that the LTR promoter activity of GD24LHB130 was significantly higher than that of GD24NH3635 (a classical ALV-K strain with an endogenous LTR; P < 0.0001) but comparable to that of ALV-J strain SCAU-HN06 (P > 0.05). These results suggest that enhanced LTR promoter activity is strongly associated with the replication advantage of GD24LHB130 (Fig. 5F).

Detection of viremia and viral shedding

To evaluate the pathogenicity of GD24LHB130, 5-day-old Qingyuan Partridge chicken embryos (in ovo) were inoculated with GD24LHB130 or DMEM (negative control) via the yolk sac route. Cloacal swabs and plasma samples were collected in 15 chickens per group from 1 to 56 dph (n = 14 in the infected group at 49 and 56 dph) for ALV p27 ELISA to assess viremia and viral shedding. The results showed that viremia in the infected group was detectable as early as 1 dph and persisted at a moderate level (≥ 35%) until the end of the experiment. Viral shedding, distinct from viremia, was first detected at 14 dph, peaked at 21, 28, and 56 dph, and persisted until the end of the experiment. No viral antigens were detected in the control group (Table 4).

Detection of body weight and immune organ index

To assess the growth-suppressive effect of GD24LHB130, chicken body weights were recorded for 15 chickens per group from 1 to 56 dph (n = 14 in the infected group at 49 and 56 dph). The infected group showed lower average body weights than the control group at all time points, with significant reduction from 21 dph onward (P < 0.001), indicating GD24LHB130 infection strongly inhibits chicken growth (Fig. 6B). Statistical analysis revealed the infected group had significantly lower thymus and bursa indexes (P < 0.0001) but a higher spleen index (P < 0.05) compared to the control group, demonstrating GD24LHB130 infection exerts severe adverse effects on immune organs (Fig. 6C).

Analysis of gross and histological lesions

Clinical observations revealed that compared with control chickens, infected chickens exhibited delayed growth, poor mental status, and reduced individual uniformity. Additionally, one chicken in the infected group died suddenly at 48 dph (1/15). PCR (Supplementary Fig. 4) and qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 5) analyses of the dead chicken’s tissues confirmed no co-infection with other exogenous ALV, REV, or MDV. Gross autopsy revealed severe abdominal cavity congestion and scattered blood blisters on the surfaces of the enlarged liver, kidneys, and spleen (Fig. 6D). H&E staining revealed massive blood clots in the liver and kidney tissues, accompanied by hyperplastic infiltration of numerous pseudo-eosinophilic tumor cells and lymphocytes (Fig. 6E). Macroscopic and pathological findings demonstrated that GD24LHB130 infection induces hemangioma-like lesions in chickens, similar to ALV-J, whereas no histological lesions were observed in controls.

Discussion

Most ALV-K isolates exhibit weak replicative capacity, often failing to reach detectable levels and thereby hindering ALV eradication efforts [11]. However, genome mutations in ALV-K or its recombination with other exogenous ALV subgroups have been reported to be associated with enhanced replicative capacity and pathogenicity [2, 12]. In this study, an ALV-K strain GD24LHB130 was isolated (Fig. 1) and genome sequence analysis identified it as a recombinant strain harboring LTR, pol, and 3’UTR of ALV-J (Fig. 2B). In vitro experiments demonstrated significantly enhanced replication of GD24LHB130, with LTR as a major contributor (Fig. 5). Animal studies revealed robust cloacal viral shedding, persistent viremia, and significant growth inhibition; importantly, this is the first report of a recombinant ALV-K strain associated with hemangioma-like lesions in chickens (Fig. 6).

Gp85, the most variable envelope glycoprotein region, dictates host range, viral binding, and subgroup classification [21–23]. In this study, phylogenetic analysis of gp85 classified GD24LHB130 as ALV-K (Fig. 2C), but its LTR, pol, and 3’UTR shared high homology with those of ALV-J (Fig. 2B), supporting the occurrence of recombination between ALV-K and ALV-J. The detailed recombination mechanism remains to be further explored since formal recombination breakpoint analysis and experimental validation of the recombination event were not conducted in this study. Previous ALV-K recombination events include the ALV-K env gene combined with ALV-E or ALV-J genomic backbones, as well as multi-subgroup recombination events [11, 12]. However, the present recombination event—occurring between ALV-K and ALV-J in local chicken breeds in south China—represents a novel pattern. The widespread prevalence of different ALV subgroups may elevate the risk of recombination, leading to the emergence of more pathogenic strains and further complicating ALV control strategies.

Compared to classical ALV-K, GD24LHB130 exhibited stronger in vitro replication, even comparable to ALV-J in the later stages of infection (Fig. 5A), which is closely related to differences in env and the recombination of the LTR, pol, and 3’ UTR. Env mediates receptor binding and membrane fusion, with key amino acid variations influencing receptor selection and viral replication [23, 24]. However, RCASBP-EGFP recombinant vectors carrying env from GD24LHB130 or classical ALV-K GD24NH3635 showed no significant replication differences (Fig. 5B, C), indicating env was not the decisive factor for the enhanced replication of GD24LHB130. Premature stop codons in the pol (encoding reverse transcriptase) of ALV-J are unlikely to impair reverse transcription activity [16, 25]. To identify critical regions for the replication advantage of GD24LHB130, a series of infectious clones were rescued by replacing the LTR, pol, env, or 3’UTR of GD24NH3635 with GD24LHB130 (Fig. 5D). In vitro infection experiments showed that only LTR substitution significantly enhanced the replication capacity of infectious clones. In contrast, the replacement of the pol, env, and 3’UTR showed no significant difference in replication (Fig. 5E), indicating that the LTR is a major contributor to the replication advantage of GD24LHB130. LTR includes U3, R, and U5 regions, among which U3 contains regulatory elements governing viral replication, transcription, and translation. ALV-J LTR exhibits stronger promoter activity than classical ALV-K, contributing to superior ALV-J replication [15]. Previous studies have shown reduced replication of ALV-J strain GX-2020-01 due to its ALV-E/K-like LTR, emphasizing the role of LTR in ALV replication [26]. In this study, GD24LHB130 LTR harbors regulatory elements similar to ALV-J (Fig. 4), suggesting enhanced promoter activity. Furthermore, Luciferase reporter assays confirmed GD24LHB130 LTR activity was comparable to ALV-J SCAU-HN06 and significantly higher than classical ALV-K GD24NH3635 (Fig. 5F), suggesting that enhanced LTR promoter activity is strongly associated with the replication advantage.

GD24LHB130 was isolated from clinically healthy chickens, which may be due to latent infection or vertical transmission. Even for highly pathogenic ALV strains, a certain proportion of early infections can remain subclinical and persistent for lifelong. In vivo experiments showed that compared to ALV-K/E recombinant strains (viremia positivity < 35%, viral shedding positivity < 21%) [11], GD24LHB130 induced more severe viremia (≥ 35%) and peak shedding (60%; Table 4), with adverse effects on body weight and immune organs, and rapid tumor induction (Fig. 6), indicating stronger pathogenicity. Previous studies have shown that the recombination of ALV-J LTR enhances the pathogenicity of ALV-K, while deletion of the r-TM and DR regions in the 3’UTR region increases the pathogenicity of the ALV-J [11, 27]. Since GD24LHB130 harbors a gp85 gene similar to that of the ALV-K/E recombinant strains, but contains an ALV-J-derived LTR region and a 3’UTR with an r-TM deletion, it is speculated that the enhanced pathogenicity of GD24LHB130 is associated with its recombination with the ALV-J LTR and 3’UTR regions. The present study verified that the ALV-J-derived LTR facilitates viral replication, which may contribute to the elevated pathogenicity, while the potential role of the 3’UTR in viral pathogenicity is supported by previous literature and warrants further experimental validation.

In this study, hemangioma-like lesion symptoms and pathological lesions similar to those induced by ALV-J were observed in GD24LHB130-infected chickens (Fig. 6D, E)—to our knowledge, this is the first report of hemangioma-like lesion caused by a recombinant ALV-K strain. Previous studies have shown that the gp85, LTR, and 3’UTR regions play an important role in the tumorigenicity of ALV [2, 12, 16]. Given that ALV-K shares high homology with FGV variants in the gp85 region, it was previously regarded as an FGV variant [9]. FGV consistently induces gliomas in poultry; however, no reports have documented glioma induction by ALV-K strains isolated in China [10]. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that the env gene, which contains gp85 gene, is not a major determinant of fowl glioma [28]. Similarly, no glioma-related clinical signs were observed in GD24LHB130-infected chickens in the present study, suggesting that gp85 is not a decisive factor in hemangioma-like lesion induction either. The LTR and 3’UTR region of GD24LHB130 with deletions in the r-TM was similar to that of the ALV-J strains that caused hemangiomas. Therefore, it is suggested that the hemangioma-like lesion-inducing potential of the recombinant ALV-K virus is associated with the LTR and 3’UTR, which requires further experimental verification.

In conclusion, this study indicates that recombinant ALV-K strains may possess enhanced replicative capacity and pathogenicity, which could pose a substantial threat to the poultry industry. Although classical ALV-K exhibits relatively weak virulence in its wild-type form, its pathogenicity and replication ability can be markedly augmented through genetic recombination with ALV-J. This highlights a critical oversight in current avian leukosis eradication programs—the co-circulation of ALV-J and ALV-K must be considered to prevent the emergence of novel recombinant variants with amplified virulence. Furthermore, deciphering the genetic characteristics of ALV-K provides a molecular basis for designing targeted prevention and control strategies, including the development of specific diagnostic reagents. Therefore, heightened vigilance and comprehensive surveillance of such recombinant strains are essential to mitigate long-term economic losses and ensure the effectiveness of disease control strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel ALV-K strain designated GD24LHB130 was isolated from Qingyuan Partridge chicken in Guangdong Province, China. Genetic characterization revealed that GD24LHB130 was a recombinant ALV-K strain harboring the pol, LTR, and 3’UTR regions of ALV-J. Compared to the classical ALV-K strains, GD24LHB130 exhibits enhanced replication capacity and LTR promoter activity, which are strongly associated with the recombinant ALV-J LTR. In vivo infection experiments demonstrated that GD24LHB130 significantly inhibited the growth, damaged immune organs, and induced more severe viremia and viral shedding in infected chickens. Notably, GD24LHB130 induced hemangioma-like lesions morphologically similar to those caused by ALV-J. These findings deepen the understanding of the epidemiological characteristics and pathogenic mechanisms of ALV, and highlight the necessity of strengthened surveillance for ALV-K recombinant variants.
