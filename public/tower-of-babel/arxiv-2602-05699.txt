On Circuit Diameter and Straight Line Complexity
Daniel Dadush, Stefan Kober, Zhuan Khye Koh

Abstract
The circuit diameter of a polyhedron is the maximum length (number of steps) of a shortest circuit walk between any two vertices of the polyhedron. Introduced by Borgwardt, Finhold and Hemmecke (SIDMA 2015), it is a relaxation of the combinatorial diameter of a polyhedron. These two notions of diameter lower bound the number of iterations taken by circuit augmentation algorithms and the simplex method respectively for solving linear programs. Recently, an analogous lower bound for path-following interior point methods was introduced by Allamigeon, Dadush, Loho, Natura and Végh (SICOMP 2025). Termed straight line complexity , it refers to the minimum number of pieces of any piecewise linear curve that traverses a specified neighborhood of the central path. In this paper, we study the relationship between circuit diameter and straight line complexity. For a polyhedron P := { x ∈ ℝ n : A x = b , x ≥ 𝟎 } P:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0}\} , we show that its circuit diameter is up to a poly ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{poly}(n) factor upper bounded by the straight line complexity of linear programs defined over P P . This yields a strongly polynomial circuit diameter bound for polyhedra with at most 2 variables per inequality. We also give a circuit augmentation algorithm with matching iteration complexity.



1
Introduction

The
simplex
method is probably the most well-known method for solving linear programs (LPs).
Starting from an initial vertex, it moves from vertex to vertex along edges of the polyhedron towards an optimal vertex.
This method is an example of
circuit augmentation
algorithms – a general algorithmic scheme for solving LPs.
Like the simplex method, given an initial feasible point, a circuit augmentation algorithm proceeds through a sequence of points on the boundary of the polyhedron.
However, instead of only moving along edges, the algorithm is allowed to travel along any direction parallel to a maximal subset of facets.
So, its trajectory may pass through the interior of the polyhedron.

Interior point methods (IPMs)
are another family of LP algorithms that move in the interior of the polyhedron.
Unlike circuit augmentation algorithms, they always stay far away from the boundary.
In this paper, we compare these two algorithmic frameworks via natural lower bounds that govern their performance.
Ultimately, we are interested in the following question:

Can an algorithm that walks from boundary point to boundary point perform as well as an algorithm that moves in the ‘deep’ interior of the polyhedron?

More formally, consider an LP in standard equality form

\min\{\left\langle c,x\right\rangle:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0}\},

(1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\mathrm{rk}(A)=m
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
P
denote the feasible region of (
1
).
For
W:=\mathrm{ker}(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we call
h\in W\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
an
elementary vector
if it is support-minimal, i.e., there is no
h^{\prime}\in W\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(h^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathrm{supp}(h)
.
The support of an elementary vector
\mathrm{supp}(h)\subseteq[n]
is called a
circuit
.
Let
\mathcal{E}(A)\subseteq W
and
\mathcal{C}(A)\subseteq 2^{n}
denote the set of elementary vectors and circuits of
A
respectively.

In a circuit augmentation algorithm, the allowable movement directions are precisely
\mathcal{E}(A)
.
In every iteration
t\geq 0
, the algorithm computes an elementary vector
h\in\mathcal{E}(A)
based on a
pivot rule
, and updates the current solution
x^{(t)}
to
x^{(t+1)}:=x^{(t)}+\alpha h
, where
\alpha>0
is the maximal step size that preserves feasibility.
The elementary vector
h
is called an
augmenting direction
if
\left\langle c,h\right\rangle<0
and
\alpha>0
.
If no augmenting direction exists, then
x^{(t)}
is optimal and the algorithm terminates.
The sequence of generated points
x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\dots
is called a
circuit walk
.

Clearly, the number of iterations taken by
any
circuit augmentation algorithm is lower bounded by the maximum length (number of steps) of a shortest circuit walk between any two vertices of
P
.
Introduced by Borgwardt, Finhold and Hemmecke
[
6
]
, this latter quantity is known as
circuit diameter
.
It is a relaxation of the standard
combinatorial diameter
– the diameter of the vertex-edge graph of
P
.
Analogously, the combinatorial diameter lower bounds the number of iterations taken by the simplex method.
Currently, the best upper bound on the combinatorial diameter is quasipolynomial
[
14
,
22
,
21
]
, having the form
m^{\log O((n-m)/\log(n-m))}
.

Interior Point Methods

While circuit augmentation algorithms iterate through points on the boundary of
P
, interior point methods (IPMs) solve (
1
) by staying in the ‘deep’ interior of
P
.
Path-following IPMs reach an optimal solution by following a smooth curve called the
central path
.
Assuming
P
is bounded and has a strictly feasible solution, this is the parametric curve
x^{\mathrm{cp}}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
defined by

x^{\mathrm{cp}}(\mu):=\argmin_{x\in P}\left\{\left\langle c,x\right\rangle-\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})\right\}.

(2)

The second term in (
2
) is called the
logarithmic barrier
, which pushes the central path away from zero on every coordinate.
As
\mu\to 0
,
x^{\mathrm{cp}}(\mu)
converges to an optimal solution of (
1
).
Path-following IPMs maintain iterates in a certain neighborhood of the central path while geometrically decreasing
\mu
, and hence the optimality gap.
They can be implemented to run in polynomial time.
Standard analyses yield a bound of
O(\sqrt{n}L)
on the number of iterations, where
L
is the total encoding length of
(A,b,c)
.

Straight Line Complexity

The trajectory of a path-following IPM is a piecewise-linear curve in a neighborhood of the central path, whose number of pieces corresponds to the number of iterations.
Thus, the minimum number of pieces of
any
piecewise-linear curve in the neighborhood provides a lower bound on the number of iterations taken by the IPM.
In a surprising result, Allamigeon, Benchimol, Gaubert and Joswig
[
1
]
constructed a parametric family of LPs such that for suitably large parameter values, any piecewise-linear curve in the neighborhood of the central path has exponentially many pieces.
This result was later generalized to arbitrary self-concordant barriers
[
3
]
.

Recently, Allamigeon, Dadush, Loho, Natura and Végh
[
2
]
complemented this lower bound by giving an IPM whose number of iterations matches it up to
\mathrm{poly}(n)
factors.
The guarantees of the IPM are stated in terms of a combinatorial proxy of the central path, which we elaborate below.

Let
v^{*}
denote the optimal value of (
1
).
For
g\geq 0
, let

P_{g}:=\{x\in P:\langle c,x\rangle\leq v^{*}+g\}

(3)

be the feasible sublevel set with optimality gap at most
g
.
Assuming
P_{g}
is bounded, the
max central path
is the parametric curve
x^{\mathfrak{m}}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
defined by

x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g):=\max\{x_{i}:x\in P_{g}\}\qquad\qquad\forall i\in[n].

(4)

For each
i\in[n]
,
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
is a piecewise-linear, concave and non-decreasing function.

For
\eta\in(0,1]
, we say that a function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
lies in the
\eta
-neighborhood
of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}
if
\eta x^{\mathfrak{m}}\leq f\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}
.
For the logarithmic barrier, its central path
x^{\mathrm{cp}}
lies in the
(1/2n)
-neighborhood of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}
.
More generally, for any
\nu
-self-concordant barrier, its central path lies in the
\Omega(1/\nu)
-neighborhood of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}

[
2
]
.
For this reason, the max central path is a good proxy of the central path.
Next, we need the following definition.

Definition 1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function and
\eta\in(0,1]
.
The
straight line complexity
of
\phi
with respect to
\eta
, denoted
\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(\phi)
, is the minimum number of pieces of a continuous piecewise linear function
\psi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying
\eta\phi\leq\psi\leq\phi
.
For
g\geq 0
, we write
\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(\phi,g)
to indicate the analogous quantity for approximating
\phi
in the interval
[0,g]
.

Recall that the trajectory of an IPM is a piecewise-linear curve in a neighborhood of
x^{\mathrm{cp}}
.
If it lies in the
\eta
-neighborhood of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}
, then its number of pieces is at least
\max_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})
.
The negative result of Allamigeon et al.
[
1
]
shows that for any
\eta\in(0,1]
, there exists an LP with
n
variables and
O(n)
constraints such that
\max_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})=\Omega(2^{n})
.
On the positive side, Allamigeon et al.
[
2
]
gave an IPM whose number of iterations is bounded by the sum of straight line complexities of every coordinate of the max central path, i.e.,

O\left(\inf_{\eta\in(0,1]}\sqrt{n}\log\left(\frac{n}{\eta}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})\right).

We remark that the neighborhood parameter
\eta
is not important, as one can show that for
0<\eta<\eta^{\prime}<1
,
\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta^{\prime}}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})=O(\log(1/\eta)/\log(1/\eta^{\prime}))\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})
.

1.1
Our Contributions

Even though circuit diameter and straight line complexity are two geometric quantities that govern the performance of two broad classes of LP algorithms, not much is known about their relationship.
From
[
14
,
1
]
, we know that the circuit diameter is smaller than the straight line complexity in the worst case (quasipolynomial vs. exponential).
However, this is unsatisfactory as it does not provide an instance-by-instance comparison between the two quantities.

Our first result shows that for any LP, the length of a shortest circuit walk from any feasible point to the optimal face can be bounded in terms of the straight line complexity.

Theorem 2
.

Given a bounded LP in the form (
1
), let
F^{*}
be its optimal face.
For any feasible point
x
with optimality gap
g\geq 0
, the length of a shortest circuit walk from
x
to
F^{*}
is

O\left(n^{2}\log(n)\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i},g)\right).

Our next result is an algorithmic implementation of
Theorem

2
.
In particular, we give a circuit augmentation algorithm that solves an LP in the same number of iterations as the bound in
Theorem

2
.
In each iteration, the algorithm calls the
Ratio-Circuit
oracle to obtain an augmenting direction.
This oracle takes as input a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, costs
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and weights
v,w\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\infty)^{n}
, and returns a basic optimal solution to the system

\min\left\langle c,z\right\rangle\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad Az=\mathbf{0},\quad\left\langle v,z^{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle w,z^{-}\right\rangle\leq 1,

(5)

where
(z^{+})_{i}:=\max\{z_{i},0\}
and
(z^{-})_{i}:=\max\{-z_{i},0\}
.
Here, we use the convention
ab=0
if
a=\infty
and
b=0
.
If bounded, a basic optimal solution is either
\mathbf{0}
or an elementary vector
z\in\mathcal{E}(A)
that minimizes
\left\langle c,z\right\rangle/(\langle v,z^{+}\rangle+\left\langle w,z^{-}\right\rangle)
.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a circuit augmentation algorithm such that given any bounded LP in the form (
1
) and any feasible point
x
with optimality gap
g\geq 0
, it returns an optimal solution using

O\left(n^{2}\log(n)\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i},g)\right)

Ratio-Circuit
augmentation steps.

Qualitatively, this signifies that circuit augmentation algorithms can achieve the convergence guarantees of path-following IPMs.
In other words, moving in the ‘deep’ interior of the polyhedron is not more powerful than iterating along boundary points via circuits.

As an immediate corollary, the circuit diameter of a polyhedron is at most the straight line complexity of its associated LPs up to
\mathrm{poly}(n)
factors.

Corollary 4
.

For a polyhedron
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0}\}
, its circuit diameter is

O\left(n^{2}\log(n)\sup_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})\right\}\right).

Consequently, for a polyhedron, if the straight line complexities of its associated LPs are strongly polynomial, so is its circuit diameter.
Recently, strongly polynomial bounds were obtained for the straight line complexity of LPs in the form (
1
) whose constraint matrix
A
has at most 2 non-zeroes per column
[
8
]
.
In particular,
\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})=O(mn\log n)
for all
i\in[n]
.
This class of LPs is equivalent to the minimum-cost generalized flow problem.
Applying
Corollary

4
yields a strongly polynomial circuit diameter bound for these polyhedra.

Corollary 5
.

For a polyhedron
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax=b,x\geq\mathbf{0}\}
where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has at most 2 non-zeroes per column, its circuit diameter is
O(mn^{4}\log^{2}n)
.

Previously, Kalai
[
15
]
gave an upper bound of
(n-m)^{\log_{11/10}19}=(n-m)^{30.893\dots}
on the combinatorial diameter of generalized flow polyhedra with arc capacities, where
m
and
n
are the number of nodes and arcs respectively.
With arc capacities,
\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})=O(n^{2}\log n)
for all
i\in[n]
, so our bound in
Corollary

5
becomes
O(n^{6}\log^{2}n)
.

Further, it was shown in
[
2
]
that the straight line complexity of the primal and dual variables are essentially the same.
In particular, for the dual LP of (
1
)

\max\{\langle b,y\rangle:A^{\top}y+s=c,s\geq\mathbf{0}\},

(6)

we have
\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(s^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})=O(\mathrm{SLC}_{\eta}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}))
for all
i\in[n]
.
Hence, we also obtain a strongly polynomial circuit diameter bound for polyhedra with at most 2 variables per inequality.

Corollary 6
.

For a polyhedron
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:A^{\top}y\leq c\}
where
A^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
has at most 2 non-zeroes per row, its circuit diameter is
O(mn^{4}\log^{2}n)
.

Both of these bounds are an improvement over those in terms of the circuit imbalance measure
[
9
]
, which could be unbounded for such polyhedra.

1.2
Technical Overview

The starting point of proving
Theorem

2
is to decompose the max central path
x^{\mathfrak{m}}
into at most
2\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{SLC}_{1/2}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i})

polarized segments
.
A segment of the max central path
\{x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g):g\in[g_{0},g_{1}]\}
is
polarized
if the variable set
[n]
can be partitioned into
B\cup N
such that the coordinates in
B
are barely changing, while the coordinates in
N
are scaling down linearly with the optimality gap
g
.
More precisely, for every
g\in[g_{0},g_{1}]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g_{1})

\displaystyle\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g_{1})\qquad\quad\;\;\forall i\in B

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{g}{g_{1}}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g_{1})

\displaystyle\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)\leq\frac{4g}{g_{1}}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g_{1})\qquad\forall i\in N

We call
[g_{0},g_{1}]
a
polarized interval
.
Since the max central path multiplicatively approximates the central path, the latter also admits the same polarized decomposition.
The connection between straight line complexity and polarization was already pointed out in the work of
[
2
]
.

Given a current point
x\in P
with optimality gap
g
, let
[g_{0},g_{1}]
be the polarized interval containing
g
.
To finish the proof, it suffices to traverse the interval in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
iterations, i.e., reach a point
x^{\prime}\in P
with optimality gap
g^{\prime}\leq g_{0}
.
We will take two types of circuit steps, which can be obtained using the
Ratio-Circuit
oracle.
The first type of circuit step is obtained by setting
v=\mathbf{0}
and
w=1/x
.
First proposed by Wallacher
[
23
]
for minimum-cost flow, it decreases the optimality gap by a factor of at least
1-1/n

[
19
]
.
So, if
g_{1}/g_{0}\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, then repeating this step for
\mathrm{poly}(n)
iterations does the job.
We remark that for flow problems, such a minimum-ratio cycle can be found in strongly polynomial time.

On the other hand, if
g_{1}/g_{0}\gg 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, then we can no longer rely on the geometric decay of the optimality gap.
Let
(B,N)
be the bipartition associated with this polarized interval.
From (
7
), we know that the contribution of
B
to the optimality gap in this interval is negligible.
So, the goal is to decrease the coordinates in
N
.

For simplicity of notation, we now assume that
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g_{1})=1_{n}

1
1

1

This is achieved by rescaling the instance:
A\rightarrow A{\rm diag}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g_{1}))
and
c\rightarrow{\rm diag}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g_{1}))c
.
. Unlike an IPM, a circuit step inevitably sets a coordinate to zero.
So, one needs to be careful about which coordinates are zeroed out in order to avoid oscillations.
To this end, we consider the
lifting operator

\ell^{W}_{N}:\pi_{N}(W)\to\pi_{B}(W)

\ell^{W}_{N}(z)=\argmin_{(y_{B},y_{N})\in W}\left\{\|y_{B}\|:y_{N}=z_{N}\right\},

(8)

where
\pi_{B}
and
\pi_{N}
denote the coordinate projections onto
B
and
N
respectively.
Using its singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}
, we further subdivide
[g_{0},g_{1}]
into subintervals

\left[g_{0},\frac{g_{1}}{\sigma_{a}}\right],\left[\frac{g_{1}}{\sigma_{a}},\frac{g_{1}}{\sigma_{a+1}}\right],\dots,\left[\frac{g_{1}}{\sigma_{b}},g_{1}\right].

(9)

Now, suppose that
g
lies in some long subinterval
[g_{1}/\sigma_{j},g_{1}/\sigma_{j+1}]
.
By (
7
) and (
8
), any step that significantly decreases the coordinates in
N
must be close to the singular subspace corresponding to
\sigma_{j+1},\sigma_{j+2},\dots,\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}
.
Otherwise, the induced change on
B
is too big and violates feasibility.
Hence, this suggests to move as much as possible in the singular subspace to ensure significant progress.

Let
S\subseteq N
be the set of ‘small’ coordinates relative to their max central path values at the end of the subinterval, i.e.,
S:=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g_{1}/\sigma_{j})\}
.
In the singular subspace, we show the existence of an elementary vector
z\in\mathcal{E}(A)
such that 1)
\left\langle c,z\right\rangle\leq-g/(2n)
; 2)
z_{S}\geq 0
; and 3)
z_{S},z_{B}
are ‘small’.
This motivates our second type of circuit step, where we choose
v
and
w
to induce these properties in an optimal solution of (
5
).
This step ensures that a coordinate in
N\setminus S
is set to zero, while keeping the coordinates in
S
‘small’.
Thus, in
|N|
iterations, we have
S=N
.
At that point,
g/(g_{1}/\sigma_{j})\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}
, so we can use the first type of circuit step to traverse the subinterval in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
iterations.

The augmentation scheme described above is almost fully algorithmic, except that it uses the max central path and its decomposition into polarized segments.
We cannot rely on these additional input when proving
Theorem

3
.
As our proof of
Theorem

2
is robust to
\mathrm{poly}(n)
approximations, it suffices to be able to approximate them.
A straightforward way of doing so is to simply compute the trajectory of the IPM
[
2
]
.
We show that one can avoid running the IPM by estimating all the required information using
\mathrm{poly}(n)
calls to
Ratio-Circuit
together with additional linear algebraic operations.
Details of the algorithmic implementation can be found in
Section

4
.

1.3
Related Work

Besides the simplex method, many network optimization algorithms can be seen as circuit augmentation algorithms.
A prominent example is the Edmonds–Karp–Dinic algorithm
[
10
,
11
]
for maximum flow, which is an efficient implementation of the Ford-Fulkerson method
[
12
]
.
Bland generalized this result by giving a circuit augmentation algorithm for LP
[
5
,
16
]
.
Building on this work, De Loera, Hemmecke and Lee
[
17
]
analyzed different pivot rules for LP, and also extending them to integer programming.
De Loera, Kafer and Sanità
[
18
]
studied the computational complexity of carrying out these rules, as well as their convergence on 0/1-polytopes.

Dadush, Koh, Natura and Végh
[
9
]
upper bounded the circuit diameter in terms of the circuit imbalance measure of
A
.
They also gave a circuit augmentation algorithm with a similar iteration bound.
Circuit diameter bounds have been shown for some combinatorial polytopes such as dual transportation polyhedra
[
6
]
, matching, travelling salesman, and fractional stable set polytopes
[
13
]
.
Nöbel and Steiner proved that computing the circuit diameter is strongly NP-hard
[
20
]
, which was then strengthened to a
\Pi_{2}^{p}
-completeness result by Wulf
[
24
]
.
Black, Nöbel and Steiner showed that approximating the length of a shortest monotone circuit walk is already hard in dimension 2
[
4
]
.

Preliminaries

If not specified, we use the
\ell_{2}
-norm, i.e.,
\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

Let
v^{*}
be the optimal value of (
1
), which we assume to be finite.
For
x\in P
, we denote its optimality gap as
g(x):=\left\langle c,x\right\rangle-v^{*}
.
Given an elementary vector
h\in\mathcal{E}(A)\setminus\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, we define
\mathrm{aug}_{P}(x,h):=x+\alpha h
where
\alpha=\max\{\bar{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}:x+\bar{\alpha}h\in P\}
.

For
g\geq 0
, we define
\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(g):=(1/n)\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathrm{argmax}\{x_{i}:x\in P_{g}\}
as a feasible approximation of the max central path.
Note that
\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(g)\in P

x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g)/n\leq\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(g)\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g)
.

2.1
The
Ratio-Circuit
Oracle

We will use the following oracle to perform circuit augmentations.

Oracle 2.1.

Ratio-Circuit
(A,u,v,w)

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, costs
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and weights
v,w\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\})^{n}
, the output is a basic optimal solution to the system:

\min\;\left\langle u,z\right\rangle\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad Az=\mathbf{0},\,\quad\left\langle v,z^{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle w,z^{-}\right\rangle\leq 1\,,

(10)

and an optimal solution to the following dual program:

\max\;-\lambda\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad A^{\top}y+s=u,\,\quad-\lambda v\leq s\leq\lambda w\,.

(11)

Recall that we use the convention
ab=0
if
a=\infty
and
b=0
.
Note that (
10
) can be reformulated as an LP using auxiliary variables:

\displaystyle\min\;\left\langle u,z^{+}\right\rangle-\left\langle u,z^{-}\right\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad Az^{+}-Az^{-}=\mathbf{0}\,,

\displaystyle\left\langle v,z^{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle w,z^{-}\right\rangle\leq 1\,,

\displaystyle z^{+},z^{-}\geq\mathbf{0}\,,

and its dual LP can be equivalently written as (
11
).
For a feasible solution
(z^{+},z^{-})
to (
12
), we define
z:=z^{+}-z^{-}
as the corresponding solution to (
10
).
By a basic optimal solution to (
10
), we mean the corresponding solution of a basic optimal solution to (
12
).

Lemma 7
.

If (
10
) is bounded, then a basic optimal solution is either
\mathbf{0}
or an elementary vector
z\in\mathcal{E}(A)

2.3
The Lifting Operator and Its Singular Subspaces

Recall the definition of the lifting operator in the introduction.

Definition 14

(Lifting Map and Operator)
.

Given a linear subspace
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a partition
(I,J)
of
[n]
, we define the lifting map

L_{I}^{W}(x):=\arg\min_{w\in W}\{\|w\|:w_{I}=x\}.

Further, we define the lifting operator

\ell_{I}^{W}(x):=\pi_{J}(L_{I}^{W}(x))
.

Since

is a linear operator, it admits a singular value decomposition.

Definition 15

(Singular value decomposition)
.

Let

and

be linear subspaces.
Let

be a linear operator and

Then,

\mathcal{M}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{rk}(T)}\sigma_{i}(T)v_{i}u_{i}^{\intercal},

\sigma_{1}(T)\geq\sigma_{2}(T)\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{\mathrm{rk}(T)}(T)>0
.
We also set
\sigma_{i}(T):=0
for all
\mathrm{rk}(T)<i\leq\dim(U)
, and
\sigma_{0}(T):=\infty
(with the convention that
1/\infty=0
).
We denote by
\sigma_{\min}(T):=\sigma_{\dim(U)}(T)
the smallest singular value of
T
.

Given an interval

, we define

to count the number of singular values of

in

.
Note that
\sigma_{i}(T)\geq\alpha
if and only if

.

For the algorithmic implementation in Section

Our framework can also handle approximate SVD computations using the techniques developed in
[

.

The concept of singular subspaces will be crucial to our analysis.

Definition 16

Let

are exactly the subspaces that attain the minimum in

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(T)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{dim}(S)\geq\mathrm{dim}(U)-i+1\\
S\subseteq U\end{subarray}}\ \max_{x\in S\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\frac{\|T(x)\|}{\|x\|}

(14)

3.2
Bounding the Number of Augmentation Steps

In order to bound the number of augmentation steps carried out by Algorithm
1

1/\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}
in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
iterations.
We achieve this by showing that the smallest singular value of

can never be too big.

Lemma 26
.

Let
\tilde{g}\leq 1/8n
.
Then,
\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}(\ell_{N}^{W})\leq 2n^{3/2}
.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
N
is non-empty (otherwise, there is no positive singular value and the claim holds immediately).
By definition of the smallest singular value, we have
\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}(\ell_{N}^{W})=\min_{x\in\pi_{N}(W)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\|\ell_{N}^{W}(x)\|/\|x\|
.
Choose
z:=\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(\tilde{g})-\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)
.
By construction,
z\in W
, and
z_{N}\in\pi_{N}(W)
.
By (
19
) we have that

\|z_{N}\|=\|\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)_{N}-\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(\tilde{g})_{N}\|\geq\|(1/n-4\tilde{g})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}\|\geq\|1/(2n)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}\|=\sqrt{|N|}/2n.

On the other hand,
\|\ell_{N}^{W}(z_{N})\|\leq\|z_{B}\|\leq\sqrt{|B|}
.
Therefore,
\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{N}(W))}\leq 2n\sqrt{|B|/|N|}\leq 2n^{3/2}
.
∎

i\in[n]
.
To approximate
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)
, we run
Ratio-Circuit
(A,-e_{i},s/\lambda,2/x-s/\lambda)
.
By the constraint
\mathbf{0}\leq s\leq\lambda/x
in (
11
), we have

\frac{s}{\lambda}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{2}{x}-\frac{s}{\lambda}\geq\frac{1}{x}\geq 0

(22)

so this is a valid input to
Ratio-Circuit
.
The next theorem shows that the returned solution yields an
O(n)
-approximation of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)
.

Theorem 29
.

Let
x
be a feasible solution to (
1
) with optimality gap
g\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Let
(z,s,\lambda)
be the primal-dual solution returned by
Ratio-Circuit
(A,c,\mathbf{0},1/x)
.
Let
z^{\prime}
be the primal solution returned by
Ratio-Circuit
(A,-e_{i},s/\lambda,2/x-s/\lambda)
.
Then,
x+z^{\prime}
is a feasible solution to (
1
) with optimality gap at most
2g
and

\frac{x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)}{2n}\leq x_{i}+z^{\prime}_{i}\leq 2x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g).

Proof.

The system (
10
) with
u:=-e_{i}
,
v:=s/\lambda
and
w:=2/x-s/\lambda
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\max\;z_{i}

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad Az=\mathbf{0}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle c,z\right\rangle}{\lambda}+\left\langle\frac{2}{x},z^{-}\right\rangle\leq 1\,.

Notice that
\left\langle c,z\right\rangle=\left\langle s,z\right\rangle
for all
z\in\ker(A)
because
s\in\mathrm{im}(A)+c
.

First,
x+z^{\prime}
is feasible to (
1
) because
z^{\prime}\in\ker(A)
and

\left\langle\frac{1}{x},(z^{\prime})^{-}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\frac{s}{\lambda},(z^{\prime})^{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{2}{x}-\frac{s}{\lambda},(z^{\prime})^{-}\right\rangle\leq 1

by (
22
).
Next, letting
s^{*}
be any optimal solution to (
6
),
x+z^{\prime}
has optimality gap

\left\langle s^{*},x+z^{\prime}\right\rangle=g+\left\langle c,z^{\prime}\right\rangle\leq g+\lambda\leq 2g.

Hence,

x_{i}+z^{\prime}_{i}\leq x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(2g)\leq 2x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)

where the first inequality is by the monotonicity of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
, while the second inequality follows from the concavity and nonnegativity of
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
.

It is left to show the lower bound.
Let
\hat{x}
be a feasible solution to (
1
) such that
\hat{x}_{i}=x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)
.
Since
(\hat{x}-x)/(2n)
is feasible to (
23
), we have
z^{\prime}_{i}\geq(x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)-x_{i})/(2n)
by the optimality of
z^{\prime}
to (
23
).
Thus,

x_{i}+z^{\prime}_{i}\geq\frac{x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)}{2n}+\frac{2n-1}{2n}x_{i}\geq\frac{x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g)}{2n}.\qed

4.2
Guessing the Polarized Partition

In this subsection, we show how to guess the polarized partition
(B,N)
of the current polarized interval, when the interval is sufficient long.
The main idea is to keep track of an approximation of the max central path over a sequence of calls to
Ratio-Circuit
with Wallacher’s rule.
At the end of this sequence, the variables that did not change much (compared to the start) will form our guess of
B
, while the rest will form our guess of
N
.
The number of such calls will be
O(n\log n)
, which can be easily paid for by the overall running time analysis.

We first set up the required notation.
For
t\geq 0
, let
x^{(t)}
be the
t
-th iterate of our circuit walk.
Let
g^{(t)}
denote the optimality gap of
x^{(t)}
.
By applying
Lemma

28
to
x^{(t)}
, we can approximate
g^{(t)}
.
In particular, we obtain
(z^{(t)},s^{(t)},\lambda^{(t)})
such that

\frac{g^{(t)}}{n}\leq\lambda^{(t)}\leq g^{(t)}.

(24)

By applying
Theorem

29
to
x^{(t)}
, we can also approximate
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t)})
.
In particular, for every
i\in[n]
, we obtain
z^{(t,i)}
such that
x^{(t,i)}:=x^{(t)}+z^{(t,i)}
is feasible to (
1
) with optimality gap at most
2g^{(t)}
.
Moreover, denoting
\hat{x}^{(t)}_{i}:=x^{(t,i)}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, we have

\frac{x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t)})}{2n}\leq\hat{x}^{(t)}\leq 2x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t)}).

(25)

Let us define
\bar{x}^{(t)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{(t,i)}/n
as a feasible approximation of
\hat{x}^{(t)}
.
Clearly,
\bar{x}^{(t)}
is feasible to (
1
) with optimality gap at most
2g^{(t)}
.
Furthermore,

\frac{x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t)})}{2n^{2}}\leq\bar{x}^{(t)}\leq 2x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t)}).

(26)

Now, fix an iteration
t_{0}
.
We know that
g^{(t_{0})}
lies in some polarized interval
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
, which is unknown to us.
To guess the polarized partition
(B,N)
of
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
, let
t\geq t_{0}
be an iteration after more than
\lceil 4n\log(4n)\rceil
calls to
Ratio-Circuit
(A,c,\mathbf{0},1/x)
starting from iteration
t_{0}
.
We define our guess as

\tilde{B}:=\left\{i\in[n]:\frac{1}{16n}\leq\frac{\hat{x}_{i}^{(t)}}{\hat{x}_{i}^{(t_{0})}}\leq 4n\right\}\qquad\qquad\tilde{N}:=\left\{i\in[n]:\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{(t)}}{\lambda^{(t_{0})}}\leq\frac{\hat{x}_{i}^{(t)}}{\hat{x}_{i}^{(t_{0})}}\leq 16n^{2}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{(t)}}{\lambda^{(t_{0})}}\right\}.

(27)

Like
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
, the subinterval
[g_{j},g^{(t_{0})}]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized and has the same polarized partition
(B,N)
.
This is because (
13
) holds when
g_{j+1}
is replaced with
g^{(t_{0})}
.
To simplify the analysis, we will assume that
g^{(t_{0})}=1
and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g^{(t_{0})})=1
for all
i\in[n]
.
We also denote
\tilde{g}:=g_{j}
.

The following proposition shows that our guess is correct whenever
g^{(t)}
lies in the same polarized interval as
g^{(t_{0})}
.

Proposition 30
.

If
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
, then
\tilde{B}=B
and
\tilde{N}=N
.

Proof.

By (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{i}\leq 2

\displaystyle\forall i\in[n].

Since
[\tilde{g},1]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized, by (
24
) and (
25
) we also have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8n}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{x}^{(t)}_{i}\leq 2

\displaystyle\forall i\in B,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{(t)}}{n\lambda^{(t_{0})}}\cdot\frac{1}{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{x}^{(t)}_{i}\leq 8\cdot\frac{n\lambda^{(t)}}{\lambda^{(t_{0})}}

\displaystyle\forall i\in N.

Hence,
B\subseteq\tilde{B}
and
N\subseteq\tilde{N}
.
To show equality, it suffices to show that
\tilde{B}\cap\tilde{N}=\emptyset
.
By
Corollary

9
,

\lambda^{(t)}\leq g^{(t)}<\frac{1}{16^{2}n^{4}}\leq\frac{\lambda^{(t_{0})}}{16^{2}n^{3}},

(28)

which completes the proof upon rearranging terms.
∎

4.3
Computing the Points
\bar{x}
and
y^{(0)}

Equipped with a candidate partition
(\tilde{B},\tilde{N})
for the polarized interval
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
, the next step is to compute the lifting operator
\ell^{W_{j}}_{\tilde{N}}:\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W_{j})\to\pi_{\tilde{B}}(W_{j})
, where
W_{j}:={\rm diag}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g_{j+1}))^{-1}W
.
This seems difficult because we don’t know
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g_{j+1})
.
Fortunately, we have an approximation of the max central path at
g^{(t_{0})}
.
So, let
\ell^{W^{\prime}}_{\tilde{N}}:\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W^{\prime})\to\pi_{\tilde{B}}(W^{\prime})
where
W^{\prime}:={\rm diag}(x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})}))^{-1}W
.
We compute the lifting operator
\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}:\pi_{\tilde{N}}(\tilde{W})\to\pi_{\tilde{B}}(\tilde{W})
, where
\tilde{W}:={\rm diag}(\hat{x}^{(t_{0})})^{-1}W
.
Letting
M
be a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis of
\tilde{W}
, it can computed as

\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}=M_{\tilde{B},\bullet}M^{\dagger}_{\tilde{N},\bullet}.

See Lemma 2.3 of
[
7
]
for a proof of the formula above.

Let
\sigma:=\sigma(\ell^{W^{\prime}}_{\tilde{N}})
and
\tilde{\sigma}:=\sigma(\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}})
be the vector of sorted singular values of the two lifting operators.
Assuming that
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})})>\mathbf{0}
, both vectors contain
\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))
entries.
By (
25
) and
Lemma

18
, we have

\frac{1}{4n}\cdot\tilde{\sigma}\leq\sigma\leq 4n\cdot\tilde{\sigma}.

(29)

So, the singular values of
\ell^{W^{\prime}}_{\tilde{N}}
and
\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}
are multiplicatively close.

Let
k
be the largest integer such that
4n^{2}\lambda^{(t_{0})}/\tilde{\sigma}_{k}<\lambda^{(t)}
(we set
k=0
if no such integer exists).
For simplicity, we will again assume that
g^{(t_{0})}=1
and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g^{(t_{0})})=1
for all
i\in[n]
.
We also denote
\tilde{g}:=g_{j}
.

Proposition 31
.

If
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
, then
k<\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))
.

Proof.

By (
28
), we have
\tilde{g}\leq g^{(t)}\leq 1/(8n)
. So, we can apply
Lemma

26
and (
29
) to obtain
\tilde{\sigma}_{\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))}\leq 4n\sigma_{\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))}\leq 8n^{5/2}
.
Then,

\frac{4n^{2}\lambda^{(t_{0})}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))}}\geq\frac{4n^{2}(1/n)}{8n^{5/2}}=\frac{1}{2n^{3/2}}>\lambda^{(t)}

where the last inequality is due to (
28
).
∎

If
k\geq 1
, then by (
29
) and (
24
)

\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}\leq\frac{4n}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}\leq\frac{4n^{2}\lambda^{(t_{0})}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}<\lambda^{(t)}\leq g^{(t)}.

(30)

Let
\hat{g}:=\max\{1/\sigma_{k},\tilde{g}\}
(we set
\hat{g}:=\tilde{g}
if
k=0
).
Our goal is to reduce
g^{(t)}
to below
\hat{g}
.
Due to our choice of
k
,

g^{(t)}\leq n\lambda^{(t)}\leq\frac{4n^{3}\lambda^{(t_{0})}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}}\leq\frac{16n^{4}}{\sigma_{k+1}}.

We may assume that

g^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{512n^{7/2}\sigma_{k+1}}.

(31)

by performing additional
\lceil n\log(8192n^{15/2})\rceil
calls to
Ratio-Circuit
(A,c,\mathbf{0},1/x)
.

The hard case is when
g^{(t)}>f_{1}(n)\cdot\hat{g}
.
In order to apply
Long-Steps
, we need to produce a feasible point
\bar{x}
satisfying Properties
(i)
and
(ii)
, and another feasible point
y^{(0)}
satisfying Properties
(a)
and
(b)
.

We set
\bar{x}:=\bar{x}^{(t)}
.
Then, Property
(i)
holds because
g(\bar{x})\leq 2g^{(t)}
, while
Property
(ii)
is satisfied by (
26
).

To produce
y^{(0)}
, let
V_{d}
be the singular subspace for
\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}
of dimension
d:=\dim(\pi_{\tilde{N}}(W))-k
, and let
\Pi_{V_{d}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
V_{d}
.
We project
-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}/\hat{x}_{\tilde{N}}^{(t_{0})}
onto
V_{d}
, apply the lifting operator
\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}
, and rescale by
\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}
to obtain the following vector in
W

\Delta x:=\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}\circ\left(\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{\tilde{N}}\bigl(\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}/\hat{x}_{\tilde{N}}^{(t_{0})})\bigr),\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}/\hat{x}_{\tilde{N}}^{(t_{0})})\right).

The following lemma bounds the distortion caused by the projection on
\tilde{N}
.

Lemma 32
.

If
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
, then
\|\Delta x_{\tilde{N}}+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}\|\leq 48n^{5/2}\hat{g}
.

Proof.

Since
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
, we have
\tilde{B}=B
and
\tilde{N}=N
.
Let us denote
z:=(\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}(\tilde{g})-\bar{x}^{(t)})/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}\in\tilde{W}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta x_{N}+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\circ\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})})+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})})+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})})+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\right\|

((
25
) and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})})=\mathbf{1}
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\Pi_{V_{d}}(z_{N})+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\right\|

(orthogonal projection is closest)

\displaystyle=2\left\|\Pi_{V_{d}}(z_{N})-z_{N}+\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{N}(\tilde{g})/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\left\|\Pi_{V_{d}}(z_{N})-z_{N}\right\|+\left\|\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{N}(\tilde{g})/\hat{x}_{N}^{(t_{0})}\right\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\|z_{B}\|}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}+8n^{3/2}\tilde{g}\right).

(
Lemma

17
, (
19
) and (
25
))

Since
[\tilde{g},1]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)=\mathbf{1}
, for every
i\in B
, we have

\tilde{x}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(\tilde{g})\leq 1
by (
18
) and
\bar{x}_{i}^{(t)}\leq 2
by (
26
).
Hence,
|z_{i}|\leq 4n
for all
i\in B
by (
25
) and

\left\|\Delta x_{N}+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}\right\|\leq\frac{8n^{3/2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}+16n^{3/2}\tilde{g}\leq\frac{32n^{5/2}}{\sigma_{k}}+16n^{3/2}\tilde{g}\leq 48n^{5/2}\hat{g},

where the second inequality is by (
29
).
∎

By
Lemma

32
, if
\|\Delta x_{\tilde{N}}+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}\|\geq 48n^{7/2}\lambda^{(t)}/f_{1}(n)
, then
\lambda^{(t)}\leq f_{1}(n)\hat{g}/n
.
Combining this with (
24
) yields
g^{(t)}\leq f_{1}(n)\hat{g}
.
In this case, we can cross
\hat{g}
in
\lceil n\log(f_{1}(n))\rceil=O(n^{2}\log n)
iterations of
Ratio-Circuit
(A,c,\mathbf{0},1/x)
.
Henceforth, we assume that

\left\|\Delta x_{\tilde{N}}+\bar{x}^{(t)}_{\tilde{N}}\right\|<\frac{48n^{7/2}\lambda^{(t)}}{f_{1}(n)}.

(32)

The next lemma bounds the norm of
\Delta x_{B}
.

Lemma 33
.

If
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
and (
31
) holds, then
\|\Delta x_{B}\|\leq 1/(16n^{2})
.

Proof.

Since
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
, we have
\tilde{B}=B
and
\tilde{N}=N
.
As
\Delta x_{B}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{B}=\ell^{\tilde{W}}_{N}(\Delta x_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N})
and
\Delta x_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N}\in V_{d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta x_{B}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{B}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}\left\|\Delta x_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}\left\|\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N}\right\|

(
\Delta x_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N}=\Pi_{V_{d}}(-\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{N})
)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}\left\|2n\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}\right\|

(by (
25
) and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})})=\mathbf{1}
)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}\left\|4nx^{\mathfrak{m}}_{N}(g^{(t)})\right\|

(by (
26
))

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{k+1}\cdot 16n^{3/2}g^{(t)}

(
[\tilde{g},1]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{32n^{2}}.

(by (
31
))

By (
25
) and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})})=\mathbf{1}
again, we get

\left\|\Delta x_{B}\right\|\leq\left\|\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{B}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\Delta x_{B}/\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{B}\right\|\leq\frac{2}{32n^{2}}=\frac{1}{16n^{2}}\qed

Let
\alpha>0
be the maximal step size such that

x^{\prime}:=\bar{x}^{(t)}+\alpha\Delta x

satisfies
x^{\prime}_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\tilde{B}
and
x^{\prime}_{i}\geq 8n^{2}\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{i}/\tilde{\sigma}_{k}
for all
i\in\tilde{N}
.
We claim that
x^{\prime}
is the desired point
y^{(0)}
.
First, observe that for every
i\in N
, we have

x^{\prime}_{i}\geq\frac{8n^{2}\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{i}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}\geq\frac{4nx^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g^{(t_{0})})}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}=\frac{4n}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}.

The second and last inequalities follow from (
25
) and (
29
) respectively, while the equality is due to
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(g^{(t_{0})})=\mathbf{1}
.
So, it is left to show that the optimality gap of
x^{\prime}
is at most
f_{6}(n)\cdot\hat{g}
.

Lemma 34
.

If
g^{(t)}\in[\tilde{g},1]
and (
31
),(
32
) hold, then
g(x^{\prime})\leq f_{6}(n)\cdot\hat{g}
.

Proof.

For every
i\in N
, we have

\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}+\Delta x_{i}\leq\left\|\bar{x}^{(t)}_{N}+\Delta x_{N}\right\|<\frac{48n^{7/2}\lambda^{(t)}}{f_{1}(n)}\leq\frac{48n^{7/2}g^{(t)}}{f_{1}(n)}\leq\frac{48n^{7/2}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g^{(t)})}{f_{1}(n)}\leq\frac{96n^{11/2}\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}}{f_{1}(n)}.

The second inequality is by (
32
), the third inequality is by (
24
), the fourth inequality is due to
Lemma

11
and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)=\mathbf{1}
, and the last inequality follows from (
26
).
Hence,

\alpha\leq\frac{\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}}{-\Delta x_{i}}\leq\left(1-\frac{96n^{11/2}}{f_{1}(n)}\right)^{-1}<2.

(33)

Since
\bar{x}^{(t)}_{j}\geq 1/(8n^{2})
for all
j\in B
by (
26
) and the fact that
[\tilde{g},1]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized, it follows from
Lemma

33
that the step size is limited by some coordinate
i^{*}\in N
.

From the definition of
x^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{64n^{3}}{\sigma_{k}}\geq\frac{16n^{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}=\frac{16n^{2}x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i^{*}}(g^{(t_{0})})}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{8n^{2}\hat{x}^{(t_{0})}_{i^{*}}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{k}}

(by (
29
) and (
25
))

\displaystyle=x^{\prime}_{i^{*}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}+\alpha(\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}+\Delta x_{i^{*}})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}-48\alpha n^{5/2}\hat{g},

(by
Lemma

32
)

which implies that
\alpha\geq(\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}-64n^{3}/\sigma_{k})/(\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}+48n^{5/2}\hat{g})
.
Hence, for every
i\in N
, we have

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}+\alpha(\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}+\Delta x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{48n^{5/2}\hat{g}+64n^{3}/\sigma_{k}}{\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}+48n^{5/2}\hat{g}}\cdot\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}+96n^{5/2}\hat{g}

(by (
33
) and
Lemma

32
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{112n^{3}\hat{g}}{\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i^{*}}}\cdot\bar{x}^{(t)}_{i}+96n^{5/2}\hat{g}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{112n^{3}\hat{g}}{x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i^{*}}(g^{(t)})/(2n^{2})}\cdot 2x^{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}(g^{(t)})+96n^{5/2}\hat{g}

(by (
26
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{112n^{3}\hat{g}}{g^{(t)}/(2n^{2})}\cdot 8g^{(t)}+96n^{5/2}\hat{g}

(
[\tilde{g},1]
is
\frac{1}{4}
-polarized and
x^{\mathfrak{m}}(1)=\mathbf{1}
)

\displaystyle\leq 1888n^{5}\hat{g}

On the other hand, for every
j\in B
, we have
x^{\prime}_{j}=\bar{x}^{(t)}_{j}+\alpha\Delta x_{j}<1+2/(16n^{2})\leq 9/8
by
Lemma

33
.
Therefore,
Lemma

19
yields

g(x^{\prime})\leq 8\tilde{g}\|x^{\prime}_{B}\|_{1}+2\|x^{\prime}_{N}\|_{1}\leq 9n\tilde{g}+3776n^{6}\hat{g}\leq 3785n^{6}\hat{g}=f_{6}(n)\cdot\hat{g}.\qed

4.4
Putting Everything Together

Fix an iterate
x^{(t_{0})}
and let
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
be the polarized interval containing
g^{(t_{0})}
.
Let
x^{(t)}
be an iterate after more than
\lceil 4n\log(4n)\rceil
calls to
Ratio-Circuit
(A,c,\mathbf{0},1/x)
, starting from
x^{(t_{0})}
.
We have shown how to compute a guess
(\tilde{B},\tilde{N})
of the polarized partition for
[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
.
This guess is correct if
g^{(t)}\in[g_{j},g_{j+1}]
.
We have also shown how to compute the points
\bar{x}
and

An Improved Amortized Iteration Bound

In this section, we show that the analysis of the number of iterations in the proof of
Theorem

27
is not tight, and we can improve upon it by a factor of
n
.
A similar observation has been used by Allamigeon et al.
[
2
]
to improve the iteration bound of their interior point method.
We remark that an adaption of their proof strategy would imply a similar result for our case, but we choose to provide a shorter new analysis.
The main idea relies on the notion of an
ideal potential function
based on a carefully scaled lifting operator, that scales according to the gap of the iterate.
With the help of this function, we can show that we do not need to deal with the full range of singular values within each polarized interval.
Further, the singular value decomposition that we use within Algorithm
1
approximates the one used in the ideal potential function, which ensures that it is valid to measure our progress in this way.

We begin by giving a stronger version of
Lemma

13
, that is,
Theorem

35
.
In addition to the polarized decomposition of the max central path, we obtain a polynomial factor approximation of the max central path that respects the polarized decomposition and has strong polarization properties itself.
We note that our notion of polarization is defined with respect to the max central path, as opposed to
[
2
]
.
The statement of
Theorem

can be obtained as an implicit corollary of
[
2

We remark that our indexing of the partitions
(B^{(j)},N^{(j)})
is shifted by one, for consistency with the remainder of our paper.
Further, we omit one term on the left-hand side of the inequality of

Theorem 35

(

Let
