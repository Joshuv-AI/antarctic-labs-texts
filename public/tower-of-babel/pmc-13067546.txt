Letter to the Editor Coccolini F, Cremonini C, Moore EE, Civil I, Balogh Z, Leppaniemi A, Horer T, Reva V, Ball C, Kirkpatrick AW, Colli A. Thoracic trauma WSES-AAST guidelines. World Journal of Emergency Surgery. 2025 Oct 15;20(1):78
Authors: Yasir Musa Kesgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traumatic diaphragmatic injuries are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. Particular attention to isolated diaphragmatic injuries and not overlooking them is critical to preventing damage from hernias that may occur immediately or later. The use of a thoracic tube during repair of a diagnosed diaphragmatic injury should be considered a necessary routine procedure. Clearly, the current guidelines resulting from the WSES-AAST collaboration reiterate this traditional recommendation. However, selective tube use should be considered, and the “one-size-fits-all” approach should be discussed. This detail deserves reconsideration to minimize patient morbidity and discomfort.

Comment

Dear Editor

While reading the guidelines on thoracic trauma, prepared in cooperation with WSES-AAST, I noticed a few things regarding diaphragmatic injuries and would like to share them. Overall, the preparation of these guidelines, this cooperation quite valuable [1]. I would like to thank everyone involved for contributing this guideline to the surgical literature.

My point of contention is that there’s a note stating that chest tube insertion is absolutely necessary after repairing a diaphragm injury. And this recommendation is described as having a moderate level of evidence, with a “strong grade of evidence.” Frankly, when we look at the guideline text, while it states that chest tube insertion is necessary, there’s no reference to a study with a high level of evidence according to the Oxford Level of Evidence, the SIGN System, or any other evidence level system. In fact, the sentence itself lacks any reference. Given this situation, I must say that the “strong grade of recommendation” is also surprising. I should also mention that while the necessity of a chest tube is discussed, there’s no mention of whether the assumed repair is performed thoracoscopically or laparoscopically.

Yes, diaphragm injury should not be overlooked. Indeed, spontaneous healing is not expected, and abdominal organ herniation may occur, either rapidly or over several years [2]. It would be best to resolve the problem before it escalates. Leaving aside combined injuries, as a general surgery clinic, we routinely perform diaphragmatic evaluation via diagnostic laparoscopy, especially in cases of penetrating left thoracoabdominal trauma. Our clinic’s general approach is somewhat different. When repairing an isolated diaphragm injury, we instruct the anesthesiologist to initiate positive pressure ventilation before closing the defect or tying the final suture. While positive pressure ventilation continues, we tie the final suture and close the defect. We monitor patients with chest X-rays in the first 12–24 h postoperatively. In the last decade, we have used this approach in approximately one hundred diaphragm repairs, while using a thoracic tube selectively. We believe that making decisions on a case-by-case basis will result in less morbidity. It is important to keep in mind that the traditional approach of using a thoracic tube in all diaphragm repairs may be associated with significant patient discomfort [3].

Sincerely
