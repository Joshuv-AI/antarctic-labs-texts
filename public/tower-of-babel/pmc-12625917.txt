Mind the implementation gap: departure from national guidelines for thromboprophylaxis in traumatic brain injury highlights an urgent need for better evidence
Authors: Jacob Stubbs, James P Byrne
Published: 2025

Patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) are at high risk of experiencing thromboembolic complications. Therefore, the timely initiation of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis is imperative. However, fear of provoking expansion of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) has historically led physicians to delay this decision. Recent studies have shown that thromboprophylaxis within 24–72 hours is feasible without increasing the odds of ICH-related complications. National guidelines offer a framework, typically using the modified Berne-Norwood criteria to stratify by risk of ICH expansion and deferring thromboprophylaxis until repeated head CT is stable.1,3 Despite this current evidence and guidelines, the extent to which practice variation persists has remained unclear.

In a survey of 1700 members of the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST), Burczak et al sought to evaluate current practice variation among trauma surgeons for thromboprophylaxis in TBI.4 Branched logic questions limited the survey to participants who routinely cared for patients with TBI. The survey collected data related to demographics, practice setting, institutional protocols, and factors influencing decision-making for starting thromboprophylaxis. Importantly, questions around thromboprophylaxis initiation centered on preferences for timing after stable head CT.

Of 374 completed surveys (22% of AAST members), 365 respondents routinely cared for patients with TBI. Most participants practiced at institutions with clinical guidelines (92%). Where guidelines existed, 97% of protocols incorporated findings on repeat head CT into the decision-making algorithm. The most frequent preference for starting thromboprophylaxis was ≤24 hours from a stable head CT (43%). When confronted with clinical scenarios, 92% of respondents indicated a goal of initiating thromboprophylaxis within 72 hours of injury. These findings are important because while the American College of Surgeons requires clinical practice guidelines for trauma center verification,5 guideline adoption has historically been inconsistent.6 Clinical guidelines for thromboprophylaxis in TBI now appear to be common. By using repeat head CT and prioritizing initiation of thromboprophylaxis within 72 hours, most surgeons appear to practice in accordance with national guidelines and the modified Berne-Norwood criteria, through which low or moderate-risk patients receive thromboprophylaxis within this timeframe.

While this study finds that thromboprophylaxis after TBI is now more widespread, it shows that significant practice variation continues to persist. Most respondents indicated that they complied with institutional guidelines in only 25–75% of patients. Common reasons for deviating were concern for ICH progression, overall burden of injury, and disagreement with neurosurgery. High rates of departure from protocols show that surgeons are often confronted with clinical dilemmas not addressed in the guidelines, prompting individualized decision-making. This observation is a wake-up call to outstanding gaps in the evidence. Retrospective studies based on large databases lack the granularity to make inferences relevant to patient-level decision-making. The only level 1 evidence on this subject was reported by Phelan et al in the DEEP I study,7 which showed that thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin within 24 hours was safe in patients with low-risk patterns of ICH and stable head CT. There remains a lack of prospective interventional studies evaluating protocols for patients with progression of ICH beyond 24–72 hours or who have undergone neurosurgical interventions. Until such evidence exists, guidelines will fail to stem the degree of practice variation reported in this study.

Major limitations of the study are those of all surveys. Findings were self-reported, and the perspectives of respondents may not be representative of the practice of all AAST members, let alone all trauma surgeons. It is likely that large, academic, level 1 trauma centers are over-represented. To overcome challenges such as this, the AAST Multi-Institutional Trial Committee is leading an initiative to create a representative cohort approach for future surveys. By surveying a random sample of AAST members who have agreed to participate, near-complete response rates will provide insights into current practice in trauma and acute care surgery that are representative and generalizable.

Despite the limitations of the current study, we applaud the authors for their work shedding light on current practice variation in thromboprophylaxis for patients with TBI. These findings show that progress has been made toward appropriate thromboprophylaxis after TBI, but reaffirms that more high-quality evidence is needed to clarify optimal management in patients with high-risk injury patterns.
