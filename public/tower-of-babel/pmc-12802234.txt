SIRT3 deficiency exacerbates cognitive decline by disrupting mitochondrial antioxidant homeostasis in D-galactose-induced aging mice
Authors: Xiaobin An, Chenhong Li, Lu Zeng, Chao Wang, Siyu Huang, Tingting Li, Xing Luo, Yiyuan Guo, Hang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge-related cognitive decline poses a growing clinical burden, with mitochondrial oxidative stress recognized as a key mediator. Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), a mitochondrial deacetylase, is a potential regulator of redox balance, but its role in hippocampal function and cognitive aging, particularly its translational potential, remains unclear.MethodsWe used D-galactose (D-gal)-treated mice (150 mg/kg/day for 8 weeks) to model accelerated aging and neuron-specific SIRT3 knockout (SIRT3cKO) mice. Transcriptomic profiling, behavioral tests, electrophysiological recordings, and mitochondrial analyses were performed. Therapeutic potential was assessed via intrahippocampal AAV-SIRT3 overexpression.ResultsD-gal induced memory deficits, disrupted hippocampal theta oscillations, and mitochondrial degeneration, with transcriptomics identifying SIRT3 as the sole mitochondrially localized differentially expressed gene. SIRT3cKO mice recapitulated these deficits, showing elevated ROS, reduced ATP, aberrant Hif-1α activity, and synaptic/theta rhythm impairments. AAV-SIRT3 overexpression reversed D-gal-induced pathology, which was manifested by the restoration of SOD2 activity, mitochondrial crista density, and theta power, as well as the attenuation of cellular senescence.ConclusionsSIRT3 is a critical regulator of hippocampal mitochondrial redox homeostasis. Augmenting hippocampal SIRT3 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for age-related cognitive impairment, supporting the translation of these findings to clinical interventions for neurological decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07495-y.Keywords: Aging, Cognitive impairment, Synaptic plasticity, Theta rhythm oscillations, Oxidative damage, SIRT3

Introduction

Aging represents an irreversible biological process intimately linked to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders, most notably dementia, a condition characterized by progressive cognitive decline that poses a mounting healthcare burden globally [1, 2]. A defining feature of aging is the systemic accumulation of mitochondrial oxidative damage, driven by chronic imbalances in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses [3, 4]. This oxidative burden disrupts cellular homeostasis, triggering DNA damage, protein misfolding, and lipid peroxidation, which collectively accelerate neuronal degeneration and contribute to cognitive impairment [5, 6]. Importantly, mitochondrial dysfunction, which is characterized by compromised ROS scavenging capacity, has emerged as a key mediator of age-related cognitive decline, highlighting mitochondrial oxidative stress as a critical therapeutic target with translational potential.

Central to mitigating oxidative damage is Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase that governs the mitochondrial redox equilibrium and mediates mitochondrial protein posttranslational modifications (PTMs), with acetylation being pivotal for metabolic enzyme activity and mitochondrial homeostasis [7–9]. In addition to its well-characterized role in regulating the acetylation status of enzymes critical for ROS detoxification (e.g., superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2)) [10], SIRT3 targets a broad spectrum of mitochondrial proteins to orchestrate cellular metabolism across core pathways. The deacetylase activity of SIRT3 modulates enzyme function in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to sustain energy production efficiency while enhancing substrate utilization in fatty acid β-oxidation by targeting rate-limiting enzymes [9]. For example, SIRT3-mediated deacetylation of peroxiredoxin 3 (PRDX3) enhances peroxidase activity to clear mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide [11], directly linking PTM control to antioxidant defense. These molecular actions collectively buffer against oxidative insults, preserving mitochondrial integrity in aging tissues. Preclinical studies have revealed that SIRT3 expression decreases with age, which is correlated with increased mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, ROS overproduction, and memory loss [12–14]. For example, in the hippocampal neurons of aged mice, SIRT3 deficiency exacerbates oxidative damage, impairing long-term potentiation (LTP) and cognitive function, which are reversed upon SIRT3 overexpression [15]. Despite these advances, the precise mechanisms by which SIRT3 orchestrates region-specific antioxidant responses in the aging brain and their translational relevance to human cognitive decline remain incompletely defined.

D-galactose (D-gal), a small-molecule monosaccharide, acts as an essential physiological nutrient for organisms within its physiological concentration range, as it can be oxidized to glucose to supply energy. However, when the level of D-gal in the body increases significantly, its oxidation process abnormally generates aldose and hydrogen peroxide; the latter can further induce the production of superoxide anions and oxygen free radicals, ultimately leading to pathological changes in the physiological activity and function of the organism. Studies have demonstrated that long-term excessive intake of D-gal can trigger systemic oxidative stress, inflammatory response, apoptosis, and cognitive impairment, and that its effects simulate the natural aging process of an organism to a certain extent [16]. Therefore, the D-gal-induced aging model has been widely used in pharmacodynamic evaluation and mechanistic research on aging and aging-related cognitive impairment [17, 18].

In the present study, we investigated the spatiotemporal relationships among SIRT3 depletion, mitochondrial oxidative damage, and cognitive decline in a D-galactose-induced mouse model of accelerated aging. We hypothesize that SIRT3 deficiency disrupts mitochondrial antioxidant systems, leading to irreversible oxidative injury and the collapse of energy metabolism in vulnerable neuronal populations. By mapping SIRT3-dependent redox networks, this work aims to identify precision strategies to amplify endogenous antioxidant defenses, ultimately providing a molecular blueprint to delay or mitigate age-related cognitive decline with direct implications for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (8-week-old, 20 ± 2 g) were procured from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, Liaoning, China) and acclimatized for one week under controlled conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to autoclaved water and standard rodent chow. Aging was induced via daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of D-galactose (D-gal, 150 mg/kg body weight) dissolved in saline for eight consecutive weeks [19, 20]. SIRT3flox/flox mice and Map2-2 A-CreERT2 transgenic mice (Cre-loxP system for conditional gene manipulation) were generated by Saiye Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Harbin Medical University and the Chinese Association for Laboratory Animal Sciences (CALAS Approval No. A5655-01). The experiments strictly adhered to the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on animal welfare and the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for reporting in vivo research.

Behavioral test

The animals underwent a standardized behavioral acclimatization protocol consisting of two sequential phases: initial daily 2-min handling periods by experimenters for two successive days, followed by environmental habituation sessions within the experimental arena for 30 min immediately preceding each test session. To minimize potential interference between experimental measures, a mandatory 24-h intertest recovery period was enforced between consecutive behavioral assessments. The cognitive evaluation employed two validated behavioral paradigms: the novel location recognition (NLR) test for spatial memory assessment and the novel object recognition (NOR) test for episodic memory evaluation. All the behavioral parameters were quantitatively analyzed via the XinRuan behavior analysis system (Shanghai, China). Behavioral experiments were performed as previously described [21, 22].

Novel location recognition:

In the NLR behavioral test for mice, short-term (15-min) and long-term (24 h) memory assessments are conducted through a two-phase protocol. During the habituation phase, two identical objects are symmetrically placed in the apparatus, allowing the mice to explore freely for 8 min to establish spatial familiarity. The testing phase involves two sequential evaluations: (1) For short-term memory, 15 min post-habituation, one object is relocated to a novel position, and the exploration time for both old and new locations is recorded over a 5-min session. (2) For long-term memory, the same relocation and recording procedure is repeated 24 h after habituation.

Novel object recognition:

On Day 1 (Habituation phase), two identical objects were symmetrically placed in the arena, and the mice were allowed 8 min of unrestricted exploration to establish familiarity with the objects. On Day 2 (testing phase), which was conducted after a 24-h retention interval, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object of similar dimensions but differing in shape and surface texture, whereas the other remained unchanged.

All objects and the arena surface were thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. The behavioral parameters were quantified via two metrics: the preference index (PI) = Tnovel or Tfamiliar / (Tnovel + Tfamiliar) and the discrimination index (DI) = Tnovel − Tfamiliar / (Tnovel + Tfamiliar).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the hippocampal tissue samples and cultured neuronal populations via TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following standardized isolation protocols. Reverse transcription of miR-23a was conducted with the ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo Life Science, Osaka, Japan), followed by quantitative amplification via TaqMan™ Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The thermal cycling parameters were as follows: (1) initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min; (2) 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s); and (3) melt curve analysis (65–95 °C, 0.5 °C increments) to verify amplification specificity. Relative quantification of target SIRT3 expression was performed via the comparative 2 − ΔΔCt method, with normalization against GAPDH as an internal reference.

Western blot

Protein extraction was performed following established methods [23, 24] with modifications. Hippocampal tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Beyotime) via a mechanical homogenizer, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined via a BCA assay, with equal amounts (30 µg/lane) of denatured proteins resolved on 10% SDS‒PAGE gels. Electrophoretically separated proteins were transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies. After three 10-min TBST washes, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following additional TBST washes, immunoreactive bands were visualized via an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR). Band intensity was quantified with Image Studio 5.2.5 software (LI-COR), normalized to β-actin expression, and expressed as the fold change relative to the control groups.

Local field potential

The experimental mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 20% urethane solution (1.2 g/kg body weight) for stable anesthesia maintenance and were subsequently immobilized in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, China). The core body temperature was regulated at 37 ± 0.5 °C via a feedback-controlled heating pad throughout the procedure. Anesthetic depth was dynamically maintained through periodic toe-pinch reflex assessments while preserving spontaneous theta oscillations (4–12 Hz) as electrophysiological indicators of appropriate anesthesia levels. Stereotaxic implantation of recording electrodes was performed according to the mouse brain atlas coordinates (Paxinos and Franklin, 2019): anterior-posterior − 2.2 mm from bregma, mediolateral ± 1.35 mm, and dorsoventral − 1.8 mm from the skull surface, targeting the pyramidal cell layer of dCA1. A monopolar tungsten microelectrode (1–2 MΩ impedance; tip diameter 50 μm) served as the recording probe, with an epidural reference electrode positioned 2 mm posterior to the recording site and a subcutaneous ground electrode implanted in contralateral parietal tissue. Neural signals were conditioned via an ME-1 biosignal preamplifier (Chengdu Taimeng Software Co., China) with 0.1–50 Hz bandpass filtering, followed by digitization at a 1 kHz sampling frequency through a BL-420 S data acquisition system (Chengdu Taimeng Software Co.). After 10 min of system stabilization postimplantation, continuous baseline LFP data were acquired for 120 s under resting-state conditions.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) detection

ATP levels were quantified via a commercial ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s specifications. Protein-containing supernatants were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) of lysed cell/tissue homogenates prepared in ice-cold RIPA buffer. Total protein concentrations were determined via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) with absorbance measurements at 562 nm on a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices) for subsequent ATP normalization. ATP concentrations were calculated via four-parameter logistic regression of standard curve data (R² >0.99), with final values expressed as nmol ATP per mg total protein. All reagents were protected from light exposure and maintained at -20 °C until use.

Reactive oxygen species quantification

The levels of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were determined via a fluorescent probe-based assay kit (Beyotime, China) with dihydroethidium (DCFH-DA) as the oxidation-sensitive fluorophore. After anesthesia was administered to the mice, their brains were rapidly harvested. The bilateral hippocampi were then isolated, cut into small pieces (1–2 mm³ in size), and transferred into prewarmed digestion solution. The mixture was incubated in a 37 °C constant-temperature incubator for 15–20 min, with gentle inversion of the container every 5 min to ensure uniform digestion. Upon completion of incubation, an equal volume of prewarmed termination solution was added to halt the digestion process. The digested tissue suspension was transferred to a ground-glass homogenizer and manually homogenized until the tissue clumps dissipated, and the suspension appeared slightly turbid. The homogenized suspension was subsequently filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer into a new Eppendorf (EP) tube. The strainer was rinsed with 500 µL of washing solution to collect residual cells, and the resulting filtrate served as the crude single-cell suspension. Prior to staining, the single-cell suspension was washed twice with prewarmed PBS (pH 7.4) and then loaded with 10 µM DCFH-DA working solution, which was prepared by diluting the stock solution 1:1000 in serum-free DMEM. Following 20 min of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove residual probe. The fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 488/525 nm) was quantified via a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) equipped with a 488 nm laser, with acquisition parameters standardized to 10,000 events per sample at a 35 µL/min flow rate. Data analysis was performed via CytExpert software (v2.4, Beckman Coulter), with the fluorescence intensity normalized to that of the unstained controls.

Superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) activity

For mitochondrial SOD (SOD2/Mn-SOD)-specific measurements, the samples were pretreated with 5 mM potassium cyanide (Cu/Zn-SOD inhibitor) for 45 min at 25 °C prior to the assay. Reaction mixtures containing 20 µL of sample, 160 µL of WST-8 working solution, and 20 µL of enzyme working solution were incubated in 96-well plates (Corning® Costar®) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded via a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), with the reference wavelength set at 630 nm.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase histochemical staining

Hippocampal senescence was evaluated via an SA-β-gal staining kit (Beyotime, China) with optimized tissue processing protocols. Freshly harvested hippocampal samples were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 16–18 h at 4 °C, followed by gradient dehydration in 30% sucrose/PBS solution at 4 °C until cryoprotection. Sagittal sections (20 μm thickness) were obtained via a cryostat microtome (Leica CM1950) maintained at -20 °C and mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides. The staining procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were taken via an LSM800 710 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy

Hippocampal tissues were microdissected from 500-µm coronal brain sections and immediately immersion-fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 8 h. Following three 15-min rinses with PBS, the samples were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1.5 h at room temperature. Dehydration was achieved through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) followed by acetone substitution. The tissues were infiltrated with epoxy resin (Epon 812) and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were obtained via a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome equipped with a diamond knife, collected on 200-mesh copper grids, and double-contrasted with 2% uranyl acetate (15 min) followed by Reynolds’ lead citrate (5 min). The mitochondrial ultrastructure was analyzed via a JEOL JEM-1220 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV. Digital micrographs were acquired via a Gatan Orius SC1000 CCD camera system (Gatan Inc., USA).

Viral microinjection

The recombinant adeno-associated viral (rAAV) constructs, including the experimental chimeric AAV2/8 vector encoding SIRT3 (AAV-SIRT3) and the control vector containing mNeonGreen without SIRT3 (AAV-VEH), were produced by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China) with titers exceeding 1.0 × 10¹² viral genomes/ml. Both vectors featured a CMV promoter-driven expression cassette. For hippocampal delivery, bilateral stereotaxic injections (0.5 µl per hemisphere) were administered to the CA1 subregion (anteroposterior + 2.20 mm, mediolateral ± 1.35 mm, and dorsoventral − 1.80 mm). Following viral infusion, the animals underwent an 8-week recovery period to allow transgene expression prior to initiating D-galactose treatment. Successful viral transduction in the target region was subsequently verified through Western blot analysis of SIRT3 expression.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data met parametric assumptions and are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Continuous variables were analyzed via two-tailed Student’s t test for pairwise comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for multigroup comparisons. All the statistical tests were implemented in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with graphical representations generated via the software’s built-in data visualization toolkit.

Results

D-galactose-induced cognitive decline and cellular senescence in mice

To systematically investigate age-related cognitive deterioration, we developed a chronic D-galactose (D-gal) administration paradigm (150 mg/kg/day, 8 weeks) to establish an accelerated aging mouse model. Longitudinal behavioral phenotyping revealed temporally dissociable impairments in hippocampus-dependent memory domains. The novel location recognition (NLR) test revealed distinct temporal patterns in spatial memory processing. During the training phase with identical objects, all groups presented comparable exploration times and interaction frequencies (Fig. 1A-B), confirming intact sensory perception and the absence of spatial bias. Notably, when a relocated object was tested 15 min post-training, both groups exhibited similar exploration patterns (Fig. 1A-B), suggesting preserved short-term spatial memory. However, a striking difference emerged in the 24-h retention test, where D-gal-treated mice presented a significantly reduced novel location discrimination index in time and frequency (Fig. 1C-D), suggesting specific impairment in long-term memory consolidation rather than acquisition or retrieval processes.

Complementary findings from the novel object recognition test revealed parallel deficits in episodic memory consolidation. Compared with control mice, D-gal- treated mice presented significantly lower novel object preference (NOR) ratios at 24-h retention (Fig. 1E-F), whereas normal novelty preference was maintained during immediate testing. Electrophysiological characterization of synaptic plasticity mechanisms revealed a dissociation between early- and late-phase plasticity. While short-term potentiation measured 10 min post-theta burst stimulation (TBS) remained intact, late-phase LTP maintenance at 60 min post-TBS was severely compromised in D-gal-treated mice (Fig. 1G-H). This temporal profile of plasticity deficits mirrors the behavioral dissociation between intact short-term and impaired long-term memory. Histological analysis via senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining revealed a 2-fold increase in positive cells within the hippocampal DG subfield of D-gal mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 1I-J). Age-related biomarkers such as doublecortin (Dcx) and mini-chromosome maintenance complex component 2 (Mcm2) have been found to decrease with physiological aging. Our qRT‒PCR results revealed that the expression levels of Dcx and Mcm2 in the hippocampi of D-gal-treated mice were significantly lower than those in the hippocampi of WT mice (Fig. S1). These convergent lines of evidence demonstrate that chronic D-gal administration induces cellular senescence and selectively impairs long-term memory processes while preserving short-term memory capacity.

D-galactose impaired hippocampal theta rhythmogenesis in mice

Cross-regional neural coordination across distributed brain networks serves as a fundamental mechanism underlying complex cognitive processes, particularly in memory formation and system consolidation [25, 26]. Within this framework, the hippocampus plays a pivotal role as the primary generator of theta rhythm, which is a 4–12 Hz oscillatory pattern that facilitates temporal coordination of neuronal ensembles through phase synchronization and cross-frequency coupling. Given that hippocampal theta oscillations (4–12 Hz) are neurophysiological hallmarks of spatial navigation and memory consolidation [27], we performed in vivo electrophysiological recordings to investigate the network-level consequences of D-gal exposure (Fig. 2A). Heatmap visualization of time‒frequency decomposition revealed significant alterations in theta-band activity across the hippocampus (Fig. 2B). Power spectral analysis revealed that the peak theta rhythm power was significantly lower in D-gal mice than in WT mice (Fig. 2C-D). In addition, the theta frequency with peak power in D-gal mice was lower than that in WT mice (Fig. 2C and E). This dual impairment in both amplitude and frequency characteristics suggests fundamental alterations in hippocampal network dynamics.

SIRT3 deficiency contributes to mitochondrial oxidative damage in D-gal-treated mice

To investigate the mechanisms underlying D-gal-induced cognitive decline, we performed transcriptome sequencing on hippocampal tissues from D-gal-treated mice. As shown in Figs. 3A and 318 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 238 significantly upregulated genes and 80 downregulated genes, were identified in D-gal-treated mice. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were predominantly associated with oxidative stress regulatory pathways (Fig. 3B), suggesting that oxidative stress may represent a central mechanism in D-gal-induced cognitive impairment. To validate these findings, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in hippocampal cells were quantified via fluorescent probes and flow cytometry. The results revealed a marked increase in ROS levels in D-gal-treated mice compared with those in WT control mice, accompanied by reduced ATP levels (Fig. 3C-E). Transmission electron microscopy further revealed structural remodeling of the hippocampal mitochondria in D-gal-treated mice, characterized by diminished cristae and increased vacuolation (Fig. 3F).

To elucidate the molecular basis of oxidative damage in D-gal-treated mice, we conducted a focused analysis of pathway-associated genes. Among these genes, we examined the subcellular localization and functions of 9 significantly altered genes in the pathway. While SIRT3 has occasionally been reported in the nucleus, in neurons, it is primarily localized to mitochondria where it regulates antioxidant defense [8]. For this reason, we identified SIRT3 as the core target for subsequent research (Fig. 3G). Database interrogation via the Human Protein Atlas confirmed high SIRT3 expression in both human and murine hippocampal tissues (Fig. 3H-I). Western blot analysis confirmed the significant downregulation of the SIRT3 protein in D-gal-treated mice (Fig. 3J). Collectively, these findings indicate that SIRT3 deficiency contributes to mitochondrial oxidative stress in D-gal-induced aging mice.

Neuron-specific knockout of SIRT3 triggers hippocampal oxidative damage

Our electrophysiological findings indicated that cognitive decline in D-gal-induced aging mice is associated with neuronal dysfunction. To clarify whether the downregulation of SIRT3 in neurons is a key mediator of this pathological process, we generated neuron-specific SIRT3 knockout (SIRT3cKO) mice via tamoxifen-induced Cre–loxP recombination. One month post-induction, SIRT3cKO mice presented significant reductions in both SIRT3 mRNA and protein levels in the hippocampus (Fig. 4A-B). Given that superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), a critical mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme, requires SIRT3-mediated deacetylation for activation [28], we assessed SOD2 activity and observed a concomitant decrease in SOD2 activity in the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometric quantification revealed elevated hippocampal ROS levels in the SIRT3cKO mice compared with those in the WT controls (Fig. 4D-E). Moreover, a reduction in ATP levels was detected in the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. 4F). Ultrastructural analysis further revealed mitochondrial abnormalities in SIRT3cKO mice, including crista loss and vacuolation (Fig. 4G), mirroring the pathological features observed in aged D-galactose-treated mice.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (https://www.kegg.jp/pathway) revealed that SIRT3 modulates the ROS-dependent regulation of Hif-1α transcripts. Notably, Hif-1α activation exacerbates oxidative damage through the transcriptional reprogramming of stress-responsive genes [29, 30]. Targeted qRT‒PCR profiling of Hif-1α-associated oxidative stress markers confirmed altered transcriptional activity in the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. 4H). Additionally, β-galactosidase staining revealed accelerated cellular senescence in the hippocampi of the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. 4I-J). Consistently, qRT‒PCR analysis revealed significant downregulation of the mRNA expression of the senescence markers Dcx and Mcm2 in the hippocampi of the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. S2). These data collectively demonstrate that neuron-specific SIRT3 ablation disrupts antioxidant homeostasis in the hippocampus, driving oxidative injury and premature aging phenotypes.

Neuron-specific knockout of SIRT3 impaired the cognitive function of mice

Having established that neuronal SIRT3 ablation induces oxidative damage, we further assessed cognitive function in SIRT3cKO mice. In the novel location recognition test, while SIRT3cKO mice retained intact sensory perception and exhibited no spatial bias under baseline conditions, they displayed significantly reduced exploration of the relocated object compared with WT controls, indicating impaired spatial memory (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the novel object recognition test revealed a diminished preference for unfamiliar objects in the SIRT3cKO mice, suggesting compromised episodic memory (Fig. 5B).

In addition to behavioral deficits, synaptic plasticity is markedly impaired in SIRT3cKO mice. We monitored TBS-induced hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) and observed a time-dependent attenuation of normalized field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) slopes in the SIRT3cKO mice compared with their WT littermates (Fig. 5C-E). Furthermore, neuronal SIRT3 deficiency disrupted hippocampal theta rhythm dynamics. Time‒frequency decomposition heatmaps revealed prominent alterations in theta-band oscillatory activity (4–12 Hz) in the SIRT3cKO mice (Fig. 5F-G). Power spectral analysis revealed a significant reduction in peak theta rhythm power and a leftward shift in the dominant theta frequency in the SIRT3cKO mice compared with the WT controls (Fig. 5H-J). Notably, SIRT3cKO mice presented parallel theta rhythm abnormalities (Fig. 5H-J), reinforcing the translational relevance of this model. Collectively, these behavioral and electrophysiological findings demonstrate that neuron-specific SIRT3 deletion critically impairs hippocampus-dependent cognitive functions.

Hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression alleviates cognitive decline in D-gal-treated mice

To determine whether hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression rescues cognitive deficits in D-gal mice, we administered bilateral intrahippocampal injections of adeno-associated virus carrying the SIRT3 gene (AAV-SIRT3) or control vector (AAV-VEH, NC group) 8 weeks prior to D-gal treatment. Western blot analysis confirmed robust SIRT3 upregulation in the hippocampi of WT-OE mice compared with those of NC control mice (Fig. S3), confirming the successful construction and functional validity of our SIRT3-overexpressing AAV-SIRT3. Additionally, intrahippocampal injection of AAV-SIRT3 reversed the D-gal-induced downregulation of SIRT3 in the hippocampi of D-gal-induced aging mice (Fig. 6A). In the novel location recognition test, SIRT3 overexpression significantly rescued the deficit in spatial memory, as evidenced by the increased exploration time of the relocated object in the AAV-SIRT3 mice compared with the D-gal mice (Fig. 6B). Similarly, the novel object recognition test demonstrated that SIRT3 restoration attenuated episodic memory decline, with AAV-SIRT3 mice exhibiting prolonged interaction time and frequency with the novel object compared with D-gal mice (Fig. 6C).

In addition to its ability to improve behavior, SIRT3 overexpression ameliorated impairments in hippocampal synaptic plasticity in D-gal-treated mice. TBS-induced LTP, as measured by normalized fEPSP slopes, was partially restored in the AAV-SIRT3 mice compared with the D-gal mice (Fig. 6D‒F). Furthermore, spectral analysis of local field potentials revealed that, compared with D-gal, SIRT3 overexpression mitigated theta rhythm abnormalities, as shown by increased peak theta power and a normalized theta frequency distribution in the AAV-SIRT3 mice (Fig. 6G-K). These findings collectively demonstrate that targeted SIRT3 overexpression in the hippocampus counteracts D-gal-induced cognitive decline and restores synaptic plasticity and oscillatory network activity.

Hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression alleviates mitochondrial oxidative damage in D-gal-treated mice

To further determine whether SIRT3 overexpression improves cognition by mitigating oxidative damage, we systematically evaluated oxidative stress parameters across the experimental groups. SIRT3 overexpression markedly increased SOD2 activity in the hippocampi of D-gal-induced aging mice (Fig. 7A). Concurrently, hippocampal ROS levels were significantly reduced, whereas ATP levels were restored to near wild-type levels in SIRT3-overexpressing mice (Fig. 7B-D). Ultrastructural analysis via transmission electron microscopy revealed that SIRT3 overexpression ameliorated mitochondrial abnormalities, including cristae regeneration and reduced vacuolation, in D-gal-treated mice (Fig. 7E).

At the molecular level, SIRT3 restoration normalized aberrant Hif-1α transcript expression profiles in D-gal mice, as quantified by qRT‒PCR analysis of oxidative stress-associated Hif-1α targets (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, SIRT3 overexpression attenuated cellular senescence and increased the mRNA levels of Dcx and Mcm2 in the hippocampi of D-gal mice (Figs. 7G-H and S4). Collectively, these data establish that hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression alleviates mitochondrial oxidative damage.

Discussion

The current study delineates a critical role for SIRT3 in mitigating D-galactose-induced cognitive decline through preservation of mitochondrial redox homeostasis and maintenance of hippocampal network dynamics. Our findings establish that chronic D-gal administration recapitulates key features of brain aging with translational relevance, including oxidative stress-dependent synaptic dysfunction, impaired theta rhythmogenesis, and cellular senescence. Crucially, we demonstrated that neuronal SIRT3 deficiency serves as a pivotal molecular link between mitochondrial oxidative damage and age-related cognitive deterioration, while targeted hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression reversed these deficits. These results advance our understanding of the role of mitochondrial sirtuins in brain aging and highlight SIRT3 as a promising therapeutic target for age-associated neurodegenerative conditions, with direct implications for the development of disease-modifying interventions.

Central to the observed cognitive impairments is the selective vulnerability of long-term memory consolidation, which aligns with the temporal dissociation in synaptic plasticity deficits. Although the interaction frequencies between D-gal-treated and control groups during the 15-min post-training test in the NLR task did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), a subtle downward trend in exploration time was observed in the treatment group. This trend may indicate compensatory mechanisms of early synaptic function, as short-term memory encoding might rely on the remaining intact synaptic plasticity, such as the STP, which could buffer the impact of chronic D-gal exposure. The preservation of short-term memory and early-phase LTP, in contrast with impaired late-phase LTP maintenance, suggests that D-gal-induced oxidative stress preferentially disrupts protein synthesis-dependent processes required for enduring synaptic modifications. This finding parallels clinical observations of delayed memory consolidation deficits in age-related cognitive decline [31], underscoring the translational relevance of our model. The concurrent attenuation of hippocampal theta oscillations in D-gal mice further implicates network-level dysregulation in cognitive impairment. Given that theta rhythms coordinate cross-regional communication during memory encoding [32], their disruption likely contributes to inefficient information transfer between the hippocampus and cortical regions, impairing system consolidation.

Our multiomics approach identified SIRT3 downregulation as a key driver of mitochondrial dysfunction in D-gal-treated mice. As a major mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase, SIRT3 regulates antioxidant defense systems through the activation of SOD2 [33]. The observed reduction in SOD2 activity and subsequent accumulation of ROS in SIRT3-deficient mice directly links mitochondrial oxidative stress to synaptic dysfunction. Notably, ultrastructural mitochondrial abnormalities in both D-gal- and SIRT3cKO mice were characterized by crista loss and vacuolation, suggesting conserved mechanisms of age-related mitochondrial deterioration. The downstream activation of Hif-1α signaling in SIRT3-deficient hippocampi may further exacerbate oxidative damage through the transcriptional upregulation of glycolytic enzymes and ROS-generating pathways [34], creating a pathogenic feedback loop that accelerates cellular senescence.

The rescue of cognitive and electrophysiological deficits through hippocampal SIRT3 overexpression provides compelling evidence for its therapeutic potential. By restoring SOD2 activity and suppressing Hif-1α-mediated oxidative stress, SIRT3 augmentation preserved mitochondrial integrity and ATP levels, thereby rescuing late-phase LTP and theta rhythm coherence. These findings support previous reports of the neuroprotective effects of SIRT3 in metabolic disorders [35] through its ability to reverse age-related network dysfunction. The partial restoration of theta rhythm parameters following SIRT3 overexpression suggests that additional mechanisms, such as ion channel regulation or interneuron dysfunction [36–38], may contribute to oscillatory deficits and warrant further investigation. Notably, SIRT3 activity often increases under cellular stress conditions [33], which contrasts with the SIRT3 downregulation we detected in D-gal-induced aging mice. This discrepancy, however, can be resolved by distinguishing between the nature and duration of stress stimuli, as well as the adaptive capacity of SIRT3-mediated pathways. The well-documented increase in SIRT3 activity in response to stress primarily reflects the adaptive response of the cell to acute or mild stress [33, 39]. In contrast, the SIRT3 downregulation observed in our study is a consequence of chronic, aging-related stress induced by long-term D-gal administration. However, the significant decrease in SOD2 activity in the hippocampal tissue of D-gal-treated mice also indirectly indicates that the activity of SIRT3 is concomitantly reduced rather than increased. The core basis for this association lies in the fact that the activation of SOD2 activity is completely dependent on the deacetylation mediated by SIRT3 [28]. Moreover, since the CMV promoter is not neuron specific, the SIRT3-overexpressing virus may drive SIRT3 expression in both neuronal and nonneuronal cells (e.g., astrocytes and microglia) in the hippocampus. Although our results confirmed the therapeutic potential of global SIRT3 overexpression, clarifying the specific role of neuron-specific SIRT3 overexpression remains essential for in-depth research.

While this study focused on hippocampal mechanisms, several limitations merit consideration. First, the D-gal model primarily mimics accelerated oxidative aging, which may not fully capture the multifactorial nature of human brain aging, including amyloid-beta accumulation and neuroinflammation [40]. Second, the contribution of nonneuronal SIRT3 in glial cells to cognitive outcomes remains unexplored. Given that astrocytic SIRT3 modulates antioxidant responses [41, 42], future studies should delineate cell type-specific roles. Third, this study did not quantify the ratio of inactive (44 kDa) to active (28 kDa) SIRT3 isoforms, which hinders precise clarification of whether D-gal-induced aging impairs SIRT3 function by reducing total expression and disrupting maturation into the active 28 kDa form. Future studies should measure this ratio to elucidate the role of SIRT3 in hippocampal aging and improve targeted therapy specificity. Finally, the therapeutic window for SIRT3 intervention in age-related cognitive decline requires longitudinal assessment in natural aging models, ideally alongside cognitive testing paradigms validated for translational relevance [43].

In conclusion, our results elucidate a SIRT3-dependent pathway through which mitochondrial oxidative stress disrupts hippocampal plasticity and network dynamics to drive cognitive decline (Fig. 8). By identifying theta rhythm abnormalities as a functional consequence of SIRT3 deficiency, this work links molecular mechanisms with systems-level neurophysiology. These findings position SIRT3 enhancers as a promising strategy to counteract age-related memory impairment and highlight the preservation of the mitochondrial redox balance as a critical target for healthy brain aging, supporting further development of SIRT3-directed therapies in clinical trials for the early stage of cognitive impairment.
