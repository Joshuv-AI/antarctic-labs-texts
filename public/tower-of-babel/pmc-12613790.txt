Influence of early enteral nutrition on mortality of adult patients with sepsis: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jing Xu, Junli Zhang, Libin Yang, Zhongji Han, Maokui Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly enteral nutrition (EEN) has been proposed to improve clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with sepsis. However, the survival benefit of EEN remains uncertain. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effect of EEN on all-cause mortality in adult patients with sepsis.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang was conducted up to May 25, 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing EEN (initiated within 24–48 h of admission or sepsis diagnosis) vs. non-early enteral nutrition (delayed enteral nutrition or parenteral nutrition) in adult septic patients were included. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using a random-effects model by incorporating the influence of potential heterogeneity.ResultsTen RCTs involving 582 patients (281 EEN, 301 controls) were included. Compared to controls, EEN significantly reduced all-cause mortality (OR: 0.59; 95% CI 0.37–0.94; p = 0.03), with no significant statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results (OR range: 0.48–0.67; all p < 0.05). Subgroup analyses showed the mortality benefit of EEN was consistent across subgroups defined by sepsis severity (OR: 0.61 vs. 0.56), sepsis diagnostic criteria (Sepsis-1.0/2.0: OR 0.83 vs. Sepsis-3.0: OR 0.51), patient age, sex distribution, EEN initiation time (within 24 vs. 48 h), and mortality follow-up duration (p for subgroup difference all > 0.05).ConclusionsEEN significantly reduces mortality in adult patients with sepsis. High-quality RCTs are warranted to determine the optimal regimen of EEN in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03340-6.Keywords: Early enteral nutrition, Sepsis, Mortality, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1, 2]. It is a major global health burden, affecting over 48.9 million people annually and accounting for nearly 20% of all deaths worldwide [3, 4]. Despite advances in critical care, sepsis continues to carry high mortality, particularly among patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission [5, 6]. In this context, identifying effective supportive interventions to reduce sepsis-related mortality is of utmost clinical importance [7]. Nutritional support plays a vital role in the management of critically ill patients with sepsis by modulating immune response, maintaining gut barrier function, and preventing malnutrition and catabolism [8, 9]. Among available nutritional strategies, early enteral nutrition (EEN)—defined as the initiation of enteral feeding within the first 24–48 h of ICU admission or sepsis diagnosis—has emerged as a potentially beneficial intervention [10, 11]. Compared to delayed enteral nutrition (DEN) or parenteral nutrition (PN), EEN may offer several physiological advantages, including attenuation of systemic inflammation, preservation of gastrointestinal integrity, and reduction in infectious complications [12–14]. However, its impact on survival in septic patients remains controversial.

A growing number of studies have investigated the clinical benefits of EEN in sepsis [15–24], including a recent comprehensive meta-analysis of randomized and non-randomized trials that showed EEN could reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation and improve Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores [25]. However, the same study found no statistically significant reduction in mortality, likely due to the inclusion of heterogeneous study designs and patient populations [25]. Furthermore, the previous meta-analyses have not focused exclusively on high-quality RCTs, and the effects of EEN on mortality remain to be determined. To address this gap, we conducted an updated meta-analysis including only RCTs to evaluate the influence of EEN on all-cause mortality in adult patients with sepsis. This study aimed to provide more robust evidence regarding the survival benefit of early nutritional intervention and to explore potential modifiers through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Materials and methods

The guidelines set forth by PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [26] and the Cochrane Handbook [27] were followed in the design and performing of the study. The protocol of the meta-analysis has been registered at PROSPERO with the identifier CRD420251119194.

Reviews, editorials, preclinical studies, non-RCTs, studies involving pediatric populations or non-septic patients, without control groups of non-EEN, or not reporting mortality outcomes were excluded. In cases where studies included overlapping patient populations, the one with the largest sample size was selected for inclusion in the meta-analysis.

Database search

The PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, Wanfang, and CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure) databases were searched using the combination of the following terms: (1) “early” OR “premature”; (2) “enteral nutrition” OR “enteral feeding” OR “tube feeding” OR “enteric nutrition” OR “enteric feeding” OR “intragastric nutrition” OR “intragastric feeding” OR “intestinal nutrition” OR “intestinal feeding” OR “intraintestinal nutrition” OR “intraintestinal feeding”; (3) “sepsis” OR “septic” OR “septicemia”; and (4) “random” OR “randomly” OR “randomized” OR” randomized” OR “placebo” OR “control” OR “allocated”. Only studies that included human subjects and were published as full-length articles in peer-reviewed journals in English or Chinese were considered. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of relevant reviews and primary studies were also examined. The final search of the databases was completed on May 25, 2025.

Data collection and quality evaluation

Two reviewers independently performed the literature search, data extraction, and methodological quality evaluation. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the corresponding author. Extracted data included general study characteristics (e.g., first author, year of publication, and study country), design type (double-blind, single-blind, or open-label), participant demographics (including diagnosis, sample size, average age, and sex distribution), details of EEN and controls, and follow-up duration of mortality. The methodological quality of RCTs included in this meta-analysis was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool [27]. This tool evaluates five domains of potential bias: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was judged as having low risk of bias, some concerns, or high risk of bias, and an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned accordingly. The certainty of evidence was independently evaluated by two reviewers using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach, which considers factors, such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and potential publication bias [28]. Evidence quality was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with the corresponding author.

Statistical analysis

The influence of EEN on mortality of patients with sepsis was summarized as odds ratio (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) [27]. Significance of statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test [27]. The I2 statistic was also calculated, with I2 < 25%, 25–75%, and > 75% indicating mild, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively [29]. A random-effects model was applied to synthesize the data, as it accounts for potential variability across studies [27]. Sensitivity analysis by excluding one study at a time was performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings [27]. Subgroup analysis was also conducted to evaluate the impact of study characteristics on outcomes, such as the diagnosis of the patients (sepsis only or also involving septic shock), diagnosis criteria for sepsis, mean ages of the patients, proportions of men, timing of EEN (within 24 or 48 h of admission), and timing of follow-up (within hospitalization, 28 or 30 days). The medians of continuous variables were selected as the cutoffs for defining subgroups, to maintain a balanced number of studies in each subgroup. Publication bias was assessed through visual examination of funnel plots and Egger’s regression asymmetry test [30], with a p value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan (version 5.3; Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Study characteristics

An overview of the included studies is presented in Table 1. This meta-analysis included 10 randomized controlled trials published between 2003 and 2025, conducted in China [16–20, 23, 24], the USA [21, 22], and Italy [15], with a total of 582 adult patients with sepsis or septic shock. Sepsis was diagnosed according to Sepsis-1.0 in two studies [15, 18], Sepsis-2.0 in one study [19], or Sepsis-3.0 in seven studies [16, 17, 20–24]. The sample sizes ranged from 31 to 79 patients per trial. The mean age of participants varied from 54.8 to 70.3 years, and the proportion of male patients ranged from 52.9% to 69.3%. EEN was initiated within 24–48 h of admission or diagnosis, using nasogastric or nasojejunal routes with various formulas (e.g., polymeric, peptide-based, immunonutrition, or dextrose). Comparator groups received DEN in nine [16–24] studies or PN [15] in another study, typically initiated 48–72 h after admission or after clinical stabilization. Follow-up durations for the outcome of mortality ranged from in-hospital monitoring in three studies [16, 18, 21] to 28 or 30 days in the other seven studies [15, 17, 19, 20, 22–24]. No serious adverse events related to EEN were reported in the included studies.

Risk-of-bias evaluation

As shown in Table 2, one study [22] was rated as low risk of bias across all domains using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. The remaining nine studies were judged to have some concerns [15–17, 19–21, 23, 24] or high risk [18], primarily due to open-label designs, lack of blinding, and unclear allocation concealment. Nonetheless, outcome measurement bias was low in all studies since mortality, as the primary outcome, is objective and unlikely to be influenced by blinding status. Most studies used intention-to-treat analyses and had low attrition. Selective reporting was generally not a major concern, although several trials lacked protocol registration or detailed descriptions of control interventions [15, 18, 24]. Overall, the risk of bias was considered acceptable for inclusion but varied across studies.

Influence of EEN on mortality of adult patients with sepsis

Overall, 281 patients with sepsis were allocated to the EEN group and 301 patients in the control group. The pooled results of the 10 RCTs [15–24] showed that compared to controls with DEN or PN, EEN significantly reduced the risk of mortality in adult patients with sepsis (OR: 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.94, p = 0.03; Fig. 2) with no significant heterogeneity (p for Cochrane Q test = 0.45; I2 = 0%). Further sensitivity analysis by excluding one study at a time showed consistent results (OR: 0.48–0.67, p all < 0.05). In particular, the sensitivity analysis excluding the only one study with high risk of bias [18] showed similar results (OR: 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.99, p = 0.04; I2 = 3%). Subsequent subgroup analyses showed similar results in studies including patients with sepsis only and in those also including patients with septic shock (OR: 0.61 vs. 0.56, p for subgroup difference = 0.89; Fig. 3A), in studies with sepsis diagnosed with the Sepsis-1.0/2.0 criteria and the Sepsis-3.0 criteria (OR: 0.83 vs. 0.51, p for subgroup difference = 0.51; Fig. 3B), in studies with the mean ages of the patients < or ≥ 60 years (OR: 0.67 vs. 0.51, p for subgroup difference = 0.61; Fig. 4A), and in studies with the proportion of men ≤ or > 55% (OR: 0.85 vs. 0.42, p for subgroup difference = 0.16; Fig. 4B). Finally, consistent results were observed in studies with EEN initiated within 24 or 48 h of admission (OR: 0.56 vs. 0.62, p for subgroup difference = 0.87; Fig. 5A), and in studies reporting inhospital mortality and the mortality within 28 or 30 days (OR: 0.55 vs. 0.61, p for subgroup difference = 0.84; Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This meta-analysis demonstrated that EEN, initiated within 24–48 h of ICU admission or sepsis diagnosis, significantly reduced all-cause mortality in adult patients with sepsis compared to non-early enteral nutrition strategies, including DEN and PN. The observed mortality benefit was consistent across various patient characteristics and clinical contexts, with robust findings confirmed by sensitivity analyses and no evidence of significant heterogeneity or publication bias. These results support the integration of EEN into standard sepsis care and provide updated evidence based solely on RCTs, strengthening the validity of the survival benefit.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the association between EEN and reduced mortality in septic patients. First, early initiation of enteral feeding helps maintain gut mucosal integrity and reduces intestinal permeability, thereby limiting bacterial translocation and systemic inflammatory responses [31, 32]. EEN also promotes the release of gastrointestinal hormones and supports splanchnic perfusion, contributing to improved metabolic and immunological homeostasis during critical illness [33]. Compared to PN, EEN avoids the complications associated with central venous catheters [34] and hyperglycemia [35], both of which are linked to increased infection risk and worse outcomes. In contrast to DEN, EEN facilitates earlier delivery of essential nutrients, prevents cumulative caloric deficits, and may attenuate catabolic processes that compromise immune function and organ recovery [36–38]. However, it should also be noted that the definition of “early” enteral nutrition varies across the literature. While we defined EEN as initiation within 24–48 h of admission or diagnosis of sepsis, some guidelines and clinical studies consider feeding within 72 h as “early” in the ICU setting [39]. This broader interpretation underscores that the mortality benefit observed in our analysis may be consistent with a spectrum of early initiation windows, although more precise comparisons between < 48 h and 48–72 h warrant further investigation.

The subgroup analyses further reinforce the overall findings by showing that the mortality benefit of EEN persisted across multiple stratifications. The beneficial effect was not significantly modified by sepsis severity (sepsis vs. septic shock), diagnostic criteria (Sepsis-1.0/2.0 vs. Sepsis-3.0), patient age, sex distribution, timing of EEN initiation (within 24 vs. 48 h), or mortality follow-up duration. These findings suggest that the survival advantage conferred by EEN is robust and not confined to specific subpopulations or definitions of sepsis. Notably, trials using Sepsis-3.0 criteria—associated with more precise identification of patients with organ dysfunction—tended to show stronger effects, which may reflect a greater potential for nutritional interventions to alter outcomes in more clearly defined, high-risk populations [40]. Sensitivity analyses excluding each trial one at a time yielded stable effect sizes, indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled results. Of note, one included RCT [22] tested a low-dose enteral dextrose infusion (50% dextrose at 10 mL/h for 24 h) rather than a conventional enteral nutrition formula. This was a standardized, commercially available solution, not an in-house preparation, and it was administered under strict trial conditions to explore mechanistic effects on incretin hormones and glucose metabolism. The caloric provision was equivalent to trophic feeding (~ 400 kcal/day) and not intended to represent full enteral nutrition [22]. These unique aspects should be considered when interpreting pooled results, as the intervention differs from typical protein- or peptide-based formulas used in most other trials.

The strengths of this meta-analysis include a comprehensive and up-to-date literature search across six major English and Chinese databases, the inclusion of only RCTs to minimize confounding, the use of rigorous methods for risk of bias assessment and evidence grading, and detailed subgroup and sensitivity analyses. These methodological features enhance the reliability and generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, mortality, as a clinically objective outcome, minimizes detection bias and strengthens the robustness of the conclusions drawn. The absence of statistical heterogeneity and publication bias also suggests a consistent and unbiased effect estimate. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample sizes of individual RCTs were modest, and most were single-center trials, which may limit external validity. Second, the definitions and compositions of EEN and control interventions varied across studies. Some trials used immunonutrition or dextrose-based formulas, while control groups ranged from PN to DEN with different initiation thresholds and caloric targets. This heterogeneity in nutritional regimens may influence the magnitude of observed effects and limits direct comparisons. In addition, feeding modalities of EEN (continuous or intermittent) may also affect the mortality outcome of these patients, and future studies are warranted for further investigation [41]. However, subgroup analyses by dose or regimen of EEN could not be performed. The included RCTs applied variable feeding formulas and caloric targets, and the reporting of actual delivered doses was inconsistent. This heterogeneity precluded meaningful analysis of dose–response relationships, which warrants further investigation in future trials with standardized nutrition protocols. Third, while risk of bias was moderate in most studies, one trial was rated as high risk due to unclear randomization or unblinded design. Another limitation is that more than half of the included RCTs were conducted in China, with only a few trials from Western countries. This geographic homogeneity may have contributed to the favorable and consistent outcomes observed, but it also limits the external validity of the findings. The relatively small number of eligible RCTs (n = 10) further constrains the strength of evidence, highlighting the need for larger, multicenter, and geographically diverse studies to confirm the generalizability of the survival benefit of EEN in sepsis. Finally, the potential impact of co-interventions, such as glycemic control, antimicrobial timing, or vasopressor use, was not assessable due to limited reporting in the original trials.

Another limitation of the present study is that none of the included RCTs reported the incidence of mesenteric or bowel ischemia, which remains a major safety concern for early enteral feeding. Although randomized evidence is lacking, case reports suggest that EEN may occasionally precipitate catastrophic complications. One report described bowel necrosis associated with early postoperative jejunal feeding, estimating an incidence of mesenteric ischemia of up to 3.5% among surgically fed patients, with nearly universal mortality [42]. Another case described non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia in a critically ill patient on vasopressors despite cautious advancement of enteral feeding [43]. Furthermore, a recent narrative review [44] highlighted the uncertainty around the safest timing of EEN initiation in patients with shock receiving vasoactive drugs, underscoring the need for cautious, individualized approaches. Together, these findings indicate that while EEN appears beneficial for survival, careful monitoring is warranted in hemodynamically unstable patients, and future high-quality RCTs should explicitly assess the risk of mesenteric ischemia. Similarly, none of the included RCTs reported acute kidney injury (AKI) as an outcome, preventing a quantitative analysis of this clinically important endpoint. This is notable given the growing interest in the relationship between nutritional support, protein or amino acid provision, and renal outcomes. Recent studies have suggested that protein or amino acid intake may modulate renal function and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients [45–48]. However, the results across different populations remain inconsistent, ranging from potential renal protective effects to neutral or adverse associations. Likewise, studies in other critically ill cohorts reported variable results regarding the impact of EEN on AKI risk, with one study suggesting reduced incidence [49], while another one suggesting that the energy level of EEN may affect their influence on AKI [50]. These findings underscore the need for future high-quality RCTs in septic patients to evaluate AKI systematically as a prespecified outcome, which would provide critical evidence on the renal safety and potential benefits of EEN.

Clinically, the findings of this meta-analysis highlight the potential importance of early nutritional strategies in sepsis management. EEN is a low-cost, readily implementable intervention that may offer survival benefits with minimal adverse effects. These results support current guideline recommendations favoring early enteral feeding in critically ill patients and underscore the need for structured nutrition protocols in sepsis care pathways. However, given the limitations outlined above, particularly regarding the variability of nutritional regimens and study settings, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Future research should focus on identifying the optimal timing, route, composition, and caloric targets of enteral nutrition in septic patients. Large, multicenter RCTs with standardized EEN protocols and stratified randomization by sepsis phenotype are warranted. Another important clinical consideration is that many patients admitted to the ICU with sepsis present with organ dysfunction and hemodynamic instability. Sepsis can impair splanchnic circulation and increase the risk of intestinal hypoperfusion, which may complicate the safety of early feeding [44]. Therefore, EEN should ideally be initiated once vital signs are stabilized, intestinal perfusion is restored, and no evidence of mesenteric ischemia is anticipated [44]. This pragmatic approach balances the potential benefits of early nutrition with the need to minimize gastrointestinal complications in unstable patients. Moreover, studies should evaluate additional clinically meaningful outcomes, such as long-term functional recovery. Investigating the interactions between nutrition and other sepsis therapies, as well as the role of personalized nutrition strategies based on metabolic profiling, may further enhance the effectiveness of EEN.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials provides evidence that early enteral nutrition significantly reduces all-cause mortality in adult patients with sepsis. The findings support the early initiation of enteral feeding as part of comprehensive sepsis management. While these results are promising, further high-quality research is needed to refine the optimal nutritional approach and confirm these findings in diverse clinical settings.
