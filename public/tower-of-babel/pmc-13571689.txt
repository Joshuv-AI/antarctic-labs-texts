Deconstructing Risk: A Machine Learning-Guided Logistic Regression Model for Predicting Early Mucosal Edema Following Prophylactic Ileostomy in Elderly Rectal Cancer Patients
Authors: Yu Zeng, Li Bao, Jian Li, Yanyan Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly mucosal edema is a common and burdensome complication following prophylactic ileostomy in elderly rectal cancer patients, yet effective tools for its pre-operative prediction are lacking. This study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable, machine learning-guided logistic regression model for this specific outcome.Patients and MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 296 eligible patients were included. The cohort was randomly split into a training set (n=207) for model development and an internal test set (n=89) for validation. A consensus machine learning approach, integrating Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, stepwise forward logistic regression, and Random Forest with Recursive Feature Elimination (RF-RFE), was employed to select core predictors from comprehensive clinical data. The final predictive model is a standard multivariable logistic regression. Model performance was validated via internal bootstrap resampling, ten-fold cross-validation, and external dataset testing, and assessed by discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis, with interpretability enhanced using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values. Generalizability was tested in an external cohort (n=40).ResultsFive variables were consistently selected as core predictors: the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), intraoperative fluid intake, abdominal wall opening size, and time to first postoperative bowel movement. The model incorporates early postoperative variables and is intended for early perioperative risk stratification, not solely preoperative prediction. The final multivariable logistic regression model, presented as a nomogram, demonstrated good discriminatory ability in the training (AUC=0.838, 95% CI: 0.706–0.845) and test (AUC=0.826, 95% CI: 0.743–0.910) sets. Internal robustness was supported by ten-fold cross-validation (mean AUC=0.813, 95% CI: 0.680–0.960). The model maintained acceptable performance in the external validation cohort (AUC=0.820), though some miscalibration was noted. Subgroup analysis suggested that a smaller abdominal opening and delayed bowel function were specifically associated with progression to severe edema.ConclusionWe developed and validated an interpretable, machine learning-guided logistic regression model for early mucosal edema, incorporating five readily available clinical variables. The model, available as an online nomogram, provides a practical tool for perioperative risk stratification, potentially guiding individualized patient counseling and targeted management strategies to mitigate this complication.

Introduction

Prophylactic ileostomy is widely adopted in rectal cancer surgery to mitigate the consequences of anastomotic leakage, particularly in elderly patients undergoing low anterior resection.1 Without effective risk stratification and timely intervention, this condition may lead to prolonged hospitalization, repeated nursing interventions, and increased healthcare costs. However, this procedure introduces a distinct set of early stoma-related complications, among which mucosal edema is predominant.2,3 Early edema not only impedes stoma care and appliance fitting, leading to peristomal skin breakdown, but also serves as a precursor to severe complications like ischemia and stenosis in vulnerable geriatric populations.4 Current management remains largely reactive, focusing on local measures such as hypertonic saline wet compresses once edema has developed.5 A critical gap exists in preemptive risk stratification to guide perioperative fluid management and surgical technique optimization.6

The pathophysiological basis of early stoma edema involves a complex interplay of local surgical factors and systemic patient vulnerability.7 Mechanistically, venous and lymphatic outflow obstruction-often exacerbated by a tight fascial opening-combined with intraoperative fluid overload and systemic inflammation, drives interstitial fluid accumulation.8,9 Elderly patients are particularly susceptible due to age-related declines in physiological reserve, with preoperative malnutrition and systemic inflammation (reflected by markers like PNI and NLR) being strongly implicated in impaired tissue repair and edema persistence.10,11 While previous efforts have modeled general stoma complications,12 existing tools often lack specificity for edema, fail to incorporate key intraoperative variables such as precise fascial aperture size and intraoperative fluid dynamics, or provide insufficient interpretability for bedside clinical decision-making in the geriatric context.13

To address this unmet need, we aimed to develop and validate an interpretable, machine learning-guided logistic regression model for early perioperative risk stratification of early mucosal edema following prophylactic ileostomy in elderly rectal cancer patients. Leveraging a comprehensive set of preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative predictors, we rigorously constructed a multivariable logistic regression model visualized as a nomogram. The model was rigorously assessed for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, with its decision logic further elucidated using SHAP analysis to ensure transparency. Furthermore, we validated the model’s generalizability through both internal hold-out testing and an independent external cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

In this retrospective cohort study, we enrolled consecutive patients aged ≥ 65 years with histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma who underwent elective radical resection with a concomitant prophylactic loop ileostomy at a tertiary referral center, identified from a retrospectively reviewed colorectal surgery database. The study period spanned six years, from January 2019 to December 2025. The primary endpoint was the development of early mucosal edema at the stoma site, clinically assessed and recorded within the first postoperative week.

Patients were identified from a prospectively maintained colorectal surgery registry. We excluded those who underwent emergency procedures, presented with metastatic disease at surgery, had a history of inflammatory bowel disease, or lacked complete perioperative documentation. A total of 296 eligible patients constituted the derivation cohort. This cohort was randomly split into a training set (n=207, 70%) for model development and an internal test set (n=89, 30%) for initial validation. To rigorously evaluate generalizability, an additional independent cohort of 40 patients meeting the same eligibility criteria was included from an affiliated hospital, serving as the external validation set.

Data Collection and Definitions

The primary outcome of this study was early mucosal edema at the stoma site, clinically defined as visible, persistent swelling of the protruded mucosa requiring intervention and documented in the nursing or surgical record within the first 7 postoperative days, consistent with previously established criteria for stoma-related complications.14 Cases of edema were identified and verified by two independent clinicians. Data on all predictor variables were systematically extracted from the institutional electronic medical records. The collected variables were categorized into three domains: preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative. Preoperative data included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and key laboratory-based indices calculated from routine blood tests, such as the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). Intraoperative variables encompassed the surgical approach (laparoscopic or open), operative duration, estimated blood loss, crystalloid infusion volume, and the precisely measured fascial aperture size. Early postoperative data focused on the time to first flatus and stoma appearance within the initial 48 hours. All data abstraction was performed by trained personnel using a pre-piloted, standardized form to ensure accuracy and consistency. Importantly, in our cohort, the median time to first postoperative bowel movement was 3 days (IQR: 2–4 days), whereas the median time to edema diagnosis was 4 days (IQR: 3–6 days), confirming that bowel movement preceded edema onset in the majority of cases. This temporal sequence supports the use of this variable as a predictor rather than a concurrent diagnostic indicator.

Prediction Model Construction and Assessment

The overall machine learning workflow is summarized in Figure 2. To identify the most predictive features from the comprehensive set of candidate variables, we first performed univariate screening for descriptive purposes. However, rather than restricting the modeling pool to univariate-significant variables, all preoperative, intraoperative, and early postoperative variables were entered into the initial pool for the subsequent consensus machine learning approach. This design avoids arbitrary exclusion of potentially informative variables and lets the data-driven algorithms (LASSO, SF-LR, RF-RFE) perform the selection. The three methods were applied in parallel, and only variables selected by all three were entered into the final multivariable logistic regression. Three distinct feature selection algorithms were applied in parallel to the training set: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, Stepwise Forward Logistic Regression (SF-LR), and Random Forest combined with Recursive Feature Elimination (RF-RFE). The rationale for using three methods was to enhance robustness; a variable was considered a core predictor only if it was consistently selected by all three independent algorithms. This consensus set of predictors was then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate the final coefficients and construct the clinical prediction model, which was presented as a nomogram. Although time to first bowel movement is a postoperative variable, it was included because the model is designed for early perioperative risk stratification rather than purely preoperative prediction; this variable reflects intraoperative gut handling and fluid load, and can be incorporated after bowel function returns to update the initial risk estimate. To ensure comparability, all continuous variables were standardized prior to analysis.Figure 2Machine learning workflow for feature selection and model construction.Diagram showing three feature-selection methods merging into a predictor set, then validation and SHAP.

The model’s performance was rigorously evaluated. Discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to estimate the clinical net benefit across a range of probability thresholds. To explain the model’s predictions at both global and individual levels, the Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework was applied. Furthermore, a stratified analysis was conducted among patients who developed edema to explore if risk factors differed between mild-to-moderate and severe cases. Finally, the model’s generalizability was rigorously tested in an independent external validation cohort (n=40).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28.0) and R (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on normality assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group comparisons used the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data occurred in two key variables: intraoperative fluid volume (missing in 8.1% of cases) and time to first postoperative bowel movement (missing in 12.5%). Given that the missingness proportion was below 20%—a common threshold for reliable imputation in clinical prediction models—we applied multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) using the predictive mean matching method with 5 nearest neighbors, generating 5 imputed datasets across 10 iterations. The imputation model included all candidate predictors and the outcome variable. To verify robustness, a sensitivity analysis using complete-case data was performed; results were consistent with the primary analysis and are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Model development and validation followed the multi-stage feature selection and consensus modeling approach described in Prediction Model Construction and Assessment section was assessed via the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals computed from 1000 bootstrap resamples, calibration plots, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and decision curve analysis. Model interpretability was enhanced using SHAP values. All model-related computations utilized R packages (glmnet for LASSO, randomForest for RF, shap for SHAP values, and rmda for decision curves). For a logistic regression model with 5 predictors and an anticipated AUC of 0.80, a minimum of 50 events is required assuming an EPV ≥ 10.15 Our training set contained 71 events, satisfying this criterion. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed in the training set to determine the optimal cutoff values for PNI and NLR, defined by the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). These cutoffs were used solely for descriptive purposes in univariate comparisons; the continuous forms of these variables were retained in the final model to preserve statistical power.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 296 elderly patients with rectal cancer who underwent elective resection and prophylactic loop ileostomy were included in the analysis, of whom 71 (24.0%) in the training set developed early mucosal edema. The cohort was randomly divided into a training set (n=207, 70%) and an internal test set (n=89, 30%). Baseline characteristics of the entire cohort and the two subsets are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. The training and test sets were well-balanced, with no statistically significant differences observed for any demographic, comorbidity, preoperative laboratory, tumor-related, intraoperative, or early postoperative variables (all P > 0.05). This confirmed the comparability of the two subsets for the subsequent model development and validation.

Feature Selection and Predictive Model

Univariate analysis in the training set identified significant differences in a range of variables between patients with and without early mucosal edema (Supplementary Table 3). The edema group had worse preoperative nutritional and inflammatory status (lower PNI, lymphocyte count, and higher neutrophil count and NLR), a higher prevalence of congestive heart failure, greater intraoperative fluid intake, poorer stoma blood flow, smaller abdominal wall opening, and delayed return of bowel function. Given the number of potential predictors emerging from this screening, we applied a consensus machine learning approach to select a robust and parsimonious set for the final model. As a supplementary analysis, ROC curves were generated in the training set to determine the optimal cutoff values for PNI and NLR based on the maximum Youden index. The optimal cutoff for PNI was 45.2 (AUC = 0.71, sensitivity = 76.3%, specificity = 64.1%), and for NLR was 2.61 (AUC = 0.68, sensitivity = 70.4%, specificity = 62.8%).

To distill the initial candidate variables into a robust and parsimonious set, we employed three complementary feature selection strategies in parallel: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression (penalty parameter λ selected via 10-fold cross-validation), Stepwise Forward Logistic Regression (SF-LR; entry P = 0.05, removal P = 0.10), and Random Forest combined with Recursive Feature Elimination (RF-RFE; 500 trees, mtry = sqrt(p), performance metric = AUC). The goal was to identify a consensus set of predictors by taking the intersection of the variables retained by each independent method.

First, the LASSO regression (Figure 3) applied a penalty to shrink coefficients, selecting five non-zero variables: Prognostic Nutritional Index(PNI), Preoperative Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (Preop_NLR), Intraoperative Fluid Intake, Abdominal Wall Opening Size, and Time to First Postoperative Bowel Movement. Of note, although Surgical_approach was retained by SF-LR (Table 1), it was not selected by LASSO at the optimal λ.1se nor ranked within the top 5 by RF-RFE (Table 2). Following our pre-specified consensus rule—requiring selection by all three algorithms—Surgical_approach was excluded from the final model. Second, the SF-LR analysis (Table 1) identified these five predictors as independently associated with the outcome. The OR estimates in Table 1 reflect the full SF-LR model, whereas Table 3 presents the final consensus model with all five predictors entered simultaneously; therefore, minor differences in OR point estimates and confidence intervals between the two tables are expected. Finally, the RF-RFE algorithm (Figure 4) provided a feature importance ranking. Model Validation and Performance Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated on both the training and test sets. In the training cohort, the AUC was 0.838 (95% CI: 0.706–0.845) with a sensitivity of 84.5% and specificity of 70.6%. In the test cohort, the AUC was 0.826 (95% CI: 0.743–0.910) with a sensitivity of 87.9% and a specificity of 67.9%. The model’s predictive performance was supported by ROC curves with bootstrap resampling (Figure 6), indicating its potential utility for clinical assessment.Figure 6ROC Curves with Bootstrap for Training (A) and Test (B) Sets. Discussion

Postoperative complications in elderly patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery pose a significant challenge to clinical management.16,17 In this context, we aimed to develop a practical tool to quantify the risk of a specific early stoma-related issue. By applying a consensus machine learning framework18 to integrate multi-faceted clinical data, we identified five key predictors: the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), intraoperative fluid intake, abdominal wall opening size, and time to first postoperative bowel movement. A nomogram constructed from these variables showed consistent predictive accuracy in both internal and initial external validation.

The five variables selected in our model correspond to distinct, well-established pathophysiological pathways involved in edema formation. The prognostic nutritional index and preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio reflect systemic patient vulnerability.19,20 A low PNI signals nutritional depletion and weakened immunity, compromising the substrate for tissue repair.21 Simultaneously, an elevated NLR indicates a baseline pro-inflammatory state, suggesting the patient may mount an exaggerated local inflammatory response to surgical trauma, which increases vascular permeability and promotes fluid leakage.22,23

The model also integrates key local and perioperative factors. The size of the abdominal wall opening is a critical technical variable. An excessively tight fascial aperture mechanically constricts venous and lymphatic return from the stoma, directly leading to venous congestion and interstitial fluid accumulation.24 High-volume intraoperative crystalloid administration further worsens this by increasing capillary hydrostatic pressure, driving more fluid into the tissue.25 Finally, delayed return of bowel function is commonly associated with postoperative ileus and local intestinal wall inflammation. This state can both arise from existing edema and contribute to its persistence.26,27 Consequently, the model connects preoperative patient status, a decisive surgical factor, and modifiable perioperative management into a plausible, multi-mechanism explanation for early mucosal edema.

Our model offers several practical advancements over existing approaches to stoma complication risk assessment. In terms of predictive performance, our model achieved an AUC of 0.838 in the training set and 0.826 in the test set, which is comparable to or slightly higher than previously reported models for stoma-related complications. For instance, Greijdanus et al reported an AUC of 0.78 for their stoma-free survival model, and Garfinkle et al achieved an AUC of 0.74 for predicting postoperative ileus after loop ileostomy closure. The addition of intraoperative variables (fascial aperture size, fluid volume) in our model likely accounts for this improvement in discrimination. First, it targets a specific, clinically relevant endpoint-early mucosal edema. Previous risk models have typically focused on composite outcomes like stoma-related complications28 or surgical site infection,29 which encompass heterogeneous events with differing pathophysiology. By concentrating on edema, a common and burdensome early issue that directly impacts nursing care and patient comfort, the tool addresses a narrower but operationally meaningful clinical gap. The predictors identified in our study are broadly consistent with findings from other regions. For example, a European multicenter study reported that intraoperative fluid balance was a key determinant of stoma-related complications,12 while a North American cohort identified nutritional status as a dominant risk factor.13 Our model extends these observations by demonstrating that the combination of systemic inflammatory markers and precise intraoperative technical factors yields superior predictive performance across diverse clinical settings. Second, the predictor set integrates variables from distinct clinical domains that are usually considered in isolation. It combines established systemic markers of physiological reserve (PNI, NLR) with a precise, operator-dependent technical factor-the measured size of the abdominal wall opening. While systemic scores are increasingly used in surgical risk prediction,30 objective intraoperative technical measurements are seldom incorporated into predictive algorithms, despite their mechanistic relevance. This combination provides a more holistic view of risk that spans patient biology and surgical technique. Finally, the presentation as a nomogram, supplemented by SHAP-based interpretation, moves the model from a statistical exercise toward a usable clinical decision aid. The nomogram allows quick, visual risk estimation at the point of care.31 SHAP analysis further clarifies how each variable contributes to an individual’s risk, making the model’s logic accessible to clinicians.32 This emphasis on interpretability is critical for fostering trust and facilitating the integration of predictive analytics into routine perioperative planning.

The performance assessment across different datasets suggests the tool has reasonable predictive ability. The model achieved AUCs of 0.838 in the training set and 0.826 in the test set, indicating stable discrimination internally. This internal consistency is supported by a ten-fold cross-validation, which resulted in a mean AUC of 0.813. In a separate, smaller external cohort, the discrimination remained acceptable with an AUC of 0.820. However, the calibration slope in this external cohort was 0.821, pointing to some degree of miscalibration when applied outside the development setting.

The subgroup analysis of patients who developed edema provided an exploratory insight. Within this group, the progression to severe edema showed a suggestive association with a smaller abdominal wall opening and a longer time to first bowel movement, whereas the other systemic predictors (PNI, NLR, intraoperative fluid) were not significant. However, due to the limited number of severe edema cases, the confidence intervals for these estimates were extremely wide, indicating statistical instability. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to determine whether the mechanisms driving initial edema development differ from those influencing its severity. Meanwhile, once edema is present, clinical management could usefully focus on assessing and relieving potential mechanical constriction at the fascial opening and on measures to promote the return of bowel function, as these local factors appear more directly linked to worse outcomes.33

Several important limitations should be considered. The study’s retrospective nature and single-center origin introduce several potential sources of bias, including selection bias related to referral patterns at a tertiary center, information bias from retrospective chart review, and unmeasured confounding from variables not captured in the registry (eg, surgeon experience, specific fluid composition). Despite statistical adjustments, these biases cannot be fully ruled out. Furthermore, while external validation provided an initial assessment of generalizability, the small size of that cohort (n = 40) substantially limits the precision of AUC and calibration estimates, and the observed miscalibration (slope = 0.821) may partly reflect overfitting to the development cohort. Therefore, these results should be considered preliminary, and confirmation in larger, multi-center cohorts is essential before clinical application. A practical challenge lies in the standardization of some predictor measurements, particularly the fascial opening size, which can be subjective and technique-dependent. Additionally, it is important to clarify that the model incorporates one early postoperative variable (time to first bowel movement) and is therefore intended for early perioperative risk stratification rather than purely preoperative prediction. Preoperatively, clinicians can use the model to obtain a partial risk estimate based on PNI, NLR, and planned abdominal opening size; the full risk assessment can be updated after bowel function returns in the early postoperative period. This two-stage approach maximizes the model’s clinical utility while respecting the temporal nature of the predictors. Finally, and most critically, this work demonstrates the model’s predictive accuracy but not its therapeutic utility; it remains to be seen whether using the tool to guide perioperative decisions actually leads to a reduction in edema incidence or severity. Prospective, intervention-based studies are required to answer that question.

Despite its limitations, the model holds practical potential. The visual nomogram and the online tool (available at https://predict-2026.shinyapps.io/DynNomapp/) are designed for ease of use at the bedside. Preoperatively, the tool could help surgeons identify high-risk patients, allowing for tailored discussions about stoma care expectations and informing specific perioperative plans, such as adopting a more restrictive fluid strategy or paying particular attention to the fascial opening size. Postoperatively, early identification of high-risk patients could enable nursing staff to implement proactive stoma care and monitoring, potentially mitigating complications. In conclusion, we have developed and provided initial validation for an interpretable, machine learning-guided logistic regression model for early mucosal edema. To translate this tool into clinical benefit, the logical next steps involve its further validation in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts, its potential integration into electronic health record systems for automated risk alerts, and ultimately, the design of randomized trials to determine whether using the model to guide perioperative management actually improves patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed and provided initial validation for an interpretable, machine learning-guided logistic regression model for early perioperative risk stratification of early mucosal edema following prophylactic ileostomy in elderly rectal cancer patients. The model incorporates PNI, preoperative NLR, intraoperative fluid intake, abdominal wall opening size, and time to first bowel movement. It is important to note that the inclusion of time to first bowel movement, a postoperative variable, positions this model as an early perioperative risk assessment tool rather than a purely preoperative predictive instrument. Preoperatively, clinicians can obtain a partial risk estimate based on the four preoperative and intraoperative variables; the full risk assessment can be updated after bowel function returns in the early postoperative period. Its dynamic nomogram facilitates point-of-care calculation, potentially aiding in individualized patient counseling and targeted perioperative management. For clinical practice, this model can help surgeons identify high-risk patients preoperatively, enabling tailored discussions about stoma care expectations and informing specific perioperative plans, such as adopting a more restrictive fluid strategy or optimizing the fascial opening size. Postoperatively, early identification of high-risk patients could enable nursing staff to implement proactive stoma care and monitoring. However, the external validation cohort showed notable miscalibration (intercept = −2.719, Brier score = 0.168), indicating that the model’s probability estimates require recalibration before application in new clinical settings. Additionally, the subgroup analysis of severe versus mild-to-moderate edema should be considered purely exploratory due to the limited number of severe cases and the resulting wide confidence intervals. Therefore, the current results should be considered preliminary, and future research should focus on prospective validation in larger, multi-center cohorts and on evaluating whether implementing this model to guide clinical decisions improves patient outcomes.
