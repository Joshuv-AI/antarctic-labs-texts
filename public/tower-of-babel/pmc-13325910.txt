Pyrocarbon implants in shoulder arthroplasty: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Juan Aguilar-Gonzalez, Valeria Vismara, Jose María Lamo-Espinosa, Riccardo Ranieri, Jose Enrique Aroca-Navarro, Joaquin Sanchez-Sotelo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChrome-cobalt alloy, pyrocarbon (PyC), and ceramic have all been used for humeral head replacement in shoulder arthroplasty. It remains unclear whether outcomes differ by material. PyC implants are increasingly used, including PyC interposition shoulder arthroplasty (PISA) and PyC hemiarthroplasty (HA-PyC). The objective of the present study is to systematically evaluate implant survival, functional outcomes (Constant score, visual analog scale for pain, and Subjective Shoulder Value), revision rates (defined as any reoperation involving implant removal, replacement, or conversion), complications, and radiological changes associated with PyC in shoulder arthroplasty.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was performed. MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched on April 25, 2025, without language or date restrictions. Studies with ≥1-year follow-up were included. Data were pooled using random-effects models; quality was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies tool, and certainty was graded according to Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation guidelines. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251059452).ResultsTwenty studies (2017–2025) were included. 1,117 patients had undergone either HA-PyC or PISA. Follow-up ranged from 1 to 11 years. Primary osteoarthritis was the most common indication (31%–86%). Pooled implant survival was 96% (95% confidence interval (CI), 94%-98%) at the most recent follow-up, with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 56.8%). Subgroup analysis revealed numerical differences across groups: survival was lower after PISA (87%) compared to stemmed HA-PyC (98%) and PyC resurfacing implants (96%). The overall revision rate was 5% (12% PISA; 3% HA-PyC; and 5% PyC resurfacing). The pooled mean age was approximately 50 years. Functional outcomes, Constant score, visual analog scale for pain, and Subjective Shoulder Value improved significantly, alongside improvements in range of motion. Glenoid wear rate varied widely, ranging from 6% to 100% in the HA-PyC group and from 3% to 90% in the PISA group. When longitudinal progression was evaluated, glenoid erosion increased by 6% to 39% in the HA-PyC group. In the PISA group, progression was reported in only one study, with a rate of 71%. Despite the radiographic incidence of glenoid wear, revision due to symptomatic (painful) erosion was uncommon, occurring in only 4 patients.ConclusionsThe reported survival rate of stemmed HA-PyC is high and encouraging. When PyC has been used for resurfacing HA or in the form of interposition (PISA), reported survivorship has been inferior. Careful patient selection and surgical technique are essential. Further high-quality comparative trials are required to clarify long-term benefits and material-specific risks.

Pyrocarbon (PyC) was introduced in orthopedics in the 1980s. It was first applied in hand and wrist replacement surgeries, with some small trials in hip surgery as well.7,16 PyC has a modulus of elasticity similar to skeletal tissues, and it is thought to lead to the formation of a neo-membrane at the joint surface, minimizing friction with reduced bone and cartilage wear compared to other materials such as metallic alloys.16,42

PyC has been introduced in shoulder surgery, mostly when arthroplasty is considered for primary osteoarthritis (OA).22,23 In young and active patients, the glenoid component remains a common source of long-term failure after anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA). Some studies have reported that Polyethylene wear and loosening occur at a high rate in the long term, representing a major cause of revision.9,41,48 Moreover, the revision is a challenging procedure and associated with a high rate of complications.5,37 For these reasons, hemiarthroplasty (HA) is usually recommended in young patients. Despite providing functional improvement, historically the use of a metallic humeral head has been associated with a substantial rate of incomplete pain relief as well as radiologic and symptomatic glenoid erosion.10,34,39 With the aim to reduce glenoid erosion and revision, PyC has been introduced for humeral head replacement. Its tribological properties24,44 may lead to a decreased glenoid wear, theoretically reducing the rate of revision.

In a recent study by Khoriati et al,30 analyzing 1,564 shoulder arthroplasties from the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry, metallic HA (HA-ME) showed higher revision rates compared to PyC HA (HA-PyC). In HA-ME, glenoid erosion accounted for 50% of revisions, whereas in HA-PyC, instability accounted for 28%.30 Recently, several case series on the use of PyC implants have been published with promising results, and 2 systematic reviews had previously been published: Franceschetti et al reported an implant survival rate of 93% at 2-year follow-up17; Park et al reported a cumulative complication rate of 8.6% and a revision rate of 7.7%.42 However, they have mostly focused on the short-to-mid-term outcomes, with limited data available on long-term results. An updated systematic review is needed to better assess cumulative revision and complication rates, functional outcomes, and explore more insights and comparisons across different implant types.

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the clinical performance of PyC humeral head implants in patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty. Specifically, we aimed to assess implant survival, revision rates, and functional and patient-reported outcomes, including Constant score (CS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV).

We hypothesized that PyC shoulder arthroplasty would be associated with high implant survival rates and significant improvements in functional and patient-reported outcomes at mid- to long-term follow-up. Medium-term follow-up was defined as 1–5 years and long-term as more than 5 years. Additionally, we aimed to explore potential differences in outcomes according to implant configuration, including stemmed HA (HA-PyC), resurfacing implants, and PyC interposition shoulder arthroplasty (PISA).

Methods

Study selection

A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines,38 and the Cochrane's Handbook of Systematic Reviews of Interventions.25 The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251059452).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included in the systematic review if they met the following criteria: 1) original clinical studies (retrospective or prospective cohorts, or registry reports); 2) full-text articles published and available in peer-reviewed journals; 3) patients aged >18 years undergoing primary shoulder arthroplasty; and 4) use of PyC humeral head implants in stemmed, stemless, resurfacing, or interposition (PISA) configurations. Studies were excluded if they focused on joints other than the shoulder (eg, wrist, elbow, or hip), involved anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) (unless outcomes for PyC arthroplasty were separately reported), or investigated nonarticular or nonorthopedic applications of PyC. Revision procedures, defined as surgeries performed to correct or replace a failed or ineffective prior shoulder arthroplasty, were also excluded. Animal and biomechanical studies, reviews, case reports, expert opinions, editorials, commentaries, and conference abstracts without a full peer-reviewed publication were excluded as well. The research question was structured according to the PICO framework to ensure clarity in the definition of inclusion and comparison criteria (Table I). Search strategy and data collection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on April 25, 2025, across 5 major databases (MEDLINE [via Ovid], Embase [via Ovid], Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials [via Ovid], Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews [via Ovid], Scopus [via Elsevier], and Web of Science), without restrictions on publication date or language. A professional medical librarian, in collaboration with study investigators, designed and executed the search strategies. A combination of controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH and Emtree terms) and relevant keywords was applied to ensure comprehensive and sensitive search (Supplementary Table S1).

Data extraction and data items

The screening process was conducted by 2 independent reviewers, and duplicates were removed. The eligibility of the articles was assessed by title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer. Reference lists of eligible articles and prior systematic reviews were also screened to identify additional studies.

Two reviewers independently performed data extraction, and a third reviewer was consulted to resolve any discrepancies and achieve consensus. Baseline characteristics of participants in the studies included, as well as study settings, were recorded using Microsoft Excel. For each article, the following data were extracted: first author, year of publication, study design, sample size, follow-up duration, patient age, sex distribution, diagnosis, implant type, indications for surgery, and reported radiological assessments.

Assessment of the quality of studies and certainty of evidence

Two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality of the studies included using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria.46 The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.18

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software with the meta, metafor, forestplot, dplyr, and tidyr packages, and a P value <. 05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. For continuous outcomes, such as the CS, the primary effect measure was the mean difference between post-operative and pre-operative assessments. The standard error of the mean difference was calculated using the reported standard deviations, assuming a conservative within-group correlation coefficient of 0.5 for paired data when the true correlation was not provided. For dichotomous outcomes, including revision and complication rates, pooled proportions were calculated using a Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation to stabilize variances, and a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to studies with zero events. All pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model to account for anticipated heterogeneity.13 This heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic26 and tested for significance with Cochran's Q test (P < .10). To evaluate the robustness of the findings and explore sources of heterogeneity, several sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed, including a leave-one-out analysis, a subgroup analysis based on MINORS criteria (including only studies with ≥12 points for noncomparative and ≥18 points for comparative studies), and, where applicable, analysis by implant material type. When possible, subgroup analysis was performed to compare PISA, HA-PyC, and/or resurfacing.

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and formally tested with Egger's linear regression test,15 with P < .05 considered indicative of significant asymmetry.

Results

Study selection

The comprehensive database search yielded 224 records. After removing duplicates, 144 were excluded, leaving 80 records for title and abstract screening; of these, 18 were excluded. Subsequently, 62 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 20 studies met the inclusion criteria and were finally included. Most studies followed a retrospective or prospective cohort design, with follow-up ranging from 1 to 11 years. HA-PyC and PISA studies were evaluated independently due to substantial heterogeneity between studies. PyC humeral head implants were the predominant type used, either exclusively or within comparative study designs. No randomized controlled trials comparing PyC arthroplasty with other implants were identified. Comparative evidence comes from retrospective cohort studies and national joint registry analyses,14,19,29,30,32,36 which have compared HA-PyC with HA-ME, aTSA, and/or RSA.

In the HA-PyC group, revision surgery was reported in several cases, mainly due to rotator cuff failure, either of the subscapularis or of the posterosuperior cuff (n = 12), painful glenoid wear (n = 6), implant-related complications, such as malpositioning or overstuffing (n = 4), infection (n = 4), persistent pain (n = 3), instability (n = 2), and periprosthetic fracture (n = 1). Revision rates ranged from 0% to 13%; revision specifically for painful glenoid wear ranged from 0% to 8%.

In the PISA group, common reasons for revision were painful glenoid wear, either in isolation or combined to humeral wear (n = 12), rotator cuff failure (n = 7), persistent pain (n = 3), posterior humeral head subluxation (n = 2), and inferior GH impingement (n = 2). In some studies, patients were converted to a different arthroplasty style. Revision rates ranged from 0% to 50%; revision specifically for painful glenoid wear ranged from 0% to 50%.

Implant survival rate

Survival analysis was performed using the data reported in 17 studies. Using a random-effects model, the overall implant survival rate was estimated to be 96% (95% CI: 94% to 98%), with the minimum follow-up being one year and maximum eleven years. The medium-term follow-up was defined as 1–5 years and long-term as more than 5 years. This analysis showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 46.3%, P = .0165). Three different subgroups were independently analyzed: stemmed HA-PyC, PyC resurfacing, and PISA. Numerical differences were found across groups. For PISA implants4,21,27,28 (4 studies), the survival rate was 88% (95% CI: 78% to 98%, range 50% to 100%). Stemmed HA-PyC3,14,19,22,23,29,32,35,40,43,47 (11 studies) demonstrated a survival rate of 98% (95% CI: 96% to 99%, range 83% to 100%). When only studies with a mean follow-up (FU) >/ = 5 years were included (5 studies), the survival range was 96-100%. The sensitivity analysis of 6 high-quality studies showed a survival rate of 92.8% (95% CI: 88.5% to 97.2%). A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the result. However, the enhanced funnel plot was asymmetrical, and a significant Egger's test (P = .0001) indicated the presence of publication bias.

Functional and patient-reported outcomes

Absolute CS assessment, VAS, and SSV were analyzed separately for stemmed HA-PyC and PISA. For the stemmed HA-PyC group, the random-effects model showed an improvement of 40.2 points in CS (95% CI: 34.3-46.1). There was an estimated reduction in VAS of 5.3 points (95% CI: −5.9 to −4.6) (5 studies),21,32,33,43,47 while the SSV improved by 46.6 points (95% CI: 40-53.2).2,3,12,20,27,29,33,43 For the PISA group, the random-effects model demonstrated an improvement of 41.8 points in CS (95% CI: 27.5-56); a reduction of 4.5 points in VAS (95% CI: −5.1 to −3.9)4; and an improvement of 50.2 points in SSV (95% CI: 31.6-68.8). Furthermore, the funnel plot was visually symmetrical, and Egger's test confirmed no evidence of significant publication bias for either CS score assessment or VAS score (P = .294 and P = .188, respectively).

Glenoid erosion rate

Glenoid wear rate reportedly ranged from 6% to 100% in the HA-PyC group and from 3% to 90% in the PISA group. When progression of glenoid wear was assessed before and after surgery, glenoid erosion progression ranged from 6% to 39% in the HA-PyC group, while it was only reported in one study in the PISA group (71%)4 (Tables IV and V).

Discussion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized data from 20 studies involving 1,117 patients to evaluate the clinical outcomes, implant survival, and complication rates associated with PyC implants in shoulder arthroplasty with a follow-up period ranged from 1 to 11 years (follow-up between 1 and 5 years was classified as midterm, and follow-up beyond 5 years as long term). Although the pooled analyses include heterogeneous subgroups, such as PISA, resurfacing, and stemmed PyC, this reflects the diversity of clinical applications rather than a methodological weakness. The subgroup analyses remain the most informative and clinically meaningful approach to interpret the performance of each implant design.

Survival at the medium term varies according to the type of implant chosen, with stemmed HA-PyC outperforming other types of PyC arthroplasty. Although implant survival rates appear robust, their clinical interpretation requires caution given the very low certainty of evidence. Despite a pooled survival of 98%, Kany et al29 reported HA-PyC survival to be 83%, the lowest in the analyzed studies. In this multicenter study, in which only young individuals (<50 years old) were included, at a minimum follow-up of two years, a stemmed HA-PyC implant was not superior in terms of revision to TSA (17% vs. 9%). More importantly, the mean follow-up time was longest in the case of TSA, compared to the other 2 groups (95 vs. 36 months for TSA and HA-PyC, respectively). Similar findings were reported by Douven et al,14 at a longer mean follow-up (7 years), where the overall revision rate for HA was found to be significantly higher (7.6%) compared to that of TSA (1.2%). With a mean follow-up equal to or greater than 5 years, survival ranged between 96% and 100%, possibly highlighting the presence of early failures that then stabilize over time.

A previous systematic review and meta-analysis assessing short- to early-mid-term clinical outcomes and survival of PyC shoulder implants reported favorable short- to early-mid-term outcomes.17 The authors reported high implant survival rates, generally exceeding 90%, along with significant improvements in functional outcomes, including CS, and substantial reductions in pain as measured by the VAS. Although overall revision rates were relatively low, they varied depending on implant type and were most attributed to glenoid wear and persistent pain. Radiographic glenoid erosion was frequently observed; however, it did not consistently translate into clinical symptoms requiring revision. Despite these encouraging findings, the study highlighted considerable heterogeneity among included studies and emphasized that the overall quality of evidence remains limited, largely due to the predominance of retrospective designs and the absence of high-quality randomized controlled trials.17

These findings reinforce the growing preference for PyC materials when implanting an HA, which may offer superior wear characteristics and biocompatibility compared to cobalt-chrome. Recently, Boileau et al8 published the largest series on HA-PyC with the longest FU. It reports a revision-free rate of 94% at 5 years and of 89% at 10 years. Young individuals with OA might benefit from HA, but long-term studies are needed to corroborate this hypothesis.

Early metal-head HA series, including those from the Mayo Clinic,45 demonstrated reasonable pain relief but unacceptably high rates of progressive glenoid erosion and rotator cuff failure, fueling skepticism about the long-term durability of HA for glenohumeral arthritis. Many of these failures, however, are attributable to deliberate humeral head lateralization or overstuffing, which was then believed to improve optimize deltoid and cuff mechanics, but now recognized to accelerate eccentric glenoid wear and cuff degeneration. In contrast, modern PyC systems emphasize adequate humeral head sizing and truly anatomic restoration of the geometry of the proximal humerus, yielding an implant survivorship exceeding 90% and lower rates of glenoid erosion at mid-term follow-up.17,42 Therefore, outcome comparisons between historical metal-head cohorts and current PyC-based implants must account for advances in material science, implant geometry, and surgical technique, rather than relying on outdated benchmarks to assess efficacy and durability.

Persistent pain is among the most common causes of revision surgery in HA-PyC, despite being a symptom and not being a true diagnosis. Prior to revision, the underlying cause should always be sought, even if difficult and even if concomitant causes may play a role. Glenoid erosion, rotator cuff failure, or suboptimal implant positioning (mostly associated with overstuffing) should be considered as possible etiologies, and infection, when everything seems in order, should be ruled out, better with biopsies. Infection should always be suspected in case of painful arthroplasty when no other causes can be identified. Biopsies are the most reliable indicator of the presence or absence of infection. In vitro, PyC implants with their smooth surface have shown to decrease bacterial adherence and biofilm formation,6 which is intriguing, especially in the setting of patients who have had prior stabilization surgery or open reduction and internal fixation, considered at a higher risk for a low-grade infection.2

Although not always symptomatic, progressive glenoid erosion can compromise long-term outcomes and lead to revision surgery. While reported glenoid erosion after HA-PyC in the studies captured by our meta-analysis ranged between 12% (in young patients with primary OA and mostly glenoid Walch type A)29 and 100% (in patients with either B2 or B3 glenoid),43 actual progression appears uncommon regardless of pre-operative glenoid morphology. Ranieri et al43 found that even in patients with glenoid erosion (Walch type B2 and B3), erosion remained stable relative to the pre-operative status in 72% at final follow-up. Similarly, Garret et al20 reported that, when comparing with immediate post-operative radiographs, 10 out of 37 (27%) shoulders exhibited progression of glenoid erosion by either 1 or 2 grades.

A unique failure mode exclusively reported with the PyroTitan PyC resurfacing implant is humeral-head fracture, not typically observed with metallic heads or other stemmed PyC designs. This rare but severe event has been reported in several case studies, often occurring between 1 and 6 years postimplantation, predominantly in shoulders with overstuffing or subscapularis insufficiency. Implant breakage accounted for 58% of revision cases in a comparative study conducted by McBride et al.36 Additionally, irreversible damage and erosion of any PyC implant's surface can occur if the PyC head contacts metallic devices, such as screws or metallic anchors, which should always be removed at the time of implantation of any HA-PyC.

Pyrocarbon interposition shoulder arthroplasty subgroup

Despite primary OA being the most common indication, PISA has also been used for post-traumatic OA and avascular necrosis. Hudek et al28 reported a case series of 10 patients, in which PISA was used as primary treatment in case of advanced collapse of the humeral head, often accompanied by shortening of soft tissue and medialization of the humerus. PISA requires less exposure, which could help the surgeons in case of a very tight joint. The same can be said on case of post-traumatic arthritis, where the humerus has significant deformity.1 Moreover, the absence of a metallic stem could be interesting, especially in those patients with previous failed surgery, in which the risk of infection is higher after shoulder replacement.4 Barret et al4 reported that the prosthesis survival rate was 90% at 5 years and 87% at an average follow-up of eleven years (10–14 years). With a CS of 72 points and an SSV of 75%, the functional and subjective results were favorable for the patients who still had the implant at the 10-year follow-up. The best indication seems to be type 1 fracture sequelae, with a 100% survival at a minimum of 10 years and no evidence of glenoid erosion or posterior humeral head subluxation. PyC implants ought to be saved for patients who are younger or whose bone stock and quality are adequate.4

Strengths and limitations

This study offers a comprehensive analysis with a range of countries and diverse patient populations, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings and applied a robust statistical approach, incorporating sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses based on the risk of bias, and formal assessments of publication bias, all of which strengthen the validity and reliability of the meta-analytic results. A notable strength of this review is the inclusion of both objective outcomes, such as implant survival, and subjective measures like the SSV and CS, providing a thorough and balanced evaluation of functional and patient-reported outcomes. Furthermore, the application of established tools such as the MINORS criteria for methodological quality assessment and the GRADE framework for evaluating the certainty of evidence allows for a transparent and systematic appraisal of the included studies.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Most outcomes demonstrated substantial to extreme heterogeneity (I2 > 75%), which limits the precision and interpretability of the pooled effect estimates. The quality of the evidence was generally low, as many of the studies included were retrospective in design, involved small sample sizes, lacked adequate control groups, and reported incomplete methodological details. Moreover, the data on long-term outcomes were limited, with few studies reporting results beyond five years, restricting the ability to conclude about the durability of PyC implants over time. Future studies should include quantitative assessments of erosion and consider incorporating insights from relevant basic science research to better contextualize the long-term biological interactions of implant materials with native glenoid structures.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis seems to indicate that a PyC-coated shoulder HA provides favorable outcomes in terms of implant survival, functional improvement, and pain reduction. Stemmed HA-PyC showed the highest survival rates and consistent functional gains, while PISA implants, although effective, were associated with higher revision and glenoid erosion rates. Improvements in range of motion and subjective outcomes were also clinically meaningful across implant types. However, the certainty of evidence was low due to methodological limitations, heterogeneity, and possible publication bias. Future high-quality comparative studies are needed to clarify the long-term performance and safety of different implant materials and designs.
