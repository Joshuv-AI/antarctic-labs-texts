On a boundary of the central limit theorem for strictly stationary, reversible Markov chains
Richard C. Bradley

Abstract
Consider the class of (functions of) strictly stationary Markov chains in which (i) the second moments are finite and (ii) absolute regularity ( β \beta -mixing) is satisfied with exponential mixing rate. For (functions of) Markov chains in that class that are also reversible, the central limit theorem holds, as a well known byproduct of results of Roberts, Rosenthal, and Tweedie in two papers in 1997 and 2001 involving reversible Markov chains. In contrast, for (functions of) Markov chains in that class that are not reversible, the central limit theorem may fail to hold, as is known from counterexamples, including ones with arbitrarily fast mixing rate (for absolute regularity). Here it will be shown that for Markov chains in that class that are reversible, the “borderline” class of mixing rates (for absolute regularity) for the central limit theorem is in fact exponential. That will be shown here with a class of counterexamples — strictly stationary, countable-state Markov chains that are reversible, have finite second moments, and satisfy absolute regularity with mixing rates that can be arbitrarily close to (but not quite) exponential, but fail to satisfy the central limit theorem.



Keywords:
strictly stationary, reversible Markov chain;

strong mixing; absolute regularity; central limit question

In this paper, for simplicity, every Markov chain

Of course the term “reversible” simply means that

In particular, we mention here (as well as in Section

Such use of mixing conditions and their dependence coefficients

[

]
,

[

]

The aforementioned “key result” has the following well known consequence:
For (functions of) strictly stationary Markov chains that satisfy geometric
ergodicity — that is, absolute regularity with at least exponentially fast
mixing rate — the extra property of reversibility, if satisfied, provides
considerable extra leverage in the development of central limit theory.
(That is reviewed in a bit of detail in Section
2
.) A natural question is the following:
For (functions of) strictly stationary Markov chains that satisfy absolute
regularity but with a mixing rate that is slower than exponential,
does the extra property of reversibility, if satisfied, still provide considerable
extra leverage in the development of central limit theory?

For mixing rates of power-type
O(n^{-p})

the answer to that question seems to be “essentially no”.
That was illustrated with a class of Markov chains constructed by

[

sense as sharp as possible.
(Those examples are reversible, though that property does not appear to be
mentioned explicitly in that paper.)

Here we shall examine that question for mixing rates that are close to
exponential.
Our main result, Theorem
3.3
in Section

of counterexamples that will show that under reversibility,
finite second moments, and absolute regularity with mixing rates arbitrarily
close to (but short of) exponential, the central limit theorem may fail to hold.

-measurable

-measurable.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq 1;\quad{\rm and}

(2.4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 4\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\leq 1.

(2.5)

(See e.g. [

]

Notations 2.3
.

Suppose
X:=(X_{k}
,

is a (not necessarily Markovian)

.
For each integer

For each positive integer

\displaystyle\alpha(n)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{X}(n):=\alpha(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n});

(2.6)

\displaystyle\beta(n)

\displaystyle=\beta_{X}(n):=\beta(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n});\,\,\mbox{and}

(2.7)

“

.

The strong mixing (

-mixing) condition is due to

[

]

The absolute regularity (

-mixing) condition was first studied by

The

-mixing condition is due to

]

By (

) and (

), one has that for each positive integer

,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha(n)\leq\beta(n)\leq 1,\,\,\mbox{and}

(2.9)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 4\alpha(n)\leq\rho(n)\leq 1.

(2.10)

By (

), absolute regularity (

-mixing) implies strong mixing

\alpha
-mixing); and

),

-mixing implies strong mixing (

-mixing).

Remark 2.4
.

Now suppose that

X:=(X_{k},k\in\mathbb{Z})
is a strictly stationary
Markov chain
(with the random
variables
X_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
being real-valued, possibly discrete).
(No assumption of “reversibility” yet.)

As a well known consequence of the Markov property, for each positive

, eqs. (

)-(

forms for the given (strictly stationary) Markov chain

\displaystyle\alpha(n)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{X}(n)=\alpha(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n})=\alpha\bigl(\sigma(X_{0}),\sigma(X_{n})\bigl);

(2.11)

\displaystyle\beta(n)

\displaystyle=\beta_{X}(n)=\beta(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n})=\beta\bigl(\sigma(X_{0}),\sigma(X_{n})\bigl);

(2.12)

\displaystyle\rho(n)

\displaystyle=\rho_{X}(n)=\rho(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-\infty},\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{n})=\rho\bigl(\sigma(X_{0}),\sigma(X_{n})\bigl).

(2.13)

(See e.g. [
[
4
]
, Vol. 1, Theorem 7.3].)

By strict stationarity and (
2.11
)-(

), one has that for

, the (strictly stationary)

satisfies (i)
\alpha(n)=\alpha(\sigma(X_{j}),\sigma(X_{j+n}))

Here are some special facts involving the

\rho(n)
,

.
As an elementary consequence of (

), for any two

-fields

\rho(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\sup\|E(Y|\mathcal{B})\|_{2}/\|Y\|_{2}

-measurable

(When necessary, interpret 0/0 := 0.) As a well known application of that fact

, one has that
for any pair of positive integers

\rho(m+n)\leq\rho(m)\cdot\rho(n).

(2.14)

As a well known consequence, for the given strictly stationary Markov chain

is

-mixing,

In Remark

Remark 2.5

For any given (say real) strictly stationary Markov chain

Remark 2.6
.

There is a considerable history of the use of (functions of) strictly stationary
Markov chains to illuminate the “borderline” of central limit theorems
under weak dependence.

(A) For example, consider the following central limit theorem of

[
13
]
,
stated here as in Theorem 18.5.4 in the book by Ibragimov and Linnik

]
:

Suppose

Then
\sigma^{2}:=E(X_{0}^{2})+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}E(X_{0}X_{n})
exists in

[0,\infty)

\sigma^{2}>0
then

(1/(n^{1/2}\sigma))\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
converges in distribution to
N(0,1)

as
n\to\infty
.

(B) Davydov [
[
8
]
, Example 2] constructed a class of
examples of bounded, strictly stationary, countable-state Markov chains, for which
the partial sums, suitably normalized, converge in distribution to a symmetric
stable law with index (exponent) strictly between 1 and 2.
By the Theorem of Types (see e.g. Theorem 14.2 in
[
1
]
),
in those examples, regardless of what normalization is employed,
normalized partial sums cannot converge in distribution to a nondegenerate
normal law.
As Davydov
[
8
]
pointed out, for any given
\varepsilon>0
,
those examples include ones for which [see (
2.9
)]

\alpha(n)\leq\beta(n)=O(n^{-1+\varepsilon})
as
n\to\infty
.
[Davydov’s discussion there focused on the dependence coefficient

\alpha(n)
; however, with calculations on pp. 327-328 in that paper

[
8
]
, he implicitly verified the same mixing rate
for
\beta(n)
as for
\alpha(n)
(modulo a constant factor).] With those examples, Davydov showed that the central limit theorem
of Ibragimov stated in (A) above is very sharp.

(C) Ibragimov
[
13
]
also proved a CLT for
(not necessarily Markovian) strictly stationary
un
bounded sequences of
random variables having certain finite moments of higher than
second order, with (as compensation) certain mixing rates for
\alpha(n)
faster
than the summable rate in the CLT (for bounded sequences) stated in (A) above.
In essentially the same way as in (B) above,
Davydov [
[
8
]
, Example 1] showed with a class of

[The counterexamples alluded to here and in (B) above

[
7
]
without emphasis on mixing conditions.]

(D) For (not necessarily Markovian) strictly stationary sequences of (say)

[

\beta_{X}(n)\to 0

n\to\infty
.
Then

\sigma^{2}:=E[[h(X_{0})]^{2}]\allowbreak+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}E[h(X_{0})\,h(X_{n})]
exists in

[0,\infty)
, the sum being absolutely convergent; and if
\sigma^{2}>0
then
(1/(n^{1/2}\sigma))\sum_{k=1}^{n}h(X_{k})
converges in distribution to
N(0,1)

as
n\to\infty
.

(B) By combining the comment in Remark
2.9
(B) with references such
as the ones mentioned here in (A) above, one obtains this CLT under
the mixing assumption of strong mixing (instead of absolute regularity)
with
\alpha(n)\to 0
at least exponentially fast as
n\to\infty
.

The purpose of this paper here is to show that for (functions of) strictly stationary,
reversible Markov chains in the CLT reviewed in Remark
2.10
(A) above,
in essence the (exponential) mixing rate cannot be slowed down at all
(without strengthening or adding other assumptions).
For any proposed mixing rate (for absolute regularity or for strong mixing) that is
slower than exponential, there exists a (nontrivial) counterexample
whose mixing rate is at least as fast.
That result, the main result of this paper,
will be formulated in Theorem
3.3
in the next section.

3
Formulation of the main result

The main result of this paper will be formulated in Theorem
3.3
below,
with the use of material presented in Remark
3.1

and Notations
3.2
.

Remark 3.1
.

The new examples, as described in Theorem
3.3
below,
will be strictly stationary random sequences

(X_{k},k\in\mathbb{Z})
(reversible Markov chains)
that satisfy the conditions

E(X_{0}^{2})<\infty\indent{\rm and}\indent EX_{0}=0,

(3.1)

as well as the condition

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\textrm{\rm Var}\biggl(\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\biggl)\ =\ \infty

(3.2)

and even the (stronger) condition

{\rm for\ every}\ b>0,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\biggl[\,\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\,P\biggl(r-b\ <\ n^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\ <\ r+b\biggl)\biggl]\ =\ 0.

(3.3)

Of course (
3.3
) [in conjunction with
(
3.1
)] implies (
3.2
) by
Chebyshev’s inequality.
Eq. (
3.3
) might be referred to as the “complete dissipation” of the
random variables
n^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
as
n\to\infty
.
For a given fixed positive integer
n
, the term in the brackets
in (
3.3
) is, as a function of
b>0
, a well known
“concentration function” of the random variable
n^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
.

Of course, by a trivial argument, (
3.3
) is equivalent to the
(at first sight seemingly weaker) condition that there
exists
a
positive number
b
such that the equality in (
3.3
) holds.
In particular, to verify (
3.3
), it suffices to carry out
the argument for (say) just the case
b=1
, allowing one
to slightly simplify the arithmetic.

Notations 3.2
.

In the examples constructed in this paper, a certain non-degenerate,
non-normal distribution, labeled below as “
\mu_{\rm P1sL}
”, will
play a role as a “partial limiting distribution” — a limit law for

That distribution is formulated in part (B) below, employing brief

(A) The Laplace (“double exponential”) distribution is the

.

(B) Let

\displaystyle=\ E[e^{itY}]\ =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\biggl(P(N=n)\cdot E\Bigl[e^{itY}\Bigl|N=n\Bigl]\,\biggl)\ =\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{1}{n!}e^{-1}\cdot E\biggl[\exp\biggl(it\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta_{k}\biggl)\biggl|N=n\biggl]\,\biggl)

(3.5)

Now here is our main result:

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3}\dots)
is a sequence
of positive numbers such that (a)
\lim_{n\to\infty}\zeta_{n}=0
, and (b) for every
c>0

\lim_{n\to\infty}[\,\zeta_{n}/e^{-cn}]=\infty
.

Then there exists a (real) strictly stationary, countable-state
Markov chain
X:=(X_{k},k\in\mathbb{Z})
which is reversible, such that
the following four conditions hold: (i) eq. (
3.1
) holds:
E(X_{0}^{2})<\infty
and
EX_{0}=0
; (ii)
\alpha_{X}(n)\ \leq\ \beta_{X}(n)\ \leq\ \zeta_{n}
for every

; (iii) eqs. (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) hold; and (iv) there exists a strictly increasing sequence

\bigl(J(1),J(2),J(3),\dots\bigl)
of positive integers,
and a sequence of positive constants

(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\dots)
satisfying

as

, such that

b_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{J(n)}X_{k}\ \to\ \mu_{\rm P1sL}\ \ {\rm in\ distribution\ as}\ n\to\infty.

(3.6)

Remark 3.4
.

Of course assumption (b) is that the convergence of the positive numbers

\zeta_{n}
to 0 is at a rate that is slower than any exponential decay.

By (
3.6
) and the Theorem of Types
(see e.g. Theorem 14.2 in
[
1
]
again),
for the Markov chain

in this theorem,
regardless of what normalization is employed,
the normalized partial sums cannot converge in distribution to a
nondegenerate normal law.

The pertinence of property (iii) in the conclusion of this theorem may warrant
a comment.
In the study of CLTs for sequences
(X_{k},k\in\mathbb{Z})
of dependent random variables
(say strictly stationary, with mean 0 and typically with finite second moments),
there is sometimes an interest in whether

4
Background on the partial limit distributions

Notations 4.1
.

In the convergence in distribution alluded to in the first paragraph of
Notations
3.2
, a key role will be played by the following
class of probability functions for discrete random variables.

(A) For each pair of real numbers
a,p\in(0,1)
(the open unit interval),
define the function
{\bf g}_{a,p}:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,1]
as follows:

{\bf g}_{a,p}(0)\ :=\ 1-a,\indent{\rm and\ for\ each}\ n\in\mathbb{N},\indent{\bf g}_{a,p}(n)\ :=\ {\bf g}_{a,p}(-n)\ :=\ (a/2)p(1-p)^{n-1}\ .

(4.1)

For any given pair of numbers
a,p\in(0,1)
, the
numbers
{\bf g}_{a,p}(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
are nonnegative and
(by a simple calculation) add up to 1.
That is,
{\bf g}_{a,p}
is a probability function on the set
\mathbb{Z}
of all integers.
For typographical convenience, the probability function
{\bf g}_{a,p}
will
sometimes be denoted as
{\bf g}[a,p]
; that is,

{\bf g}[a,p](k)={\bf g}_{a,p}(k)
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(B) Next, as an elementary tool for use below,
for each complex number
c
,
define the complex number
\upsilon_{c}
by

\upsilon_{c}\ :=\ e^{c}-(1+c)\ =\ \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}c^{k}/k!\ .

(4.2)

Of course
\upsilon_{0}=0
.
For each complex number
c
,

|\upsilon_{c}|\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}|c|^{k}/k!\leq|c|^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}|c|^{k-2}/(k-2)!=|c|^{2}\cdot e^{|c|}
.
Hence

\upsilon_{c}/c\to 0\ \ {\rm as}\ \ c\to 0\ ({\rm in}\ \mathbb{C}-\{0\}).

(4.3)

(C) For each pair of real numbers
a,p\in(0,1)
, the characteristic
function
\varphi_{a,b}
for the probability function
{\bf g}_{a,p}
can be
computed in the following steps:
First, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
employing (
4.1
) and (
4.2
),

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ e^{itn}{\bf g}_{a,p}(n)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl[e^{it}\cdot e^{it(n-1)}\cdot(a/2)p(1-p)^{n-1}\Bigl]\ =\ (a/2)pe^{it}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}[e^{it}(1-p)]^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\ (a/2)pe^{it}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{it}(1-p)}\ =\ (a/2)p\cdot\frac{1}{e^{-it}-(1-p)}

\displaystyle=\ (a/2)p\cdot\frac{1}{1-it+\upsilon_{-it}-(1-p)}\ =\ (a/2)p\cdot\frac{1}{p-it+\upsilon_{-it}}\ .

(4.4)

By an analogous calculation,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ e^{it(-n)}{\bf g}_{a,p}(-n)\ =\ (a/2)p\cdot\frac{1}{p+it+\upsilon_{it}}\ .

(4.5)

Now by (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) and
the first equality in (
4.1
)
(together with the trivial fact that
e^{it\cdot 0}=1
),

\displaystyle\varphi_{a,p}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{itk}{\bf g}_{a,p}(k)\ =\ (1-a)\cdot 1\ +\ a\cdot\frac{p}{2}\biggl[\frac{1}{p-it+\upsilon_{-it}}\ +\ \frac{1}{p+it+\upsilon_{it}}\biggl]

\displaystyle=\ 1\ +\ a\cdot\biggl(\frac{p}{2}\biggl[\frac{1}{p-it+\upsilon_{-it}}\ +\ \frac{1}{p+it+\upsilon_{it}}\biggl]\ -\ 1\biggl)\ .

(4.6)

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that for every ordered pair

(4.8)

Proof.
For convenience, for each ordered pair

(a,p)\in(0,1)^{2}
, we shall define the random variable

Y^{(a,p)}
by

Y^{(a,p)}\ :=\ p\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{J(a,p)}\zeta^{(a,p)}_{k}\

(4.9)

[the left side of (
4.8
)], and we shall employ the
(slight abuse of) notation

to denote its characteristic function.
Then for each

,

in (

.
Applying (

) (with

there replaced by

\displaystyle\varphi_{Y(a,b)}(t)\

\displaystyle=\ E\biggl[\exp\Bigl(itY^{(a,p)}\Bigl)\biggl]\ =\ E\biggl[\exp\biggl(it\sum_{k=1}^{J(a,p)}p\thinspace\zeta^{(a,p)}_{k}\biggl)\biggl]\ =\ \biggl(E\Bigl[\exp\bigl(itp\thinspace\zeta^{(a,p)}_{1}\bigl)\Bigl]\biggl)^{J(a,p)}

\displaystyle=\ \biggl[1\ +\ a\cdot\biggl(\frac{p}{2}\biggl[\frac{1}{p-itp+\upsilon_{-itp}}\ +\ \frac{1}{p+itp+\upsilon_{itp}}\biggl]\ -\ 1\biggl)\biggl]^{J(a,p)}

\displaystyle=\ \Biggl[1\ +\ \frac{1}{J(a,p)}\cdot\Biggl(a\cdot J(a,p)\cdot\biggl(\frac{1}{2}\biggl[\frac{1}{(1-it)+\upsilon_{-itp}/p}\ +\ \frac{1}{(1+it)+\upsilon_{itp}/p}\biggl]\ -\ 1\biggl)\Biggl)\Biggl]^{J(a,p)}\ .\quad

(4.10)

Now for the calculations to follow here, suppose

Let us first look at the first “big fraction” (inside the

Refer again to (

) and (

[For the case

.

Thus by (

Lemma 6.3

Suppose

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r_{k}y_{k}\neq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r_{k}z_{k}

Proof.
Let

Let

denote the greatest element of
\{1,2,\dots,m\}

such that
y_{j}\neq z_{j}

If

Therefore, suppose instead that

Obviously it suffices to show that

\sum_{k=1}^{j}r_{k}y_{k}\neq\sum_{k=1}^{j}r_{k}z_{k}

For each

r_{k}\leq 3^{k-j}r_{j}

|r_{k}y_{k}-r_{k}z_{k}|=r_{k}|y_{k}-z_{k}|\leq 3^{k-j}r_{j}\cdot 2

Hence

\biggl|\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}r_{k}y_{k}\ -\ \sum_{k=1}^{j-1}r_{k}z_{k}\biggl|\ \leq\ 2r_{j}\cdot 3^{-j}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}3^{k}\ =\ (2r_{j}\cdot 3^{-j})\cdot(1/2)\cdot(3^{j}-3)\ <\ r_{j}.

(6.1)

Also,

|r_{j}y_{j}-r_{j}z_{j}|=r_{j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|\geq r_{j}\cdot 1

Lemma 8.4
