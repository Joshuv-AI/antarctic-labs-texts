An advanced LLM-powered article extraction pipeline delivers a database of buffers for protein studies and protein expression conditions
Authors: Zhuojian Chen, Eng Aloysius, J Sivaraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing protein expression and buffer conditions is a major challenge in life-science research and biotechnology. We present an LLM-powered extraction pipeline that systematically converts unstructured scientific literature into a structured, queryable database. Leveraging advanced optical character recognition, custom reranker models, and large language models, our system achieves ~97% extraction accuracy. By processing 100 353 structural biology publications associated with the RCSB Protein Data Bank, we constructed a database containing 947 153 distinct buffer entries and 73 210 expression protocols for 35 690 unique proteins, establishing the largest resource of its kind. The ProBufEx (https://www.probufex.org/) web platform enables rapid exploration of optimal conditions by protein properties or functions. Case studies reveal distinct patterns in chemical usage on proteins with different post-translational modifications, and function-dependent expression strategies for proteins with different enzymatic activities. This resource transforms vast structural biology literature into actionable knowledge, accelerating evidence-based protocol design, reducing trial-and-error, and streamlining experiments in protein science.

Introduction

The acquisition of pure, homogeneous protein samples is a critical prerequisite for a wide range of biochemical and biophysical studies [1]. High sample quality underpins not only structural methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), where purity and homogeneity strongly affect experimental success and attainable resolution [2], but also many downstream applications including enzymatic and binding assays, thermodynamic stability measurements, interaction mapping, and preclinical evaluation in animal models. Reliable protein characterization therefore depends fundamentally on sample purity, integrity, and conformational uniformity [3].

Despite its importance, obtaining high-quality protein samples remains a major bottleneck in biological research, particularly for the characterization of certain classes of proteins, such as membrane proteins, which are notoriously difficult to express and stabilize outside their native lipid environment [4, 5]. Successful protein production hinges on many interdependent factors, including the choice of protein expression system and culture conditions [6], precise buffer pH control and choice of buffering agents [7], and the careful tuning of salt concentrations and redox environments. Likewise, the inclusion of solubility-enhancing additives, like detergents, glycerol, or polyethylene glycol to prevent aggregation and maintain protein stability [8, 9], also determines production success.

The scientific literature represents a rich repository of successful experimental strategies. Every 3D structure deposited in the public Protein Data Bank (PDB) is accompanied by a publication detailing the successful production and purification of the protein(s). Collectively, these articles form a massive knowledgebase that could reveal actionable design principles for optimizing purification strategies. However, manually filtering these protocols is extremely labor-intensive and impractical, since the PDB contains over 255 630 structures as of June 2026 [10].

High-performance Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at natural language processing and present a promising avenue for automating the analysis of this vast scientific corpus [11]. However, most state-of-the-art LLMs are trained on general language data, and their direct application to highly specialized scientific texts, such as protein production protocols, often yields suboptimal accuracy. The nuanced jargon and implicit knowledge within this domain pose significant challenges [12, 13]. While fine-tuning an LLM on a curated dataset of biological literature can improve performance, this approach requires immense effort in data preparation and substantial computational resources. Moreover, with the rapid evolution of AI, a custom fine-tuned model risks becoming quickly outdated by newer, more powerful foundation models.

To circumvent these limitations, our previous work introduced a modular LLM approach for extracting information from protein studies [14]. Building on this, we have re-engineered the pipeline using principles from modern information retrieval, with improved Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and article segmentation strategy, refined prompt engineering, and an integrated custom-trained reranker model to balance efficiency with high precision [15]. This powerful pipeline has expanded our analysis to cover 100 353 articles, a 54.6% increase from our previous tool (64 909 articles). These high-accuracy extractions enabled the creation of ProBufEx, the world’s largest database, dedicated to protein expression, purification, and characterization. ProBufEx contains 35 690 proteins with unique UniProt IDs, making it over 7.5 times larger than PurificationDB [16], a previously established database of purification buffers in this field. Beyond purification buffers, ProBufEx also curates protein expression conditions and buffers used for protein characterization, and it provides semantic search, step-specific insights across workflows, and built-in statistical visualizations. It marks a major advancement in transforming unstructured scientific text into structured and searchable knowledge in protein-related studies. ProBufEx is accessible via a user-friendly web server (www.probufex.org), designed to assist researchers in streamlining experimental design for protein structure determination and in vitro characterization.

Results

An LLM-powered pipeline for high-throughput information extraction

Our LLM-powered tool utilizes a sophisticated, multi-stage pipeline (Fig. 1) to accurately and efficiently extract key information from scientific articles for protein structural studies. Running on a local workstation with two GPUs (RTX 5090), the system can process up to 10 000 articles per day. The process begins with robust document preparation, designed to accommodate the diverse formatting styles across journals. To ensure that the logical flow of the text is correctly maintained, articles in PDF format are first converted into machine-readable, markdown-format text powered by MinerU [17], an advanced AI-based OCR engine that outperforms the capabilities of Unstructured OCR package used in our previous tool to deliver superior conversion fidelity.

Workflow of the LLM-powered information extraction pipeline for chemical and buffer details. Scientific articles in PDF format are first converted to markdown text by MinerU (v1.3.6) and segmented by their subtitles. A custom reranker model selects the most relevant segments, in which a chemical identifier program then bolds the names of chemicals. The exact target protein names are determined using an LLM-based identifier. This processed text is incorporated into a one-shot, three-step answer style prompt and processed by the Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct model. The output of the model, in JSON format, is refined to correct inaccuracies and construct the final database (ProBufEx) of extracted information.

Following conversion, the pipeline addresses the challenge of article length, which averages 17 800 LLM tokens. Recognizing that relevant information is often localized, articles are segmented first by subtitle to preserve contextual integrity. A fine-tuned reranker model then intelligently selects the most pertinent segments for a given query. For example, when extracting buffer details, this model effectively reduces the input text to an average of 3800 tokens. This pre-selection cuts down computational resource consumption by ~ 80% and significantly improves model performance and accuracy by removing irrelevant content.

To further enhance extraction accuracy, we address key terminological challenges inherent to biochemical literature, such as abbreviations. The LLM-based program first identifies abbreviations in each article and resolves them to their official UniProt IDs. This crucial disambiguation step prevents confusion and ensures subsequent analyses focus on the correct target protein. Similarly, to tackle the issue of chemical synonyms, we developed a comprehensive dictionary containing 284 commonly used chemicals and their 1441 alternative names from our extraction results. Benchmarking against standard libraries revealed that this custom approach achieved a mapping correctness of 89.0% and a processing speed of 232.02 words/second, significantly outperforming PubChemPy and ChemSpiPy (Supplementary Fig. 1). A dedicated program scans this dictionary and highlights in bold all identified chemical names as an attention mechanism for the LLM. This has resulted in an 11.1% increase in buffer-type extraction in our test dataset. In the final stage, an optimized prompt—incorporating relevant information from the UniProt, RCSB PDB, and PubMed databases—is combined with the selected article segments. This comprehensive input is processed by the Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct model, chosen to balance high accuracy with computational efficiency [18].

Fine-tuned reranker models demonstrate high F-scores in segment selection

In our previous article extraction tool [14], we used an embedding model to select segments containing relevant information. However, its accuracy was limited when extracting detailed content, as the embedding selection process is primarily optimized to handle millions of entries rather than capture finer or nuanced content within an article. Given that a single article can be divided into 30 to 150 segments, reranker models are generally more effective for precise selection at this scale. To explore this, we evaluated the BGE family models, known as leading embedding and reranker models in 2023 [19]. The leading reranker model in 2025, qwen3-reranker-8B, was also included in our comparison [20]. The swarm plot in Fig. 2A shows the distribution of scores among different models. The BGE-embedding model demonstrated a better initial classification of positive (P) and negative (N) article segments pertaining to buffer details in protein purification and characterization compared with the BGE-reranker model. However, as illustrated in Fig. 2B, both models exhibited suboptimal performance: the BGE-embedding model recorded a 19.1% false-positive (FP) rate and a 20.3% false-negative (FN) rate, while the BGE-reranker model showed a 44.9% FP rate and a 13.5% FN rate. Such high error rates are unsatisfactory for our purposes.

Comparative performance analysis of the BGE-embedding model and reranker models. Performance was evaluated on a held-out test set constructed at the article level to prevent data leakage. Articles were split before text segmentation, ensuring that all chunks from the same article were assigned to the same dataset partition. The task was binary segment selection, identifying whether text chunks contained target protein purification details or not. The specific retrieval query used for classification was: ‘Is it a section relating to methods of protein purification?’ Positive chunks contain relevant text of protein purification methods, whereas negative chunks come from unrelated sections such as introduction, results, discussion, or structural interpretation. For improved clarity, the scores of 32 consecutive negative samples were averaged and are represented as a single black dot. (B) The corresponding false positive (FP) and false negative (FN) rates are shown for each model. (C) F-beta (with β set to 3 to emphasize recall), precision, and recall scores are presented, highlighting the superior performance of the fine-tuned models.

To address these limitations, we fine-tuned the BGE-reranker model using a dataset distilled from our previous LLM-based article extraction tool and undertook a 5-step data cleaning strategy (Supplementary Fig. 2). This fine-tuning process substantially improved performance. Even without the data cleaning process, the fine-tuned model achieved significantly lower error rates than the original, with a 2.67% FP rate and a 0.39% FN rate. The data cleaning strategy further enhanced model precision, reducing the FP rate to a mere 0.44%. These improvements are visually corroborated on the swarm plot (Fig. 2A), with a remarkable sixfold reduction in false positives.

The F-score comparisons (Fig. 2C) further validated the high performance of our custom ‘Buffer-Reranker’ model. The primary function of a reranker model is to prepare data for subsequent LLM processing; thus, recall is more critical than precision. Indeed, failure to select a segment containing crucial buffer details would preclude the LLM from analyzing and extracting that information. Consequently, we evaluated the performance of the model using the F-beta score, setting β to 3 to emphasize recall. The final fine-tuned Buffer-Reranker model achieved an F-beta score of 0.979, a significant improvement over the score of 0.399 achieved with the original model. Moreover, our model outperforms the current state-of-the-art open-source model, Qwen3-reranker-8B, which scored 0.346. Notably, our Buffer-Reranker model achieves this superior performance with just 1/14 of the parameters of the Qwen3 model, highlighting its efficiency and effectiveness.

Enhanced high-accuracy extraction of protein expression, purification, and characterization data for a comprehensive database

Our previous tool lacked precision and coverage for buffer types and compositions. By integrating our fine-tuned reranker models and citation-tracker program, the current pipeline delivered greater accuracy and scalability, supporting the construction of a comprehensive database of protein expression, purification, and characterization data. To construct ProBufEx, we analyzed 100 353 unique publications, including 68 248 publications related to depositing protein 3D structures and 32 105 key cross-references. Full-text sources of the PDB-associated publications were available in multiple formats: 68 248 publications had a PDF version and 65 247 had an HTML version. These are format-specific source counts, not counts of unique publications, and they are therefore not additive. When both PDF and HTML versions were available for the same publication, both were processed independently, and the extraction with the higher reranker confidence score was retained. An additional 74 047 supplemental files associated with these publications were also analyzed. A significant challenge was that many articles associated with depositing protein PDB structures did not list comprehensive methodological details of the target protein production, purification and characterization. Instead, they referred to prior work for such methods. Our pipeline identified 36 806 such back references from those articles. To capture detailed experimental protocols, our LLM-based citations identifier (Supplementary Fig. 3) successfully processed 32 105 articles from these key external citations and 24 105 of those resulted in valid extraction records. Thus, the relevant method details of 66 863 articles linked to direct depositions of experimentally determined protein 3D structures were recovered, which covers 85.3% of the articles documented in RCSB PDB (Fig. 3A). The remaining articles were not processed due to unavailability for download or lack of specific methodological details in the article itself or any external source.

To evaluate the accuracy of our LLM-powered extraction pipeline, we manually curated a random selection of 150 articles, covering 24 DOI registrants and 76 distinct journals. While errors in LLM-based data extraction are inherent, our optimized strategy achieved a high average accuracy of ~97% (Fig. 3B). Performance varied based on task complexity: simpler tasks, such as identifying buffer chemical components and induction temperature in recombinant protein expression, reached 98.22% and 98.15% accuracy, respectively, whereas more complex tasks, like summarizing buffer function, achieved 96.1%. This high-fidelity extraction process resulted in a comprehensive database containing 947 153 distinct buffer entries related to protein purification and characterization. Each entry included detailed information such as buffer name, pH, concentrations and units of chemical components, buffer function, and the experimental steps. Data analysis revealed the most frequent applications: protein activity assays (192 248 cases), elution buffers in purification (82 588 cases), and lysis buffers (57 697 cases) as the top three (Fig. 3C). In addition, each buffer entry was mapped to the target protein, covering 35 690 proteins with unique UniProt IDs.

In addition to buffer information extraction, our pipeline cataloged 73 210 instances of recombinant protein expression conditions. The E. coli expression system was the most prevalent, accounting for 61 646 cases, followed by the insect cell system with 4202 cases (Fig. 3D). Each ProBufEx entry captured critical parameters of the expression protocol, including the specific cell line, culture and induction temperatures, the inducing chemical and its concentration, the duration of protein expression, and shaker speed during incubation. In cases where the source article did not specify one or more of these conditions, our pipeline classified the corresponding data field as ‘unknown,’ maintaining the integrity and transparency of the extracted information. These structured buffer and expression fields enabled the following case studies, in which ProBufEx records were aggregated by UniProtKB annotations and experimental workflow steps to reveal protein-class-dependent protocol patterns.

Case study 1: buffers for purifying and characterizing proteins with different post-translational modifications

Within our ProBufEx database, proteins were systematically annotated and grouped using 1145 unique UniProtKB keywords, which are in turn classified into seven categories: biological process, cellular components, coding sequence diversity, domain, ligand, molecular function, and post-translational modification. A key advantage of this article extraction tool is its ability to identify the specific experimental steps in which a buffer was used, providing a more granular analysis than our previous tool [14]. This distinction is important because buffer composition often changes between lysis, binding, washing, elution, and storage, with each step requiring tailored conditions to maximize yield, purity, and protein stability [21–24]. To demonstrate the utility of ProBufEx, we analyzed how chemical usage differs during the purification and characterization of proteins across five common post-translational modifications (PTMs): acetylation, disulfide bonds, glycosylation, lipidation, and phosphorylation. We analyzed the most commonly used chemicals and identified statistically significant compositional trends.

Figure 4A provides a comprehensive overview of differential chemical usage, derived from an analysis of 35 690 unique proteins with purification and characterization records. MgCl2 and CaCl2 are among the most frequently used divalent cation salts in protein purification. Proteins with acetylation or lipidation were associated with significantly high usage of MgCl2 (P < .001) across all six purification buffer types examined. Conversely, proteins containing disulfide bonds exhibited significantly low usage of MgCl2 (P < 10−4). Regarding CaCl2, its use is significantly elevated (P < 10−5) only for proteins with glycosylation or disulfide bonds, and specifically within buffers intended for protein activity assays. This pattern indicates that divalent ions such as Mg2+ and Ca2+ are differentially utilized to stabilize proteins with distinct post-translational modifications, reflecting PTM-specific requirements for structural maintenance and stability during purification.

Chemical preferences in protein purification and characterization associated with PTM. (A) Five PTMs were analyzed: Acetylation, disulfide bond, glycosylation, lipidation, and phosphorylation. This analysis was on 35 690 unique proteins with experimental 3D structures. To minimize outlier effects, only chemicals used in more than 50 cases within each buffer category of a given protein group were included. One-sided Z-tests compared proteins with and without each PTM in a certain type of experiment. Data points with P < .05 were considered significantly different and are shown in the heatmap. (B) The violin plot of the distribution of detergent concentrations in lysis buffer (left panel) and elution buffer (right panel) in purifying lipoproteins. Buffering agents are essential for maintaining a stable pH environment that preserves protein structure and activity, minimizing chemical degradation throughout the purification process [25]. Figure 4A indicates that Tris and potassium phosphate buffer were used significantly less frequently when purifying the proteins with glycosylation (P < 10−4) or disulfide bonds (P < 10−4.8), while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used in a significantly higher frequency (P < 10−6) for these same protein classes. Furthermore, HEPES demonstrates a strong association with acetylated proteins, both across all six purification buffer types (P < 10−7.7) and in characterization buffers (P < .01) used for methods like protein activity assays and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC).

When the significant enrichment/depletion patterns in Fig. 4A are interpreted as protocol profiles, three practical groups emerge. First, acetylated and phosphorylated proteins show a reducing, cytosolic-like profile: DTT (dithiothreitol, P < 10−10), TCEP (tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine, P < 10−10), and β-ME (2-mercaptoethanol, P < 10−4.8) were used at significantly higher frequency across purification buffers for these proteins. DTT (P < 10−5.2) and TCEP (P < 10−9.3) also remained enriched in characterization buffers, including circular dichroism spectroscopy, surface plasmon resonance, and ITC. Second, glycosylated and disulfide-bonded proteins show a nonreducing, physiological-buffer profile, with reduced use of DTT, TCEP, and β-ME and increased use of PBS. This is consistent with the established practice of avoiding reducing conditions when native disulfide bonds must be preserved [26]. Third, lipidated/lipoprotein targets show a detergent-assisted profile, especially during lysis, consistent with the need to solubilize lipid-associated proteins while preserving native complexes [27].

These profiles can be used as task-specific starting recommendations rather than universal recipes. For example, for a native disulfide-bonded or glycosylated protein intended for structural characterization, the ProBufEx-derived routine starting condition would be using PBS or another mild physiological buffer as the preferred buffer class in Fig. 4A, together with physiological ionic strength and EDTA-free protease inhibitors. DTT, TCEP, and β-ME should generally be omitted unless the source protocol has a specific redox-related justification, because these reagents reduce protein disulfide bonds [28]. If the target is clearly soluble, detergent should initially be omitted to minimize unnecessary additives. If detergent is required for extraction or solubility, Fig. 4A suggests prioritizing Triton X-100, CHS (cholesteryl hemisuccinate), or DDM (n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside) for disulfide-bonded or glycosylated proteins, whereas Tween-20 does not show the same preference and should not be selected as the default detergent from this profile. This is consistent with the broader use of mild detergents for protein extraction and membrane/lipid-associated protein stabilization [29, 30].

Including an appropriate non-denaturing detergent in the purification buffer is critical when purifying lipoproteins, as it facilitates the solubilization of the lipid-protein complex into mixed micelles while preserving native lipidation [27]. An analysis of purification protocols reveals a statistically significant prevalence of five specific detergents in lysis buffers: Triton X-100 (P < 10−10), NP40 (P < 10−10), CHAPS (P < 10−6.9), CHS (P < 10−6.2) and DDM (P < 10−5.3). In elution buffers, however, the number of significantly prevalent detergents narrows to two: CHS (P < 10−10) and DDM (P < 10−7.8). To further investigate these detergent profiles of lipoproteins, we generated the distribution plots from ProBufEx (Fig. 4B). The resulting violin plots illustrate distinct concentration clusters for these detergents. In lysis buffers, NP40, DDM, Digitonin and Triton X-100 typically cluster around a 1.0% concentration. In contrast, CHAPS clusters at a lower 0.5%, albeit with a wider distribution (0.2% to 1.0%). A clear distinction is observed in elution buffers, where detergent concentrations are markedly lower; the typical concentrations for NP40 and DDM decrease to 0.1%, and the concentration of CHS drops from 0.2% to 0.02%. This marked reduction in both detergent variety and concentration from lysis to elution suggests that while higher concentrations are essential for efficient initial membrane solubilization, lower concentrations are sufficient and likely optimal for maintaining lipoprotein complex stability during subsequent purification steps.

These findings show that ProBufEx can support task-specific starting protocols by filtering records by protein annotation and workflow step. The patterns mainly summarize established purification principles at scale rather than represent de novo biochemical rules, and the novelty of ProBufEx is the systematic quantification of such patterns across the 66 863 articles associated with PDB structure depositions. Protocols that deviate from the enriched profiles, such as DTT-containing solubilization/dialysis buffers reported for disulfide-containing gastric H(+), K(+)-ATPase β subunit [31], can be flagged as cautionary or potentially informative cases for user verification.

Case study 2: divergent expression strategies for proteins with distinct enzymatic activities

To investigate how protein function influences expression strategies, we applied ProBufEx to analyze E. coli expression profiles across protein classes. ProBufEx supports keyword-based queries using UniProtKB annotations and retrieves recombinant protein production data from published literature. In this case study, we focused on four key induction parameters: temperature, duration, IPTG concentration, and shaking speed. Our extraction pipeline identified expression condition profiles for 1145 distinct protein properties. Of these, we isolated eight common enzymatic activities for detailed comparison, as presented in Fig. 5A. The analysis revealed that recombinant proteins with different enzymatic properties exhibit distinct preferences for induction temperatures and durations. Serine/threonine-protein kinases and serine proteases were contrasted to illustrate these functional dependencies.

The recombinant proteins with different enzymatic properties showed different preferences for induction temperatures and durations in the previous successful cases of protein expression. For instance, from the 778 expression cases for serine/threonine-protein kinases, the statistical analysis revealed a strong preference for low-temperature expression at 18°C (P < 10−4) and 17°C (P < .01) (Fig. 5A). This suggests that the folding or solubility of these kinases may benefit from slower expression kinetics at reduced temperatures. In contrast, serine proteases were more commonly expressed at intermediate temperatures closer to room temperature, including 23°C (P < .01) and 22°C (P < .05). Induction at lower temperatures was significantly less common for this group, such as at 15°C (P < .05), indicating a possible tradeoff between yield and folding efficiency when expressed too slowly. Induction duration was also functionally dependent: serine/threonine-protein kinases tended to be induced for longer durations, with significant peaks at 12, 14, 15, and 22 hours (P < .001), aligning with the notion that extended induction at lower temperatures reduces the risk of aggregation and promotes correct folding. Serine proteases, on the other hand, were more commonly induced for shorter times, ~4 hours (P < .001) and 5 hours (P < .05), consistent with faster expression kinetics at moderate temperatures.

Beyond temperature and time, the ProBufEx database reveals granular differences in chemical induction and physical agitation. Figure 5B shows that the distribution of IPTG concentrations used for induction differed between the serine proteases and serine/threonine-protein kinases, with 500 μM IPTG primarily used for serine/threonine-protein kinases, and 1000 μM for serine proteases, with a secondary cluster at 500 μM for serine proteases. This variation may reflect differences in how tightly expression must be regulated to avoid toxicity or misfolding [32]. The distributions of the shaker speed chosen by previous publications between serine proteases and serine/threonine-protein kinases also varied (Fig. 5C). For serine proteases, the distribution of shaking speeds is tightly clustered around 210 rpm, with a minor cluster at 250 rpm. Serine/threonine-protein kinases, however, exhibit a broader distribution with two prominent clusters at 200 rpm and 250 rpm.

Together, these results suggest that serine/threonine-protein kinases are generally more sensitive to induction conditions and benefit from slow expression at low temperatures with prolonged induction time and moderate IPTG concentrations, whereas serine proteases tolerate faster expression, higher temperatures, shorter induction durations, and higher IPTG concentrations. These ProBufEx-derived findings indicate that protein function is linked to non-random expression strategies, supporting the utility of the extracted expression records for evidence-based recombinant protein production design.

Discussion

To reduce computational demands and hallucination in LLMs, we significantly shortened the prompt length [33] by selecting only the most relevant segments associated with the query from each article. Embedding models or reranker models are effective tools for identifying such segments [34]. Within this ‘Reranker-LLM’ architecture, a fast, computationally inexpensive reranker first scans the full article to identify relevant candidate paragraphs. Then, a more powerful but computationally intensive LLM processes the prompt containing those paragraphs [35]. However, even top-ranked models, such as those from the BGE and Qwen3 families, failed to achieve satisfactory accuracy for our domain-specific queries. This is likely because such models are trained on general-purpose datasets and are not optimized for protein-related research. Similar limitations have been observed in reasoning-driven medical retrieval benchmarks [36]. Fine-tuning these models on our custom dataset largely reduced the error rate by ~10-fold for protein-related literature tasks. To reduce manual effort, we used LLMs to extract relevant segments from articles and prepare the training dataset, limiting human involvement to final quality control (Supplementary Fig. 2). Each reranker model only required ~100 GPU hours for training and 10 man-hours for data cleaning. Natural language inference (NLI) provides an alternative approach to segment selection by treating an article segment as the premise and the target information statement as the hypothesis [37]. Although not evaluated in this study, future work could compare NLI-based selection with our task-specific rerankers or use it as an additional validation step.

High-performing LLMs excel at processing scientific articles, but most state-of-the-art models are closed-source [38], and their API (application programming interface) usage is expensive, particularly when analyzing large datasets of articles [39]. Apart from the cost, closed-source models occasionally returned content warnings or declined to process articles involving biologically sensitive subjects, especially the articles related to viruses and infectious diseases. Our optimized article extraction pipeline maximizes the capabilities of LLMs, enabling us to use smaller open-source models to reduce costs without sacrificing accuracy. It runs efficiently on a local workstation with two GPUs (RTX 5090) and can process around 10 000 articles per day. Given the rapid growth of scientific literature, economical and efficient pipelines, such as automatic data extraction using neural networks [40] and retrieval-augmented architectures for scalable text analysis [41], allow researchers to analyze more data in less time. Our fully open-source pipeline can be deployed locally to ensure data privacy and security. And we plan to update the database using the latest high-ranking LLM annually.

Using our LLM-powered article extraction and analysis pipeline, we successfully processed 100 353 articles associated with depositing protein 3D structures to the PDB. These articles provide rich descriptions of successful protein expression and purification protocols, ultimately yielding high-quality samples suitable for structural studies and protein functional assays. Apart from converting unstructured text from published articles into structured data entries, we built a comprehensive database and developed a web platform to maximize utility. These entries were grouped using text embeddings or categorized based on target protein properties, allowing researchers to rapidly analyze thousands of protocols in a comparative framework. Moreover, by applying statistical tests, ProBufEx highlights biases and significant parameter differences between protein types, enabling more evidence-based protocol design. The recovery of PTM-dependent buffer preferences and enzyme-function-dependent expression strategies further supports that the extracted records retain meaningful experimental context beyond field-level accuracy. Therefore, our pipeline helps shift protein production from trial-and-error to a data-driven strategy, reducing uncertainty and accelerating recombinant protein research, which aligns with recent efforts to combine AI with high-throughput biological data for rational experiment planning [42, 43]. Efficient generation of high-quality protein samples enables reliable downstream applications including structural studies, quantitative binding and enzymatic assays, stability profiling, interaction mapping, and functional testing in cellular and in vivo models.

Despite these strengths, ProBufEx should be interpreted with several validity limitations in mind. Internally, errors may arise from OCR conversion, article segmentation, reranker false negatives, LLM hallucination or incomplete interpretation of complex biological descriptions, ambiguous protein or chemical names, unit normalization, and inaccuracies propagated from back-referenced papers. We mitigated these risks through UniProt/PDB-based target mapping, chemical-name normalization, fine-tuned rerankers and structured JSON prompts, but systematic errors may remain, especially in unusually complex articles. In addition, each record in ProBufEx is accompanied by the original evidence sentence and source article metadata (DOI and link), allowing users to verify the extracted condition against the primary literature. Externally, because ProBufEx is built from PDB-associated structural biology literature, it is biased toward successful protein production and structure-determination experiments. Thus, its patterns may not fully generalize to failed or unpublished protocols, industrial workflows, non-English papers, inaccessible full texts, or all areas of protein biochemistry; broader applications should therefore be interpreted cautiously and validated experimentally where possible.

Looking forward, this large-scale dataset offers more than a reference resource: it is a rich training ground for predictive tools. The patterns linking protein annotations to expression, purification and characterization are ripe for exploitation by machine learning, and ideal for training deep learning models that predict optimal expression conditions and buffer components directly from the prior knowledge of protein sequence or functions. These predictive tools will guide the design of protein production pipelines, enabling faster and more reliable structural studies, functional assays, interaction mapping, and therapeutic discovery.

Methods

We built an automated LLM-based pipeline that converts PDFs to structured text, normalizes chemical/protein names, uses fine-tuned reranker models to select relevant sections, and integrates Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct to extract standardized JSON records for protein expression, purification, characterization, and citations. Full details are provided in the Supplementary Methods; statistical comparisons used one-sided Z-tests (P < .05).
