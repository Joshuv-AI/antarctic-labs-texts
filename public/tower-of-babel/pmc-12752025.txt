Effect of serum ferritin on the prognosis of patients with coronary heart disease combined with heart failure
Authors: Nilupaer Aisikeer, Jian Zhang, Wei-tong Gao, Zhi-ying Wen, Xue-Chun He, Hong-Xia Chen, Yan-Xiao Li, Ying-Ying Zheng, Yi-tong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo study the effect of serum ferritin (SF) on the prognosis of patients with coronary artery disease combined with heart failure.MethodsWe retrospectively included 3,424 adult patients with combined coronary artery disease (CAD) and chronic heart failure (CHF) from an initial screening of 4,442 patients. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, presence of CAD (≥ 50% stenosis of major vessels) confirmed by angiography, and fulfillment of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the diagnosis of CHF. One thousand one hundred and eight patients who might interfere with the primary outcome were excluded, including those with comorbid advanced cancer, severe organ dysfunction, immune or psychiatric disorders, and those with recent major trauma/surgery. Patients were categorized into a high SF group (≥ 230.465 ng/ml) versus a low SF group (< 230.465 ng/ml) based on the optimal threshold value of serum ferritin (SF). Clinical data were collected for between-group comparison and all-cause mortality assessment. Kaplan–Meier curves were used for survival analysis and differences between groups were compared by log-rank test; multivariate Cox regression analysis was also applied to screen independent risk factors affecting patients’ prognosis.ResultCompared with the group with low SF values, the percentage of men, all-cause mortality, and B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) were higher in the group with high SF values than in the group with low SF values (P < 0.05); After adjusting for potential confounders, the multivariable Cox regression analysis identified that age, albumin, and high-density lipoprotein were independent factors affecting survival risk, and the high SF group had a 47% higher risk of events compared to the low SF group. (HR:1.472, 95%CI: 1.174–1.846 P = 0.001).ConclusionSF is an important prognostic biomarker in patients with coronary artery disease combined with heart failure, independent of traditional risk factors, and elevated SF levels are associated with poor prognosis in patients with coronary artery disease combined with heart failure.Keywords: Serum ferritin, Iron storage, Coronary heart disease, Heart failure, Prognosis

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease are the leading causes of cardiovascular disease (CVD) death and disability worldwide [1], and a multinational collaborative study has shown that the burden of cardiovascular disease is increasing in almost all countries [2]. Coronary artery disease is the most common cause of heart failure, which in turn further exacerbates myocardial ischemia, creating a vicious cycle. When the two coexist, the clinical outcome of the patient is significantly worse [3]. Iron (Fe) is an essential micronutrient that performs a variety of functions in biochemical and physiological processes, including oxygen transport and storage, mitochondrial respiration, and redox reactions [4–6]. SF, a marker of body iron stores, maintains excess iron in a safe and bioavailable form and correlates linearly with body iron stores [5], which is significantly up-regulated in response to inflammatory signaling [7] and correlates with poor prognosis within the context of cardiovascular disease [8]. Patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure are often associated with a chronic inflammatory response. Elevated levels of SF, an acute time-phase reactive protein, reflect the degree of inflammation in the body [9] rather than the body's iron levels [10]. Studies have demonstrated that elevated SF is associated with an increased risk of worsening coronary artery disease [11–13]. Inflammatory responses exacerbate myocardial oxidative stress injury, which in turn affects cardiac function and prognosis [14]. This study aimed to investigate the effect of SF levels on the prognosis of patients with coronary heart disease combined with heart failure.

Background

Advancements of the Present Study Compared to Previous Work:The role of iron metabolism, especially SF, in cardiovascular disease is of increasing interest. Since Sullivan's hypothesis in 1981 that iron stores are associated with coronary heart disease [17], several studies, such as the 1992 Finnish report, have further confirmed that SF is positively associated with the risk of heart attack in men [18]. Recent studies have shown that elevated SF is associated with poor prognosis in heart failure, for example, a prospective study of 526 heart failure patients showed that high SF significantly increased the composite risk of rehospitalization or cardiovascular death within 180 days [11]. Another study indicated that SF could synergize with inflammatory markers such as IL-6 to predict mortality in patients with peripheral arterial disease [9].

However, most of the existing studies have focused on a single disease or have not adequately validated the independent predictive value of SF in multivariate models, and especially lack in-depth exploration for the co-morbid group of coronary heart disease combined with heart failure. In the present study, based on a large single-center retrospective cohort (n = 4442) focusing specifically on this population, SF was confirmed as an independent risk factor by multivariate Cox regression adjusting for confounders such as age, albumin, and high-density lipoprotein, and a clinically meaningful cut-off value (230.465 ng/mL) was determined. Compared with previous studies, this study has a well-defined population and a large sample size, providing a clear risk ratio and cut-off value, and the conclusions are more clinically informative.

Clarification of the Research Question: Based on Existing Gaps in the Literature. There are several key gaps in the current body of knowledge regarding the prognostic significance of SF in patients with coronary heart disease combined with heart failure. First, there is a lack of large-scale clinical studies specifically targeting this cohort of patients to clarify the independent predictive role of SF. Second, specific risk cut-off values for SF levels have not been harmonized in the literature, which limits the translation of SF from a biomarker to a tool for clinical application.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study were obtained from a retrospective cohort study in the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. The study cohort consisted of hospitalized patients admitted for acute and exacerbated chronic heart failure. The study protocol followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval number: 202207–019). Data collected included patients' demographic characteristics, laboratory findings during hospitalization, and regular follow-up information.

Study methods subjects

A total of 4442 consecutive patients with a confirmed diagnosis of CHF were initially enrolled as the study cohort. The patient selection strictly adhered to diagnostic and clinical guidelines, specifically referencing the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Heart Failure [15] and the 2019 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes [16] to define the inclusion criteria.

Eligible participants must meet two core requirements: 1) being 18 years of age or older (to ensure adult clinical relevance), and 2) having a coronary angiography report confirming ≥ 50% stenosis in at least one major coronary vessel (to standardize the severity of underlying coronary artery involvement).

After applying the above inclusion criteria, a total of 1018 patients were excluded from the final analysis. The exclusion reasons were clearly categorized to ensure transparency of cohort selection: presence of advanced cancer (to avoid confounding by terminal illness), severe dysfunction of major organs (e.g., liver, kidney, or lung failure, which may independently affect prognosis), diagnosed immune system disorders, including autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis) and immunodeficiency states, based on ICD-10 coding and clinical diagnosis records, documented psychiatric disorders (to reduce bias from mental health-related treatment adherence issues), and a history of serious injuries (e.g., fractures, major surgery) within the past three months (to exclude short-term physiological stressors unrelated to CHF) (in Fig. 1).

Data collection

The data collected included a range of parameters, including demographic details, medical history, physical examination findings, and laboratory test results. Specifically, we assessed laboratory indicators such as white blood cell count, creatinine, uric acid, triglycerides, total cholesterol, total protein, and BNP levels. These indices were measured by fasting peripheral venous blood collected on the day of admission or the following morning.

Diagnostic criteria

In this study, the diagnosis of CHF strictly adhered to the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute and Chronic Heart Failure [15], while the diagnosis of coronary heart disease was based on the 2019 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes [16]. A comprehensive dataset was compiled for all enrolled patients, encompassing demographic details, cardiovascular risk factors, and laboratory values (including hematologic and blood chemical test results), as well as information on chronic diseases (such ashypertension and diabetes mellitus) and pharmacologic interventions. Cardiovascular risk factors included gender, age, and chronic conditions like hypertension and diabetes mellitus.Hypertension was defined as a documented medical history, ongoing antihypertensive treatment, or sustained elevation of blood pressure (≥ 140/90 mmHg) at multiple visits. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a documented diagnosis with glucose-lowering medication treatment, confirmed by elevated blood glucose thresholds: fasting blood glucose > 7.1 mmol/L and two-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L. Both diagnoses were established in accordance with standard clinical criteria.For SF measurement, participants underwent venous blood collection, and blood ferritin levels were determined using methods such as chemiluminescent assays and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA).

Follow-up

All patients were followed up after hospital discharge, with the follow-up interval varying from 36 months. Additional assessments were conducted as needed based on individual patient conditions, and follow-up was mainly carried out via outpatient visits. The primary follow-up endpoint was all-cause mortality (ACM), defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period regardless of the underlying cause, and the type and timing of events at this endpoint were recorded.Although all-cause mortality was a robust and unbiased endpoint, the inclusion of heart failure-specific rehospitalization and other cardiovascular events as primary outcome indicators would have provided deeper insights into disease-specific pathophysiology. The failure to analyze the entire cohort for rigorously adjudicated cause-specific mortality is a limitation that could be addressed by future studies. The study duration had a median of 22.75 months, and the study was designed to assess ACM to comprehensively evaluate the overall risk of death in the study population.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test, one-way ANOVA was applied for parametric variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-parametric variables. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with group comparisons made by the log-rank test. Discussion

SF levels affect the prognosis of patients with coronary artery disease combined with heart failure. SF is an acute-phase reactive protein, and high levels may reflect the state of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are associated with the progression of atherosclerosis, myocardial remodeling, and worsening of heart failure. Several previous studies have also shown the poor prognosis associated with high SF. In 1981, Dr. Sullivan was the first to suggest that increased iron stores in the body are associated with the development of coronary heart disease [17]. In 1992, a Finnish study demonstrated that SF was positively associated with the risk of acute myocardial infarction in men [18]. In a prospective randomized study, a robust positive correlation between SF, interleukin 6 IL-61 tissue necrosis factor-a, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein was observed by blood sampling in patients with peripheral arterial disease, and elevated SF and IL-6 levels were associated with increased mortality [9]. In a cohort of 526 patients evaluating the relationship between SF and clinical outcome in heart failure (180 days), higher SF levels were associated with a higher composite risk of heart failure good hospitalization or cardiovascular death (tertile 2: HR 1.75; 95% C1.10–2.79; p = 0.017; tertile 3: HR 1.79; 95% C1 1.08–2.97; p = 0.025) [11]. The idea that elevated SF levels are independently associated with poor prognosis in patients with coronary artery disease combined with heart failure has been gradually emphasized in recent years, and the underlying mechanism involves multiple pathophysiological processes, such as abnormal iron metabolism, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress, etc., and excess iron can enter the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes and generate a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the Fenton reaction (Fenton Reaction)which leads to lipid peroxidation, DNA damage and protein oxidation, ultimately causing cardiomyocyte death and fibrosis [19]; iron overload exacerbates inflammatory responses by promoting macrophage M1-type polarization, forming a vicious circle and accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis [20], through regular testing of SF levels, patients who may be at higher risk can be identified early so that appropriate preventive measures can be taken. In addition, SF levels can be used as an important indicator to guide treatment and assess efficacy. There are several limitations to this study. First, the analysis relied only on serum ferritin levels and did not incorporate other key iron metabolism parameters, such as serum iron versus transferrin saturation. Because ferritin is also an acute-phase reactive protein and its concentration is susceptible to systemic inflammation, it is difficult to accurately differentiate between iron loading in the real body and secondary alterations caused by inflammatory states such as anemia of chronic disease in the absence of the above mentioned supporting indicators. Future studies that systematically assess the complete iron metabolism profile, including regulatory factors such as ferritin, will help to more comprehensively understand the pathomechanisms of iron metabolism disorders in this population. Second, methemoglobinemia itself is not disease-specific, and its elevation, in addition to reflecting iron overload, is also common in a variety of clinical conditions such as liver dysfunction, chronic infections, autoimmune disorders, and alcohol abuse. Although we excluded patients with a definitive diagnosis of severe organ insufficiency or immune system disease at the enrollment stage, residual confounding by minor, subclinical, or undiagnosed comorbidities could not be completely avoided. Such potential etiologic confounding may interfere to some extent with the observed association between serum ferritin and mortality.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that serum ferritin is a predictor of all-cause mortality independent of traditional risk factors in patients with coronary heart disease combined with heart failure. Specifically, SF levels ≥ 230.465 ng/ml were significantly associated with a 1.47-fold increased risk of death. As a routine and cost-effective biomarker, SF shows important potential for clinical application in risk stratification of such patients, or may help to identify high-risk individuals and guide their intensive management. However, as this study was designed as a single-center retrospective study and ferritin levels are affected by multiple factors, the above conclusions need to be further validated in a prospective, multicenter study to confirm the generalizability thresholds and ultimately to clarify whether modulation of iron metabolism can directly improve patients’ prognosis.
