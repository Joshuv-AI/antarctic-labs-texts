Comparative effects of three mucoadhesive gels containing lidocaine, zinc acetate, and tannic acid on the gag reflex of dental patients: a randomized double-blind clinical trial
Authors: Nafiseh Nikkerdar, Hosna Seyedi, Shahla Mirzaeei, Roya Safari-Faramani, Amin Golshah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the effects of three mucoadhesive gels containing lidocaine, zinc acetate, and tannic acid on the gag reflex of dental patients.MethodsThis randomized double-blind clinical trial was conducted on 228 dental patients with gag reflex. The patients were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 57) of placebo, 2% lidocaine, 2% tannic acid, and 2% zinc acetate. The severity of the gag reflex of patients was initially scored by stimulating their soft and hard palate with a radiographic film using a 0–5 visual analog scale. After 5 min, mucoadhesive gels containing 2% lidocaine, zinc acetate, or tannic acid were applied on the hard and soft palate for 5–10 min, and the severity of the gag reflex was scored again. Data were analyzed by ANOVA and paired t-test for the single group comparison (alpha = 0.05).ResultsThe four groups had no significant difference in the baseline mean gag score (p = 0.05). The difference in the mean gag score was significant among the four groups after the intervention (p < 0.00), and the mean score in the experimental groups was significantly lower than that in the placebo group (p < 0.00). The difference in the mean gag score was not significant between the experimental groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe mucoadhesive gels containing 2% lidocaine, tannic acid, or zinc acetate significantly decreased the gag reflex in dental patients, with all three treatments showing comparable efficacy compared to the placebo. These findings suggest that each gel formulation is a viable alternative for managing the gag reflex during dental procedures.

Introduction

The gag reflex is an involuntary reflex that serves as a defense mechanism against pharyngeal stimulations. The cranial nerves 9 (glossopharyngeal) and 10 (vagus) play an important role in occurrence of the gag reflex. Following stimulation of the pharynx and tonsils, first a pause in respiration occurs, and then the thoracic, abdominal, oral, and pharyngeal muscles are contracted by neural stimuli, and gagging occurs. Elimination or reduction of the effects of stimuli is the main strategy to decrease the gag reflex [1]. Many dental procedures such as intraoral radiography, impression making, examination of the throat and pharynx, and endodontic and restorative procedures can stimulate the gag reflex [2]. The gag reflex during dental procedures can decrease patient cooperation and quality of treatment. Moreover, it can lead to discontinuation of the treatment procedure, waste time and cost, and cause patient dissatisfaction [3, 4]. Although different strategies have been proposed to control the gag reflex, an optimal method with negligible side effects and wide acceptance by patients is not yet available [2, 3].

Rosted et al. [5] suggested that acupuncture can be used to successfully decrease the gag reflex when making alginate impressions from the maxilla with minimal complications. Sari and Sari [6], Rosted [7], and Eitner et al. [8], also showed the positive effects of acupuncture on the gag reflex. Nonetheless, acupuncture is not often well accepted by patients. Sounders and Cameron [9] discussed that hypnotherapy and psychological interventions can effectively decrease the gag reflex; however, such methods are time-consuming and necessitate several sessions of counseling by a psychologist. Neumann and McCarty [10] reported that behavioral control and distraction with different strategies can decrease the gag reflex. Chidiac et al. [11] introduced edible salt for nausea control, and explained that it stimulates the taste buds in the anterior portion of the tongue and subsequently stimulates the chorda tympani nerve and inhibits nausea as such. Packer et al. [12] suggested the use of analgesics, sedatives, and tranquilizers to control the gag reflex. However, these medications (including diazepam and NO gas) have several side effects such as drowsiness, dizziness, and amnesia [13]. Long-lasting effect and the need for a companion when leaving the office are among other drawbacks of such medications [14].

Administration of local anesthetic agents like lidocaine in the form of spray or infiltration injection has also been suggested to decrease the gag reflex [15]. Evidence shows that medications that desensitize the stimulable mucosa in the posterior parts of the oral cavity also impede the nausea impulses. Duration of sensory and motor block by 2% lidocaine is approximately 60 min [16]. Medicinal herbs with local stringent effects have also been recently suggested to control the gag reflex. Evidence shows that tannin or tannic acid of plants has a direct local stringent effect on oral mucosa [17]. Tannin is also highly effective for enhancement of healing of oral aphthous ulcers and has anti-cancer properties. Pomegranate is rich in tannins [18, 19]. Pomegranate, with the scientific name Punica granatum L. belongs to the Punicaceae family [20]. Pomegranate peel accounts for 26–30% of the pomegranate fruit weight, and has extensive therapeutic applications due to its anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, and anti-sclerotic effects [21–24]. The pomegranate peel has high anti-oxidant activity due to the presence of phenols such as flavonoids (anthocyanins, catechins), and tannins ( punicalin, punicalagin, gallic acid, ellagic acid) [25, 26]. The pomegranate peel extract is commonly used in traditional medicine in many Asian countries for treatment of diarrhea, oropharyngeal inflammation, and occasionally stomach problems [27]. Lee et al. [28] reported optimal efficacy of the phenolic compounds present in pomegranate peel extract for treatment of inflammation and microbial infections, asthma, rheumatism, rheumatoid arthritis, and rheumatic heart disease [28]. Tannins are abundantly found in the pomegranate fruit peel, and Shadmehr et al. [29], and Hekmatian et al. [30] have shown optimal efficacy of tannins for reduction of the gag reflex.

Zinc acetate has a chemical formulation of C4H10O6Zn (dihydrate) [31]. The complexes formed freely with hydrated Zn2+ ions, such as zinc acetate are stringent [32]. Also, zinc acetate decreases skin irritability and inflammation due to its stringent property [33]. Thus, it may also be able to decrease the gag reflex.

The previously suggested techniques (i.e., acupuncture, hypnotherapy, behavioral control, and consumption of synthetic chemical drugs) have some limitations for use during dental procedures, are costly and time consuming, or are not well accepted by patients. Also, conclusive evidence does not exist regarding the efficacy and complications of each method [3, 34, 35]. Thus, it is imperative to find an effective and safe alternative with short-term effect. Also, the efficacy of zinc acetate for reduction of the gag reflex has not been previously evaluated. Thus, this study aimed to compare the effects of three mucoadhesive gels containing lidocaine, zinc acetate, and tannic acid on the gag reflex of dental patients.

Methods

This study was conducted at the Radiology Department of School of Dentistry, Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences between December 2022 and February 2023. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the university (IR.KUMS.REC.1401.384) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20220211053997N1)(19/12/2022).

The first registration was on 19/12/2022.

Trial design

A double-blind parallel-design randomized clinical trial was designed with 1:1 allocation ratio in which the three experimental groups received mucoadhesive gels containing lidocaine, zinc acetate, and tannic acid to control their gag reflex while the placebo group received a placebo gel. The results were reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials.

Participants, eligibility criteria, and settings

The inclusion criteria were having gag reflex upon placement of dental radiographic film in the oral cavity, age over 18 years, and willingness for participation in the study and signing informed consent forms.

The exclusion criteria were central or peripheral nervous system disorders, oral lesions, and history of substance abuse.

The sample consisted of patients presenting to the Radiology Department of School of Dentistry of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences for dental radiography who were selected by using convenience sampling.

Interventions

After obtaining written informed consent from the participants, the severity of their gag reflex was evaluated by placing an intraoral conventional dental radiographic film (size 2) behind their second and third molars of the maxillary right or left quadrant, and their gag reflex was recorded by using a self-reported 0–5 visual analog scale, with 0 indicating no reflex and 5 indicating most severe reflex [30]. The patients with gag reflex, irrespective of its severity, were enrolled. The patients were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 57) of placebo, lidocaine mucoadhesive gel, tannic acid mucoadhesive gel, and zinc acetate mucoadhesive gel (Fig. 1). Considering their random allocation, the four groups had no significant difference in baseline gag reflex score as well as age and sex distribution (Table 1). Five minutes after baseline assessment of the gag reflex, the gels (2 g) were applied distal to the maxillary right/left second and third molars over the soft and hard palates for 5–10 min and then the gag reflex was scored again as explained earlier. The gels were used only once and applied by using a disposable swab(Fig. 2). Gels contained 2% tannic acid, lidocaine, or zinc acetate with a gel base. The gels contained hydroxy propyl methyl cellulose, polyvinyl chloride, and hydroxy ethyl cellulose as the base, to which, 2% lidocaine, zinc acetate, or tannic acid was added. All constituents were FDA approved, and the gels had high adhesion capacity. The physicochemical properties, microbial control, viscosity, pH, and safety of the gels had all been previously evaluated and confirmed. No radiographic exposure was performed. The intervention was performed in the morning 1 h after breakfast for all patients. The patients were requested to refrain from using a chewing gum or smoking for 1 h prior to the intervention.

Outcomes (primary and secondary)

The main objective of this study was to assess the effect of mucoadhesive gels containing 2% lidocaine, tannic acid, or zinc acetate on the gag reflex of dental patients. There was no secondary outcome.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated to be 57 in each of the four groups according to a study by Uri et al. [36], assuming the mean gag reflex score to be 2.5 in the lidocaine group and 4 in the placebo group, standard deviation of the mean gag reflex score to be 3 in the lidocaine and 2.4 in the placebo group, alpha = 0.05, statistical power (1 – β) = 0.8, and minimum clinically important difference set at 1.5 visual analog scale score using PASS 11.

Interim analyses and stopping guidelines

No interim analyses were performed, and no stopping guidelines were established.

Randomization

The patients were randomly assigned to three groups by block randomization. For this purpose, all 24 four-digit combinations were written down. Next, since the sample size was calculated to be 57 in each group, the four-digit combinations were randomly selected 57 times (with replacement). To do so, the binary sequence of a table of random numbers was used. Binary combinations out of the range of 01 to 24 were not considered.

Blinding

Scoring of the gag reflex was performed by one calibrated dental clinician blinded to the study hypothesis and group allocation of patients. The patients were informed about applying gel for them, but were blinded to the type of gel since all gels had the same appearance. Also, the patients were not aware of the effect of gels on their gag reflex. The consent form signed by patients also read that a mucoadhesive gel would be used for patients, but the type was not specified. Only the main researcher was aware of the type of gel used for each patient (according to its serial number). Thus, the study had a double-blind design.

Statistical analysis

Depending on the normality of data distribution or meeting the assumption of homogeneity of the variances (which was assessed by the Bartlett’s test), the gag reflex data before the intervention were analyzed by the Kruskal-Wallis test (since the abovementioned assumption was not met) while the gag reflex data after the intervention were analyzed by ANOVA since the assumption was met. The Chi-square test was used to compare males and females. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were carried out using STATA Statistical Software version 14 (College Station, STATA Corp. LLC., TX, USA).

Results

Participant flow

The initial sample consisted of 252 patients; out of which, 20 were excluded due to not having the gag reflex, and 4 others were excluded due to having oral lesions. Finally, 228 patients were evaluated in four groups of 57. Figure 3 shows the CONSORT flow-diagram of patient selection and allocation. There were 28 males and 29 females in each group. The four groups had no significant difference regarding gender distribution (p = 1.00). The mean age was 24.4 ± 3.5 years in the lidocaine, 24.1 ± 3.5 years in the zinc acetate, 25.1 ± 3.2 years in the tannic acid, and 24.6 ± 3.5 years in the placebo group. The four groups had no significant difference in the mean age (p = 0.451).

Harms

No patients were harmed during the study.

Subgroup analyses

Primary outcome. Table 2 shows the mean gag reflex score in the four groups before and after the intervention. The four groups had no significant difference in the baseline mean gag reflex score (p = 0.145). However, the difference in the mean gag reflex score was significant among the four groups after the intervention (p < 0.0001). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the mean gag reflex score in all three experimental groups was significantly lower than that of the placebo group (p < 0.0001). However, the differences between the experimental groups were not significant in this respect (p > 0.05). Within-group comparisons showed significant differences within all groups such that the mean gag reflex score after the intervention was significantly lower than the baseline score (p < 0.0001 for all groups).

Discussion

This study assessed the efficacy of mucoadhesive gels containing 2% lidocaine, tannic acid, and zinc acetate in reducing the gag reflex during dental procedures. The results indicated that all three formulations significantly decreased the gag reflex compared to the placebo, with no notable differences in effectiveness among the three active treatments. The majority of the patients in this study were in their third decade of life, reflecting the age range during which the gag reflex is more prevalent and can significantly impact dental care [35, 37, 38]. The groups were well-matched in terms of age and gender, and there were no significant differences in the baseline gag scores.

The comparable efficacy of the gels suggests that all three active formulations—lidocaine, tannic acid, and zinc acetate—are effective alternatives for managing the gag reflex. This finding implies that clinicians have multiple effective options to enhance patient comfort and cooperation during dental procedures. The findings underscore the benefits of mucoadhesive gels as a localized and well-tolerated approach to gag reflex management.

Although this study focused on adult patients, it is important to consider the differences in gag reflex management between children and adults. Studies show that while both age groups may experience similar triggers, pediatric patients often require different approaches due to developmental and psychological factors. For instance, Samaleti and Jawdekar [39] assessed the use of acupressure with an earplug in children and found no significant effect on reducing the gag reflex, suggesting that acupressure may not impact gagging controlled by the glossopharyngeal nerve, which is predominantly active during dental procedures. However, Cakmak et al. [40] demonstrated that earplugs could be effective for managing the gag reflex in children during oral procedures, such as impression-taking of maxillary teeth. Additionally, other studies [41, 42] explored the use of low-level laser therapy to control the gag reflex in pediatric patients by stimulating the PC6 acupuncture point. The authors reported that this method was effective in suppressing the gag reflex in children, with the potential explanation being its distracting effects [43]. These techniques, while successful in children, differ from those used in adult populations, where behavioral modification techniques are often consistently effective in managing the gag reflex across age groups [44].

However, the methods used to control severe gag reflexes often vary depending on the patient’s age. Adults with severe gag reflex issues may benefit from more specialized strategies, such as the use of training denture bases or palateless dentures, which provide more localized control over the gag reflex. These approaches may not be as suitable for children, who respond better to simpler distraction and sensory desensitization techniques [45]. The present study’s mucoadhesive gels were evaluated exclusively on adults, where such localized treatment may offer more direct control over the gag reflex during dental procedures.

One limitation of the present study is that the study population only included adult patients, which limits the generalizability of the findings to younger age groups. Pediatric patients often respond differently to gag reflex management techniques, as seen in the aforementioned studies. Future research should include a broader range of ages, particularly children and elderly individuals, to assess how these interventions affect various patient demographics and evaluate their broader applicability. Additionally, future studies could explore different interventional applications, such as dental impressions, treatments, and imaging, as well as alternative agents and techniques to manage the gag reflex more effectively in diverse patient populations.

In summary, while mucoadhesive gels containing lidocaine, tannic acid, or zinc acetate were shown to be effective in adults, the differences in gag reflex control methods between children and adults highlight the need for tailored approaches in younger populations [46]. The localized nature and tolerability of the gels present a promising alternative to methods like acupuncture or salt use, which may have limitations in terms of training requirements or patient acceptance.

Conclusion

The mucoadhesive gels containing 2% lidocaine, tannic acid, or zinc acetate significantly decreased the gag reflex in dental patients, with all three treatments showing comparable efficacy compared to the placebo. These findings suggest that each gel formulation is a viable alternative for managing the gag reflex during dental procedures.
