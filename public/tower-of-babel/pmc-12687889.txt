The cost of caring: professional differences in the burden of trauma-related work
Authors: Anna Swantje van der Meer, Hanna Christiansen, Josephine Maya Fuchs, Katharina Szota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Although children and adolescents exposed to traumatic events face a high risk of developing mental health disorders, access to trauma-specific care in Germany remains insufficient. Professionals involved in the care of affected children and adolescents experience work-related stress. Along with structural barriers and limited systemic support, work-related stress can negatively impact the quality of care. This study investigates the concerns, strains, consequences of perceived stress and needs of these professional groups and examines differences in their experiences to derive implications for improving organizational and professional deficiencies as well as care of affected children and adolescents.Methods: Data were collected and analyzed from N = 637 participants via an online survey. Our sample included child and youth psychotherapists (CP), youth welfare professionals (YWP), and psychiatric care staff (PCS), and was assessed using the Trauma Professionals’ Burden Scale (TPBS). ANOVAs and Pearson correlations, were conducted, with adjustments for multiple testing and covariates like age, professional experience, working hours and gender.Results: Our study identified concerns, strains, needs and stress consequences faced by professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents. The greatest concern among participants was the risk of children being agitated through external triggers, while the most significant strain involved children having to return to potentially violent homes. A need for better structural conditions was also expressed. The most significant stress was reported by YWP while CP revealed the lowest stress levels.Discussion: Our results highlight the distress among professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents. These findings suggest that working with traumatized children and adolescents is itself a stressor, warranting additional research. Our results highlight the need for structural improvements, such as increased staff. Moreover, greater access to training to acquire evidence-based knowledge and skills might help to alleviate the stress experienced by professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents.KEYWORDS: Professionals stress, care of traumatized children and youth, mental health care, youth welfare, traumatized children and youth

Background

The awareness of the burden experienced by children and adolescents exposed to potentially traumatizing life events is steadily rising (Lewis et al., 2019; McLaughlin et al., 2013). Exposure to trauma during childhood and adolescence is strongly associated with elevated risks for a range of mental health disorders, including depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorders (Lewis et al., 2019). If left untreated, these conditions may persist into adulthood and significantly impair social, emotional, and occupational functioning (Clemens et al., 2018; Curran et al., 2018; Ganser et al., 2016).

Despite the growing recognition of trauma-related mental health needs among children and adolescents, current service systems frequently fail to meet treatment demands. Concurrently, workforce shortages and a lack of specialized expertise, especially in low-resource settings, pose significant challenges to effective service delivery in youth mental health care (McGorry et al., 2024). Limited access to psychotherapy (Bundespsychotherapeutenkammer, 2018), high dropout rates in outpatient psychotherapy (Abel et al., 2022), a lack of capacity in trauma-specific youth services (Blum et al., 2018) as well as conceptually unclear trauma-specific care models (Bendall et al., 2021) contribute to inadequate care.

Trauma-focused care for children and adolescents necessitates a multidisciplinary workforce with appropriate qualifications, comprising both clinical and non-clinical professionals, such as child and adolescent psychiatrists/psychotherapists (CP), youth welfare professionals (YWP) and care staff in inpatient child and adolescent psychiatric care (PCS) (Goldbeck et al., 2007; Münzer et al., 2015; Reddemann et al., 2020). Professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents encounter a complex set of demands, including the management of externalizing and dysregulated behaviours, exposure to distressing trauma narratives, and engagement with highly demanding caregivers (Blum et al., 2018; Kind et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020). In daily practice, they are often confronted with dysfunctional relationship patterns, partly accompanied by aggressive behaviour, which can significantly undermine their perceived professional competence and job satisfaction (Armes et al., 2020; Lang, 2013; Morse et al., 2012; Zielke-Nadkarni & Cormann, 2009).

Previous research has identified numerous ways in which high levels of workplace- related stress impact healthcare and child welfare systems, with consequences manifesting at three levels: professionals, organizations, and children and adolescents (Beer et al., 2021; Godifay et al., 2018; Graham et al., 2014; Luther et al., 2017).

At the professional level working in the health care system and youth welfare services, consistently high levels of work-related stress, maladaptive coping strategies (e.g. social withdraw, rumination), and adverse psychophysiological health outcomes (Meyers & Comille, 2002; Wilke et al., 2020) are reported. Furthermore, research indicates that professionals feel insufficiently equipped to respond appropriately to trauma disclosures and frequently report a lack of systemic and institutional support (Burgess et al., 2023; Nooteboom et al., 2021). Until now, research on work-related stress among professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents is often focused on Secondary Traumatic Stress (STS), Compassion Fatigue (CF) and Burnout (BO). STS can occur as a reaction to indirect exposure to traumatic events experienced by others, e.g. clients, patients or others, and can manifest in mental, physical and emotional symptoms (Bride et al., 2007; Molnar et al., 2020). CF describes a work-related stress response in healthcare providers that is a consequence of healthcare workers disregarding their own self-care needs when focusing on the needs of clients and patients (Figley, 2002; Molnar et al., 2020; Sinclair et al., 2017). BO is characterized as a prolonged reaction to persistent interpersonal stressors at work. Associated symptoms of BO include overwhelming exhaustion, feelings of cynicism and detachment from the job, as well as a sense of ineffectiveness and lack of achievement (Maslach & Leiter, 2016). Beyond STS, CF and BO, the role of health professionals is associated with a range of physical and psychological consequences, such as depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances (Zhou et al., 2017). The care of trauma-affected children and adolescents thus entails sustained exposure daily workplace related stress among CP, YWP and PCS (Graham et al., 2014; Wilke et al., 2020).

At the organizational level, elevated workplace-related stress is linked to reduced productivity, increased absenteeism, and high turnover, with role ambiguity as key contributing factors (Coffey et al., 2004; Hämmig, 2018). Health-related stress can further exacerbate staffing shortages (Evans et al., 2023). Previous research identified work-related stress as the primary factor influencing social service workers’, child therapists’ as well as nurses’ decisions to leave the profession (Aminihajibashi et al., 2022; Brewer et al., 2012; Coffey et al., 2004). Moreover, high levels of STS, CF and BO have been shown to impair professionals’ perceived competence, and may thereby contribute to attrition from the field (Armes et al., 2020; Bride et al., 2007; Morse et al., 2012).

Regarding children and adolescents in mental health and youth care, workplace-related stress among professionals can negatively impact them by reducing clinicians’ emotional availability, which in turn may weaken the therapeutic alliance and trust (Hahn, 2004; He et al., 2018). High absenteeism and turnover further disrupt care continuity and impair treatment outcomes (Woltmann et al., 2008). Moreover, STS and BO may impair clinicians’ capacity for empathy toward clients (Bride et al., 2007; Maslach & Jackson, 1981) and diminish their perceived quality of care (Baugerud, 2018; Luther et al., 2017; Salyers et al., 2015; Sprang et al., 2011).

In summary, work-related stress among professionals not only impacts their own well-being but also has negative consequences for the institutions involved and for the care of children and adolescents who rely on support services. While previous research has primarily focused on the psychological burden experienced by professionals through the concepts of STS, BO, and CF, it is equally important to assess not only their stress experiences but also their perceived needs and concerns. To enable comparability across professional groups, a comprehensive assessment tool is required that integrates these multiple dimensions and can be applied across different types of professionals. Such an instrument would allow for a nuanced analysis of occupational differences in stress experiences in relation to specific needs and concerns. Based on these insights, targeted implications can be derived, which in turn may inform the development of profession-specific interventions aimed at improving working conditions and the quality of care. To capture these aspects in a differentiated manner, the Trauma Professionals’ Burden Scale (TPBS) was developed in previous studies (Szota et al., 2024; van der Meer et al., in preparation). Based on a content analysis of qualitative interviews with professionals (CP, YWP, PCS), specific factors were identified that professionals associated with concerns, burdens, and needs in their work with traumatized children and adolescents (van der Meer et al., in preparation). These qualitative findings were subsequently transformed into a quantitative questionnaire (Szota et al., 2024). To adequately account for the heterogeneity of the involved professional groups, a differentiated, occupation-specific analysis is required. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess these dimensions using the TPBS across CP, YWP and PCS. Identifying group-specific differences can help highlight areas where additional support at professional and organizational level is needed to reduce professionals’ burden and improve the quality of care of affected children and adolescents.

Therefore, the current study surveyed professionals in mental health care and youth welfare services through an online survey to explore their concerns, stress, and strains and the consequences of perceived stress, as well as their needs, in working with traumatized children and adolescents, in order to address the following research questions:

Which concerns, strains, consequences of perceived stress and needs affect CP, YWP and PCS most in their day-to-day work?

Which concerns, strains, consequences of perceived stress and needs affect CP, YWP and PCS most in their day-to-day work?

What kind of differences in concerns, strains, consequences of perceived stress and needs exist between the professional groups?

Methods

Recruitment and data collection

Data was collected anonymously from 23 March 2022 to 14 October 2022 via SoSci Survey (www.soscisurvey.de). Recruitment was conducted via e-mail lists of professional organizations, universities, training institutes, and psychiatric and psychotherapeutic in- and outpatient institutions, as well as groups on social media of the professional groups. The present study was approved by the University of Marburg Ethics Committee (approval number: 2021-90k). Participants received study information and provided informed consent.

Participants

Participants were included if they belonged to one of the following professional groups:

Licenced psychotherapists for children and adolescents as well as psychotherapists enrolled in postgraduate training, referred to as child and youth psychotherapists or CP,

Licenced psychotherapists for children and adolescents as well as psychotherapists enrolled in postgraduate training, referred to as child and youth psychotherapists or CP,

Professionals of inpatient child and youth welfare services (pedagogical or psychological), briefly referred to as youth welfare professionals or YWP,

Professionals of inpatient child and youth welfare services (pedagogical or psychological), briefly referred to as youth welfare professionals or YWP,

Professionals of inpatient child and youth psychiatric institutions (education or nursing) briefly referred to as psychiatric care staff or PCS.

Professionals of inpatient child and youth psychiatric institutions (education or nursing) briefly referred to as psychiatric care staff or PCS.

No exclusion criteria were applied.

Measures

Demographics and information on training and profession

Standard demographic information and professional information were assessed. Participants reported their age and gender, professional group, the federal state they work in and whether their working area is rather rural or urban, the average hours that they work per week, and their professional experience in years, including years of professional training after graduating university.

Trauma professionals’ burden scale (TPBS)

The TPBS was used to assess trauma professionals’ burden and needs when working with traumatized children and adolescents. It was created based on qualitative interviews with professionals (Szota et al., 2024) and psychometrically investigated in an online-survey (Szota et al., 2024). Respondents rated their agreement via a total of 28 items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘to a very great extent’), all worded in such a way that a higher score indicates a stronger endorsement of concerns, strains, consequences of stress or needs (e.g. ‘With regard to traumatized children and adolescents that you work with, to what extent do you fear that they are agitated by triggers?’). The subscales Concerns (mean score of 8 items, ω = .76), Strains (7 items, ω = .77), Consequences (7 items, ω = .89) and the second-order scale of these subscales’ means, Total stress (22 items, ω = .88), showed acceptable to good internal consistencies in the current sample, as did the subscale Needs (6 items, ω = .80). After responding to the items of all subscales, participants could report additional or alternative concerns, strains, consequences of stress and needs in a free text format. These concerns, strains, consequences of stress and needs were extracted and content grouped by one author.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 28 for Windows (Chicago, IL, USA). Figures were created in RStudio (Posit team, 2025). Omega ω (McDonald, 1999) was calculated to assess internal reliability coefficients. Values lower than 0.7 are assumed as inadequate, values equal or higher than 0.8 as good (Cheung et al., 2024). P values < .05 were set as thresholds for statistical significance. Since we conducted separate tests on the five TPBS subscales/total score, we corrected the alpha level for these analyses to α = 0.01 using the Bonferroni procedure to reduce the risk of familywise error due to multiple testing.

We assessed potential differences between the professional groups on demographic and professional information using the chi-square test for categorical data (gender, east vs. west federal state and rural vs. urban working area) and using a MANOVA for continuous data (age, working hours and professional experience). In addition, we used Pearson correlations to assess associations between the TPBS scales and age, working hours and professional experience. Potential gender differences on the TPBS scales were explored using Welch’s unequal variances t-tests for independent samples. Hereby, data from participants who did not disclose their gender (n = 6) or identified as non-binary (n = 3) were not included due to the small sample sizes. In case of significant correlations of TPBS scales with age, working hours or professional experience, the responding variables were included as covariates in the assessment of differences between the professional groups on the TPBS subscales, conducting an ANCOVA. In case of significant gender differences on TPBS scales, we conducted the analyses separately for males and females.

Subsequently, ANOVAs or ANCOVAs were calculated to examine differences on the TPBS scales between the professional groups. Prior to that, homogeneity of variances was examined using Levene’s test (Levene, 1960). In case of heteroscedasticity, ANOVAs or ANCOVAs were conducted using bootstrapping procedures with 5000 samples. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey post hoc tests or in case of unequal variances, Games-Howell post-hoc tests. Multicollinearity between covariates was checked using the tolerance value and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with tolerance values < .10 and VIF > 10 indicating potential multicollinearity.

Results

Sample

A total n = 836 individuals met the inclusion criteria (profession) and provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Two participants were excluded due to implausible answers (e.g. being 2 years old). N = 197 participants dropped out of the survey on the first page. Of the remaining n = 637 participants, all but one provided sociodemographic information: Female gender was reported by 513 individuals (80.7%), male gender by 114 (17.9%), diverse gender by 3 participants (0.4%), and 6 chose not to disclose (0.7%). The age ranged from 20 years to 73 years (M = 40.42, SD = 12.22). Roughly half of the sample (55.1%) stated to be working in the child and youth welfare system (YWP), followed by 29.7% working as child psychotherapists (CP), and 15.1% working as pedagogues or nurses in inpatient psychiatric clinics (PCS). Specific concerns

Regarding the concerns of professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents, the item on children and adolescents being agitated by external triggers (M = 3.31, SD = 0.97) revealed the strongest approval, and the weakest for being unable to build a relationship with the children and adolescents (M = 2.18, SD = 0.92). The concern related to children and adolescents being agitated by triggers was the most strongly endorsed item among YWP, while the strongest approval for CP and PCS emerged for the item on self-harming behaviour (see Table 2). Overall, 56 participants (9.7%) reported additional or other concerns about inadequate access to mental health care or youth welfare being most frequently stated (see Table 3).

Strains

Specific strains

Regarding the strains of professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents, the strongest approval was found for the item on children and adolescents going back to potentially violent homes (M = 3.82, SD = 0.91), and the weakest for verbally aggressive behaviour of children and adolescents (M = 2.35, SD = 0.86) for professional groups of YWP and PCS. For CP, the lowest endorsement was found for the item physical aggressive behaviour. Working with caregivers who were perpetrators reached as high an approval as did children and adolescents having to return to potentially violent homes by PCS (see Table 2). Overall, 31 participants (5.5%) reported additional or other strains, with limited competencies of other professionals as the most frequently stated (see Table 3).

Differences between professional groups

No significant correlation of the TPBS subscale Strains was found for participants’ age; r = .06, p = .148; working hours; r = −.04, p = .306; or professional experience; r = .06, p = .192. Significant differences between males and females emerged; t(546) = 2.82, p = .005. Accordingly, we conducted the analysis on differences between professional groups separately for males and females.

Levene’s test indicated homogeneity of variances for the analysis on females; p = .088; and males; p = .445. No significant differences between the professional groups emerged on the TPBS subscale Strains for females; F(2; 448) = 2.17, p = .116; η2 = .010, nor for males; F(2; 94) = 1.76, p = .177, η2 = .036 (see Table 4 and Figure 1).

Consequences of stress

Specific consequences of stress

Addressing the consequences of stress professionals experience when working with traumatized children and adolescents, we observed the highest approval for a greater need for social withdrawal (M = 2.63, SD = 1.22), and the lowest for somatic symptoms (M = 1.98, SD = 1.12). This result applied to all professional groups. Overall, 11 participants (2.0%) reported additional or other consequences of stress, mostly psychological symptoms (see Table 3).

Differences between professional groups

No significant correlation of the TPBS subscale Consequences was found with participants’ working hours; r = .01, p = .778; but with participants’ age; r = −.13, p = .002; and their professional experience; r = −.09, p = .047. Significant differences were observed between males and females; t(153.90) = 2.36, p = .020. Accordingly, we conducted the analysis on differences between professional groups separately for males and females, and with age and professional experience as covariates.

Levene’s test indicated heterogeneity of variances for the analysis on females; p = .004; but homogeneity for the analysis on males; p = .267. For females, significant differences between the professional groups emerged using Bootstrapping procedure; F(2; 432) = 16.40, p < .001, η2 = .071. CP showed significantly lower scores than YWP; p < .001; while there were no significant differences between YWP and PCS; p = .084; or CP and PCS; p = .095. For males, significant differences between the professional groups were also found; F(2; 92) = 13.79, p < .001, η2 = .235. YWP showed significant higher scores than both CP; p < .001; and PCS; p < .001; while there were no significant differences between CP and PCS; p = .324 (see Table 4 and Figure 1).

Total stress

Differences between professional groups

No significant correlation of the higher-order TPBS score Total stress was found with working hours; r = −.01, p = .765; and professional experience; r = −.04, p = .323; but we found a significant correlation with participants’ age r = −.10, p = .019; and between male and female professionals; t(146.79) = 2.63, p = .009. Accordingly, we conducted the analysis on differences between professionals separately for males and females and with age as a covariate.

Levene’s test indicated homogeneity of variances for the analysis with females; p = .442; and males; p = .531. For females, significant differences between the professional groups emerged; F(2; 444) = 27.48, p < .001, η2 = .110. CP reported significantly lower scores than YWP and PCS; both p < .001; while YWP and PCS showed no significant difference; p = .680. For males, significant differences between the professional groups emerged as well; F(2; 93) = 13.37, p < .001, η2 = .223. Here, YWP reported significantly higher scores than CP; p < .001; and PCS; p = .026; while CP and PCS showed no significant difference; p = .054 (see Table 4 and Figure 1).

Needs

Specific needs

Regarding the needs of professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents, the highest approval was found for requirement of better structural conditions, e.g. more staff (M = 4.05, SD = 1.17), and the lowest for supervision (M = 3.13, SD = 1.21). This also applied to YWP and PCS. For CP, the least endorsed need was more or better strategies to relieve the burden due to confrontation with traumatic experiences of children and adolescents (see Table 2). Overall, 37 participants (6.7%) reported additional or other needs, with increased capacities to enhance children and adolescents access to adequate care being the most frequently stated (see Table 3).

We found no significant correlation of the TPBS subscale Needs with professionals’ working hours; r = .03, p = .464; but significant correlations with age; r = −.21, p < .001 and professional experience; r = −.15, p < .001. Significant differences for sex were observed; t(141.88) = 4.26, p < .001. Discussion

This study is the first to present the specific concerns, strains, needs and consequences of stress of professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents. Our results indicate that professionals face occupational concerns, such as the risk of inducing further stress in affected children and youth, as well as experiencing strains and stress due to structural issues within the youth welfare and mental healthcare systems. Our first research question addressed the concerns, strains, consequences of perceived stress and needs that impact CP, YWP and PCS the most in their day-to-day work based on mean comparisons across all professional groups.

Concerns

Across all professional groups, participants expressed particular concern about children and adolescents becoming agitated by triggers that remind them of potentially traumatizing life events. The professionals also reported fears of self-harming or suicidal behaviour in children and adolescents, as well as concerns that they may never fully recover from their traumatic experiences. The professionals also shared the fear of burdening the children and adolescents if they were to discuss the potentially traumatizing life events they had experienced. Previous research has identified only a few studies, primarily focusing on CP, that highlight similar concerns among those working with traumatized children and adolescents (Becker et al., 2004; Billings et al., 2003; Nelson et al., 2009). In these studies, CPs also reported being concerned that exposure or talking about the experienced trauma with children and adolescents could lead to self-harming behaviours or the worsening of symptoms (Becker et al., 2004). Other concerns among professionals treating traumatized children and adolescents included the fear that addressing the traumatic events could cause the children and adolescents to discontinue contact or treatment (Sprang et al., 2011), although this concern was not strongly expressed in our study. Previous studies have yielded results resembling our study’s, namely that YWP and PCS experience self-harming behaviour in children and adolescents as overwhelming and burdensome (Bosman & van Meijel, 2008; Groen, 2017).

Strains

Professionals across all groups stated that they found the experience of children and adolescents sometimes returning to their violent families of origin to be most troubling. Other reported occupational strains include working with relatives who are perpetrators, and the inadequate support or care provided to affected children and adolescents. The latter aspect is similarly reflected in the Needs subscale, which concerns the demand for improved structural conditions. The professionals also reported feeling burdened by demanding (e.g. unreliable or verbally aggressive behaviour) relatives and by an overwhelming sense of responsibility for the well-being of traumatized children and adolescents, or of being strained by physical aggressive behaviour of children and adolescents. Previous research on strains of professionals focuses in particular on the potential burden of being confronted with details of traumatic life events of patients and clients (Jurisch et al., 2009; Vang et al., 2020). In our study, the professionals highlighted the stress caused by contextual care factors (i.e. working with caregivers, the return of children and adolescents to dysfunctional families, inadequate care structures). Strains created through intra-personal factors (e.g. feeling an overwhelming sense of responsibility) on the one hand versus contextual factors related to the work environment on the other, is reflected in current research as well (Endsjø et al., 2024). Hensel et al. (2015) highlighted the role of intra-personal influences on the burden experienced by professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents, while more recent research (Endsjø et al., 2024) suggested that contextual factors and the work environment contribute primarily to this burden. Our results support the pronounced influence of contextual factors and highlight that professionals across all groups report the detrimental influence of structural and systemic stressors.

Consequences of stress

Our results show high levels of mental distress across all professional groups. Among our participants, the most frequently reported consequence of occupational stress was the need for social withdrawal, increased irritability and an ongoing feeling of restlessness due to work-related stress, sleep disturbances, decreased concentration and reduced job motivation. In previous studies, professionals also reported psychological and somatic symptoms of strain associated with the care of traumatized patients and clients. Somatic symptoms as consequences of work-related stress were reported by the professionals in our study as being only weakly pronounced. Some reported reduced emotional regulation and/or less interest in activities that had once been engaging. Symptoms of hyperarousal, such as sleep disturbances, concentration difficulties, hypervigilance, or irritability, were also reported (Palm et al., 2004), as well as headaches and palpitations (Figley, 2013; Pistorius et al., 2008). As mentioned above, further research mainly focuses on the symptom patterns of STS, CF, and BO. The stress symptoms reported by professionals in studies on STS and CF are similar to those in our study and include increased vigilance, as well as reduced social participation in private life and declining self-care (Bride et al., 2007; Molnar et al., 2020). The participants in our study experienced somatic symptoms or experiences of dissociation to a lesser extent, whereas other studies also report a pronounced impairment of professionals in this regard (Colombo et al., 2019; Samson & Shvartzman, 2018).

Needs

Professionals in our survey indicated a need for improved overall structural conditions and of fostering better interdisciplinary cooperation. Previous research has similarly shown that, at the organizational level, structural conditions like a lack of support, long working hours, and time pressures create considerable stress (Wilke et al., 2020). Furthermore, in our study professionals emphasized the importance of having more highly qualified colleagues as well as more training opportunities to acquire more trauma-related knowledge. However, research in this regard, indicates that there is still little consensus on what constitutes the content of trauma-informed care and the corresponding training (Bendall et al., 2021).

In comparison to the need for greater structural support (e.g. improved cooperation and general conditions) and the need for enhanced knowledge (e.g. more trauma-specific training and better-qualified colleagues), the participating professionals in our study identified the need for supervision as the least pressing. This finding contrasts with recent research, which highlights the importance of regular supervision within supportive supervisory relationships, strong peer support networks, balanced and diverse caseloads, and fostering organizational satisfaction among professionals (Molnar et al., 2020; Sutton et al., 2022).

Differences between professionals

Our results reveal differences between professional groups regarding concerns, perceived stress, and needs as put to question by our second research question: What kind of differences in concerns, strains and needs exist between the professional groups?

We found that CP experience less stress and fewer concerns than YWP and PCS. Previous research supports our findings that different professional groups report varying levels of stress and concerns (Schwegler et al., 2025). Professionals in youth welfare, in particular, appear to experience the highest levels of burden (Lasalvia et al., 2009; Schwegler et al., 2025) especially when compared to CP, who generally report lower levels of concern (Billings et al., 2003; Johnson et al., 2012; Nelson et al., 2009). This difference is also evident in BO rates, with YWP reporting higher levels than CP (Letson et al., 2020; O’Connor et al., 2018). However, some studies have found no significant differences in work-related stress between professional groups (Bennett, 2005; Jørgensen et al., 2021). Overall, our results support the idea that different professional groups are affected differently by the challenges of working with traumatized children and adolescents. One possible explanation for the reported differences in experienced stress is that professionals in inpatient settings, such as YWP and PCS, may be more exposed to the pronounced behaviours and relational dynamics of children and adolescents compared to CP (Steinkopf et al., 2021). CP often work in outpatient settings, and evidence suggests that self-employed therapists have lower BO rates than those working for organizations (McCormack et al., 2018). These lower rates may be attributed to the greater autonomy perceived by self-employed professionals, which acts as a protective factor against BO and seems to have a corresponding effect on the feeling of stress (Alarcon, 2011; O’Connor et al., 2018). Additionally, in full inpatient psychiatric care professionals’ tasks include accompanying children and adolescents during day and night, leading (therapeutic) group programmes and working with parents and other relatives (Meyer, 2006; Scheydt & Hegedüs, 2021). This range of tasks means that professionals working in inpatient care (e.g. PCS and YWP) usually have the longest and most frequent contact with children and adolescents compared to professionals in outpatient settings (Arolt et al., 2011) and experience violent behaviour of children and adolescents often (Lang, 2013; Zielke-Nadkarni & Cormann, 2009). Additionally, patients in outpatient settings often show lower symptom burden and less pronounced psychopathology (e.g. less suicidality, self-harming behaviour) compared to inpatient patients (Ford et al., 2011; Pottick et al., 1995).

There is also evidence that increased competence can act as a protective factor against perceived stress in care settings (Lannin et al., 2019). Therefore, the lower levels of stress and concerns reported by CPs compared to YWP and PCS may be attributed to their higher evidence-based competencies, which underscores the need for enhanced trauma-specific training and education in youth welfare and mental healthcare institutions. A study from Switzerland which examined longitudinal changes through trauma-informed care training in residential facilities for child and youth welfare demonstrated positive effects on professionals’ burden (Schmid et al., 2020). In facilities that offered training, professionals showed significantly lower psychophysiological stress markers, as measured by hair cortisol concentration, and experienced less frequent verbal and physical aggression from the children and adolescents (Schmid et al., 2020). There is initial evidence that the implementation of trauma education concepts for protecting children and adolescents leads to improved care of traumatized children and adolescents (Bunting et al., 2019). Studies on the teaching of trauma education skills to nursing staff also highlight positive effects on care, particularly through improved trauma-sensitive care for traumatized children and adolescents. For instance, nurses who underwent training showed more specialist knowledge and a stronger feeling of competence in applying the strategies they had learned (Cannon et al., 2020).

Our results show that female sex was associated with higher levels of needs, strains, stress and consequences of stress. While sex differences in relation to stress experienced when working with traumatized individuals have seldom been investigated, previous research suggests that women report higher levels of STS when working with traumatized children and adolescents than men (Baum, 2016), which aligns with our study results. Regarding the influence of age: our findings indicate that higher age is associated with reduced concerns, consequences of stress, total stress and needs, supporting previous research (Salloum et al., 2015). Older age has been associated with lower levels of work-related stress and mental burden like BO, STS and CF in the care of traumatized children and adolescents (Craig & Sprang, 2010; Dagan et al., 2016; Vang et al., 2020). However, these findings could be the result of healthy worker survivor bias (Arrighi & Hertz-Picciotto, 1994), insofar as only professionals who are able to withstand high levels of stress remain in their profession long-term. Overall, research on personal factors (such as age and gender) influencing the stress experienced by professionals working with traumatized children and adolescents has been heterogeneous, highlighting the need for further investigation in this area (Schwegler et al., 2025; Vang et al., 2020). In our study, we also found that age and gender were not significant when considering the differences between professional groups.

Implications

Based on our findings, the outlined implications target potential improvements at both the professional and organizational levels, thereby contributing to enhanced care for affected children and adolescents.

Alleviating the reported concerns, such as traumatized children and adolescents in the care of professionals being agitated by triggers or engaging in self-harming behaviour, might be achieved through the transmission of knowledge and competencies regarding trauma-specific care. Specifically, increasing the competencies of professional in dealing with agitated children and adolescents and/or self-harming behaviour might reduce the burden induced by these concerns. Previous research shows that trauma-specific training can be a protective factor for BO (Craig & Sprang, 2010) and for mental health issues in general (Makadia et al., 2017). Furthermore, studies indicate that additional trauma-specific training leads to an increased willingness of therapists to treat patients with severe post-traumatic stress symptoms (Schnell et al., 2015), and leads to higher perceived levels of self-efficacy and competence, all of which can in turn alleviate these reported concerns (Szota et al., 2021).

When seeking to address the sources of strain identified in our study, the necessity for both systemic reforms and individual support measures becomes evident. Participants reported experiencing significant strain due to the return of affected children and adolescents to violent home environments, and the challenges of working with abusive parents. Chronic underfunding and understaffing of child protection services may contribute to the inappropriate reintegration of traumatized children and adolescents into harmful family settings (Bates, 2015; Strolin et al., 2006). Addressing these structural deficiencies by ensuring adequate funding and staffing could mitigate such professional strain. This measure would also directly address the identified need in the current study, as the professionals reported a need for better structural conditions most frequently.

Regarding the reported strain due to interactions with violent parents, targeted individual support for professionals may offer a means of alleviation. Studies show that one-to-one peer support in mental health services can have a positive impact on empowerment and self-reported recovery, and that it can influence the working alliance between service users and mental health professionals (White et al., 2020). However, in youth welfare and mental healthcare systems in particular, supervision is often not available in sufficient scope, which prevents professionals from addressing personal stress in a protected setting (Choy-Brown & Stanhope, 2018).

In addition to the derived implications and suggested measures of improvement such as trauma-specific training, improved structural conditions and individual supervision, our results regarding the professionals overall stress indicate the need for measures directly addressing resilience to workplace-related stress. For example, training programmes regarding self-care (in mental health and youth welfare services) can help reduce work-related burden by teaching strategies for handling stressful work experiences (Posluns & Gall, 2020). Moreover, teaching professionals coping skills like relaxation, cognitive coping, affective expression and modulation as well as facilitating positive workplace characteristics including having supervisor and coworker support (Baugerud, 2018; Molnar et al., 2017, 2020) are known to reduce workplace related stress (Deblinger et al., 2024). Furthermore, interventions promoting resilience of mental health professionals can address the higher than average levels of workplace-related stress (Cleary et al., 2018). Importantly, resilience interventions of longer duration tend to be more effective (Cleary et al., 2018; Rowe, 2006).

While the identified implications concerning the need for change at both professional and organizational levels are relevant across all professional groups, our findings indicate that YWP report the highest levels of stress, the most severe consequences of stress, and the most pronounced workplace-related strains and needs. Therefore, the group of YWP should be given priority in future research in order to systematically identify both stress-inducing and stress-reducing and resilience factors.

Limitations

When interpreting our results, some limitations should be taken into account. First, our data was collected in a convenience sample, which means that particularly high-stress professionals may have been more likely to take part in our study than others, which could limit the generalizability of our results. The sampling method we chose also limits our results’ generalizability to professionals working in welfare and healthcare systems in other countries.

Second, there are no validated instruments addressing these specific concerns, strains and needs. To counter this, the present study was conducted with the newly developed TPBS (Szota et al., 2024).

Third, we did not assess trauma-specific knowledge and competencies in our sample or if all participants have relevant practical experience. Neither did we assess the frequency with which professionals were confronted with trauma narratives of affected children and adolescents. Thus, we cannot conclude with certainty that contextual factors are stronger contributors to professionals stress than exposure to traumatic narratives. This limitation could be addressed in a follow-up project including a self-assessment rating regarding trauma-related knowledge and consequences of exposure to trauma narratives.

Fourth, the professionals participating in our study work with children and adolescents who probably have additional comorbid disorders. Although we asked participants to focus their responses on the perceived burden of working with traumatized children and adolescents, we do not know for sure whether the concerns and strains of professionals are specifically related to trauma symptoms or to related comorbid symptoms. While this is a confounding influence, comorbidities might be a result of trauma-related distress and thus need to be addressed by youth welfare and mental healthcare professionals as well.

Finally, we did not assess how the concerns and strains of professionals impact the quality of care they provide to children and adolescents, which should be addressed in future studies. For example, one could implement assessments of concerns and strains in implementation studies of trauma-related interventions and analyze how these concerns and strains impact the screening for traumatic events among children and adolescents, the quality of treatment, and its compliance with evidence-based treatment recommendations.

Conclusions

To our knowledge our study is the first to research specific concerns, strains, consequences of stress and needs of professionals of three occupational groups working with traumatized children and adolescents. Our results indicate that professionals are highly stressed and report a large number of concerns and strains related to their work. Their burden could be reduced by both improvements on the health policy and organizational level, such as increased staffing and supervision, as well as on the individual level, such as greater access to training for trauma-specific knowledge and evidence-based competencies. The results of our study highlight that such training, support and the transmission of knowledge and competencies must be incorporated systematically to deal with the individual and structural concerns, strains and needs reported by professionals. In turn, this could improve the care for affected children and adolescents.
