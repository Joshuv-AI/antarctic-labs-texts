Variable Stepsize Distributed Forward-Backward Splitting Methods as Relocated Fixed-Point Iterations
Felipe Atenas, Minh N. Dao, Matthew K. Tam

Abstract
We present a family of distributed forward-backward methods with variable stepsizes to find a solution of structured monotone inclusion problems. The framework is constructed by means of relocated fixed-point iterations, extending the approach introduced in arXiv:2507.07428 to conically averaged operators, thus including iteration operators for methods of forward-backward type devised by graphs. The family of methods we construct preserve the per-iteration computational cost and the convergence properties of their constant stepsize counterparts. Specifically, we show that the resulting methods generate a sequence that converges to a fixed-point of the underlying iteration operator, whose shadow sequences converge to a solution of the problem. Numerical experiments illustrate the behaviour of our framework in structured sparse optimisation problems.



Keywords:

Splitting algorithms,
forward-backward methods,
variable stepsize,
monotone inclusion,
fixed-point iterations.

Introduction and motivation

Many modern applications in statistics
[
26
,
23
]
, machine learning
[
12
]
, and optimisation
[
13
]
, can be abstractly modelled as a structured inclusion problem of the form

\text{find~}x\in X\text{~such that~}0\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}B_{j}x,

(1)

where
X
is a real Hilbert space,
A_{i}:X\rightrightarrows X
is a set-valued operator for
i=1,\dots,n
, and
B_{j}:X\to X
is a single-valued operator for
j=1,\dots,p
. Splitting methods exploit the structure of problem (
1
) by performing separate operations on the model components. The family of splitting methods includes the classical
forward-backward
and
Douglas–Rachford
methods
[
17
,
20
,
11
]
that can solve, respectively, the cases
n=1
and
p=1
, and
n=2
and
p=0
. A unifying manner to encode this family of methods is through
fixed-point iterations
. For instance, the forward-backward method defines its iterates via

z_{n+1}=T_{\gamma}z_{n}\quad\text{for all ~}n\in\mathbbm{N},

(2)

where
\gamma>0
is a parameter, and
T_{\gamma}:=J_{\gamma A_{1}}(\Id-\gamma B_{1})
is the corresponding iteration operator,
J_{\gamma A_{1}}=(\Id+\gamma A_{1})^{-1}
is the
resolvent
of
\gamma A_{1}
, and
\Id
denotes the identity operator on
X
. When
n=2
and
p=1
in (
1
), the
Davis–Yin three-operator splitting method

[
15
]
generates its iterates via (
2
) with

T_{\gamma}=\Id+J_{\gamma A_{2}}\big(2J_{\gamma A_{1}}-\Id-\gamma B_{1}\big)-J_{\gamma A_{1}}.

(3)

These methods enjoy convergence guarantees under mild regularity assumptions, yielding, in the limit, a point
z\in X
such that
z=T_{\gamma}z
, that is, a
fixed-point
of the operator
T_{\gamma}
. In the case of the forward-backward method,
z
is a solution to (
1
), and for the Davis–Yin method, a solution to (
1
) can be recovered by computing
x=J_{\gamma A_{1}}z
. The parameter
\gamma>0
, also known as the
stepsize
, plays a crucial role in controlling the convergence of the methods. When the stepsize
\gamma>0
is fixed, traditional convergence guarantees require this parameter to lie in an interval of the form
(0,\frac{2}{L})
, where
L>0
is a constant measuring some regularity condition of
B_{1}
, usually
Lipschitz continuity
or
cocoercivity
. Once convergence is established, the next natural question is efficiency. Improvements in the performance of the methods can be achieved, for example, by allowing the stepsize parameter
\gamma>0
to vary throughout the iterations. Another approach that varies a different type of parameters is the
Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm
(FISTA)
[
8
]
, and
Nesterov’s acceleration

[
19
]
, based on the idea of
momentum
.

In this work, we present a systematic framework that allows variable stepsizes while preserving convergence guarantees of methods of forward-backward type to solve problem (
1
). This unifying theory is realised by means of
relocated fixed-point iterations
, originally devised in
[
3
]
for averaged nonexpansive operators. In this work, we show that the framework can be extended to the
conically averaged
setting, providing more flexibility to the selection of parameters. We apply the theory to obtain convergent variable stepsize multioperator forward-backward methods, extending the results originally presented in
[
3
]
for variable stepsize resolvent splitting methods. As far as the authors know, this is the first work that establishes convergence of multioperator forward-backward splitting methods with variable stepsizes.

A key ingredient of the analysis is the concept of a
fixed-point relocator
(see Definition
3.1
). An archetypal example of a fixed-point relocator is the one associated with the
Douglas–Rachford
operator. For
n=2
and
p=0
in (
1
), and
\gamma>0
fixed, the Douglas–Rachford algorithm defines iterates via (
2
) with

T_{\gamma}=\Id+J_{\gamma A_{2}}\big(2J_{\gamma A_{1}}-\Id\big)-J_{\gamma A_{1}},

(4)

that is, (
3
) with
B_{1}=0
. A fixed-point relocator of the operator in (
4
) is given, for all
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, by

Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}:=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\Id+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)J_{\gamma A_{1}}.

(5)

The operator in (
5
) has the following two properties:
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{-1}=Q_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}
, and
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}(\Fix T_{\gamma})=\Fix T_{\delta}

[
3
, Lemma 3.1, Theorem 3.5]
. The second property motivates the name fixed-point relocator: for any
x\in\Fix T_{\gamma}
,
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}x\in\Fix T_{\delta}
. The associated relocated fixed-point iteration thus is

z_{k+1}=Q_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}T_{\gamma_{k}}z_{k}\text{~for all~}k\in\mathbbm{N}.

(6)

As we shall see in (
39
), the operator in (
5
) is also a fixed-point relocator of the Davis–Yin operator (
3
), which yields the relocated three-operator scheme in Algorithm
1
.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we define the setting of conically averaged operators, and extend (a version of) the so-called
demiclosedness principle
to this family of operators, crucial for the posterior convergence analysis. In Section
3
we present the main components of the fixed-point relocator framework from
[
3
]
, and its version for conically averaged operators. We continue in Section
4
with the description of a family of relocated forward-backward methods to solve problems of the form (
1
), and in Section
5
we examine graph-based forward-backward methods for multioperator inclusions, including the relocated Davis–Yin three operator splitting algorithm as a special case. We also investigate different fixed-point relocators depending on the graph structure used to devise these methods, with the property that they maintain the per-iteration computational cost. Finally, we finish in Section
7
with some concluding remarks, and future research directions.

Preliminaries

We denote the set of positive real numbers by
\mathbbm{R}_{++}
. For any matrix
M\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times p}
, we use the notation
\mathbf{M}:=M\otimes\Id
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. The bold vector
{\boldsymbol{1}}
denotes the vector of
1
’s, whose dimension will depend on the context. We also denote by
M^{\dagger}
the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
M
. For a vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
,
\diag(x)\in\mathbbm{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the coordinates of the vector
x
, namely, for all
i=1,\dots,d
,
\diag(x)_{ii}=x_{i}
, and
0
otherwise.

Let
X
be a real Hilbert space endowed with inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
and induced norm
\|\cdot\|
. We use
X^{\text{strong}}
to denote the space
X
endowed with the norm topology, and
X^{\text{weak}}
to denote
X
endowed with the weak topology. As is customary,
\to
denotes convergence in the strong topology, while
\>{\rightharpoonup}\;
denotes convergence in the weak topology. Given
n\geqslant 1
, the elements in the space
X^{n}:=\Pi_{i=1}^{n}X
will be denoted with bold symbols, namely,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. We endow the space
X^{n}
with the
\ell_{2}
-norm, that is, for all
\mathbf{x}\in X^{n}
,
\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|^{2}}
, which results in
X^{n}
being a Hilbert space itself. We do not make a distinction in the notation for the norm in
X
,
X^{n}
and the matrix norm, since it is clear from the context which one we refer to.

Given a set-valued operator
A:X\rightrightarrows X
, its
graph
is denoted by
\gra A:=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:y\in Ax\}
, its set of
zeros
by
\zer A:=\{x\in X:0\in Ax\}
, and its set of fixed-points by
\Fix A:=\{x\in X:x\in Ax\}
. We also denote the
inverse
of
A
by
A^{-1}:X\rightrightarrows X
which is defined by
\gra A^{-1}:=\{(y,x)\in X\times X:y\in Ax\}
. We say
A
is
monotone
if for all

(x,y),(u,v)\in\gra A
,
\left<x-u,y-v\right>\geqslant 0,

and
maximally monotone
if it has no proper monotone extension. For a maximally monotone operator
A
on
X
, the resolvent of
A
,
J_{A}:=(\Id+A)^{-1}
, is well-defined on
X
and single-valued
[
7
, Proposition 23.8]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Properties of resolvents
[
3
, Lemma 3.1, Proposition 3.4]
)
.

Let
A
be a maximally monotone operator on
X
. Then

(i)

For all
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,
J_{\delta A}\Big(\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\Id+\big(1-\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\big)J_{\gamma A}\Big)=J_{\gamma A}
.

(ii)

X\times(0,+\infty)\ni(x,\gamma)\mapsto J_{\gamma A}x
is (strongly) continuous.

Given a nonempty closed convex set
C\subseteq X
,
P_{C}:X\to C
denotes the
projection operator
onto
C
, that is, the mapping such that for all
x\in X
,
\min_{y\in X}\|y-x\|=\|P_{C}x-x\|
. The mapping
N_{C}:X\rightrightarrows X
, given by
N_{C}(x):=\{v\in X:\langle v,y-x\rangle\leqslant 0\text{~for all~}y\in C\}
if
x\in C
, and
N_{C}(x)=\varnothing
otherwise, is the
normal cone
of
C
, and satisfies
P_{C}=J_{N_{C}}
.

We use
T:X\to X
to denote a single-valued operator on
X
. We say a single-valued operator
T
on
X
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous with constant
L>0
, if for all
x,y\in X
,
\|Tx-Ty\|\leqslant L\|x-y\|
. We say
T
is
nonexpansive
if
T
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous. For
\beta>0
, a single-valued operator
T
on
X
is said to be
\beta
-
cocoercive
if for all
x,y\in X
,
\left<Tx-Ty,x-y\right>\geqslant\frac{1}{\beta}\|Tx-Ty\|^{2}
. In particular, if
T
is
\beta
-cocoercive, then
T
is monotone and
\beta
-Lipschitz continuous, and thus maximally monotone
[
7
, Corollary 20.28]
. We say
T
is
conically

\eta
-averaged

[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
for
\eta>0
, if for all
x,y\in X
,

\|Tx-Ty\|^{2}+\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\|(\Id-T)x-(\Id-T)y\|^{2}\leq\|x-y\|^{2}.

Equivalently, an operator
T
on
X
is conically
\eta
-averaged if there exists a nonexpansive operator
N
on
X
such that
T=(1-\eta)\Id+\eta N

[
6
, Definition 2.1]
. When
\eta\in(0,1)
,
T
is said to be
\eta
-averaged nonexpansive. For a maximally monotone operator
A
on
X
, its resolvent
J_{A}
is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged nonexpansive. Iteration operators of forward-backward methods are conically averaged, see Lemma
4.3
below.

Let
\mathbbm{E}
be a finite-dimensional Euclidean space. The
demiclosedness principle
is a fundamental tool in fixed-point theory used in the convergence analysis of fixed-point iterations. An operator
T
on
X
is
demiclosed
if
\gra(\Id-T)
is sequentially closed in
X^{\text{weak}}\times X^{\text{strong}}
. The demiclosedness principle states that every nonexpansive operator is demiclosed
[
10
]
. For a parametrised family of operators, a generalisation of the demiclosedness principle was presented in
[
3
]
. First, we recall the definition of a
parametric demiclosed
family of operators, and then present the
parametric demiclosedness principle
. Originally, the results were shown for parameters in
\mathbbm{E}=\mathbbm{R}
, but they can be directly extended for any finite-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbbm{E}
, as in the latter case the strong and weak topologies coincide.

Definition 2.2

(Parametric demiclosedness)
.

Let
D\subseteq X
be a nonempty sequentially closed set, and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbbm{E}
be a nonempty set. We say an operator
G:D\times\Gamma\to X
is
parametrically demiclosed
if
\gra G
is sequentially closed in
(X^{\text{weak}}\times\mathbbm{E})\times X^{\text{strong}}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Parametric demiclosedness principle
[
3
, Theorem 3.9]
)
.

Let
D\subseteq X
be a nonempty weakly sequentially closed set,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbbm{E}
be a nonempty closed set such that
0\notin\mathcal{P}
, and let
(T_{p})_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
be a family of nonexpansive operators from
D
to
X
, such that
(\forall x\in D)\;p\mapsto T_{p}x
is (strongly) continuous. Then the operator

\displaystyle D\times\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\to X\colon(x,p)\mapsto(\Id-T_{p})x

is parametrically demiclosed.

Remark 2.4
.

In the context of Theorem
2.3
, suppose there exist a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
in
D
converging weakly to
x\in D
, and a sequence
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
in
\mathcal{P}
converging to
\overline{p}\in\mathcal{P}
. If
x_{k}-T_{p_{k}}x_{k}\to 0
, then the parametric demiclosedness principle implies that
x\in\Fix T_{\overline{p}}
.

Theorem
2.3
requires the family of operators to be nonexpansive. A demiclosedness principle for conically averaged operators is shown in
[
5
, Theorem 3.3]
, although not for the parametric case. In the next result, we extend the parametric demiclosedness principle to the class of conically averaged operators.

Corollary 2.5

(Parametric demiclosedness principle for conically averaged operators)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbbm{E}
be a nonempty closed set such that
0\notin\mathcal{P}
, and
(T_{p})_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
be operators on
X
. Suppose there exists a continuous function
\eta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbbm{R}_{++}
such that for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, the operator
T_{p}
is conically
\eta(p)
-averaged. Moreover, suppose that for all
z\in X
, the mapping
p\mapsto T_{p}z
is (strongly) continuous. Then, the operator

\displaystyle D\times\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\to X\colon(x,p)\mapsto(\Id-T_{p})x

is parametrically demiclosed.

Proof.

In view of the definition of conical averagedness, for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists a nonexpansive operator
N_{p}
on
X
such that
T_{p}=(1-\eta(p))\Id+\eta(p)N_{p}
. First, we show that for any
z\in X
, the mapping
p\in\mathcal{P}\mapsto N_{p}z
is strongly continuous. Let
p_{k}\to p\in\mathcal{P}
, then from continuity of
p\mapsto T_{p}z
, it follows that

(1-\eta(p_{k}))z+\eta(p_{k})N_{p_{k}}z=T_{p_{k}}z\to T_{p}z.

Furthermore, continuity of
p\mapsto\eta(p)
implies
\eta(p_{k})\to\eta(p)
, and thus

\eta(p_{k})N_{p_{k}}z=T_{p_{k}}z-(1-\eta(p_{k}))z\to T_{p}z-(1-\eta(p))z=\eta(p)N_{p}z.

Since
\eta(p)>0
, then
N_{p_{k}}z\to N_{p}z
, and thus the claim holds. Next, suppose
z_{k}\>{\rightharpoonup}\;z
,
p_{k}\to p
, and
z_{k}-T_{p_{k}}z_{k}\to u
. Our goal is to show that
z-T_{p}z=u
. Indeed, since
\eta(p_{k})(z_{k}-N_{p_{k}}z_{k})=z_{k}-T_{p_{k}}z_{k}\to u
and
0\notin\eta(\mathcal{P})
, then
z_{k}-N_{p_{k}}z_{k}\to\frac{u}{\eta(p)}
. From Theorem
2.3
,
(N_{p})_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
is parametrically demiclosed, then
\frac{1}{\eta(p)}(z-T_{p}z)=z-N_{p}z=\frac{u}{\eta(p)}
, from where the conclusion follows.
∎

One of the main contributions of
[
3
]
is developing a convergence framework of parametrised fixed-point iterations, called relocated fixed-point iterations, without resorting to the classical argument of Fejér monotonicity. Instead, it is based on the concept of an
Opial sequence

[
2
]
. A sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
in
X
is
Opial
with respect to a nonempty set
C\subseteq X
, if for all
c\in C
,
\lim_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}\|x_{k}-c\|
exists. This notion captures essential properties of Fejér monotone sequences without requiring monotonicity. Because of the “lack of structure” in the definition due to nonmonotonicity, showing that the property holds might be challenging in general. In the following result, we show a practical, constructive way of using this property that fits the algorithmic application in our present work.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
be a sequence in
X
, and
C\subseteq X
a nonempty set. Suppose for all
c\in C
, there exists a sequence
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
in
X
converging to
c
, such that
\lim_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}\|z_{k}-c_{k}\|
exists. Then, the following statements are true.

(i)

(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
is bounded.

(ii)

(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
converges weakly to a point in
C
if and only if all weak cluster points of
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
lie in
C
.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality, it holds for all
{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
,

\|z_{k}-c_{k}\|-\|c_{k}-c\|\leq\|z_{k}-c\|\leq\|z_{k}-c_{k}\|+\|c_{k}-c\|.

Taking the limit as
k\to+\infty
, it follows that
\lim_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}\|z_{k}-c\|=\lim_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}\|z_{k}-c_{k}\|
, and thus
(z_{k})_{{k\in\mathbbm{N}}}
is Opial with respect to
C
. The results follow from
[
2
, Proposition 2.1, Corollary 2.3, Lemma 5.2]
.

3
Relocated fixed-point iterations of conically averaged operators

In this section, we present the framework of relocated fixed-point iterations for conically averaged operators. We start by defining the concept of a fixed-point relocator. Given a family of operators
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}}
, an operator is a fixed-point relocator if, in particular, it transforms (relocates) fixed-points of
T_{\gamma}
to fixed-points of
T_{\delta}
for all
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
. This property is crucial to guide the convergence of relocated fixed-point iterations in the form of (
6
).

Definition 3.1

(Fixed-point relocator)
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbbm{R}_{++}
be a nonempty set, and let
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
be a family of operators on
X
. We say that the family of operators
(Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma})_{\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma}
on
X
defines
fixed-point relocators
for
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
with Lipschitz constants
(\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma})_{\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma}
in
[1,+\infty[
if the following hold.

(i)

For all
\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma
,

Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}|_{\Fix T_{\gamma}}
is a bijection from
\Fix T_{\gamma}
to
\Fix T_{\delta}
.

(ii)

For all
\gamma\in\Gamma,\;x\in\Fix T_{\gamma}
,
\Gamma\ni\delta\mapsto Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}x\in X
is continuous.

(iii)

For all
\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon\in\Gamma,\;x\in\Fix T_{\gamma}
,
Q_{\varepsilon\leftarrow\delta}Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}x=Q_{\varepsilon\leftarrow\gamma}x
.

(iv)

For all
\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma
,
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
is
\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
-Lipschitz continuous.

Remark 3.2

(Consequences of the fixed-point relocator definition)
.

In view of
[
3
, Remark 4.3]
, given fixed-point relocators
(Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma})_{\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma}
for
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}}
, the following holds for all
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,

Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\Id\text{~on~}\Fix T_{\gamma},\text{~and~}(Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}|_{\Fix T_{\gamma}})^{-1}=Q_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}|_{\Fix T_{\delta}}.

Remark 3.3

(Multiple fixed-point relocators)
.

As discussed in
[
3
, Remark 4.2]
, given fixed-point relocators
(Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma})_{\gamma,\delta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}}
for
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}}
, observe that Definition
3.1
(i)
–
(iii)
only need to hold on
\Fix T_{\gamma}
. Therefore, any Lipschitz continuous operator
\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
such that
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}|_{\Fix T_{\gamma}}=\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}|_{\Fix T_{\gamma}}
also defines a fixed-point relocator for
(T_{\gamma})_{\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}}
.

In the following, we introduce the sequence
(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
constructed in
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
, which combined with Lemma
2.6
is the basis of the convergence analysis of the relocated fixed-point iterations of conically averaged operators.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Theta,\Gamma\subseteq\mathbbm{R}_{++}
be nonempty, and
(T_{\theta,\gamma})_{(\theta,\gamma)\in\Theta\times\Gamma}
be a family of operators on
X
. Let
\overline{\theta}\in\Theta
, such that for all
(\theta,\gamma)\in\Theta\times\Gamma
,
\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}=\Fix T_{\overline{\theta},\gamma}\neq\varnothing
. Let
(Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma})_{\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma}
be operators on
X
satisfying Definition
3.1
(i)
-
(iii)
for
(T_{\overline{\theta},\gamma})_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
. Let
(\theta_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
be a sequence in
\Theta
, and
(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
be a sequence in
\Gamma
that converges to
\overline{\gamma}\in\Gamma
. Choose
c_{0}\in\Fix T_{\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}}
, and for all
{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
, set

c_{k+1}:=Q_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}c_{k}.

Then for all
{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
,
c_{k}\in\Fix T_{\theta_{k},\gamma_{k}}
and

(28)

3
, Section 5]
, originally devised for graph-based pure resolvent splitting methods
[
9
]
.

Proposition 5.3

(Graph-based fixed-point relocators)
.

Given
\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, define
\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}:X^{n-1}\to X^{n-1}
as, for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
,

\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}):=P_{\range(\mathbf{M})}\left((\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})\right)_{i=1}^{n}.

Then, for all
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
, the operator defined as

\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}=\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}+\left(1-\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})

(32)

is a fixed-point relocator for the operator (
29
)-(
30
) with Lipschitz constant

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\max\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\|\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}\|\sqrt{C_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\left(C_{i}(\gamma)+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{C_{j}(\gamma)}{d_{j}}\right)^{2}}\right\},

where
C_{1}=\sqrt{m}\|\Inc(G^{\prime})\|
and for
i=2,\dots,n
,
C_{i}(\gamma)=\sqrt{m}\|\Inc(G^{\prime})\|+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{C_{j}(\gamma)}{d_{j}}+\gamma\beta\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sum_{t=1}^{j-1}\frac{C_{t}(\gamma)}{d_{t}}
. We set
C_{1}(\gamma):=C_{1}
for ease of notation.

Proof.

In this setting and considering the change of variables (
31
), the fixed-point relocator in Proposition
4.6
takes the following form: for all
\gamma,\delta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
,

Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}=\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}+2\left(1-\tfrac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}\mathbf{e}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}),

where

\mathbf{e}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})=P_{\range(\mathbf{M})}\left(\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{i}\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})-\sum_{j\twoheadrightarrow i}\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})\right)_{i=1}^{n}.

Given
\theta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
, Proposition
4.4
(i)
yields

\sum_{j\twoheadrightarrow i}\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})=\kappa_{i}^{+}\overline{x}=\kappa_{i}^{+}x^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z}).

Hence, for all
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
,
\mathbf{e}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})
, and thus
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}=\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}
. Furthermore, it follows directly from Proposition
4.6
that
\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant specified above. In view of Remark
3.3
, the result follows.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

In practice, using the fixed-point relocator in (
32
) may require extra resolvent evaluations, as argued in
[
3
, Section 5.2]
, which increases the per-iteration cost of the resulting algorithm. More specifically, given
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
,
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, for each
i=1,\dots,n
, the
i
-th component of the fixed-point relocator in (
32
) is determined by the incidence matrix via the term

\displaystyle\big(\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})\big)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}_{ij}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}_{ij}\left((\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+}){\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\kappa_{\ell}-2\kappa_{\ell}^{+}){\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}_{ij}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+}){\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\kappa_{\ell}-2\kappa_{\ell}^{+}){\mathbf{x}}^{\gamma}_{\ell}(\mathbf{z})\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(G^{\prime})^{\dagger}_{ij}.

In view of (
38
), in each iteration of the relocated fixed-point iteration, the evaluation of the
i
-th component
Q_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}
requires, in principle, the calculation of
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})
and
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{w})
, where
\mathbf{w}=T_{\theta,\gamma}\mathbf{z}
. In total, in each iteration one would need to evaluate
2n
resolvents, while in a traditional fixed-point iteration, one would only need
n
resolvent evaluations. In order to overcome this issue, we will use Lemma
2.1
(i)
and Remark
3.3
to construct fixed-point relocators that do not require extra resolvent evaluations, dependent on the structure of the incidence matrix. These “cheaper” fixed-point relocators recycle previous resolvent evaluations, and have simpler Lipschitz constants.

The following result introduces different fixed-point relocators for three different graph structures.

Proposition 5.5

(Graph-dependent fixed-point relocators)
.

Given a graph
G=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{E})
satisfying (
28
) and Assumption
5.1
, consider the following directed trees:

(i)

Inward star-shaped subgraph:
\widecheck{G}=(\mathcal{N},\widecheck{\mathcal{E}})
,
\widecheck{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,n):i=1,\dots,n-1\}
. Define for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
and
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,

\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}:=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right){\diag(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})_{i=1}^{n-1}}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z}).

(33)

(ii)

Outward star-shaped subgraph:
\widehat{Q}=(\mathcal{N},\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
,
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=\{(n,i):i=2,\dots,n\}
. Define for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
and
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,

\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}:=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}-\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right){\diag(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})_{i=2}^{n}}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z}).

(34)

(iii)

Sequential subgraph:
\overline{G}=(\mathcal{N},\overline{\mathcal{E}})
,
\overline{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,i+1):i=1,\dots,n-1\}
. Define for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
,
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, and
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}:=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right){\diag\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+}\right)_{i=1}^{n-1}}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z}).

(35)

Then, if for each pair
(Q^{\prime},G^{\prime})\in\{(\widecheck{Q},\widecheck{G}),(\widehat{Q},\widehat{G}),(\overline{Q},\overline{G})\}
,
G^{\prime}
is a subgraph of
G
, then for all
\delta,\gamma,\theta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,
Q^{\prime}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}:X^{n-1}\to X^{n-1}
defines a fixed-point relocator for the iteration operator
T_{\theta,\gamma}
defined by the incidence matrix of
G^{\prime}
, and

\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\delta}(Q^{\prime}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}).

(36)

Proof.

Let
\delta,\gamma,\theta\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
. From Proposition
4.4
(i)
, given
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
, there exists
\overline{x}\in X
such that for all
i=1,\dots,n
,
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})=\overline{x}
. Then

\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})\text{~and~}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\overline{x}=0,

(37)

where the last equality follows from the handshaking lemma (see, e.g.
[
3
, Fact 5.1 (i)]
):
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})=0
). We now proceed to show that for each graph structure, the given operator is a fixed-point relocator that satisfies (
36
).

(i)

For the inward star-shaped subgraph, the incidence matrix is given by, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\Inc(\widecheck{G})_{ii}=1
,
\Inc(\widecheck{G})_{ni}=-1
, and
0
otherwise. In particular,
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=J_{\frac{\gamma}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{z}_{1})
. Moreover, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

\Inc(\widecheck{G})^{\dagger}_{ii}=1-\frac{1}{n}
, and for all
j=1,\dots,n
,
j\neq i
,
\Inc(\widecheck{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=-\frac{1}{n}
. Thus, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(\widecheck{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=0
. In view of Remark
5.4
, then

\displaystyle\big(\Inc(\widecheck{G})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})\big)_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z}).

Then, for all
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
, (
32
) and (
37
) imply, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}.

Hence,
\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\widecheck{Q}_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}
over
\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
. Furthermore, for all
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in X^{n-1}
and
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, since

\|\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{w})\|\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{1}\|,

then

\|\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}-\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{w}\|\leqslant\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})^{2}}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{1}\|.

This proves that
\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\max\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\kappa_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})^{2}}\right\}.

Furthermore, for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
, from (
33
) and Lemma
2.1
(i)
, it holds

\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\delta}(\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z})=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{1}\mathbf{z}\right)=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})\right)=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}).

(ii)

For the outward star-shaped graph, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\Inc(\widehat{G})_{1i}=1
and
\Inc(\widehat{G})_{(i+1)i}=-1
, and
0
otherwise. In particular,
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=J_{\frac{\gamma}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{z}_{i})
. Moreover, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\Inc(\widehat{G})^{\dagger}_{i(i+1)}=\frac{1}{n}-1
, and for all
j=1,\dots,n
,
j\neq i+1
,
\Inc(\widehat{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}
. Thus, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(\widehat{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=0
. In view of Remark
5.4
, then

\displaystyle\big(\Inc(\widehat{G})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})\big)_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i+1\end{subarray}}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})+\left(\frac{1}{n}-1\right)(\kappa_{i+1}-2\kappa_{i+1}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i+1}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})-(\kappa_{i+1}-2\kappa_{i+1}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{i+1}(\mathbf{z})

Then, for all
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
, (
32
) and (
37
) yield for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}_{i}-\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)(\kappa_{i+1}-2\kappa_{i+1}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=\widehat{Q}_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}\mathbf{z}.

Hence,
\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\widehat{Q}_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}
over
\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
. Moreover, for all
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in X^{n-1}
and
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
, since

\|\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{w})\|\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{i}\|,

then

\|\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}-\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{w}\|\leqslant\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{i}\|.

This proves that
\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\max\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})^{2}}\right\}.

Furthermore, since
-\kappa_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})
, then for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
, (
34
) and Lemma
2.1
(i)
yield

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\delta}(\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{z}_{i}-\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})\right)

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})\right)=\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z}).

(iii)

For the sequential graph, the incidence matrix is given by: for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\Inc(\overline{G})_{ii}=1
,
\Inc(\overline{G})_{(i+1)i}=-1
, and
0
otherwise. In particular,
\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=J_{\frac{\gamma}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{z}_{1})
. Moreover, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\Inc(\overline{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=1-\frac{i}{n}
for
j=1,\dots,i
, and
\Inc(\overline{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=-\frac{i}{n}
for
j=i+1,\dots,n
. Thus, for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Inc(\overline{G})^{\dagger}_{ij}=0
. In view of Remark
5.4
, then

\displaystyle\big(\Inc(\overline{G})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{e}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z})\big)_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{i}{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})-\frac{i}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{j}(\mathbf{z})

Then, for all
\mathbf{z}\in\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
, (
32
) and (
37
) yield for all
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}_{i}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{i}(\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})=\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}.

Hence,
\tilde{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\overline{Q}_{\gamma\leftarrow\delta}
over
\Fix T_{\theta,\gamma}
. Moreover, since for all
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in X^{n-1}
and
\delta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
,

\|x^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})-x^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{w})\|\leqslant\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{1}\|,

then

\|\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z}-\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{w}\|\leqslant\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+}\right)^{2}}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{w}_{1}\|.

This proves that
\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant

\mathcal{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\max\left\{1,\frac{\delta}{\gamma}+\left|1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right|\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\kappa_{j}-2\kappa_{j}^{+}\right)^{2}}\right\}.

Furthermore, for all
\mathbf{z}\in X^{n-1}
, from (
35
) and Lemma
2.1
(i)
, it holds

\mathbf{x}^{\delta}_{1}(\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}\mathbf{z})=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{1}\mathbf{z}\right)=J_{\frac{\delta}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})\right)=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}).

In view of Remark
3.3
and Proposition
5.3
, then
\widecheck{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
,
\widehat{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
, and
\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
are fixed-point relocators of their corresponding iteration operators.

∎

Remark 5.6

(Convergence of relocated graph-based forward-backward algorithms)
.

In view of Proposition
5.3
and
5.5
, convergence of relocated fixed-point iterations for operators in the form (
29
) follows directly from Theorem
4.7
, and using a similar argument as in Remark
4.8
to show that the condition
\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{N}}(\mathcal{L}_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}-1)<+\infty
holds. The advantage of the structure-dependent fixed-point relocators in Proposition
5.5
is that no extra resolvent evaluations are needed in comparison to the non-relocated version of the method, thus preventing an increase in the computational cost per (relocated) iteration. More precisely, the iterates in (
38
) take the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_{k}&=\mathbf{x}^{\gamma_{k}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\text{~from \eqref{e:FB-resolvent}}\\
\mathbf{w}_{k}&=\mathbf{z}_{k}-\lambda_{k}\theta_{k}\Inc(G^{\prime})^{*}\mathbf{x}_{k}\\
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}&=Q^{\prime}_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}\mathbf{w}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

(38)

where
Q^{\prime}_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}
is a fixed-point relocator dependent on the graph structure of
G^{\prime}
in Proposition
5.5
. For
Q^{\prime}\in\{\widecheck{Q},\widehat{Q},\overline{Q}\}
, (
36
) implies

\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma_{k+1}}(\mathbf{z}_{k+1})=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma_{k+1}}(Q^{\prime}_{\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\gamma_{k}}\mathbf{w}_{k})=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\gamma_{k}}(\mathbf{w}_{k}).

Hence, for all
{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
,

(i)

For the inward star-shaped subgraph,
\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}=J_{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{w}_{1,k})
, and from (
33
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\mathbf{w}_{k}+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right){\diag(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})_{i=1}^{n-1}}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\gamma_{k}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{1,k}\\
\mathbf{w}_{2,k}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{w}_{n-1,k}\end{pmatrix}+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\\
(\kappa_{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\\
\vdots\\
(\kappa_{n-1}-2\kappa_{n-1}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(ii)

For the outward star-shaped subgraph,
\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}=J_{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{w}_{i,k})
, and from (
34
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\mathbf{w}_{k}-\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right){\diag(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})_{2=1}^{n}}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\gamma_{k}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{1,k}\\
\mathbf{w}_{2,k}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{w}_{n-1,k}\end{pmatrix}-\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)\begin{pmatrix}(\kappa_{2}-2\kappa_{2}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\\
(\kappa_{3}-2\kappa_{3}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\\
\vdots\\
(\kappa_{n}-2\kappa_{n}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(iii)

For the sequential subgraph,
\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}=J_{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\kappa_{1}}A_{1}}(\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}}\mathbf{w}_{1,k})
, and from (
35
), we get

\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i}\mathbf{z}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)\kappa_{1}\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z})\\
\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}^{i-1}\mathbf{z}+\frac{\delta}{\gamma}(\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{i-1})+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}^{\gamma}_{1}(\mathbf{z}),\text{~for~}i=2,\dots,n-1,\end{cases}

and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{1,k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\mathbf{w}_{1,k}+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)\kappa_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1},

and for
i=2,\dots,n-1
,

\mathbf{z}_{i,k+1}=\mathbf{z}_{i-1,k+1}+\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}(\mathbf{w}_{i,k}-\mathbf{w}_{i-1,k})+\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\gamma_{k}}\right)(\kappa_{i}-2\kappa_{i}^{+})\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}.

In each case, since
\mathbf{x}_{1,k+1}
is computed in iteration
k
, then this iterate can be recycled in iteration
k+1
and only compute
\mathbf{x}_{2,k+1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n,k+1}
in the beginning of the iteration
k+1
.

5.2
A variable stepsize forward-backward three-operator splitting method

The Davis–Yin three-operator splitting method
[
15
]
is a method of the forward-backward family aiming to solve the following problem:

\text{find~}x\in X\text{~such that~}0\in A_{1}x+A_{2}x+Bx,

where
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are maximally monotone operators on
X
, and
B
is a
\beta
-cocoercive operator. To recover the Davis–Yin iteration operator from either the inward star-shaped or sequential graphs in Proposition
5.5
, set
n=2
,
\mathcal{N}=\{1,2\}
and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{(1,2)\}
, so that
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{2}^{+}=1
and
\kappa_{1}^{+}=0
. Then, the Davis–Yin iteration operator is defined by: for
\theta,\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}_{++}
and
z\in X
,

T^{DY}_{\theta,\gamma}z:=z-\theta(x_{1}^{\gamma}(z)-x_{2}^{\gamma}(z)),\text{~where~}\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{1}^{\gamma}(z)&:=J_{\gamma A_{1}}(2z)\\
x_{2}^{\gamma}(z)&:=J_{2\gamma A_{2}}\big(-z+2x_{1}^{\gamma}(z)-\gamma B_{1}x_{1}^{\gamma}(z)\big).\end{aligned}\right.

In view of Proposition
5.5
, the a fixed-point relocator for
T^{DY}_{\theta,\gamma}
is given by:

\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}=\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\Id+\left(1-\frac{\delta}{\gamma}\right)x_{1}^{\gamma}.

(39)

Observe that
\overline{Q}_{\delta\leftarrow\gamma}
coincides with the fixed-point relocator of the Douglas–Rachford method from
[
3
, Lemma 4.7]
. The resulting variable stepsize version of the three-operator Davis–Yin splitting method is presented in Algorithm
1
(cf.
[
3
, Algorithm 1]
).

(40)

Remark 5.8

(On the selection of variable stepsizes)
.

One of the conditions of the general convergence result in Theorem

6.1
Constrained LASSO

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbbm{R}^{q\times d}
and a vector
b\in\mathbbm{R}^{q}
, the LASSO
[
24
]
problem seeks to select variables from a vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
such that
Ax\approx b
. In order to choose the variables that explain the reconstruction of the vector
b
, the LASSO uses the
\ell_{1}
-norm to promote sparsity (variable selection) in the solution. In this section, our goal is to reconstruct a vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
in a predetermined box, such that
Ax=b
in the least squares sense. We model this problem as the constrained LASSO problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\phi(x):=\|Ax-b\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda\|x\|_{1}\text{~s.t.~}x\in[-u,u]^{d},

(43)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularisation parameter, and
u>0
defines the bounds of the box constraints. Since this problem shows a three-operator structure, we can employ Algorithm
1
to find a solution. Through this simple example, we will illustrate that larger constant stepsizes do not necessarily yield better numerical performance of Algorithm
1
, justifying the need for splitting methods that can vary stepsizes throughout iterations. We set
A_{1}=\lambda\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
A_{2}=N_{[-u,u]^{2}}
, and
B_{1}=A^{\top}(A\cdot-b)
. Clearly,
B_{1}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L=\lambda_{\max}(A^{\top}A)
, where
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
is the maximum eigenvalue function. As
B_{1}
is the gradient of a convex function, it is
L
-cocoercive. We generate
A
and
b
randomly, and control the eigenvalues of
A
to prevent the Lipschitz constant
L
from exploding or become too small. We also choose the small regularisation parameter
\lambda=10^{-3}
and the box bound
u=50
.

Following Remark
5.8
, we iteratively construct the sequence of stepsizes by setting
\zeta_{k}=\frac{0.1}{(k+1)^{1.5}}
for all
{k\in\mathbbm{N}}
, and pick
\tau_{k}=\proj_{[\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\max}]}(t_{k})

(44)

t_{k}=\frac{-2\gamma_{k}^{2}\cdot\frac{2-1.99}{2L}+\sqrt{\gamma_{k}^{4}\cdot\frac{(2-1.99)^{2}}{L^{2}}+4\gamma_{k}^{2}}}{2}\text{~inspired by \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{davis2017three}{}{}, eq. (3.6)]}},

(45)

or

t_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}\text{~inspired by \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Wittmann1992Approximation}{}{}]}}.

(46)

6.2
Nonnegative elastic net

The elastic net model
[
26
]
is a generalisation of the LASSO for sparse linear regression in the presence of highly correlated features. In this section, we consider the elastic net problem with nonnegative variables. Given
A\in\mathbbm{R}^{q\times d}
, and
b\in\mathbbm{R}^{q}
, the nonnegative elastic net problem is the following constrained and penalised least squares optimisation problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}}\phi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|x\|_{1}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\text{~s.t.~}x\in[0,+\infty)^{d},

(47)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
are regularisation parameters. The
\ell_{2}
-norm squared induces shrinkage and handles variable correlation. The combination of the two regularisation terms prevents overfitting to improve performance and reliability: highly correlated predictors have similar coefficients. The nonnegative constraint in this case is necessary in applications where it is known a priori that the variables cannot be negative, e.g. in the air quality regression problem
[
25
]
, where the variables represent concentration of chemicals.

Adding a constraint in the elastic net problem in (
47
) naturally yields a multioperator optimisation problem that can be handled by the forward-backward splitting methods in Section
5.1
. We choose
n=3
and

A_{1}=N_{x\geqslant 0}
,
A_{2}=A_{3}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}

B_{1}=A^{\top}(A\cdot-b)
and
B_{2}=\lambda_{2}I
. Since
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are Lipschitz continuous and subdifferentials of a convex function, then both are
\beta
-cocoercive with
\beta=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(A^{\top}A),\lambda_{2}\}
. We generate synthetic data to emulate the air quality dataset from
[
25
]
. In particular, we generate the matrix
A
from an exponential distribution, and then impose linear dependency between some columns (to induce correlation between features). Furthermore, we also draw a vector
x^{*}
from an exponential distribution and then set
b
to be

plus some random normal noise. In addition, we choose
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=10^{-2}
as regularisation parameters. Similarly to the previous section, we do not report the results for larger values of the regularisation parameters, as in those cases, better numerical performance is observed for larger stepsizes.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we develop a framework of forward-backward methods with variable stepsize to solve multioperator monotone inclusions, based on the relocated fixed-point iterations theory. This type of method requires the definition of a so-called fixed-point relocator. We show that for forward-backward methods, these are closely related to the fixed-point relocators presented in
[
3
]
for pure resolvent splitting methods. Convergence guarantees are established, and graph-based implementations are also discussed with their corresponding fixed-point relocators.

The results obtained in this paper can be deemed as the first step for designing efficient splitting methods for distributed problems. The second step would correspond to devising theoretically grounded stepsize rules that consistently display better numerical performance. As shown in the numerical experiments section, the relocated fixed-point iterations setting represents a promising alternative to tune the stepsize parameters automatically. Further research is required to discover fully the practical benefits of relocated fixed-point iterations.
