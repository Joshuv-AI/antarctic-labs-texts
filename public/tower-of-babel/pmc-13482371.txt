Statistical modeling and enhanced performance of a hybrid catalytic photomicroelectrolysis (CPMC) system for tetracycline removal: a Box-Behnken design approach
Authors: Hakimeh Mahdizadeh, Majid Amiri Gharaghani, Yousef Dadban Shahamat, Neda Javid, Hossein Khosravi, Faegheh Torabi, Alireza Nasiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTetracycline (TC), a widely used and persistent antibiotic in veterinary and human medicine, is considered a major aquatic environmental contaminant. This study aimed to synthesize and evaluate a novel TiO2-functionalized layered double hydroxides (LDH@TiO₂) as a catalyst in a catalytic photomicroelectrolysis system (CPMC) for the efficient removal of TC from aqueous solutions. The study was conducted in three phases: 1- Nanoparticle fabrication using a simple and low-cost co-precipitation method and investigation of its structural, morphological and surface properties using FESEM, EDX, Mapping, XRD, FTIR BET analyses, 2- Optimization of process parameters affecting TC removal including initial pH (3–11), contact time (5–60 min), initial concentration (10–100 mg/L) and carbon to iron ratio (C/Fe: 1/1, 2/1, 1/2) using Box-Behnken design (BBD) approach in design expert software and modeling of the TC removal process in the microelectrolysis (MC) system, 3- Comparison of the performance of the MC system in the presence of catalyst (CMC) and UV light (CPMC). The results showed that the synthesized nanocatalyst has a uniform structure with an average particle size of approximately 50 nm and a homogeneous elemental distribution. Optimization via BBD identified optimal conditions as: pH: 3.5, contact time 55 min, initial TC concentration 22 mg/L, and C/Fe ratio 1:2, yielding 97% removal efficiency in the MC system. When integrated with 0.05 g/100 mL LDH@TiO₂ under UV irradiation (CPMC), removal efficiency increased to 98% at 100 mg/L TC concentration. The presence of LDH@TiO₂ significantly increased the TC removal efficiency in the catalytic microelectrolysis process. The CPMC system achieved 98% TC removal efficiency at 100 mg/L concentration within 55 min, representing a 36, 13, and 5% improvement over MC (62%), LDH alone (85%), and CMC (93%) systems, respectively. The quadratic model demonstrated excellent fit (R² = 0.942, adjusted R² = 0.918, p < 0.001). The results of this study showed that the synergy between LDH@TiO₂ photocatalysis and redox processes at the iron-carbon microelectrolysis scale can be effectively, economically, and sustainably applied to remove resistant antibiotics such as tetracycline in pharmaceutical wastewater.Keywords: Photomicroelectrolysis, Response surface methodology, Statistical optimization, Pharmaceutical wastewater, LDH@TiO₂Subject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Tetracycline antibiotics (TCs) represent a widespread class of contaminants of emerging concern (CECs), primarily originating from the discharge of effluents from pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities, veterinary practices, and, to a lesser extent, municipal wastewater treatment plants receiving high usage runoff. The current environmental status is characterized by their ubiquitous detection in surface waters, groundwater, and even soils globally, often at concentrations ranging from ng/L to μg/L levels, signaling chronic low-level contamination. TCs constitute approximately 45% of global antibiotic consumption in veterinary and human medicine, resulting in widespread environmental contamination. Recent monitoring studies have detected TCs in 78% of global river systems (range: 12 ng/L–47 µg/L), 63% of groundwater samples near agricultural zones (up to 18 µg/L), and municipal wastewater effluents (0.8–22 µg/L) that bypass conventional treatment plants1,2.

The persistent nature of TCs, owing to their complex chemical structures, allows them to resist natural attenuation processes. TC’s environmental persistence stems from its structural stability: the conjugated tricarbonylamide system resists hydrolysis, while its zwitterionic nature (pKa₁ = 3.3, pKa₂ = 7.7, pKa₃ = 9.7) enables partitioning across diverse environmental matrices.

The principal ecological threat posed by this persistence is the promotion of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in microbial communities, leading to the selection and proliferation of multi-drug resistant (MDR) pathogens. Critically, chronic exposure to TCs at environmentally relevant concentrations (≥ 1 µg/L) has been demonstrated to accelerate horizontal gene transfer of tet(M) and tet(O) resistance genes in aquatic biofilms by 3–5 orders of magnitude3, directly linking environmental TCs residues to clinical resistance emergence3. Consequently, regulatory bodies are increasingly imposing stringent discharge limits; for instance, many jurisdictions are moving towards near-zero discharge standards or mandating the removal down to ng/L levels in the final effluent, often significantly below the traditional regulatory benchmarks for general pollutants2,4,5. Hybrid advanced oxidation processes effectively mineralize recalcitrant organic dyes6.

Conventional wastewater treatment processes, such as activated sludge systems, coagulation-flocculation, and standard chlorination, have demonstrated pronounced inadequacy in effectively eliminating TCs1,7. Processes relying on adsorption or simple chemical oxidation often suffer from low mineralization efficiency, leading merely to the transformation of the parent compound into potentially more toxic or equally persistent transformation products, rather than complete degradation3,8. Furthermore, methods like chlorination risk generating undesirable, toxic disinfection by-products (DBPs). The high operational costs associated with advanced chemical oxidation or energy-intensive membrane filtration) when applied to high-volume streams (further underscore the need for novel, cost-effective technologies capable of achieving the required, ultra-low effluent standards necessary to mitigate the global public health crisis linked to antibiotic resistance1,9. Integrating renewable energy and storage systems enhances sustainability and reduces costs10–12. Conventional wastewater treatment methods, utilizing standard physical (e.g., separation, filtration) and chemical (e.g., coagulation–flocculation, oxidation) processes, remain inadequate for the complete removal of persistent and emerging contaminants1,13. Key limitations include incomplete degradation of complex organics (especially in chemical methods), high operational costs (for advanced oxidation), and the formation of toxic by-products (common in chemical disinfection). These shortcomings frequently result in effluent quality that fails to meet stringent regulatory standards. Therefore, there is an increasing need to develop new treatment methods that can effectively remove emerging contaminants from aquatic environments9,13,14.

The advent of micro-electrolysis technology marks a significant paradigm shift in pollutant treatment, offering compelling advantages over conventional methods. Despite the widespread use of conventional treatment methods, these methods are often criticized due to their technological complexity, high operating costs, and the risk of secondary pollution15,16. In contrast, micro-electrolysis systems, typically involving iron filings and carbon in an aqueous environment, generate a robust internal galvanic cell17. This setup simultaneously facilitates powerful redox reactions and promotes Fenton-like processes, effectively degrading complex organic pollutants, reducing heavy metal ions, and breaking down recalcitrant compounds into more biodegradable forms18. The in-situ generation of nascent hydrogen, ferrous/ferric ions, and reactive oxygen species (e.g., hydroxyl radicals) significantly enhances treatment efficiency, often leading to higher chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal, improved biodegradability, thereby presenting a more sustainable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign alternative for tackling challenging industrial wastewaters19–21. Industrial by-products like red mud offer stable, cost-effective matrices for heavy metal immobilization22–24. Three-dimensional electrochemical systems offer advanced solutions for pharmaceutical pollutant removal25. Electro-generated H₂O₂ coupled with UV effectively mineralizes recalcitrant organics26.

The incorporation of nanoparticles into micro-electrolysis systems significantly enhances treatment efficiency by drastically increasing the specific surface area available for reactions27,28, thus promoting faster electron transfer and pollutant degradation kinetics29,30. Furthermore, the nanoscale dimensions enable the creation of more active catalytic sites and facilitate greater contact with pollutants, leading to superior removal rates for even recalcitrant heavy metals31,32. This amplified reactivity and improved mass transfer within the nano-scale environment translate into more compact, efficient29, and potentially cost-effective wastewater treatment solutions compared to conventional micro-electrolysis setups28. Thermodynamic models incorporating temperature and seepage effects accurately predict contaminant transport in porous media33–36. Heterogeneous catalysts enable efficient and selective transformations in complex matrices37. ZnO-based nanofluids serve as versatile catalysts for diverse chemical reactions38. Enhanced mass transfer in nano-environments improves kinetics, akin to advanced nanofluid systems39.

Among nanomaterials, Layered Double Hydroxides (LDHs) have shown excellent performance in removing organic pollutants due to their layered structure and high anion exchange capacity40,41. LDHs are formed from the combination of divalent (M²⁺) and trivalent (M³⁺) cations with hydroxyl (OH⁻) groups, creating a layered structure with a positive charge These features enable effective anion adsorption in the interlayers. Advanced constitutive frameworks accounting for particle breakage and crystallization enhance geo-environmental predictions42,43. Modified biochars with metal oxides improve adsorption capacity for emerging contaminants44. In addition to their adsorption applications, LDHs are also used as catalysts, ceramic precursors, and flame retardants45,46.

Characteristics such as a large specific surface area, high thermal stability, and high anion exchange capacity make Layered Double Hydroxides (LDHs) an ideal option for wastewater treatment47,48. Metal-organic crystalline materials offer tunable physicochemical and antimicrobial functionalities49.

Additionally, the use of metallic compounds or certain organic materials in combination with LDHs can have a synergistic or enhancing effect, leading to increased process efficiency41. LDHs provide an attractive platform for adsorption or catalytic processes due to their tunable composition, high surface area, and ability to intercalate various species between their layers. These characteristics lead to the formation of composite materials with enhanced light absorption, charge separation, and catalytic activity. Furthermore, the ion-exchange capability of LDHs allows for the combination of various catalytic species or functional groups, further improving their performance50,51. Integrating LDHs with biological reactors significantly enhances degradation of persistent pollutants52.

Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) catalyst is considered one of the most important materials used in semiconductors due to its good stability, large surface area, corrosion resistance, non-toxicity, relatively low cost, and stable colloidal suspension53. It is an environmentally friendly material with high adsorption capacity and catalytic activity, making it valuable for the removal of pollutants54,55. However, the limitation of TiO₂ catalyst recyclability is a major drawback that restricts its application. To overcome this limitation, various strategies have been implemented, including doping with metal or non-metal intermediates and coupling with high porosity materials to enhance the catalytic activity of TiO₂56. Supported ZnO nanoparticles in hybrid AOPs enhance degradation of persistent dyes57. Precise catalyst synthesis and microenvironment engineering significantly boost performance53,58.

Now, the combination of LDHs with nanoparticles such as titanium dioxide (TiO₂) can significantly enhance the efficiency of adsorption and catalytic processes59. This combination, due to its unique properties, such as the large surface area and high adsorption capacity of LDHs and the strong catalytic activity of TiO₂, results in the production of highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals. Additionally, LDHs serve as an effective carrier for TiO₂, preventing the aggregation of TiO₂ particles, thereby improving the recovery and recyclability of the photocatalyst60. Furthermore, this combination can modify physicochemical properties by altering cations and simultaneously act as an adsorbent for pollutants. Therefore, it can be said that the combination of LDHs with TiO₂ in the form of LDH@TiO₂ nanocomposites can enhance catalytic activity and light adsorption, leading to improved efficiency in the removal of pollutants61–63. Nanocatalysts anchored on carbon nanotubes significantly enhance photodegradation of pharmaceuticals64.

Rationale for tetracycline selection

Tetracycline was selected as the model contaminant for three critical reasons: (i) It represents one of the most frequently detected antibiotics in global aquatic environments, with concentrations ranging from ng/L in surface waters to mg/L in pharmaceutical wastewater effluents14; (ii) Its complex molecular structure containing multiple functional groups (phenolic diketone, dimethylamino, and carboxamide moieties) confers high persistence against conventional biological treatment, making it a representative recalcitrant pharmaceutical pollutant; (iii) TC exerts strong selective pressure for antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) even at sub-inhibitory concentrations (µg/L range), directly contributing to the global antimicrobial resistance crisis recognized by WHO as a top-10 public health threat3. Recent regulatory frameworks in the EU and North America now mandate TC removal to < 100 ng/L in discharge waters, necessitating advanced treatment technologies beyond conventional processes. Therefore, this research aimed to synthesize and apply an effective catalyst (LDH@TiO₂) in a catalytic photomicroelectrolysis (CPMC) system to enhance the removal of TC antibiotic from aqueous solutions.

Study innovation

While LDH@TiO₂ composites and microelectrolysis systems have been separately investigated, this study introduces three key innovations: (i) development of a novel MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocomposite synthesized via a simplified co-precipitation/hydrothermal route without complex surface modification; (ii) integration of this catalyst within a hybrid catalytic photomicroelectrolysis (CPMC) configuration that synergistically combines electrochemical oxidation, Fenton-like reactions, and photocatalysis; and (iii) comprehensive statistical optimization using Box-Behnken design to elucidate interactive effects of four critical operational parameters on TC removal kinetics. The primary objective is to develop a simple and rapid purification technology capable of achieving > 98% removal of high-concentration TC (100 mg/L) in a relatively short time. This hybrid system takes advantage of the enhanced light absorption, charge separation, and high surface area provided by the LDH/TiO₂ heterogeneous junction, which effectively reduces electron-hole recombination, while also benefiting from the robust, in-situ redox reactions inherent in the microelectrolysis cell (such as the generation of hydroxyl radicals). Additionally, unlike most advanced oxidation hybrid systems, it does not require the use of strong chemical oxidants.

Materials and methods

Materials

Magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl₂·6 H₂O, ≥ 99.0%), aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl₃·6 H₂O, ≥ 98.0%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 99.0%), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 95–98%), and titanium dioxide (TiO₂, anatase phase, ≥ 99.5%, particle size < 25 nm) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Tetracycline hydrochloride (C₂₂H₂₄N₂O₈·HCl, ≥ 95%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). All solutions were prepared using deionized water produced by a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, France).

Methods

This study was conducted in several stages: 1- preparation of nanoparticles using a simple and low-cost co-precipitation method and investigation of their structural, morphological and surface properties 2- Experimental design using the Box-Behnken Design (BBD) under the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) approach in Design Expert 8.0 software, process modeling and optimization of parameters affecting TC removal including initial pH3–11, contact time (5–60 min), initial concentration (10–100 mg/L) and carbon to iron ratio (C/Fe: 1.1, 2.1, 1.2) in the microelectrolysis (MC) system, 3- Determination of the optimal dosage of LDH@TiO2 nanoparticles (0.025, 0.05, 0.1 g/100 mL) in the CMC process 4- Comparison of the performance of the MC system with CMC and CPMC processes.

Preparation of MgAl-LDH@ TiO2 nanoparticles

The MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst was synthesized via a co-precipitation method conducted at ambient temperature (23 ± 5 °C). An aqueous solution of 50 mL containing 10.165 g of magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl₂·6 H₂O), 9.63 g of aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl₃·6 H₂O), and 5 g of TiO₂ powder was added dropwise to 50 mL of 4 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) under vigorous stirring. The resulting mixture was then transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 48 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was separated by centrifugation and thoroughly washed with deionized water to eliminate residual ions and impurities. The final white solid product was dried overnight at 60 °C, yielding the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocomposite65,66.

Characterization

A comprehensive suite of characterization techniques was employed to evaluate the properties of the synthesized MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocomposite. Surface morphology and microstructural features were examined via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; MIRA III, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Elemental composition and quantitative weight percentages were determined using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), complemented by elemental mapping to visualize spatial distribution. Crystallographic phases and structural identification were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD; PW1730, PHILIPS, Netherlands) across a 2θ range of 10° to 80°. Functional groups and chemical bonding were characterized through Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; AVATAR, Thermo). Furthermore, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis (BELSORP MINI II, BEL, Japan) was applied to assess the specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter.

Experimental design and procedure

The BBD method was used to design experiments, model, and optimize variables in the MC process. This method is a type of response surface design that is suitable for experiments involving up to four factors. Factor selection was guided by three criteria: (i) mechanistic relevance to microelectrolysis kinetics (pH governs Fe²⁺ dissolution and •OH generation; C/Fe ratio controls galvanic cell density); (ii) environmental relevance (TC concentrations reflect pharmaceutical wastewater ranges); and (iii) practical operational constraints (contact time ≤ 60 min for continuous-flow feasibility). Ranges were established through preliminary batch experiments (n = 5) identifying thresholds where removal efficiency plateaued (< 5% change beyond range limits). BBD was selected over Central Composite Design (CCD) or factorial designs because: (a) it requires fewer experimental runs, reducing catalyst consumption; (b) all experimental points lie at practical mid-levels (no extreme axial points that might produce non-representative results); and (c) it provides uniform precision across the design space, critical for process scale-up67.

The total number of experiments (N) in BBD is calculated using the formula: N = 2k(k − 1) + C₀, where k represents the number of independent variables, and C₀ is the number of central points, typically set to 367.

Based on the variables considered in this study including: initial antibiotic concentration (10–100 mg/L), contact time (5–60 min), pH3–11, and carbon-to-iron ratio (C/Fe:1:1, 1:2, 2:1) a total of 51 experimental runs were randomly generated using Design Expert software. The process variables were ultimately modeled and optimized.

Results and discussion

Characterization of LDH@TiO2

FESEM, EDS, mapping

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was employed to examine the morphology of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂71. The micrographs demonstrated that the constituent particles possess a relatively uniform and homogeneous distribution, displaying a well-ordered dimensional arrangement. To obtain quantitative confirmation, particle size measurements were performed on three independent samples. The analysis indicated an average particle diameter of approximately 50 nm, confirming the formation of a nanostructure suitable for a broad spectrum of scientific and industrial applications. Such morphological attributes are expected to improve the adsorbent’s efficiency across diverse operational processes72. The FESEM image illustrating the morphology and surface features of the synthesized MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ is presented in Fig. 2.

FESEM images of LDH@TiO2 nanocatalyst.

Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to identify the constituent chemical elements of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ and to quantify their relative abundances (Fig. 3). The spectrum confirmed the presence of oxygen, magnesium, aluminum, and titanium within the material’s composition. Among these, aluminum exhibited the highest proportion, accounting for 39.59 wt%, while titanium, recognized as a critical component influencing the catalyst’s distinctive properties, was detected at 7.78 wt%. The measured elemental concentrations align well with theoretical expectations for this nanocatalyst, thereby affirming its designed compositional framework. Such compositional insights provide valuable context for interpreting the adsorbent’s functional behavior and potential applicability in various operational processes73,74.

EDS and mapping of LDH@TiO2 nanocatalyst.

Elemental mapping analysis was performed to accurately characterize the spatial distribution and relative abundance of elements within the LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst at a point-specific scale (Fig. 3). This technique enabled precise visualization and assessment of the localization and concentration of individual elements in the nanocatalyst framework. The mapping profiles revealed a uniform distribution of aluminum (Al), oxygen (O), magnesium (Mg), and titanium (Ti) across the surface of the material. Such homogeneity in elemental dispersion suggests that the catalyst possesses a chemically stable and structurally coherent framework, which is advantageous for enhancing catalytic performance. Moreover, the observed even elemental distribution reflects the efficiency of the synthesis and preparation protocols employed, reinforcing the material’s potential for adsorption processes and catalytic applications75,76.

FTIR

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was conducted using the KBr pellet method to investigate the chemical structure and identify the functional groups present in the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst. The corresponding FTIR spectrum is presented in Fig. 4.

Fourier transform spectroscopy (FTIR) of LDH@TiO2 nanocatalyst.

The Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum revealed multiple characteristic peaks, each associated with distinct molecular vibrations and structural features of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst. A prominent absorption band at 3397 cm⁻¹ was attributed to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (-OH) groups, arising from both the intrinsic hydroxyls within the LDH lattice and water molecules adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface. The presence of this peak confirms the coexistence of hydroxyl functionalities and adsorbed water, both of which are known to play a significant role in enhancing the catalytic performance of nanocatalysts77,78.

An additional absorption band observed at 628 cm⁻¹ is characteristic of metal–oxide vibrations, confirming the presence of metallic structures within the nanocatalyst matrix. Such bands are particularly relevant for evaluating the electrochemical and catalytic behaviors of advanced materials. Furthermore, a distinct bifurcated peak appearing between 1613 and 1637 cm⁻¹ corresponds to vibrations associated with metal-bound hydroxyl groups, indicating strong interactions between metal centers and hydroxyl functionalities. These structural features are likely to promote enhanced chemical interactions, thereby improving the overall catalytic efficiency of the material78,79.

A peak detected at approximately 630 cm⁻¹ is attributed to hydrogen bonding arising from interactions between hydroxyl groups within the MgAl-LDH layers79,80. Such hydrogen bonds are believed to enhance structural stability while facilitating charge exchange and electron transfer during catalytic processes. The FTIR results further confirm the presence of metal–oxide bonds in both the TiO₂ and LDH frameworks of the final catalyst. These functional groups promote stronger interactions between the catalyst surface and target pollutants, thereby contributing to improved catalytic activity. Collectively, these favorable structural characteristics are expected to increase the efficiency of the catalyst in pollutant removal and in a wide range of chemical reactions.

XRD

The crystalline structure of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst was examined using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis, with the resulting diffractogram displayed in Fig. 5.

XRD analysis of LDH@TiO2 nanocatalyst.

The XRD pattern exhibited sharp and well-defined peaks at 2θ values of 20.99°, 21.88°, 31.02°, 31.79°, 33.21°, 34.02°, 45.78°, 49.56°, 54°, 56°, and 82°, indicating the presence of distinct crystalline phases. These observations confirm the formation of a well-ordered crystalline structure within the LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst79,80.

Beyond the previously identified peaks, the XRD analysis of the final catalyst exhibited additional reflections at 2θ values of 27.13°, 36.28°, and 54.53°, unequivocally confirming the incorporation of TiO2 phases within the crystalline framework. This indicates the introduction of at least one new crystalline phase into the composite system. Moreover, the XRD results demonstrate that the intrinsic crystalline structure of the LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst remains substantially intact upon integration with TiO₂, preserving the material’s characteristic two-dimensional architecture. Such structural integrity suggests that the catalyst retains its durability and stability throughout the synthesis procedure79,80.

The crystallite size of the LDH@TiO₂ sample was calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation (D = Kλ/βcosθ), where K is the shape factor assumed to be 0.9, λ is the X-ray wavelength of Cu Kα₁ radiation (1.54060 Å), β denotes the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the selected diffraction peaks expressed in radians after instrumental broadening correction, and θ represents the Bragg angle, defined as half of the corresponding 2θ value and expressed in radians. The crystallite size of the LDH@TiO₂ sample was calculated based on selected diffraction peaks at 2θ = 20.94°, 21.70°, 27.43°, 31.81°, 33.92°, 45.45°, 49.34°, and 54.36°. The average crystallite size was calculated to be approximately 28 nm, confirming the nanocrystalline nature of the synthesized material.

Analysis of porosity and specific surface area of MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst

Porosimetric characterization and specific surface area determination of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst were carried out using the BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) method, with the results depicted in Fig. 6.

Porosimetric analysis and determination of the specific surface area of the LDH@TiO2 nanocatalyst and the T-Plot and BJH analysis.

The catalyst exhibited a BET surface area of 10.621 m²/g, indicating a sufficiently high active surface area conducive to catalytic activity. Furthermore, the total pore volume measured at a relative pressure (p/p₀) of 0.990 was found to be 0.062514 cm³/g, suggesting notable adsorption capacity for both gaseous and liquid species. The average pore diameter was calculated to be approximately 23.544 nm, reflecting the favorable porosity characteristics of the nanocatalyst81.

The pore dimensions of the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst are optimized to facilitate adsorption and reaction with small molecules while effectively preventing the entrapment of larger species. Consequently, the BET analysis results affirm the material’s adequate potential for diverse catalytic applications. Furthermore, T-Plot and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analyses, illustrated in Fig. 7, provide detailed insights into the surface properties and porosity of the nanocatalyst. The initial surface area was recorded at 4.3735 m²/g, which increased to 12.257 m²/g in the secondary surface area measurement, indicative of enhanced catalytic activity during the adsorption process. The pore volume was determined to be 0.064288 cm³/g, with a dominant pore diameter of 1.21 nm. Collectively, these data emphasize the nanocatalyst’s efficacy in adsorbing and facilitating the exchange of small molecular species77.

According to the classification criteria established by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), porous materials are categorized into three types based on their pore sizes: micropores (< 2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (> 50 nm). The pore size measurements indicate that the MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocatalyst belongs to the mesoporous category. The effect of initial solution pH

The effect of initial solution pH on TC removal efficiency with increasing contact time by the microelectrolysis (MC) process is shown in the three-dimensional response surface plots at the values of the center points of other variables in Fig. 7.

One of the important parameters affecting the MC processes for the pollutants removing from aqueous solutions is pH. As can be seen, most of the removal efficiency was achieved in almost acidic and neutral solutions, such that after about 50 min of reaction, the removal efficiency of about 95% and above occurred in this range. However, as the solution pH increased from 7 to 10, a corresponding decrease in removal efficiency was noted.

The observed high removal efficiency for TC under acidic to neutral condition is fundamentally rooted in the synergistic electrostatics between the catalyst surface and the antibiotic molecule. With a pHZPC of 8.2, the TiO2/LDH composite exhibits a net positive surface charge in this operational range. Given that TC is an amphoteric species, lowering the pH drives the protonation of its basic groups (like the dimethylamino group), which can shift the overall molecular charge towards a favorable state (often resulting in a net positive or near-neutral charge distribution that minimizes electrostatic hindrance). This configuration provides the necessary strong chemisorption required for effective degradation pathways within the microelectrolysis setup, ensuring the TC molecules are held long enough at the electrode/catalyst interface to interact with electrochemically generated reactive species17,82.

The sharp decline in removal efficiency as the pH approaches 10 directly correlates with the inversion of the surface potential. When pH> pHZPC, the catalyst surface becomes definitively negative. Concurrently, TC molecules predominantly exist in their anionic forms (TC−) due to the deprotonation of their acidic moieties. This leads to a pronounced electrostatic repulsion barrier between the catalyst surface and the TC anions. This repulsion fundamentally prevents the requisite intimate contact for high-rate heterogeneous reactions, thereby dominating the overall rate-limiting step and causing the efficiency of the microelectrolysis process to plummet, irrespective of the electrochemical potential applied17,82.

Comparative studies support these mechanistic explanations. For example, a study by Wang et al. (2013) on the removal of TC by electrolysis-assisted NZVI found that decreasing the pH from 7 to 3 significantly increased the removal efficiency due to enhanced iron dissolution and increased adsorption of TC onto the iron surface83.

Similarly, studies by other researchers (Zou et al.) on the removal of contaminants from aqueous solutions in microelectrolysis systems have shown that lower pH levels lead to more hydroxyl radical production and, consequently, a higher rate of contaminant degradation. These findings, which are similar to the results of this study, highlight the critical role of pH in optimizing microelectrolysis processes for TC removal by influencing both the antibiotic’s properties and the reaction dynamics of the process itself. At all pH values, the removal efficiency also increased with increasing contact time28.

The effect of initial TC concentration

The effect of initial TC concentration with increasing contact time on removal efficiency is shown in the three-dimensional response surface plots at the center point values of other variables in Fig. 8.

Effect of initial TC concentration with increasing contact time on its removal efficiency by the MC process in the linear and three-dimensional response surface plots at the values of the center points of other variables.

The removal efficiency increased with increasing TC concentration from 10 to 55 mg/L. However, with further increasing TC concentration from 55 to 100 mg/L with a constant amount of adsorbent, the removal efficiency also decreased.

In microelectrolysis processes, the removal efficiency of TC typically exhibits a non-linear relationship with the initial antibiotic concentration, where an initial increase in concentration can lead to improved removal rates, followed by a decline at higher concentrations. At low initial concentrations, increasing the TC level enhances the interaction between the antibiotic molecules and the active sites on the microelectrolysis material (e.g., iron). This leads to a more efficient utilization of the available reactive sites, as the mass transfer limitations are minimized and the reaction kinetics are favored84,85. Consequently, the removal efficiency increases as more TC molecules are readily adsorbed and degraded.

However, as the initial TC concentration continues to rise, the microelectrolysis system approaches saturation. The number of available reactive sites on the iron surface becomes a limiting factor. Beyond a certain concentration threshold, the antibiotic molecules start competing for the limited adsorption sites, causing a decrease in the fractional removal rate. Furthermore, high concentrations can lead to the formation of multiple layers of adsorbed TC, hindering the access of reactants (such as Fe2+ or hydroxyl radicals) to the reactive surface. This crowding effect reduces the efficiency of the degradation process, leading to a decline in overall removal efficiency86.

Comparative studies support this observation. Research by Malakootian et al. (2020) on the removal of acid red 18 by a hybrid micro-electrolysis process reported an initial increase in removal efficiency with increasing dye concentration up to a certain point, beyond which the efficiency decreased. The authors attributed this phenomenon to the saturation of active sites and increased mass transfer resistance at higher concentrations. Similarly, most microelectrolysis degradation studies or other progressive oxidation studies have shown that the reaction rate constant decreases with higher initial contaminant concentrations, indicating a saturation effect that limits the overall removal efficiency87. These findings highlight the importance of optimizing the initial TC concentration to achieve the highest removal efficiency in microelectrolysis systems. Consequently, while the absolute amount of pollutant removed may increase with concentration, the proportion of pollutant removed relative to the initial concentration diminishes, leading to a lower overall removal efficiency28.

In all concentrations studied, the removal efficiency also increased with increasing contact time.

Effect of C/Fe ratio

The effect of the C/Fe ratio with increasing contact time on the TC removal efficiency is shown in the line graphs at the center point values of the other variables in Fig. 9.

The effect of the C/Fe ratio with increasing contact time on the TC removal efficiency by the MC process at the center point values of the other variables.

The efficiency of pollutant removal in MC systems hinges on the optimal Fe/C ratio, with a 1:2 C/Fe ratio generally outperforming others like 1:1 or 2:1. This advantage stems from the enhanced formation of micro-galvanic cells. At a 1:2 C/Fe ratio, the increased proportion of iron (Fe) acts as a more active anode, leading to greater dissolution of Fe²⁺ ions. These ferrous ions are crucial catalysts for Fenton-like reactions, significantly boosting the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH).

Concurrently, the carbon (C) serves as the cathode, facilitating the reduction of oxygen to produce hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The synergistic effect of abundant Fe²⁺ and sufficient H₂O₂ at the 1:2 C/Fe ratio maximizes the oxidative capacity of the system. Ratios like 1:1 may not provide enough iron sites, while a 2:1 ratio can lead to an excess of carbon, potentially hindering iron’s anodic activity or diluting its effectiveness. Therefore, a carbon to iron ratio of 1:2 optimizes the balance between anodic and cathodic processes, leading to better degradation of pollutants.

Comparative studies on the Fe/C ratio in microelectrolysis systems support these findings. In the study by Lu et al. (2025), which investigated the treatment of textile wastewater using an iron–carbon microelectrolysis system combined with H₂O₂, it was demonstrated that increasing the proportion of iron and optimizing the Fe/C ratio enhanced the formation of micro-galvanic cells and promoted oxidative reactions. Although the specific 1:2 ratio was not explicitly reported in this work, the results confirmed that sufficient iron to generate Fe²⁺ ions, together with carbon serving as the cathode, plays a critical role in improving pollutant removal efficiency88.

In the other study, Ren et al. (2020), investigated different Fe/C mass ratios. Their results showed that an Fe/C ratio of 1.5:1 at pH = 5 achieved approximately 73% COD removal, while an Fe/C ratio of 1:2 under more acidic conditions (pH = 3) yielded an even higher efficiency of 76% for the degradation of 2,4-DCP. The authors attributed this improvement to the enhanced formation of micro-galvanic cells and the intensified redox reactions facilitated by higher Fe availability, which led to greater Fe²⁺ release and hydroxyl radical generation89.

Similarly, Li et al. (2018), reported that an Fe: C ratio of 1:1 for Fe⁰/Fe–C MEMs treating high-COD agricultural wastewater achieved around 64% COD removal. Beyond removal efficiency, this study emphasized the role of catalyst stability and biochar support, noting that excessive iron may lead to surface passivation via oxide layer formation, whereas surplus carbon could diminish anodic activity and hinder overall degradation performance90. Wang et al. (2025) reported a 34.4% increase in methane production and substantial enhancement of key enzyme activities (62.2–273.2%) in anaerobic digestion of waste activated sludge using Fe-Cu microelectrolysis, attributed to a shift in electron transfer pathways from interspecies hydrogen transfer (IHT) to direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET)91.

Increasing the catalyst dosage in catalytic microelectrolysis processes generally leads to an enhanced removal efficiency of pollutants from aqueous solutions, up to a certain optimal level. As shown in Fig. 13, in the microelectrolysis processes alone (MC), MC with TiO2, LDH, LDH@TiO2 and LDH@TiO2 nanoparticles in the presence of UV light, the removal efficiencies after 50 min were 46, 62, 85, 93, and 98%, respectively.

LDH nanoparticles have a high adsorption capacity in removing pollutants from aqueous solutions due to its unique layered structure, but in order to achieve the best result in the shortest possible time in water and wastewater treatment systems, combining nanoparticles or similar materials is a good idea to increase the efficiency of the process99. Since the use of metal compounds or some organic materials in combination with LDH can have a synergistic or intensifying effect and lead to increased process efficiency100. Therefore, the presence of TiO2 nanoparticles in the LDH structure increases the efficiency of the material compared to LDH and TiO2 alone. On the other hand, the presence of UV light was another intensifying factor. The superior performance of the TiO2/LDH nanocomposite in photocatalysis is fundamentally attributed to the electronic engineering achieved through the formation of a Type-II heterojunction at the interface. This specific junction configuration results in a crucial band edge alignment between the TiO2 and LDH phases. This staggered energy landscape generates an internal electric field (IEF) that provides a strong thermodynamic driving force, compelling the photogenerated electrons (e−) and holes (h+) to migrate spatially to opposite domains. This spatial separation effectively bypasses the intrinsic kinetic bottleneck of the single-semiconductor system100.

Consequently, this effective segregation drastically diminishes the probability of non-radiative recombination events, thereby leading to a significant prolongation of the charge carrier lifetime (τ). A longer carrier lifetime ensures that a much higher population of separated charge carriers remains active and available to engage in surface redox reactions with adsorbed pollutants. Thus, the enhanced photocatalytic activity observed is a direct, predictable outcome of this structural design, which successfully alleviates the limiting factor of rapid electron-hole annihilation prevalent in pristine TiO2100. This principle of optimizing catalytic performance through precise electronic and orbital engineering to decouple reaction pathways is further corroborated by recent advancements in electrocatalysis, where strategic modulation of orbital hybridization has been shown to significantly enhance charge utilization and reaction selectivity101.

Kinetics

Chemical kinetics is fundamental to understanding the rates and mechanisms of chemical reactions, providing critical insights into how quickly reactants transform into products. The study of reaction kinetics allows researchers to optimize reaction conditions, control product yields, and design efficient chemical processes108. By determining the rate law, which expresses the reaction rate as a function of reactant concentrations, scientists can elucidate reaction pathways and identify rate-determining steps. This understanding is particularly crucial in fields such as catalysis, pharmaceuticals, environmental remediation, and industrial manufacturing, where precise control over reaction speed is paramount for achieving desired outcomes and ensuring process safety and economic viability. Without a thorough kinetic analysis, the development and scale-up of chemical transformations would be largely empirical and inefficient109. In examining the kinetic equations, the process followed first-order kinetics, which is shown in Fig. 14.

Linear diagram of first-order kinetics of TC removal in the CPMC system.

The kinetic behavior of many chemical reactions can be effectively described by order equations, with first and second-order kinetics being the most commonly encountered. A first-order reaction, where the rate is directly proportional to the concentration of a single reactant, is characterized by a linear relationship between the natural logarithm of the reactant concentration and time110.

First-order kinetic processes are fundamental to understanding the rate of many chemical reactions and physical phenomena across various scientific disciplines. In such systems, the reaction rate is directly proportional to the concentration of a single reactant. This linear relationship simplifies the mathematical description and allows for straightforward prediction of how a system will evolve over time. For instance, in environmental science, the degradation of pollutants in water or air often follows first-order kinetics. Understanding these rates is crucial for predicting the persistence of contaminants, designing effective remediation strategies, and establishing safe environmental discharge limits111.

The ability to accurately model and predict behavior based on first-order kinetics empowers scientists and engineers to control processes, optimize outcomes, and ensure safety and efficiency in a wide array of applications. The inherent simplicity and widespread applicability make the study and understanding of first-order kinetics a cornerstone of quantitative scientific inquiry112.

Conclusion

This study developed a simple and self-powered catalytic photomicroelectrolysis (CPMC) system by integrating MgAl-LDH@TiO₂ nanocomposite for TC degradation. The synergy between microelectrolysis, photocatalysis, and internal galvanic coupling greatly enhanced redox activity, achieving 98% TC removal under optimal conditions (Time:55 min, C/Fe : 1:1, TC:100 mg L⁻¹). Mechanistic analysis revealed that Fe anodes generate Fe²⁺ ions and •OH radicals, while photogenerated electrons from TiO₂ accelerate Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺ reduction, sustaining a self‑regenerative Fenton‑like loop without external H₂O₂. This closed redox cycle boosts mineralization efficiency with minimal energy and reagent input. The process operates exclusively via microgalvanic potential and a 20 W UV-C source, and its energy consumption is estimated to be less than 0.3 kWh/h, significantly less than conventional electro-Fenton systems. Thus, the proposed reactor offers faster kinetics and higher efficiency using inexpensive materials and no external voltage.

From an industrial perspective, the use of low-cost, abundant materials (iron, carbon, and TiO₂) and the absence of external energy input make the CPMC system suitable for decentralized and large-scale wastewater applications. However, several limitations remain. Catalyst recovery and reusability were not performed in this study, constituting a key drawback that must be addressed to assess long-term stability and economic viability. Additionally, catalyst leaching, performance under real wastewater matrices, and continuous-flow scalability require further validation.

Overall, this research introduces a self-powered, hybrid catalytic system that bridges microelectrolysis and photocatalysis into a unified process capable of achieving fast, efficient, and environmentally benign degradation of antibiotic contaminants. The findings provide both mechanistic and engineering insights toward the development of practical, low-energy, and sustainable wastewater treatment technologies ready for pilot and industrial implementation.
