Gastrointestinal Side Effects of Low-Dose Oral Minoxidil in Patients with Alopecia: An Underrecognized Association
Authors: João Soares, Manuel Martínez-Sellés, Rui Oliveira Soares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLow-dose oral minoxidil (LDOM) has become an increasingly used therapy for alopecia. While dose-dependent hypertrichosis is the most common side effect, gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects are plausible due to smooth muscle relaxation, but they have not been systematically reported in clinical studies.ObjectivesTo investigate the frequency and characteristics of GI adverse effects in patients treated with LDOM for alopecia.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study at a trichology unit including patients attending their second consultation after initiating oral minoxidil between May and August 2025. A structured questionnaire assessed new GI symptoms, temporal relationship with treatment, and concomitant medication use.ResultsAmong 302 patients (218 females, 84 males; mean age 46 years), 22 (7.3%) reported new GI symptoms. The most frequent symptoms were flatulence (N=18), heartburn (N=13), abdominal distension (N=12), abdominal pain (N=10), and aerophagia (N=7). Introduction

Oral minoxidil is increasingly prescribed in dermatology as an off-label treatment for different types of alopecia, including androgenetic alopecia and scarring alopecias [1]. At low doses (0.25–5 mg/day), it has demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety profile. Dose-dependent hirsutism is the most common side effect [2–6].

From a pharmacological standpoint, minoxidil is a potent arterial vasodilator that acts by opening ATP-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to membrane hyperpolarization and muscle relaxation [5]. While this mechanism explains its antihypertensive properties, it also suggests potential effects on gastrointestinal smooth muscle and esophageal sphincters, theoretically predisposing to symptoms such as aerophagia, abdominal distension, pyrosis, flatulence, and abdominal pain.

Pharmacologically-induced aerophagia, abdominal pain and distension, and flatulence have been reported with several drug classes. Agents that lower esophageal sphincter tone, such as calcium channel blockers [7] and nitrates [8,9], may predispose to gastroesophageal reflux and aerophagia [10]. Metformin and other antidiabetic agents frequently cause abdominal bloating and flatulence through increased intestinal gas production [11]. Proton pump inhibitors and antibiotics can also lead to dysbiosis, contributing to abdominal distension and gas-related complaints [12]. These examples highlight that drug-related aerophagia and functional GI symptoms are not uncommon in clinical practice, even if they have not been systematically reported with minoxidil.

Despite the plausible mechanism, clinical studies to date have not systematically reported gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects. The largest multicenter cohort (N=1404) and several meta-analyses identified hypertrichosis, edema, lightheadedness, and tachycardia as the most common events, but no GI symptom was highlighted [2]. A recent in silico toxicology analysis predicted a high probability of GI system involvement with minoxidil [13], yet this was not corroborated in clinical cohorts [2–6].

Thus, while the pharmacological basis for GI involvement exists, and computational models support this risk, there remains a gap in real-world clinical evidence.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine the frequency and clinical characteristics of new-onset gastrointestinal symptoms in patients receiving low-dose oral minoxidil for alopecia, with particular attention to aerophagia. Secondary objectives were to characterize the temporal relationship between these symptoms and treatment — specifically resolution after discontinuation and recurrence after reintroduction — and to explore associations between gastrointestinal complaints and daily minoxidil dose, sex, and concomitant use of vasodilator drugs.

Methods

Study Population and Data Collection

We conducted an observational prospective study at the Trichology Unit of Centro de Dermatologia de Lisboa, focusing on patients who had recently started oral minoxidil who were attending their second trichology consultation between 20 May and 20 August 2025. Three inclusion criteria were applied: i) patients prescribed oral minoxidil for alopecia; ii) only those at their second visit were considered, as patients treated for longer periods might not reliably recall early symptoms; and iii) observation within the predefined 3-month recruitment period. All consecutive patients with any type of alopecia presenting to the clinic and receiving low-dose oral minoxidil were eligible for inclusion, without restriction to a specific alopecia subtype. Oral minoxidil was generally prescribed as a once-daily dose, most commonly taken in the morning directly at intended dose, in accordance with routine clinical practice.

At the end of the consultation, participants were invited to complete a structured paper questionnaire (Table 1) after providing written informed consent (File S1). The questionnaire collected demographic data (age, sex), treatment details (type of alopecia, dose of oral minoxidil, treatment duration), and the presence of new-onset gastrointestinal symptoms since initiation of therapy. Symptoms specifically assessed were aerophagia, abdominal distension, flatulence, heartburn, and abdominal pain, with additional space for free-text reporting. Patients were asked whether symptoms resolved upon discontinuation of minoxidil and recurred after reintroduction, as well as to list all ongoing concomitant medications in use.

Statistical Analyses

Quantitative variables are expressed as mean with standard deviation (SD); qualitative variables are expressed as number and percentage. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

All statistical analyses were performed using PASW Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA).

All patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 302 patients were included, of whom 218 were female (72.2%) and 84 were male (27.8%) (Table 2). The mean age was 46 years (range 12–88; 44 in females, 51 in males). The mean daily dose of oral minoxidil was 0.7 mg in females (range 0.25–2.25 mg) and 3.2 mg in males (range 1.25–10 mg). The indications for oral minoxidil were androgenetic alopecia (N=215, 71%), alopecia areata (N=18, 6%), lichen planopilaris (N=20, 7%), frontal fibrosing alopecia (N=28, 9%), and fibrosing alopecia in a pattern distribution (FAPD/FADP) (N=21, 7%). Thirty-nine patients were receiving concomitant vasodilator therapy, including nitrates (N=4), amlodipine (N=10), and nifedipine (N=25). In addition to vasodilators, other commonly used medications included proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole and related compounds, N=19), anxiolytics or hypnotics (N=17), and lipid-lowering agents (N=12). No supplement with known gastrointestinal effects was overrepresented among patients reporting gastrointestinal symptoms.

Overall, 22 patients (7.3%) developed new gastrointestinal complaints after starting oral minoxidil (Figure 1). Flatulence was the most frequent symptom (18 patients), followed by heartburn (13), abdominal distension (12), abdominal pain (10) and aerophagia (7). Most patients reported more than one symptom, and two described diarrhea that disappeared when minoxidil was discontinued and recurred after reintroduction. Among the 16 patients who interrupted therapy, 15 experienced complete resolution of symptoms, while 14 of 15 reported recurrence on restarting minoxidil. Six patients chose to maintain treatment despite persistence of gastrointestinal complaints.

Patients with gastrointestinal symptoms received higher mean doses than those without (2.7 ± 1.6 mg vs. 1.3 ± 1.2 mg, P=0.01, Figure 2). This association was present in both sexes: in males, 3.9 mg vs. 3.1 mg (P=0.02), and in females, 1.0 mg vs. 0.7 mg (P<0.001). Gastrointestinal adverse effects were more common in males than in females (15.5% vs. 4.1%, P<0.001). Concomitant vasodilator therapy was also associated with a higher frequency of complaints (Figure 3), reported in 10 of 39 patients on vasodilators (25.6%) compared with 12 of 263 patients not receiving them (4.6%, P<0.001).

Discussion

In this cohort of over 300 patients treated with low-dose oral minoxidil for alopecia, we identified a spectrum of gastrointestinal complaints, including eructation/aerophagia, abdominal distension, flatulence, heartburn, and abdominal pain. To our knowledge, these symptoms have not been systematically reported in prior clinical studies of oral minoxidil [2–4].

The temporal relationship (resolution on discontinuation and recurrence upon rechallenge) supports a causal association between minoxidil and these GI effects. Importantly, a dose-response gradient was observed: patients with symptoms received higher mean doses than those without, and events were more frequent in males, who generally required higher doses. Concomitant use of vasodilators further increased risk, suggesting a pharmacological interaction.

The mechanism is biologically plausible. Minoxidil acts by opening ATP-sensitive potassium channels in smooth muscle cells, inducing relaxation [5]. While this underlies its vasodilatory and antihypertensive properties, it may also affect gastrointestinal smooth muscle or esophageal sphincters, predisposing to aerophagia, abdominal distension, and pyrosis. An in silico toxicology study previously predicted gastrointestinal involvement, although clinical cohorts had not confirmed this risk [13]. Our findings fill this gap, bridging theoretical prediction with real-world evidence.

From a clinical standpoint, the GI effects observed were generally mild, and only a minority of patients chose to discontinue therapy forever. Nonetheless, these symptoms can impact quality of life and adherence, particularly as oral minoxidil is often prescribed for cosmetic indications in otherwise healthy individuals. Dose titration may represent a strategy to improve tolerability and could be explored in future studies

Limitations include the reliance on self-reported symptoms and the single-center design over a three-month recruitment period. Preexisting gastrointestinal disorders were not systematically recorded or excluded, which may have influenced symptom reporting. The questionnaire did not capture the precise timing of gastrointestinal symptom onset between visits, precluding a formal time-to-onset analysis. In addition, the dose and clinical indication for concomitant vasodilator therapy were not systematically recorded, preventing dose-effect or indication-specific analyses. The strengths of the study include the systematic prospective assessment using a structured questionnaire, the large sample size, and the demonstration of challenge–dechallenge–rechallenge relationships.

Conclusion

Gastrointestinal complaints, especially aerophagia, abdominal pain and distension, and flatulence, represent a previously underrecognized adverse effect of low-dose oral minoxidil. Clinicians should proactively ask about bloating, belching, or distension during follow-up, particularly at higher doses and in patients on concomitant vasodilators. Further multicenter and mechanistic studies are warranted to confirm prevalence and clarify pathophysiology.
