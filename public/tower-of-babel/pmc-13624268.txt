Temporal lag in post-mining regrowth amplifies carbon loss in tropical forests during the period 2000–2023
Authors: Tingting He, Fashuai Li, Yihua Hu, He Ren, Yanling Zhao, Zhipei Sun, Jiale Chen, Yuwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMining-induced deforestation is an under-recognized source of tropical forest carbon loss, and the extent to which post-mining regrowth offsets this loss remains unclear. Here, we reconstruct mining-related deforestation and subsequent forest regrowth across the tropics from 2000 to 2023 using high-resolution satellite observations. We estimate that mining causes ~4537 km2 of deforestation, whereas post-mining regrowth covers ~300 km2, equivalent to 6.6% of the deforested area. Forest regrowth also exhibits a pronounced temporal lag, with ~40% occurring more than five years after deforestation and ~16% occurring at least a decade later. Mining-related deforestation results in ~47.5 Tg C of carbon loss, while regrowth contributes ~3.8 Tg C of carbon gain, offsetting 8% of losses. These findings indicate that post-mining forest regrowth provides a limited carbon offset, highlighting the need to improve both the extent and timeliness of forest recovery in mining-affected tropical forests.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Environmental impact, Forestry

Introduction

Tropical forests store approximately 50% of the carbon contained in global vegetation1–3, yet they are under increasing pressure from human activities. Tropical deforestation has continued over the past two decades, driven by both large-scale land investments and utilization4,5, as well as smaller-scale activities such as gold mining6 and smallholder clearing7. Compared with deforestation associated with agricultural commodities, livestock, cash crops, and plantations8–11, forest loss related to mining has received considerably less attention. Mining activities frequently occur in forested landscapes12,13, and rising demand for minerals has further intensified pressure on these ecosystems despite increasing conservation and restoration commitments. Recent studies have documented direct mining-related deforestation across the tropics14–16. For example, one pantropical analysis estimated that industrial mining caused more than 3200 km2 of forest loss between 2000 and 201916. Mining can also generate indirect forest loss through infrastructure development, road expansion, and associated land-use change, with impacts extending well beyond mine sites17. A recent study estimated that indirect deforestation associated with mining-related land-use expansion may substantially exceed direct forest loss15.

Deforestation releases carbon stored in vegetation and reduces the carbon sequestration capacity of terrestrial ecosystems18. Although carbon uptake by global forests remains substantial, two-thirds of this carbon sink has been offset by tropical deforestation19. Continued forest loss could eventually shift tropical forests from a carbon sink to a carbon source3. The Carbon consequences of mining has received increasing attention20–24, yet the contribution of mining-related forest loss to tropical carbon dynamics remains insufficiently quantified. Mining-related deforestation immediately reduces aboveground carbon stocks and, when forest regrowth is delayed, also results in foregone carbon sequestration during the post-disturbance period (Supplementary Fig. 1). Forest regrowth through natural regeneration or active restoration can partially offset these carbon losses, but its spatial extent, recovery timing, and carbon contribution remain poorly constrained. Quantifying both carbon losses from mining-related deforestation and carbon gains from subsequent forest regrowth is therefore essential for evaluating the carbon consequences of mining in tropical forests.

Here, we quantify mining-related deforestation, associated aboveground biomass carbon loss, and carbon gains from post-mining forest regrowth across the tropics (23.5°N–23.5°S) during 2000–2023. We combine global mining datasets25,26 with Landsat time-series observations, the Continuous Change Detection and Classification (CCDC) algorithm27, the Global Forest Change (GFC) dataset28, and global forest canopy height product29,30 to reconstruct high-resolution mining-related forest cover dynamics. This framework helps address the incompleteness of existing mining datasets, which are primarily derived from visual interpretation of satellite imagery and may fail to capture historical forest cover changes due to the timing of image acquisition. We further estimate aboveground carbon losses using global biomass and forest carbon removal datasets31, and calculate carbon gains from forest regrowth using an established biomass recovery equation32. Our results show that post-mining forest regrowth covers only 6.6% of the deforested area, with more than 40% occurring over 5 years after deforestation. Forest regrowth contributes 3.8 Tg C of carbon gain, offsetting less than 8% of total losses. Given the limited extent and delayed timing of post-mining regrowth, our results highlight the importance of early-stage conservation actions during mining operations. Rather than relying solely on afforestation and other offsetting measures33,34, the mining sector should accelerate its transition towards a nature-positive pathway35–37, thereby supporting broader ecosystem integrity and long-term carbon sink capacity.

Results

Mining-related tropical deforestation

Mining caused an estimated ~4537 km2 of deforestation across the tropics during 2000–2023 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Our mapping approach detected larger deforested areas than estimates based solely on existing mining datasets, with increases ranging from 7% to 91% (~293–2162 km2; Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Indonesia was the largest contributor to mining-related deforestation, accounting for nearly half (~45%) of the tropical total. Extensive forest loss was also observed in South America, particularly in Brazil, Peru, Guyana, and Suriname, each exceeding 100 km2 of deforestation (Fig. 1b). Annual mining-related deforestation increased significantly over the study period (MK test, Z = 3.48, p < 0.001, Sen’s slope = 10.67 km2 year−1, 95% CI [6.33, 14.73]; Fig. 1c). Although annual deforestation declined during the most recent 5 years, the average annual loss remained ~227 km2, nearly 25 times higher than in 2001. We further quantified the annual proportion of mining-related vegetation loss occurring in forests (Proportion_def). Vegetation loss was derived from time-series normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) observations and included all surface vegetation types (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 4). We found that Proportion_def increased steadily throughout the study period (MK test, Z = 6.07, p < 0.001, Sen’s slope = 2.5% year−1, 95% CI [2.39, 2.71]), exceeding 50% on average during the past 3 years (Fig. 1c). This increase occurred despite an overall decline in annual vegetation loss (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that mining-related vegetation loss increasingly occurred in forests.

Limited post-mining forest regrowth

Across the tropics, post-mining forest regrowth covered ~300 km2 during 2000–2023 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with estimates based solely on existing mining datasets, our approach detected 23–113% more forest regrowth (~57–159 km2; Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Forest regrowth nevertheless remained limited across the tropics. On average, only 6.6% of total mining-related deforestation recovered during the study period (Recovery_rate). Among the 15 countries with the greatest mining-related deforestation, only five exceeded this average (Ghana, Colombia, the Philippines, India, and Vietnam; Fig. 1d), whereas the remaining ten countries, which together accounted for ~80% of total deforestation, all remained below the tropical average. Consistent with this pattern, only four countries (Indonesia, Ghana, Peru, and Brazil) recorded more than 10 km2 of total post-mining forest regrowth (Supplementary Table 2). In several countries, Recovery_rate was less than half the tropical average, whereas annual deforestation occurred at rates more than 100 times greater than forest regrowth (Supplementary Fig. 6). Despite the limited recovery extent, cumulative forest regrowth during 2013–2023 (~186 km2) still exceeded that during 2000–2010 (~81 km2; Fig. 1e). We further quantified the annual proportion of mining-related vegetation regrowth occurring as forest regrowth (Proportion_reg). Vegetation regrowth was derived from time-series NDVI observations and included all surface vegetation types (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 4). This proportion declined steadily throughout the study period (MK test, Z = −4.62, p < 0.001, Sen’s slope = −0.58% y−1, 95% CI [−0.80, −0.38]), averaging 5.3% during the most recent 5 years. Despite continued increases in vegetation regrowth, annual forest regrowth declined, particularly in Tropical Americas and Tropical Africa (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Carbon losses from mining-related deforestation

We estimated that mining-related deforestation resulted in ~43.7 Tg C of net carbon during 2000–2023 (Fig. 2a). Net carbon loss was spatially concentrated in regions where extensive deforestation coincided with limited forest regrowth, including Amazonia, Central Africa, and Southeast Asia (Fig. 2b–d). Post-mining forest regrowth contributed only 3.8 Tg C of carbon gain, offsetting 8% of the total carbon loss (~47.5 Tg C; Fig. 2e). The annual increase in carbon gain was 0.02 Tg C year−1, approximately one-tenth of the annual rate of carbon loss (Supplementary Fig. 7). The temporal interval between deforestation and the first observed forest regrowth (Diff_year) revealed a pronounced temporal lag in post-mining forest regrowth. Across the tropics, only ~46% of forest regrowth occurred within 3 years after deforestation, whereas ~30% occurred more than 6 years later and ~16% occurred ten or more years later (Fig. 3a). This pattern was observed not only in countries with relatively low forest loss (<3 km2; e.g., Guatemala, Uganda, Ecuador, Nigeria) but also in countries with extensive deforestation (Supplementary Table 3). Among the countries with extensive deforestation, more than half of forest regrowth occurred more than 3 years after deforestation, with 13.5–64.5% occurring 10 or more years later (Supplementary Fig. 8). Although the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Suriname exhibited relatively earlier regrowth, with ~70% occurring within 6 years, their Recovery_rate remained below 2%. Delayed forest regrowth tended to occur in forests with aboveground biomass densities exceeding the corresponding national averages (Fig. 3b). This temporal lag resulted in an accumulated carbon sequestration loss of 5.3 Tg C, equivalent to approximately 12% of the direct carbon stock loss from mining-related deforestation.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive quantification of mining-related forest cover dynamics and associated carbon changes across the tropics from 2000 to 2023. A key contribution is the development of a pixel-level framework that detects both mining-related deforestation and subsequent forest regrowth. This framework addresses limitations in existing global mining datasets, which are commonly derived from visual interpretation of satellite imagery and constrained by the timing of image acquisition. Consequently, direct use of these datasets may overlook historical forest cover changes within and adjacent to mine footprints. Our results show that the proposed framework substantially improves the detection of both deforestation and forest regrowth compared with methods relying solely on existing mining datasets (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although differences in forest loss detection criteria resulted in some deviations from a previous pantropical assessment16 at the national level (Supplementary Fig. 9), the total deforested area estimated here differed by less than 4%, supporting the robustness of the overall spatial and temporal patterns.

Post-mining forest regrowth occurred throughout the study period but remained limited relative to deforestation. Across the tropics, the average Recovery_rate was only 6.6%, and several South American countries (e.g., Brazil, Peru, Suriname, Guyana) had recovery rates below half of the tropical average. By contrast, higher recovery rates (>20%) were observed in countries such as Ghana, Panama, and Gabon, with Gabon reaching ~49% (Supplementary Table 2). These differences suggest that recovery outcomes vary substantially among countries, potentially reflecting differences in restoration policies, governance capacity and post-mining land management. Forest regrowth was not limited in extent but also delayed in timing. More than 40% of forest regrowth occurred more than 5 years after deforestation, particularly in countries with extensive forest loss.

Several factors may contribute to the limited extent and delayed timing of post-mining forest regrowth. Active mining operations often continue until resource depletion or mine closure, limiting opportunities for vegetation recovery within operational areas. In addition, mining landscapes commonly include waste rock dumps, tailing dams, pits, and disturbed buffer zones that extend beyond actively extracted areas. Although some of these areas may offer opportunities for phased or concurrent restoration, implementation is frequently constrained by land tenure, operational requirements, and regulatory frameworks. Restoration can also be delayed or remain incomplete because of funding limitations, insufficient technical capacity, and inconsistent restoration policies or enforcement38–41. These challenges are likely to be particularly pronounced for artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM)42, which is widespread across the tropics but often informal, poorly documented and difficult to monitor43,44. Because such activities are difficult to identify consistently from visual interpretation and lack a consistent definition27, they were not included in our assessment. Our estimates therefore probably underestimate forest loss and carbon impacts associated with informal mining.

Mining-related deforestation caused approximately 47.5 Tg C of carbon loss across tropical forests during the study period. Although this amount is small relative to the total tropical forest carbon3, mining can generate disproportionately large local carbon impacts and may contribute to additional indirect forest loss through roads, infrastructure development, and associated land-use change15–17. Furthermore, our assessment focused on aboveground biomass carbon and therefore did not include potential losses from belowground biomass, dead organic matter, or soil carbon. This limitation is particularly important in tropical peatlands, where substantial carbon stocks are stored below ground45. By overlaying mining-related deforestation with the global peatland distribution, we identified ~222 km2 of forest loss within peatland areas, primarily in Borneo, northern Peru, and Amazonia (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 4). Although this represents only a small fraction of total mining-related forest loss, peatland disturbance may increase fire risk and associated carbon emissions46–48. Consequently, estimates based solely on aboveground biomass are likely to underestimate the full carbon consequences of mining.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. We defined forest loss occurring within or near mapped mining polygons as mining-related deforestation, which introduces uncertainty in both attribution and timing. Because the mining polygons26,27 represent cumulative spatial footprints rather than temporally resolved mining activity, forest loss detected within these areas, particularly during the early study period, cannot always be definitively attributed to mining. Although we applied a 5-km buffer together with a spatial connectivity constraint to strengthen the spatial association between detected forest loss and mining footprints, other concurrent land-use drivers cannot be fully excluded. Our estimates should therefore be interpreted as forest loss spatially associated with mine sites and may overestimate mining-induced deforestation in some locations. In addition, we need to clarify the definitions adopted in our assessment of forest cover dynamics. Our assessment focuses on deforestation, defined as a pixel-level transition from forest to bare ground, and does not detect indirect deforestation or forest degradation. Forest regrowth was defined as the reappearance of forest cover in previously deforested areas, regardless of whether recovery occurred through natural regeneration, reforestation, or plantation establishment. We therefore do not assess the ecological functionality or structural integrity of regrowing forests.

Our assessment is further constrained by the limited availability of information on mineral types, geological setting, or mining techniques associated with the mapped mining polygons. We were unable to evaluate how differences in mining methods (e.g., strip mining, underground mining) or resource depletion timelines influence the feasibility of timely forest regrowth. However, this limitation does not affect the observed temporal lag in post-mining forest regrowth, which was quantified directly from annual forest cover dynamics. Moreover, mining footprints often extend beyond actively extracted areas to include waste rock dumps, tailing facilities, pits, and disturbed buffer zones, some of which may provide opportunities for phased or concurrent restoration. The ecological risks associated with prolonged disturbance further emphasize the importance of earlier and more consistent restoration planning.

Uncertainties associated with mining-related carbon accounting should also be acknowledged. Different modeling frameworks were used to estimate carbon loss and carbon gain. Direct carbon stock loss from deforestation was estimated by assuming that aboveground biomass carbon was completely removed in the year of forest clearance. Forgone carbon sequestration was estimated using temporally averaged global forest carbon removal data (2001–2023)31, assuming a constant annual sequestration rate. This simplification may introduce biases because sequestration rates vary among recovery stages, forest types, and environmental conditions. Carbon gain from forest regrowth was estimated using an empirical biomass recovery function based on field observations32, assuming a gradual recovery trajectory of forest biomass. These assumptions introduce structural uncertainty into estimates of net carbon balance. However, given the current lack of temporally continuous biomass datasets, such simplifications remain necessary for large-scale assessments of carbon dynamics. Second, the use of different biomass datasets may also affect carbon accounting results. We used a static biomass map31 to estimate direct carbon stock loss and assumed that biomass remains stable without mining disturbance throughout the study period. Although biomass may vary over time because of natural regrowth and climatic influences49–51, the consistency of carbon loss estimates across different biomass maps31,52–54 increased confidence in the robustness of our results (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 5). In addition, the biomass recovery function was primarily derived from field observations in regenerating forests in Latin America and may therefore introduce regional uncertainty when applied across the tropics. This limitation may be particularly relevant in Southeast Asia, where plantation-based restoration is widespread55 and early-stage carbon gains may be underestimated. Nevertheless, our results reveal the spatial patterns and temporal dynamics of mining-related carbon loss and carbon gain. Future studies should integrate in situ observations, LiDAR data, and high-resolution satellite imagery to improve estimates of spatial variation in biomass loss and recovery.

Mining and infrastructure development are closely linked to economic opportunities in many tropical countries, making broad restrictions on mining neither practical nor desirable56. Although increased afforestation efforts contribute to forest recovery, large-scale tree planting post-mining may face high costs and risks of reducing forest biodiversity55. Therefore, it is essential to pursue more scientifically grounded and context-appropriate strategies to reconcile economic development with mining related-forest conservation. Such strategies may include offset interventions, incentive-based ecological restoration, and planning approaches that prioritize conservation-sensitive mining practices33,44,57.

Methods

Methods summary

We developed a pixel-level framework that integrates Landsat satellite observations, the Continuous Change Detection and Classification (CCDC) algorithm27, the Global Forest Change (GFC) dataset28, and global forest canopy height products29,30 to quantify mining-related forest cover dynamics across the tropics (23.5°N–23.5°S). The framework combined existing global mining datasets25,26 with forest cover change mapping to identify mining-related deforestation and subsequent forest regrowth during 2000–2023. Annual forest cover dynamics were further linked with pixel-level carbon accounting31,32 to estimate carbon losses from deforestation and carbon gains from forest regrowth. A detailed description of the methods was provided below.

Mine site determination

We used two publicly available global mining polygon datasets developed by refs. 25,26 and hereafter referred to as Maus’s25 and Tang’s26 datasets, respectively, to determine mine site locations. Both datasets were generated through visual interpretation of satellite imagery and delineate aboveground mining features, including open pits, tailing dams, waste rock dumps, and processing infrastructure. Across the tropics, Tang’s dataset contains approximately 311% more mining polygons than Maus’s dataset but covers ~93% of its total mapped area, primarily because of differences in boundary delineation criteria. To maximize spatial coverage, we used the union of the two datasets and restricted the analysis to tropical regions (23.5°N–23.5°S). Because mining areas are dynamic and may undergo expansion, abandonment, and reclamation, these datasets represent cumulative visible mining footprints at the time of image acquisition rather than the complete history of mining activities. They may therefore fail to capture historical forest cover changes that occurred before image acquisition or in areas adjacent to mapped mining features. In particular, vegetation-covered areas surrounding mine sites may not be classified as mining features even when they represent post-mining revegetation areas26. To address this limitation, we developed a pixel-level framework based on Landsat time-series observations and change detection algorithms to identify forest cover changes spatially associated with mining, as described in the following section. We further compared forest cover change estimates derived from alternative mining footprint definitions, including Maus’s dataset alone, Tang’s dataset alone, their intersection, and their union, to evaluate how mining footprint definition influenced the estimated extent of deforestation and forest regrowth (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Satellite images collection

We used Landsat 5 Thematic Mapper (TM), Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM+), and Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) surface reflectance data to map tropical forest cover change. The Landsat series provides global observations at 30 m spatial resolution with a 16-day revisit interval, making it suitable for large-scale spatiotemporal analysis. Landsat 5 TM and Landsat 7 ETM+ surface reflectance products were atmospherically corrected using the Landsat Ecosystem Disturbance Adaptive Processing System (LEDAPS)58, whereas Landsat 8 OLI surface products were processed using the Landsat Surface Reflectance Code (LaSRC)59. Cloud, cloud shadow, and snow pixels were masked using the CFMASK algorithm60. All Landsat surface reflectance data were obtained from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform61. To ensure data quality, we retained all scenes acquired between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2023 with scene-level cloud cover below 80%.

Mining-related forest cover change mapping

Following the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), deforestation refers to the conversion of forest to non-forest land use, accompanied by a reduction in forest cover. Here, mining-related deforestation was defined as forest loss occurring within mapped mine sites or in spatially connected areas surrounding mine sites. Post-mining forest regrowth was defined as the reestablishment of forest cover in areas previously identified as mining-related deforestation. Existing global mining polygon datasets are derived from visual interpretation of satellite imagery25,26 and represent cumulative visible mining footprints at the image acquisition. They may therefore omit historical forest cover changes that occurred before mapping or in revegetated areas adjacent to mine sites. To address this limitation, we developed a pixel-level framework to map annual mining-related forest cover change from 2000 to 2023 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The framework consisted of two steps. First, we detected vegetation cover changes using Landsat time-series observations and the Continuous Change Detection and Classification (CCDC) algorithm27. Vegetation was identified using a satellite-derived vegetation index and included all vegetated land-cover types, including forests, shrubs, grasslands, and croplands. We then combined the vegetation change results with forest cover information from the Global Forest Change (GFC, v1.11) dataset28 and global forest canopy height product29,30 to identify mining-related deforestation and post-mining forest regrowth. Forest cover change was distinguished from broader vegetation cover change using tree cover and canopy height criteria.

Annual NDVI composites were generated using the maximum value composite method for 2000–2023, and pixels with annual NDVI greater than 0.4 were classified as vegetation. We further used the CCDC algorithm27 to detect temporal changes in vegetation cover. CCDC is a widely used land change monitoring algorithm specifically designed for time-series satellite data. It fits harmonic regression models to the temporal series to segment time periods and identify breaks indicative of land use change. Vegetation cover changes detected within mine site polygons were defined as mining-related vegetation cover changes. To capture historical vegetation loss or regrowth that may have occurred before or beyond the mapped mining footprint, we also analyzed vegetation cover changes within a 5-km buffer surrounding each mine site polygon. Buffer-based changes were retained only when spatially connected to changes detected within mining polygons. This connectivity constraint was used to strengthen the spatial association with mining footprints and reduce interference from unrelated land-use changes. Systematic validation across all mine site polygons confirmed that a 5-km buffer was sufficient to capture mining-related vegetation cover dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 12). For CCDC algorithm parameterization, we used an NDVI change threshold of 0.3. A decrease in NDVI greater than 0.3 between temporal segments was classified as vegetation loss, whereas an increase greater than 0.3 was classified as vegetation regrowth. When multiple temporal breaks were detected for a pixel, the first break corresponding to vegetation loss was used to determine the year of vegetation loss. Vegetation regrowth was identified from subsequent increases in NDVI following the detected vegetation loss.

For forest cover change detection, we used the GFC dataset to distinguish forest cover change from broader vegetation change. The GFC tree canopy cover layer for 2000 (treecover2000) was combined with CCDC-derived vegetation loss to generate annual maps of mining-related deforestation from 2001 to 2023. Post-mining forest regrowth was assessed from 2002 to 2023 because regrowth was defined only after a pixel had first been identified as mining-related deforestation, thereby allowing at least one annual interval between forest loss and subsequent recovery. Because the GFC dataset does not provide a tree canopy cover layer for 2023, we generated a 2023 forest cover layer (treecover2023) by combining CCDC-derived vegetation regrowth with global forest canopy height data29,30. The global forest canopy height product was generated by integrating GEDI-derived canopy height metrics with Landsat multi-temporal surface reflectance data, with a spatial resolution of ~30 m and reliable global accuracy29. Following the forest definition of the GFC dataset, pixels were classified as forest when vegetation height exceeded 5 m and canopy cover was at least 30%. We first intersected the 2020 global forest canopy height product with the GFC 2000 tree cover layer to derive forest cover in 2020. To capture forest regrowth after 2020, we applied the approach proposed by ref. 30 to map canopy height in previously deforested areas for 2023 and used the same forest definition. The resulting 2023 forest cover layer was then combined with CCDC-derived vegetation regrowth to generate annual maps of post-mining forest regrowth.

A total of 1200 deforestation pixels and 440 forest regrowth pixels were selected, including 50 deforestation pixels and 20 forest regrowth pixels sampled for each year. We used a simplified carbon accounting framework integrating a global aboveground live woody biomass density map31, the global forest carbon removal data31, and an empirical biomass recovery function32. Direct carbon stock loss from mining-related deforestation was estimated by combining annual deforestation maps with the global aboveground live woody biomass density map. We assumed that aboveground biomass would have remained unchanged in the absence of mining disturbance during the study period. For each deforested pixel x, we identified the year i and assumed that aboveground biomass carbon was fully removed in that year. To evaluate uncertainty in biomass estimates, we also used three additional biomass products52–54, hereafter referred to as Baccini’s, Saatchi’s, and Avitabile’s maps. Baccini’s map provides aboveground live woody biomass at 500 m resolution across the tropics. Saatchi’s map provides total forest carbon stock at 1000 m resolution across the tropics, and Avitabile’s map provides aboveground biomass at 1000 m resolution across tropical regions. To ensure comparability among datasets, national-scale carbon comparisons were restricted overlapping areas with valid values across all biomass products (Supplementary Table 5).
