Abstract
Recent developments in storage- especially in the area of resistive random access memory (ReRAM)- are attempting to scale the storage density by regarding the information data as two-dimensional (2D), instead of one-dimensional (1D). Correspondingly, new types of 2D constraints are introduced into the input information data to improve the system reliability. While 1D constraints have been extensively investigated in the literature, the study for 2D constraints is much less profound. Particularly, given a constraint ℱ \mathcal{F} and a design of 1D codes whose codewords satisfy ℱ \mathcal{F} , the problem of constructing efficient 2D codes, such that every row and every column in every codeword satisfy ℱ \mathcal{F} , has been a challenge. This work provides an efficient solution to the challenging coding problem above for the binary bounded-weight constrained codes that restrict the maximum number of 1 1 ’s (called weight ). Formally, we propose a universal framework to design 2D codes that guarantee the weight of every row and every column of length n n to be at most f ⁡ ( n ) f(n) for any given function f ⁡ ( n ) f(n) . We show that if there exists a design of capacity-approaching 1D codes, then our method also provides capacity-approaching 2D codes for all f = ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) f=\omega(\log n) .

III
A Universal Framework for the 2D Bounded-Weight Constraint

Suppose that there exists an 1D encoding map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for all
{\mathbfsl{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, the sequence
{\mathbfsl y}=\phi({\mathbfsl{x}})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is
f
-bounded, i.e.
{\rm wt}({\mathbfsl y})\leqslant f(n)
. For arrays of size
n\times n
, we set

c=\Big\lceil\frac{n}{f(n)}\Big\rceil\lceil\log n+6\rceil,\text{ and }m=n-c.

Our encoding algorithm works if
c<n
or
f(n)\gtrsim\log n+6
.

A high-level description of our encoder is as follows.

•

(Using 1D codes)
The encoder uses the map
\phi
to encode the first
m
rows to be
f
-bounded, and obtains an array
A
of size
m\times n
, whose weight is at most
\lfloor mf(n)\rfloor
.

•

(Divide-and-conquer procedure)
The encoder uses Lemma
2
to encode
A
to an array
B
of size
m\times n
where each row remains as a
f
-bounded sequence, and the weight of each column is at most
\lfloor mf(n)/n\rfloor
.

•

(Encoding the redundancy)
The encoder encodes the redundancy used in the divine-and-conquer procedure into an array
C
of size
c\times n
such that every row of
C
is
f
-bounded, while every column weights is at most
\lfloor cf(n)/n\rfloor
. Finally, the encoder outputs the concatenation of
B
and
C
, which is an array
D
of size
n\times n
. We observe that the weight of every column of
D
is at most

\displaystyle\lfloor mf(n)/n\rfloor+\lfloor cf(n)/n\rfloor

\displaystyle\leqslant\lfloor mf(n)/n+cf(n)/n\rfloor

\displaystyle=\lfloor f(n)\rfloor\leqslant f(n).

Thus, the output array
D\in{\mathcal{B}}(n;f)
.

The following results are crucial to the divide-and-conquer procedure. Lemma
1
is immediate.

.

Let
{\mathbfsl y}=y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{n}
and
{\mathbfsl{z}}=z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{n}
. Consider

\operatorname{wt}(\operatorname{Swap}_{t_{1}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}))=\alpha
and
\operatorname{wt}(\operatorname{Swap}_{t_{2}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}))=\beta

. For arbitrary integer

, there exists an index
t
between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
such that
\operatorname{wt}(\operatorname{Swap}_{t}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}))=\gamma
.

Lemma 2
.

Given
n>0
,
{\mathbfsl y},{\mathbfsl{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, where
\operatorname{wt}({\mathbfsl y})>\alpha
and
\operatorname{wt}({\mathbfsl{z}})<\alpha
. We then conclude:

i)

There exists

. Furthermore, if

-th position of
\operatorname{Swap}_{t}({\mathbfsl{z}},{\mathbfsl y})
is

.

ii)

Similarly, we have the following claim. There exists
t
,

, such that
\operatorname{wt}(\operatorname{Swap}_{t}({\mathbfsl y},{\mathbfsl{z}}))=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor

\operatorname{Swap}_{t}({\mathbfsl{z}},{\mathbfsl y})

Proof.

Due to space constraints, we only show the correctness of the first statement. The second statement can be proved similarly. Since
\operatorname{wt}({\mathbfsl y})>\alpha

\operatorname{wt}({\mathbfsl{z}})<\alpha

III-A

The Divide-and-Conquer Procedure: Main Idea

Suppose that at some encoding step
i
, we have an array
{\mathbfsl{S}}
containing
n_{i}
columns, and the overall weight
\operatorname{wt}({\mathbfsl{S}})\leqslant\lfloor n_{i}\alpha\rfloor
for some
\alpha>0
. In the initial step
i=0
, we have
n_{0}=n
.

•

(Case 1)
If
n_{i}
is even, the encoder divides
{\mathbfsl{S}}
into two subarrays
L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
and
R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
of equal size, each containing exactly
n_{i}/2
columns. The encoder then uses Lemma
2
to swap the bits between two subarrays until the weight of each subarray is at most
\lfloor(n_{i}/2)\alpha\rfloor
. The number of swapped bits, called
the swapping index
, will be recorded for the decoding procedure. In addition, a flipping action to convert a ‘1’ to a ‘0’ in one of the subarrays may be needed, and we refer it to as the
flipping index
.

•

(Case 2)
On the other hand, if
n_{i}
is odd, the encoder divides
{\mathbfsl{S}}
into:
L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
consisting of the first
(n_{i}+1)/2
columns, and
R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
consisting of the remaining
(n_{i}-1)/2
columns. To use Lemma
2
(which only applies to two sequences of equal length), the encoder removes one column from
L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
(the column’s index will be recorded, and referred to as the
excluding index
) and proceeds to the swapping algorithm. The goal is to ensure that the weight of
L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
is at most
\lfloor((n_{i}+1)/2)\alpha\rfloor
, while the weight of
R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
is at most
\lfloor((n_{i}-1)/2)\alpha\rfloor
.

Output of the divide-and-conquer procedure.
We observe that at every step
j
, for any new subarray consisting of
n_{j}
columns, its weight is enforced to be at most
\lfloor n_{j}\alpha\rfloor
. The procedure terminates when each subarray contains exactly one column, and the weight is at most
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
. Therefore, if the overall weight in the initial step of an array is at most
\lfloor mf(n)\rfloor
, then in the final step, the weight of every column is at most
\lfloor mf(n)/n\rfloor
. There are three types of indices:

•

Swapping index
(
t_{i}
): represents the number of swapped bits between
L_{S}
and
R_{S}
. Recall that all created subarrays have exactly
m
rows. If the array
{\mathbfsl{S}}
consists of
n_{i}
columns, we need
\lceil\log(mn_{i}/2)\rceil
bits to record
t_{i}
.

•

Flipping index
(
\tau_{i}
): indicates the need to transform a ‘1’ to a ‘0’ in one of the subarrays. Here,
\tau_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
. Particularly,
\tau_{i}=0
indicates that no flipping action is needed,
\tau_{i}=1
indicates that such a flipping action is done in the left subarray
L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
, while
\tau_{i}=2
indicates that the flipping action is done in the right subarray
R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
. Hence, two redundant bits are sufficient to record
\tau_{i}
.

•

Excluding index
(
\Gamma_{i}
): only be used when
n_{i}
is odd. In other words, if
n
is a power of
2
, then
\Gamma_{i}=0
for all
i
. This index indicates the column in
\mathbfsl{L}_{{\mathbfsl{S}}}
that would be excluded before we swap the bits. It is easy to see that we need
\lceil\log(n_{i}+1)/2\rceil
bits to record
\Gamma_{i}
.

The Divide-and-Conquer Procedure: Detailed Steps

Consider an array
{\mathbfsl{S}}

{\rm wt}(R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}})>(n_{i}/2)\alpha

\operatorname{Swap}_{t_{i}}(L_{{\mathbfsl{S}}},R_{{\mathbfsl{S}}})
and we have
\tau_{i}=1

t_{i}

III-C

Encoding The Redundancy

Let
{\rm Re}(n)
be the sequence obtained by concatenating the binary representations of all the indices
(\Gamma_{i},t_{i},\tau_{i})
from the divide-and-conquer procedure for a given array of size
m\times n
. For all integers
N
,
1\leqslant N\leqslant\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, we have:

\displaystyle|{\rm Re}(2N)|\leqslant 2\times|{\rm Re}(N)|+\lceil\log mN\rceil+2,\text{ and }

\displaystyle|{\rm Re}(2N+1)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|{\rm Re}(N+1)|+|{\rm Re}(N)|+\lceil\log mN\rceil+\lceil\log(N+1)\rceil+2.

Via mathematical induction, we can show that the size
|{\rm Re}(n)|\leqslant n(6+\log m)<n(6+\log n)
bits.

The encoder then encodes the
{\rm Re}(n)
into an array
C
of size
c\times n
. For simplicity, we assume that
\log n
is integer, and
\operatorname{Re}(n)=x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n(\log n+6)}
. We fill the bits
\operatorname{Re}(n)
into
C
as follows: in every row (or column) there are at least
(\lceil n/f(n)\rceil-1)
0’s between two consecutive bits in
\operatorname{Re}(n)
. Formally, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,
i=i_{0}\times\lceil n/f(n)\rceil+j
for some integers
i_{0},j
, where
1\leqslant j\leqslant\lceil n/f(n)\rceil
, we set the
i
th column:

\displaystyle C^{i}=

\displaystyle(0^{j-1}x_{(\log n+6)(i-1)+1}0^{\lceil\frac{n}{f(n)}\rceil-j})\ldots

\displaystyle\ldots(0^{j-1}x_{(\log n+6)i}0^{\lceil\frac{n}{f(n)}\rceil-j}),

where
0^{m}
denotes
m
consecutive 0’s. Clearly,

•

Every row of
C
is
f
-bounded.
The weight of every
\lceil n/f(n)\rceil
consecutive bits is at most one, and hence, the weight of each row of
C
is at most
n/\lceil n/f(n)\rceil\leqslant f(n)
.

•

Every column of
C
has weight at most
\lfloor cf(n)/n\rfloor
.
Similarly, since the weight of every
\lceil n/f(n)\rceil
consecutive bits is at most one, the weight of every column of size
c
of
C
is at most
c/\lceil n/f(n)\rceil\leqslant cf(n)/n
. Since the weight is an integer number, it is then less than
\lfloor cf(n)/n\rfloor
.

Finally, the encoder outputs the concatenation of
B
and
C
, which is an array
D
of size
n\times n
. Clearly, every row of
D
is
f
-bounded, while the weight of every column of
D
is at most

\displaystyle\lfloor mf(n)/n\rfloor+\lfloor cf(n)/n\rfloor

\displaystyle\leqslant\lfloor mf(n)/n+cf(n)/n\rfloor

\displaystyle=\lfloor f(n)\rfloor\leqslant f(n).

.
We now discuss the efficiency of our encoder. Let
{\bf c}_{f}
denote the channel capacity of the 1D constraint, i.e. given a function
f
, the weight of every codeword of length
n
is at most
f(n)
. Suppose that
{\bf r}_{\phi},{\bf Re}_{\phi}
are the rate and the redundancy of the capacity-approaching 1D encoding map
\phi
, respectively. Here,
{\bf r}_{\phi}=1-{\bf Re}_{\phi}/n
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\bf r}_{\phi}={\bf c}_{f}
.

Theorem 1
.

Our encoder provides capacity-approaching 2D codes for all
f=\omega(\log n)
.
In addition, the channel capacity of the 2D constraint is equal to the channel capacity of the 1D constraint. In other words, we have
{\bf cap}_{f}\equiv{\bf c}_{f}
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that the total redundancy for encoding an
n\times n
array is
(n-c){\bf Re}_{\phi}+cn
(bits), where
c=\lceil n/f(n)\rceil\lceil\log n+6\rceil
. For simplicity, we assume that
c=n(\log n+6)/f(n)
is an integer. The rate of our encoder, denoted by
{\bf r}_{{\textsc{Enc}}_{f}}
, is then

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{{\textsc{Enc}}_{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}-(n-c){\bf Re}_{\phi}-cn}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}-n{\bf Re}_{\phi}+c{\bf Re}_{\phi}-cn}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{{\bf Re}_{\phi}}{n}\Big)+\frac{{\bf Re}_{\phi}(\log n+6)}{nf(n)}-\frac{\log n+6}{f(n)}

\displaystyle={\bf r}_{\phi}+\frac{{\bf Re}_{\phi}(\log n+6)}{nf(n)}-\frac{\log n+6}{f(n)}.

For all
f=\omega(\log n)
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n+6}{f(n)}=0
. Therefore,
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\bf r}_{{\textsc{Enc}}_{f}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\bf r}_{\phi}={\bf c}_{f}
. We then conclude that our encoder provides capacity-approaching 2D codes for all
f=\omega(\log n)
, and
{\bf cap}_{f}\equiv{\bf c}_{f}
.
∎

IV
Conclusion

We have presented a universal framework to design 2D codes that guarantee the weight of every row and every column of length
n
to be at most
f(n)
for arbitrary function
f(n)
, through efficient encoding and decoding algorithms. We have shown that if a design of capacity-approaching 1D codes exists, then our encoder provides capacity-approaching 2D codes for all
f=\omega(\log n)
.
