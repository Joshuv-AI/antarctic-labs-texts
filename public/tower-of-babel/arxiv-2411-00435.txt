One for All: A Universal Quantum Conic Programming Framework for Hard-Constrained Combinatorial Optimization Problems
Lennart Binkowski, Tobias J. Osborne, Marvin Schwiering, René Schwonnek, Timo Ziegler

Abstract
We present a unified quantum-classical framework for addressing NP-complete constrained combinatorial optimization problems, generalizing the recently proposed Quantum Conic Programming (QCP) approach. Accordingly, it inherits many favorable properties of the original proposal such as mitigation of the effects of barren plateaus and avoidance of NP-hard parameter optimization. By collecting the entire classical feasibility structure in a single constraint, we enlarge QCP’s scope to arbitrary hard-constrained problems. Yet, we prove that the additional restriction is mild enough to still allow for an efficient parameter optimization via the formulation of a generalized eigenvalue problem (GEP) of adaptable dimension. Our rigorous proof further fills some apparent gaps in prior derivations of GEPs from parameter optimization problems. We further detail a measurement protocol for formulating the classical parameter optimization that does not require us to implement any (time evolution with a) problem-specific objective Hamiltonian or a quantum feasibility oracle. Lastly, we prove that, even under the influence of noise, QCP’s parameterized ansatz class always captures the optimum attainable within its generated subcone. All of our results hold true for arbitrarily-constrained combinatorial optimization problems.

I
Introduction

Optimization is a ubiquitous aspect of various disciplines, arising naturally in economics, society, and science.
Decades of advancements in electrical engineering and algorithmic design have pushed the frontier of what is feasible to compute and solve, to the point where even minute improvements often lead to meaningful gains.
One promising candidate for significant advancement is the development of quantum computers, where recent experimental milestones
Google Quantum
AI 2023
;
Evered et al. 2023
;
Bluvstein et al. 2024
;
Iqbal et al. 2024
;
da Silva et al. 2024
;
PsiQuantum
Team 2024
are expected to ultimately pave the way from the current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ)
Preskill 2018
devices to fault-tolerant, large-scale quantum processing units (QPUs).
Seminal works by
Grover 1997
,
Kadowaki and Nishimori 1998
, and
Farhi et al. 2000
have sparked lasting interest in quantum search and quantum optimization
Abbas et al. 2024
, leading to the proposal of frameworks like quantum branch-and-bound
Montanaro 2020
;
Ambainis and Kokainis 2017
;
Chakrabarti et al. 2022
, quantum dynamic programming
Ambainis et al. 2019
, and quantum solvers for semidefinite programs (SDPs)
Brandão and Svore 2017
;
van Apeldoorn et al. 2020
;
Brandão et al. 2019
.
The framework most suited for the NISQ era is often deemed to be variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Farhi et al. 2014
;
Hadfield et al. 2019
;
Cerezo et al. 2021a
, consisting of a parameterized quantum circuit (PQC)
V_{L}(\theta_{L})\cdots V_{1}(\theta_{1})
dependent on a series of parameters
\theta_{j}
to be optimized by a classical optimization algorithm.
The parameters are optimized in order to yield a high-quality approximation of a given target state, typically the ground state of a target Hamiltonian.
However, significant hurdles remain for the widespread application to classical optimization problems:
The design of PQCs that respect an optimization problem’s constraints is highly non-trivial and problem-specific
Hadfield et al. 2019
, the parameter optimization problem handed to the classical optimizer is often at least as hard as the original problem
Bittel and Kliesch 2021
, and VQAs often suffer from the barren plateau phenomenon
McClean et al. 2018
;
Campos et al. 2021
;
Cerezo et al. 2021b
;
Arrasmith et al. 2021
;
Ortiz Marrero et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
, where vanishing gradients slow or hinder convergence to an (even local) optimum.

In order to address these and other issues, we propose a natural generalization of Quantum Conic Programming (QCP), as introduced in
Binkowski et al. 2023
, by expanding QCP from a VQA subroutine for unconstrained optimization problems into a general framework for constrained combinatorial optimization problems.
QCP and similar frameworks
McClean et al. 2017
;
Huggins et al. 2020
;
Bharti et al. 2022
mitigate the effect of barren plateaus and avoid the overly complex parameter optimization tasks by utilizing non-unitary parameterized gates.
These gates are implemented via linear combinations of unitaries (LCU)
Childs and Wiebe 2012
;
Chakraborty 2023
,
the classical optimization task involves solving a generalized eigenvalue problem (GEP) or, more generally, an SDP.
In our generalization, we keep the fundamental design principles of QCP while extending its scope to constrained problems:
We enforce feasibility preservation of an LCU step by introducing an additional constraint to the associated parameter optimization problem; yet, under minimal assumptions, we ensure that the resulting optimization problem can again be reduced to a GEP without incurring additional sampling overhead for the QPU.
Moreover, we obtain a feasibility-preserving LCU operation even if the individual unitaries are not guaranteed to fully preserve feasibility, thus eliminating the need to handcraft problem-specific PQCs.

After reviewing fundamental notions of constrained combinatorial optimization problems, VQAs — in particular, the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
Farhi et al. 2014
and the Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz (QAOA)
Hadfield et al. 2019
—, and QCP in
Section II
, we present our general framework in
Section III
.
Here, we first discuss the incorporation of hard-constraints into the parameter optimization problem of QCP before detailing how to formulate the optimization task for a classical computer with the aid of moment matrices.
For the latter, we provide a specialized measurement protocol that fully utilizes the underlying classical structure manifested through, e.g., commutativity among all relevant observables.
Subsequently, we rigorously reduce the original doubly-constrained parameter optimization problem to a GEP.
Our detailed proof of
Theorem 3
not only extends, but also completes earlier derivations of the singly-constrained case in terms of mathematical rigor and proper handling of degenerate cases.
We further comment on the LCU implementation and its success probability, once the optimal parameters have been obtained, and we later re-establish the connection between QCP and QAOA with its well-established theory for feasibility-preserving PQCs.
We conclude this section by revisiting the aforementioned steps from a density-operator perspective.
In this framework, we show that even noisy input states require only a pure state preparation for LCU implementation.
In
Section IV
, we discuss the implications and open questions raised by our work.
We highlight our framework’s interplay with QAOA, as well as its conceptual independence from it.
In particular, we conclude that generalized QCP restores out-of-the-box applicability for hard-constrained problems, a feature unfortunately missing in the extended QAOA framework.

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Constrained combinatorial optimization problems

Let
\mathbbmss{b}\coloneqq\{0,1\}
.
A
constrained combinatorial optimization problem
(CCOP) is formally defined as a triple
(n,c,S)
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the problem size,
c:\mathbbmss{b}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the objective function, and
S\subseteq\mathbbmss{b}^{n}
is the feasible set.
Given a CCOP, the task is to minimize
c
over all
\bm{b}\in S
.
Prominent examples of CCOPs are the Job-Shop Scheduling problem
Manne 1960
;
Jain and Meeran 1999
;
Zhang et al. 2019
, the Knapsack problem
Martello and Toth 1990
;
Kellerer et al. 2004
, the Maximum Independent Set problem
Tarjan and Trojanowski 1977
;
Boppana and Halldórsson 1992
;
Fomin et al. 2009
, the Minimum Spanning Tree problem
Graham and Hell 1985
, and the Traveling Salesperson Problem
Applegate et al. 2006
, for many of which the corresponding decision problem is NP-complete
Karp 1972
.
1
1

1

The decision problem associated to a given optimization problem is to determine whether there exists a feasible solution with objective above/below a given threshold.
The complexity class NP is, in the narrow sense, only defined for decision problems.

Throughout this article, we will only consider CCOPs that lie in NP as this implies that, for any given
\bm{b}\in\mathbbmss{b}^{n}
, calculating
c(\bm{b})
and verifying whether
\bm{b}\in S
can be done in time polynomial in
n
.

In order to study CCOPs with the aid of a quantum computer, we first need to quantize the problem:
For each classical bit, we introduce a representing qubit
\mathbbmss{q}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
, yielding an
n
-qubit register
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\mathbbmss{q}^{\otimes n}
.
The classical bit strings now form the
computational basis
(CB)
\{\ket{\bm{b}}\,:\,\bm{b}\in\mathbbmss{b}^{n}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
The solution space
\mathcal{S}
is the subspace spanned by all the feasible CB states, i.e.
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\spn(\ket{\bm{b}}\,:\,\bm{b}\in S)
.
More generally, we consider all states
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}
, i.e. arbitrary superpositions of feasible CB states, to be feasible states.
The classical objective function
c
is translated to an
n
-qubit objective Hamiltonian
C\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, whose action on the CB is defined by
C\ket{\bm{b}}\coloneqq c(\bm{b})\ket{\bm{b}}
.
That is,
C
is diagonal in the CB.
The minimization task readily translates to finding an eigenstate of
C
with minimal eigenvalue within the solution space
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., to finding a ground state of
C|_{\mathcal{S}}
.
By the Rayleigh-Ritz inequality, the ground state search, in turn, is equivalent to finding a minimizer of
\braket{\psi|C|\psi}
among all feasible states
\ket{\psi}
.

The class of
variational quantum algorithms
(VQAs)
Cerezo et al. 2021a
constitutes a promising family of hybrid heuristics to approximate such a ground state on a quantum computer.
In a nutshell, a VQA introduces a parameterized manifold of easily preparable states
\ket{\bm{\theta}}=V(\bm{\theta})\ket{\iota}
by specifying an initial state
\ket{\iota}
as well as a unitary
parameterized quantum circuit
(PQC)
V(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq V_{L}(\theta_{L})\cdots V_{2}(\theta_{2})V_{1}(\theta_{1})
.
The
L
components of the PQC are assumed to be easy to implement quantum gates
V_{j}(\theta_{j})
which depend on a single parameter
\theta_{j}
.
The actual parameter optimization is carried out by a CPU which can query the QPU to yield an estimate of the energy expectation value
E(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\braket{\bm{\theta}|C|\bm{\theta}}
via repeated state preparation and subsequent measurement.
By making measurements at low circuit depth, VQAs thereby actively mitigate the severe effects of quantum decoherence
Brandt 1999
.
Most of the optimization methods used, although sophisticated and varied (see e.g.
Kingma and Ba 2017
;
Kübler et al. 2020
;
Sweke et al. 2020
;
Wilson et al. 2021
), are based on gradient optimization strategies or (quasi-)Newton methods to perform line search on
E(\bm{\theta})

Bonet-Monroig et al. 2023
.
For convex optimization problems these methods are guaranteed to find the optimum up to an arbitrary precision in a finite number of steps, but
E(\bm{\theta})
is a convex function only for very special cases.

II.2
Quantum approximate optimization algorithm/quantum alternating operator ansatz

The pioneering VQA for unconstrained combinatorial optimization problems is the
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
(QAOA)
Farhi et al. 2014
which prescribes the uniform superposition
\ket{+}
of all CB states as initial state.
Specifying a depth
p\in\mathbb{N}
, its PQC is build from the alternating
p
-fold application of phase separator gates
\exp(-i \gamma C)
and mixer gates
\exp(-i \beta B)
, where

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{x}^{(i)}

(1)

is the QAOA-mixer Hamiltonian.
Initial state, mixer, and phase separator are chosen in such a way that one is guaranteed to approximate a ground state arbitrarily well for sufficiently high values of
p

Binkowski et al. 2024
.

Although originally formulated for unconstrained problems, the QAOA’s scope can be extended to general CCOPs by introducing soft-constraints:
The objective function
c
is altered to penalize infeasible bit strings, e.g., by choosing a penalty
\alpha>0
and redefining

\displaystyle\tilde{c}(\bm{b})\coloneqq\begin{cases}c(\bm{b}),&\text{if }\bm{b}\in S\\
c(\bm{b})+\alpha,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(2)

If the value for
\alpha
is chosen sufficiently high, the optima of
\tilde{c}
will again coincide with the optima of
c
, so applying QAOA to the soft-constrained problem could potentially yield a good approximation of those optima.
However, empirical studies show that the hyperparameter
\alpha
has to be carefully fine-tuned to sufficiently suppress infeasibility on the one hand, but to not dominate the optimization landscape on the other
de la Grand’rive and Hullo 2019
;
van Dam et al. 2021
;
Baker and Radha 2022
.

As an alternative to employing soft-constraints,
Hadfield et al. 2019
developed a more general framework, the
Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz
(same acronym: QAOA), which considers feasible initial states and feasibility-preserving mixers to strictly ensure fulfillment of the constraints throughout the entire routine, rendering additional soft-constraints obsolete.
In this settings, the constraints are thus hard-constraints which are never to be violated.
Formally, an
n
-qubit operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is called feasibility-preserving if it maps feasible states to feasible states, i.e. if
A(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.
Provided that we start with a feasible initial state
\ket{\iota}\in\mathcal{S}
and only make use of feasibility-preserving operations, we have effectively restricted the entire problem to the subspace
\mathcal{S}
.
Note that since the objective Hamiltonian
C
is diagonal in the CB, so is the phase separator
\exp(-i \gamma C)
for all parameter values
\gamma
;
hence it is especially feasibility-preserving.
Therefore, it suffices to choose a feasible initial state and a feasibility-preserving mixer to restrict the entire QAOA to the feasible subspace
\mathcal{S}
.

A proper QAOA-mixer
U_{\text{M}}
not only has to preserve feasibility, but also has to fulfill certain mixing properties.
In
Hadfield et al. 2019
, the relevant mixing property is to provide transitions between all feasible CB states, that is for every pair
\bm{b},\bm{y}\in S
there should exist some parameter value
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
and some power
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\braket{\bm{b}|U_{\text{M}}^{r}(\beta^{*})|\bm{y}}\neq 0
.
Strengthening this condition to irreducibility and element-wise non-negativity of the reduced mixer Hamiltonian
B_{\text{M}}|_{\mathcal{S}}
– thus reintroducing the two crucial properties of (
1
) – such that
U_{\text{M}}(\beta)=\exp(-i \beta B_{\text{M}})
allows to deduce the same convergence result as for the original QAOA
Binkowski et al. 2024
.

The generalization to the quantum alternating operator ansatz has undoubtedly advanced the variational toolbox for CCOPs and has influenced numerous problem-specific improvements of the QAOA.
However, it is lacking the charm of the original QAOA proposal regarding out-of-the-box applicability:
In the original proposal, initial state and mixer are problem-independent and are therefore always the same.
The problem’s structure entirely enters via the phase separator and how the expectation value is calculated, and both are uniquely determined by the classical objective function (modulo soft-constraints).
In contrast, the construction of a problem-specific mixer does not follow a universal pattern.
Mixer have to be handcrafted individually for every CCOP and typically require deep insights into the problem’s feasibility structure and symmetries.
For example,
Hadfield et al. 2019
already gives constructions for several important CCOPs such as MaxIndependentSet, MinGraphColoring, or the Traveling Salesperson Problem which do not follow a clear unique engineering rule.
The recent proposal of Grover-mixer QAOA
Bärtschi and Eidenbenz 2020
gives a somewhat unified approach to constructing problem-specific, feasibility-preserving mixers:
For a given CCOP, let
\ket{S}\coloneqq\lvert S\rvert^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{b}\in S}\ket{\bm{b}}
be the uniform superposition of all feasible CB states and
U_{S}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be the unitary circuit generating that superposition, i.e.,
U_{S}\ket{\bm{0}}=\ket{S}
.
Then,
\exp(- i \beta\ketbra{\solset}{\solset})
constitutes a valid QAOA-mixer in the sense of
Hadfield et al. 2019
.
Note that this mixer can be constructed from
U_{S}
via
\exp(-i \beta\ketbra{\solset}{\solset})=U_{S}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\mathds{1}-(1-e^{-i\beta})\outerproduct{\bm{0}}{\bm{0}})U_{S}^{\dagger}
.
Formally, this ansatz introduces a unique mixer design and specific constructions for Max
k
-VertexCover, the Traveling Salesperson Problem, and discrete portfolio rebalancing were discussed in
Bärtschi and Eidenbenz 2020
.
However, their is no general recipe how to construct the crucial state preparation circuit
U_{S}
for a given problem.

Lastly, an issue shared by all discussed variants of QAOA as well as of many other VQAs is the phenomenon of
barren plateaus
(BPs)
McClean et al. 2018
.
They describe the (mathematical and numerical) observation that for PQCs that can produce all states sufficiently uniformly, the expectation values of the gradients of
E(\bm{\theta})
are zero while the variance decreases exponentially with the number of qubits.
This implies that starting from random initial parameters, the slope has to be resolved using exponentially many measurements.
The parameter landscape becomes practically flat almost everywhere which not only effects gradient-based methods, but also gradient-free optimization routines
Arrasmith et al. 2021
.
BPs also emerge from quantum device noise for PQCs growing linearly with the number of qubits
Wang et al. 2021
and from quantum entanglement itself
Ortiz Marrero et al. 2021
.
They further exist even in shallow circuits, provided that
E(\bm{\theta})
constitutes the expectation value of global observables
Cerezo et al. 2021b
.

II.3
Quantum conic programming

All versions of the QAOA have in common that the parameter optimization to be carried out by the CPU takes place in a multi-dimensional, non-convex setting.
These optimization tasks are notoriously hard, in a certain sense even harder than the ground state search they are derived from
Bittel and Kliesch 2021
.
This is why some recent proposals suggest PQCs which formulate parameter optimization problems with
convex
objective functions for the classical computer
McClean et al. 2017
;
Huggins et al. 2020
;
Bharti et al. 2022
;
Binkowski et al. 2023
.
The proposed algorithms are very similar in how they formulate the parameter optimization as a
generalized eigenvalue problem
(GEP) or, more generally, as a
semidefinite program
(SDP), and how they implement the update of the quantum state via
Linear Combinations of Unitaries
(LCU)
Childs and Wiebe 2012
;
Chakraborty 2023
.
In the following, we provide a more detailed recap of
Quantum Conic Programming
(QCP) as introduced in
Binkowski et al. 2023
which is already specifically tailored to (unconstrained) combinatorial optimization problems and, similar to other proposals, designed in such a way that the effect of BPs are mitigated.

Starting from some state
\ket{\phi}
which is the result of applying an already optimized QAOA-PQC to the initial state
\ket{+}
, we introduce the set of unitaries
\{U_{1}\}_{i=1}^{3}
where
U_{1}=\exp(-i \delta_{1} B)
,
U_{2}=\exp(-i \delta_{2} C)
, and
U_{3}=\mathds{1}
with fixed (random) angles
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
.
2
2

2

The construction is actually formulated for an arbitrary number
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
of unitaries, but we restrict this recap to the QAOA-specific case for which
\ell=3
.

Using these unitaries, we define the parameterized ansatz class

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}\ket{\phi}\coloneqq\frac{M_{\bm{\alpha}}\ket{\phi}}{M_{\bm{\alpha}}\norm{M_{\bm{\alpha}} \ket{\phi}}},\quad\text{where}\quad M_{\bm{\alpha}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{3}\alpha_{j}U_{j},\quad\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}.

(3)

The
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}
are therefore non-unitary LCU-circuits, parameterized by three complex numbers
\bm{\alpha}
.
Note that since
U_{3}=\mathds{1}
, choosing
\bm{\alpha}=(0,0,1)
always reproduces the initial state
\ket{\phi}
;
hence minimizing over the ansatz class (
3
) cannot worsen the result.
The parameter optimization for the proposed ansatz class translates into

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}}

\displaystyle\braket{\phi|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}CM_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}

(QCP-OPT.1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\braket{\phi|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}=1.

(QCP-OPT.2)

In order to obtain a tangible version of the abstract problem (
3
) to be handed to a CPU, we introduce the moment matrices
\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}
defined via

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{jk}\coloneqq\braket{\phi|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathbf{H}_{jk}\coloneqq\braket{\phi|U_{j}^{\dagger}CU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}\quad\text{such that}

(4)

\displaystyle\braket{\phi|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\overline{\alpha_{j}}\braket{\phi|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}\alpha_{k}=\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\bm{\alpha}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\braket{\phi|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}CM_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\overline{\alpha_{j}}\braket{\phi|U_{j}^{\dagger}CU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\phi}\alpha_{k}=\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\bm{\alpha}.

(5)

Therefore, (
3
) has been transformed into

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}}\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\bm{\alpha}\\
&\text{s.t. }\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\bm{\alpha}=1\end{split}

(6)

which may, in turn, be reformulated as a three-dimensional GEP
\mathbf{H}\bm{\alpha}=\lambda\mathbf{E}\bm{\alpha}
, where the minimal generalized eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{E}
and the corresponding generalized eigenvector coincide with the optimum and optimizer of (
3
).
We defer the discussion on how to obtain the matrix elements of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
as well as the detailed problem derivations to the
next section
.

Lastly, we wish to implement the LCU with parameters corresponding to the optimizer of (
3
).
This generally non-unitary and thus inherently non-deterministic step comes with a certain failure probability which can be suppressed, although never eliminated, by more involved state preparation and measurement protocols on a two-qubit ancilla register.
The best strategy found in
Binkowski et al. 2023
is to prepare the state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}\coloneqq\frac{(\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}},0)}{\norm{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}}_{2}}=\frac{(\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}},0)}{\sqrt{\norm{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}}

(7)

in the ancilla register, where
\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}
is the vector obtained by taking the entry-wise (principle) square root of
\bm{\alpha}
, and
(\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}},0)
means to pad this vector with a zero in order to obtain a vector of length
4=2^{2}
.
Subsequently, we apply the compound unitary

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq C_{01}(U_{2})C_{00}(U_{1})=U_{1}\otimes\outerproduct{00}{00}+U_{2}\otimes\outerproduct{01}{01}+U_{3}\otimes(\outerproduct{10}{10}+\outerproduct{11}{11})

(8)

to the product state
\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
, where, e.g.,
C_{00}(U_{1})
denotes the unitary
U_{1}
applied to the main register, controlled on the
\ket{00}
-state in the ancilla register.
Finally, we apply the inverse state preparation circuit of the state

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{\ancilla}\coloneqq\frac{\big(\overline{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}},0\big)}{\norm{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}}_{2}}=\frac{\big(\overline{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}},0\big)}{\sqrt{\norm{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}}

(9)

to the ancilla register and conduct a binary measurement in the CB, distinguishing between the all-zero bit string and all other bit strings.
The failure probability corresponds exactly to the probability of not measuring all-zero and is precisely given by
\norm{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}^{-2}
.
Classical post-processing on whether the measurement outcome is all-zero then implements the entire LCU which updates the quantum state according to the chosen parameter vector
\bm{\alpha}
.

III
Framework

As a first step, we conceptually decouple QCP from the QAOA.
This allows for formulating the former in a more compact way, highlighting its essential building blocks and their properties.
From this cleansed point of view, we generalize QCP from unconstrained combinatorial optimization problems to general CCOPs without introducing soft-constraints, lifting the many favorable features of QCP such as mapping the ground state search to an easy to solve classical problem as well as mitigation of BPs to a more general class of problems.
Unlike the generalization of the QAOA as discussed in the
Section II
, our generalization of QCP is again constructive in the sense that knowledge of the classical objective function and some classical feasibility oracle suffices to directly construct the entire QCP routine.
Finally, we reconnect the generalized QCP to the QAOA and discuss how problem-specific mixers can further improve the execution of QCP.

III.1
Parameterized ansatz class

Let
(n,c,S)
be a generic CCOP in NP.
First, we require the initial state
\ket{\iota}
of the QCP routine to be feasible, i.e.
\ket{\iota}\in\mathcal{S}
.
It may correspond to a feasible bit string, obtained from some classical approximation algorithm and implemented in a depth-one layer of
X
-gates on all qubits to be set to one, or, more generally, a superposition of feasible CB states, obtained by some more sophisticated state preparation routine.

Second, let
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be set of unitaries such that
U_{\ell}=\mathds{1}
.
We call such a set a collection of search unitaries.
The unitaries do not have to fulfill any additional requirements (in contrast to QAOA mixers), but we discuss favorable properties later.
The parameterized ansatz class is the same as proposed in
Binkowski et al. 2023
, i.e. (
3
) with summation over
\ell
unitaries instead of three unitaries.
An approach utilizing soft-constraints, as described in the
Section II
, would involve altering the cost function and thus the objective Hamiltonian.
As a soft-constraint-free alternative, we propose to leave them both unchanged, but to directly introduce hard-constraints to the optimization task:
Let
\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the orthogonal projection onto the feasible subspace
\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the orthogonal projection onto its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
.
In addition to the normalization constraint (
QCP-OPT.2
), we also require that
(\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}})\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}\ket{\iota}=0
, i.e. that parameterized candidate states are orthogonal to
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
.
In summary, we arrive at the generalized optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\braket{\iota|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}CM_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}

(GenQCP-OPT.1)

s.t.

\displaystyle\braket{\iota|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}=1

(GenQCP-OPT.2)

\displaystyle\braket{\iota|M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}})M_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}=0.

(GenQCP-OPT.3)

III.2
Moment matrices

The two moment matrices introduced in the original QCP proposal encapsulate the correct normalization (
\mathbf{E}
) as well as the expectation value which is to be minimized (
\mathbf{H}
), but do not contain any information regarding feasibility.
Since we do not make any modifications to the objective function or Hamiltonian, the
\mathbf{H}
-matrix stays the same.
Furthermore, we keep the definition of the
\mathbf{E}
-matrix, but rename it to
\mathbf{F}
.
Lastly, we have to introduce an additional matrix
\mathbf{G}
which incorporates orthogonality to
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
.
We start by formalizing the process of constructing general moment matrices.

1
.

For every state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
and every collection of unitary operators
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, the map

\displaystyle\begin{split}T:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})&\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell},\\
A&\mapsto\big(\hskip-3.0pt\braket{\psi|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\psi}\hskip-3.0pt\big)_{j,k=1}^{\ell}\end{split}

(10)

is positive.
Furthermore, if the family of states
\{U_{j}\ket{\iota}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,\ell\}
is linearly independent and
B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is positive-definite, so is
T(B)
in
\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
.

Proof.

For a given, arbitrary choice of
\ket{\psi}
and
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
, let
\ket{j}\coloneqq U_{j}\ket{\psi}
, i.e.
\big(T(A)\big)_{jk}=\braket{j|A|k}
.
Let
W
denote the up to
\ell
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
spanned by the states
\ket{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, and let
\mathds{P}_{W}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
W
.
It is then evident that
T
is the composition of
[A\mapsto(\mathds{P}_{W}A\mathds{P}_{W})|_{W}]
with a subsequent matrix representation in the basis
\{\ket{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,\ell\}
.
Since sandwiching with an orthogonal projection and subsequent restriction to the range of that projection as well as choosing any spanning set for a matrix representation of a linear operator are positive maps, so is their composition
T
.

Let
B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be positive-definite.
Then its restriction
(\mathds{P}_{W}B\mathds{P}_{W})|_{W}
to
W
is also positive-definite, since for every
ket{\phi}\in W
it holds that

\braket{\phi|\mathds{P}_{W}B\mathds{P}_{W}|\phi}=\braket{\phi|B|\phi}>0
by the positive-definiteness of
B
.
In addition, let the family
\{\ket{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,\ell\}
be linearly independent, i.e. let it be a basis of
W
, then the matrix representation of the positive-semidefinite operator
(\mathds{P}_{W}B\mathds{P}_{W})|_{W}
in that basis yields a positive-definite matrix.
∎

Our three moment matrices will therefore be the images under
T
of the positive operators
\mathds{1}
,
\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and
C
, respectively.
That is, we define

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{jk}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T(\mathds{1})_{jk}

\displaystyle=\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}\mathds{1}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{jk}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T(\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}})_{jk}

\displaystyle=\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}})U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{jk}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T(C)_{jk}

\displaystyle=\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}CU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}.

(13)

Since
T
is positive according to
1
,
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{G}
, and
\mathbf{H}
are positive-semidefinite matrices.
In addition, choosing the unitaries
U_{i}
such that
\{U_{i}\ket{\iota}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,\ell\}
is linearly independent,
1
further guarantees that
\mathbf{F}
is also positive-definite.
We may further assume w.l.o.g. that
C
is positive-definite (this can be simply achieved by adding a sufficiently large offset to the classical objective function).
Then, again by
1
, also
\mathbf{H}
will be positive-definite.
For
\mathbf{G}
, we cannot make such a claim in full generality.
A calculation analogous to (
5

9
) is equivalent to the more tangible problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}}\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}\bm{\alpha}\\
&\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\alpha} \in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}}$}][l]{\ \,\text{s.t.}}\ \mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger} \mathbf{G} \bm{\alpha}$}][l]{\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{\alpha}}=1\\
&\hphantom{\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}}}\ \bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}\bm{\alpha}=0.\end{split}

(14)

Typically, the moment matrices’ off-diagonal entries are calculated with the aid of the Hadamard test
Cleve et al. 1998
;
Ekert et al. 2002
and Pauli measurements
Bharti and Haug 2021
for all the Pauli terms in the respective observables.
However, note that we do not have direct access to the infeasible projection
\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Additionally, all three operators
\mathds{1}
,
\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and
C
mutually commute as they are all diagonal in the CB, meaning that they can be simultaneously measured.
In fact, we propose to implement neither of these operators directly on the QPU, but to approximate the matrix entries solely via sampling bit strings from the states
U_{i}\ket{\iota}
via measurements in the CB, and to apply the classical objective function and feasibility oracle, respectively.

The diagonal entries
\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}=\braket{j|A|j}
,
A\in\{\mathds{1},\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}},C\}
are the usual expectation value of
A
in the states
\ket{j}\coloneqq U_{j}\ket{\iota}
.
We jointly estimate them via sampling
m\in\mathbb{N}
bit strings from
\ket{j}
and calculating the empirical average of the classical objective function (for
A=C
) and the frequency of infeasible states (for
A=\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
), respectively.
For the off-diagonal entries, we make use of the fact that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}=&\frac{1}{2}\big(\braket{\iota|(U_{j}^{\dagger}+U_{k}^{\dagger})A(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})|\iota}-\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}-\braket{\iota|U_{k}^{\dagger}AU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}\big)\\
&-\frac{i}{2}\big(\braket{\iota|(U_{j}^{\dagger}-iU_{k}^{\dagger})A(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+iU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})|\iota}-\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}-\braket{\iota|U_{k}^{\dagger}AU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}\big),\end{split}

(15)

which follows from the polarization identity.
We already covered how to obtain the expectation values/diagonal entries
\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}AU_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota}
.
In order to get an estimate for the off-diagonal entries via (
15
), the remaining quantities to estimate are therefore the expectation values of
A
in the unnormalized states
(U_{j}^{\dagger}+U_{k}^{\dagger})\ket{\iota}
and
(U_{j}^{\dagger}+iU_{k}^{\dagger})\ket{\iota}
, for all combinations of
j,k=1,\ldots,\ell
such that
j\neq k
.
Since all moment matrices are themselves hermitian, it suffices to restrict the calculation to
k<j
.
We introduce an additional ancilla qubit and use a binary LCU operation of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{\real}^{(jk)}&\coloneqq(\mathds{1}\otimes H)C_{1}(U_{k})C_{0}(U_{j})(\mathds{1}\otimes H)\Rightarrow\\
\Lambda_{\real}^{(jk)}\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}&=(\mathds{1}\otimes H)\big(U_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+U_{k}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}\big)(\mathds{1}\otimes H)\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\big(U_{j}+U_{k}\big)\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}+\frac{1}{2}\big(U_{j}-U_{k}\big)\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{1}\end{split}

(16)

which is very similar to the Hadamard test for calculating (the real part of) inner products of states.
After obtaining the state (
16
), a measurement of the ancilla qubit in the CB with outcome zero readily prepares the normalized version of
(U_{j}+U_{k})\ket{\iota}
in the main register.
The inverse of the normalization factor is given by

\displaystyle\big\lVert(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})\ket{\iota}\big\rVert=\sqrt{\braket{\iota|(U_{j}^{\dagger}+U_{k}^{\dagger})(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})|\iota}}=\sqrt{2+2\real(\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota})}.

(17)

Similarly, the probability of obtaining zero as the measurement result on the ancilla qubit is given by

\displaystyle p_{jk}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\big\lVert(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})\ket{\iota}\big\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\real(\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota})\big).

(18)

By inserting an additional
S
-gate into the LCU operation, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{\imaginary}^{(jk)}&\coloneqq(\mathds{1}\otimes H)C_{1}(U_{k})C_{0}(U_{j})(\mathds{1}\otimes S)(\mathds{1}\otimes H)\Rightarrow\\
\Lambda_{\imaginary}^{(jk)}\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}&=(\mathds{1}\otimes H)\big(U_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}+U_{k}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}\big)(\mathds{1}\otimes(SH))\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\big(U_{j}+iU_{k}\big)\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{0}+\frac{1}{2}\big(U_{j}-iU_{k}\big)\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{1}.\end{split}

(19)

Measuring zero on the ancilla qubit then implements the normalized version of
(U_{j}+iU_{k})\ket{\iota}
in the main register with inverse normalization factor

\displaystyle\big\lVert(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+iU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})\ket{\iota}\big\rVert=\sqrt{\braket{\iota|(U_{j}^{\dagger}-iU_{k}^{\dagger})(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+iU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})|\iota}}=\sqrt{2-2\imaginary(\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota})}.

(20)

The probability of measuring zero is

\displaystyle q_{jk}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\big\lVert(U_{j}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+iU_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}})\ket{\iota}\big\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-\imaginary(\braket{\iota|U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}|\iota})\big).

(21)

In order to obtain the remaining expectation values, we execute the respective LCU operations plus subsequent measurement of the ancilla qubit.
If we measure zero on the ancilla, we continue with measuring the qubits of the main register in the CB, yielding a bit string as output.
We use the obtained bit string samples to estimate the expectation values within the normalized versions of
(U_{j}+U_{k})\ket{\iota}
and
(U_{j}+iU_{k})\ket{\iota}
in the same manner as for the diagonal matrix entries.
To obtain the final expectation value of the unnormalized states, we have to divide by the respective squared normalization factor.
Note that when forming the respective empirical averages, we have to divide by the number of zero measurements which is given by
p_{jk}(0)\cdot m
and
q_{jk}(0)\cdot m
, respectively, where
m
is the number of total repetitions of the LCU operation.
According to (
18
) and (
21
), the probabilities are proportional to the squared inverse normalization factors such that those cancel each other out, leaving a pre-factor of
4/m
for the empirical averages.

III.3
Generalized eigenvalue problem

We proceed by translating (
14
) into a solver-friendlier form: a
generalized eigenvalue problem
(GEP).
This transformation has already been discussed for the singly-constrained case such as (
6
).
In this work, we extend the transformation to the doubly-constrained case and fill some gaps present in the derivations in, e.g.,
McClean et al. 2017
;
Huggins et al. 2020
;
Ghojogh et al. 2019
;
Binkowski et al. 2023
with mathematical rigor.
The following assumption will be central for further derivations.

Assumption
.

The initial state
\ket{\iota}\in\mathcal{H}
and the search unitaries
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
are chosen in such a way that
\{U_{j}\ket{\iota}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,\ell\}
constitutes a linear independent set of states.

Note that, as discussed after
1
, this
Assumption
implies that
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{H}
will be positive-definite (i.e. positive-semidefinite and invertible).
This, in turn, constitutes a crucial assumption for the derivation of the GEP.

Quadratic optimization problems such as (
14
) are well-studied objects in the optimization theory literature with many results on solvability conditions and duality structure
Anstreicher et al. 1999
;
Thoai 2000
;
Beck and Eldar 2006
.
In particular,
Nguyen 2022
provides a rich collection of tools for investigating strong duality of quadratic problems of which we merely require a small subset.

The first step is to formulate the complex problem (
14
) over a real vector space by identifying
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}
via
\mathbb{C}\ni\bm{\alpha}=\real(\bm{\alpha})+i\imaginary(\bm{\alpha})\,\widehat{=}\,(\real(\bm{\alpha}),\imaginary(\bm{\alpha}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}
.
Additionally, we introduce the real
2\ell\times 2\ell
symmetric block matrices

\displaystyle\bm{F}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\real(\mathbf{F})&-\imaginary(\mathbf{F})\\
\imaginary(\mathbf{F})&\hphantom{-}\real(\mathbf{F})\end{pmatrix},\ \bm{G}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\real(\mathbf{G})&-\imaginary(\mathbf{G})\\
\imaginary(\mathbf{G})&\hphantom{-}\real(\mathbf{G})\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }\bm{H}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\real(\mathbf{H})&-\imaginary(\mathbf{H})\\
\imaginary(\mathbf{H})&\hphantom{-}\real(\mathbf{H})\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

Note that
\bm{F}
and
\bm{H}
are again positive-definite while
\bm{G}
is guaranteed to be positive-semidefinite.
We can then readily rewrite (
14
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{x}\raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}}\bm{x}^{T}\bm{H}\bm{x}\\
&\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x} \raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{2 \ell}}$}][l]{\ \,\text{s.t.}}\ \mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\bm{x}^{T} \bm{G} \bm{x}$}][l]{\bm{x}^{T}\bm{F}\bm{x}}=1\\
&\hphantom{\min_{\bm{x}\raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}}}\ \bm{x}^{T}\bm{G}\bm{x}=0.\end{split}

(23)

Since
\bm{G}
is positive-semidefinite, it holds, given an
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\ell}
, that
\bm{x}^{T}\bm{G}\bm{x}=0
if and only if
\bm{G}\bm{x}=\bm{0}
.
Therefore, we effectively optimize over
\ker(\bm{G})
which we can absorb as an implicit constraint into the problem’s domain.
In practice, to formulate the problem again in a concrete coordinate space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
m
is the dimension of
\ker(\bm{G})
, we may conjugate both
\bm{F}
and
\bm{H}
with the basis transform
\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}
into the eigenbasis of
\bm{G}
and then execute the sandwiching with the projection onto
\ker(\bm{G})
by deleting all rows/columns that correspond to non-kernel eigenvectors of
\bm{G}
.

\bm{F}
and
\bm{H}
both being positive-definite readily implies that also
\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{\vphantom{-1}}\bm{F}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{-1}
and
\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{\vphantom{-1}}\bm{H}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{-1}
are positive definite.
By the same argument as in the proof of
1
, also their restrictions to the
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-subspace
\tilde{\bm{F}}\coloneqq(\mathds{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{\vphantom{-1}}\bm{F}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{-1}\mathds{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}})|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
and
\tilde{\bm{H}}\coloneqq(\mathds{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{\vphantom{-1}}\bm{H}\bm{B}_{\bm{G}}^{-1}\mathds{P}_{\mathbb{R}^{m}})|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
are positive-definite.
It then holds that (
23
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\bm{x}\raisebox{0.45206pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{m}}\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{x}\\
&\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x} \raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{m}}$}][l]{\ \,\text{s.t.}}\ \bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{F}}\bm{x}=1.\end{split}

(24)

The Lagrange function for (
24
) is

\displaystyle\begin{split}g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}&\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
(\bm{x},\lambda)&\mapsto\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{x}+\lambda(1-\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{F}}\bm{x}).\end{split}

(25)

and, after rearranging the terms of (
25
), we find that the Lagrangian dual problem has the form

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\raisebox{0.45206pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}}\min_{\bm{x}\raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{m}}g(\bm{x},\lambda)=\max_{\lambda\raisebox{0.45206pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\lambda+\min_{\bm{x}\raisebox{0.3014pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}^{m}}\bm{x}^{T}\Big(\tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}}\Big)\bm{x}\bigg).

(26)

Note that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for which the symmetric matrix
\tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}}
is not positive-semidefinite, we can find a direction
\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\hat{\bm{x}}^{T}(\bm{H}-\lambda\bm{F})\hat{\bm{x}}<0
such that minimizing over all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is unbounded below.
In contrast, in case of positive-semidefinitness, the minimal possible value is zero which is attained by choosing, e.g.,
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
.
This shows that the Lagrangian dual problem (
26
) has the equivalent form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{\lambda\raisebox{0.45206pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}}\lambda\\
&\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\raisebox{0.45206pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\in$}\mathbb{R}}$}][l]{\ \text{s.t. }}\ \tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}}\succeq 0.\end{split}

(27)

We further use the following adapted version of

(
Nguyen 2022
, Lemma 2.1)
to express the positive-semidefiniteness constraint with a more convenient system of inequalities.

2
.

Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be positive-semidefinite.
Then
\bm{A}\succeq 0
is equivalent to the inequality system

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{x}\geq 0\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ satisfying }\bm{x}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{x}=1,

(28)

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{x}\geq 0\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ satisfying }\bm{x}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{x}=0.

(29)

Proof.

That
\bm{A}\succeq 0
implies both (
28
) and (
29
) is trivial.
In order to show the opposite direction, let
\bm{A}\not\succeq 0
, i.e. there exists
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\bm{y}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{y}<0
.
If either
\bm{y}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{y}=1
or
\bm{y}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{y}=0
, we are already done.
If neither of these conditions are fulfilled, consider
\bm{x}\coloneqq\bm{y}/\sqrt{\bm{y}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{y}}
which, by construction, fulfills
\bm{x}^{T}\bm{B}\bm{x}=1
, but also
\bm{x}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{x}<0
, i.e. (
28
) is violated.
∎

Applying
2
to the family
\tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}}
of symmetric matrices and the fact that
\tilde{\bm{F}}\succ 0
translate the positive-semidefiniteness constraint into

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\text{ satisfying }\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{F}}\bm{x}=1\,:\,\bm{x}^{T}(\tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}})\bm{x}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{x}\geq\lambda,

(30)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\text{ satisfying }\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{F}}\bm{x}=0\,:\,\bm{x}^{T}(\tilde{\bm{H}}-\lambda\tilde{\bm{F}})\bm{x}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{x}\geq 0.

(31)

Clearly, (
31
) is trivially satisfied since
\tilde{\bm{H}}\succ 0
.
This leaves us with the equivalent problem

\displaystyle\max\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,\lambda\leq\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{H}}\bm{x}\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\text{ satisfying }\bm{x}^{T}\tilde{\bm{F}}\bm{x}=1\}

(32)

III.4
LCU implementation

Solving (

What remains to show is how to prepare the given states on a QPU.
The procedure was already explained in
Binkowski et al. 2023
and applies analogously to our generalization.
In this subsection, we merely provide some additional details of the LCU construction.

For
\ell
search unitaries, we have to introduce a
\lceil\log_{2}(\ell)\rceil
-qubit ancilla register
\mathcal{H}_{\ancilla}
on which we prepare the quantum state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}\coloneqq\frac{(\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{0})}{\sqrt{\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}}}

(37)

where the padding with
2^{\lceil\log_{2}(\ell)\rceil}-\ell
many zeros is necessary in order to obtain a valid
\lceil\log_{2}(\ell)\rceil
-qubit state vector.
The state preparation of
\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
may be executed with one of the many available state preparation circuits
Plesch and Brukner 2011
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Ramacciotti et al. 2024
with
\mathcal{O}(\ell\log_{2}\ell)
-many gates.
Following the state preparation of
\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
, we apply the unitary

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}C_{j}(U_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}U_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{j}{j}_{\ancilla}+\mathds{1}\otimes\Bigg(\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{2^{\lceil\log_{2}(\ell)\rceil}}\outerproduct{j}{j}_{\ancilla}\Bigg)

(38)

to the product state
\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
, where we label the ancilla register’s CB states with integers
j
rather than bit strings.
Note that since
\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
does not have any components in the CB states with index larger than
\ell
, the second term does not contribute when applying
\mathcal{U}
to
\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}
.
Subsequently, we conduct a binary measurement in direction of

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{\ancilla}\coloneqq\frac{\big(\overline{\sqrt{\bm{\alpha}}},\bm{0}\big)}{\sqrt{\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}}}

(39)

by first applying its inverse state preparation and then conducting a measurement in the CB on the ancilla register, distinguishing between
\ket{\bm{0}}_{\ancilla}
and all other states.
All these steps taken together have the following action

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big(\mathds{1}\times\bra{\xi}_{\ancilla}\big)\mathcal{U}\big(\ket{\iota}\otimes\ket{\psi}_{\ancilla}\big)&=\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{\ell}\big(\mathds{1}\otimes\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\bra{i}_{\ancilla}\big)\big(U_{j}\otimes\outerproduct{j}{j}_{\ancilla}\big)\big(\ket{\iota}\otimes\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\ket{k}_{\ancilla}\big)\\
&=\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}^{-1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}\big(\mathds{1}\otimes\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\bra{i}_{\ancilla}\big)\big(U_{j}\ket{\iota}\otimes\sqrt{\alpha_{j}}\ket{j}_{\ancilla}\big)\\
&=\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{j}U_{j}\ket{\iota}=\lVert\alpha\rVert_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}\ket{\iota}.\end{split}

(40)

The factor
\lVert\bm{\alpha}\rVert_{1}^{-1}
constitutes the probability of measuring the all-zero bit string upon measurement on the ancilla register, that is, the success probability of implementing the LCU correctly.
A single successful application of the LCU update therefore implements the
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}\ket{\iota}
on the main register.

III.5
Feasibility-preserving unitaries

While a key advantage of our construction is the ability to ensure that a successful LCU step is feasibility-preserving even if the comprising search unitaries

(and

The problem (
9

3

The subsequent derivation of strong duality in

still holds for the then trivial case of

and

, and

implies that the minimizer and minimum of (

\displaystyle\bm{H}\bm{x}=\lambda\bm{F}\bm{x}.

(41)

We further note that for many CCOPs the feasible subspace

is much smaller dimension than the entire quantum Hilbert space

.

III.6
Robustness of purity against noise

The above scheme is formulated for any device that solely operates on pure quantum states, representable by a state vector
\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}
.
In reality however, we are often confronted with the situation where we intentionally or inevitably operate on
mixed
quantum states, described by the set of density operators
\mathfrak{S}\coloneqq\{\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}):\rho\succeq 0\ \text{and}\ \tr[\rho]=1\}

.
Naturally, this situation arises when we deploy a quantum computer with access to only imperfect gate operations, e.g., a NISQ device, to solve the optimization task (
9
).
This section will prove a certain level of noise resistance to our framework alleviating the effects of noise to the classical optimization problem one has to solve in order to optimize the quantum state.
Namely, we prove that the suitably reformulated (
9

\ket{\iota}
is replaced with a
feasible
mixed state

,

.
That is, even under the influence of noise in the feasible subspace, it suffices to prepare a pure state in the ancilla register in order to update the main register’s mixed state to the optimal solution found in the parameterized ansatz class.

Note that by switching to the density operator formalism, the task of minimizing the objective Hamiltonian’s expectation value becomes a constrained semidefinite program over the cone of linear, positive-semidefinite operators on the main register’s Hilbert space

The normalization condition is now to be expressed in terms of having a unit trace.

&\hphantom{\min_{\rho\in\PSD(\mathcal{H})}}\,\tr[\rho(\mathds{1}-\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{S}})]=0.\end{split}

In the context of a statistical ensemble,

IV
Discussion and Conclusions

In this article, we have introduced a natural extension of recent proposals for non-unitary parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) for unconstrained/soft-constrained to general hard-constrained combinatorial optimization problems.
With this we are aiming at a unified framework for addressing arbitrarily-constrained combinatorial optimization problems already on quantum devices with short to medium coherence times.
However, our approach also finds applicability on far-term devices which may still outsource suitable subroutines to CPUs.
Like its predecessors, our hybrid framework introduces an ansatz class reachable by (non-unitary) linear combinations of unitaries, parameterized by a complex coefficient vector, an ansatz that naturally mitigates the effect of barren plateaus and local minima.
It also formulates the task of minimizing the objective Hamiltonian’s expectation value over this ansatz class as a classical optimization over a vector space much smaller than the underlying Hilbert space of the quantum system.
Previous methods formulated a generalized eigenvalue problem (GEP) for the unconstrained case or, more generally, a semidefinite program.
Within this article, we extend the derivation of a GEP for the constrained case and also fill some gaps in prior derivations with mathematical rigor.
This problem transformation requires recording several expectation values and state overlaps, for which we introduce a specialized measurement protocol, solely based on sampling bit strings from the main register and applying the classical objective function and a classical feasibility oracle.
In fact, the objective Hamiltonian does not even have to be implemented on the quantum computer.

Our framework draws inspiration not only from preceding non-unitary methods, but also from the Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz (QAOA) which first gave a formal treatment of ensuring hard-constraints with PQCs.
One of the QAOA’s biggest strengths – its general formalism – is also its major drawback:
There is no universal design pattern for constructing feasibility-preserving and well-mixing PQCs.
With this work, we restore out-of-the-box applicability by integrating the classical objective function and feasibility oracle into the framework;
yet, our approach is universally applicable, sharing this utmost important feature with the QAOA.

With this article, we have laid the foundation for a unified framework.
Further theoretical strengthening as well as extensive numerical experiments are crucial.
We are currently preparing a high-performant simulator, specifically tailored to applying linear combinations of unitaries to arbitrary input states, with which we will further gauge the performance of our framework on prominent examples such as the Traveling Salesperson Problem.
