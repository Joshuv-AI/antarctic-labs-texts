Polynomial extensions of Raimi’s theorem
Norbert Hegyvári, János Pach, Thang Pham

Abstract
Raimi’s theorem guarantees the existence of a partition of ℕ \mathbb{N} into two parts with an unavoidable intersection property: for any finite coloring of ℕ \mathbb{N} , some color class intersects both parts infinitely many times, after an appropriate shift (translation). We establish a polynomial extension of this result, proving that such intersections persist under polynomial shifts in any dimension. Given non-constant polynomials P ( 1 ) , … , P ( f ) ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ x ] P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(f)}\in\mathbb{Z}[x] with positive leading coefficients and P ( j ) ​ ( 0 ) = 0 P^{(j)}(0)=0 , we construct a partition of ℕ k \mathbb{N}^{k} such that for any coloring of ℕ k \mathbb{N}^{k} with finitely many colors, there exist x 0 ∈ ℕ x_{0}\in\mathbb{N} and a single color class that meets all partition pieces after shifts by x 0 + P ( j ) ​ ( h ) x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h) in each of the k k coordinate directions, for every j j and infinitely many values h ∈ ℕ h\in\mathbb{N} . Our proof exploits Weyl’s equidistribution theory, Pontryagin duality, and the structure of polynomial relation lattices. We also prove some finite analogues of the above results for abelian groups and S ​ L 2 ​ ( 𝔽 q ) SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) .

Introduction

Ramsey theory admits two complementary perspectives. The
coloring approach
begins with an arbitrary finite partition of the integers and seeks structures that some color class must contain, for instance, Van der Waerden’s theorem
[
25
]
guarantees arbitrarily long monochromatic arithmetic progressions. The
density approach
assumes only that a set has positive upper density and proves the occurrence of prescribed patterns. Szemerédi
[
24
]
established the existence of arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions in sets of positive density, while Sárközy
[
22
]
and independently Furstenberg
[
6
]
proved that such sets contain pairs whose difference is a nonzero square.

Raimi
[
21
]
introduced a complementary viewpoint that begins with neither a given dense set nor a given coloring. Instead, one constructs a specific partition of
\mathbb{N}
with an unavoidable intersection property: for every finite coloring, there exists a shift such that a single color meets every piece of the partition in an infinite set. Informally, while classical Ramsey results ask “what patterns are forced by density or by finite colorings?”, Raimi’s perspective asks “which partitions are unavoidable in any finite coloring after an appropriate shift?”

Raimi’s theorem reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Raimi
[
21
]
)
.

There exists a subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with the following property. For every partition of
\mathbb{N}
into finitely many parts,
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}F_{j}
with
t\in\mathbb{N},
there exist

j\in\{1,2,\ldots,t\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that both
(F_{j}+k)\cap E
and
(F_{j}+k)\setminus E
are infinite sets.

In other words, after an appropriate shift
k
, the set
F_{j}+k
unavoidably intersects both
E
and its complement in infinitely many elements. This theorem was originally proved by Raimi using topological methods, and subsequently Hindman
[
13
]
provided an elementary proof. Strengthened versions specifying the densities of the partition sets or guaranteeing positive densities in the conclusion can be found in
[
1
,
10
]
.

Throughout this paper,
\mathbf{1}_{k}
stands for the element of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
whose each coordinate is
1
. For any finite or infinite set
X
of integers (or elements of a group equipped with a binary operation
+
), let
\mathrm{FS}(X)
denote the set of all finite sums formed by the elements of
X
.

Raimi’s theorem extends naturally to higher-dimensional lattices using diagonal shifts.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r,t,k\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a partition

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that for any partition
\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}F_{j}
of
{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
into
t
parts, there exist
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,t\}
, an element
x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

in
\mathbb{N}
such that for every
h
from the set
x_{0}+\mathrm{FS}(\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1})
and every

, the set
(F_{m}+h\mathbf{1}_{k})\cap E_{i}
has infinitely many elements.

While Theorem
1.2
extends the intersection property to higher dimensions with diagonal shifts, a natural question arises: does this property persist under
polynomial
shifts? This question is motivated by Bergelson and Leibman’s celebrated extension
[
2
]
of Szemerédi’s theorem from arithmetic progressions to polynomial progressions. Their work demonstrated that polynomial patterns are just as robust as linear patterns in dense sets.

Our main result establishes an analogous phenomenon for Raimi-type intersections, showing that partition unavoidability extends to polynomial shifts and revealing deep connections to Weyl’s polynomial equidistribution theory.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
r,t,k,f\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(f)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be non-constant polynomials with the properties that
P^{(1)}(0)=\cdots=P^{(f)}(0)=0
and the leading coefficients are positive.

There exists a partition

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that for any partition
\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}F_{j}
of
{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
, there exist
m\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, an element
x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, and a set
H\subset\mathbb{N}
of positive lower density with the property that for every
h\in H
and every
j\in\{1,\dots,f\}
, the set

\big(F_{m}+(x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h))\mathbf{1}_{k}\big)\cap E_{i}

has infinitely many elements.

While Theorem
1.3
extends the intersection property to polynomial shifts, it does not formally generalize Theorem
1.2
. The latter provides shifts forming an FS-set with additive structure, while Theorem
1.3
guarantees infinitely many individual shifts that simultaneously work for all polynomial shifts
P^{(j)}(h)
,
1\leq j\leq f
.

Beyond the infinite setting, we can ask whether similar phenomena occur in finite algebraic structures. We answer this affirmatively, establishing finite analogues in both abelian groups and the non-abelian group
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. These results demonstrate that Raimi-type intersection properties extend to finite algebraic structures, with quantitative bounds replacing infinite intersections.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let

The same conclusion holds for any finite abelian group

If the finite abelian group

Theorem 1.5
.

Let

The above results illustrate that Raimi-type intersection properties are robust across diverse mathematical structures, from infinite lattices under polynomial shifts to finite groups, both abelian and non-abelian.

1.1
Proof ideas and techniques

Our proofs employ a variety of methods from combinatorics, harmonic analysis, and equidistribution theory.

For Theorem
1.2
, we construct a partition of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
using a coloring based on
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent real numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
. The key is to define
\varphi(\mathbf{a})=\{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}a_{i}\}\in(0,1)
and color points based on which interval in a dyadic partition of
(0,1)
contains
\varphi(\mathbf{a})
. By choosing
\beta=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}
irrational, we can apply Lemma
2.2
to find a sequence whose finite sums land in prescribed narrow intervals modulo
1
, ensuring the desired intersection property.

Theorem
1.3
requires significantly deeper machinery. The main step is to simultaneously control the fractional parts
\{\beta P^{(j)}(h)\}
for all
j=1,\ldots,f
. While Weyl’s classical theorem handles a single polynomial, we need Theorem
3.1
, which guarantees a set of positive lower density satisfying these constraints simultaneously. The proof relies on Weyl’s equidistribution criterion (Theorem
3.5
), Pontryagin duality for locally compact abelian groups (Proposition
3.6
), and careful analysis of the relation lattice
R
and the associated subtorus
H\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{f}
. We show that the sequence
\{\mathbf{v}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
,
\mathbf{v}(n)=(\{\beta P^{(1)}(n)\},\ldots,\{\beta P^{(f)}(n)\})
, is equidistributed on
H
(Lemma
3.4
), which provides many choices of
h
with the required properties.

For the finite group results, the techniques shift to combinatorics and probability.

Since
G
is a finite cyclic group, it is sufficient to consider the case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Theorem
1.4
uses an iterative refinement argument: we partition
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
into nested intervals of decreasing size, apply a density-averaging at each stage, and carefully track how intersections propagate through the hierarchy.

Theorem
1.5
employs a probabilistic method based on Chebyshev’s inequality. We project
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
onto
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, then apply Lemma
5.2
, which shows that for any sufficiently large subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, there exists a direction such that most lines in that direction contain many points from
U
. We then lift the configuration back to the group using a carefully chosen element
h\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. The key is that most lines contain a substantial portion of the projected set, which translates to large intersections in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

By
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
we mean a sequence with infinitely many elements
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
, and if the sequence has
k
elements, then we write
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{k}
. For any

, let

stand the fractional part of

. For

, we define the

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}

Proof.

There is an integer

We proceed along the same lines as in the proof of Theorem

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}
. Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(f)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]

of positive lower density such that for each fixed

The case

Lemma 3.2

(Weyl
[

27
]
)

Let
\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}

P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Let

H=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\{\beta P(n)\}\in J\}

If, in addition, we also assume that the polynomials

guarantees the existence of a set of such values

J+\sigma\ \supset\ S_{j^{\ast}}\quad\text{mod }1.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that

S_{j^{\ast}}\ \subset\ (J+\sigma)_{-\varepsilon}:=\{u\in J+\sigma:\ \operatorname{dist}(u,\partial(J+\sigma))>\varepsilon\}.

It follows from Theorem
3.1
that there exists a set
X_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{N}
of positive lower density such that for any
h\in X_{\varepsilon}
one has

\|\{\beta P^{(j)}(h)\}\|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,f.

Moreover, since the leading coefficient of each
P^{(j)}
is positive,
we may choose
h
large enough to make sure that
P^{(j)}(h)\geq 0
for all
j
. The set of
h
satisfying this condition still has positive lower density.

For each fixed
j
, set

\gamma_{j}^{\prime}:=\sigma+\{\beta P^{(j)}(h)\}\mod 1,

Then
\|\gamma_{j}^{\prime}-\sigma\|=\|\{\beta P^{(j)}(h)\}\|<\varepsilon
, and by Lemma
2.3
,

(J+\sigma)_{-\varepsilon}\ \subset\ J+\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\ \subset\ (J+\sigma)_{+\varepsilon}.

In particular,

J+\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\ \supset\ (J+\sigma)_{-\varepsilon}\ \supset\ S_{j^{\ast}}.

Since
P^{(j)}(h)\geq 0
, the translate
F_{m}+(x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h))\mathbf{1}_{k}
lies in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and

\varphi\!\big(\mathbf{x}+(x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h))\mathbf{1}_{k}\big)\;=\;\varphi(\mathbf{x})+\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\mod 1.

Because
\varphi(F_{m})
is dense in
J
, it follows that
\varphi(F_{m}+(x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h))\mathbf{1}_{k})

is dense in
J+\gamma_{j}^{\prime}
, hence intersects each tile
T_{j^{\ast},i}\subset S_{j^{\ast}}
infinitely many times.
By the definition of the color classes
E_{i}
, we conclude that

\big(F_{m}+(x_{0}+P^{(j)}(h))\mathbf{1}_{k}\big)\cap E_{i}\quad\text{is infinite for every }i=1,\dots,r.

This holds for every
j=1,\dots,f
at the same

.
Since there are infinitely many such
h
,
the theorem follows. ∎

3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We first recall Weyl’s equidistribution criteria in
[
17
]
.

•

One-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}
: A sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, with
x_{n}\in\mathbb{T}
, is equidistributed iff

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ikx_{n}}\;=\;0\qquad\text{for every }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}.\quad\quad(\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{book3}{}{}, \mbox{Theorem 2.1, Chapter 1}]})

•

Multidimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{f}
: A sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, with
\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{T}^{f}
, is equidistributed iff

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n}}\;=\;0\qquad\text{for every }\mathbf{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}.\quad\quad(\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{book3}{}{}, \mbox{Theorem 6.2, Chapter 1}]})

•

For a closed subgroup
H\leq\mathbb{T}^{f}
(subtorus version): Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset H
. Then
\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is equidistributed
on
H
iff for every
non-trivial character
\chi
of
H
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\chi(\mathbf{x}_{n})\;=\;0.\quad\quad(\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{book3}{}{}, \mbox{Corollary 1.2, Chapter 4}]})

Note that every character of
H
is of the form

\chi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{z})\;=\;e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}},\qquad\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}.\quad\quad(\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{EW}{}{}, \mbox{Appendix C.3}]})

By non-trivial on
H
, we mean that
\mathbf{m}
is not in the annihilator

\Ann(H):=\;\big\{\,\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\;:\;\chi_{\mathbf{m}}\!\restriction_{H}\equiv 1\,\big\}.

So the criterion becomes

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{n}}\;=\;0\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\setminus\Ann(H).

(1)

Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(f)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
with
P^{(1)}(0)=\cdots=P^{(f)}(0)=0
, and let
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.
Define

\mathbf{v}(n):=\big(\{\beta P^{(1)}(n)\},\dots,\{\beta P^{(f)}(n)\}\big)\in\mathbb{T}^{f},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}},

the
relation lattice

R:=\Big\{\,\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{f})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}:\ Q_{\mathbf{m}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{f}m_{i}P^{(i)}\equiv 0\text{ in }{\mathbb{Z}}[x]\,\Big\},

the relation-defined subtorus

H:=\{\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{T}^{f}:\ e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=1\ \text{ for all }\mathbf{m}\in R\,\},

and the closed subgroup generated by the sequence
\{\mathbf{v}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}

H^{\prime}:=\overline{\ \langle\,\mathbf{v}(n):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,\rangle\ }\ \leq\ \mathbb{T}^{f}\quad(\text{closure of all finite ${\mathbb{Z}}$-linear combinations}).

Note that closure of a subgroup is a subgroup.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
makes use of the following lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
H=H^{\prime}
, that is, the relation-defined subtorus
H
equals the closed subgroup generated by the sequence
\{\mathbf{v}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The sequence
\{\mathbf{v}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
is equidistributed on
H
with respect to Haar measure
m_{H}
.

We now prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Consider the open box

U:=(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{f}\subset\mathbb{T}^{f}.

Since
\mathbf{0}\in H
and
U
is a neighborhood of
\mathbf{0}
, we have
m_{H}(U\cap H)>0
(see
[
7
, page 341]
).

By Lemma
3.4
, there exist infinitely many
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
with

\mathbf{v}(h)=\big(\{\beta P^{(1)}(h)\},\dots,\{\beta P^{(f)}(h)\}\big)\in U\cap H.

To see this, we argue as follows.

We know that
H
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{T}^{f}
(from Lemma
3.3
or its definition), so it is locally compact. Thus, the measure
m_{H}
is regular (for this fact, see, for example,
[
7
, Chapter 2]
or
[
8
, Chapter 44]
). Assume that
m_{H}(U\cap H)=c>0
, then we can find a compact set
K\subset U\cap H
such that
m_{H}(K)\geq c/2
. Notice that
H
is compact Hausdorff, and therefore normal, so one can apply Urysohn’s lemma (see, for example,
[
19
, Theorem 33.1]
) to find a continuous function
F\colon H\to[0,1]
such that
\mathbf{1}_{K}\leq F\leq\mathbf{1}_{U\cap H}.

This implies

\int_{H}F\,dm_{H}\geq\int_{H}\mathbf{1}_{K}\,dm_{H}=m_{H}(K)\geq c/2.

On the other hand, from the equidistribution of
\mathbf{v}(h)
in
H
, we know that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=1}^{N}F(\mathbf{v}(h))=\int_{H}F\,dm_{H}\geq c/2.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{U\cap H}(\mathbf{v}(h))\geq F(\mathbf{v}(h))
for all
h
, we have:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{U\cap H}(\mathbf{v}(h))\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=1}^{N}F(\mathbf{v}(h)).

So

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\#\{h\leq N:\mathbf{v}(h)\in U\cap H\}\geq c/2>0.

This implies the existence of the set
X_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{N}
with positive lower density such that for each fixed
h\in X_{\varepsilon}
, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,f\}
, we have
\left\|\{\beta P^{(i)}(h)\}\right\|<\varepsilon
. Note that the density of
X_{\varepsilon}
depends on
\varepsilon
and polynomials.
∎

3.2
Proofs of Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4

We first recall some results from
[
7
,
17
]
.

The following is known as the 1-dimensional form of Weyl’s polynomial equidistribution criterion.

Theorem 3.5

(Theorem 3.2, Chapter 1,
[
17
]
)
.

If
Q(x)=c_{d}x^{d}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is a non-constant polynomial
and at least one coefficient
c_{j}
,
j>0
, is irrational, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\,Q(n)}\;=\;0.

Equivalently, the sequence
\{Q(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly distributed mod
1
.

Since
\mathbb{T}^{f}
is a locally compact abelian group, the next proposition follows by Pontryagin duality.

Proposition 3.6

(Proposition 4.39,
[
7
]
)
.

Let
K\leq\mathbb{T}^{f}
be a closed subgroup and

\Ann(K):=\{\,\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}:\ e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=1\ \text{ for all }\mathbf{z}\in K\,\}.

Then

K\;=\;\big\{\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{T}^{f}:\ e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=1\ \text{ for all }\mathbf{m}\in\Ann(K)\,\big\}.

(2)

To complete the proof of Lemma
3.4
, we also need to establish that
\Ann(H)=R
. This will be done in two steps: first show that
\Ann(H^{\prime})=R
, then we prove that
H=H^{\prime}
(Lemma
3.3
).

Proposition 3.7
.

The annihilator of
H^{\prime}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}
equals
R
, i.e.

\Ann(H^{\prime})=\{\,\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}:\ e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}}=1\ \text{for all }\mathbf{z}\in H^{\prime}\,\}=R.

Proof.

The characters of
\mathbb{T}^{f}
are
\chi_{\mathbf{m}}(z)=e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}}
with
\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}
.
By definition,

\Ann(H^{\prime})=\{\,\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}:\ \chi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{z})=1\text{ for all }\mathbf{z}\in H^{\prime}\,\}.

We first prove that
R\subseteq\Ann(H^{\prime})
.
If
\mathbf{m}\in R
, then
Q_{\mathbf{m}}\equiv 0
. Hence, for every
n
,

\chi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{v}(n))=e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{v}(n)}=e^{2\pi i\,\beta\,Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n)}=e^{2\pi i\cdot 0}=1.

So,
\chi_{\mathbf{m}}
equals
1
on
\{\mathbf{v}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
and, therefore, on the subgroup they generate,
and by continuity also on its closure
H^{\prime}
. Thus,
\mathbf{m}\in\Ann(H^{\prime})
.

We now show that
\Ann(H^{\prime})\subseteq R
.
Let
\mathbf{m}\in\Ann(H^{\prime})
. In particular, we have
\chi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{v}(n))=1
for all

, i.e.

e^{2\pi i\,\beta\,Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n)}=1\quad\text{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\beta\,Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ \text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Suppose, by contradiction, that
Q_{\mathbf{m}}\not\equiv 0
. Then there exists
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}

with
Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n_{0})\neq 0
. From
\beta\,Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n_{0})\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we get

\beta=\dfrac{u}{Q_{\mathbf{m}}(n_{0})}

for some

, which makes
\beta
rational, which leads to a contradiction since

\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}
. Hence,
Q_{\mathbf{m}}\equiv 0
, i.e.
\mathbf{m}\in R
.

In other words,
\Ann(H^{\prime})=R
.
∎

.

From Proposition

.

For any closed subgroup

(3)

Applying (

∎

.

Let

be the character group of

. Then, we know from Proposition

and Lemma

that

. Any non-trivial in

Finite abelian groups – Proof of Theorem
1.4

Since every finite cyclic group is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
for some
N
, we first prove the theorem for
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
for cyclic groups.

Choose
k:=1+2^{r+3}t
. Set
\Delta_{1}:=k^{r-1}\left\lfloor\frac{N-1}{k^{r-1}S_{k}}\right\rfloor
, where
S_{k}=1+\frac{1}{k}+\cdots+\frac{1}{k^{r-1}}
. Then
k^{r-1}\mid\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{1}S_{k}\leq N-1
.

First, we define
E_{1}
as
E_{1}=[0,\Delta_{1}]\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. For
1<i\leq r
, let
\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{i-1}/k
,
E_{i}=(u_{i},v_{i}]
, where
u_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\Delta_{j}
and
v_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Delta_{j}
. Note that if
\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus(E_{1}\cup\cdots\cup E_{r})=R
, then
|R|<k^{r-1}S_{k}
. We can add
R
to any set
E_{i}
, say, to
E_{r}
, to extend it to a complete covering of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, and will not use it in the next steps. Notice that
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
are intervals.

Let
\chi
be any
t-
coloring of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and let
F_{m}
be one of the largest parts. Then we have
|F_{m}|\geq N/t=:\beta\cdot N
.

Let
h_{1}
be a translation such that
|(F_{m}+h_{1})\cap E_{1}|\geq\beta|E_{1}|
. It is not hard to see that such an
h_{1}
exists. Indeed, denote by
E_{1}(x)
the indicator of the interval
E_{1}
and
F_{m}(x)
the indicator function of
F_{m}
. We have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}E_{1}(x)F_{m}(x+h)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}E_{1}(x)\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}F_{m}(x+h)=\frac{|F_{m}|}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}E_{1}(x)\geq\beta|E_{1}|.

The left-hand side is the average of
|(F_{m}+h)\cap E_{1}|
, hence there is an
h_{1}
, for which
|(F_{m}+h_{1})\cap E_{1}|\geq\beta|E_{1}|
.

In the next step, we need to define
h_{2},\ldots,h_{r}
such that
h:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}
satisfies the desired property. We present a complete argument for
h_{2}
and
E_{2}
. For
i\geq 3
, we repeat the same process.

We divide
E_{1}
into
k
equal intervals, say
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
, and note that
|E_{1}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1})|\geq\beta|E_{1}|
. There exists an interval
I_{j}=(a_{j},b_{j}]\subset E_{1}
with
|I_{j}|=\frac{\Delta_{1}}{k}
such that
|I_{j}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1})|\geq\frac{\beta}{2}|I_{j}|
. Assume
I_{j}
is the right most interval with this property. Since
I_{j}
is the right most interval, the intervals
I_{j+1},\ldots,I_{k}
together contain at most
\beta\Delta_{1}/2
elements.

Choose
h_{2}=u_{2}-a_{j}
, then it is clear that
h_{2}\leq\Delta_{1}
,
I_{j}+h_{2}=E_{2}
,
and

|E_{2}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1}+h_{2})|\geq\frac{\beta}{2}|E_{2}|.

Note that
I_{1}+h_{2},\ldots,I_{j-1}+h_{2}
are intervals fully contained in
E_{1}
, we have

|E_{1}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1}+h_{2})|\geq\beta\Delta_{1}-\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}|I_{i}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1})|-|I_{j}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1})|\geq\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2}-\frac{\Delta_{1}}{k}\geq\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{4}

when
k>\frac{4}{\beta}
.

We repeat this process for
E_{3},\ldots,E_{r}
. As a consequence, we obtain
h_{3},\ldots,h_{r}
. Let
h:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}
. At the end, we have
|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{s+1}h_{i})|\geq\frac{\beta}{2^{s+1}}|E_{s}|
for all
1\leq s\leq r-1
, and
|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}h_{i})|\geq\frac{\beta}{2^{s}}|E_{s}|
for all
1\leq s\leq r
.
Using the facts that
k=1+2^{r+3}t
and
h_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
for all
i\geq 2
, we have that for each
1\leq s\leq r-2
,

\sum_{i=s+2}^{r}h_{i}\leq\Delta_{s+1}+\cdots+\Delta_{r-1}\leq\Delta_{s+1}\left(1+\frac{1}{k}+\cdots+\frac{1}{k^{r-s}}\right)\leq\frac{k\Delta_{s+1}}{k-1}\leq\frac{\beta\Delta_{s}}{2^{s+3}}

holds. Therefore,

|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+h)|\geq|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1}+\cdots+h_{s+1})|-\sum_{i=s+2}^{r}h_{i}\geq|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+h_{1}+\cdots+h_{s+1})|-\frac{\beta\Delta_{s}}{2^{s+3}}.

Hence,

|E_{s}\cap(F_{m}+h)|\geq\frac{\beta\Delta_{s}}{2^{s+1}}-\frac{\beta\Delta_{s}}{2^{s+3}}\geq\frac{\beta\Delta_{s}}{2^{s+2}}.

This is equal to

|G|\cdot\frac{\Delta_{1}}{tk^{s-1}2^{s+2}|G|}.

Set
\alpha=\frac{\Delta_{1}}{tk^{r-1}2^{r+2}|G|}
. Note that we need
|G|\geq 1+k^{r-1}|S_{k}|
to guarantee that
\Delta_{r}\geq 1
. A direct computation shows that

\alpha\geq\frac{2}{(1+t2^{r+3})^{r}-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
for general abelian groups.

Assume without loss of generality that
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}\times G^{\prime}
, where

is a finite abelian group and

. We denote the zero element in

by

.

.

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\widetilde{E}_{i}

for each fixed

For each

. Define

Then

\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.

Applying Theorem
1.4
to
\mathbb{Z}_{N}

m^{\ast}\in\{1,\dots,t\}
and an element

such that, for all

h=(h_{1},0_{G^{\prime}})\in G

\displaystyle\big|(F_{m^{\ast}}+h)\cap\widetilde{E}_{i}\big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in E_{i}}\big|\{y\in G^{\prime}:(x-h_{1},y)\in F_{m^{\ast}}\}\big|.

Restricting the sum to those

\big|(F_{m^{\ast}}+h)\cap\widetilde{E}_{i}\big|\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in E_{i}\\

\big|\{y\in G^{\prime}:(x-h_{1},y)\in F_{m^{\ast}}\}\big|=|A_{m^{\ast}}(x-h_{1})|\geq\frac{|G^{\prime}|}{t}.

Therefore,

\big|(F_{m^{\ast}}+h)\cap\widetilde{E}_{i}\big|\geq\frac{|G^{\prime}|}{t}\cdot\big|E_{i}\cap(C_{m^{\ast}}+h_{1})\big|.

Moreover, we know from (
4
) that

\big|E_{i}\cap(C_{m^{\ast}}+h_{1})\big|\geq\alpha(r,t)N,

which implies

\big|(F_{m^{\ast}}+h)\cap\widetilde{E}_{i}\big|\geq\frac{|G^{\prime}|}{t}\cdot\alpha(r,t)N=\frac{\alpha(r,t)}{t}|G|.

This completes the proof.
∎

Special linear groups – Proof of Theorem
1.5

To prove this theorem, we make use of the following lemmas.

Lemma 5.1

(Chebyshev’s inequality)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable with mean

\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
and variance

\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^{2}
.
For every
t>0
,

\Pr\!\bigl(|X-\mu|\geq t\bigr)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t^{2}}.

If
\theta<\mu
then

\Pr\bigl[X\leq\theta\bigr]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(\mu-\theta)^{2}}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix a constant
t\geq 1
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
satisfy

|U|=q^{2}/t
.
For every real number
c
with
0<c<1
and every prime power

q>\frac{4t}{c},

there exists a nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
such that

\#\Bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}:\bigl|U\cap\ell_{\mathbf{v},\lambda}\bigr|\geq\frac{q}{2t}\Bigr\}\geq(1-c)q,

where

\ell_{\mathbf{v},\lambda}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:(x,y)\cdot\mathbf{v}=\lambda\}.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
define

m(\mathbf{v},\lambda):=\bigl|U\cap\ell_{\mathbf{v},\lambda}\bigr|.

Set

\mu:=|U|/q=q/t.
By a direct computation, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}}\sum_{\lambda}m(\mathbf{v},\lambda)^{2}=(q-1)\bigl(|U|^{2}-|U|\bigr)+(q^{2}-1)|U|=(q-1)|U|\bigl(|U|+q\bigr).

There are
q^{2}-1
nonzero vectors
\mathbf{v}
, so for at least one

\mathbf{v}^{\ast}

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}m(\mathbf{v}^{\ast},\lambda)^{2}\leq\frac{(q-1)|U|\bigl(|U|+q\bigr)}{q^{2}-1}\leq\frac{|U|^{2}}{q}+|U|=\frac{q^{3}}{t^{2}}+\frac{q^{2}}{t}.

Slightly abusing the notation, we write
m(\lambda):=m(\mathbf{v}^{\ast},\lambda)
. Let
X
be a random variable defined by
X(\lambda)=m(\lambda)
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is chosen uniformly. Using the above estimate

\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda}(m(\lambda)-\mu)^{2}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\lambda}m(\lambda)^{2}-\mu^{2}\leq\frac{q}{t}.

Set
\theta:=\mu/2=q/(2t)
and define

\mathcal{A}:=\{\lambda:m(\lambda)<\theta\}
.
Applying Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

\Pr\bigl[X<\theta\bigr]\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{(\mu-\theta)^{2}}=\frac{q/t}{(q/2t)^{2}}=\frac{4t}{q}.

Note that
\Pr[X<\theta]=\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{q}
. So
|\mathcal{A}|\leq 4t
.
If
q>4t/c
, then there exists a set
\mathcal{B}
of at least
(1-c)q
elements
\lambda
such that
m(\lambda)\geq q/2t
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
I_{1}\cup I_{2}\cup\cdot\cup I_{r}
be a partition of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
|I_{1}|=\cdots=|I_{r-1}|=\left\lfloor\frac{q}{r}\right\rfloor
and
|I_{r}|=q-(r-1)\left\lfloor\frac{q}{r}\right\rfloor
.

Let
E_{i}
be the set of matrices of the form

\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix},

where
xw-yz=1
,
x\in I_{i}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
y,z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then the sets
E_{i}
form a partition of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

For each partition
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}F_{i}
of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, let
F_{m}
be the set such that
|F_{m}|\geq q^{3}/2t
. Assume without loss of generality that the projection of
F_{m}
onto the first two coordinates, denoted by
F_{m}^{1,2}
, is of size at least
q^{2}/4t
, and for each
(x,y)\in F_{m}^{1,2}
, the number of
z
is at least
q/4t
.

Applying Lemma
5.2
with
c=\frac{1}{2r}
and
U=F_{m}^{1,2}
, there exists a nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
such that

\#\Bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}:\bigl|F_{m}^{1,2}\cap\ell_{\mathbf{v},\lambda}\bigr|\geq\frac{q}{8t}\Bigr\}\geq(1-c)q,

where

\ell_{\mathbf{v},\lambda}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:(x,y)\cdot\mathbf{v}=\lambda\},
provided that
q
is large enough. Let

h=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&-\frac{1}{v_{2}}\\
v_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

We now show that
|F_{m}\cdot h\cap E_{i}|\geq\frac{q^{3}}{4rt^{2}}
.
Indeed, since
\#\{(x,y)\cdot\mathbf{v}\colon(x,y)\in F_{m}^{1,2}\}\geq(1-c)q
, we can conclude that the set
\mathbf{v}\cdot F_{m}^{1,2}
intersects
I_{i}
in at least
|I_{i}|/2
elements. For each element
\lambda
in the intersection, we also have that

\#\{(x,y)\in F_{m}^{1,2}\colon(x,y)\cdot(v_{1},v_{2})=\lambda\}\geq\frac{q}{8t}.

Furthermore, for each
(x,y)\in F_{m}^{1,2}
, the number of
z
such that

\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix}\in F_{m}

is at least
q/4t
. Hence, for each
\lambda
, there are at least
\frac{q^{2}}{32t^{2}}
matrices
M
in
F_{m}
such that

M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&-\frac{1}{v_{2}}\\
v_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&*\\
*&*\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ \mbox{where}\penalty\ \penalty\ *\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.

This means that
|F_{m}\cdot h\cap E_{i}|\geq|E_{i}|/2=\frac{q}{2r}\cdot\frac{q^{2}}{32t^{2}}=\frac{q^{3}}{64rt^{2}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎
