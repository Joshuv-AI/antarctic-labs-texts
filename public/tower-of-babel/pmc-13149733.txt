How parents and community care professionals use a genetic diagnosis to inform care: expanding the concept of utility
Authors: Amy A. Lemke, Katelyn C. McNamara, R. Jean Cadigan, Roselle Ponsaran, Aaron J. Goldenberg, Carla A. Rich, Candice R. Finnila, Gregory M. Cooper, Kyle B. Brothers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on genomic testing outcomes for children with neurodevelopmental conditions often emphasizes clinical and personal utility, but rarely considers how a genomic diagnosis impacts care from non-physician community-based professionals such as those in private practice, early intervention, or school settings. Therefore, this study explored how caregivers and community care professionals use a genomic diagnosis to inform care. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 23 caregivers of children with a genomic diagnosis and 30 community care professionals providing care to the child. Directed and summative content analysis was performed. Reported impacts included (1) improved care provision, (2) informed future planning, (3) enhanced social and community support, (4) enhanced understanding, (5) increased access to therapy-related services and equipment, (6) improved service quality and 5) elevated patient advocacy and engagement. Potential disutility arose from limited diagnostic information, provider knowledge gaps, insurance denials, and provider fear of treating rare conditions. A key neutral/mixed finding was that the diagnosis did not always impact care. Barriers to potential utility included financial burden, confusion about next steps, provider knowledge gaps, and lack of evidence-based guidance for rare diagnoses. Findings highlight that caregivers and community professionals may experience impacts from a genomic diagnosis (positive, negative, or neutral) that are understudied in empirical research, potentially because traditional concepts of utility fail to incorporate these domains. Broadening this conceptual framing could inform empirical research focused on identifying these effects and informing potential interventions. Further work is needed to characterize and quantify genomic test utility and disutility across diverse contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12687-026-00891-x.

Introduction

In recent decades, advancements in genomic diagnostic technologies have transformed physicians’ ability to identify underlying causes for children’s health conditions, including neurodevelopmental conditions like autism, as well as conditions formerly thought to be driven primarily by non-genetic mechanisms, such as cerebral palsy. Parallel with these advancements, multidisciplinary researchers have sought to define and document the utility created by these genomic diagnoses (Smith et al. 2021). This emphasis on the potential value of diagnostic genomic testing may have impacted how researchers and clinicians conceptualize and empirically assess positive, negative, and neutral aspects of a genomic diagnosis. Utility has tended to be classified either as clinical utility, the potential for a diagnosis to directly inform medical management, or as personal utility, the psychological or informational value a diagnosis may have for patients and caregivers. However, even with this dichotomization of utility, there has been a stronger emphasis on clinical utility, given its emphasis on the potential for a genomic diagnosis to lead to curative interventions or mitigate significant morbidity associated with genetic conditions. To that end, insurance coverage or other funding decisions also tend to emphasize clinical utility because of its relevance to medical management and potential cost-effectiveness (Phillips et al. 2022).

Yet not all discourse on the value of a genomic diagnosis has made such distinctions (Watts and Newson 2025) and conceptual frameworks for clinical utility in particular have varied significantly. Some more inclusive frameworks consider the other practical value a genomic diagnosis might carry (Burke et al. 2002; Smith et al. 2022). For example, in one account, clinical utility is considered to encompass any application of a diagnosis that “will lead to an improved health outcome” (Burke et al. 2002). Nevertheless, most conceptualizations of clinical utility have focused primarily on curative medical interventions or those that prevent symptom progression through pharmaceutical or surgical means (Hayeems et al. 2020).

The dichotomous framework for classifying the utility of a genomic diagnosis has tended to leave some potential forms of utility understudied or undervalued. Prior empirical research on the perspectives of parents, guardians, and other caregivers of children (hereafter caregivers) has revealed dimensions of potential utility that tend to be underrecognized. Caregivers interviewed about why they desired a genomic diagnosis for their child stated that they would like to take action if their child were to receive a genomic diagnosis, such as developing a better overall strategy for working with community care professionals who provide services through community institutions and organizations, or helping their child gain access to various services, including within the school system (Childerhose et al. 2021). Building on these findings, we introduced the notions of middle ground utility to describe an underdeveloped space between clinical and personal utility, and community care utility, which we defined as the “potential effects on psychological, social, and health outcomes as a result of modifications made to care provided by educators, physical or occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, behavior analysts, and non-physician mental health providers after a child has received a genomic diagnosis.” (Brothers et al. 2024).

As described, most research examining the outcomes of genomic testing has focused on clinical and personal utility. However, studies based on this dichotomous conception of utility have tended to overlook the potential impact of a genomic test diagnosis (positive, negative, and neutral) on access to and care provided by community care professionals. We use the term “community care professionals” to include physical therapists, speech and language pathologists, occupational therapists, and educators who work in private practice, early intervention, or school settings. To address this gap, we conducted a cross-sectional qualitative interview study to examine how caregivers and community care professionals attempt to use a genomic diagnosis to inform care and gain access to resources and services, in their efforts intended to improve quality of life for children. In doing so, we aim not only to describe these impacts but also to contribute to evolving conceptualizations of utility by incorporating domains relevant to community care contexts.

Methods

Study overview

The study design was guided by quality standards for qualitative research and the standards for reporting qualitative research (O’Brien et al. 2014; Miles, Huberman, and Saldana 2019). The University of Louisville’s Institutional Review Board approved this study and participants consented to take part in the research.

Data collection

Sampling

Children were included who had received a diagnosis for a pediatric medical condition by exome or genome sequencing, and who were obtaining care from one or more community care professionals for that condition. The phenotype for inclusion was intentionally left open-ended in order to elicit as broad a range of experiences as possible.

A maximum variation sampling strategy (Palinkas et al. 2015) was used in selection of site of care, child’s phenotype and clinical diagnosis, child’s race and ethnicity, child’s age and gender and rural vs. suburban residence. Caregivers of children who met criteria were recruited at three clinical sites (University of Louisville, Vanderbilt University, University of Alabama at Birmingham) and three cohorts were established to include (1) children who received a genetic diagnosis 1–10 years ago from previous research studies (Cohort A), (2) children who received a recent diagnosis (6 to < 12 months) through existing sequencing at the three clinical sites (Cohort B) and (3) children who would have otherwise qualified for the study but who were unable to access existing opportunities for sequencing (lack of health insurance, etc.) (Cohort C). The third cohort received no cost sequencing from a clinical sequencing laboratory at one of the study sites. Orthogonal confirmation of all pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in a CAP/CLIA accredited laboratory were obtained before results return. Findings were reported to clinical sites, who disclosed them to caregivers using existing clinical workflows.

Recruitment

Once clinical site staff determined eligibility and obtained informed consent, they connected families with qualitative research staff to schedule an interview with the caregiver. Study personnel approached one or both parents/caregivers to participate in the study. Caregivers were asked to complete a HIPAA/FERPA release form granting permission for the child’s community care professionals (e.g., teachers, physical or occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, behavior analysts, non-physician mental health providers) to discuss the child’s case. Study personnel then contacted these professionals by phone, email or text message to invite them to enroll in the study.

Brief surveys

Caregivers and community care professionals completed a brief demographic survey in-person or online. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Louisville.

Interviews

Individuals receiving positive genomic results as a part of the study (Cohort C) were interviewed six months after getting their results. Semi-structured interviews allowed for in-depth discussion of rich, detailed and nuanced participant perspectives. Interview guides were developed with input from an expert study Advisory Committee (which included, among others, the parents of children with genomic diagnoses) and pilot tested with individuals similar to the study population.

Caregiver and community care professional interview guides included questions about any changes in care or services for the child because of the genomic diagnosis as well as steps taken to learn more about the diagnosis. The caregiver interview guide also included questions about seeking and receiving the diagnosis, sharing results with professionals, and connecting with other caregivers. The community care professional interview guide also included questions about training and experience in working with children with genetic conditions.

The caregivers and professionals completed recorded telephone interviews lasting approximately 60 min. Caregivers received $100 as compensation for their time. The community care professionals were not offered compensation, as past experience had indicated that professionals are often willing to participate in order to improve understanding of their profession and are often unsure whether they are permitted by their employer to accept an incentive. Interviews were conducted until saturation of key concepts, or where additional data did not lead to new insights, was noted (Starks and Trinidad 2007; Saunders et al. 2018).

Data analysis

Interview recordings were professionally transcribed and uploaded to Dedoose (version 9.0.107), a qualitative data management and analysis tool (SocioCultural Research Consultants, LLC 2023). A provisional codebook was developed deductively based on the domains incorporated into the interview guide, and additional codes were added inductively after team review and discussion of a subset of ten initial transcripts. Coding on this subset was performed by one coder, and then reviewed for completeness and consistency by another coder. Three coders were involved in the data analysis and all bring extensive experience in qualitative research and in the ethical, legal and social implications of genomics, as well as prior experience working with families and other stakeholders in the genomics field. The coders participated in collaborative development of the interview guides, which helped to establish a shared framework and interpretive lens prior to data collection. This shared background and preparation may have informed their understanding of participant experiences and shaped how they approached coding and interpretation of responses related to the impact of genomic diagnoses. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussion, including thorough refinement of code definitions, renaming codes, etc. This process was repeated with new transcripts over the course of the study, with periodic checks of inter-coder agreement, revisions to the codebook, and recoding transcripts as needed to capture any refinements (Bradley et al. 2007; Raskind et al. 2019).

Based on participants’ responses to neutral, open-ended questions about changes following the genomic diagnosis (e.g., “Did sharing NAME’S genetic diagnosis with the school lead to any changes? If so, what were they?”), we inductively developed codes for the types of changes described, including a code for “no changes.” After coding all transcripts, coders reviewed each transcript in full and wrote analytic memos linked to relevant passages. These memos documented whether descriptions of changes (or lack thereof) reflected a positive impact, negative impact, mixed impact, or no impact, and noted any barriers to achieving positive impact.

For this process, four domains emerged: potential utility (positive effects), potential disutility (negative effects), neutral/mixed effects, and barriers to potential utility. The domains were not predetermined but emerged from this iterative process. We then analyzed the memos to identify themes within each domain. Directed and summative content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005) of these memo types (n = 656), with reference to underlying quotes as needed, facilitated synthesis of these concepts and generation of key findings. In this paper, we present the main findings along with illustrative participant quotes, including concrete examples of perceived positive and negative effects.

Although our focus was on effects realized in community care settings, we also documented potential effects outside these settings to provide context. This approach allowed us to explore whether community care impacts fit within the traditional dichotomous model of utility (personal vs. clinical), or whether these impacts suggest the need to revise this conceptual model of utility to include other types of utility beyond personal vs. clinical.

Results

Child diagnoses

Given that many of the genomic diagnoses received by participating children were for rare and ultra-rare conditions, individual genomic diagnoses are not reported as this information could be identifying. Overall, the majority of participating children had received genomic diagnoses for neurodevelopmental conditions, with a minority receiving a genomic diagnosis for neuromuscular conditions, congenital structural differences, or hearing impairment. The genetic variation underlying these conditions varied greatly, including mosaic chromosomal aneuploidy, copy number and other structural variation, and sequence variants.

Interview findings

We present findings following the four categories described above: 1) Potential Utility, 2) Potential Disutility, 3) Neutral/Mixed and 4) Barriers to Achieving Potential Utility. Utility memos documented the perceived or potential value and benefits of genomic testing.

Potential Utility

Caregiver potential utility

Caregivers described positive impacts of a genomic diagnosis in four main areas: (1) emotional or cognitive benefit, (2) improved care provision, (3) informed future planning, and (4) enhanced social and community support.

With respect to emotional or cognitive aspects, relief to have a genomic diagnosis and knowing more about the child’s condition was helpful in setting or changing expectations within the context of community care. One mother described:

I think having the diagnosis gives us a more clear picture that she’s always probably going to be behind in the physical milestones. And it takes her a little bit more time to hit those goals and hit those markers. (Cohort A)

I think having the diagnosis gives us a more clear picture that she’s always probably going to be behind in the physical milestones. And it takes her a little bit more time to hit those goals and hit those markers. (Cohort A)

Another mother shared:

But there’s something that’s very powerful in having an answer, and being able to know what to look for, instead of not knowing. And you can prevent as much as you can. So I think it was kind of a relief that we finally had some answers, and we could take control, as best we could, of the situation. (Cohort C)

But there’s something that’s very powerful in having an answer, and being able to know what to look for, instead of not knowing. And you can prevent as much as you can. Community care professional potential utility

The main areas of genomic diagnosis utility described by community care professionals included: (1) enhanced understanding, (2) increased access to therapy-related services and equipment, (3) improved service quality, (4) informed family planning, and (5) enhanced parent engagement and advocacy on behalf of the child.

The community care providers described the impacts of the genomic diagnosis in providing a better understanding to guide effective treatment and goal setting. An early intervention occupational therapist described:When there is an existing diagnosis, it definitely helps prepare my interventions going in, as well as expectations. It kind of gives me a perspective of what to expect, a more realistic goal-setting. (Cohort C)

When there is an existing diagnosis, it definitely helps prepare my interventions going in, as well as expectations. It kind of gives me a perspective of what to expect, a more realistic goal-setting. (Cohort C)

The community care professionals also perceived that the genomic diagnosis led to improved access to care by increasing frequency of services, and helped facilitate approval of equipment and assistive devices. One early intervention physical therapist mentioned:

It helps drastically with insurance companies, because insurance companies understand diagnoses more than they understand individual issues. If I’m just saying, “Oh, they’re just not walking,” I think an insurance company more or less looks at it like, “Well, I don’t know. Figure out why they’re not walking.” Versus, if you have a label on them, I think it just helps pull more for those kinds of different services than if you don’t have them. (Cohort B)

Potential disutility

Memos on “disutility” captured negative aspects or impacts of a genomic testing diagnosis. There was some unavoidable overlap of this theme with “barriers to utility.” For the sake of analysis, however, an effect was classified as “potential disutility” rather than a “barrier to potential utility” if interview participants emphasized negative consequences of a test result (emotional effects, etc.).

Caregiver potential disutility

Caregivers described negative impacts related to getting a rare genetic diagnosis including: (1) distress in having little information about the diagnosis, (2) frustration with a dearth of providers able to understand and treat the condition, and (3) concerns about insurance denials. Of those, the most often mentioned impact described by the parents was lack of information on the genomic test result. This caused distress, a “gut punch” in finding no information available on the rare disease. A mother described:

So it did take a process, in some instances, to try to get those services even after diagnosis, because most people they look at you and go, ‘What? What’s that?’ Or they’ll try to Google it [the diagnosis] and they can’t really find anything about it. And they are like, ‘Are you making this up?’ No, I’m not. Thanks. It was still a fight at first. (Cohort A)

So it did take a process, in some instances, to try to get those services even after diagnosis, because most people they look at you and go, ‘What? What’s that?’ Or they’ll try to Google it [the diagnosis] and they can’t really find anything about it. And they are like, ‘Are you making this up?’ No, I’m not. Thanks. It was still a fight at first. (Cohort A)

This lack of information also made it difficult for caregivers to provide justification to insurance for services. Another mother discussed concerns about insurance discrimination and the financial impact of lack of insurance for her child due to the diagnosis:Mainly because pretty much he’s knocked out of insurance now. Once he’s off our health insurance, we’ve been told that health insurance, if he can get it, is going to be outrageous. He’s denied life insurance. So financially, that’s the biggest hit that’s really going to put on him. (Cohort A)

Community care professional potential disutility

Community care professionals likewise indicated a lack of information about the diagnosis as a problem in advising about future care planning. One potentially negative impact, gleaned from a provider comment, was that a genetic diagnosis with a poor prognosis might make the provider more likely to quit trying new skills with a child, or that parents may treat a child differently because of the diagnosis. This could affect expectations and service opportunities for the child. One speech-language therapist in an outpatient setting described fear in managing a rare diagnosis:

Some people [therapists, school personnel] can see some diagnoses and get scared and not know what to do. We have therapists in the past that have been like, “I don’t know what to do with that.” (Cohort A).

Some people [therapists, school personnel] can see some diagnoses and get scared and not know what to do. We have therapists in the past that have been like, “I don’t know what to do with that.” (Cohort A).

Neutral/Mixed

Recognizing that not all impacts can be classified easily as positive or negative, we developed a third memo category to document a neutral perspective or a combination of both positive and negative impacts from receiving a genomic diagnosis. One area noted in this category was that in some cases the genomic diagnosis did not lead to changes in care. It may have depended on the type of setting or the child may have already qualified for services without the genomic diagnosis. One physical therapist in an outpatient setting described how they focus on the child’s presentation rather than the genetic diagnosis to guide care:

Like you treat what you see in front of you. You’re not treating a diagnosis. So we rely heavily on our handling skills, or ability to look at the patient and see what their particular deficits are, and where they need to work. And not what their diagnosis is and not what the paper says. (Cohort A)

Like you treat what you see in front of you. You’re not treating a diagnosis. So we rely heavily on our handling skills, or ability to look at the patient and see what their particular deficits are, and where they need to work. And not what their diagnosis is and not what the paper says. (Cohort A)

Some participants brought up how a genomic diagnosis is only part of the puzzle and that there are other factors that may impact a child’s progress. In addition, a rare genomic condition may have a spectrum or an unknown presentation, as well as variability in penetrance, so the initial diagnosis itself might not offer a clear pathway. Also noted as a “mixed” impact, a genomic diagnosis may frame expectations in ways that offer increased hope for identifying better care or alternatively could have negative consequences by indicating limited possibilities for effective treatment or narrowing the scope of achievable goals.

Barriers to achieving potential utility

In some cases, participants encountered challenges that hindered the ability to achieve the potential benefits of a genomic diagnosis. So while there may have been acknowledgement or desire for possible benefit from a genomic diagnosis, various hurdles impeded the receipt of those benefits.

Caregiver - barriers to potential utility

In terms of barriers to achieving utility post-diagnosis, caregivers discussed how the diagnosis led to financial strain from therapy costs, equipment expenses, and work adjustments needed to accommodate care, lack of clarity about next steps, and inability to find informational resources. Services were limited locally for some, and one parent described disagreement with a provider about services needed based on the diagnosis.

One mother shared how she adapted her work schedule:

I work weekends now. I shifted my work schedule from during the week to weekends only. So that I can make sure that I get her to therapies and to all her appointments. There’s not really not a portion of my life that the diagnosis hasn’t touched (Cohort A).

I work weekends now. I shifted my work schedule from during the week to weekends only. So that I can make sure that I get her to therapies and to all her appointments. There’s not really not a portion of my life that the diagnosis hasn’t touched (Cohort A).

Another mother described how her husband took on childcare:

My husband actually quit his job to stay home full-time with [child], which was definitely the right decision… definitely changed our way of life in jobs and financially. (Cohort A)

My husband actually quit his job to stay home full-time with [child], which was definitely the right decision… definitely changed our way of life in jobs and financially. (Cohort A)

In describing costs associated with the diagnosis and care, one father said:

Together, these quotes demonstrate barriers that families encountered in trying to make use of their child’s genomic diagnosis, and the changes in their lives they decided to make to accommodate or work around those barriers.

Community care professionals - barriers to potential utility

One barrier that community care professionals mentioned was the need for further education, due to a scarcity of readily available information on rare genetic diagnoses. They also indicated lack of available clinical genetics training opportunities and continuing education class costs. In addition to limited informational resources on the rare genetic diagnosis, lack of evidence-based guidelines was mentioned as a problem and the impact that this has on developing successful care plans. Community care professionals also shared a challenge in having limited time to address rare genomic conditions that involve more complicated care coordination. They also acknowledged system failure to meet the needs of children and caregivers. One early intervention physical therapist described a barrier encountered with insurance companies:We were able to justify a few more services through our program, but insurance is a funny little beast. They don’t always understand some of the genetic disorders, and so they don’t cover them as readily. But I really think it’s much easier for them to understand cerebral palsy or autism. That’s something they’ve heard of. They know what that is. And honestly, it’s somebody in an office that is reviewing this that may or may not have medical knowledge. It may be a nurse, but it could just be a caseworker that doesn’t have any medical background, depending on how they hire and all that. (Cohort A)

Discussion

Caregivers have long expressed interest in using genomic information to improve the support they receive from community care professionals (Brothers et al. 2024). However, empirical research is lacking on whether a genomic diagnosis impacts care in these contexts in positive, negative, or neutral ways. We hypothesized that this gap in the empirical literature had been driven, at least in part, due to a conceptual model that dichotomizes utility (and disutility) into clinical utility and personal utility, and which therefore does not draw attention to the possibility that a genomic diagnosis could affect outcomes in community care settings. Our goal in this study, then, was to explore the effects of a genomic diagnosis in the care provided by community care professionals to begin to understand (1) whether such effects actually occur after a genomic diagnosis is received. And, if such effects are occurring, (2) how they seem to relate to clinical utility and personal utility from a conceptual perspective, and (3) what types of effects we should be identifying in future research.

Given that we were conducting qualitative research with a relatively small sample of caregivers and community care professionals, we did not set out to prove that such a diagnosis can create a net benefit or to quantify how frequently those benefits are realized. However, in seeking to demonstrate what types of impact genomic information may be having, we did uncover examples where caregivers or community care professionals perceived that a genomic diagnosis had created a tangible benefit (like justifying insurance payment for a wheelchair) or harm (distress about lack of information related to how therapies can be tailored to the diagnosis). We include these here as examples of the types of impacts that might be excluded by the dichotomous conceptual model and that future empirical work should seek to characterize and quantify.

Specifically, perceived benefits that extend beyond traditional definitions of clinical utility include more informed long-term planning and improvements in care coordination and access to services. While these outcomes are sometimes categorized under the umbrella of personal utility, such a framing may obscure the broader implications for health and well-being that these findings suggest: beyond the merely personal, to instead suggest practical value for the child that can potentially impact their quality of life. The diagnosis in some cases also served to enhance caregivers’ confidence, tailor therapeutic interventions, and foster child advocacy. These findings suggest that genomic diagnoses have the potential to influence the therapies families pursue and inform the practices and perspectives of community professionals. More broadly, they support an expansion of the conceptual model of utility that not only encompasses treatment decisions in hospitals or doctor’s offices, but also the ongoing processes in community care contexts through which care is planned, coordinated, and delivered.

On the other hand, participants also reported negative impacts of genomic diagnosis, particularly in the context of rare genetic conditions. For conditions whose natural history or penetrance is uncertain or poorly understood, or whose symptoms present along a spectrum, genetic information may be less valuable to a family or community care professional, or even lead to false assumptions about attainable health goals or treatment effectiveness. However, more certainty about penetrance, natural course, and severity of symptoms could also serve insurance companies and others to justify limiting educational and therapeutic services. Furthermore, a rare genetic diagnosis may not have a corresponding billing code for community care professionals, which could result in insurance companies being less likely to reimburse for services than a more general diagnosis such as developmental delay. Finally, eligibility for early intervention or special education services varies significantly by state, with some programs requiring specific diagnostic labels that rare genetic conditions may not meet (MacDuffie et al. 2025).

A subset of participants described the genomic diagnosis as having little or no impact on care, reflecting a more neutral or mixed experience. In these cases, the diagnosis was not perceived to change therapy, provision of services, or educational support. While genetic information may not impact the care for every child, these findings suggest that more work is needed to understand whether there are actions community care professionals could take to better evaluate and practically integrate genomic information into the services they provide, and whether such actions could create a net benefit in some cases. Notably, both caregivers and community care professionals identified several barriers to achieving potential utility. Perhaps the most compelling were the lack of training for community care professionals on how to incorporate genomic diagnoses into care and the limited information available online about these conditions. In many cases, there are very few children described in the literature with these conditions (Hamosh et al. 2022), and few research networks exist to aggregate and disseminate relevant knowledge. In response, many community care professionals and caregivers turned to listservs and online parent groups as informal hubs for knowledge-sharing and practical advice. However, these resources are typically parent-focused and rarely offer information or recommendations designed specifically for or by community care professionals. This could result in caregivers having more general information about a condition than professionals, and neither having the sort of specialized information professionals would need to tailor their care for specific conditions.

Research on the diagnostic odyssey highlights the prolonged and difficult journey many families face before receiving a diagnosis, as well as the limited clinical utility for some rare diagnoses particularly when no targeted treatments exist (Childerhose et al. 2021). Our results reinforce these findings and highlight the care intervention journey that occurs post-diagnosis, including the spectrum of impacts that a genetic diagnosis may have in the context of community-based care. In fact, this study’s novelty is its focus on community care professionals, who have been overlooked in studies on the impact of a genomic diagnosis. Our findings suggest that the dichotomous model of utility does not account for positive, negative, and neutral impacts being created by genomic diagnoses in educational, occupational, physical, speech, and other therapeutic contexts.

These findings also point to important implications for systems and policy. They suggest the need for more education in genomics across these professional roles, increased care coordination across sectors, and policy reforms to improve access to services (MacDuffie et al. 2025). Interventions could include online platforms for bi-directional collaboration across professional sectors, and between parents and professionals. Community providers often develop valuable insights into how best to care for children with different genomic diagnoses. These insights could be beneficial not just to each other, but also to community care professionals and physicians. Additional interventions could address support in creating care plans, such as genomics-informed care guidelines tailored to community care professionals. At the policy level, interventions could include the development of standardized billing codes for rare genetic conditions. States could also improve access to services by diversifying the diagnoses that qualify children for programs like early intervention and special education.

This qualitative study provides rich insights that may inform future research and community care strategies. It is among the first to elicit perspectives from non-physician professionals about their ability to utilize a genomic diagnosis in practice. However, the study has limitations. Because our goal was to understand how caregivers and professionals applied a genomic diagnosis to care, we did not interview those whose children underwent sequencing but received no diagnosis or only variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Future research on these populations would be helpful, however, as receiving no diagnosis or receiving a VUS result could also have impacts – positive, negative, or neutral – on community care received by these children. Additionally, participants were from the U.S. Southeast and Midwest, so findings may not be generalizable to other regions or populations. Another lack of variability is that the sample was primarily white, educated, urban, and employed. Because study-based sequencing was offered in Cohort C, participation may have been influenced by access to testing, introducing potential participant bias and limiting generalizability. Although we adopted a sampling strategy designed to maximize heterogeneity, our sample remained relatively homogenous due to the nature of the larger population from which is was drawn. State policies and the availability of community resources differ, which likely influenced participants’ access and experiences with community services. Future research should examine the potential effects of a genomic diagnosis in community care contexts across a broader range of geographic settings and sociocultural backgrounds.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight opportunities to broaden the concept of utility used to inform the design of empirical studies focused on the real-world impacts of a genomic diagnosis. This broader framing may also have important implications for professional practice guidelines, education and training, insurance coverage, and policies governing early intervention and special education. By expanding the framing of utility, we can better identify a range of implications and potential interventions for the social and organizational systems serving children with complex genetic conditions. However, additional research is needed to address the barriers to achieving these potential benefits in practice, particularly in light of regional and contextual differences in service provision.
