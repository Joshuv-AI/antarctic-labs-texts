Strategies for strengthening eye care in India: a consensus-based recommendations
Authors: Taraprasad Das, Rohit Khanna, Radhika Krishnan, Umang Mathur, Somasheila Murthy, Maneck Nicholson, Dhivya Ramasamy, Varsha Rathi, Thulsiraj D. Ravilla, Shalinder Sabherwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIndia is committed to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness “2030 In Sight” recommendations to improve eye health and visual impairment. Public health experts in India were invited to discuss and recommend strategies to reduce blindness by 2030 and consolidate the gains related to quality eye care by 2047. The experts reviewed the World Report on Vision, WHO’s 2030 In Sight strategy, and the status of eye care in India, drawing on published literature and policy documents. They proposed 10 interlinked strategic pillars: universal coverage, human resources, financial sustainability, equity, partnerships, health information systems, quality care, elderly and marginalised eyecare, technology adoption, and monitoring of key indices. Using the Likert scale the experts arrived at consensus on the following recommendations: task shifting, measuring quality eyecare indicators, integrating health services, leveraging technology, expanding cashless services, and improving referral eyecare system. The recommendations also include target-oriented spending, centralised management of available resources, and interministerial commitment.FundingHyderabad Eye Research Foundation, Hyderabad, India.

Introduction

Globally, at least 2.2 billion people live with visual impairment.1 The burden of vision loss is disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries. In recent years, India has reduced blindness from 1.1% in 1976 to 0.36% in 2019, primarily through concentrated efforts of the National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment (NPCB VI) and private stakeholders.2 Existing national strategies like national programme for cataract surgery, school eye health initiatives, and collaboration with non-governmental organisations demonstrate India’s capacity to provide eye care on a large scale. However, with rising life expectancy, rapid urbanisation, and the growing burden of uncorrected refractive error, Diabetic Retinopathy (DR), glaucoma, and Age-related Macular Degeneration (AMD), there is a need to strengthen and reform existing strategies.3 The World Report on Vision (WRV, 2019)1 recommended the Integrated People-Centred Eye Care (IPEC) framework to shift eye care from vertical programme to integrated approaches within universal health coverage.4 The “2030 In Sight” initiative by the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness (IAPB) focuses on eye health and visual impairment to improve eye care services worldwide.5 To advance eye health and accelerate progress towards global goals, India needs to adopt several new eye health strategies over the next decade. Currently, the strategies are fragmented, and the inter-stakeholder cooperation is weak.

Here we outline the strategic directions and recommend actionable pathways to ensure that every person living in India has access to quality eye care by 2030, and to consolidate the gains in eye health by 2047 based on expert consensus recommendations.

We invited public eye health experts from secondary and tertiary eye care facilities in India to form a discussion group. An invitation was sent to 13 experts identified at eye care facilities with extensive clinical, public health, and research experience. It included four eye care institutes (including one public health institute) and one public health foundation. Ten experts agreed to contribute, and one agreed to serve as an expert reviewer. They belonged to three zones of India: two from north (Delhi), three from west (Mumbai), three from central (Hyderabad), and two from south (Madurai). Two experts declined due to time constraints. The experts’ pool consisted of public health ophthalmologists (three), public health experts (four), and public health administrators (three).

Search strategy

A structured PubMed search was undertaken using ‘MeSH’ (Medical Subject Headings) and ‘tiab’ (Title and Abstract) words: Eye Diseases OR Ophthalmology OR Blindness OR Visual impairment OR Epidemiology OR Prevalence OR Mass Screening OR Delivery of Health Care OR Health Services Accessibility OR Epidemiology OR Health system OR Service delivery OR AI in Ophthalmology, all confined to India. The generated data was shared with the experts to review vis-à-vis the WRV1 and the IAPB 2030 In Sight strategy document5 to identify key pillars for developing an India-specific strategy framework. The group examined each strategy along three formats: (1) assess current position, (2) identify the gaps, and (3) suggest solutions to address them. The group referenced relevant articles on the strategies to reduce the eye care burden in India to inform these suggestions. Voting on each proposed statement was conducted using a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree, and strongly disagree). Consensus was defined as three-fourths or more of the experts voting “strongly agree” or “agree”. There were no disagreements. The final document was developed following several rounds of discussion (via electronic sharing) before finalisation.

The experts identified ten interlinked strategic pillars to provide a framework for achieving universal, equitable, and sustainable eye health.

Identified gaps and recommendations for quality eye care.

Integration of eye care into Universal Health Coverage (UHC)

India has taken meaningful steps to integrate eye care into the UHC framework. Health & Wellness Centres, which serve 3000–5000 people, manned by a community health worker provide primary-level eye care services.6

The important gaps are limited access to specialised eye care to the rural, tribal, and marginalised populations, inadequate integration of eye care into primary healthcare systems, and significant out-of-pocket spending for subspecialty eye care. Experts suggested four strategies in following priority: (1) Integrating eye health into the existing primary health care system in India through augmented resources and training of manpower (2) Optimising the public health insurance system to provide cashless eye care services for specialised eye care needs, (3) Developing multisectoral collaboration between departments of education, labour, and maternal and child health at the level of program implementation, and (4) Integrating eye care with other systems of medicine, such as AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy) wherever feasible.7 The voluntary eye care sector in India has demonstrated successful integrated eye care models in its operational areas, which could be explored for further expansion.8

Strengthening human resources

The population of India is likely to exceed to 1.5 billion by 2030. According to IPEC specifications, India would need 300,728 community eye care facilities, each serving a population of 5000; 30,072 primary eye care facilities, each serving a population of 50,000; 3007 secondary eye care facilities, each serving a population of 500,000; and 300 tertiary eye care facilities, each serving a population of 5 million.9 India needs qualified eye health workforce, including ophthalmologists and allied ophthalmic personnel, to meet this demand.

The WHO recommendation for ophthalmologists in Asia is 10 per million population.9 In India, it is 15 per million population. While it exceeds the WHO recommendations for Asia, there is significant geographic variation in the distribution of specialists within the country, including the urban-rural divide. For example, in 2022–2023, the mean number of ophthalmologists per million people in Maharashtra state (state with the highest share of India’s GDP) was 32, and in the Andamans and Nicobar Islands state (state with the lowest share of India's GDP) was 4.10 In the city of Mumbai, one of the largest city in India, the distribution of ophthalmologists per 100,000 people according to urban rural area was 12.4 and 3.0 per million people respectively.11

Addressing gaps in human resources could involve: (1) Task shifting from ophthalmologists to other trained ophthalmic personnel on selective non-invasive tasks, (2) Expanding the training of allied ophthalmic personnel and effectively utilising their services, (3) Standardised curriculum, accreditation, and national certification for ophthalmic services, (4) Rural retention and incentivisation of specialists, and (5) Developing career pathways and local engagement. Several institutions in India offer training and capacity-building for staff at different levels, particularly allied ophthalmic personnel.12 Task shifting allows efficient use of specialist time; while incentivising rural service ensures equitable coverage.13 Community health workers, including Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA) and Anganwadi workers could be strengthened in their capacity to offer basic eye care services and referrals.14 Promoting skills among local youths and engaging them in eye care has also improved access to eye care and reduced blindness.15

Enhancing eye health information systems

India has undertaken several initiatives to gather eye health data, including Rapid Assessment of Avoidable Blindness (RAAB) surveys and reporting mechanisms under the NPCB VI.16 These surveys have generated valuable insights into the prevalence of visual impairment secondary to cataract and refractive error, as well as on service outputs such as cataract surgical volume. However, RAAB, and similar surveys only provide cross-sectional snapshots of particular age groups (>50 years), focussing narrowly on blindness and visual impairment without corresponding clinical outcome related information.17 Other major gap lies in the fragmented data and limited integration between public and private eye care providers.

India needs a national registry of common eye diseases, which functions as integrated, outcome-oriented, equity-sensitive, and actionable eye health information system.18 The captured eye health indicators should be comprehensive, including disease burden, service provision, and outcome. India’s National eHealth Authority (NeHA) and the Ayushman Bharat Digital Health Mission could be leveraged to develop a uniform electronic health record (EHR) for public and private use, documenting and longitudinally monitoring morbidity, patient acceptance, and treatment outcomes. An incentive system could also work, like in Rwanda. The addition of indicators for eye services at the primary level to Rwanda's performance-based health financing scheme, which pays for outputs or results, led to improved primary eye care activities.19 The WHO’s Eye Care Indicators20 designed to cover all aspects—input, process, output, outcome, and impact, could serve as a framework for collecting eye care data in India.

Improved access to quality eye care

Since the launch of national programme for control of blindness, the country has achieved a cataract surgical rate (CSR) exceeding 6800 per million people annually.21 While India has implemented a three-tier health system at the district hospitals, sub-divisional hospitals, and primary/community health centre,22 eye care is not always provided in all these facilities. Eye care is often complemented by private and voluntary providers who deliver services at subsidised costs.23

The vertical eye care program in India that has helped improve eye care supply and uptake (such as improved CSR) is not necessarily integrated. India has not prepared itself for the higher burden of people with corneal blindness, glaucoma, and diabetic eye disease. Access is further limited for people residing in rural areas, those with poor socio-economic status, women, people with disabilities, ethnic minorities (like tribal population), etc. The referral uptake for advanced eye disorders also remains poor.24, 25, 26

India needs a system to close the care pathway loop from identification to referral and treatment. Studies have shown that proximity to eye care improves accessibility and acceptance among people living in urban slums, rural, tribal settings, and among people living with disabilities.13,27 However, it is equally important to equip these centres with appropriately trained personnel and equipment.23 Decentralising eye care at primary level and leveraging technology (described later) to address access barriers are the key recommendations to improve access to eye care.

Strengthening elderly and marginalised eye health program

By 2050, India’s elderly population is projected to reach 319 million, representing nearly one-fifth of the total population (19.5%).28 Though majority of older adults continue to live within families, a larger proportion reside independently in community settings, often in rural areas or urban slums, where access to reliable and affordable eye care remains limited. Unlike in elder homes, where outreach can be organised, the community-dwelling elderly population often lack structured screening or referral systems. The tribal population of India and elderly people are often disadvantaged due to mobility challenges and limited access to eye care. Barriers are compounded by poverty, low awareness, the absence of escorts for hospital visits, gender inequities, and geographical isolation.29

Community-based eye care closer to people with targeted population-specific programs along with active outreach activities should be prioritised, and integrated social and health systems approach are the key recommendations. A strategy focused on the tribal and elderly community has yielded good results, such as the Tribal Odisha Eye Disease Study (TOES) for the particularly vulnerable tribal group in the state of Odisha, India, and the Hyderabad Ocular Morbidity in Elderly Study.30,31

Navigating with emerging technology

Two emerging areas where technology can play a crucial role are the deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) in diagnostic devices and remote consultation/treatment using teleophthalmology. But telemedicine and AI applications remain concentrated within large hospital networks, with limited penetration in public health systems and remote areas. It is however concerning to note that there are insufficient regional validation and standardisation available for these AI-enabled tools. Data safety related concerns related to governance, patient trust, data privacy, and medico-legal accountability further hinder their interoperability with national health systems.

However, with recent advancement in the country’s digital health infrastructure, progress has been visible. The Central Drugs Control Organisation (CDSCO) of India has approved portable devices for imaging the anterior and posterior segments of the eye. Several Indian studies have documented the ease of use and accuracy of screening for several eye diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, retinopathy of prematurity, age-related macular degeneration, and glaucoma32, 33, 34, 35 as well as the medical, economic, and environmental benefits of tele ophthalmic consultations.36,37

The Indian state of Kerala has taken the lead in policy planning for diabetes and DR screening using AI-enabled devices and a referral system for treatment.38 These initiatives could be explored further encompassing other eye diseases. It would be prudent to scale these along with expanded digital infrastructure in rural and tribal regions through Health and Wellness Centres.

Mobile referral systems represent another promising innovation. Digital triage pathways using color-coded categories (green, yellow, red) have shown that up to 84% of patients can be managed at the primary level, with only high-risk patients referred for advanced care. This reduces unnecessary travel, shortens waiting times, and ensures timely escalation for severe disease.38 At the systems level, platforms such as e-Sanjeevani and the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) are creating frameworks for integrated electronic records and cloud-based data sharing.39

Ensuring financial sustainability

In India, eye care delivery is through a hybrid system of public, voluntary, and private healthcare systems. While basic eye care is offered free of cost at public eye care facilities, patients incur costs for advanced services, such as premium medical devices (e.g., trifocal or extended-depth-of-focus IOLs) and cosmetic refractive surgery. However, the private and public (federal and several state governments) health insurance schemes do offer some financial protection.

Despite these efforts, out-of-pocket expenditure remains high.40 The insurance systems in India do not cover all services, and the voluntary services are invariably donor-dependent.

Expanding existing public funding and private insurance, keeping in line with expanding treatment modalities, would reduce the financial burden. At the same time, the Indian pharmaceutical and device industries could be engaged to offer affordable products to meet the growing needs of the population. The public health insurance system must be made more comprehensive in terms of remuneration for private-sector eye care facilities to enable more people to receive treatment with public health insurance; this would decrease out-of-pocket expenses. One strategy in the public sector could be to integrate public and private insurance providers, link reimbursement to outcomes, and leverage technology to reduce costs and improve efficiency.

Promoting equity and inclusion

India has demonstrated policy and programme-level commitments to equity and inclusive eye care. The principal gaps include inequity in health service delivery studies have shown that women are less likely to receive cataract surgery or refractive error correction,40 illiterate individuals have higher odds of blindness,41 rural populations face barriers of access, cost, and distance,42 and the poor people in the urban slums face fragmented services and financial barriers.42

Integration of eyecare into primary care within existing health care system could leverage established infrastructure for maternal and child health to reach majority population. Integrated care models delivered through combining private, Non-governmental Organisation (NGO), and government services could also increase uptake of eye care services among all sections. Quality and equity in eye care could be improved by adopting a standard treatment protocol and delivering unified care for all patients, irrespective of their payment status.

Strengthening partnership

Many national and international NGOs work in the Indian health sector; nearly all voluntary eye care programs partner with the NPCB VI at the national level. There is insufficient coordination among stakeholders to reduce program duplication. The government's reimbursements to voluntary eye care organisations are not fully organised. Despite the pharmaceutical and device industries contributing to the growth of eye care, there is no systematic industry-academia collaboration in India.

Key strategies could include Government led partnership towards a uniform, equitable, and target-oriented corporate social responsibility spending and centralised governance of the pooled NGO resources, as is partly practiced in countries like Bangladesh43 and a stronger academia-industry collaboration to design innovative and less expensive devices in eye care.

Monitoring progress through comprehensive indicators

India has accepted the World Health Assembly’s global targets for cataract surgery and refractive error, two regional targets for diabetic retinopathy and trachoma and tracer indicators of eye health.7,44

Though the cataract surgical coverage (CSC) in India is high, the same cannot be said for the outcome (effective cataract surgical Coverage (eCSC)).45 The consistent 15–20% point difference between CSC and eCSC indicates systemic challenges in surgical quality, postoperative care, and follow-up. Inequities exacerbate this gap.46,47 There are currently no mechanisms in place to collate indicators on refractive error services or diabetic retinopathy.

Current monitoring frameworks that report only CSCs fail to capture these inequities or quality concerns, potentially overstating progress. A uniform, structured mechanism for data collection and analysis is required. A national registry of eye care and treatment outcomes as proposed in previous section could address these shortcomings. A shift from measurement of progress based on volume-centric indicators to outcome and quality-centric indicators would promote good eye health monitoring. Using the WHO Refractive Error Situation Analysis Tool (RESAT) to obtain baseline information and align with the WHO SPECS strategy could be explored.48,49

Discussion

Public health experts in India identified 10 strategic priority areas, assessed the current position, highlighted gaps, and suggested recommendations to enable quality and equitable eye care to all sections of society, by 2030, and to continue consolidating thereafter. These 10 strategies can be integrated into an interdigitated system framework of 4 functional layers: (1) Foundation layer-universal health coverage, human resources, and financing; (2) Service delivery layer-access, equity, and elderly/marginalised care; (3) Intelligence layer-technology, and hospital information; and (4) Governance layer-partnership and monitoring. These layers are mutually reinforcing, linked through a continuous patient care pathway-the generated data inform governance decisions and resource allocation. This integrated model shifts eye care from fragmented, volume-driven programs to an equitable and outcome-oriented health system.

India has a robust GDP growth of 8.15%. But spending on health is behind many developed countries. A substantial share of health spending in India comes from private/out-of-pocket payments. According to the National Health Accounts estimate, the eye health spending is approximately 4–5% of India’s total health expenditure (2020–2021 and 2021–2022 data).50 Additionally, India's epidemiological transition varies across states.51

Despite these financial constraints, India has made progress in delivering cataract surgery to a large proportion of the population. Multiple factors are behind this success, including the national program planning, the World Bank-assisted cataract surgery program,52 and matching support from the Indian ophthalmic pharmaceutical and device industry. India and other countries in South Asia, such as Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, have pioneered mass eye screening and cataract surgery programs. However, the outcomes of cataract surgery remain suboptimal in these mass screenings, as symptomatic older people with advanced diseases attend these eye camps.53 Another biggest drawback is that these screening camps are episodic, usually scheduled once or twice annually. It is also important to note that eye disorders other than cataract did not receive the same level of support within the program. India’s strategy should align with the IPEC model of care which provides a clear pathway from screening to referral and treatment with comprehensive eye care at every level of service.

Traditionally, the barriers in eye care have been access and finance. These barriers are being reduced as thrust in public and private eye care delivery increases through primary and secondary centers, public insurance systems, and corporate funding. Another important barrier to the uptake of eye care is also the suboptimal quality and uncertain outcomes.53 In addition to early access to care, surgical outcome from provider side could improve only with intensive training during ophthalmology residency and periodic assessment of trainees. India needs a national patient-reported outcome registry for various eye disorders, similar to those in many developed countries (e.g., Northern Europe) and the American Academy of Ophthalmology. Currently, the NPCB VI’s collection of cataract surgical data is only a program reporting, not a registry with clinical outcomes.

Eye health information is the foundation of effective eye care systems. It measures burden of disease and resource allocation, monitors effective and comprehensive coverage, promotes equity, nudges toward improved quality, helps integrate health systems, and, finally, enables region-specific evidence-based treatment decisions. Hence, all strategies discussed here must be complemented with robust data management to monitor progress in eye care. It must begin with a federal architecture, possibly hosted by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare or the NPCB VI, integrated with the National Health Authority. All eye health data, public or private, must flow to this registry and must include patient-related outcomes, with built-in privacy and data protection (Fig. 1). Improving eye health accelerates progress across multiple Sustainable Developmental Goals (SDGs) and builds more inclusive societies. Given that health and family welfare are state subjects in India, integrating eye care into Universal Health Coverage could be challenging. Strong political commitment is required for the adoption of successful NGO models, revision of policies to address task shifting, additional resources for dedicated eye care for the elderly and marginalised groups, and consistent reimbursement policies.

There are limitations to our expert consensus-based recommendations. This analysis does not include a situation analysis of every state in India. Hence, the recommendations must align with the locally available resources. Health care in India is a state responsibility, and hence, there is a risk of differences between federal and state health planning. These risks could be reduced by framing and executing a national health framework. Experts from medical schools and the regional institute of ophthalmology were not directly involved in developing these policy recommendations. However, the current experts in this review are engaged in ophthalmology residency training.

India has made substantial progress but remains off track to fully meet SDG-aligned eye health targets by 2030. The above summarised strategic recommendations provide a roadmap through ten interlinked pillars: universal coverage, human resources, financial sustainability, equity, partnerships, health information systems, quality care, elderly and marginalised eye care programs, technology adoption, and monitoring of key indices. By aligning these pillars with national policy, India could achieve universal, equitable, and high-quality eye health.
