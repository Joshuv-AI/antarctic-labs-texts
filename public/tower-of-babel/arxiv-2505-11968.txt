Kulkarni limit sets for cyclic quaternionic projective groups
Sandipan Dutta, Krishnendu Gongopadhyay and Rahul Mondal

Abstract
. We consider the natural action of the quaternionic projective linear group PSL ⁡ ( n + 1 , ℍ ) \displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}) on the quaternionic projective space ℙ ℍ n \displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}} . We compute the Kulkarni limit sets for the cyclic subgroups of PSL ⁡ ( n + 1 , ℍ ) \displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}) .



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 20H10; Secondary 15B33, 22E40

1.
Introduction

One way to study discrete subgroups of Lie groups is through their dynamical properties. A notable example involves Kleinian groups—discrete subgroups of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
—which act on the Riemann sphere via Möbius transformations. Their orbits, if infinite, accumulate on a boundary-invariant subset called the
limit set

[
CG
,
Mas
]
, with the group acting properly discontinuously on its complement. The properties of the limit sets carry a striking analogy with the behavior of rational maps of the Riemann sphere; cf.
[
Mc
]
.

A natural question in higher-dimensional dynamics concerns the behavior of discrete subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{C})
. With the motivation to build a connection between the dynamics of projective automorphisms of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
and the discrete subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{C})
, Seade and Verjovsky
[
SV99
]
,
[
SV01
]
have initiated investigation of the ‘Complex Kleinian groups’, that is, discrete subgroups of complex projective linear transformations. A central object in this study is the Kulkarni limit set, introduced by Kulkarni in
[
Ku
]
, who proposed a domain of discontinuity for any locally compact group acting on a locally compact Hausdorff space. Following the foundational work of Seade and Verjovsky, the study of Kulkarni limit sets for discrete subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{C})
, particularly in the case
\displaystyle n=3
, has been further developed by Barrera, Cano, Navarrete, and others; see, for instance, the monograph
[
CNS
]
and the articles
[
BCNS
,
CS14
,
CS10
,
Na
,
Na06
]
.

To generalize this framework to the quaternionic setting, we consider discrete subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{H})
. The group
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{H})
corresponds to the isometry group of five-dimensional real hyperbolic space. This naturally leads to the intriguing question of understanding higher-rank projective linear groups over the quaternions. In
[
DGL
]
, Kulkarni limit sets for cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(3,\mathbb{H})
were classified. The goal of this paper is to extend that classification to Kulkarni limit sets of cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{H})
for arbitrary
\displaystyle n
, thereby generalizing the results of
[
DGL
]
.

It is worth noting that Kulkarni limit sets for cyclic subgroups of complex projective transformations in
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n,\mathbb{C})
were computed by Cano et al. in
[
CLU
]
. Inspired by their approach, we adopt a similar methodology in this work. However, due to the non-commutativity of the quaternions, the computations require a different treatment.

Since
\displaystyle{\rm SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
is a double cover of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
by
\displaystyle\{\pm\mathrm{I}_{n}\}
, we often lift elements from the projective group to
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
and consider their matrix representations.

The limit sets will be described in the following table. Before that, we first recall the definition of the Kulkarni limit sets.

Definition 1.1
.

[
Ku
]

Let
\displaystyle P_{X}=\{A_{\beta}:\;\beta\in B\}
be a family of subsets of
\displaystyle X
(where
\displaystyle B
is an infinite indexing set). A point
\displaystyle p
is called a
cluster point
of
\displaystyle P_{X}
if for every neighbourhood
\displaystyle N(p)
of
\displaystyle p
, the subset of the indexing set
\displaystyle B
defined as
\displaystyle\{\beta\in B:\;N(p)\cap A_{\beta}\neq\emptyset\}
is infinite.

Consider the natural action of a subgroup
\displaystyle G
of
\displaystyle{\rm SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
on the
\displaystyle n
-dimensional quaternionic projective space
\displaystyle X=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Consider the following three sets

(a)

\displaystyle L_{0}(G):=
the closure of the set of points of
\displaystyle X
which have an infinite isotropy group,

(b)

\displaystyle L_{1}(G):=
the closure of the cluster points of orbits of points in
\displaystyle X\setminus L_{0}(G)
, and

(c)

\displaystyle L_{2}(G):=
the closure of the cluster points of
\displaystyle\{g(K)\}_{g\in G}
, where
\displaystyle K
runs over all the compact subsets of
\displaystyle X\setminus\{L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)\}
.

These sets are called the Kulkarni sets.

Definition 1.2
.

[
Ku
]

With
\displaystyle G
and
\displaystyle X
as defined above, the
Kulkarni limit set
of
\displaystyle G
is

(1.1)

\Lambda(G):=L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)\cup L_{2}(G).

The
Kulkarni domain of discontinuity
of
\displaystyle G
is defined as
\displaystyle\Omega(G)=X\setminus\Lambda(G)
.

We remark that the Definition
1.2
is valid for any group
\displaystyle G
acting on a locally compact Hausdorff space
\displaystyle X
and
Kulkarni proved the following proposition in
[
Ku
]
.

Proposition 1.3

(cf.
[
Ku
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space and
\displaystyle G
be a group acting on
\displaystyle X
, then
\displaystyle G
acts properly discontinuously on
\displaystyle\Omega(G)
. In addition,
\displaystyle\Omega(G)
is an open subset of
\displaystyle X
. Further, if
\displaystyle\Omega(G)\neq\emptyset
, then
\displaystyle G
is discrete.

To classify the limit sets, we pick up Jordan forms in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{H})
and compute the limit set for the cyclic group generated by that Jordan form. The elements of
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
can be divided into three mutually exclusive classes as follows.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\displaystyle g
be an element in
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.

(i)

\displaystyle g
is called elliptic if it is semisimple and the eigenvalue classes are represented by unit modulus quaternionic numbers.

(ii)

\displaystyle g
is called loxodromic if it is semi-simple and not all the eigenvalue classes are represented by unit modulus quaternionic numbers.

(iii)

\displaystyle g
is called parabolic if it is not semi-simple and eigenvalues have unit modulus quaternionic numbers.

(iv)

\displaystyle g
is called loxoparabolic if it is not semi-simple and at least one of the eigenvalues do not have unit modulus.

These classes can be further refined. An elliptic is rational if all of
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are rational, where
\displaystyle e^{2\pi i\alpha_{i}}
’s are representative eigenvalues of
\displaystyle g
.

Finally, we remark that the results in the quaternionic setting differ in several instances from those in the complex setting, as presented in
[
CLU
]
. Even when the outcomes coincide, the computational methods diverge due to the non-commutative structure of the quaternions. Notably, the computation of the first Kulkarni limit set
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)
often deviates from the corresponding results in
\displaystyle{\rm PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{C})
(cf.
[
CLU
]
).

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
denote the division ring of Hamilton’s quaternions. Every element
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{H}
can be written as

a=a_{0}+a_{1}i+a_{2}j+a_{3}k,

where
\displaystyle a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the quaternion units satisfy
\displaystyle i^{2}=j^{2}=k^{2}=ijk=-1
. The conjugate of
\displaystyle a
is defined by

\bar{a}=a_{0}-a_{1}i-a_{2}j-a_{3}k.

We identify the real subspace
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i
with the complex plane
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
. For a detailed treatment of the theory of quaternions and matrices over quaternions, see
[
Ro
]
and
[
FZ
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be an element of the algebra
\displaystyle\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, consisting of all
\displaystyle n\times n
matrices with entries in
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
. A non-zero vector
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is called a (right) eigenvector of
\displaystyle A
corresponding to the (right) eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
if

Av=v\lambda.

The eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
occur in similarity classes: if
\displaystyle v
is a (right) eigenvector corresponding to
\displaystyle\lambda
, then for any
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{*}
, the vector
\displaystyle v\mu
is a (right) eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle\mu^{-1}\lambda\mu
. Each similarity class contains a unique complex number with non-negative imaginary part. In what follows, we represent each similarity class by this
unique complex representative
and refer to it simply as an
eigenvalue
.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
and write
\displaystyle A=(A_{1})+(A_{2})j
, where
\displaystyle A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{C})
. Now consider the embedding
\displaystyle\Phi:\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})\longrightarrow\mathrm{M}(2n,\mathbb{C})
defined as

(2.1)

\Phi(A)=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
-\overline{A_{2}}&\overline{A_{1}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, the determinant of
\displaystyle A
is denoted by
\displaystyle{\rm det}_{\mathbb{H}}(A)
and is defined as the determinant of the corresponding matrix
\displaystyle\Phi(A)
, i.e.,
\displaystyle{\rm det}_{\mathbb{H}}(A):={\rm det}(\Phi(A))
, see
[
FZ
]
. Because of the
Skolem-Noether theorem
, the above definition is independent of the choice of the embedding chosen
\displaystyle\Phi
.

Consider the Lie groups
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})=\{g\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})\mid{\rm det_{\mathbb{H}}}(g)\neq 0\}
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{H})=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})\mid{\rm det_{\mathbb{H}}}(g)=1\}.

Definition 2.3

(cf.
[
He
, p. 302]
)
.

A Jordan block
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}(\lambda,m)
is a
\displaystyle m\times m
matrix with
\displaystyle\lambda
on the diagonal entries, 1 on all of the super-diagonal entries, and zero elsewhere. We will refer to a block diagonal matrix where each block is a Jordan block as
Jordan form
.

Next we recall the Jordan form in
\displaystyle\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, see
[
Ro
, Theorem 5.5.3]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Jordan form in
\displaystyle\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
, cf.
[
Ro
]
)
.

For every
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{M}(n,\mathbb{H})
there is an invertible matrix
\displaystyle S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})
such that
\displaystyle SAS^{-1}
has the form

(2.2)

SAS^{-1}=\mathrm{J}(\lambda_{1},m_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{J}(\lambda_{k},m_{k})

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}
are complex numbers (not necessarily distinct) and have non-negative imaginary parts. The forms (
2.2
) are uniquely determined by
\displaystyle A
up to a permutation of Jordan blocks.

2.1.
The Quaternion Projective Space
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}

Consider the right
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-module
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. The
\displaystyle n
-dimensional quaternionic projective space
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is defined as the quotient of
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
under the equivalence relation
\displaystyle\sim
, where for
\displaystyle z,w\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
,

z\sim w\iff z=w\,\alpha\quad\text{for some non-zero quaternion }\alpha\in\mathbb{H}.

Let

\mathbb{P}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}

denote the canonical quotient map. A non-empty subset
\displaystyle W\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is called a projective subspace of dimension
\displaystyle k
if there exists a
\displaystyle(k+1)
-dimensional
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-linear subspace
\displaystyle\widetilde{W}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{W}\setminus\{0\})=W.

Projective subspaces of dimension 1 in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
are referred to as
lines
.

Given a point
\displaystyle p=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we denote its image under
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
by

[x_{1}:x_{2}:\cdots:x_{n+1}]:=\mathbb{P}(p).

Note that for any non-zero quaternion
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

[x_{1}:x_{2}:\cdots:x_{n+1}]=[x_{1}\alpha:x_{2}\alpha:\cdots:x_{n+1}\alpha].

Given a set of points
\displaystyle{S}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
, we define:

\langle{\,S\,}\rangle=\bigcap\{H\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}:H\hbox{ is a projective subspace containing }{S}\}.

Clearly,
\displaystyle\langle{\,S\,}\rangle
is a projective subspace of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
. If
\displaystyle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}
are distinct points then
\displaystyle\langle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\rangle
is the unique proper quaternion projective subspace
passing through
\displaystyle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The complex projective space is
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
defined as

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}=\left\{[w]\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}:\;w=\lambda z,\;\lambda\in\mathbb{H}^{*},\;z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\right\}.

2.2.
Projective Transformations

Action of an arbitrary element
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is given by:

\gamma(\mathbb{P}(z))=\mathbb{P}(\gamma(z))\hbox{ for all }z\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}.

Note that for any non-zero
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\displaystyle(r\gamma)(\mathbb{P}(z))=\gamma(\mathbb{P}(z)),\,\forall\,z\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}.

We denote the corresponding quotient map by
\displaystyle\pi:\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})\longrightarrow\mathrm{PGL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.
The group of projective transformation of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is:

\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}):=\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})/\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})),

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H}))=\{\pm\mathrm{I}_{n+1}\}
and it acts by the usual scalar multiplication on
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Given
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
, we say that
\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
is a lift of
\displaystyle\gamma
if
\displaystyle\pi(\widetilde{\gamma})=\gamma
. There is exactly two lifting for each transformation
\displaystyle\gamma
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
,
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}
and
\displaystyle-\tilde{\gamma}
. Note that
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
acts transitively on
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
by taking projective subspace into projective subspace.

Let
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a projective transformation, then it is called elliptic, loxodromic and parabolic according as its lift
\displaystyle\tilde{g}\in\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{H})
, see Definition
1.4
. Note that this classification is well defined in view of Jordan decomposition of matrices over quaternions, see Lemma
2.4
.

2.3.
Pseudo-projective Transformations

We recall that the notion of pseudo-projective transformations for complex projective space was introduced by Cano and Seade in
[
CS10
]
, and has proven to be a useful tool in the study of complex Kleinian groups. We extend this concept to the quaternionic setting.

Let
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}:\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be a non-zero (right)
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
-linear transformation, which is not necessarily invertible. Let
\displaystyle\ker(\widetilde{M})\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
denote its kernel. Then
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}
induces a well-defined map

M:\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus\ker(M)\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}},

defined by

M(\mathbb{P}(v))=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{M}(v)),

where
\displaystyle\ker(M)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is the image of
\displaystyle\ker(\widetilde{M})\setminus\{0\}
under the projective map
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
. This map
\displaystyle M
is well-defined because for all
\displaystyle v\notin\ker(\widetilde{M})
, we have
\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(v)\neq 0
, and projective equivalence is preserved: for any non-zero quaternion
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{H}
,

M(\mathbb{P}(v\alpha))=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{M}(v\alpha))=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{M}(v)\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{M}(v)).

We refer to the map
\displaystyle M
as a
pseudo-projective transformation
, and denote by
\displaystyle\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
the space of all pseudo-projective transformations of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
. Thus,

\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})=\{M\mid\widetilde{M}\text{ is a non-zero linear transformation of }\mathbb{H}^{n+1}\}.

Note that the group
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
of quaternionic projective linear transformations is contained in
\displaystyle\mathrm{QP}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.

In what follows, we use pseudo-projective transformations, together with a classification of elements in lifts of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
(see Definition
1.4
), to study the limit sets of various cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.

2.4.
Grassmanian

In this article we denote Grassmanian
\displaystyle Gr_{\mathbb{H}}(k,n)
as the space of all
\displaystyle k
-dimensional projective subspaces of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
which is compact in Hausdorff topology.

Some useful lemmas

.

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\{g_{n}(K)\}

for all compact set
\displaystyle K\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus C

\displaystyle L_{2}(G)\subseteq C

2.5.1.
Dual Projective Space

The dual of quaternionic projective space is denoted by
\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}})^{*}
are set of all hyperplanes, that is

l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})=\{[x_{1}:x_{2}:\ldots:x_{n+1}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}:\;\langle x,\alpha\rangle=\overline{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}+\overline{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}+\ldots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}}x_{n+1}=0\}.

Observe that for any
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
,
\displaystyle\overline{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}\lambda+\overline{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}\lambda+\ldots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}}x_{n+1}\lambda=0
implies that hyperplanes are well defined.

Lemma 2.7
.

The hyperplanes
\displaystyle l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})
and
\displaystyle l(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
are equal if and only if
\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}:\alpha_{2}:\ldots:\alpha_{n+1}]=[\beta_{1}:\beta_{2}:\ldots:\beta_{n+1}].

Proof.

Assume that
\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}:\alpha_{2}:\ldots:\alpha_{n+1}]=[\beta_{1}:\beta_{2}:\ldots:\beta_{n+1}]
; that is, there exists
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\displaystyle\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}\lambda
for all
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n+1
. Let
\displaystyle[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]\in l(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})
, i.e.,

\overline{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}+\overline{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}+\cdots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}}x_{n+1}=0.

Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\beta_{1}}x_{1}+\overline{\beta_{2}}x_{2}+\cdots+\overline{\beta_{n+1}}x_{n+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\overline{\alpha_{1}\lambda}x_{1}+\overline{\alpha_{2}\lambda}x_{2}+\cdots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}\lambda}x_{n+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda}\left(\overline{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}+\overline{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}+\cdots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}}x_{n+1}\right)=0.

Hence,
\displaystyle l(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})\subseteq l(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
, and by symmetry, equality holds:

l(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})=l(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1}).

Conversely, suppose
\displaystyle l(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})=l(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
, but

\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}:\alpha_{2}:\cdots:\alpha_{n+1}]\neq[\beta_{1}:\beta_{2}:\ldots:\beta_{n+1}]
. There exist
\displaystyle\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}\lambda
,
\displaystyle\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2}\mu
, where
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\neq 0
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\neq 0
, and
\displaystyle\lambda\neq\mu
.

Now consider the vector
\displaystyle[1,-\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}},0,\ldots,0]\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. We observe:

\overline{\alpha_{1}}\cdot 1+\overline{\alpha_{2}}\cdot(-\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}})=\overline{\alpha_{1}}-\overline{\alpha_{2}}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}=\overline{\alpha_{1}}-\overline{\alpha_{1}}=0,

so this vector lies in
\displaystyle l(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})
, and therefore also in
\displaystyle l(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
.

Then,

\overline{\beta_{1}}-\overline{\beta_{2}}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}=\overline{\alpha_{1}\lambda}-\overline{\alpha_{2}\mu}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}=\overline{\lambda}\overline{\alpha_{1}}-\bar{\mu}\overline{\alpha_{2}}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}.

Using
\displaystyle\overline{\alpha_{2}}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}=\overline{\alpha_{1}}
, we get:

\overline{\beta_{1}}-\overline{\beta_{2}}\cdot\overline{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{-1}}=\overline{\lambda}\overline{\alpha_{1}}-\overline{\mu}\overline{\alpha_{1}}=(\overline{\lambda}-\overline{\mu})\overline{\alpha_{1}}=0.

Since
\displaystyle\overline{\alpha_{1}}\neq 0
, this implies
\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}=\overline{\mu}
, and hence
\displaystyle\lambda=\mu
, which contradicts the assumption
\displaystyle\lambda\neq\mu
.

Therefore, we must have
\displaystyle[\alpha_{1}:\cdots:\alpha_{n+1}]=[\beta_{1}:\cdots:\beta_{n+1}]
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma
2.7
gives us that, the set of all hyperplanes in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
are in a one to one correspondence with points
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
. We are identifying the set of hyperplanes in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
with the quaternionic projective space itself.

Lemma 2.8
.

The projective transformation maps the hyperplane in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
itself i.e.,
\displaystyle g(l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))=l(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n})
where
\displaystyle\alpha=[\alpha_{1}:\alpha_{2}:\ldots:\alpha_{n}],\;\beta=[\beta_{1}:\beta_{2}:\ldots:\beta_{n}]
and
\displaystyle\beta=(g^{-1})^{*}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Consider a hyperplane defined by

l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})=\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}:\;\langle x,\alpha\rangle=0\},

where the Hermitian form is given by

\langle x,\alpha\rangle=\bar{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}+\ldots+\overline{\alpha_{n+1}}x_{n}=\alpha^{*}x.

Let
\displaystyle\beta=(g^{-1})^{*}\alpha.
We want to show that
\displaystyle g(x)\in l(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
. Indeed,

\langle g(x),\beta\rangle=\langle g(x),(g^{-1})^{*}\alpha\rangle=\alpha^{*}g^{-1}gx=\alpha^{*}x=0.

Conversely, suppose
\displaystyle y\in l(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
then,
\displaystyle g^{-1}(y)\in l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})
as,

\langle g^{-1}y,\alpha\rangle=\langle g^{-1}y,g^{*}\beta\rangle=y^{*}(g^{*})^{-1}g^{*}\beta=y^{*}\beta=0

So,
\displaystyle g
takes the hyperplane
\displaystyle l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1})
to
\displaystyle l(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n+1})
, where
\displaystyle\beta=(g^{-1})^{*}\alpha
.
∎

In this section we observe the Kulkarni limit set for the cyclic subgroup of

Before proceeding to the main theorem, we shall discuss some of the useful lemmas that are needed to prove the main theorem.

Lemma 3.1

Then there exists a sequence
\displaystyle(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\displaystyle g^{n_{k}}\rightarrow I_{n+1}
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
(
\displaystyle I_{n+1}
is the identity matrix of order
\displaystyle n+1
).

This lemma serves as a direct extension of the result we have proven for
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(3,\mathbb{H})
,
[
DGL
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{g}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be an elliptic translation given by the lift
\displaystyle g=\mathrm{D}[e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\alpha_{n+1}}]\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
. Then the Kulkarni limit set
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)
for the cyclic group
\displaystyle G:=\langle\tilde{g}\rangle
are as follows:

(i)

Rational elliptic: If all
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are rational, then
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=\emptyset
.

(ii)

Simple irrational elliptic: If all
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are equal and irrational numbers, then
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.

(iii)

Compound irrational elliptic: If
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are of mixed type, i.e. it can be both rational and irrational, then
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proof.

The proof of the theorem can be divided in several sub-cases depending upon the rationality of
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s (
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots n
).

(i)

Rational elliptic:
Let
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
for all
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots n
. There exists
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle e^{2\pi in_{0}\alpha_{i}}=1
for all
\displaystyle i
. As
\displaystyle g
is of finite order hence
\displaystyle\langle g\rangle
is.

Therefore,
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=\emptyset
as no element have infinite isotropy group. Consequently
\displaystyle L_{1}(G)=\emptyset
and
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)=\emptyset
and
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=\emptyset
.

(ii)

Simple irrational elliptic:
Let
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\ldots=\alpha_{n}
and
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. Recall that
\displaystyle zj=j\bar{z}
for each
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{C}
. Consider a copy of complex projective space

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}=\left\{[w]\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}:\;w=\lambda z,\;\lambda\in\mathbb{H}^{*},\;z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\right\}.

Hence,
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Since for any
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
there exists a sequence
\displaystyle(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\displaystyle e^{2\pi i\alpha n_{k}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}1
,
[
KH
]
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\lim_{n_{k}\rightarrow\infty}g^{n_{k}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}
for all
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
So
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\subseteq L_{1}(G)
and hence
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Consequently,
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)=\emptyset
.
Therefore, the Kulkarni limit set is
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}=L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)\cup L_{2}(G)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.

(iii)

Compound elliptic:
Assume that some of the
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are rational and some are irrational. In this case
\displaystyle g
is a diagonal matrix of the form

g=\mathrm{D}(e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\alpha_{k}},e^{2\pi i\beta_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\beta_{l}},e^{2\pi i\gamma_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\gamma_{m}}).

Here,
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s are rational numbers,
\displaystyle\beta_{i}
’s are equal irrational numbers and
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}
’s unequal irrational numbers with
\displaystyle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{p}
are rational screws i.e., there exist
\displaystyle n_{1}
such that
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{n_{1}}=\ldots=\gamma_{p}^{n_{1}}
and others are not. Also,
\displaystyle k+l+m=n+1
.
Let

a_{1}=\mathrm{D}(e^{2\pi i\alpha_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\alpha_{k}}),a_{2}=\mathrm{D}(e^{2\pi i\beta_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\beta_{l}})\text{ and }a_{3}=\mathrm{D}(e^{2\pi i\gamma_{1}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\gamma_{k}}).

As
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
, hence there exist
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that,
\displaystyle e^{2\pi n_{0}\alpha_{i}}=1
for all
\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Assume
\displaystyle A_{1}:=\langle a_{1}\rangle,A_{2}:=\langle a_{2}\rangle
and
\displaystyle A_{3}:=\langle a_{3}\rangle
. Therefore, as
\displaystyle A_{1}^{n_{0}}=I
hence
\displaystyle Fix(A_{1}^{n_{0}})=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
. Also
\displaystyle L_{0}(A_{2})=L_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}\}
. As
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}^{\prime}s
are rational screws hence
\displaystyle L_{0}(A_{3})=L_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}\}\cup\{e_{p+1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
. From this we can declare that
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\cup L_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{k+l}\}\cup L_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{k+l+1},\ldots,e_{k+l+p}\}\cup\{e_{p+1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
.

Let
\displaystyle q\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\setminus L_{0}(G)
. Since

\displaystyle g^{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow\infty}I
hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}\setminus L_{0}(G)\subseteq L_{1}(G)
therefore

\displaystyle L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Consequently,
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)=\emptyset
. Hence,
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Parabolic case

In this section we discuss the Kulkarni limit sets for parabolic elements of
\displaystyle\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
.
There are several cases according to the Jordan blocks.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{g}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
be a parabolic translation whose lift
\displaystyle g\in\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
is given by
\displaystyle g=\mathrm{J}(\lambda,n+1)
, where
\displaystyle|\lambda|=1
.

The Kulkarni sets for the cyclic group
\displaystyle G:=\langle\tilde{g}\rangle
are

\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=\{e_{1}\}=L_{1}(G)
and
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2}\ldots e_{n}\}
. Therefore the Kulkarni limit set would be
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots e_{n}\}
.

Proof.

The proof can be divided in three parts each for
\displaystyle L_{0}(G),L_{1}(G)
and
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)
.

Observe that,
\displaystyle g^{m}(x)=x
for infinitely many
\displaystyle m
if and only if
\displaystyle x=\{e_{1}\}
. For the calculation of
\displaystyle g^{m}
one can refer to subsection
1.2
. Hence
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=\{e_{1}\}.

Let us now determine
\displaystyle L_{1}(G)
. The action of
\displaystyle g^{m}
on a projective co-ordinate of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle g^{m}[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n+1}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bigg[\lambda^{m}x_{1}+{m\choose 1}\lambda^{m-1}x_{2}+\cdots+{m\choose n}\lambda^{m-n}x_{n+1}:\lambda^{m}x_{2}+{m\choose 1}\lambda^{m-1}x_{3}+\cdots

\displaystyle\displaystyle+{m\choose n-1}\lambda^{m+n}x_{n+1}:\cdots:\lambda^{m}x_{n+1}\bigg].

If
\displaystyle x_{p}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle x_{p+1}=x_{p+2}=\ldots=x_{n+1}=0
then

\frac{1}{{m\choose p-1}\lambda^{m-p+1}}g^{m}(x)\rightarrow e_{1}.

Similar calculations can be performed for
\displaystyle g^{-m}
and in both cases the only converging point is
\displaystyle\{e_{1}\}
and hence
\displaystyle L_{1}(G)=\{e_{1}\}
.

Next consider,
\displaystyle\frac{1}{{m\choose n}\lambda^{m-n}}g^{m}
converges to
\displaystyle M
in the space of pseudo-projective transformations where
\displaystyle M
has the form
\displaystyle\mathrm{Z}(1,n+1)
(cf. subsection
1.2
). Since its
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(M)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(M)=\{e_{1}\}\in L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)
. Thus, by Lemma
2.6
we have

L_{2}(G)\subseteq L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}.

Again, if we take the hyperplane
\displaystyle l(1:0:\ldots:0)
then
\displaystyle g^{-m}(l(1,0,\ldots,0))\rightarrow l(0,0,\ldots,1)
. As

(g^{m})^{*}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\lambda^{m}}\\
{m\choose 1}\overline{\lambda^{m-1}}\\
\vdots\\
{m\choose n}\overline{\lambda^{m-n}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{{m\choose n}}\overline{\lambda^{n}}\\
\frac{{m\choose 1}}{{m\choose n}}\overline{\lambda^{n-1}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{{m\choose n}}{{m\choose n}}\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}e_{n+1}

hence
\displaystyle g^{-m}(L\{e_{2},\ldots,e_{n+1}\})\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.

Therefore
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
and thus the Kulkarni limit set is

\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots e_{n}\}.

This proves the theorem.
∎

Next, we consider elements
\displaystyle\tilde{g}\in\mathrm{PSL}(n+1,\mathbb{H})
whose Jordan form contains two distinct types of blocks, corresponding to different classes of eigenvalues. To distinguish these, we will employ block matrix notation. The relevant theorems are stated and proved below. The arguments naturally extend to the case where more than two types of Jordan blocks are present.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(k+l,\mathbb{H})
whose lift is
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(k+l,\mathbb{H})
such that

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})&\\
&\mathrm{J}(1,l)\end{bmatrix}.

If
\displaystyle G:=\langle\tilde{\gamma}\rangle
then
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l-1}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A=\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})=\mathrm{D}(e^{2\pi i\theta_{1}},e^{2\pi i\theta_{2}},\ldots,e^{2\pi i\theta_{k}})
, and without loss of generality assume that
\displaystyle\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}
are rational numbers, and the remaining
\displaystyle k-p
are irrational. Furthermore, let
\displaystyle\lambda_{p+1},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
be irrational screws satisfying
\displaystyle\lambda_{p+i}/\lambda_{p+i+1}=e^{2\pi i\alpha_{i}},\;\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. It implies
\displaystyle\lambda_{p+1}^{n_{0}}=\cdots=\lambda_{q}^{n_{0}}
for some
\displaystyle n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Therefore,
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p},e_{k+1}\}\cup L_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_{p+1},\ldots,e_{q}\}\cup\left[\bigcup\limits_{i=q+1}^{k}\{e_{i}\}\right]
.

Next, if either one of the
\displaystyle x_{k+2},x_{k+3},\ldots,x_{k+l}
is non-zero where
\displaystyle x=[x_{1}:x_{2}:\ldots:x_{k+l}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{H}}
then

\gamma^{n}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{n}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{k}^{n}x_{k}\\
x_{k+1}+{n\choose 1}x_{k+2}+\ldots+{n\choose l-1}x_{k+l}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k+l}\end{bmatrix}.

By dividing
\displaystyle\gamma^{n}
by suitable scalar it convereges to
\displaystyle\{e_{k+1}\}
. Also for every
\displaystyle y\in L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}\setminus L_{0}(G)
we can find
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{P}^{k+l}\setminus L_{0}(G)
such that
\displaystyle g^{n_{k}}(x)\rightarrow y
. This implies

L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}.

For calculating
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)
we shall use induction on
\displaystyle l
.

If
\displaystyle l=2
then
\displaystyle\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})&\\
&\mathrm{J}(1,2)\end{bmatrix}
. Then
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
and
\displaystyle\gamma^{n}\rightarrow\tau
in the space of pseudo projective transformations, where
\displaystyle\tau=\mathrm{Z}(k+1,k+2)
(see subsection
1.2
). Since
\displaystyle Im(\tau)=\{e_{k+1}\}\subset L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)
and
\displaystyle Ker(\tau)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
then we can use the Lemma
2.6
and show
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)\subseteq L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
by assuming
\displaystyle C=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
. Hence,
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
.

Suppose the statement is true for
\displaystyle l
then we have to prove this for
\displaystyle l+1
.

Assume that

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{D}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})&\\
&\mathrm{J}(1,l+1)\end{bmatrix}

then
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+1}\}
. Also,
\displaystyle\gamma^{m}\rightarrow{Z}(k+1,k+l+1)=M
(say) and
\displaystyle Im(M)=\{e_{k+1}\}
,
\displaystyle Ker(M)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l}\}=C
say. By applying Lemma
2.6
we get
\displaystyle L_{2}(G)\subseteq L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l}\}
.

Let us define,
\displaystyle h_{1}=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+l-1}\}\text{ and }h_{2}=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k+l}\}
. To proceed, it suffices to show that for
\displaystyle q\in h_{1}\setminus L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
such that
\displaystyle L\{q,e_{k+l}\}\subseteq\Lambda_{Kul}(\langle\gamma\rangle)
.
By applying, the inductive hypothesis to the restriction of
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle h_{2}
, we have,

\Lambda_{Kul}(\gamma|_{h_{2}})=h_{1}.

Hence there exists a sequence
\displaystyle(q_{1m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq h_{2}
such that, the cluster point of
\displaystyle(q_{1m})
lies outside of

\displaystyle L_{0}(G)\cup L_{1}(G)
and moreover,
\displaystyle\gamma^{m}(q_{1m})\rightarrow q
.
Since
\displaystyle h_{2}
is compact, we can assume
\displaystyle q_{1m}\rightarrow q_{1}
where

q_{1}=[q_{1}^{\prime}:q_{2}^{\prime}:\cdots:q_{k+l}^{\prime},0]\text{ with }\sum_{j=1}^{l}|q^{\prime}_{k+j}|^{2}\neq 0.

Let
\displaystyle h_{3}=L\{e_{k+2},\ldots,e_{k+l+1}\}
then by the previous theorem we can conclude that, there exists a sequence
\displaystyle(q_{2m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\displaystyle\gamma^{m}(q_{2m})\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}e_{k+l}
. Define
\displaystyle l_{m}=L\{q_{1m},q_{2m}\}
. Therefore
\displaystyle\gamma^{m}(l_{m})\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}L\{q,e_{k+l}\}
and hence
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{k+l-1}\}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathrm{PSL}(2l,\mathbb{H})
which have a lift
\displaystyle\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}(2l,\mathbb{H})
such that

{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{J}(1,l)&\\
&\mathrm{J}(1,l)\end{bmatrix}.

Then
\displaystyle\Lambda_{Kul}(G)=L\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{l-1},e_{l+1},\ldots,e_{2l-1}\}
where
\displaystyle G:=\langle{\tilde{\gamma}}\rangle
.

Proof.

It is easy to prove that,
\displaystyle L_{0}(G)=L_{1}(G)=L\{e_{1},e_{l+1}\}
.

Let
\displaystyle K_{1}=L\{e_{2},\ldots,e_{l}\}
and
\displaystyle K_{2}=L\{e_{l+2},\ldots,e_{2l}\}
be two compact sets. By the previous result we know
