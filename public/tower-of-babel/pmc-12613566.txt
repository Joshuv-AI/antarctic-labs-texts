A systematic review of TMAO, microRNAs, and the oral/gut microbiomes in atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction: mechanistic insights and translational opportunities
Authors: Luigi Regenburgh De La Motte, Fabiana Giarritiello, Lucia La Sala, Lorenzo Drago
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide, driven by complex interactions among atherosclerosis, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation. Recent studies have identified the gut and oral microbiomes—through their metabolism of dietary precursors into trimethylamine (TMA) and its hepatic conversion to trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO)—as pivotal contributors to CVD risk. In parallel, microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as crucial regulators of inflammatory and metabolic pathways that further modulate these processes. This review examines recent human studies (2020–2025) to explore how microbiota-derived TMAO, miRNA regulatory networks, and changes in the oral microbiome contribute to the development of atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction. It also highlights potential therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways, providing insights for precision medicine in cardiovascular disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07224-5.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, TMAO, Gut microbiome, Oral microbiome, MicroRNA, Atherosclerosis, Endothelial dysfunction

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually. Traditional risk factors such as atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and metabolic disturbances have long been associated with CVD. However, recent evidence suggests that the gut and oral microbiomes play a significant role in influencing these pathogenic processes. One prominent example is trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a metabolite produced by gut bacteria from dietary precursors like choline, carnitine, and betaine. Once produced, TMAO is oxidized in the liver by flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3), and has emerged as a key contributor to atherosclerosis and other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [1].

Beyond the gut microbiome, the oral microbiome plays an important role in TMAO metabolism. Oral bacteria such as Prevotella, Fusobacterium, Porphyromonas, and Streptococcus are capable of metabolizing dietary choline into trimethylamine (TMA), which is subsequently converted to TMAO in the liver. Oral dysbiosis, such as that seen in periodontitis, can elevate systemic TMAO levels, further contributing to cardiovascular risk [2, 3].

In addition to their impact on TMAO production, microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators in the interplay between diet, microbiomes, and cardiovascular health. miRNAs, such as miR-21 and miR-126, modulate genes involved in inflammation, lipid metabolism, and endothelial function, mediating the downstream effects of TMAO on cardiovascular tissues [4]. miRNAs also influence gut barrier integrity and immune responses, indirectly impacting TMA production and TMAO synthesis.

Recent studies highlight the potential to modulate TMAO production as a therapeutic strategy. For example, deficiency of Proline-Serine Rich Coiled-Coil 1 (PSRC1) accelerates atherosclerosis by increasing TMAO production through changes in the gut microbiota and FMO3 activity [5]. In patients with atrial fibrillation, elevated gut-derived TMAO is associated with more advanced disease Mechanistically, TMAO may promote atrial fibrillation through induction of oxidative stress, atrial fibrosis, and structural remodeling, as demonstrated in both clinical cohorts [6] and preclinical models where SCFA supplementation attenuated TMAO-induced atrial remodeling [7]. However, recent meta-analyses indicate heterogeneity in this association, with some 2025 data reporting no significant link, underscoring population-specific effects. Therapies such as berberine and Ginkgolide B have shown promise in reducing TMAO production by modulating the gut microbiome and inhibiting FMO3 expression [8, 9].

Animal studies have also contributed to our understanding. For instance, a study in laying hens demonstrated that changes in the gut microbiome and elevated TMAO levels were associated with cholesterol deposition in the liver [10]. However, in human studies on cerebrovascular atherosclerosis, TMAO levels did not significantly differ between symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, indicating that TMAO may not always correlate with disease severity [11].

Interestingly, recent findings have uncovered alternative microbial pathways for choline degradation. A novel strain of Citrobacter amalonaticus (CJ25) has been identified that degrades choline without producing TMA, suggesting non-atherogenic metabolic routes for choline in the gut [12]. This discovery adds a new dimension to understanding how dietary choline metabolism could potentially be redirected away from TMAO production.

In summary, a complex, bidirectional interplay exists between diet, the oral and gut microbiomes, and miRNA regulatory networks that influence TMAO production and cardiovascular risk. This review aims to synthesize these findings and explore the therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways to manage cardiovascular diseases.

Materials and methods

In this systematic review, we aimed to explore the interaction between trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), microRNAs (miRNAs), and the gut and oral microbiomes, focusing on their bidirectional influences and roles in the development of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), particularly atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease. To ensure a comprehensive and unbiased approach, we conducted a systematic search using two major databases: PubMed and Scopus. The search query was as follows: (“TMAO” OR “trimethylamine N-oxide”) AND (“miRNA” OR “microRNA”) AND (“gut microbiome” OR “oral microbiome” OR “gut microbiota”) AND (“cardiovascular disease” OR “atherosclerosis” OR “heart disease” OR “coronary artery disease”).

We limited our search to original studies published between 2020 and 2025, excluding reviews, commentaries, and conference abstracts. In PubMed, the search returned 25 articles. After excluding reviews and ineligible studies, 18 full-text articles remained. In Scopus, 11 records were retrieved, with 3 meeting the inclusion criteria. We also conducted manual reference screening and citation tracking, which yielded 24 additional eligible original studies relevant to the role of the oral microbiome, TMAO metabolism, and miRNA-mediated mechanisms in cardiovascular pathophysiology. Notably, this included recent experimental studies that investigated the molecular and cellular effects of TMAO and its regulation by specific miRNAs in both in vitro and in vivo models. Overall, a total of 45 articles were included: 34 original research articles (encompassing human clinical studies, animal experiments, and mechanistic in vitro investigations) and 11 reviews or meta-analyses. These studies were analyzed qualitatively to provide a comprehensive overview of the molecular pathways linking microbiome-derived metabolites, epigenetic regulators, and cardiovascular risk. Results and discussion

Microbial metabolism of dietary precursors and TMAO production

Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) is a gut microbiota-derived metabolite that has emerged as a significant contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD) pathogenesis. It is produced through a two-step process: gut microbiota metabolize dietary nutrients such as choline, L-carnitine, and betaine into trimethylamine (TMA), which is subsequently oxidized to TMAO by hepatic flavin-containing monooxygenases (mainly FMO3) [13]. Accumulating evidence indicates that elevated circulating TMAO levels are associated with increased risk of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure [14, 15]. Multiple human and animal studies have demonstrated that specific gut bacterial taxa influence TMA production. Notably, taxa from the Firmicutes and Proteobacteria phyla harbor the cutC gene, which encodes choline TMA-lyase, a key enzyme in TMA synthesis [16]. The relative abundance of these TMA-producing bacteria correlates with circulating TMAO concentrations and cardiovascular risk [4]. Furthermore, dietary habits, antibiotic exposure, and genetic polymorphisms in FMO3 can significantly modulate individual TMAO levels [17]. Beyond the gut, emerging data suggest that oral microbiota may also contribute to systemic TMAO levels. For instance, Wang et al. demonstrated that oral inoculation with Porphyromonas gingivalis in mice upregulated hepatic FMO3 expression and increased TMAO plasma levels [3]. These findings underscore the relevance of oral dysbiosis in modulating systemic metabolic pathways implicated in CVD. TMAO exerts its proatherogenic effects through several mechanisms. It promotes endothelial dysfunction by enhancing vascular inflammation, reduces reverse cholesterol transport, and facilitates foam cell formation [18]. Mechanistic studies further show that TMAO activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells, driving osteogenic reprogramming and vascular calcification [19]. This provides a biological explanation for the association between plasma TMAO and higher coronary artery calcium scores in clinical cohorts [20]. Moreover, TMAO activates pro-thrombotic pathways and contributes to platelet hyperreactivity [15]. In models of vascular injury, TMAO has been shown to aggravate endothelial dysfunction and impair repair responses [21, 22], while recent evidence suggests that short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate can counteract these effects by preserving endothelial integrity (Cheng et al., 2024) [7, 15]. Given its multifaceted role, TMAO has garnered interest as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target. Interventions aiming to reduce TMAO levels—such as dietary modulation, probiotics, and TMA-lyase inhibitors—are under active investigation. Notably, Roberts identified microbial enzyme inhibitors, fluoromethylcholine (FMC) and iodomethylcholine (IMC), that selectively block microbial TMA production without disrupting overall microbial viability, representing a promising avenue for microbiome-based CVD therapy [23].

Oral microbiome contributions to TMAO levels

While the gut microbiota has traditionally been considered the primary contributor to trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) biosynthesis, recent studies have highlighted a significant role for the oral microbiome in modulating systemic TMAO levels. The oral cavity harbors a complex and dynamic microbial community, including both commensal and pathogenic species, many of which are implicated in periodontitis and cardiovascular disease. Notably, P. gingivalis, a keystone pathogen in periodontal disease, has emerged as a critical mechanistic link between oral dysbiosis and systemic TMAO regulation. In an experimental model, Wang et al. demonstrated that oral inoculation with P. gingivalis in mice significantly increased hepatic FMO3 expression and plasma TMAO levels [3]. These results were corroborated in vitro using HepG2 hepatocyte models, where exposure to P. gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced upregulation of FMO3, the hepatic enzyme responsible for converting trimethylamine (TMA) to TMAO. More recent work shows that P. gingivalis not only raises plasma TMAO by upregulating hepatic FMO3 but also alters hepatic lipid metabolism, reinforcing an oral–gut–liver axis [24]. Mechanistic reviews further describe periodontal LPS as a driver of FMO3 expression, intestinal barrier disruption, and systemic TMAO increases, providing a broader conceptual framework for the oral–gut axis [25]. Clinically, non-surgical periodontal therapy has been shown to attenuate elevated plasma TMAO in patients with periodontitis, suggesting a modifiable oral contribution to systemic TMAO burden. In addition to P. gingivalis, other oral pathogens such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, and Streptococcus spp. are frequently enriched in dysbiotic periodontal biofilms and may indirectly contribute to systemic TMAO production. These bacteria can enhance local and systemic inflammation, increase epithelial permeability, and facilitate the translocation of bacterial components or metabolites into circulation—thereby amplifying hepatic FMO3 activity or enhancing TMA precursor delivery to the liver [12, 26–28]. Furthermore, oral–gut translocation has been proposed as a route through which oral pathogens influence gut microbiota composition and metabolic activity. Chronic oral infections may allow oral taxa to colonize the gut, where they can alter microbial ecology and increase the abundance of TMA-producing species, indirectly elevating systemic TMAO levels [12, 29]. Importantly, chronic periodontitis has been associated with elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, CRP), endothelial dysfunction, and accelerated atherogenesis, all of which overlap with the pathogenic effects of TMAO. Observational studies support a link between poor oral health and increased cardiovascular risk, reinforcing the potential systemic impact of oral microbiota on TMAO-mediated vascular damage [2, 30]. Together, these findings suggest that the oral microbiome contributes to systemic TMAO levels via both direct mechanisms (hepatic FMO3 upregulation) and indirect pathways (barrier dysfunction, oral–gut microbial transfer, systemic inflammation). Importantly, emerging clinical evidence indicates that periodontal therapy may represent a novel, modifiable strategy to reduce TMAO-related cardiovascular.

miRNA regulatory networks in TMAO metabolism and cardiovascular disease

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression and have emerged as key players in cardiovascular pathophysiology. Recent studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between TMAO metabolism and miRNA expression, adding complexity to the mechanisms linking the microbiome to cardiovascular disease (CVD). On one hand, TMAO has been shown to modulate the expression of specific miRNAs involved in inflammation, endothelial function, and lipid metabolism. Díez-Ricote et al. demonstrated that elevated TMAO levels upregulate miR-21-5p and miR-30c-5p in hepatic cells, resulting in suppression of their common target gene PER2, a regulator of circadian rhythms and metabolism [31, 32]. Sahoo et al. further identified that TMAO induces members of the pro-inflammatory miR-17/92 cluster in THP-1 monocytes and liver organoids, contributing to increased expression of IL-12 A and SERPINE1 (PAI-1) [33], both implicated in atherothrombosis. Conversely, some evidence hints that miRNAs may also regulate TMAO production or its upstream pathways. For example, miR-146a-5p was found to be inversely associated with plasma TMAO levels in hyperlipidemic mice, potentially through modulation of inflammatory and metabolic genes involved in the gut–liver axis [31]. However, these associations remain correlative, and no study to date has demonstrated a direct mechanistic link whereby miRNAs suppress hepatic FMO3 or microbial TMA production in vivo. Recent studies suggest that TMAO also interacts with non-coding RNA regulatory loops that amplify its pathogenic impact. In particular, long non-coding RNA NEAT1 has been implicated in forming a positive feedback axis with miR-370-3p, STAT3, and FMO3, whereby TMAO promotes NEAT1 expression, leading to suppression of miR-370-3p, derepression of STAT3, and subsequent upregulation of hepatic FMO3. This loop enhances TMAO production and atherogenesis, creating a self-reinforcing cycle [34]. Such RNA-mediated feedback mechanisms offer novel targets for therapeutic intervention, particularly through modulation of specific miRNAs or lncRNAs. Importantly, several miRNAs altered by TMAO exposure have known roles in CVD. MiR-21 is pro-fibrotic and pro-hypertrophic, contributing to pathological cardiac remodeling. MiR-30c exhibits anti-atherogenic properties by inhibiting MTP expression and reducing apoB-containing lipoproteins. The miR-17/92 cluster is linked to vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. These pathways suggest that TMAO may exert cardiovascular effects partly through miRNA modulation. While these studies support a regulatory interplay between TMAO and cardiovascular-relevant miRNAs, the field is still in its early stages. Most findings stem from in vitro models or murine studies, and robust in vivo evidence connecting TMAO-induced miRNA changes to specific CVD phenotypes is limited. Further research is needed to validate these pathways in clinical settings and to explore whether targeting TMAO–miRNA interactions may represent a novel therapeutic strategy. Interestingly, mechanistic evidence also suggests that short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, can counteract TMAO-induced pathophysiology [35], reported that butyrate supplementation mitigated atrial structural remodeling and arrhythmia susceptibility induced by TMAO, highlighting how microbial metabolites other than TMAO can act in an antagonistic fashion. This provides proof-of-concept that dietary modulation of the gut microbiota may shift the balance between pro-atherogenic and protective microbial metabolites, offering translational potential for cardiovascular prevention.

Therapeutic strategies targeting the TMAO pathway

Given the established role of TMAO in promoting atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, significant interest has emerged in developing therapeutic strategies to attenuate TMAO production or block its pathological effects. These approaches target various steps of the metabolic cascade, including dietary precursors, gut microbial enzymes, hepatic oxidation, and downstream signaling pathways. One promising strategy involves the use of small-molecule inhibitors of microbial TMA lyases, such as 3,3-dimethyl-1-butanol (DMB), which has been shown to reduce plasma TMAO levels and atherosclerotic lesion development in animal models [36]. However, clinical application remains limited due to off-target effects and microbial adaptability. In this context, the compounds FMC and IMC, identified by Roberts et al. [23], represent innovative microbial enzyme inhibitors that selectively block choline-to-TMA conversion without disrupting overall gut microbiota composition. These agents have demonstrated preclinical efficacy in reducing TMAO levels and mitigating cardiovascular risk, highlighting a potential avenue for precision microbiome-based therapy. Beyond microbial enzyme inhibition, recent studies show that natural compounds can also act on host pathways. Berberine lowers circulating TMAO by inhibiting microbial choline→TMA conversion and by reducing hepatic FMO3 expression in choline-fed mice. Moreover, FXR is a known regulator of hepatic FMO3, and berberine can modulate FXR signaling in liver–intestine contexts; however, a direct FXR/SHP-mediated suppression of FMO3 by berberine has not yet been demonstrated [37–39]. This dual action—microbial modulation and host regulation—broadens the therapeutic landscape. Another approach is dietary modulation. Restriction of TMAO precursors such as choline, carnitine, and betaine—abundant in red meat and dairy products—has been associated with reduced TMAO production [14, 40]. Conversely, diets enriched in fiber and polyphenols may foster the growth of non-TMA-producing commensals, indirectly attenuating TMAO synthesis [41]. Meta-analytic evidence supports these findings: adherence to Mediterranean or plant-based diets is consistently linked to reduced TMAO levels and lower cardiovascular event rates [42]. These dietary patterns enrich butyrate-producing taxa while reducing TMA-generating microbes, demonstrating both microbial and clinical benefit. Prebiotic supplementation (e.g., inulin, resistant starches) and dietary interventions such as the Mediterranean diet have shown favorable effects on TMAO levels and cardiovascular markers [8, 33]. Additionally, probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have been explored for their potential to reshape gut microbial ecology. Although the long-term effects on TMAO production are not fully characterized, initial findings suggest that certain probiotic strains may reduce TMA-producing bacteria, thereby lowering systemic TMAO [43]. Interestingly, intervention studies suggest that synbiotic combinations—prebiotic fiber plus probiotic strains such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus—may help reduce circulating TMAO-producing taxa while enriching beneficial commensals (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia) and thus decrease systemic TMAO levels [44–46].Hepatic FMO3 inhibition represents another target, though it carries the risk of accumulating TMA, which can cause trimethylaminuria and related toxicities [47]. Therefore, strategies modulating FMO3 must balance efficacy with safety. Finally, emerging studies have begun to investigate whether specific miRNAs or epigenetic modulators could regulate key enzymes and inflammatory pathways involved in TMAO metabolism [32, 33], although this remains largely experimental. Taken together, therapeutic interventions span microbial enzyme inhibitors, host enzymatic regulators, dietary and nutraceutical strategies, probiotic/synbiotic therapies, and epigenetic approaches. While most remain in preclinical or early clinical phases, the breadth of options underscores the translational potential of targeting the TMAO axis in precision cardiovascular medicine.

Clinical studies and population-based evidence

Several observational and clinical studies have examined the association between circulating TMAO levels and cardiovascular outcomes in diverse populations. These studies consistently report a positive correlation between elevated plasma TMAO and increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality. In community-based cohorts, longitudinal TMAO levels were linked with increased ASCVD risk [48], and EPIC‑Norfolk data showed baseline TMAO predicted coronary artery disease [49] Importantly, pooled analyses from the Cardiovascular Health Study and MESA further demonstrated that higher levels of TMAO and related metabolites—such as choline and crotonobetaine—are independently linked with incident heart failure development over long-term follow-up [50]. Similarly, in the Cardiovascular Health Study, dietary meat intake and TMAO-related metabolites were prospectively associated with CVD [51]. More recent community-based studies, including analyses from the MESA cohort, further support the prognostic role of TMAO in predicting ASCVD events and mortality [52]. Additional evidence from high-risk clinical groups—such as patients with CAD, diabetes, acute coronary syndrome, or after PCI—also suggests that TMAO may be a useful prognostic biomarker, although findings are not yet consistent across all studies) [2]. Other large-scale studies, such as those by Cho et al. and Lin et al., support these findings by linking higher TMAO levels with impaired endothelial function, increased arterial stiffness, and systemic inflammation [4, 53]. These clinical correlations are further strengthened by studies that concurrently measured dietary intake, gut microbiota composition, and TMAO metabolites, thereby reinforcing the microbiome–TMAO–CVD axis. Recent investigations have also begun to explore the interplay between TMAO and circulating miRNAs as potential biomarkers of cardiovascular risk. For instance, Díez-Ricote et al. and Sato et al. found altered expression of specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-21-5p, miR-30c-5p) in patients with elevated TMAO levels, suggesting their utility as combined diagnostic indicators [32, 33]. Although most evidence remains correlative, the consistent findings across ethnically and geographically diverse cohorts underscore the clinical relevance of TMAO as a biomarker and potential therapeutic target in cardiovascular disease. Further longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify causality and validate the clinical application of TMAO-based stratification and management strategies.

Bacterial signature and its role in cardiovascular disease

Emerging evidence suggests that specific bacterial signatures—distinct features of the oral and gut microbiomes—are closely associated with the development and progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD). In the oral cavity, microbial dysbiosis in periodontitis is characterized by an overabundance of pathogenic species, such as P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans [26, 27]. These pathogens possess choline TMA-lyase activity, enabling the conversion of dietary choline into trimethylamine (TMA), a precursor to TMAO, and exhibit invasive capabilities that allow them to enter and persist within vascular tissues. Once localized in atherosclerotic plaques, these bacteria can contribute to endothelial dysfunction and sustain local inflammatory cascades [29, 54]. Meanwhile, gut microbiome studies have demonstrated that dysbiosis—often characterized by a disrupted Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio—is linked to increased TMAO synthesis and impaired lipid metabolism [13]. This microbial imbalance enhances the conversion of dietary precursors to TMA by increasing the abundance of TMA-producing bacteria, thereby raising systemic TMAO levels and promoting pro-atherogenic processes [16]. The interaction between oral and gut microbiota further complicates this relationship. Recent findings suggest that oral microbes can translocate to the gut via swallowing, influencing the microbial composition and metabolic function of the gut microbiota [12]. This cross-colonization may amplify TMA production by introducing more TMA-generating species into the intestinal environment, thereby contributing to a systemic pro-atherogenic state. Elevated oral pathogens in the gut have been associated with increased inflammation and disrupted lipid homeostasis, both of which are key features in atherosclerosis [3]. Molecular and culture-based studies have also confirmed the presence of oral bacterial DNA in atherosclerotic plaques, providing direct evidence of their role in vascular inflammation and damage [16, 29]. These findings underscore the potential for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Targeting these pathogens through antimicrobial interventions, probiotic supplementation, or modulating host immune responses could represent promising approaches for reducing CVD risk [43].

Conclusion

Emerging evidence highlights the complex, multifactorial interplay between TMAO production, microbiome composition (both oral and gut), and miRNA regulatory networks in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. Dietary nutrients such as choline, carnitine, and betaine undergo microbial metabolism, resulting in TMAO formation, which is intricately linked to atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [13–15]. At the same time, the oral microbiome plays a pivotal role, particularly through pathogens like P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, which contribute to both local and systemic inflammatory responses [2, 33]. Additionally, miRNAs such as miR-21-5p, miR-30c-5p, and miR-126 regulate key pathways involved in inflammation, lipid metabolism, and endothelial function, providing a connection between microbial metabolism and cardiovascular outcomes [32, 33]. Therapeutic interventions targeting these pathways—whether by modulating the microbiota or utilizing miRNA-based strategies—hold great promise for personalized cardiovascular disease management. Future research that integrates multi-omics and standardized methodologies will be crucial in further elucidating these interactions and translating them into effective clinical therapies. Future directions

Several critical areas need further investigation to fully comprehend the complex relationship between the microbiome, TMAO, and cardiovascular disease. One key focus is the integration of multi-omics approaches. By combining metagenomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics, researchers can better understand how dietary components, microbial metabolites, and miRNA regulation interact. This approach will help uncover the molecular mechanisms driving cardiovascular disease and identify new therapeutic targets. Standardizing methodologies also remains essential for advancing research. Uniform protocols for sampling both the oral and gut microbiota, as well as for miRNA quantification, will improve data consistency across studies and facilitate large-scale longitudinal research. This would help us understand how long-term changes in microbiota composition impact cardiovascular risk. Translating animal model findings into clinical interventions is another priority. While significant mechanistic insights have been gained from animal studies, these need to be applied in human settings. Interventions aimed at modulating the microbiota, targeting miRNAs, or exploring oral microbiota transplantation could provide more precise ways to regulate cardiovascular risk by influencing microbial communities and their metabolic outputs. In addition, bacterial signature-based therapies represent a promising direction. Identifying specific bacterial signatures linked to elevated TMAO production in the oral cavity and gut could enable the development of treatments that target these communities directly, reducing cardiovascular risk by limiting the production of harmful metabolites like TMAO. Finally, personalized nutrition strategies tailored to individual microbiota profiles hold the potential to optimize cardiovascular health. Since TMAO production varies between individuals, dietary interventions customized to a person’s unique microbiome and miRNA profiles could improve cardiovascular outcomes in a more targeted and effective manner. These directions underscore the need for continued interdisciplinary research to better understand the microbial mechanisms of cardiovascular disease. As these approaches evolve, they will pave the way for more precise, personalized interventions that can significantly reduce cardiovascular disease risk.

Translational pathways for clinical implementation

Translating the mechanistic and observational insights regarding TMAO, the microbiome, and miRNA regulation into clinical practice requires an integrated, multidimensional approach. This involves combining advanced molecular diagnostics with tailored dietary interventions, longitudinal patient monitoring, and focused clinical research efforts. The goal is to create actionable strategies for cardiovascular risk stratification and management based on the individual’s microbial and molecular profile.

From a diagnostic perspective, recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and biomarker quantification enable early identification of patients at risk of microbially driven cardiovascular disease. Stool-based gut microbiota profiling, such as 16 S rRNA sequencing or metagenomic analyses, can detect the presence of TMA-producing bacterial taxa [13, 14]. Similarly, the oral microbiota, often overlooked, can be assessed via targeted qPCR to identify pathogens like P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, which have been linked to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [2, 3]. In parallel, plasma levels of TMAO—measured via LC-MS/MS—serve as a functional readout of microbial metabolism [15], while circulating microRNAs such as miR-21-5p and miR-126 offer additional insight into host inflammatory and vascular responses [32, 33]. These combined diagnostic modalities may be particularly relevant for patients with metabolic syndrome, poor oral health, or a family history of cardiovascular disease.

In terms of intervention, there is growing evidence that dietary and nutraceutical strategies can modulate the microbiota–TMAO axis. Diets rich in plant-based foods or modeled on the Mediterranean pattern have been associated with reduced TMAO levels and improved cardiovascular outcomes [8, 41]. Several natural compounds also show promise in targeting TMAO production. For example, berberine specifically, downregulates hepatic FMO3 via FXR/SHP signaling and inhibits microbial CutC activity, thereby reducing both microbial TMA generation and hepatic oxidation to TMAO [47, 55, 56], while specific probiotic strains—such as Bifidobacterium and other butyrate producers—can displace TMA-generating taxa [43]. Creatine, by competing with choline for microbial metabolism, may also reduce TMA synthesis, although this mechanism requires further clinical validation. These interventions could be considered in individuals with early signs of vascular dysfunction or elevated circulating TMAO, particularly as a complement to lifestyle modifications and conventional therapy.

Longitudinal monitoring offers an opportunity to personalize follow-up and prevention strategies. In patients with established cardiovascular risk factors—such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, or periodontal disease—it may be appropriate to implement regular assessments of TMAO levels and microbiome composition, alongside conventional risk markers. Microbiome-based stratification could also be applied in healthy individuals for early detection of dysbiosis and subclinical endothelial dysfunction. Periodontal evaluations, often neglected in cardiovascular prevention, may provide an accessible marker of systemic inflammatory burden, while serial measurements of circulating miRNAs may help track the progression or resolution of vascular injury.

To strengthen the clinical relevance of these approaches, targeted research is needed to validate the diagnostic and therapeutic value of microbiome–TMAO–miRNA interactions. Clinical studies should focus on identifying robust correlations between TMAO levels, specific miRNA signatures, and vascular outcomes, such as plaque stability or endothelial function. Randomized controlled trials assessing the efficacy of microbiota-modulating nutraceuticals—including probiotics and berberine—are critical to define optimal formulations, dosages, and patient selection criteria. Moreover, emerging interventions such as oral microbiota transplantation (OMT) deserve exploration, particularly for their potential to reprogram gut microbiota composition and reduce systemic inflammation. Finally, large-scale longitudinal studies incorporating multi-omics profiling will be key to unraveling how microbiome, epigenetic, and metabolic shifts converge in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. This framework supports the implementation of precision medicine in cardiovascular care, bridging the gap between mechanistic insights and clinical translation.

In conclusion, this review outlines that a possible future precision approach to cardiovascular prevention would integrate gut and oral microbiome profiling, LC-MS/MS TMAO quantification, and a selected panel of circulating miRNAs into care pathways, which could trigger individualized interventions (diet–probiotics/berberine–periodontal management) with longitudinal monitoring—and would be enabled by assay standardization, multicenter RCTs to define actionable thresholds and identify responders, and EHR-integrated decision support that could drive guideline adoption.

Final remarks

The multifaceted interplay between TMAO, microRNAs, and the oral and gut microbiomes represents a novel paradigm in cardiovascular disease research. Our review underscores that dietary nutrients are not only substrates for microbial metabolism but also act as triggers for epigenetic modifications via miRNA regulatory networks. This dual mechanism links microbial metabolism directly to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction. Notably, the identification of alternative choline-degrading pathways and the distinct bacterial signatures from both oral and gut microbiomes highlight innovative therapeutic targets. Such targets include strategies for microbiome modulation—using agents like berberine and ginkgolide B—as well as emerging miRNA-based therapies aimed at restoring homeostatic gene expression (e.g., through PER2 regulation). These integrated insights pave the way for precision medicine approaches that could ultimately reduce cardiovascular risk and improve patient outcomes. The convergence of these pathways offers a robust framework for the development of combined therapeutic interventions that address both upstream microbial metabolism and downstream host responses.
