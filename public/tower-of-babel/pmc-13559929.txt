Planned cesarean section or attempted vaginal delivery: Which is more environmentally friendly? An observational life cycle assessment study in France
Authors: Sherazade Jaafar, Sayed Gillani, Sophie Cordier, Charles Garabedian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhile the health sector is known to contribute significantly to environmental degradation, little is known about the specific ecological footprint of childbirth. We thus aimed to compare the environmental impacts of attempted vaginal delivery and planned cesarean section (c‐section), and to identify opportunities for improvement.Material and MethodsWe performed a life cycle assessment to quantify 16 environmental impact indicators from vaginal and c‐section deliveries. Data were collected at Lille University Hospital in 2024. Hotspot analyses were undertaken to identify the most impactful stages within each scenario.ResultsAttempted vaginal delivery consistently had a lower environmental impact compared with planned c‐section, the carbon footprints of which were 23.2 kg carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) versus 42.9 kg CO2e, respectively. Compared with planned c‐section, attempted vaginal delivery reduced greenhouse gas emissions by 46%, water consumption by 26%, fossil resource use by 44%, and mineral and metal resource use by 30%.ConclusionsAttempted vaginal delivery is more environmentally sustainable compared with planned c‐section. Perinatal health professionals should incorporate environmental considerations into clinical and public health decision‐making, and should engage in other efforts to reduce this sector's contributions to climate change.

INTRODUCTION

In 2000, the World Health Organization published a report placing health at the center of climate change concerns, emphasizing both direct and indirect health impacts and attributing approximately 150 000 deaths in 2000 to climate change. 1 , 2 In 2009, The Lancet published the article Managing the Health Effects of Climate Change, 3 in which global warming was described as “the biggest global health threat of the 21st century”. Thus, health became a driving force behind climate action. These findings are particularly relevant to gynecology and obstetrics, given the observed increases in preterm births, congenital anomalies, pregnancy‐induced hypertension, and low birth weight, all of which are attributable to climate change. 4 At the same time, studies have also shown that the healthcare sector itself has a considerable carbon footprint. As early as 2016, Lancet Countdown reports attributed 4%–5% of global greenhouse gas emissions to the health sector. 5 In France, The Shift Project's 2023 report estimated the health sector's emissions at 9 million tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), accounting for approximately 8% of national emissions. 6

Globally, one of the most performed interventions is the cesarean section (c‐section) delivery. In 2015, c‐sections constituted a significant 21.1% of all births worldwide, marking a substantial increase from 12.1% in 2000. 7 The usual debate surrounding c‐section versus vaginal delivery centers on the risks of maternal and neonatal complications and potential risks to future pregnancies. 8 Thus, the growing rate of c‐section use raises questions about both medical implications and environmental impacts.

However, although comparison of the ecological impacts of delivery modes is particularly interesting, they have been evaluated in only a few studies. In the United States (US), Campion et al. 9 used life cycle assessment (LCA) to analyze the environmental impacts of vaginal (i.e., only end‐of‐labor, without anesthesia) and c‐section deliveries. They showed that most of the carbon impact is from delivery/operating room ventilation and heating, waste disposal, and disposable custom packs. Using data from the United Kingdom (UK) and the Netherlands, Spil et al. 10 also applied LCA to compare the carbon footprints of delivery modes, and showed that the results were highly dependent on analgesia use. While these studies analyzed the effective mode of delivery, they did not consider attempted vaginal delivery, which can be highly complex. Thus, our objective was to compare the environmental impacts of attempted vaginal delivery and scheduled c‐section and to identify opportunities for improvement.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This single‐center LCA study was conducted in Lille, France.

Life cycle assessment method

LCA is a systematic methodology used to evaluate the environmental impacts associated with all stages of a product's life cycle: raw material extraction, manufacturing, transportation, use phase, and end‐of‐life treatment.

This approach adheres to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) frameworks 14 040 and 14 044, ensuring methodological consistency, transparency, and comparability of results.

The LCA follows a methodology: the product environmental footprint (PEF). This is the framework recommended by the European Commission for evaluating the environmental performance of products and services—including, in this context, healthcare procedures. The PEF includes 16 environmental impact indicators, organized into four categories: ecosystem degradation, climate change, human health degradation, and resource scarcity (Figure S1). 11 Rather than analyzing all 16 indicators with equal weight, PEF guidelines recommend focusing on those that collectively account for at least 80% of the total environmental impact of the system under study. This threshold‐based selection allows the assessment to concentrate on the most significant contributors while remaining tractable, and was applied here to each delivery mode independently.

Functional unit and system boundary

The LCA approach involved defining the study's goal and boundaries—herein, the carbon footprint of a delivery—from a woman's admission to the labor ward to her discharge. Thus, induction, postpartum stay, and neonatal care were excluded. The functional unit was the birth of a singleton term pregnancy (≥37 weeks gestation) with cephalic presentation. The goal was to quantify the environmental impacts of each delivery mode and identify areas for improvement.

Scenario

Two main scenarios were studied: attempted vaginal delivery and planned c‐section (Figure S2). We also considered various complications and occurrences in both scenarios. The attempted vaginal delivery scenario included manual rotation, artificial rupture of membranes, oxytocin use, instrumental delivery (forceps or vacuum or sequential instruments), postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), vaginal tearing (simple or obstetrical anal sphincter injury [OASIS]), and c‐section during labor. Each complication and occurrence has been added to the vaginal birth impact depending on its frequency in our center, using the hospital's coding database covering the period from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2024. This allowed us to obtain our annual carbon footprint. For c‐section, during labor, all equipment used in the delivery room prior to the cesarean procedure was taken into account. The planned c‐section scenario included PPH and use of vacuum or forceps. Different anesthesia and analgesia methods were also evaluated. 12

Data collection: Life cycle inventory (Figure S3)

We observed clinical practices during vaginal and c‐section deliveries, recording the type and quantity of medical devices used and estimating the resulting waste. All the devices are described in supplementary tables (Tables S1–S13).

Single‐use devices, including their primary and secondary packaging, were individually weighed with a precision scale (to 0.01 g). Primary packaging was defined as the first layer in direct contact with the device, and secondary packaging as the outer layer; shipping cartons were excluded. For each device and its packaging, the main raw material was identified from technical data sheets (via the UniHA procurement platform). Disposal pathways were also considered. Drug production was excluded due to unavailable emission factors, but packaging and administration processes were included. For reusable equipment, we included the sterilization method and the number of possible reuses; for textiles, detergent use and washing machine capacity were considered.

Waste

At the hospital, healthcare waste is categorized into two main types: general, non‐hazardous waste (waste similar to household waste, DAOM in French) and hazardous, infectious healthcare waste (DASRI in French). Each category follows a distinct disposal pathway. Non‐hazardous waste can be either landfilled or incinerated, whereas infectious risk waste must undergo high‐temperature incineration or a neutralization process. This neutralization involves physical or chemical disinfection as a pretreatment, followed by either incineration or landfilling.

At our facility, non‐hazardous waste is managed by the Lille Metropolitan Authority. As for infectious risk waste, 95% of the waste undergoes neutralization and is subsequently treated as general waste at a facility located 18 km from the hospital. The remaining 5% is directly incinerated at another center located 34 km from the hospital.

To estimate the amount of waste produced during a delivery, we determined the disposal route for each medical device and its packaging, observing the daily practices of our center's teams.

Sterilization

Regarding reusable materials, they first undergo a predisinfection process that takes place directly in the birth center, either through a washer‐disinfector or by manual predisinfection. Then, the devices are washed and sterilized in a center located less than 2 km from our center. Neither their production nor their disposal was accounted for herein because they are considered amortized.

Laundry facility

Our center's laundry facility is located 3.5 km from the maternity ward. The washing machines have a capacity of 45 kg per wash. All laundry is designed for multiple uses, theoretically allowing ~80 washes. The linen from our center is not subject to a different protocol and is not considered particularly soiled. We were unable to obtain information regarding the place of production of the linen.

Energy

Energy consumption by the obstetric unit (labor ward and c‐section room) could not be separated from the total energy consumption of our hospital (which includes gynecological and obstetrical emergencies, the hospitalization service, the gynecological operating room, and the entire pediatric section). A recording device was therefore installed on the electrical distribution panel of one c‐section room for 24 h, and on one delivery room for one hour; all devices present in the room were switched on during this period to provide a conservative estimate and avoid underestimating the room's overall energy consumption.

In the c‐section room, during the 24‐h recording period, three c‐sections took place, and the total energy consumption was 87.6 kWh. We therefore considered that the energy consumption of a c‐section was 29.2 kWh.

In the delivery room, the 1‐h energy consumption was 2.96 kWh. The measured one‐hour consumption was then extrapolated according to the occupancy time of the delivery room.

Calculations, modeling, and impact assessment

The analysis was conducted in several steps. First, the environmental impact categories accounting for 80% of each scenario's total impact were selected. Next, the environmental impact of each delivery scenario and its associated complications was assessed, enabling a comparison between attempted vaginal delivery and elective c‐section. For each scenario and subscenario, the most impactful stages were identified, allowing us to pinpoint environmental hotspots among the medical devices and/or therapeutic interventions.

We also analyzed our labor and delivery ward data using the hospital's coding database covering the period from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2024, to obtain our number of planned c‐section, the number of vaginal births, and the number of each complication and occurrence considered. We then applied our findings to these data to obtain our annual carbon footprint.

As noted, labor ward occupancy duration is ~9 h in our center, and in the case of a c‐section during labor, the operative time is ~75 min. The average duration of occupancy of the operating room in the case of a scheduled c‐section is 114 min.

This analysis was conducted in collaboration with Primum Non Nocere (First, Do No Harm), 13 a French organization specializing in social and environmental responsibility within the healthcare sector. Modeling was performed using SimaPro software and the Ecoinvent database. 14

RESULTS

Figure 1 illustrates the environmental impact indicators that collectively account for 80% of the total impact for each delivery mode, organized according to the four PEF categories. The nine most significant environmental impact categories for both vaginal and c‐section deliveries were climate change (21% and 25%, respectively), fossil resource use (16% and 18%), mineral and metal resource use (12% and 10%), and water use (14% and 10%), representing 63% of their total environmental impact. Other categories were acidification, human carcinogenic toxicity, particulate matter (respiratory effects), freshwater nutrient overload, and stratospheric ozone depletion (Figure 1).

Environmental impact categories representing 80% of the total impact.

Table 1 presents the nine relevant impact categories according to delivery mode. Regarding the climate change impact category specifically, in the attempted vaginal delivery group, physiological vaginal delivery without analgesia emits 13.9 kg CO2e. More than half of these emissions are attributable to the care provided during the delivery itself, and are particularly due to the use of single‐use scissors. Manual rotation produces 0.32 kg CO2e, while artificial rupture of membranes accounts for 0.97 kg CO2e. For both events, the main contributors were single‐use sterile gloves. The administration of oxytocin during labor (excluding active management of the third stage) represents 0.41 kg CO2e, primarily due to the use of single‐use infusion tubing. Delivery assisted by forceps results in an additional 2.10 kg CO2e, the use of a Kiwi vacuum extractor emits 2.45 kg CO2e, and the use of sequential instruments 2.48 kg CO2e. The occurrence of a first‐degree perineal tear or an episiotomy adds 4.15 kg CO2e, while an OASIS accounts for 2.70 kg CO2e. This difference is related to the use of a single‐use suture kit for minor tears, and a reusable suture tray for more complex cases. Finally, the carbon footprint of an attempted vaginal delivery ranges from 13.9 kg CO2e to 39.95 kg CO2e in case of c‐section during labor (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

Attempted vaginal delivery has a lower environmental impact compared with elective c‐section across all evaluated environmental indicators and all different situations, except c‐section during labor. Choosing vaginal delivery over scheduled c‐section, when clinically appropriate, could reduce the impact by 46% in terms of climate change.

Few studies have investigated the environmental impacts of childbirth. Our methodology is most closely aligned with Spil et al., 10 whose study was published during our data collection period. However, our results differ from theirs, with our observed values being higher, corresponding to approximately twice their reported carbon footprint. When excluding analgesia, the carbon emissions associated with uncomplicated physiological vaginal and c‐section deliveries in their study were 5.64 and 19.24 kg CO2e, respectively, compared with 13.9 and 35.7 kg CO2e in the present study. This variation may be explained by several factors, including the use of different emission factor databases, inclusion of secondary packaging, and variations in medical device types. However, in terms of impact reduction, the results are consistent. Spil et al. reported a 70% reduction in favor of vaginal delivery, compared with the reduction of 61% noted in our study. Regarding the incidence of complications, Spil et al. based their analysis on national data, 15 reporting rates of forceps, vacuum extraction, and c‐section during labor of 8.5%, 5.8%, and 11.8%, respectively. These values are similar to those observed in our center during 2024, with corresponding rates of 7.16%, 5.66%, and 10.86%. When including analgesia, the environmental impact of vaginal delivery reported in Spil et al. averaged 180.79 kg CO2e, primarily due to the use of nitrous oxide. This value is considerably higher than the impact associated with vaginal deliveries in our center, whether performed under EA (16.65 kg CO2e) or late‐stage spinal analgesia (15.92 kg CO2e). These findings highlight the substantial contribution of anesthesia—particularly nitrous oxide—to the overall environmental footprint of childbirth. 12

In the US, Campion et al. 9 compared vaginal delivery to c‐section, considering only the delivery itself, and concluded that the reduction in environmental impact of vaginal delivery was more than 50% compared with c‐section for all impact categories observed (global warming, acidification, carcinogens, noncarcinogens, respiratory effects, eutrophication, ozone depletion, ecotoxicity, and smog). Consistent with our findings, they also showed that most carbon impact was due to ventilation and heating of the delivery/operating room, waste disposal, and disposable custom packs.

These findings underscore that every obstetric decision carries an environmental footprint, and therefore encourage reflection on a circle of reciprocal interactions. This relation has been well documented, including in the context of obstetrics. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) has alerted the medical community to the association between environmental toxicity and adverse obstetric outcomes, as well as its potential impact on fertility. 16 This highlights the importance of addressing environmental factors to improve health issues. As healthcare providers, we have a responsibility to integrate environmental considerations into our decision‐making processes. Today, physical, emotional, and environmental safety must be considered synergistically. 17 Numerous studies have highlighted areas for improvement and the importance of incorporating ecological issues into the training of health professionals, as recommended by ACOG. 18 , 19 , 20

Ultimately, our study emphasizes that reducing environmental impacts can be achieved through a single lever, namely changing the delivery mode. However, the environmental impact hotspot identification also highlights the potential for many additional levers of emission reduction. These include reducing the use of medical devices and consumables, as well as acting directly on the devices themselves, in particular through improvements in raw material sourcing and local manufacturing. The 2025 report by The Shift Project illustrates the importance of these strategies, estimating that greenhouse gas emissions related solely to medical devices amounted to 7.4 metric tons of CO2e in 2023 in France. 21 The report proposes a roadmap for the healthcare industry to reduce these emissions by 72% by 2050 and identifies three key action areas: engaging suppliers committed to decarbonization and prioritizing local sourcing; decarbonizing industrial energy consumption through energy efficiency measures; and relocating the production of high‐use, high‐emission strategic medical devices such as gloves, surgical gowns, and drapes.

We used LCA in accordance with internationally recognized ISO standards, ensuring a rigorous, standardized, and transparent framework for evaluating environmental impacts. We also analyzed attempted vaginal deliveries, not just vaginal delivery itself, allowing consideration of all possible birth outcomes, in addition to considering c‐sections. We also accounted for multiple anesthesia modes, allowing a wider environmental impact assessment. Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. Analyses were limited to the labor ward and did not focus on the labor onset mode (i.e., spontaneous or induced) or on the length of hospital stay. We chose not to use National Health Service data because hospital stay in England does not necessarily reflect practices within the French maternity department. 22 Regardless, a hospital stay after c‐section is longer than after vaginal delivery and requires more nursing care, which would necessarily increase the differences between attempted vaginal and planned c‐section delivery outcomes, only amplifying the environmental gap between the two modes and further consolidating the greater carbon footprint of planned c‐section. Regarding room occupancy duration, our use of average values means that the impacts of an individual birth likely vary. We also averaged material quantities because we could not rely on medical orders, which are not separated in our center based on scheduled versus emergency orders. Regarding energy consumption, we faced challenges in accurately recording the precise energy use of the delivery rooms. While metering the entrances to one delivery room for 1 h and one c‐section room for 24 h enabled estimates of their energy consumption, we acknowledge a margin of uncertainty in the precision of these results. Indeed, it is difficult to obtain sub‐metered measurements in older units without separate monitoring devices. However, the risk of bias leans towards overestimation rather than underestimation, as energy consumption was calculated assuming all devices were active throughout the entire duration of labour, whereas some are only operated during the delivery itself.

External validity is another issue, as the anesthesia practice in our center (and throughout France) reflects a high epidural rate and no nitrous oxide use. Consequently, our findings may not generalize to locations such as the UK, where nitrous oxide plays a significant role in maternity services, accounting for 30% of greenhouse gas emissions from the anesthetics department. Furthermore, nitrous oxide has a remarkably high carbon impact, with a global warming potential over 100 years of 265. 12 , 23

CONCLUSION

Attempted vaginal delivery is more environmentally friendly than a planned c‐section. As perinatal healthcare professionals, it is our responsibility to integrate this issue into public health discussions and to actively contribute to the fight against climate change, without compromising the safety or quality of obstetric care. Beyond the mode of delivery, the production and use of single‐use medical devices should be brought to the forefront of the debate, with a focus on reducing their utilization and prioritizing more locally sourced manufacturing. This study identified key environmental hotspots, and future work should focus on addressing these and on quantifying the effects of clinical practice changes.
