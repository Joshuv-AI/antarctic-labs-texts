Three-dimensional transient thermal barcode for waste plastic identification
Authors: Kunal Singh, Thomas Thundat, Amit Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow global recycling rates of plastics are constrained by the lack of rapid, accurate, and scalable technologies for waste sorting. Here we report a standoff Transient Thermal Barcode (TTB) technique that identifies plastics using their mid-infrared (IR) absorption-induced thermal signatures. When the IR irradiation wavenumber matches a polymer absorption band in the molecular fingerprint regime, the non-radiative decay produces localized heating, yielding a transient hotspot that can be recorded with a thermal camera. Scanning with a tunable mid-IR source produces thermal spectra that closely track conventional FTIR spectrum. We show that a set of six mid-IR wavelengths can generate distinct thermal barcodes that enable the unique identification of all six major commercially relevant plastic classes in waste plastics. These results show the potential of mid-IR based non-contact plastic identification according to their resin codes for automated sorting in recycling.Subject terms: Characterization and analytical techniques, Infrared spectroscopy

Introduction

Plastics have unique and useful physical and chemical properties, and hence plastic products have become an integral part of modern society1,2. The low cost of virgin plastic materials, together with the ease of mass production of disposable plastic products, makes them ubiquitous in society3,4. The ever-growing demand for low-cost plastic products as an alternative to more durable metal and wood has been fueling an ever-increasing production of plastic materials worldwide. The US leads the world in the generation of plastic waste, with around ~42 million tons in 2023 or ~20% of the worldwide plastics production of ~220 million tons5,6. The global recycling rate for plastics, however, remains around 9%7,8. Most of the discarded waste plastic ends up in landfills or in the ocean, contaminating the environment. It is estimated that over 5.25 trillion discarded plastic products are present in the oceans9,10. The waste plastic materials further break down into micro and nanoplastics, which, when imbibed by living creatures and humans, can lead to health issues. Plastic pollution, therefore, poses a growing global threat to environmental health11,12.

One way to overcome the challenge posed by waste plastic is to increase the recycling rate of waste plastic materials. To facilitate easy recycling, commonly used plastic materials are identified by their resin identification code (RIC #) based on their chemical properties13,14. Mechanical sorting of waste plastics according to their RIC has the potential to make recycling economical15–17. Although using recycled plastics offers many economic and business opportunities, cross-contamination by other plastics significantly diminishes the quality of the recovered plastics and their economic advantages over virgin plastics. Therefore, enhancing the quality of the recycled materials remains a pressing challenge due to persistent cross-contamination by other plastic materials, due to inefficient and costly techniques for accurately sorting plastics18,19. Making the recycled materials cost-competitive by assuring their quality against virgin materials has the potential to encourage the use of recycled materials in products. Therefore, developing advanced techniques that can cost-effectively and accurately sort waste plastics according to their RIC numbers in a high-throughput fashion could have a transformative impact on the plastics industry and the environment.

Waste plastics put in recycling bins at the community level are sent to material recovery facilities (MRF), where plastics, along with other wastes, are sorted simultaneously. Plastic sorting in an MRF is predominantly done manually, which results in significant false identification and hence poor quality of sorted bales20,21. In addition, manual sorting exposes the workers to toxic additives in the materials22,23. Secondary plastic sorting facilities24,25 take in the unsorted plastics from MRFs and use a more mechanized approach of sorting plastics, which makes it more efficient and accurate than MRFs. Various machines using different techniques are used in secondary sorting facilities, which makes the sorting process in secondary sorting facilities more efficient than MRF sorting. However, the cost-effectiveness and accuracy of these advanced techniques have not yet reached an optimal level for widespread commercial deployment. Therefore, an efficient sorting process that can be implemented in the MRF itself is of utmost importance. Currently, recycling plastics is mostly limited to plastics with RIC numbers 1 and 224,25. Higher RIC number plastics are not recycled because of the lack of cost-competitive sorting techniques that have high sensitivity and selectivity in molecular identification26,27. Robotic arms using visible-range (RGB) imaging can sort plastics by appearance, whereas polymer identification typically requires spectroscopic sensing coupled with classification algorithms28. Limited availability of high-throughput technologies capable of rapid and selective molecular identification of waste plastics under real-world conditions is one of the major factors contributing to the currently low global recycling rates21,29.

To overcome this challenge, many different techniques have been proposed or are in development. However, many of these approaches face many technical challenges. For example, near-IR spectroscopic techniques, though attractive and cost-effective, suffer from selectivity issues because of the presence of overtones in the spectra of polymer materials30–32. Near-IR in combination with machine learning (ML) has achieved high accuracy for targeted tasks such as aged polypropylene classification with drift/baseline mitigation. However, this does not establish a general high-throughput, multi-polymer sorting approach under realistic mixed-stream conditions33.Techniques, such as laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) and Raman spectroscopy, have poor sensitivity, subpar selectivity, and an inability to detect black plastics, which increases the risk of false identification34–36. Many laser-based techniques, for example, LIBs, require powerful lasers and can only provide elemental information. Despite the high molecular selectivity of Raman spectroscopy in identifying plastic materials, the signals are extremely weak for practical applications. Irradiating the plastic sample using a broadband IR source and analyzing the scattered light using wavelength-dispersive spectroscopic detection has been demonstrated successfully in simulated conveyor belt conditions37,38. However, the technique suffers from noise issues because of the poor signal strength arising from the dispersion of the scattered light.

ATR-FTIR/FTIR-based mid-IR spectra paired with AI/ML have demonstrated strong polymer classification. However, these conventional approaches are generally contact-based and limited by slow spectral acquisition, which constrains direct translation to high-throughput sorting lines39,40. Dispersive mid-IR microbolometer systems are promising but can be constrained by optical complexity and limited standoff/field-of-view. Because detector pixels are used for spectral encoding, they do not naturally enable multiple regions of interest (ROIs) spatial tracking across belt width, so scaling may require replicated modules41. Mid-IR spectroscopy using a wavelength-dispersive source has been demonstrated to provide unique chemical identification of waste plastics42. Although this technique can provide high chemical selectivity in plastic identification, it is inherently slow because of the time needed for tuning the wavelength. The detection of various plastics using standoff detection techniques using thermally sensitive microcontilevers have been demonstrated before43,44. In this standoff spectroscopy, the sample is irradiated locally with an IR beam from a tunable source at a distance. They have reported high molecular selectivity and sensitivity for the identification of plastics, even those contaminated with food and other residues. To overcome the slower response time of microcantilevers, the use of a resonating quartz crystal tuning fork as the IR detector in standoff spectroscopy is also demonstrated45.

The IR spectroscopy, where the plastic material is illuminated with a broadband IR source and the scattered light is analyzed using a dispersive technique, can provide faster molecular selectivity46,47. Despite recent advances, the wavelength-dispersive technique for IR has poor sensitivity due to the weak signal. The dispersion of scattered light decreases the already weak signal by orders of magnitude. This technique is presently constrained by the need for stringent optical alignment. The angular position of the plastic sample with respect to the mirror collecting the IR light needs to be maintained steady for adequate signal collection. The added cost and complexity of instrumentation to reduce signal-to-noise ratio and limited sensitivity for consumer plastic products of diverse textures and thicknesses further highlight the need for robust standoff technology. An ideal technique should capture the molecular signature of waste plastics rapidly from a large distance and be amenable to retrofitting into existing sorting facilities with minimum instrumentation complexity and cost. Currently available techniques and methods do not offer a clear path for developing such a highly accurate sorting technique for waste plastics with minimal false identification that can be implemented in real-world conditions.

Therefore, an immediate and widespread need exists for the development of a robust, cost-competitive technique that can accurately and rapidly identify plastic waste in a high-throughput manner for integration into the harsh environments in material recycling facilities (MRF). The development of such a technique will have a transformative impact on increasing plastics recycling rates. It will also reduce plastics in landfills, incinerators, and the environment, as well as the production of virgin plastics and the resultant decrease in greenhouse gas emissions. It is estimated that one ton of recycled plastic saves 5.7 MWh of electricity, 685 gallons of oil, and 30 cubic yards of landfill space48,49. Developing technologies capable of reducing energy and emissions through a reduction in primary material consumption and an increase in secondary feedstock in the energy-intensive plastic industry.

Here, we report a standoff Transient Thermal Barcode (TTB) technique for identifying plastics using their mid-infrared-induced thermal signatures. The mid-IR region is known as the “molecular fingerprint regime” because of the unique spectral peaks that can be used for identifying organic materials. Whenever the irradiating wavenumber matches the absorption wavenumber, the temperature of the sample increases due to non-radiative relaxation. A plastic sample, when sequentially irradiated with mid-IR wavelength from a tunable source, shows a hotspot whose intensity varies with its IR absorption characteristics. The intensity of the hot spot is imaged with a thermal camera. A plot of IR image contrast of the hotspot as a function of wavenumber shows an excellent match with the sample’s FTIR spectra. Since the mid-IR region has unique absorption peaks, irradiating a plastic sample with selected wavelengths can generate localized hot spots. We identified six IR wavelengths that can generate a unique heating pattern, resembling a thermal barcode, for the molecular identification of all six types of plastic waste. Here, we demonstrate this technique of TTB for identifying waste plastics by using image contrast in an IR camera.

Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the experimental setup employed for the standoff IR thermal spectroscopy. The mid-infrared light from the tunable source, which is a quantum cascade laser (QCL) (Model LaserTune™ from Block Engineering), is directed at an angle of 45o using two unprotected gold mirrors (ThorLabs PF10-03-M03) and focused on the plastic sample using a ZnSe (Zinc Selenide) plano-convex lens (Thorlabs LA7656-E4). The range of mid-infrared wavelength from this source varies from 5.2 to 12.8 µm (1900 to 780 cm−1). The hotspot generated on the plastic sample is imaged with a thermal camera (Model FLIR A500 from Teledyne FLIR) through a ZnSe holographic wire grid polarizer (Thorlabs WP25H-Z). The infrared radiation detected by the camera is a combination of unpolarized thermal radiation generated by the plastic due to resonant IR absorption and the polarized infrared reflection due to the polarized nature of the infrared light used for the experiments. Imperfections on the surface of plastic cause the scattering of the polarized radiation in all directions, resulting in reduced intensity of reemitted radiation. The ZnSe polarizer suppresses the stray polarized reflections and enhances the detection of molecular vibrational signals. Further details on the experimental setup are included in the Supplementary Materials section (see S1 in Supplementary Materials).

Schematic diagram of the experimental setup for monitoring the IR-induced thermal response of plastics.

Since the IR absorption coefficients of plastics are not exactly zero at non-resonant wavelengths, due to their amorphous nature, background heating is present for all irradiating wavelengths (see Fig. S6 and Table S3 in Supplementary Materials). Removing the background allows obtaining the true thermal image due to IR-induced sample heating (see S1.2 in Supplementary Materials). Figure S2 and S3 in S1.1. illustrates the methodology to remove the background signal from the thermal signatures or hotspot profiles. In these experiments, the standoff distance was around 20 cm. The relative peak temperature of the thermal hot spot recorded by the thermal camera, when plotted as a function of irradiating wavelength, shows excellent agreement with the spectrum obtained with an ATR-FTIR using the same sample. Therefore, IR spectroscopy conducted in a standoff fashion by tuning the wavelength over the mid-IR range can acquire molecular signatures of plastics with high selectivity. Unlike near-IR spectra, which are plagued by overtones, the mid-IR region is free from overtones and is known as a “molecular fingerprint” regime.

Figure 2 shows the relative thermal intensities of hotspots, obtained from standoff thermal images, plotted as a function of irradiation wavelength for thin pieces of waste plastics from commercial products. The standoff distance from the plastic depends on the field of view (FOV) of the infrared lens and the detector resolution of the thermal camera. The present experiments were performed using a 240 × 180 FOV infrared lens together with a 464 × 348 pixel thermal detector, which determines the measurement area and spatial sampling of the thermal image. This configuration provides 161,472 effective measurement points per thermal frame, which is sufficient to observe the TTB effect on plastic samples at a standoff distance of 20 cm. However, standoff distance can be further extended by employing a higher-resolution thermal sensor, which preserves image detail at longer ranges. The thermal spectra in Fig. 2 shows excellent agreement with the FTIR-ATR spectra obtained for the same plastics. Therefore, it is obvious that resonant IR excitation of molecular bonds in plastic samples can generate hotspots indicative of IR absorption. The long-wave infrared (LWIR) thermal camera operates in the 7.5–14 µm spectral range (1333–714 cm−1), which overlaps with the polymer fingerprint region. However, unlike conventional FTIR measurements that record wavelength-resolved absorption spectra, the present approach detects temperature contrast generated by narrowband QCL illumination. When monochromatic radiation impinges on semi-crystalline polymers such as HDPE, wavelength dependent specular and diffuse reflections from crystalline domains can contribute to the detected signal, producing thermal reflection features that do not directly correspond to FTIR absorption intensities. Figure 2b illustrates such reflection induced thermal contrast in HDPE, and similar behavior was observed consistently across multiple HDPE samples. Despite these differences from FTIR spectral profiles, the reflection-assisted thermal contrast remains reproducible and provides material discrimination in the imaging-based identification approach used here. Although the thermal spectra of thin plastic samples agreed well with the ATR-FTIR spectra, those of thicker samples showed broader absorption peaks. However, the peak positions remained unchanged for thick and thin samples. Figure 3 shows the thermal spectra of thicker waste plastic samples. The FTIR-ATR geometry probes only a small evanescent region (a few wavelengths) near the surface and is surface sensitive. The near-surface region typically contains many defects and imperfections and differs from the bulk. Therefore, the IR spectra obtained using FTIR-ATR do not show any dependence on sample thickness. On the other hand, in the standoff technique, the penetration depth (propagating wave) is much higher than the non-propagating evanescent wave in FTIR-ATR and contains spectral information from deeper levels (see Fig. S7 in Supplementary Materials).

The reflectance of plastics in the mid-IR can influence the signal in the thermal image. Reflectance of plastic depends on its crystalline structure, density, and emissivity. The reflectance from the sample can be significantly reduced by using a polarizer. Although increased reflectance makes the absorption band broad, the peak position (wavenumber) does not change. In addition, the relative height ratios of different absorption bands for a plastic are unaffected by the reflectance. Figure 4 shows a comparison of thermal contrast spectra taken by the IR camera for thin and thick waste plastics.

As shown in Fig. 4a–c, multiple samples of the same polymer collected from different commercial sources exhibit highly consistent thermal response profiles across the investigated wavenumber range. The close agreement in peak positions and overall response patterns demonstrates the reproducibility of the thermal spectral signatures. Variations in sample thickness influence the band shape of the measured thermal spectral response, as shown in Fig. 4d–i. Thicker samples tend to exhibit broader spectral features and smoother responses, consistent with increased thermal mass and through thickness heat diffusion that temporally and spatially averages the temperature rise. Very thin samples, by comparison, often show sharper features and more pronounced local variations, reflecting faster heat dissipation and reduced thermal averaging. In this study, very thin samples are investigated to demonstrate the close qualitative agreement between ATR-FTIR and the thermal spectral response under conditions that yield cleaner features. As demonstrated in Fig. 3, multiple samples of each polymer collected from different commercial sources show consistent peak trends, indicating that beyond a moderate thickness the characteristic polymer thermal signatures are maintained for material discrimination (Also see S2.3 in Supplementary Materials). Although the mid-IR standoff spectroscopy can generate IR absorption peaks of the sample accurately, the scanning time can take many minutes, depending on the wavelength resolution. However, with the TTB technique, the time penalty challenge of tuning the wavelength over a broad range can be overcome by irradiating samples with only a few selected wavelengths. As thin consumer plastics agree very well with the ATR-FTIR spectrum, the thermal spectra of thin plastics were chosen as a baseline to select six IR wavelengths per resin code that can uniquely identify all plastics (see S1.2 and Table S2 in Supplementary Materials).

Irradiating the sample with these six unique wavelengths simultaneously will generate thermal hot spots on the sample, generating a thermal barcode through our background removal technique (see S1.2, Figs. S4 and S5 in Supplementary Materials). Figure 5 shows a table of hotspots obtained via the TTB technique when thin consumer plastics were illuminated with the selected wavelengths. Like a barcode, the hotspots rapidly imprint transient heat patterns on the plastic within milliseconds (Fig. 5). These invisible heat patterns are unique to the molecular nature of the resin. Since plastics have poor thermal diffusivity, the heat pattern generated by the IR stays discrete for imaging. Unlike other IR spectroscopic methods, where the scattered light from the sample needs to be collected and dispersed for identification, the TTB technique can image the hot spots from a large distance. In the TTB method, selected IR wavelengths heat the sample, and every pixel in the IR image is an integrated signal. Superimposition of the IR-induced heat patterns (barcodes) with a visible camera image allows molecular identification of the plastic samples (also see Fig. S8 in Supplementary Materials).

The results indicate that the selected six mid-IR wavelengths provide distinct thermal signatures for the investigated plastics. For clarity, a three-dimensional representation of the transient thermal barcode technique is also shown in Fig. 6g. The TTB technique has the potential as a technique for rapid sorting of plastics in an MRF. Utilizing this principle of comparing the relative intensities as a barcode, the 3D structure of TTB condenses the spectral information of plastics within a few wavelengths. Figure 6a–f illustrates that each polymer exhibits a distinct relative-intensity pattern across the selected diagnostic wavelengths, forming a unique spectral barcode. While absolute intensity values may vary slightly due to experimental conditions, the relative ordering and distribution of the normalized intensities remain characteristic for each polymer type. For example, PET shows a dominant response near 1463 cm−1, whereas HDPE and LDPE exhibit stronger responses at lower wavenumbers, and PS displays a pronounced feature near 1376 cm−1. These distinct intensity patterns enable discrimination of plastics using only a limited number of wavelengths, supporting the applicability of TTB technique for rapid material sorting. This molecular identification, together with computer vision-based sorting, has the advantage of chemical identification of waste plastics at large standoff distances. The raw visual data of thermal hotspots and the TTB output can be further seamlessly integrated into an AI/ML framework to create a quick and precise plastic identification system. Although this technique uses the QCL as a light source, it can be practiced utilizing mid-IR LEDs (Light Emitting Diodes).

In the current setup, a plastic sample was irradiated with specific IR wavenumbers sequentially. However, with a dedicated light source designed for the TTB, the sample can be irradiated simultaneously with these selected wavenumbers. Numerous wavenumber regions of interest can be created within a thermal image, and thermal data of these bond vibrations at different wavenumbers can be collected simultaneously, providing a method for high-throughput sorting of plastics using the TTB. The current experiments to validate the TTB technique were conducted using an uncooled microbolometer thermal detector. The response time of such detectors depends on the transfer of heat within the pixel, which leads to a delayed response time, typically around 15 to 25 ms. Such a delayed response limits its frame rate and dynamic measurement capability. The data collection rate is therefore limited to 30 Hz in the current setup. Also, the thermal sensitivity of this thermal detector is typically around 40 mK, with the 240 FOV lens creating thermal noise in the spectral data. Our experiments indicate that even with such thermal noise, the obtained thermal spectra of plastics are in excellent agreement with the corresponding ATR-FTIR spectra. Unlike other spectroscopy techniques, thermal signals do not require additional use of lock-in amplifiers to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

For a single wavelength, the irradiation dwell time is 100 ms, corresponding to approximately three acquired frames per dwell. The ROI temperature rise (ΔT) is computed after one-time baseline subtraction, which contributes only computational latency and does not affect the physical acquisition time. In practical waste streams, plastics contain manufacturing additives (e.g., stabilizers, fillers, pigments), surface contamination, and labels, all of which may influence IR measurements. The samples used in this study were obtained from commercial consumer products of varying color and appearance (see Fig. S1 in Supplementary Materials), and therefore inherently contained typical industrial additives. Despite this variability, the dominant thermal spectral features correspond to the polymer backbone, and no systematic peak shifts were observed across samples of the same polymer type (Fig. 3). While high-loading additives may influence signal amplitude, the extracted barcode features remained consistent under the tested conditions. Controlled contamination experiments (see S2.2 and Fig. S9 in Supplementary Materials) further showed that thin surface contaminants (coffee, tea, milk, oil) do not obscure the characteristic polymer thermal signature, producing only minor amplitude variations. Label removal was performed to ensure repeatable measurements. Adhesive layers or multilayer labels may introduce additional surface contributions; however, as demonstrated in the overlapping layer study (see S2.4 in Supplementary Materials), the measured response is governed primarily by the topmost exposed material within the selected ROI. Fully covering labels would therefore yield the spectral response of the label material itself.

In addition, black and heavily pigmented variants of the investigated polymers were evaluated (see Fig. S13 and S2.5 in Supplementary Materials). Black pigmentation introduces enhanced infrared absorption, leading to differences in signal amplitude. However, the characteristic peak positions and relative spectral structure remained comparable to those of non-black samples under identical measurement conditions. It is noted that heavily pigmented black plastics may exhibit variations in barcode contrast relative to non-black variants due to increased absorption. In practical implementations, such differences could be addressed by incorporating auxiliary RGB imaging to distinguish black from non-black items and applying calibrated barcode thresholds accordingly. A systematic investigation of pigmentation dependent calibration is reserved for future study. These results indicate that typical commercial additives and light surface contamination do not fundamentally alter the polymer-specific thermal signature under the present measurement conditions.

For high-speed plastic sorting using the TTB in MRF environments, which demands rapid sensing and precise classification, the capabilities of a cooled thermal detector can be utilized. These detectors operate by counting photons. Unlike microbolometers, within each pixel of this detector, the photons are converted to electrons and stored in an integration capacitor. The detector is made of Indium Antimonide (InSb) sensitive to mid-infrared frequencies, and its response time depends on its integration capacitor time, which typically ranges in nanoseconds, making it suitable for high-speed TTB. Also, the thermal sensitivity of this detector is typically less than 20 mK and possesses low thermal noise due to its cooled nature. Further, due to improved resolution and low integration time of such a detector, accurate information on hotspot size and its spreading rate can offer opportunities to expand the current capability of the TTB. However, operational longevity poses a distinct factor, where uncooled microbolometers can sustain continuous operation without maintenance, the photon counter detectors are limited by the finite operational time of their coolers and require periodic servicing. Simplified systems for the TTB can be developed using microelectromechanical systems (MEMS). A microcantilever is one such device that is sensitive to IR and offers a faster response time compared to uncooled microbolometers. Its small size, low energy requirements, and capacity for bulk manufacturing improve the economics to integrate them into compact TTB-based plastic sorting systems. However, certain instrumental challenges need to be addressed for such systems, like precise focus of reflected signals, compact signal-to-noise amplification systems, mechanical stability, calibration, and standardization. For industrial implementation, the TTB sensing approach can be integrated with conveyor-based sorting systems using high-speed scanning optics to interrogate plastics across the conveyor width. Real-time spectral identification from multiple regions of interest can be processed through a dedicated acquisition and control platform and transmitted directly to standard execution units, such as high-speed air-jet actuators or mechanical diverters, already used in material recovery facilities. Unlike optical appearance-based sorting approaches that rely on visual features, TTB identifies polymers through wavelength-selective thermal responses linked to polymer-specific molecular vibrational signatures, making classification less sensitive to surface color, printing, or contamination. By compressing chemically relevant information into a small set of unique wavelengths, the approach enables real-time decisions with minimal spectral sampling and can reduce the need for repeated sorting passes when high-purity outputs are required. In contrast to conventional NIR/SWIR sorting systems that primarily rely on weaker overtone and combination bands, the TTB approach leverages the stronger and more chemically specific fundamental vibrational features in the mid-IR fingerprint regime. While this does not imply a universal cost advantage over established visual or NIR/SWIR sorting systems, it provides a potentially scalable alternative to broadband mid-IR hyperspectral imaging hardware for achieving fingerprint-level chemical selectivity. In summary, we have demonstrated a novel technique, the Transient Thermal barcode technique (TTB), for rapid identification of plastic materials according to their resin identification codes. Using six unique wavelengths, we have selectively identified all six plastic types. Unlike conventional barcodes, this technique can provide 3D barcodes. The TTB technique has several unique advantages over other currently used or proposed techniques, such as selectivity, sensitivity, and speed of detection for high-throughput sorting. The future of TTB extends well beyond overcoming the current challenge of high-speed plastic sorting, as it provides a general platform for rapid, non-contact chemical recognition of materials, with potential applications in automated materials sorting, recycling infrastructure, and industrial process monitoring. This three-dimensional rapid plastic identification technique can serve a pivotal role in the circular economy, while simultaneously addressing many pressing scientific, industrial, and societal challenges.
