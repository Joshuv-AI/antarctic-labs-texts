Maximality and completeness of orthogonal exponentials on the cube Thanks: N.L. is supported by ISF Grant No. 1044/21. M.M. is supported by the Hungarian National Foundation for Scientific Research, Grants No. K132097, K146387.
Mihail N. Kolountzakis, Nir Lev, Máté Matolcsi

Abstract
. It is possible to have a packing by translates of a cube that is maximal (i.e. no other cube can be added without overlapping) but does not form a tiling. In the long running analogy of packing and tiling to orthogonality and completeness of exponentials on a domain, we pursue the question whether one can have maximal orthogonal sets of exponentials for a cube without them being complete. We prove that this is not possible in dimensions 1 1 and 2 2 , but is possible in dimensions 3 3 and higher. We provide several examples of such maximal incomplete sets of exponentials, differing in size, and we raise relevant questions. We also show that even in dimension 1 1 there are sets which are spectral (i.e. have a complete set of orthogonal exponentials) and yet they also possess maximal incomplete sets of orthogonal exponentials.

42B10, 42C05, 52C22

1.
Introduction

1.1.

If
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a measurable set of finite measure, we call it
spectral
if there exists a countable set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that the system
of exponential functions

E(\Lambda)=\{e^{2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

(1.1)

forms an orthogonal basis in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The set of frequencies
\Lambda
is then called a
spectrum
of
\Omega
. The properties of spectral sets, especially in comparison with properties of sets that tile by translations, have been a subject of intense research for decades (see, for instance, the recent survey
[
Kol24
]
). The Fuglede conjecture
[
Fug74
]
stated that the spectral sets are precisely those that can tile the space by translations (this means that one can find a collection of translates of the set such that almost every point of the space belongs to exactly one translate), but this conjecture is now known to be false in dimensions
3
and higher
[
Tao04
]
,
[
Mat05
]
,
[
KM06
]
,
[
FMM06
]
,
[
FR06
]
. Still a lot more is known and continues to be discovered about the connection of spectrality to tiling, a major recent result being the truth of the Fuglede conjecture for convex sets
[
LM22
]
.

For the unit cube
Q=[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{d}
, which is of course both a spectral set and a translational tile in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, a lot more is known. In particular it is known that the spectra
\Lambda
of
Q
are precisely the tiling complements of
Q
, namely the translation sets
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\{Q+\lambda\}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
, constitutes a tiling
[
LRW00
]
,
[
IP98
]
,
[
Kol00b
]
.

For any set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the two exponentials
e^{2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}
and
e^{2\pi i\langle\mu,x\rangle}
are easily seen to be orthogonal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
if and only if
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\Omega}(\lambda-\mu)=0
,
where
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\Omega}(\xi)=\int_{\Omega}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}dx

is the Fourier transform of the
indicator function
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}
.
Since the unit cube
Q
is a product set, we can easily compute
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{Q}
and find that its zero set is

G=\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\text{there is $j$ such that $\xi_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}$}\}.

(1.2)

At the same time a collection of translates
\{Q+t\}
,
t\in T
, forms a packing (i.e. the translates overlap at measure zero only) if and only if
(T-T)\cap(-1,1)^{d}=\{0\}
. Hence if
\Lambda
is an orthogonal set for the cube
Q
then
\Lambda-\Lambda\subset G\cup\{0\}
, which in turn implies, via the characterization (
1.2
), that
\{Q+\lambda\}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
, is a packing. In short, any orthogonal set for
Q
is also a packing set for
Q
(note, however, that the converse is not true).

1.2.

We now come to the question of maximality. A packing of
Q
is called
maximal
if it is not possible to add another translated copy of
Q
so that it remains a packing. Clearly every tiling is a maximal packing. It is easy to see though that there exist maximal packings of
Q
by a set of translates
T
which are not tilings. For instance, in dimension one, consider
T
to contain all numbers of the form
\pm(n-\frac{1}{4})
,
n=1,2,3,\dots
. The same is true in all dimensions.

An orthogonal set of frequencies
\Lambda
is similarly called maximal if it is not possible to add another point to it so that it remains orthogonal. Any spectrum of
Q
(i.e. an orthogonal and complete set of frequencies) is of course maximal. But now it is not clear whether maximality fails to imply completeness, just as maximality of a packing fails to imply tiling.

In fact, it is easy to see that in dimension one, the maximality of an orthogonal set of frequencies does imply completeness. Indeed, if the exponential system
E(\Lambda)

is orthogonal in
L^{2}([-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}])
then
\Lambda-\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
. Fix
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
. It follows that
\Lambda\subset\lambda_{0}+{\mathbb{Z}}
and, since all frequencies in
\lambda_{0}+{\mathbb{Z}}
are orthogonal to each other, maximality of
\Lambda
implies that
\Lambda=\lambda_{0}+{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is,
\Lambda
is a spectrum of
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
.

Is the same still true in higher dimensions? That is, if
\Lambda
is a maximal orthogonal set for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, must it be also complete?
In §
2
we show that every maximal orthogonal set for the square in
2
dimensions is also complete, just as in dimension
1
. Then in §
3
we show that this is not the case in dimension
3
. The maximal incomplete set we find there has the maximum possible density. In §
4
we give an alternative construction, again in dimension
3
, where our maximal set is now much thinner, almost a finite union of planes. We then show in §
5
how we can extend the results from dimension
3
to higher dimensions. In §
6
we examine some general properties that maximal orthogonal sets for the cube must have. Closing, in §
7
we show that if we are willing to examine spectral sets other than the cube then even in dimension
1
we can find an example of a spectral set which has a maximal incomplete set of exponentials.

2.
Maximal orthogonal sets in two dimensions

First we show that in two dimensions, the maximality of an orthogonal set for the unit square implies completeness, similar to the one-dimensional case.

Theorem 2.1
.

Any maximal orthogonal set for the unit square
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is also complete, i.e. it is a
spectrum. More generally, any orthogonal set
for the unit square can be embedded as a
subset of some spectrum.

The proof will require
a lemma which
can be found in

[
Kol00a
, Observation 1]
as well as in
[
GL17
, Lemma 11.4]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a subset of an abelian group
H
, and let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two subgroups of
H
. Assume that
X-X\subset H_{1}\cup H_{2}
. Then
X-X\subset H_{1}
or
X-X\subset H_{2}
.

Now let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

be an orthogonal set for the unit square.
Then
\Lambda-\Lambda\subset G\cup\{0\}
,
where
G
is the zero set from (
1.2
)
(with
d=2
). It follows that if we define

H_{1}=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
and
H_{2}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
which are both subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset H_{1}\cup H_{2}
.
Lemma
2.2
then implies that

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
or

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Let us consider the case where

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}

(the other case is similar).
By translating the set
\Lambda
we may assume that it contains the origin, so this implies that

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}
.
We now observe that if

(n,t)
and
(n,s)
are two distinct
points in
\Lambda
, then orthogonality implies
that
t-s
must be an integer. Hence

\Lambda
is contained in a set of the form

\{(n,k+t(n)):n,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

(2.1)

where
t(n)
are real numbers.
But it is known, see

[
JP99
, Theorem 5]
, that
any set of the form
(
2.1
)
is a spectrum of the unit square.
Hence
\Lambda
can be embedded as a
subset of a spectrum.
In particular,
\Lambda
is maximal
if and only if it coincides with
a spectrum.

3.
A maximal incomplete orthogonal set
in three dimensions

3.1.

Next we show that Theorem
2.1
does not extend to three dimensions. Namely, we will prove the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists an
orthogonal set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

for the unit cube,
which is maximal but incomplete.

Our strategy is to start from the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
which is a spectrum for the unit cube. We then remove a small part of this spectrum, contained in the union of the three coordinate planes, which leaves us with a subset
B
of the integer lattice. The set
B
is still orthogonal, but of course it is not maximal as it embeds in a spectrum. However, we will show that one can add a small set
A
to
B
such that the orthogonality is still preserved, but at the same time it eliminates the possibility of further extending
A\cup B
to a spectrum, or stronger, to any larger orthogonal set.

3.2.

We now turn to the details of the construction.
Fix three real numbers
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that none of them is an integer.
Let
A
be the set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
consisting of all points that have
one of the following forms:

(0,\beta-k,\gamma),\quad(\alpha,0,\gamma-k),\quad(\alpha-k,\beta,0)

(3.1)

where
k
is a nonzero integer.
Notice that
A
is contained in the union of three lines.

Let
B
be the set of all vectors
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose coordinates are
nonzero integers.
This set contains all integer vectors
except those lying in a union of three planes.

The sets
A
and
B
are disjoint.
Let
\Lambda=A\cup B
be their union.
It is straightforward to check that

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset G\cup\{0\}
,
where
G
is the zero set from (
1.2
)
(with
d=3
). Hence

\Lambda
is an orthogonal set for the unit cube
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In particular,
Q+\Lambda
is a packing.

On the other hand we claim that
\Lambda
is not a complete set of frequencies. Indeed, if it was complete then
Q+\Lambda
would be a tiling, see

[
LRW00
]
,
[
IP98
]
,
[
Kol00b
]
.
But notice that each one of the three slabs

[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbb{R}\times[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]\times\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]

(3.2)

is scarcely covered by the cubes in the packing
Q+\Lambda
, so this packing is not a tiling.

3.3.

To continue we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and suppose that
s-B\subset G
.
Then at least one of the coordinates of
s
is zero.

Proof.

Let
s=(u,v,w)
and
suppose to the contrary that all the coordinates
u,v,w
are nonzero.
Since we have
(u,v,w)-(1,1,1)\in G
then at least one of the
coordinates
u,v,w
must be an integer. Hence, say,
u=j

for some nonzero integer
j
. Next, since
we also have
(u,v,w)-(j,1,1)\in G
,
then at least one of the other two coordinates
v,w

must be an integer. Hence, say,
v=k

for some nonzero integer
k
. Finally, since

(u,v,w)-(j,k,1)\in G
,
then
w
must be an integer, so
w=l

for some nonzero integer
l
. But this implies that
the zero vector

(u,v,w)-(j,k,l)
is in
G
, a contradiction.
∎

3.4.

Finally we show that
\Lambda

is a maximal orthogonal set for the unit cube
Q
.
Suppose to the contrary that this is not the case,
and let

s=(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be
a point not belonging to
\Lambda
,
such that
\Lambda\cup\{s\}
is still an orthogonal set. In other words, this means that we have

s-\Lambda\subset G
.

In particular, we have
s-B\subset G
, hence by Lemma
3.2

at least one of the coordinates
u,v,w
is zero.
Let us consider the case where
u=0

(the other two cases are similar).
We then have
(0,v,w)-(0,\beta-k,\gamma)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
k
. This implies that
either
w-\gamma
is a nonzero integer, or that
v=\beta
.

If
v=\beta
, then
s=(0,\beta,w)
.
We now have
(0,\beta,w)-(\alpha,0,\gamma-k)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
k
. This implies that
w=\gamma
.
We thus obtain
s=(0,\beta,\gamma)
, but this
contradicts the condition that

s-(\alpha-k,\beta,0)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
k
. This case cannot therefore happen.

Hence we must have
w=\gamma-l
where
l

is a nonzero integer. Then
s=(0,v,\gamma-l)
.
The condition
s-(\alpha,0,\gamma-l)\in G

now implies that
v=m
for some nonzero integer
m
.
We therefore obtain that
s=(0,m,\gamma-l)
, but this again
contradicts the condition that

s-(\alpha-k,\beta,0)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
k
. Hence
this case cannot happen either.

This shows that
\Lambda
is indeed
a maximal orthogonal set for the unit cube.

4.
A “thin” maximal set in three dimensions

4.1.

The maximal incomplete orthogonal set
constructed in Section
3

is, in a sense, almost as large as a
spectrum. Indeed, this set contains
all integer vectors except those
lying in the union of the three
coordinate planes, and
in particular, the set has
the maximum possible density.

In this section we give an alternative
construction which, somewhat surprisingly,
produces a much thinner set.

Theorem 4.1
.

There is an orthogonal
set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
for the unit cube,
which is maximal but
contained in a finite union of
planes (and, as a consequence, is incomplete).

4.2.

Fix again three real numbers
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
such that none of them is an integer.
Let
\Lambda
be the set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
consisting of all points that have
one of the following forms:

(0,0,0),\quad(n,\beta-k,\gamma),\quad(\alpha,n,\gamma-k),\quad(\alpha-k,\beta,n)

(4.1)

where
n
and
k
are nonzero integers.

It is straightforward to check that

\Lambda-\Lambda\subset G\cup\{0\}
, hence

\Lambda
is an orthogonal set for the unit cube
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. On the other hand,
\Lambda
is contained in the union of three planes plus the origin,
hence
Q+\Lambda
is not a tiling
and
\Lambda
is not a spectrum for
Q
.

4.3.

On the other hand we now claim that
\Lambda

is a maximal orthogonal set for the unit
cube. Indeed, if not then there is
a point
s=(u,v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

not belonging to
\Lambda
,
such that
\Lambda\cup\{s\}
is still an
orthogonal set. In other words, this means that

s-\Lambda\subset G
.

The condition
(u,v,w)-(0,0,0)\in G
implies that
at least one of
u,v,w
is a nonzero integer.
Let us consider the case where
u=j
for some nonzero integer
j

(the other two cases are similar).
We then have
(j,v,w)-(j,\beta-k,\gamma)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
k
. This implies that
either
w-\gamma
is a nonzero integer, or that
v=\beta
.

If
v=\beta
, then
s=(j,\beta,w)
.
We have
(j,\beta,w)-(\alpha,n,\gamma-k)\in G
for every two
nonzero integers
n
and
k
. This implies that
w=\gamma
.
We thus obtain
s=(j,\beta,\gamma)
, but this
contradicts the condition that

s-(\alpha-k,\beta,n)\in G
for every two
nonzero integers
n
and
k
. So this case cannot happen.

Hence we must have
w=\gamma-l
where
l

is a nonzero integer. Then
s=(j,v,\gamma-l)
.
The condition
s-(\alpha,n,\gamma-l)\in G
for every
nonzero integer
n
now implies that
v=0
.
We therefore obtain that
s=(j,0,\gamma-l)
, but this again
contradicts the condition that

s-(\alpha-k,\beta,n)\in G
for every two
nonzero integers
n
and
k
. It follows that
this case cannot happen either.

This shows that
\Lambda
is indeed
a maximal orthogonal set for the unit cube.

4.4.

We leave open the following question, which arises naturally.

Problem 4.2
.

Does there exist a maximal
orthogonal set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
for the unit cube,
such that
\Lambda
is contained in
a union of finitely many lines?

5.
Extensions to higher dimensions

We will now use
the results of Sections
3
and
4
in order to construct examples of maximal incomplete orthogonal sets for the cube also in dimensions greater than
3
.
We will present two methods that allow us to
use lower-dimensional examples in order to produce examples in higher dimensions.

5.1.

Suppose that
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a maximal
and incomplete orthogonal set for
the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We will show how to use this set
\Lambda
in order to
construct a set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

which is a maximal
and incomplete orthogonal set for the unit cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We take
\Gamma=A\cup B
to be
the union of two disjoint sets
A
and
B
,
defined as follows. Let
A
be
the set of all vectors
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
of the form

(\lambda,0)
where
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
We also let
B
be the set of vectors

(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m}

such that the vector
q

has at least one nonzero coordinate,
i.e.
q
is not the zero vector.

It is clear that
A
and
B
are indeed two
disjoint sets. It is also straightforward to check that their union
\Gamma=A\cup B

is an orthogonal set for the unit cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a maximal
and incomplete orthogonal set for
the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
the set
\Gamma
constructed above
is a maximal
and incomplete orthogonal set for the unit cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let us first show that
\Gamma
is maximal.
Suppose to the contrary that this is not the case,
so there is a point
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

which is orthogonal to both
A
and
B
.
If
v
is the zero vector, then
orthogonality to
A
implies that
the vector
u
is orthogonal to
\Lambda

(with respect to the
unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) which
contradicts the maximality of
\Lambda
.
Hence this cannot happen, so

v
must be a nonzero vector.
We now define a vector

q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
by taking
the coordinate
q_{j}
to
coincide with the corresponding
coordinate
v_{j}
of
v
if it is
an integer,
and otherwise we let
q_{j}
be an arbitrary
nonzero integer.
This implies that
q
is a
nonzero vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
which is not
orthogonal to
v
(with respect to the
unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
).
But now
(u,v)

is orthogonal to all vectors of the
form
(p,q)
,
p\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, since these
vectors are in
B
. However this
is possible only if
u

is orthogonal to all vectors

p\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which contradicts
the completeness of the spectrum

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This shows that
\Gamma
is indeed
maximal.

It remains to show that
\Gamma
is not
complete. Indeed, if it was complete
then
\Gamma
would be a tiling set
for the unit cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
again due to the result in

[
LRW00
]
,
[
IP98
]
,
[
Kol00b
]
.
In turn this would imply that
\Lambda

is a tiling set for the unit cube
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As a consequence,

\Lambda
would be a spectrum,
contrary to our assumption.
∎

5.2.

Another possibility for using
lower-dimensional examples
in order to construct examples
in higher dimensions, is to
utilize cartesian products.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

be two maximal orthogonal sets
for the unit cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\mathbb{R}^{m}
respectively. Then the cartesian
product
A\times B

is a maximal orthogonal set
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Moreover,
A\times B
is a complete set if and only if
both
A
and
B
are complete sets.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that

A\times B

is an orthogonal set
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Suppose to the contrary that
A\times B
is not
maximal, and let
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

be orthogonal to all the points of
A\times B
.
The maximality of
A

implies the existence of a point
a\in A

which is not orthogonal to
u
.
Similarly, by the maximality of
B

there is
b\in B
which is not orthogonal to
v
.
It follows that
(u,v)

is not orthogonal to
the point

(a,b)
which belongs to
A\times B
,
a contradiction. Hence
A\times B
must be
a maximal set.

Finally, it is well known,
see e.g.
[
JP99
, Theorem 4]
,
that if both
A
and
B
are complete sets,
then also
A\times B
is complete.
The converse is also true,
see
[
JP99
, Lemma 2]
.
∎

5.3.

It is natural to pose the following question, which generalizes Problem
4.2
.
Let us define the

affine dimension
of a
discrete set

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the smallest
integer
k
such that

\Lambda
can be covered by a
finite number of translated

k
-dimensional subspaces.

Problem 5.3
.

How small can be the
affine dimension of a maximal
orthogonal set
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
?

If we write
d=3q+r
where

r\in\{0,1,2\}
, then the set

\Gamma=\Lambda\times\cdots\times\Lambda\times\mathbb{Z}^{r}

is a maximal incomplete orthogonal set for the unit
cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

is the set from Theorem
4.1
,
which appears
q
times in the product.
We observe that this set
\Gamma

is contained in a finite union of translated
subspaces of dimension
k=2q+r
.

6.
Some general properties of maximal orthogonal sets

6.1.

In the previous sections,
we have constructed examples of
maximal incomplete orthogonal sets
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 3
.
Moreover, we have seen
examples of rather “thin”
maximal orthogonal sets.
A natural question arises as to
how small can these sets be.
The following proposition
gives a necessary condition on a maximal set,
which in particular shows that it
cannot be “too small” in a sense.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a maximal orthogonal set
for the unit cube. Then for every point

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\Lambda
and for every integer
n
,
the number
\lambda_{1}+n

must appear as the first
coordinate of some point from
\Lambda
.
Similarly,
\lambda_{2}+n

must appear as the second
coordinate of some point from
\Lambda
,
and so on.

Proof.

By translating the set
\Lambda
we may assume
that the point

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d})
lies at the origin.
By symmetry it also suffices to prove that every integer
n
must appear as the first coordinate of some point from
\Lambda
.

Suppose to the contrary that
n
is an integer which does not appear as the first coordinate of any point from
\Lambda
. We then claim that the point
\xi=(n,0,0,...,0)
is orthogonal to all points of
\Lambda
, which contradicts the maximality of
\Lambda
. Indeed,
n
must be a nonzero integer and hence
\xi
is orthogonal to the origin. Now let
\mu
be any point of
\Lambda
other than the origin. Then
\mu
is orthogonal to the origin and so at least one of the coordinates of
\mu
is a nonzero integer. If this coordinate is not the first one then
\xi
is orthogonal to
\mu
and we are done. Otherwise the first coordinate of
\mu
is a (nonzero) integer, which cannot be equal to
n
by assumption. This once again implies that
\xi
is orthogonal to
\mu
, and so the assertion is established.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.1
we obtain:

Corollary 6.2
.

Any maximal orthogonal set
for the unit cube must be an infinite set.

Moreover, it follows
that a maximal orthogonal set
cannot be “too localized” in space.
Specifically,
consider an axis-aligned
slab of width one,
i.e. a set of the form

S_{j}(a)=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}):a\leqslant x_{j}\leqslant a+1\},\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant d,\quad a\in\mathbb{R}.

(6.1)

Since any closed interval of length
1

contains an integer,
Theorem
6.1
implies:

Corollary 6.3
.

A maximal orthogonal set
for the unit cube must intersect
any axis-aligned slab of width one.

6.2.

Assume now that

\Lambda
is a
finite
orthogonal set
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\Lambda
is not maximal (Corollary
6.2
).
A standard application of Zorn’s lemma shows that
\Lambda

can always be embedded as a subset of a maximal orthogonal set.
Is it always possible to
embed
\Lambda
as a subset of
an orthogonal and complete set
(i.e. a spectrum) of the cube?

Theorem
2.1
tells us that
the answer is ‘yes’ in two dimensions.
Let us show that it is
‘no’ in dimensions
3
and higher:

Theorem 6.4
.

There exists a finite
orthogonal set
for the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 3
,
which cannot be embedded
as a subset of any spectrum of the cube.

Proof.

By our previous results there exists
a maximal but incomplete
orthogonal set
\Lambda

for the unit cube.
For each
n
define

\Lambda_{n}:=\Lambda\cap[-n,n]^{d}
.
The set
\Lambda_{n}
is finite for every
n
.
We claim that there is
n

such that
the set
\Lambda_{n}
cannot be embedded
as a subset of any spectrum.
Indeed, if this is not the case then
each
\Lambda_{n}
is contained in some
spectrum
\Gamma_{n}
of the cube.
It is known,
see
[
GL16
, Section 3]
,

[
Kol16
]
,
that there exists a weakly
convergent subsequence

\Gamma_{n_{j}}
whose weak limit

\Gamma
is also a spectrum for the cube.
We have
\Lambda_{m}\subset\Lambda_{n_{j}}\subset\Gamma_{n_{j}}

for
n_{j}>m
, and letting
j\to+\infty

it follows that the weak limit
\Gamma

contains the set
\Lambda_{m}
. Since
m

is arbitrary we conclude that

\Lambda\subset\Gamma
, that is,
\Lambda
can be embedded in a spectrum, in contradiction to its maximality and incompleteness.
∎

7.
Spectral sets other than the cube

As we saw in the introduction, in the case of the cube in dimension
1
(i.e. the unit interval), any maximal orthogonal set of frequencies necessarily forms a spectrum. A natural question arises whether this is true for any spectral set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
. Below, we utilize an idea of the previous sections to show that this is not the case.

Theorem 7.1
.

There exists a spectral set
H\subset\mathbb{R}
(a union of finitely many intervals with integer endpoints) which admits a maximal but incomplete orthogonal set.

We motivate the concrete example given below by a short informal discussion. We are looking for a set
H\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
H
is spectral, but there exists a maximal orthogonal set of exponentials in
L^{2}(H)
which is not complete. As Fuglede’s conjecture is still open in
\mathbb{R}
, we may as well assume that
H
tiles
\mathbb{R}
by translations. If so, it is known that any tiling of
\mathbb{R}
by a bounded region
H
is periodic
and is essentially equivalent to a tiling of
\mathbb{Z}

(see
[
LW96
]
).
Furthermore, any tiling of
\mathbb{Z}
is known to arise as a periodic extension of a tiling of a finite cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{N}=\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
(see
[
New77
]
).
It is therefore natural to look for the set
H
in the form
H=\cup_{j=1}^{k}[h_{j},h_{j}+1)
, where the set
H_{0}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\}\subset\{0,1,2,\dots,N-1\}
is a tile (and a spectral set) in the group
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
(where we have identified
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
with the set
\{0,1,2,\dots,N-1\}
in the natural way).

However, we note that if the cardinality of the set
H_{0}
is a prime number, then the structure theory of tiling of finite abelian groups indicates that any maximal orthogonal set for
H_{0}
is necessarily a spectrum. Presumably the same holds if the number of elements of the set
H_{0}
is a prime power, or even a product of two prime powers,
see
[
CM99
]
.
This suggests that we should be looking for a more complicated example.

In the concrete choice of
N
and the set
H_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{N}
we shall therefore mimic the “thin” example of Section
4
, given in dimension
3
for the cube. Let
p<q<r
be distinct odd primes, and let
N=p^{2}q^{2}r^{2}
. Then
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
is isomorphic to the product group
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}
, with a group isomorphism being the mapping
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
given by

\phi(a,b,c)=q^{2}r^{2}a+p^{2}r^{2}b+p^{2}q^{2}c

(7.1)

(clearly this mapping is a
homomorphism, and one can easily check that
it has a trivial kernel,
hence it is an isomorphism).

Let us now consider the “discrete cube”
H_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}
given by

H_{0}=\{(a,b,c):\ 0\leqslant a\leqslant p-1,\ 0\leqslant b\leqslant q-1,\ 0\leqslant c\leqslant r-1\}.

(7.2)

The Fourier transform
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{H_{0}}
is
a function on the dual group, which we can
also identify with

\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}
.
The zeros of
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{H_{0}}
are then the vectors
whose first coordinate is a nonzero multiple of
p
, or the second coordinate is a nonzero multiple of
q
, or the third coordinate is a nonzero multiple of
r
. As a consequence,
H_{0}
is a spectral set in the group
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}
and the set

\Gamma_{0}=\{(u,v,w):\ p|u,\ q|v,\ r|w\}
serves as a spectrum for
H_{0}
. Indeed,
\Gamma_{0}
is an orthogonal set of frequencies for
H_{0}
and it has the same cardinality as
H_{0}
(see also
[
AGK18
]
).

We will now exhibit a maximal but incomplete set of frequencies for
H_{0}
. We let
\Lambda_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q^{2}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{r^{2}}
be the set of all vectors that have one of the following forms:

(0,0,0),\quad(n,1-k,1),\quad(1,k,1-m),\quad(1-n,1,m)

(7.3)

where
n
is a nonzero multiple of
p
,
k
is a nonzero multiple of
q
and
m
is a nonzero multiple of
r
.
(Notice that these elements of
\Lambda_{0}

are analogous to those in (
4.1
).)
It is then straightforward to check that
\Lambda_{0}
forms an orthogonal set of frequencies for
H_{0}
, and one can repeat the proof of
Section
4
verbatim to show that
\Lambda_{0}
is maximal. It is also clear that
\Lambda_{0}
is not a spectrum of
H_{0}
, since we have
|H_{0}|=pqr
while

|\Lambda_{0}|<pq+qr+rp<3qr\leqslant pqr.

(7.4)

Via the isomorphism (
7.1
) we deduce that
\phi(H_{0})\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
is a spectral set which has a maximal, incomplete set of frequencies
\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}
. We view
\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}
as a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
via the identification of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
with its dual group.

Finally, let
H\subset\mathbb{R}
be the set defined by
H=\cup_{h\in\phi(H_{0})}[h,h+1)
, or equivalently,

\mathds{1}_{H}=\mathds{1}_{\phi(H_{0})}\ast\mathds{1}_{[0,1)}
. This implies that the zero set of the Fourier transform
\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{H}
satisfies

\big(\{\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{H}=0\}\cup\{0\}\big)\cap\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{Z}}=\frac{1}{N}\big(\{\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\phi(H_{0})}=0\}\cup\{0\}\big)+{\mathbb{Z}},

(7.5)

where we view
\{\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\phi(H_{0})}=0\}
as a subset of
\{0,1,2,\ldots,N-1\}
. Define also

\Lambda=\frac{1}{N}\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}+{\mathbb{Z}}

(7.6)

and observe that this is an orthogonal set for
H
because of (
7.5
). For the same reason we have that if
\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}
is a spectrum of
\phi(H_{0})
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
then
\Gamma=\frac{1}{N}\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}+{\mathbb{Z}}
is a spectrum of
H
in
{\mathbb{R}}
. The orthogonality of
\Gamma
follows from (
7.5
) and the completeness follows from (a) having the right density and (b) being periodic, as (a) and (b) together imply that the “packing”
|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{H}|^{2}+\Gamma
is actually a tiling
(see
[
Kol24
, Section 2]
).

It remains to show the maximality of
\Lambda
(the incompleteness of
\Lambda
is guaranteed since its density is too small). Assume that
\Lambda\cup\{x\}
is an orthogonal set of frequencies for
H
, where
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Lambda
. By the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodicity of
\Lambda
we may assume that
x\in[0,1)
.
Now, if we knew that

x=k/N
for some
k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,N-1\}\setminus\Lambda^{\prime}_{0}
, then again by
(
7.5
) this would
imply that
\Lambda^{\prime}_{0}\cup\{k\}
is an orthogonal set of frequencies for
\phi(H_{0})
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
, which contradicts the maximality of
\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}
.
To conclude the proof we therefore need to rule out the possibility that
x
is a real number in
[0,1)
which is not of the form
k/N
.

To this end we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

If
H_{0}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{2}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{q^{2}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{r^{2}}
is the set defined by (
7.2
) and
\phi
is given by (
7.1
)
then, viewing the set
\phi(H_{0})\subset\{0,1,2,\dots,N-1\}

as a subset of the integer group
\mathbb{Z}
, the Fourier transform of its indicator function (viewed as a function on the dual group
{\mathbb{T}}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
) has no zeros outside the set

\frac{1}{p^{2}q^{2}r^{2}}{\mathbb{Z}}.

(7.7)

Proof.

We must show that if

\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\phi(H_{0})}(\xi/N)=0
then
\xi\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
A=\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
,

B=\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}

and
C=\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}

viewed as subsets of the integers.
We make the following observation:
if we interpret
the expression (
7.1
)
as an integer (and not as an
element of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
),
then all the values attained by
this expression for

a\in A
,
b\in B
and
c\in C

lie in the subset

\{0,1,2,\dots,N-1\}

of the integers. Indeed,
all these values are nonnegative and
not greater than

q^{2}r^{2}p+p^{2}r^{2}q+p^{2}q^{2}r=pqr(qr+pr+pq)<pqr(3qr)\leqslant p^{2}q^{2}r^{2}=N.

(7.8)

This means that as the triple
(a,b,c)

goes through the elements of

A\times B\times C
, then
the expression (
7.1
)
not only goes through the
elements of
\phi(H_{0})

viewed as congruence classes
modulo
N
, but it also
goes through the representatives
in
\{0,1,2,\dots,N-1\}

(as a subset of
\mathbb{Z}
)
of the elements of the set

\phi(H_{0})
.

This observation allows us to compute the
Fourier transform

\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\phi(H_{0})}(\xi/N)
.
Assume that
\xi\in\mathbb{R}

is not an integer, then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\phi(H_{0})}(\xi/N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}\sum_{c\in C}\exp(-2\pi i\phi(a,b,c)\xi/N)

(7.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}\sum_{c\in C}\exp(-2\pi i(q^{2}r^{2}a+p^{2}r^{2}b+p^{2}q^{2}c)\xi/N)

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}e^{-2\pi ia\xi/p^{2}}\sum_{b=0}^{q-1}e^{-2\pi ib\xi/q^{2}}\sum_{c=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi ic\xi/r^{2}}

(7.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/p}}{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/p^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/q}}{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/q^{2}}}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/r}}{1-e^{-2\pi i\xi/r^{2}}},

(7.12)

where the observation made above was used
in
(
7.9
) and (
7.10
).
It remains to notice that the quantity
in (
7.12
)
is nonzero, as otherwise
\xi
would have to be an integer divisible by
p
,
q
or
r
. Hence the lemma
is established.
∎

Finally, we use the lemma to
finish the proof of the
maximality of
\Lambda
. Recall that we have
assumed that
\Lambda\cup\{x\}
is an orthogonal
set of frequencies for
H
, where
x
is a
real number in
[0,1)\setminus\Lambda
.
Since
0\in\Lambda
, Lemma
7.2

allows us to conclude that
x=k/N
for some

k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N-1\}\setminus\Lambda^{\prime}_{0}
.
As we have seen,
this implies that
\Lambda^{\prime}_{0}\cup\{k\}
is
an orthogonal set of frequencies
for
\phi(H_{0})\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
,
contradicting the maximality of
\Lambda_{0}^{\prime}
.

Note added in proof

After submitting our paper we learned that maximal orthogonal sets for the unit cube in low dimensions were studied in the paper
[
LP11
]
, where they are referred to as sets which determine an unextendible system of cubes. In particular
[
LP11
, Theorem 5]
gives a complete structural description of the maximal incomplete orthogonal sets for the unit cube in three dimensions. One can infer from this result a negative answer to Problem 4.2.
