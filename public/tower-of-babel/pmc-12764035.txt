Cumulative childhood lead exposure estimation and school-age IQ in a prospective birth cohort
Authors: Aimée Vester, Yingying Xu, Nicholas C Newman, Melinda C MacDougall, George D Papandonatos, Patrick J Parsons, Christopher D Palmer, Joseph M Braun, Bruce P Lanphear, Aimin Chen, Kim M Cecil, Kimberly Yolton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLead is a well-known neurotoxicant with no identified safe level. Prior studies found that childhood lead exposure is associated with decreased intelligence quotient (IQ) scores. However, most studies rely on a limited number of blood lead measurements. In this prospective pregnancy and birth cohort, we estimated cumulative childhood lead exposure using repeated blood lead concentrations and regression calibration, allowing for more accurate assessment of lead burden over time and its association with IQ. MethodsThis prospective study included 262 mother-child dyads from Greater Cincinnati enrolled in the Health Outcomes and Measures of the Environment (HOME) Study from 2003 to 2006. We obtained serial blood lead measurements and estimated cumulative childhood lead exposure using a regression calibration method. Outcome was assessed via Wechsler-based IQ testing at ages 5-12 years. We examined the association between estimated cumulative childhood lead exposure and child IQ using linear regression models. ResultsOur cohort had low levels of estimated lifetime average lead exposure (geometric mean: 1.21 μg/dL). Overall, estimated lead exposure decreased from age 12 months to time of IQ test. Cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate was associated with decreased IQ at ages 5-12 years in unadjusted analyses, but not after adjusting for maternal IQ, household income, reported prenatal vitamin use, Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment score, and maternal serum cotinine. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for prenatal total folate did not markedly change our results. We assessed early-life, school-age, or concurrent blood lead exposure estimate in place of cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and observed a similar pattern of results. ConclusionsWe used a regression calibration method to leverage robust, repeated lead exposure data in our prospective pregnancy and birth cohort. In this cohort with low levels of lead exposure, cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate was negatively associated with school-age IQ in unadjusted analyses but not adjusted analyses. We considered sociodemographic and maternal factors previously associated with cognitive development. Background

Lead is a well-known neurotoxicant with no known safe level of exposure [1]. The detrimental effects of lead on cognition have been described extensively, with lasting impacts on cognitive development and intelligence quotient (IQ) [2–11]. A recent estimate suggests an average loss of 2.6 IQ points per person living in the United States (US) is attributable to lead exposure, representing a significant impact at a population level [12]. While the removal of lead from gasoline and paint in the 1970 s greatly reduced the lead exposure burden in US children [13], an estimated 2% of US children 1–5 years still have blood lead levels ≥ 5 µg/dL, and the geometric mean blood lead level in children 1–5 years was 0.83 µg/dL from 2011 to 2016 [14]. Thus, ongoing lead exposure remains a significant public health concern.

Optimizing cognitive potential is imperative to maximizing child health outcomes for all children [15]. However, lead exposure often does not occur randomly or independently given that disproportionate environmental exposures exist in the US [16]. In a nationally-representative cohort of children ages 1–11 years, for example, higher blood lead level was associated with poverty, older housing, and non-White race [14]. Chemical exposures and sociodemographic factors may thus overlap and should carefully be considered together.

A comprehensive approach is especially important in studies of cognitive outcomes as multiple factors have been associated with child cognition, including psychosocial factors, co-exposures, and potential protective factors. Psychosocial factors such as family socioeconomic position [17, 18] and a child’s home environment [18] impact cognitive outcomes. One validated measure of home environment quality is the Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment Inventory [19], which has been independently associated with child cognition in previous studies [18, 20]. This inventory scores multiple aspects of the home environment, including the physical state of the home, play materials available, and degree of stimulation a child may experience. In addition to psychosocial factors, co-exposures are important to study. For example, tobacco smoke exposure, quantified via maternal serum cotinine during pregnancy in our study, is a known co-exposure associated with lead [21–23] and IQ [24–30]. To evaluate possible protective factors, we also contemplated self-reported prenatal vitamin use and prenatal serum total folate concentration as potential effect modifiers, as maternal nutrient status is associated with cognitive outcomes [31]. A randomized controlled trial of continued prenatal folic acid supplementation found higher Wechsler-based IQ scores in children at age 7 years, compared to children of mothers receiving only folic acid supplementation during the early prenatal period [32]. Another randomized controlled trial observed higher IQ scores in children at ages 6–7 years if mothers received supplementation with 15 micronutrients (including folic acid), compared to supplementation with folic acid alone [33]. Our study thus aims to more expansively address potential contributors to cognitive outcomes by carefully considering psychosocial factors, co-exposure to tobacco smoke, and possible protective factors.

In this study, we examined the association between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and school-age IQ in a prospective pregnancy and birth cohort with relatively low levels of lead exposure. Previous pediatric studies have assessed a limited number of lead exposure timepoints in later [34–39] or concurrent [3, 5, 37, 40, 41] cognitive outcomes. Our prospective study addresses this notable gap in lead exposure characterization. We performed repeated child blood lead concentration measurements up until measurement of our outcome of interest. Utilizing a regression calibration technique, we then calculated an estimate of cumulative lead exposure for most of childhood via serial blood lead measurements while accounting for measurement error that can occur with repeated, longitudinal data.

Methods

Study design

The Health Outcomes and Measures of the Environment (HOME) Study is a longitudinal pregnancy and birth cohort study. The study enrolled 468 pregnant women between 2003 and 2006 from nine prenatal practices affiliated with three hospitals in the Greater Cincinnati region [42, 43]. To capture children at increased risk of lead exposure, eligible women resided in homes built in or before 1978 within a nine-county region including urban (50%), suburban (38%), and rural areas (12%) [44]. Additionally, women were >18 years old, were < 19 weeks pregnant, did not live in a mobile or trailer residence, were fluent in English, and planned to reside in the study area for at least one year. Women were excluded if they were diagnosed with: HIV, diabetes mellitus, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, seizure or thyroid disorders that required medications, and/or cancer requiring treatment with radiation or chemotherapy.

Study participants

Of the 468 pregnant women enrolled, 59 women dropped out of the study. There were 409 live births, of which 398 were singleton births. To ensure independence of each participant and reduce the influence of contextual characteristics, we randomly selected one twin per twin gestation to include in our study (n = 11). Additionally, 17 participants did not have a child lead measurement available between ages 1–12 years; and 130 participants did not undergo IQ testing at the 5-, 8- or 12-year study visits. Thus, our analysis included 262 unique mother-child dyads (Supplemental Fig. 1). We found no significant differences in demographics between those included and excluded from the analysis (Table 1, end of document). The HOME Study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) and all delivery hospitals. The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) Laboratory relied on the CCHMC IRB. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating caregivers.

Exposure assessment

To measure chemical exposures, pregnant women and their children provided several biological samples during routine prenatal care visits and visits to the study clinic, respectively. HOME Study staff collected maternal venous blood samples at 16- and 26-week prenatal visits, as well as at delivery. Child venous blood samples were collected at ages 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, and 12 years. We prescreened sample collection materials to ensure they were free of significant lead contamination and stored samples at −80 °C until transport to the laboratory. Samples obtained at the 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8-year visits were analyzed by the CDC Environmental Health Laboratory and samples obtained at the 12-year visit were analyzed by the Trace Elements section within the Laboratory of Inorganic and Nuclear Chemistry at the Wadsworth Center, New York State Department of Health (NYSDOH). Blood lead concentrations were determined using well validated methods at CDC [45] and NYSDOH labs [46] based on inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) [45]. All analytical batches included multilevel blood-based quality control samples and reagent blanks (coefficient of variation < 3.5%). The blood lead limit of detection (LOD) was 0.25 µg/dL for the 1–8-year samples, and 0.06 µg/dL for the 12-year samples. For the single measurement below the LOD, we imputed using the LOD/ per Hornung and Reed [47].

We accounted for measurement error in repeated measures of lead exposure via a regression calibration approach [48]. We utilized the available subject-specific blood lead measurements to estimate true childhood blood lead with a random effects model and obtained the intercept and slope for each subject-specific linear trajectory of child blood lead levels, on a natural log scale. We derived a cumulative lead exposure estimate from age 12 months to the exact age in months at the time of IQ testing by exponentiating the data and integrating them in closed form to obtain the total area under the curve over the exposure window for each participant. To calculate estimated lifetime average lead exposure, we divided cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate by age at IQ test in months. For clarity, we use the term “blood lead concentration” whenever we describe the sampled blood lead concentration, and “cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate” whenever we describe the approximated blood lead level throughout the manuscript.

Neurocognitive assessment

To assess cognitive ability, we administered the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scales of Intelligence-III (WPPSI-III) [49] at the 5-year study visit and the Wechsler Intelligence Scales for Children-IV (WISC-IV) [50] at the 8-year study visit. If a participant did not complete an 8-year study visit, we administered the WISC-IV at the 12-year study visit. The WPPSI-III and WISC-IV assessments both provide comparable measures of verbal IQ (VIQ), performance intelligence (PIQ), processing speed quotient (PSQ), and full-scale IQ (FSIQ). The WISC-IV also provides a working memory index (WMI). We standardized IQ scores to population norms (mean:100, SD: 15) and for child age according to the publisher’s guidelines [49, 50]. An experienced developmental psychologist (KY) trained and certified all research coordinators and performed quality checks every six months to ensure accuracy in the administration and scoring of the instruments. All IQ tests were administered in a quiet research clinic testing room free of distraction.

In our analysis, we prioritized the 8-year IQ measure (n = 222) because most study participants completed the 8-year assessment. We supplemented the measure at 5 years (n = 25) or 12 years (n = 15) if the 8-year IQ was not completed. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for full-scale IQ scores at 5, 8, and 12 years of age was 0.79, indicating excellent reliability [51].

Covariates

We considered potential covariates a priori based on prior studies of lead exposure, child IQ, and previously described confounding variables. We developed a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) using DAGitty software (Supplemental Fig. 2) to identify the minimal sufficient adjustment set (Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment score, household income, maternal IQ, and maternal serum cotinine). Next, we performed collinearity and correlation analyses, described further in the statistical methods below, to refine the final adjusted model. Furthermore, we considered potential effect modifiers such as child sex, prenatal vitamin use, and maternal total folate. Since prenatal vitamins contain folate, we elected to use prenatal vitamin use in the primary adjusted model and maternal folate in sensitivity analyses.

Trained research assistants obtained sociodemographic variables via standardized interviews at study enrollment. They additionally collected dietary information that included the frequency of prenatal vitamin use [42, 52, 53]. Trained research assistants completed the Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment inventory during home visits at age 12 months. Maternal IQ was assessed with the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI) during a research clinic visit.

We measured serum cotinine in maternal samples at 16- and 26-weeks of gestation as a biomarker of tobacco exposure during pregnancy [54] and used the average of these two measures in our analyses as a continuous measure. For descriptive purposes, women were categorized as active smokers if serum cotinine levels were ≥ 3 ng/mL [55]. Maternal serum cotinine measurements were performed by the CDC Environmental Exposures laboratory. Maternal serum samples were stored at −80 °C before shipment to the CDC. Cotinine was measured via high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry [56]. The limit of detection was 0.015 ng/mL.

The study completed a within-cohort randomized control trial, randomizing participants to a home lead reduction or safety intervention (e.g. placement of baby gates) study arm. There were 38 study participants whose landlord refused to participate in the lead or safety intervention, and they were categorized in the “no intervention” group [57]. Study arm randomization was included in the sensitivity analysis as lead intervention or “other”. The “other” group included participants randomized to the safety intervention study arm and those who did not receive either intervention due to landlord refusal. Research coordinators were blinded to the randomized control trial intervention group assignment.

We obtained whole blood samples during pregnancy and at birth, and measured whole blood total folate as previously described [53]. Samples were stored at −20 °C until shipment to the CDC Environmental Exposures laboratory for analysis via HPLC-MS/MS [58]. The laboratory measured five folate vitamers and then calculated whole blood total folate as the sum of all folate vitamers. 5-methyltetrahydrofolate represents approximately 80% of total folate. We included average whole blood total folate in our sensitivity analyses, and this was calculated from levels at 16 weeks gestation and birth. Whole blood total folate was only measured at birth in women who did not have whole blood total folate measurements available at 16 weeks gestation (N = 42).

Statistical analysis

We conducted univariate analyses examining the data distributions and computed descriptive statistics for child lead measures, IQ scores, demographic and other participant characteristics. Cumulative child blood lead and gestational serum cotinine concentrations were log2-transformed prior to further analyses to reduce the influence of extreme values. We evaluated correlations of child lead measures and IQ scores with covariates of interest (Tab. 3). We examined the association between child lead and IQ in bivariate linear regression models, followed by multivariable linear regression models adjusting for the following covariates: sex, household income (categorical: <$20,000, $20,000–40,000, $40,001–80,000, >$80,000), maternal full-scale IQ, maternal prenatal vitamin use (daily versus not daily), log2-transformed maternal serum cotinine, and Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment score. We calculated the adjusted generalized standard error inflation factor (aGSIF) to assess multicollinearity in our primary adjusted models for covariates of interest [59]. We utilized a threshold of 1.6 (correlating with a VIF = 2.5) [60] to identify whether there may be multicollinearity influencing our model. We performed sensitivity analyses that additionally adjusted for maternal total folate in our primary adjusted model of the association between child lead and IQ. Maternal total folate was log2-transformed to reduce the influence of extreme values. We also assessed for interaction between maternal total folate and cumulative childhood lead exposure by including their product term in the model. Moreover, we assessed for interaction between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and each of the following: prenatal vitamin use, child sex, and household income. Additionally, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses: [1] including study intervention arm (Lead Intervention versus Safety or No Intervention) [2], using log2-transformed 2-year child blood lead exposure estimate as the exposure variable [3], using log2-transformed 5-year child blood lead exposure estimate as the exposure variable [4], using lifetime average lead exposure estimate instead of cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate as the exposure variable [5], using concurrent blood lead exposure estimate or the concurrent blood lead concentration instead of cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate as the exposure variable [6], excluding log2-transformed maternal serum cotinine, and [7] including only study participants with IQ assessment available at age 8 years, as the majority of participants (n = 222) had IQ assessments available at age 8. Lastly, we conducted post hoc power analysis to calculate the sample size necessary to detect the effect size shown in our primary adjusted model for full-scale IQ, with 80% power at a significance level of α = 0.05. We utilized R software (Version 4.2.3) for all of our statistical analyses, except the post hoc power analysis. Post hoc power analysis was conducted using nQuery software (Version 9.4).

Results

Among the 262 mother-child dyads included in our study, 55% of children were female. At study enrollment (2003–2006), median household income for the pregnant women was $55,000/year (Interquartile Range (IQR): $62,500/year). Mothers were more likely to be college graduates (49%) and the average maternal full-scale IQ was 105.2 points (standard deviation 15.2), which is similar to the WASI full-scale standard mean of 100 points (standard deviation 15 points) [61]. Most mothers reported taking prenatal vitamins daily (74%), and most (89%) were not active tobacco smokers based on serum cotinine concentrations during pregnancy (< 3 ng/mL [55]). The mean FSIQ, VIQ, PIQ, PSQ, and WMI at ages 5–12 years were 102.1, 100.4, 105.5, 96.6, and 101.7 points, respectively. Of the mother-child dyads included in the within-study randomized trial, 42% were randomized to the lead intervention group (Table 1, at end of document). Our demographic findings are consistent with previous reports from the HOME Study cohort [9, 42, 43, 62, 63].

The geometric mean of lifetime average lead exposure estimate was 1.21 µg/dL (Table 1). The distribution of the mean child blood lead exposure estimates from age 12 months to time of IQ test was right-skewed. There was only one blood lead measurement that was below the LOD. Serial child blood lead concentrations were similar at ages 12 and 24 months (median 1.63 µg/dL and 1.60 µg/dL, respectively) and generally decreased over time, as did the serial estimates of child blood lead exposure (Supplemental Fig. 3). Log2-transformed cumulative childhood lead exposure estimates resulted in a roughly normal distribution. In an unadjusted linear regression model, cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate was inversely associated with FSIQ at 5–12 years. Each doubling of cumulative lead was associated with 6.8 lower FSIQ points (95% Confidence Interval (CI): −9.2, −4.4). After adjusting for child sex, household income, maternal WASI FSIQ score, prenatal vitamin use, Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment score, and log2-transformed maternal serum cotinine, the association between cumulative child lead exposure estimate and FSIQ was attenuated to the null (Table 2). This same pattern emerged in bivariate versus adjusted analyses of VIQ, PIQ, PSQ, and WMI (Table 2). After adjustment, the point estimates were still negative in direction.

In our analyses we also noted that household income and maternal IQ were moderately and positively correlated with IQ at 5–12 years. In addition, household income and maternal education were moderately correlated, and maternal IQ and maternal education were strongly correlated [64]. We thus removed maternal education as a covariate in our final adjusted model. In our collinearity assessments, while no covariate crossed the pre-determined aGSIF threshold of 1.6, including both maternal IQ and maternal education led to aGSIF values of 1.59 and 1.50, respectively (Supplemental Table 3). Furthermore, prenatal vitamin use was positively associated with FSIQ at 5–12 years in our adjusted models. Taking prenatal vitamins daily was associated with an increase of 5.5 FSIQ points in children at 5–12 years (95% CI: 1.5, 9.6), when compared to pregnant women who reported not taking prenatal vitamins daily. There was no significant interaction between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and prenatal vitamin use. There was also no significant interaction between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and child sex or household income (Supplemental Table 4).

Additional adjustment for log2-transformed maternal total folate or study intervention arm did not markedly change the association between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and child FSIQ at 5–12 years. We found no significant interaction between maternal total folate and cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate (results not shown). We conducted additional sensitivity analyses to compare our study findings to prior studies that assessed child lead exposure estimate in early-life (2 years) or school-age (5 years) with child IQ. As with the cumulative childhood lead exposure estimates, we observed that child lead exposure estimates at either of these two timepoints were significantly negatively associated with child IQ in unadjusted but not adjusted analyses. After adjustment, the point estimates were also still negative in direction. When using lifetime average lead exposure estimate as the exposure variable in place of cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate, there was a significant negative association with child IQ in unadjusted but not adjusted analyses as well. Furthermore, using concurrent blood lead exposure estimate, calculated based on the age at IQ assessment in months, there was also a significant negative association with child IQ in unadjusted but not adjusted analyses. Similarly, 230 study participants had a laboratory value for concurrent blood lead concentration available at the age of IQ assessment and concurrent blood lead concentration was not significantly associated with child IQ. We performed sensitivity analyses excluding log2-transformed maternal serum cotinine from our models given its association with the exposure [21–23] and outcome [24–30]. There was no significant association between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and child IQ in our adjusted analyses excluding maternal serum cotinine. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses that included only study participants who had completed the IQ assessment at age 8 years (n = 222) showed no significant association between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and IQ at age 8 years in adjusted analyses. Lastly, our post hoc power analysis showed that it would require a sample size of 497 participants to detect an effect size as shown in our adjusted model for full-scale IQ (e.g. −1.0 IQ point, see Table 2).

Discussion

Our results differ from those of prior studies, which showed child blood lead concentration was a significant negative predictor of child IQ. There are several reasons why our results may vary. Although study recruitment prioritized the enrollment of pregnant women living in pre-1978 housing, the geometric mean of estimated lifetime average blood lead exposure was 1.21 µg/dL. This is a rather low level of childhood lead exposure compared to some earlier studies illustrating a lead-associated decrease in FSIQ. For example, Lanphear and colleagues analyzed a pooled cohort of 1,333 children while also adjusting for maternal education and smoking, among other covariates, and reported a significant decrease in FSIQ associated with blood lead concentrations < 7.5 µg/dL. In this pooled cohort the lifetime average blood lead concentration was 12.4 µg/dL [5]. In the Cincinnati Lead Study, participants were enrolled approximately two decades prior to the HOME Study – from 1979 to 1984. The lifetime average blood lead concentration in the Cincinnati Lead Study was 13.4 µg/dL from age 1 to 6 years [65, 66]. Additionally, the Rochester Lead Study found a lifetime average blood lead concentration of 7.4 µg/dL from age 1 to 5 years [67]. Another longitudinal study conducted in China noted a negative association between blood lead concentration at age 3–5 years and adolescent IQ, with a mean blood lead concentration of 6.46 µg/dL at age 3–5 years [68]. Interestingly, a Japanese prospective birth cohort examined blood lead concentration and IQ at age 12 years, noting a negative association with IQ only in males. The cohort’s mean blood lead concentration at age 12 years was lower than our cohort – 0.70 µg/dL, though this represents a single timepoint of lead exposure rather than a lifetime average [69].

Hornung et al. conducted several analyses to determine the age of highest susceptibility to lead exposure and observed that concurrent blood lead concentration was the strongest predictor of child IQ rather than early-life, peak, or lifetime average blood lead concentrations [70]. Chen et al. investigated periods of greater susceptibility via cross-sectional analyses of data from a clinical trial, also finding that concurrent blood lead concentration, not early-life or peak blood lead concentrations, accounted for stronger inverse associations with IQ [41, 71]. We performed sensitivity analyses utilizing either log2-transformed early-life or school-age blood lead exposure for comparison. In our cohort, early-life and school-age child blood lead exposure estimate was negatively associated with IQ in unadjusted analyses. Lead exposure estimate was not significantly associated with IQ in adjusted analyses, though point estimates remained negative after adjustment. Potentially, the concurrent blood lead concentration was most strongly associated with child IQ due to the cumulative, rather than solely concurrent, effects of lead.

Studies suggest numerous pre- and postnatal factors can influence intelligence [33, 72–74]. These sociodemographic and maternal factors included in our analyses could confound the association between estimated cumulative childhood lead exposure and child IQ in this cohort. We adjusted for maternal IQ to account for genetic influences on child IQ as well as impacts maternal IQ may have on mother-child interactions and parenting behaviors [18, 75, 76]. We also adjusted for environmental factors such as family SES by including household income as a covariate (Supplemental Fig. 2). Several studies suggest that SES is associated with effects on cognition and neuroanatomy. Cognitive outcomes such as child IQ, vocabulary, and kindergarten readiness are associated with household SES [17, 77–79]. At least some of the variation in IQ observed in twins is accounted for by shared environmental factors, such as household SES, as shown in the Texas Twin Project [80]. Structurally, Pollock and colleagues describe differential infant brain growth in families living in poverty [81], as well as reductions in regional gray matter volumes and concomitant decreased standardized test scores [82] for families living below 150% of the federal poverty line. Other studies illustrate the prefrontal cortex [83], hippocampus [84, 85], and amygdala [86] are smaller in size in children of lower SES groups.

Given household income is associated with cognitive development and blood lead concentration [87], it remains an important covariate to consider in our analysis. However, household income is moderately correlated with maternal IQ and Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment score in our study (Tab.3). A previous analysis from the Port Pirie Study illustrated that socioeconomic position, maternal intelligence, and home environment were all positively associated with child cognitive outcomes after adjusting for average child blood lead and maternal smoking [18]. Potentially, these factors all play a role in neurocognitive outcomes that attenuate the role of very low-level lead exposure in child IQ. One of these three factors may act as an intermediate, leading to overadjustment in our model [88].

Additionally, we found self-reported prenatal vitamin use was positively associated with child IQ at ages 5–12 years. Commonly, prenatal vitamins are considered an important source of folate to prevent neural tube defects [89]. However, prenatal vitamins contain multiple nutrients associated with improved cognitive outcomes in children, including folate [32, 90, 91], vitamin D [92, 93], and vitamin B12 [94]. For example, maternal folate supplementation in the second and third trimester was associated with higher word reasoning score on the WPPSI-III at age 7 years and higher PSQ on the WISC-IV at age 11 years in a small randomized controlled trial [91]. It is thus feasible that optimal prenatal vitamin intake could be positively associated with cognition. Moreover, in a nationally-representative US cohort, prenatal vitamin use was positively associated with family income-to-poverty ratio [95]. Another study suggests there may be a role of food security and access to healthcare in this relationship between prenatal vitamin use and income [96]. To further explore whether maternal folate status was associated with child IQ, we performed sensitivity analyses that included maternal total folate as a covariate in our model. Interestingly, maternal total folate was not significantly associated with child IQ in adjusted analyses and was only slightly correlated with prenatal vitamin use (Tab. 3). In summary, our analysis suggests that sociodemographic and maternal factors may confound the negative association between very low-level childhood lead exposure and child IQ in this cohort, highlighting the importance of incorporating these factors. Future studies could investigate the potential protective effects of these factors in lead exposure and cognitive development.

Some limitations should be noted in this study. First, post hoc power analysis illustrates that our study was underpowered to detect an effect size of −1 IQ point per doubling of cumulative childhood lead exposure with our modest sample size of 262 study participants. Additionally, pregnant women in our cohort were more likely to be college-educated and had a median annual household income of $55,000/year at enrollment. While the study oversampled those at risk for lead exposures based on age of housing and oversampled women who self-identified as Black, the study did not purposely oversample pregnant women in lower SES groups. Over the course of the study, the HOME Study participants that were lost to follow up were more likely to be those in lower SES groups [42, 43]. Future studies to further investigate the relationships between child lead exposure, household income, and IQ would benefit from increased sampling and particularly within those groups thought to be at greatest risk. Secondly, our analysis results could have been affected by additional unmeasured confounders within our population, such as additional environmental toxicant exposures associated with lead exposure and cognition. Lastly, it is possible there is not enough exposure variance within our sample to observe differences in child IQ in our adjusted models. While this is the first described analysis of cumulative childhood lead exposure and IQ in the HOME Study, previously published results from this cohort illustrated that higher child blood lead concentrations were associated with at-risk behavioral scores on the Behavioral Assessment for Children-2 (BASC-2) at ages 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8 years in adjusted models [9]. This suggests there are measurable neurotoxic effects of lead in this study population.

Our study also has several strengths. Study recruitment prioritized pregnant women living in housing built before 1978, the year that residential lead-based paint was banned in the US. Thus, these women and children were likely at higher risk for residential lead exposures than the general population. The study also obtained extensive participant data from questionnaires, biomarker samples, and assessments at several timepoints from pregnancy to age 12 years. Secondly, our regression calibration method accounts for measurement error that can occur in repeated, longitudinal measures and addresses bias due to missingness within our sample. This allowed us to examine temporal associations from the exposures starting in the prenatal period to school-age cognitive outcomes. It also enabled us to estimate blood lead level at the exact same age in months for each study participant at each study visit, allowing us to more accurately measure exposure at specific timepoints (e.g. ages 2 and 5 years) for our sensitivity analyses. Importantly, our exposure measure encompasses estimated cumulative child lead exposure across childhood, rather than child lead exposure at discrete time points.

Conclusions

We investigated the association between cumulative childhood lead exposure estimate and IQ at ages 5–12 years in a prospective pregnancy and birth cohort with low levels of lead exposure. In adjusted analyses, we did not observe a statistically significant negative association between cumulative childhood blood lead exposure and IQ. We performed several sensitivity analyses to test these findings. A post hoc power analysis shows that with our modest sample size of n = 262, the analysis is underpowered to detect the effect size of −1 FSIQ point per doubling of cumulative childhood lead exposure. We noted significant positive associations between self-reported prenatal vitamin use and IQ in adjusted analyses. Our study also shows a low-level pattern of child blood lead exposure that decreases over time, rather than peaking at age 2 years. As child blood lead concentrations continue to decline nationally due to public health efforts [14], it is important to understand the role low-level lead exposure may play in cognitive outcomes. Furthermore, consideration of sociodemographic and maternal factors such as household income, home environment, and prenatal nutrition remains important as these factors may confound the association between child lead exposure and child IQ.
