“From Cholestasis to Interface Hepatitis: A Rare Case of Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis–Autoimmune Hepatitis Overlap in a 20-Year-Old Male”
Authors: M. Hassaan Shah, Aeman Daud Sethi, Muhammad Osama, Samar Hasnain, Rahat Shah, Irfan khan, Aamir Khan, Pawan Kumar Thada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOverlap syndrome refers to the coexistence of more than one immune-mediated liver disease in a single patient. Primary sclerosing cholangitis–autoimmune hepatitis (PSC–AIH) overlap is a rare but clinically important entity, as delayed recognition may lead to progressive liver injury, cirrhosis, and liver failure. Differentiating this condition from isolated PSC or AIH remains challenging due to overlapping clinical, biochemical, and radiological features.Case PresentationA 20-year-old male presented with progressive jaundice, pruritus, acholic stools, and dark urine for one month. Laboratory evaluation revealed marked cholestasis with significantly elevated alkaline phosphatase and transaminases, hyperbilirubinemia, and coagulopathy, while viral and metabolic causes were excluded. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography demonstrated characteristic intrahepatic biliary duct irregularities consistent with primary sclerosing cholangitis. However, disproportionately elevated alanine aminotransferase and serum immunoglobulin G levels prompted further evaluation. Liver biopsy revealed features of both diseases, including interface hepatitis with chronic portal inflammation and plasma cell infiltration, along with bile duct injury, ductopenia, and onion-skin fibrosis. Based on histology and diagnostic scoring, a diagnosis of PSC–AIH overlap syndrome was established. The patient was treated with ursodeoxycholic acid, corticosteroids, and azathioprine, resulting in marked clinical and biochemical improvement on follow-up.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of considering PSC–AIH overlap syndrome in young patients with cholestatic liver disease and unexpectedly elevated transaminases or immunoglobulin G levels. Early recognition and combined immunosuppressive and supportive therapy can lead to favorable outcomes and may prevent irreversible liver damage.

Introduction

The common immune mediated liver diseases are autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). A few patients have mixed manifestations (AIH, PBC, and PSC), and are called ‘overlapping syndrome’ (OS). 1

Overlap Syndrome (OS) is a concurrent presentation of more than one immune-mediated hepatobiliary disorders in a single patient. 2 PSC and AIH are distinct immune-mediated liver diseases, each characterized by unique clinical, laboratory, and microscopic features. Occasionally, these manifestations present in uncommon combinations, resulting in overlap syndromes; a rare subset of autoimmune disorders. 1 The coexistence of PSC and AIH, termed PSC-AIH OS, is uncommon but a clinically significant entity. Its prevalence among autoimmune liver disease is around 1.4% to 8%. 3 Failure to treat this overlap condition may result in irreversible liver damage, including cirrhosis and liver failure.

PSC is a slowly progressive liver disorder involving inflammation, sclerosis, and narrowing of intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic bile ducts, ultimately leading to biliary obstruction. 4 A significant number of individuals diagnosed with PSC also have ulcerative colitis, underscoring the strong association between the two conditions. 5 PSC is diagnosed primarily through radiological investigations such as magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic retrograde cholangio-pancreatography (ERCP), showing bile duct abnormalities (e.g., Strictures, dilations), microscopic findings of bile duct injury, including portal fibrosis & exclusion of secondary etiologies of cholestatic liver disease, such as biliary obstruction or infection. 6

AIH is an autoimmune chronic inflammation of liver leading to hepatocyte injury. Key diagnostic criteria include; histopathological evidence of Interface hepatitis (previously termed piecemeal necrosis with plasma cell infiltration, elevated serum aminotransferases (AST/ALT) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels, presence of characteristic autoantibodies (ANA, ASMA, anti-LKM1) and exclusion of other liver diseases, such as hepatitis caused by, viruses, drugs, and metabolic disturbance. 4 The ‘International Autoimmune Hepatitis Group (IAIHG) diagnostic criteria for autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)’ yields a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 97%, reflecting their strong diagnostic accuracy. The parameters include sex, age, autoantibodies, absence of viral hepatitis, histology, and immunoglobulin levels. A score of 6 on the simplified IAIHG criteria supported a diagnosis of probable autoimmune hepatitis, in the setting of established PSC, confirming PSC–AIH overlap syndrome. 7

Among individuals diagnosed with PSC, significantly elevated ALT levels (more than five times the upper limit of normal) should raise suspicion for an AIH overlap syndrome, IgG levels are elevated along with raised antibody titers (ANA or ASMA) (greater than 1:40) and moderate to severe interface hepatitis on microscopy. 8 Treatment of Overlap syndrome involves Ursodeoxycholic acid and supportive management for the PSC component, steroids and immunosuppressants such as azathioprine for the AIH component. 9

Our case report describes a patient with PSC-AIH overlap syndrome, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, therapeutic strategies, and the importance of early recognition to prevent disease progression. By examining this case, we aim to contribute to the growing literature on this rare condition and underscore the need for vigilance in patients with immune mediated hepatopathies.

Case Summary

A 20-year-old male presented to the medical outpatient department. He presented with progressive jaundice involving the skin and sclera along with nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and pain in the abdomen. He also complained of generalized pruritis, acholic stools, and dark urine for the past month. He denied any bleeding, abdominal distention, gall stones, worm excretion in feces or vomitus, or use of any offending drugs and alcohol. There was no history of significant weight loss.

At presentation, he had a regular pulse of 60 bpm, blood pressure of 130/80 mmHg, respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min and he was afebrile.

On examination, he was deeply jaundiced with prominent scratch marks on the limbs. Abdominal examination showed hepatomegaly, while the remaining abdominal and genital examination was unremarkable. Hepatic flap was negative and there were no peripheral signs of Chronic Liver disease. No Kayser-Fleischer rings were seen on the Slit lamp examination. Clinical evaluation did not suggest Wilson disease or hemochromatosis.

His initial investigations (Table 1) revealed a direct hyperbilirubinemia of 22.3 mg/dL with a total bilirubin of 26.7 mg/dL, markedly raised alkaline phosphatase (ALP) of 800 IU/L with a raised alanine aminotransferase (ALT) value of 443 IU/L. His coagulation profile was also deranged with an international normalized ratio (INR) of 4.4, prothrombin time (PT) of 49s, and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 69s. Following the MRCP findings, the diagnosis of Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis was confirmed and he was started on supportive treatment with Ursodeoxycholic acid, Cholestyramine, and Vitamin K supplementation for his coagulopathy.

Despite being commenced on symptomatic treatment for PSC the patient showed no signs of improvement. Also, the significantly raised ALT levels didn’t go well with the diagnosis of PSC, so to dig out some other causes specifically Autoimmune Hepatitis, Autoimmune liver profile, and IgG levels was requested. The Autoimmune Liver Profile came back negative but Serum IgG levels were significantly raised.

For further confirmation, a liver biopsy was done which showed; Ductular reaction, cholestasis, moderate chronic portal inflammation with interface hepatitis (Hallmark of Autoimmune Hepatitis) and also onion skin fibrosis and bile duct obliteration, and ductopenia (suggestive of PSC) (Figures 2 and 3).Figure 2.Low-power liver biopsy shows portal-based chronic inflammation with interface hepatitis, ductular reaction, and onion-skin periductal fibrosis, consistent with features of autoimmune hepatitis and primary sclerosing cholangitisFigure 3.High-power view reveals hepatocellular cholestasis with swollen hepatocytes and bile pigment accumulation, indicative of ongoing cholangiopathic liver injury

Typical biopsy findings, negative virology, and significantly elevated IgG levels resulted in a score of 6 on the diagnostic criteria for autoimmune hepatitis and hence a definitive diagnosis of PSC-AIH Overlap Syndrome was made.

So, after the diagnosis of Overlap syndrome (OS PSC-AIH) steroids, prednisolone was initiated at 30 mg daily and Azathioprine at 50mg twice daily, and on a follow-up visit he showed remarkable clinical and biochemical improvement. His fresh labs showed a bilirubin level of 1.1, ALT of 28, and ALP of 200. The coagulation profile also improved with a fresh INR of 1.1. Patient is followed up regularly every month and is compliant to his medications.

Discussion

Overlap Syndrome (OS (PSC-AIH)) consists of AIH and the presence of cholestatic liver diseases such as PBC and PSC. 6 Men are commonly affected by OS PSC-AIH, accounting for 56.09% of cases. 10 However, OS PSC-AIH is reported to be more common in pediatric population, which contrasts with our case of a male aged 20. 11

AIH overlap syndrome is reported in less than 6% of individuals presenting with PSC 12 This case describes a young male who presented with chief complaints of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, dark-colored stools, and pale urine. He was visibly jaundiced. A systematic review indicates that jaundice is the most common presentation of PSC-AIH OS, occurring in 28.7% of cases, followed by fatigue (20.3%) and abdominal pain (19.4%). 10

Overlap syndrome between Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis and Autoimmune Hepatitis is thought to result from immune dysregulation directed against both hepatocytes and the biliary epithelium. Primary sclerosing cholangitis is characterized by fibro-obliterative inflammation affecting the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, whereas autoimmune hepatitis typically manifests as interface hepatitis with prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltration of the hepatic parenchyma. In the overlap syndrome, these pathogenic mechanisms occur concurrently, leading to a combined pattern of hepatocellular inflammation and cholestatic bile duct injury. 13

Our differential diagnosis included primary biliary cholangitis, IGG4 related sclerosing cholangitis, drug induced liver injury and primary sclerosing cholangitis. Primary sclerosing cholangitis was initially suspected, and he was commenced on supportive treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid and cholestyramine. However, there was no improvement in laboratory findings or clinical features. This finding suggests that ursodeoxycholic acid alone is insufficient for treating OS (PSC-AIH). Similar findings were reported in a study conducted by Igarashi et al. 12

The patient’s autoimmune profile was negative. However, a study by Moran et al revealed that antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were positive in 8–77% of patients and anti-smooth muscle antibodies (SMA) in 0–83% of the patients of PSC-AIH OS. 14 IgG and ALT levels were significantly raised in patients with PSC-AIH OS than PSC alone. 12 Similarly, our patients exhibited elevated levels of IgG and ALT. AST levels were also significantly elevated. A subsequent liver biopsy confirmed autoimmune hepatitis. He was then commenced on steroids, azathioprine, and ursodeoxycholic acid, to which he responded well; consistent with existing literature. 15 A study by Gargouri et al reported that normalization of aminotransferases and gamma globulin levels was observed in approximately 94% of patients within 4–6 weeks after initiating treatment. Prednisone is usually started first and then gradually tapered, with maintenance therapy continued using azathioprine and ursodeoxycholic acid. 16 In our case, the patient showed a similar biochemical improvement following this treatment regimen, which further supports the effectiveness of this therapeutic approach.

A systematic review by Ballotin et al suggested a correlation between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and PSC-AIH OS that was present in approximately 76% of patients. However, our patient had no such association. 10 Despite treatment, PSC-AIH OS remains highly progressive. In twelve years of follow-up, patients developing liver cirrhosis increased from 19% to 56% at the end of observation. However, early diagnosis and timely intervention can delay disease progression. Early referral for liver transplantation can improve long-term survival. 17

A study by Rust C et al claimed that PSC-AIH overlap syndrome had better survival than classical PSC when assessed using the ‘Mayo Index’, a prognostic score. 18 Our patient showed marked improvement in his clinical appearance and laboratory results on his subsequent visit, which shows the potential of using steroids, azathioprine, and ursodeoxycholic acid in treating such patients.

Although PSC–AIH overlap is uncommon, it is not exceedingly rare, limiting the novelty of the report, with its primary value lying in detailed clinicopathological correlation. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period restricts assessment of long-term outcomes and disease progression.

Conclusion

PSC–AIH overlap syndrome should be suspected in patients with PSC who demonstrate disproportionately elevated transaminases or immunoglobulin G levels and fail to improve with ursodeoxycholic acid alone. Early liver biopsy and timely initiation of immunosuppression are essential to delay disease progression.
