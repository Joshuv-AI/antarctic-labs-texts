Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) spectacle lenses versus 0.01% atropine for myopia control: a randomised trial in children from Central Europe
Authors: Emilia Wnękowicz-Augustyn, Edward Wylęgała, Martyna Kobielnik, Sławomir Teper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimsThe global increase in childhood myopia underscores the need for effective control strategies. This study compares the effectiveness of Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) spectacle lenses versus 0.01% atropine eye drops in slowing myopia progression in European children. This report summarises 1-year outcomes from an ongoing 2-year clinical trial.MethodsA total of 110 Polish children aged 6–16 years, without significant ocular pathology, were randomly assigned to two groups. Group A received single-vision spectacles plus nightly 0.01% atropine drops; group B received DIMS spectacle lenses plus nightly placebo drops. Primary outcomes were changes in cycloplegic spherical equivalent refraction (SER) and axial length, measured every 6 months.ResultsAfter 12 months, mean axial elongation was 0.19±0.03 mm in group A and 0.11±0.02 mm in group B. Among 6–11-year olds, group A showed greater elongation (0.30±0.05 mm) than group B (0.15±0.02 mm). For 12–16-year olds, axial elongation was 0.10±0.02 mm in group A and 0.07±0.02 mm in group B. Mean SER progression was 0.37±0.06 D in group A and 0.23±0.05 D in group B. In the younger group (6–11 years), SER progression was higher in group A (0.60±0.1 D) than group B (0.25±0.05 D). Among older children (12–16 years), SER changes were similar between groups (0.19±0.05 D vs 0.21±0.08 D).ConclusionDIMS spectacle lenses demonstrated superior efficacy to the 0.01% atropine in controlling myopia progression in this cohort, particularly among children aged 6–11 years. These findings support DIMS lenses as an effective intervention for myopia control in Caucasian populations.

The efficacy of Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) spectacle lenses in controlling myopia progression has been well established in Asian populations, particularly among children with low to moderate myopia. However, the optimal concentration of atropine for myopia control remains the subject of ongoing debate. In addition, there is a recognised need to assess the effectiveness of these interventions in more diverse populations, including older children and those presenting with higher baseline refractive errors.

Introduction

The global increase in myopia prevalence has also been documented in European populations.1 2 Higher levels of myopia are associated not only with reduced uncorrected visual acuity but also with an increased risk of sight-threatening ocular pathologies, including retinal detachment, glaucoma and myopic maculopathy.3 In addition, greater refractive errors pose increased challenges for optical correction.4 These epidemiological patterns and associated risks underscore the growing interest in interventions to slow myopia progression.5

Understanding the natural progression of myopia is essential for developing age-appropriate treatment strategies. The Drentse Refractive Error and Myopia study, a retrospective cohort analysis of 2555 individuals in Europe who first wore spectacles before the age of 10, showed that refractive error may continue to progress into late adolescence.6 Spherical equivalent refraction (SER) at age 10 was a strong predictor of adult high myopia (SER ≥ –6.00 D), with all children presenting an SER of –3.00 D or greater at age 10 developing high myopia by adulthood. These findings highlight the need for early and effective intervention.

Among available treatments, low-dose atropine eye drops (0.01%, 0.025% and 0.05%) is one of the most widely used pharmacological options for myopia control.7 DIMS spectacle lenses offer a non-invasive optical alternative, inducing simultaneous myopic defocus.8 Light focused anterior to the retina is thought to suppress ocular growth, while light focused posterior to the retina may stimulate it. The role of peripheral defocus has been extensively investigated, with animal models providing key insights into its effects on ocular development.9

Although the efficacy of DIMS spectacle lenses is well established in Asian populations, evidence in Caucasian children remains limited. Most trials have focused on younger cohorts, typically under 13 years of age.8 10 We, therefore, evaluated the effectiveness of DIMS spectacle lenses in a broader paediatric population aged 6–16 years. Given age-related differences in myopia progression, participants were stratified into two groups: 6–11 years and 12–16 years. In European children, expected axial elongation is approximately 0.20–0.35 mm/year in younger age groups and 0.12–0.20 mm/year in older children, with faster progression in myopes compared with emmetropes and hyperopes. The expected annual change in SER is approximately −0.30 to −0.50 D in younger children and −0.15 to −0.30 D in older children, again with greater shifts in myopes.11

Many previous studies have restricted inclusion to children with SER not exceeding –5.00 D and astigmatism below 1.50 D.8 10 In contrast, our study enrolled children with myopia up to –9.00 D SER, enabling evaluation of intervention efficacy across a wider refractive spectrum.

Materials and methods

At the time of study design, local clinical practice patterns in myopia management were reviewed. Ophthalmologists in the region predominantly prescribed 0.01% atropine eye drops for myopia control, whereas the use of DIMS spectacle lenses was relatively new and not widely implemented. This context informed the study design, with the primary objective of comparing these two interventions within locally relevant therapeutic frameworks.

The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The cohort comprised 58 girls and 52 boys, aged 6–16 years. All participants were Caucasian, of Polish or Ukrainian nationality. Prior to enrolment, they had been under the care of various paediatric ophthalmology clinics in the region. Children with myopia corrected by single-vision (SV) spectacles were informed about the trial and invited to participate.

The principal inclusion criterion was myopia in at least one eye, defined as a cycloplegic SER of –0.50 dioptres or greater, not exceeding –9.00 dioptres. Exclusion criteria included significant ocular comorbidities such as manifest strabismus, moderate to severe amblyopia (best-corrected visual acuity 20/40–20/80 (0.3–0.6 logMAR)) or congenital anomalies including congenital cataract, congenital glaucoma, optic nerve hypoplasia and retinal dystrophies. Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to two groups using a computer-generated simple randomisation sequence. The sequence was prepared by a statistician independent of enrolment and concealed from investigators responsible for recruitment and outcome assessment. Both participants and outcome assessors remained masked throughout the trial.

Group A received SV spectacle lenses and nightly 0.01% atropine eye drops in each eye. Group B received DIMS spectacle lenses and nightly placebo eye drops in each eye. Active and placebo formulations were prepared under pharmaceutical-grade conditions and dispensed in sterile bottles designed to maintain solution sterility. Bottles were indistinguishable in appearance and distributed by a technician not involved in participant assessment.

Spectacle lenses and eye drops were provided free of charge; the only cost incurred by participants was the purchase of spectacle frames. All spectacle fitting and lens mounting were performed by certified opticians.

Each participant attended three visits during the study period. At baseline, eligibility was confirmed, randomisation performed, and a lifestyle questionnaire covering outdoor time and near-work activities was completed with parental oversight (online supplemental appendix 1). The questionnaire was repeated at each follow-up. Participants and their parents also received counselling on lifestyle modifications relevant to myopia control.

Comprehensive ophthalmological assessments included uncorrected and best-corrected visual acuity, ocular motility and alignment, cycloplegic and non-cycloplegic refraction, and optical biometry. Cycloplegia was induced with 1% cyclopentolate (Cykloftyal), instilled two times at 5 min intervals with refraction measured 40 min after the first instillation. Measurements were obtained using the Tomey OA-2000 biometer and the Righton Speedy K2 autorefractometer. Biometric parameters recorded included corneal curvature, axial length (AXL), anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, central corneal thickness, pupil diameter and white-to-white distance. Anterior and posterior segment examinations were performed with slit-lamp biomicroscopy and indirect ophthalmoscopy.

Follow-up visits were conducted at 6-month intervals. At each visit, baseline examinations were repeated, and participants were assessed for potential diverse effects of eye drops (blurred vision, photophobia, allergic reactions) by history and ophthalmic examination.

The primary outcomes were change in AXL over 12 months, measured with the Tomey OA-2000 optical biometer, and change in SER under cycloplegia measured with the Righton Speedy K2 autorefractometer. The secondary outcome was the AXL-to-corneal radius (AXL/CR) ratio, and the complementary outcome was self-reported near-work activity.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculations were based on detecting a mean difference of 0.32 mm in AXL and 0.55 dioptres in SER between treatment groups at 24 months, assuming SD of 0.3 mm and 0.6 D, respectively, based on published data.8 To achieve 80% power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05, the required sample sizes were 15 and 20 participants per group, respectively. Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, target enrolment was 17 and 23 participants per group, yielding minimum total sample sizes of 34 and 46.

Based on the results of the Wilcoxon test, changes in parameter values were comparable between the left and right eyes. The test yielded p=0.86 and p=0.17 for comparisons of increases in AXL and SER values, respectively. Consequently, the study was conducted using data from the right eyes only. Baseline characteristics between the two groups (presented in table 1) were compared using the t test for normally distributed variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. To assess the comparability of the groups with respect to time spent on near activities, Pearson’s χ2 test was applied.

Results

Trial population

A total of 110 children were recruited into the trial between February and November 2023. Of these, 104 participants (94.5%) completed the 1-year follow-up period. Five participants withdrew following the first follow-up visit, and one did not attend the second follow-up appointment. Reasons for withdrawal from the study were not provided; despite repeated attempts, participants could not be contacted by telephone. The dropout rate was higher in group A (9.1%) compared with group B (1.8%). In the group receiving intervention A, 22 children were aged 6–11 years and 28 were aged 12–16 years. In the group receiving intervention B, 32 children were aged 6–11 years and 22 were aged 12–16 years.

Adherence to spectacle wear was high in both groups, with participants and their parents consistently reporting daily use. By contrast, adherence to eye-drop administration was lower; several children acknowledged occasional lapses, most commonly during short periods of travel or episodes of illness-related discomfort.

Principal outcomes—changes in AXL and SER

All randomised participants who completed the 1-year follow-up were included in the analysis. Mean changes in AXL and SER from baseline to the 12-month follow-up were assessed for the overall cohort and stratified by age group (6–11 vs 12–16 years).

Significant increases in AXL over 12 months were observed in both treatment groups. In group A, the mean change in AXL was 0.19±0.03 mm (t=–6.24, p<0.001). In group B, the mean change was 0.11±0.02 mm (t= –4.53, p<0.001).

Age-stratified analysis showed that these changes remained statistically significant in all subgroups except for older participants (12–16 years) in group B (t=–0.98, p=0.34). Among younger participants (6–11 years), group A exhibited a mean AXL increase of 0.30±0.05 mm (t=–6.06, p<0.001), while group B showed a mean increase of 0.15±0.02 mm (t=–5.12, p<0.001).

In the older age group, participants in group A demonstrated a mean AXL change of 0.10±0.02 mm (t=–3.65, p=0.001), whereas the change in group B (0.07±0.02 mm) was not statistically significant.

Refractive outcomes

Changes in SER between baseline and the 12-month follow-up were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. In group A, the mean change in SER was 0.37±0.06 D (median: 0.25 D; Z=3.75, p<0.001). In group B, the mean change was 0.23±0.05 D (median: 0.13 D; Z=2.19, p=0.03).

Age-stratified analysis revealed that significant changes in SER were present only in the younger age group (6–11 years). In this subgroup, group A demonstrated a mean SER change of 0.60±0.10 D (median: 0.50 D; Z=3.02, p=0.002) while group B showed a change of 0.25±0.05 D (median: 0.13 D; Z=2.28, p=0.02). Among older participants (12–16 years), changes in SER were not statistically significant: in group A, 0.19±0.05 D (median: 0.00 D; Z=1.93, p=0.053); and in group B, 0.21±0.08 D (median: 0.06 D; Z=0.74, p=0.46).

Between-group comparisons

Comparative analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test demonstrated a significant difference in AXL progression between groups A and B (Z=2.15, p=0.031). This difference remained statistically significant in the younger subgroup (Z=2.51, p=0.012), but not among older participants (Z=1.66, p=0.10).

Differences in SER progression between groups were not statistically significant overall (Z=1.41, p=0.16). However, a significant intergroup difference was observed within the younger age group (Z=2.78, p=0.005), while no significant difference was found in the older subgroup (Z=0.01, p=0.992).

Secondary outcomes: corneal curvature

We evaluated whether corneal curvature parameters differed significantly between treatment groups and age categories. Statistical analysis using the Kruskal-Wallis test revealed no significant differences across the combined categories of age and treatment (H=3.67, p=0.30). Similarly, the Mann-Whitney U test showed no significant difference in corneal curvature between treatment methods alone (Z=–0.46, p=0.65).

Supplementary outcomes: self-reported near-work activity

To assess potential behavioural confounders, we examined whether participants differed in self-reported near-work activity. The distribution of time spent on near activities, analysed using the Pearson χ² test, showed no significant differences between group A and group B, either on school days (χ²=2.06, p=0.56) or at weekends (χ² = 0.5, p=0.92).

Online supplemental figure 1 shows the frequency distribution of time spent on near activities during school days and weekends, stratified by age group. Questionnaire data indicated that older children in the cohort engaged in more near work than their younger counterparts. This difference in visual behaviour may, in part, account for the reduced treatment efficacy observed in the older age group for both DIMS lenses and low-dose atropine.

These findings highlight the continuing challenge of limiting near work, particularly among older children. Although the precise mechanism of action of atropine in myopia control remains incompletely understood, and current evidence does not strongly support an accommodative pathway, the potential interaction between lifestyle factors and treatment response warrants further investigation.

Discussion

In this cohort, DIMS spectacle lenses were found to be more effective than 0.01% atropine in controlling myopia progression, with the most pronounced effect observed in children aged 6–11 years. These findings support the role of DIMS spectacle lenses as a myopia control intervention in Caucasian populations. Interestingly, among older children, 0.01% atropine demonstrated slightly greater efficacy than DIMS lenses in reducing SER progression. However, with respect to AXL, DIMS lenses continued to show marginally superior (in numerical, not statistical sense) outcomes. It is important to note that the natural rate of myopia progression tends to decline with age,12 which may have reduced the statistical power to detect differences within the older subgroup.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first double-masked trial to directly compare the efficacy of DIMS spectacle lenses with 0.01% atropine in a European paediatric population. Previous studies have primarily involved children of East or Southeast Asian ethnicity, and the limited available data on European cohorts are largely derived from non-masked or observational designs. For example, Nucci et al conducted a non-randomised, experimenter-masked prospective study in children aged 6–18 years, evaluating DIMS, 0.01% atropine and their combination.13 Neller et al reported retrospective real-world outcomes from a German cohort,14 while other investigations in non-Asian populations have focused on alternative lens designs, such as the Shamir Myopia Control lens in Israeli cohorts.15 Our findings, therefore, provide novel and clinically relevant evidence supporting the use of optical and pharmacological interventions in European populations.

The existing literature presents conflicting evidence regarding the efficacy of low-concentration atropine. Zadnik and colleagues reported results from a double-masked, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, phase 3 clinical trial comparing daily placebo, 0.01% atropine and 0.02% atropine over 36 months.16 The study included children aged 3–16 years from 26 sites in North America and five European countries, with baseline SER ranging from – 0.5D to – 6.0D. Based on changes in both SER and AXL, the authors concluded that the low-dose atropine demonstrates sufficient efficacy and safety to support its role in myopia management.

In contrast, Repka et al conducted a multicentre randomised clinical trial in the USA involving 187 children aged 5–12 years from diverse ethnic backgrounds.13 17 No significant differences in SER progression or axial elongation were observed between 0.01% atropine and placebo, with 24-month mean changes almost identical in both groups. These findings stand in contrast to our results, where a meaningful treatment effect was observed, particularly in the older age group. Several factors may account for this discrepancy, including differences in ethnic composition, environmental exposures, baseline characteristics and adherence patterns between the two study populations. In our cohort, younger children showed responses more consistent with the expected natural course of myopia progression in European populations, whereas older children demonstrated greater benefit, potentially owing to better adherence to treatment or higher baseline myopia. Taken together, these observations highlight the possibility that the efficacy of 0.01% atropine may vary substantially across populations, raising questions about the generalisability of trial findings and the need for region-specific data.

Meta-analytical evidence may help clarify the discrepancies observed in clinical outcomes with low-dose atropine. In a recent meta-analysis, Ha et al18 identified 0.05% atropine as the most effective concentration for myopia control, underscoring the need to balance efficacy and safety when selecting an appropriate dosage. Further contributing to this discussion, a Point–Counterpoint article by Phillips et al19 cautioned against the widespread assumption that 0.01% atropine is an effective first-line therapy, particularly in younger children, and advocated for a more evidence-based and individualised treatment approach.

Evidence is emerging to suggest that combination therapies may offer an additive effect in myopia control. Xu et al20 reported that 0.01% atropine combined with orthokeratology produced greater reductions in axial elongation over 2 years compared with either treatment alone in children aged 8–12 years; however, the modest sample size and per-protocol analysis (30–34 participants per group) limit the strength of the conclusions. In a European cohort, Nucci et al13 observed the least axial elongation among children treated with 0.01% atropine plus DIMS spectacle lenses compared with monotherapy or SV lenses. Yet, the study was non-randomised and treatment allocation may have been influenced by clinical preferences, introducing potential bias. More recently, a controlled trial from Spain21 found that children receiving DIMS lenses with 0.025% atropine had significantly less axial elongation at 12 months than those treated with SV lenses and 0.025% atropine alone, again supporting an additive benefit. Nevertheless, these studies remain heterogeneous in design, dose and population, and their methodological limitations constrain the generalisability of the findings.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its rigorous design. The trial was double-masked, with participants randomly allocated to intervention groups. Although DIMS and SV spectacle lenses may be distinguishable on close inspection,8 most children and their parents remained unaware of these differences, thereby preserving masking integrity.

The use of optical biometry, a highly accurate and reproducible method, ensured robust measurement of AXL. While cycloplegic SER is subjected to greater interindividual and intraindividual variability, it remains a clinically valid measure when applied using standardised protocols. Participants’ motivation and high adherence to spectacle wear throughout the study period further strengthen the reliability of the findings.

The primary limitation of our study concerns differential compliance between treatment groups. While compliance with spectacle wear was generally high, adherence to nightly eye-drop administration was lower, with lapses most commonly reported during illness or travel. This difference may partly explain the observed superiority of DIMS lenses, particularly in the younger, more rapidly progressing age group. Consequently, it is not possible to determine with certainty whether the differences in outcomes reflect the biological efficacy of the interventions or are confounded by adherence levels. This issue also highlights a practical advantage of spectacle-based interventions, which may achieve higher real-world compliance than pharmacological approaches requiring daily administration.

A limitation of our study is also the absence of a placebo group, which was not included for ethical and practical reasons, as it would have required withholding a potentially beneficial intervention. This limits conclusions on absolute efficacy versus natural progression. However, by comparing two active treatments, the study provides clinically relevant evidence on their relative effectiveness, which may be of greater value to both clinicians and families in real-world decision-making.

Conclusion

DIMS spectacle lenses demonstrated greater efficacy in slowing myopia progression in Caucasian children compared with 0.01% atropine, particularly among those aged 6–11 years. Across the full cohort, DIMS lenses were associated with a 38% lower mean increase in SER and 42% lower mean increase in AXL compared with atropine.

In the younger group, the mean increase in AXL was 50% lower with DIMS (0.15 mm) compared with atropine (0.30 mm), and the mean increase in SER was 58% lower in the DIMS group (0.25 D vs 0.60 D, respectively). In older participants, divergent tendencies were observed for SER. The mean increase in SER was 11% higher in the DIMS group compared with atropine (0.21 D vs 0.19 D), while, the mean increase in AXL remained lower in the DIMS (0.07 mm vs 0.10 mm), representing a 30% reduction.

These findings support the early use of DIMS lenses as an effective first-line intervention for myopia control in European children. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying refractive progression and to inform the development of personalised treatment strategies that optimise efficacy, safety and cost-effectiveness.
