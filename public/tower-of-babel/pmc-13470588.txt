CuS–MoS2 nanosheet-catalyzed selective oxidative azo-coupling of anilines
Authors: Dinesh Kumar, Pappu Shriwas, Santosh Kumar Prajapati, Ashish Kumar Singh, Sevi Murugavel, Subhash Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cu containing MoS2 nanosheets were synthesized via a facile hydrothermal method and employed as an efficient heterogeneous catalyst for oxidative azo-coupling of aromatic amines. Structural and morphological characterisation (PXRD, FTIR, SEM-EDX, ICP-MS, HRTEM and XPS) confirmed the successful formation of a CuS–MoS2 composite. ∼18 wt% Cu is present in the CuS–MoS2 as indicated by EDX and ICP-MS, whereas XPS confirmed the presence of a predominant Cu+ state together with Cu2+. The catalyst demonstrated excellent activity, affording a wide range of azo compounds in high yields (75–96%) with outstanding chemoselectivity under mild conditions using TBHP as an oxidant. Both homo- and hetero-coupling reactions proceeded efficiently. The enhanced catalytic performance is attributed to the synergistic interactions between Cu species and the MoS2 support, which promotes effective charge transfer and redox cycling. The catalyst is recyclable for up to 7 cycles without significant loss of activity and is used in gram-scale synthesis. This work provides a simple, robust, sustainable, and cost-effective catalytic system, expanding the utility of metal-doped MoS2 materials in selective organic transformations.

Introduction

Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) belongs to the transition metal dichalcogenide family and has a layered S–Mo–S structure.1–3 The layers of MoS2 contain edge-active sulphur sites and an inert basal plane, where molybdenum atoms are sandwiched between sulphur layers and held together by weak van der Waals forces. This structure enables facile exfoliation into two-dimensional sheets with tunable electronic, optical, and catalytic properties, making MoS2 a key material in condensed-matter physics, chemistry, and nanotechnology.4,5 Additionally, doping MoS2 with Ni, Co, Fe, Cu, Pt, etc., significantly enhances its catalytic activity as a result of modification in its electronic structure, increasing the density of active sites, by improving charge transfer efficiency and was applied extensively for electrochemical water splitting reactions.6–11 Furthermore, Pd, Ag, Cu, Ni, and Cr metal-loaded MoS2 materials have been developed for efficient CO oxidation to CO2.12 Also, Co, Pt-doped MoS2 materials13,14 were applied for the catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 to methanol. Despite these advances, their catalytic potential in organic transformations leading to valuable organic molecules remains largely unexplored, with only a few reports available in the literature.15–17 Nevertheless, some transition metal-doped MoS2 materials, such as Ni(OH)2 on CdS–MoS2,18 Pt NPs/MoS2 nanoflower,19 Fe1/MoS2 nanosheets,20 MoS2/Ta3N5 nanocomposite,21 and Cu–Cu2O/MoS2,22 have been reported to promote the selective oxidation of alcohols. Further, Cu/MoS2 was used for the catalytic oxidation of phenolic pollutants.23 These materials exhibit improved catalytic activity due to the synergistic effect between metallic nanoparticles and the MoS2 support, as well as better recyclability and resistance to sintering due to significant interactions between the metal and the support.

The synthesis of aromatic azo compounds is important because these molecules are key structural motifs with widespread applications in the production of synthetic organic dyes, indicators, food additives, optical materials, chemosensors, and prodrugs.24–27 Traditionally, aromatic azo compounds have been obtained via diazotisation of anilines,28 oxidation of anilines,29 reduction of azoxybenzenes.24,30 In contrast, modern strategies increasingly emphasise transition metal-catalysed direct oxidative coupling of anilines, offering a more straightforward and sustainable approach.

Among other transition metals,31–36 Cu-based materials37–43 were preferred due to their significantly lower cost, higher natural abundance, and excellent stability. However, all the above-mentioned reported methods still exhibit several notable drawbacks, such as the use of expensive Pd, Ru, Au, etc, metal-based catalysts, use of homogeneous catalysts, toxic nitrogen-containing bases or additives, use of excess toxic oxidants or hazardous, as well as the requirement for molecular oxygen, which reduces their sustainability and limits their practical applicability. However, to the best of our knowledge, the oxidation of aromatic amines to aromatic azo compounds has not been investigated. Here, we report the synthesis of Cu-doped MoS2 material and investigated its application in direct oxidative azo-coupling of anilines to aromatic azos (Scheme 1).

Characterization of CuS–MoS2

The UV-vis absorption spectra of CuS–MoS2 presented in Fig. 1 show prominent absorptions at around 312 nm and 315 nm, respectively, resulting from interfacial interactions, confinement effects, and electronic transitions.45,46 Tauc's analysis reveals an indirect band gap of about 2.77 eV CuS–MoS2.

(a) UV-vis spectrum and (b) Tauc plot of CuS–MoS2.

The FTIR spectra of the MoS2 and CuS–MoS2 are presented in Fig. 2. The characteristic peaks observed at 596, 948 and 1382 cm−1 correspond to Mo–S stretching, S–S vibration and S–Mo–S bending modes in the FTIR spectrum of MoS2 (blue line in Fig. 2a). On the other hand, CuS–MoS2 exhibits distinct bands at 538, 663, 932, and 883 cm−1 corresponding to sulphur-related interaction such as Mo–S vibrations, Cu–S interaction, S–S vibrational modes within CuS–MoS2 (red line in Fig. 2b).47,48 The Cu–S interactions at 663 cm−1 consistence with the presence of Cu species on CuS–MoS2. The additional bands observed at 1615 cm−1 (blue line in Fig. 2a), 1621 cm−1, 3395 (red line in Fig. 2b), are attributed to H–O–H bending of physically adsorbed water and O–H stretching, while the bands at 1109 cm−1 correspond to O–Mo–O stretching due to partial surface oxidation.48 Overall, FTIR data of CuS–MoS2 support the coexistence of defect-related sulfur modes and partial surface oxidation while maintaining the basic MoS2 structure.

FTIR spectra of (a) MoS2 (blue line) and (b) CuS–MoS2 (red line).

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns for MoS2 and CuS–MoS2 are shown in Fig. 2. The pristine MoS2 exhibits well-defined diffraction peaks at 2θ of 14.46°, 29.35°, 33.14°, 39.36°, 48.22°, 58.34°, and 60.48° corresponding to (002), (004) planes of MoS2 (Fig. 3, red line). In contrast, CuS–MoS2 (Fig. 3, black line) exhibited a distinct pattern at 2θ of 14.17°, 28.47°, 31.20°, 33.04 and 58.45° corresponding to (002), (004), (001), (101), (103), (105), and (110) planes, which are indexed to the hexagonal 2H-MoS2 (ICDD PDF no. 96-900-9145 and JCPDS 37-1494).15,44 In the PXRD pattern, CuS–MoS2 retained the diffraction peaks of MoS2, indicating that the layered hexagonal framework is preserved after hydrothermal synthesis. A slight peak shift, along with a reduction in peak intensity, was observed in the CuS–MoS2 (002) plane, suggesting a structural perturbation associated with the incorporation of Cu species and the interaction of Cu with MoS2. In addition, the sample exhibited reflection at 27.41° and 30.3° in the (101) and (102) planes for the CuS phase (PDF# no. 06-0464), while diffraction peaks appeared at 19.22°, 26.5°, 35.6° and 53.4° corresponding to Cu1.38Mo3S4 (PDF# no. 71-0216).14,49,50 Moreover, CuS–MoS2 exhibits diffraction at 23.9° in the (110) plane corresponding to MoO3 (PDF #05-0508), suggesting partial oxidation of Mo species during synthesis or exposure to air.51 The presence of the (002) reflection in both samples indicates multilayer MoS2 nanosheets with well-ordered stacking of S–Mo–S sheets.52 The average crystalline size is calculated to be 8.97 nm for CuS–MoS2 along the (002) plane using the Debye–Scherrer formula.53

PXRD pattern of MoS2 and CuS–MoS2.

Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) measurements were carried out to examine the morphology of CuS–MoS2. The SEM image of CuS–MoS2 shows the formation of flakes that are reassembled into a flower-like morphology with varying sizes and shapes (Fig. 4a). The noticeable sheet-like structures suggest Cu-induced lattice strain.53 CuS–MoS2 exhibits enhanced structural complexity. More surface roughness is visible, suggesting further strain and Cu incorporation.54 To ascertain the components, an EDX measurement was performed. In the EDX graph (Fig. 4b and c), peaks corresponding to molybdenum (Mo), sulfur (S), copper (Cu) and oxygen (O) were observed, confirming the presence of Mo, S, and Cu as the major elements in the sample.44 Further elemental distributions were quantitatively investigated via EDX elemental mapping and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS), presented in SI (SI-5). The results show that the elemental ratios Cu : Mo : S are 0.50 : 1 : 1.55. Additionally, both EDS and ICP-MS quantify a Cu loading of about 18 wt% in the MoS2 framework (SI-4). Results obtained from both techniques are in good agreement, indicating successful incorporation of Cu species within the Cu-containing MoS2 material.

(a and b) SEM images of CuS–MoS2 at different magnifications, and (c) SEM-EDS spectrum corresponding to the selected area in (b).

A high-resolution transmission electron microscopic (HRTEM) image of CuS–MoS2 is displayed in Fig. 5a, indicating the formation of a sheet-like structure. The interplanar spacing measured from HR-TEM images of CuS–MoS2 was approximately 0.65 nm, corresponding to the (002) plane of hexagonal (2H-MoS2), consistent with the XRD data.44 The recorded SAED image of CuS–MoS2 (Fig. 5b) shows distinct concentric diffraction rings, which indicate the crystalline nature of the nanocomposites. The well-defined rings are indexed to the (002), (100), (103), and (110) planes of the hexagonal 2H-MoS2 phase, closely matching previous reports (JCPDS card no. 37-1492 and 6-27). The associated interplanar spacings (d-values) are approximately 0.65, 0.27, 0.24, and 0.16 nm, respectively, which closely match standard values for MoS2. Interestingly, these tiny crystallites combine to form nanolayers, which significantly influence the development of distinct lattice strains within the nanolayer.

(a) HRTEM and (b) SAED image of CuS–MoS2.

Further, the valence and chemical states were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in Fig. 6. In Fig. 6a, the survey spectra confirm the presence of constituent elements in CuS–MoS2. Fig. 6b reveals the deconvoluted peaks at 932.13 eV and 951.79 eV binding energies associated with Cu2+ 2p3/2 and Cu2+ 2p1/2, representing the dominant Cu2+ species.49,55 Additionally, the small peak appeared at 943.32 eV due to the paramagnetic chemical state of Cu2+.49,55 The Mo 3d spectrum in Fig. 6c depicts characteristic peaks at 227.83 eV, 231.32 eV, and 234.97 eV, indicating the Mo4+ state of Mo 3d5/2 and Mo 3d3/2 and the Mo6+ state of Mo 3d3/2, respectively, with the small peak at 225.11 eV attributed to S2− 2s.49,55 For the XPS spectrum of S 2p presented in Fig. 6d, the characteristic peaks appeared at binding energies of 160.74 eV and 161.97 eV, corresponding to S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, components of S2−, respectively, whereas a high binding energy at 168.12 eV suggests oxidized S species.49,55

Investigation of catalytic activities of CuS–MoS2

Oxidative azo-coupling of anilines

Initially, we examined the oxidative azo coupling of p-toluidine using a CuS–MoS2 catalyst under aerobic conditions. When p-toluidine (1.0 mmol) was stirred with CuS–MoS2 (5 mg) in acetonitrile (2 mL) for 12 h at room temperature under an open atmosphere, (E)-1,2-di-p-tolyldiazene (2a) was obtained in 20% yield (entry 1, Table 1). Delighted with this result, we next run the above reaction under refluxing conditions in the presence of tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) as the oxidant; a significant improvement in yield is observed, and 90% of product (2a) is obtained within 2 h (entry 2, Table 1). In a control experiment, in which the model reaction is performed in the absence of CuS–MoS2, no product is obtained, even after 12 hours of reflux in the presence of TBHP (entry 3, Table 1). TBHP is a better oxidant than H2O2 (entry 4, Table 1), and acetonitrile is a better solvent than toluene (entry 5, Table 1). Furthermore, it is observed that decreasing the temperature significantly reduces the yield of the product (entry 6, Table 1).

Additionally, the optimal catalyst amount was found to be 5 mg to achieve the highest yield of product 2a. Increasing the catalyst loading to 10 mg did not result in any appreciable improvement in yield (entry 7, Table 1), whereas using 2 mg led to a marked decline in the product yield (entry 8, Table 1). To elucidate the role of MoS2, the model reaction was carried out using MoS2 alone in the absence of Cu (entry 9, Table 1). A trace amount of product formation was observed. This can be ascribed to the limited redox capability and sluggish electron-transfer kinetics of pristine MoS2, which hinder efficient oxidation of aryl amines. These results highlight the crucial role of Cu species in enhancing charge separation and facilitating rapid redox cycling at the MoS2 interface. Therefore, refluxing 1.0 mmol of p-toluidine with CuS–MoS2 (5 mg) in the presence of 2.5 equivalents of TBHP in acetonitrile (2 mL) for 2 h under was selected as optimized reaction conditions (entry 2, Table 1).

Next, we performed the reaction using Cu(NO3)2 under the same reaction conditions to verify the synergistic role of Cu and MoS2. The reaction afforded a 65% yield of the desired product, significantly lower than that of CuS–MoS2 (Table 1, entry 11). It should be noted that the reaction was performed using identical catalyst mass and was intended to provide a qualitative assessment of the role of MoS2 support. These results suggest that the enhanced catalytic activity originated from the synergistic interaction between Cu species and MoS2.

Next, using optimised reaction conditions, i.e., refluxing a mixture of aromatic amine (1.0 mmol), TBHP (2.5 equivalents), and CuS–MoS2 (5 mg) in 2 mL of MeCN, we explored the scope of this methodology. It was observed that the CuS–MoS2 efficiently catalysed the oxidation of a wide range of structurally diverse aryl amines, affording the corresponding azo compounds in good to excellent yields (80–95%) within 2–4 h, as summarized in Table 2. Conspicuously, electron-rich mono-substituted aryl amines exhibited excellent reactivity, affording the corresponding azo compounds in high isolated yields (>90%). In contrast, di-substituted aryl amines were also selectively oxidized, albeit with slightly lower yields (75–88%), likely due to steric hindrance around the amino groups. Despite these, electron deficient substrate containing electron withdrawing groups exhibited and satirically hundred aromatic amines exhibited comparatively reduced reactivity and required longer reaction time, highlighting the current limitation of the catalytic system. The superior catalytic performance of CuS–MoS2 can be attributed to the synergistic interfacial interaction between CuS and MoS2, which promotes efficient charge separation and accelerates electron transfer between the active metal sites and oxygen species. This enhanced redox interplay facilitates the smooth oxidation of aryl amines to azo compounds.

To examine the heterogeneity of the materials and the contribution of leached Cu species in the reaction, we have performed the hot filtration experiment. To investigate this, the catalyst was removed from the reaction mixture after 20 minutes under hot conditions, and the remaining filtrate was further allowed to react under the same conditions. However, no significant conversion was observed, indicating material heterogeneity and no Cu leaching during the reaction (Fig. 7b). Finally, we have evaluated the stability and recyclability of the CuS–MoS2 materials in the oxidative azo-coupling of aniline as a model reaction under optimized reaction conditions. The catalyst was recovered from the reaction mixture by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with water and ethanol, dried in an oven at 80 °C, and reused for subsequent cycles. The results are shown in Fig. 7a. The results clearly indicate that both CuS–MoS2 materials were stable under the reaction conditions and were reused for up to 7 runs with minimal loss in azobenzene yield. The SEM image of the recycled CuS–MoS2 reveals that the material's 2D sheet-like structure remains intact even after the 7th run (Fig. 7c). Furthermore, the powder XRD pattern of recovered CuS–MoS2 closely matched that of the fresh catalyst, indicating the phases and crystalline nature remained intact after the 7th run (Fig. 7d).

Investigation of catalytic activities of CuS–MoS2via controlled experiments

Finally, to investigate the mechanism, we performed several controlled experiments. First, the reaction was performed under optimized conditions (Table 1, entry 2), resulting in an excellent yield in (eqn (1)). Next, we performed the reaction under an N2 atmosphere in the absence of TBHP, where the reaction did not initiate (eqn (2)); this confirms the role of the oxidizing agent. Further, the optimized reaction condition was performed in the presence of (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl)oxyl (TEMPO), a radical quencher (eqn (3)); the reaction was significantly quenched (30% yield), indicating the possibility of formation of a radical intermediate. The formation of aniline cation radical has also been reported in the literature during the oxidative azo-coupling of aniline.36,43 Furthermore, the reaction was performed under the identical reaction conditions using pristine MoS2 in the absence of Cu (eqn (4)). However, no product was formed, indicating that the pristine MoS2 is essentially inactive for the transformation. The poor activity of pristine MoS2 rather than the excellent performance of CuS–MoS2 provides strong experimental evidence for the synergetic effect between Cu active sites and MoS2 support (Scheme 5).

Controlled experiment for mechanistic investigation.

Finally, a plausible mechanism has been proposed based on experimental and previously reported mechanisms.36,43 The plausible mechanism is provided in Scheme 6. Initially, aniline is absorbed on CuS–MoS2 nanosheets, followed by the formation of a surface-bound aniline (Int-I).43 Simultaneously, catalytically active CuS centres activate tert-butoxy hydroperoxide to produce tert-butyl hydroperoxyl radical (tBuO˙). The tBuO˙ radical then oxidized the adsorbed aniline, leading to the formation of aniline radical cation, intermediate (Int-II). The Cu-catalyzed formation of aniline radical cation is also supported by a literature report.36,43 Thereafter, the Int-II coupled with Int-I afforded the hydrazobenzene radical cation (Int-III), which accepted an electron and changed into hydrazobenzene (Int-IV).43 Finally, activated TBHP species further oxidized the Int-IV, affording the azo product, followed by regeneration of CuS–MoS2 catalyst.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report a highly efficient Cu-doped MoS2 nanosheet catalyst synthesized via a facile hydrothermal strategy, exhibiting outstanding performance in the oxidative azo-coupling of aromatic amines. The catalyst delivers excellent yields (up to 96%) with complete selectivity under optimized conditions. The superior catalytic activity is driven by strong synergistic interactions between Cu redox centers and the defect-rich MoS2 support, enabling rapid electron transfer and effective radical generation. Importantly, the catalyst demonstrates excellent durability, recyclability (up to seven cycles), and scalability, underscoring its practical applicability. This work establishes CuS–MoS2 as a promising and sustainable platform for advanced organic transformations, paving the way for the future design of metal-doped TMDC-based catalysts.
