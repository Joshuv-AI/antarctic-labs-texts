Feasibility of a standardised National induction programme for Home Support Workers (HSWs) in the Republic of Ireland? Results from a National consultation with stakeholders from the sector
Authors: Mary Rose Sweeney, Nipuna Thamanam, Karl F Conyard, Shuhua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHome Support Workers (HSWs) make a valuable contribution to healthcare globally. They are the most rapidly increasing workforce in the Republic of Ireland (ROI) however no standardised National Induction Programme (NIP) exists to ensure consistency in their role. The primary aim of this study was to explore the feasibility of a NIP for HSWs in ROI. To inform the primary aim, we also explored the scope of practice of HSWs by examining the tasks that should be part of their role/the areas where they should provide support to patients/clients. Advantages, disadvantages and barriers to the implementation of a standardised NIP were explored.MethodsA mixed-methods design was used, combining online surveys and one-to-one interviews/focus groups. Three separate surveys were administered via the Zoho platform to (1) Key Stakeholders, (2) Induction Programme Providers (IPPs), and (3) HSWs. The surveys explored the feasibility of a standardised NIP, HSW roles/scope of practice, as well as advantages, disadvantages and barriers to its implementation. Semi-structured interviews and focus groups were conducted with Key Stakeholders to further explore these topics. Qualitative data were analysed by thematic analysis, while quantitative data were analysed descriptively in Microsoft Excel.ResultsA total of 38 Key Stakeholders, 11 IPPs, and 18 HSWs participated in the online surveys. Eight Key Stakeholders participated in one-to-one interviews or focus groups. All (100%) Key Stakeholders and 91% of IPPs agreed that a NIP with standardised content is feasible for HSWs across both public and private sectors. The majority of participants felt that a standardised NIP should be mandatory for all HSWs, including current staff. Thematic analysis found a high level of consensus on the feasibility, necessity for, and potential benefits of a NIP. Specific knowledge and skills requirements were identified across a wide range of theoretical and clinical areas.ConclusionsThe study reports a high level of consensus around the feasibility and importance of a NIP for HSWs, which would improve the quality and consistency of care across this sector. A proposed curriculum for a standardised NIP based on this study was developed and will be presented in a subsequent publication to follow.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13744-z.Keywords: Home Support Worker, Standardised induction programme, Ireland

Background

A variety of factors, including an ageing population, combined with recruitment and retention challenges in nursing & midwifery over the past 20 years or more, have led to the increasing demand for and availability of Home Support Workers (HSWs) in Ireland and globally [1]. HSWs have slowly expanded their roles and taken over certain tasks from nurses and midwives, supporting medical staff in providing care in hospital settings [2–5]. Irish health policy, Sláintecare Action Plan 2023 emphasises a fundamental shift for care to be provided in homes and in our communities [6]. This transition of care from hospital to home has resulted in traditional Health Care Assistant (HCA) roles being redesigned, rescoped and repurposed in the form of HSWs. The scope of practice associated with HSWs varies across different countries in the EU as well as the levels of care expected or delivered (from basic to complex) but can include: monitoring and measuring vital signs; cleaning and washing of manual and mechanical equipment; preparing and serving food and drinks to clients or patients; undertaking quality and safety procedures; supporting other health professionals; providing sanitary care support for patients; communicating with patients or clients; providing basic and preventive care; administering first aid; assisting in moving and transferral of patients; supporting in activities of daily living; managing patient intake, discharge and documentation of care; providing education/supervision/professionalisation; and supporting relatives/family members [7].

In the Irish context, the demand for HSWs is significantly outpacing supply, driven by a rapidly ageing population. The old-age dependency ratio is expected to double from 24% to 47% within the next 30 years. In Ireland, by 2051, the number of people aged 65 years and over is projected to rise from 629,800 in 2016 to between 1.53 and 1.6 million, while the population aged 80 years and older is anticipated to grow from 147,800 in 2016 to between 535,900 and 549,000 [8]. The Economic and Social Research Institute (ESRI) estimated that, relative to a baseline of the provision of 24.7 million hours of home support provided in 2019, an additional 3.5 to 8 million hours per annum will be needed under a number of projected scenarios [9]. Furthermore, a recent report published by ESRI in Ireland projects that by 2040, one in five people in Ireland will be aged 65 or older [10], meaning that the demand for long-term residential and home care will increase significantly. This enhances the requirement for standardised induction programmes in homecare sector.

The Health Service Capacity Review (2018), commissioned by the Department of Health in Ireland, projects a 120% rise in home support services, increasing from 10.6 million to 23.1 million hours, along with the need for an additional 10,500 long-term care beds (from a baseline of 26,200), by 2031 [11]. The ongoing Sláintecare reform of the Irish health and social care system aims to enable individuals with care needs to live independently in their homes and communities for as long as possible. However, long-term residential care will remain essential for those with more complex requirements. Therefore, it is vital to ensure that such care is of high quality, efficient, effective and safe while remaining responsive to the needs of service users. Thus, the need and demand for HSWs will increase as Sláintecare becomes a reality. The estimated total number of HSWs and qualified carers is in the region of 70,000 [12], with 1.5 HCA/HSW to every 1 registered nurse. In July 2022, the Health Service Executive (HSE) employed 22,495 HCA grades. The HSE sources home-support services from both not-for-profit and for-profit providers through tendering processes [13]. The funding for indirectly provided home-support services comes from the HSE’s allocated annual budget and is determined based on the unit cost per hour of support. By July 2022, 38% of home-support services under the HSE’s Services for older people were directly provided, while 62% were from private providers, including companies such as Blue Bird Care, Home Instead, and Comfort Keepers, etc [13].

A number of significant challenges arise within the HSW sector and these have been highlighted in several recent reports and forums [9, 11] including - recruitment & retention, training/education, competencies, knowledge and skill requirements, Continuing Professional Development (CPD) requirements, career development opportunities, pay and conditions, barriers to employment (part-time or full-time), as well as patient safety issues. In addition to this, HSWs are not a regulated sector, and there is no system of registration with any professional body such as CORU (https://www.coru.ie/) or the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Ireland (NMBI) (https://www.nmbi.ie/Home). In Ireland, induction training refers to the initial structured training provided to HSWs when they begin employment to ensure they understand the core requirements of their role, relevant standards, and basic care practices. Currently, the induction training/education and CPD requirements of HSWs in Ireland are ad hoc, with no single national induction or training programme being provided or mandated by a single designated entity/authority.

In Ireland, Quality Qualifications Ireland (QQI), previously known as the Further Education and Training Awards Council (FETAC), is the educational authority responsible for the development, review and accreditation of the Level 5 National Framework of Qualifications (NFQ) and Level 4 the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) training. Currently, QQI offers five main awards within Level 5 training. These include Community Care, Health Service Skills, Healthcare support, Nursing Studies and Community Health Services. Some modules include skill demonstrations, while others use simulated work experience. However, the majority of learning occurs through practical work experience, which is a mandatory component for obtaining the major award. In the meantime, it is required by most care settings that candidates need to have completed the full major award before starting full-time employment. However, to meet urgent staffing demands, certain areas of care have deviated from this standard to facilitate a faster recruitment process of new employees. While this approach may help address immediate workforce shortages, it raises significant concerns regarding care quality, worker preparedness, and potential risks to service users.

The HSE’s home care tender requires private providers to ensure that workers complete at least two modules – Care of the Older Person and Care Skills, before starting, with the remaining modules to be finished within 11 months to get the full major award. Similarly, in the nursing home sector, these modules are also required to begin full-time work, with the remaining completed on the job to obtain the full award. The Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 [14] clearly emphasises the need for employers to manage safety and health at work in order to prevent workplace injuries and ill health, and this extends to homecare work. The laws associated with this act specify that employees have the skills, knowledge and experience to carry out their duties safely. As such, any service which works for or through the HSE is required to ensure that HSWs have the following certifications before they begin employment, these include Basic Life Support; Manual and People Handling; Safeguarding and Protection; Fire Safety; Infection Prevention and Control; Non-Violent Crisis Intervention Training; and Lone Workers Safety Policy.

HSW induction programmes are offered by multiple providers in Ireland across public and private sectors and are typically overseen, coordinated and managed by nurses and midwives. HSWs are required to undergo a minimum set of training before they are permitted to engage with their clients in the community. This educational standard safeguards the health and security of the vulnerable service-users for whom home care support workers are responsible. However, these ‘induction requirements’ are non-transferrable: while the majority of the trainings covered in one organisations, HSWs’ induction programme is identical to the trainings covered by another home care organisations induction programme, every time a support worker moves between home care providers, they are expected to do a new induction programme and hence to repeat the trainings they have completed elsewhere, irrespective of the time between trainings. This repetitious exercise is costly in both time and money and places unnecessary strain on individual employers and employees. A national induction programme would permit greater mobility for workers across different regions in the country and facilitate greater mobility across the public and private sectors.

The primary aim of this study was to explore the feasibility of a standardised induction programme for HSWs in the Republic of Ireland. To inform the primary aim, we also explored the scope of practice of HSWs by examining the tasks that should be part of their role and the areas where they should provide support to patients/clients. Advantages, disadvantages and barriers to the implementation of a standardised national induction programme were also explored.

Method

Study participants recruitment

Three groups were invited to take part in the present study, including (1) Key Stakeholders, (2) Induction Programme Providers (IPPs) and (3) HSWs. The “appropriate authority respondent” from each organisation (across private and public sectors) involved in homecare in Ireland was contacted via email. This included - operations, regulators, unions, front-line staff, policy, voluntary organisations, advocacy, homecare providers, representative bodies, as well as education and training providers. Both Key Stakeholders and IPPs were recruited through this approach. HSWs were invited via an online “members only” social network group of HSWs, with approximately 4500 members (of which 60% work in the community sector), via a gatekeeper (Study design, Fig. 1).

Study design

Key Stakeholders, IPPs and HSWs were invited to participate in online surveys administered via the Zoho platform. For these online surveys, participants were provided with a participant information sheet, and informed consent was obtained through an embedded consent process at the beginning of the survey. In addition, Key Stakeholders were invited to take part in a one-to-one interview or a focus group. Invitation emails with participant information sheets were sent to potential participants to explain the study aims and procedures, as well as the consenting procedures and principles. Signed electronic consent forms were returned to the research team and checked prior to participation.

Survey development

As no appropriate survey had previously been developed to capture the variables of interest to this study, we developed surveys specifically for the purposes of the study using published reports and peer-reviewed publications to guide the content. We also established an Advisory Group (AG) with senior personnel working at the national level in this sector who advised us on the research questions, methodology and draft study instruments. The AG had representation from the following organisations - the Chief Nurses Office in the Department of Health (DOH), The Home Support Reform Unit in the Health Service Executive (HSE), Nursing Homes Ireland (NHI), Right at Home Ireland, Senior Health Support Worker, Home and Community Care Ireland. On the advice of the AG, the team also sought advice from the Health Information and Quality Authority (HIQA), the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Ireland (NMBI) (former senior staff member) and National Community Care Network (NCCN), a not-for-profit organisation, in relation to the study design and instruments to be used. The surveys for the Key Stakeholders, the IPPs and HSWs are provided in the Supplementary file.

Data collection

The study was conducted between August 2023 and May 2024 by researchers from the Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery, Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland (RCSI). The online surveys were administered via the Zoho online platform. The focus group and one-to-one interviews were conducted online via Microsoft Teams. These were attended by two members of the research team, one to lead the questions and one to take notes.

The data was collected in two stages. In stage 1, the online surveys were conducted to explore the feasibility of a national induction programme for HSWs, capturing the views of Key Stakeholders, IPPs and HSWs. The surveys also captured the respondents’ views on the tasks that should be undertaken by HSWs as well as the areas where they should provide support to clients/patients (scope of practice). Respondents were also asked to give their views on the advantages and disadvantages of a national induction programme and potential barriers to its implementation. Demographic variables were also collected. In stage 2, the semi-structured one-to-one interviews and focus groups were conducted among Key Stakeholders using a topic guide (attached in the supplementary files), exploring similar areas explored in the surveys but in more depth. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, in the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland (RCSI) in January 2024 (Reference number: REC 202310023).

Data analysis

Data from the three online surveys were collected using the Zoho platform and analysed descriptively in Microsoft Excel. The data were cleaned, and missing responses were excluded from the analysis. For the majority of questions, responses were mandatory, so all participants were required to answer each question before proceeding to the next. The percentages presented in the following section, therefore, are based on these mandatory questions, where the total number of responses equals the total number of participants. Three groups were all asked to provide comments on the advantages, disadvantages and barriers of implementing a national induction programme. Comments were reviewed and cross-checked against the other two groups’ responses to determine whether the same or closely aligned points were raised. Shared comments were then labelled to indicate the contributing groups (e.g., KS/IPP, KS/HSW). Qualitative data from the one-to-one interviews and focus groups were recorded and transcribed using Microsoft Teams. Thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework: data familiarisation, initial code generation, theme searching, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and final reporting [15]. The transcripts from Microsoft Teams were downloaded immediately after each interview/focus group and were checked by the lead researcher (MRS) to ensure that the recordings had accurately captured the discussion. Any errors were amended, and a second researcher (NT) confirmed that the transcripts were correct. Two members of the research team independently coded the transcripts. These codes were then compared, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus on the coding framework. Themes were developed iteratively, with provisional labels revised and refined in consultation with the wider research team. Themes were continuously reviewed against the raw data to ensure accurate representation. Quotes representing the themes were selected to illustrate them.

Key Stakeholders findings

Key Stakeholders survey

Survey respondents represented a broad range of stakeholders, including government departments, statutory bodies, professional regulators, private and voluntary sector organisations, healthcare professionals, representative organisations, advocacy agencies and home support workers. More details of the organisations and the roles of respondents are outlined in Table 1.

A total of 38 Key Stakeholders responded to the survey. All (100%) of the Key Stakeholders felt that a national induction programme with standardised content is feasible for HSWs working in the Republic of Ireland. The majority (86.84%) of them felt that it is feasible for HSWs working across both private and public sector settings. Among the respondents, 97.36% agreed or strongly agreed that a brief local induction programme (in addition to the national induction programme) is necessary at each site to familiarise new recruits with the local policies and procedures when they move to a new employment setting. 84% responded that a national induction programme with standardised content would raise the standard of care provided by HSWs. 74% felt that a national induction programme with standardised content should be mandatory for all HSWs, including those already working in the homecare system, not just new recruits. Discussion

This research study set out to explore the feasibility of having one induction programme for HSWs in the Republic of Ireland. The advantages and disadvantages, as well as potential barriers to the implementation of such a programme were also explored. Sláintecare Ireland’s health policy emphasises that delivery of care should be as local as possible and reduce the reliance on nursing home care by increasing homecare and community care based provision. This means that the role of the HSWs is increasingly becoming a cornerstone in national care delivery [16]. Our analysis compared the perspectives of Key Stakeholders and current IPPs regarding the scope of practice of HSWs. The results revealed a high level of consensus about the feasibility of such a programme and the value it could bring.

To help inform the curriculum for a national induction programme, the Key Stakeholder respondents were asked which tasks they feel should be part of HSW’s roles. There was strong agreement on core care activities such as personal hygiene, dressing, and feeding, reflecting a clear understanding of HSWs’ fundamental role. However, discrepancies emerged in relation to more complex or clinical tasks. For instance, while over 90% of programme providers viewed recognising health deterioration as within scope, only 63% of stakeholders agreed. Similarly, documentation of care and social support were more strongly endorsed by programme providers than by stakeholders. This mix-match of understanding or defining scope has been noted throughout Irish literature [7, 12], but also, as noted in the European Commission’s CC4HCA [17]. Internationally, there are issues aligning a definitive scope in countries similar to Ireland, which have not legislated and attained registration and licensure of this workforce.

These variations found within our own analysis and both within national and international evidence point to inconsistencies in how the HSW role is understood. Tasks such as managing swallowing difficulties or arranging appointments illustrate ongoing uncertainty about boundaries and responsibilities. This lack of clarity has persisted since the role’s creation in 2001, primarily due to limited planning around the scope of practice. The HSE’s 2018 Review of Role and Function of Healthcare Assistants similarly noted the need for a clearer definition [18]. Without shared understanding, risks include role drift, underutilisation, or care gaps increase in society. A national induction programme could provide a unified foundation, clarifying expectations, supporting equity in preparation, and strengthening the quality of care. As Ireland expands home-based services in line with Sláintecare [16], defining and supporting HSWs role and clinical scope is vital for sustainable, community-focused care.

All surveyed stakeholders (100%) believed a national induction programme with standardised content is feasible. This aligns with the Cavendish’s report [19] in the UK, which recommended unified training for support workers, and with EU analyses such as Braeseke et al.’s SANCO/1/2009 report [20] and the European Commission’s CC4HCA report [17]. However, feasibility is only an initial step. Implementation will require addressing logistical, organisational, financial, and policy-level issues. Most stakeholders (87%) felt such a programme was feasible across both public and private sectors, though 13% raised concerns about practical challenges. 74% agreed it should be mandatory for all HSWs, including existing staff, consistent with Conyard et al.’s recommendation for universal education to avoid deviations from best practice [12]. Stakeholders highlighted multiple advantages of a national programme: consistency, safety, and quality of care; clear scope and responsibilities for workers; and alignment of training with best practice. Similar benefits were emphasised by Metcalfe et al. [21] and Morrow et al. [22], who linked structured training and career pathways to workforce resilience, reduced turnover, and improved retention. Morrow et al. also noted the importance of communication skills, recognition of training achievements, and opportunities for skill development, which are factors critical to sustaining the workforce and safeguarding institutional knowledge [22]. These factors play a crucial role in alleviating socioeconomic burdens on the workforce and enhancing staff retention, thus safeguarding valuable skills and knowledge within national workforces. Morrow et al. underscored the importance of transforming home support services to meet the evolving needs of ageing populations [22]. As highlighted in their review paper, there exists a significant need for clarifying the expectations surrounding home support. In our study, we scoped the tasks and support which should be undertaken by HSWs from Key Stakeholders’ views. Morrow et al. also highlighted the need of effort to bring together HSWs and healthcare providers to contribute to the improvement of efficiencies and coordination at the health system level [22]. Through our survey, three sectors – Key Stakeholders, IPPs and HSWs were engaged to contribute to the development efforts. Barriers in relation to implementing a national induction programme for HSWs, as elucidated by Key Stakeholders, predominantly revolved around issues such as consensus on the format and content of HSWs’ induction programme, resource constraints, as well as concerns regarding time and funding. This underscores the importance of effective communication and collaboration solutions across healthcare sectors, and the need of a national induction programme for HSWs.

Our survey highlighted several advantages of a national induction programme, including consistent training quality, clear role expectations and clinical scope, improved recruitment, and stronger inter-organisational communication and support. From an economic perspective, Morrow et al. noted that tailored HSW training offers cost benefits and potential healthcare savings [22]. Specialised programmes, such as carer training, supervision frameworks, and advanced modules in areas like heart failure and end-of-life care, were also emphasised for enhancing the workability of early-career HSWs [22]. Additionally, ensuring such training is matched with recognition and fair compensation remains vital, particularly in systems where discrepancies between care costs and wages persist [12, 23, 24].

In addition, the research also identified areas for ongoing CPD as HSWs’ careers develop. Respondents prioritised end-of-life care, stoma and catheter care, and care for specific groups (e.g. autism, intellectual disabilities, dementia) as CPD driven training. While debate exists on how specialised HSWs should become, international evidence (e.g., CC4HCA [17]) recognises the value of equipping HSWs with a varied skillset, including collaborative skill sharing with registered nurses. This was later solidified by Stone and Bryant [25], noting the possible benefits of health aids (USA’s HSW comparative) in becoming certified nurse assistants (CNAs) in which being certified linked to positive outcomes for both workforce and service users, particularly in specialist areas such as dementia and end-of-life care [25]. Conversely ringing emergency services and not carrying out emergency management (first aid) in fear of worsening patient outcome was surprisingly noted as a direct qualitative quote by a service provider in IPH’s review of public consultation for planned regulations for homecare [26]. Ireland’s shortage of public health nurses (PHNs), noted in the Dáil Éireann debate (July 2022), highlights the need for alternative solutions [27]. As noted by O’Brien et al., training HSWs in wound care and skin integrity management may help bridge this gap [28]. Evidence already supports the benefit of educating HCAs in this area, particularly for pressure ulcer prevention [27, 28].

The main themes emerging from the one-to-one interviews/focus group with Key Stakeholders largely concurred with the findings of the Key Stakeholders survey responses as captured, including Feasibility, Necessary, Benefits, Care Provision, Clarity around the scope and expectation of HSW role, Funding Model, Resistance, Raising the profile of the profession, Continuous Professional Development (CPD), Medication Support, Deterioration of patients, Mentorship and Supervision, Specific knowledge. Of note, dementia, intellectual disability and autism were not considered by the majority of the Key Stakeholders survey respondents as important for inclusion in the national induction programme curriculum and expressed a preference for them to be covered as part of ongoing CPD instead. This was also the case for specialist areas of care such as Stomas and Urinary Catheters.

The views and opinions of the IPPs largely concurred with the views and opinions of the Key Stakeholders in relation to the feasibility of a national induction programme. Eleven induction programme providers from 14 organisations responded to the survey. 91% of them felt that a national induction programme with standardised content is feasible for all HSWs working in the homecare sector. The majority agreed that a national induction programme with standardised content is feasible for HSWs working across both private and public sector settings. Most IPPs respondents agreed that a national induction programme with standardised content should be mandatory for all HSWs including those already working in the homecare system, not just new recruits. Only 18 HSWs out of a total of approximately 4500 completed the HSW survey exploring the feasibility of a national induction programme. Due to the low response rate and associated lack of representativeness of the sample, we have not included data from their surveys except for responses provided in the open text boxes included in the survey, as these can be considered as qualitative data providing a qualitative snapshot of the opinion of a key focus group. HSWs stated that there are advantages to implementing a national induction programme for HSWs. The advantages stated by respondents include - Support for the increasing number of the ageing population in Ireland, increasing medical needs of the population, having the option to receive care in the comfort of their homes, and HSW competence improvement to work more confidently at home. These findings are congruent with Conyard et al. [12], where education and training were being assessed in the Irish population of HCAs including HSWs.

In addition, 88% of the HSW respondents stated that there are disadvantages to implementing a national induction programme for HSWs. For example, time-consuming and financial burden, with passing of time and the long hours worked, carers may be reluctant to upskill and retrain, not many people will speak up, different people have different needs, more training, delaying start time. This will take time to implement but it will provide a better balance of training required for carers. HSWs (52%) also stated that there are barriers to implementing a national induction programme for HSWs, such as political motivation, it might take a while to build up staff, new and old, to pass induction, cost and willingness of carers and companies to undertake.

Interestingly, little attention was paid to digital skills, aside from the need for mobile phones. This reflects current reliance on handwritten documentation. Health promotion was also rarely mentioned, despite evidence from Lämås et al. [29] that HSWs can enhance quality of life and awareness through health-promoting interactions in homecare. The report published by the ESRI in 2025 estimated a significant increase in the requirement for home support hours, from 28.7 million annually in 2022 to between 44.9 million to 54.9 million annually by 2040 [10]. This projected demand underscores the urgent need for a well-trained, competent, and sustainable home support workforce. Establishing a standardised national induction programme could therefore play a crucial role in ensuring consistency and quality in care delivery, supporting workforce development, and strengthening the overall capacity of the home support system to meet future population needs.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that stakeholders working/involved in homecare in Ireland believe that a single induction programme is feasible and important for all HSWs across private and public sector settings. A national induction programme could potentially raise the standard and consistency of care delivered to service users by HSWs and raise the profile and the level of professionalism of HSWs. While there was strong support for a national induction programme, some stakeholders raised concerns about potential challenges, such as implementation costs, impact on recruitment, and the risk of reduced flexibility for providers. These findings highlight the importance of addressing practical barriers and ensuring continued dialogue with stakeholder groups when developing the programme. A proposed curriculum for a standardised national induction programme based on the findings of this study has been developed as part of this project – this will be presented in a subsequent publication to follow.

Strengths and limitations

This research provides novel insight from an extensive consultation with stakeholders relevant to home support in Ireland, including the DOH, the HSE, HIQA, a range of Private Home Care Providers, HSWs, IPPs, healthcare Unions, Regulators (NMBI and CORU), Healthcare professionals and a range of organisations across the voluntary sector. A key limitation of this study, however, is the under-representation of HSWs. Despite extensive efforts to engage this cohort through gatekeepers such as home care providers and sector networks, participation was low, with only 18 out of approximately 4500 HSWs responding. It is possible that the scope and challenges of the HSW role may be perceived differently by those performing it compared with those managing, regulating, or commissioning services, which may have influenced the conclusions drawn. As a result, the perspectives captured from HSWs in this study may not be representative of the wider population. Low response rate may be due to a range of factors, including time pressures, digital access issues, or organisational barriers to participation. Future research should consider alternative strategies to increase engagement from HSWs, such as workplace-based recruitment, partnerships with unions, or in-person engagement through local provider networks. Further research is needed to better capture the perspectives of HSWs and explore the implications of a national induction programme from their viewpoint. Additionally, there is a risk of researcher bias in the analysis and interpretation of qualitative data. To minimise this, multiple team members were involved in data coding and cross-checking to enhance the rigour of the findings. Due to the small and potentially identifiable nature of the participant group, individual roles were not linked to specific quotes in the one-to-one interview/focus group findings to maintain confidentiality, which may limit the ability to attribute comments to particular stakeholder categories.
