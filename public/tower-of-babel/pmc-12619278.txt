From declarations to accountability: Nigeria 2026 and the global fight against antimicrobial resistance

In July 2025, Nigeria announced that it will host the 5th Global High-Level Ministerial Conference on Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) in Abuja on 29–30 June 2026, the first such meeting in Africa [1]. This is momentous, as AMR already directly claims 1.27 million lives annually and contributes to about 5 million associated deaths globally [2]. In Nigeria alone, 64,500 deaths were directly attributed to AMR in 2021, with 263,400 associated deaths, a 7% reduction in gross domestic product (GDP), and an 11% decline in livestock productivity [1]. The question now is whether Abuja will mark a true inflection point from rhetoric to results. Against this backdrop, hosting the summit provides an opportunity for African leadership to reposition global AMR governance towards equitable and actionable outcomes. To seize that opportunity, hosting the conference in Abuja should not be a ceremonial first. Done well, it can rebalance the agenda toward challenges that are prevalent in low- and middle-income settings, including unreliable drug quality, weak prescribing controls, fragmented supply chains, and gaps in water and sanitation.

Nevertheless, global commitments have not translated into sufficient action. The Muscat Ministerial Manifesto (2022) set ambitious targets to reduce antimicrobial use in agrifood systems by 30–50% by 2030, preserve critically important antibiotics for human medicine, and ensure that Access group antibiotics comprise at least 60% of consumption [3]. The Jeddah Commitments (2024) noted that while 178 countries have national AMR action plans, only 52% have functional coordinating mechanisms, and 68% implement their plans [4]. The same document warns that, without control, AMR could cause over 39 million deaths between 2025 and 2050, cost US$412 billion in health expenses, and US$1–3.4 trillion in annual GDP losses by 2030 [4]. Nigeria’s data echo these stakes, yet surveillance remains limited and antibiotic sales remain largely unregulated.

The Abuja meeting must therefore move beyond declarations. First, it should adopt an Abuja Outcome Document with measurable targets for human, animal, and environmental sectors, building on Muscat’s three global targets and incorporating commitments to reduce death tolls and economic losses. Second, the conference should champion sustainable financing. Global health investment in AMR is minuscule compared with pandemic preparedness, and as such, Nigeria and other African countries need resources to expand laboratory capacity, infection prevention and control, water-, sanitation-, and hygiene infrastructure, and access to diagnostics. Third, Nigeria can leverage its leadership of the AMR conference to integrate AMR control with existing health programmes. For example, the World Health Organization’s human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) service guideline emphasises integrating non-communicable disease and mental-health care with HIV services [5]. Similarly, AMR stewardship should be mainstreamed into primary care, maternal and child health programmes, and agriculture. Fourth, the conference should prioritise One Health surveillance. Only integrated surveillance of antimicrobial use and resistance across humans, animals, and the environment will provide reliable data to guide policy. Together, these steps define what Abuja should do; the next question is how to ensure they are delivered.

To make these commitments bite, accountability must be concrete and comparable. Abuja should launch an annual AMR scorecard with a short list of metrics reported by all countries: the share of access to antibiotics in human use, overall antibiotic consumption in defined daily doses, prescription-only enforcement in retail sales, global antimicrobial resistance and use surveillance system reporting coverage for tertiary hospitals, veterinary sales of highest-priority critically important antibiotics, and the number of wastewater or environmental sentinel sites reporting resistance data [6]. Each metric should have targets for 2027 and 2030, with public financing commitments tied to progress and independent verification. An African AMR financing facility, co-financed by development banks and matched by domestic resources, could underwrite laboratory upgrades, stewardship training, and water, sanitation, and hygiene expansion with transparent ledgers of disbursements. But metrics and money alone will not be enough; legitimacy and representation must anchor the agenda.

Finally, for that legitimacy, the Abuja meeting must amplify African voices. Africa bears a disproportionate AMR burden but has limited influence in global governance. Hosting this summit gives Nigeria the platform to advocate for fair access to new antibiotics and vaccines, equitable intellectual property arrangements, and investment in African research and manufacturing. African institutions should co-design the scorecard and the financing facility and share governance over their implementation. It should also demand accountability from high-income countries and pharmaceutical companies to curb the overuse of antimicrobials in agrifood systems. Turning declarations into action will require courage and collaboration, but the cost of inaction, measured in millions of lives and billions of dollars, makes such courage non-negotiable.
