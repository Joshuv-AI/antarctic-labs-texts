Higher-order transmissibility and its linear approximation for in-service crack identification in train wheelset axles
Ehsan Naghizadeh ,1, Paolo Tiso 1, Eleni Chatzi 2

Abstract




Abstract
: In-service structural health monitoring is a so far rarely exploited, yet potent option for early-stage crack detection and identification in train wheelset axles. This procedure is non-trivial to enforce on the basis of a purely data-driven approach and typically requires the adoption of numerical, e.g. finite element-based, simulation schemes of the dynamic behavior of these axles. Damage in this particular case can be formulated as a breathing crack problem, which further complicates simulation by introducing response-dependent nonlinearities into the picture. In this study, first, a new crack detection feature based on higher-order harmonics of the breathing crack is proposed, termed Higher-Order Transmissibility (HOTr), and, secondly, its sensitivity and efficacy are assessed within the context of crack identification. Next, the mentioned feature is approximated via use of linear system theory, delivering a surrogate model which facilitates the computation and speeds up the crack identification procedure. The accuracy of the proposed method in reproducing the delivered HOTr is compared against the nonlinear simulation model. The obtained results suggest that the approximation of the HOTr can significantly reduce the computational burden by eliminating the need for an iterative solution of the governing nonlinear equation of motion, while maintaining a high level of accuracy when compared against the reference model. This implies great potential for adoption in in-service damage identification for wheelset axles, feasibly within a near real-time context.

Keywords
: wheelset axle monitoring, breathing crack, higher-order harmonics, crack identification, transmissibility

1. Introduction

The structural integrity of wheelsets comprises an important aspect in condition monitoring of trains. The high risk of train derailment as a result of a failure in the wheelset advocates the development of efficient schemes for Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) of the wheelset axles
[
1
,
2
]
. Incipient cracks, which cause high cycle fatigue failure due to crack propagation, form a main threat to the integrity of the train wheelsets
[
3
,
4
]
. As a result,
early-stage
crack detection and identification in train wheelset axles have served as the subject of investigation for numerous research studies in the past century
[
5
,
6
]
.

The most common industry practice for crack identification lies in the adoption of Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) schemes, which are usually applied within a dedicated workshop facility. In particular, Ultrasound Testing (UT) is shown to be capable of detecting the so-called incipient cracks, which are sized between 1-5 percent of the shaft diameter
[
7
,
8
]
. However, the implementation of NDT methods, including UT, requires the train to be stationed and, thus, out of service for a prolonged inspection period, while further necessitating sophisticated equipment and high-cost facilities. In such a setting, the inspection intervals are prescribed at scheduled periods, while the optimal planning of these intervals, which is likely non-regular, poses a non-trivial task requiring verifiable residual life estimation of the wheelset
[
1
,
9
,
10
]
. Studies on the crack propagation and residual life estimation of the wheelset axle under operational conditions form an ongoing research topic that is challenged by various factors such as, among others, the temporal and spectral nature of the applied external load
[
11
,
12
]
, the involved material characteristics
[
13
]
, crack closure
[
14
,
15
]
and the possible development of residual stresses
[
16
]
. The lack of precise knowledge on the mentioned parameters results in associated uncertainties and prompts the adoption of conservative safety factors for the scheduling of appropriate inspection intervals
[
1
]
. An alternate approach to the current practice lies in the broader exploitation of in-service health monitoring schemes. Such techniques rely on continuous evaluation of the axle integrity through real-time data from a set of sensors implemented on the axle. The continuous surveillance scheme reduces the risk of axle failure, increases the availability of trains due to reduced maintenance, and most importantly, eliminates the need for a verifiable crack growth model. However, such an approach is challenged by several factors like in-service data acquisition (the difficulty in mounting permanent sensors on the rotating axle as achieved in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
), extraction of robust crack identification features, and the need to run the identification procedure in real or close to real time, so as to facilitate data compression, thus lowering storage and transmission requirements.

Unlike damage detection, crack identification techniques require information from a model of the structure that is parametrized with respect to the crack characteristics (location, size, and orientation) and is also capable of predicting the response of the shaft to varying operational loads
[
20
]
. Identification is then typically achieved by solving an inverse problem through optimization to minimize the error between a Damage Sensitive Feature (DSF) derived from the measured response and the corresponding value calculated using the model. The mentioned models can be largely classified into either the purely data-driven or physics-based class. The primary obstacle for the former is the actual availability of high-quality data
[
21
]
. Specifically, in the case of axle cracks, such data are not available due to the rarity of such damage occurrences. To alleviate such a hurdle, data-driven models could be trained on synthetic data derived from high-fidelity physics-based models. Nevertheless, seeding a large pool of solutions to span the parameter space of the problem often requires the repetitive evaluation of the high-fidelity model for a prohibitively large number of times, rendering the process inefficient. Physics-based models, on the other hand, don’t need training for the evaluation of a forward problem. However, during the solution of the inverse problem, the model must be evaluated for a significant number of times. Therefore, a computationally efficient model is of paramount importance.

Substantial literature exists on crack detection and identification, within the context of vibration-based monitoring and non-destructive evaluation. The interested reader is referred to the works of Carden & Fanning
[
22
]
and Sabnavis
et al.

[
5
]
. Here, we confine our analysis to the availability of vibration-based monitoring data, owing to the merits and applicability of associated sensors, such as accelerometers and strain gauges, for in-service surveillance
[
23
]
. A comprehensive review on traditional vibration-based damage identification is given in
[
24
]
. An issue commonly faced by conventional DSFs that relate to vibration-based monitoring, such as modal properties, mode shape curvatures, transmissibility functions, and structural flexibility, is their insensitivity to small cracks
[
25
]
. Ideally, a DSF that is suited for crack detection and characterization should be both global, in the sense that it can be detected from sparse measurements, and simultaneously local to the crack, i.e., exhibiting sufficient sensitivity to the crack characteristics (location and size). Toward extracting such DSFs, crack breathing phenomena have been capitalized upon in order to extract the so-called higher-order harmonics as features that meet the aforementioned criteria
[
26
,
27
]
. In this context, Reduced-Order Modeling (ROM) can be used as a framework that supports the affordable simulation of associated crack models, which are often computationally taxing. However, such ROMs often struggle to cover the large parameter space often encountered in crack identification problems
[
28
]
.

The implementation of breathing-induced higher-order harmonics for online SHM dates back to 1989
[
29
]
, and has found applications for crack detection in different structures
[
30
]
. Two are the primary approaches to simulating breathing cracks. The first is to model the alternating bending stiffness due to the crack as a harmonic function of time that is synchronized with the rotational speed of the axle
[
31
,
32
]
. Although the mentioned methods are computationally less expensive, they provide a very limited resolution for crack parameters and are generally used for a qualitative study of cracked axles. The second, more accurate, approach is based on the adoption of a Finite Element (FE) model of the axle with a fine mesh and explicit modeling of the contact between the crack faces. The crack can then be represented either through meshless techniques, such as eXtended Finite Element Method (XFEM)
[
33
]
coupled with appropriate parametrization schemes, such as mesh morphing
[
34
]
, or confining the crack shape to the underlying mesh. The dependency of the crack behavior on the contact state of the crack faces renders the analysis nonlinear. The fundamentals underlying the nonlinear analysis of a shaft with a breathing crack are offered in
[
35
,
36
]
. In recent years, Lin & Ng implemented a higher-order Frequency Response Function (FRF) for the identification of localized nonlinearities in structures, with a focus on breathing cracks
[
37
]
. The effect of imbalance and its interaction with higher-order harmonics stemming from the breathing crack phenomenon is examined in
[
38
,
39
]
. This concept was later extended to the identification of multiple cracks
[
40
]
. Higher-order harmonics have recently been shown to comprise robust features for detecting breathing cracks in fluid-delivering pipes
[
41
]
.

The nonlinear analysis that is necessary, in order to account for crack face interaction, increases the computational complexity entailed in a forward model evaluation. The need for fast calculation of the model response, especially within inverse problem settings, remains a major challenge and the main repelling factor for implementing the nonlinear DSF for the in-service SHM of the wheelset axles. In this work, we introduce the novel concept of Higher-Order Transmissibility (HOTr) as a crack detection feature to address the problems associated with the high computational cost of the mentioned nonlinear analysis. HOTr is derived by extending the concept of the transmissibility function that has been advocated in the literature for open cracks (i.e. without contact)
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
to higher-order harmonics induced by breathing cracks as described in Section
3
. We show that the profile of the crack face contact force can be approximated by the constraint forces of a closed crack, resorting to linear system theory. The latter results in a significant reduction in the required computational time for obtaining a sufficient approximate solution. The efficiency of the proposed DSF for crack identification is tested on two numerical examples.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we postulate that the presence of a breathing crack leads to higher-order harmonics in the response signal. In section
3
detailed mathematical modeling of the FE discretized axles with breathing cracks is presented, the HOTr is defined, their linear approximation is formulated, and the inverse problem for the crack identification based on HOTr is presented. Section
4
discusses two numerical examples by first evaluating the sensitivity of the proposed HOTr to crack parameters, then inspecting the effectiveness of the proposed method in approximating HOTrs, and finally performing crack identification and giving a statistical view on identified crack parameters. Conclusions on the obtained results are summarized in section
5
.

2. Postulation - Single Degree of Freedom system

To illustrate the appearance of higher-order harmonics due to the presence of a breathing crack, let us consider a Single Degree of Freedom (SDoF) mass-spring-damper system featuring an additional unilateral spring subjected to sinusoidal forcing, as shown in Figure
1
. This model qualitatively reproduces the differentiation in the bending stiffness due to the opening and closing of the crack as a cracked beam vibrates in its first bending mode. The governing equation of motion can be written as follows:

\text{m}\ddot{\text{x}}\text{(t)}+\text{c}\dot{\text{x}}\text{(t)}+\text{k}\text{x}\text{(t)}+\text{H}\text{(\text{x})}\text{k}_{0}\text{x}\text{(t)}=\text{q}\text{(t)}=sin(\omega_{\text{f}}\text{t}).

(1)

where
\text{x}(\text{t})
represents the beam tip displacement, and
H
(
x
)
is the step function defined as:

\text{H}\text{(\text{x})}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{x}\geq\delta\\
0,&\text{x}<\delta\end{cases}.

(2)

The former model can be used to reflect both the case of a healthy (uncracked) and damaged (cracked) beam. The system parameters for the healthy model are defined as
\text{m}=1
,
\text{k}=1
,
\text{c}=0.02
,
\text{k}_{0}=0
, which obviously implies that the discontinuous (Heaviside) function remains inactivated. For the cracked case, the stiffness parameters are modified to reflect the same overall stiffness when the crack gap is closed:
\text{k}=0.9
,
\text{k}_{0}=0.1,\delta=0
, while the further parameters remain the same.

SDoF contact model representing bending beam with crack.

We have simulated the response of both healthy and cracked beams using this simple model under harmonic excitation at
\omega_{\text{f}}=0.6[\text{rad/s}]
with a unit amplitude as shown in Eq.
1
. The simulation was run until steady-state was reached. The resulting spectrum of the response, depicted in Figure
2
, reveals the presence of higher harmonics which are attributable to the breathing phenomenon. The higher-order harmonics are distinctive features attributed to the breathing phenomena that arise from the crack, making these a suitable DSF for crack identification. The extraction of the mentioned harmonics from the noisy acceleration and/or strain measurements presents further challenges due to the low amplitude associated with these features, as seen in Figure
2
. In this work, we focus on the second-order harmonic, which is expected to feature a more notable energy with respect to higher-order such features.

3. Methods

3.1.   Train axle with breathing crack in operation - Equation of Motion

Different approaches exist for modeling a wheelset axle depending on the goal of the analysis. In this study, we focus on the nonlinearity induced by the breathing crack as a damage identifier. Therefore, the devised model should be crafted in a manner that intentionally captures the nonlinear feature associated with the breathing phenomenon. To this end, a FE model of a train axle is adopted. The main reason for this choice is the fact that such a discretized numerical approximation allows for explicit parametrization of the crack with respect to its location and size. The equation of the motion can then be written as:

\boldsymbol{M}\ddot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}\text{(t)}+\boldsymbol{C}\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}\text{(t)}+\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\text{(t)}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\text{(t)})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}\text{(t)}.

(3)

In Eq.
3
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x(\text{t})}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of displacements at the discretized degrees of freedom (DoFs), with
n
denoting the number of DoFs, with
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\dot{x}(\text{t})}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\ddot{x}(\text{t})}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denoting the velocity and acceleration, respectively. The mass, damping, and stiffness matrices are denoted as
\boldsymbol{M}
,
\boldsymbol{C}
, and
\boldsymbol{K}

\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
respectively. The contact between the crack faces is modeled with the penalty method by introducing a node-to-node piecewise linear contact law. The resulting bilinear force, oriented normal to the crack face, and exerted between a pair of nodes at the crack faces (see Figure
4
),
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\text{(t)})
can be formulated as

\text{f}_{\text{c}}(\text{x}_{\text{rel}}\text{(t)})=\begin{cases}\text{k}_{\text{p}}\text{x}_{\text{rel}}\text{(t)}&\text{if }\text{x}_{\text{rel}}\geq 0\\
0&\text{otherwise }\end{cases}.

(4)

In Eq.
4
,
\text{k}_{\text{p}}
is a scalar penalty value , and
\text{x}_{\text{rel}}\text{(t)}
is the relative displacement between a pair of contact nodes defined as:

\text{x}_{\text{rel}}\text{(t)}=\text{x}_{\text{c}}^{+}\text{(t)}-\text{x}_{\text{c}}^{-}\text{(t)}.

(5)

where
\text{x}_{\text{c}}^{+}
and
\text{x}_{\text{c}}^{-}
denote the displacement at the two opposite sides of the crack face, as shown in Figure
4
. The vector of nonlinear forces
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(\text{t}))
is then a collection of contact forces acting on the contact pairs. It should be mentioned that the focus of this study lies in the identification of small-sized cracks (i.e. early stage); therefore, additional damping due to the presence of the crack was neglected in the model. Moreover, the friction between the crack surfaces is considered negligible and, thus, disregarded. Throughout the study, it is assumed that the nonlinearity is confined to the nonlinear forces at the crack faces, and there exist no other sources of nonlinearity in the system.

3.2.   Steady-state response and higher-order harmonics

The axle is assumed to be subject to a four-point bending load due to the weight of the train coach and the reaction force from the rails, as shown in Figure
3
. The weight distribution (i.e.
W_{l}
, and
W_{r}
) is assumed to be known and time-invariant for the time window of interest. An approximation of this distribution can be obtained from strain measurements on the axle. In normal operating conditions, the wheelset rolls without slip with angular velocity
\omega
proportional to the train speed.

Free body diagram of a wheelset axle in operation.
W_{l}
, and
W_{r}
denote the load from the weight of the wagon coach. The reaction force form the interaction of the wheels with the rail is denoted as
f_{l}

The equation of motion can then be written with respect to a rotating reference frame attached to the axle and rotating with it. In this reference frame, the bending load rotates with
\omega
. By considering a constant speed for the train, the external forces exerted on the axle become a single-tone harmonic excitation with frequency
\omega
. Note that additional stresses due to the rotation of the axle, i.e. rotordynamic effects and the whirl motion caused by the eccentricity of the axle, are neglected here due to the high rigidity and low operating rotational speed of the train axles. Then, the external force
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}\text{(t)}
can be approximated using a Fourier series expansion as

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{q}}\text{(t)}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}}(\omega)e^{i{\omega}\text{t}}+\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}}}(\omega)e^{-i{\omega}\text{t}},

(6)

where,
i
is the imaginary unit,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the Fourier coefficient of the harmonic function, and
\overline{\square}
denotes the complex conjugate of a vector. We then seek a steady-state solution to Eq.
3

It is worth mentioning that special attention should be paid to the nonlinear crack face forces due to their non-smooth nature. To the best of the author’s knowledge, there is no rigorous mathematical proof for the existence of a steady-state solution for a continuous (i.e.
C^{0}
) piece-wise linear dynamical system. However, for the cases where the piece-wise linear force is small (in our case implying a small crack), steady-state response is observed in time-domain simulations as well as experiments. In this research, the MHB results are verified by initially performing a convergence analysis to establish the number of harmonics included in the MHB Ansatz. Following this, the system is solved in the time domain for selected frequencies via direct time integration to allow for comparison. The generalized alpha method
[
45
]
was employed to avoid so-called spurious oscillations
[
46
,
47
]
as a numerical artifact due to the non-smoothness of the nonlinear forces.

Let us assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\text{(t)}
is the steady-state response to
3

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(\text{t})\approx\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{0}(\omega)+\sum_{\text{p}=1}^{h}[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)e^{i\text{p}{\omega}\text{t}}+\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{p}}}(\omega)e^{-i\text{p}{\omega}\text{t}}],

where,

\text{T}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}
and therefore decomposed in a Fourier series basis as

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\text{t})\approx\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{0}(\omega)+\sum_{\text{p}=1}^{h}[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)e^{i\text{p}{\omega}\text{t}}+\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}}(\omega)e^{-i\text{p}{\omega}\text{t}}].

(8)

The

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\text{T}}\int_{0}^{\text{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\text{t})e^{-i\text{p}{\omega}\text{t}}dt,\quad\text{p}=0,1,2,...,h.

(9)

The transition of the contact state in the time domain renders the evaluation of the above integrals non-trivial. Therefore, the Fourier coefficients of the nonlinear force,
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}(\omega)}
are derived either by analytical evaluation of the transition angles (i.e. points in the periodic solution where the crack changes from open to closed in the time domain)

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{h}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{0}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{h}}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{0}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{h}}\\
\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}}_{\text{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}\\

(10)

where

is the

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{R}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s}}):=\boldsymbol{D}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s}}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s}})-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}},

where the residual,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{R}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s+1}})\approx\boldsymbol{\mathrm{R}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s}})+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{s}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s+1}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}_{\text{s}})=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}.

(13)

In Eq.

3.3.   Higher-order transmissibility as a DSF and its linear approximation

In its more general form, transmissibility is defined as a frequency-dependent ratio of responses (e.g., displacement, velocity, or acceleration) between two points in a structure or system
[
51
]
. Transmissibility functions were first devised for periodically forced vibration of linear stable dynamical systems and have been successfully implemented in SHM
[
34
,
52
]
. The main advantage of using transmissibility functions for SHM lies in the fact that these functions do not depend on the magnitude of the external force. This is of great relevance for in-service monitoring, as the external loading changes continuously during operation and is therefore not easily monitored. However, the insensitivity of the linear features (including transmissibility functions) for identification of the incipient cracks renders the methods based on conventional transmissibility ineffective. As a remedy for the aforementioned challenge, we here define the transmissibility of higher-order harmonics, here referred to as HOTr, as a DSF that is tailored to crack detection.

Figure 4:
(a) Closed crack configuration solved by enforcing compatibility at the crack faces
(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{-}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}})
. (b) Open crack configurations and corresponding nonlinear crack face forces.

We define
\text{p}^{th}
-order transmissibility
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1}
, between the
\text{p}^{th}
-order frequency domain response at
\text{m}^{th}
DoF
\hat{\text{x}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m)}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1}
, and
\text{n}^{th}
DoF
\hat{\text{x}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(n)}}\in\mathbb{C}^{1}
, as:

\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}:=\frac{\hat{\text{x}}^{\text{(m)}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)}{\hat{\text{x}}^{\text{(n)}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)}.

(14)

The dependence of
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}
on
\omega
is dropped in Eq.
14
. It is worth mentioning that the phase difference between the responses at two points is captured by the complex nature of
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}
.

As shown in the previous section, the set of nonlinear algebraic equations, reflected in Eq.
10
, has to be solved to calculate
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{p}}
. Since this is computationally expensive, the solution of the inverse problem associated with crack parameter identification can be severely hampered. Therefore, we here propose an approximation to the HOTr that avoids the iterative solution in Eq.
13
. In fact, the
\text{p}^{th}
-order harmonic solution of Eq.
10
is given by:

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{p}}=[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{p}}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}.

(15)

Therefore, the corresponding
\text{p}^{th}
order transmissibility between any two points can be written as:

\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}=\frac{[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{p}}]_{m}^{-1}\ \boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}}{[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{p}}]_{n}^{-1}\ \boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}},

(16)

where
[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{p}}]_{j}^{-1}
is the
j^{th}
row of the
[\boldsymbol{D}_{\text{p}}]^{-1}
matrix. It is evident that
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{\text{p}}^{\text{(m,n)}}
only depends on the particular shape of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{f}}}_{\text{p}}
. We then seek an approximation to the shape of the mentioned nonlinear force. Let us start with partitioning the system’s DoFs as:

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{c}}^{+},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{c}}^{-},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{r}}]^{\text{T}},

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{c}}^{+},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{c}}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{nc}
represents the degrees of freedom (DoFs) for the crack face nodes, with
nc
being the number of nodes per crack face as depicted in Figure
4
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{\text{r}}
encompasses all other DoFs. The solution of the pristine axles, i.e. the one without a crack as indicated in Figure
4
(a), can be obtained by enforcing the compatibility condition between the crack faces in dual form (see
[
53
]
). The set of equations can be written as:

\boldsymbol{D}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{+}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{-}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{r}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{\lambda}}}\\
-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{\lambda}}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{q}}},

(18)

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{x}}}_{\text{c}}^{-}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}.

(19)

In Eq.
18
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\hat{\lambda}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{nc}

3.4.   Crack identification setup

Let us now assume that a crack can be defined through a set of parameters contained in the entries of the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{v}

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tilde{\theta}}}\simeq\text{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\Theta}}}J_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\theta}}}.

The extraction of higher-order harmonics from the time-domain measurements is achieved by a recursive correlation method proposed in

The solution to the optimization problem can be realized through diverse schemes. In our case, a closed-form dependency relationship of the objective function to the parameters is not available. In addition, the objective function is highly non-convex and non-smooth. Therefore, we opt to apply gradient-free optimization methods here. Examples of such methods that are often adopted within inverse problem settings in an SHM context include Genetic Algorithms (GA)

The hyper-parameters of the method, such as the population size and maximum number of generations, are chosen according to the specific parameter space for the numerical examples at hand, see Section

In this setup, the crossover coefficient, representing the proportion of the next generation (excluding elite children) generated by crossover, is fixed at 0.8. The solution of the optimization problem through the GA algorithm is realized by repeatedly solving the forward problem (i.e., simulation of the response of the structure with a given

4. Numerical examples

In this section, we demonstrate the concepts previously introduced using numerical examples. Specifically, we consider a two-dimensional and three-dimensional FE model of a train wheelset axle. Initially, we examine the sensitivity of the second-order harmonic response to crack parameters, illustrating the impact of varying crack positions on this inferrable quantity. Next, we evaluate the proposed method for approximated HOTrs, focusing on accuracy and computational speedup. Finally, we implement the second-order transmissibility of strain readings for crack identification and assess the capability of the proposed approach via Monte Carlo analysis.

To facilitate the solution of the derived equations, two different reduced-order models are considered here. The first model, referred to as ”RB,” is derived by retaining crack interface DoFs and external forcing DoFs and reducing all other DoFs via the Rubin method
[
63
]
. The other reduced-order model, here denoted as ”SUB,” is obtained by substructuring. The idea is to divide the structure into two possible substructures: substructure A, which is the area of the structure where possible cracks are expected to form, and substructure B, which contains the remaining domain (see Figure
6
and Figure
12
for a graphical rendition of the 2D and 3D mode, respectively). It should be noted that no specific assumption other than the fact that cracks would form on the surface of the wheelset axle is made on the selection of substructure A. Then, substructure A is kept as a full-order model, while substructure B is reduced via the Rubin method, keeping only the DoFs at the interface with substructure A and external forcing DoFs. The two substructures are then assembled by imposing compatibility of the displacements at their interface. The resulting RB model is smaller in terms of DoFs and is thus faster for a single forward evaluation. The main advantage of using the SUB model over the RB model is the fact that the construction of substructure B can be done offline (once for all crack cases). This reduces the overall online cost drastically, as shown in the upcoming sections. The FE implementation is performed on the MATLAB-based YAFEC code
[
64
]
.

5. Conclusions

In this study, we consider the higher-order harmonics arising from the breathing of cracks as a potential feature for crack identification. We observed that for contact-type problems (including breathing crack phenomena) the second-order harmonic delivers a prominent damage-sensitive feature. We, thus, propose the second-order transmissibility
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{2}
function as a crack identification feature for in-service health monitoring of the train wheelset axles. In contrast to their first-order counterparts, second-order harmonics prove high sensitivity to variation in crack location and size, as shown in Figure
13
and Figure
7
. Moreover, these features can be detected from sparse measurements, making them efficient crack identification features.

One major bottleneck for implementing the nonlinear features for crack identification is their associated high computational cost. This is primarily because the identification procedure requires numerous forward solutions of the computational model, which, given the nonlinear nature of the higher order harmonics, requires an iterative solution procedure (e.g. Newton-Raphson). To alleviate this toll, we propose an approximation for HOTr that eliminates the need for the expensive nonlinear solution process. The main idea for the proposed method is to approximate the nonlinear crack face forces with the linear solution to the constraint forces that keep the crack closed. This method works well for incipient cracks where the overall response of the cracked axle is not significantly different from the healthy one. The method is not expected to work for large cracks. The proposed method reduces the
solution
time by three orders of magnitude for both 2D and 3D cases. The accuracy of the proposed method is tested for 2D and 3D cases where the ratio of the crack depth to axle diameter was 15% and 11.5%, respectively. For the 2D case, the method achieves excellent accuracy for the entire frequency range. The accuracy for the 3D case is reduced but, overall, the method achieves good accuracy; one that proves sufficient for the identification task.

Another computational challenge in calculating the HOTr is the model construction. Since the source of the nonlinear force is confined to crack faces, Reduced-Order Modeling (ROM) techniques like the Rubin method are very appealing. However, a new ROM needs to be constructed for every new set of crack parameters, rendering the procedure ineffective. To overcome this challenge, we used a substructuring technique to reduce the partition of the model that is not affected by the crack once for all parameters and keep the other subdomain unreduced. The combination of the proposed approximation method for
\hat{\text{Tr}}_{2}
and such a substructuring technique significantly accelerates the derivation of the HOTr.

Finally, crack identification is conducted using the proposed feature for the 2D and 3D cases using the GA algorithm. For the 2D case, we observed that for cracks of size 10% and 15% of the beam thickness, the location can be identified with high accuracy. For smaller cracks and noise levels higher than 5%, the localization accuracy drops. For the 3D case, similar trends were observed. When the localization of the cracks larger than 8% of the axle diameter showed a probability of 0.95. The identified crack parameters for the cases with smaller cracks and higher noise are still in a reasonable vicinity of the target.

An acknowledged limitation of this study is its reliance on a mesh-based model for crack geometry. Future research could extend this work by employing meshless techniques, such as the XFEM, for improved crack representation. Additionally, experimental validation of our results is crucial and should be the focus of subsequent investigations.
