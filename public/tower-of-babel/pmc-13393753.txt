Systemic lupus erythematosus presenting with slurred speech: a case report
Authors: Reza Azizi Malamiri, Negar Dinarvand, Mohammad Reza Fathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus is a chronic autoimmune disease with heterogeneous clinical manifestations. Neuropsychiatric involvement is relatively common in systemic lupus erythematosus; however, isolated dysarthria or slurred speech is an exceedingly rare presentation. Reporting such atypical manifestations may improve clinical awareness and facilitate early diagnosis of neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus.Case presentationA 14-year-old Iranian girl with no family history of autoimmune disease was referred to the rheumatology department with persistent fatigue, weakness, arthralgia, and progressive slurred speech, which significantly impaired her academic performance. Physical examination revealed malar rash, oral ulcers, alopecia, arthritis, and dysarthria. Maintenance therapy with mycophenolate mofetil was initiated, and the patient remained clinically stable during follow-up.ConclusionThis case highlights isolated dysarthria as a rare but clinically significant manifestation of neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disorder characterized by multi-organ involvement. The cause of SLE remains unknown, with various factors believed to play a role, including environmental, genetic, and hormonal influences [1]. Previous studies indicate that female sex hormones elevate the risk of developing this disease [2], making it nine times more prevalent in women than in men [3].

The presence of autoantibodies and immune complexes in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) instigates inflammation in the connective tissue, resulting in damage to numerous organs [1], including the cardiopulmonary, renal, mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, and central nervous systems [3]. SLE presents with various clinical manifestations and a wide spectrum of symptoms ranging from mild to severe [3, 4]. However, diagnosis of SLE typically requires the presence of at least four of the criteria outlined by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR). These criteria include: malar rash, discoid rash, oral ulcers, photosensitivity, arthritis, serositis, kidney disorders (such as glomerulonephritis and proteinuria), blood disorders (such as leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia), neurological symptoms (such as seizures, stroke, and psychosis), and immunological features, including anti-dsDNA antibodies, anti-cardiolipin antibodies, anti‑smith (sm), anti-beta 2 glycoprotein 1 (anti-β2GP1), and the presence of anti-nuclear antibodies [1, 5].

Neurological manifestations constitute 1 of the 11 diagnostic criteria established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [3]. Neuropsychiatric features in SLE include a wide range of central and peripheral nervous system presentations [8]; however, isolated dysarthria or slurred speech is exceptionally uncommon. Reporting such an atypical manifestation enhances clinical awareness and supports early recognition of rare forms of neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE).

Case report

The patient is a 14-year-old Iranian girl without a family history of autoimmune disease, who was referred to the rheumatology department owing to persistent fatigue, weakness, arthralgia, and a speech disorder. These symptoms not only affected her overall well-being but also hindered her academic performance. Teachers had noticed her difficulty in answering questions effectively and expressed concern about her communication skills. Upon physical examination, notable findings included a malar rash, oral ulcers, alopecia, arthritis, bilateral knee swelling, and a significant observation of “slurred speech.”

Prior to referral to the rheumatology clinic, the patient experienced progressive lower limb pain and swelling, along with pain and erythema of the right great toe, for which she was initially evaluated by an orthopedic specialist. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the foot revealed no significant abnormalities. Subsequently, she developed left ear pain accompanied by hearing impairment and was evaluated by an otolaryngologist. According to the patient’s history, a transient auditory nerve dysfunction was diagnosed, and she received treatment for 2 weeks with complete clinical resolution; however, formal documentation of this evaluation was unavailable.

Over the following weeks, the patient developed malar rash and decreased appetite and was evaluated by an internist. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed no significant findings. Owing to the development of pancytopenia, she was referred to a hematologist–oncologist and underwent bone marrow aspiration, which ruled out malignancy. She was then referred to the rheumatology clinic for further evaluation. The entire clinical course evolved over approximately 2 months.

Hematologic and biochemical test results revealed leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and decreased levels of complement proteins (C3: 37 mg/dL [normal range: 90–180], C4: 5.4 mg/dL [normal range: 12–40], CH50 complement: 60%). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 132, and anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) titer was 1/1000. In addition, anti-histone antibody levels were > 180, anti-double stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibody levels were > 150, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 1.6. Tests for lupus anticoagulant and anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I antibodies (anti-B2GPI) (Ab G, M) returned strongly positive results. Liver and kidney function tests were within normal limits.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed prominent bright signals in the centrum semiovale on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), T1GAD, and T1 axial sequences (Fig. 1), indicating potential pathology. Such signals are commonly observed in individuals with vasculitis and are well documented in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Ultrasonography of both knees demonstrated joint effusion, with a maximum effusion depth of 10 mm in the right knee. Echocardiography and bone marrow aspiration results were normal.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain in presented axial sequences showed a left-to-right progression—FLAIR, T1GAD, and T1—prominent bright signals revealed in the centrum semiovale

A detailed neurological examination was performed. Cranial nerve function was intact, and no motor, sensory, or cerebellar deficits were identified. The patient’s speech abnormality was consistent with dysarthria, characterized by impaired articulation without evidence of aphasia or language disturbance. Because the patient had no seizures, altered consciousness, fever, or meningeal signs, electroencephalography (EEG) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were not performed.

Management

Treatment was initiated after confirmation of systemic lupus erythematosus with neuropsychiatric (neuropsychiatric SLE [NPSLE]) involvement. The patient received three consecutive pulses of intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g/day), followed by monthly intravenous cyclophosphamide (500 mg) as induction therapy for 4 months. Oral prednisolone was initiated at a dose of 60 mg/day, along with hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day) and aspirin (80 mg/day), given the presence of positive antiphospholipid antibodies.

A total of 1 month after initiation of therapy, marked clinical and laboratory improvement was observed. The patient’s dysarthria improved significantly, and inflammatory markers normalized, with ESR decreasing from 132 mm/hour to 13 mm/hour. Hematologic abnormalities, including leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, resolved, and anemia showed partial improvement. The patient was hospitalized for subsequent cyclophosphamide pulses.

During treatment with high-dose corticosteroids, the patient developed oral candidiasis, which was successfully treated with nystatin oral drops and oral fluconazole. A total of 3 months after discharge, she was readmitted owing to herpes zoster infection and was treated with intravenous acyclovir, resulting in complete resolution.

After completion of four cycles of cyclophosphamide and in view of the complete resolution of dysarthria and significant improvement in systemic manifestations, the treatment strategy was modified. Given the 2019 coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic and to reduce hospital visits and infection risk, cyclophosphamide was discontinued and replaced with mycophenolate mofetil as maintenance therapy, while oral prednisolone was gradually tapered.

The patient was followed regularly on an outpatient basis. In total, 2 years after disease onset, she remained clinically stable on prednisolone (6.25 mg/day), mycophenolate mofetil (1500 mg/day), hydroxychloroquine (200 mg/day), and aspirin (80 mg/day), with no active complaints. At the most recent follow-up, 5 years after initiation of treatment, the patient had normal speech, complete resolution of cutaneous manifestations, and normal laboratory findings, including anti-dsDNA antibodies, complement levels, complete blood count, ESR, and CRP.

Discussion

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease known for its involvement across multiple systems. It is diagnosed through the presence of at least 4 of the 11 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria, revised in 1997, or the detection of specific autoantibodies (such as positive antinuclear antibodies, considered an inclusion criterion) along with ten weighted criteria from the European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) [6, 7]. In this case, the diagnosis of SLE was established on the basis of these classification criteria, considering both clinical presentation and laboratory findings.

While SLE predominantly affects women owing to hormonal disparities, symptoms such as skin rash, fatigue, and joint pain are similar in both sexes. However, various studies indicate that men with SLE may experience a more complex clinical course, with studies suggesting a higher prevalence of renal failure, central nervous system involvement, and vascular disease [7].

Neuropsychiatric involvement is relatively common in SLE, although only one-third of these symptoms are attributed directly to SLE. The mechanisms contributing to neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) are generally categorized into two major pathways: immune-mediated inflammation of neural tissue and vascular injury involving small or large cerebral vessels. Because no single laboratory test definitively establishes the diagnosis, the evaluation of NPSLE relies primarily on clinical judgment supported by complementary investigations. These assessments may include testing for relevant autoantibodies, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, electrophysiological studies, neuropsychological evaluation, and neuroimaging techniques, all of which help clarify the nature and extent of neurological involvement [8].

In our patient, the presence of antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) along with other typical manifestations of SLE, including hematologic abnormalities, arthritis, malar rash, oral ulcers, and alopecia, aided in confirming the diagnosis. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement is relatively common in SLE, and in this instance, brain MRI revealed distinct lesions attributable to immune reactions against neural tissue. Interestingly, “slurred speech” was the sole sign of nervous system involvement, an uncommon manifestation of SLE.

While other studies have reported cases of slurred speech in SLE, they often presented alongside extensive neurological symptoms. For instance, M. Sankar Raj V. reported a case of a 10-year-old girl with slurred speech, accompanied by motor difficulties [9]. Treatment with methylprednisolone led to improvement, as seen in similar cases reported by Chen W.S. et al. [10]. Cao et al. also documented a case of a 51-year-old woman with SLE and slurred speech, among other symptoms, whose condition improved with corticosteroids and gamma globulin yet ultimately succumbed [5].

Although neurological symptoms are included in the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria for SLE diagnosis, the specific inclusion of “slurred speech” as a distinguishing feature is novel. The scarcity of documented cases underscores the importance of reporting such atypical manifestations to enhance understanding and diagnosis of the diverse neurological symptoms associated with SLE.

Treatment management for SLE typically involves glucocorticoids, aspirin, hydroxychloroquine, and mycophenolate mofetil or cyclophosphamide pulse therapy, with some studies advocating for the use of antimalarial drugs like hydroxychloroquine to reduce morbidity and mortality rates [7]. Cyclophosphamide was selected as the induction therapy because it is recommended as a first-line agent for severe neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) according to the EULAR treatment guidelines. Alternative therapies such as rituximab or belimumab were considered as second-line options for nonresponders. Plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are generally reserved for refractory cases or for patients with thrombotic microangiopathy [11]. In our case, neither of which were present and the patient responded significantly to this treatment regimen, with non-recurrence and normalization of examination results after 2 years indicating the efficacy and durability of the chosen approach.

Compared with previous reports describing slurred speech in patients with SLE, most published cases included additional severe neurological deficits such as motor weakness, dysphagia, gait disturbance, or cognitive impairment. In contrast, our patient presented with isolated dysarthria without other neurological abnormalities, making this presentation particularly uncommon. This distinction underscores the importance of considering NPSLE even when speech impairment occurs as the only neurological feature.

Ultimately, this case report aims to raise awareness among healthcare professionals, facilitating timely diagnosis and treatment to mitigate complications and improve patients’ quality of life.

Conclusion

In this case report, the diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), along with the rare presentation of slurred speech as a manifestation of neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE), was supported by the patient’s characteristic clinical findings and immunological test results. This atypical neurological symptom should be considered among the possible differential diagnoses when evaluating patients with suspected NPSLE.
