Iron-rich food consumption and predictors among children aged 6–59 months old in Ethiopia using 2005–2016 national representative data
Authors: Girma Beressa, Kenenisa Beressa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Children who do not consume enough iron have decreased growth, reduced immunity, and poor cognitive development. This study aimed to assess the pooled iron-rich food consumption, trends, and predictors among children aged 6–59 months old in Ethiopia. This study used Ethiopian demographic and health survey (EDHS-2005–2016) data with a total weighted sample size of 26,773 among children aged 6–59 months old. A multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression analysis was used to identify predictors of good iron-rich food consumption. The pooled proportion of iron-rich food consumed among children aged 6–59 months in Ethiopia was 11.77% (10.71, 12.92%). In Ethiopia, the proportion of iron-rich food intake by children aged 6–59 months was slightly increased from 9.25% (8.04, 10.62%) in EDHS 2005 to 10.86% (9.06, 12.95%) in EDHS 2011, and sharply increased to 17.49% (15.04, 20.24%) in EDHS 2016. The findings indicated that being children born to mothers aged 20 to 34 years old [AOR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.35, 0.89], mothers aged ≥ 35 years old [AOR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.82], married parents [AOR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.14, 2.48], completed primary education [AOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.73], secondary education [AOR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.53, 4.59], a higher education status of mother [AOR = 3.98, 95% CI: 1.92, 8.23], medium family [AOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.89], rich family [AOR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.95], media exposure [AOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.72], residents of the Tigray [AOR = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.51, 4.79], Oromia [AOR = 3.30, 95% CI: 1.30, 4.02], Benishangul [AOR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.15, 3.88], Gambela [AOR = 5.85, 95% CI: 2.97, 11.50], and Dire Dawa region [AOR = 3.38, 95% CI: 1.76, 6.47], were significantly associated with good iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old. The findings revealed that iron-rich food intake was considerably low among Ethiopian children aged 6–59 months. Increasing women’s literacy and economic empowerment improves iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated a 42.6% global incidence of anemia among children, making it the most common micronutrient shortfall, impacting more than 2 billion people1. Iron insufficiency is mostly caused by low intake, restricted absorption, and infection2. Anemia in children under the age of five has several consequences, including impaired mental function, low resistance to infection, and mortality from anemic heart failure3.

The study indicated that preschool children with inadequate iron consumption had slower growth, less immunity, and poorer cognitive development4. Studies revealed that the proportion of iron-rich food consumption in early childhood varies from 21.41% in low- and middle income countries (LMICs) to 90% in high-income countries (HICs)5,6. The most feasible and realistic strategy of lowering iron deficiency in under-5 children in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is to promote the consumption of iron-rich foods7–9.

Environmental enteric dysfunction (EED) can cause systemic inflammation and nutritional malabsorption, both of which contribute significantly to iron deficiency anemia in children. Higher levels of EED biomarkers are associated with decreased hemoglobin concentrations, indicating that EED exacerbates iron shortages caused by gastrointestinal malabsorption10,11. Interventions to improve sanitation and hygiene, such as limiting exposure to feces and increasing water quality, are critical for preventing EED and, subsequently, anemia in children10. Improved water quality is required to lessen the burden of enteric infections and enhance overall health outcomes for children11,12. Inadequate healthcare services hinder the capacity to detect and treat underlying diseases like nutritional deficits. Comprehensive healthcare, including nutritional assistance and infection control, is critical for treating anemia in young children. Programmes that combine healthcare with sanitation and nutrition measures are likely to be more successful10,12.

The public health strategy supports initiatives to minimize child malnutrition in general, and child anemia in particular, by encouraging dietary diversification, deworming, iron supplementation during pregnancy, and malaria control13. Ethiopia’s Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) revealed that the prevalence of anemia among children under five years old varied from 44% in 2011 to 57% in 2016 2. The proportion of children who were given iron-rich foods in Ethiopia is 27.99% 14.

Individual-level characteristics such as low ANC visits, institution delivery, child age, gender, and birth order, as well as community-level variables such as regions and community women’s education, all revealed a strong association with iron consumption among Ethiopian children aged 6–59 months5,14.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends daily iron intake as a recommended method for the treatment and prevention of iron deficiency anemia (IDA)15,16. Egg intake was found to be the highest; whereas the least consumed foods were fish and shellfish5. Even though animal-source foods are high in protein, fat, and minerals, their high cost limits their consumption in Ethiopia. Because of this, low-income countries, including Ethiopia, lack the physical access and economic capacity to acquire fortified animal products. Micronutrient deficiency, such as anemia, can develop from a lack of animal-based meals17.

Identifying significant individual and community-level predictors of iron-rich food intake is crucial to improving iron-rich consumption in Ethiopia. This study’s findings can foster initiatives and policies that align with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Moreover, these findings contribute to efforts to improve iron status and enhance nutritional and health outcomes for children under the age of five18–20. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the pooled iron-rich food consumption, trends, and predictors among children aged 6–59 months old in Ethiopia using Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS-2005–2016) data.

Methods

Study design, data source, and subjects

The data used for this study were accessed from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS-2005–2016) data, which was employed using a community-based cross-sectional study. The standard EDHS data set has a large sample size, which helps to generate parameters21. The source population was all children aged 6–59 months, whereas the study population was all chosen or sampled living children aged 6–59 months who lived with their mother in the selected areas. The EDHS used a two-stage sampling approach to gather data from nine regional states and two city administrations.

In the first phase, 305 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected with a probability proportionate to their size based on the 2005, 2011, and 2016 primary health care (PHC) frames and independent selection in each sample strata. The second phase involves systematic random sampling of dwellings in each cluster, or EA, followed by interviews with chosen mother-child pairs.

Data collection instruments

Child age, child sex, marital status, religion, maternal education, wealth index, birth order, and antenatal care (ANC) were individual-level factors, whereas residence and region were community-level factors2. Wealth index was computed using principal component analysis (PCA). The wealth index was determined using the principal components analysis based on the number and types of consumer goods they own, ranging from a television to a bicycle or car, as well as housing characteristics such as source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials. Details are described elsewhere2,22–24. Media exposure was assessed using three variables: frequency of viewing TV, reading newspapers, and listening to radio. Individuals were coded as yes if they were exposed to all or any of the three, and no if they were not exposed to at least one of them21,25.

Outcome assessment

If the children aged 6–59 months residing with their mother consumed at least one iron-rich food at any time in the 24 h before the interview, among four food items, eggs, organ meat (liver, heart, or other organs), meat (beef, pork, lamb, or chicken), and fish were considered good consumption, otherwise poor consumption5,22.

Data processing, model building, and analysis

Stata™ 14 was used to conduct the data analysis. Descriptive statistics, such as frequency and percentage, were employed to describe study participants. The proportions and frequencies were weighted. All analyses used the individual sample weight (v005/1,000,000) to account for over- and under-sampling. The EDHS dataset is hierarchical, with children nested in households and households within clusters. A correlation matrix was used to assess multicollinearity among predictors. Variables with a bivariable multilevel mixed effect logistic regression coefficient less than 0.25 were included in the multivariable multilevel mixed effect logistic regression analyses to control for potential confounding effects. Nevertheless, known predictors such as region were included in the final model regardless of the p-value assumption. Four models were used: the null model (no predictors), model 1: individual-level variables, model 2: community-level factors, and model 3: individual and community-level factors. Multilevel mixed effects logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between individual and community-level characteristics and iron-rich food consumption (yes = 1, no = 0). The data were analysed using a complex sample survey multilevel mixed effects logistic regression approach (melogit [pweight = swt] || v001:). The Stata command “svyset” was used to set survey data and estimate the proportion of iron-rich food consumption. The strength of the relationship between predictors and interest variables was estimated using both the crude odds ratio (COR) and adjusted odds ratio (AOR), as well as a 95% confidence interval. The model was assessed for goodness-of-fit using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and log-likelihood ratio (LLR). The model with the lowest AIC and BIC and highest LLR was deemed the best fit.

The Median Odds Ratio (MOR), defined as the median value of the odds ratio between the areas at the lowest and highest risk when two clusters are randomly chosen, was used to quantify variation. MOR = e0.95√VA or exp. [√ (2 × VA) × 0.6745], where VA represents the area-level variation. The proportional change in variance (PCV) measures the variance in iron-rich intake among children aged 6–59 months, which is explained by several variables. The PCV is calculated as Vnull-VA/Vnull* 100. Where Vnull is the initial model’s variance and VA is the model’s variance with added terms. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) measures the variation in iron-rich intake between clusters. It is computed as ICC = VA ÷ VA + 3.29 ∗ 100%, where VA = area/cluster level variance27. A p value less than 0.05 was declared statistically significant.

Ethical approval

Ethical permission was received from Measure DHS using a data access request form. A written informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). The EDHS data is available to the general public in various formats upon request from the Measure DHS website (www.measuredhs.com). All approaches followed the relevant tenets of the Helsinki Declaration28.

Results

Sociodemographic and economic factors

This study employed a weighted sample of 26,773 mother-child pairs from the EDHS 2005–2016 datasets (EDHS 2005 = 9,144, EDHS 2011 = 10,247, and 2016 = 7,384). Most 2,600 (66.94%), 6,316 (84.44%), and 5,667 (66.03%) of children were in the categories of 24–59 month ages in the EDHS in 2005, 2011, and 2016, respectively. In the 2005, 2011, and 2016 EDHS surveys, an overwhelming majority of the respondents, 8,451 (91.93%), 9,061 (87.14%), and 8,836 (92.78%), respectively, were married. In EDHS 2005, 2011, and 2016, most of children’s mothers, 7,093 (77.19%), 7,037 (70.27%), and 6,182 (64.91%), were uneducated, respectively. Nearly half 4,069 (44.28%), 5,097 (48.98%), and 5,178 (54.27%) of the study subjects were in the poor wealth quintile in the EDHS in 2005, 2011, and 2016, respectively. Most 1,789 (19.56%), 1,555 (14.95%), and 1,402 (14.72%) respondents were residents in the Oromia region, Ethiopia, in the EDHS 2005, 2011, and 2016, respectively (Table 1).

Proportion of iron-rich food consumption

The pooled proportion of iron-rich food consumed among children aged 6–59 months in Ethiopia was 11.77% (10.71, 12.92%). In Ethiopia, the proportion of iron-rich food intake by children aged 6–59 months was slightly increased from 9.25% (8.04, 10.62%) in EDHS 2005 to 10.86% (9.06, 12.95%) in EDHS 2011, and sharply increased to 17.49% (15.04, 20.24%) in EDHS 2016.

Individual and community-level predictors of iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old

The ICC for the null model was 20.02%. This indicated that there was variation in the iron-rich food intake of children aged 6–59 months among clusters. The full model had the lowest AIC and highest LLR; hence, it was chosen as the best-fit model. The multivariable multilevel mixed effect logistic regression analyses indicated that being children born to married parents [AOR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.14, 2.48], completed primary education [AOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.73], secondary education [AOR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.53, 4.59], a higher education [AOR = 3.98, 95% CI: 1.92, 8.23], medium family [AOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.89], rich family [AOR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.95], media exposure [AOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.72], residents of the Tigray [AOR = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.51, 4.79], Oromia [AOR = 3.30, 95% CI: 1.30, 4.02], Benishangul Gumuz [AOR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.15, 3.88], Gambela [AOR = 5.85, 95% CI: 2.97, 11.50], Dire Dawa region [AOR = 3.38, 95% CI: 1.76, 6.47], and EDHS 2016 [AOR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.51, 3.30], were significantly positively associated with good iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old. Nevertheless, children born to mothers aged 20 to 34 years [AOR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.35, 0.89] and mothers aged ≥ 35 years old [AOR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.82] were significantly negatively associated with good iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the pooled iron-rich food consumption, trends, and predictors among 6–59-month-old children in Ethiopia. The findings clearly pointed out that 11.77% of children aged 6–59 months consumed iron-rich foods. Being children born to mothers aged 20 to 34 years old, mothers aged ≥ 35 years old, married parents, children born to mothers who completed a primary education, secondary education, higher education, being born to the medium family, rich family, media exposure, residents of the Tigray, Oromia, Benishangul Gumuz, Gambela, and Dire Dawa regions were significantly associated with iron-rich food consumption among children aged 6–59 months old.

The pooled proportion of good consumption of iron-rich foods among children aged 6–59 months was 11.77%. This finding was higher than a study conducted in Ethiopia14. Nevertheless, this finding was lower than studies conducted in Ethiopia5,26,29, Rwanda30, Afghanistan31, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)7, and Sierra Leone32. This could be attributed to differences in media outlet exposure and socioeconomic status.

Children born to mothers who married were 1.68 times more likely to consume iron-rich foods. This agrees with studies carried out in Ethiopia14 and Rwanda30. Children born to mothers who finished a higher education level were 3.98 times more likely to consume iron-rich meals than children born to mothers who did not have an education. This study corresponded with research carried out in Ethiopia5,26,29, Rwanda30, Afghanistan31, and India33. This might be because educated women are exposed to a variety of media channels and realize the importance of diet for health. Moreover, educated women enhance their health-seeking behaviors and obtain counselling services. Other relevant studies indicate that children born to illiterate women are more likely to develop anemia and consume fewer iron-rich foods than children born to educated mothers, and vice versa5,30,34.

Children born to mothers in the rich were 2.45 times more likely to consume iron-rich meals compared to children born to poor mothers. This agreed with research undertaken in Ethiopia5,26, Rwanda30, and SSA7. The possible explanation could be that children born to mothers from wealthy families have access to and can afford sources of iron-rich food.

Children born to mothers who had media exposure were 1.35 times more likely to consume iron-rich food compared to their counterparts. This finding agreed with studies conducted in SSA7 and Rwanda30. Children born to mothers who resided in the Tigray, Oromia, Benishangul, Gambela, and Dire Dawa regional states of Ethiopia were more likely to consume good iron-rich foods compared to children born to mothers who resided in Addis Ababa city. This might be due to differences in sample size, as the current study used a larger sample size.

Policy implications

The findings of this study have important implications for food and nutrition policy. Despite the advantages of iron-rich foods, limited consumption suggests a policy gap in Ethiopian maternal and child nutritional status. The findings have implications for enhancing food and nutrition policy (FNP), national nutrition strategy (NNS), and national nutrition program (NNP) to increase mothers’ educational level and economic empowerment in order to promote iron-rich food intake in children aged 6–59 months.

Public health initiatives should emphasize teaching parents, particularly mothers, on the benefits of iron-rich meals for their children’s development35. Strategies for increasing the availability and cost of iron-rich foods are critical35,36. Interventions should take into account cultural norms and perceptions about food intake. Involving local communities in the creation of these initiatives may assist guarantee that they are culturally relevant and acceptable. It is vital to establish strong monitoring and evaluation systems for assessing the effectiveness of initiatives. This can assist determine which tactics are effective and where changes are required to increase iron-rich food intake33.

To address the knowledge gaps regarding iron-rich food intake among children aged 6–59 months in Ethiopia, future research directions should focus on several critical areas: Research understanding the challenges that lower-income families have in gaining access to certain meals might lead to tailored interventions26,35. Given the beneficial relationship between maternal education and children’s consumption of iron-rich foods, future research should assess the efficacy of educational programs designed to improve mothers’ nutritional awareness. This might include community-based interventions that encourage knowledge of the need for iron in children’s diets, as well as practical ways for including iron-rich foods into meals26,36.

Research should look at how cultural beliefs and behaviours influence the intake of animal-source meals high in iron. For example, in certain areas, animal-based meals are seen as luxury products rather than essential nutritional components. Addressing these cultural views through tailored nutrition education may increase food intake among young children36. A future study should concentrate on identifying distinct geographical obstacles and facilitators of iron-rich dietary consumption. This might include localized studies that analyze the availability, affordability, and consumption patterns of iron-rich foods in various locations, particularly in areas identified as having low consumption rates, such as Afar and Somali regions35.

Research should also look at the influence of current nutrition policies and programs on young children’s consumption of iron-rich foods. This involves evaluating the success of government measures to improve food security and nutrition. Evidence-based research should educate policymakers in developing comprehensive policies to improve access to iron-rich foods, particularly in disadvantaged groups14,37. Longitudinal studies might yield useful information about the long-term impact of iron-rich food intake on children’s health outcomes, such as growth, cognitive development, and general well-being. Such research would aid in the identification of causal associations and the development of future dietary recommendations for Ethiopian young children. By addressing these issues, future research can help to get a better knowledge of the variables that influence iron-rich food consumption in Ethiopian children, eventually guiding effective interventions to prevent iron deficiency and enhance child health outcomes26.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Because this dataset is a pooled weighted larger sample nationwide, it might be representative of the country. However, the data were gathered through self-reports, which might contribute to recall and social desirability bias. Variables such as the mother’s household empowerment, health education, child’s birthplace, and maternal health conditions were not assessed. Moreover, there was a lack of literature on the study. Furthermore, since the study is cross-sectional, a cause-and-effect relationship might not be established.

Conclusion

The finding revealed that there was a low consumption of iron-rich foods among children aged 6–59 months in Ethiopia. Ethiopia should implement strategies to increase iron-rich food consumption throughout these critical periods of growth and development. This study’s findings would have implications for decision-makers in Ethiopia. Further studies are warranted to obtain robust results.
