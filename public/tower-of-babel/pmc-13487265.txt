Constraint or catalyst? Unraveling the nonlinear relationship between industrial linkages and CO2 emissions
Authors: Zhilong Chen, Mingsheng Yuan, Shiyi Chen, Dabin Weng, Qianwen Li, Chuanwang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIndustrial linkages shape production efficiency, resource allocation, and technology diffusion, yet their environmental consequences remain underexplored. This paper investigates the relationship between industrial linkages and sectoral CO2 emissions. We construct weighted forward and backward linkage indicators based on input-output tables, covering 24 harmonized sectors across 30 Chinese provinces. Empirical findings reveal an inverted-U relationship between industrial linkages and CO2 emissions: stronger linkages are initially associated with higher emissions, but beyond a threshold they are linked to lower emissions. Mechanism analysis identifies three impact channels: output scale effect, technological innovation effect, and energy intensity effect. Furthermore, we construct a measure of regional industrial linkage structures to help identify key areas for emissions reduction. The results highlight regions such as Anhui, Henan, Liaoning, and Shaanxi as priority zones for targeted emissions mitigation. Our study provides empirical insights and policy implications for understanding the role of industrial linkages in reducing carbon emissions.Keywords: industrial linkages, CO2 emissions, input-output analysis

Introduction

The UN Environment Programme emphasizes the critical need for all countries to expedite comprehensive green transitions to realize the goals of the Paris Agreement. Mitigating carbon emissions while sustaining economic growth has become a central challenge.1,2 Adjusting industrial structure is a critical component in achieving green transformation and lowering carbon emissions.3,4 However, industrial structure adjustment may also impose short-term economic costs on certain industries. In particular, the phase-out of carbon-intensive capacity, tighter environmental regulations, and the reallocation of capital and labor toward cleaner sectors may weaken output growth, profitability, or employment in traditional industries during the transition process.5 As a result, green transformation often involves balancing environmental gains with transitional economic pressures across sectors. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to how industrial linkages influence carbon emissions from a holistic perspective,6 as production linkages across industries can shape both the environmental effectiveness and economic costs of structural change. Unlike industrial structure, industrial linkages capture inter-sector dependence and production-chain transmission, directly affecting how shocks, technologies, and emissions propagate across the economy. Understanding the relationship between industrial linkages and carbon emissions can help policymakers better coordinate industrial restructuring with green and low-carbon transition goals.

Industries are connected through supply chains in which upstream sectors provide intermediate inputs to downstream sectors, while downstream demand feeds back to upstream production.7,8 These industrial linkages shape resource allocation, production efficiency, and structural transformation, and may therefore have important implications for energy-related CO2 emissions. However, stronger linkages may generate conflicting environmental effects: they can expand production scale and energy demand, but they may also facilitate technology diffusion and efficiency improvement. Understanding this relationship is particularly important in China, where deep industrial networks coexist with ambitious carbon reduction targets and substantial regional heterogeneity. Examining China’s experience may also provide useful insights for other emerging economies undergoing rapid industrialization. Taking China as a case study, we examine whether industrial linkages exert nonlinear effects on sectoral carbon emissions.

Our study explores the nonlinear nexus between industrial linkages and sectoral CO2 emissions. The study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, we advance the theoretical understanding of the environmental consequences of production networks by arguing that industrial linkages generate competing effects on emissions. We test and document a nonlinear relationship between industrial linkages and sectoral energy-related CO2 emissions across Chinese provinces and industries.

Second, we deepen the mechanism-based explanation of how industrial linkages shape carbon emissions. We explore the potential mechanisms from output scale effect, technological innovation effect, and energy intensity effect, which can better explain the dynamic nexus between industrial linkages and sectoral energy-related CO2 emissions. This mechanism framework helps reconcile why industrial linkages may initially intensify CO2 emissions but later support carbon mitigation as production systems upgrade.

Third, we extend the analysis to regional structural implications. We construct interpretable indicators of regional industrial linkage composition and use them to identify priority areas where weak production connectivity coincides with high emissions. Rather than proposing a purely technical index, this framework translates sector-level evidence into place-based decarbonization guidance and offers practical support for coordinating industrial upgrading with emissions reduction.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. The literature review section summarizes related studies, while the theoretical analysis section develops the conceptual framework and research hypotheses. The results section describes the data, empirical strategy, and main findings. Finally, the discussion section interprets the results, highlights their implications, and concludes the study.

Literature review

Existing studies primarily explore the relationship between production networks and carbon emissions, with most research concentrating on embodied emissions and their determinants within input-output (I-O) frameworks. Industrial linkages refer to collaborative interactions among firms across different stages of production chains, which aim to enhance competitiveness and resilience. These linkages are closely associated with structural transformation and industrial upgrading, and are fundamentally driven by imbalances in industrial structure, providing a theoretical foundation for analyzing inter-sectoral I-O relationships.

One strand of the literature estimates embodied carbon emissions across countries and sectors, including studies on China, India, Japan, and the United States.9,10,11,12 These studies generally show that sectors occupying central positions in production networks tend to have higher embodied emissions.13 A second strand of literature examines the drivers of sectoral carbon emissions. Final demand expansion significantly increases emissions through production-chain transmission.14 Natural resource endowments, economic development levels, and structural factors are also important determinants of sectoral emissions.14,15 A third strand of literature emphasizes that industrial linkages not only reflect intermediate input flows but also capture the transmission of labor, capital, and technology across sectors, thereby forming complex production networks. Within such networks, energy-related CO2 emissions propagate through supply-chain interactions, making it essential to examine the underlying transmission mechanisms. Empirical studies based on I-O analysis, STIRPAT, SFA, and CGE models have shown that inter-industry connections significantly shape both the scale and structure of emissions.16,17 For instance, energy-intensive upstream industries such as metals, electricity, and fossil fuel extraction are closely linked to downstream production activities and exert strong effects on emissions through supply-chain propagation.18 In addition, carbon-intensive material industries (e.g., steel and cement) constitute important sources of embodied emissions in downstream sectors.19 Transport and logistics sectors further amplify emissions through energy-intensive freight activities.20 Conversely, service sectors such as wholesale, retail, and finance may contribute to emission reduction by improving allocation efficiency and facilitating structural upgrading.21

Despite the valuable contributions of prior literature, two limitations remain. First, most existing studies treat industrial linkages primarily as an accounting channel for measuring embodied emissions,11,12 rather than as an explanatory structural factor that may independently shape sectoral environmental performance. Second, limited attention has been paid to whether the effects of industrial linkages are nonlinear. Addressing these gaps is particularly important for emerging economies such as China, where energy-related emissions account for the majority of total CO2 emissions and industrial networks remain central to economic activity. Understanding how industrial linkages influence sectoral CO2 emissions can therefore provide useful guidance for industrial upgrading and low-carbon transition policies.

Theoretical analysis

Industrial linkages reflect the intensity of inter-industry connections within production networks, where upstream sectors provide intermediate inputs to downstream sectors and downstream demand feeds back into upstream production.7,8 By shaping the organization of production, resource allocation, and inter-sectoral coordination, industrial linkages can exert important but potentially conflicting effects on sectoral CO2 emissions. Theoretically, the environmental impact of industrial linkages can be understood as the result of two competing forces: a scale expansion effect and a reallocation and efficiency effect. The interaction among these effects suggests that the environmental impact of industrial linkages may be inherently nonlinear.

First, industrial linkages tend to expand production scale through intensified I-O connections. Industrial restructuring and spatial reallocation may increase emissions in the short run due to the construction of new facilities and capital investment, which are typically energy-intensive processes.21,22 As industries become more interconnected, intermediate demand increases and production chains lengthen, which amplifies overall economic activity.23 This expansion typically leads to higher energy consumption, particularly in the early stages of development when production remains relatively energy-intensive and relies more heavily on fossil fuels. As a result, the scale expansion effect implies a positive relationship between industrial linkages and CO2 emissions. There is also considerable evidence that industry linkages generate positive output scale effects.24 From a micro-level perspective, the “cost correlation effect” incentivizes firms to strengthen their linkages with others, aiming to lower operational costs from suppliers.25 Meanwhile, firms can ensure a reliable consumer market through backward linkages.24 There exists a strong correlation between industry linkages and the output scale,26 indicating that stronger linkages may facilitate a larger output scale under similar conditions.

Second, industrial linkages may also generate a reallocation and efficiency effect that mitigates emissions. Stronger inter-industry connections facilitate specialization, improve the division of labor, and enhance coordination across sectors.27,28 These processes can promote the diffusion of cleaner technologies,29 optimize resource allocation,30 and reduce redundant production through cross-industry learning effects.31 Conflicts within industrial sectors are gradually mitigated as their coordination capabilities improve, while factors such as technological advancements and policy frameworks become increasingly mature and stable.32 Meanwhile, strengthened linkages are conducive to reducing energy intensity in this stage.33,34 Better integration of supply chains due to industry linkages can promote cross-industry collaboration to reduce energy intensity.35,36

Recent studies also suggest that the relationship between industrial linkages and carbon emissions may be nonlinear and heterogeneous across sectors and regions.37,38 In particular, the environmental effects of industrial linkages may vary with the level of industrial integration, technological innovation, and energy intensity, potentially exhibiting threshold or inverted U-shaped patterns.39,40 Therefore, the relationship between industrial linkages and sectoral CO2 emissions is expected to exhibit an inverted U-shaped pattern. According to the analyses above, we propose the following hypotheses.

H1: there exists an inverted U-shaped nexus between industry linkages and sectoral energy-related CO2 emissions.

H2: sectoral CO2 emissions can be affected by industry linkages through output scale effect, technological innovation effect, and energy intensity effect

Results

Variable selection

Explained variable: sectoral energy-related carbon emissions

This study estimates energy-related carbon emissions for two-digit industrial sectors across 30 provinces over time. The calculation is based on sector-level consumption data for 19 energy types (raw coal, cleaned coal, other washed coal, briquettes, coke, coke oven gas, other gas, other coking products, crude oil, gasoline, kerosene, diesel oil, fuel oil, LPG, refinery gas, other petroleum products, natural gas, heat, electricity) combined with the standard emission factors provided by the IPCC guidelines. The energy consumption data are sourced from provincial statistical yearbooks in China, which provide officially reported energy use at the aggregated industrial sector level.

Explanatory variables: industrial linkages

Following Sun et al. (2020),13 we construct industrial weighted forward linkages (WFLs) and weighted backward linkages (WBLs) using the absolute weighted method (AWM) based on Leontief and Ghosh I-O frameworks. These weighted indicators account for sectoral scale differences and capture the structural position of industries within production networks. Detailed calculations are provided in the Method Details section.

Data

The I-O tables are derived from Provincial Bureaus of Statistics (PBSs) of China; the raw data of sectoral energy consumption are derived from the CEADs Database; and the raw data of other variables are from PBS and the CEIC Database. All variables used in this research are shrunk by 1% to minimize the influence of extreme samples on the baseline empirical results. Due to the publication years of regional I-O tables by PBS, the sample period in this paper includes 1997, 2002, 2007, 2012, and 2017. Meanwhile, the regions of Tibet, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan are not considered due to the unavailability of data.

The sectoral classification of I-O tables is based on China’s national economic industrial classification system, in which two-digit sectors represent major industry categories in the national accounts. Due to revisions in the national industrial classification standards over time, the naming conventions and classification structures of two-digit sectors vary across I-O tables in different years. To ensure consistency and comparability across I-O tables from 1997 to 2017, we construct a harmonized sector classification by aggregating and aligning sector definitions across different years. The harmonization procedure follows three principles.

(1) Industries with identical economic meaning but slightly different naming conventions across I-O tables are treated as the same sector. This ensures consistency in sector identification over time despite changes in nomenclature. (2) When the level of disaggregation varies across different I-O tables (for example, general equipment manufacturing and special equipment manufacturing are reported separately in some years but combined in others), we adopt a “higher-level consistency” rule. Specifically, we aggregate such sectors into a unified category (e.g., “General and special equipment manufacturing”) to maintain comparability across time. (3) We retain only industrial sectors in the empirical analysis. Since the dependent variable—carbon emissions—is constructed based on sectoral energy consumption data, which are only available for industrial sectors from the province-level PBS.

This harmonization procedure allows us to construct a consistent panel dataset of industrial sectors across different I-O tables while preserving the comparability of sectoral economic structure and environmental outcomes over time. The harmonized sectors are shown in Table S2 in the SI Appendix. The detailed mapping between original sectors in the 1997–2017 I-O tables and the corresponding harmonized sectors is reported in Tables S3 and S4 in SI Appendix to ensure transparency and replicability.

Robustness check

Endogeneity issue

Potential endogeneity may arise from reverse causality and omitted time-varying factors. On the one hand, changes in sectoral carbon emissions may induce adjustments in production organization, thereby affecting industrial linkages. On the other hand, unobserved factors such as industrial policies, technological progress, and environmental regulation may simultaneously influence both industrial linkages and carbon emissions. To address potential endogeneity concerns, we construct the Bartik instrumental variable (IV) for industrial linkages, referring to Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020).43 The intuition is that initial industry linkage levels capture pre-existing structural differences, while aggregate growth reflects broader changes in the industrial linkage environment over time. Extending the sample using interpolation

The sample period of this study spans from 1997 to 2017. Given that official I-O tables are only available for selected benchmark years (2002, 2007, 2012, and 2017), we address this limitation by constructing interpolated I-O data for the missing years. Specifically, we apply a linear interpolation method based on the benchmark I-O tables for 2002, 2007, and 2012 to generate annual province-level I-O information over the period 1997–2017. This approach is commonly used in the literature for low-frequency I-O data, as it allows for a more continuous representation of structural changes over time while preserving the underlying cross-sectional variation.44,45,46 Based on the interpolated dataset, we re-estimate the baseline model. If industry linkages can increase sectoral carbon emissions through output scale effects, then the larger the industry scale, the stronger the effect of industry linkages on sectoral carbon emissions. Mechanism test

These results confirm the inference proposed above, and support the hypothesis that industry linkages may promote the industry output scale, subsequently leading to an increase in their energy-related carbon emissions levels. Industry linkages can not only provide manufacturers with a stable source of intermediate input through forward linkage, but also ensure a reliable consumer market through backward linkages.24 For the sectors, the increase in industry linkages has a positive output effect, thereby increasing energy-related carbon emissions at the production end.

Technological innovation effect

If industry linkages reduce sectoral energy-related carbon emissions through technological innovation effects, then we can infer that greater technological innovation reduces the promotion effect of industry linkages on sectoral carbon emissions. The regression coefficient of lnWFL × Tech in column (3) is negative, and that of lnWBL × Tech in column (4) is negative at the 10% level. The results suggest that technological innovation strengthens the energy-related carbon emissions reduction effect of industrial forward linkages and industrial backward linkages. The results can indirectly infer that forward linkages may affect sectoral energy-related carbon emissions through technological innovation.

Industries with high industrial linkages have closer ties with other industries, making the technological spillover effect between industries more obvious, thus conducive to carbon emission reduction. However, the upstream industries are mainly large-scale monopoly resource-based industries and have weak spillover effects on knowledge and technology. Thus, the technological promotion effect of industrial backward linkages may be less than that of industrial forward linkages.

Energy intensity effect

Similarly, if industry linkages reduce sectoral energy-related carbon emissions by lowering energy intensity, then sectors with lower energy intensity will experience a more pronounced carbon reduction effect from industry linkages.

Columns (5) and (6) in Table 5 display the empirical findings related to the energy intensity effect. The regression estimated coefficient of lnWFL × EI is positive, passing the 1% level of significance test, while that of lnWBL × EI cannot pass the 10% significance level test. These outcomes indicate that for industries with lower energy intensity, the effect of forward industry linkages on carbon emissions is more negative. Thus, it can be inferred that forward industry linkages can affect sectoral energy-related carbon emissions through reducing energy intensity, but backward industry linkages may not.

Further analysis

The baseline results indicate that industrial linkages may exert heterogeneous environmental effects across stages of structural development. While stronger industrial linkages can initially raise emissions through scale expansion, they may also facilitate technological diffusion and efficiency improvement over time. To translate these sector-level findings into regional implications, this section examines whether the composition of regional industrial linkages is associated with carbon emissions and identifies areas with greater structural mitigation potential.

The impact of regional industry linkages structure on carbon emissions

Building on the above findings, we develop a practical screening framework to identify regions with greater structural mitigation potential. A province is classified as a priority area for carbon mitigation when it simultaneously exhibits: (1) above-median sectoral carbon emissions in a given year; and (2) above-median share of weak-linkage sectors (Low) in the same year.

This dual criterion captures two dimensions simultaneously: current emissions pressure and structural upgrading potential. Regions with high emissions but relatively strong industrial connectivity may require technology-oriented decarbonization strategies, whereas regions with both high emissions and weak production linkages may benefit more from supply-chain integration, industrial coordination, and structural upgrading policies.

Based on this framework, provinces such as Anhui, Henan, Liaoning, and Shaanxi are repeatedly identified as priority regions during the sample period (see Table 7). Most of these provinces are generally characterized by relatively carbon-intensive industrial structures, indicating a higher dependence on energy-intensive production activities. At the same time, they tend to exhibit weaker internal production connectivity compared with more developed regions, reflecting less efficient inter-industry coordination and limited spillover effects within their industrial systems.

Discussion

This study constructs measures of forward and backward industrial linkages for 24 merged two-digit sectors across 30 provinces in China over the period 1997–2017, and examines their relationship with sectoral energy-related CO2 emissions. The empirical findings reveal a robust nonlinear relationship between industrial linkages and emissions, characterized by an inverted U-shaped pattern. This nonlinear relationship reflects the coexistence of competing effects associated with industrial linkages. Further analysis suggests that this relationship operates through three key channels: output scale, technological innovation, and energy intensity. While the scale effect initially dominates, leading to higher emissions, the technological and efficiency effects gradually offset and eventually outweigh it. This mechanism highlights the importance of considering both expansion and efficiency dimensions when evaluating the environmental consequences of industrial linkages.

In addition, the results indicate substantial regional heterogeneity. Regions with relatively weaker industrial linkage structures tend to exhibit higher emission levels, suggesting that the organization of production networks plays an important role in shaping environmental outcomes. Identifying such regions helps to better target emission reduction efforts and improve the effectiveness of place-based policies.

These findings contribute to the existing literature by emphasizing the role of industrial linkages as a structural determinant of emissions,9,10,11,12 rather than merely an accounting framework. They also complement prior studies on production networks and environmental performance by uncovering a nonlinear relationship and its underlying mechanisms.

Based on the findings of this paper, three policy implications are proposed:

First, dynamically adjust energy-related carbon emissions reduction and industrial policies. The findings of our study reveal that carbon emissions reduction strategies should be dynamically adjusted according to different stages of industry linkages. The initial focus should be on promoting green technology and clean energy applications to reduce carbon intensity. After entering the mature stage, policymakers should focus on strengthening the industry linkages to guide the low-carbon transformation of the industrial sectors.

Second, promote industrial technological innovation and reduce energy intensity. The findings of this paper show that technological innovation and energy intensity are important paths for industry linkages to reduce sectoral energy-related carbon emissions. Therefore, in order to better leverage the environmental benefits of industry linkages, the government should focus on inter-industry linkages in technological progress and energy efficiency. For example, establish a sharing mechanism and taxation support for technology exchanges between industries.

Third, optimize the industry linkage structure and promote balanced development. Based on our study, the weaker the regional industry linkages, the higher the sectoral carbon emissions. Thus, the government should optimize the industry linkage structure to mitigate carbon emissions. Specifically, policies should be made to promote R&D investment, cross-industry technological innovation collaboration, encourage the vertical deepening and horizontal expansion of the industrial chain, and build a more balanced industrial development pattern.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on I-O tables that are available at multi-year intervals, which may not fully capture short-term dynamics. Second, the study focuses on China, and the generalizability of the findings to other contexts may be limited. Future research could address these limitations by employing higher-frequency data and extending the analysis to other emerging economies.

The use of weighted linkage measures aims to mitigate the influence of sector size and capture the structural position of industries within the production network. Empirical strategy

To examine the relationship between industrial linkages and sectoral CO2 emissions, we estimate the baseline model using a high-dimensional fixed effects (HDFEs) ordinary least squares (OLSs) specification as Equation 10. This approach allows us to control for the unobserved heterogeneity across provinces, industries, time, thereby mitigating concerns arising from omitted variable bias at multiple levels.

The explained variable CE represents sectoral energy-related carbon emissions; X denotes the main explanatory variable, industrial weighted forward linkage (WFL) and weighted backward linkage (WBL), X2 is the square term; con represents the control variables. ε is the error term. λ, μ , θ, ρ, and δ respectively represents the time fixed effect, region fixed effect, industry fixed effect, industry-year joint fixed effects, and province-year joint fixed effects. Specifically, the inclusion of province fixed effects captures time-invariant regional characteristics; industry fixed effects account for persistent sectoral heterogeneity in production technology and energy intensity; and time fixed effects absorb common macroeconomic shocks. In addition, industry-year and province-year fixed effects control for evolving sectoral trends and region-specific macro dynamics. Given this high-dimensional fixed effects structure, the baseline OLS estimates are interpreted as conditional associations rather than strict causal effects.
