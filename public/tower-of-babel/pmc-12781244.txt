The effect of simulator-assisted application on anxiety, satisfaction, and self-confidence level of students taking internal medicine nursing course: a randomized controlled and experimental trial
Authors: Sibel Serçe, Özlem Ovayolu, Nimet Ovayolu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNursing education has increasingly benefited from innovative teaching methods, such as simulation-based learning, to enhance engagement, satisfaction, and self-confidence of students while managing the emotional challenges associated with clinical training.MethodsThis study was conducted as a randomised controlled and experimental trial with students who took the course of internal medicine nursing in the nursing department of the faculty of health sciences of a state university in the autumn term of the 2022–2023 academic year. The students in the intervention group were provided with the scenarios generated by the researchers using the simulation model. Before starting the simulation application, a questionnaire, the state-trait anxiety inventory, and the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale were applied to both intervention and control groups and the scales were repeated after the application.ResultsThe mean scores of the students in the intervention group on the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale were 3.8 ± 0.6 before the simulation and increased to 4.4 ± 0.6 after the simulation (p < 0.05). While their mean score of the state anxiety inventory increased from 39.5 ± 5.8 to 40.6 ± 5.3, their mean score of the trait anxiety inventory decreased from 46.6 ± 6.0 to 46.1 ± 5.5 (p > 0.05).ConclusionsConsequently, it was determined that simulation-assisted education did not affect the anxiety level of the students and significantly affected their levels of satisfaction and self-confidence in learning.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Education, Internal medicine nursing, Simulation, Anxiety, Self-confidence, Satisfaction, Nursing

Introduction

The fundamental aim of nursing education is to improve the affective, cognitive, and psychomotor skills of students and to enable them to assume protective, rehabilitative, managerial, educational, and decision-making roles [1]. At this point, contemporary educational approaches that ensure especially students get prepared before commencing their clinical practice are of critical importance [2]. Today, educators must employ innovative teaching methods that meet the needs of the era due to the increasing use of technology, the rapid transfer of information, and changes in students’ expectations [3]. Simulator-based education [4], which forms the foremost pillar of these methods, is also supported by important organisations such as the World Health Organisation and the National Council of State Boards of Nursing in the United States [5]. Simulation-based education involves the adequate, reliable, and realistic replication of the clinical practice setting [6], and has rapidly become a fundamental component of nursing students’ preparation for clinical practice [7]. Therefore, simulation-based education provides students with a realistic clinical environment experience, which can reduce their rate of mistakes in the clinic [8]. A study reported that simulation training provided to students alongside clinical practice lowered their anxiety levels and improved their self-confidence and care skills [9]. Another study conducted with a high-fidelity patient simulator indicated that students were highly satisfied with simulation training and their self-confidence improved [10]. Another study evaluating the effect of simulation training, provided within the scope of the course in internal medicine nursing, on students’ anxiety levels reported that simulation training lowered students’ anxiety levels, but their anxiety levels elevated during their clinical practice [11]. However, many institutions in Turkey that provide nursing education lack simulation-based education opportunities. Accordingly, this study was conducted to determine the effect of simulation-based training on anxiety, learning satisfaction, and self-confidence levels related to clinical practice among students taking the course of Internal Medicine Nursing.

Materials and methods

Design of the study

The study was designed as a randomised controlled experimental trial. Students in the intervention group practiced in the simulation lab using scenarios generated by researchers in addition to clinical practice. Students in the control group continued to attend only clinical practice education.

Setting and sample of the study

This study was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Unit of Gaziantep University during the COVID-19 pandemic, and a simulation laboratory was established at the faculty accordingly. Therefore, face-to-face education was initiated after the completion of the simulation laboratory, and the practices related to the research were carried out during the 2022–2023 academic year.

The population consisted of all students (n = 240) enrolled in the course of internal medicine nursing during the autumn term of the academic year 2022–2023. The sample size of the study was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.4 software, taking the confidence level as 80% and the significance level as α = 0.05 [10].

The students who were enrolled in the course of Internal Medicine Nursing, did not previously receive simulation-based education for nursing practice, were not foreign nationals, and were voluntary to participate in the study were included in the study. There were 240 students enrolled in the course of internal medicine nursing at the Department of Nursing of the Faculty of Health Sciences, where the study was conducted. Before assigning the students to the intervention and control groups, the researcher delivered a simulation-based presentation to them, and those who did not attend the class on the day of the presentation (n = 18) and were foreign nationals (n = 26) were excluded from the study. A total of 196 students were assigned to the intervention and control groups through simple random sampling method. The study was completed with 85 students in the intervention group and 98 students in the control group, as 13 students in the intervention group were excluded from the study due to their irregular attendance at the simulation training sessions (Fig. 1).

Data collection tools

A questionnaire, the state-trait anxiety inventory and “Student Satisfaction and Self-confidence in Learning Scale” were used in the study.

Questionnaire

This questionnaire, prepared by the researchers based on a literature review [12, 13], consists of 23 questions about the students’ socio-demographic characteristics and their concerns regarding distance education and its practice in relation to COVID-19 and the Internal Medicine Nursing course.

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)

The Inventory was developed by Spielberger et al., (1970). Its Turkish reliability study was conducted by Öner and Le Compte (1975) and its Turkish validity study was conducted by Öner (1977). For reliability study of the scale, reliability of both the original and Turkish versions of the inventory was tested using the Kuder-Richardson reliability test [14]. The inventory, developed to determine individuals’ anxiety levels, consists of a total of 40 items in total. This four-point Likert Inventory consists of two components: the State Anxiety Inventory (first 20 questions) and the Trait Anxiety Inventory (last 20 questions). The State Anxiety Inventory determines how an individual feels at a specific time and under specific conditions, while the Trait Anxiety Inventory determines how an individual feels regardless of the circumstances and conditions they are exposed to. The scales in the State and Trait Anxiety Inventory contain direct and reverse-scored items. While direct statements indicate negative feelings, reverse statements indicate positive feelings. There are ten reverse items in the State Anxiety Inventory (items 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16, 19, and 20) and seven reverse items in the Trait Anxiety Inventory (items 21, 26, 27, 30, 33, 36, and 39). A score between 0 point and 19 points on the inventories indicates that the individual has no anxiety, a score between 20 points and 39 points indicates a mild level of anxiety, a score between 40 points and 45 points indicates a moderate level of anxiety, and a score between 60 points and 79 indicates a severe level of anxiety. Furthermore, individuals who get a score of 60 points or above are indicated to require professional assistance. In its validity and reliability study conducted by Öner, the internal consistency and homogeneity coefficient was calculated as 0.83 to 0.87 for the State Anxiety Inventory and 0.94 to 0.96 for the Trait Anxiety Inventory [15]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was calculated to be 0.693 for the State Anxiety Inventory and 0.713 for the Trait Anxiety Inventory.

Student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale

Jeffries and Rizzolo (2006) originally developed the scale with 13 items, and Unver et al. conducted its Turkish validity and reliability study. During the adaptation process to Turkish, the total number of items was reduced to 12. This 5-point Likert-type scale composed of two subscales: “Satisfaction with Current Learning” and “Self-confidence in Learning.” The “Satisfaction with Current Learning” subscale consists of 5 items, while the “Self-confidence in Learning” subscale consists of 7 items, and there are no negative items. The scores on the scale range from 1 point to 5 points, and high scores indicate that students have high levels of satisfaction and self-confidence in learning [16]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were reported to be 0.85 for the “Satisfaction with Current Learning” subscale, 0.77 for the “Self-confidence in Learning” subscale, and 0.89 for the overall scale. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the scale were calculated to be 0.939 for the “Satisfaction with Current Learning” subscale and 0.910 for the “Self-confidence in Learning” subscale.

Implementation of the study

This study was completed in four stages: “use of data collection tools”, “simulation-based presentation”, “assigning of students into groups and simulation application”, and “evaluation”.

First stage

Use of data collection tools

After the necessary ethics committee approval and institutional permission were granted for this study, data collection tools (Questionnaire, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and Student Satisfaction and Self-confidence in Learning Scale) were applied to the students who met the inclusion criteria.

Second stage

Simulation-based presentation

After using the data collection tools, the researchers delivered a presentation to the students that included simulation. The presentation primarily discussed the simulation laboratory and the characteristics of the model. Subsequently, the practices that would take place in the simulation laboratory were described. This briefing was a common intervention applied equally to both groups and conducted to familiarise students with the simulation environment and ensure compliance with ethical approval.

Third stage

Assigning of students into groups and simulation application

In this stage, the researchers primarily informed students that participation was entirely voluntary, that all participants could withdraw from the study at any time without incurring academic consequences, and participation would not affect their course grades. The students were then assigned to intervention and control groups by using a simple random sampling method and taking their numbers into account. The students in the intervention group practiced on scenarios created by the researchers on a simulation model (cardiac rhythms, pupil assessment, and lung sounds) besides clinical practice training. The students in the intervention group were taken to the laboratory in groups of 10 and practiced the planned scenarios with each student. The simulation was run in three sections for each student group. These were a preliminary briefing about the scenario and an explanation of the application goals (5 min), the application process (10–15 min), and the analysis (15–20 min) [17]. The students in the control group continued only with their clinical practice training during this time; simulation training was held with students who volunteered at the end of the study.

The simulation design, prebriefing, facilitation, and debriefing phases were conducted in accordance with the INACSL Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice ® (Prebriefing, Debriefing 2025).

Fourth stage

Evaluation

After the simulation application was completed, data collection tools (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, Student Satisfaction, and Self-confidence in Learning Scale) were applied again to students in the intervention and control groups simultaneously.

Data analysis

The sample size in the study was determined in accordance with the power analysis done beforehand (the sample size of the study was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.4 software with a confidence level of 80% and a significance level of α = 0.05, and the minimum sample size was determined as 75), and all analyses were done over the complete data set since no missing data were found.

The data of the study were analysed using SPSS 23.0 software. Number, percentage, and arithmetic mean were used to analyse descriptive characteristics of the students. After randomisation, independent samples t-test and chi-square test were run to evaluate the homogeneity of the groups according to demographic data. Student t, Mann-Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were run to compare socio-demographic data with the mean scores of the scales. Student t and Mann-Whitney U tests were run for the intragroup and intergroup comparisons of mean scores of the students, and Spearman correlation test was run to analyse the correlations between the scales. The value of p < 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Gaziantep University Clinical Trials Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2021/77). The participation was completely voluntary, and all participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any academic consequences and that participation would not affect their course grades. Furthermore, personal data were processed in strict confidentiality and were not shared with third parties under any circumstances. The students in the control group were informed that they would be allowed access to the simulation laboratory after the study was completed.

Findings

Findings related to the comparison of the students’ socio-demographic characteristics and their mean scores of the STAI and the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale

It was found that 57.6% of the students in the intervention group were aged between 18 and 20 years, 76.5% were female, 67.1% lived in a dormitory or a bed and board, 80.6% chose the nursing profession willingly, and 38.8% thought that the nursing profession was suitable for them. On the other hand, 73.5% of the students in the control group were aged between 18 and 20 years, 76.5% were female, 49% lived with their family, 35.7% chose the nursing profession willingly, and 38.8% thought that the nursing profession was suitable for them.

A significant correlation was found between the intervention (p = 0.001) and control (p = 0.010) groups in terms of the male gender and the mean score of the state anxiety inventory and between the female gender and the mean score of the trait anxiety inventory in the control group (p = 0.038). However, while the mean scores of the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale were higher in the female gender in both groups, this was statistically significant only in the intervention group (p = 0.016) (Table 1).

Findings related to the comparison of students’ some covid-19-related conditions and their mean scores of the STAI and the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale

The findings revealed that 76.5% of the students in the intervention group claimed that COVID-19 impaired their quality of life, 49.4% claimed suffering from stress, 91.8% reported that their educational life was negatively affected, 83.5% claimed that online education was insufficient in theoretical education and 81.2% in practical terms. 61.2% stated that they had problems during online education, 64.7% stated that they had knowledge about simulation, 92.9% reported that they wanted to receive education in the simulation laboratory during this period, and 94.1% reported that education in the simulation laboratory would make a positive contribution to their educational process.

On the other hand, 69.4% of the students in the control group claimed that COVID-19 impaired their quality of life, 40.8% claimed suffering from stress, 85.7% reported that their educational life was negatively affected, 84.7% claimed that online education was insufficient in theoretical education and 95.9% in practical terms. 74.5% stated that they had problems during online education, 59.2% stated that they had knowledge about simulation, 90.8% reported that they wanted to receive education in the simulation laboratory during this period, and 95.9% reported that education in the simulation laboratory would make a positive contribution to their educational process.

The mean score of the state anxiety inventory was higher in those who claimed that COVID-19 did not impair their quality of life (p = 0.040) and educational life (p = 0.020) in the intervention group. The mean score of the trait anxiety inventory was higher in those who thought that COVID-19 had no impact on their educational life and those who stated that they wanted to receive education in the simulation laboratory in both the intervention (p = 0.015) and control groups (p = 0.009). Moreover, it the students in the intervention group who had knowledge about simulation (p = 0.002), wanted to receive education in the simulation laboratory (p = 0.009), and thought that this education would be useful had higher mean scores on student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale (Table 2).

Findings related to intra-group and inter-group comparisons of the mean scores of the STAI and student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale before and after the simulation application

The mean score of the students in the intervention group on student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale was 3.8 ± 0.6 before the simulation and increased to 4.4 ± 0.6 after the simulation, their mean score of the state anxiety inventory rose from 39.5 ± 5.8 to 40.6 ± 5.3, and their mean score of the trait anxiety inventory decreased from 46.6 ± 6.0 to 46.1 ± 5.5. The intra-group (p = 0.001, p = 0.047) and inter-group (p = 0.016, p = 0.001) comparisons of the mean scores of student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale were statistically significant in both the intervention and control groups. This finding revealed that the mean scores of the intervention group on the student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scale increased compared to pre-simulation application mean scores, while the mean scores of the control group decreased (Table 3).

Discussion

Students are expected to acquire theoretical and clinical practice skills during nursing education. Therefore, current technology methods used, especially in education, contribute significantly to the development of students’ clinical decision-making skills, lower their anxiety levels, and improve their self-confidence through experience-based learning [11].

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the effect of simulator-assisted application on anxiety, satisfaction, and self-confidence levels in students who took the course of internal medicine nursing.

The COVID-19 pandemic necessitated the shift to remove education, significantly disrupting both theoretical and practical education. The qualitative study conducted by Eminoğlu and Doruk reported that the students had internet problems; therefore, the internet connection was lost during online education and they had difficulty in continuing the course [18]. In their study, Kürtüncü and Kurt reported that nursing students suffered from serious infrastructure problems during distance education during the COVID-19 period; therefore, this education was insufficient in theoretical and practical terms. Another study reported that the failure to provide practical education in clinics due to COVID-19 pandemic may negatively affect the professional development of students, leading to an elevated level of stress and a fall in academic achievement [19–21]. In their study, Savitsky et al., found an elevated level of anxiety in nursing students due to economic uncertainties related to the pandemic, fear of infection, and difficulties in distance education [22]. Durgun et al., stated that nursing students had a moderate level of anxiety towards distance education [23]. On the other hand, in their study, Li et al., reported that nursing students were satisfied with distance education during COVID-19 pandemic [24]. The present study also revealed that the students suffered from a moderate level of anxiety and had some problems during distance education, thus elevating their anxiety levels and the students who thought that online education was insufficient, especially in terms of practice, had higher mean score of the state-anxiety inventory. As shown, the nursing profession, which focuses on humans and care and gives high importance to practical education, brings along many concerns about the educational quality of students through online education.

Although theoretical and practical motivation problems are experienced even during formal education in nursing, it has been reported that distance education seriously affects the motivation level of students [25]. In a systematic review, Sabancı examined the learning motivation of students in online education and revealed that this education negatively affected the motivation of 54% of the students [26]. Online education may also affect students’ level of professional self-efficacy. In their study, Ozer et al., analysed the level of professional self-efficacy of intern nursing students during the COVID-19 pandemic and reported that students who thought that the nursing profession was suitable for them and regularly attended online courses had higher scores of professional self-efficacy [27]. This study also indicated that both the anxiety level and the mean score of self-confidence and satisfaction in learning were higher in those who willingly chose the nursing profession and thought that the profession was suitable for them. The high anxiety level of students who willingly chose the nursing profession in particular can be attributed to the fact that they considered online education insufficient in terms of vocational education.

The pandemic and natural disasters have indicated that programmes offering practical education, especially in the field of nursing, should act innovatively, flexibly, and quickly in terms of educational methods [28]. Therefore, it is highly important to regulate new teaching methods that would support formal education and clinical teaching in nursing education based on today’s conditions [29, 30]. Different teaching technologies have been utilised at every stage of the education process, and a significant portion of these technologies include computer-assisted education and simulation approaches [31]. Simulation is crucial as it allows students to confidently practice their newly acquired skills in a comfortable and supportive atmosphere [31]. This is due to the repetitive practices in simulation laboratories, which subside the anxiety of the students and raise their self-confidence, thus delivering better quality service [32]. In a study conducted by Ayed et al., with nursing students, they detected a significant difference in students’ perceptions of self-awareness, empathy, and patient-centred care am [33]. In a study, students stated that their expectations were not met during clinical practice and there were differences between what was taught in theory and what was practised in the clinic [31]. Therefore, it has become essential to support students’ practical training in nursing skills and simulation laboratories where the clinical setting closely mirroring real-life practice is presented [34]. The literature has reported that the use of innovative approaches in nursing education improves the effectiveness of the education, students’ learning skills, self-confidence, and motivation [5]. In their study, Jawabreh et al., concluded that simulation-based practice was critical to ensure integrity in the theoretical and clinical practice education of nursing students [35]. A qualitative study reported that simulation supported an effective learning and theoretical education [36]. Similar studies have emphasised that the use of simulation practices in nursing education improves students’ knowledge, skills, and communication levels and contributes to their critical thinking, self-efficacy, and self-confidence [37, 38]. A meta-analysis revealed that simulation-based education enhanced students’ knowledge, psychomotor, and communication skills and elevated their self-confidence, critical thinking, and self-efficacy levels [39]. In their study, Ayhan et al., stated that the majority of students stated that they needed simulation education in the fields of surgery (70.5%) and internal medicine nursing (59.6%) [4]. In an experimental study conducted by Toqan et al., with nursing students, they reported that students subjected to simulation application had low levels of self-confidence and satisfaction [40].

This study showed that the scores of student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning, which were “moderate” in the intervention group before the simulation application, reached a “high” level after the simulation application, and the scores of student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning, which were “moderate” in the control group before the simulation application, did not change. Also, those who wanted to receive education in the simulation laboratory and those who thought that simulation would make a positive contribution to education had higher scores of student satisfaction and self-confidence in learning. However, the anxiety level of the students who practiced with the simulation-assisted programme did not change statistically significantly compared to the control group. This can be attributed to the excitement and reaction of the students who faced the simulation for the first time.

Conclusion

In this study, it was concluded that students taking the course of internal medicine nursing suffered from a moderate level of anxiety, and simulation did not affect the level of anxiety; however, the satisfaction and self-confidence in learning scores of the students in the intervention group reached a ‘high’ level after the simulation. Therefore, it is recommended to use computer-aided simulations in nursing skills laboratories and to support practical training with simulation.
