Endovascular treatment of a traumatic aortic transection via implantation of self-expanding nitinol stent with open-cell design: a case report
Authors: Ali Keivanjah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic aortic transection is a life-threatening injury with an acute mortality rate approaching 80%, typically resulting from high-energy blunt trauma, such as motor vehicle collisions. Without intervention, the overall mortality rate increases to 90–94%, primarily due to the rupture of pseudoaneurysms or expanding hematoma leading to exsanguination. Although delayed surgical repair may decrease immediate mortality, it is associated with significant perioperative risks. Endovascular techniques are becoming increasingly prevalent and have demonstrated lower morbidity and mortality rates in adult trauma patients compared with traditional open surgery.Case presentationA 32-year-old female Filipino healthcare worker presented to our institution in February 2021 following a high-speed head-on collision with seat-belt-related trauma. She exhibited respiratory distress, mild hypotension, and abdominal wall bruising. Initial hemoglobin was 12.6 g/dL and remained stable. Imaging (computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography) revealed a right pneumothorax, along with a partial pulmonary contusion, a suprarenal aortic transection with severe luminal narrowing, distortion of the celiac and superior mesenteric artery origins, and a small periaortic and suprarenal hematoma, without contrast extravasation. Following stabilization, including right-sided chest tube placement and comprehensive discussion of all therapeutic options, the patient declined open surgical repair. Due to the severe suprarenal aortic narrowing, fenestrated endograft deployment was not feasible. On 4 March 2021, she underwent percutaneous endovascular repair using a self-expanding nitinol stent (Sinus XL Flex, 14 × 80 mm; OptiMed, Germany) via right common femoral artery access.The procedure was successful without complications, and she was discharged on postoperative day 3 with aspirin therapy. Follow-up imaging at 3 and 12 months demonstrated near-complete aortic restoration and preserved renal and visceral perfusion.ConclusionThis case highlights the efficacy of a novel endovascular approach for nonhemorrhagic traumatic aortic transection. The open-cell nitinol stent provides immediate stabilization of the aortic wall while preserving perfusion to branch vessels, thereby preventing rupture and avoiding fatal outcomes. This unique, minimally invasive technique presents a viable alternative to conventional endografts, particularly in anatomically complex cases of nonhemorrhagic traumatic aortic transection.Keywords: Traumatic aortic transection, Seat belt injury, Motor vehicle collision, Step-up deformity of abdominal aorta, Self-expanding nitinol stent with open-cell design, Case report

Introduction

Traumatic aortic transection (TAT), which involves a near-complete tear through all the layers of the aorta, requires immediate medical treatment [1]. These types of conditions commonly arise from motor vehicle crashes, pedestrian accidents, or falls. Unfortunately, approximately 80% of patients experiencing aortic transection do not survive long enough to undergo medical assessment and expire at the location of the incident [2]. Reports indicate that seat belt injuries are responsible for nearly 50% of all blunt abdominal aortic traumas. Delayed repair is purportedly a possible treatment option, as it has been associated with reduced total mortality compared with early repair. However, it comes with increased complications, including prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays and hospitalization [3, 4]. Recent data confirm that endovascular treatment methods have resulted in lower perioperative morbidity and mortality rates than open surgical approaches [5]. Herein, we present a case report that illustrates a unique endovascular treatment for nonhemorrhagic TAT that occurred as a result of seat-belt-induced trauma during a motor vehicle collision.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old female Filipino healthcare professional presented to our institution in February 2021 following a high-velocity head-on motor vehicle collision with resultant seat-belt-related trauma. Initial assessment revealed respiratory distress, slight hypotension, and extensive abdominal wall ecchymoses (Fig. 1). Hemoglobin level upon admission was 12.6 g/dL and remained stable during the following blood tests. Furthermore, on admission, the levels of amylase at 143 U/L and lipase at 172.5 U/L were elevated as signs of traumatic pancreatitis, which developed gradually toward the normal range during further tests. Chest radiography identified a right-sided pneumothorax. Advanced vascular imaging [computed tomography angiography (CTA) with confirmatory magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)] demonstrated a right-sided partial pulmonary contusion, TAT with marked step-off deformity and significant luminal narrowing at the suprarenal segment, morphological distortion of the celiac axis and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) origin, and a small periaortic and suprarenal hematoma without contrast extravasation (Figs. 2, 3). Following stabilization, including right-sided chest tube placement and comprehensive discussion of all therapeutic options, the patient declined open surgical repair. Although fenestrated endovascular repair represents the preferred treatment for TAT involving the mesenteric and renal arteries, the severe luminal narrowing at the transection site of our case precluded endograft deployment. As a result, we implemented an innovative endovascular approach, using a self-expanding nitinol stent with an open-cell design to stabilize the aortic wall at the transection level and maintain blood flow to the intestinal and renal arteries. On 4 March 2021, the patient underwent a percutaneous endovascular reconstruction of the aorta under general anesthesia (Fig. 4). The procedure utilized a self-expanding nitinol stent with open-cell design (Sinus XL Flex, 14 × 80 mm; OptiMed, Germany), which was deployed via right common femoral artery access. As per instruction for use (IFU), this stent is dedicated to use for the aorta and veins, but it has never been utilized for this segment of aorta with intestinal and renal branches. The procedure was conducted in a hybrid operating theater. Cell-saver and multiple tested blood packages were on standby for potential transfusion if needed. The entire thoracic and abdominal area was prepared sterile for possible conversion to open surgery in the case of massive bleeding, and all necessary instruments, including multiple aortic grafts, were readily available. Additionally, aortic balloons were on hand for temporary intraluminal blockage in the event of bleeding. Postoperative course was unremarkable, with ICU discharge at 24 hours and subsequent hospital discharge on postoperative day 3. An anticoagulation with daily aspirin (100 mg) was initiated. Serial follow-up imaging at 3 and 12 months demonstrated near-complete anatomical restoration of the aortic wall with preserved perfusion to visceral and renal vascular beds and no signs of bleeding (Figs. 5, 6).

Discussion

Blunt force trauma and significant shearing forces on the aorta are the primary causes of traumatic aortic transection (TAT), most often resulting from motor vehicle crashes. These injuries frequently occur alongside polytrauma [6], contributing to an overall mortality rate of approximately 90–94% [7]. In-hospital mortality typically results from the rupture of a pseudoaneurysm or expanding hematoma, leading to fatal hemorrhage [8]. Treatment options for TAT depend on the severity of the aortic wall damage. Grade I injuries, characterized by an intimal tear, and grade II injuries, involving an intramural hematoma, can often be managed conservatively through blood pressure regulation. In contrast, grade III injuries, which involve pseudoaneurysm formation, and grade IV injuries, marked by complete aortic wall rupture, require surgical or interventional treatment (Fig. 7) [9]. In our case, the TAT was classified as grade III according to the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (Fig. 7). Minimally invasive techniques are generally preferred for these injuries. Hybrid procedures—such as the implantation of an octopus graft combined with the deployment of a stent graft at the transection level—are also viable options. Experience with less invasive endovascular repairs in adults continues to grow [10]. Following stabilization, including right-sided chest tube placement and a comprehensive discussion of all therapeutic options, the patient declined open surgical repair. Although fenestrated endovascular repair represents the preferred treatment for TAT involving the mesenteric and renal arteries, the severe luminal narrowing at the transection site of our case precluded endograft deployment. As a result, we implemented an innovative endovascular approach, using a self-expanding nitinol stent with an open-cell design to stabilize the aortic wall at the transection level and maintain blood flow to the intestinal and renal arteries.

Conclusion

The interventional management of a nonhemorrhagic TAT using a self-expanding nitinol stent with an open-cell design represents an innovative strategy that offers a practical, off-the-shelf solution in emergency settings. This approach effectively stabilizes the aortic wall while maintaining branch vessel perfusion, thereby preventing complete rupture of the aorta and its potentially fatal consequences. This procedure should be performed in a hybrid operating theater, with all necessary precautions in place for possible conversion to open surgery in case of a complete aortic wall rupture and subsequent bleeding. Long-term follow-up, including annual evaluations via aortic CTA, remains essential to monitor outcomes.
