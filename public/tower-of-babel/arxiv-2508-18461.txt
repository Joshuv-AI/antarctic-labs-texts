Universal Matrices for Counting Fibonomial and C C -nomial Coefficients by their p p -adic Valuations
Arav Chand

Abstract
. Rowland found a matrix product formula for generating functions counting binomial coefficients by their p p -adic valuations. A natural generalization of binomial coefficients was introduced by Knuth and Wilf defined by a sequence C C . We obtain analogous matrix product formulas counting these C C -nomial coefficients when C C is a strong divisibility sequence. Surprisingly, the matrices are universal in the sense that they are independent of C C . We further extend this product to C C -multinomial coefficients.



1.
Introduction

Pascal’s triangle has been studied by mathematicians for hundreds of years, especially for its numerous number theoretic properties. Fine
[
3
]
studied the divisibility of the entries on the
n
th row of Pascal’s triangle by a prime
p
. Specifically, let
F_{p}(n)
be the number of integers
m
in the range
0\leq m\leq n
such that
\binom{n}{m}
is not divisible by
p
, and let
n=n_{\ell}\cdots n_{1}n_{0}
be the standard base-
p
representation of
n
. Fine showed that

F_{p}(n)=(n_{0}+1)(n_{1}+1)\cdots(n_{\ell}+1).

Many authors have since been interested in generalizing Fine’s theorem in various ways, such as counting
p
-adic valuations greater than
0
. One way this has been studied is through the generating function

T_{p}(n,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\left(\binom{n}{m}\right)}.

This polynomial encodes the
p
-adic valuations of binomial coefficients, where
\nu_{p}(n)
denotes the exponent of the highest power of
p
dividing
n
. The coefficient on
x^{i}
in
T_{p}(n,x)
counts the number of
m
for which
\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}\right)=i
.

Rowland
[
6
]
proved a matrix product formula for
T_{p}(n,x)
. For each
d\in\{0,1,\dots,p~-~1\}
, let

M_{p}(d)=\begin{bmatrix}d+1&p-d-1\\
dx&(p-d)x\end{bmatrix}.

Rowland showed

T_{p}(n,x)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}M_{p}(n_{0})M_{p}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p}(n_{\ell})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

In this paper, we generalize this matrix product formula to analogues of binomial coefficients defined with respect to strong divisibility sequences.

A sequence
C=C_{1},C_{2},\dots
of nonzero integers is a
strong divisibility sequence
if it satisfies

\gcd(C_{n},C_{m})=C_{\gcd(n,m)}

for all positive
n
and
m
.
For example, the Fibonacci sequence

(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}\colon 1,1,2,3,5,8,11,\dots

is an example of a strong divisibility sequence. Furthermore, all Lucas sequences
(U_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that have relatively coprime
U_{2}
and
U_{3}
are also strong divisibility sequences.

Notation
.

For a strong divisibility sequence
C
, define the
C
-orial
by
0!_{C}=1
and
n!_{C}=C_{n}C_{n-1}\cdots C_{1}
for positive
n
, and define the
C
-nomial coefficient
by

\binom{n}{m}_{C}=\frac{n!_{C}}{m!_{C}(n-m)!_{C}}

for non-negative
n
and
m
. Knuth and Wilf
[
5
]
proved that
\binom{n}{m}_{C}
is an integer.
Furthermore, let
T_{p,C}(n,x)
denote the generating polynomial for the
p
-adic valuations of
C
-nomials.
That is,

T_{p,C}(n,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{C}\right)}.

The coefficient on
x^{i}
in
T_{p,C}(n,x)
counts the number of
m
for which
\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{C}\right)=i

We introduce some more notation to state the main theorems. For a strong divisibility sequence
C
, we define the
rank of apparition

\alpha(m)
to be the index of the first term in
C
divisible by
m
, if such a term exists.
If
\alpha(p)
is not defined, the generating polynomial is trivially
T_{p,C}(n,x)=n+1
.
When
\alpha(p^{k})
is defined for all
k\geq 1
, we will also be interested in the sequence of ratios
(a_{k}(p))_{k\geq 1}
defined by

a_{1}(p):=\alpha(p),\qquad a_{2}(p):=\frac{\alpha(p^{2})}{\alpha(p)},\qquad a_{3}(p):=\frac{\alpha(p^{3})}{\alpha(p^{2})},\qquad\dots.

To give some intuition regarding this sequence, a value of
a_{2}(p)=1
means the appearance of the factor
p^{2}
in the sequence
C
is at the same index as the appearance of the factor
p
. A value of
a_{3}(p)=p
means the first term that divides
p^{3}
is at an index exactly
p
times the index of the first term that divides
p^{2}
. We use this ratio sequence to define three classes of primes.

Definition
.

Let
p
be a prime and
C
a strong divisibility sequence such that
\alpha(p^{k})
is defined for all
k\geq 1
. Following Knuth and Wilf
[
5
]
, the prime
p
is
ideal
for
C
if there exists an integer
s(p)\geq 1
such that the sequence of ratios
(a_{k}(p))_{k\geq 2}
satisfies

a_{k}(p)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }2\leq k\leq s(p)\\
p&\text{if }k>s(p).\end{cases}

We say
p
is
acceptable
if there exists an integer
s(p)\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle a_{k}(p)

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{if }2\leq k\leq s(p)

\displaystyle a_{k}(p)

\displaystyle=p\quad\text{if }k>s(p).

A prime is
unacceptable
if it is not acceptable.
Note that every ideal prime is acceptable.
We mention that
s(p)
is in fact
\nu_{p}\!\left(C_{\alpha(p)}\right)
.

show that certain subsequences are similarly
p
-regular. That is,
(T_{p,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r))_{n\geq 0}
for any
0\leq r<\alpha(p)
is a
p
-regular sequence, and
(T_{p,k,C}(\alpha(p^{s(p)})\cdot n+r))_{n\geq 0}
for any
0\leq r<\alpha(p^{s(p)})
is a
p
-regular sequence.

We set up necessary identities for the subsequent proofs in Section
2
. We consider ideal and acceptable primes in separate sections, proving Theorems
1
and
3
in Section
3
dealing with ideal primes. We prove Theorem
4
in Section
4
dealing with acceptable primes. Note we do not provide a separate proof for Theorem
2
as it is a subcase of Theorem
4
that is not substantially simpler to prove.
Since ideal primes are also acceptable and
C
-nomials are
C
-multinomials with
k=2
, Theorem
4
is our most broad statement for a matrix product formula that counts generalized binomial coefficients by their
p
-adic valuation.

2.
Valuation Identities

In this section we obtain identities for the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-orial,
C
-nomial, and
C
-multinomial coefficients in the cases of ideal and acceptable primes.

We begin by finding a Legendre-like formula for the
C
-orial:

Proposition 5
.

For a strong divisibility sequence
C
, prime
p
, and integer
n\geq 0
, we have

\nu_{p}(n!_{C})=\sum_{k\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Given integers
m
and
n
, let
d_{m}(n)
be the number of indices
1\leq j\leq n
such that
m\mid C_{j}.

The
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-orial is then

\nu_{p}(n!_{C})=\sum_{k\geq 1}d_{p^{k}}(n).

Knuth and Wilf
[
5
]
proved that the strong divisibility property of a sequence
C
is equivalent to the following characterization: for each integer
m>0
, either there exists a positive integer
\alpha(m)
such that

m\mid C_{j}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(m)\mid j\quad\text{for all }j>0,

or else
m\nmid C_{j}
for any
j>0
, in which case one formally sets
\alpha(m)=\infty
.

The
d
functions for a strong divisibility sequence then have the simple form

d_{m}(n)=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(m)}\right\rfloor.

Substituting
d_{p^{k}}(n)=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor
, we arrive at the proposed equation.
∎

The valuation of the
C
-nomial and
C
-multinomial can be stated in terms the valuation of the
C
-orial as

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{\!C}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(n!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m!_{C})-\nu_{p}((n-m)!_{C})

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}}_{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(n!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m_{1}!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m_{2}!_{C})-\cdots-\nu_{p}(m_{k}!_{C}).

Specifically, Proposition
5
yields explicit expressions for the valuation of the
C
-orial depending on the class of primes and subsequently simplifying
\alpha(p^{k})
.

3.
Ideal Primes

We first consider ideal primes and prove the matrix product formulas for the generating functions that count
C
-nomial and
C
-multinomial coefficients by their
p
-adic valuations for ideal prime
p
. From the definition of ideal prime and the constraints on
a_{k}(p)_{k\geq 2}
, the rank of apparition for powers of
p
satisfies

\alpha(p^{k})=\begin{cases}\alpha(p)&\text{if }1\leq k\leq s(p)\\
p^{k-s(p)}\alpha(p)&\text{if }k>s(p).\end{cases}

This specifies our Legendre-like formula in Proposition
5
into one for ideal primes:

Lemma 6
.

Let
p
be an ideal prime for a strong divisibility sequence
C
. Then for any integer
n\geq 0
,

\nu_{p}(n!_{C})=s(p)\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor+\nu_{p}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor!\right).

Proof.

We split the sum in Proposition
5
based on the index
k
as

\nu_{p}(n!_{C})=\sum_{1\leq k\leq s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor+\sum_{k>s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{k-s(p)}\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor.

Let
m=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor
. Then by Legendre’s formula, the second addend becomes

\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{m}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor=\nu_{p}(m!),

which completes the proof.
∎

Using this valuation of the
C
-orial and a modular casework analysis, we now relate the generating function counting
C
-nomials

T_{p,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r,x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\alpha(p)n+r}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n+r}{m}_{C}\right)}

to generating functions counting binomials

\sum_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}\right)},\hskip 28.45274pt\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}x^{1+\nu_{p}\!\left(n\binom{n-1}{m}\right)},

which arise naturally as we analyze the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-nomial.

Lemma 7
.

Let
p
be an ideal prime for a strong divisibility sequence
C
. For any integer
n\geq 0
, let
n=\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r
where
0\leq r<\alpha(p)
and
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\sum_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{C}\right)}=(r+1)\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{n^{\prime}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}\right)}+(\alpha(p)-r-1)x^{s(p)-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{n^{\prime}-1}x^{1+\nu_{p}\!\left(n^{\prime}\binom{n^{\prime}-1}{m^{\prime}}\right)}.

Proof.

We first analyze the exponent of the left-hand-side

\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{C}\right)=\nu_{p}(n!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m!_{C})-\nu_{p}\!\left((n-m)!_{C}\right).

It is convenient to write
m_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=m=\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}
, and
m_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(n-m)=\alpha(p)m_{2}^{\prime}+r_{2}
, where
0\leq r_{1},r_{2}<\alpha(p)
and
m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}_{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}\!\left((\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r)!_{C}\right)-\nu_{p}\!\left((\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1})!_{C}\right)-\nu_{p}\!\left((\alpha(p)m_{2}^{\prime}+r_{2})!_{C}\right).

After substituting Lemma
6
, this becomes

\displaystyle s(p)\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)m_{2}^{\prime}+r_{2}}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle+\nu_{p}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha(p)m_{2}^{\prime}+r_{2}}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor!\right).

Simplifying each floor, this valuation equals

(1)

\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}_{C}\right)=s(p)\left(n^{\prime}-m_{1}^{\prime}-m_{2}^{\prime}\right)+\nu_{p}\left(n^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{1}^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{2}^{\prime}!\right).

Our analysis now splits based the value of
\lambda:=n^{\prime}-m_{1}^{\prime}-m_{2}^{\prime}
.
Because
n=m_{1}+m_{2}
, we may state

r+\lambda\alpha(p)=r_{1}+r_{2}.

Therefore, by the bounds on
r
,
r_{1}
, and
r_{2}
,
\lambda
may only take on the value of
0
or
1
.

The first case we consider is
\lambda=0
. In this case,
n^{\prime}-m_{1}^{\prime}-m_{2}^{\prime}=0
. Thus Equation (
1
) simplifies to

\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}_{C}\right)=\nu_{p}\left(n^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{1}^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{2}^{\prime}!\right)=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{m_{1}^{\prime}!m_{2}^{\prime}!}\right)=\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}!}{m_{1}^{\prime}!}\right).

We substitute this for the right hand side of the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-nomial, finding

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}_{C}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}}{m_{1}^{\prime}}\right).

The other case is
\lambda=1
.
In this case,
n^{\prime}-m_{1}^{\prime}-m_{2}^{\prime}=1
. Thus the first addend on the right hand side of Equation (
1
) evaluates to
s(p)
. Since
m_{1}^{\prime}+m_{2}^{\prime}=n-1
, notice that the latter addends simplify as

\nu_{p}\left(n^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{1}^{\prime}!\right)-\nu_{p}\left(m_{2}^{\prime}!\right)=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{n^{\prime}(n^{\prime}-1)!}{m_{1}^{\prime}!m_{2}^{\prime}!}\right)=\nu_{p}\!\left(n^{\prime}\binom{n^{\prime}-1}{m_{1}^{\prime}}\right).

Therefore, the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-nomial in this case is

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}}_{C}\right)

\displaystyle=s(p)+\nu_{p}\!\left(n^{\prime}\binom{n^{\prime}-1}{m_{1}^{\prime}}\right).

Now let’s interpret what these cases mean and how they allow us to simplify the summation

\sum_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}_{C}\right)}=\sum_{\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}=0}^{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r}{\alpha(p)m+r_{1}}_{C}\right)}.

When
\lambda=0
,
r=r_{1}+r_{2}
. Therefore, by the bounds on
r_{i}
,
r_{1}\in\{0,1,\ldots,r\}
and from the iterating variable
\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}
running up to
\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r
, the valid values of
m_{1}^{\prime}
are
0\leq m_{1}^{\prime}\leq n^{\prime}
. Therefore, this evaluates the summation in this case to

(r+1)\sum_{m_{1}^{\prime}=0}^{n^{\prime}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}}{m_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}.

When
\lambda=1
,
r+\alpha(p)=r_{1}+r_{2}
. In this case, valid values are
r_{1}\in\{r+1,\ldots,\alpha(p)-1\}
, and from the iterating variable
\alpha(p)m_{1}^{\prime}+r_{1}
running up to
\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r
, the valid values of
m_{1}^{\prime}
are
0\leq m_{1}^{\prime}\leq n^{\prime}-1
. Therefore, this evaluates the summation in this case to

(\alpha(p)-r-1)\sum_{m_{1}^{\prime}=0}^{n^{\prime}-1}x^{s(p)+\nu_{p}\!\left(n^{\prime}\binom{n^{\prime}}{m_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}.

Adding these two components completes the proof.
∎

The proof of Theorem
1
, which states

T_{p,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r,x)=\begin{bmatrix}r+1&(\alpha(p)-r-1)x^{s(p)-1}\end{bmatrix}M_{p}(n_{0})M_{p}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p}(n_{\ell})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}

follows from connecting Lemma
7
to a linear algebra-powered efficiency improvement.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

For the midst of his proof
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, Rowland essentially showed

(2)

M_{p}(n_{0})M_{p}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p}(n_{\ell})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}\right)}\\
\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n-1}x^{1+\nu_{p}\!\left(n\binom{n-1}{m}\right)}\end{bmatrix}.

In the binomial case as Rowland studied, a leading factor of the vector
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}
on the product was sufficient to isolate the requested generating function counting binomials. In our generalized case, this vector changes to encompass the complexity of a general strong divisibility sequence. Surprisingly however, we only need to change this vector; we can build off the same matrices Rowland used to make up the latter partial product. In other words, the matrices are independent of
C
. Specifically, rewriting the right hand side of Lemma
7
with the coefficients as the entries of the vector, we have

\sum_{m=0}^{\alpha(p)n+r}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{\alpha(p)n+r}{m}_{C}\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}r+1&(\alpha(p)-r-1)x^{s(p)-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m}\right)}\\
\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n-1}x^{1+\nu_{p}\!\left(n\binom{n-1}{m}\right)}\end{bmatrix}.

Notice the left-hand-side summation is
T_{p,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r,x)
. Substituting this and the right hand side’s latter factor with Equation (
2
) completes the matrix product formula for counting
C
-nomial coefficients according to their
p
-adic valuation for an ideal prime
p
as

T_{p,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r,x)=\begin{bmatrix}r+1&(\alpha(p)-r-1)x^{s(p)-1}\end{bmatrix}M_{p}(n_{0})M_{p}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p}(n_{\ell})\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

∎

We continue by extending the theorem to its
C
-multinomial analogue. Rowland defined a series of generating functions counting binomials supplemental to his multinomial proof. Specifically, for
k\geq 2
and each
\lambda
in the range
0\leq\lambda\leq k-1
,

T_{p,k,\lambda}(n,x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0\leq n\leq\lambda-1\\
x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n!}{(n-\lambda)!}\right)+\lambda}T_{p,k}(n-\lambda,x)&\text{if }n\geq\lambda.\end{cases}

Recall

T_{p,k}(n-\lambda,x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m}=n-\lambda\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n-\lambda}{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,\dots,\,m_{k}}\right)}.

and note
T_{p,k,0}(n,x)=T_{p,k}(n,x)
.
We further use the coefficient defined in Section
1
. Namely, for each
0\leq\lambda\leq k-1
, let

u_{p,k,C,\lambda}(r)=x^{(s(p)-1)\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}\binom{r+(\lambda-j)\alpha(p)+k-1}{k-1}.

Lemma 8
.

Let
p
be an ideal prime for a strong divisibility sequence
C
and integer
k\geq 2
. For any integer
n\geq 0
, let
n=\alpha(p)n^{\prime}+r
where
0\leq r<\alpha(p)
and
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m}=n\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,\dots,\,m_{k}}_{C}\right)}=\sum\limits_{\lambda=0}^{k-1}u_{p,k,C,\lambda}(r)T_{p,k,\lambda}(n,x).

Proof.

We similarly notate
m_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=m_{i}^{\prime}\alpha(p)+r_{i}
where
0\leq r_{i}<\alpha(p)
and
m_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Similar to Equation (
1
), the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-multinomial may be written as

\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{k}}_{C}\right)=s(p)\left(n^{\prime}-\total\mathbf{m^{\prime}}\right)+\nu_{p}(n^{\prime}!)-\sum\nu_{p}(m_{i}^{\prime}!).

Analogously to our proof of Lemma
7
, our casework is split based on the value of
\lambda=n^{\prime}-\total\mathbf{m^{\prime}}
. Specifically, since
n=\total\mathbf{m}
, we may state

r+\lambda\alpha(p)=\total\mathbf{r}.

Therefore, by the bounds on
r
and
r_{i}
,
\lambda
can take on values in
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
. We analyze each by considering
\lambda
as a variable.
Because

\nu_{p}(n^{\prime}!)-\sum\nu_{p}(m_{i}^{\prime}!)=\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{(n^{\prime}-\lambda)!}\right)+\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}-\lambda}{m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime},\cdots,m_{k}^{\prime}}\right),

the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-multinomial is then

(3)

s(p)\lambda+\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{(n^{\prime}-\lambda)!}\right)+\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}-\lambda}{m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime},\cdots,m_{k}^{\prime}}\right).

This allows us to simplify the summation

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m}=n\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,\dots,\,m_{k}}_{C}\right)}

by considering the valuation of a
C
-multinomial in terms of the valuation of multinomials.

For this, we must count the number of tuples
\mathbf{r}\in\{0,1,\ldots\alpha(p)-1\}^{k}
such that
\total\mathbf{r}=r+\lambda\alpha(p)
. This is a type of stars-and-bars counting problem which can be enumerated via the principal of inclusion-exclusion, finding the number of tuples as

(4)

\sum_{j=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}\binom{r+(\lambda-j)\alpha(p)+k-1}{k-1}.

This count is multiplied atop

(5)

x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{(n^{\prime}-\lambda)!}\right)+s(p)\lambda}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m^{\prime}}=n^{\prime}-\lambda\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}(\binom{n^{\prime}-\lambda}{m_{1}^{\prime},\,m_{2}^{\prime},\,\dots,\,m_{k}^{\prime}})}

which comes from Equation (
3
), since for every tuple
\mathbf{m^{\prime}}
where
\total\mathbf{m^{\prime}}=n^{\prime}-\lambda
, each tuple
\mathbf{r}
where
\total\mathbf{r}=r+\lambda\alpha(p)
corresponds to a unique tuple
\mathbf{m}
where
\total\mathbf{m}=n
.
Doing this for every
\lambda\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
enumerates every tuple covered in the

-nomial generating function summation. Therefore, adding the product of (

) and (

) for every

completes the proof.
∎

This leads easily into the proof of Theorem

, which states

T_{p,k,C}(\alpha(p)\cdot n+r,x)=u_{p,k,C}(r)M_{p,k}(n_{0})M_{p,k}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p,k}(n_{\ell})e^{\intercal}

where

.

.

For his definition of

4.
Acceptable Primes

For an acceptable prime
p
, the matrix product formula for counting
C
-nomials are not essentially simpler than the formula for counting
C
-multinomials. We thus do not include a separate proof, as plugging in
k=2
reduces to the binomial analogue.

The rank of apparitions for powers of
p
follow that, for
2\leq k\leq s(p)
, the apparition of
p^{k}
,
\alpha(p^{k})
, may be any value, as long as it is a multiple of the apparition of the previous power
\alpha(p^{k-1})
. For
k>s(p)
, similar to ideal primes, each rank of apparition is
p
times the previous, or
\alpha(p^{k})=p\alpha(p^{k-1})
. In other words, for an acceptable prime
p
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha(p^{k-1})\mid\alpha(p^{k})\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }2\leq k\leq s(p)

\displaystyle\alpha(p^{k})=p^{k-s(p)}\alpha(p^{s(p)})\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }k>s(p).

The Legendre-like formula for acceptable primes is similar but less specific as we expand into more general behavior for
\alpha(p^{k})
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
p
be an acceptable prime for a strong divisibility sequence
C
. Then for any integer
n\geq 0
,

\nu_{p}(n!_{C})=\sum_{1\leq k\leq s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor+\nu_{p}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor!\right).

Proof.

We similarly split the summation in Proposition
5
based on
k
as

\nu_{p}\left(n!_{C}\right)=\sum_{1\leq k\leq s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor+\sum_{k>s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor.

Given that
p
is acceptable, we may use
\alpha(p^{k})
’s form to substitute in the latter addend

\nu_{p}\left(n!_{C}\right)=\sum_{1\leq k\leq s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{k})}\right\rfloor+\sum_{k>s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{k-s(p)}\alpha(p^{s(p)})}\right\rfloor.

The latter term can be similarly simplified as with ideal primes using Legendre’s formula

\sum\limits_{k>s(p)}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{k-s(p)}\alpha(p^{s(p)})}\right\rfloor=\nu_{p}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{s(p)})}\right\rfloor!\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Notably, we now have not only

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor

but all of

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p)}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{2})}\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\alpha(p^{s(p)})}\right\rfloor

present within our
p
-adic valuation of a
C
-orial for an acceptable prime
p
.
This presents a difficulty when simplifying the
p
-adic valuation of a
C
-multinomial. Namely, our casework requires not only comparing each
m_{i}\bmod\alpha(p)
vs.
n\bmod\alpha(p)

as before, but also

m_{i}\bmod\alpha(p^{2})
vs.
n\bmod\alpha(p^{2})
,
\ldots
,
m_{i}\bmod\alpha(p^{s(p)})
vs.
n\bmod\alpha(p^{s(p)})
.

To address this, we use the initial vector as defined in Section
1
. Namely, let

f_{p,k,C,\lambda}(r,\mathbf{r})=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{s(p)}\frac{\alpha(p^{s(p)})}{\alpha(p^{j})}+\sum_{j=1}^{s(p)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{r}{\alpha(p^{j})}\right\rfloor-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lfloor\frac{r_{i}}{\alpha(p^{j})}\right\rfloor\right).

Further, for integer
k\geq 2
, we define a coefficient for each
0\leq\lambda\leq k-1

u_{p,k,C,\lambda}(r)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha(p^{s(p)})-1\}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{r}=r+i\alpha(p^{s(p)})\end{subarray}}x^{f_{p,k,C,\lambda}(r,\mathbf{r})-\lambda}.

Lemma 10
.

Let
p
be an acceptable prime for a strong divisibility sequence
C
and integer
k\geq 2
. For any integer
n\geq 0
, let
n=\alpha(p^{s(p)})n^{\prime}+r
where
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq r<\alpha(p^{s(p)})
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m}=n\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,\dots,\,m_{k}}_{C}\right)}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}u_{p,k,C,i}(r)T_{p,k,i}(n,x).

Proof.

We rely on generalizing the many similarities to the proof of Lemma
8
.
First, we notate

m_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\alpha(p^{s(p)})m_{i}^{\prime}+r_{i}

where
0\leq r_{i}<\alpha(p^{s(p)})
and
m_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.
We start by updating the
p
-adic valuation of the
C
-multinomial given that
p
is acceptable. Recall for an ideal prime, this valuation was (
3
)

s(p)\lambda+\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{(n^{\prime}-\lambda)!}\right)+\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}-\lambda}{m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime},\dots,m_{k}^{\prime}}\right).

While the latter addends are still present in the acceptable valuation, the first addend
s(p)\lambda
is not.
Instead, that addend is replaced with

f_{p,k,C,i}(r,\mathbf{r})=\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{s(p)}\frac{\alpha(p^{s(p)})}{\alpha(p^{j})}+\sum_{j=1}^{s(p)}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{r}{\alpha(p^{j})}\right\rfloor-\sum_{h=1}^{k}\left\lfloor\frac{r_{h}}{\alpha(p^{j})}\right\rfloor\right)

based on the summation in Lemma
9
evaluated over

\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}}_{C}\right)=\nu_{p}(n!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m_{1}!_{C})-\nu_{p}(m_{2}!_{C})-\cdots-\nu_{p}(m_{k}!_{C}).

Like Lemma
8
, enumerating every valid tuple
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{m^{\prime}}
combines to every tuple
\mathbf{m}
. Therefore, the product of

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha(p^{s(p)})-1\}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{r}=r+i\alpha(p^{s(p)})\end{subarray}}x^{f(r,r_{i})}

and

x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}!}{(n^{\prime}-\lambda)!}\right)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m^{\prime}}=n^{\prime}-\lambda\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}\!\left(\binom{n^{\prime}-\lambda}{m_{1}^{\prime},\,m_{2}^{\prime},\,\dots,\,m_{k}^{\prime}}\right)}

for each
0\leq\lambda\leq k-1
is equivalent to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
\total\mathbf{m}=n\end{subarray}}x^{\nu_{p}\left(\binom{n}{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,\dots,\,m_{k}}_{C}\right)}

since the coefficients match for each exponent on
x
, counting the
p
-adic valuation of
C
-multinomials. This summation of products completes the proof.
∎

T_{p,k,C}(\alpha(p^{s(p)})\cdot n+r,x)=u_{p,k,C}(r)M_{p,k}(n_{0})M_{p,k}(n_{1})\cdots M_{p,k}(n_{\ell})e^{\intercal}

Proof of Theorem

.

We reuse the column vector of multinomial generating functions from the proof of Theorem

u_{p,k,C}(r)=\begin{bmatrix}u_{p,k,C,0}(r)&u_{p,k,C,1}(r)&\cdots&u_{p,k,C,k-1}(r)\end{bmatrix}.

This completes the matrix product formula for counting
C
-multinomial coefficients according to their
p
-adic valuation for an acceptable prime
p
.
∎

This is the most general statement our current definitions are capable of finding a formula for. It would be interesting to see if there is another way to count the coefficient
u_{p,k,C,i}(r)
for acceptable primes, perhaps using the principle of inclusion-exclusion repeatedly over each
\alpha(p^{j})
for

. The universality of the matrices, namely their independence on the sequence
