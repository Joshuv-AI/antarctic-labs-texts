Six Times to Spare: LDPC Acceleration on DGX Spark for AI-Native Open RAN
Ryan Barker 1 , Fatemeh Afghah 1

Abstract
Low-density parity-check (LDPC) decoding is one of the most computationally intensive kernels in the 5G New Radio (NR) physical layer and must complete within a 0.5 ms transmission time interval while sharing the budget with FFT, channel estimation, demapping, HARQ, and MAC scheduling. Many open and proprietary stacks still execute LDPC on general-purpose CPUs, raising concerns about missed-slot events and limited scalability as bandwidths, modulation orders, and user multiplexing increase. This paper empirically quantifies the benefit of offloading 5G-style LDPC5G decoding from a Grace CPU to the integrated Blackwell GB10 GPU on an NVIDIA DGX Spark platform. Using NVIDIA Sionna PHY/SYS on TensorFlow, we construct an NR-like link-level chain with an LDPC5G encoder/decoder, 16-QAM modulation, and AWGN, and sweep both the number of codewords decoded in parallel and the number of belief-propagation iterations, timing only the decoding phase while logging CPU and GPU utilization and power. Across the sweep we observe an average GPU/CPU throughput speedup of approximately 6 × 6\times , with per-codeword CPU latency reaching ≈ 0.71 \approx 0.71 ms at 20 iterations (exceeding the 0.5 ms slot), while the GB10 GPU remains within 6–24% of the slot for the same workloads. Resource-usage measurements show that CPU-based LDPC decoding often consumes around ten Grace cores, whereas GPU-based decoding adds only ≈ 10 − 15 \approx 10-15 W over GPU idle while leaving most CPU capacity available for higher-layer tasks. Because our implementation relies on high-level Sionna layers rather than hand-tuned CUDA, these results represent conservative lower bounds on achievable accelerator performance and provide a reusable, scriptable methodology for evaluating LDPC and other physical-layer kernels on future Grace/Blackwell and Aerial/ACAR/AODT platforms.



I
LDPC, 5G RAN, and Computational Ceilings

Fifth–generation (5G) and emerging sixth–generation (6G) radio access networks push the physical layer toward the limits of software realization. New Radio (NR) mandates low–density parity–check (LDPC) codes for data–channel forward error correction (FEC), replacing turbo codes and requiring iterative message–passing decoders over quasi-cyclic base graphs spanning many block lengths and code rates
[
2
,
5
]
. These decoders must complete within the stringent real-time budget of NR: roughly a 0.5 ms transmission time interval (TTI) per slot in frequency range 1 (FR1), or 1 ms per subframe, while sharing the same deadline with FFTs, channel estimation and equalization, demapping, HARQ combining, and MAC-layer scheduling. In practice LDPC decoding becomes a dominant compute hotspot in layer 1 (L1), and many open/proprietary stacks still rely on CPU-only implementations
[
6
]
. As bandwidths, modulation orders, and user multiplexing increase, such CPU-bound LDPC decoders strain timing and power budgets, motivating migration of FEC to accelerators including GPUs, FPGAs, and ASICs
[
1
]
.

Most existing LDPC acceleration results, however, are reported either for custom hardware or for high-end discrete datacenter GPUs. FPGA and ASIC designs can reach tens of Gbit/s, but at the cost of long design cycles and reduced flexibility. Recent GPU-based LDPC decoders demonstrate multi-Gbit/s throughput with hand-tuned CUDA kernels, advanced data scheduling, and early-termination mechanisms
[
1
]
, yet they typically assume large standalone GPUs with generous power and cooling budgets. Much less is known about how LDPC behaves on emerging edge–HPC platforms that co-locate a modest GPU with a general-purpose CPU under tight power envelopes. NVIDIA’s DGX Spark, based on a single Grace CPU and integrated Blackwell GB10 GPU, represents this class of device: powerful enough to host RAN workloads, but far from the scale of an eight–GPU training server. From a RAN system-design perspective, articulated in the framework for hardware partitioning in Open and AI-native RANs, an important open question is therefore:
for a realistic 5G-style LDPC chain on such a Grace+GPU platform, how much benefit does GPU offload provide over CPU execution in terms of latency, throughput, and resource usage?

To answer this question we implement a NR-like LDPC5G link-level benchmark using NVIDIA Sionna PHY/SYS on top of TensorFlow
[
4
]
. We instantiate the same LDPC encoder/decoder, modulation, and AWGN channel model on both the Grace CPU and the GB10 GPU, and sweep key parameters including the number of codewords decoded in parallel and the number of belief-propagation iterations. For each configuration we time the LDPC decoding phase, average over multiple repetitions, and log latency and throughput into a CSV dataset. In parallel, monitoring scripts record CPU utilization and GPU utilization and power via
nvidia-smi
, enabling us to correlate performance with system-level resource consumption. Despite using a high-level Python+TensorFlow implementation (far from hand-tuned CUDA), the results are already striking: across the sweep, GPU decoding delivers a roughly
6\times
throughput speedup over the CPU, with per-codeword GPU latency corresponding to only 6–24% of the 0.5 ms NR slot, while CPU decoding often consumes 70% or more of the slot and can exceed the deadline at higher iteration counts. This conservative setup thus provides a lower bound on the benefit of moving LDPC (and, by extension, other numerically intensive L1 kernels) from CPU to accelerators such as GH200.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

We compare LDPC5G decoding on the Grace CPU and integrated GB10 GPU of an NVIDIA DGX Spark, using Sionna PHY/SYS to implement an NR-style LDPC chain and sweeping both codeword batch size and decoder iteration count.

•

We relate per-codeword CPU and GPU latency to the 0.5 ms NR slot time, showing that CPU-only LDPC can exceed slot duration at moderate iteration counts, whereas GPU decoding remains within 6–24% of the slot across all tested configurations.

•

We measure resource usage during the sweep, including Grace CPU core occupancy, GB10 utilization, and GPU power draw; demonstrating that LDPC is a major CPU hotspot but a modest GPU load, adding only about 10–15 W over GPU idle.

•

We interpret these findings in a RAN-architecture context, reinforcing the argument that heavy L1 kernels should be mapped to accelerators while CPUs focus on control, MAC, and orchestration, and we position our results relative to prior GPU- and FPGA-based LDPC decoders.

II
Jumpstarting DGX Spark: Experimental Setup and Measurement Methodology

Our experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA DGX Spark development system equipped with a single Grace CPU and integrated Blackwell GB10 GPU. The Grace CPU exposes 20 Armv9 cores (10 Cortex–X925 and 10 Cortex–A725), while the GB10 is a modest Blackwell GPU integrated into the same SoC with a 140 W platform TDP. The software stack consists of Ubuntu on
aarch64
, a Python 3 virtual environment, TensorFlow 2.17, and Sionna 1.2.1 (PHY/SYS)
[
4
]
. We construct a 5G NR–like LDPC chain using Sionna’s LDPC5G encoder and decoder, a 16–QAM constellation, and an additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) channel. The encoder maps
k=512
information bits to
n=1024
coded bits, yielding a rate
R=k/n=1/2
. Modulated symbols are corrupted by complex AWGN with noise spectral density
N_{0}
obtained from a target
E_{b}/N_{0}
value via
N_{0}=\tfrac{E_{s}}{R\cdot 10^{E_{b}/N_{0}/10}}
, and Sionna’s demapper produces log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) that are consumed by the LDPC5G decoder.

For each configuration of decoder parameters we generate one large dataset of random codewords and corresponding LLRs and reuse this dataset across devices to avoid confounding timing with channel variability. Concretely, we draw
N_{\mathrm{cw}}
random information blocks of length
k
, pass them through the encoder, QAM mapper, and AWGN channel, and store the resulting LLR tensor of shape
[N_{\mathrm{cw}},n]
. We then instantiate the same TensorFlow graph twice, once under
/CPU:0
and once under
/GPU:0
, so that the only difference between the two executions is the underlying hardware. For each device we perform a warm-up decode that is excluded from timing to trigger any lazy graph compilation, and then execute a loop of
\texttt{repeat}=10
decodes on the fixed LLR tensor. If
t_{\mathrm{tot}}
denotes the wall-clock time for these ten decodes, we define the average latency per decode (one batch of
N_{\mathrm{cw}}
codewords) as
t_{\mathrm{dec}}=t_{\mathrm{tot}}/10
and the corresponding information-bit throughput as

T_{\mathrm{thr}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{cw}}\cdot k\cdot 10}{t_{\mathrm{tot}}}\quad[\text{bit/s}],

(1)

which we log in Mbit/s. We also derive per-codeword latency
t_{\mathrm{cb}}=t_{\mathrm{dec}}/N_{\mathrm{cw}}
for later comparison against the 0.5 ms NR slot time.

We explore LDPC decoder performance over a range of batch sizes and iteration counts. An initial coarse ablation uses
N_{\mathrm{cw}}\in\{4096,8192,16384\}
and a number of belief-propagation iterations
I\in\{5,10,20\}
, mirroring typical operating points in NR. To obtain finer-grained trends for our final analysis, we extend this into a dense sweep where
N_{\mathrm{cw}}
ranges from 4096 to 20480 in steps of 256 and
I
ranges from 4 to 22 in single-iteration increments. For every pair
(N_{\mathrm{cw}},I)
we execute the procedure described above on both Grace and GB10, and append a row to a master CSV file
ldpc_sionna_spark.csv
. Each row includes the LDPC configuration
(k,n,R,I)
, batch size
N_{\mathrm{cw}}
,
E_{b}/N_{0}
, the
repeat
count, device-agnostic metadata (timestamp, host, software versions), the measured CPU and GPU latencies and throughputs, and the derived speedup metrics
S_{\mathrm{lat}}=t_{\mathrm{dec}}^{\mathrm{CPU}}/t_{\mathrm{dec}}^{\mathrm{GPU}}
and
S_{\mathrm{thr}}=T_{\mathrm{thr}}^{\mathrm{GPU}}/T_{\mathrm{thr}}^{\mathrm{CPU}}
.

To correlate performance with system-level resource usage, we concurrently run lightweight monitoring tools. The first tool periodically invokes
nvidia-smi
at 1 Hz and logs GB10 utilization, memory usage, temperature, and instantaneous power draw into
gpu_ldpc_sweep_stats.csv
. The second tool uses
pidstat
at 1 Hz to record user and system CPU usage for the Python LDPC process, writing a textual log that we later parse into
pid_ldpc_sweep_stats.log.csv
. From these logs we derive approximate Grace core usage by dividing the aggregate CPU percentage by 100 and tag each sample as “active” or “idle” according to simple thresholds on GPU utilization and process CPU usage. A post-processing pipeline joins configuration metadata with time-series statistics where appropriate, and computes a range of aggregate metrics that we later report in the results section. These include, for each
(N_{\mathrm{cw}},I)
configuration and across the full sweep: means, standard deviations, minima, maxima, and selected percentiles (e.g., median, 5th/95th) of throughput, latency per decode, per-codeword latency
t_{\mathrm{cb}}
, GPU/CPU speedup, GPU utilization and power, and effective Grace core usage. We also derive quantities such as the fraction of the 0.5 ms slot consumed by LDPC, the distribution of speedup across batch sizes and iteration counts, and total decode time accumulated per device.

III-D

Resource Usage: CPU vs GPU

Figure

The left panel shows the distribution of approximate Grace core usage for the Python LDPC process, derived from per-process CPU percentages sampled at 1 Hz. Active samples (where the process CPU exceeds a modest threshold) cluster tightly around 10–12 cores, with a long tail reaching up to roughly 17 cores; the mean active usage is close to 10 cores on this 20-core system. This confirms that LDPC decoding is a substantial CPU hotspot: even in the presence of our relatively small LDPC configuration, the decoding process frequently consumes about half of the available cores, leaving limited headroom for MAC, RLC/PDCP, scheduler threads, and container or OS overheads on the same node.

The right panel of Figure

During decoding, GB10 is typically highly utilized: the median active utilization is around 94%, and the mean is roughly 72%. Despite this, power draw remains modest. Active samples have an average GPU power of approximately 25–26 W, with peaks around 32 W, compared to an idle power of about 13–14 W. Thus, LDPC decoding roughly doubles the GPU power consumption but remains well below the SoC’s 140 W TDP and far below the power envelopes of large discrete accelerators. The GPU provides substantial computational acceleration at relatively low incremental power cost, while simultaneously freeing a significant fraction of the Grace CPU for other RAN tasks.

IV
Implications for Hardware Partitioning in AI-RAN Architectures

The results in Section
III
empirically support the view that LDPC and similar numerically intensive L1 kernels are ill-suited to CPU-only execution at realistic iteration counts and carrier bandwidths. On Grace, LDPC decoding frequently consumes roughly half of the available cores and can exceed the 0.5 ms NR slot at higher iteration counts, leaving little slack for FFTs, channel estimation, demapping, HARQ combining, or MAC scheduling. Offloading the same decoder to GB10 both restores timing headroom and frees on the order of ten CPU cores that can instead host MAC/RLC/PDCP processing, control-plane functions, and container or orchestration overheads. From a system-design perspective, these cores are often the difference between a single-node RAN deployment that remains within timing margins and one that requires additional hosts or hardware accelerators.

It is important to emphasize that Grace+GB10 represents a relatively modest accelerator configuration. The GB10 is an integrated GPU with a modest power envelope, and our implementation relies on high-level Sionna layers executed through TensorFlow, without any custom CUDA kernels or hand-optimized memory layouts. Larger Grace–Hopper and Grace–Blackwell systems (GH200, GB200) paired with Aerial cuPHY-style kernels are expected to outperform this baseline substantially, both by exploiting more GPU SMs and by reducing software overheads. As such, the latency and throughput figures reported here should be interpreted as conservative lower bounds on achievable LDPC performance on modern accelerator-rich RAN platforms.

The per-codeword latency fractions relative to the 0.5 ms NR slot provide concrete guidance for RAN scheduling and hardware partitioning. On the CPU, LDPC decoding alone can consume 70–140% of the slot at typical iteration counts, forcing operators either to reduce iterations (and potentially degrade block error rate) or to accept missed-slot events. On the GPU, by contrast, LDPC occupies only 6–24% of the slot in our experiments, leaving hundreds of microseconds for other L1 and L2 tasks. This slack is particularly important in URLLC scenarios and high-MCS FR1/FR2 operation, where higher reliability requirements and harsher channel conditions push iteration counts upward and where deterministic, low-jitter scheduling is critical.

At the same time, several simplifying assumptions in our methodology must be acknowledged. Because Sionna-RT is currently incompatible with DGX OS’s supported TensorFlow, our link-level benchmark uses an AWGN channel rather than realistic 3GPP fading models, omits HARQ combining and early-termination logic, and fixes a single LDPC code configuration; each of these choices trades realism for experimental clarity. Further, our implementation is built on Sionna and TensorFlow rather than vendor-optimized kernels, and the current DGX Spark environment cannot run NVIDIA Aerial/ACAR with DOCA-based fronthaul due to lack of platform-level support for GPUDirect RDMA (despite an onboard ConnectX7 NIC). Notably, most of these limitations bias
against
the GPU: realistic channels and early termination typically reduce average decoding effort, while lower-level CUDA implementations reduce framework overhead. Consequently, the qualitative gap between CPU and GPU observed here is likely to persist (or widen) under more realistic stacks.

Finally, there are methodological aspects that could be refined in future work. We average over ten decodes per configuration to smooth short-term variability and sample CPU and GPU telemetry at 1 Hz, which is sufficient for aggregate statistics but cannot resolve fine-grained intra-decode dynamics. Higher-frequency sampling, explicit separation of one-time graph-building costs from steady-state decode latency, and hardware performance-counter measurements (e.g., memory bandwidth, cache misses) would provide a more detailed picture of where time is spent on each device. Nevertheless, the current methodology already yields a clear and internally consistent picture of LDPC behavior on Grace and GB10, sufficient to support the architectural conclusions drawn in this paper.

V
LDPC Acceleration and Digital-Twin Toolchains for 5G/6G RANs

Low-density parity-check codes were first introduced by Gallager in the 1960s
[
2
]
and later rediscovered and popularized by MacKay and Neal, who demonstrated their near-capacity performance on a variety of channels. Since then, LDPC codes have been adopted in numerous communication standards, including WiFi (IEEE 802.11n/ac/ax), WiMAX, DVB-S2, and more recently 5G New Radio, where they serve as the mandated data-channel FEC for both downlink and uplink shared channels
[
5
]
. The combination of sparse parity-check matrices and iterative belief-propagation decoding offers excellent error-correction performance but poses significant implementation challenges, particularly in systems with tight real-time constraints and high codeword throughput requirements.

A large body of work has explored dedicated LDPC decoders implemented on ASICs and FPGAs, often achieving multi-Gbit/s throughputs and low latencies by exploiting massive parallelism and carefully optimized memory architectures. These designs typically target specific standards (e.g., DVB-S2 or 5G NR base graphs) and make extensive use of pipelining, quantization, and architecture-specific scheduling to maximize area and energy efficiency. While such accelerators deliver impressive absolute performance, they are costly to design and lack the flexibility of SDN approaches: changing code structures, decoder algorithms, or system-level integrations often requires substantial redesign. Moreover, they are not directly comparable to the general-purpose, accelerator-enabled research platforms considered here, where ease of experimentation and co-location with higher-layer software are key.

In parallel, several works have demonstrated that GPUs can serve as powerful LDPC accelerators. Dai
et al.
propose a multi-Gbit/s GPU LDPC decoder that combines layered belief propagation, early termination, and memory-coalescing strategies to achieve throughputs competitive with FPGA solutions on discrete GPUs
[
1
]
. Other studies extend GPU-based decoding to 5G NR scenarios, exploring different base-graph mappings, quantization schemes, and kernel organizations for cloud-RAN deployments. These efforts generally focus on maximizing standalone LDPC throughput on high-end desktop or datacenter GPUs, using hand-tuned CUDA kernels and low-level optimizations. By contrast, our goal is not to propose a new fastest decoder, but to quantify the
relative
performance and resource usage of CPU versus GPU execution for a realistic LDPC5G workload on an integrated Grace+GB10 SoC, within a full Python/TensorFlow/Sionna stack.

Our work is also closely related to NVIDIA Sionna and to broader efforts around RAN digital twins. Sionna provides a differentiable, GPU-accelerated library for link-level simulation, exposing LDPC, polar codes, OFDM, and 3GPP-compliant channel models as TensorFlow layers to enable end-to-end learning and rapid prototyping
[
4
,
3
]
. We can frame the evolving O-RAN and AI-RAN ecosystem in terms of three classes of “computers”—CPUs, accelerators (GPUs/ASICs/FPGAs), and testbed/digital twin platforms—and argue for careful hardware partitioning across them. Our experiment occupies the Sionna-based, accelerator-aware link-level tier in this taxonomy: we use Sionna to implement NR-like LDPC workloads and then measure how moving them from CPU to GPU affects timing and resource usage. These results in turn motivate future work that integrates Aerial/ACAR/AODT on GH200-class platforms to extend the same methodology to end-to-end RAN digital twins.

VI
Summary of Findings and Architectural Lessons for Accelerator-Centric PHY

This paper presented an empirical study of LDPC5G decoding on an NVIDIA DGX Spark, comparing execution on the Grace CPU and the integrated Blackwell GB10 GPU using a Sionna PHY/SYS implementation of a 5G NR–like link-level chain. Across a sweep of batch sizes and iteration counts, we observed a consistent GPU/CPU throughput speedup of approximately
6\times
, with individual configurations ranging between about
5.5\times
and
6.3\times
. When translated into per-codeword latency, Grace CPU decoding can exceed the 0.5 ms NR slot at 20 iterations, while GB10 remains within 24% of the slot at the same operating point and as low as 6% at fewer iterations. Resource-usage measurements further show that CPU-based decoding routinely consumes on the order of ten Grace cores, whereas GPU-based decoding adds only about 10–15 W over GPU idle while keeping most CPU cores available for higher-layer tasks. Together, these results quantify how LDPC decoding behaves on a realistic edge/HPC platform and underscore the efficiency of accelerator offload in both time and power.

These findings have important implications for 5G and emerging 6G RAN design. They reinforce that hardware acceleration (via GPU, FPGA, or ASIC) is not optional for LDPC5G decoding at realistic iteration counts and reliability targets: CPU-only implementations quickly erode or exceed slot budgets as bandwidths, modulation orders, and user multiplexing increase. At the same time, the results demonstrate that even a relatively small, integrated GPU such as GB10 can meaningfully reshape the timing and resource profile of the L1 stack, converting LDPC from a slot-dominating bottleneck into a modest consumer of the TTI. By relying on a Sionna- and TensorFlow-based implementation rather than hand-tuned CUDA, our methodology provides a conservative lower bound on accelerator gains and a reusable, scriptable template for evaluating other platforms and PHY kernels with the same experimental harness.

More broadly, the study supports a move toward accelerator-centric physical layers in open and AI-native RAN stacks. Within distributed architectures, LDPC and related L1 kernels clearly belong on the “accelerator computer,” while the “CPU computer” focuses on MAC, RLC/PDCP, orchestration, and AI-driven control. Our measurements on DGX Spark provide one concrete data point in this design space and help inform future deployments of NVIDIA Aerial, ACAR, and the Aerial Omniverse Digital Twin on Grace/Blackwell platforms. We expect that the qualitative conclusions—that accelerators are necessary to meet deterministic slot timing for LDPC and that relatively modest GPUs can already provide substantial benefits—will hold across a wide range of RAN hardware configurations.

VII
Toward Sionna-RT and Aerial ACAR/AODT on Grace-Hopper/Grace–Blackwell and GH200/GB200

Several extensions follow naturally from this work. On the link-level side, we plan to incorporate early-termination logic and more realistic 3GPP channel models (e.g., TR 38.901 fading) into the Sionna experiment to better capture iteration variability and channel-induced workload fluctuations. Doing so on DGX Spark will require enabling Sionna-RT in addition to the PHY/SYS components used here, since geometry-based and ray-traced channels are exposed through Sionna’s RT module. At present, this expansion is blocked by an environmental dependency conflict between the GB10-enabled TensorFlow build supported by DGX OS (i.e.,
nvidia-tensorflow
2.17.0+nv25.2) and Sionna/Sionna-RT’s declared requirement
(
tensorflow!=2.16,!=2.17,>=2.14
)
, which causes
pip
to attempt to install an upstream TensorFlow 2.20 wheel and Sionna-RT (via
mitsuba
) that are not available for
aarch64
. Resolving this conflict—either by aligning Sionna-RT with the NGC TensorFlow build or refactoring the installation procedure so that RT features can be used without overwriting the GB10-supported TensorFlow stack—is therefore a prerequisite for extending our experiment to full Sionna-RT channel models.

In parallel, exploring a broader set of LDPC configurations, including different base graphs, block lengths, rates, and layered decoding schedules, as well as extending the benchmarking framework to other PHY kernels such as OFDM FFT/IFFT, channel equalization, and MIMO detection, will help build a more complete hardware performance profile for the L1 stack. On the measurement side, higher-frequency telemetry, explicit separation of graph-build overhead from steady-state decoding, and access to hardware performance counters would refine our understanding of where time and energy are spent and further inform accelerator-centric PHY design.

In parallel, additional work is required to bring the broader AI-RAN software stack into alignment with DGX Spark and future Blackwell architecture systems. Our current study uses Sionna PHY/SYS in a Python environment that is already adapted to GB10, but NVIDIA Aerial components such as cuPHY, cuMAC, PyAerial, and DOCA- or GPUNetIO-based fronthaul still require platform-specific enables and CUDA kernel tuning for Grace–Blackwell targets. This includes compiling all CUDA code for the appropriate SM architectures, reworking kernels to exploit Blackwell tensor cores and memory hierarchies, resolving container-level GPU allow-list checks, and bridging x86-only dependencies (e.g., MATLAB-based test-vector generation) into workflows that can feed ARM/GB10 hosts. Incorporating these low-level optimizations and platform support into an integrated testbed will allow future experiments to transition from high-level Sionna benchmarks to production-quality AI-RAN pipelines that run natively on DGX Spark and related systems.

In the longer term, we aim to port the methodology to GH200, GB200, and BF-3 DPU-class systems and integrate it with NVIDIA Aerial cuPHY and ACAR/AODT to study end-to-end RAN pipelines, including LDPC, scheduling, and fronthaul, under realistic traffic patterns. Within the “three computer” framework, such experiments would enable systematic co-design of CPU, accelerator, and control-plane partitioning, using the LDPC results presented here as one anchor point in a broader exploration of AI-RAN architectures across multiple hardware generations. Ultimately, we envision a workflow in which link-level studies like ours, conducted on accelerator-rich platforms with Sionna and related tools, feed directly into digital-twin evaluations of full O-RAN deployments and guide practical hardware and software decisions for 5G and 6G networks on Grace/Blackwell and successor platforms.
