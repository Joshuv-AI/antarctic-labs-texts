Differential associations between early life adversity subtypes and adolescent stress reactivity across neuroendocrine, autonomic, immune, and metabolic systems
Authors: Kate R Kuhlman, Alexander J Pugh, Emma L Rodgers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly life adversity (ELA) is a heterogeneous construct that can include the consequences of poverty, maltreatment, or serious accidents. This study characterized associations between subtypes of ELA and the physiological responses of multiple systems to acute psychosocial stress. Studies to date have focused on HPA axis reactivity, often neglecting the stress-reactive SNS, HPG axis, metabolic, and innate immune systems. We hypothesized that emotional, physical, and non-intentional ELA would differentially affect stress reactivity in each of these systems. Nine salivary samples, collected serially across the administration of a Trier Social Stress Test for Children (TSST-C), were collected from 92 adolescents [mean age 13.9 (SD = 1.57)] and assayed for cortisol, testosterone, DHEA, uric acid, salivary ɑ-amylase, and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Emotional ELA was associated with a steeper cortisol response to the stressor [b(SE) = 0.007 (0.003), p = .013]. Physical ELA was associated with IL-6 having steeper activation [b(SE) = 0.002 (0.001), p = .047] and recovery slopes [b(SE) = 0.00002 (0.00001), p = .03]. After accounting for age, sex, and body mass index, associations between physical ELA and IL-6 were attenuated. Findings are aligned with the theory of biological salience, which states physiological systems are differentially sensitive to experiences of adversity throughout childhood. Indeed, that different types of ELA are differentially associated with stress reactivity suggests a need for specificity and nuance in studies exploring associations between childhood environment and later biological functioning. Different forms of ELA are likely differentially salient to physiological systems that have distinct and evolutionarily conserved functions which may shape how the individual responds to psychosocial stress later in life. If confirmed with further evidence, biological salience may help to explain previously equivocal associations between ELA and adverse lifespan health outcomes.

Introduction

Early life adversity (ELA) comprises experiences of chronic stress during childhood such as living in poverty, maltreatment by caregivers, housing or food instability, and exposure to household dysfunction including violence, substance abuse, or mental illness (Felitti et al., 1998; Kessler et al., 2010; McLaughlin et al., 2010). Cumulative exposure to ELA has been linked to myriad stress-related disorders across the lifespan, most notably depression (Chapman et al., 2004; Walker et al., 1999). One mechanism linking ELA to stress-related disorders may be that ELA alters physiological responses to stress. Many studies have focused on exploring associations between ELA and reactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA axis) to stress, but associations between ELA and other physiological systems known to be responsive to psychosocial stressors remain understudied in comparison. The present study aimed to extend understanding of ELA's role in physiological responses to stress as indexed by reactivity within the HPA axis, hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG axis), autonomic nervous system (ANS), innate immune system, and the metabolic system.

Several physiological systems show increased activity in the acute aftermath of psychosocial stress (Allen et al., 2014; Russell and Lightman, 2019). Upon recognition of a threat, the ANS is engaged (including sympathetic nervous system and catecholamine signaling). The salivary glands are under autonomic control which can lead to changes in salivary flow as well as salivary secretions, including salivary ɑ-amylase (Bosch et al., 2011; Nater and Rohleder, 2009). ɑ-amylase is an enzyme that breaks down carbohydrates into simple sugars that can be readily used for energy. Autonomic activity also causes circulating immune cells to release innate proinflammatory cytokines, which help to initiate both sickness behavior as well as recruit immune cells for pathogen defense (Bluthé et al., 2000). One of the earliest and most abundant cytokines released in this context is interleukin (IL)-6 (Marsland et al., 2017; Steptoe et al., 2007). Acute psychosocial stress additionally activates both the HPA axis and the HPG axis. Activation of the HPA axis ultimately results in the release of glucocorticoids (cortisol) from the adrenal gland, which serves to convert nutrients into readily available energy as well as inhibit activation of the axis via negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary. The adrenal gland can also release dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) which serves a neuroprotective role against glucocorticoids as well as a precursor to gonadal hormones, such as testosterone (Maninger et al., 2009). This is, in part, how HPA axis activation is linked with the release of testosterone via the HPG axis. Acute increases in testosterone are associated with greater muscle strength and increased motivation that can facilitate dominance or survival in altercations with other individuals. Acute psychosocial stress also leads to an increase in uric acid (Acevedo et al., 2022; Lucas et al., 2020). Uric acid is a waste product made in the liver and filtered through the kidneys after breaking down purines in food and other cells. Uric acid increases as a byproduct of increased demands for energy in the context of stress and therefore represents a downstream marker of metabolic activity. Given these parallel physiological processes, ɑ-amylase, IL-6, cortisol, DHEA, testosterone, and uric acid are all biomarkers with the potential to reflect individual differences in response to acute psychosocial stress.

ELA can lead to poorer lifespan health via alterations to these stress response systems. ELA may alter the systems responsible for allostasis, or physiological adaptations to changing environmental conditions or demands. Chronic activations of autonomic, neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic systems that occur in response to ELA may lead to cumulative damage to these systems (Danese and McEwen, 2012). In some cases, this damage can be manifested as either hyper- or hypo-responsiveness to subsequent stressors, as seen among patients with depression or posttraumatic stress disorder respectively (Heim and Nemeroff, 2001; Mayer et al., 2020). Indeed, seminal preclinical experiments have shown that variability in the type, timing, and quality of early caregiving can lead to both hyper- and hyporesponsivity of the HPA axis (Levine, 2000). Changes to physiological systems that occur in the wake of chronic stress exposure are commonly referred to as allostatic load. Over time, frequent stress-system activation may tune how people respond to stress, helping them function in similar adult environments (Del Giudice et al., 2011; Kuhlman et al., 2017). For example, a meta-analysis of HPA axis activity following psychosocial stress in the laboratory showed that individuals with more exposure to ELA show attenuated responses (Bunea et al., 2017) suggesting the system has reduced capacity for reactivity over time or has proactively reduced reactivity to minimize exposure to the neurotoxic effects of glucocorticoids. As previously noted, many studies have focused on the HPA axis, indexed predominantly by cortisol, neglecting the other systems known to be activated by psychosocial stressors and essential to an individual's success in responding to their environment. The present study extends this literature by characterizing the associations between ELA and acute reactivity to psychosocial stress during adolescence as indexed simultaneously by markers reflecting autonomic, neuroendocrine, innate immune, and metabolic reactivity.

Further, existing literature linking ELA to acute reactivity of the HPA axis has been equivocal. Meta-analytic evidence supports a link between ELA and attenuated HPA reactivity to acute stress in adults (Bunea et al., 2017), whereas other studies have documented exaggerated HPA reactivity. These mixed associations likely reflect the heterogeneity of ELA and complexity of effects different forms of ELA may have on development. One factor that may contribute to heterogeneity in associations between ELA and physiological stress reactivity is the type of ELA. ELA can vary widely from experiences that are characterized by threat and physical injury (e.g., physical maltreatment, sexual assault), unpredictability (unstable housing, non-intentional traumas such as motor vehicle accidents), or absence of nurturing caregiving (loss of a parent, emotional or physical neglect). While different types of ELA frequently co-occur making it difficult to isolate associations, each of these types of ELA differ substantially in their salience to developing biological systems (Kuhlman et al., 2017). For example, repeated exposure to conditions that require the innate immune system to engage in wound repair or preempt protection from bacterial infection after an injury may have different developmental implications than developing under conditions in which a caregiver isn't able or willing to facilitate recovery from emotionally arousing situations. Indeed, when the roles of different types of ELA are isolated, physical ELA has been related to a more rapid activation of the HPA axis while emotional ELA has been associated with a slower down-regulation of cortisol during stress recovery (Kuhlman et al., 2015). Although the cooccurrence of different forms of stress and interdependence of physiological stress response systems make clear one-to-one associations elusive, the present study aimed not only to link ELA to acute reactivity across multiple stress-responsive physiological systems, but also to isolate the role of different types of ELA in these patterns of reactivity.

In summary, the present study aimed to extend understanding of the links between ELA and physiological stress reactivity in two ways: 1) by characterizing the association between both cumulative ELA and subtypes of ELA with physiological responses to acute psychosocial stress in the laboratory, and 2) by indexing physiological stress reactivity across multiple systems using five salivary biomarkers: cortisol, DHEA, ɑ-amylase, IL-6, testosterone, and uric acid. We hypothesized that the associations between ELA and physiological stress reactivity would not be specific to the HPA axis, and that ELA characterized by physical threat would be associated with exaggerated reactivity as measured by markers reflecting downstream effects of autonomic activity (e.g., ɑ-amylase, IL-6), whereas emotional ELA would be more closely associated with exaggerated reactivity within neuroendocrine systems (e.g., cortisol, DHEA, testosterone). Importantly, these hypotheses were broad, theory-guided predictions about relative patterns of association rather than system-specific responses to individual ELA subtypes.

Method

Participants

Participants in this study were 92 adolescents between the ages of 11 and 17 years [mean age 13.9 (SD = 1.57); 45.7% female] who participated in the Teen Resilience Project (Kuhlman et al., 2022, 2023, 2025; Rodgers and Kuhlman, 2023) and provided a minimum of one pre- and one post-stress saliva sample. Participants were excluded from participation in the parent project if they had a current or past diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), were prescribed and taking medications that affect the HPA axis or the immune system on a daily basis, had an intellectual disability, could not speak or understand English, or had a chronic medical condition that would impact primary study outcomes (e.g., cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, a bleeding disorder).

Procedures

All study procedures were approved by the institutional review boards at the Irvine and Los Angeles campuses of the University of California. Participants were recruited via mass mailing based on addresses with children likely to be in the target age range (12-15 years) based on data provided during the most recent census. Mailings included a study flyer with details on the purpose of the study, the procedures involved in participating, and eligibility criteria, as well as a letter of invitation to participate. Interested families could contact the lab directly to express their interest in learning more or participating. Once a family contacted the lab, they completed a 15-min phone interview and eligible participants were scheduled for their enrollment appointment. All data for the present analyses were collected during this enrollment visit.

All enrollment visits began between 12pm and 4pm to constrain the influence of circadian variation on biological markers. Additionally, participants were asked not to eat within an hour of arrival to the visit. With very few exceptions, no food intake was allowed or provided until after the final [90 min post-Trier Social Stress Test for Children (TSST-C) onset) biological samples were collected. Participants were in the lab for 3 h, during which they and their parents provided informed assent and consent, respectively, completed questionnaires, completed computer-administered behavioral tasks, had an in-dwelling catheter inserted for serial blood collection, provided 9 saliva samples, and underwent the TSST–C. The catheter insertion and initial blood collection occurred immediately before the start of the TSST-C to consolidate potentially stressful procedures within the protocol. The catheter insertion and the start of the TSST-C also occurred at least 60 min after arrival to allow for accommodation to the laboratory environment. More details are provided in Kuhlman and colleagues (2022). Saliva samples were collected by trained research staff via passive drool into sterile salivettes. Participants had 2 min to provide each sample, and a minimum of 1 ml of saliva was collected at each timepoint.

The TSST-C was used to induce acute psychosocial stress (Buske-Kirschbaum et al., 1997). The TSST-C involved two trained research staff wearing white laboratory coats evaluating the participant after telling them to prepare and deliver an “exciting ending” to a story (10 min) and then perform mental arithmetic (5 min). These TSST-C judges remained affectively neutral to emphasize the socially-evaluative nature of the task. Immediately after completing the mental arithmetic portion of the task, the judges left the experiment room while another staff member collected an affective rating and a saliva sample. Once these measures had been collected, the judges returned to the experiment room to debrief the participant. After this debrief, participants entered a recovery phase of the laboratory visit during which they watched a nature video while providing serial saliva and affect ratings at 15-min intervals. After collection, all saliva samples were refrigerated and then transferred to the UCI Center for Biomarker & Biobehavior Research (formerly Institute for Interdisciplinary Salivary Bioscience), where they were stored at −80 °C until data collection was completed. All assays were conducted in a single batch and all samples from the same participant were assayed on the same plate. Prior to assay, each sample was weighed for total saliva volume and coded for contamination, discoloration, and mucin content. Observations were then dummy coded for the presence of these factors and sensitivity analyses were conducted to determine whether concentrations varied meaningfully as a function of them. See previous publications from the parent study for more details on procedures (Kuhlman et al., 2022, 2023; Rodgers and Kuhlman, 2023).

Measures

Early life adversity

Participants self-reported their exposure to ELA via the Early Trauma Inventory (ETI) (Bremner et al., 2000, 2007). Participants were asked to indicate whether they had been exposed to 37 adversity experiences with either a yes or no. These adversity experiences fall into 4 mutually exclusive categories: non-intentional trauma, physical trauma, emotional trauma, and sexual trauma. Very few participants endorsed experiencing sexual trauma, so analyses were unable to model independent associations with that subtype.

Cortisol

Salivary cortisol was used to index HPA axis reactivity to psychosocial stress. Cortisol was measured in saliva using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Salimetrics, Inc.). Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 13 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. The range of detection for this assay kit was 0.007 - 3.00 μg/dL. In order to compute intra-assay CV, 20% of samples were assayed in duplicate. Inter-assay CV for cortisol in this sample were 3.04% and intra-assay CV were 3.73%.

ɑ-amylase

ɑ-amylase was used as a salivary index of autonomic arousal in the context of psychosocial stress. Salivary ɑ-amylase was quantified using an enzymatic assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Salimetrics, Inc.). Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 10 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. The range of detection for this assay kit was 0.40 - 400 U/ml. To compute intra-assay CV, 20% of samples were assayed in duplicate. Samples that returned a concentration outside the range of detection for the assay or that demonstrated low concordance with their duplicate assay were retested (n = 13) to confirm the result. Inter-assay CV for ɑ-amylase in this sample were 3.69% and intra-assay CV were 3.72%.

Interleukin 6

Salivary IL-6 was used to index innate immune reactivity to psychosocial stress. IL-6 was measured in saliva using a standard ELISA kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Specifically, IL-6 was measured in a multiplex alongside IL-10 and TNF-α using the V-PLEX Custom Human Cytokine Proinflammatory Panel on the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) electrochemiluminescence platform and Discovery Workbench software (MSD, Rockville, MD). Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 7 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. The range of detection for IL-6 in this assay kit was 0.63-488 pg/mL. All samples were tested in duplicate. Inter-assay CV for IL-6 in this sample were 6.2% and intra-assay CV were 6.06%.

Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)

Salivary DHEA was also used to index HPA axis reactivity to psychosocial stress. DHEA was measured in saliva using an ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions (Salimetrics, Inc.). The range of detection for this assay kit was 5.0 - 1000.0 pg/ml. Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 14 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. To compute intra-assay CV, 20% of samples were assayed in duplicate. Inter-assay CV for DHEA in this sample were 6.96% and intra-assay CV were 9.60%.

Testosterone

Salivary testosterone was used to index HPG axis reactivity to psychosocial stress. Testosterone was measured in saliva using ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions (Salimetrics, Inc.). The range of detection for this assay kit was 1.00 - 600 pg/ml. Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 6 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. To compute intra-assay CV, 20% of samples were assayed in duplicate. Samples that returned a concentration outside the range of detection for the assay or that demonstrated low concordance with their duplicate assay were retested (n = 2) to confirm the result. Inter-assay CV for testosterone in this sample were 9.88% and intra-assay CV were 13.79%.

Uric acid

Salivary uric acid was used to index metabolic reactivity to psychosocial stress. Uric acid was measured in saliva using an enzymatic assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Salimetrics, Inc.). Of the 811 samples sent for assay, 7 had insufficient saliva quantity to conduct the assay. The range of detection for this assay kit was 0.07 - 20.0 mg/dl. To compute intra-assay CV, all samples were assayed in duplicate. Inter-assay CV for sUA in this sample were 5.58% and intra-assay CV were 5.03%.

Covariates

Key covariates in the present analyses were age, sex, and BMI, which have each been associated with differences in either basal concentrations of the biomarkers measured in this study or their reactivity to acute psychosocial stress. A research staff member measured each participant's height and weight at their study enrollment visit. These height and weight measurements were then used to compute each participant's body mass index (BMI) using the formula weight in kilograms (kg) divided by height in meters (m) squared. Participants' parents reported their child's age at the time of enrollment as well as their child's sex (male or female).

Data analysis

All hypotheses were tested using separate linear mixed models for each outcome. Each outcome was modeled with individual outcome concentrations nested within individuals and modeled as a function of time in minutes since the onset of the stress procedures. An unadjusted model of each outcome was conducted comparing the model fit using different covariance matrices, including unstructured, auto-regressive 1 (AR1), compound symmetry, and variance components. Variance components matrices were the best fit for cortisol, IL-6, DHEA, testosterone, and uric acid; AR1 was the best fitting covariance matrix for ɑ-amylase. All models included a random intercept and slope as well as fixed effects for time. Each outcome's reactivity to stress was modeled as a linear, quadratic, and a cubic function and Aike's Information Criteria (AIC) was used to determine which was a better fit to the outcome (lower AIC = better fit). Primary hypotheses were tested by modeling each outcome as a function of ELA, time, and the interaction between ELA and time.

We report all estimated effects with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to emphasize effect size and precision. For comparability with prior work, we also note results with p < .10 as suggestive. Given testing across multiple outcomes, we use p < .01 as a conservative threshold for statistical significance across outcomes; however, inference is primarily guided by the magnitude of effects and the width of the 95% CIs.

All models were conducted unadjusted for covariates first, then adjusted for age, sex, and BMI because each of these covariates was associated with either baseline concentrations or reactivity for at least one of the outcomes. To determine the role of key covariates (age, sex, and BMI) in reactivity of each outcome to the TSST-C, a separate model was conducted predicting concentrations of each outcome as a function of time, each covariate, and the interaction between the covariate and time.

Age was not associated with cortisol or IL-6 in this sample, all ps > 0.22. Older participants had higher ɑ-amylase at baseline, b(SE) = 0.09 (0.04), p = .042, 95%CI[0.003, 0.176], but no differences in ɑ-amylase reactivity to stress, all p > .42. Older participants had higher DHEA at baseline, b(SE) = 0.132 (0.041), p = .002, 95%CI[0.051, 0.214], but no differences in DHEA reactivity to stress, all p > .55. Older participants showed slightly elevated testosterone concentrations at baseline, b(SE) = 0.079 (0.042), p = .061, 95%CI[-0.004, 0.162], and a steeper increase in testosterone following stress onset, age x time b(SE) = 0.004 (0.002), p = .029, 95%CI[0.00, 0.008]. Older participants had somewhat higher uric acid concentrations at baseline, b(SE) = 0.064 (0.038), p = .09, 95%CI[-0.01, 0.139], but no differences in uric acid reactivity to stress, p > .49.

Sex was not associated with ɑ-amylase or uric acid in this sample, all p > .35. Male participants had higher cortisol at baseline, b(SE) = 0.40 (0.16), p = .015, 95%CI[0.079, 0.716], but no differences in cortisol reactivity to stress, all p > .68. Male participants had somewhat higher IL-6 at baseline, b(SE) = 0.43 (0.22), p = .059, 95%CI[-0.017, 0.869], but no differences in IL-6 reactivity to stress, all p > .21. Male participants showed a more attenuated increase in DHEA following stress onset, sex x time b(SE) = -0.014 (0.007), p = .036, 95%CI[-0.027, −0.001], but no differences in DHEA at baseline, p = .35. Male participants had higher testosterone at baseline, b(SE) = 0.44 (0.127), p < .001, 95%CI[0.184, 0.689], but no differences in testosterone reactivity to stress, all p > .62.

BMI was not associated with cortisol, IL-6, or ɑ-amylase in this sample, all ps > 0.11. Participants with a higher BMI had higher DHEA concentrations at baseline, b(SE) = 0.49 (0.016), p = .002, 95%CI[0.018, 0.08], but no differences in DHEA reactivity to stress, all p > .17. Participants with a higher BMI showed a larger increase in testosterone following stress onset, BMI x time b(SE) = 0.002 (0.001), p = .005, 95%CI[0.001, 0.003], and a slower recovery, BMI x time2 b(SE) = -0.000044 (0.00002), p = .029, 95%CI[-0.000008, −0.000004]. Participants with a higher BMI had more elevated uric acid at baseline, b(SE) = 0.037 (0.014), p = .009, 95%CI[0.01, 0.065] but no differences in uric acid reactivity to stress, p > .25.

ELA exposure consisted of experiences that ranged from non-intentional ELA to physical and emotional ELA. On average, participants reported experiencing 4.73 ± 2.64 non-intentional ELA (range = 0 to 12), 2.11 ± 1.90 experiences of physical ELA (range = 0 to 6), and 2.79 ± 2.17 experiences of emotional ELA (range = 0 to 8).

Non-intentional ELA and physical ELA were not associated with cortisol reactivity or recovery from stress, all p > .39. Discussion

The purpose of the present study was to examine the associations between ELA and ELA subtypes and physiological responses to stress as indexed by biomarkers reflecting activity of the ANS, HPA, HPG, innate immune, and metabolic systems. All measured biomarkers showed a reliable increase following the stress task, though biomarkers indexing the HPA axis (cortisol, DHEA) were most robust. More cumulative exposure to ELA was associated with higher baseline concentrations of DHEA as well as steeper increases in cortisol and testosterone following stress onset. Analyses focused on the independent associations by ELA subtype are broadly consistent with the idea that adversity exposures differing in developmental meaning may relate to partially distinct profiles of physiological responding. While these are relative associations and not meant to reflect one-to-one associations between a single ELA subtype and a single physiological system, the results may reflect the role of biological salience in developmental plasticity (Kuhlman et al., 2017). For example, emotional ELA was associated with more exaggerated HPA activity in the aftermath of the stressor as indexed by cortisol, as well as exaggerated down-regulation of innate immune activity as indexed by IL-6. Evidence for subtype-specific effects of ELA on stress physiology is consistent with a developmental psychobiology perspective in which the nature of early experience shapes physiological sensitivity to salient environmental challenges. More specifically, the present data aligns with the biological salience model and offers a refined framework for understanding how specific early experiences may shape lifelong stress reactivity and could inform models of the pathogenesis of stress-related disorders.

In response to psychosocial stress, the human body undergoes a rapid shift in physiological priorities that promotes availability of energy, facilitation of physical ability, and anticipation of injury. Yet the literature looking at links between ELA and physiological responses to stress to date have been biased in favor of biomarkers of the HPA axis (e.g., Bunea et al., 2017; Kuhlman et al., 2015) or the innate immune system (Carpenter et al., 2010; Shalev et al., 2019; Kuhlman et al., 2022; Steptoe et al., 2007). While activity of other stress sensitive systems have been investigated in response to acute laboratory stress (e.g., Kuras et al., 2017; Lam et al., 2019), these studies have almost exclusively used adult participants. The present study demonstrated that salivary markers of ANS, HPA, HPG, innate immune, and metabolic activity all show reactivity to acute psychosocial stress in an adolescent sample. These data underscore the feasibility and necessity of measuring HPA axis activity in physiological context by expanding measurement to systems that coordinate closely with or share an upstream physiological path with the HPA axis. That biomarkers of HPA and HPG activity showed the most robust and reliable increases following the stressor may reflect their greater sensitivity to psychosocial stress and involvement in the physiological stress response, or their historical use in developing and validating the specific stress task used in this study (TSST-C). Innate immune and metabolic activity following psychosocial stress, as indexed by salivary IL-6 and uric acid respectively, showed less traditional patterns of reactivity following stress-onset. This may suggest physiologically meaningful differences in stress reactivity time course that should be considered when designing studies intending to measure these biomarkers. Specifically, both markers showed their highest average concentrations or peaks at 90 min post-stress onset, and continuing to monitor concentrations of IL-6 and uric acid until a reliable pattern of recovery was observable may have been informative. While IL-6 in circulating blood is known to show a reliable pattern of increase within 120 min of acute laboratory stress (Marsland et al., 2017), and salivary IL-6 appears to demonstrate a more prominent increase following acute psychosocial stress than plasma IL-6 (Antici et al., 2026), fluctuations in both IL-6 and uric acid could also be driven by other factors such as the in-dwelling catheter, fasting during the laboratory visit, hydration, or other unmeasured factors.

The present study shows that ELA may be associated with greater acute coactivation of cortisol and testosterone following psychosocial stress. Specifically, more cumulative exposure to ELA in this sample was associated with steeper increases in cortisol and testosterone following stress onset. Cortisol and testosterone share central regulation in response to stress via the hypothalamus and pituitary. The positive association between ELA and both cortisol and testosterone activity following acute stress observed in this study implicates either ELA-related central or adrenal/gonadal calibration in the context of acute laboratory stress that affects both HPA and HPG axis outputs, reflecting altered feed-forward drive or feedback sensitivity. Converging evidence for altered central stress regulation following ELA exposure comes from reports that, independent of psychopathology, physical abuse was specifically linked to larger increases in ACTH following metyrapone administration (Mayer et al., 2020). Further, the absence of ELA-related differences in DHEA activity following acute stress refutes the argument that ELA increases generalized adrenal androgen output. Further, it may be important to note that the present data diverges from the existing meta-analysis linking ELA to cortisol responses to acute stress (Bunea et al., 2017), which showed an overall inverse relationship particularly among adults and among individuals exposed to maltreatment specifically. This divergence could reflect the younger age of, or variability in type of ELA exposure among, these participants. ELA may potentiate HPA axis activity during childhood and adolescence, whereas exaggerated activity of these systems may result in attenuation in adulthood (Trickett et al., 2010). However, longitudinal data that speaks to this developmental phenomenon remains rare, and has focused on trait-like measures of neuroendocrine regulation (i.e., diurnal cortisol) rather than reactivity to acute psychosocial stress in the laboratory.

When ELA was separated by subtype, emotional and physical ELA were associated with divergent patterns of cortisol and IL-6 activity following the stressor. Emotional ELA was associated with a more exaggerated increase in cortisol following stress onset and lower IL-6 across the recovery period. By contrast, physical ELA was associated with higher concentrations of IL-6 following stress onset. This pattern may reflect disparate biological salience of emotional and physical adversity experiences as they shape various physiological response systems. A robust innate immune response preempts anticipatory host defense and can involve wound repair and protection against bacterial pathogens (Dhabhar, 2008). This response is more likely to be adaptive in the context of physical threat where injury is more likely than in the context of emotional threat. In the present sample, individuals exposed to the most physical ELA showed the highest concentrations of IL-6 throughout the post-stress recovery period, which could reflect immune-related efforts toward host defense. Importantly, the present study design cannot determine whether IL-6 activity across the study protocol was a result of venipuncture, the psychosocial stress procedures, or other aspects of the laboratory environment. Of these possibilities, venipuncture might be the most biologically salient exposure to an immune system shaped by physical ELA, and therefore the most likely to elucidate this primed host defense phenotype. That being said, previous studies have linked childhood maltreatment to exaggerated IL-6 responses to both acute laboratory stress (Carpenter et al., 2010) and daily stressors (Gouin et al., 2012), though physical neglect (e.g., not enough food, caregivers unable or willing to keep you safe, no access to medical care, ill-fitting or unclean clothes) is the only ELA subtype to be specifically linked to exaggerated IL-6 reactivity in previous studies (Schreier et al., 2020). Whether the types of experiences that fall into the domain of physical neglect but not physical injury (e.g., having too little food or well-fitted seasonal clothing) hold similar immune salience to the developing individual remains unaddressed in the ELA literature, but would be valuable to explore. Physical neglect isn't a domain of ELA assessed by the items on the ETI. By contrast, individuals in the sample with the highest exposure to emotional ELA showed more exaggerated cortisol increases after stress onset and the lowest IL-6 concentrations across the recovery period. Together, this pattern may reflect tissue-specific differences in glucocorticoid sensitivity with increased suppression of cytokine signaling within immune cells alongside altered feedback regulation of cortisol centrally during stress recovery. The present study did not model the well-established and dynamic interdependence of neuroendocrine and immune activity (Pariante, 2017), and trajectories of IL-6 across the protocol may reflect suppression of IL-6 secretion by increases in cortisol following stress onset. Nor did the present study model interactions between ELA subtypes. Poly-victimization is the norm rather than the exception (Kuhlman, 2024), meaning that unquantified variability at the biologically-meaningful subtype level may obscure developmentally meaningful individual differences within and across studies.

In adults, emotional ELA has been linked to exaggerated ACTH and cortisol activity following acute laboratory stress (Mayer et al., 2020). Experiences in childhood that are characterized by the absence of a warm or supportive caregiver may shape the sensitivity of glucocorticoid receptors which mediate regulation of both cytokine signaling and HPA axis activation. This interpretation converges with seminal experiments in rodents showing that the quality of maternal caregiving can shape glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity in the hippocampus in ways that lead to changes in HPA activation and regulation (Levine, 2001; Liu et al., 1997; Meaney, 2001). It is also notable that some associations between ELA and reactivity following stress were no longer significant when modeling different types of ELA separately. For example, the association between ELA and testosterone reactivity following stress were not significant when ELA subtypes were modeled separately. This may indicate that the ELA-related differences in testosterone following stress are shaped by cumulative or chronic stress as opposed to developmentally shaped by the biological salience of a specific ELA subtype.

The results of this study should be considered in the context of their limitations. First, this study was cross-sectional, and ELA was assessed retrospectively via adolescent self-report. While retrospective assessment of ELA is standard, retrospective reports of ELA are vulnerable to measurement error and recall biases (Monroe, 2008; Reuben et al., 2016). These measurement errors and biases can affect reports of both cumulative ELA and ELA subtype, decreasing the precision of estimates on subtype-specific associations. Retrospective reports of ELA, relative to prospectively assessed reports, are more strongly associated with psychopathology and other subjective outcomes than prospectively measured ELA (Baldwin et al., 2019), thus introducing the potential confounding role of concurrent mood and psychological well-being on ELA's effects on physiological responses to stress. Further, the results of this study may have broad implications for ELA-exposed populations, as well as specific implications for populations exposed to physical or emotional ELA. However, exposure to sexual ELA was very infrequent in this sample and therefore the results cannot be extended to that subtype at this time.

The purpose of the present study was to examine physiological responses to acute psychosocial stress. Psychosocial stress in this study was induced using the TSST-C. While this is a well-established and reliable way to induce standardized psychosocial stress in the laboratory (Allen et al., 2014; Dickerson and Kemeny, 2004; Kudielka et al., 2007), it is distinct from both ecological stressors as well as other laboratory stress paradigms. Most notably, the TSST-C is longer than other stress paradigms (e.g., socially-evaluated cold pressor) (Schwabe et al., 2008; Schwabe and Schächinger, 2018) and involves multiple components (speech preparation, speech delivery, mental arithmetic). These factors make inferences about temporal dynamics and the specific psychological experience that contributed to physiological activation somewhat ambiguous. Temporal dynamics are particularly relevant to the present data because all biomarkers were measured in saliva, some of which loosely approximate physiological markers in circulation and others which are unique to the oral cavity, making interpretations about their change across the protocol more difficult. For example, ɑ-amylase is directly synthesized by the salivary glands while the remaining outcomes used in the present analyses either arrive in saliva through passive diffusion (cortisol, DHEA, testosterone) or ultrafiltration (uric acid, IL-6) from the blood. Notably, IL-6 and other cytokines do not show strong concordance between blood and saliva (Antici et al., 2026), in part because cytokines can also be produced by immune cells locally in the mouth. Overall, the outcomes measured in this study can only be interpreted as activity of these systems within the oral cavity. Further confounding our interpretation of the present data is the absence of a no-stress comparison group which would enable us to isolate the effect of the TSST-C on physiological systems from other potentially arousing or activating components of the laboratory visit including accommodation to the laboratory environment itself, insertion of an in-dwelling catheter, or completion of questionnaires assessing psychosocial history and psychiatric symptoms. Future studies incorporating pre-laboratory saliva collection could help clarify whether baseline concentrations reflect anticipatory physiological activity or laboratory arrival (e.g., Alpers et al., 2003). Finally, there are many potential developmental and contextual confounds to consider in any study of the associations between ELA and physiological stress reactivity. In the present models, chronological age, BMI, and sex were accounted for. One important factor to consider that was not accounted for in the present analyses was pubertal development which contributes to variation in biomarkers such as cortisol, DHEA, and testosterone. The present sample had relative underrepresentation of early pubertal youth which limited the developmental conclusions that could be drawn from these data.

In conclusion, the present study extends a foundational developmental psychobiology tradition which demonstrated that early-life experience can induce enduring individual differences in stress physiology. The present study confirmed the sensitivity of neuroendocrine systems to early experiences relative to immune or metabolic markers following acute psychosocial stress. Further, the present data extends support for the role of biological salience in developmental plasticity. Specifically, different types of ELA may differentially calibrate the physiological stress response systems of adolescents. This indicates different types of ELA may pose different threats to the developing individual and require distinct adaptations that may carry forward into adulthood. In this case, exposure to physical ELA was linked to higher concentrations of IL-6 during stress recovery while emotional ELA was linked to exaggerated cortisol and lower concentrations of IL-6 during stress recovery. Predominant approaches that combine maltreatment exposures may have obscured the role of biological salience in shaping individual differences in development and adaptation.
