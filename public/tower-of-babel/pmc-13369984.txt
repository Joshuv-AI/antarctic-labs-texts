Bridging hydrogen-bond-stabilized polymeric coacervate core micelles for high-efficiency drug encapsulation and delivery
Authors: Jiafeng Wang, Jiajia Ying, Jianxiang Huang, Longchen Zhu, Hang Yang, JunJun Li, Zimeng Liu, Yongli Mu, Changhuo Xu, Kefeng Ding, Xin Zhang, Tianhua Zhou, Xiangrui Liu, Youqing Shen, Quan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymeric nanoparticles represent one of the most promising classes of non-lipid-based drug delivery vehicles. However, their clinical translation remains limited by poor drug loading capacity and suboptimal delivery efficiency. We here develop one type of polymeric nanoparticles, referred to as PCCMs (Polymeric Coacervate Core Micelles), which are assembled from a sulfoxide-containing block polymer. Hydrophobic drugs can be efficiently encapsulated into PCCMs via a fast, low-energy “Mix-and-Go” approach, reducing reliance on organic solvents and simplifying the drug-loading process. Moreover, PCCMs show high drug loading capacities and improved stability towards versatile hydrophobic drugs. Upon evaluating 40 different drugs, 45% of the tested compounds achieve drug loading contents exceeding 40%, 80% exceed 30%, and 100% surpass 20%. Both quantitative structure-property relationship (QSPR) calculations and experimental methods identify that hydration water within PCCMs’ self-coacervated cores acts as the molecular barrier, substantially inhibiting long-range-ordered drug molecule packing and stabilizing drug aggregates at the nanoscale via bridging hydrogen bonds among the polymer–water–drug. Finally, we demonstrate that PCCMs exhibit significantly higher systemic delivery efficacy compared to commercial polymeric micelle formulations in female BALB/c mice. The high loading capacity, versatility, and efficacy of PCCMs pave the way for the clinical translation of polymeric nanoparticles.

Introduction

Around 40% of approved drugs and 90% of drugs in the development pipeline exhibit poor water solubility, significantly compromising therapeutic efficacy and hindering the progression of promising candidates to clinical stages1–3. Polymeric micelles (PMs) are one of the most extensively studied polymeric nanoparticles that have shown great potential in drug delivery due to their simplicity and ability to physically encapsulate hydrophobic drugs in their cores4–8. Nevertheless, low drug loading contents (LCs) (<10%, even 1%), complicated encapsulation process, and impaired delivery efficiency significantly impede practical applications, leading to the failure of most PM-based nanomedicines in clinical trials9–11.

Conventional physical drug loading into PMs typically involves three sequential steps: (i) dissolving hydrophobic drug powders using appropriate organic solvents; (ii) co-assembly of drug molecules with amphiphilic polymers to form drug-loaded micelles; and (iii) removal of the organic solvent via evaporation or dialysis. Despite their wide use, these procedures suffer from intrinsic limitations that hinder clinical translation. The reliance on organic solvents not only introduces toxicity and elevates production costs but also renders the process labor-intensive and time-consuming12. Furthermore, poor intrinsic compatibility between hydrophobic drugs and polymer matrices often leads to precipitation or large drug aggregates, severely limiting drug LCs13,14. While strategies such as dimeric prodrug design15, incorporation of coordination interactions16, and complex sequential nanoprecipitation17 have been explored to improve LCs, these approaches frequently require structural modifications, lack scalability, and exhibit narrow applicability across drug types. Thus, the development of PMs capable of uniformly, efficiently, and robustly encapsulating a broad spectrum of hydrophobic drugs without complex processing remains an unmet challenge.

The sulfoxide group is a versatile functional moiety found in numerous organic molecules that are significant for applications in biology, chemistry, and material sciences18–20. Its high polarity and unique electronic structure make dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) a universal solvent for a wide range of compounds. The incorporation of sulfoxide moieties into polymers also imparts excellent physicochemical properties to the resulting sulfoxide-containing polymers, including low toxicity, high biocompatibility, and strong hydrogen bonding-accepting capacity21–25. These properties indicate that the sulfoxide-containing polymers could be ideal scaffolds for building nanocarriers with excellent compatibility for various drugs24,26,27.

In this work, we show that the sulfoxide-containing homopolymer poly(2-(butylsulfinyl)ethyl acrylate) (PBuSEA) exhibits a lower critical solution temperature (LCST)-type liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), yielding liquid droplets with high polarity and low viscosity—properties that enable efficient sequestration and solvation of hydrophobic drugs (Fig. 1a). PEGylation produces PEG-PBuSEA polymeric coacervate core micelles (PCCMs) with liquid-like cores and high loading across a structurally diverse panel of poorly soluble compounds (Fig. 1b). In contrast to conventional PMs, PCCMs directly dissolve and encapsulate drug molecules via a rapid, one-step “Mix-and-Go” method, which streamlines the formulation process and minimizes—or in some instances eliminates—the use of organic solvents (Fig. 1c). Mechanistically, sulfoxide groups form bridging hydrogen bonds (HBs) with drug nanoaggregates and water, enhancing hydration and preventing long-range-ordered drug packing. Surface-exposed hydrogen bond acceptors, particularly nitrogen-containing moieties, engage in the sulfoxide-dominated HB network and further boost loading (Fig. 1d). Results

Self-coacervation of the PBuSEA homopolymer

The PBuSEA homopolymer, synthesized via RAFT polymerization with a tailored molecular weight of approximately 12 kDa, exhibited a LCST-type phase transition (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 1–6). At 4 °C, PBuSEA readily dissolved in water, demonstrating a solubility exceeding 25 mg/mL. However, upon warming to room temperature (r.t.) or 37 °C, the solution transformed into an emulsion characterized by the formation of microscopic droplets (Fig. 2b, c). The cloud point temperature (Tcp) of PBuSEA in water was approximately 16 °C, with slight variations observed in physiologically relevant media such as PBS (~ 16 °C) and FBS (~ 18 °C, Supplementary Fig. 7). Next, salt ions from the Hofmeister series and urea were introduced to elucidate the primary driving force behind the phase transition28,29. The salting-out ion Na2SO4 lowered Tcp to ~4 °C and the salting-in ion NaSCN elevated it to ~43 °C, consistent with established literatures (Fig. 2d)30,31. Urea is widely reported to enhance the solvation of hydrophobic groups32,33 and thus elevated Tcp of PBuSEA (Supplementary Fig. 8). Next, the liquid-like nature of PBuSEA was confirmed by the fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiment. Following bleaching of TAMRA-labeled PBuSEA droplets, the fluorescence rapidly recovered with a T1/2 of 2.69 s (Fig. 2e). The fusion behavior among multiple droplets was also observed, providing compelling evidence of coacervate formation via LLPS (Fig. 2f). Variable temperature 1H NMR was further applied to characterize the coacervates and revealed slight line broadening above the Tcp, yet notably, no peaks completely disappeared34. This observation confirms the highly hydrated nature of PBuSEA coacervates, suggesting maintained molecular tumbling within the phase-separated state (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To elucidate whether highly polar sulfoxide groups in the polymer can impart unique physicochemical properties to coacervates, we quantified the polarity and viscosity of PBuSEA coacervates using fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM), with microenvironment-sensitive fluorescent probes SBD and BODIPY28,35. As controls, a series of structurally analogous polymer, including PPeSEA, PHeSEA, PBuTEA, and PBuSFO, were synthesized via RAFT polymerization, with their molecular weights tailored to approximately 12 kDa (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Figs. 10–17, and Supplementary Table 1)24. These polymers were insoluble in water and didn’t exhibit obvious thermosensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 18). All synthesized polymers exhibited Tg < 0 °C (Supplementary Table 2). As anticipated, water-rich PBuSEA coacervates demonstrated the highest polarity, with a SBD mean fluorescence lifetime (τ) of 4.87 ns corresponding to a calculated dielectric constant (ε) of 27.83, and the expressively low viscosity, evidenced by a BODIPY τ of 1.76 ns, translating to a calculated viscosity (η) of merely 0.15 Pa·s. Extension of side chains and reduction or oxidation of sulfoxide groups leaded to dramatic decreases in polarity and increases in viscosity, indicating the polymer phase transition from a liquid-like state to a more solid-like state. The clinically applied polymer, PDLLA, dominated by strong hydrophobic interactions, showed the highest viscosity (τ = 5.58 ns, η = 3.23 Pa·s) and the lowest polarity (τ = 15.24 ns, ε = 2.99) among all tested polymers (Fig. 2g). Interestingly, retrospective analysis of previous studies reveals that PBuSEA coacervates possess higher polarity and lower viscosity compared to most protein-based coacervates, highlighting the unique properties of the sulfoxide-containing polymer system28,35. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were also employed to gain a comprehensive understanding of polymer properties. The aggregation behaviors of PBuTEA, PBuSEA, and PBuSFO were investigated and the polymers revealed distinct morphologies. PBuSEA displayed a loose and irregular morphology with the strongest hydrogen bonding interactions and the largest solvent accessible surface area (SASA) compared with PBuTEA and PBuSFO, which aligns with our previous experimental observations (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Fig. 19, and Supplementary Movie 1). These findings demonstrate PBuSEA can form highly hydrated coacervates with low viscosity and high polarity in the physiological environment, laying the foundation for partitioning and solvation of guest molecules.

We subsequently examined the partitioning capacity of coacervates for a range of guest molecules. Our investigations revealed PBuSEA coacervates selectively sequestered hydrophobic molecules (Nile red, Hoechst 33342, DiI, Cy5, and curcumin) with high partition coefficient K exceeding 100 (K = cin droplet/cin solution). In the case of partially water-soluble molecules such as 5-aminofluorescein, the partition coefficient K decreased significantly to 3.5. Highly hydrophilic PEG, dextran, and ssDNA were largely excluded from the coacervates, exhibiting low partition coefficients of K = 0.7, 0.2, and 0.1, respectively (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Table 3). These observations underscore the predominant role of hydrophobic interactions in governing molecular partitioning within PBuSEA coacervates, highlighting the potential for encapsulating hydrophobic drugs.

Preparation and characterization of the polymeric coacervate core micelles (PCCMs)

Despite the effective encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs by coacervates, their applications in drug delivery are hindered by the excessive size and inherent instability due to Ostwald ripening and coalescence36. To overcome these limitations, a PEG shell was introduced to stabilize the formed coacervates (Fig. 3a)37. The PEG5k-PBuSEA12k block copolymer was synthesized via RAFT polymerization using a macromolecular chain transfer agent PEG5k-PETTC, with its molecular weight tailored to approximately 17 kDa, as verified by both GPC and 1H NMR (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Additionally, a series of control polymers, including PEG-PPeSEA, PEG-PHeSEA, PEG-PBuTEA, and PEG-PBuSFO, all with comparable molecular weights, were prepared under similar conditions (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 22–25 and Supplementary Table 1). PEG-PBuSEA can self-assemble into micelles by simply dissolving the polymer in water, standing in contrast to other amphiphilic block copolymers, which require specialized techniques such as nanoprecipitation or thin-film hydration. We next determined whether the core of PEG-PBuSEA micelles retained the physicochemical characteristics of PBuSEA coacervates after PEGylation. The polarity and viscosity properties of prepared micelles were first evaluated. Notably, τ values of BODIPY and SBD probes remained consistent between non-PEGylated polymer dispersions and PEGylated micelles (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 27). This consistency suggests that the PBuSEA chains within the micelle cores maintain a microenvironment similar to that of self-coacervated droplets. PEG-PBuSEA micelles maintained high hydration across a wide temperature range, with the 1H NMR at 45 °C remaining virtually unchanged compared to those at lower temperatures (Fig. 3f). Furthermore, PEG-PBuSEA micelles exhibited only a slight decrease in 1H NMR signal intensity and minor line broadening compared to the spectrum obtained in CDCl3, where polymers were fully dissolved. In contrast, PEG-PPeSEA and PEG-PHeSEA micelles demonstrated progressive dehydration as the side chains extended, evidenced by the gradual attenuation of 1H NMR signals. The commercial PEG2k-PDLLA1.75k micelles almost entirely suppressed signal production from their cores, indicative of a highly compact and aggregated state (Fig. 3g). These findings demonstrate that, in comparison to other control micelles, the core of PEG-PBuSEA micelles exhibits high hydration and maintains a liquid-like coacervate state. Based on these unique characteristics, herein, we defined this micelle type as “PCCMs” (Fig. 3a).

PCCMs as universal and instant-loading vehicles for hydrophobic pharmaceuticals

The drug loading capacity of PCCMs was first assessed using two model drugs: paclitaxel (PTX), a clinically approved chemotherapeutic agent, and MZ-1, a PROTAC currently in the clinical pipeline. The typical thin-film dispersion method was utilized to prepare the drug-loaded micelles. Consequently, PCCMs demonstrated a high LC of 30.1% for PTX and an extraordinarily high LC of 61.7% for MZ-1, with encapsulation efficiencies (EEs) exceeding 99% for both drugs. Significantly, structural modifications to the polymer, such as extending side chains and altering sulfoxide groups by reduction or oxidation, led to dramatic decreases in the LCs. DLS analysis revealed that PEG-PBuSEA micelles loaded with PTX and MZ-1 exhibited unimodal size distributions with diameters of 153.7 and 77.7 nm, respectively, while other micelle formulations displayed multimodal distributions indicative of aggregate formation (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Table 4). PEG-PBuSFO/PTX micelles exhibited instability, demonstrating rapid precipitation within 30 min after preparation. Notably, PEG-PBuSEA/PTX micelles could maintain stability after lyophilization, storage for 10 weeks, and subsequent reconstitution, even in the absence of freeze-drying protective additives. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) values of PCCMs in FBS (to simulate the blood serum environment) were systematically determined by a typical fluorescence method, and 4-hydroxybenzylidene-imidazolinone analogue-based fluorophore was employed as an aggregation indicator here to minimize background fluorescence from FBS22,38. The empty PCCM exhibited a relatively high CMC of 245.4 μg/mL due to the loosely packed structure of the micellar cores. In contrast, the incorporation of hydrophobic drugs markedly reduced the CMC values. PCCMs loaded with MZ-1 and PTX with a LC of 10% showed significantly lower CMC values of 90.3 and 75.1 μg/mL, respectively. When loaded to their maximum capacities, MZ-1 and PTX-loaded PCCMs further decreased the CMC values to 38.2 and 32.7 μg/mL, respectively—values comparable to those of conventional micellar formulations (Supplementary Fig. 29)39.

Inspired by the remarkable loading capacity of PCCMs for PTX and MZ-1, we sought to investigate whether these micelles could be explored as universal vehicles for poorly soluble pharmaceuticals. A comprehensive array of 40 commercially available, poorly water-soluble drugs was selected for evaluation of their LCs and EEs. The structural diversity was corroborated through chemical clustering analysis, which revealed that no cluster contained more than two drugs at the default similarity threshold, ensuring a wide range of chemical scaffolds (Supplementary Fig. 30)40. Three well-established micelle systems—PEG-PCL, PEG-PLGA, and PEG-PDLLA—were used as control carriers, and the typical thin-film hydration method was parallelly employed to prepare the drug-loaded micelles. Impressively, PCCMs demonstrated high LCs across the spectrum of drugs tested. All investigated formulations (40/40) exhibited LCs exceeding 20%, with 80% of drugs (32/40) achieving LCs greater than 30%, and notably, a substantial proportion (45%, 18/40) surpassing an extraordinary LC of 40%. In contrast, most PEG-PCL and PEG-PLGA formulations exhibited LC below 10%, which is in agreement with previous studies2,10. While PEG-PDLLA, the polymer used in the clinically approved PTX formulation Genexol-PM with optimized molecular weights41, showed obvious improvement, it still significantly underperformed compared to PEG-PBuSEA (Fig. 4e, f, Table 1, and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Combination therapy is commonly used in clinical practice to enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome drug resistance. However, it remains challenging to encapsulate two distinct drugs with high LCs within a single delivery system42. We speculated that the high drug loading capacity of PCCMs might provide potential for codelivery applications. Thus, we evaluated the drug LCs of PCCMs for three representative drug pairs: PTX/sorafenib, osimertinib/sotorasib, and V9302/DC66143,44. Remarkably, PCCMs successfully co-encapsulated these drug pairs, achieving total LCs of 45.2, 55.2, and 57.3%, respectively, while maintaining unimodal size distributions (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8 and Supplementary Fig. 32). These findings highlight the potential of PCCMs as versatile nanocarriers for combination therapies.

Given that PCCMs effectively loaded various hydrophobic drugs, we hypothesized that these micelles might serve as solvent-like nanocarriers and directly encapsulate flocculated drug particles. PTX was used as the model drug and initially dissolved in a minimal amount of DMSO, followed by the addition of empty PEG-PBuSEA micelles. As expected, flocculated aggregates formed and precipitated. Amazingly, through repetitive pipetting, PCCMs demonstrated the capacity to effectively solubilize and encapsulate these PTX aggregates within one minute (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Movie 2). We also found this method proved universally applicable, successfully loading nearly all 40 drugs tested within PCCMs, including those with a relative low LC, such as curcumin and sorafenib (Fig. 4h). This Mix-and-Go method enabled PCCMs to achieve high LC of 24.8% for PTX, 20.6% for sorafenib and 12.9% for curcumin, all maintaining uniform size distributions (Supplementary Fig. 33a). Meanwhile, the behavior sharply contrasted with that observed with PEG-PDLLA, where flocculated aggregates failed to be encapsulated and instead progressed to form solid precipitates (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Movie 3). Further, a more straightforward and organic solvent-free Mix-and-Go process was available for drugs whose LCs exceeded 40%. Simply, the drug and PEG-PBuSEA powders were mixed with a small volume of water and dispersed. Then, additional water was added to achieve the desired concentration, resulting in the formation of uniform micelles. For instance, PCCMs loaded with MZ-1, chloroquine, and ticagrelor were prepared by this method, showing uniform size distributions and achieving LC of 21.1, 20.1, and 11.4%, respectively (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 33b).

To underscore the significance of the sulfoxide group, we synthesized a control micelle—PEG-poly(2-(methacryloyloxy)ethyl 5-oxopyrrolidine-2-carboxylate) (PHEPGA) (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35a). Previous studies have indicated that PHEPGA could undergo LLPS and produce micrometer-sized liquid droplets30. Similar to PEG-PBuSEA, PEG-PHEPGA exhibited micelle formation with an average diameter of 22.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 35b). However, when loaded with PTX and MZ-1, PEG-PHEPGA demonstrated significantly lower LCs compared to PEG-PBuSEA employing the standard thin-film hydration method, and failed to encapsulate these drugs via the Mix-and-Go method (Supplementary Fig. 35c, d).

PCCMs stabilize drug aggregates at the nanoscale

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the high loading capability of PCCMs, we next examined internal structures of drug-loaded micelles using TEM. Intriguingly, PCCMs displayed distinct morphologies compared to traditional PEG-PDLLA micelles. PEG-PBuSEA/PTX micelles exhibited cavity structures with visible drug nanoaggregates inside the cores (Fig. 5a). Considering that the water evaporation during TEM sample preparation might influence the morphology, cryo-TEM was applied, and it revealed the presence of worm-like drug aggregates within the micellar cores, accompanied by a distinct shell-like structure (Fig. 5b). The formation of the shell is likely attributed to the aggregation of PTX at the boundary between the aqueous phase and the coacervate phase. PEG-PBuSEA/MZ-1 micelles with high LC showed no cavities, with drug aggregates entirely filling the cores (Fig. 5c). The amorphous nature of the drug aggregates was further validated by both electron diffraction and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses (Fig. 5b and d). These findings suggest that, instead of homogeneously dispersing drug molecules, PCCMs preferentially stabilize drug aggregates. This phenomenon was further confirmed by the fluorescence behavior of DiI, a fluorescent probe sensitive to its aggregation state45,46. Remarkably, PEG-PBuSEA/DiI exhibited a significant red shift in emission wavelength compared to PEG-PDLLA/DiI, even at a low LC of 0.5% (Fig. 5e). This bathochromic shift is characteristic of J-aggregate formation, providing additional evidence for drug aggregation within the PCCMs. MD simulations of PBuSEA incorporating PTX or MZ-1 also revealed distinct distribution patterns within condensed polymers. PBuSEA effectively embedded these drug molecules and they tended to aggregate rather than disperse as individual molecules, consequently reducing SASA significantly. In contrast, PTX and MZ-1 merely adsorbed onto the surface of sulfide-containing PBuTEA, exhibiting the largest SASA due to overly strong carrier-carrier interactions, partly explaining the low drug loading capacity of PEG-PBuTEA micelles. Nuclear overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY), a powerful technique for identifying spatially proximate nuclei, was further utilized to study drug-carrier and drug-drug interactions12,47,48. To avoid signal overlap between the benzene rings in MZ-1 and CTA, we resynthesized PEG-PBuSEA via an amine-NHS click reaction (Supplementary Fig. 36a). NOESY spectrum in CDCl3 revealed that free MZ-1 exhibited signals with opposite signs to diagonal peaks. However, upon encapsulation within PEG-PBuSEA micelles, these signals shifted to the same sign as the diagonal peaks. Besides, despite some peak overlap in the high-field region, we clearly observed cross-peaks between the low-field H of MZ-1 and the side-chain H of PBuSEA (highlighted in the red box). These cross-peaks serve as direct evidence of spatial proximity and unequivocally demonstrate strong drug-carrier interactions. Cross-peaks between drug molecules (highlighted in the black box) were also observed (Supplementary Fig. 36b, c). This phenomenon, in conjunction with the attenuated NMR signal intensity and line broadening, provided compelling evidence for the restricted tumbling of MZ-1 within the micelles, suggesting the formation of drug nanoaggregates within micelles.

Drugs containing N-containing substituents tend to achieve high drug LCs within PCCMs

PCCMs exhibit remarkably high LCs for a range of tested drugs; however, the LCs vary substantially—from 22.3 to 61.7%—depending on drug structures. This observation prompted us to figure out which drug-specific physicochemical properties determine their LCs. Therefore, common drug properties, with a specific focus on LogP (the logarithm of the octanol-water partition coefficient), topological polar surface area (tPSA), and Hansen solubility parameter (HSP) distance, were firstly investigated (Supplementary Table 9). These parameters have been widely reported to play crucial roles in influencing the loading capability of traditional polymeric carriers13,14. However, for PCCMs, no significant correlations were observed between LC and either LogP or tPSA (Pearson correlation coefficient, |R| <0.15). A moderate correlation emerged between LC and HSP distance (R = −0.41), suggesting that drug-carrier compatibility contributes to the loading capability of PCCMs (Fig. 6a). Nevertheless, this correlation alone insufficiently explains LC variability, suggesting the influence of yet unidentified factors. This insight necessitates more advanced analytical approaches to decipher the intricate mechanisms. Therefore, E-Dragon software was used to generate an extensive set of 1666 chemical descriptors from selected drug structures and QSPR analysis was conducted by constructing a linear regression model correlating these descriptors with the observed LCs49–52. This approach identified three molecular descriptors—N075, H049, and nN (detailed definitions are presented in the figure legend)—that exhibited strong associations with the LCs, yielding R exceeding 0.5 (Fig. 6b). For simplicity, N075 and H049 roughly reflect the number of aromatic nitrogen(N)-containing heterocycles in our drug set, while nN denotes the total number of N atoms. The consistent emphasis on aromatic N-heterocycles prompted us to stratify our drug set based on the presence or absence of these structural features. Statistical analysis revealed that drugs containing aromatic N-heterocycles exhibited significantly higher LCs compared to those without these rings (p = 0.0007). Remarkably, 67% (14/21) of drugs featuring aromatic N-heterocycles demonstrated the LC exceeding 40%. Subsequently, we attempted to validate whether incorporating aromatic N-heterocycles could enhance drug LCs in PCCMs. Gemfibrozil, a compound that exhibited a relatively low LC of 26.6% in our PCCMs (Table 1), was selected as the first model drug. A series of gemfibrozil derivatives G1–G9 were successfully synthesized and characterized (Supplementary Figs. 37–45). Surprisingly, not only aromatic N-heterocycles, but also any N-containing functional groups substituting the carboxyl group resulted in a significant increase in the LC, elevating it beyond 40%. In contrast, hydrophobic substitutes such as the aliphatic chain, benzene, or dichlorobenzene had negligible impact on the LC (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 46). Similar trends were observed in 7-ethyl-10-hydroxycamptothecin (SN38), which resisted encapsulation within all carriers due to its highly crystalline structure. Substitution of the phenolic hydroxyl group with N-containing moieties even rescued the encapsulation of SN38 (Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48). For instance, irinotecan, a representative derivative of SN38, achieved a remarkably high LC of 44.3% (Fig. 6e).

Collectively, both QSPR analysis and experimental data indicate that drugs bearing N-containing substituents are more readily encapsulated and the incorporation of such substituents via a prodrug strategy markedly enhances drug LCs within PCCMs.

Subsequently, we investigated the influence of Hofmeister series salt ions on the LC. The salting-out Na2SO4 depleted water in cores of PCCMs and thus significantly decreased LCs of both drugs, while salting-in NaSCN showed much less effect, supporting our hydration-based hypothesis (Fig. 7c). Urea with the capability to enhance the solvation of hydrophobic groups exhibited similar effects to NaSCN (Supplementary Fig. 50). Further, 1H-NMR of PEG-PBuSEA/PTX and PEG-PBuSEA/MZ-1 micelles in D2O revealed that the encapsulation of both drugs induced dehydration of micelles. PTX, characterized by multiply hydroxy HB donors and a lack of N-HB acceptors, exhibited a markedly stronger dehydration effect. This was evidenced by a more pronounced decrease in polymer signal intensity and the complete disappearance of drug peaks for PTX, in contrast to the less severe effects observed with the N-HB acceptor-rich MZ-1 (Fig. 7d). The stronger dehydration induced by PTX necessitated more sulfoxide groups for compensation, potentially explaining its relatively lower LC compared to MZ-1. Moreover, nearly all compounds featuring HB donors, such as carboxyl and multiple hydroxyl groups, exhibited relatively low LCs in our drug set, underscoring a critical relationship between HB and LCs.

Besides, we observed a notable temperature sensitivity in the size of PCCMs loaded with certain drugs containing N-HB acceptors (e.g., MZ-1, LC-2, chloroquine, ketoconazole, bifonazole). For instance, MZ-1-loaded PCCMs showed a marked increase in size when cooled from 37 to 4 °C and could revert upon reheating (Fig. 7e). These behaviors strongly suggest that sulfoxide-driven hydration plays a dominant role in determining the micelle structure. Furthermore, hydration of drug aggregates facilitated the stabilization of resulting micelles. For example, PEG-PBuSEA/MZ-1 with the LC of 62% exhibited excellent stability, maintaining the consistent size for 48 h even in complete FBS. While PEG-PBuSEA/PTX at the maximum LC of 30% was unstable and precipitated within 24 h, a slightly lower LC (26%) yielded stable micelles for 48 h in PBS and 24 h in 10% FBS. In contrast, PEG-PDLLA micelles loaded with PTX or MZ-1 aggregated and precipitated within 12 and 24 h, respectively, corroborating previous findings on the instability of PEG-PDLLA micelles (Fig. 7f)41. Both PTX and MZ-1 demonstrated sustained release kinetics from the PCCMs, with cumulative releases reaching 73.8 and 84.1% over 72 h, respectively. In contrast, drug-loaded PEG-PDLLA micelles underwent precipitation as previously described (Supplementary Fig. 51).

Finally, changes in the HB network of PBuSEA coacervates upon guest molecule partitioning were investigated using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). N-benzyl-N-ethylethanamine was employed as a model compound. The characteristic peak of the sulfoxide group in PBuSEA was observed at 1011.43 cm−1, consistent with literature reports indicating that sulfoxide groups engaged in two HBs with water exhibit a peak near 1012 cm−155. At the tested concentration, N-benzyl-N-ethylethanamine displayed negligible absorption in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region. However, upon incorporation of the guest molecule into the coacervates, the sulfoxide peak shifted to 1016.67 cm−1 (Fig. 7g). To minimize the interference from N-benzyl-N-ethylethanamine, spectral subtraction was performed, resulting in a calibrated sulfoxide peak at 1016.59 cm−1 with minimal deviation (Supplementary Fig. 52). This distinct peak shift provides strong evidence for the formation of HB bridges involving guest molecules, water, and sulfoxide groups, further supporting the conclusion that sulfoxide-driven hydration is the key mechanism underlying the stabilization of drug aggregates in PCCMs.

Based on these findings, we conclude that PCCMs stabilize drug nanoaggregates through the formation of bridging HBs among water, drug aggregates, and sulfoxide groups pended on the carriers. Hydrogen bond acceptors, particularly N-containing moieties, actively participate in the sulfoxide- dominated HB network, thereby reducing the solvation free energy and further enhancing aggregate stability. Conversely, HB donors, e.g., hydroxy and carboxyl groups, directly form HBs with sulfoxide groups or other HB acceptors in the drug structures (either intra- or intermolecularly), thereby leading to dehydration and destabilization of aggregates, and ultimately manifesting as a relatively low LC (Fig. 7h).

In vivo drug delivery efficacy of PCCMs

Before the evaluation of delivery efficacy of PCCMs, blood clearance kinetics and tissue distribution were firstly investigated using PTX as the model drug. Clinically approved PEG-PDLLA/PTX micelles (Genexol-PM) served as the control. The blood clearance profiles and distribution in main visceral organs were similar between PEG-PBuSEA/PTX and Genexol-PM (Supplementary Fig. 53–54a). However, PCCMs successfully delivered significantly more PTX to tumors than the Genexol-PM. As the experimental timeline progressed from 3 to 8 h, and ultimately to 36 h, the intratumoral PTX concentration in the PEG-PBuSEA/PTX group became 1.2, 1.5, and ultimately 4.4 times that of the Genexol-PM group, which might be attributed to the enhanced drug LCs (Supplementary Fig. 54b).

Subsequently, the in vivo antitumor efficacy of PEG-PBuSEA/PTX micelles was assessed in a triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 subcutaneous tumor model. PEG-PBuSFO/PTX and Genexol-PM were incorporated as controls, while PEG-PBuTEA-based formulations were excluded from the study due to their insufficient loading capacity for PTX. After four intravenous doses of Genexol-PM or PEG-PBuSEA/PTX micelles, all tumors exhibited gradual shrinkage, culminating in near-complete regression within 16 days (Supplementary Fig. 55a). In contrast, PEG-PBuSFO/PTX micelles failed to impede tumor progression, likely due to their instability, with micelles precipitating in 30 min after preparation. No significant body weight loss was observed in the treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 55b).

In clinical practice, large tumors pose significant clinical challenges and are associated with poor prognosis56. We next challenged the efficacy of PEG-PBuSEA/PTX micelles and Genexol-PM against tumors with initial volume of ~400 mm³, simulating advanced cancer conditions with large, unresectable tumors. Following a regimen of three doses, every mouse in the PEG-PBuSEA/PTX group showed a dramatic reduction in tumor size, with 25% (2/8) achieving complete tumor regression (Supplementary Fig. 56). In contrast, Genexol-PM failed to efficiently inhibit tumor growth in half of the treated mice, while the remaining mice experienced recurrence and rapid tumor growth during the treatment-free interval (Fig. 8a). Consequently, mice treated with PEG-PBuSEA/PTX showed significantly longer median survival (44 days) than those treated with Genexol-PM (26 days, Fig. 8b). To further validate the enhanced PTX delivery efficacy of our PCCMs, we established a more aggressive subcutaneous model using 4T1 triple-negative breast cancer cells where Taxol, the conventional PTX formulation, exhibited no obvious effects (Supplementary Fig. 58)57,58. Consequently, PEG-PBuSEA/PTX demonstrated significantly superior tumor inhibition activity compared to Genexol-PM (p = 0.0031, Fig. 8c). The therapeutic advantage was further evidenced by a marked prolongation of median survival. Mice treated with PEG-PBuSEA/PTX exhibited a median survival of 30 days, significantly outlasting those treated with Genexol-PM (22 days, Fig. 8d). Also, no obvious body weight changes were observed during the experiment (Supplementary Fig. 59). Histopathological analysis using H&E staining revealed no discernible pathological alterations in major organs (Supplementary Fig. 60).

Apart from enhancing delivery of clinically approved chemotherapeutics, the excellent drug loading capacity and improved stability of our PCCMs may offer opportunities for pipeline hydrophobic drugs8. This innovation is particularly significant for compounds necessitating high dosages to achieve therapeutic efficacy, a challenge that has historically constrained the application of traditional carriers. For instance, the remarkably high loading capacity of PCCMs for MZ-1 enabled administration at substantially elevated dosages of 80 mg/kg. In contrast, PEG-PDLLA could only accommodate dosages of up to 15 mg/kg due to its limited loading capacity. As depicted in Fig. 8e–g, four administrations of a suboptimal dosage (15 mg/kg) resulted in only moderate tumor inhibition for both PEG-PBuSEA and PEG-PDLLA formulations. However, PEG-PBuSEA still exhibited superior MZ-1 delivery efficacy compared to PEG-PDLLA, corroborating our previous observations with PTX. Strikingly, when leveraging the high loading capacity of PEG-PBuSEA to deliver a high dosage of MZ-1 (80 mg/kg), merely two administrations were sufficient to effectively halt tumor progression. Western blotting analysis of resected tumors also exhibited obviously lower abundance of the BRD-4 protein (Supplementary Fig. 61). H&E staining of tumor tissues revealed vacuolization in both low-dose groups, whereas pus-like fluid devoid of intact cellular structures was observed in the high-dose MZ-1 group, indicative of extensive tumor necrosis (Supplementary Fig. 62). No obvious body weight changes were observed during the experiment, and H&E staining showed no discernible pathological alterations in major organs, indicating the safety of PEG-PBuSEA/MZ-1 micelles, even at such a high dose of MZ-1 (Supplementary Figs. 63 and 64).

PCCMs also demonstrated the significant potential for co-delivery applications, as they were capable of encapsulating multiple drugs with high LCs (Supplementary Table 7). We previously demonstrated that the combination of the PPT1 inhibitor DC661 and the ASCT2 inhibitor V9302 elicited strong synergistic effects against KRAS-mutant tumor cell lines in vitro (Fig. 8h). However, their simultaneous in vivo administration remains challenging due to the poor co-loading capacity of conventional polymeric carriers. The co-loading capability of PCCMs for both agents enables a comprehensive evaluation of their combined antitumor efficacy in vivo. Thus, a subcutaneous CT26 tumor model harboring KRAS mutations was established. Co-administration of these free drugs resulted in severe toxicity, with a single injection causing ~15% body weight loss and repeated dosing leading to 100% mortality. H&E staining showed cytoplasmic vacuolation of liver and villous atrophy of intestine (Supplementary Fig. 65). Encapsulation of both drugs within PCCMs markedly attenuated systemic toxicity, as evidenced by stable body weights and no discernible pathological alterations of major organs (Fig. 8i and Supplementary Figs. 65 and 66). Consequently, PEG-PBuSEA/DC661&V9302 micelles exhibited superior antitumor efficacy compared to micelles encapsulating either drug alone (Fig. 8j, k and Supplementary Fig. 67).

Finally, comprehensive toxicity evaluations of PCCMs were conducted both in vitro and in vivo. PCCMs showed negligible cell toxicities even at concentrations as high as 1000 μg/mL determined by the CCK8 assay (Supplementary Fig. 68). PCCMs could be hydrolyzed by pig liver esterase (Supplementary Fig. 69). In vivo distribution profiles showed PCCMs were mainly cleared by liver and kidney, with a half-life period of ~30 h (Supplementary Fig. 70). In addition, fifteen systemic injections of PEG-PBuSEA at a high dose of 300 mg/kg in two months induced neither lesions in the major organs nor significant changes in body weight (Supplementary Figs. 71 and 72). Blood routine and serum chemistry tests also showed no significant changes compared to the control group (p > 0.05), suggesting the excellent biocompatibility of PEG-PBuSEA (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11).

Discussion

In sum, we successfully integrated self-coacervated sulfoxide-containing polymers into micelle cores, resulting in the development of one type of micelles—PCCMs. PCCMs possess a high loading capacity for a wide range of hydrophobic drugs and provide a simple, cost-effective, and even organic-solvent-free methodology for hydrophobic drug encapsulation. Moreover, PCCMs exhibit enhanced in vitro stability and in vivo drug delivery efficacy compared to clinically established PMs, highlighting its clinical translational potential in hydrophobic drug delivery.

In contrast to conventional PMs, PCCMs exhibit remarkably high drug loading across a broad spectrum of poorly water-soluble compounds, underscoring their superior versatility and formulation potential. Despite this advantage, LCs of PCCMs vary substantially depending on the molecular structures of the encapsulated drugs. Mechanistic analyses revealed that the presence of HB acceptors, such as N-HB acceptors, rather than hydrophobicity-related parameters such as logP, exhibited a positive influence on LCs59. Conversely, HB donors showed a negative impact due to their propensity to form direct HBs with sulfoxide groups or other HB acceptors within the drug structures (intra- or intermolecularly), thereby reducing HB interactions with water and causing aggregate dehydration. These observations align with established guidelines for drug design that HB donors tend to be less hydrated compared to HB acceptors and lead to very poor solubility53,54.

PEG was employed as the hydrophilic shell to stabilize the coacervates and regulate the size37,60. However, accumulating evidence suggests that PEG can elicit immunogenicity and is increasingly linked to adverse pharmacokinetic profiles due to the induction of anti-PEG antibodies in vivo61,62. Moreover, PEGylated nanocarriers often suffer from limited cellular uptake and poor tumor penetration, ultimately compromising therapeutic efficacy. To overcome these limitations, ongoing efforts are focused on the development of PEG alternatives, such as sulfoxide-containing polymers22 and N-oxide-bearing polymers63,64, to mitigate PEG-related drawbacks and further enhance the delivery performance of PCCMs.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiment

TAMRA-labeled PBuSEA solution (2 mg/mL) was prepared in deionized water following the previously described method. The polymer solution was then transferred to a 35 mm confocal dish and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to allow the coacervates to settle at the bottom of the dish. FRAP experiments were conducted using an Olympus IX83-FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, JP). A defined region of interest (ROI) was photobleached using a laser at 60% power. Subsequently, time-lapse images were acquired at 560 ms intervals to monitor fluorescence recovery. Quantitative analysis of polarity and viscosity

The methodology for measuring polarity and viscosity was previously described in the previous work28,35. Polarity- and viscosity-sensitive fluorescence probes, SBD and BODIPY, were kindly presented by Prof. Xin Zhang from Westlake University.

For the preparation of PBuSEA samples, the polymer was dissolved in deionized water at a concentration of 2 mg/mL (1 mL total volume) at 4 °C. Subsequently, this solution was mixed with either SBD or BODIPY at a final concentration of 10 μM (1 μL of 10 mM stock solution). Probe-loaded microspheres prepared by a (W/O/W) double emulsion method. Briefly, 10 mg of polymers and the corresponding fluorescent probe were co-dissolved in 1 mL of DCM. Then, 200 μL of water was added into the organic phase, followed by treatment with an ultrasonic crusher (JY92-IIN, SCIENTZ, China) at 200 W for 1 min. Next, 4.8 mL of water was added, and the mixture was re-treated with an ultrasonic crusher at 200 W for 1 min. DCM was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. Probe-loaded micelles were prepared using the thin-film hydration method. Briefly, 2 mg of the polymer and the corresponding fluorescent probe were co-dissolved in 0.5 mL of ethanol or DCM. The solvent was then evaporated to form a thin film. Subsequently, 1 mL of deionized water was added to hydrate the film, and the resulting mixture was sonicated for 3 min in an ultrasonic bath to achieve dispersion.

Lifetime information was acquired by a FS5 spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK.) or a Leica STELLARIS 8 FALCON confocal microscope equipped with a 405-nm laser and a pulsed white light laser (Leica, Germany) at 37 °C. FLIM images of SBD-loaded and BODIPY-loaded samples were imaged using pulsed laser lines of 448 and 488 nm with a repetition rate of 10 MHz. ROI was selected using LAS X FLIM/FCS software to give fluorescence lifetime histograms and fluorescence lifetime decay curves. Fluorescence lifetime decay was fitted using a multi-exponential reconvolution model to give intensity-weighted mean fluorescence lifetime. Subsequently, the fluorescence lifetimes were converted into dielectric constant or viscosity values using standard calibration curves. The BODIPY calibration curve was obtained by measuring fluorescence lifetimes of fluorophores in glycerol at different temperatures. The SBD calibration curve was obtained by measuring fluorescence lifetimes of fluorophores in methanol–dioxane mixtures at different mixing ratios.

Preparation of drug-loaded micelles

Drug-loaded micelles were prepared using the typical thin-film hydration method. Specifically, PEG-PBuSEA (1 mg) and the corresponding drugs were dissolved in 200 μL ethanol or DCM, depending on the solubility. The solvent was then removed using a rotary evaporator to form a thin film. Hydration was achieved by adding 1 mL of water, followed by immediate shaking in a 45 °C water bath for 1 min. These steps were sufficient to prepare drug-loaded PCCMs with uniform size distributions for most tested drugs. However, for certain drugs—including PTX, DTX, gemfibrozil, rifaximin, curcumin, reserpine, permethrin, apalutamide, eltrombopag, and ARV-825—additional processing was required. After hydration, these mixtures were subjected to sonication in an ultrasonic bath for 1 min, followed by treatment with an ultrasonic crusher (JY92-IIN, SCIENTZ, China) at 200 W for 1 min to ensure the formation of nanosized micelles. The prepared drug-loaded micelles were stored at 37 °C. Size distributions of the micelle samples were analyzed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS at 37 °C without any further treatment such as filtration or centrifugation. All control micelles—PEG5k-PCL10k, PEG5k-PLGA10k, and PEG2k-PDLLA1.75k—were prepared using the latter method involving hydration and ultrasonic dispersion. As an example, PEG-PBuSEA/PTX and PEG-PDLLA/PTX micelles were prepared by thin-film hydration method. PEG-PBuSEA or PEG-PDLLA (1 mg), and PTX (0.43 mg or 0.3 mg, respectively) were dissolved in 200 μL DCM. The solvent was then removed using a rotary evaporator and 1 mL of water was added. These mixtures were subjected to sonication in an ultrasonic bath for 1 min, followed by treatment with an ultrasonic crusher at 200 W for 1 min.

“Mix-and-Go”-mediated drug loading

PTX solution (1.65 mg in 10 μL DMSO) was carefully deposited at the bottom of a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Subsequently, 500 μL of pre-formed PEG-PBuSEA or PEG-PDLLA micelle solution (10 mg/mL) was gently added to the same tube. The addition of the micelle solution resulted in the immediate formation of visible PTX aggregates. The sample was then subjected to repetitive pipetting for 1 min and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 3 min. Detailed operation procedure was shown in Supplementary Movies 2 and 3. In the cases of sorafenib and curcumin, sorafenib (1.5 mg in 8 μL of DMSO) or curcumin (0.8 mg in 5 μL of DMSO) was deposited at the bottom of a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and underwent the same experimental process as described for PTX.

The sample was subjected to sonication in a water bath for 5 min at 37 °C to wet and “dissolve” the drug candidates. Subsequently, an additional 950 μL of water was introduced to the mixture, followed by a second round of sonication for 5 min.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The structures of drugs and monomers BuTEA, BuSEA, and BuSFO were constructed using the Avogadro software65. These structures were then submitted to the Paramchem server to derive their respective CGenFF force field parameters66,67. Subsequently, polymer chains with 20 repeat units, denoted as PBuTEA, PBuSEA, and PBuSFO, were generated based on their monomeric parameters. Firstly, three systems were created by randomly placing ten homogeneous polymer chains into 10 × 10 × 10 nm³ simulation boxes to study the one-component polymer’s aggregation. Similarly, to study the potential drug loading mechanism, additional drug-polymer systems were created by inserting ten drug molecules into the aforementioned simulated boxes. All simulation boxes were further solvated with TIP3P water68.

The MD simulation systems were conducted with a cut-off of 1.2 nm for the van der Waals interactions and electrostatic interactions, while the long-range electrostatics were treated with the PME method69. Energy minimizations were conducted with the steepest descent algorithm and MD simulations were run with the default leap-frog integrator using a time-step of 2 fs for the integration in the isobaric-isochoric ensemble. The temperature (T = 300 K) and pressure (P = 1 atm) were maintained using a stochastic velocity rescaling thermostat70 and Parrinello-Rahman barostat71, respectively. All MD simulations lasted for 200 ns and were performed by using the GROMACS 2020 package72. The simulation snapshots were then rendered with VMD73, while Python library MDAnalysis74,75 was adopted for a further analysis of the MD simulation results.

Quantitative structure-property relationship (QSPR) analysis

Forty drugs from Table 1 were selected for QSPR analysis. Molecular structure files in SDF format were retrieved from PubChem database. A set of 1666 chemical descriptors were calculated using E-Dragon (https://vcclab.org/lab/edragon/)49,50. A linear correlation model was constructed to elucidate the relationship between these molecular descriptors and the corresponding LCs using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software. The analysis revealed that three molecular descriptors, N075, H049, and nN, demonstrated strong correlations with the LC values.

N-075 represents the atom-centered fragment R–N–R/R–N–X (R represents any group linked through carbon; X represents any electronegative atom; – represents an aromatic bond as in benzene or delocalized bonds). H-049 represents the number of hydrogen atoms attached to C3(sp3), C2(sp2), C3(sp2), or C3(sp) carbon atoms, in which the superscripts mean formal oxidation number. nN represents the number of nitrogen atoms.

After the experiments, major viscera (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining for histopathological analysis. For Western blot analysis, the tumor model was re-established, and mice were subjected to the same dosing regimen. Tumors were collected two days after the final injection for subsequent protein analysis.

In vivo antitumor activity of PCCMs co-loading DC661 and V9302

The CT26 subcutaneous tumor model was established by inoculation of CT26 cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse) in the right flank of female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old). When the average tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly grouped. PBS, PEG-PBuSEA/DC661 (LC = 10%, 5 mg/mL), PEG-PBuSEA/V9302 (LC = 32%, 45 mg/mL), PEG-PBuSEA/DC661&V9302 (total LC = 50%; DC661, 5 mg/mL; V9302, 45 mg/mL), or free DC661&V9302 (5 and 45 mg/kg) were administered intravenously for a total of three doses (Day 0, 2, and 5). Two administrations of free DC661&V9302 leaded to 100% mortality. Major viscera (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and intestine) were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining for histopathological analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software and presented as means ± SD. One-way ANOVA tests with Tukey’s multiple comparisons were applied unless otherwise indicated. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The experiments shown in Figs. 2c–e, g, j, 3d, and 5a–c were repeated independently three times with similar results.
