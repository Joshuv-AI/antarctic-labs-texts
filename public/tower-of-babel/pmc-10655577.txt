Sculptor merges engineering and art to conjure connections among people and nature
Authors: Stephen Ornes
Published: 2023

Artist Janet Echelman thinks big. One of her recent installations is a 229-foot-long mesh sculpture suspended over a downtown intersection in Columbus, Ohio—the largest public artwork in the city’s history. Unveiled in June 2023 and titled Current, it sweeps down from its highest perch more than 120 feet above the ground into a series of cascading forms that look like complicated waves. It’s large enough that it can be viewed from below, by pedestrians, or from above, by passengers in airplanes. At night, it glows red and blue. Current is not Echelman’s largest, however. It's outdone by Skies Painted with Unnumbered Sparks, which she installed in 2015 over Vancouver. Her first big-scale permanent sculpture, She Changes, was installed in a small town north of Porto, Portugal, in 2005 and measures 300 feet long.

As an artist, Echelman specializes in enormous sculptures built from nets that float over open public places. Her sculptures are brightly colored and dramatically illuminated and have appeared in dozens of cities on five continents, with new installations in progress in Frisco, Texas, and Cambridge, Massachusetts. Her expansive works transform urban spaces, inviting viewers to ponder how the world is connected in unexpected ways—or to simply relax as the net above undulates with the wind. The works also connect people, bringing viewers of varying backgrounds into the same space.

But sparking connectedness in town squares with huge outdoor mesh sculptures requires more than an artistic sensibility. For Echelman’s designs and installations, she has called on the expertise of aeronautical experts, lighting designers, computer scientists, and engineers. The sculptures they produce are the result of years-long collaborations that blend aesthetic considerations with intensive engineering, computational modeling, and software design.

All that computation and analysis, she says, can inform the evolution of the creative idea and the final appearance. The ultimate form of Current, for example, emerged after several iterations of inspirational sketches, computational models, engineering adjustments, and architectural tweaks. When Echelman designs a sculpture for a space, she intentionally plans how it will coexist with the surrounding structures. Perhaps most importantly, she wants her pieces to cooperate with the weather, and not stand against it.

“In a lot of structures like buildings and bridges, we try to resist nature,” says Sigrid Adriaenssens, a civil engineer at Princeton University who has collaborated with Echelman. “But her sculptures don’t resist. They actually flow with nature; they visualize the wind as it goes through it. I think that’s what is beautiful in Janet’s work. I think that’s why engineers are fascinated with it.”

Working with Mother Nature

While Echelman’s works appear abstract, they require precise, real-world calculations involving variables such as torque, force, and pressure—especially important when trying to predict how the piece will respond to the weather. “My sculptures have to withstand the same core requirements as a skyscraper,” Echelman says. “If a building on the coast of Florida has to withstand 150-miles-per-hour winds on the coast, my sculpture also needs to be able to withstand 150-miles-per-hour winds.”

Over the last three decades, she has earned a legion of fans. Echelman’s work shows how science and art can speak to each other in the languages of inspiration, design, and computation. “She has the uncommon ability to ask probing questions, even in domains she knows nothing about,” says oncologist and cancer researcher Todd Golub, the director of the Broad Institute in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Golub, who met Echelman a decade ago through one of the institute’s first artists-in-residence programs, has been helping to bring one of her sculptures to the Broad. “That’s where I think the artist–scientist dialogue gets the most interesting—when the artist manages to ask questions that make the scientist think about assumptions we take for granted,” he says.

Nets on a Beach

Echelman’s studio is full of nets. Heavy white nets with big holes dangle from rectangular wooden frames just below the ceiling. Dense tangles of blue, red, and orange nets, made from thin fibers, float like prismatic clouds over the door. Black-and-white sketches of nets cover walls and cabinets, alongside computer-generated images of large, netted blobs.

Some people look at nets and think fishing. But, to Echelman, nets provide a material way to probe complex ideas about how systems and scientific disciplines connect to each other. “When any single node in the mesh is moved, every other node is consequently changed,” she says. “My work, in a very obvious and literal sense, is a manifestation of interconnectedness.”

To create Current, which will be a seasonal installation, Echelman used nearly 80 miles of red and blue polyethylene fiber, engineered to be 15 times stronger than steel. The nets alone weigh more than a ton. The fiber is the same material that NASA used to tether Mars rovers to parachutes as they descended to the surface of the Red Planet. The shape is held together by more than a half-million knots and cables that anchor it to surrounding buildings. A large portion were tied by hand; the rest by machine. Echelman’s sculptures often take on the appearance of prismatic ghostly networks in the sky. Every push or pull—by wind, hand, or rain—temporarily reshapes the whole structure.

“Like a 4D structure, her work continually adjusts its shape to be in equilibrium with whatever loads are on it at the time,” says Adriaenssens. For Echelman, that equilibrium reflects the ways in which dynamic systems respond to each other, a concept that comprises a big part of her approach to sculpture.

Echelman’s artistic career began more than 35 years ago. She studied calligraphy and painting in Hong Kong after college, in the late 1980s, and afterward, she worked with textile artists in Bali. In 1997, as part of a Fulbright scholarship, Echelman was supposed to conduct a painting exhibition in the coastal fishing village of Mahabalipuram, in southern India, but her paints were delayed in transit. Desperate for a last-minute substitution, she observed fishermen using nets on the beach and got an idea.

Nets, she realized, offered a way to create large-volume sculptures without bulky solid materials like metals. They could produce big shapes—not by gluing or fixing fibers in place, but by making intentional decisions about where and how hard to pull on the mesh, and in what direction. “These [works] can be unintuitive and tricky to design,” says architect and structural engineer Caitlin Mueller, at MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, who has co-taught design classes with Echelman and is currently collaborating with her on a forthcoming installation for the MIT Museum. “When you have materials like steel or timber, they go where they tell us to go,” she says. “But structures made out of flexible materials don’t do that.” The shape of soft sculptures depends on tensile equilibrium, Mueller says, and designing them requires physical experimentation—pushing and pulling in different ways.

Expert Counsel

In 2004, Echelman was commissioned to create She Changes, the giant, billowing sculpture over a public park near the city of Porto, Portugal. The scale of the project was larger than anything she’d made before, and conventional net fibers wouldn’t provide sufficient strength to hold it up. She needed stronger materials—and outside expertise.

“That was the first time where I worked with computer scientists in computation,” she says. “We had to prove it could withstand a hurricane.” Architects and engineers worked with her to develop computer simulations. She Changes shifts as ocean winds blow off the Atlantic, and the motion is projected by its shadows on the grass.

Since then, for every design, she has worked with an interdisciplinary team to generate intricate, 3D computer models that simulate the forces holding the pieces together and show how it will respond to outside forces like wind and rain. She has collaborated with engineers from the architectural firm of Skidmore, Owings, & Merrill, which designed the Burj Khalifa, the world’s tallest building, in Dubai.

Scientists and engineers don’t just help construct Echelman’s works; their data have helped inspire the woven sculptures. For 1.8 Renwick, a rippling red-and-orange net sculpture currently displayed at the Renwick Gallery of the Smithsonian American Art Museum in Washington, DC, Echelman looked to an intriguing dataset from Richard Gross, a geophysicist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, California, who studies variations in Earth’s gravity and rotation. In the wake of the 2011 magnitude 9.0 earthquake and tsunami that originated about 80 miles off the coast of Japan—and subsequently tragically killed nearly 20,000 people and swamped a nuclear reactor—Gross calculated that the disturbance had sped up the Earth’s rotation enough to shorten the length of a day by roughly 1.8 millionths of a second.

“An earthquake in one location can create a tsunami that affects the wave heights as they change throughout the ocean,” Echelman says. Her Renwick piece represents that connection through far-reaching, colorful waves of varying dimension. “I’m often looking for datasets that reveal unexpected linkages.” Echelman says her goal is to invite viewers to think about their own networks. “They remind us where we are in the larger unfolding of time,” she says.

Mueller, at MIT, says that Echelman’s approach to sculpture— which embraces the notion of experimenting with forces—is not dissimilar from techniques used by some groundbreaking architects. Catalan architect Antoni Gaudí, for example, used ropes, levers, and weights to experiment with the catenary arches found in the Sagrada Familia in Barcelona and his other buildings. “He used trial and error, and he interacted with the form as it emerged from physical simulations,” Mueller says. “So it’s not this one shot: I draw it, I build it. It’s more subtle.”

Together with students in her lab, Mueller has helped design computational programs specifically developed to answer the physics and engineering questions posed by Echelman’s nets. They can model thousands or millions of real-world experiments simultaneously. The computer does the heavy numerical lifting, Mueller says, “and we are able to think more abstractly. The discovery process is still fully human.”

It’s also iterative. “Frequently, feedback from our engineering collaborators comes into my mind and changes the way I think,” Echelman says. They often recommend incorporating a saddle-shaped area in the structure, for example, to keep it stable, she says. “And the more I come to understand how those forces interact, the more it influences how I think, aesthetically.” She also points to the evolution of her bespoke software tools: In early versions, they didn’t flag configurations of nets that were physically unbuildable, leading to the need for corrections in sculptures, and in the tools.

Over the years, through trial and error, she and her team have improved their ability to model interactive forces and predict buildable designs, which guide her final decisions. For Golub, Echelman’s approach echoed the way he thinks about genomics. Recent research, he notes, has revealed that the genome itself is not a rigid string of nucleotides. “The more we learn about the genome, the more we see that it’s not static; it’s a dynamic folding and unfolding,” Golub says, evoking an Echelman sculpture moving rhythmically with the breeze. “Almost like a breathing thing.”

Indeed, the design for the Broad Institute has been inspired by her conversations with genomics experts and other scientists. Their research, Echelman says, “gives me insight, and that work becomes reference points.” The sculpture will appear to unfold as it flows from outside the main building into the glass-filled foyer, she says. “It will lead you into the building,” she says. “And it’s exploring how the basic science research that unfolds there impacts life itself.” Her new piece in Frisco, Texas, was inspired by monarch butterflies, which migrate through the region every year, and for her upcoming installation for the MIT Museum, she’s invited choreographers to interact with hanging nets, to experiment with changing forms that emerge from human interactions. What ties all these works together, she says, is how they draw on the shifting structures of networks.

“All my work, in a way, is about resilience, about movingand shifting and changing,” she says. It’s also, ultimately, about time, Echelman says. “How do we enter into an experience that reminds us of our interconnectedness with these larger cycles of the planet, and of time?”
