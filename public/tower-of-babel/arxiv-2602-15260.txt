Fast and Effective On-policy Distillation from Reasoning Prefixes
Dongxu ZhangZhichao YangSepehr JanghorbaniJun Han Andrew Ressler II Qian Qian Gregory D. Lyng, Sanjit Singh Batra Robert E. Tillman Optum AI

Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD), which samples trajectories from the student model and supervises them with a teacher at the token level, avoids relying solely on verifiable terminal rewards and can yield better generalization than off-policy distillation. However, OPD requires expensive on-the-fly sampling of the student policy during training, which substantially increases training cost, especially for long responses. Our initial analysis shows that, during OPD, training signals are often concentrated in the prefix of each output, and that even a short teacher-generated prefix can significantly help the student produce the correct answer. Motivated by these observations, we propose a simple yet effective modification of OPD: we apply the distillation objective only to prefixes of student-generated outputs and terminate each sampling early during distillation. Experiments on a suite of AI-for-Math and out-of-domain benchmarks show that on-policy prefix distillation matches the performance of full OPD while reducing training FLOP by 2×–47×.



1
Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) continue to scale in size, model compression
2
becomes increasingly important for deploying LLMs to real-world applications where resources are often limited.
Knowledge distillation
9
is a widely-used technique in machine learning for compressing a large teacher model into a smaller student model while retaining the teacher’s capabilities.

In the context of LLMs, distillation is commonly performed via

sequence-level knowledge distillation
(SeqKD), where the teacher first generates a fixed set of off-policy token trajectories and the student is trained via next-token prediction on these teacher outputs (i.e., SFT on teacher-generated sequences)

14
;
27
;
33
;
24
.
Despite its broad adoption, this approach has a notable limitation, known as the
exposure bias problem
, where the training is exclusively using the teacher-generated data; therefore, the student rarely learns from its own induced states and errors can accumulate at test time
19
.
More broadly, recent work suggests that aggressive SFT can be associated with degraded generalization
(
3
)
, catastrophic forgetting
(
23
;
13
)
, and increased hallucination rates
(
4
;
12
)
, highlighting the need for training signals that better match the student’s deployment-time state distribution.
These limitations also motivate moving beyond purely off-policy imitation.

On-policy learning
34
, widely studied in reinforcement learning (RL), trains a model using trajectories sampled from its own policy, reducing train–test mismatch.
In LLM training, this means sampling from the
current
student during optimization rather than relying on a fixed teacher-generated dataset.
However, many on-policy RL methods require full rollouts to obtain terminal rewards
18
, which can be prohibitively expensive for long-form reasoning.

On-policy distillation (OPD)
(
19
;
5
;
16
;
1
;
22
)
addresses key limitations of these issues from SFT and RL by minimizing the divergence between policies of student-generated outputs (SGO) and a teacher at token level.
Compared to reward-based RL, OPD provides dense, token-level teacher feedback without requiring task-specific reward design.
This dense supervision can make OPD more learning-efficient than sparse-reward RL; however, each update is still expensive:
to preserve the on-policy property, OPD must generate student rollouts on the fly at each update rather than reusing a static off-policy dataset.
In reasoning-heavy tasks, these solution trajectories can span tens of thousands of tokens, making both sampling and teacher scoring prohibitively expensive.

Figure 1:
On-policy prefix distillation with prefix scheduling.
During training, gradients are backpropagated only through the first
L
tokens of each student rollout, and sampling is terminated after
L
. Prefix scheduling increases
L
by a fixed amount at each training step. At test time, the student generates autoregressively until producing the final answer.

Because OPD provides token-level teacher feedback, full-length supervision may be unnecessary.
This raises our central research question:

Can we preserve the benefits of on-policy distillation while
only
supervising the prefix of each student trajectory, thereby reducing the training cost?

We test two hypotheses: (a) OPD’s learning signal is assigned unequally towards early tokens; (b) aligning this prefix is often sufficient for the student to complete the remaining reasoning correctly.
Section
2
supports these hypotheses, and experiments on math-reasoning benchmarks show that prefix OPD achieves performance close to full OPD while reducing training compute significiantly.

Contributions.

•

We propose
on-policy prefix distillation
, which truncates rollouts during training and applies distillation only on the prefix.

•

We introduce a simple
prefix scheduling
strategy that progressively increases the trained prefix length over training.

•

On AI-for-Math and out-of-domain benchmarks, prefix OPD closely matches full OPD while reducing training FLOP by 2×–47×.

2
Preliminary Study

In this section, we first describe the learning objective of on-policy distillation. Then, we provide experimental analysis to validate the idea of on-policy prefix distillation.

2.1
On-policy Distillation

On-policy distillation generalizes classical off-policy knowledge distillation where at each iteration the student policy
\pi_{s}
gathers data and is updated to maximize the similarity to a fixed teacher
\pi_{T}
using Kullback–Leibler divergence:

\min_{\pi_{s}}\;\Bigl\{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{s}\,\|\,\pi_{T})\Bigr\}.

Since the expectation is taken over the student’s own state distribution, OPD avoids the distributional mismatch that plagues off-policy imitation and yields gradients aligned with the deployment policy.

We choose to use the reverse KL divergence because it encourages the student to assign high probability to teacher-preferred actions, reducing opportunities for reward hacking, which often happens when using forward KL. Its mode-seeking nature also reduces the risk of spreading the student policy over many suboptimal options
5
;
7

1
1

1

We use AI4Math benchmarks in this work for evaluation, and often a math problem can be solved by more than one solution path. For example, in Appendix
F
, we show that AIME-2024 has approximately 5.4 unique solutions per question.
.
In addition, though previous work
1
also allows using a mixture of forward and reverse KL, it shows that for arithmetic reasoning, such Jensen-Shannon divergence could lead to decreased performance.

During training, since it is intractable to sample all possible responses, we adopt the following unbiased estimation via sampling:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{s}\,\|\,\pi_{T})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:L}\sim\pi_{s}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{L-1}\log\frac{\pi_{s}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})}{\pi_{T}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})}\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{L_{i}-1}\log\frac{\pi_{s}(x^{(i)}_{t+1}\mid x^{(i)}_{1:t})}{\pi_{T}(x^{(i)}_{t+1}\mid x^{(i)}_{1:t})},

(1)

where
L
is the maximum response length and
N
is the number of samples. Note that this also allows one to avoid storing logits over the full vocabulary for each token in the trajectory
2
2

2

A single sample of response with 16k tokens would take 4.8GB memory under BF16. This was not a challenge in the previous on-policy distillation work such as GKD, MiniLLM, DistillLM as the max response length was often 512.
.

Optionally, a student can also receive rewards from the environment and/or use entropy loss or KL regularization to its old policy for robustness and specific downstream needs. However, in this work we do not explore these losses for simplicity and focus on the on-policy distillation component.

Note that we leverage vllm
17
to speed up sampling process. To correct the off-policy issue due to floating-point non-associativity during rollouts
7
,
we adopt importance sampling during gradient update

3
3

3

For more details, please refer to
https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/losses#policy-gradient-importance_sampling
. We also follow this implementation and sum the token-level losses over the sequence length.
.

Does Prefix Receive More Training Signals?

To understand where the learning signal originates from, we measure the token-level average reverse-KL loss from Eq.
1
over tokens grouped into each positional bin along the trajectory during on-policy distillation training.

We use Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base
36
as the student models, and Qwen3-8B as the teacher model.
Figure
2
shows the distribution of the average magnitude of reverse-KL loss at 10th, 20th, and 30th steps of full on-policy distillation training on the questions sampled from OpenThoughts3
(
6
)
dataset. Each point in the curve corresponds to a positional bin covering 256 tokens, with a maximum output length of 16,384 tokens.
During training, batch size 512 and learning rate 5e-5 are used, following
22

The distribution plot reveals that training signal is concentrated in the earlier generated tokens, and this effect is consistent across various training steps and model sizes. We conjecture that this pattern reflects the student model being weaker at high-level planning captured early in the trajectory than at executing later steps of a plan.

2.3
Can a Student Model Complete a Truncated Response from the Teacher without Training?

Another question is whether a student that receives supervision only at the beginning of its responses can subsequently finish the solution correctly.
To probe this, we conduct a
teacher-prefix
study: we let the teacher LLM (Qwen3-8B) generate full solutions, feed the first
L
tokens (
L\!\in\!\{2048,4096,6144,8192\}
) to un-trained students, and ask them to continue the reasoning unaided.
This experiment is purely probing the model’s ability to “fill in the tail.”

on AIME-24 when an untrained student (Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-1.7B-Base) continues from varying lengths of a teacher (Qwen3-8B)-generated prefix.

Figure
3
reports mean accuracy at 16 samples on AIME-24. During this experiment, we also ensure that teacher’s final answer is not likely in the prefix by enforcing
L_{p}\leftarrow\min\{L_{p},L_{r}-512\}
where
L_{p}
is the length of teacher’s prefix and
L_{r}

Firstly, the plot shows that teacher’s reasoning prefix does improve students’ performances, suggesting that the early guidance from the teacher can effectively help students to infer the remaining steps even without further training.
In addition, the curve also shows that the performance jumps sharply given the first few thousands teacher tokens and the increase slows down thereafter. This again verifies that the prefix is crucial during the reasoning process. We further provide a case study from AIME-24 to illustrate the effect of the teacher’s reasoning prefix in Section
4.4
.

3
Methodology

Figure
1
shows the main idea of prefix distillation.
In this work, we enforce prefix-only training by limiting the maximum number of tokens from generated response during
training
to
L^{\text{train}}
.
No additional modification to the loss or sampling procedure is required; we simply early-stop the on-policy generation once the budget is reached, back-propagate through the truncated trajectory for gradient updates, then proceed to the next batch. The on-policy property is preserved for the first
L^{\text{train}}
tokens.

At evaluation time we remove the cap
4
4

4

In the rest of the experiments, we allow up to
L^{\text{test}}{=}16384
tokens
, ensuring that final task performance is not artificially constrained.
This design choice keeps the implementation minimal, yet yields significant savings of computation during training.

Special Tokens.

Though memory friendly for long reasoning scenarios, we observe that it is challenging for the
sample-based
KL estimation in Eq.
1
to learn from generating special tokens. When such supervision is provided from the teacher, the student repeatedly attempts to switch to a different token which leads to generating unreadable content. To avoid falling into such a scenario, in our training,
<think>
is enforced at the end of the input prompt, to align with the teacher’s behavior.

Prefix Scheduling.

Although short prefixes reduce cost, later tokens in the student’s rollout can still carry valuable supervision when scored under the teacher. To progressively expose the student to longer-horizon feedback, we explore a simple linear schedule that increases the training prefix length after each gradient update:

L^{\text{train}}\leftarrow L^{\text{train}}+\Delta_{L}
.
In our experiments,
L^{\text{train}}
starts with 1 and
\Delta_{L}=256
.

Main Results

Table

The best and second-best results within each column are highlighted with bold and underline accordingly, excluding results with sufficient budgets.

Observing the baselines, it is apparent that given a sufficient training budget, full OPD shows better out-of-domain performance, and similar or slightly worse in-domain performance than SeqKD, indicating that on-policy distillation indeed generalizes better than off-policy distillation.

In-domain.

From in-domain results, OPD with prefix 1024 / 2048 outperforms baselines with limited budgets in AIME-24 and AIME-25 with half the FLOP. On MATH500, OPD with prefix outperforms OPD (full, 10 steps), while performing only slightly worse than SeqKD (300 steps).
When using Qwen3-8B-Base as the student, prefix OPD approaches the performances with sufficient budgets while use less than 10% the FLOP.
These results indicate that prefix OPD can effectively reduce the training cost while maintaining a competitive in-domain performance.

Out-of-domain.

We observe different out-of-domain behavior depending on student size.
For Qwen3-8B-Base, prefix OPD improves over the base model, and performance
tends to move closer to full OPD as we increase
L^{\text{train}}

(Table
2
).
In contrast, for Qwen3-1.7B-Base, training on very short prefixes can largely reduce
out-of-domain accuracy below the pre-trained student (Table
1
).
One plausible explanation is that, for small students, optimizing only early tokens
can improve how the solution starts but worsen the model’s ability to continue reliably
over long responses (also see
Finding 3
).
In these cases, prefix scheduling (gradually increasing
L^{\text{train}}
during training)
is a more robust choice.
As shown from Table
1
, OPD with prefix scheduling leads to competitive out-of-domain performance.

Scheduling.

Our linear prefix scheduling strategy provides benefits of both prefix distillation and full trajectory distillation. Across all settings, scheduled prefix OPD matches the upper-bound performance of full OPD while using less than 50% of the FLOP. These results suggest that allocating compute progressively to longer prefixes is effective. It is worth exploring more adaptive schedulers (e.g., curriculum based on validation performance or KL/entropy signals) in future work.

In particular, we apply a sliding window of same length to the rollouts and only pass through policy gradients within the window. The windows we applied are [1024, 2047], [2048, 3071], [3072, 4095] and 1024 tokens on the tail.

4.4
Case Study

We use an AIME-2024 example to illustrate how
early
guidance can prevent compounding reasoning errors and why prefix-only supervision can be effective. In this example, we use Qwen3-8B-Base as the initial student and Qwen3-8B as the teacher model.

An AIME-2024 Problem.

Let
x,y
and
z
be positive real numbers that satisfy the following system of equations:

\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{yz}\right)=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{y}{xz}\right)=\frac{1}{3},\qquad\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{z}{xy}\right)=\frac{1}{4}.

Then the value of
\left|\log_{2}(x^{4}y^{3}z^{2})\right|
is
\tfrac{m}{n}
where
m
and
n
are relatively prime positive integers. Find
m+n
.

A Valid Solution Sketch.

Let
a=\log_{2}x
,
b=\log_{2}y
,
c=\log_{2}z
. Using

\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{yz}\right)=\log_{2}x-\log_{2}y-\log_{2}z
,
the system becomes

a-b-c=\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad b-a-c=\tfrac{1}{3},\qquad c-a-b=\tfrac{1}{4}.

Solving yields
a=-\tfrac{7}{24}
,
b=-\tfrac{3}{8}
,
c=-\tfrac{5}{12}
, hence

\bigl|\log_{2}(x^{4}y^{3}z^{2})\bigr|=\bigl|4a+3b+2c\bigr|=\tfrac{25}{8},

so
m+n=33
.

Behavior Comparison:

–

Base student (incorrect).

When prompted directly, the model makes an early conversion error, e.g., rewriting the system as

x=2^{1/4}yz,\;y=2^{1/5}xz,\;z=2^{1/6}xy

(already inconsistent with
\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{4}
), then carries this mistake through and produces a confident but wrong final answer (e.g.,
m+n=5
), without detecting the inconsistency.

–

Base student + teacher prefix (correct).

If we prepend a
short
teacher-generated prefix (128 tokens from Qwen3-8B; containing only the initial reasoning, excluding the final answer), the same student follows the correct transformations
(e.g.,
\frac{x}{yz}=2^{1/2}
), then leverages algebraic elimination and reaches the correct result
m+n=33
.

–

Student after prefix OPD (correct).

After on-policy prefix distillation, the student no longer requires an explicit teacher prefix: given the original question alone, it immediately introduces
(a,b,c)
, solves the linear equations, computes

4a+3b+2c=-\tfrac{25}{8}
, and returns
m+n=33
.

This case study highlights that aligning the
start
of the student’s reasoning can prevent early, compounding errors and change the final outcome. Full prompts and generations are provided in Appendix
A
.

Cost and Performance Trade-off

If we compare performances on the left sides (10th and 20th steps) of each curve, we can see that shorter prefix can often enable significant accuracy gain in comparison to a longer prefix.
For instance, the bottom figure shows that SeqKD requires

, and full OPD requires
2.7\times 10^{19}

5.7\times 10^{17}

In contrast, if we compare accuracies of right sides (50th and 60th steps) of each curve, we often see that a longer prefix can lead to higher accuracies
9
9

9

Exceptions are often due to variances during training.
. This suggests that when given sufficient budgets for training, a longer prefix is preferred for better performances, and that
prefix scheduling
is highly recommended rather than a full distillation from the beginning of training. This strategy can often save at least half of the training cost.

5.2
How Does Prefix Learning Affect the Tail?

To investigate the effect of prefix-only learning beyond the prefix length, we plot a token level loss distribution with OPD with prefix=1024 on Qwen3-8B-Base on Openthoughts3.

Finding 2: Prefix on-policy distillation can reduce the loss on the tokens outside of prefix.

Fig
5
shows the distribution over different position of rollout at step 10, 20 and 30. It shows that with more prefix OPD training, token losses outside the prefix (position 1024) also decrease (shown as the down arrows). The decrease is most significant when close to the prefix and fades away on the tail.
This is not surprising as the knowledge learned at the prefix may also be leveraged during the rest of the output through the model’s parameters.

Finding 3: Learning the prefix benefits the tail for a student model with sufficient parameters, but can hurt the tail given a small model size.

Interestingly, from Fig
5
, for a compact model like Qwen3-1.7B-Base, the loss is increased on the tail with more training steps (shown as the up arrow in the figure). This indicates that a small model size could lead to the prefix ”competing” the loss reduction with the tail rather than ”sharing” the benefit during on-policy distillation.

Why can off-policy be challenging under prefix truncation?

A plausible explanation is that truncation amplifies distribution mismatch: with off-policy training, the student is optimized on prefix states induced by another policy that it may not reliably visit when sampling from its own policy. Since supervision is limited to only the first
L^{\text{train}}
tokens, the student must still generate the entire tail from these potentially mismatched states, which can negate the benefit of prefix-level distillation.

Finally, we note that most related work (ImitKD, GKD, DistiLLM, etc.) compute full-distribution divergences over the vocabulary can be memory-intensive for long contexts. In our GKD experiments with Qwen3 models, natively increasing the rollout length (e.g.,
L^{\text{train}}{>}512
) can lead to out-of-memory errors. By restricting distillation to a short prefix, our approach substantially reduces the memory footprint of full-distribution distillation, making these objectives more accessible for long-form reasoning.

6
Related Work

On-policy Distillation.

On-policy learning is a long-standing paradigm in reinforcement learning
34
;
29
, in which the data used for learning are collected by the same policy that is being optimized. This alignment between data collection and the learned policy reduces distributional mismatch between training and deployment, often leading to more robust behavior. Recent work on on-policy distillation
(
19
;
5
;
16
;
1
;
22
;
15
)
adapts this idea to a distillation setting: the student samples its own responses, then a divergence loss aligns its token-level policy with that of a fixed teacher. Because expectations are taken under the student’s state distribution, OPD mitigates the distributional shift issues that affect off-policy imitation and can yield better generalization more effectively.
Nevertheless, existing OPD methods typically require generating full, often long rollouts at each step and calculate divergence over the full vocabulary, which is prohibitively expensive for reasoning-heavy tasks. Our approach preserves the on-policy nature of OPD while drastically reducing training cost by supervising only short prefixes of each on-policy trajectory.

Token selection during LLM training.

Several works show that not all tokens in the reasoning traces are equally useful during LLM training.
Rho 1
21
use a reference model trained on clean corpus to help select high-quality tokens during the LLM pretraining stage.
Entropy-based token selection has also been shown to achieve similar or even better performance than full policy gradient
(
31
)
.
SelecTKD
10
adopts more sophisticated token selection criteria to reduce noisy signals from teacher via top-k token verification and non-greedy spec-k verification.
AdaKD
35
further considers the real-time learning difficulty of each token for the student and lets the student focus on the most valuable token during training.
A closely related work by
11
points out that early segments of chain-of-thought contain critical signals and later tokens can become increasingly noisy or redundant. While our task is different, we share a similar instinct: our distillation is focused on the informative early tokens, yielding substantial compute savings while retaining most of the benefits of full OPD on long rationales.

7
Conclusion

We introduced on-policy prefix distillation, a simple modification of on-policy distillation that truncates student rollouts and applies the token level feedback only to the prefix of each sample. Experiments on AI4Math benchmarks demonstrate that our approach attains substantial gains over the base model and remains competitive with full on-policy distillation, while significantly reducing training cost.

8
Limitations

We evaluate our approach exclusively on reasoning tasks assuming the output contains long chain-of-thought reasoning. It remains unknown whether prefix distillation can be more generally adopted in other long-form generation scenarios such as summarization, story generation, etc.
Moreover, prefix-only training presumes that the base model can already follow instructions and produce valid output formats; for weaker students, an initial warm-up phase of SFT or full OPD may still be necessary.
Finally, like standard OPD, our method requires teacher and student to share a same vocabulary so that token-level losses are well-defined.

Risks

Since gradients are applied only to the early portion of each student rollout, prefix-only learning may preferentially transfer the teacher’s initial reasoning style while under-training behaviors that typically emerge later in a response (e.g., safety refusals, calibration, or self-correction). As a result, the student may appear well-aligned in its opening tokens but deviate in longer completions. For applications where late-stage safety or calibration is important, we recommend prefix scheduling (progressively increasing the trained prefix length so later tokens are also supervised by the teacher by the end of training) and explicit end-to-end evaluation on safety/calibration benchmarks before deployment.

Appendix B
FLOP Estimation Details

We report training compute using an estimated GPU FLOP count to facilitate
relative
cost comparisons across methods. This estimate is not meant to be an exact measurement of hardware-executed FLOP, since realized cost depends on implementation details (e.g., kernels, padding, KV cache, and parallelism). We therefore use a single, consistent accounting scheme for all experiments.

Training FLOP from
Verl
.

We use the FLOP counter in
Verl

30
for Qwen3 models to estimate the compute of one student training update (including forward + backward + optimizer step) on a sequence of length
L
. We denote it by
F_{\text{train}}(M,L)
for model
M
.
11
11

11

We slightly modified the reference code to return cumulative FLOP (rather than a rate) by removing the division by elapsed time:

https://github.com/volcengine/verl/blob/c936ec7d5cebcc1d40f50296d28699696fbad6f5/verl/utils/FLOP_counter.py#L130
.

Forward-only approximation.

OPD additionally requires forward-only computation (sampling and log-prob evaluation). We approximate the forward-only FLOP as

F_{\text{fwd}}(M,L)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{3}\,F_{\text{train}}(M,L),

following the common rule-of-thumb that training cost is roughly
\approx 3\times
the forward cost. This approximation is applied uniformly across methods.

Per-rollout estimate for OPD.

For each student rollout of supervised length
L
, we estimate total compute as

F_{\text{OPD}}(L)\;\approx\;F_{\text{train}}(S,L)\;+\;2F_{\text{fwd}}(S,L)\;+\;F_{\text{fwd}}(T,L),

where
S
and
T
denote the student and teacher, respectively. Intuitively, this sums one student update plus forward passes for (i) sampling, (ii) student log-probs, and (iii) teacher log-probs. For prefix OPD,
L
is capped by the training prefix budget
L^{\text{train}}
.

Total FLOP.

If each optimization step uses batch size
B
prompts and
K
sampled rollouts per prompt, then

F_{\text{step}}\;\approx\;B\cdot K\cdot F_{\text{OPD}}(L),

and total training FLOP are obtained by summing
F_{\text{step}}
over steps (evaluation excluded).

Notes.

This estimate does not capture effects such as KV-cache reuse, communication overhead, CPU or I/O costs. We therefore treat FLOP as a consistent proxy for comparing training cost within our experimental setup.
