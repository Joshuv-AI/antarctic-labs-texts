A comparative study of single-stage and dual-stage classification models for OPMDs
Authors: Jiayin Yu, Rui Huang, Xuan Wang, Yiran Zhang, Yueling Li, Yuhang Ye, Mei Ouyang, Weilan Su, Jing Wen, Zihe Zhang, Qinwen Liu, Zhiwen Liu, Wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly diagnosis of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) and oral cancer is crucial for reducing oral cancer incidence and mortality. With advancements in deep learning for oral image recognition, this study addressed the limitations of public datasets for oral mucosal disease, which are often restricted in size and insufficient in disease-type coverage. Through comparative analysis, a dual-stage multi-classification approach was proposed.MethodsThis study established and publicly released a high-quality oral mucosal disease dataset comprising 1,348 images, covering five categories: normal oral mucosa, oral leukoplakia, oral lichen planus, oral submucous fibrosis, and oral cancer. After image preprocessing, we trained and evaluated the models. Ten pre-trained models commonly used in oral image recognition (DenseNet-169, EfficientNet-B0, HRNet-W18-C, Inception-V4, MixNet-S, MobileNetV3-Large, ResNet-101, Swin Transformer, ViT-B, and YOLOv11l) were trained using two classification pathways: single-stage and dual-stage. Model performance was evaluated using metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, area under the curve (AUC), and confusion matrix.ResultsThe dual-stage classification model based on Swin Transformer and DenseNet-169 achieved an accuracy of 0.9029, precision of 0.9082, recall of 0.8995, F1-score of 0.9032, and an AUC of 0.9735. Among the single-stage classification models, the best-performing EfficientNet-B0 model achieved an accuracy of 0.8710, precision of 0.8715, recall of 0.8719, F1-score of 0.8698, and an AUC of 0.9766. Based on these metrics, the dual-stage classification model demonstrated superior performance compared to the single-stage classification model.ConclusionsThis study established a publicly available high-quality dataset of 1,348 oral mucosal disease images. Furthermore, it proposed a novel dual-stage classification model integrating Swin Transformer and DenseNet-169, which was demonstrated to outperform conventional single-stage classification model across key performance metrics such as accuracy and precision.

Introduction

Oral cancer (OCA) refers to all malignant neoplasms that arise in the lip, buccal mucosa, hard and soft palate, floor of the mouth, gingiva, anterior two-thirds of the tongue and other unspecified parts of the mouth, with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) being the most common type [1]. Globally, it is estimated that there are 370,000 new cases of OCA and 170,000 OCA-related deaths each year [2], with a five-year mortality rate of approximately 50% [3]. This makes OCA a public health issue worthy of attention.

Notably, OSCC often develops from oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), primarily oral leukoplakia (OLK), oral lichen planus (OLP) and oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) [4]. Previous studies indicate that early detection and intervention of OPMDs can prevent malignant transformation in approximately 88% of OPMDs cases [5, 6], and early diagnosis of cancerous lesions can reduce cancer staging and significantly improve patient outcomes [7]. Thus, improving early detection and diagnosis is essential for reducing the incidence and mortality of OCA.

Conventional diagnostic approaches for OCA include clinical oral examination (COE) and histopathological biopsy. However, the accuracy of COE is heavily dependent on the clinician expertise, introducing subjectivity, particularly in resource-limited settings where misdiagnosis is common. Although histopathology remains the gold standard, its invasive nature and potential for sampling bias limit its applicability [8]. These limitations underscore the need for an efficient, non-invasive adjunctive tool. Given the widespread adoption of smartphones, advancements in imaging technology, and the superficial location with direct visual accessibility of oral mucosa, the acquisition of oral images demonstrates significant feasibility and universal applicability. As both OCA and OPMDs exhibit morphological features that can be visually assessed, previous studies have demonstrated moderate-to-high concordance between specialist COE and the remote interpretation of oral photographs [9]. Therefore, an artificial intelligence (AI) system capable of matching the visual diagnostic proficiency of specialists holds excellent clinical application prospects.

The rapid evolution of AI has driven notable progress in analyzing natural-light oral images using deep learning (DL) techniques [10]. Currently, binary classification tasks have yielded promising results. For instance, Warin et al. [11] employed a DenseNet121 model to classify a dataset of 350 normal mucosa images and 350 OSCC images, achieving an accuracy of 99% and a sensitivity of 98.75%. Fu et al. [12] proposed a cascaded convolutional neural network (CNN) that localized lesions via object detection before performing classification. This dual-stage framework achieved a sensitivity of 97.4% for early OSCC detection, marking a significant advance in clinical utility. Using an Inception-v3 architecture, Keser et al. [13] attained 100% classification accuracy for OLP images, further validating AI’s efficacy in diagnosing specific oral diseases.

Substantial progress has also been made in the multi-class classification of oral mucosal diseases. In experiments on a three-class dataset (Normal, OPMDs, and OCA), models including ResNet-152, DenseNet-161, Inception-V4, and EfficientNet-B4 were compared. Results indicated that EfficientNet-B4 and Inception-V4 were the most promising, with an F1-score of 85.8% [14]. As for object detection and image classification of OLK, OLP and OSCC, both Vision Transformer model and YOLOX-based CNN model achieved precision above 85% and specificity up to 95%. The latter model reached a level comparable to the specialists of oral mucosal disease in multi-center clinical evaluations [15, 16]. Although existing models have achieved preliminary success in multi-classification tasks for oral mucosal diseases, there still remain limitations such as insufficiently detailed disease coverage, suboptimal accuracy, and inability to fully meet the needs of precise clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methodology

Establishment of dataset

This study compiled a dataset of 1,348 intraoral mucosal images categorized into five classes: Normal oral mucosa, OLK, OLP, OSF, and OCA. As shown in Table 1; Fig. 1, the image count for each category ranges from 225 to 310 (16.69%–23.00%), indicating a relatively balanced distribution. Annotations were performed by four oral mucosal disease specialists, each possessing over ten years of clinical experience. To ensure label reliability, cases with initial diagnostic disagreements were first discussed in a consensus meeting. Images for which a consensus could not be reached were excluded from the final dataset. However, it should be acknowledged that the final labels were based solely on clinical evaluation in the absence of histopathological confirmation. Images of normal mucosa were captured from various anatomical sites and angles, while the other four types of oral images clearly displayed surface features of the lesions (Fig. 2). These lesions were located on diverse oral sites, including the lip, buccal mucosa, hard and soft palate, floor of the mouth, gingiva, and anterior two-thirds of the tongue. The dataset exhibited significant variability in quality (including lighting, scaling, angle, and clarity) and resolution. This heterogeneity enhances the dataset’s applicability to real-world scenarios and provides valuable samples for training classification models with robust generalization capabilities.

All images in the dataset were stored in JPG format and organized into separate directories labeled Normal, OLK, OLP, OSF, and OCA. The naming convention for image files was ‘category number-image number’. The directory names corresponded to the assigned category numbers (1–5), specifically Normal (1), OLK (2), OLP (3), OSF (4), and OCA (5). This naming scheme struck a balance between simplicity and organization, providing clear labels and an efficient framework for data management during model training.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changsha Stomatological Hospital (Ethics Number: 2025 − 169). All data were anonymized to exclude patients’ personal and private information.

Data preprocessing

All images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio. To mitigate the risk of overfitting resulting from the limited dataset size, data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set. The augmentation techniques included rotation (90°, 180°, 270°), horizontal flipping, horizontal and vertical translation (by 10% of the image size), and color space adjustments (brightness = 0.5, contrast = 0.1, saturation = 0.01) [17].

Model selection

This study employed ten pre-trained models encompassing a spectrum of classical architectures, which are widely adopted and have demonstrated strong performance in oral mucosal disease image recognition. The selected models are described below.

DenseNet-169 is a 169-layer CNN characterized by its dense connectivity, where each layer receives input from all preceding layers. This promotes feature reuse, mitigates the vanishing gradient problem, and reduces model parameters. The architecture comprises dense blocks connected by transition layers for dimensionality reduction, ending with a global average pooling and a linear layer for classification [18].

EfficientNet-B0 utilizes a compound scaling method to systematically balance the scaling of depth, width, and resolution via a single coefficient [19]. The core architecture consists of MBConv blocks, further integrated with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) modules and the Swish activation function to enhance model capacity and feature learning [20, 21].

Across its 18-layer architecture, HRNet-W18-C maintains multi-resolution representations by simultaneously processing feature maps at multiple scales through parallel branches. The architecture employs cross-resolution bidirectional fusion modules and a reduction in the channel width of high-resolution representations to form a lightweight design [22].

Inception-V4 enhances the Inception family through refined architecture, employing a deep stem for initial feature extraction, factorized Inception modules for efficient multi-scale processing, and specialized reduction blocks for controlled downsampling. This design achieves an improved balance between accuracy and computational cost [23].

MixNet-S employs mixed depthwise convolution. This operation partitions channels into groups that are processed by kernels of different sizes, enabling the efficient capture of multi-scale features. This design allows MixNet-S to achieve a competitive 75.8% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with a computational cost of only 256 M FLOPs, striking an excellent balance between efficiency and performance [24].

MobileNetV3-Large was co-designed by neural architecture search and manual refinement, integrating a lightweight SE module and the hardware-efficient h-swish activation function. This approach achieves a strong balance between computational cost and model accuracy [25].

ResNet-101 addresses deep network training challenges through two core designs: a bottleneck structure for computational efficiency, and a residual framework with skip connections to mitigate vanishing gradients. Stacking these blocks enables stable training and hierarchical feature construction, preventing performance degradation despite increased depth [26].

Swin Transformer is a state-of-the-art vision model that introduces a shifted window self-attention mechanism as its core innovation. This mechanism limits computational complexity by confining attention to non-overlapping local windows. By shifting these windows between layers, it efficiently models global context, achieving an optimal balance between accuracy and efficiency [27].

ViT-B is a pure Transformer architecture for computer vision. It operates by splitting images into patches, adding positional embeddings, and processing them via global self-attention. While this enables powerful visual relationship modeling, it makes the model data-intensive. Consequently, ViT-B excels when pre-trained on large datasets but underperforms ResNet on small-scale data [28].

YOLOv11 introduces an optimized CSPDarknet backbone that replaces C2F with efficient C3K2 modules, reducing parameters and computation while preserving the receptive field. It further incorporates a C2PSA module after SPPF to enhance multi-scale feature extraction, significantly boosting small object detection performance [29].

Model parameter settings

The experimental training was conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

For model training, we initially set a maximum of 100 epochs. An early-stopping strategy was implemented to prevent overfitting: the training process would terminate prematurely if the validation accuracy did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs. The best-performing model was selected based on the highest validation accuracy achieved during training.

Model architecture

Figure 3 illustrates the overall workflow of this study. Following data collection and preprocessing, images are processed through two parallel approaches for model training and evaluation.

Fig. 3Overall workflow

Overall workflow

The first approach employed a single-stage five-classification strategy. It utilized ten pre-trained DL models to classify oral images into one of five categories: Normal, OLK, OLP, OSF or OCA.

The second approach employed a pipeline-based dual-stage five-classification strategy. In the first stage, the same ten pre-trained models were trained and evaluated to differentiate among three categories: Normal, OPMDs, and OCA. In the second stage, the models were trained to subclassify OPMDs into OLK, OLP, and OSF. The best-performing models from each stage were then integrated into a conditional workflow: an input image first undergoes initial classification by the first-stage model; if predicted as OPMDs, it is forwarded to the second-stage model for fine-grained classification into one of the three OPMD subtypes. Otherwise, the initial prediction (Normal or OCA) is taken as the final output.

The overall performance of both strategies was evaluated and compared.

Performance comparison of single-stage classification models

In the single-stage classification model, EfficientNet-B0 demonstrated the highest overall performance. It achieved mean values of 0.8710 (accuracy), 0.8715 (precision), 0.8719 (recall), 0.8698 (F1-score), and 0.9766 (AUC) over five runs. While its AUC is marginally lower than that of Inception-V4, all other metrics ranked highest among the ten evaluated models (Table 3). This superior performance is attributed to EfficientNet-B0’s compound-scaling strategy, which delivers optimal computational efficiency by markedly reducing the parameter count while maintaining outstanding accuracy within the same architectural family [30]. Notably, the model demonstrates limited discriminative capability for OLK and OCA (Fig. 6), with class-specific accuracies of only 0.78 and 0.80, respectively.

Consequently, based on the observed variability in feature learning across models, a dual-stage classification strategy was implemented. This framework simplifies the learning objective at each stage, which stands in contrast to prevalent dual-stage classification strategy that typically cascades an object detection or segmentation model with a classifier. Instead of first localizing lesion regions, our approach employs a hierarchical classification. The first-stage model is dedicated to differentiating the three major categories—Normal, OPMDs, and OCA—allowing it to concentrate on their core discriminative features. Subsequently, for cases identified as OPMDs, a specialized second-stage model is employed to discern the more subtle feature differences between OLK, OLP, and OSF subcategories.

For the first stage of the dual-stage classification strategy, ten DL models were trained and evaluated on the triple-classification task (Normal, OPMDs, and OCA). Swin Transformer emerged as the optimal model, achieving superior five-run mean values for accuracy (0.9159), precision (0.9097), recall (0.9038), F1-score (0.9056), and AUC (0.9697), as detailed in Table 4. Its strong performance is attributed to the shifted-window attention mechanism [27], which excels at capturing global contextual information by efficiently modeling long-range dependencies.

As shown in Table 6, different studies have demonstrated that when using pre-trained DL models for oral lesion recognition, the model performance is affected by multiple factors such as dataset size, image quality, classification tasks, and model architecture. Sharma et al. [6] achieved only 76% accuracy with their best model due to the small dataset size, clearly highlighting the limitations of small samples on model generalization capabilities; Although Tanriver et al. [14] achieved an accuracy of 87.7%, the simplicity of their classification limited its clinical utility. While Lin et al. [32] and Mira et al. [35] subdivided OPMDs into low-risk and high-risk subgroups, the resulting model accuracy of 84.3% indicates that there remains significant room for improvement.

In comparison, our study constructed a dataset comprising 1,348 high-quality oral images sourced from smartphone capture, professional camera acquisition, and literature sources. More significantly, we designed one of the most granular classification tasks reported in the literature: distinguishing not only between Normal, OPMDs, and OCA but further subdividing OPMDs into three specific lesion types: OLK, OLP, and OSF. To address this complex task, we proposed and validated an innovative dual-stage classification model (Swin Transformer and DenseNet-169). This model achieved a high level of comprehensive performance, outperforming the results of other classification studies referenced in Table 6.

It’s worth noting that the image labels in this study lack confirmation from histopathological examination. This limitation may affect the model’s generalization ability in cases where clinical and pathological findings are inconsistent. For example, the model may struggle to accurately distinguish between OPMDs that have progressed to early carcinoma and those that have not. Therefore, in subsequent studies, we will incorporate histopathologically confirmed images to validate and enhance the model’s performance.

Conclusion

The application of AI for identifying OPMDs and OCA through image analysis holds substantial clinical value for screening and early prevention. This study established a high-quality oral dataset of 1,348 oral mucosal images. We further proposed a dual-stage multi-classification framework, implementing a specific model based on Swin Transformer and DenseNet-169 for five-class identification. This model demonstrated superior performance compared to conventional single-stage approaches, offering a promising computer-aided solution for OCA screening and diagnosis. Moreover, to foster further research, we have publicly released the dataset, thereby expanding the available data resources for the early detection of oral cancer.

Future work

Although the dual-stage classification model demonstrated significant advantages in our experiments, several challenges remain for its practical application. Most notably, our dataset lacks histopathological confirmation and the model’s accuracy in detecting OCA and OLK is relatively insufficient. Other limitations include incomplete coverage of OPMDs’ subtypes, insufficient model stability, and a lack of clinical validation. To address these issues, future work will focus on constructing a multimodal dataset of histopathologically confirmed cases that integrates images and clinical text. Leveraging this dataset, a multimodal DL model can be developed, incorporating textual features to further enhance recognition performance. Additionally, we will investigate synergistic fusion mechanisms between image segmentation and classification models to improve the extraction and recognition of key lesion features. For validation, the study will establish an external independent test set in the future. The model’s clinical applicability will be rigorously validated using an external independent test set, with performance on key metrics (e.g., accuracy, recall) systematically benchmarked against diagnoses made by clinical practitioners.
