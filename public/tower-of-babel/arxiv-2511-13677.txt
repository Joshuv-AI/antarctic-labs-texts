Open-shell frozen natural orbital approach for quantum eigensolvers
Angela F. Harper, Xiaobing Liu, Scott N. Genin, Ilya G. Ryabinkin

Abstract
We present an open-shell frozen natural orbital (FNO) approach, which utilizes the second-order Z-averaged perturbation theory (ZAPT2) , to reduce the restricted open-shell Hartree–Fock virtual space size with controllable accuracy. Our ZAPT2 frozen natural orbital (ZAPT-FNO) selection scheme significantly outperforms the canonical molecular orbital virtual space truncation scheme based on Hartree–Fock orbital energies, especially when using large multiple-polarized and augmented basis sets. We demonstrate that the ZAPT-FNO -selected virtual orbitals lead to a systematic convergence of the correlation energies, but more importantly to the singlet-triplet T 1 -S 0 energy gaps with respect to the complete active spaceoccupied + virtual (CAS) size. We confirm our findings by simulating T 1 -S 0 gaps in H 2 ​ O 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} and O 2 \text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} molecules using the traditional complete active space configuration interaction (CASCI) approach, as well as in stretched CH 2 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}} , for which we also employed the iterative qubit coupled cluster (iQCC) method as a quantum eigensolver. Finally, we applied the iQCC method with ZAPT-FNO -selected active space to the phosphorescent Ir ( ppy ) 3 \text{Ir}\text{(}\text{ppy}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}} complex with 260 260 electrons, where extended basis sets are required to achieve chemical ( ca. 1 mE 1\text{\,}\mathrm{mE} ) accuracy. In this case, CASCI results are not available; however, the iQCC -computed T 1 -S 0 gaps show robust convergence with enlarging basis set and CAS size, approaching the experimental value. Thus, the ZAPT-FNO method is very promising for improving the accuracy of quantum chemical modelling in a resource-efficient manner, and opens the door to simulating open-shell states of large materials within realistic active space sizes and without compromising on basis-set quality.



I
Introduction

Open-shell systems are ubiquitous in practical applications of transition metal complexes.
For example, phosphorescent emitters, vital to the multi-billion dollar
organic light-emitting diode (OLED)
industry, often have ground or low-lying electronic states with unpaired electrons.
Accurate
ab initio
modelling of their electronic structure requires large (extended)
atomic
basis sets with multiple polarization shells and diffuse orbitals.
This results in a huge number of
molecular
orbitals, prompting researchers to look for methods with lower computational costs.
One such method is the
frozen natural orbital (FNO)
approach
Kumar and Crawford 2017
;
Taube and Bartlett 2005
;
Manisha and Manohar 2025
;
Taube and Bartlett 2008
;
Landau et al. 2010
;
Verma et al. 2021
.
Unfortunately, existing implementations are largely limited to closed-shell systems; the only exception to this being Ref.
7
; however their use of an
unrestricted Hartree–Fock
reference, makes this approach incompatible with most common quantum eigensolvers.

Solving difficult electronic structure problems in quantum chemistry is a powerful driving force for the development of quantum algorithms and hardware; ultimately the aim is to achieve a quantum advantage over classical methods (see, however, a critical opinion in Ref.
8
).
Among the various approaches proposed for
noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ)
devices
Kandala et al. 2017
, the
variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)

Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Wecker et al. 2015
has emerged as a leading framework, due to its adaptability to hardware limitations and compatibility with fermionic Hamiltonians.
The
iterative qubit coupled cluster (iQCC)
method
Ryabinkin et al. 2018
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2020
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2021
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2023
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2025
is a variant of
VQE
for variational energy minimization of qubit Hamiltonians on classical hardware.
This “quantum-on-classical” approach achieves linear scaling with the size of the qubit Hamiltonian, making it a practical tool for simulating large quantum systems.

In addition to emulating quantum-hardware calculations,
iQCC
has demonstrated competitive accuracy with classical quantum chemistry methods such as
density-functional theory (DFT)
and
coupled-cluster singles and doubles (CCSD)
across a variety of chemical systems
Genin et al. 2022
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2023
.
A key advantage of
iQCC
is its ability to simulate Hamiltonians of sizes that exceed the capabilities of current quantum hardware.
For context, the largest simulated quantum computation to-date is the Hartree–Fock simulation of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{12}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{12}}\text{}}
hydrogen chain from Google AI Quantum, using
12
logical qubits in a CAS(12,12) active space
Quantum et al. 2020
.
By comparison,
iQCC
has simulated systems of up to
72
qubits [or CAS(36,36)
Genin et al. 2022
], and has the potential to simulate even larger systems, limited only by the capabilities of modern CPU hardware
Ryabinkin et al. 2025
.
Nevertheless, in both the Google AI Quantum and
iQCC
case, the size of the initial qubit Hamiltonian was reduced from including all electrons and orbitals, in order for the computation to be feasible within the limits of the quantum or classical hardware on which it was simulated.
This is a common practice in quantum chemistry, called “active space selection”, in which the total number of orbitals is reduced to a smaller set which contribute the most to the total energy of the system.
The goal of active space methods is to select a set of orbitals which are both chemically relevant, and also computationally tractable, such that the final Hamiltonian is small enough to be simulated on the available hardware.

Active space selection is an established field in quantum chemistry
Stein and Reiher 2019
;
Lei et al. 2021
;
Kaufold et al. 2023
, as performing many body calculations which scale as
\mathcal{O}
(N
7
), is only feasible for small systems and basis sets.
While occupied orbitals are well-defined across basis set representations, virtual orbitals can form a dense manifold of states above the highest occupied molecular orbital
Schmidt et al. 2015
.
In augmented basis sets, the virtual space contains many diffuse orbitals which form a continuum of orbital states that are close in energy, making it difficult to extract chemically relevant low-lying virtual states.
Therefore, several methods aim at reducing the size of the valence-virtual space, to a selected active set of orbitals, while simultaneously retaining the most chemically relevant orbitals.
For example, the optimized orbital
CCSD
method yields so-called Brückner orbitals
Sherrill et al. 1998
;
Brueckner 1954
, in which the correlation energy is minimized with respect to orbital variations between occupied and virtual orbitals in the active space.
Other methods include pair
natural orbital (NO)
truncation
Löwdin 1955
;
Neese et al. 2009a
;
Neese et al. 2009b
, to recover the correlation energy in medium to large systems (
50100
atoms) with computational efficiency.
Building from this, the
FNO
approach
Taube and Bartlett 2005
;
Taube and Bartlett 2008
in which the
FNOs
are computed as the virtual-virtual orbitals of the
second-order Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2)
density matrix
Landau et al. 2010
.
More recently, the AB2 (antibonding second-order perturbation) method has been proposed as an approach to constructing valence antibonding orbitals by maximizing opposite-spin
MP2
pair correlation amplitudes, yielding a robust way to define molecule-adapted minimal bases
Aldossary and Head-Gordon 2022
.

Of the aforementioned approaches for truncating the virtual space, the
FNO
approach is well-suited for quantum computing applications, as it has been extensively used for closed-shell restricted Hartree Fock orbital selection
Taube and Bartlett 2005
;
Taube and Bartlett 2008
.
The resulting set of orbitals from such a restricted method can easily be transformed by standard fermion to qubit transformations
Nielsen et al. 2005
, to be suitable for input to a quantum eigensolver, such as
iQCC
.
To extend the
FNO
approach to open-shell systems, we propose a variant based on
second-order Z-averaged perturbation theory (ZAPT2)

Fletcher et al. 2002
;
Aikens et al. 2006
, which uses an
restricted open-shell Hartree–Fock (ROHF)
reference.
This simple but effective extension to the
FNO
approach, opens up avenues for computing energy gaps in molecules with Rydberg orbitals and strong correlation, where diffuse basis functions are essential to recover accurate ground state energies.

The manuscript is organized as follows.
We first present an overview of the
FNO
method and
ZAPT2
perturbation theory, and describe how their combination yields a practical
ZAPT2 frozen natural orbital (ZAPT-FNO)
approach compatible with quantum computing methods.
We then benchmark
ZAPT-FNO
against
canonical molecular orbital (CMO)
selection for several systems, using
iQCC
to perform the energy minimization.
For
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
, we demonstrate that
ZAPT-FNO
recovers correlation energy more efficiently in a smaller active space size, particularly for augmented basis sets.
Additionally, we demonstrate that the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
triplet-singlet (T
1
-S
0
) energy gap converges smoothly with respect to
complete active space (CAS)
size for the
ZAPT-FNO
approach, while the
CMO
energy gaps
may
achieve chemical accuracy only as a result of fortuitous error cancellation.
We further validate our approach by computing the singlet and triplet states of stretched
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
across a range of
\text{C}{-}\text{H}
distances, and achieve chemical accuracy relative to
complete active space configuration interaction (CASCI)
calculations with a higher quality basis set than previously reported
Liepuoniute et al. 2025
in the same active space size.
Finally, we consider the
260
electron (61 atoms)
\text{Ir}\text{(}\text{ppy}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
complex, which is a challenging system to simulate due to both its size, and the Jahn-Teller distortion in the triplet state
Fine et al. 2012
.
Despite the complexity of the system,
ZAPT-FNO
enables accurate prediction of the T
1
–S
0
gap using a compact active space, thereby illustrating the method’s promise for simulating large complexes with chemical accuracy.

II
Methods

II.1
Second-order Z-averaged perturbation theory (ZAPT2)

The
ZAPT2
correlation correction to the energy is an extension of the
MP2
perturbation theory to open-shell systems
Fletcher et al. 2002
;
Aikens et al. 2006
.
The correction is based on the closed-shell
MP2
theory, with differences arising from the necessity for treatment of the singly occupied orbitals.
The
ZAPT2
energy correction is expressed in the
molecular orbital (MO)
basis, as a sum over two-electron integrals
(pq|rs)
and their amplitudes.
In the case of a closed shell system, the
E^{(2)}_{\text{ZAPT}}
energy correction is equivalent to the
E^{(2)}_{\text{MP}}
energy correction, as we will show below.

In the following equations, orbital indices are defined as follows:
i
,
j
,
k
index doubly occupied active
MOs
,
w
,
x
,
y
,
z
index singly occupied active
MOs
, and
a
,
b
,
c
index virtual, unoccupied active
MOs
.
General
MOs
are indexed by
p
,
q
,
r
,
s
, and
atomic orbitals (AOs)
are indexed by
\mu
,
\nu
,
\lambda
,
\sigma
.
The
ZAPT2
energy correction is then given by the following expression,

\displaystyle E_{\text{ZAPT}}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\sum_{p,q}^{\mathit{s.v.}}\frac{(ip|jq)[C_{pq}(ip|jq)-(iq|jp)]}{D_{ij}^{pq}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,q}^{\mathit{d.s.}}\sum_{a,b}\frac{(pa|qb)[C_{pq}(pa|qb)-(pb|qa)]}{D_{pq}^{ab}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{ixya}(ix|ya)\frac{(ix|ya)}{D_{iy}^{xa}}+\sum_{ixya}(ix|xa)\frac{(iy|xa)}{D_{ii}^{aa}}

(1)

where for the set of singly occupied orbitals
\{X\}
,

C_{pq}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{for both }p\in\{X\}\text{ and }q\in\{X\},\\
2,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2)

and denominators are a summation over the orbital energies,
\varepsilon
, defined as

D_{pq}^{rs}=\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{q}-\varepsilon_{r}^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{s}^{\prime},

(3)

The notation
s.v.

and
d.s.

refers to summations over the singly occupied and virtual indices, and doubly occupied and singly occupied indices, respectively.
The orbital energies for the singly occupied orbitals include an additional integral component, which is a sum of exchange integrals over the singly occupied orbitals:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{pp}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y}(py|py),

\displaystyle\text{for }p\in\{X\},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}_{p}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{pp}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y}(py|py),

\displaystyle\text{for }p\in\{X\},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}_{p}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{p}=\varepsilon_{pp},

\displaystyle\text{for }p\notin\{X\},

(4)

where
\varepsilon_{pp}
is the orbital energy of the
p
th orbital in the
MO
basis.
The third and fourth terms in Eq.
1
are summations over the singly occupied orbitals, and are only present in the open-shell case.
If we consider the closed-shell case (
i.e.

no singly-occupied orbital indices,
\varepsilon_{p}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{p}
, and
C_{pq}=2
), we can see that Eq.
1
reduces to the
MP2
energy correction
Shavitt and Bartlett 2009
,
E^{(2)}_{\text{MP}}
,

\displaystyle E_{\text{MP}}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{a,b}\sum_{i,j}\frac{(ij|ab)[2(ij|ab)-(ij|ba)]}{\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{a}-\varepsilon_{b}}.

Given the above similarities between
ZAPT2
and
MP2
derivations, there is a natural extension of the
FNO
approach, described in the following section, to open-shell systems using
ZAPT2
.

II.2
Frozen natural orbital (FNO)
approach

The
FNO
approach is a well-established method for truncating the virtual orbital active space in correlated wavefunction methods such as
CCSD
and
coupled-cluster singles and doubles and non-iterative triples (CCSD(T))
, and has been widely adopted for closed-shell systems
Taube and Bartlett 2005
;
Kumar and Crawford 2017
;
DePrince III and Sherrill 2013
.
However, direct extension of the
FNO
approach to open-shell systems is not straightforward.
Pokhilko and authors
Pokhilko et al. 2020
showed that using an unrestricted Hartree–Fock reference leads to inconsistent truncation of the
\alpha
and
\beta
virtual orbital spaces, which causes “erratic” behavior in the final total energies.
They solve this problem by introducing a new extension to the
FNO
approach using the equation-of-motion coupled cluster approach, which uses a single value decomposition of the
\alpha
and
\beta
orbitals overlap matricies to treat the singly occupied orbital space, and remove the inconsistencies between the
\alpha
and
\beta
orbital channels.
The equation-of-motion
FNO
method, implemented in Q-Chem
Epifanovsky et al. 2021
, has been shown to yield accurate results for the triplet-singlet energy gaps in small molecules, such as ethene, methanol, and cyclobutadiene, with errors in the gap energy of less than
2\text{\,}\mathrm{mE}

Manisha and Manohar 2025
.
While effective for the
CCSD(T)
application case, the equation-of-motion
FNO
approach suffers from the fact that the resulting unrestricted reference contains two spin channels, and is therefore incompatible with fermion-to-qubit mappings for single fermionic Hamiltonians.

To address this limitation, we propose an alternative
ZAPT-FNO
scheme based on a
ROHF
reference, using
ZAPT2
perturbation theory
Fletcher et al. 2002
;
Aikens et al. 2006
.
As a spin-restricted method,
ZAPT2
yields a single set of orbitals and is thus naturally compatible both with the original
FNO
formalism and with standard fermion-to-qubit mappings.
Below, we outline the general
FNO
algorithm which is applicable both to
restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF)
and
ROHF
references, using either
MP2
or
ZAPT2
to compute the
NOs
.
The algorithm is as follows:

1.

Perform a self-consistent field calculation using either an
RHF
or
ROHF
reference to obtain the
CMOs

2.

Compute the virtual-virtual part of the
MP2
or
ZAPT2
one-particle density matrix (
P^{(2)}
)

3.

Diagonalize the virtual-virtual part of
P^{(2)}
to obtain the
NOs
, sorted in decreasing order of occupation

4.

Select the desired number of
NOs
to retain, either using a threshold of occupation or a set number of orbitals.
The remaining virtual orbitals with occupation number lower than this threshold are frozen (
i.e.

not used).

5.

Semicanonicalize the active (unfrozen) orbitals.

6.

Transform the one- and two-electron integrals into the new
orbital basis of semicanonicalized orbitals for all active orbitals.

7.

Perform a transformation from the fermion to qubit
representation of the final active space, and perform the
iQCC
calculation in the new basis.

Details of how to compute the
ZAPT2
one-particle density matrix are given in Appendix
A
.
As a result of truncating the active space
NOs
in Step 4, the final energy calculated by
iQCC
does not include the contributions from the frozen orbitals.
However, because the
NOs
are selected based on their contributions to the correlation energy, a correction can be applied to the final energy to recover the energy of the frozen orbitals.
The correction is computed as the difference between the
MP2
(or
ZAPT2
) correlation energy (defined in Eqs.
1
and
) evaluated in the full
MO
basis and the truncated
NO
basis,

\displaystyle\Delta E_{\text{FNO}}

\displaystyle=E_{\text{MP/ZAPT}}^{\text{MO}}-E_{\text{MP/ZAPT}}^{\text{NO}}

such that the corrected
iQCC
energy is given by,

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{iQCC}{{{}}iQCC}}}^{\text{FNO}}

\displaystyle=E_{\mathrm{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{iQCC}{{{}}iQCC}}}^{\text{NO}}+\Delta E_{\text{FNO}}.

This correction allows systematic improvement of the
iQCC
minimized energy by accounting for dynamical correlation outside the active space.
Implementing the
ZAPT-FNO
scheme for the open-shell
ZAPT2
case thus requires that we compute two main quantities: the virtual-virtual block of the
ZAPT2
one-particle density matrix (P
(2)
), and the corresponding
ZAPT2
correlation energy for both the selected
NOs
and the full set of
MOs
.

II.3
Outline of
iQCC

To avoid self-repetition, we refer the reader to our recent work outlining the
iQCC
approach in detail at Ref.
16
, and provide only a brief overview in this section.

The
iQCC
approach targets the ground-state energy of an active-space second-quantized Hamiltonian
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Ryabinkin et al. 2023
,

\hat{H}_{e}=E_{\text{core}}+\sum_{ij}f_{ij}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}g_{ijkl}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{l}\hat{a}_{k},

(8)

where
E_{\text{core}}
is the energy of the frozen-core orbitals,
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}
and
\hat{a}_{j}
are the creation and annihilation operators, and
f_{ij}
,
g_{ijkl}
are one- and two-electron integrals over the chosen active space.

Using a
Jordan–Wigner (JW)
transformation
Nielsen et al. 2005
, the Hamiltonian is mapped to,

\hat{H}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}C_{k}\hat{P}_{k},

(9)

where
\hat{P}_{k}
are Pauli words and coefficients
C_{k}
are determined by the electronic integrals.

Finally, one most choose the initial reference vector
\ket{0}
, which is a direct product state of spin-orbitals,

\ket{0}=\prod_{k=1}^{n_{e}}\ket{\downarrow}_{k}\prod_{k=1}^{n-n_{e}}\ket{\uparrow}_{k},

(10)

where
n_{e}
is the number of electrons in the active space, and
n
is the total number of spin-orbitals in the active space, which determines the total number of qubits in the system.

The
iQCC
algorithm then aims to variationally minimize the energy of the Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}
, via

E_{\mathrm{iQCC}}[\hat{T}]=\min_{t}\bra{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{H}\hat{U}(t)\ket{0},

(11)

where
\hat{U}(t)
is a unitary operator which is realizable on a quantum computer.
The
iQCC
approach iteratively optimizes the amplitudes
t
of the unitary operator, via an iterative process of generator selection, energy minimization, and amplitude updating.
Further details of this method, are provided in Refs.
12
;
13
;
14
;
15
, and improvements to the amplitude optimization as well as a detailed overview of the iQCC approach are provided in Ref.
16
.

It is the reference state, given in Eq.
10
, that defines the
CAS
size and therefore the number of qubits, and ultimately computational cost of the calculation.
The
CAS
is usually defined as CAS(n
e
,n
o
), where n
e
is the number of electrons in the active space, and n
o
is the number of spin-orbitals in the active space.
The number of qubits required for this active space is
n=2n_{o}
, as each spin-orbital is represented by a single qubit in the
JW
transformation.
By improving the quality of the fermionic Hamiltonian given in Eq.
8
, as well as limiting the
CAS
size by obtaining a higher quality set of orbitals, we aim to improve the convergence of the
iQCC
energy minimization, and ultimately the accuracy of the final energy.

We can attribute some portion of the discrepancy between

To confirm this, we compute the overlap between the

and the

for the
\text{Ir}\text{(}\text{ppy}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}

The overlap matrix between the molecular orbitals using the
CMO
and
FNO
approaches,
S_{\text{MO}}
is computed as,

S_{\text{MO}}=\mathbf{C}_{\text{CMO}}^{\top}S_{\text{AO}}\mathbf{C}_{\text{FNO}}

(14)

where
\mathbf{C}_{\text{CMO}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\text{FNO}}
are the molecular orbital coefficient matrices corresponding to the virtual
CMOs
and semicanonicalized
FNOs
, respectively, and
S_{\text{AO}}
is the atomic orbital overlap matrix.
A value of
S_{\text{MO}}=1
corresponds to perfect overlap between a pair of molecular orbitals from the two sets, meaning the orbitals are identical (up to a phase); a value of
S_{\text{MO}}=0
corresponds to no overlap between a pair of molecular orbitals from the two sets, indicating that the orbitals are orthogonal in the atomic orbital basis.

IV
Conclusions

We have introduced the
ZAPT2 frozen natural orbital (ZAPT-FNO)
strategy for orbital selection in quantum eigensolvers, and demonstrated its accuracy and efficiency for open-shell systems.
Across diverse test cases, including the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
triplet-singlet gap,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
bond dissociation, and
260
electron
\text{Ir}\text{(}\text{ppy}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
complex, the
ZAPT-FNO
approach consistently outperforms canonical molecular orbital (
CMO
) selection, by achieving chemical accuracy with limited active space sizes.
For
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
, we demonstrate that we can achieve chemical accuracy for the T
1
-S
0
energy gap across a broad range of bond lengths, including in strongly correlated regimes.

The advantages of the
ZAPT-FNO
approach are most pronounced for large, diffuse basis sets, such as aug-cc-pVTZ or LANL2TZ, where canonical virtual orbitals are dominated by diffuse orbitals that do not contribute significantly to the total correlation energy.
By enabling the use of these larger basis sets without expanding the active space,
ZAPT-FNO
retains orbitals that have high natural occupation, while freezing out Rydberg orbitals.
Even for compact basis sets, the method performs comparably to
CMO
selection, underscoring its robustness across regimes.
Limitations arise only at stretched bond lengths, where second-order perturbation theory is known to break down.

Overall, the extension of the
FNO
approach to open-shell systems provides a robust framework for preparing compact, accurate active spaces for quantum simulations with complex electronic structures that require high-quality basis sets.
This makes
ZAPT-FNO
a compelling choice for both classical and hybrid quantum–classical algorithms, such as
iQCC
, which require compact active spaces, and opens the door to accurate quantum simulations of challenging open-shell and excited-state systems.
