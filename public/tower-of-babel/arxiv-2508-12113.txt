Abstract
. A hyperplane arrangement 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is said to be free if the corresponding Jacobian ideal J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}} is Cohen-Macaulay. In particular, if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is free then J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}} is unmixed (i.e. equidimensional). Freeness is an important property, yet its presence is not well understood. A well-known conjecture of Terao says that freeness of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} depends only on the intersection lattice of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . Given an arbitrary arrangement 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , we define the ideal J 𝒜 t ​ o ​ p J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top} to be the intersection of the codimension 2 primary components of J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}} . This ideal is again unmixed, but not necessarily Cohen-Macaulay, though if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is free then J 𝒜 = J 𝒜 t ​ o ​ p J_{\mathcal{A}}=J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top} . In this paper, we develop a new method for studying the ideals J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}} and J 𝒜 t ​ o ​ p J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top} and use it to establish results in the spirit of Terao’s conjecture, but focusing on J 𝒜 t ​ o ​ p J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top} rather than J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}} . It is based on a novel application of liaison theory, namely the notion of the general residual of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . This residual ideal defines a scheme with surprisingly simple properties. These allow us to track back to J 𝒜 t ​ o ​ p J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top} . Considerably extending earlier results with Schenck, we identify mild conditions on a hyperplane arrangement which imply that the Hilbert function of Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) t ​ o ​ p \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top} or even all the numerical information of the resolution of Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) t ​ o ​ p \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top} , i.e. its graded Betti numbers, is determined by the intersection lattice of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . As another application, we establish new bounds on the global Tjurina number of a hyperplane arrangement. For line arrangements, we have additional results. Note that in this case Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) t ​ o ​ p \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top} is the same as the saturation of Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}) . We show, for an arbitrary line arrangement 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , that the graded Betti numbers of Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) s ​ a ​ t \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat} determine the graded Betti numbers of Jac ⁡ ( f 𝒜 ) \Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}) , and of the corresponding Milnor module J 𝒜 s ​ a ​ t / J 𝒜 J_{\mathcal{A}}^{sat}/J_{\mathcal{A}} . Furthermore, we obtain a new freeness criterion for line arrangements – it highlights the fact that free line arrangements are special by proving that a related codimension two ideal has the least possible number of generators, namely two, if and only if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is free. We illustrate our results by computing the graded Betti numbers for a number of basic arrangements that were not accessible with previous methods.

1.
Introduction

Hyperplane arrangements are of interest in several areas and have been intensely investigated using tools from algebra, algebraic geometry, combinatorics, topology and other areas.
In this paper, we introduce a new method to investigate properties of Jacobian ideals of hyperplane arrangements.

We work over a field
K
of characteristic zero and we set
S=K[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
, the polynomial ring over
K
with standard grading. A subscheme
X
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is said to be
arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (ACM)
if
S/I_{X}
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a finite set of hyperplanes of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Abusing notation, we sometimes do not distinguish between the set
\mathcal{A}
and the hyperplane arrangement
\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{A}}L
. The arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is defined by a product
f_{\mathcal{A}}
of linear forms. Let
J_{\mathcal{A}}=\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
be the Jacobian ideal of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
, i.e. the ideal generated by the
n+1
partial derivatives of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
. The ideal
J_{\mathcal{A}}
is not necessarily saturated. The saturation
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{sat}
in general is not unmixed, i.e. there may be embedded components. Both
J_{\mathcal{A}}
and
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{sat}
define the same subscheme
X_{\mathcal{A}}
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Given a primary decomposition of
J_{\mathcal{A}}
, it is a standard fact in commutative algebra that the intersection of primary components of codimension 2 is uniquely determined. The resulting ideal is unmixed (i.e. no embedded or isolated components) by construction. We denote by
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
this ideal, and by
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
the codimension 2 equidimensional scheme defined by
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
. This is a subscheme of
X_{\mathcal{A}}
.

When
n=2
,
\mathcal{A}
is a line arrangement and
X_{\mathcal{A}}
is a zero-dimensional scheme. In this case there are no embedded points, and
J^{sat}
is unmixed so
X_{\mathcal{A}}=X_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
. The Milnor module is the graded
R
-module
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{sat}/J_{\mathcal{A}}
. The global Tjurina number is then the degree of
X_{\mathcal{A}}
, an invariant studied by many authors. We extend the notion of global Tjurina number to hyperplane arrangements in any projective space: the global Tjurina number for any hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the degree of
X_{\mathcal{A}}
, which coincides with the degree of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
since components of codimension

\geq 3
do not contribute to the degree (see the paragraph following Proposition
4.3
).

In earlier work
[
15
]
(also with H. Schenck) and
[
14
]
, the authors introduced the use of liaison addition and basic double linkage to the study of the schemes
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
, with a focus on their Hilbert functions and structure. In this paper we introduce a very strong new method from liaison theory, which we call the
general residual
, and we apply it together with the older methods to the study of graded Betti numbers.

The general residual is defined as follows. Let
g
be a general linear combination of the generators of
J_{\mathcal{A}}=\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
. We interpret
g
as being the derivative of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
with respect to a general linear form
\ell
. Then
(f_{\mathcal{A}},g)
is a regular sequence (Lemma
4.4
), so it links
J_{\mathcal{A}}
to an unmixed ideal
r_{\mathcal{A}}
that we call the general residual of
\mathcal{A}
.

In liaison theory, it is often advantageous to use minimal links. In contrast, a general residual is
not
a minimal link of a Jacobian ideal. A benefit of our choice of link is that it allows us to describe the linked ideal, that is, the general residual rather explicitly. In particular, we describe the geometry of the scheme defined by a general residual:
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is supported on the singular locus of
\mathcal{A}
, and each component is a complete intersection, one of whose generators is linear (Proposition
4.6
). Using the input data, this describe this linear generator explicitly. Note that the support of the scheme defined by
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is completely determined by the codimension two subspaces in the intersection lattice of
\mathcal{A}
(see
Corollary

4.8
). In fact, the ideal itself is determined by these data and the choice of the general linear form
\ell
. Of course the precise residual depends on
\ell
, but the generality of the form implies that properties such as graded Betti numbers and so Cohen-Macaulayness do not depend on the choice of the form. Linking back using the same complete intersection gives us
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
.
Under mild assumptions, these simple properties of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
allow us to describe its primary decomposition, regularity and graded Betti numbers using geometric and liaison-theoretic methods. We then use liaison theory to derive the analogous information about
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
. In particular, we find the graded Betti numbers of
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
for large classes of hyperplane arrangements.

Before giving the main results of this paper (that work in any projective space), we describe three applications for line arrangements. First, we determine new bounds for the global Tjurina number of line arrangements, extending work of du Plessis and Wall, as well as of Beorchia and Miró-Roig. Second, in the special situation of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, through
r_{\mathcal{A}}
(under mild assumptions) we determine the graded Betti numbers of
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{sat}
, which in turn allows us to determine the graded Betti numbers of both the Milnor module and of the original Jacobian ideal
J_{\mathcal{A}}
. Third, through our study of the general residual, we are also able to give a new freeness criterion for line arrangements. It highlights the fact that free line arrangements are special by proving that a related codimension two ideal has the least possible number of generators, namely two, if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is free.

We now give a more detailed overview of the paper, including the main results. In Section
2
we recall some of the main tools that we use in this paper. These include a geometric description of
X^{top}
(Proposition
2.2
), arrangement-theoretic versions of liaison addition (Theorem
2.3
) and basic double linkage (Corollary
2.4
), and a duality result that will be crucial when we study the Milnor module later in this paper (
Theorem

2.9
).
Section
3
gives, via some technical lemmas, a tool connecting the ideal
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with the ideal of the line arrangement in a general plane
\Lambda
obtained by intersecting
\mathcal{A}
with
\Lambda
, in the case that
X^{top}
is ACM (Corollary
3.8
).

Section
4
introduces the general residual described above. An important ingredient is the point
\ell^{\vee}
dual to the linear form
\ell
(whose coordinates are the coefficients of
\ell
), which we view as being in the same projective space. Another is the set
S(\mathcal{A})
, the
top support of the singular locus
, which is the set of codimension two subspaces defined by all pairs of hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
. Also, if
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
then
g_{P}
is the product of linear forms (up to scalar multiple) vanishing along
P
, and
t_{P}=\deg g_{P}
. We show (Lemma
4.4
) that

(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(g_{p},\frac{\partial g_{p}}{\partial\ell})\ \ \hbox{ and }\ \ |\mathcal{A}|\cdot(|\mathcal{A}|-1)=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}t_{p}(t_{p}-1).

This leads to the striking primary decomposition for
r_{\mathcal{A}}
(Proposition
4.6
and Corollary
4.8
):

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},\frac{\partial g_{P}}{\partial\ell})=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1}).

The main result of
Section

4
is Theorem
4.12
giving an upper bound for the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, namely
\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}\leq|\mathcal{A}|-1
. We combine some of these ideas in Corollary
4.14
, where we show that if
X^{top}
is ACM and
\Lambda
is a general 2-plane, and if
\mathcal{B}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to
\Lambda
, then
r_{\mathcal{B}}
has the same graded Betti numbers as
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, and in fact the scheme defined by
r_{\mathcal{B}}
is the intersection of that defined by
r_{\mathcal{A}}
with
\Lambda
.

The main result of
Section

5
(Theorem
5.2
) uses the theory of E-type and N-type resolutions from liaison theory to give the surprising conclusion that for any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the initial degree of
J_{\mathcal{A}}^{top}
is precisely
|\mathcal{A}|-1
. As a consequence (Corollary
5.3
), we show that the bound on
\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}
is sharp:
\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}=|\mathcal{A}|-1
for any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.
Section
5
also introduces an auxiliary ideal,
I=r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap I_{\ell^{\vee}}
. Proposition
5.4
shows that
r_{\mathcal{A}}=(I,\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and that
\reg I=|\mathcal{A}|
.

The remarkable properties of general residuals suggests that it is worthwhile to study a larger class of ideals whose primary components have the same simple structure as those of general residuals. We dub these ideals residual-lie ideals and characterize which residual-like ideals are actually general residuals. The result is one of the ingredients of the mentioned new freeness criterion.

Section

7
gives our new bounds for the global Tjurina number for line arrangements mentioned above. Specifically, we prove

Theorem.
(Theorem
7.1
)

For any arrangement
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})
of
d\geq 3
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, one has:

(a)

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\geq\binom{d}{2},

and equality is true if and only if no three lines of
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent.

(b)

If the

\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent then

Furthermore, equality is true if and only if
d-1
lines, but not
d
lines of
\mathcal{A}

(c)

If
\mathcal{A}

Using the hyperplane section methods mentioned above, Corollary

extends this to hyperplane arrangements in

.

2.
Background

In this section, we fix notation and recall some important concepts and results.

Let
S=K[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring, where
K
is a field of characteristic zero. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by a product
f_{\mathcal{A}}
of linear forms. Denote by
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
the corresponding Jacobian ideal, generated by the first partial derivatives of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

A standard fact, easily shown, is that if
\mathcal{A}
consists of
e
hyperplanes and with the property that there is a codimension 2 linear variety
\Lambda
such that every hyperplane of
\mathcal{A}
contains
\Lambda
then
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is a saturated complete intersection of type
(e-1,e-1)
.

More generally, if
\mathcal{A}
is a free arrangement then
S/\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is Cohen-Macaulay. Most arrangements, however, are not free and there are many possible reasons for the failure of
S/\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
to be Cohen-Macaulay. It could fail to be saturated. But even the saturation can fail to be Cohen-Macaulay. One reason could be that it has embedded components, so we can remove these and study what is left. But even the remaining ideal can fail to be Cohen-Macaulay for more mysterious reasons. We denote this remaining ideal by
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
. The paper
[
15
]
studied conditions that force
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
to be Cohen-Macaulay, and it studied this mysterious failure of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
to be Cohen-Macaulay in some cases.

More precisely, consider a primary decomposition of
J
:

(2.1)

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathfrak{q}_{r}

and let
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
be the associated prime of
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
. Removing all associated primes of height
>2
, the remaining intersection of primary ideals is well-defined, and we denote by
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
this intersection. Thus in (
2.1
)
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
is the intersection of the
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
of height 2. Similarly, the intersection
of all associated primes absorbs all the associated primes of height
>2
, and the resulting ideal is the radical

\sqrt{\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})}=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathfrak{p}_{r},

where each component is the ideal of a linear variety of codimension 2.

We will be interested in the geometry of the schemes corresponding to
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
for hyperplane arrangements. We denote by
X^{top}
such a scheme, and our main (but not exclusive) interest is when
X^{top}
is arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (ACM).

The papers
[
15
]
(for hyperplane arrangements) and
[
14
]
(for hypersuface arrangements) introduced the use of liaison methods to study these propertis. In this paper we will introduce new liaison methods to study the minimal free resolutions of many Cohen-Macaulay ideals arising in this way. We first study these problems in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and then give a reduction to the case of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Our last results are in this latter setting.

First we recall the previous methods. Liaison Addition was originally described by P. Schwartau in his Ph.D. thesis
[
20
]
(unpubished), and subsequently studied in
[
8
]
. Basic double linkage was originally introduced by R. Lazarsfeld and P. Rao in
[
12
]
and studied more carefully in
[
1
]
and considerably extended in
[
10
]
. We refer to these sources for some of the machinery used below.

In the following result we denote by
L_{i}
a hyperplane and by
\ell_{i}
the linear form defining it (up to scalars).

Proposition 2.2

(
[

Let

3.
Reducing to the case of line arrangements

For any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
and any hyperplane
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
that is not in
\mathcal{A}
, we
may consider the set of codimension two subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n}

\mathcal{A}\cap H=\{L\cap H\;\mid\;L\in\mathcal{A}\}

as a set of hyperplanes of
H
. Thus, they determine a hyperplane arrangement of
H
to which we refer as the restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to
H\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. Repeating this restriction step
n-2
times, we obtain a line arrangement in a projective plane. The goal of this section is to show that some properties of the original hyperplane arrangement can be inferred from its restriction to a line arrangement. Later on, this will allow us to simplify some arguments by focussing on line arrangements only.

We begin with a general observation.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I\subset S
be an unmixed ideal with minimal primary decomposititon

I=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak{q}_{t}.

Assume
\dim S/I\geq 2
, and let
x\in[S]_{1}
be a nonzerodivisor of
S/I
. For any ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset S
and any
f\in S
, denote by
\bar{\mathfrak{a}}
and
\bar{f}
the image of
\mathfrak{a}
and
f
, respectively, in the polynomial ring
\bar{S}=S/xS
. Then one has

(\bar{I})^{top}=\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{1}}^{top}\cap\cdots\cap\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{t}}^{top}.

Proof.

Passing to
\bar{S}
, we obtain

\bar{I}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{t}}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{1}}^{top}\cap\cdots\cap\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{t}}^{top}.

Localizing at any associated prime of
S/\bar{I}^{top}
then provides

(\bar{I})^{top}\subseteq\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{1}}^{top}\cap\cdots\cap\overline{\mathfrak{q}_{t}}^{top}.

The assumption on
x
yields that both ideals of the inclusion have the same degree as
I
, which implies the desired equality.
∎

The next result is likely well-known. Note though that we do not assume that
x
is general.

Lemma 3.2
.

If ideals
I
and
J
of
S
are linked by a Gorenstein ideal
\mathfrak{c}
, and a linear form
x\in S
satisfies
\mathfrak{c}:x=\mathfrak{c}
, then
(\bar{I})^{top}
and
(\bar{J})^{top}
are linked by
\bar{\mathfrak{c}}
.

Proof.

The assumption on
x
ensures that the passage from
I,J
and
\mathfrak{c}
to their images in
\bar{S}
preserves degrees.
Furthermore,
I\cdot J\subseteq\mathfrak{c}
implies
\bar{I}\cdot\bar{J}\subseteq\bar{\mathfrak{c}}
, and so

(\bar{I})^{top}\subseteq\bar{\mathfrak{c}}:\bar{J}=\bar{\mathfrak{c}}:(\bar{J})^{top}.

Both ideals have the same degree, and so they must be equal.
∎

Turning to Jacobian ideals, we need further notation.
Consider any linear form
x\neq 0
of
S
. We want to identify
S/xS
and a suitable subring
R
of
S
. To this end choose
linear forms
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in S
such that
S=K[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
and
\frac{\partial x}{\partial y_{i}}=0
for any
i
. Any homogeneous
g\in S
can be uniquely written as

(3.1)

g=\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}g_{j}\quad\text{ with homogeneous $g_{j}\in R=K[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]$}.

Then
g_{0}
is the restriction of
g
to
R
and we write
\bar{g}=g_{0}
. Moreover, the Jacobian ideal of
\bar{g}
, denoted by
\Jac(\bar{g})
, is the ideal of
R
that is generated by the partial derivatives of
\bar{g}
with respect to
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
. Note that

\frac{\partial x}{\partial y_{i}}=0
implies
\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial x}=0
, and so
\frac{\partial\bar{g}}{\partial x}=0
for any
\bar{g}\in R
.

For a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a hyperplane
H=V(x)\notin\mathcal{A}
that does not contain any intersection of two
distinct hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
, denote by
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
the restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to
H
. Thus, if
f_{\mathcal{A}}=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d}\in S
, then
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is defined by

(3.2)

f_{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}=\overline{\ell_{1}}\cdots\overline{\ell_{d}}=\overline{f_{\mathcal{A}}}\in R.

Is is again a squarefree polynomial.

Equation

3.1
immediately implies:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
g\in S
, one has

\overline{\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_{i}}}=\frac{\partial\bar{g}}{\partial y_{i}}.

Proof.

Writing
g=\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}g_{j}
as in
Equation

3.1
and using
\frac{\partial x}{\partial y_{i}}=0
, one gets
\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_{i}}=\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}\frac{\partial g_{j}}{\partial y_{i}}
, which gives
\overline{\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_{i}}}=\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial y_{i}}.
We conclude as
\bar{g}=g_{0}
.
∎

The following observation is needed when we restrict Jacobian ideals of hyperplane arrangements.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a codimension two subspace that is not contained in
H=V(x)
. Fix generators
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in S
of
I_{\Lambda}
. Then one has for any homogeneous
g\in K[\ell_{1},\ell_{2}]
an equality of ideals of
R
,

\overline{\Jac(g)}=\Jac(\bar{g}).

Proof.

By assumption, we know
x\notin(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
. Thus, there are linear forms
m_{1},m_{2}\in R
and
a,b\in K
such that, possibly after scaling, one has

\ell_{1}=x+am_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\ell_{2}=x+bm_{2}.

Since
g\in K[\ell_{1},\ell_{2}]
, we get

\displaystyle\Jac(g)

\displaystyle=(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\ell_{1}},\frac{\partial g}{\partial\ell_{2}})=(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}+a\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}+b\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{2}})

\displaystyle\subseteq(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x},\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{2}})\subseteq\Jac(g).

It follows that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\in(\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{2}})

and, by a straightforward computation using
Equation

3.1
,

\overline{\Jac(g)}=\overline{(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x},\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g}{\partial m_{2}})}=(g_{1},\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{2}}).

By our definition of the restriction, we have
\bar{g}=g_{0}\in K[m_{1},m_{2}]
, and so

\Jac(\bar{g})=(\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{2}})
.
Passing to the restriction of Relation (
3.3
) to
R
, we conclude
g_{1}\in(\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{1}},\frac{\partial g_{0}}{\partial m_{2}})
. Combining this with the last two equalities gives
\overline{\Jac(g)}=\Jac(\bar{g})
.
∎

The analog of

is not true for an arbitrary hyperplane arrangement.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
be a hyperplane arrangement consisting of four general planes. Then

is the saturated ideal of the six lines of a tetrahedral configuration, and so

is free. Let

be a general plane. The restriction of

to

is a general set of four lines in

, and thus the corresponding Jacobian ideal

defines six points with generic Hilbert function. The ideal of these six points has four minimal generators, which shows that

is not saturated.
Since
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is a Cohen-Macaulay ideal, so is its restriction

\overline{\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})}\neq\Jac(f_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}})
. However, one has
\overline{\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})}=\Jac(f_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}})^{sat}
.

Notice, if instead we take five general planes for

then even

Nevertheless, an analog of

.

Using

we know

where each

is the product of linear divisors of

that are contained in the ideal

. Thus,

3.4
gives

\overline{\Jac(g_{P})}=\Jac({\overline{g_{P}}})=\Jac(g_{\bar{P}})
, where

denotes the hyperplane
section of

Invoking

∎

4.
General residuals of arrangements

The purpose of this section is to introduce the concept of a general residual of a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and to establish some of its properties. In particular, we describe its minimal primary decomposition, establish an upper bound on its Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity and consider its behavior under when one restricts an arrangement to a hyperplane.

We denote by
S
a polynomial ring
K[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
in
n+1
variables. For a linear form
\ell=\sum a_{i}x_{i}\in S
, we write
\ell^{\vee}=(a_{0}:\cdots:a_{n})
for the point dual to
\ell
, though we will consider
\ell^{\vee}
as a point in the original
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

We begin with some needed technical results.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
f\in S
is any homogeneous polynomial of positive degree and
\ell\in[S]_{1}
is general then

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}(\ell^{\vee})\neq 0.

Proof.

Put
d=\deg f
. Let
\ell=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}
, and so
\ell^{\vee}=(a_{0}:\cdots:a_{n})
. Since we assume that the characteristic of
K
is zero, the Euler derivative of
f
is

d\cdot f=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}.

Since

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}

the equality
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}(\ell^{\vee})=0
implies

d\cdot f(\ell^{\vee})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(\ell^{\vee})=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}(\ell^{\vee})=0,

and so
f(\ell^{\vee})=0
. This is a contradiction because the point
\ell^{\vee}
is general.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T
be a polynomial ring in two variables over
K
. Consider any polynomials
0\neq\ell\in[T]_{1}
and
f,g\in[T]_{d}
for some
d\geq 1
. If both
f
and
g
are not divisible by
\ell
then there is an equality of ideals,

(\ell,f)=(\ell,g).

Proof.

This follows since
[(\ell,f)]_{d}=[T]_{d}
, which is equivalent to
[T/(\ell,f)]_{d}=0
. The latter is true as
\reg(T/(\ell,f)])=d-1
since

\ell
and
f
form a regular sequence.
∎

Consider now a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by a squarefree product of linear forms

f_{\mathcal{A}}
. We define the
top support of the singular locus
\Sing(\mathcal{A}
)
as the set of codimension two subspaces

formed by
distinct hyperplanes

of
\mathcal{A}
,

S(\mathcal{A})=\{H\cap H^{\prime}\ |\ H\neq H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Thus, the ideal of
S(\mathcal{A})
is

I_{S(\mathcal{A})}=\bigcap_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime},\text{ divisors of }f_{\mathcal{A}}}(\ell,\ell^{\prime}).

Moreover, for any
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
, we denote by
g_{P}
the product of linear factors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that are contained in
I_{P}
. We set
t_{P}=\deg g_{P}
to be the number of hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
that contain
P
.
We refer to
t_{P}
as the

of
P
in
\mathcal{A}
.
Using this notation, we can state
Proposition

2.2
more explicitly.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is any hyperplane arrangement then the top-dimensional part of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}\Jac(g_{P}),

where each
\Jac(g_{P})
is a complete intersection of type
(t_{P}-1,t_{P}-1)
.

Observe that this result implies

I_{S(\mathcal{A})}=\sqrt{\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}}=\sqrt{\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}}).

The local and global Tjurina number are measures of singularities of any plane curve. We can extend the definition to hyperplane arrangements of arbitrary dimension as follows.
The

of the hyperplane arrangement defined by
f_{\mathcal{A}}
at

is the length of
(S/\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))_{I_{P}}
. The

is the sum over the local Tjurina numbers. It follows that the global Tjurina number is equal to

\deg\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})=\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top})=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(t_{P}-1)^{2}
.

For the remainder of this section we denote by
\ell
a general linear form in
S
. We write
|\mathcal{A}|
for the cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
.

We want to link the Jacobian ideal of
\mathcal{A}
using a suitable complete intersection. The following result describes in particular the support of the scheme defined by this link.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is any hyperplane arrangement and
\ell\in[S]_{1}
is a general linear form then
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
is a complete intersection with a minimal primary decomposition

(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(g_{p},\frac{\partial g_{p}}{\partial\ell}).

In particular, one has

(4.1)

|\mathcal{A}|\cdot(|\mathcal{A}|-1)=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}t_{p}(t_{p}-1).

Proof.

We begin with establishing
Equation

4.1

and set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}-L=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{L\}
. Consider any

. Temporarily, denote the number of hyperplanes of

and

that contain

by

and

We now consider three cases.

First, assume

such that

Setting

the above observations can be summarized as follows:

(4.2)

\displaystyle t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}t_{P,\mathcal{A}}&\text{ if }P\in S(\mathcal{A})\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L)\\
t_{P,\mathcal{A}}-1&\text{ if }P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L.\end{cases}

Now we use induction on

is true.
Assume

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in S(A)}t_{P,\mathcal{A}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in S(A)\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L)}t_{P,\mathcal{A}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}}-1)+\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}t_{P,\mathcal{A}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in S(A)\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L)}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}-1)+\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}+1)t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in S(A)\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L)}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}-1)+\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}-1)+2\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(d-1)(d-2)+2\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},

where the last equality is due to the induction hypothesis. Since
L
intersects each
H\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
in a codimension two subspace we get

\sum_{P\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cap L}t_{P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|=d-1.

Combining the last two equations, we obtain
\sum_{P\in S(A)}t_{P,\mathcal{A}}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}}-1)=d(d-1)
, as claimed.

Now we establish the claimed primary decomposition.
Consider any
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
.
To simplify notation write
f=f_{\mathcal{A}}=g\cdot h
with
g=g_{P}
. By definition of
g
, we know
h\notin I_{P}
, and so
h
is a unit in the localization
S_{I_{P}}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})_{I_{P}}

\displaystyle=(gh,\frac{\partial g}{\partial\ell}\cdot h+g\frac{\partial h}{\partial\ell})_{I_{P}}

\displaystyle=(g,\frac{\partial g}{\partial\ell})_{I_{P}},

which shows that the primary component of
(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})
associated to
I_{P}
is
(g,\frac{\partial g}{\partial\ell})
. It remains to prove that
(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})
has no associated prime ideals other than
I_{P}
with
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
. Comparing degrees of
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and

I=\bigcup_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(g_{p},\frac{\partial g_{p}}{\partial\ell})
, this follows from Equation (
4.1
).
∎

We need another preparatory result. We continue to use the above notation.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\ell\in[S]_{1}
is a general linear form then one has, for any
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
,

\displaystyle(g_{P},\frac{\partial g_{P}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(g_{P})

\displaystyle=(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})

\displaystyle=(\ell_{P},\frac{\partial g_{P}}{\partial\ell}),

where
\ell_{P}
is a linear form defining the linear span of
P
and
\ell^{\vee}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
\widetilde{\ell_{P}}
is any linear divisor of
g_{P}
.

Proof.

Possibly changing coordinates, we may assume that
I_{P}=(x_{0},x_{1})
and
\widetilde{\ell_{P}}=x_{0}
.
Note that the second claimed equality follows by
Lemma

4.2
. It remains to justify the first equality.

To simplify notation write
g_{P}=x_{0}\cdot h
with
h\in K[x_{0},x_{1}]
. Thus, we have to show that

(x_{0}h,\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(x_{0}h)=(\ell_{P},h).

Put
d=\deg h
. Since
(x_{0}h,\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell})
and
\Jac(x_{0}h)
are complete intersections of type
(d,d+1)
and
(d,d)
, respectively, liaison theory gives that
(x_{0}h,\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(x_{0}h)
is a complete intersection of type
(1,d)
. Hence, it suffices to show that

(\ell_{P},h)\cdot\Jac(x_{0}h)\subseteq(x_{0}h,\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell}).

Since
(\ell_{P},h)=(\ell_{P},\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell})
we are done if we know that

\ell_{P}\cdot\Jac(x_{0}h)\subseteq(x_{0}h,\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial\ell}).

To verify this, we simplify notation. Let
\ell=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}
with
a_{i}\in K
and set
p=\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial x_{0}}
and

q=\frac{\partial x_{0}h}{\partial x_{1}}
. Note that the hyperplane spanned by
P
and
\ell^{\vee}
is defined by

\ell_{P}=a_{1}x_{0}-a_{0}x_{1}
. Thus, using also
(d+1)\cdot x_{0}h=x_{0}p+x_{1}q
, the last inclusion becomes

(a_{1}x_{0}-a_{0}x_{1})\cdot(p,q)\subseteq(x_{0}p+x_{1}q,a_{0}p+a_{1}q).

It is true because

(a_{1}x_{0}-a_{0}x_{1})\cdot p=a_{1}\cdot(x_{0}p+x_{1}q)-x_{1}(a_{0}p+a_{1}q)

and

(a_{1}x_{0}-a_{0}x_{1})\cdot q=-a_{1}\cdot(x_{0}p+x_{1}q)+x_{0}(a_{0}p+a_{1}q).

∎

We are ready to link the Jacobian ideal and to describe the residual.

Proposition 4.6
.

For any general linear form
\ell\in S
, the residual

r_{\mathcal{A}}=(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})

has minimal primary decompositions

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},\frac{\partial g_{P}}{\partial\ell})=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}}).

Proof.

Note that
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})=(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
.
Since
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
have the same associated prime ideals by
Lemma

4.4
and
Proposition

4.3
, the result follows by computing locally at
I_{P}
for any
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
. Hence we conclude by applying
Lemma

4.5
.
∎

Obviously, the residual
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
depends on
\ell
. However, by the generality of
\ell
, its structure and, in particular, its graded Betti numbers are independent of
\ell
. Thus, abusing notation slightly, we introduce:

Definition 4.7
.

For any general linear form
\ell\in S
, the ideal

(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is said to be a
general residual of
\mathcal{A}
(with respect to
\ell
) and denoted by
r_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Using
Lemma

4.2
, one can describe the primary decomposition of the general residual without making any choices (besides
\ell
).

Corollary 4.8
.

For any general linear form
\ell\in S
, the minimal primary decomposition of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1}),

where
t_{P}
denotes the number of hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
that contain
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
. In particular, the radical of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is determined by the intersection lattice of
\mathcal{A}
as

\sqrt{r_{\mathcal{A}}}=I_{S(\mathcal{A})}.

Our next goal is to estimate the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
. One ingredient is the following observation.

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider any hyperplane
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
that is not in
\mathcal{A}
. If
H
is defined by a linear form
x\in S
, then one has

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}:x=r_{\mathcal{A}},

where
\mathcal{A}+H
is short for the hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\cup\{H\}
.

Proof.

By definition of the top support of the singular locus of
\mathcal{A}+H
, one has
S(\mathcal{A}+H)=S(\mathcal{A})\cup(\mathcal{A}\cap H)
, where, slightly abusing notation, we set

\mathcal{A}\cap H=\{H^{\prime}\cap H\;\mid\;H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Thus, applying
Proposition

4.6
to the arrangement
A+H
, we get

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})-(\mathcal{A}\cap H)}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})\cap\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{A}\cap H}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}}\cdot x),

where, for
P\in(\mathcal{A}\cap H)-S(\mathcal{A})
, the polynomials
g_{P}
and
\widetilde{\ell_{P}}
both define the unique hyperplane in
\mathcal{A}
containing
P
, and so
(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}}\cdot x)=(\ell_{P},x)=I_{P}
.

If
P
is in
(\mathcal{A}\cap H)\cap S(\mathcal{A})=S(\mathcal{A})\cap H
then
t_{P}\geq 2
and
(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})\neq S
is a complete intersection of type
(1,t_{P}-1)
. It follows that

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{A}+H}:x

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})-(\mathcal{A}\cap H)}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})\cap\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})\cap H}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},\frac{g_{P}}{\widetilde{\ell_{P}}})

\displaystyle=r_{\mathcal{A}},

where the last equality is true by
Proposition

4.6
.
∎

The above proof also gives the following observation.

Corollary 4.10
.

If a hyperplane
H=V(x)
is not in
\mathcal{A}
, then

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+|\mathcal{A}\cap H|.

We want to apply the following special case of
[
5
, Corollary 3.7]
. We denote by
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
the homogeneous maximal ideal of
S
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Consider proper homogeneous ideals
J_{1},\ldots,J_{t}
and
J
of
S
. If there are homogeneous ideals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{t}
such that

I_{k}\cdot J_{k}\subseteq J\subseteq J_{k}

for each
k
and
I_{1}+\cdots+I_{t}=\mathfrak{m}
, then

\reg J\leq 1+\max\{\reg J_{k}\;|\;1\leq k\leq t\}.

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 4.12
.

For any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, one has

\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}\leq|\mathcal{A}|-1.

Proof.

It there is a point that is contained in every hyperplane of
\mathcal{A}
, passing to the projection from this point,
we may consider
\mathcal{A}
in a lower-dimensional space in order to establish the claim. Thus, we may assume that the ideal
I\subset S
that is generated by the linear divisors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
equals
\mathfrak{m}
. This implies
|\mathcal{A}|\geq n+1
.

We use induction on
|\mathcal{A}|
. If
|\mathcal{A}|=n+1
, then we may assume
f_{\mathcal{A}}=x_{0}\cdots x_{n}
, and so

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{i\neq j}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Thus,
r_{\mathcal{A}}
defines a star configuration and
\reg(r_{\mathcal{A}})=n=|\mathcal{A}|-1
, as desired.

Assume
|\mathcal{A}|>n+1
. By induction, we know for any linear divisor
x
of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that

\reg r_{\mathcal{A}-V(x)}\leq|\mathcal{A}|-2.

Using the definition of a colon ideal and
Lemma

4.9
, we get

x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}-V(x)}\subseteq r_{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq r_{\mathcal{A}-V(x)},

where
\mathcal{A}-V(x)
is short for the arrangement
\mathcal{A}\setminus\{V(x)\}
.
Since we assumed that
I=(x\;|\;\text{ $x$ a linear divisor of $f_{\mathcal{A}}$})
equals
\mathfrak{m}
, applying
Proposition

4.11
with
J=r_{\mathcal{A}}
and
J_{x}=r_{\mathcal{A}-V(x)}
gives
\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}\leq|\mathcal{A}|-1
.
∎

We conclude this section with a result in the spirit of
Section

3
.
As for Jacobian ideals of hyperplane arrangements, it is useful to consider restrictions of general residuals. Recall that the scheme defined by a general residual

is supported on

. We use the notation introduced in

Consider a hyperplane arrangement

, and let

(a)

\overline{(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})}=(f_{\bar{\mathcal{A}}},\frac{\partial f_{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}{\partial\bar{\ell}})

(b)

\overline{r_{\mathcal{A}}}^{top}=r_{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}

\overline{\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}}={\frac{\partial\overline{f_{\mathcal{A}}}}{\partial\bar{\ell}}}=\frac{\partial f_{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}{\partial\bar{\ell}}.

For (b), recall that the general residual of

5.
A lower degree bound for generators of the top-dimensional part

The first goal of this section is to show that, for any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, the initial degree of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
is exactly
|\mathcal{A}|-1
. This result has two applications. First, we use it to determine the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of any general residual.
Second, in
Section

9
we will use it to infer the graded Betti numbers of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
for any line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
from the graded Betti numbers of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
.
Our second goal, for use in subsequent sections, is to derive properties of an ideal that is closely related to a general residual (see
Proposition

5.4
).

We begin by introducing some useful notation. For any graded
S
-module
M
, the
S
-dual
of
M
is the graded module
M^{*}=Hom_{S}(M,S)
. The
K
-dual
of
M
is the graded
S
-module
M^{\vee}=Hom_{K}(M,K)
, where
K\cong S/\mathfrak{m}
is considered as a graded module concentrated in degree zero. The
initial degree
of
M
is

a(M)=\inf\{j\in\mathbb{Z}\;|\;[M]_{j}\neq 0\}.

Its
end
is

e(M)=\sup\{j\in\mathbb{Z}\;|\;[M]_{j}\neq 0\}.

In particular, in the case where
M=0
one has
e(M)=-\infty
and
a(M)=\infty
. If
M\neq 0
then
a(M)
and
e(M)
are integers.
Moreover, we set

e^{+}(M)=e(M/\mathfrak{m}M).

It denotes the maximum degree of a minimal generator of
M
.
Thus, for a finitely generated graded free
S
-module
F
, one has
a(F^{*})=-e^{+}(F)
.

We need some concepts and results from liaison theory and use
[
17
]
as our main reference. Since we consider only ideals of codimension two we specialize the general liaison result to this situation.
Let
I\subset S
be a homogeneous ideal of
S
having codimension two.
Following
[
17
, Definition 3.1]
, an
E
-type resolution of
I
is an exact sequence of finitely generated graded
S
-modules

0\to E\to F\to I\to 0,

where
F
is a free
S
-module. An
N
-type resolution of
I
is an exact sequence of finitely generated graded
S
-modules

0\to G\to N\to I\to 0,

where
G
is a free
S
-module and
H^{n}_{\mathfrak{m}}(N)=0
.
We use
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)
to denote the
i
-th local cohomology module of
M
with support in
\mathfrak{m}
.
The above resolutions are said to be
minimal
if it is not possible to split
off free direct summands.

If
S/I
is Cohen-Macaulay then its resolutions of
E
- or
N
-type are free resolutions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Any homogenous codimension two ideal
I
of
S
admits minimal resolutions of
E
-type and
N
-type. These are uniquely determined up to isomorphisms of exact sequences of graded
S
-modules.

Moreover, an
N
-type resolution
0\to G\to N\to I\to 0
is minimal if and only if there is a graded isomorphism
G^{*}\otimes_{K}S/\mathfrak{m}\cong Ext^{1}_{S}(I,R)\otimes_{K}S/\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

Combine
[
18
, Lemma II.1.4]
and
[
17
, Remark 3.2]
.
∎

If
I
and
J
are two ideals linked by a complete intersection then an
N
-type resolution of
I
determines an
E
-type resolution of
J
, see
[
17
, Proposition 3.8]
. This is crucial for the proof of the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is any (finite) hyperplane arrangement then the initial degree of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
is at least
|\mathcal{A}|-1
, that is,

a(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top})=|\mathcal{A}|-1.

Moreover,
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
has as at least as many minimal generators of degree
|\mathcal{A}|-1
as
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
.

In particular, this result says that the minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
do not have degrees less than the degree of the minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

First, we show the estimate

a(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top})\geq|\mathcal{A}|-1.

Let
\ell\in S
be a general linear form and put
d=|\mathcal{A}|
.
Since
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
is contained in
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, we obtain a commutative diagram

\displaystyle\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>S(-2d+1)@>{}>{}>S(-d+1)\oplus S(-d)@>{}>{}>(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{}V{}V\\
0@>{}>{}>G@>{}>{}>N@>{}>{}>r_{\mathcal{A}}@>{}>{}>0,\\[3.0pt]
\end{CD}

where the vertical map is injective and the horizontal sequences are minimal resolutions of
N
-type of
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, respectively. Since

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}=(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):r_{\mathcal{A}},

lifting the vertical map to a morphism of complexes, one obtains the following
E
-type resolution of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
,

0\to N^{*}(-2d+1)\to G^{*}(-2d+1)\oplus S(-d)\oplus S(-d+1)\to\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}\to 0.

It follows that

\displaystyle a(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{d-1,a(G^{*}(-2d+1))\}

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\min\{d-1,a(G^{*})+2d-1\}.

where equality is true if the
E
-type resolution is minimal. By duality (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Proposition 2.1]
), there is a graded isomorphism

Ext_{S}^{1}(r_{\mathcal{A}},S)\cong H^{n-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}})^{\vee}(n+1).

Hence, using also
Lemma

5.1
, the minimality of the
N
-type resolution of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
implies

a(G^{*})=a(Ext_{S}^{1}(r_{\mathcal{A}},S))=-e(H^{n-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}}))-n-1.

By
Theorem

4.12
and the cohomological characterization of the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity, one has

e(H^{n-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}}))+n-1\leq\reg(S/r_{\mathcal{A}})\leq d-2,

and so we get

\displaystyle a(G^{*})+2d-1

\displaystyle=-e(H^{n-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}}))-n-2+2d

\displaystyle\geq n-1-d+2-n-2+2d=d-1.

Taking into account Inequality (
5
), this yields

a(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top})\geq d-1,

as claimed.

Second, to complete the argument recall that the minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
have degree
d-1
. Since
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is contained in
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
the above degree estimate implies that the minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
are also minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
.
∎

As a consequence, we show that the estimate in
Theorem

4.12
is in fact an equality.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is any (finite) hyperplane arrangement then the general residual has a minimal generator of degree
|\mathcal{A}|-1
and Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity

\reg r_{\mathcal{A}}=|\mathcal{A}|-1.

Moreover, if
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
is Cohen-Macaulay and
t\geq 2
denotes the number of minimal generators of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
with degree
|\mathcal{A}|-1
then the general residual
r_{\mathcal{A}}
has exactly
t-1
syzygies of degree
|\mathcal{A}|
.

Proof.

We reverse the roles of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
in the proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

Let
\ell\in S
be a general linear form and put
d=|\mathcal{A}|
.
Since
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
is contained in
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
, we obtain a commutative diagram

\displaystyle\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>S(-2d+1)@>{}>{}>S(-d+1)\oplus S(-d)@>{}>{}>(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{}V{}V\\
0@>{}>{}>E@>{}>{}>S^{t}(-d+1)\oplus F@>{}>{}>\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}@>{}>{}>0,\\[3.0pt]
\end{CD}

where the vertical map is injective and the horizontal sequences are minimal resolutions of
E
-type of
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
, respectively. By definition of
t
, we have
a(F)\geq d
.
Since

r_{\mathcal{A}}=(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top},

lifting the vertical map to a morphism of complexes, one obtains the following
N
-type resolution of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
,

0\to F^{*}(-2d+1)\oplus S^{t-1}(-d)\to E^{*}(-2d+1)\oplus S(-d+1)\to r_{\mathcal{A}}\to 0,

where we have taken into account the cancellation caused by the fact that
\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}
is a minimal generator of

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
. Since
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is not a minimal generator of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
it also is not a minimal generator of

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
. Hence, there is no further cancellation in the mapping cone, and so the stated
N
-type resolution of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is minimal. It shows that
r_{\mathcal{A}}
has a minimal generator of degree
d-1
. It also follows that
\reg(r_{\mathcal{A}})\geq d-1
. Together with
Theorem

4.12
, this gives
\reg(r_{\mathcal{A}})=d-1
.

For the additional claim, note that
E
is a free
S
-module if
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
is Cohen-Macaulay. Thus, the above
N
-type resolution is a minimal free resolution of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Using that
a(F)\geq d
, it follows that

e^{+}(F^{*}(-2d+1))=e^{+}(F^{*})+2d-1=-a(F)+2d-1\leq d-1.

Hence,
r_{\mathcal{A}}
has exactly
t-1
first syzygies of degree
d
, and
d
is the maximum degree of a first syzygy.
∎

By the definition of the general residual
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, it is clear that it contains
\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}
. The following result shows that this polynomial is an element of a minimal generating set of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
. Thus, it identifies a minimal generator of degree
|\mathcal{A}|-1
of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
whose existence is guaranteed by
Corollary

5.3
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
r_{\mathcal{A}}
be the general residual of a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
with respect to a general linear form

\ell
. Set

I=r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap I_{\ell^{\vee}}.

Then one has:

(a)

There is a minimal generating set of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
that contains
\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}
and

r_{\mathcal{A}}=(I,\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}).

(b)

The Hilbert function of
S/I
is

\displaystyle h_{S/I}(j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}h_{S/r_{\mathcal{A}}}(j)&\text{ if }j\leq|\mathcal{A}|-2;\\
h_{S/r_{\mathcal{A}}}(j)+1&\text{ if }j\geq|\mathcal{A}|-1.\end{cases}

(c)

The Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
I
is

\reg I=|\mathcal{A}|.

Proof.

For ease of notation, set
d=|\mathcal{A}|
,
f=f_{\mathcal{A}}
and
Q=\ell^{\vee}
.

We begin by establishing the following claim:

(5.2)

\displaystyle[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{j}=[I]_{j}\quad\text{ if }j\leq d-2,

which is the heart of the proof of the Proposition.

First, we reduce to the case where
n=2
. We use the notation introduced in
Section

3
.
Assume
n\geq 3
and fix a general linear form
y
in the ideal
I_{Q}
. Denote by

\bar{g}
the image of
g\in S
in the subring
R\cong S/yS
of
S
and similarly for ideals of
S
. We may assume
y=x_{n}
.
Suppose on the contrary there is a polynomial

g\in[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{j}
with
j\leq d-2
that is not in
I
. The latter is true if and only if
g
is not in
I_{Q}
.
By the definition of the residual, the first part means that

g\cdot\Jac(f)\subseteq(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}).

Using
Lemma

3.3
,
this implies

\bar{g}\cdot\Jac(\bar{f})\subseteq(\bar{f},\frac{\partial\bar{f}}{\partial\bar{\ell}}).

It remains to show that
\bar{g}
is not in the ideal
I_{\bar{\ell}^{\vee}}
of
R
. Since
x_{n}
is in
I_{Q}
one has in
R
for the point
\bar{\ell}^{\vee}\in\Proj(R)
that
I_{\bar{\ell}^{\vee}}=\overline{I_{Q}}
. Hence
g\notin I_{Q}
implies
\bar{g}\notin I_{\bar{\ell}^{\vee}}
, as desired. Using induction on
n
, it follows that it suffices to show Claim (
5.2
) if
n=2
.

Assume
n=2
. We begin by considering the cases where
d\in\{2,3\}
. If
S(\mathcal{A})
consists of one point,
P
, then
Proposition

4.6
gives that
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is a complete intersection of type
(1,d-1)
. In fact, taking also
Lemma

4.2
into account we get
r_{\mathcal{A}}=(\ell_{P},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})
with
\ell_{P}\in I_{Q}
. Since
\ell_{P}\in I_{Q}
, we get that
I=(\ell_{P},g)
for some multiple
g
of
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}
with degree
d
, and so
r_{\mathcal{A}}=(I,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})
as desired. The only other option is that
d=3
and
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is the ideal of three reduced points. Again, the computation is straightforward. So now we assume
d\geq 4
. We proceed in several steps.

(I) Note that any syzygy
(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2})^{T}
of
\Jac(f)
gives rise to a syzygy of the ideal
(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}})
because

h_{0}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}}+h_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}+h_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}=0

implies

(h_{0}x_{2})\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}}+(h_{1}x_{2})\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}+h_{2}(x_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}})=0.

Thus, we get a graded homomorphism of
S
-modules

\varphi\colon\Syz(\Jac(f))(-1)\to\Syz\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}\right),\;(h_{0},h_{1},h_{2})^{T}\mapsto(x_{2}h_{0},x_{2}h_{1},h_{2})^{T}.

As
S
is an integral domain,
\varphi
is injective.

(II) Choose two further general linear forms
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in S
. Since
\Jac(f)
has codimension two, the derivatives

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}
and
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}}
form a regular sequence in
S
. Furthermore, the fact that
(\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
implies that

(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}}):\ell_{2}=(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}}).

It follows that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}},\ell_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{2}}\right):\ell_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}}\right):\ell_{2}+\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{1}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Jac(f).

Changing coordinates, we assume
\ell=x_{0},\ \ell_{1}=x_{1}
and
\ell_{2}=x_{2}
. Note that then
I_{Q}=(x_{1},x_{2})
.
Thus, we get a short exact sequence of graded
S
-modules

(5.3)

0\to(S/\Jac(f))(-1)\to S/\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}\right)\to S/\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\right)\to 0.

A graded minimal free resolution of the module on the right-hand side is given by the Koszul complex:

0\to S(-2d+1)\to S^{2}(-d)\oplus S(-2d+2)\to S^{2}(-d+1)\oplus S(-1)\to S\to S/\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\right)\to 0.

Hence, the long exact
\Tor
sequence induced by Sequence (
5.3
) induces a homomorphism

\Tor_{1}^{S}(S/\Jac(f),K)(-1)\to\Tor_{1}^{S}(S/(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}),K)
,
which gives an isomorphism

[\Tor_{1}^{S}(S/\Jac(f),K)]_{j-1}\to[\Tor_{1}^{S}(S/\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}\right),K)]_{j}

in every degree
j
with
d<j<2d-2
. Combined with the injectivity of
\varphi
, we conclude that

(5.4)

\varphi\colon[\Syz(\Jac(f))(-1)]_{j-1}\to\big[\Syz\big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}\big)\big]_{j}\quad\text{ is an isomorphism if }d<j<2d-2.

(III) Assume on the contrary that there is a polynomial
g\in[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{j}
with
j\leq d-2
that is not in
I
, that is,
g
is not in
I_{Q}
. If
j<d-2
, then, for a general form
h\in S
of degree
d-2-j
, one also has
gh\in[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{d-2}\setminus I_{Q}
. Thus, we may assume there is some

g\in[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{d-2}\setminus I_{Q}.

Since
r_{\mathcal{A}}=(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}):\Jac(f)=(f,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}}):\Jac(f)
(because we have assumed
\ell=x_{0}
), there are homogeneous polynomials
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
such that

g\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}=s_{1}\cdot(df)+s_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}}.

Note that this implies
\deg s_{1}=d-3
and
\deg s_{2}=d-2
. Invoking the Euler relation, this yields

0=(s_{1}x_{0}+s_{2})\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}}+(s_{1}x_{1}-g)\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}+s_{1}\cdot x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}},

which corresponds to a syzygy of
\Syz\big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},x_{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}\big)
of degree
2d-3
. Using Isomorphism (
5.4
), it follows that
(s_{1}x_{0}+s_{2},s_{1}x_{1}-g,s_{1})
is in the image of
\varphi
. In particular, this gives that
s_{1}x_{1}-g
is a multiple of
x_{2}
, and so
g\in(x_{1},x_{2})=I_{Q}
, which is a contradiction to our assumption

g\in[r_{\mathcal{A}}]_{d-2}\setminus I_{Q}
. This concludes our argument for establishing Claim (
5.2
).

We now turn to the assertions of the statement and consider an arbitrary hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we know that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}
is not in
I_{Q}
. Combined with Fact (
5.2
), we conclude that the least degree of a form in
r_{\mathcal{A}}
that is not in
I
is
d-1
. Since
I_{Q}
is the ideal of a point we get that
[(I_{Q},\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell})]_{j}=[S]_{j}
if
j\geq d-1
.
It follows that

r_{\mathcal{A}}/I\cong(r_{\mathcal{A}}+I_{Q})/I_{Q}\cong(S/I_{Q})(-d+1).

This isomorphism shows that
r_{\mathcal{A}}/I
is a cyclic module that is generated by the image of
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\ell}
, which implies
r_{\mathcal{A}}=(I,\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
, as claimed in Part (a).
Since
\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}
is not in
I
, it can be chosen as part of a minimal generating set of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Moreover, we get an exact sequence of graded
S
-modules

(5.5)

0\to(S/I_{Q})(-d+1)\to S/I\to S/r_{\mathcal{A}}\to 0.

It yields Claim (b). Furthermore, it induces a long exact local cohomology sequence, which provides
isomorphisms

H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/I)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}})\quad\text{ if }i\geq 2

and an exact sequence

0\to H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/I_{Q})(-d+1)\to H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/I)\to H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/r_{\mathcal{A}})\to 0.

The largest degree

such that
[H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S/I_{Q})(-d+1)]_{j}
is not zero is
d-2
. Since
\reg S/r_{\mathcal{A}}=d-2
by
Corollary

5.3
, we obtain
\reg S/I=d-1
, as claimed in Part (c).
∎

(a).

Corollary 5.5
.

For any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
one has:

(a)

The general residual

with respect to

has a minimal generating set

with homogeneous polynomials

whose degree is at most

.

(b)

If

r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap I_{\ell^{\vee}}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{s},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell}\cdot I_{\ell^{\vee}}).

Proof.

Set

and

.

(a) Using that

by

has a minimal generating set

with homogeneous polynomials

whose degree is at most

. We have to show that we may assume that the polynomials

are in

. Since

has degree

this is automatically true if

for any

, because

5.4
(a). Assume that for some

and

6.
Residual-like ideals

In this section, we introduce a class of ideals, called residual-like ideals, that contains all general residuals. Residual-like ideals define particularly structured non-reduced schemes. It would be interesting to explore to what extend results on general residuals can be extended to any residual-like ideal. We leave this for future investigations. Here, we characterize for later use which residual-like ideals are actually general residuals.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defining a hyperplane arrangement
\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{A}}H
.
Recall that for any
P\in S(A)
, we denote by
t_{P}\geq 2

the number of hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
that contain
P
.
Let
\ell\in[S]_{1}
be a general linear form. By
Corollary

4.8
,
the general residual of the hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
with respect to
\ell
is

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1}),

where
\ell_{P}
is a linear form defining the linear span of
P
and
\ell^{\vee}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Below, we will consider ideals with a similar structure, which we codify now.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a non-empty finite set of codimension two linear subspaces and let
E=(e_{P})_{P\in Y}
be a sequence of positive integers. Choose a general point
Q\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
and denote by
\ell_{P}
a linear form defining the linear span of
P\in Y
and
Q
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Consider the ideal

(6.1)

I(Y,E)=\bigcap_{P\in Y}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{e_{P}}).

We refer to it as the
residual-like ideal determined by
Y
and
E
(with respect to
Q
)
.

Remark 6.2
.

Note that each of the ideals
(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{e_{P}})
is a complete intersection generated by two polynomials, of degree one and
e_{P}
, respectively. Thus, the right-hand side of Equation (
6.1
) is the unique minimal primary decomposition of
I(Y,E)
.

If
n=2
then
Y
is a finite set of points and the scheme defined by
I(Y,E)
is contained in the fat point scheme
\sum_{P\in Y}e_{P}P
. Algebraically, this means

\bigcap_{P\in Y}I_{P}^{e_{P}}\subseteq I(Y,E)\subseteq I_{Y}=\bigcap_{P\in Y}I_{P}.

Considering again any
n\geq 2
,
observe that
I(Y,E)
defines a locally complete intersection scheme, whereas
\sum_{P\in Y}e_{P}P
does not have this property in general.

If
|Y|=1
, then
I(Y,E)
is the general residual of any hyperplane arrangement consisting of
e_{P}+1
hyperplanes of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
containing the unique subspace
P\in Y
. If
Y
has at least two elements it is no longer true that every ideal
I(Y,E)
is a general residual.

Proposition 6.3
.

Consider
Y,E
and
Q
as in
Definition

6.1
. Assume
|Y|\geq 2
and denote by
\mathcal{L}
the set of hyperplanes of

\mathbb{P}^{n}
that are spanned by two distinct subspaces of
Y
. (Note that
\mathcal{L}
can be the empty set.) Set

d-1=\max\Big\{\sum_{P\in Y\cap H}e_{P}\;\mid\;H\in\mathcal{L}\Big\},

where
Y\cap H
denotes the subset of elements of
Y
that are contained in
H
(and not the scheme-theoretic intersection of
Y
and
H
) and
d=1
if
\mathcal{L}=\emptyset
.
Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The ideal
I(Y,E)
is the general residual of a hyperplane arrangement with respect to the linear form
Q^{\vee}
.

(ii)

The set

\mathcal{K}=\Big\{H\in\mathcal{L}\;\mid\;\sum_{P\in Y\cap H}e_{P}=d-1\Big\}

has cardinality
d
and, for each
P\in Y
, there are exactly
e_{P}+1
hyperplanes in
\mathcal{K}
that contain
P
. In this case,

I(Y,E)
is the general residual of the hyperplane arrangement determined by
\mathcal{K}
and
Y=S(\mathcal{K})
, and so
t_{P}=e_{P}+1
.

Proof.

In the argument, we will consider various sets of codimension two subspaces. Thus, for any such set
Y
, we write
\mathcal{L}(Y)
for the set of hyperplanes that are spanned by two distinct subspaces of
Y
.

We begin by showing that (i) implies (ii) using induction on the cardinality of
\mathcal{A}
.
Assume

I(Y,E)=r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1}),

for some hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
. Set
d=|\mathcal{A}|
.
Since we assume that
Y=S(\mathcal{A})
has at least two elements we get
d\geq 3
. Moreover, if
d=3
then the three hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
must be in general position, i.e.,
|S(\mathcal{A})|=3
and
t_{P}=2
for each
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
. It follows that
I(Y,E)
satisfies the conditions in (ii).

Now assume
d=|\mathcal{A}|\geq 3
. Let
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be any hyperplane that is not in
\mathcal{A}
. We have to show that the residual

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}
also satisfies the conditions in (ii). To distinguish multiplicities for
\mathcal{A}
and for
\mathcal{A}+H=\mathcal{A}\cup\{H\}
write

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1}).

Notice that
H
meets each of the
d
hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
in a codimension two subspace
P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)
. Moreover, by the definition of
t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}
, the hyperplane
H
intersects precisely
t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1
hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
in
P
. It follows that

\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap H}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1)=d.

Consider now any hyperplane
L\neq H
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. If
L
is in
\mathcal{A}
, then we apply the above argument to
(\mathcal{A}+H)-L
and obtain

\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1)=d.

Setting
e_{P}=t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1
, this shows that the set

\mathcal{K}=\Big\{L\in\mathcal{L}(S(\mathcal{A}+H))\;\mid\;\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L}e_{P}=d\Big\}

contains
\mathcal{A}+H
. To show equality
it remains to check that

\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1)<d

if
L\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a hyperplane that is not in
\mathcal{A}+H
. Consider the codimension two subspace
P^{\prime}=L\cap H
. If
P^{\prime}
is not in
S(\mathcal{A}+H)
then one has, for any
P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L
that
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
and
t_{P}=t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}
. Thus, the induction hypothesis gives

\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1)=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})\cap L}(t_{P}-1)<d-1.

If
P^{\prime}
belongs
S(\mathcal{A}+H)
then
t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}=2
if
P
is not in
S(\mathcal{A})
and
t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}=t_{P}+1
otherwise. In both cases we get

\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A}+H)\cap L}(t_{P,\mathcal{A}+H}-1)\leq 1+\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})\cap L}(t_{P}-1)<1+d-1=d,

as desired.

We now turn to the reverse implication (ii) implies (i). Since each
e_{P}
is positive, Condition (ii) gives that every
P\in Y
is contained in

e_{P}+1\geq 2
hyperplanes of
\mathcal{K}
, and so
P
is in
S(\mathcal{K})
and
e_{P}+1=t_{P,\mathcal{K}}
. It also follows that
Y\subseteq S(\mathcal{K})
. We need to show equality.
To this end, assume
S(\mathcal{K})-Y
is not empty and set
e_{P^{\prime}}=t_{P^{\prime},\mathcal{K}}-1\geq 1
for
P^{\prime}\in S(\mathcal{K})-Y
.
Let
H\in K
be a hyperplane containing
P^{\prime}
.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle 1\leq e_{P^{\prime}}\leq\sum_{P\in(S(\mathcal{K})-Y)\cap H}e_{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{K})\cap H}e_{P}-\sum_{P\in Y\cap H}e_{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in S(\mathcal{K})\cap H}(t_{P,\mathcal{K}}-1)-(d-1)

\displaystyle=0,

where we used (ii) to get the equality in the second line and applied the first implication to
\mathcal{K}
to see the last equality. This contradiction proves that
Y=S(\mathcal{K})
.
Applying now
Corollary

4.8
to the hyperplane arrangement determined by
\mathcal{K}
, we see that the general residual
r_{\mathcal{K}}
of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to
Q^{\vee}
equals
I(Y,E)
.
∎

Recall that in
Corollary

5.3
we determined the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of any general residual. It would be interesting to establish an upper bound on the regularity of an arbitrary residual-like ideal, which is sharp for any general residual.

7.
Bounding the degree of the Jacobian ideal

For a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, the global Tjurina number
\tau(C)
is a measure of its singularities. In particular, one might ask what is the maximum possible Tjurina number given the degree of the curve, and in the case of line arrangements one can also ask for the minimum. This problem was studied by Du Plessis and Wall in
[
6
]
in general, and by Beorchia and Miró-Roig
[
2
]
for conic-line arrangements. In both cases, it was observed that if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})
consists of
d
concurrent lines then
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is a complete intersection and
\tau(\mathcal{A})=(d-1)^{2}
is the maximum possible. After this case,
[
2
]
showed that for conic-line arrangements, there is a gap:
\tau(C)\leq(d-1)(d-2)+1
, and they classify the conic-line arrangements for which equality holds. In particular, one possibility is a line arrangement (no conics) with
d-1
of the lines concurrent. In this section we continue the study of possible Tjurina numbers for line arrangements. We establish that there is another gap in the range of possible
\tau(\mathcal{A})
. We also provide new arguments for the quoted results of du Plessis and Wall and of Beorchia and Miró-Roig, and we establish a lower bound.

For any hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the global Tjurina number
\tau(\mathcal{A})
is equal to the degree of the Jacobian ideal
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
. Thus, combined with our results on restrictions of Jacobian ideals in Section
3
, the result on line arrangements implies an analogous statement for arbitrary hyperplane arrangements in any projective space, which we state as Corollary
7.3
.

The following estimates comprise the main result of this section:

Theorem 7.1
.

For any arrangement
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})
of
d\geq 3
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, one has:

(a)

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\geq\binom{d}{2},

and equality is true if and only if no three lines of
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent.

(b)

If the
d
lines of
\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent then

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\leq d^{2}-3d+3.

Furthermore, equality is true if and only if
d-1
lines, but not
d
lines of
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent.

(c)

If
\mathcal{A}
does not have a subset of
d-1
concurrent lines (hence in particular
d\geq 4
) then

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\leq d^{2}-4d+7.

Moreover, if
d\geq 5
then equality is true if and only if
\mathcal{A}
consists of
d-2
concurrent lines and the two other lines meet in a point which is on one of the
d-2
lines.

Remark 7.2
.

(i)
Part (b) corresponds to the mentioned results by Du Plessis and Wall (see
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
and Beorchia and Miró-Roig (see
[
1
, Theorem 3.5]
and also
[
4
, Proposition 4.7]
,
[
7
, Proposition 1.1]
).

(ii)
There is not another immediate gap in the range of degrees of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
below the bound given in (c). Indeed, if
\mathcal{A}
consists of
d-2
concurrent lines and two more generic lines then

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))=d^{2}-4d+6.

Proof of
Theorem

7.1
.

We argue by using the general residual
r_{\mathcal{A}}
of
\mathcal{A}
. Since

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=d(d-1)-\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))

it is enough to show the corresponding bounds for
r_{\mathcal{A}}
. Recall that by
Corollary

4.10
, we know for any
hyperplane
H=V(x)
that is not in
\mathcal{A}
that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+|\mathcal{A}\cap H|\leq\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+|\mathcal{A}|,

where

\mathcal{A}\cap H=\{H^{\prime}\cap H\;\mid\;H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

In particular,
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|
counts the points in the support of the scheme
(\bigcup_{H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}H^{\prime})\cap H
(i.e. it ignores multiplicity).
Moreover, every primary component of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
is supported at a point and its degree is one less than the number of lines in
\mathcal{A}
passing through this point (see
Proposition

4.6
).

We begin by establishing the lower bound on the degree of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Theorem

5.2
gives that
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
does not contain any nonzero polynomial of degree
d-2
.
Since the Hilbert function of a zerodimensional scheme
X
is strictly increasing until it is equal to the degree of
X
it
follows that

\displaystyle\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))=\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat})

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{K}[S/\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat}]_{d-2}

\displaystyle=\dim_{K}[S]_{d-2}=\binom{d}{2},

as claimed.
To discuss sharpness of this bound assume
\mathcal{A}
contains three concurrent lines. The generic residual to the arrangement of these three lines alone has degree two. Thus, adding one line at a time, repeated application of Inequality
7.1
gives

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}\leq 2+3+4+\cdots+d-2+d-1=\binom{d}{2}-1,

which yields
\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\geq\binom{d}{2}+1
and so completes the proof of Claim (a)

We now consider the upper bounds for
\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))
. We use induction on the cardinality
d
of
\mathcal{A}
and denote by
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
a line that is not in
\mathcal{A}
. The following statements play a key role in our arguments.

Claim
:

(1)

If
|S(\mathcal{A})|\geq 2
and
H\notin\mathcal{A}
then
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|\geq 2
.

(2)

Equality is true in (
1
) if and only if the following holds:
\mathcal{A}
is a disjoint union
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
of nonempty subsets
\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
of concurrent lines, with at least two lines in one of the subsets, say
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, and if
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}|=1
then
H
does not contain the center of the pencil
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
(We refer to this configuration of
\mathcal{A}
as a
disconnected 2-pencil
.)

Indeed, notice that
|S(\mathcal{A})|=1
if and only if the
d
lines of
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent. Moreover,
|S(\mathcal{A})|\geq 2
forces
d\geq 3
. Hence,
H
meets
\mathcal{A}
in at least two distinct points, say,
P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
. If there is a line of
\mathcal{A}
that is not passing through either
P^{\prime}
or
P^{\prime\prime}
then
H
meets this line, and so
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|\geq 3
. Therefore, the condition
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|=2
forces that every line of
\mathcal{A}
passes through one of the points
P^{\prime}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
.
It follows that
\mathcal{A}
is a disconnected 2-pencil, which shows that (1) implies (2). The reverse implication is clear.

Note that Assertion (b) in the statement of the theorem is equivalent to the following statement:

Claim (b’):
If the lines in
\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent then

(7.2)

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}\geq 2|\mathcal{A}|-3,

and equality if true if and only if
|\mathcal{A}|-1
lines of
\mathcal{A}
, but not all lines of
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent.

We use induction on
d=|\mathcal{A}|\geq 3
to show Claim (b’). If
d=3
then, by assumption,
S(\mathcal{A})
consists of three distinct points and
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=3
, and so Inequality (
7.2
) is an equality.

Let
d\geq 3
and denote by
H
any line that is not in
\mathcal{A}
. As above, we write
\mathcal{A}+H
for
\mathcal{A}\cup\{H\}
. We want to show Claim (b’) for
\mathcal{A}+H
assuming it is true of
\mathcal{A}
.
We consider two cases.

Case 1
: Assume the lines in
\mathcal{A}
are concurrent. It follows that
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=d-1
. By assumption, the lines in
\mathcal{A}+H
are not concurrent, that is,
H
meets
\mathcal{A}
in
d
distinct points. Hence, we get from Equation (
7.1
)

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=d-1+d=2|\mathcal{A}+H|-3,

as claimed.

Case 2
:The lines in
\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent. Thus, the induction hypothesis gives

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}\geq 2d-3.

Using the above Claim and Equation (
7.1
), we get

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}\geq 2d-3+2=2|\mathcal{A}+H|-3,

establishing the asserted lower bound. Moreover, equality is true if and only if
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=2d-3
and
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|=2
. The former condition forces that
\mathcal{A}
contains a pencil of
d-1

H
must pass through the center of this pencil. Hence
\mathcal{A}+H
contains a pencil of
|\mathcal{A}+H|-1

We proceed similarly to establish Claim (c). Note that it is equivalent to the following statement:

Claim (c’):
If
\mathcal{A}
does not have a subset of
|\mathcal{A}|-1
concurrent lines then

(7.3)

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}\geq 3|\mathcal{A}|-7.

Moreover, if
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 5
then equality is true if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is the union of a pencil of
|\mathcal{A}|-2\geq 3
concurrent lines and a pencil of three concurrent lines. (Observe that this forces that the line that is spanned by the centers of the pencils belongs to
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, we refer to this line arrangement as a
connected 2-pencil
.)

Again, we use induction of
d=|\mathcal{A}|
. Notice that the assumption forces
d\geq 4
. Assume
d=4
. Thus, one has
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=6
if
\mathcal{A}
does not contain three concurrent lines and
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=5
otherwise.

Let
H
be a line not in
\mathcal{A}
. We now consider two cases.

Case 1
: If
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=6

\mathcal{A}
pass through any point of

. It follows that
\mathcal{A}\cap H

consists of two points if and only if
H

. In this case, one has

In any other case,

contains at least three points. Hence, Equation (

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}\geq 6+3>8=3|\mathcal{A}+H|-7.

Thus, the lower bound in Claim (c’) is satisfied but never sharp.

Case 2
: Assume
\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=5
. Thus,
\mathcal{A}
has a subset
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
of three concurrent lines meeting in a point

. It follows that

consists of two points if and only if

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}\geq 5+3=3|\mathcal{A}+H|-7,

, which means that

. In this case,

is the desired connected 2-pencil.
This completes the argument for Claim (c’) if

.

Assume now

. Again, we consider two cases.

Case 1
: Assume
\mathcal{A}
contains a subset of
d-1
concurrent lines meeting in a point

. Then we know by Claim (b’),

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}=2d-3.

By assumption on

that pass through any point

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}\geq 2d-3+d-1=3|\mathcal{A}+H|-7.

Furthermore, equality is true if and only if

, which means that

is the desired connected 2-pencil.

Case 2
: Assume
\mathcal{A}
has no subset of
d-1

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}\geq 3d-7,

and equality is true if and only if
\mathcal{A}

3

. We consider two subcases.

Case 2.1
: If

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+|\mathcal{A}\cap H|\geq 3d-7+3=3|\mathcal{A}+H|-7.

Case 2.2

As promised,

gives a general result for hyperplane arrangements.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(f_{\mathcal{A}})
be an arrangement of
d\geq 3
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and let
S(\mathcal{A})
be the top support of the singular locus of
\mathcal{A}
. One has:

(a)

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\geq\binom{d}{2},

and equality is true if and only if no three hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
share a common element of
S(\mathcal{A})
.

(b)

If the
d
hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
do not all share a common element of
S(\mathcal{A})
then

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\leq d^{2}-3d+3.

Furthermore, equality is true if and only if
d-1
hyperplanes, but not
d
hyperplanes, of
\mathcal{A}
share an element of
S(\mathcal{A})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{A}
does not have a subset of
d-1
hyperplanes sharing an element of
S(\mathcal{A})
then

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\leq d^{2}-4d+7.

Moreover, if
d\geq 5
then equality is true if and only if
\mathcal{A}
consists of
d-2
hyperplanes sharing an element of
S(\mathcal{A})

and two other hyperplanes, which share an element of
S(\mathcal{A})
with one of the first
d-2
hyperplanes.

Proof.

Intersecting
\mathcal{A}
with a general 2-dimensional linear space
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
gives a line arrangement
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
in
\Lambda
and a bijection between
S(\mathcal{A})
and
S(\overline{\mathcal{A}})
. Applying

Proposition

3.6

n-2
times, we obtain the top-dimensional part of the Jacobian ideal of
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
. The result follows since the degree of a subscheme is preserved under general hyperplane sections.
∎

We record the following consequence.

Corollary 7.4
.

The Jacobian
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
of any hyperplane arrangement determined by
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a complete intersection if and only if all hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
contain a subspace
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension
n-2
.

Proof.

If all hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
contain a subspace
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension
n-2
then the minimal generating set of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
consists of two polynomials, and so
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is a complete intersection.

For the reverse implication, assume there is no such subspace
\Lambda
. Note that this forces
d=|\mathcal{A}|\geq 3
. Hence,
Theorem

7.1
gives

\deg(\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}}))\leq d^{2}-3d+3<(d-1)^{2},

which shows that
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
cannot be a complete intersection generated by two forms of degree
d-1
.
∎

8.
Minimal free resolutions for certain arrangements

The goal of this section is to show that there are large classes of hyperplane arrangements for which the graded Betti numbers of the general residual and of the top-dimensional part of the Jacobian ideal are combinatorially determined. Our methods use liaison-theoretic techniques.

Given a hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
of
d
hyperplanes, in
[
14
]
, we used a basic double link to describe the change of the top-dimensional parts of the Jacobian ideals when passing from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}+H
, where
H
is a generic hyperplane, that is,
H
intersects the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
in
d
distinct codimension two subspaces. Here, we show that the change of general residuals can be described similarly. A key new insight is that this latter description is in fact true in some greater generality, namely for any
almost generic
hypeplane. It allows us to determine the graded Betti numbers of general residuals in many cases.
We then show that the graded Betti numbers of the top-dimensional part of the Jacobian ideal are determined by those of the general residual if the latter is Cohen-Macaulay. We illustrate our results by establishing explicit results for arrangements satisfying a mild condition, introduced in
[
15
]
. Then we consider some arrangements which do not satisfy this condition.

8.1.
Adding an almost generic hyperplane

We use the notation introduced in
Section

4
. The following result is a central tool for this section.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be any set of
d
hyperplanes. Consider a hyperplane
H
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
that is defined by a linear form
x\in S
and not in
\mathcal{A}
. If
H
contains at most one subspace
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
, then the general residual of
\mathcal{A}+H
is a basic double link of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, that is,

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(p),

where
p\in r_{\mathcal{A}}
is a form of degree
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|
,
\mathcal{A}\cap H
denotes the set of codimension two subspaces
H^{\prime}\cap H
with
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
and
x,p
is a regular sequence.

If a hyperplane
H
satisfies the condition in the above statement, we say that
H
is
almost generic
(with respect to
\mathcal{A}
). If
H
does not contain any subspace of
S(A)
then
H
is a called
generic
hyperplane (with respect to
\mathcal{A}
).

Proof of
Proposition

8.1
.

According to
Corollary

4.8
, we have

r_{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1}),

where
\ell_{P}
is a linear form defining the linear span of
P
and
\ell^{\vee}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. We consider two cases.

First assume that
H
is a generic hyperplane for
\mathcal{A}
. Then, we get
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|=|\mathcal{A}|=d
and

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{A}\cap H}I_{P}.

Since
S(\mathcal{A})\cap H=\emptyset
by the genericity of
H
, for each
P\in\mathcal{A}\cap H
there is a unique hyperplane in
\mathcal{A}
containing
P
. This hyperplane is defined by a linear divisor of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
. It follows that

\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{A}\cap H}I_{P}=(x,f_{\mathcal{A}}).

Since
x,f_{\mathcal{A}}
is a regular sequence and
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is in
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, the basic double link
x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is unmixed and has degree
d+\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}
. It is contained in

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap(x,f_{\mathcal{A}}).

Comparing degrees we conclude that
r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(f_{\mathcal{A}})
, as desired.

Second, assume there is unique subspace of
S(\mathcal{A})
that is contained in
H
. Denote it by
Q
.
We begin with the special case where
|S(\mathcal{A})|=1
. Thus, by
Corollary

4.8
,

r_{\mathcal{A}}=(\ell_{Q},I_{Q}^{d-1}),

and since
x\in I_{Q}
as
Q
is in
H
by assumption, we get

(\ell_{Q},I_{Q}^{d})=r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(\ell_{Q})

as desired.

Now assume
|S(\mathcal{A})|\geq 2
.
Define ideals

\mathfrak{a}=\bigcap_{P\in S(\mathcal{A})-Q}(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1})

and

\mathfrak{b}=(\ell_{Q},I_{Q}^{t_{Q}-1})=(\ell_{Q},g_{Q}),

where
g_{Q}
is the product of
t_{Q}-1
distinct linear divisors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that each define a hyperplane containing
Q
. Thus,
r_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{b}
.

Using
Corollary

4.8
again, we get

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\mathfrak{a}\cap(\ell_{Q},xg_{Q})\cap\bigcap_{P\in(\mathcal{A}\cap H)-Q}I_{P}.

For each
P\in(\mathcal{A}\cap H)-Q
, there is exactly one linear divisor of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that defines a hyperplane containing
P
. Hence
I_{P}
is generated by this divisor and
x
. Denote by
h
the product of these divisors. Then it follows that

\bigcap_{P\in(\mathcal{A}\cap H)-Q}I_{P}=(x,h),

where the degree of
h
is
|\mathcal{A}\cap H|-1
. Notice the following containment

(\ell_{Q},xg_{Q})\cap(x,h)\supseteq(x\ell_{Q},xg_{Q},\ell_{Q}h)=x\cdot\mathfrak{b}+(\ell_{Q}h).

Since
x,\ell_{Q}h
is a regular sequence and
\ell_{Q}h\in\mathfrak{b}
, the ideal on the right-hand side is a basic double link. So it is unmixed of codimension two with degree
\deg\mathfrak{b}+\deg\ell_{Q}h=|\mathcal{A}\cap H|+t_{Q}-1
, which is equal to the degree of the unmixed ideal on the left-hand side. Hence, the two ideals are equal, and we obtain

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\big(x\cdot\mathfrak{b}+(\ell_{Q}h)\big).

By definition of
h
, we have
f_{\mathcal{A}}=h\cdot q
, where
q
is the product of linear divisors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that define hyperplanes containing
Q
. Consider any
P\in S(\mathcal{A})-Q
. There are
t_{P}
linear divisors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that define hyperplanes containing
P
. At most one of them also contains
Q
. Hence, there are at least
t_{P}-1
linear divisors of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
that do not have the latter property, and so these divisors must divide
h
. It follows that
h
is in
(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-1})
for every
P\in S(\mathcal{A})-Q
, and hence
h\in\mathfrak{a}
. Since
\ell_{Q}
is in
\mathfrak{b}
, we get

r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\big(x\cdot\mathfrak{b}+(\ell_{Q}h)\big)\supseteq x\cdot(\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{b})+(\ell_{Q}h)=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(\ell_{Q}h),

where
\ell_{Q}h
is in
r_{\mathcal{A}}
and
x,\ell_{Q}h
are a regular sequence. Hence the ideal on the right-hand side is a basic double link of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
, and so it is unmixed and its degree is

\deg\big(x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(\ell_{Q}h)\big)=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+\deg h+1.

By
Corollary

4.10
, the unmixed ideal on the left-hand side has degree

\deg r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+|\mathcal{A}\cap H|=\deg r_{\mathcal{A}}+\deg h+1.

Comparing degrees, we conclude that the two ideals are equal, that is,
r_{\mathcal{A}+H}=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}}+(\ell_{Q}h)
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 8.8

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{A}(fg)}=r_{\mathcal{A}(f)}\cap r_{\mathcal{A}(g)}\cap\underbrace{\left[\bigcap_{i,j}(f_{i},g_{j})\right]}_{=\ (f,g)}.

(c)

\oplus\\

is a graded minimal free resolution of

, where

,

,

Proof.

Observe that Condition (i) means that no subspace

is contained in any hyperplane of

\mathcal{A}(f)

are the same in

and the analogous statement is true for any

proves Claim (a).

We know that

are relatively prime by Conditions (i) and (ii). Thus, Claim (b) is a consequence of Liaison Addition. It also follows that there is a short exact sequence

Proposition 8.17
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be an arrangement consisting of
d
hyperplanes. Assume that
\mathcal{A}
can be built up recursively by adding almost generic hyperplanes, that is, starting with an arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{2}
consisting of two hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
, produce an arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{3}
by adding to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
a hyperplane
H_{2}
of
\mathcal{A}
that is almost generic with respect to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, and continue such that

\mathcal{A}_{d}=\mathcal{A}
. Then
S/r_{\mathcal{A}}
and
S/\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
are Cohen-Macaulay and their
Hilbert functions
are uniquely determined and can be computed explictly from the knowledge of the numbers
|\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap H_{i}|
for
i=2,3,\ldots,d-1
, where
\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap H_{i}
denotes the set of codimension two subspaces
H\cap H_{i}
with
H\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Proof.

The general residual of
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is a complete intersection, and so we know its Hilbert function. By
Proposition

8.1
, the general residual of a hyperplane arrangement that is obtaining by adding an almost generic hyperplane to a given arrangement is a specific basic double link of the general residual of the original arrangement. The gives the claimed Cohen-Macaulayness results. For considering Hilbert functions, fixing
i
and avoiding some indices, one has

r_{\mathcal{A}_{i+1}}=x\cdot r_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}+(p),

where
x
defines
H_{i}
and
\deg p=|\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap H_{i}|
. Using the short exact sequence

0\to S(-1-\deg p)\to S(-\deg p)\oplus r_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(-1)\to r_{\mathcal{A}_{i+1}}\to 0,

it follows that the Hilbert function of
r_{\mathcal{A}_{i+1}}
can be computed from knowing the Hilbert function of
r_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}
and
\deg p
. Using this repeatedly, the assertion follows for
r_{\mathcal{A}}
. We conclude for the Hilbert function of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{top}
using a standard fact from liaison theory, see, e.g,
[
16
, Theorem(iii)]
.
∎

Remark 8.18
.

Theorem

8.11
gives an explicit condition on a hyperplane arrangement, which guarantees that it can be built by adding almost generic hyperplanes, and so
Proposition

8.17
applies to it. This follows from the proof of
Theorem

8.11
. However,
Proposition

8.17
is applicable to many arrangements which do not satisfy the assumptions of
Theorem

8.11
. For instance, this is true for all the arrangements discussed in
Example

8.16
(ii)-(v).

Note that a more careful analysis often allows one to determine for an arrangement that can be obtained by adding almost generic hyperplanes not only the Hilbert function but also the graded Betti numbers as illusttrated in
Example

8.16
.

There are arrangements to which
Proposition

8.17
cannot be applied. One of the first such examples are Fermat arrangements. In
\mathbb{P}^{2}
these are defined by the polynomial
(x^{k}-y^{k})(y^{k}-z^{k})(z^{k}-x^{k})
. Such an arrangement has multiplicity
k
at each of the three coordinate points, but also it has triple points at all points of intersection of
(x^{k}-y^{k})
and
(y^{k}-z^{k})
since
z^{k}-x^{k}
is in the pencil defined by
(x^{k}-y^{k})
and
(y^{k}-z^{k})
.
Note however, that we can use the tools in this section to analyze many more arrangements than covered by
Proposition

8.17
. For example, using arrangements for which we know the graded Betti numbers of the general residuals as building blocks and using
Proposition

8.14
, one can determine the graded Betti numbers of the general residual of the resulting arrangement.

For instance, it is well-known that any Fermat arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
is free, and so we know the graded Bettti numbers of
r_{\mathcal{A}}
. Add to
\mathcal{A}
three generic lines. These three lines meet in three points. Now add almost generic lines passing to one of the three points.
Denote by
\mathcal{B}
the arrangement formed by the lines added to
\mathcal{A}
. In
Example

8.16
, we determined the graded Betti numbers of

. Hence,
Proposition

8.14
gives the graded Betti numbers of

by

8.8
.

9.
Resolutions of certain Milnor modules and of Jacobian ideals

The main result of this section shows that one can read off the graded Betti numbers of the Milnor module and the Jacobian ideal of any line arrangement from the graded Betti numbers of the saturation of the Jacobian ideal. As an application we show that these graded Betti numbers are combinatorially determined for any union of disconnected pencils.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be any line arrangement. Write the graded minimal free resolution of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat}
as

0\to G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}S^{b}(-d+1)\oplus F\to\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat}\to 0,

where
d=|\mathcal{A}|
. If
\mathcal{A}
is not free then
b\geq 3
and the Milnor module
M
and the Jacobian ideal have graded minimal free resolutions of the form

0\to S^{b-3}(-2d+2)\oplus F^{*}(-3d+3)\to G^{*}(-3d+3)\to G\to S^{b-3}(-d+1)\oplus F\to M\to 0

and

0\to S^{b-3}(-2d+2)\oplus F^{*}(-3d+3)\to G^{*}(-3d+3)\to S^{3}(-d+1)\to\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})\to 0.

Proof.

If
\Jac(\mathcal{A})
has two minimal generators then it is a complete intersection, and so
\mathcal{A}
is a free arrangement; a contradiction to our assumption. Hence,
\Jac(\mathcal{A})
has exactly three minimal generators of degree
d-1
. Thus,
Theorem

5.2
gives
b\geq 3
.

Define a graded
S
-module
E
as first syzygy module of
\Jac(\mathcal{A})
, and so we have an exact sequence

(9.1)

0\to E\to S^{3}(-d+1)\to\Jac(\mathcal{A})\to 0.

Note that
E
is a reflexive
S
-module of rank two and that the vector bundle
\widetilde{E}
has first Chern class
-3d+3
. Thus,
[
9
, Proposition 1.10]
implies

(9.2)

E^{*}\cong E(3d-3).

Consider the exact sequence

0\to\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})\to\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})^{sat}\to M\to 0.

Taking into account the cancellation due to the fact that the minimal generators of
\Jac(\mathcal{A})
are part of a minimal generating set of

\Jac(\mathcal{A})^{sat}
, the mapping cone provides the exact sequence

(9.3)

0\to E\to G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\longrightarrow}}S^{b-3}(-d+1)\oplus F\to M\to 0.

Notice that
\delta
is a minimal map because
\gamma
has this property by the assumed minimality of the given resolution of
\Jac(\mathcal{A})^{sat}
.

Consider a graded minimal free resolution of
E
over
S
,

0\to P_{2}\to P_{1}\to E\to 0.

Now we dualize the sequence (
9.3
). Since
M
has finite length we obtain the exact sequence

0\to F^{*}\oplus S^{b-3}(d-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta^{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}G^{*}\to E^{*}\to 0,

where
\delta^{*}
is a minimal map because its dual
\delta
has this property. Hence, this sequence is a graded minimal free resolution of
E^{*}
. Using the uniqueness of such a resolution up to isomorphism of complexes and
E^{*}\cong E(3d-3)
, it follows that there are graded isomorphisms

(9.4)

G^{*}\cong P_{1}(3d-3)\quad\text{ and }\quad F^{*}\oplus S^{b-3}(d-1)\cong P_{2}(3d-3).

Splicing the resolution of
E
and the Sequence (
9.3
), we get an exact sequence

(9.5)

0\to P_{2}\to P_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\longrightarrow}}G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\longrightarrow}}S^{b-3}(-d+1)\oplus F\to M\to 0,

which is a graded free resolution of
M
. We claim it is minimal. Otherwise, the map
\rho
is not minimal, and one can split off a free direct summand, say
S(-t)
. That means, one can write
\rho
as a direct sum of a map
\rho^{\prime}\colon P_{1}^{\prime}\to G^{\prime}
and an isomorphism

\iota\colon S(-t)\to S(-t)
, where
P_{1}=P_{1}^{\prime}\oplus S(-t)
and
G=G^{\prime}\oplus S(-t)
. It follows that

E=\ker\delta=\im\rho\cong S(-t)\oplus\im\rho^{\prime}.

Hence,
\im\rho^{\prime}
is a reflexive module of rank one, which implies that it is free, and so
E
is a free
S
-module as well. This is a contradiction to our assumption that
\mathcal{A}
is not a free arrangement. It shows that the Sequence (
9.5
) is a graded minimal free resolution of
M
. Taking into account the Isomorphisms (
9.4
), our first assertion follows.

We get the second assertion by splicing the free resolution of
E
and Sequence (
9.1
).
∎

Corollary 9.4

For any line arrangement

\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})

(ii)

\mathcal{A}

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}
is not free the assertion is implied by

by noting, using its notation, that we may assume

and so

if

Remark 9.5

10.
A freeness criterion

In this section, we consider only line arrangements. By combining earlier results, we establish a freeness criterion for line arrangements. In particular, it shows that a line arrangement is free if and only adding a certain point to its general residual gives a zero-dimensional subscheme that is the intersection of two hypersurfaces.

We recall the following result of Kreuzer.

Theorem 10.1

(
[
11
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

A
0
-dimensional subscheme
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is arithmetically Gorenstein if and only if there is some integer
a_{X}\geq 0
such that
h_{X}(j)=\deg X-h_{X}(a_{X}-j)
for any integer
j
and, for any subscheme
Y\subset X
with degree
-1+\deg X
, one has
h_{Y}(a_{X})=\deg X-1
.

The conditions on the subschemes
Y
is the statement is exactly saying that
X
satisfies the Cayley-Bacharach property.

By
Corollary

7.4
, we know that the Jacobian of a line arrangement is a complete intersection if and only if the lines are concurrent. Thus, it is harmless to rule out this case in the following definition and theorem. Recall that an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is said to be free with
exponents

e_{1}
and
e_{2}
if the syzygy module of
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is a free
S
-module with two generators of degree
d-1+e_{1}
and
d-1+e_{2}
, respectively.

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of
d
distinct lines of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Assume the lines in
\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent.
Let
\ell
be a general linear form and let
r_{\mathcal{A}}
be the general residual with respect to
\ell
.
For each point
P\in S(\mathcal{A})
, consider the ideal

\mathfrak{a}_{P}=(\ell_{P},I_{P}^{t_{P}-2})\cap\bigcap_{P^{\prime}\in S(\mathcal{A})-P}(\ell_{P^{\prime}},I_{P^{\prime}}^{t_{P^{\prime}}-1}).

Define the ideal

I=r_{\mathcal{A}}\cap I_{\ell^{\vee}}.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

The line arrangement determined by
\mathcal{A}
is free with exponents
a-1
and
b-1
.

(b)

The ideal
I

is generated by two forms of degree
a
and
b

a+b=d+1
and

.

(c)

The

is symmetric, i.e., for any integer

satisfies

h_{S/I}(d-2-j)=\deg I-h_{S/I}(j),

and, for any point

h_{S/\mathfrak{a}_{P}\cap I_{\ell^{\vee}}}(d-2)=\deg I-1.

Proof.

Our assumption that the lines in
\mathcal{A}
are not concurrent gives that

.

We begin by showing the equivalence of (a) and (b). First assume (a). Using
Corollary

7.4
, we know that
\Jac(f_{\mathcal{A}})
is minimally generated by the three partial derivatives of
f_{\mathcal{A}}
. It is saturated. by the assumed freeness of
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, it has a graded minimal free resolution of the form

satisfying
a+b=d-1
, and so

. Linking by
(f_{\mathcal{A}},\frac{\partial f_{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial\ell})
and taking into account
the cancellation due to the fact that

is a minimal generator of both

and the complete intersection, the mapping cone shows that the general residual has a graded minimal free resolution of the form

Taking into account that

we rewrite this as

By

(a), we may assume that the minimal generator of

with degree

is

and

with degrees

and

, respectively.
Using the above resolution, a computation shows that

The Sequence (

(10.1)

\displaystyle\begin{CD}00\\

Using the Horseshoe lemma, it implies that a graded minimal free resolution of
