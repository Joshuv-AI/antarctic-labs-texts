Understanding HBV immunopathogenesis to inform future immune-based therapies
Authors: Margaux Bossis, Phuong-Ha Le, Tarik Asselah, Thomas F. Baumert, Pierre Tonnerre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide, with more than 250 million people living with chronic infection despite effective prophylactic vaccination. While current treatments provide durable suppression of viral replication and reduce liver-related complications, they rarely achieve functional cure, defined as sustained hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss after treatment cessation. Viral persistence is maintained by intrahepatic covalently closed circular DNA and HBV DNA integration, which sustain antigen production and contribute to immune dysfunction. This review summarizes current understanding of HBV immunopathogenesis across acute resolution and chronicity, emphasizing the compartmentalized and tolerogenic liver microenvironment, impaired innate sensing, and progressive dysfunction of HBV-specific B- and T-cell responses, including exhaustion phenotypes shaped by antigen specificity and intrahepatic priming. We also discuss immune correlates associated with functional cure, including partial restoration of polyfunctional HBV-specific T cells and intrahepatic immune remodeling. Finally, we discuss human clinical trials of immune-based therapies highlighting emerging combination strategies designed to couple antigen reduction with immune restauration, defined as the recovery of functional HBV-specific antiviral responses. Collectively, preclinical and clinical data suggest that achieving durable off-treatment control will likely require approaches that address both viral replication, antigen burden and HBV-specific immune responses.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health challenge, significantly contributing to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), despite the availability of a very efficient prophylactic vaccine (Keating and Noble, 2003). As of 2022, an estimated 254 million people were living with chronic hepatitis B (CHB), and HBV-related complications account for nearly 83% of the 1.3 million annual deaths from viral hepatitis (Global hepatitis report, 2024). Although effective prophylactic vaccines have reduced new infections, particularly in low-endemic areas, transmission persists in high-burden regions of sub-Saharan Africa and East Asia (Dunn et al., 2022). Vertical transmission remains the primary route of infection, with over 90% of perinatally infected infants developing chronic infection due to immune immaturity and tolerance to HBV antigens (Yang and Kao, 2016).

At the virological level, HBV is a small, partially double-stranded DNA virus of the Hepadnaviridae family that infects hepatocytes. HBV itself is considered as non-cytopathic and liver injury results primarily from the host immune response directed against infected hepatocytes rather than from direct viral toxicity (Tsukuda and Watashi, 2020).

The clinical outcome of HBV infection – spontaneous clearance or chronicity – is largely determined by the quality, timing, and coordination of the host immune response (Ferrari, 2015; Tan et al., 2015). Acute, self-limited infections are characterized by robust, polyfunctional antiviral responses that effectively eliminate infected hepatocytes without causing excessive liver damage. In contrast, chronic HBV infection results from a failure to mount or maintain effective immunity, leading to viral persistence, immune exhaustion, and progressive liver pathology (Tan et al., 2015). The pathophysiology of CHB thus reflects a dual-edged process in which the immune system is both insufficient to clear the virus and simultaneously responsible for ongoing liver inflammation and tissue damage.

The immunological environment of the liver further shapes these outcomes. Constant exposure to dietary and microbial antigens from the gut maintains a tolerogenic milieu that HBV capitalizes on by dampening pattern-recognition receptor signaling and interferon pathways through viral proteins, thereby avoiding early innate detection (Kuipery et al., 2020).

Current antiviral therapies, such as nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs), effectively suppress viral replication and prevent disease progression (Nasser et al., 2023). However, they rarely achieve “functional cure,” defined as sustained loss of HBsAg and undetectable HBV DNA in serum after therapy cessation. The persistence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) in hepatocytes and the integration of viral DNA into the host genome maintain viral antigen production, even under antiviral therapy, and perpetuate immune dysfunction. Consequently, there is an unmet need for innovative therapeutic strategies, encompassing both direct acting antivirals and immune-based approaches to restore HBV-specific immune responses and enable durable immune control. Here we review the mechanisms of HBV-induced immune dysfunction and immunopathogenesis, describe their contribution to fibrosis and carcinogenesis, and discuss examples for immune-based therapies aiming to achieve functional cure.

Host immune responses to HBV

Acute, self-limiting HBV infection

In acute, self-limited HBV infection, the immune response unfolds in a coordinated and sequential manner. However, the extent to which HBV induces a robust innate immune response during the earliest phase of infection remains debated. In chimpanzee models, little intrahepatic transcriptional evidence of innate immune activation was detected during the initial phase of viral entry and expansion, supporting the concept of HBV as a “stealth” virus (Wieland et al., 2004). Nevertheless, innate immune cells, including NK cells, Kupffer cells, and dendritic cells —that produce pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-12, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and chemokines— are thought to contribute to shaping the hepatic immune environment and to support the development of effective adaptive responses (Rehermann and Thimme, 2019). Adaptive immunity is crucial for viral clearance. HBV-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) recognize infected hepatocytes via viral peptides presented on MHC class I molecules and exert antiviral activity through both cytolytic and non-cytolytic mechanisms (Fig. 1A and B). The latter, mediated by IFN-γ and TNF-α, suppresses viral transcription and replication without inducing cell death, limiting liver injury (Guidotti et al., 1999). CD4+ T helper cells provide essential support for CTLs and B cells (Manigold and Racanelli, 2007), promoting the development of high-affinity anti-HBs antibodies that neutralize circulating virions and prevent reinfection (Ferrari, 2015). Single-cell RNA sequencing of 243,000 cells from paired liver and blood samples across HBV phases (immune tolerant, active, acute recovery) revealed exhausted intrahepatic CD8+ T cells as hall-marks of immune-active disease, driven by interactions with regulatory CD4+ T cells and FCGR3A+ macrophages via HLA-I/CANX and LILRB pathways, while CX3CR1+GNLY+ central memory CD8+ T cells marked viral resolution (Zhang et al., 2023).

Animal studies, notably in chimpanzee and woodchuck models, have provided critical insights into the kinetics of immune activation and viral resolution. In these models, viral clearance correlates with broad, multispecific T-cell responses and minimal hepatocellular necrosis, highlighting the efficiency of coordinated immunity (Asabe et al., 2009; Thimme et al., 2003). Although transcriptomic analyses during the clearance phase demonstrate induction of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), chemokine signaling, and antigen presentation pathways, these responses appear temporally associated with T-cell-mediated viral control rather than early innate sensing (Wieland et al., 2004). Paradoxically, upon hepatocellular antigen recognition, effector HBV-specific CD8+ T cells produce IL-10, which enhances IL-2 responsiveness and prevents antigen-induced apoptosis, thereby promoting their survival and potentially contributing to liver immunopathology (Fioravanti et al., 2017).

Dysregulated immunity in CHB

In chronic HBV infection, immune responses are dampened both systemically and locally in the liver. While peripheral blood analyses reveal signs of immune dysfunction, key pathological mechanisms are compartmentalized within the liver microenvironment (Venzin et al., 2024).

Innate immune cells in chronic HBV patients often exhibit a dysfunctional phenotype. NK cells show reduced cytotoxicity and cytokine production, with altered expression of inhibitory receptors such as NKG2A and KIRs (Maini and Peppa, 2013). Monocytes and dendritic cells demonstrate impaired maturation and antigen presentation capacity (van der Molen et al., 2004). Hepatic innate cells, including Kupffer cells and intrahepatic NK cells, are exposed to persistent HBV antigen and immunosuppressive signals (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), leading to an anti-inflammatory or dysfunctional state (Faure-Dupuy et al., 2019; Yuan et al., 2017). Plasmacytoid dendritic cells exhibit defective IFN-α production, contributing to insufficient antiviral signaling (Shi et al., 2012). Furthermore, local expression of checkpoint molecules (PD-L1, Galectin-9) inhibits immune cell activation (Chen et al., 2007; Dey et al., 2024). In addition to impaired innate responses, CHB is also associated with quantitative and qualitative adaptive immune cell response abnormalities. HBV-specific B cells are rare and often dysfunctional, particularly those targeting HBsAg (Bert et al., 2020; Salimzadeh et al., 2018). Regulatory B cells (Bregs) are expanded in some patients and may contribute to immune suppression via IL-10 production (Das et al., 2012). Antibody responses are typically delayed or absent, contributing to HBsAg persistence (Burton et al., 2018). Additionally, the frequency of HBV-specific T cells in peripheral blood decreases sharply and is inversely proportional to the viral load detected (Boni et al., 2007). Moreover, the phenotype of these CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are significantly altered (Blank et al., 2019; Kurktschiev et al., 2014). Multiple mechanisms may contribute to HBV-specific T cell dysfunction, such as persistent viral antigen stimulation leading to expression of inhibitory molecules, including PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3, CTLA-4, and excessive immunosuppressive signals in the liver microenvironment. Unlike most organs, the liver’s unique vasculature allows direct hepatocyte-mediated priming of CD8+ T cells, which can induce a distinct differentiation program characterized by reduced effector function (Bénéchet et al., 2019). Recent work further indicates that intrahepatic CD8+ T-cell dysfunction cannot be fully explained by classical exhaustion alone. The “liver rheostat” model proposes that antigen presentation within the tolerogenic hepatic microenvironment actively calibrates CD8+ T-cell differentiation, generating graded functional states that balance antiviral activity with limitation of immunopathology (Bosch et al., 2024). Using HBV-specific CD4+ T cell receptor transgenic mice, Venzin et al. showed that CD4+ effector T cells can prevent or reverse hepatocellular priming–induced CD8+ T cell dysfunction through CD40–CD40L–mediated licensing of Kupffer cells. Licensed Kupffer cells produce IL-12 and IL-27, the latter restoring antiviral CD8+ T cell function and identifying IL-27 as a potential immunotherapeutic target in chronic HBV infection (Venzin et al., 2025).

Although the phenotype known as T cell dysfunction or “exhaustion” has been extensively studied, the variety of observed phenotypes and possible associated functions is still debated today (Blank et al., 2019; Kurktschiev et al., 2014; Masopust et al., 2025) (Fig. 1A–C). Intrahepatic T cells often exhibit deeper functional impairment than their circulating counterparts with upregulated TOX and other exhaustion markers (Montanari et al., 2022). Helper T cells are also compromised, with reduced proliferation, impaired cytokine support, and skewing toward regulatory phenotypes (Buschow and Jansen, 2021a). The loss of CD4+ T cell help contributes to ineffective CD8+ T cell responses and impaired B cell maturation (Buschow and Jansen, 2021b). Importantly, previous work has demonstrated that the degree of T cell exhaustion and the associated phenotypes vary depending on the specific HBV antigens targeted (Hoogeveen et al., 2019; Schuch et al., 2019). T cells directed against surface antigens (HBsAg) are often profoundly impaired or undetectable in chronic HBV infection, likely owing to a combination of persistent antigen exposure, functional exhaustion, and progressive deletion or contraction of HBs-specific responses (Hoogeveen et al., 2019; Le Bert et al., 2020). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of blood and liver fine needle aspirations (FNAs) from NUC-treated chronic HBV patients revealed that HBsAg levels minimally affect overall leukocyte composition but selectively modulate NK, CD8+ T, and memory B-cell subsets, challenging the notion of strong HBsAg-driven immunosuppression (Beudeker et al., 2024). TCR and BCR repertoire sequencing revealed that chronic hepatitis B patients with concurrent HBsAg and anti-HBs positivity exhibit distinct immune signature patterns and reduced receptor diversity, correlating with poorer clinical outcomes and unique immune microenvironmental alterations compared to recovered and healthy individuals (Liang et al., 2025). While detectable HBV-specific T cells remains diminished, particularly their capacities in producing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, polymerase-specific T cells retain some level of cytotoxicity to contain infection (Heim et al., 2024). Notably, single-cell analysis of liver samples from CHB patients revealed a liver-resident, polyclonal CD8+ T cell subset with a highly activated phenotype that mediates nonspecific, Fas ligand–driven hepatocyte killing, identifying a key immune mechanism underlying liver injury during chronic HBV infection (Nkongolo et al., 2023). These observations suggest a key role for T cells both in controlling viral replication and contributing directly or indirectly to the liver tissue damage.

Immune restoration and correlates of functional cure

In HBV chronic infection, only a minority of patients achieves functional cure either spontaneously or following prolonged antiviral therapy. Recent studies have reported partial restoration of HBV-specific immunity, with the re-emergence of polyfunctional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells capable of producing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 (Andreata and Iannacone, 2024; Hoogeveen et al., 2022; Narmada et al., 2024) (Fig. 1A and B). Intrahepatic analyses of FC subjects revealed remodeling of the innate immune response, accompanied by reduced PD-1 expression and enhanced memory differentiation of CD8+ T cells, as well as the emergence of CD4+ T cells exhibiting a cytotoxic transcriptomic signature, collectively suggesting immune reinvigoration against HBV (Narmada et al., 2024). However, persistent expression of exhaustion-associated transcriptional regulators such as TOX following HBsAg loss indicates that prior chronic antigen exposure may leave a durable molecular imprint, suggesting that immune restoration after chronic HBV infection reflects partial functional reprogramming rather than complete reversion to classical memory differentiation (Heim et al., 2020).

Clinical studies of finite NA therapy or pegylated interferon-based regimens have shown that decreasing HBsAg levels often precede immune recovery (Jiang et al., 2024; Tout et al., 2020; Yip et al., 2019). Importantly, international practice guidelines from EASL, AASLD, and APASL recognize quantitative HBsAg as a key biomarker associated with sustained response after NA discontinuation, although no universal stopping threshold (e.g., <100 IU/mL) is formally recommended across all settings (Cornberg et al., 2025; Kao et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2025). In a longitudinal study of chronic hepatitis B patients undergoing NA therapy discontinuation, immune profiling revealed that the presence of functional HBV-specific PD-1+ T cells during viral suppression was associated with sustained post-treatment control (Rivino et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings suggest not only that reducing antigen burden may be a prerequisite for effective immune reconstitution and functional cure, but also that the quality and preservation of virus-specific T-cell responses during therapy critically determine durable immune control after treatment withdrawal.

HBV-induced fibrogenesis and liver disease progression

Chronic HBV infection drives a spatiotemporally dynamic dialogue between infected hepatocytes, immune cells, and stromal elements that progressively reshapes liver architecture, culminating in fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Persistent viral antigen stimulation sustains low-grade inflammation, recruiting innate and adaptive immune cells whose cytokines and oxidative stress both attempt viral control and promote hepatocyte death, matrix deposition, and oncogenic transformation.

Kupffer cells and monocyte-derived macrophages play central roles in this process. Kupffer cells (KCs) sense the virus through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLR2, triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL-1β) and chemokines like CCL2 (Durantel and Zoulim, 2012). CCL2 axis which recruits Ly6Chi monocytes to the liver; these monocytes differentiate into Ly6Chi macrophages that secrete profibrotic mediators such as TGF-β1 and PDGF, activating hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and promoting fibrogenesis (Boltjes et al., 2015; Li et al., 2012, 2015; Movita et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2021). Activated HSCs acquire a myofibroblast-like phenotype and secrete type I collagen, leading to extracellular matrix accumulation and architectural distortion (Mederacke et al., 2013; Seki and Brenner, 2015).

Dendritic cells (DCs) further modulate disease progression. Their frequency and functional capacity are diminished in CHB, with impaired expression of co-stimulatory molecules (OX40L and 4-1BBL) and reduced ability to prime T cells (Martinet et al., 2012; Ouaguia et al., 2019; Yonejima et al., 2019). In CHB, intrahepatic BDCA3+ DCs are increased but exhibit impaired maturation and reduced interferon-λ production, suggesting that while these antiviral DCs remain present in the liver and blood, their functional restoration may be essential for effective DC-based immunotherapy (Aa et al., 2016). HBV can directly interfere with TLR-triggered DC activation, further weakening adaptive immunity (Woltman et al., 2011). Although the functionality of DCs in CHB remains debated, most studies show that myeloid and plasmacytoid DCs retain normal frequency and function ex vivo, except for reduced IFN-α production by pDCs—which correlates with liver inflammation rather than viral load, suggesting minimal direct suppression by HBV antigens (Gehring and Ann D’Angelo, 2015).

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have provided un-precedented insight into the immune and fibrotic landscape in HBV-infected livers. A previous study used fine-needle aspirate (FNA)–based scRNA-seq to profile intrahepatic immunity in chronic HBV patients, revealing compartment-specific differences in which myeloid cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, show greater sensitivity to the tissue microenvironment than lymphocytes (Genshaft et al., 2023). Ramachandran et al. validate the longstanding importance of transdifferentiated HSCs as central mediators of hepatic fibrosis and present circulation-derived TREM2+ CD9+ macrophages as key regulators of the fibrotic niche (Ramachandran et al., 2019). More recently, by integrating MERFISH spatial mapping with single-nucleus RNA sequencing, Watson et al. delineates the zonal architecture and cell–cell interactions of the healthy and fibrotic human liver, revealing spatially distinct hepatocyte, macrophage, and mesenchymal subsets and the emergence of injury-associated hepatocytes lacking clear zonation (Watson et al., 2025). Although studies directly examining HBV-specific fibrogenesis remain limited, these findings collectively suggest that HBV-driven liver injury shares core mechanisms with other liver fibrotic diseases and represents a spatially and temporally dynamic crosstalk between infected hepatocytes and immune cells.

Immune-mediated contribution to hepatocarcinogenesis in chronic HBV infection

Chronic HBV infection is a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide and accounts for nearly half of all HCC cases (Chan et al., 2025). The mechanisms linking chronic HBV infection to carcinogenesis are multifactorial, involving direct oncogenic effects of the virus and indirect consequences of long-standing inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis.

Persistent inflammation caused by ineffective antiviral immunity is a major driver of liver oncogenesis. Cytotoxic T cells, Kupffer cells, and macrophages in HCC release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), inducing DNA damage, mutations, and malignant transformation in hepatocytes (Yuan et al., 2025). Continuous cycles of hepatocyte death and compensatory regeneration favor clonal expansion of preneoplastic cells (Hernandez–Gea et al., 2013). The fibrotic extracellular matrix provides a stiffened scaffold that alters cellular signaling and facilitates malignant transformation (Nouri et al., 2025). Cytokines such as IL-6, TGF-β, and TNF-α, produced by both immune and stromal cells, activate oncogenic STAT3 and SMAD pathways (Kim and Seki, 2025). Comprehensive immune profiling using mass and flow cytometry revealed that HBV infection alters intrahepatic leukocyte function in a disease stage–dependent manner, with high HBsAg levels linked to reduced T cell exhaustion, whereas HBV-associated HCC exhibits profound leukocyte dysfunction marked by elevated PD-1, TIM-3, CTLA-4, and TOX expression, highlighting key immune features relevant to prognostic assessment and immunotherapy development (Pan et al., 2024).

Recent advancements in single-cell omics, particularly through scRNA-seq, have significantly enhanced our understanding of intertumor and intratumor heterogeneities in liver diseases. Using scRNA-seq coupled with assembled T cell receptor (TCR) sequences, Zheng et al. revealed that specific T cell subsets such as exhausted CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) are preferentially enriched and clonally expanded in HCC (Zheng et al., 2017). Exhausted CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells cluster around tumor margins, while tumor-associated macrophages expressing VEGF and NOTCH ligands create a “fetal-like” endothelial microenvironment that supports angiogenesis (Lim et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2017). Infiltration of double-positive CD4+/CD8+ T cells with high PD-1 and ICOS expression has been associated with favorable prognosis, suggesting that residual antiviral immunity can restrain tumor progression (Zheng et al., 2020). Viral-Track-integrated scRNA-seq of 70,056 cells from HBV- and non-HBV-HCCs confirmed HBV lymphotropism in T/B cells, revealed depleted NK/DC/macrophages and MT1G-enriched TAMs in HBV-HCC, and identified dysfunctional CTLA4+ MDK+ CD8+ T cells driving immunosuppression and viral persistence (Liu et al., 2025). Comprehensive scRNAseq of over 186,000 immune cells from blood and liver of chronic HBV patients revealed transcriptional programs underlying HBV-specific T cell dysfunction and exhaustion, providing a valuable resource for identifying biomarkers and therapeutic targets to restore antiviral immunity (Hatje et al., 2024). ScRNAseq of HBV-associated HCC uncovered a highly immuno-suppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by TIGIT–NECTIN2-mediated T cell inhibition, macrophage-driven exclusion of T cell infiltration, and profound intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity that collectively promote immune exhaustion and cancer progression (Ho et al., 2021). These findings suggest that HBV-associated HCC develops within a chronically inflamed yet immunosuppressed liver, where both viral and host factors cooperate to promote malignancy.

Few studies have delved into the intricate and systemic interactions among various cell types and their spatial locations in liver cancer tissues using spatial transcriptomics (ST). This approach overcomes the limitation of scRNA-seq by capturing RNA information in situ via spatial barcoding technology, thereby preserving the spatial context of the tissue area (Marx, 2021). Utilizing high-resolution ST in primary liver cancers (PLCs) biopsies from seven patients, Wu et al. explored the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) across various regions. Their findings highlight the impact of the tumor capsule on intratumor spatial characteristics, bidirectional ligand-receptor interactions at cluster boundaries, and the spatial distribution of cancer stem cell niches and tertiary lymphoid structures, offering valuable insights into the diverse PLC ecosystem with potential implications for cancer intervention (Wu et al., 2021). Integrating RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and network analyses of TCGA HCC data revealed two key gene modules linked to cirrhosis: one enriched in immune cells and another in malignant cells, both promoting myofibroblast activation through diverse signaling pathways. Spatial mapping further showed close interactions among hepatocytes, macrophages, and myofibroblasts, suggesting their coordinated role in fibrosis progression within HCC-associated cirrhotic livers (Xuan et al., 2024).

Immune-based therapeutic strategies

The recognition that HBV persistence and associated pathology stem from immune dysfunction has driven the development of therapies aimed at restoring antiviral immunity. Immune-based interventions can be broadly categorized into checkpoint blockade, therapeutic vaccination, innate immune activation, antibody-mediated therapies, and adoptive cellular approaches. In this review, we focus on strategies evaluated in human clinical trials. Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of immune-based therapies currently under clinical evaluation for chronic hepatitis B. In the following sections, we highlight representative approaches for which clinical and immunological data provide mechanistic insight into antiviral immune restoration (Fig. 2). The overarching goal is to achieve a functional cure by combining immune reconstitution with viral antigen reduction.

Antigen-reducing strategies enabling immune restoration

Persistent high-level antigen exposure, including HBsAg, is thought to contribute to immune dysfunction in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and has been associated with T-cell exhaustion, impaired cytokine production, and defective antiviral effector function, although direct causal mechanisms remain incompletely defined. Therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing antigen burden are therefore hypothesized to create a permissive immunological environment that may allow partial restoration of HBV-specific immunity and increase the likelihood of durable functional cure.

Bepirovirsen (GSK3228836) is an antisense oligonucleotide designed to bind HBV transcripts and induce their degradation through RNase H1–mediated cleavage, thereby reducing viral protein synthesis, including HBsAg. In the phase 2b B-Clear trial, 24 weeks of weekly bepirovirsen administration in patients receiving background nucleos(t) ide analogue (NA) therapy resulted in sustained HBsAg and HBV DNA loss 24 weeks after end of treatment in approximately 9–10% of participants, with higher response rates observed among individuals with lower baseline HBsAg levels (Yuen et al., 2022). The treatment was generally safe and well tolerated. In addition to its direct antiviral effect, exploratory biomarker analyses from the B-Together study suggest that bepirovirsen may modulate host immune responses, as evidenced by transient increases in immune effector and apoptotic markers correlating with ALT elevations and HBsAg decline (Joshi et al., 2025). These findings raise the possibility that immune-mediated killing of infected hepatocytes may contribute to antiviral responses; however, the precise mechanisms and clinical relevance of this immunomodulatory activity remain to be fully elucidated. B-United Phase 2b trial, a multi-centre, randomized, double-blind study, is testing sequential therapy with the siRNA combination daplusiran/tomligisiran followed by the antisense oligonucleotide bepirovirsen, on top of nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy, to assess safety and efficacy toward a potential functional cure in adults with CHB. More recently, two large placebo-controlled phase 3 trials (B-Well 1 and B-Well 2) evaluating bepirovirsen in combination with standard NA therapy reported achievement of their primary endpoint, demonstrating a statistically significant and clinically meaningful functional cure rate compared with NA therapy alone, according to sponsor-released topline results. AHB-137 is a novel unconjugated antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) developed using AusperBio’s Med-O-ligo™ platform to target HBV transcripts and reduce viral antigen production, with the aim of facilitating immune-mediated control. Preliminary results presented at HEP-DART 2025 (not peer-reviewed) reported functional cure in a subset of HBeAg-negative patients receiving AHB-137 monotherapy. Peer-reviewed data are awaited to confirm these findings. Ongoing studies are assessing long-term durability of off-treatment control, predictors of response, and rational combination strategies designed to further enhance antigen suppression prior to immune-mediated clearance.

Capsid Assembly Modulators (CAMs) are small molecules that disrupt nucleocapsid assembly, inhibiting pgRNA encapsidation and potentially limiting cccDNA replenishment. While CAMs do not directly target viral transcripts, deep suppression of viral replication and reduced recycling of nucleocapsids to the nucleus may contribute indirectly to antigen decline, supporting their evaluation in combination strategies aimed at functional cure (Taverniti et al., 2022). Clinical-stage examples include pevifoscorvir sodium (ALG-000184), an oral CAM-E that blocks capsid assembly and pgRNA encapsidation, thereby preventing cccDNA establishment/replenishment and lowering viral DNA, RNA, and antigens (HBsAg, HBeAg, HBcrAg), with the goal of deeper antiviral activity, immune restoration, and HBsAg decline. The Phase 2 B-SUPREME trial (NCT06963710) is a randomized, double-blind, active-controlled, multicenter study in approximately 200 untreated HBeAg+ and HBeAg− adults with CHB, comparing 48 weeks of pevifoscorvir monotherapy versus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate. The primary endpoint is HBV DNA < LLOQ (10 IU/mL; target detected [TD] or target not detected [TND]) in HBeAg+ participants and HBV DNA < LLOQ (10 IU/mL; TND) in HBeAg− participants, with additional assessment of safety, pharmacokinetics, and secondary/exploratory biomarkers including HBV antigens. Dosing began in August 2025, with interim analyses planned for 2026 and topline data expected in 2027. Preclinical studies further reinforce the conceptual link between antigen reduction and immune restoration. In a humanized mouse model of chronic HBV infection, treatment with the CAM GLP-26 induced profound suppression of viral replication and antigen expression, which was associated with restoration of antiviral immune responses and sustained viral control following treatment withdrawal (Fernandes et al., 2026). These data provide mechanistic support for the hypothesis that deep and sustained antigen decline may relieve immune exhaustion and permit reconstitution of adaptive antiviral responses.

Collectively, both clinical and preclinical evidence support a paradigm in which antigen-lowering strategies serve as a foundational step toward immune reconstitution. This framework provides a rational basis for combining potent antigen-reducing agents with therapies directly targeting innate and adaptive immune pathways.

Innate immune activators

Given HBV’s ability to dampen innate sensing, pharmacologic activation of innate immunity offers another avenue to overcome tolerance. Among these, Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists—particularly TLR7 and TLR8 agonists—have shown the greatest promise. Selgantolimod (GS-9688), an oral and well tolerated TLR8 agonist, stimulates monocytes and dendritic cells to produce IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, indirectly enhancing HBV-specific T-cell responses. In a phase II trial, two of thirtynine CHB patients achieved HBsAg loss after 48 weeks, and many showed declines in serum HBsAg accompanied by increased serum IL-18 levels, indicating immune activation(Amin et al., 2021; Gane et al., 2021, 2022). Ongoing studies are assessing Selgantolimod in HBV/HIV co-infected individuals (National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), 2025). Other agonist (TLR7) such as Vesatolimod (GS-9620) have reached phase II, but did not result in clinically significant HBsAg decline in any patient group who received Vesatolimod at different doses (1, 2 or 4 mg) (Agarwal et al., 2018).

Ruzotolimod is currently being investigated in combination with sRNAi, such as Xalnesiran, during a phase II trial involving patients with chronic HBV infection. In this trial, 12% of the patients achieved HBsAg loss at 24 weeks, with 3% of the patients experiencing HBsAg seroconversion. Importantly, this HBsAg loss, with or without seroconversion, was observed exclusively in participants whose HBsAg level was below 1000 UI/mL (Hou et al., 2024).

Beyond TLR agonists, STING and RIG-I pathway agonists emerged as novel candidates capable of inducing type I interferon responses in hepatocytes. Preclinical studies in 2022 demonstrated that selective hepatic delivery of STING agonists can suppress cccDNA transcription without significant hepatotoxicity (Li et al., 2022). The RIG-I agonist inarigivir demonstrated antiviral and immunomodulatory activity at lower dose levels in a phase 2, open-label, randomized study in treatment-naïve patients with chronic hepatitis B. Administration of inarigivir at doses ranging from 25 to 200 mg for 12 weeks, followed by tenofovir, was associated with greater on-treatment reductions in HBV DNA, HBV RNA, and HBsAg compared with placebo, with dose-dependent antiviral effects and modest declines in HBsAg that persisted after transition to nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy. These data were generated in a dose-ranging program distinct from subsequent phase 2b studies in which higher-dose inarigivir (400 mg) was associated with clinically significant safety signals, leading to suspension of dosing and discontinuation of further HBV development (Yuen et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings underscore both the biological activity of innate immune agonists and the narrow therapeutic window that may limit their clinical applicability in chronic hepatitis B. Additional TLR7/8 agonists currently under clinical evaluation include ruzotolimod (RO7020531) and JNJ-64794964, although clinical efficacy data remain limited to date.

Immune checkpoints inhibitors

In CHB and HBV-related HCC, HBV-specific T cells express high levels of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and CTLA-4, which limit their effector function. Blocking these pathways can reinvigorate antiviral responses (Park et al., 2021). In the CheckMate-040 trial, the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab achieved an objective response rate of 14% in HBV-associated HCC; when combined with the CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab, responses increased to approximately 33%. Importantly, viral reactivation was rare under appropriate antiviral prophylaxis, suggesting that checkpoint blockade can be used safely in this context (El-Khoueiry et al., 2018; Yau et al., 2020).

In CHB without HCC, a phase Ib study demonstrated that a single dose of nivolumab led to >0.5 log10 IU/mL HBsAg reduction in several patients, with one achieving complete HBsAg clearance out of 24 participants. Notably, this patient was the only one in the cohort to exhibit maximal T cell responses. This reduction correlated with transient expansion of effector-memory CD8+ T cells and increased IFN-γ production (Gane et al., 2019). In contrast, a randomized, placebo-controlled study evaluating a single 45 mg subcutaneous dose of cetrelimab, a fully human anti–PD-1 monoclonal antibody, in virologically suppressed CHB participants demonstrated a favorable safety profile but no significant impact on HBV biomarkers, and further clinical development for HBV was discontinued (Asselah, 2026).

ASC22 (envafolimab), another PD-L1 inhibitor, was evaluated in patients with virally suppressed chronic hepatitis B (CHB). Participants were assigned to three groups receiving ASC22 at doses of 1 mg/kg or 2.5 mg/kg, or placebo. In the 1 mg/kg group, three of ten participants with baseline HBsAg levels <100 IU/mL achieved HBsAg loss during treatment, which was sustained through 24 weeks of follow-up (Qian et al., 2025).

RG6084, a liver-directed N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNac)-conjugated locked nucleic acid (LNA) single-stranded oligonucleotide (SSO) that induces RNaseH-mediated degradation of PD-L1 mRNA, was discontinued before entering phase II because of Grade 2/3 side effects (Results presented at AASLD The Liver Meeting, San Diego, CA, 2024). Newer trials are evaluating combination regimens pairing PD-1 blockade with therapeutic vaccination (e.g., VTP-300 + nivolumab) or RNA-interference-based antigen suppression (e.g., VIR-2218 + anti-PD-1). These dual strategies aim to first lower antigen load, then restore exhausted T-cell function. Despite promising signals, response rates remain low. Predictive biomarkers such as baseline HBsAg level, intrahepatic PD-L1 expression, and T-cell receptor clonality are under investigation to identify patients most likely to benefit. Other check-point inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, such as the anti-PD-1 antibody cemiplimab and the anti-PD-L1 candidate AB-101, are currently under early clinical evaluation, although clinical efficacy data in chronic hepatitis B remain limited.

Therapeutic vaccines

Therapeutic vaccines seek to elicit de novo or enhanced HBV-specific immune responses in patients already chronically infected. Conventional prophylactic vaccines are ineffective in this setting because high antigenemia and immune tolerance suppress vaccine responsiveness (Cargill and Barnes, 2021). To overcome this, several novel platforms are under development (Marrapu et al., 2025).

The TG-1050 vaccine, from which T101 originates, is an adenovirus-based vaccine encoding multiple HBV antigens (core, polymerase, and envelope). In a phase 1b clinical trial in patients with viral suppression ensured by nucleos(t)ide analogs, the vaccine induced a decrease in HBsAg of around 0.2 log10 IU/mL in 19% of vaccinated individuals (n= 7) compared to 8% in non-vaccinated individuals (n = 3). It was shown that 4 out of 7 patients who received the vaccine had T cell responses against at least one antigen present in the vaccine, including two patients who developed a response against multiple antigens (Zoulim et al., 2020).

In early-phase clinical studies, the therapeutic vaccine BRII-179, which incorporates all three HBV surface antigens (Pre-S1, Pre-S2, and S), was shown to be safe and immunogenic in virally suppressed chronic hepatitis B patients, inducing HBV-specific B-cell responses and restoring multispecific IFN-γ–producing T-cell activity, although with minimal impact on circulating HBsAg levels when administered alone or with low-dose interferon-α (Ma et al., 2021). Subsequent combination studies demonstrated that while HBsAg reduction achieved by siRNA therapy alone was insufficient to substantially restore antiviral immunity, its combination with BRII-179 significantly enhanced humoral immunity and selectively expanded Pre-S1/Pre-S2-specific helper T cells, suggesting that antigen load reduction can synergize with therapeutic vaccination to promote more sustained and functional HBV-specific immune responses (Ji et al., 2025).

New generation recombinant vaccines include the VTP-300, a heterologous prime-boost regimen combining ChAdOx1-HBV and MVA-HBV. Preliminary published results demonstrated significant and durable HBsAg reductions of 0.9–1.4 log10 IU/mL in monotherapy, with complete HBsAg loss in two patients receiving combination therapy with nivolumab (Tak et al., 2024). Combination regimens integrating siRNA-mediated antigen reduction (AB-729) with therapeutic vaccination (VTP-300) and immune checkpoint blockade are currently under clinical evaluation, reflecting efforts to synergistically enhance antiviral immunity.

VRON-0200, a new therapeutic vaccine who started its phase Ib, is an association of two 2 distinct chimpanzee adenoviral vectors (AdC6 and AdC7), containing parts of hepatitis B virus (HBV) core and polymerase antigens fused within Herpes Simplex Virus glycoprotein D. The HSV glycoprotein D plays the role of an immune checkpoint modulator that increased the co-stimulation signal through LIGHT-HVEM by blocking BTLA to activate early the antigen specific CD8+ T cell (Hasanpourghadi et al., 2020).

Recent immunomonitoring studies reveal that therapeutic vaccination can transiently restore HBV-specific T-cell proliferation and cyto-kine secretion but that sustained responses likely require concurrent reduction of circulating HBsAg to relieve immune exhaustion (Cargill and Barnes, 2021). Hence, vaccines are now often envisioned as part of combination regimens rather than standalone therapies. Several additional therapeutic vaccine platforms, including GS-4774, CVI-HBV-002, and TherVacB, are under clinical investigation, reflecting continued efforts to optimize antigen design, delivery platforms, and combination strategies.

Anti-HBV immunoglobulins

Anti-HBV immunoglobulins have drawn significant attention from researchers because they can reduce circulating HBsAg and block the virus’s entry. They can reduce circulating antigen levels, limit viral spread, and potentially relieve immune exhaustion (Fung et al., 2022). Approved formulations such as Hepatect CP and Zutectra are already used to prevent reinfection following liver transplantation (Haute Autorité de Santé, 2025). Next-generation mAbs, including VIR-3434, are engineered for extended half-life and enhanced effector functions. In combination with VIR-2218, a small-interfering RNA that suppresses HBV gene expression, this approach led to a 3.1 log10 IU/mL HBsAg reduction after 20 weeks, although rebound occurred after treatment cessation (Gane et al., 2023). The decrease in HBsAg in serum was more significant when combining VIR-3434 and VIR-2218 than in monotherapy. However, no patient has achieved HBsAg seroclearance (Gane et al., 2023). Like the previous molecules, Lenvervimab allows a decrease in the HBsAg level to become undetectable for one month in 2 out of 9 patients - in the multiple injection arm (4 injections) and the highest dose (240,000 IU) - before returning to its initial value upon treatment cessation (Lee et al., 2020).

While monotherapy results remain modest, antibody-based therapies are expected to synergize with antivirals, RNAi agents, or vaccines by reducing antigen burden and enhancing immune visibility of infected cells (Fung et al., 2022). Additional monoclonal antibodies targeting HBsAg, including HH-003 and Brelovitug, are currently under clinical evaluation.

Bi-specific T-cell engagers

Bi-specific molecules that redirect effector T cells toward HBV-infected hepatocytes represent an innovative therapeutic strategy. These constructs typically fuse a high-affinity HBV-specific T-cell receptor (TCR) recognizing viral peptides presented on MHC I with an anti-CD3 antibody fragment that recruits bystander T cells (Fergusson et al., 2020). The prototype, IMC-I109V, targets an HBV S antigen epitope and can trigger potent cytotoxicity at sub-nanomolar concentrations in preclinical assays. They have now moved on to phase I (NCT05867056) to evaluate the safety profile and its antiviral activity.

Adoptive T-cell therapies (CAR-T or TCR-T)

Proof of concept originates from clinical observations: For example, patients with CHB who received hematopoietic stem cell transplants from HBV-vaccinated donors successfully eliminated the virus (Lindemann et al., 2016). Similarly, liver transplant recipients with pre-existing HBV immunity have demonstrated HBV clearance, highlighting the potential of immune reconstitution strategies (Qasim et al., 2015). However, these therapies pose challenges, including potential off-target effects, autoimmunity, and the need to enhance the persistence of T cells (Sitia et al., 2004). In the realm of chronic HBV infection, T-cell therapy aims to restore HBV-specific immune responses. Strategies include HBV-specific CAR-T cells and TCR-T cells. The main difference between these two types of modified cells is their modality of antigen recognition.

The CAR-T cells, which stand for Chimeric Antigen Receptor T cells, are T cells collected from the patient and genetically modified ex vivo. A gene introduced into their nucleus leads to the production of “chimeric antigen receptors” on their surface. In this case, these receptors can recognize the native HBsAg on the surface of infected hepatocytes (Bertoletti and Tan, 2020). Although potent in vitro, CAR-T cells risk off-target activation by circulating HBsAg or antigen-bearing monocytes, raising safety concerns. To date, most CAR-T programs for HBV remain preclinical, focusing on optimizing antigen affinity and safety switches (Festag et al., 2019).

TCR-T cells, by contrast, employ natural T-cell receptors that recognize HBV peptides presented by specific HLA molecules (Bertoletti et al., 2017; Tan and Schreiber, 2020). Integration of HBV DNA into the human genome during chronic infection leads to the expression of whole or partial HBV antigens in HCC cells, allowing for the development of targeted therapies with limited off-tumor adverse events (Tan et al., 2019). Initial clinical approaches have relied on transient expression of HBV-specific TCRs using mRNA electroporation, allowing controlled and time-limited receptor expression to mitigate the risk of excessive immune-mediated liver damage. Early clinical studies of transient mRNA HBV-specific TCR-T cells (NCT03899415; NCT04745403) demonstrated the feasibility of this approach and provided initial evidence of antiviral activity with acceptable safety profiles (Meng et al., 2021; Qasim et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2019, 2022). The LioCyx-M product (NCT05195294), evaluated alone or in combination with lenvatinib, represents a further development of this strategy based on mRNA-engineered HBV-specific TCR-T cells. More recently, SCG101 (NCT05339321) has been developed as a TCR-T product expressing a stably integrated HBV-specific TCR, potentially enabling sustained effector function and improved antiviral activity (Wu et al., 2026). Clinical studies of HBV-specific TCR-T therapies, including SCG101, have provided preliminary evidence of antiviral and antitumor activity in patients with HBV-related HCC, with observed reductions in tumor burden and declines or stabilization of serum HBsAg levels (Wu et al., 2026). Key challenges include achieving sufficient antiviral efficacy while limiting immune-mediated liver toxicity, optimizing T-cell persistence, and identifying patients most likely to benefit from this approach. However, these approaches are constrained by HLA restriction and the logistical complexity of individualized cell manufacturing. Indeed, TCR-T cells are restricted by the recognition of a single HBV epitope presented by a single MHC class I molecule (Banu et al., 2014). Although challenges remain, T-cell-based immunotherapy holds sub-stantial promise for achieving long-term immune control.

Conclusion and future perspectives

HBV persistence and its associated pathologies are fundamentally immune-driven processes. The virus exploits the liver’s tolerogenic environment to evade innate detection and progressively reshape adaptive immunity, resulting in heterogeneous states of immune dysfunction rather than uniform exhaustion. Increasing evidence indicates that antigen reduction alone is often insufficient to restore durable antiviral immunity, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that address both viral persistence and the underlying immune dysregulation. While current antivirals suppress replication, they do not eliminate the cccDNA reservoir or restore immune competence. The next generation of therapies aims to shift this paradigm by combining direct antiviral suppression with immune-modulating interventions (Lim et al., 2023). The ultimate success of these approaches will depend on their rational integration and on identifying biomarkers that predict immune responsiveness.

Future research should further define the mechanisms and limits of immune reversibility in chronic infection. Advances in single-cell and spatial technologies are beginning to reveal the complexity of intrahepatic immune organization and may help identify cellular states associated with immune control or persistent infection. These approaches may enable improved patient stratification and guide the rational sequencing of antigen-reducing and immune-modulating therapies. A key challenge will be to determine the degree and durability of immune restoration required to achieve sustained functional cure while limiting immunopathology. Addressing these questions will be essential for the development of personalized combination strategies capable of achieving long-term immune control of HBV infection.
