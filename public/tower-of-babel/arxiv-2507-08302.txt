Arbitrage on Decentralized Exchanges
Xue Dong He, Chen Yang, Yutian Zhou

Abstract
Decentralized exchanges (DEXs) are alternative venues to centralized exchanges (CEXs) for trading cryptocurrencies and have become increasingly popular. An arbitrage opportunity arises when the exchange rate of two cryptocurrencies in a DEX differs from that in a CEX. Arbitrageurs can then trade on the DEX and CEX to make a profit. Trading on the DEX incurs a gas fee, which determines the priority of the trade being executed. We study a gas-fee competition game between two arbitrageurs who maximize their expected profit from trading. We derive the unique symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium and find that (i) the arbitrageurs may choose not to trade when the arbitrage opportunity and liquidity is small; (ii) the probability of the arbitrageurs choosing a higher gas fee is lower; (iii) the arbitrageurs pay a higher gas fee and trade more when the arbitrage opportunity becomes larger and when liquidity becomes higher; (iv) the arbitrageurs’ expected profit could increase with arbitrage opportunity and liquidity. The above findings are consistent with our empirical study.



Keywords:
arbitrage, automated market maker, decentralized exchange, mixed Nash equilibrium

1
Introduction

The Decentralized Exchanges (DEXs) are exchanges where transactions occur directly between traders without the need for a central party. In recent years, DEXs have attracted lots of transactions of crypto assets. In contrast to the Centralized Exchanges (CEXs) that predominantly use limit order book, the most widely adopted trading mechanism on DEXs is the Automated Market Makers (AMMs), which determines the exchange rate algorithmically through a pricing function. Although both DEXs and CEXs are active in the trading of crypto assets nowadays, the exchange rate on DEXs can deviate from that on CEXs, leading to potential arbitrage opportunities. Indeed,
Heimbach et al., 2024
empirically find that arbitraging between DEXs on the Ethereum blockchain and external exchanges accounts for over a quarter of the volume on Ethereum’s five largest DEXs. Furthermore, as illustrated by our empirical study, blocks with arbitrage opportunities exhibit a higher number of trades per block compared to blocks without arbitrage opportunities, and this difference is primarily attributed to the presence of arbitrage trades. Arbitrages expose liquidity providers on DEXs to significant losses, which is the essential problem in the design of DEXs.

While arbitrageurs are an important type of participant on DEX, the existing literature takes a simplified approach to modeling their actions, mainly focusing on a single arbitrageur (e.g.,
He et al., 2024
,
Milionis et al., 2023
,
Evans et al., 2021
). In practice, however, faced with competition, arbitrageurs’ strategies are much more complex. To wit, transactions on DEXs are typically conducted in conjunction with the generation of blocks, which occurs every 12 seconds on the Ethereum platform. Furthermore, gas fees, paid to validators or miners, are the dominant factor that influences the trade execution order on DEXs. When two arbitrageurs submit arbitrage orders simultaneously, the arbitrageur whose order gets executed later can be exposed to substantially reduced profits, if not losses, due to the positive first-mover advantage. Consequently, the arbitrageurs face a tradeoff between increasing the likelihood of being the first mover and saving on gas fees, which reduces their net profit. This leads to several interesting questions: What is the arbitrageur’s optimal strategy, including the choice of gas fees, in a competitive environment? Should arbitrageurs exploit every arbitrage opportunity? Moreover, do competitions necessarily lead to a zero arbitrage profit?

To answer these questions, we propose an equilibrium model for the competition between two arbitrageurs, incorporating the practical blockchain features of trade execution orders and gas fee selection. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to explicitly model the strategic impact of gas fees and trading amount among arbitrageurs. Based on a common set of information about the current market environment, they decide whether or not to participate in arbitrage, and if so, choose the optimal gas fee and trading amount to maximize their arbitrage profit net of gas and trading fees.

Our main contributions are threefold. First, we show the existence and uniqueness of the symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium for the arbitrageurs’ gas fee competition game, and the equilibrium consists of randomized rather than pure strategies. The equilibrium strategy has a clear structure: the arbitrageur optimally chooses a probability of participating in the arbitrage, and conditional on participation, the probability distribution of the gas fee to be paid and the trading amount, given the chosen gas fee. We focus on a simultaneous-move symmetric game for the following three reasons. First, private pools have been widely implemented recently to mitigate the front-running attacks

Second, our model unveils several insights on the arbitrage strategy. The arbitrageur is more likely to choose a low gas fee than a high one, and a larger trading amount typically accompanies a higher chosen gas fee. Besides, despite the competition, the arbitrageur’s expected net profit can be positive when the base gas fee level is sufficiently low relative to the arbitrage opportunity and the pool liquidity. This is in stark contrast to the setting in
Capponi and Jia, 2021
, where arbitrageurs always have zero net profit. Nevertheless, under a high base gas fee, the arbitrageur’s expected net profit reduces to zero, and consequently, they may not participate in the arbitrage. Furthermore, both the gas fee paid by the arbitrageur and the trading amount relative to the pool size increase with the pool liquidity and the size of the arbitrage opportunity.

Third, we conduct extensive empirical tests using data from Binance and Uniswap to evaluate several testable implications of our model. On the one hand, empirical studies demonstrate that our model setting is aligned with practice. We find that over 96.5% of the blocks in our sample do not have arbitrage opportunities, and, on average, arbitrage opportunities last for only 1.5 blocks, while the absence of arbitrage lasts for 44.3 blocks. Even within the small proportion of blocks with arbitrage opportunities, arbitrageurs do not appear in 42.7% of the blocks, consistent with our randomized equilibrium strategy. Furthermore, only 1.2% of such blocks attract more than two arbitrage trades, consistent with our choice of modeling the game between two arbitrageurs. On the other hand, empirical studies support our insight into arbitrageurs’ strategies derived from our model. More specifically, our empirical test confirms that the first mover has a larger likelihood of profit than the second mover, that the likelihood of an arbitrageur choosing a larger gas fee is smaller, and that the swap amount relative to the pool size increases with the gas fee. We also find that arbitrageurs pay a higher gas fee, trade more, and have a higher expected return when the arbitrage opportunity becomes larger.

1.1
Related Literature

Arbitrage between DEXs and CEXs.

An extensive body of literature compares DEXs as an emerging type of exchange with traditional CEXs in terms of their trading mechanisms and efficiency, e.g.,
Han et al., 2021
,
Lehar and Parlour, 2025
,
Barbon and Ranaldo, 2021
,
Aoyagi and Ito, 2024
. A critical question in the comparison concerns price consistency across exchanges, as price discrepancies naturally create arbitrage opportunities.
Angeris et al., 2021
,
Angeris et al., 2019
, and
Lo and Medda, 2021
find that prices on DEXs tend to closely track those on CEXs, primarily due to arbitrage activities.
However, on the study of arbitrage, the existing research mainly focuses on cyclic arbitrage across different cryptocurrency pools within DEXs (e.g.,
Wang et al., 2022
,
Zhang et al., 2024a
,
Zhang et al., 2024b
), while the exploration on the arbitrage mechanism between DEXs and CEXs remains largely open.
Arbitrageurs operating across exchanges play a crucial role in aligning prices. By detecting and exploiting price discrepancies, arbitrageurs’ trades effectively narrow the gaps.
Our paper specifically addresses the behavior of arbitrageurs operating between DEXs and CEXs in the presence of arbitrage opportunities.

Most existing literature (e.g.,
He et al., 2024
,
Milionis et al., 2023
,
Evans et al., 2021
) models arbitrageurs on DEXs as single-agent optimization problems. For instance,
He et al., 2024
and
Evans et al., 2021
simplify arbitrageur behavior to a single-agent optimization problem while ignoring gas fees.
Grivol and Reed, 2025
mainly analyze how optimal DEX arbitrage strategies depend on the CEX price model in the single-arbitrageur case and extend to the cooperative multiple-arbitrageur case.
However, our empirical study in Section
4
reveals that multiple trades by arbitrageurs can exist within a single block. Consequently, arbitrageurs’ actions exhibit strategic interdependence under competition. To model this interaction, we develop a game theory model between two arbitrageurs operating on DEXs.

Arbitragers’ strategy setting.

In the single-arbitrageur model, arbitrageurs only need to determine the optimal trading amount to maximize their profit based on the observed reserves, as they do not need to consider the trading order in competition.
Capponi and Jia, 2021
consider a model with multiple arbitrageurs and adopt a simplified setting by directly assuming that the gas fee of arbitrageurs equals the highest possible profit. However, in Section
4
, we empirically observe that the profit is not always equal to the gas fee. Additionally, even in the two-arbitrageur case, the gas fee is crucial, as it can affect the transaction order, which in turn can impact the profits of arbitrageurs.
As described by
Buterin et al., 2013
, the free-market nature of gas prices allows users to bid for faster transaction processing. Using simulation,
Sylvester et al., 2022
find that with only one arbitrageur in the market system, it could achieve the highest average performance. This suggests that arbitrageurs can take more advantage of arbitrage opportunities when they face less competition and supports the first-mover advantage in our model, as the first mover’s trade is executed first, with no other arbitrageurs sharing the profit.

Although gas fee is a significant component of traders’ costs, most of the existing literature analyzing gas fees only focuses on improving blockchain efficiency (
Kim and Kim, 2024
,
Azouvi et al., 2023
) and miners’ profit (
Donmez and Karaivanov, 2022
). From the users’ perspective,
Laurent et al., 2022
propose a method to determine the minimum gas fee based on the prediction of execution probability using the Monte Carlo method in the Ethereum blockchain. Instead of focusing on the execution of transactions or the average gas fee, our work focuses on the profit of arbitrageurs. For the trading amount strategy, considering sandwich attacks,
Son et al., 2025
and
Heimbach and Wattenhofer, 2022
develop a game model for traders, including both attackers and victims, based on their respective profits. Our model differs in that it constructs a model focused on the strategic behavior of two arbitrageurs, taking into account both gas fees and trading amounts.

Arbitrageurs’ equilibrium strategy.

Our paper establishes the existence and uniqueness of a mixed strategy equilibrium, due to the non-existence of a pure equilibrium caused by the discontinuity in the payoffs of arbitrageurs when they submit the same gas fee.
In a general setting,
Reny, 1999
and
Ewerhart and Reny, 2022
provide theoretical support for the existence of equilibrium strategies when the payoff function is discontinuous. In our specific problem, we also prove the uniqueness of the equilibrium in addition to the existence.

Several empirical and simulation studies support our result. In the equilibrium strategy of our model, trading volumes and gas fees are positively related.
Ante and Saggu, 2024
empirically demonstrate a bidirectional causal relationship between gas fees and trading volume on decentralized exchanges using time-varying Granger tests. As trading volume increases, competition for block space drives up gas fees. Conversely, rising gas fees can reduce user activity, as higher costs discourage trading.

Our model suggests that the equilibrium strategy should rely on the base gas fee, liquidity, and arbitrage opportunities.
Gogol et al., 2024
empirically find that the gas fee has a positive relationship with the decay time of these price disparities. As part of the gas price, the base gas fee can directly influence arbitrageurs’ costs. Liquidity depth can affect traders’ returns through slippage. When market liquidity is low, arbitrageurs may experience high slippage, particularly those with larger trading volumes.
Sylvester et al., 2022
employ an agent-based model to simulate the behavior of arbitrageurs under different market conditions and find that larger liquidity depth can increase the amount of arbitrageurs and price stability on AMMs. Deeper liquidity pools mean higher potential arbitrage trading amounts as the price is less likely to have large fluctuations, which is attractive for arbitrageurs. The size of the arbitrage opportunity also constrains the behavior of arbitrageurs. When price differences in the market are insufficient to cover trading costs, arbitrage opportunities cease to exist.

3
Model

In this section, we lay out the model framework, state the main theoretical results on the existence and uniqueness of equilibrium, and explore its properties.

3.1
Arbitrageurs’ Payoff

We focus on the behavior of arbitrageurs, who make decisions based on their own expected profits. Arbitrageurs will engage in trades that align with the direction of arbitrage. For instance, in the case we are considering, arbitrageurs all aim to purchase asset B and will choose a gas fee that is greater than or equal to the base gas fee
\hat{g}_{L}
; i.e.,
\hat{g}_{L}
is equal to the units of gas multiplied by the base fee per gas.

The execution order of trades is determined by the magnitude of the gas fee. which is assumed to follow the rule outlined in Assumption
3.1
below.

Assumption 3.1

(Trades Execution Order Rule)
.

When multiple trades are submitted, execution order follows the rules:

(i)

Trades with higher gas fees are executed first.

(ii)

If multiple trades have the same gas fee, their execution order is determined randomly, with each possible permutation of the trades being equally likely. Specificly, if there are
n
such trades, each trade has an equal probability of
1/n
of being executed in any specific position within the sequence.

Assumption
3.1
(i) aligns with practice, as miners tend to prioritize executing trades with higher gas fees. If the trades have the same gas fee, they are indistinguishable to the miners. Therefore, we assume in Assumption
3.1
(ii) that the probability distribution of the execution order for trades with the same gas fee is uniform.

We examine a symmetric game involving two arbitrageurs. Under Assumption
3.1
, when two arbitrageurs choose the same gas fee, there is an equal probability for their trades to be executed first. As mentioned in Section
2

(p_{B}/p_{A})/(y_{A}/y_{B})\in[1/(1+f),1+f]

\displaystyle L_{B}:=y_{B}p_{B},~~O:=\frac{y_{B}p_{B}}{y_{A}p_{A}(1+f)},

3.2
Mixed Equilibrium

Because the payoff function does not depend on the choice of the gas fee
g
and trading amount
d_{A}
given that
\xi=0
, the arbitrageur’s randomized strategy can be represented by a pair
(\alpha,\mu)

\alpha\in[0,1]
stands for the probability of trading, and
\mu

[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]\times[0,\hat{D}_{A}]

(g,d_{A})
given that the arbitrageur trades.
In other words, the arbitrageur’s choice of trading
\xi
follows a Bernoulli distribution such that the probability of
\xi=1
is
\alpha
. Furthermore, conditional on
\xi=1
, the joint distribution of the random variables
\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{D}
, representing the gas fee paid and trading amount, is given by
\mu
. For completeness, we define
\tilde{g}=\tilde{D}=0
on the event that
\xi=0
, i.e., the arbitrageur chooses not to trade.

The payoff of the mixed game is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi(\alpha,\mu;\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}):&=\mathbb{E}[R]=\alpha(1-\bar{\alpha})\int R_{F}(g,d_{A})\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}d_{A})\\
&+\alpha\bar{\alpha}\bigg[\int R_{F}(g,d_{A})\mathbf{1}_{g>\bar{g}}\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}d_{A})\bar{\mu}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\\
&+\int\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{F}(g,d_{A})+R_{S}(g,d_{A};\bar{d}_{A})\right)\mathbf{1}_{g=\bar{g}}\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}d_{A})\bar{\mu}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\\
&+\int R_{S}(g,d_{A};\bar{d}_{A})\mathbf{1}_{g<\bar{g}}\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}d_{A})\bar{\mu}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\bigg],\end{split}

(13)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the random action
(\xi,g,d_{A})
chosen by the arbitrageur and
(\bar{\xi},\bar{g},\bar{d}_{A})
chosen by the competitor. Here, the first term on the right hand side corresponds to the case where the competitor does not trade and hence the aribtrageur automatically becomes the first mover. The second term corresponds to the case where both the arbitrageus and the competitor trade, and whether the arbitrageur is the first or second mover follows from the analysis above. Finally, the payoff is zero if the arbitrageur does not trade.

Because the pure game is symmetric, the mixed game is also symmetric. Therefore, we look for symmetric equilibria.

Definition 3.1

(Symmetric Mixed Nash Equilibrium)
.

A mixed strategy
(\alpha^{*},\mu^{*})\in[0,1]\times{\cal U}
is a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium of the gas competition game if

\displaystyle(\alpha^{*},\mu^{*})\in\underset{(\alpha,\mu)\in[0,1]\times{\cal U}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\Pi(\alpha,\mu;\bar{\alpha}^{*},\bar{\mu}^{*}),

where
{\cal U}
denotes the set of probability measures on the action space
[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]\times[0,\hat{D}_{A}]
.

The existence of a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium can be established based on the results in
Reny, 1999
; see Theorem
A.1
in the Appendix. In the following, we first state some properties satisfied by the equilibria, and then establish the uniqueness of the equilibrium.

First, we define the
response function

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(g,d_{A}):&=(1-\alpha^{*})R_{F}(g,d_{A})+\alpha^{*}\bigg[\int R_{F}(g,d_{A})\mathbf{1}_{g>\bar{g}}\mu^{*}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\\
&\quad+\int\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{F}(g,d_{A})+R_{S}(g,d_{A};\bar{d}_{A})\right)\mathbf{1}_{g=\bar{g}}\mu^{*}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\\
&\quad+\int R_{S}(g,d_{A};\bar{d}_{A})\mathbf{1}_{g<\bar{g}}\mu^{*}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{d}_{A})\bigg].\end{split}

(14)

The response function stands for the arbitrageur’s expected profit of trading an amount
d_{A}
with a selected gas fee
g
, given that the competitor follows the equilibrium strategy.

Proposition 3.1

(Properties of Equilibria)
.

Suppose that
(\alpha^{*},\mu^{*})
is a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium. Then,

(i)

\alpha^{*}>0
;

(ii)

For each
g\geq 0
, the response function
h(g,d_{A})
is continuous and strictly concave in
d_{A}\geq 0
and hence admits a unique maximizer in
d_{A}\in[0,\hat{D}_{A}]
.

As shown by Proposition
3.1
, the probability of trading is positive. This is because the profit of trading as the first mover is positive due to the arbitrage opportunity. Furthermore, the arbitrageur’s trading amount, give a selected gas fee
g
, is not random but rather representable through a deterministic function
D_{A}^{*}(g)
defined as

\displaystyle D_{A}^{*}(g)=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{d_{A}\in[0,\hat{D}_{A}]}h(g,d_{A}).

(15)

Subsequently, we only need to search symmetric mixed Nash equilibria that are represented by triplet
(\alpha,\Phi,D_{A})
, where
\alpha\in(0,1]
represents the probability of trading,
\Phi
is a probability measure on
[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
representing the randomized gas fee strategy, and
D_{A}
is a function on
[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
representing the trading amount for each chosen gas fee. With a bit abuse of notations, we still use
\Pi
to denote the payoff function, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pi(\alpha,\Phi,D_{A};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\Phi},\bar{D}_{A})

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-\bar{\alpha})\int R_{F}(g,D_{A}(g))\Phi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha\bar{\alpha}\bigg[\int R_{F}(g,D_{A}(g))\mathbf{1}_{g>\bar{g}}\Phi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g)\bar{\Phi}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g})

\displaystyle\quad+\int\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{F}(g,D_{A}(g))+R_{S}(g,D_{A}(g);\bar{D}_{A}(\bar{g}))\right)\mathbf{1}_{g=\bar{g}}\Phi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g)\bar{\Phi}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g})

\displaystyle\quad+\int R_{S}(g,D_{A}(g);\bar{D}_{A}(\bar{g}))\mathbf{1}_{g<\bar{g}}\Phi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g)\bar{\Phi}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g})\bigg].

(16)

The new structure
(\alpha,\Phi,D_{A})
allows us to further investigate the equilibrium gas fee and trading amount strategies.

Proposition 3.2

(Properties of Gas Fee Strategy)
.

Suppose that
(\alpha^{*},\Phi^{*},D_{A}^{*})
is a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium. Then,

(i)

\Phi^{*}\big(\{g\in[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]:D_{A}^{*}(g)=0\}\big)=0
.

(ii)

\Phi^{*}
does not have atoms on
[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
.

(iii)

The response function defined in (
14
) takes the following form:

\displaystyle h(g,d_{A})

\displaystyle=R_{F}(g,d_{A})-\alpha^{*}\int_{g}^{\hat{g}_{H}}V(d_{A},D_{A}^{*}(\bar{g}))\Phi^{*}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{g}).

(17)

Furthermore,
h(g,D_{A}^{*}(g))
is constant for
g\in[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
. More precisely, let
M
and
m
be the essential supremum and essential infimum of
h(g,D_{A}^{*}(g)),g\in[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
under
\Phi^{*}
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle M:=\sup\{a\in\mathbb{R}:\Phi^{*}(\{g\in[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]:h(g,D_{A}^{*}(g))>a\})>0\},

\displaystyle m:=\inf\{a\in\mathbb{R}:\Phi^{*}(\{g\in[\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]:h(g,D_{A}^{*}(g))<a\})>0\}.

Then,
M=m
. Moreover,
M\geq 0
and the inequality becomes an equality if
\alpha^{*}<1
.

(iv)

The support of
\Phi^{*}
is
[\hat{g}_{L},g_{H}]
for some
g_{H}\in(\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}]
.

Proposition
3.2
-(i) shows that the trading amount must be positive if the arbitrageur chooses to trade.
Proposition
3.2
-(ii) shows that in a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium, the probability of choosing any fixed gas fee cannot be positive, confirming that the equilibrium is not a pure strategy equilibrium. Indeed, if her competitor chooses a certain gas fee
g_{0}
with a positive probability, the arbitrageur would choose a gas fee slightly higher than
g_{0}
in order to be the first mover. Equation (
17
) provides an alternative interpretation of the response function as the expected profit of the first mover minus the expected loss of being the second mover. Proposition
3.2
-(iii) then shows that the response function at the optimal trading amount must be constant with respect to gas fee levels that the arbitrageur can possibly choose. Otherwise, the arbitrageur would simply assign more probability to gas fee levels with higher response function values. Finally, Proposition
3.2
-(iv) stipulates that if the arbitrageur considers choosing a certain gas fee
g_{0}
, she will also consider choosing any fee
g
that is lower than
g_{0}
. To understand this, if the competitor does not choose gas fees in a certain range lower than
g_{0}
, the arbitrageur can lower her gas fee without changing the priority of trading; consequently, the game is not in equilibrium.

Based on Proposition
3.2
, it suffices to consider the set of strategies

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal A}:=\big\{&(\alpha,\Phi,D_{A}):\alpha\in(0,1],\Phi\text{ admits a continuous density on its support}\\
&[\hat{g}_{L},g_{H}]\text{ for some }g_{H}\in(\hat{g}_{L},\hat{g}_{H}],\text{ and }D_{A}\text{ is continuous on the support of }\Phi\big\},\end{split}

(18)

and we show the existence and uniqueness of the symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium in the set
{\cal A}
.

Before presenting the main result, we provide some heuristic derivation to illustrate the idea of finding the symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium. Suppose
(\alpha^{*},\Phi^{*},D_{A}^{*})\in{\cal A}
is a symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium and denote by
\phi^{*}
the density of
\Phi^{*}
, and recall the response function
h(g,d_{A})
defined in (
14
). Thanks to Proposition
3.2
-(iii), for
g\in[\hat{g}_{L},g_{H}]
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial g}h(g,D_{A}^{*}(g))=\frac{\partial h}{\partial g}(g,d_{A})|_{d_{A}=D_{A}^{*}(g)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial R_{F}}{\partial g}(g,d_{A})|_{d_{A}=D_{A}^{*}(g)}+\alpha^{*}V(D_{A}^{*}(g),D_{A}^{*}(g))\phi^{*}(g),

where we use
\frac{\partial h}{\partial d_{A}}(g,d_{A})|_{d_{A}=D_{A}^{*}(g)}=0
due to (
15
).
Because
\frac{\partial R_{F}}{\partial g}(g,d_{A})=-1
, we immediately derive

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(g)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{*}V(D_{A}^{*}(g),D_{A}^{*}(g))},\quad g\in[\hat{g}_{L},g_{H}],

which stipulates that the density of the gas fee is
inversely proportional to
the first-mover advantage.
Plugging the above into the first-order condition
\frac{\partial h}{\partial d_{A}}(g,d_{A})|_{d_{A}=D_{A}^{*}(g)}=0
, and applying the change of variable
\hat{x}(z):=D_{A}^{*}(g_{H}-L_{B}z)/y_{A}
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(x(z))=\int_{0}^{z}K(\hat{x}(z),\hat{x}(\bar{z}))d\bar{z},\quad z\geq 0,

(19)

where

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(x):

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{A}}{L_{B}}\frac{\partial R_{F}}{\partial d_{A}}(g,y_{A}x)=\frac{1}{(1+x)^{2}}-O^{-1},\quad x>0,

(20)

\displaystyle K(x,\bar{x}):

\displaystyle=y_{A}\frac{\partial V}{\partial d_{A}}(y_{A}x,y_{A}\bar{x})/V(y_{A}\bar{x},y_{A}\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1+x)(1+\bar{x})(1+2\bar{x})+\bar{x}(1+\bar{x})(1+2\bar{x})}{2\bar{x}(1+x)^{2}(1+\bar{x}+x)^{2}},\quad x,\bar{x}>0.

(21)

Let us emphasize that (
19

\hat{x}(z)
nonlinearly. Thus, the existence and uniqueness of its solution is not straighforward to prove.
To show that
\hat{x}(z)
is well-defined, we need to impose the following technical assumption on the arbitrage opportunity
O
. Note that this assumption is very mild, since the size of the arbitrage opportunity is not large as illustrated by our empirical study in Section
4

Assumption 3.3
.

The arbitrage opportunity
O\leq 3

Define
\hat{z}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\hat{z}}\frac{L_{B}}{V(y_{A}\hat{x}(\bar{z}),y_{A}\hat{x}(\bar{z}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{z}=1,

we can show that there exists a unique positive continuous solution

to the ODE (

A.3
in the Appendix, which also naturally leads to a method to compute the solution numerically.

We are now ready to state the main theorem on the existence and uniqueness of equilibrium.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a unique symmetric mixed Nash equilibrium
(\alpha^{*},\Phi^{*},D_{A}^{*})
in

, which is given as follows:

(i)

(ii)

and

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\int_{0}^{(\hat{g}_{H}-\hat{g}_{L})/L_{B}}\frac{L_{B}}{V(y_{A}\hat{x}(\bar{z}),y_{A}\hat{x}(\bar{z}))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{z}<1.

(22)

In both cases,

\displaystyle D_{A}^{*}(g)=y_{A}\hat{x}\big((g_{H}-g)/L_{B}\big),~~\phi^{*}(g)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{*}V(D_{A}^{*}(g),D_{A}^{*}(g))},\quad g\in[\hat{g}_{L},g_{H}],

where
\phi^{*}
is the density of

Effect of base gas fee.

In case (i), where the base gas fee is low, the arbitrageur is still willing to trade under a small increase in the base gas fee, and therefore, the gas fee paid by the arbitrageur is increasing in the base gas fee. Moreover, the relative trading amount is independent of the base gas fee, because the base gas fee is a sunk cost as long as the arbitrageur trades and thus does not affect the optimal trading amount. In contrast, in case (ii), an increase in the base gas fee decreases both the gas fee paid by the arbitrageur and the relative trading amount, since the arbitrageur has a zero expected net profit, and an increase in the base gas fee further discourages her participation.

Effect of pool liquidity.

4.3
Justification for the assumptions

We estimate whether an arbitrage opportunity exists through the CEX price (CEX exchange rate), the pool reserves (DEX exchange rate), and the estimated base gas fee.
Recall that the base gas fee is the product of the gas used and the block’s base gas fee per gas. We use the mode of gas used (107,176) in the data as the estimated gas usage and change the base gas fee per gas from ETH to USD by
p_{A}
. Multiplying the estimated gas usage by the base gas fee per gas of the block, we can calculate whether arbitrage opportunities exist. We define a block with an arbitrage opportunity as an
arbitrage block
.

Notably, for all 78,980 blocks containing the swaps we collected, they all have only one trading direction with an arbitrage opportunity. Here, we define an arbitrage swap as a swap that occurs in a block with arbitrage opportunities, where the trade direction matches the direction of the arbitrage opportunity.

Focusing on the interactions between two arbitrageurs is reasonable.

Our model focuses on the interactions between two arbitrageurs, a setting that captures the majority of cases. As shown in Table

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a model of two arbitrageurs competing for an arbitrage opportunity on a Decentralized Exchange with Automated Market Maker, who strategically choose whether or not to participate in the arbitrage, and if so, the gas fee and the trade amount. We proved the existence and uniqueness of the mixed symmetric Nash equilibrium for this gas fee competition game, and characterized the equilibrium strategy analytically in terms of the solution to a nonlinear ODE. We identified scenarios where the arbitrageurs have a zero expected net profit and a participation probability of less than 1, or they have a positive expected net profit and will participate with certainty. Furthermore, we found that both the gas fee paid by the arbitrageurs and the trading amount relative to the pool size increase with the pool liquidity and the size of the arbitrage opportunity. Finally, using data from Binance and Uniswap, we conducted an empirical study and found that the empirical results are consistent with the testable model implications.
