Early tracheostomy in ventilated COVID-19 patients reduces incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia
Authors: Małgorzata Grotowska, Tomasz Skalec, Iga Wójtowicz, Jarosław Kędziora, Waldemar Goździk, Wiesława Duszyńska
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracheostomy can reduce mechanical ventilation (MV) duration, ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS), and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) risk in critically ill patients. The timing of tracheostomy in COVID-19 patients has been studied, but its impact on VAP incidence has rarely been analyzed. This study investigated tracheostomy timing’s impact on VAP incidence, ventilation time, ICU and hospital LOS, and mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients. It was conducted at the University Hospital in Wroclaw, Poland, from October 1, 2020, to June 30, 2021. Of 60 tracheostomized patients, 21 (35%) developed VAP. Early tracheostomy (≤ 13 days) resulted in 8/42 (19%) VAP cases, while late tracheostomy (> 13 days) had 13/18 (72%) VAP cases, showing a significantly lower VAP risk in the early group (p < 0.05). VAP incidence rates were 7.9 and 22.8 per 1000 patient-days for early and late groups, respectively. Early tracheostomy patients had shorter median MV duration (18 vs. 33 days, p < 0.05), ICU LOS (20 vs. 31 days, p < 0.05) and hospital LOS (25 vs. 47 days, p < 0.05). Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has placed a tremendous burden on healthcare systems worldwide, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs) where patients with severe respiratory failure often require prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV). One of the most common complications of mechanical ventilation is ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), which can increase morbidity and mortality, generate higher healthcare costs, and prolong the duration of ventilation and ICU stay1–3. According to newly published data, patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 are at a high risk of VAP4,5. Additionally, in our ICU, VAP occurred more frequently in patients with COVID-19 than in those without COVID-19 [52/234 (22.2%) vs. 180/1506 (11.95%), p < 0.01]6. Various preventive measures have been implemented to reduce the incidence of VAP, including the use of selective digestive decontamination (SDD) with antibiotics, oral care protocols, patient positioning and others7–10.

Several studies have reported that tracheostomy can reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay in critically ill patients, including those with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and sepsis11–13. Furthermore, tracheostomy has been suggested to reduce the risk of VAP by enabling better oral care and reducing the risk of colonization of the oropharyngeal tract with potential pathogens11. However, the timing of tracheostomy has been a controversial issue, with some studies suggesting that early tracheostomy (i.e., within the first 7 to 14 days of intubation) may be associated with a lower incidence of VAP and a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, while others have found no significant differences between early and late tracheostomy11–15.

The role of tracheostomy timing in the management of COVID-19 patients requiring mechanical ventilation has been extensively studied, primarily focusing on outcomes such as duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU and hospital lengths of stay and mortality rates16,17. However, its correlation with the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) was less frequently addressed18,19. While early tracheostomy is believed to potentially reduce VAP by facilitating earlier weaning from ventilation, evidence remains inconclusive. Some studies suggest a positive impact of early tracheostomy on VAP rates20. Other analyses report no significant effect on VAP occurrence, indicating a need for more targeted research21,22.

Recent evidence suggests that COVID-19 patients may have distinct clinical features that may affect the optimal timing of tracheostomy. For example, COVID-19 patients may have a higher risk of bleeding due to coagulopathy, which may increase the risk of complications associated with tracheostomy23,24. Moreover, COVID-19 patients may be more prone to developing severe VAP due to their immunosuppressive state and the cytokine storm that often accompanies the disease25,26. It is essential to investigate whether early tracheostomy is beneficial in COVID-19 patients.

Therefore, this study aimed to address this issue by investigating whether tracheostomy timing has an impact on the incidence of VAP and ventilation time in critically ill patients with COVID-19. Since the COVID-19 pandemic has ended, we conclude that the results of this study may have important implications for the management of other patients, especially immunosuppressed or hospitalized in the ICU.

Materials and methods

This single-center observational study was conducted at two Clinical Departments of Intensive Therapy of the University Hospital (UH) in Wroclaw, Poland, between October 1, 2020, and June 30, 2021. In this study, the medical records of all consecutive patients (n = 240) hospitalized in ICUs due to COVID-19, with positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for SARS-CoV-2 in nasopharyngeal swabs or deep tracheal aspirates were analyzed (Fig. 1). Patients without tracheostomy, patients undergoing ECMO therapy, ventilated, or hospitalized at ICU ≤ 48 h were excluded from the study. The final analysis included 60 patients who underwent tracheostomy and were ventilated for > 48 h.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki on Human Research and was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Wroclaw Medical University (WMU) (no: 576/2016). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, Bioethics Committee of WMU waived the need of obtaining informed consent.

Data collection

The following patient data were recorded: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), acute physiological and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II and sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores at admission, ventilation length before tracheostomy, total ventilation length, VAP occurrence and culture growths from endotracheal aspirate. We also documented the length of ICU hospitalization, ICU mortality, and the total hospital length of stay (LOS). VAP criteria

VAP in tracheostomized patients was defined according to the definitions established in 2013 and updated in 2015 by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for infective Ventilator associated events (VAE) as possible VAP (PVAP). The criteria applied to patients ventilated for at least 48 h and included respiratory deterioration (daily increase of positive end-expiratory pressure [PEEP] ≥ 3 cmH2O or an increase in fraction of inspired oxygen [FiO2] ≥ 20 points sustained for ≥ 2 days), body temperature disorders (> 38 °C or < 36 °C), or abnormal white blood count (≥ 12.000 cells/mm3 or ≤ 4000 cells/mm3), a new antimicrobial agent has been started and sustained/continued for ≥ 4 days, positive culture from endotracheal aspirate (> 105 colony forming units (CFU)/mL) and/or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) (> 104 CFU/mL), and chest radiography alterations for pneumonia were not included27. The risk of VAP ended with cessation of mechanical ventilation.

Microbiological qualitative and quantitative diagnosis of VAP as well as bacterial strain susceptibility and resistance mechanism assessments were carried out at the UH in Wroclaw according to the recommendation of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) and established MDR pathogen definitions28,29.

Bronchial secretions were also microbiologically diagnosed using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR ) multitest (FilmARRAY RespiratoryPanel, BioFire Diagnostics, USA).

Ventilator weaning

Weaning was conducted through CPAP with a low level of pressure support (5–8 cmH2O), followed by high-flow oxygen therapy (HFO) or standard oxygen therapy. All patients underwent routine lung ultrasound to search for lines B and consolidations, which appeared to be helpful in combination with ventilatory parameters for deciding to wean. The criteria for weaning were FIO2 > 50% O2, PEEP > 5, intact acid-base balance, and normalized cardiovascular and hemodynamic status. Weaning failure was defined as a failed spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) or reinstitution of mechanical ventilation within 2–7 days after extubation30.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the TIBCO Software Inc. (2017). Statistica (data analysis software system), version 13. The data are presented as number (percentage) for categorical variables and median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables. Tracheostomy timing was analyzed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to analyze this variable as a predictor of VAP. Youden’s index was calculated to identify the optimal cutoff-point for sensitivity and specificity of this parameter. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the distributions. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test with Yates correction or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Significance was evaluated at p < 0.05.

Of the 60 patients who underwent tracheostomy, 21 (35%) developed VAP. In our research cohort, we reported an incidence rate of VAP 13.3 per 1000 patient-days and an incidence density of VAP 14.0 per 1000 ventilator days. In the early tracheostomy group (≤ 13 days, n = 42), ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) occurred in 8 out of 42 patients (19%) 95% CI (7.2–30.9), which corresponds to 13% of the total cohort (8/60) (Table 2). In contrast, in the late tracheostomy group (> 13 days, n = 18), VAP was observed in 13 out of 18 patients (72%), 95% CI (51.5–92.9), representing 22% of the overall cohort (13/60). Notably, 38% of all VAP cases (8/21) were identified in the early tracheostomy group, while 62% (13/21) were observed in the late tracheostomy group. The analysis demonstrated that the early tracheostomy group had a significantly lower risk for VAP (p < 0.01). The incidence rate of VAP was 7.9 and 22.8/1000 patient days in the early and late tracheostomy groups, respectively. The incidence densities of VAP/1000 ventilation days amounted to 9.3 and 20.3%, respectively, for the above groups.

Patients who underwent tracheostomy ≤ 13 days after intubation in comparison to late tracheostomy group had shorter median duration of MV (18 vs. 33 days, p < 0.01), and shorter median ICU (20 vs. 31 days, p < 0.01) and hospital LOS (25 vs. 47 days, p < 0.01) (Table 2). Importantly, this association holds even though patients who underwent tracheostomy (for both groups, ≤13 days and > 13 days post-intubation) were still ventilated for nearly the same time after the procedure.

Tracheostomy performed ≤ 13 days after intubation was associated with more ventilator-free days (VFD) at 28 days. The median VFD for the early tracheostomy group was equal to 14 (IQR, 0–28) days, whereas for late tracheostomy group to 0 (IQR, 0–0) days.

Patients with early tracheostomy were characterized by higher mortality rate. It was equal to 23/42 (55%), whereas in late tracheostomy group to 5/18 (28%). However, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.10).

In our cohort of 240 Covid-19 patients, we registered only 24 survivors who were successfully weaned from ventilation and extubated. The median ventilation time was 7 (IQR 6–10) days. In our cohort of 60 tracheostomized patients, only three extubation attempts were possible (on days 7, 11, and 12 after intubation). Discussion

In the context of intensive care, early mortality among COVID-19 patients during the pandemic was significant. Studies demonstrate that median mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients tended to occur relatively early during their ICU stay, which is crucial in understanding the dynamics of care and resource allocation31,32. For those who survive the initial critical phase in the ICU, complications, such as infections, thrombosis, and bleeding, played critical roles in determining the overall prognosis. Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) remains a significant challenge in these settings. Recent research indicates that the median VAP rate among ICU patients with COVID-19 was very high, often exceeding 50%, and the severity of this complication must not be underestimated33–35. However, few studies have been conducted on VAP reduction strategies.

The main finding of our study was that early tracheostomy reduced the risk of VAP. The threshold for a markedly increased risk of VAP was over 13 days from intubation to tracheostomy. This effect is probably due to the significantly shorter length of ventilation and ICU hospitalization in early tracheostomy group. This may result from the earlier cessation of sedation, better airway control and clearance, and active rehabilitation. Importantly, these associations hold when we exclude patients with an ICU length of stay of < 13.

This conclusion is in accordance with those of other studies. Tavares et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 15 studies on early versus late tracheostomy in various ICU settings36. Their results showed the advantage of early tracheostomy, leading to a reduction in VAP, length of ICU stay, and hospital stay, without a statistical difference in mortality rate. Quinn et al. analyzed 19 studies and concluded that early tracheostomy (variously defined according to the study) reduced not only the length of ventilation and ICU stay but also short-term mortality37. Interestingly, the results provided by other studies vary markedly. The analysis completed by Deng et al. showed that early tracheostomy reduced the length of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay without affecting mortality and the rate of VAP38. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Wang et al. did not demonstrate a significant reduction in mortality, VAP, mechanical ventilation duration, or length of ICU and hospital stay14.

The incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia in our tracheostomized cohort during the COVID-19 period exceeded the rates previously reported in a pre-COVID study from 201839. In the earlier analysis, we observed a VAP rate of 8.7% (50/578 episodes) with an incidence of 8.6 per 1000 patient days and a mean incidence density of 10.8 per 1000 ventilaton days. The heightened incidence in the current cohort could be attributed to chronic immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to co-infections among COVID-19 patients. Notably, the late tracheostomy group experienced a VAP incidence of 20.3 per 1000 ventilation days, a figure alarmingly close to the 24.5 per 1000 ventilator-days reported in critically ill cancer patients by Stoclin et al. in a 12-year retrospective study40. This comparison underscores the vulnerability of COVID-19 patients. Conversely, despite having a high percentage of VAP, the early tracheostomy group showed an incidence rate and density comparable to our pre-COVID findings. Our study results indicate that immunosuppressed patients, including critically ill ICU patients whose condition lowers immunity, may particularly benefit from early tracheotomy.

Although some studies have shown that early tracheostomy may be advantageous, there is still much controversy regarding its timing12,14,38,41. Various clinical trials have addressed this issue; however, definitive conclusions are yet to be reached, possibly because of the extensive heterogeneity among ICU patients. This diversity includes variations in patient demographics, underlying comorbidities, organ failure, and the nature of their critical illnesses. An individualized approach tailored to each patient is crucial when determining the timing of tracheostomy. Our findings indicate that earlier intervention resulted in a greater decrease in the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia, duration of ventilation, and presumably a reduction in mortality when tracheostomy was performed ≤ 8 days (the last finding was not statistically significant). The reduction in the last parameter is strongly convincing in decision making. However, early decisions regarding tracheostomy are often challenging owing to the unpredictable duration of mechanical ventilation required. In particular, patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), who have a low respiratory reserve and may require prone positioning, present additional complexities that could delay tracheostomy. Prolonged mechanical ventilation increases the risk of VAP, subsequently complicating the decision to perform tracheostomy.

It is also noteworthy that, in the late tracheotomy group, a significant majority of the procedures were performed using surgical technique. This suggests that these patients likely had more challenging anatomical conditions for tracheotomy or severe coagulopathy, which delayed the decision to perform the procedure. Moreover, the need for a surgical technique probably not only postponed the tracheostomy but also prolonged the time of ventilation and ICU stay, as described by Beltrame et al.42.

Mortality outcomes demonstrated variability across studies. Notably, in our study, the early tracheostomy group exhibited higher mortality rate than the late tracheostomy group did. It is important to recognize that while some studies have identified a decrease in mortality with early tracheostomy, they have not consistently shown a benefit in reducing the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia43,44. This discrepancy may be attributed to the multitude of factors affecting mortality in critically ill patients, which extend beyond infections to include various complications arising from the critical illness itself.

As expected, our study identified a predominance of gram-negative rods in the pathogenesis of ventilator-associated pneumonia. The most prevalent pathogens were Acinetobacter baumannii, or Klebsiella pneumoniae MDR or XDR along with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) resistance mechanism. This finding aligns with earlier research from our institution, which also reported Acinetobacter baumannii XDR as the principal causative agent in the majority of VAP cases from 2011 to 201839,45. Our findings align with those from other centers4,35. However, the published results exhibit significant variability. In certain centers, Pseudomonas aeruginosa was more prevalent (only one case in our study), Acinetobacter spp was absent from tracheal aspirates, or gram-positive bacteria were the dominant pathogens34,46,47. This variability underscores the importance of close monitoring of local pathogen trends to guide appropriate antibiotic strategies in each ICU for effective infection control and antibiotic stewardship.

The notable prevalence of drug-resistant bacteria among COVID-19 patients raises significant concerns, particularly due to the widespread overuse of antibiotics. Upon admission, all patients were administered antibiotics; however, de-escalation of antimicrobial therapy likely occurred at a suboptimal pace. Consequently, careful prescription and use of antibiotics is necessary to prevent the growth and spread of drug-resistant bacteria.

Limitations of the study

Our study had several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, which may not represent broader ICU practices as indicated by variations in VAP incidence, density, and pathogenesis across different facilities. Additionally, the cohort was heterogeneous; while all patients were affected by COVID-19, their underlying health conditions varied significantly. This variability was not accounted for in our analysis, as the focus of the study was not on underlying diseases, but rather on the immediate clinical outcomes associated with tracheostomy timing in COVID-19 patients.

Moreover, late tracheostomy group was heterogeneous in terms of tracheostomy technique. Additionally, immortal time bias is a critical consideration when analyzing the timing of tracheostomy and most importantly, its impact on ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Patients with late tracheostomy have a systematically longer exposure to the risk of VAP. These two factors could have influenced the occurrence of VAP in late tracheostomy group.

The study showed that VAP in COVID-19 patients, irrespective of tracheostomy timing, was caused mainly by multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria (Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae). This finding was alarming and consistent with the microbiological profile of our department during the pandemic, supporting the need for constant surveillance after the COVID-19 pandemic.
