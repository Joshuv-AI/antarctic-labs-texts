The degree of polymerization influences the effects of pectic oligosaccharide on intestinal histomorphology, barrier function and microbial community structure in broilers
Authors: Chong Ling, Jiarong Fang, Songtao Liu, Shitang Zhang, Furui Liang, Hui Ye, Qingyun Cao, Jianjun Zuo, Weiwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate whether the degree of polymerization (DP) influences intestinal health benefits of pectic oligosaccharide (POS) in broilers. A total of 360 one-day-old female Huanghua yellow-feathered broilers were randomly allocated into 3 groups (10 replicates/group), which received a basal diet alone or supplemented with 600 mg/kg low‑DP (DP = 10) POS (LPOS) or 600 mg/kg high‑DP (DP = 17) POS (HPOS) over a 21‑day experimental period. The results showed that supplemental LPOS and HPOS tended to reduce (P = 0.081) average daily feed intake, but tended to increase (P < 0.10) relative duodenal weight and length along with relative ileal length. They also increased (P < 0.05) relative cecal weight and length, ileal villus height (VH), as well as duodenal and ileal VH to crypt depth ratio (VCR). LPOS showed a greater (P < 0.05) effect on ileal VCR, whilst HPOS additionally increased (P < 0.05) duodenal VH. Both LPOS and HPOS reduced (P < 0.05) serum endotoxin level, with HPOS also lowering (P < 0.05) serum D-lactic acid level. LPOS upregulated (P < 0.05) duodenal Zonula Occludens (ZO)-1 expression, while HPOS upregulated (P < 0.05) ileal Claudin-1 and ZO-1 expression. Cecal microbial analysis demonstrated that HPOS elevated (P < 0.05) most α‑diversity indices, including Simpson index, Shannon index, Chao1 index, and phylogenetic diversity, whereas LPOS only increased (P < 0.05) Simpson index. Notably, LPOS, rather than HPOS, reduced (P < 0.05) the within‑group β‑diversity index (heterogeneity) of cecal microbial communities, and caused greater microbial separation (P < 0.05) from control group. Further analysis indicated that HPOS enriched (P < 0.05) a greater variety of beneficial bacteria in the cecum than LPOS. Moreover, LPOS increased (P < 0.05) cecal isovaleric acid concentration, while HPOS elevated (P < 0.05) cecal concentrations of propionic, valeric, isobutyric, and isovaleric acids. In summary, LPOS and HPOS differentially promoted intestinal health in broilers, with HPOS being superior to LPOS in improving intestinal barrier and microbial community structure. These findings highlight an influence of the DP on the efficacy of POS, supporting the efficient application of POS in poultry production.

Introduction

The intestine serves not only as the primary organ for digestion and absorption in poultry, but also contributes to maintaining systemic health via its barrier function (Ducatelle et al., 2023). In modern intensive poultry production, numerous dietary and environmental factors can directly or indirectly impair intestinal health, subsequently compromising poultry production performance (Aminullah et al., 2026). Although antibiotics were once widely used to improve poultry gut health, the resultant increases in antimicrobial resistance and antibiotic residues have raised prominent public health concerns. Under the global trend toward banning or restricting the usage of in-feed antibiotics, developing functional oligosaccharides capable of promoting intestinal health has become an important direction in poultry nutrition research (Prusty et al., 2026).

As an emerging class of functional oligosaccharides, pectic oligosaccharide (POS) is obtained from pectin degradation and composed of 2-20 D-galacturonic acid residues linked by glycosidic bonds (Wang et al., 2026). POS may benefit poultry gut health through various bioactivities especially the prebiotic effect (Wang et al., 2026), as POS withstands the actions of host digestive enzymes and then selectively stimulates the growth of specific beneficial bacteria to optimize gut microbial composition (De Alencar et al., 2025). Notably, bacterial fermentation of POS generates certain beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which can reduce intestinal luminal pH and repress harmful bacterial growth, thus further improving intestinal microenvironment (Wang et al., 2026). Moreover, SCFAs serve as energy substrates for intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and as signaling molecules that activate specific signaling pathways to promote tight junction (TJ) protein expression, ultimately sustaining intestinal development and barrier function (Aminullah et al., 2026). In recent years, the application of POS in poultry nutrition has attracted increasing attention. For instance, it was reported that supplemental POS ameliorated growth performance and duodenal villus morphology, as well as elevated cecal isobutyric acid concentration in broiler chickens (Wang et al., 2019). Likewise, Mao et al. (2021) detected elevations in cecal abundances of certain SCFA‑producing bacteria and jejunal expression of specific TJ proteins in broiler breeders fed with POS, thus benefiting intestinal barrier function and laying performance.

Although the above studies have revealed the beneficial effects of POS on poultry gut health potentially through the prebiotic action, the relationships between in vivo efficacy of POS and its structural characteristics remain poorly understood. The degree of polymerization (DP) is a critical structural characteristic influencing the bioactivities of POS (Wang et al., 2026), because it determines the chain length, molecular weight (MW), and solubility of POS. Since different bacteria vary in their capacity to degrade oligosaccharides (De Alencar et al., 2025), POS with different DPs may be fermented differently in the gut. In vitro human fecal fermentation experiments have evidenced that lower-MW POS exerted stronger effects in facilitating beneficial bacterial growth and SCFA production (Yeung et al., 2021; Vásquez et al., 2024), whilst conflicting results have been described elsewhere (Wilkowska et al., 2025). Consequently, the influences of DP (a determinant of MW) on the in vivo efficacy of POS deserve investigation. We hypothesized that different DPs of POS interact differentially with gut microbiota, conferring distinct benefits for poultry gut health. Accordingly, this study aimed to compare the effects of two POS fractions with low and high average DPs (2-10 and 11-20, respectively) on intestinal histomorphology, barrier function and microbial community structure in broilers. The findings were expected to provide a basis for the efficient application of POS as an improver of intestinal health in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Preparation of POS

According to our previous study (Wang et al., 2026), POS with two different DPs were prepared through enzymatically hydrolyzing citrus-derived polygalacturonic acid (> 90% purity; Yihe Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) that acts as a backbone of pectin, followed by purification via the ethanol fractional precipitation method. The purities of the above POS preparations were determined as 96.1% and 97.2%, respectively. Based on the weight-average MW determined by GPC-20A gel permeation chromatography (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), the average DPs of the above POS preparations were calculated as 10 and 17, respectively, with corresponding polydispersity indexes calculated as 1.27 and 1.08, suggesting narrow MW distributions for both. The average DPs of the two POS preparations were 10 and 17, falling within the ranges of 2-10 and 11-20, respectively. Thus, they were designated as low-average-DP POS (LPOS) and high-average-DP POS (HPOS), respectively.

Animals and experimental design

The animal experimental protocols of the present study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of South China Agricultural University (Guangzhou, China). A total of 360 one-day-old female Huanghua yellow-feathered broilers were randomly assigned into 3 groups. Each group consisted of 10 replicates with 12 birds per replicate. The initial body weight (34.91 ± 0.10 g) was comparable across all replicates. Birds in control group received a basal diet, while those in treatment groups received a basal diet with supplementation of 600 mg/kg LPOS and HPOS, respectively. The additive level of POS was selected in accordance with our previous study (Ling et al., 2026). The experimental period lasted 21 d, since prebiotics are more likely to confer growth benefit for broilers during the early growth stage, when intestinal structure remains incompletely developed and microbial community is still maturing (Rezaei et al., 2015; Shani and Irani, 2024). The composition of basal diet is detailed in Table 1 (Ling et al., 2026), with its nutrient levels established referring to the Chinese Feeding Standard of Yellow-feathered Chickens (NY/T 3645-2020). During the experimental period, all birds were reared in metallic cages, with unlimited access to feed and water. Illumination was provided continuously (24 h/d). Sample collection

At 21 d of age, one bird per replicate cage was randomly chosen and sacrificed. Blood was collected from the jugular vein, after which the abdominal cavity was rapidly incised to expose the intestine. The proximal intestine (duodenum) and distal intestine (ileum and cecum) were excised to measure their relative weight, relative length, and density. The middle segments of duodenal and ileal segments were separately rinsed with phosphate buffered saline to remove luminal contents, followed by division into two portions. One portion was immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation, while the other was snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Additionally, cecal digesta from each bird was collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent storage at -80 °C.

Growth performance measurement

At 21 d of age, feed intake and live body weight of broilers in each replicate cage were recorded. The final body weight (FBW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), along with feed conversion ratio (FCR) during 1–21 d of age were calculated.

Serum indice assay

Blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resultant serum samples were then harvested. Serum endotoxin level was determined using a commercial kit that employs a quantitative chromogenic assay (Bioendo Technology Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). Serum D‑lactic acid (DLA) was quantified with a colorimetric commercial kit (Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

Measurements of relative weight, relative length, and density of the intestine

The collected duodenum, ileum, and cecum were carefully trimmed of mesenteric fat, and gently handled to prevent tissue distortion. The weight and length of each intestinal segment were measured to calculate relative weight and relative length, which were calculated as intestinal segment weight (g) and length (cm) per 100 g live body weight, respectively. Subsequently, intestinal density, namely the ratio of intestinal weight (g) to intestinal length (cm), was calculated.

Determination of intestinal villus morphology

Duodenal and ileal tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin-eosin, and then coverslipped after dehydration. For each stained section, representative villi with intact structure were chosen for determining intestinal villus morphology under a Moticam2306 optical microscope (Motic Industrial Group Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). Villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the VH/CD ratio (VCR) were then measured using ImageJ software.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from duodenal and ileal tissues using the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The concentration and purity of RNA samples were measured with an UV spectrophotometer Nano-Drop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, reverse transcription was carried out with the HiScript II qRT SuperMix for qPCR kit (Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) to synthesize cDNA, which was then diluted with RNase‑free water and used as the PCR template. Gut microbial analysis

Bacterial genomic DNA was isolated from cecal digesta using the CretMag™ Power Soil DNA Kit (Cretaceous Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). DNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and its integrity was verified via agarose gel electrophoresis. Using the purified DNA as the template, the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was then amplified with the forward primer GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and reverse primer GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT. The resulting amplicons were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis and purified with the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Bioscience, Union City, CA, USA). Purified products were quantified with a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS® Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for MGI V2 (Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Following quality control, paired-end sequencing was performed on the MGI platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). After quality filtering and length trimming, the raw tags were subjected to chimera removal. The resulting effective tags were then denoised to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASV) for taxonomic annotation. Subsequently, we evaluated microbial α-diversity using multiple indicators, including the observed ASV, Chao1 and ACE indexes (reflecting richness), Shannon and Simpson indexes (reflecting richness and evenness), as well as phylogenetic diversity (PD) whole tree (reflecting evolutionary breadth). Note: PD whole tree was defined as the total branch length of the phylogenetic tree connecting all species in the community. In addition to α-diversity, microbial β‑diversity analysis, including principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA), β-diversity index assessment, and analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) between groups were also performed. Finally, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) combined with effect size measurement (LEfSe) was employed to identify differentially abundant taxa (LDA > 2.0, P < 0.05) among groups.

Quantification of cecal SCFAs

The concentrations of SCFAs (acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, valeric acid, isobutyric acid, isovaleric acid, and total SCFAs) in cecal digesta were measured using a Shimadzu Nexis GC‑2030 gas chromatograph (Kyoto, Japan) with 2‑ethylbutyric acid serving as the internal standard, following the procedures described in our previous research (Ren et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Growth performance was measured with each replicate pen as the experimental unit, whereas the other indicators were measured with each bird as the experimental unit. Gut microbial analysis-related data were subjected to the Kruskal-Wallis test; while data for other indicators were analyzed using the one-way ANOVA of SPSS 25.0, followed by assessment of the differences among groups using the Duncan's multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 was considered a trend toward significance.

Discussion

Intestinal development can be largely evaluated by morphological indices, such as relative intestinal weight and length, as well as intestinal villus morphology (e.g., VH, CD, and VCR) (Zhang et al., 2022). In this study, both LPOS and HPOS increased relative weight and length of the cecum, as well as tended to increase those of the duodenum coupled with relative length of the ileum. They also elevated duodenal VCR along with ileal VH and VCR. These observations suggested that LPOS and HPOS facilitated intestinal development in broilers, which were similar to previous reports on POS (Wang et al., 2019; Ling et al., 2026) in broilers. Although the microbial community in broiler duodenum is relatively low in abundance, it exhibits a complex interaction network (Feng et al., 2023). Within this network, specific beneficial bacteria, especially Lactobacillus spp. that predominate in the duodenum (Stamilla et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2026), are vital for sustaining broiler gut health (Liao et al., 2020; Deng et al., 2026). In this study, LPOS and HPOS might act in part as substrates to stimulate duodenal Lactobacillus growth (Yeung et al., 2021; Vásquez et al., 2024), subsequently boosting duodenal development in broilers (Liao et al., 2020). In fact, recent evidences have revealed the effectiveness of prebiotics in animal duodenum (Stanley et al., 2025). Given the longer chain length of HPOS than LPOS, HPOS might also remain relatively intact in the duodenum, where it possibly functioned as a ligand for specific signal receptors and then activated proliferation‑related signaling pathway in IECs (Zhao et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). This could explain why HPOS elevated duodenal VH more markedly than LPOS. The ileum and cecum are densely populated with microbes (Feng et al., 2023). Upon arrival at these segments, LPOS and HPOS were likely fermented by beneficial anaerobes to generate abundant metabolites, particularly SCFAs (Wang et al., 2026). These metabolites could supply energy to IECs and activate G‑protein‑coupled receptors that drive IEC proliferation (Aminullah et al., 2026), probably mediating the promoting effect of LPOS and HPOS on ileal and cecal development in broilers. Interestingly, LPOS was less effective than HPOS in increasing relative ileal weight but showed a superiority in increasing ileal VCR. We postulated that the shorter chain of LPOS rendered it more readily fermented by gut microbiota (Pan et al., 2026). The rapidly produced metabolites, especially SCFAs, might act earlier and more promptly on ileal villi, which exhibit high turnover rates (Neurath et al., 2025), thus leading to a more pronounced improvement in ileal villus morphology. Conversely, HPOS, due to its longer chain, was fermented more slowly but over a longer duration (Yeung et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2026). This action might enable the fermentation metabolites to exert more sustained effects on the ileum, thus better supporting the gradual process of increasing relative ileal weight.

Intestinal physical barrier consists of IECs and intercellular junctional complexes, among which TJ proteins, including Claudins, Occludin, and ZOs, serve as the core structural components (Neurath et al., 2025). POS supplementation has been reported to upregulate jejunal and ileal TJ protein expression in broilers (Mao et al., 2021; Ling et al., 2026). Similarly, this study showed that LPOS upregulated duodenal ZO‑1 expression, while HPOS upregulated ileal Claudin‑1 and ZO‑1 expression, demonstrating that these two oligosaccharides reinforced intestinal barrier integrity in a segment‑dependent manner. We speculated that the lower MW of LPOS rendered a relatively larger proportion susceptible to fermentation by duodenal Lactobacillus, a primary fermenter abundant in the duodenum (Stamilla et al., 2021). This fermentation would elevate duodenal lactate level and then promote TJ protein expression in this segment (Huang et al., 2021). On the contrary, HPOS, due to its higher MW, might preferentially reach the hindgut (ileum and cecum), where more diverse and abundant microbial communities exist. In such microenvironments, HPOS might be extensively fermented and favor the production of end‑products (SCFAs) (Tingirikari, 2018; Zhao et al., 2025b). This production could be partially supported by cecal SCFA data and might subsequently promote ileal TJ protein expression.

TJ determines intestinal permeability and restrains the translocation of luminal bacterial products into blood (Neurath et al., 2025). Accordingly, serum bacterial products, such as endotoxin and DLA, are the biomarkers of intestinal permeability (Tan et al., 2025). It was reported that POS alleviated lipopolysaccharide‑induced elevation in serum endotoxin level in broilers (Ling et al., 2026). Likewise, this study revealed that both LPOS and HPOS reduced serum endotoxin level, with HPOS also lowering serum DLA level. These results indicated that HPOS conferred an advantage over LPOS in reducing intestinal permeability. Considering the higher microbial density in the ileum than the duodenum (Feng et al., 2023), we deduced that HPOS‑elicited upregulation of ileal TJ proteins (Claudin‑1 and ZO‑1) better reduced intestinal permeability and limited microbial product translocation when compared with LPOS‑elicited upregulation of duodenal ZO‑1.

Gut microbiota exerts close linkages with intestinal development and function of poultry (Liao et al., 2020; Ducatelle et al., 2023). Among the various intestinal compartments, the cecum exhibits the highest microbial diversity and abundance in chickens (Stamilla et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2023). In this study, cecal microbial α-diversity, including richness and evenness, was first assessed. The results indicated that LPOS increased only the Simpson index, while HPOS elevated the observed ASV, Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson index. These results revealed a superiority of HPOS over LPOS in increasing cecal microbial richness and evenness, which could better maintain microbial stability (Spragge et al., 2023). Similarly, an ex vivo fermentation study indicated that high‑DP fructan was more effective than low‑DP fructan in increasing human gut microbial α-diversity, with these inulin fractions showing greater efficacy than fructooligosaccharide (Astó et al., 2019). We also found that HPOS (rather than LPOS) elevated the PD whole tree, an indicator of phylogenetic breadth (Liu et al., 2026b), suggesting that cecal microbiota in HPOS group was more evolutionarily diverse. Integrating all α‑diversity indexes, we inferred that HPOS was more beneficial than LPOS for weakening the predominance of originally dominant taxa in broiler cecum. Such action could propel the release and differentiation of ecological niches, allowing the growth of relatively rare taxa with distinct evolutionary backgrounds (Yao et al., 2025). This inference was supported by cecal microbial distribution data, which disclosed that LPOS reduced the abundance of the top‑ranked genus (Alistipes) but elevated the abundances of second‑ to fifth‑ranked genera (Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Negativibacillus, and Phascolarctobacterium). In comparison, HPOS decreased the abundances of top three genera while elevating the abundances of most genera ranked fourth and beyond. A potential explanation was that oligosaccharides with longer chain were slowly and progressively fermented, which provided stepwise substrates for microbial taxa occupying different niches (Pascale et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2026), supporting a richer and more diverse community. This could interpret the observed advantage of HPOS over LPOS in elevating cecal microbial α‑diversity.

Unlike α‑diversity, β‑diversity reflects the dissimilarity between communities (Liao et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2023). In this study, we employed multiple metrics to evaluate cecal microbial β‑diversity. PCoA and ANOSIM revealed a greater separation of cecal microbiota in LPOS group from control group than that in HPOS group, indicating that POS, especially LPOS, reshaped cecal microbiota. This was analogous to a previous finding that fructooligosaccharide reshaped human gut microbiota more profoundly than fructan (Astó et al., 2019). Further analysis showed that LPOS, instead of HPOS, lowered the within‑group β‑diversity index of cecal microbiota, implying that LPOS exerted a stronger and directional reshaping effect that diminished dispersion or heterogeneity among individuals within the group. It was potential that the shorter chain of LPOS allowed it to rapidly stimulate several specific microbes prevalent across different individuals, thus driving cecal microbial communities of different individuals toward a relatively homogeneous state (Van den Abbeele et al., 2023). A similar finding was described for alginate oligosaccharide (Liu et al., 2026a) and pectin (Van den Abbeele et al., 2023). Although the directional reshaping effect rendered cecal microbiota of LPOS group more distinct from that of control group, this effect might weaken microbial α‑diversity enhancement (as reflected by the elevation of only Simpson index in LPOS group). A similar phenomenon was also observed in other biological communities (He et al., 2025). In contrast to LPOS, the longer chain of HPOS rendered it unlikely to be rapidly and predominantly utilized by a few microbial taxa, thus maintaining inter‑individual heterogeneity of microbial communities within the group (as reflected by the unchanged β‑diversity index in HPOS group).

LEfSe analysis revealed that HPOS enriched a broader range of bacterial taxa in broiler cecum than LPOS, further supporting the advantage of HPOS over LPOS in enhancing cecal microbial α‑diversity. Analogously, Xiao et al. (2025) found that high‑DP fructan was more effective than fructooligosaccharide in enriching specific bacteria and increasing microbial richness in mice colon. Notably, the enriched taxa in LPOS group included several gut health‑promoting bacteria, such as the family Prevotellaceae (Tajasuwan et al., 2023), the genera Phascolarctobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Alloprevotella (Tajasuwan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025a), together with several putative probiotics (e.g., Bifidobacterium pullorum, Anaerotignum lactatifermentans, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, and Enterococcus faecium). Likewise, Mao et al. (2021) reported that POS supplementation increased cecal Phascolarctobacterium abundance in broiler breeders. Since Phascolarctobacterium and Alloprevotella are potential producers of isovaleric acid (Fan et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025), their increased abundances might contribute to the elevation in cecal isovaleric acid content observed in LPOS group. Relative to LPOS, HPOS enriched more beneficial bacteria capable of directly improving intestinal homeostasis or generating SCFAs, including the phylum Actinobacteriota (Binda et al., 2018), the order Lactobacillales (Zhang et al., 2025a), accompanied by the genera Olsenella (Chen et al., 2022), Butyricimonas (Liu et al., 2018a), Lactobacillus (Liao et al., 2020), Romboutsia (Liu et al., 2018a), and Lachnospira (Bang et al., 2018). Furthermore, HPOS enriched plentiful probiotic candidates, among which Lactobacillus aviarius (Hong et al., 2021), Lactobacillus agilis (Hong et al., 2021), Lactobacillus helveticus (Zhang et al., 2025c), Bacteroides acidifaciens (Li et al., 2022), Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (Zhang et al., 2025b), and Bacteroides barnesiae (Pu et al., 2022) could promote intestinal development or protect intestinal morphology. Strikingly, HPOS‑induced enrichments of Lachnospira and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron aligned with previous reports showing that in vitro fermentation of pectic polysaccharides markedly increased these bacteria (Bang et al., 2018; Tingirikari et al., 2019).

As the key metabolites derived from gut microbiota, SCFAs are mainly produced in the hindgut and can sustain intestinal development of chickens throughout the growth period (Liao et al., 2020). A previous study disclosed that alginate oligosaccharide elevated intestinal SCFA contents in mice, with isovaleric acid being one of the two major products (Teng et al., 2024). In this study, LPOS increased cecal isovaleric acid concentration, while HPOS elevated cecal concentrations of propionic, valeric, isobutyric, and isovaleric acids. These findings confirmed the superiority of HPOS over LPOS in optimizing cecal fermentation pattern, which could be associated with the observed enhancement of cecal microbial α-diversity in HPOS group. Similarly, Wallace et al. (2026) observed that xylooligosaccharide with higher DP was more conducive to improving broiler cecal fermentation. It appeared that longer chain oligosaccharides allowed a greater proportion to reach the hindgut and continuously nourish the resident microbiota (Tingirikari, 2018; Pan et al., 2026), therefore better modulating cecal fermentation pattern. It is known that propionic, valeric, isobutyric, and isovaleric acids impel intestinal development in animals (Liu et al., 2018b; Zhou et al., 2019). Consequently, the increased cecal isovaleric acid concentration might partially account for the observed elevations in relative cecal weight and length caused by both LPOS and HPOS. Likewise, the elevated concentrations of cecal propionic, valeric, and isobutyric acids could also contribute to these developmental changes of the cecum in HPOS group. Intriguingly, LPOS did not broadly increase cecal SCFA concentrations, although it enriched several beneficial bacteria in the cecum. It was assumed that LPOS caused only a moderate increase in SCFA production, and most of the produced SCFAs were subsequently absorbed by cecal epithelial cells, which might be responsible for the unaltered concentrations of the primary SCFAs (acetic, propionic, butyric, and valeric acids) in cecal lumen of LPOS group (Montoya et al., 2016; Sakata, 2019). Comparatively, HPOS likely triggered a greater production of SCFAs. Even if a large portion of these SCFAs were absorbed by cecal epithelial cells, the remaining SCFAs might still be sufficient to elevate their concentrations in cecal lumen, presumably explaining the increases in multiple SCFAs of cecal lumen observed in HPOS group.

Strikingly, although LPOS and HPOS improved broiler gut health, they did not significantly improve broiler growth performance. This result contrasted with previous findings that supplemental 400 mg/kg POS tended to increase ADG and reduce ADFI in aflatoxin B₁‑exposed broilers from 1-21 d of age (Ye et al., 2025), and that 600 mg/kg POS increased FBW and ADG in lipopolysaccharide‑challenged broilers over the same period (Ling et al., 2026). Such discrepancies could be attributed to variations in the physiological states of broilers. It has been recognized that functional feed additives prefer to benefit growth performance of poultry under stressed state (Abdelli et al., 2021). Regarding POS, it might be more favorable in eliciting growth‑promoting effects when broilers were subjected to specific stressors (Chen et al., 2017; Ling et al., 2026). Under non‑stressed state, however, the margin for POS‑mediated growth improvement was relatively narrow, as broilers might have already attained optimal performance. This assumption could be supported by previous reports, which indicated that certain oligosaccharides improved intestinal health (including histomorphology and barrier function) without translating into growth performance gains in broilers (Wang et al., 2020; Lan et al., 2024; Ruangpanit et al., 2025). Nevertheless, our results revealed that LPOS and HPOS tended to lower ADFI and numerically reduced FCR of broilers from 1-21 d of age, implying a potential of them to reduce feed costs during the early growth phase of broilers. Because only two POS preparations with different DPs were successfully obtained in this study, we compared the effects of these two POS preparations, which did not differ markedly with respect to many of the measured parameters. Future studies are needed to compare the application effects of POS with a broader range of DPs in poultry, particularly in birds subjected to intestinal injury stress. Besides, dose-response studies are warranted to compare the effects of POS preparations differing in DP and to determine the optimal dose for each preparation.

Conclusions

Supplementation of both LPOS and HPOS ameliorated intestinal histomorphology in broilers, but HPOS was more effective than LPOS in strengthening intestinal barrier integrity and optimizing gut microbial community structure. Specifically, LPOS reduced inter‑individual heterogeneity of cecal microbial communities potentially via exerting a directional reshaping effect. By contrast, HPOS maintained inter‑individual heterogeneity of cecal microbial communities, as well as exhibited a superiority over LPOS in increasing microbial α-diversity and enriching beneficial bacteria in the cecum, thereby better favoring the production of certain SCFAs (propionic, valeric, and isobutyric acids). These findings emphasize an influence of DP on the efficacy of POS in promoting broiler gut health, which is expected to provide a basis for the application of DP‑optimized POS in poultry production.
