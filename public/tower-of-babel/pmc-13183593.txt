Life's Essential 8, genetic susceptibility, and risk of cognitive impairment: results from an elder population‐based cohort study
Authors: Yutong Wang, Bowen Lei, Ke Jiang, Xin Chen, Yang Qu, Xin Song, Xinyang Dui, Qingwen Zhao, XunYing Zhao, Yanqiu Zou, Tianpei Ma, Di Zhang, Jinyu Xiao, Mengyu Fan, Jiaqiang Liao, Lu Long, Tao Zhang, Chuan Wang, Jiayuan Li, Xia Jiang, Ben Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) framework has been linked to cognitive impairment, but evidence in Chinese older adults remains limited.METHODSThis cross‐sectional study included 7469 adults aged ≥60 years from the West China Health and Aging Cohort. Modified Poisson regression with robust error variance was used to examine the associations of LE8 scores and genetic risk, as well as their joint associations, with cognitive impairment.RESULTSCompared with the low LE8 score group, the high LE8 score group had a prevalence ratio (PR) of 0.65 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.56 to 0.76) for cognitive impairment. A joint association was observed between LE8 and genetic risk, with the highest PR of 1.19 (95% CI: 1.12 to 1.27) in the low LE8 and high‐genetic‐risk group.DISCUSSIONOptimal cardiovascular health was associated with lower cognitive impairment and attenuation of genetic risks among older adults in western China.Keywords: cardiovascular health, cognitive impairment, genetic risk, Life's Essential 8

BACKGROUND

Cognitive decline represents a significant public health challenge as populations age worldwide. 1 , 2 While some cognitive changes naturally accompany aging, severe cognitive deterioration is not an inevitable consequence of growing older. 3 Despite a lack of curative treatments or therapies that substantially delay cognitive impairment, 4 , 5 accumulating evidence suggests that many factors associated with cognitive decline are potentially modifiable through targeted lifestyle and clinical interventions. 6

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been consistently associated with cognitive impairment. Evidence shows elevated cognitive impairment among individuals with coronary heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, 7 likely operating through multiple pathophysiological pathways. 8 More importantly, both CVD and cognitive impairment share common risk factors – hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and suboptimal lifestyle behaviors. 1 , 9 , 10 Such an interconnection suggests that comprehensive cardiovascular health (CVH) promotion may also be relevant to cognitive health.

The Life's Essential 8 (LE8) framework, developed by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2022, serves as both a comprehensive CVH metric and a health promotion strategy. It incorporates four healthy factors – body mass index (BMI), lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure – alongside four healthy behaviors – diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep. 11 Since its introduction, LE8 has received increasing attention, and accumulating evidence suggests that LE8 is associated with cognitive function and long‐term cognitive outcomes.

Cross‐sectional analyses using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey have shown that higher LE8 scores are associated with better cognitive performance. 12 , 13 Prospective cohort studies, including those based on the UK Biobank (UKB) and the All of Us Research Program, further report that favorable LE8 profiles in midlife are associated with lower risks of cognitive impairment‐related outcomes. 14 , 15 However, evidence from Chinese populations remains limited. The few available studies, all based on relatively small samples (n < 1500), have reported inconsistent associations between LE8 and cognitive outcomes, particularly across age groups. 16 , 17 Larger studies are therefore needed to clarify this relationship in the Chinese elderly population. Cognitive impairment has both environmental and genetic determinants, with the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele representing the strongest genetic risk factor, accounting for approximately 5% of lifetime cognitive decline risk and 4% of Alzheimer's disease cases. 18 , 19 , 20 Research from the UKB revealed that APOE ε4 carriers with low LE8 scores had substantially higher relative risks of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), compared with non‐carriers with high scores, while carriers maintaining high LE8 scores showed attenuated relative risks, 21 suggesting that CVH may modify the effect of genetic susceptibility.

Beyond APOE ε4, polygenic risk scores (PRSs) provide a quantitative approach to capturing the aggregated genetic susceptibility. Nevertheless, racial differences have been reported in the associations between CVH metrics and cognitive function, highlighting the need for population‐specific investigations. 22 However, whether LE8 interacts with overall genetic susceptibility to influence cognitive impairment among Chinese older adults remains unclear.

Therefore, utilizing data from the West China Health and Aging Cohort (WCHAC), this study aimed to examine the association between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment among Chinese older adults in a cross‐sectional design. Additionally, we constructed a PRS for cognitive impairment to evaluate whether the association between LE8 and cognitive impairment varied across different levels of genetic susceptibility and to explore the joint associations of CVH and genetic risk with cognitive impairment.

METHODS

Study design and population

Data were extracted from WCHAC, a bidirectional cohort study comprising 10,626 participants aged 54 years and older from the service area of the Hongguang Community Health Service Center in Pidu District, Chengdu, China. The WCHAC aimed to investigate multidimensional health‐related and genetic determinants of various diseases. The baseline data of WCHAC consisted of three main components: face‐to‐face questionnaires, anthropometry, and biological specimens from May 2022 to December 2023. Cognitive function assessment was a core component of the baseline survey and was planned for all participants. The baseline assessment was conducted in two visits, and the second visit (within a week of the first visit) included cognitive function assessment and detailed lifestyle behavior questionnaires required for LE8 calculation. Detailed study designs and methods have been reported elsewhere. 23 The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Fourth Hospital of Sichuan University (HXSY‐EC‐2022034). All participants signed informed consent.

For the current study, 8275 participants with available cognitive function assessment data were initially included. We excluded participants absent from two or more LE8 components (n = 191); those with self‐reported or case‐recorded cancer (n = 325); those with missing education level (n = 63), as this would affect the judgment of the outcome; and individuals with impaired activities of daily living (n = 227). Individuals with pre‐existing CVDs were not excluded to ensure the community‐based sample remained representative of the general Chinese elderly population, in which CVD is highly prevalent. 24 Ultimately, 7469 participants were included, of which 6703 successfully completed Illumina's Asian Screening Array Multiple Disease (ASAMD) chip and passed quality control to be included in subsequent genetic analyses (Figure 1).

Measures

Assessment of LE8

The LE8 framework encompasses two primary dimensions: healthy behaviors and health factors. The healthy behaviors dimension includes key elements such as diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep health, while the health factors dimension focuses on measurable indicators like BMI, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure. In the current study, each of these eight metrics was systematically assessed and assigned a score ranging from 0 to 100 points, with adjustments made for medication use in blood lipids and blood pressure, as described in greater detail in Table S1. The unweighted average score of the eight components makes it possible to generate the LE8 score, which ranges from 0 to 100. Following the AHA established guidelines, the resulting LE8 scores were categorized into three distinct groups: high (80 to 100 points), moderate (50 to 79 points), and low (0 to 49 points). 11 ,

Assessment of cognitive impairment

From 2022 to 2023, the Chinese version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) was administered by trained evaluators to assess the cognitive functioning of participants. The MMSE comprises 24 items, with a maximum score of 30, and evaluates five primary cognitive domains: orientation (10 points), registration (3 points), attention and calculation (5 points), recall (3 points), and language and praxis (9 points). 25 This instrument has been widely used in population‐based studies of older Chinese adults, with previous evidence supporting its validity and reliability. 26 , 27 We categorized the outcomes into normal cognition and cognitive impairment, where the cutoff scores of cognitive impairment were determined based on the participants’ level of education, with thresholds set at 16/17 for illiterate individuals, 19/20 for those with primary education, and 23/24 for those with a middle school education or higher. 28

Calculation of polygenic risk scores

The methodology for genetic testing and quality control has been described elsewhere. 23 Among the 7469 participants, 6703 had complete imputed genotype data and were included in analyses involving the PRS. This study calculated PRSs using the largest cognitive function genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary data available to date, which comprised 257,841 individuals of European ancestry and identified 225 genetic susceptibility loci significantly associated with cognitive function (p < 5×10−8). 29 PRSs for cognitive impairment were calculated using the clumping and thresholding method with PLINK. 30 To identify the optimal PRS, phenotypic data were regressed on PRSs computed at various p value thresholds for the inclusion of genetic variants. 31 The PRSs constructed using a p value threshold of 0.05 exhibited the strongest association with cognitive impairment, with each standard deviation increase in PRS associated with a 13% higher prevalence of cognitive impairment (prevalence ratio [PR]: 1.13; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.09 to 1.18, p < 0.001). This optimal PRS was subsequently z‐score standardized and incorporated into all downstream analyses.

Assessment of covariates

The analysis controlled for potential confounders of the LE8–cognition relationship. A detailed rationale for the selection of each covariate is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, and income. Alcohol consumption was defined as drinking more than once a month during the past year. Anxiety or depression was defined as having a score of 5 or higher on either the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) or the 15‐item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15). 32 , 33 The study also adjusted for participants' history of CVDs, including coronary heart disease and stroke, as determined by self‐reported diagnoses and medical records. Furthermore, genetic associations were examined with additional adjustment for the first five population principal components (PCs 1 to 5).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed for all variables, where continuous variables satisfying normal distribution were expressed as mean (± SD); non‐normally distributed variables were described as median (interquartile range [IQR]); categorical variables were reported as frequency (proportion). Group differences between cognitive impairment and normal cognition were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables and the Kruskal–Wallis Test for non‐normally distributed variables. For categorical variables, comparisons were made using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable modified Poisson regression with robust error variance 34 was employed to estimate PRs and 95% CIs for the associations between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment. LE8 scores were analyzed both categorically (low, moderate, and high groups) and continuously (per 10‐point increase), with the low LE8 group serving as the reference category. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 included additional adjustments for ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, and income. Model 3 was further adjusted for anxiety or depression and CVDs. Trend tests were performed by introducing the median LE8 score of each category as a continuous variable in the models. To examine potential linear and non‐linear associations, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed based on Model 3, with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the LE8 score distribution. Subgroup analyses were performed to verify the robustness and potential variations of the main results, stratifying participants by sex (male or female), median age (<68 years or ≥68 years), educational attainment (illiterate, primary, or junior high and higher), and a history of CVD. Multiplicative interactions were assessed by including interaction terms between LE8 scores (continuous variable) and age (dichotomous), sex, and educational attainment (three categories) in the regression models. Likelihood ratio tests compared models with and without interaction terms to obtain overall interaction p values. For educational attainment, specific interaction coefficient p values were reported for individual category comparisons (e.g., illiterate vs primary) to identify subgroup‐specific effects.

To explore the association of LE8 scores, genetic risk, and cognitive impairment, we conducted stratified analyses based on PRSs of cognitive impairment and further assessed the joint association and potential interaction between LE8 scores and genetic risk. Participants were stratified into three genetic risk groups (low, moderate, and high) based on PRS tertiles. Separate modified Poisson regression models were then constructed within each genetic risk group, with covariates adjusted as previously. For the joint association analysis, PRSs and LE8 scores were categorized into tertiles to allow assessment of potential dose–response patterns across a 3 × 3 matrix of genetic risk (low, moderate, and high) and LE8 categories (high, moderate, and low). Using the “low genetic risk–high LE8” group as the reference, PRs and 95% CIs for cognitive impairment were calculated for each combination. Additionally, for interaction analysis, participants were categorized into four groups based on dichotomized PRS and LE8 to facilitate interpretation and improve the stability of interaction estimates: (1) low genetic risk–high LE8 (PRS < mean; LE8 ≥ 80); (2) low genetic risk–low LE8 (PRS < mean; LE8 < 80); (3) high genetic risk–high LE8 (PRS ≥ mean; LE8 ≥ 80), and (4) high genetic risk–low LE8 (PRS ≥ mean; LE8 < 80). Multiplicative interaction was assessed by including an interaction term between LE8 scores and genetic risk in Model 3 and estimating the corresponding PRs and 95% CIs. Additive interaction was evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion (AP), and the synergy index (S), 35 along with their 95% CIs.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess potential selection bias due to missing cognitive assessment data, we compared baseline characteristics between participants with cognitive assessment and those without. We further conducted an inverse probability weighting (IPW) analysis to account for differential availability of cognitive assessment data. Stabilized inverse probability weights were calculated based on baseline sociodemographic characteristics and cardiometabolic health indicators and applied to the primary regression models to re‐estimate the LE8–cognitive impairment association.

RESULTS

Descriptive analysis of baseline characteristics

The characteristics of participants with cognitive impairment and normal cognition are compared in Table 1. The study included 7469 participants with a median age of 68 years (IQR: 65 to 72) among whom 1982 (26.5%) had cognitive impairment, and 4109 (55.0%) were females. The median LE8 score was 68.8 (IQR: 60.0, 76.9), with 524 participants (7.0%) in the low, 5643 (75.6%) in the moderate, and 1302 (17.4%) in the high LE8 score groups. Compared with normal participants, those with cognitive impairment were generally older, more likely to be female, and unmarried, had lower LE8 scores, and reported lower income and educational attainment, suggesting differences in sociodemographic and CVH profiles. They were also less likely to consume alcohol, experience anxiety or depression, or use cardiovascular medications. Furthermore, they scored lower on measures of blood pressure, diet, and physical activity but had better smoking scores, suggesting that differences between groups were more pronounced in selected behavioral and CVH components rather than uniformly across all LE8 domains. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in ethnicity, CVD prevalence, use of hypoglycemic medication, sleep score, BMI score, lipid score, or glycemic score (all p > 0.05).

Association between LE8 score and cognitive impairment

Table 2 presents the associations between LE8 score groups, per 10‐point increase in LE8 and cognitive impairment. The proportions of cognitive impairment in the low, moderate, and high LE8 scores were 33.6%, 26.7%, and 23.0%, respectively, suggesting a graded inverse relationship between overall LE8 status and cognitive impairment prevalence. In Model 1 adjusted for age and sex, the prevalence of cognitive impairment was significantly lower in the moderate (PR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.62 to 0.80) and high (PR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.50 to 0.69) LE8 score groups compared with the low LE8 score group. This association remained significant after further adjustment of ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, income, history of anxiety or depression, and CVDs (moderate: PR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.65 to 0.84; high: PR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.56 to 0.76).

As shown in Table S5, no statistically significant interactions were observed between LE8 score and genetic risk on either the additive (RERI = 0.04, 95% CI: −0.41 to 0.35; AP = 0.02, 95% CI: −0.24 to 0.24; S = 1.07, 95% CI: 0.58 to 5.15) or the multiplicative scales (0.98, 95% CI: 0.72 to 1.33), suggesting that the associations of LE8 with cognitive impairment are largely independent of genetic risk.

Sensitivity analysis

The distinct characteristics of participants with available cognitive assessment data suggest potential selection bias (Table S6). However, the IPW‐weighted estimates were largely consistent with the primary results. Specifically, each 10‐point increase in LE8 score was associated with a lower prevalence of cognitive impairment (PR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.86 to 0.92), and participants with moderate and high LE8 scores had lower PRs compared with those with low LE8 scores (PR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.64 to 0.87; and PR: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.52 to 0.76, respectively) (Table S7).

DISCUSSION

This study found that higher LE8 CVH scores were associated with better cognitive performance and lower cognitive impairment prevalence in Chinese older adults. This association was consistent across sex and age groups, with more pronounced inverse associations observed among those with junior high education and higher. Across genetic risk strata, more favorable CVH was associated with a lower prevalence of cognitive impairment. In addition, higher CVH scores were associated with attenuation of the association between genetic risk and cognitive impairment, though no significant interaction was observed between CVH and genetic risk.

CVH demonstrates significant associations with cognitive performance. Previous research documented that lower LE8 scores correlated with diminished cognitive function, 7 , 36 , 37 and the current study corroborates this finding in a Chinese elderly population. Additionally, LE8 components substantially overlap with seven of the 14 dementia risk factors identified by the 2024 Lancet Commission (hypertension, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, diabetes, excessive alcohol consumption, and high cholesterol), 38 further validating the importance of maintaining elevated LE8 scores in reducing cognitive impairment risk. However, upon separate analysis of individual LE8 components, behavioral factors (physical activity, diet) exhibited stronger associations with cognitive impairment than healthy factors, potentially reflecting the fundamental role these factors play in geriatric cognitive function. Meta‐analyses examining physical activity and dementia have suggested that engagement in regular physical activities correlates with reduced incidence of both all‐cause dementia and Alzheimer's disease, with associations persisting during extended follow‐up periods even after adjusting for potential reverse causation effects. 39 , 40 Dietary‐related randomized trials have also demonstrated that some dietary supplements (e.g., olive oil and nuts) are associated with cognitive performance and related pathophysiological processes (e.g., amyloid beta [Aβ] 42/total tau [t‐tau] ratio, cerebral perfusion). 41 , 42

Our analysis revealed an approximately linear relationship between LE8 scores and cognitive function without significant threshold effects. Each 10‐point increase in LE8 was associated with a 2% to 3% lower prevalence of cognitive impairment, suggesting that more favorable CVH is linked to better cognitive function. The inverse association remained consistent across sex and age groups, with no significant demographic interactions observed. However, education significantly modified this relationship, with stronger inverse associations between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment among individuals with junior high education or above compared with those who were illiterate. This suggests that education may enhance lifestyle benefits through improved cognitive reserve or better health behavior adherence. 43 These findings support maintaining optimal CVH as a broadly beneficial approach to cognitive preservation, particularly relevant given China's rapidly aging population. Future interventions should prioritize lower‐educated populations through tailored strategies, such as simplifying health information and enhancing community support, to improve effectiveness and promote health equity.

The precise mechanisms underlying the association between LE8 and cognitive impairment remain incompletely elucidated. Several plausible pathways may explain these findings. First, healthy factors of LE8 influence cognitive impairment through multiple pathways, including modulation of cerebrovascular pathology, involvement in Aβ metabolism, enhancement of neuroinflammation, exacerbation of lipid peroxidation, disruption of insulin signaling pathways, and alteration of neuronal membrane fluidity and synaptic function, collectively impacting brain tissue and ultimately leading to cognitive decline. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia accelerate small‐vessel lesion formation and white matter hyperintensities, with poorer LE8 scores associated with increased white matter hyperintensity accumulation and greater declines in total gray matter and hippocampal volume. These structural changes strongly correlate with cognitive impairment. 36 Second, behavioral LE8 components may confer protection through multiple pathways: Regular physical activity stimulates brain‐derived neurotrophic factor secretion, enhancing neuroplasticity, 48 , 49 healthy dietary patterns provide antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory substances that attenuate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, 50 and adequate sleep facilitates Aβ clearance. 51 Third, these factors likely operate synergistically, exemplified by physical activity and dietary quality jointly improving glucolipid metabolism, thereby optimizing vascular health and brain function. Additionally, body weight management may preserve cognitive function by reducing systemic inflammatory responses and insulin resistance. 52 , 53 , 54

CVH and genetic risk jointly influence cognitive impairment in Chinese older adults, with the highest risk observed in individuals exhibiting both high genetic susceptibility and poor CVH. As the first researchers to examine LE8–cognitive impairment associations across genetic backgrounds in this population, we found that the inverse association between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment was most pronounced among individuals with high genetic risk, followed by those with moderate and low genetic risk. The stronger association between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment among high‐genetic‐risk individuals may reflect dynamic balance between pathological burden and compensatory potential, where LE8 improves cerebral perfusion and inhibits neuroinflammation more effectively during early pathological stages. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 Crucially, analysis of the joint effects of CVH and genetic risk on cognitive impairment revealed that the adverse effects of genetic susceptibility could be partially attenuated by maintaining optimal CVH levels. Wang et al., based on cohort data from 126,785 participants in the UKB, found through 13 years of follow‐up that compared with participants who did not carry the APOE ε4 allele and had higher LE8 scores, among participants carrying the APOE ε4 allele, those with lower LE8 scores had a 169% increased risk of MCI, while those with higher LE8 scores had only an 89% increased risk of MCI. 21 Our study in Chinese elderly populations showed consistent patterns: Compared with individuals with high LE8 scores and low genetic risk, those with high genetic risk demonstrated a 6% increased prevalence of cognitive impairment when they had high LE8 scores and a notably higher 19% increased prevalence when they had low LE8 scores. Furthermore, our study found no significant interactions between LE8 and genetic risk, consistent with other Chinese elderly studies. 59 , 60 This absence may reflect independent pathways where genetic risk operates through molecular mechanisms like Aβ deposition, 61 while LE8 provides protection through vascular and metabolic regulation. 21 , 37 Additionally, our sample size (n = 6703) may limit the detection of weak interactions, where larger studies (n > 280,000) found significant interactions. 62 , 63 Interactions may also vary by cognitive impairment phenotype, as coronary disease patients showed LE8–genetic interactions differed between dementia subtypes. 64

This study has several strengths. We systematically evaluated the relationship between LE8 scores and cognitive impairment in Chinese elderly populations, providing evidence consistent with a protective role of optimal CVH in relation to cognitive decline. By integrating cognitive impairment PRSs with LE8 assessments, we first revealed the combined associations of genetic susceptibility and LE8 on cognitive function in Asian populations. Our findings indicated that individuals with high genetic risk and low LE8 scores exhibited the highest prevalence of cognitive impairment. In contrast, among those at high genetic risk, higher LE8 scores were associated with a weaker association between genetic susceptibility and cognitive impairment, highlighting the potential relevance of CVH metrics for risk stratification and precision‐oriented prevention strategies.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional nature of this study precludes the establishment of definitive causal relationships between LE8 and cognitive impairment. The potential for reverse causality – wherein individuals with early‐stage cognitive decline may be less likely to maintain ideal CVH – cannot be entirely ruled out. However, our findings provide a critical snapshot of these associations in a large‐scale Chinese population, offering an important foundation for ongoing longitudinal follow‐up within the WCHAC cohort. Second, selection bias due to missing cognitive assessment data cannot be completely ruled out; however, IPW‐based sensitivity analyses showed consistent results with the main analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings. Third, the PRS was constructed using GWAS summary statistics from European ancestry populations, whereas the target cohort consisted of individuals of Chinese descent. Differences in linkage disequilibrium structure, allele frequency distributions, and potentially distinct causal variant architectures between European and East Asian populations may result in reduced accuracy of effect size estimates, thereby limiting the generalizability and predictive performance of the PRS in this cohort. Future studies based on large‐scale, ancestry‐specific GWASs in East Asian populations are needed to improve the accuracy and clinical utility of PRSs in this demographic. Lastly, behavioral components of LE8 were assessed using self‐reported questionnaires and are therefore subject to recall and reporting bias. Given the composite nature of the LE8 score and the cross‐sectional study design, the direction and magnitude of potential bias cannot be fully determined, complicating interpretation of the observed associations. Future studies incorporating objective measures, such as wearable devices for monitoring physical activity and sleep, may help reduce reporting bias and improve measurement precision

In summary, this study revealed a significant linear negative correlation between the LE8 score and cognitive impairment among Chinese older adults. More favorable CVH was consistently associated with a lower prevalence of cognitive impairment and may attenuate the adverse effects of genetic risk.
