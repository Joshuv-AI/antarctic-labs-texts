Eric Godden, Jacquie Groenewegen, Michael Wheeler, Matthew K.X.J. Pan

Abstract
. This work investigates how robot-mediated physicality influences the perception of social–physical interactions with virtual characters. ETHOS (Encountered-Type Haptics for On-demand Social interaction) is an encountered-type haptic display that integrates a torque-controlled manipulator and interchangeable props with a VR headset to enable three gestures: object handovers, fist bumps, and high fives. We conducted a user study to examine how ETHOS adds physicality to virtual character interactions and how this affects presence, realism, enjoyment, and connection metrics. Each participant experienced one interaction under three conditions: no physicality (NP), static physicality (SP), and dynamic physicality (DP). SP extended the purely virtual baseline (NP) by introducing tangible props for direct contact, while DP further incorporated motion and impact forces to emulate natural touch. Results show presence increased stepwise from NP to SP to DP. Realism, enjoyment, and connection also improved with added physicality, though differences between SP and DP were not significant. Comfort remained consistent across conditions, indicating no added psychological friction. These findings demonstrate the experiential value of ETHOS and motivate the integration of encountered-type haptics into socially meaningful VR experiences.

1.
INTRODUCTION

Virtual reality (VR) offers immersive environments that support novel forms of interaction and engagement, yet these experiences remain dominated by visual and auditory feedback, with limited rendering of physicality
(
40
)
. This absence of realistic touch constrains the kinds of interactions VR can convincingly support and reduces its ability to replicate natural experiences. While most existing haptic devices deliver feedback through a single modality
(
8
)
, a promising but underexplored approach is the use of social–physical interactions mediated through human–robot interaction (HRI). By integrating the physical dynamics of contact with the social context of human exchange, social–physical HRI provides a holistic framework for recreating authentic, multimodal interactions with virtual characters and environments.

To explore this prospect, we developed ETHOS (Encountered-Type Haptics for On-demand Social interaction), a system that integrates a torque-controlled 7-DoF robotic manipulator and interchangeable props with a headset-mediated VR experience to render interpersonal gestures—object handovers, fist bumps, and high fives. This work aims to explore the impact of this system on immersion and interpersonal engagement with a virtual character, and to investigate the potential for recreating meaningful social-physical interactions in VR. To examine how such interactions influence user experience, we conducted a user study in which participants engaged with a virtual character under three conditions: no physicality (NP), static physicality (SP), and dynamic physicality (DP). These conditions isolate the role of tangible contact, ranging from purely virtual to progressively more lifelike motion and impact. We measured experiential outcomes, including presence, realism, enjoyability, comfort, and connection to the virtual character, to evaluate whether adding physicality enriches engagement. By grounding this evaluation in user perception rather than only technical performance, we demonstrate the potential of ETHOS to support more authentic and socially meaningful interactions in VR.

2.
BACKGROUND

Research on VR interaction has largely focused on visual and auditory channels, with touch remaining comparatively underexplored. Vibrotactile controllers (e.g., Meta Quest, HTC Vive) provide limited cues that rarely extend beyond surface vibrations
(
41
)
, affecting the place and plausibility illusions that users experience in virtual environments as theorized by Slater
(
36
)
. More sophisticated approaches, such as wearable haptic gloves, mid-air ultrasound feedback, and shape-changing or weight-shifting devices, broaden the design space but often experience a tradeoff between fidelity and versatility of feedback
(
27
)
. Encountered-type haptic displays (ETHDs) offer an alternative, aligning physical props with virtual representations to evoke naturalistic contact
(
17
;
1
)
. While ETHDs have shown promise for environmental realism and object interaction, their extension to socially meaningful interpersonal encounters has only recently been considered.

Social–physical interactions represent a critical avenue for recreating meaningful human experiences through HRI. Prior work shows the value of combining social engagement with physical embodiment across assistive, instructional, and affective domains, yet their use in immersive contexts remains limited. While VR can efficiently simulate diverse scenarios, it often lacks realistic physicality. Social–physical HRI offers a means of addressing this gap, with encountered-type haptic displays (ETHDs) providing a promising solution by enabling tangible contact in VR and supporting more natural, socially significant experiences.

2.1.
Social-Physical Human-Robot Interaction

Human–robot interaction (HRI) research provides insights into how physicality and sociality can be combined to create richer experiences. Traditionally, HRI has been studied through either the
physical
dimension—low-level dynamics of contact such as force exchange and control for tasks like handovers
(
5
)
or collaborative manipulation
(
2
)
—or the
social
dimension, which examines how behaviours such as gaze, gesture, or proxemics shape perception and trust
(
32
)
. Integrating these perspectives yields what we term
social–physical interactions
: exchanges where physical contact and social meaning are intertwined. This holistic approach has been explored in assistive, instructional, and affective contexts.

In assistive and instructional domains, combining physical interaction with social engagement has been shown to enhance both performance and motivation. For example, Fitter et al.
(
10
)
used a Baxter robot to create exercise games for older adults, finding that activities blending physical contact and social cues were most engaging. Granados et al.
(
15
)
applied a similar strategy in robotic dance training, where adaptive behaviour improved skill learning. In affective domains, the physical act itself carries social meaning. Block et al.
(
4
)
proposed design principles for robotic hugging that combine physical qualities (e.g., softness, warmth) with social adaptation (e.g., adjusting to user size) to improve experience. Okamura et al.
(
28
)
demonstrated how high-five gestures, when implemented in robots, embody both physical and social dimensions, enhancing engagement and communicative value.

2.2.
Encountered-Type Haptic Displays (ETHDs)

ETHDs are “systems capable of positioning all or part of themselves at an encountered location, enabling the user to voluntarily elicit haptic feedback with the environment at the appropriate time and place”
(
24
)
. In practice, they colocate physical props with virtual representations to provide passive haptic feedback
(
17
)
. This strategy reduces system complexity compared to active force rendering while delivering contact that feels authentic and immersive. ETHDs have been implemented using human operators
(
6
)
, drones
(
1
)
, and robotic manipulators, the latter offering precise positioning and stable performance through contact. Their external grounding frees users from wearable hardware during free motion while ensuring reliable feedback at first contact. Despite these advantages, most ETHD systems remain limited to rendering static objects or surfaces
(
26
;
39
)
. Some expand flexibility with interchangeable endpoints
(
3
)
or detachable task-relevant props
(
23
)
, but few extend toward social–physical interactions where realism and social meaning are both essential.

2.3.
Toward Social–Physical Interactions in VR

This gap motivates our work. We examine how varying levels of robot-mediated physicality, delivered through ETHOS (Encountered-Type Haptics for On-demand Social interaction), influence interpersonal encounters in VR. ETHOS enables the social-physical interactions of object handovers, fist bumps, and high fives with a virtual character. We assess experiential outcomes—presence, realism, enjoyability, comfort, and character connection—measures that are central to VR research
(
36
;
9
)
yet rarely studied in the context of social–physical haptics. Through this study, we aim to characterize the perceptual value of embodied social touch in VR and advance ETHDs toward dynamic, socially meaningful interactions.

4.
SYSTEM DESIGN

ETHOS (Encountered-Type Haptics for On-demand Social interaction) is designed to support virtual interactions that are both physically precise and socially meaningful. The system integrates a robotic manipulator with a VR environment by registering the robot’s coordinate frame to the virtual world using fiducial markers tracked by the headset’s onboard cameras, ensuring consistent spatial alignment. Extending prior encountered-type haptic displays, ETHOS introduces dynamic physicality—moving props and delivering impact forces—while requiring less specialized hardware than many existing approaches. Unlike typical HRI systems, which prioritize transparency through shared intent and awareness
(
21
)
, ETHOS deliberately conceals the robot behind the virtual experience, allowing users to focus on the interaction with the avatar rather than the machine. This hidden embodiment places greater demands on precise synchronization between physical and virtual domains to preserve both safety and experiential fidelity. Figure
1
illustrates ETHOS in action, highlighting the supported interactions across real and virtual spaces. Figure
2
depicts the principal components of ETHOS, spanning both the physical and virtual subsystems.

The setup integrates a VR headset for immersive visualization and user hand tracking, ChArUco-based registration to establish a shared anchor between the physical and virtual environments, a robotic manipulator equipped with interchangeable physical props to render on-demand physical contact, and a force sensor to monitor interaction forces.

4.1.
Physical Subsystem

The physical subsystem consists of interchangeable props mounted on the end effector of a torque-controlled LBR iiwa 7 R800 robotic manipulator (KUKA, Germany). The robot is mounted horizontally on a pedestal and controlled in real time at 1000 Hz via the Fast Robot Interface (FRI)
(
33
)
, connected to a desktop workstation (Intel Core i7-14700, 4.2 GHz, 32 GB RAM) running Ubuntu 22.04 with a real-time kernel and integrated with ROS 2 Humble. A six-axis Mini40 force–torque sensor (ATI Industrial Automation, USA) with a quick-release mount enables secure prop attachment and rapid swapping, with internal compensation applied to ensure accurate contact force measurements.

Gesture-specific force thresholds and custom props are key to recreating physicality in the targeted social–physical interactions. Pilot studies established thresholds that produced natural-feeling contact: 7.5 N for object handovers and 15.0 N for both fist bumps and high fives, reflecting the stronger impacts of the latter gestures. Exceeding these thresholds signals completion of the interaction, ensuring consistent and believable feedback.

To match the selected interactions, three props have been developed: a 3D-printed baton for handovers, a silicone fist for fist bumps, and a silicone open hand for high fives. Both hand models are cast using Dragon Skin FX-Pro

in their respective configurations and reinforced with galvanized steel wire to emulate internal bone structure

Beyond the technical implementation, the virtual subsystem conveys the social dimension of interaction. Because the physical robot remains hidden from the user’s view, all social cues are expressed through the virtual character’s behaviour. By leveraging an anthropomorphic character, the system provides recognizable non-verbal signals that mitigate challenges often faced with non-anthropomorphic robots
(
31
)
. Three cues are implemented: a greeting gesture (wave and eye contact upon entry), a readiness cue (raising the arm to an interaction-appropriate pose, such as an outstretched arm holding a baton for handover
(
38
)
), and a gaze pattern (looking first to the interaction site, then back to the user) following Moon et al.
(
25
)
. Together, these behaviours supply socially recognizable signals that enhance the naturalness and believability of physical interactions with a virtual character.

5.
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

The main focus of this work is the experiential evaluation of social–physical HRI in VR through engaging interpersonal interactions. To investigate this, we conducted a user study examining how varying levels of physicality influence ETHOS’s encountered-type interactions. The following subsections describe the experimental conditions, evaluation metrics, participant details, and study procedure.

5.1.
Experimental Conditions

To operationalize our experimental design, we selected three interpersonal interactions — object handover, fist bump, and high five—which together define the
interaction type
factor. These gestures are brief, socially recognizable, and among the most feasible for replication with a robot. More complex interactions, such as hugging or handshaking, demand nuanced force modulation and physical adaptation that remain challenging for robotic systems to reproduce convincingly
(
4
;
19
)
. By focusing on these “low-hanging fruit” interactions, this first implementation of ETHOS targets scenarios that balance social meaning with technical feasibility.

Each interaction was tested under three physicality conditions varying the behaviour of the robot in creating a physical interaction with a user:

(1)

No Physicality (NP)
interactions were purely virtual, providing no haptic feedback and establishing a baseline for interaction with a virtual character.

(2)

Static Physicality (SP)
introduced stationary alignment of a physical prop with its virtual counterpart, creating a tangible contact point.

(3)

Dynamic Physicality (DP)
extended the static condition by adding motion and impact behaviours common in human–human interaction
(
38
)
. Inspired by the trajectory generation of Pan et al.
(
29
)
, the robot generates an initial midpoint trajectory toward the user’s hand on approach to the interaction and then blends this trajectory to their current hand position. This behaviour creates an interaction-specific contact point and more closely resembles the true (i.e., human-human) form of these interactions.

With three interactions each with three physicality conditions, we chose to perform a mixed factorial design user study. Each participant performed one of the interactions (between-subjects) across the three physicality conditions (within-subjects).

5.2.
Evaluation Metrics

To assess experiential outcomes, we first measured “presence”, a central concept in VR and mediated interaction research. Presence relates to the psychological perception of “being there” in the mediated environment, and numerous definitions exist. Lee
(
20
)
offers a unifying definition:
a psychological state in which virtual objects are experienced as actual objects in either sensory or nonsensory ways
. Lee further identifies sub-dimensions of physical, social, and self-presence. Building on this definition, Makransky et al.
(
22
)
developed the validated 15-item Multimodal Presence Scale (MPS), which captures these sub-dimensions and their attributes (Table
1
).

5.3.
Participants

This study was reviewed and approved by the Queen’s University General Research Ethics Board. A priori power analyses were conducted to determine the sample size required for this study. With
\alpha=0.05
, we concluded that a sample size of at least 54 (i.e., 18 participants per interaction type) was needed to detect a moderate effect size (
f=0.25
) with
90\%
power (
1-\beta
), a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, and a nonsphericity correction of
\epsilon=0.75
. Recruitment was performed within Queen’s University. In total, 55 participants (24 females and 31 males), aged 18-53 [
M=26.84,SD=7.97
] were recruited for this study. One participant’s results were omitted from this study due to technical difficulties during their trial. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation; they were informed that involvement in the study was voluntary and retained the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Permission to collect both video recordings and direct quotations from the experimental sessions was also obtained. No incentives or compensation were offered for participation.

5.4.
Procedure

Each session began with an overview of the study and a walkthrough of the interaction procedure. Participants reviewed and signed an informed consent form, then completed a pre-study demographic questionnaire. They were fitted with the VR headset and noise-cancelling headphones (to mask noise generated by the robot during movement) and given time to acclimate to the virtual environment and hand-tracking system. Following acclimation, physical–virtual registration was performed as described in Section
4.3
.
Participants then completed three trials, one in each physicality condition, of the assigned interaction type. The order of conditions was counterbalanced using a Latin square design to mitigate order effects. For each trial, participants were guided to a safe interaction location (i.e., outside the robot’s workspace while retaining reachability). The virtual character then entered the scene and initiated the interaction. Upon completion, participants completed the questionnaire inside the VR environment, consistent with prior findings that in-situ administration yields more reliable presence measures
(
34
)
. After all three trials and questionnaires, participants provided open-ended feedback. Each session lasted approximately 30 minutes.

p=0.231
), nor were any between-subject effects of interaction type (
p=0.797
).

7.
DISCUSSION

By introducing social–physical HRIs into VR, ETHOS advances encountered-type haptics beyond object rendering toward interactions that are both socially meaningful and physically engaging. Unlike most ETHDs, which emphasize static props or inert surfaces, ETHOS employs a robotic manipulator to dynamically position custom props, enabling naturalistic engagement with virtual characters. This study contributes the first experiential evaluation of such interactions, shifting attention from system-level synchronization to the impact of physicality on user experience.

7.1.
Effects of Physicality

The three physicality conditions allowed us to evaluate social–physical interactions across increasing levels of embodiment: NP as a purely virtual baseline, SP as conventional ETHD implementation, and DP as our dynamic extension. Results show a clear hierarchy of outcomes, with NP performing worst, SP producing substantial improvements, and DP offering partial but inconsistent gains.

7.1.1.
No Physicality

The NP condition performed the worst across nearly all evaluated metrics. In terms of presence, both the total score and all sub-dimensions were significantly lower compared to conditions with true physicality (SP and DP). Given this work’s focus on social–physical interactions—experiences that are simultaneously physically engaging and socially meaningful—the poor performance of the purely virtual baseline underscores the importance of incorporating not only the character’s social behaviours, but also the realism of physicality within these interactions. This outcome is likely attributable to breaks in presence (BIPs) during the virtual interaction, where attention shifts away from the virtual experience to the physical world
(
14
)
. Participants reported that “the lack of physical stimuli quickly removed the feeling of reality from the virtual space” and that the NP condition “reminded me that the simulation was not real,” reinforcing that unmet expectations of physicality can lead to BIPs that negatively impact experience.

Beyond presence, realism in the handover and fist bump interactions also scored lowest in the NP condition. These poor realism scores further highlight the critical role of physicality in sustaining believable social–physical interactions. Consistent with the enjoyability and character connection results, participants described the purely virtual interaction as “unsatisfying” and “disappointing,” underscoring that the NP condition is not sufficient to support meaningful social–physical interaction.

7.1.2.
Static Physicality

The SP condition represents a first step toward recreating the true physicality of the selected interactions. Here, the role of physical props is isolated, as each remains statically aligned while participants engage with the virtual character. Presence scores, both total and across all sub-dimensions, improved significantly compared to the NP condition. This finding aligns with prior work, such as Gibbs et al.
(
12
)
, which demonstrated that introducing haptic feedback to a purely virtual baseline enhances presence in VR. Participant accounts help explain this effect, noting that the physical aspect of the interactions “helped ground me within the virtual environment” and that it “really felt like I was existing and interacting with the virtual environment” when engaging with the props.

Improvements in physical and social presence were expected, as tangible contact aligns with the intended form of these interactions. More notably, however, the increase in self-presence represents a particularly meaningful outcome. As outlined in Table
1
, self-presence encompasses bodily and emotional connectivity as well as the sense of bodily extension. Enhancements in this dimension suggest that physical rendering not only strengthens the authenticity of the interaction but also deepens the user’s perceived role within it. Without this heightened sense of self, the potential for truly meaningful social–physical interaction is diminished. This increase in self-presence aligns with prior findings that multisensory integration (e.g. visuotactile feedback) strengthens embodiment subcomponents such as body ownership and agency
(
11
)
. In VR studies comparing visual-only experience versus added tactile feedback, participants appear to show stronger illusions of being touched and higher embodiment ratings
(
35
)
, supporting the idea that physical rendering deepens users’ perceived role in interactions.

Significantly higher scores in realism, enjoyability, and character connection further support the improvement of experience within the SP condition as compared to NP. Participants emphasized this effect, noting that “the calibration [between virtual and physical worlds] was quite accurate, making the interaction feel real”, that “adding a physical component to my interaction with the character made our interaction much more enjoyable”, and that the physical contact with the prop "made me feel more connected to the virtual character." Taken together, these results demonstrate that the SP condition substantially improves the interaction experience and underscores the suitability of encountered-type haptics for recreating social–physical interactions.

7.1.3.
Dynamic Physicality

As the selected social–physical interactions inherently involve non-static contact—such as the motion and timing required for an object handover, as noted by Strabala et al.
(
38
)
—DP extends SP by introducing both motion and impact. Although DP was expected to yield broader improvements over SP, significant differences were observed only for the total presence score. This suggests that dynamic rendering may facilitate a stronger sense of physiological connection with the virtual world, yet the absence of corresponding improvements across other measures indicates that further refinement is needed in our dynamic approach. Participant feedback reflected this variability: while some described the DP interactions as “seamless” or “firm,” others reported experiences that felt “jarring” or “misaligned.”

One contributing factor may be that we employed a common trajectory generation strategy across all interaction types
(
29
)
, rather than tailoring trajectories to the unique dynamics of each experience. This likely reduced robustness and naturalism across the set of interactions. Additionally, interaction completion was detected using simple force thresholds, which may have produced unnatural sensations in the more complex contact phases of dynamic interaction. Prior work, such as Chan et al.
(
5
)
, highlights the importance of modelling transient grip forces in human–human handovers, suggesting that discrete thresholds for contact recognition are not well-suited to capture the timing and compliance required for natural recreations of these interactions.

Overall, the DP condition still improved the interaction experience compared to the virtual baseline, but the findings highlight the need for further refinement of our DP condition to fully realize the benefits of dynamic social–physical interaction.

7.1.4.
Comfort Consistent Across Conditions

Comfort scores remained stable across all physicality conditions, indicating that the addition of physical props and robot-mediated interaction did not diminish user experience. Although practical equivalence between conditions was not confirmed through the TOST analysis, the absence of significant differences is indicative that ETHOS did not introduce detrimental psychological friction simply by virtue of the robot’s physical presence. This consistency is important, as it suggests that encountered-type interactions can be integrated into virtual experiences without compromising comfort.

7.2.
Effects of Interaction Type

Across object handover, fist bump, and high five, interaction type had no main effect on presence, enjoyability, comfort, or avatar connection. This generalization suggests ETHOS is broadly applicable to short, socially meaningful gestures.

The social–physical interactions evaluated in this work were object handovers, fist bumps, and high-fives. To the authors’ knowledge, this represents the first application of social–physical HRI within VR and the first experiential evaluation of these interactions using an ETHD approach.

In our results, we found that interaction type had no main effect on presence, enjoyability, comfort, or virtual character connection. This generalization suggests ETHOS is broadly applicable to short, socially meaningful gestures.
This suggests that the level of physicality rendered is the primary determinant of experience quality, and that ETHOS is broadly applicable across interactions that share common elements but differ in their patterns of contact and motion.

However, realism revealed an interaction-specific effect: while handover and fist bump benefitted from added physicality, the high five interaction did not vary across conditions. Participant feedback indicated that the silicone hand prop lacked sufficient fidelity, limiting perceived realism even as other metrics improved. This highlights the importance of prop design and suggests that meaningful experiential gains are possible even without perfect physical replication.

7.3.
Insights and Limitations

This study represents the first user evaluation of ETHOS and provides valuable insights into the application of social–physical interactions with a naive participant population. A key takeaway is the complexity of recreating interpersonal interactions with virtual humans. Readiness and greeting cues (e.g., gaze, waving, eye contact) were effective in guiding participants within this work, yet aspects of the virtual character’s appearance and animation were described as “lifeless” or “odd,” underscoring the sensitivity to realism in virtual interpersonal contexts. This aligns with Guadagno et al.
(
16
)
, who found that behavioural realism is correlated with social presence. Future iterations of ETHOS should therefore prioritize enhancing the behavioural realism of the virtual character to foster stronger connections and more meaningful interactions.

Another avenue for improving the ETHOS experience is to increase user agency and incorporate system adaptations based on individual characteristics. At present, ETHOS is constrained to a fixed interaction sequence with a predefined end goal, limiting user control. Agency (i.e., the perceived ability to influence elements of the virtual environment) has been shown to positively affect presence
(
18
)
. Extending ETHOS to support additional non-physical interactions or to allow users to guide the flow of interaction could therefore enhance presence and deepen engagement. Participant feedback also emphasized the value of personalization, with some noting that character proximity felt “too close” or that interaction height was not “ideal.” Unlike natural human–human encounters, which involve continuous mutual adjustment
(
38
)
, ETHOS currently lacks adaptive and personalized behaviours, suggesting an important direction for future development.

8.
CONCLUSIONS

This work presented the first experiential evaluation of ETHOS (Encountered-Type Haptics for On-demand Social interaction), advancing encountered-type haptics from inert object rendering to socially meaningful interactions with virtual characters. Through a 55-participant study of object handovers, fist bumps, and high fives across no, static, and dynamic physicality conditions, we provide the first evidence of how physical contact shapes social–physical HRI in VR.

The findings are clear: purely virtual baselines fail to sustain believable engagement, while even static prop alignment substantially enhances presence, realism, enjoyability, and connection to the virtual character. Dynamic rendering offers additional gains in presence but also reveals user sensitivity to motion timing and compliance, underscoring the need for more tailored trajectory generation and adaptive force control. Importantly, comfort remained stable across all conditions, demonstrating that physicality can be integrated without adding psychological friction.

Together, these results highlight a broader lesson: physicality is likely not optional but foundational for creating meaningful social–physical interactions with characters in VR. ETHOS shows that even simple, well-aligned props can transform encounters with virtual characters, while refined dynamic strategies hold promise for richer, more lifelike engagement. Participant feedback also revealed that limitations in virtual character appearance and animation were a primary detractor, pointing to the importance of coupling physical rendering with improved behavioural realism on the virtual side.

Looking forward, future work will refine ETHOS by tailoring motion strategies to specific gestures, introducing compliance-based contact detection, and enhancing virtual character realism. Beyond system improvements, ETHOS provides a flexible platform for application in VR theatre, physical rehabilitation, and immersive training, where authentic touch and social connection are central. Ultimately, this work establishes ETHOS as a foundation for advancing social–physical HRI in VR and as a stepping stone toward virtual experiences that feel not only believable, but genuinely meaningful.
