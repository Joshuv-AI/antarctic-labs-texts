Perceptions and treatment-seeking experiences for infertility among women in rural South Ethiopia
Authors: Kassahun Fikadu, Gistane Ayele, Sultan Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Ethiopia, conventional fertility treatments are not readily available and are frequently costly. These obstacles might prompt women to seek other infertility treatment methods, including traditional healers, herbal solutions, and spiritual healing practices. Nevertheless, the actual experiences and viewpoints of women seeking these alternative treatments in Ethiopia have not been thoroughly examined. This qualitative research intends to address this gap by examining the ways in which women in rural South Ethiopia utilize traditional healers, prayer, and spiritual practices in their quest for conception. A community-based study was conducted within the Arba Minch Health and Demographic Surveillance System (AM-HDSS) site, a defined demographic surveillance area. Purposive sampling was employed with the guidance of health extension workers. Data was gathered through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 19 women with infertility chosen purposively. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed utilizing reflective thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke. Thematic analysis revealed two primary themes. The first, “Diverse Understandings and Perceptions of Infertility,” included three sub-themes: ‘Infertility is defined as not having children,’ ‘Infertility is caused by God,’ and ‘Gendered perceptions of infertility.’ The second theme, “Experience of Alternative Treatment Options,” encompassed women’s use of ‘Traditional healers,’ ‘Prayers and belief in God’s direct intervention,’ and seeking help for perceived ‘Traditional Spiritual Beliefs.’ This study shows that women’s encounters with alternative infertility treatments in rural Ethiopia are influenced by deeply embedded cultural and religious views that consider childlessness as a spiritual issue and a responsibility of women. Although these views add to gender-based stigma and highlight important disparities in access to medical care, they also shed light on the emotional and social roles of spiritual and religious practices. For numerous women, conventional healers, prayer, and spiritual methods offer hope, a feeling of control, and the ability to find meaning amid uncertainty—advantages that biomedical treatments by themselves might not provide. Dealing with infertility involves enhancing access to care based on evidence, as well as recognizing and valuing the comprehensive methods women use to manage childlessness.Keywords: Alternative treatment, Ethiopia, Infertility, WomenSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Infertility is a significant reproductive health issue that affects individuals and couples worldwide, with profound physical, emotional, and social consequences1. The ability to decide the number, timing, and spacing of children is an essential human reproductive right, yet infertility challenges the realization of this right The World Health Organization (WHO) defines infertility as a condition affecting the male or female reproductive system, characterized by the inability to become pregnant after 12 months or more of consistent unprotected sexual activity2. It can be primary or secondary. Primary infertility is when a pregnancy has never been achieved by a person, and secondary infertility is when at least one prior pregnancy has been achieved3.

Infertility impacts millions of individuals in their reproductive years around the world3–5. Infertility is a major public health concern worldwide, affecting an estimated that between 48 million couples and 186 million people are experiencing infertility on a global scale2. While global prevalence varies, the burden is particularly high in Sub-Saharan Africa, where it affects an estimated 10% to 30% of couples6,7. In Ethiopia, although infertility is a leading reason for gynecological consultations, its exact prevalence remains unknown due to a lack of well-designed studies and insufficient documentation8. Based on analysis of the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) data, secondary infertility in Ethiopia is estimated at approximately 8.7% to 9.0%9. Yet these estimates are based on national surveys and may not reflect the full extent of infertility in particular rural areas, where access to healthcare and cultural factors vary widely. Community-focused studies, like the current research, are essential to enhance DHS findings by offering a richer contextual insight into infertility experiences in rural areas.

The causes of infertility are multifaceted, ranging from abnormalities in the ovaries, uterus, fallopian tubes, and endocrine system in women to a variety of male factors. Uterine and ovulation dysfunctions are among the most common causes of female infertility10. Despite these complexities, societal perceptions often place the burden of infertility on women, irrespective of whether the underlying issue lies with the male partner11.

The consequences of infertility extend beyond the inability to conceive; they encompass profound psychological, social, and economic dimensions. Women, in particular, often face violence, divorce, social stigma, emotional stress, depression, anxiety, and diminished self-esteem as a result of infertility12. These issues underscore the critical need to address infertility as part of realizing individuals’ and couples’ rights to found a family and mitigating gender inequalities13.

To comprehend the ways in which women make decisions regarding fertility treatment in rural Ethiopia, this research utilizes the Health Belief Model (HBM)14. The Health Belief Model (HBM) suggests that a person’s decision to seek health care is shaped by their views on: their likelihood of being affected by a health issue, the seriousness of that issue, the advantages of possible actions, the obstacles to taking those actions, and the prompts that lead to those behaviors14. These concepts are especially important regarding infertility in Ethiopia. As documented in the literature women’s view about their vulnerability are often interpreted from spiritual perspectives considering childlessness as divine intention15,16. This indicates infertility is deeply rooted in social aspect involving stigma, instability in marriages as traditional healers, prayer, and spiritual practices—are recognized in relation to cultural connection, optimism, and ease of access, whereas obstacles to biomedical care consist of expenses, travel distance, and restricted availability17. The Health Belief Model (HBM) serves as a structured framework for analyzing how these overlapping views influence the experiences and behaviors related to seeking treatment for women facing infertility.

In addition, to its medical aspects, infertility is significantly influenced by cultural and social views that affect how it is perceived and experienced. In numerous communities including Ethiopia, women are unjustly assigned a greater share of blame, irrespective of the reasons for it, which perpetuates stigmas related to gender and leads to their marginalization18. These views are not fixed; they engage with religious beliefs, cultural perspectives, and community standards to influence the interpretation of infertility and determine which solutions are considered acceptable19,20. who see infertility as a spiritual concern may focus on prayer or consult traditional healers, whereas those who view it as a medical problem may pursue care from hospitals; however, these approaches often intersect20,21. Comprehending these viewpoints is crucial for interpreting the treatment options selected by women.

In many cases, the cultural and social pressures associated with infertility lead women to discontinue biomedical treatments, particularly given the uncertainties surrounding successful outcomes. For instance, 68.5% of in-vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles fail to result in a live birth15. Nevertheless, the choice to pursue complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is influenced by factors that extend well beyond considerations of effectiveness alone. In numerous low- and middle-income nations, such as Ethiopia, the shift toward complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is influenced by several structural limitations. These include the restricted access to assisted reproductive technologies (ART), the high costs associated with treatments, and the geographic difficulties in reaching specialized fertility services22,23. Additionally, cultural and religious beliefs often perceive infertility as a spiritual issue that is most effectively resolved through traditional or faith-based approaches24,25. For numerous couples, Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM) serves as both a cost-effective and accessible choice, as well as one that resonates culturally15,25 and provides psychological reassurance during the challenges of fertility difficulties24,26. As a result, many couples turn to complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) to improve their chances of conception or alleviate stress and anxiety associated with treatment17.

The patient-centered focus of CAM provides women experiencing infertility a feeling of control during a biomedicalized fertility treatment process and incorporate cultural traditions of health and fertility as well as increase feelings of hope27. The stigma, costs, and uncertainty associated with biomedical fertility treatments often give those who are having problems conceiving an alternative to using CAM as a first line of treatment before engaging in more medically invasive treatments28. CAM methods, including herbal therapies, religious interventions, and stress-relieving techniques, are deeply rooted in cultural traditions and provide patients with a sense of control during a highly biomedicalized process17.

Recent studies suggest that individuals seeking fertility care often see CAM methods as a safe and effective way to increase their fertility29, and they are not only used to increase fertility, but also to decrease patient levels of stress and anxiety during the taxing process of treatment30. Furthermore, research has revealed that 29 to 91% of individuals seeking infertility help utilize complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) alongside primary medical services31, highlighting the significant intersection between these treatment methods. Nonetheless, it is crucial to acknowledge that the research supporting the effectiveness of Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM) in enhancing fertility results or alleviating psychological distress is still restricted. A scoping review discovered that although many people undergoing fertility treatment commonly use Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM), there is a shortage of well-conducted research showing clear effectiveness for most CAM practices31. Consequently, patients may be spending their time and money on treatments that do not have strong scientific backing. This difference between how patients view things and the evidence that exists highlights the necessity for more research and for healthcare professionals to participate in clear, knowledgeable conversations with patients regarding the use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM)12.

However, the effectiveness of these methods remains underexplored, particularly in Ethiopia, where traditional healers and religious practices play a significant role in healthcare-seeking behaviors32. There is a lack of studies examining the experiences and viewpoints of women who seek these alternative therapies for infertility. Particularly, there is limited knowledge regarding how women select between biomedical and alternative treatments, the significance they ascribe to these various approaches, and the influence of their cultural and spiritual beliefs on their decision-making processes.

Understanding how women living with infertility and those interested in infertility care interpret treatment will help to develop more appropriate infertility therapies and play an important role in the design of treatment plans for Couples living with infertility33.

By delivering a detailed understanding of how women view infertility and their experiences with different treatments, the results will provide important contextual information for healthcare professionals, policymakers, and practitioners of traditional medicine who aim to create culturally appropriate reproductive health services. Because of the unequal social and psychological burden placed on women in this context, this study focuses on their experiences as an important first step; it also aims to lay the groundwork for future research that must include male partners’ perspectives in order to cultivate a comprehensive, couple-centered understanding of infertility. Integrating traditional practices with biomedical approaches can optimize treatment outcomes, reduce the psychological and social burdens of infertility, and empower women to make informed decisions about their reproductive health. This research is expected to contribute to the development of comprehensive strategies that address infertility while promoting a collaborative role for traditional and biomedical healthcare providers in Ethiopia.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) serves as the conceptual framework for this study, which seeks to better understand the health-seeking behaviors of infertile women. According to the HBM, an individual’s decision to take a health action is impacted by their assessment of susceptibility to an illness, its severity, the advantages of taking action, and the barriers to that action14. In the context of infertility in rural Ethiopia, the HBM provides an important lens through which to examine how women’s perceptions of the social severity of childlessness, the perceived benefits of alternative treatments (e.g., cultural alignment, hope), and the barriers to biomedical care (e.g., cost, distance, perceived ineffectiveness) shape their treatment choices.

Methods

Design

A qualitative and exploratory descriptive phenomenological study was carried out to investigate the views and experiences of women with infertility in seeking treatment in rural South Ethiopia.

Study setting

The research was conducted within the Arba Minch Health and Demographic Surveillance System (AM-HDSS), a community-based surveillance site that regularly collects demographic and health data from a defined population within its surveillance area every six months. It was established in 2009 with the objective of monitoring demographic changes such as mortality, fertility, migration, and changes in marital status. This surveillance system operates within nine out of the total 21 kebeles (the smallest administrative units) in the Arba Minch Zuria District, located in Arba Minch, Gamo Zone, and Southern Ethiopia. The population residing within the AM-HDSS site in 2018 was recorded as 39,197 individuals, with 49.7% being female and 50.3% being male. The target population for this study is women between the ages of 15 and 49, who reside within the Arba Minch HDSS area. According to the 2018 site report, approximately 60% of this population consisted of eligible females within the childbearing age group. The study was conducted from June 01, 2023, to August 30, 2023. This explorative descriptive phenomenological study employed purposive sampling to select women living with infertility. The study included women aged 15 to 49 years who were experiencing infertility at the time of data collection. To ensure accurate demographic and health surveillance linkage, participants were required to have resided in the Kebeles covered by the Arba Minch Health and Demographic Surveillance System (AM-HDSS) for a minimum of six months prior to their enrollment in the study. We obtained verbal informed consent from all participants prior to conducting the interviews. Women within the specified age range who were critically ill at the time of data collection were excluded from the study, as their health status would have precluded meaningful and reliable participation. Critically ill individuals identified during the screening process were immediately referred to nearby health centers or hospitals for appropriate medical care.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The in-depth interviews involved a total of 19 women. The sample size was established by the idea of thematic saturation, which occurs when no new concepts or themes arise from the data. Due to the sensitive and frequently stigmatized nature of infertility in the research community, a purposive sample strategy was originally employed to locate important informants with the assistance of Kebele Health Extension workers. A snowball sampling strategy was used with these initial individuals. This strategy is especially effective in addressing “hidden” or difficult-to-reach communities that may be hesitant to self-identify34. The initial assistance from health extension workers helped guarantee that the snowball chains originated from different community sectors, thus limiting potential selection bias by diversifying recruitment. All participants were screened and confirmed as eligible using the WHO’s case definition of infertility. This qualitative study was conducted to evaluate the lived experience of extended childlessness rather than to clinically diagnose infertility by type (primary or secondary). The sample included women who had previously initiated care at a biomedical health facility but had later terminated treatment for a variety of reasons. As a result, the goal of this analysis was not to divide individuals into diagnostic categories, but rather to investigate the motives, barriers, and decision-making processes that caused them to discontinue current infertility therapy and consider other choices. As a result, the participant cohort comprises women with both main and secondary infertility diagnoses, mirroring the real-world makeup of women seeking care in this environment.

Women of reproductive age living in the Arba Minch HDSS site share a relatively common culture and lifestyle, although there are some differences in language and lifestyle that make the intended product of qualitative information using more achievable. Therefore, individuals were chosen based on their experience with infertility, which is defined by the WHO surveillance criteria as the inability to achieve pregnancy after two or more years of consistent unprotected sexual activity. Although the general term ‘sub-fertility’ refers to any extended duration to achieve conception, this research specifically focused on women who fit the World Health Organization’s definition of infertility to maintain alignment with international monitoring standards. To ensure variety perspectives, study participants with a characteristics such as monogamous and polygamous, young and old, uneducated and educated couples were enrolled.

Operational definition

Infertility

For monitoring and surveillance purpose, WHO has defined infertility “Women of reproductive age (15–49 years) at risk of becoming pregnant (not pregnant, sexually active, not using contraception and not lactating) who report trying unsuccessfully for a pregnancy for two years or more”3.

Data collection procedures and tools

During the interviews, the interviewer employed a semi-structured framework made up of open-ended questions to draw out the participants’ experiences. This manual has been modified based on earlier literatures35,36. During the interview, the interviewer carefully noted the participants’ non-verbal signals—such as their facial expressions, pauses, and emotional reactions—to assess their comfort levels and determine when to ask more questions, provide reassurance, or permit silence for contemplation. This adaptable method contributed to establishing a secure, participant-focused atmosphere and guaranteed that delicate subjects were addressed thoughtfully. Sample inquiries included: “When did you first realize that you were experiencing difficulties in conceiving? What transpired? ““What were your thoughts on pursuing treatment from natural healers?” “You indicated that a spiritual leader provided you with treatment—could you elaborate on that experience?”

Interviews were conducted with the consent of the participants. Eligible individuals were provided with information about the purpose, goals and potential benefits of the study, the right to refuse participation in the study, and confidentiality issues. After ensuring that they understood the ongoing study, we set up a favorable private room for the participants to comfortably conduct the in-depth interviews that were conducted at the health post located in each kebele. The interviews were conducted in Amharic, which is the official working language of Ethiopia and the primary language of communication in the study area. All interviewers were native Amharic speakers, ensuring fluency, cultural understanding, and effective communication with the participants. We conducted interviews with women with known infertility to explore their experiences of using alternative or complementary treatments to help conceive. A total of 19 in-depth interviews were conducted, with each interview taking an average of 45 min to complete. All interviews were meticulously recorded using audio-tape and transcribed promptly following the interviews.

Data quality control

In order to ensure consistency and standardization in data collection techniques, training was provided to both data collectors and supervisors on conducting culturally sensitive in-depth interviews tailored to the objectives of this qualitative study. The training encompassed various topics, including the study’s goals, interview guide, approach to addressing reticent participants or those who deviate from the main agenda, as well as methods for verifying data completeness and preserving data confidentiality. Key informants with diverse characteristics and experiences related to their infertility status were recruited to provide a range of perspectives on similar issues. To ensure data quality, the team held regular meetings to discuss any issues that arose during the data collection process, and the principal investigators conducted spot checks. Data collectors were required to complete data collection, and supportive follow-up was provided by all investigators.

Furthermore, the following critical aspects were addressed to ensure data quality.

Trustworthiness: to maintain the thoroughness and high standard of this qualitative research, we followed the standards of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability established by Lincoln and Guba37. Credibility was founded on extended interaction with the study community, member verification (going back to certain participants to confirm our understanding of their stories), and discussions among the research team for feedback. Transferability was improved by offering detailed descriptions of the study environment, the traits of the participants, and the sociocultural background, which enables readers to evaluate how the findings may relate to other situations. Dependability was addressed by keeping a thorough record of all research choices, which included interview transcripts, coding frameworks, and analytical notes. Also, confirmability was obtained through reflexive journaling, where researchers recorded their own biases and assumptions during data gathering and analysis, as well as by cross-checking analysis among various team members.

Data processing and analysis

The two independent researchers carefully listened to the audio recordings of each interview, which were transcribed word for word in the local language, until they became familiar with the participants’ experiences. Subsequently, the transcripts were translated into English and compared with the original Amharic version to identify any potential contradictions. After ensuring consistency, the investigators thoroughly read and re-read the transcripts to gain insight into the participants’ thoughts. They subsequently generated memos and codes sequentially, adhering to the step-by-step approach of reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke38. Each time, relevant themes were identified and recorded in the codebook. Exhaustive codes were subsequently compared to identify similarities and variations. Similar themes were contrasted with new themes, and sub-themes were merged to capture a common perspective, while distinct themes remained separate. Following this, the codes and their descriptions were reviewed by independent researchers who specialize in qualitative data coding. Once the investigators reached a consensus on the themes and no new themes or sub-themes emerged, the thematic analysis was conducted. The Open Code version 4.02 software was utilized to facilitate the thematic analysis.

Results

A total of 19 women participated. The majority of the key informants, comprising 13 (68.4%) of the sample, were within the age range of 30 to 40. Those below the age of 30 accounted for 31.6% (6 individuals). In terms of occupation, 11 individuals (57.9%) were housewives, followed by 4 individuals (21%) who were merchants. The majority, 12 individuals (63.2%), had completed their secondary education. Among the married women, 12 (63.2%) were married before reaching the age of 18. Ten women (73.6%) had been married for more than 5 years (Table 1).

The demographic characteristics of the participants offered significant background for comprehending their experiences with infertility. Most women (63. 2%) had entered into marriage before the age of 18, indicating that child marriage is common in the area studied. These early marriages resulted in numerous women attempting to become pregnant for the majority of their reproductive years, increasing the societal pressure to have children. As one participant stated, “I got married at the age of 16, and now I am 32—I’ve spent all these years hoping for a child” [P03]. In a similar manner, the length of marriage proved to be an important factor: women who had been married for over five years (comprising 73. 6% of the sample) reported greater family pressure and a higher chance of looking for different treatments, as the extended wait for becoming pregnant intensified social stigma and personal pain.

Themes

During the analysis, two primary themes emerged from the participants’ narratives: “Diverse Understandings and Perceptions of Infertility” and “Experience of Alternative Treatment Options.”

Theme 1: Diverse understandings and perceptions of infertility

There is a strong theme indicating the perception or awareness of infertility in general. Three sub-themes emerged: ‘Infertility is defined as not having one’s own offspring,’ ‘Infertility is caused by God, so is the solution,’ and ‘Gender differences in infertility.’ Participants associated infertility with the inability to have children, divine intervention, and gender-based perceptions, where women are often labeled as primarily responsible. This could be a coping mechanism to avoid blame or because medical answers were unavailable.

Sub-theme 1.1: Infertility is defined as not having one’s own offspring

Almost all participants perceived that a woman is considered infertile when she has no children. The participants’ definition of infertility as a total lack of progeny emphasizes the perceived severity of the ailment in this rural setting, where motherhood is related to social identity and survival. A participant in their early 30s described infertility as: “Barrenness is a person who has no children. It is disheartening to contemplate the fact that I do not have any offspring, as it fills me with a sense of frustration that my name might vanish entirely upon my demise.” [P10]

Another participant expressed:

“Infertility is a woman who has not given birth and is living without a child of her own.” [P13]

A participant in mid-30s linked infertility to nulliparity and divine intervention, stating:

“In my understanding, it means a woman who has never seen any pregnancy or who never gets God’s blessing to have her own child.” [P14]

One participant highlighted the role of medical investigation while expressing personal faith:

“If you get tested tomorrow and are told you cannot have children, then you are infertile. But I am going to investigate now. I pray to God to help me. However, I understand that I have to be patient to get results.” [P11]

Sub-theme 1.2: Infertility is caused by God, so is the solution

Participants framed their perceived susceptibility through a spiritual lens by attributing infertility to divine will, hence guiding their choice of intervention. Some participants strongly believed that infertility is solely controlled by God and that seeking solutions outside divine intervention is unnecessary.

“Only God gives. How could I find a solution from other traditional crafts? It means I am not faithful. I believe in God.” [P13]

Another participant stated:

“I am living, hoping that one day God will give us a child. If a woman does not conceive after marriage, she is called infertile.” [P01]

Similarly, another participant in their late 30s emphasized:

“I believe that infertility is caused by God, which means that a child is God’s gift. If a woman does not give birth, she is referred to as infertile.” [P 02]

Sub-theme 1.3: Gender differences in infertility

Participants also discussed how infertility is perceived differently for men and women.

One participant mentioned male infertility due to azoospermia (absence of sperm):

“I have not seen many people in my village with this condition. There is only one man who has no sperm. He married many women but never had children.” [P10]

Another participant stated that infertility can occur in both men and women:

“Barrenness is a problem for both genders. In my village, I have seen men with this condition, as well as women who are married and living together without children.” [P11]

A participant acknowledged the social misconception that infertility is primarily blamed on women:

“Most of the time, it is interpreted as a women’s problem. That is it—a husband may switch and marry another wife but end up with no change. This happens when men have the condition.” [P12]

Another participant reinforced the common bias against women in infertility cases:

“I don’t know about men. We always think that the problem is with women. As far as I understand, men are not often seen with the condition.” [P15]

Theme 2: Experience of alternative treatment options

Four sub-themes emerged: Traditional healers, prayers, God’s intervention, and traditional spiritual beliefs. Women shared their experiences of seeking alternative treatments for infertility.

Sub-theme 2.1: Traditional healers (‘Debtera’)

The high cost and geographic separation of medical facilities surfaced as major perceived obstacles to contemporary treatment, causing respondents to search out traditional healers as a more easily available and cheaper substitute. A participant mentioned trying modern medical treatment but later turning to traditional healers:

“First, I went to the hospital, and there was no change, so I changed my mind and tried visiting traditional healers and took a traditional medicine.” [P10]

Another participant shared:

“I know some women who used to visit traditional healers named ‘debtera’ and have achieved pregnancy successfully. I am also ready to give it a try, as I am exhausted with modern treatment with no result.” [P05]

A participant detailed her experience with herbal medicine from a traditional healer:

“I was devastated until I got the treatment recommended by one of my friends. The traditional healer gave me some herbs, and I took them for several months before I got pregnant. I kept visiting him until I succeeded. One thing I want to mention is that a robe was tied around my belly until I delivered my child.” [P 07]

Although numerous women spoke favorably about traditional healers, a few participants raised concerns regarding the safety and authenticity of specific practitioners. A woman shared her story: “I consumed the herbs for a duration of three months; however, I began to experience intense abdominal pain. My spouse remarked, ‘this could be harming you—cease usage.’ I am unaware of the contents of those medications.”[P09]

Another participant expressed a sense of being taken advantage of after making several trips to a healer without seeing any improvement. He continued to request additional funds and more animals, assuring that this time it would be successful. “He kept asking for more money, more animals, promising that this time it would work. After the fourth time, I realized he was just taking advantage of my desperation.” [P14]

These reports indicate that although traditional healers are still regarded as a significant cultural asset, the absence of oversight puts women at risk of possible harm and financial abuse.

Include following the discussion on prayer and spiritual interventions:

Multiple participants indicated that they were exploring both biomedical and alternative approaches at the same time, rather than selecting one option to the exclusion of the other. As one woman described: “I go to the hospital for tests, but I also go to the prayer gathering every week. Why limit myself? Maybe God works through the doctors, or maybe the doctors can help after we pray. I will try everything…” [P11]

This dual usage was prevalent, as women frequently hid their use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) from medical professionals due to concerns about being judged.

“I didn’t tell the doctor about the traditional medicine. He would say it’s useless. But how does he know? He doesn’t understand our ways.”[P06]

Sub-theme 2.2: Prayers

A participant explained her decision to abandon modern treatment and turn to religious prayer:

“I had visited and finished treatment in different hospitals in Addis Ababa, but there was no change. So, I left the treatment and started visiting a Pentecostal church where there is a place to pray. The medicine is not right.” [P19]

Another participant expressed faith in religious gatherings:

“My husband and I believe in God, and there is a weekly prayer gathering. There is a reputation that prayers visit houses with certain conditions, and I was hoping it would bring blessings to my house.” [P17]

Sub-theme 2.3: Belief in God’s direct intervention as an alternative

A participant described her strong reliance on God’s intervention rather than medical treatment:

“I don’t believe in traditional crafts. First, I believe that God would provide a solution. The only thing expected is asking him wholeheartedly.” [P12]

Another participant in their mid-20s emphasized:

“I couldn’t see the benefit of getting investigated or checked… While serving the Lord, even if God doesn’t bless us with children, it is up to Him as He is the one who grants childbearing.” [P 15]

Sub-theme 2.4: Traditional spiritual beliefs

Persistent cues to action were intense societal pressure, the threat of being labeled ‘barren,’ and the dread of divorce, which drove women to simultaneously use several spiritual treatments and conventional methods. A participant in her early 40s explained how societal pressure led her to consider affliction of spiritual concerns as a solution:

“Sometimes, when you get hurt, there is nothing you can do about it. There are people who believe there can be a solution from a fortune teller. Sometimes they tried to persuade me to visit a witch’s house, but I stood firm in my faith.” [P11]

Another participant admitted considering traditional spiritual concerns affiliation after pressure from family and neighbors:

“Yes, I sometimes thought of going to a witch’s house, but I hesitated, thinking it may not be worth it. Due to persuasion from neighbors and family members, I went with my husband to a witch’s house, and they assured me that I would give birth.” [P14]

Discussion

This qualitative research examined the views on infertility and the experiences of seeking treatment among rural women in Ethiopia. It specifically highlighted their use of alternative treatment methods, such as traditional healers, prayer, and spiritual practices. Our qualitative findings reveal several key themes: the conceptualization of infertility as the absence of children, the profound gender bias in attributing blame, the role of divine or religious explanations for the condition, and the widespread use of alternative healing practices. Using the Health Belief Model (HBM) in line with these results reveals a clear decision-making process. High expenditures and a paucity of specialized clinics impede women’s access to medical care even if cultural standards result in a great awareness of problems with fertility. Because they fit cultural values and are affordable, conventional and spiritual treatments have clear advantages that are heightened. Cues to Action further increase the impact of these behaviors, particularly the continuing social stigma and impact from the husband’s family, which drives women to look for quick, different options.

The demographic information about the participants—especially the significant number of child marriages (63. 2%) and the extended lengths of marriage—illuminates the severity of the infertility challenge in this environment. Women who married as teenagers have dedicated most of their childbearing years to trying to get pregnant, frequently enduring years of societal judgment. The long-lasting experience of stigma related to infertility could clarify the significant interest in alternative treatments, as women have utilized all medical choices and lost personal optimism. These results are consistent with studies from similar situations that show how early marriage worsens the psychological and social effects of infertility15,25.

A primary finding was the conceptualization of infertility simply as “living without offspring.” This practical, experience-based definition reflects a focus on the social outcome rather than a clinical diagnosis and is consistent with findings from qualitative studies in Gambia and Ghana16. This perspective underscores how infertility is primarily understood through its social and familial consequences in many communities.

Furthermore, the study highlighted a significant gender bias in perceptions of infertility, where women are more frequently considered the source of the problem. This aligns with extensive research from Gambia16, Ghana18 and Iran39. This disparity is often rooted in social prejudice, which frames infertility as a predominantly female issue, causing women to face greater familial and social challenges than men40. The burden of reproductive health is frequently placed on women41, a phenomenon also observed in Iran where infertility is largely viewed as a female concern42. This gendered pressure is not unique to developing nations; for instance, men in the UK may conceal their infertility to avoid shame43. For women in settings with traditional gender roles, this disproportionate blame can compel them to seek drastic solutions, regardless of the actual underlying cause of infertility. Consequently, when blame is disproportionately assigned to women, they may be compelled to take drastic measures to address the issue, even if they are not the underlying cause.

A significant discovery in the initial theme was the belief that infertility is a result of divine intention. As stated in Sub-theme 1.2, participants frequently viewed their situation as a result of divine will, as evidenced by expressions like ‘Only God gives,’ which highlight the profound spiritual understanding surrounding childlessness. This belief, wherein having children is considered God’s will, has been observed in other cultures and can sometimes correlate with reduced physical violence and stigma, even in families of low socioeconomic status39. Within the specific Ethiopian cultural context, there is a prevalent belief that infertility may be a divine punishment for sins. Consequently, it is believed that conception is only possible through confession, priestly absolution, and God’s intervention15,25.

Participants explored various alternative therapies in reaction to their infertility. These activities involved seeking advice from traditional healers who provided herbal treatments, participating in religious activities like prayer, and obtaining assistance from spiritual advisors linked to local customs. This pursuit of alternative treatments is well-documented. A study in Zimbabwe highlighted that traditional healers focus on the client’s history and employ medicinal plants to address female infertility26. Similarly, research in Canada found that participants commonly utilized traditional healers, prayers, and religious talismans as alternative treatments44. Religion often plays a critical role in shaping beliefs about illness and healing, leading to the acceptance of methods advocated by religious figures, such as prayer45. Our study confirmed the importance of faith, revealing that all participants incorporated prayer into their healing journey for strength and comfort.

In addition, to recording how often CAM is used, our results highlight significant conflicts and potential dangers. Although traditional healers and spiritual methods offered hope and maintained cultural traditions for numerous women, certain participants faced negative consequences from unregulated herbal treatments or financial exploitation by dishonest practitioners. These risks are mostly not seen in existing research, yet they have significant effects on women’s physical health and financial stability. The lack of regulations or quality oversight for traditional medicines in Ethiopia may expose women to harmful herbs, incorrect dosages, or tainted products46. This discovery highlights the importance of harm reduction approaches that acknowledge traditional practices while striving to comprehend and reduce their associated risks.

Our research also highlights the intricacy of dual use, which refers to the concurrent or consecutive involvement with both biomedical and alternative therapy systems. Instead of viewing the situation as a simple choice between two options, many women in our study navigated among hospitals, traditional healers, and places of worship, frequently not sharing their complete treatment history with any one provider47. Nevertheless, it also poses possible dangers: the unreported use of herbs might affect fertility treatments, and contradictory guidance from various practitioners could lead to misunderstanding or delayed appropriate care. These results emphasize the need for healthcare providers to start open and impartial discussions regarding the use of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) and to allow women the opportunity to discuss their complete treatment stories.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study when interpreting its findings. As a qualitative study conducted in a specific geographic location (Arba Minch HDSS) with a purposively selected sample of 19 women, the results are not statistically generalizable to a broader population. The use of purposive and snowball sampling techniques may introduce selection bias; however, this was mitigated by intentionally recruiting participants with diverse characteristics to capture a wider range of perspectives. Furthermore, the data relied on participants’ self-reports, which can be subject to recall and social desirability biases. To address this, interviews were conducted by trained, native Amharic-speaking data collectors in private settings to foster trust and encourage open communication. Finally, this study focused exclusively on the experiences of women. The absence of male partners’ perspectives is a limitation, given that infertility is a challenge that affects the couple. Future research should include men to provide a more holistic understanding of the issue.

The findings of this study carry significant implications for healthcare. The deep-seated cultural and spiritual beliefs surrounding infertility necessitate a more culturally sensitive approach from healthcare professionals. It is advisable that infertility clinics acknowledge these spiritual beliefs and provide appropriate training for reproductive health specialists. Moreover, the emotional and psychological toll evidenced by the reliance on prayer and faith for strength highlights a critical gap in care. There is a clear need for mental health professionals to establish comprehensive psychosocial support and counseling programs tailored to the unique experiences of women struggling with infertility.

Although this study concentrated on women’s experiences—valid due to the uneven social burden they carry—infertility is essentially a situation that affects both partners. Male-factor infertility accounts for about 30% of cases worldwide10, and as our participants observed (Sub-theme 1.3), men in their communities also face infertility, albeit frequently unnoticed. The lack of men’s perspectives in this study restricts our knowledge of how couples make treatment choices together, how male infertility is viewed and hidden, and how gender roles influence the pursuit of assistance within relationships. Future studies using couple-focused methods—interviewing both partners together or individually—may clarify these relationship dynamics and show how couples share the meanings of infertility, make treatment decisions, and provide (or do not provide) support for each other during the emotional process. Such research is crucial for creating fully inclusive, family-focused fertility services.

Conclusion and recommendations

This qualitative research investigated the experiences and views of women in rural Southern Ethiopia concerning their use of alternative treatments for infertility. The results indicate that infertility is viewed from spiritual and gender perspectives: women consider being childless as a matter of divine intention, carry an unequal share of guilt, and face significant social stigma. In reply, individuals seek various alternative therapies, including herbal treatments from traditional healers, prayer and religious meetings, and guidance from spiritual advisors. This occurs not only due to obstacles in accessing biomedical healthcare but also as a proactive effort to obtain psychosocial support. These practices provide women with hope, a sense of control, and a way to find meaning during uncertain times, offering reassurance that biomedical methods alone might not deliver.

Using the Health Belief Model, it is evident that perceived severity (such as stigma and marital issues) and strong motivators (like family pressure) influence the decision to seek treatment. At the same time, the perceived advantages of complementary and alternative medicine (including cultural relevance and hope) and the perceived obstacles to biomedicine (such as cost and availability) determine the options women pursue. Tackling infertility in this situation necessitates a two-fold strategy: (1) enhancing access to affordable and dignified biomedical fertility services, and (2) acknowledging the overall importance of spiritual and traditional methods. Public health strategies should not disregard these practices or support untested treatments. Instead, they should pursue cooperative methods that honor women’s cultural beliefs while ensuring safety and informed decision-making. Future studies should utilize dyadic methods to incorporate the viewpoints of male partners and investigate how couples work together to manage the intricate challenges of infertility treatment.
