1.
Introduction

Let
p
be an odd prime and set
\zeta_{p}:=e^{2\pi i/p}
.
Write
K_{p}:=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p})
and
\mathcal{O}_{K_{p}}:=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{p}]
for the ring of integers.
Let
\Cl(K_{p})
denote the ideal class group of
\mathcal{O}_{K_{p}}
.
Kummer’s method for Fermat’s Last Theorem (FLT) with exponent
p

is based on the factorization

x^{p}+y^{p}=\prod_{a=0}^{p-1}(x+\zeta_{p}^{a}y)\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{O}_{K_{p}},

together with factorization properties as dictated by the class
group.
The classical criterion of Kummer, referred to as
p
-
regularity
, in modern terms,
asserts that
if
p\nmid|\Cl(K_{p})|
, then FLT holds for exponent
p
(for example,
see Kummer’s original work
[
8
]
and modern expositions such as
[
19
]
).

Regularity is a condition that is easily described in terms of Bernoulli numbers.
A prime
p
is
regular
if
p\nmid B_{2k}
for all even
2k\in\{2,\dots,p-3\}
.
For irregular primes, however, we may still consider specific indices.
With this as motivation, combined with a theorem of Jensen that asserts that
there are infinitely many irregular primes
[
5
]
, we study “partial regularity”.
Siegel’s conjecture
[
17
]
,
that irregular primes occur with asymptotic density

1-e^{-1/2}\approx 0.393469\dots,
augments interest in this direction.
We ask whether there are partial regularity results that hold for almost all primes.

To this end, we say that an odd prime
p
is
m
-regular
if

p\nmid\num(B_{2k})
for every even integer
2\leq 2k\leq\min\left(m,p-3\right).

The main result proved in this note is the following estimate for
M_{\alpha}(p)
-regular primes,
where for
\alpha>1/2
, we define

(2)

M_{\alpha}(p)\coloneq\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{p}}{(\log p)^{\alpha}}\right\rfloor.

We prove that a density-one subset of the primes is partially regular.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\alpha>1/2
and define
M_{\alpha}(p)
as in (
2
).
Then there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that as
X\rightarrow+\infty
we have

\#\Bigl\{\,p\leq X\text{ prime}:\ p\ \text{is not $M_{\alpha}(p)$-regular}\Bigr\}\ \leq\ C_{\alpha}\,\frac{X}{(\log X)^{2\alpha}}.

In particular, almost every prime is
M_{\alpha}(p)
-regular.

Remark 1.2
.

In fact, it turns out that

C_{\alpha}=10

is a suitable constant for every
\alpha>1/2
in Theorem
1.1
(as well as Corollary
1.4
).
In particular, the constant
may be chosen independently of
\alpha
.

Remark 1.3
.

The Prime Number Theorem asserts, as
X\rightarrow+\infty,
that

\pi(X)\coloneq\#\left\{p\leq X\ :\ p\ {\text{\rm prime}}\right\}\sim\frac{X}{\log X}

Therefore, Theorem
1.1
refers to a density-one subset of the prime numbers.
This is what we mean by almost all primes.

It is natural to ask whether Theorem
1.1
has implications for the
mathematics related to Fermat’s Last Theorem.
To this end, we revisit the well-studied Galois refinement of Kummer’s criterion.
We let
A_{p}
denote the
p
-part of the class group
\Cl(K_{p})
. The action of the Galois group

G_{p}\coloneq\Gal(K_{p}/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

gives the canonical decomposition of
A_{p}
into eigenspaces of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[G_{p}]
-modules

(3)

A_{p}\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{p-2}A_{p}(\omega^{i}),

where
\omega:G_{p}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
is the Teichmüller character,
which sends
a
to the
unique

(p-1)
-st root of unity in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}

satisfying
\omega(a)\equiv a\pmod{p}
.
These eigenspaces are divided into a “minus part”
A_{p}^{-}
(where
i
is odd)
and the “plus part”
A_{p}^{+}
(where
i
is even).
The latter corresponds to the
p
-part of the class group of the maximal real
subfield
K_{p}^{+}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p}+\zeta_{p}^{-1})
.

The structural understanding of
A_{p}^{-}
is provided by the Herbrand-Ribet Theorem,
which links the non-vanishing of odd eigenspaces to the divisibility of Bernoulli numbers
B_{2k}
.
For even
2k
in the range
2\leq 2k\leq p-3
, this is the statement that

(4)

A_{p}(\omega^{p-2k})\neq 0\iff p\mid B_{2k}.

This result, initiated by Herbrand
[
3
]
and completed by Ribet

[
15
]
using the theory of modular forms,
demonstrates that the “minus” components are entirely governed by the
Bernoulli numbers and the
p
-adic properties of the Riemann zeta function at
negative odd integers via Euler’s identity
\zeta(1-2k)=-B_{2k}/2k
(for example, see
[
4
]
).

While
A_{p}^{-}
is well-understood,
the “plus” part
A_{p}^{+}
is the subject of Vandiver’s Conjecture (also attributed to Kummer),
which asserts that
p
does not divide the class number
h_{p}^{+}
of
K_{p}^{+}
.
In terms of eigenspaces, the conjecture asserts the following.

Conjecture

(Kummer-Vandiver
[
7
,
21
]
)
.

If
p
is an odd prime, then we have

A_{p}(\omega^{2k})=0\quad\text{for all even }2k\in\{2,4,\dots,p-3\}.

Here we consider a weaker form of Vandiver’s Conjecture,
which pertains to the triviality of initial ranges of the eigenspaces.
The following result for almost every prime
p
follows from Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
\alpha>1/2
, then there is a constant

C_{\alpha}>0
such that as
X\rightarrow+\infty
we have

\#\Bigl\{\,p\leq X\text{ prime}:\ A_{p}(\omega^{2k})\neq 0\ \text{for some}\ 2\leq 2k\leq M_{\alpha}(p)\Bigr\}\ \leq\ C_{\alpha}\,\frac{X}{(\log X)^{2\alpha}}.

In particular, for almost every prime
p
we have

A_{p}(\omega^{2})\ =\ A_{p}(\omega^{4})\ =\ \dots\ =A_{p}(\omega^{M_{\alpha}(p)})=0.

Corollary
1.4
follows from Theorem
1.1
,
which is directly related to the odd eigenspaces by the Herbrand-Ribet Theorem.
The link between the two halves
A_{p}^{-}
and
A_{p}^{+}
of the class group is
established by a
reflection principle
, known as Leopoldt’s
Spiegelungssatz

[
10
]
.
This theorem provides a specialized injection

A_{p}(\omega^{2k})\neq 0\implies A_{p}(\omega^{p-2k})\neq 0.

This implies that any
p
-divisibility in the even Vandiver components are
reflected as
p
-divisibility in the corresponding odd Herbrand-Ribet components.
This gives a one way relationship between Bernoulli numbers and the Vandiver conjecture,
which can be viewed as a local refinement of the classical notion of
p
-regularity.

Deduction of Corollary
1.4
from Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary
1.4
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
by the
Herbrand–Ribet theorem and Leopoldt’s reflection principle

A_{p}(\omega^{2k})\neq 0\implies A_{p}(\omega^{p-2k})\neq 0.\qed

The result of Corollary
1.4
regarding the triviality of the eigenspaces

A_{p}(\omega^{2k})
has immediate consequences for
p
-adic analytic theory.
To state this, we recall the
Kubota-Leopoldt
p
-adic
L
-function

L_{p}(s,\chi)
,
which interpolates the values of Dirichlet
L
-functions
p
-adically.
By the “Main Conjecture of Iwasawa Theory” proved by Mazur and Wiles

[
13
]
and Leopoldt’s reflection principle described below,
the vanishing of the algebraic eigenspace
A_{p}(\omega^{2k})
is equivalent to
the associated
p
-adic
L
-function being a unit in the Iwasawa algebra

\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[T]]
when
p\nmid B_{2k}
.
Thus, Corollary
1.4
yields the following analytic nonvanishing theorem for
p
-adic
L
-functions,
which asserts that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a zero-free region.

Corollary 1.5
.

If
\alpha>1/2
and
M_{\alpha}(p)
is defined as in (
2
), then for almost every prime
p
,
the Kubota-Leopoldt
p
-adic
L
-functions satisfy

\big|L_{p}(s,\omega^{2k})\big|_{p}=1\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{Z}_{p},

for all even integers
2\leq 2k\leq M_{\alpha}(p)
.
In particular, these functions have no zeros on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Remark 1.6
.

It is important to note that Corollary
1.5
is qualitatively different (and, in a

p
-adic sense) and much stronger than the zero-free statement one has in the
complex-analytic setting of the classical Riemann Hypothesis.
While the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) predicts that all non-trivial
zeros of
L(s,\chi)
lie on the critical line
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
,
the
p
-adic result above asserts that there are no zeros at all in the domain
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
This phenomenon, while striking from a complex-analytic perspective, is standard in
p
-adic analysis.
It corresponds to the case where the characteristic polynomial in Iwasawa theory is a unit (degree 0).
This reflects the fact that “most” primes are expected to be regular (or satisfy Vandiver’s conjecture),
in which case the associated arithmetic objects are trivial and the analytic
functions are invertible units.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

By the standard control theorem in cyclotomic Iwasawa theory, the
\omega^{p-2k}
-eigenspace of the
unramified Iwasawa module over
K_{\infty}/K
has
\Gamma
-coinvariants canonically identified with

A_{p}(\omega^{p-2k})
(see e.g. Washington
[
19
, Ch. 13]
). Hence

A_{p}(\omega^{p-2k})=0
forces this eigenspace to be zero by Nakayama’s lemma.
By Mazur–Wiles
[
13
]
, the cyclotomic main conjecture identifies the characteristic
ideal of this eigenspace with the principal ideal generated by the Kubota–Leopoldt
p
-adic

L
-function attached to
\omega^{2k}
(equivalently, to
\omega^{1-(p-2k)}
), so that vanishing of the
module is equivalent to this
p
-adic
L
-function being a unit of
\Lambda
.
Therefore, its values on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
are
p
-adic units, i.e.

|L_{p}(s,\omega^{2k})|_{p}=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
∎

We record several further ways in which Theorem
1.1
, through the ubiquity of Bernoulli
numbers, governs phenomena across number theory and arithmetic geometry.
Classically, Bernoulli numbers first arise in formulas for sums of
powers of integers via Faulhaber’s formula (see Chapter 15 of
[
4
]
), but their deeper
significance emerges through their appearance in special values of

L
-functions. In particular, the values of the Riemann zeta function at
negative odd integers are given by

\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k},

so that divisibility properties of Bernoulli numerators encode subtle
information about these special values. This connection plays a central
role in algebraic number theory, especially in Iwasawa theory and the
study of cyclotomic fields as mentioned earlier (also see
[
19
, Chapter 5]
).

A second, striking appearance of Bernoulli numbers occurs in the theory
of modular forms. Certain congruences between Eisenstein series and
cuspidal Hecke eigenforms are controlled precisely by divisibility of
Bernoulli numbers. The most famous example is Ramanujan’s tau-function congruence

\tau(n)\equiv\sum_{d\mid n}d^{11}\pmod{691},

relating the Fourier coefficients of the weight
12
cusp form

\Delta(q)
to those of the Eisenstein series
E_{12}(q)
. This congruence
reflects the fact that

B_{12}=-\frac{691}{2730},

so that the prime
691
divides the numerator of
B_{12}
. More
generally, as explained by Swinnerton-Dyer
[
18
]
, primes dividing numerators of
Bernoulli numbers give rise to congruences between even-weight Eisenstein
series and cuspidal eigenforms, revealing deep links between special
values of
L
-functions, rational torsion on elliptic curves,
and the structure of Hecke algebras (see
[
2
,
12
,
14
]
).

Finally, Bernoulli numbers also govern torsion phenomena in algebraic

K
-theory. Algebraic
K
-groups provide a systematic way to measure the
failure of unique factorization and related structural properties in
rings, generalizing classical invariants such as the unit group and the
class group. In the case of the integers
\mathbb{Z}
, deep conjectures of
Lichtenbaum and Quillen, proved through the work of Borel and others,
relate the
p
-torsion in higher
K
-groups
K_{4k-2}(\mathbb{Z})
to divisibility
of Bernoulli numbers. Thus, just as in cyclotomic class groups and
modular forms, primes dividing Bernoulli numerators control subtle
torsion phenomena in
K
-theory. We refer the reader to

[
20
, Chapter 18]
for an accessible introduction to these ideas.

We record a convenient four-part corollary illustrating immediate consequences of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Fix
\alpha>1/2
and let
M_{\alpha}(p)
be as in (
2
).
Then for almost every prime
p
, the following hold simultaneously
for every integer
1\leq 2k\leq M_{\alpha}(p)
:

(1)

The congruence

\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}a^{2k}\ \equiv\ B_{2k}\,p\pmod{p^{2}}

holds in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
(interpreting
B_{2k}
via its reduction modulo
p^{2}
).

(2)

We have that
\zeta(1-2k)
is a
p
-adic unit.

(3)

There is no normalized cuspidal Hecke eigenform
f(q)
of level
1
and weight
2k

whose Fourier expansion satisfies

f(q)\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d\mid n}d^{2k-1}q^{n}\pmod{p}.

(4)

The group
K_{4k-2}(\mathbb{Z})
has no
p
-torsion.

Remark 1.8

(Generalized Bernoulli numbers)
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character modulo
N
.
The generalized Bernoulli numbers
B_{m,\chi}
are defined by

\sum_{a=1}^{N}\chi(a)\,\frac{te^{at}}{e^{Nt}-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}B_{m,\chi}\,\frac{t^{m}}{m!},

and satisfy

for

(see
[

A
mutatis mutandis
version of Theorem
1.1
can be proved for the

in suitable ranges, yielding
corresponding “almost all primes” corollaries for congruences of cusp forms
with Nebentypus, special values of
p
-adic
L
-functions, and torsion in

K
-groups of rings of integers.

Nuts and Bolts

We first recall the relevant preliminaries for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Throughout, we fix
\alpha>1/2
and set
M_{\alpha}(p)=\lfloor\sqrt{p}/(\log p)^{\alpha}\rfloor
.
For an integer
m\geq 1,
we define

(5)

P_{m}\coloneq\prod_{k=1}^{m}\bigl|\num(B_{2k})\bigr|\ \in\ \mathbb{Z}^{+}.

Let
\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)
denote the number of
distinct
prime divisors of a positive
integer
n
:

\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)\coloneq\#\{\ell\text{ prime}:\ \ell\mid n\}.

The next lemma bounds the number of
m
-regular primes, when
m
is a fixed positive integer.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p\geq 5
be prime and
m\geq 1
.
If
p
is not
m
-regular,
then
p\mid P_{m}
. In particular, we have

\#\{\,p\leq X\text{ prime}:\ p\text{ is not $m$-regular}\}\ \leq\ \omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m}).

Proof.

If
p
is not
m
-regular, there exists
2\leq 2k\leq\min(m,p-3)
such that

p\mid\num(B_{2k})
. By definition of
P_{m}
, the factor
|\num(B_{2k})|
appears in
the product, hence
p\mid P_{m}
. The counting inequality follows because distinct
primes
p\leq X
dividing
P_{m}
form a subset of the set of all distinct primes
dividing
P_{m}
, whose cardinality is
\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m})
.
∎

We first recall two standard facts about Bernoulli numbers.

Lemma 2.2

(Euler’s formula for
\zeta(2k)
)
.

For each integer
k\geq 1
, we have

\zeta(2k)=(-1)^{k+1}\,\frac{(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!}\,B_{2k}\ \qquad{\text{a}nd}\ \qquad\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k}.

Proof.

This is classical; see
[
1
, Ch. 12, Thm. 12.17]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(von Staudt–Clausen)
.

For each integer
k\geq 1
, we have

B_{2k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\ \mathrm{prime}\\
(\ell-1)\mid 2k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell}\ \in\ \mathbb{Z}.

In particular, we have that

\den(B_{2k})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\ \mathrm{prime}\\
(\ell-1)\mid 2k\end{subarray}}\ell,

which implies that

(6)

\den(B_{2k})\leq\prod_{\ell\leq 2k+1}\ell.

Proof.

See
[
1
, Ch. 12, Exercise 12]
for a proof.
If
(\ell-1)\mid 2k
then
\ell\leq 2k+1
, so the stated upper bound follows.
∎

We use this lemma to derive an upper bound for the numerators of Bernoulli numbers,
when expressed in lowest terms.

Lemma 2.4
.

There is an absolute constant
C_{1}>0
such that for every integer
k\geq 1
, we have

\log\bigl|\num(B_{2k})\bigr|\ \leq\ C_{1}\,k\log(2k).

Consequently, there is an absolute constant
C_{2}>0
such that for all
m\geq 2
,

\log P_{m}\ \leq\ C_{2}\,m^{2}\log m.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 1
.
By Lemma
2.2
, we have

|B_{2k}|=\frac{2(2k)!\,\zeta(2k)}{(2\pi)^{2k}}\leq\frac{2(2k)!\,\zeta(2)}{(2\pi)^{2k}}<\frac{4(2k)!}{(2\pi)^{2k}},

since
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6<2
.
Thanks to Lemma
2.3
, denominators of Bernoulli numbers are square-free, and satisfy

\den(B_{2k})\leq(2k+1)!.

Writing
B_{2k}=\num(B_{2k})/\den(B_{2k})
in lowest terms gives

|\num(B_{2k})|=|B_{2k}|\den(B_{2k})
, hence

|\num(B_{2k})|\leq\left(\frac{4(2k)!}{(2\pi)^{2k}}\right)(2k+1)!.

Taking logs and using that
-2k\log(2\pi)<0
, we may discard the negative term to obtain

\log\bigl|\num(B_{2k})\bigr|\leq\log 4+\log\bigl((2k)!\bigr)+\log\bigl((2k+1)!\bigr).

Using the elementary bound
\log(n!)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log j\leq n\log n
, we get

\log\bigl((2k)!\bigr)\leq 2k\log(2k)\quad\text{and}\quad\log\bigl((2k+1)!\bigr)\leq(2k+1)\log(2k+1).

Moreover, since
2k+1\leq 4k
, we have
\log(2k+1)\leq\log(4k)=\log(2k)+\log 2\leq 2\log(2k)
,
and also
2k+1\leq 3k
for
k\geq 1
. Hence

\log\bigl((2k+1)!\bigr)\leq(2k+1)\log(2k+1)\leq(2k+1)\log(4k)\leq 3k\cdot 2\log(2k)=6k\log(2k).

Putting these estimates together yields

\log\bigl|\num(B_{2k})\bigr|\leq\log 4+2k\log(2k)+6k\log(2k)\leq C_{1}\,k\log(2k)

for a suitable absolute constant
C_{1}>0
.

For the second claim, summing over
1\leq k\leq m
and using
\log(2k)\leq\log(2m)
gives

\log P_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\log\bigl|\num(B_{2k})\bigr|\leq C_{1}\log(2m)\sum_{k=1}^{m}k=\frac{C_{1}}{2}m(m+1)\log(2m)\leq C_{2}\,m^{2}\log m

for all
m\geq 2
and a suitable absolute constant
C_{2}>0
.
∎

In the following lemma, we apply the Prime Number Theorem.
It is a standard consequence of the asymptotic growth of the primorial.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exist absolute constants
C_{3}>0
and
N_{0}\geq 3
such that for every integer

n\geq N_{0}
, we have

\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)\ \leq\ C_{3}\,\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}.

Proof.

Let
t=\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)
and list the distinct prime divisors of
n
in increasing order:

q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots<q_{t}
. Then

n\ \geq\ q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{t}\ \geq\ 2\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots p_{t},

where
p_{t}
is the
t
th prime and the last product is the
t
th primorial

p_{t}^{\#}\coloneq\prod_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}
.
Taking logs yields

\log n\ \geq\ \log(p_{t}^{\#})=\vartheta(p_{t}),

where
\vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p
is Chebyshev’s
\theta
-function.

The Prime Number Theorem is equivalent to
\vartheta(x)\sim x
as
x\to\infty

(see
[
1
, Ch. 4]
).
In particular, there exists
x_{0}\geq 2
such that for all
x\geq x_{0}
,

(7)

\vartheta(x)\ \geq\ \frac{x}{2}.

Likewise, the Prime Number Theorem implies
\pi(x)\sim x/\log x
, hence there
exists
x_{1}\geq 3
such that for all
x\geq x_{1}
, we have

(8)

\pi(x)\ \leq\ 2\,\frac{x}{\log x}.

Fix
t_{0}
so that
p_{t}\geq\max(x_{0},x_{1})
for all
t\geq t_{0}
.

Now assume
t\geq t_{0}
.
Applying (
8
) at
x=p_{t}
gives

t=\pi(p_{t})\leq 2p_{t}/\log p_{t}
, hence

p_{t}\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\log p_{t}\geq\tfrac{t}{2}\log t
(since
p_{t}\geq t+1
).
Applying (
7
) at
x=p_{t}
yields

\vartheta(p_{t})\ \geq\ \frac{p_{t}}{2}\ \geq\ \frac{t}{4}\log t.

Therefore, for
t\geq t_{0}
, we have

\log n\ \geq\ \vartheta(p_{t})\ \geq\ \frac{t}{4}\log t.

Write
L=\log n
.
For
n
sufficiently large (equivalently
L
sufficiently large), the inequality

L\geq\tfrac{t}{4}\log t
forces
t\leq 8L/\log L
(a standard inversion of

t\log t\ll L
).
Concretely: choose
L_{0}
large enough that
\log(8L/\log L)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\log L

for all
L\geq L_{0}
. If
t>8L/\log L
and
L\geq L_{0}
, then

t\log t\geq(8L/\log L)\cdot(\tfrac{1}{2}\log L)=4L
, contradicting
t\log t\leq 4L
.
Thus
t\leq 8L/\log L
for all
L\geq L_{0}
.

Finally, absorb the finitely many integers
n
with
\log n<L_{0}
into the constant.
That is, define
N_{0}=\left\lceil e^{L_{0}}\right\rceil
and take

C_{3}\coloneq\max\!\left(8,\ \max_{3\leq n<N_{0}}\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right),

which is finite.
Then
\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(n)\leq C_{3}\,\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
holds for all
n\geq N_{0}
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Fix
\alpha>1/2
.
Let
X\geq 3
and set

m_{X}\coloneq\left\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{X}}{(\log X)^{\alpha}}\right\rfloor.

We first reduce the varying range
M_{\alpha}(p)
to the uniform range
m_{X}
.
Consider the function

f(t)=\frac{\sqrt{t}}{(\log t)^{\alpha}}

for
t>1
.
A direct calculation gives

f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{t^{1/2}(\log t)^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{\log t}\right),

so
f
is increasing for all
t>e^{2\alpha}
.
Hence, for every prime
p
with
e^{2\alpha}\leq p\leq X
, we have

M_{\alpha}(p)=\lfloor f(p)\rfloor\ \leq\ \lfloor f(X)\rfloor=m_{X}.

Therefore, if such a prime
p
is
not

M_{\alpha}(p)
-regular, it is also not

m_{X}
-regular.
The remaining primes
p<e^{2\alpha}
form a finite set depending only on
\alpha
; we absorb
their contribution into the final constant.

It remains to bound the number of primes
p\leq X
which are not
m_{X}
-regular.
By Lemma
2.1
,

\#\{p\leq X:\ p\ \text{not $m_{X}$-regular}\}\ \leq\ \omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m_{X}}).

For
X
large enough we have
P_{m_{X}}\geq N_{0}
(where
N_{0}
is from Lemma
2.5
),
so Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.4
yield

\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m_{X}})\leq C_{3}\,\frac{\log P_{m_{X}}}{\log\log P_{m_{X}}}\leq C_{3}\,\frac{C_{2}\,m_{X}^{2}\log m_{X}}{\log\log P_{m_{X}}}.

We now give a concrete lower bound for
\log\log P_{m_{X}}
in terms of
\log m_{X}
.
For any integer
m\geq 1,
we have

P_{m}=\prod_{k=1}^{m}|\num(B_{2k})|\ \geq\ |\num(B_{2m})|\ \geq\ |B_{2m}|,

since
\den(B_{2m})\geq 1
.
By Euler’s formula (i.e., Lemma
2.2
) and the trivial inequality
\zeta(2m)>1
, we have

|B_{2m}|=\frac{2(2m)!\,\zeta(2m)}{(2\pi)^{2m}}\ \geq\ \frac{2(2m)!}{(2\pi)^{2m}}.

Taking logarithms and using the elementary Stirling lower bound (for example, see
[
16
]
)

(2m)!\geq(2m/e)^{2m},

we obtain

\log P_{m}\ \geq\ \log|B_{2m}|\ \geq\ \log 2+\log((2m)!)-2m\log(2\pi)\ \geq\ \log 2+2m\log(2m)-2m-2m\log(2\pi).

In particular, there exists an absolute integer
m_{0}\geq 2
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
we have

\log P_{m}\geq m\log m,

and hence

\log\log P_{m}\geq\log(m\log m)\geq\log m.

Since
m_{X}\to\infty
with
X
, for all sufficiently large
X
we have
m_{X}\geq m_{0}
, and therefore

\log\log P_{m_{X}}\geq\log m_{X}.

Substituting this into the previous bound gives, for all sufficiently large
X
,

\omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m_{X}})\leq C_{3}\,\frac{C_{2}\,m_{X}^{2}\log m_{X}}{\log\log P_{m_{X}}}\leq C_{2}C_{3}\,m_{X}^{2}.

Finally,
m_{X}^{2}\leq X/(\log X)^{2\alpha}
, so for all sufficiently large
X
, we have

\#\{p\leq X:\ p\ \text{not $M_{\alpha}(p)$-regular}\}\ \leq\ \omega_{\mathrm{pf}}(P_{m_{X}})\leq(C_{2}C_{3})\,\frac{X}{(\log X)^{2\alpha}}.

Enlarging the constant to absorb the finitely many exceptional primes
p<e^{2\alpha}
and the finitely many
remaining values of
X
proves the theorem.
∎

What did we learn?

At first glance, the proofs found by AxiomProver do not resemble the narrative presented in this paper.
Turning a Lean file into a human-readable proof can be hard
because Lean is written as code for a type-checker,
not as an explanation for a reader.
It makes all the “obvious” bookkeeping explicit (rewrite steps, coercions, side conditions, case splits),
and it often follows whatever lemmas and tactic-driven routes are most convenient
for the library rather than the most illuminating conceptual path.

A human mathematician can usually condense this dramatically by
relying on shared historical context
