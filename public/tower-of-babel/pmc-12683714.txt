Automated endpoint titer ELISAs for high-throughput immunogenicity evaluations using a BioMek i7 liquid handler
Authors: Barbara Theriot, Andrew N Macintyre
Published: 2025

Introduction

Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISAs) are routinely used to evaluate the ability of vaccine candidates to induce antigen-specific antibodies. In an endpoint titer ELISA, serially-diluted samples are applied to antigen-coated plates and bound antibodies identified using isotype-specific detection reagents. The reciprocal of the last dilution at which the signal is significantly above background is reported as the endpoint titer. Results may also be reported as the area under the dilution curve (AUC) or the reciprocal of the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50).

Endpoint titer ELISAs can be performed and analyzed manually; however, this becomes laborious and error prone when testing large numbers of samples. Evaluation of even a single immunogen can necessitate collection of thousands of datapoints given investigators often wish to compare how various immunogen doses and routes of administration induce different antibody isotypes over time. Here, we provide the methodology and code required to run and analyze fully-automated endpoint titer assays using a Beckman Coulter BioMek i7 liquid handler. The method is based on well-established manual protocols reported previously [1]. Data generated using the automated method have been reported in multiple manuscripts evaluating the immunogenicity of gonorrhea and influenza vaccine candidates in mice [2–5] and the method can easily be adapted for other species, target antigens, and assay approaches (e.g. Singh et al. [6]). While thousands of manuscripts report endpoint titer data and many manual protocols have been published, the methods and underlying code to run this assay on the BioMek i7 platform have not previously been publicly available. There is no public repository for BioMek code sharing, and so we are distributing this methodology to facilitate transparency and code reuse.

The workflow we report here is capable of determining endpoint titers for up to 230 samples per batch and includes options to test one to six isotypes per batch; this equates to up to 1380 titer determinations per batch. The starting dilution for sample titration can be adjusted to allow testing of samples likely to contain high or low levels of total antibody (e.g. serum versus mucosal lavage). The entire workflow takes three days per batch; however, only about one to two hours of these are hands-on versus around 20 hours when running the assay manually. Central to the workflow is a BioMek i7 – a common liquid handler often found in larger academic and core service laboratories. Data analysis uses Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism, two non-specialized software tools commonly available in academic and research environments.

Detection antibody addition and plate reading

Timing: 20 min setup time. Run time is approximately 3 hours plus 11 min per assay plate. This accounts for all steps up to data collection. After all data are collected, there is one hour deep clean of the plate washer to prevent residue buildup.

This step washes each plate, adds 20 μL/well of horse radish peroxidase(HRP)-conjugated detection antibody, incubates the plates for 1 h 45 min at room temperature, washes again, adds 20 μL/well HRP colorimetric substrate, incubates for 14 min, then adds 10 μL/well sulfuric acid to stop the HRP reaction and reads plate absorbance at 450 nm. Up to six detection antibodies can be used – typically IgA, total IgG, IgG1, IgG2b, IgG3, IgM for samples from C57/B6 mice. Detection antibodies are added from reservoirs in rotation – i.e. for six detection antibody reservoirs and 18 plates, detection antibody one will be added to plates 1,7,13; antibody two to plates 2, 8, 14 etc.

NOTE: IgG2 subclass expression, and hence detection antibody selection, is mouse strain dependent [7].

Prepare 130 mL/reservoir of each antibody in secondary antibody diluent. This volume is the same regardless of the number of assay plates. This simplifies deck setup and reduces the risk of drips from the pipetting head by pipetting from the top of the liquid rather than bottom of the reservoir. When testing large numbers of plates with a small number of detection antibodies, it may be necessary to split the same detection antibody solution across multiple reservoirs (e.g. for 40 plates of one detection antibody, prepare 650 mL of detection antibody and split it across five reservoirs).

Limitations

Direct binding of peptide antigens to the assay plates can lead to an underestimation of binding titers. Peptides should be attached to the plate using streptavidin NHS ester: coat the plates with 20 μL/well 10ng/mL streptavidin (Thermo #424302) overnight at 4°C, add 20 μL/well 10ng/mL SMPH (Thermo #22363), incubate for 1 h at room temperature, wash, and then coat with peptide at 5 μg/mL in CBC buffer.

Peptides should be attached to the plate using streptavidin NHS ester: coat the plates with 20 μL/well 10ng/mL streptavidin (Thermo #424302) overnight at 4°C, add 20 μL/well 10ng/mL SMPH (Thermo #22363), incubate for 1 h at room temperature, wash, and then coat with peptide at 5 μg/mL in CBC buffer.

AUC, EC50 and endpoint titer are not linearly related and can diverge depending on the curve shape. Endpoint titers are sensitive to background noise, whereas EC50 determinations are more robust to background noise but are skewed by poor curve fits. Samples with low titers may have insufficient datapoints above background to allow for EC50 determination (e.g. Fig. 2F – green sample). All three metrics are semi-quantitative and not comparable across independent assays without a shared control.

The assay requires all wells on a plate to be coated with antigen regardless of whether or not they are completely loaded with samples. This means some antigen is wasted when the total number of samples and controls is not a factor of 23. Antigens can be expensive or time-consuming to produce, and so it may be desirable to manually coat only the wells to which samples will be added.

The data are not normalized to correct for differences in total antibody concentration. This is a concern for mucosal samples such as bronchoalveolar lavage or fecal extract as sampling efficiency and starting material mass can vary. Normalization can be performed by assaying samples with a commercial total or isotype-specific immunoglobulin quantification ELISA or multiplex kit and then dividing the endpoint titers by total antibody concentration. This also provides an assessment of sample quality as samples containing no measurable immunoglobulin should be considered for exclusion.
