Instance-optimal estimation of L 2 L_{2} -norm
Tomer Adar

Abstract
The L 2 L_{2} -norm, or collision norm, is a core entity in the analysis of distributions and probabilistic algorithms. Batu and Canonne (FOCS 2017) presented an extensive analysis of algorithmic aspects of the L 2 L_{2} -norm and its connection to uniformity testing. However, when it comes to estimating the L 2 L_{2} -norm itself, their algorithm is not always optimal compared to the instance-specific second-moment bounds, O ⁡ ( 1 / ( ε ​ ‖ μ ‖ 2 ) + ( ‖ μ ‖ 3 3 − ‖ μ ‖ 2 4 ) / ( ε 2 ​ ‖ μ ‖ 2 4 ) ) O(1/(\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})+(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})/(\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})) , as stated by Batu (WoLA 2025, open problem session). In this paper, we present an unbiased L 2 L_{2} -estimation algorithm whose sample complexity matches the instance-specific second-moment analysis. Additionally, we show that Ω ⁡ ( 1 / ( ε ​ ‖ μ ‖ 2 ) ) \Omega(1/(\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})) is indeed a per-instance lower bound for estimating the norm of a distribution μ \mu by sampling (even for non-unbiased estimators).

Introduction

The collision norm
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, which equals to the probability that two independent samples from a distribution
\mu
are the same, is a fundamental entity in the analysis of distributions and probabilistic algorithms. For example, it lies in the algorithmic core of uniformity testing
[
Pan08
]
, as the uniform distribution has the lowest collision norm among the distributions over the same domain. Uniformity testing is used as an essential module in various testing algorithms, such as
[
BFF+01
,
DK16
,
Gol20
]
. A few additional perspectives of uniformity testing, as well as its generalization of testing identity to a known distribution, have been studied by
[
VV17
,
DKN15
,
ADK15
,
DGPP16
]
.

Instance-specific testing, in which the complexity bounds are analyzed for every input (more accurately, for every
congruence class
of inputs), has been studied in
[
Val11
]
for histogram testing and in
[
VV13
]
for identity testing, in which we test an unknown input distribution for being equal to an explicit distribution
\mu
. For example, for identity testing, while the worst-case complexity is known to be
\Theta(\sqrt{N}/\varepsilon^{2})
(both upper and lower bound) where
\mu
is defined over
N
-element domain, the worst-case upper bound for an individual hard-coded
\mu
(and an unknown input
\nu
) can be much lower, since we can use knowledge about the exact structure of
\mu
to enhance the test.

In
[
BC17
]
there is an study of uniformity testing of distributions over unknown, possibly infinite discrete domains. As stated by Batu

, even though second-moment analysis shows that
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})/\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})
samples suffice for estimating the collision norm within a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-multiplicative factor, there is no algorithm that guarantees this sample complexity (in expectation) for every given
\mu
and
\varepsilon>0
. The reason is that without any prior knowledge about
\mu
, it is difficult to figure out this sufficient number of samples. In particular, estimating
(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})/\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}
within a
(1\pm O(1))
-factor seems to be as hard as the initial task of estimating
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
within a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-factor.

When drawing samples from an unknown distribution
\mu
over an unknown domain, the algorithm can only base its decisions on the
fingerpint
, also known as “histogram of histograms”. In other words, it is a sequence
(a_{1},\ldots)
for which, for every
i\geq 1
, there are exactly
a_{i}
elements that appear exactly
i
times in the sample sequence. (
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i\cdot a_{i}
is the number of samples). When the number of samples is not determined in advance, for every
m\geq 1
the algorithm can consider the fingerprint of the first
m
samples and decide whether to terminate or to draw the
m+1
st sample.

The following algorithm for estimating
\|\mu\|_{2}
appears in
[
BC17
]
: we choose some integer
k
, and then draw samples until we have at least
k
collisions. The result is
k/\binom{M}{2}
, where
M
is the number of samples. Considering the fingerprint
(a_{1},\ldots)
, the number of collisions is
\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\binom{i}{2}a_{i}
.

When fixing the number of samples to be
m
, the expected number of collisions is
m\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
. If
\binom{m}{2}\approx k/\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, then this expected value is
\approx k
, and therefore, we expect to find the
k
th collision in a range “around
m
”.

We would rather a small choice of
k
, since the expected sample complexity of the algorithm till termination is
\Theta(\sqrt{k}/\|\mu\|_{2})
. However, if
k
is too small, then the “likely termination range” around
m
would be too wide, since we have to consider the variance as well. For
m
samples, the variance is close to
\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+m^{3}\left(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)
.

When considering the worst-case, as
[
BC17
]
do, we can use
k=C/\varepsilon^{4}
. By Chebyshev’s inequality, the likely termination range is
(1\pm\varepsilon)m_{0}
, where
m_{0}=\Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2})
is the (non-integer) solution for
\binom{m_{0}}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}=k
. Note that higher-order norms can be estimated using a similar algorithmic approach.

When considering
instance-specific
sample complexity, we assume that the algorithm receives a
\Theta(1)
-approximation of
\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}
as an advice. This way, the knowledge about the variance, which is possibly much smaller then its worst-case, allows the algorithm to choose smaller values of
k
. Given this advice, we can use the worst-case algorithm to estimate
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
within a
(1\pm 1/2)
-multiplicative error, and then choose
k=\min\{C_{1}/\varepsilon^{2},C_{2}(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})^{2}/\varepsilon^{4}\|\mu\|_{2}^{6}\}
. This reduces the expected sample complexity to
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})/\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu|_{2}^{4})
. We denote the coefficient
\frac{\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}}{\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}}
by
t_{\mu}
(short form:
t
), and rewrite the instance reference complexity as
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+t_{\mu}/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Theorem 1.1

(note=Short form of Lemma
)
.

For every
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, there exists an unbiased estimator for
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
whose input is a sampling access from a discrete distribution
\mu
over an unknown domain such that:

•

With probability at least
2/3
, the output is in the range
(1\pm\varepsilon)\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
.

•

The expected sample complexity is
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+t_{\mu}/\varepsilon^{2})
, where
t_{\mu}=\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}/\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}-1
.

Whether of not the second-moment reference complexity
1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+t_{\mu}/\varepsilon^{2}
is indeed a lower-bound for every
\mu
is an open question. In this paper we show an instance-specific lower bound of
\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
, which improves on the previously known
\Omega(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|\mu\|_{2})
lower bound
[
BC17
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(note=Short form of Lemma
)
.

For every explicitly-given discrete distribution
\mu
, there exists a lower bound of
\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
for distinguishing between
\mu
and distributions
\nu
for which
\|\nu\|_{2}^{2}\notin(1\pm O(\varepsilon))\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
.

In other words, Theorem
1
states that there is an instance-specific
\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
lower-bound for estimating
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
within
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-multiplicative error by sampling a given discrete distribution
\mu
over an unknown domain.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we explicitly provide terms and notations that we use across the paper.

Standard notations

A
discrete distribution
over a domain
\Omega
is a function
\mu:\Omega\to[0,1]
for which
\sum_{i\in\Omega}\mu(i)=1
.

•

The notations
\mu(i)
and
\Pr_{\mu}[i]
are considered identical.

•

For an element
i\notin\Omega
, we consider
\mu(i)=0
.

•

For a set
A
, the notations
\mu(A)
,
\Pr_{\mu}[A]
and
\sum_{i\in A}\mu(i)
are considered identical.

•

For a set
A
for which
\mu(A)>0
, the
conditional distribution

\mu_{A}
is defined as
\mu_{A}:A\to[0,1]
using
\mu_{A}(i)=\mu(i)/\mu(A)
.

•

The notations
\mu_{A}(i)
,
\Pr_{\mu_{A}}[i]
and
\Pr_{\mu}[i|A]
are considered identical.

•

For a set
B
, the notations
\mu_{A}(B)
,
\Pr_{\mu_{A}}[B]
and
\Pr_{\mu}[B|A]
are considered identical.

For a
random variable

X
, represented as a function
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
:

•

The
expected value
of
X
(according to
\mu
) is defined as
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}_{\mu}[X]=\sum_{i\in\Omega}\mu(i)\cdot X(i)
.

•

The
variance
of
X
(according to
\mu
) is defined as
\mathop{{\rm Var}\/}_{\mu}[X]=\mathop{{\rm E}\/}_{\mu}[X^{2}]-(\mathop{{\rm E}\/}_{\mu}[X])^{2}
.

For every
k\neq 0
, the
L_{k}
-norm
of a distribution
\mu
is defined as one of the following entities:
\|\mu\|_{k}^{k}=\sum_{i\in\Omega}(\mu(i))^{k}
and
\|\mu\|_{k}=\left(\sum_{i\in\Omega}(\mu(i))^{k}\right)^{1/k}
. When relevant, we explicitly mention the entity we refer to.

Common distributions

We refer to two common distributions: the binomial distribution
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
, defined over
\{0,\ldots,n\}
as
\Pr[i]=p^{i}(1-p)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}
, and Poisson distribution
\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)
, defined over
\mathbb{N}
(including zero) as
\Pr[i]=\frac{\lambda^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda}
.

Divergence measures

We refer to a few divergence measures, mostly in the lower-bound part.

•

Total-variation distance (a metric):
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\Omega_{\mu}\cup\Omega_{\tau}}\left|{\mu(i)-\tau(i)}\right|
.

•

Kullback-Leibler divergence:
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mu\;\middle\|\;\tau\right)=\mathop{{\rm E}\/}_{\mu}[\log(\mu(i)/\tau(i))]=\sum_{i\in\Omega_{\mu}}\mu(i)\log(\mu(i)/\tau(i))
.

•

\chi^{2}
-divergence:
\chi^{2}(\mu,\tau)=\mathop{{\rm E}\/}_{\mu}[(\tau(i)/\mu(i)-1)^{2}]=\sum_{i\in\Omega_{\mu}}\frac{(\tau(i)-\mu(i))^{2}}{\mu(i)}
.

Large-deviation bounds

We use the following well-known deviation bounds:

•

Markov (first-moment): if
\Pr[X\geq 0]=1
and
a>0
, then
\Pr[X\geq a\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X]]\leq 1/a
.

•

Chebyshev (second-moment): If
a>0
, then
\Pr[\left|{X-\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X]}\right|\geq a]\leq\mathop{{\rm Var}\/}[X]/a
.

•

Chernoff (additive): if
a>0
, then
\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\leq np-a],\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\geq np+a]\leq e^{-2a^{2}/n}
.

•

Chernoff (multiplicative): If
\delta>0
, then
\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\geq(1+\delta)np]\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2+\delta}np}
.

•

Chernoff (multiplicative): If
0<\delta<1
, then
\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)\leq(1-\delta)np]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}np}
.

Note that the multiplicative Chernoff bounds are also applicable to Poisson distribution (through
\lambda=np
), and that the coefficient
\frac{\delta^{2}}{2+\delta}
is usually relaxed to
\frac{1}{3}\delta
for
\delta\geq 1
and to
\frac{1}{3}\delta^{2}
for
0<\delta\leq 1
.

Estimation behaviors

When estimating a value
\mathit{ans}
using a random variable
X
, we consider the following behaviors (which the estimator may or may not have):

•

Non-negativeness:
X\geq 0
with probability
1
.

•

Correctness of estimation: with high probability, such as
\Omega(1)
or
1-\eta
(for an explicit error parameter
\eta
), the random estimation
X
is considered accurate. For example,
\left|{X-\mathit{ans}}\right|\leq\varepsilon
or
\left|{X/\mathit{ans}-1}\right|\leq\varepsilon
(for an explicit accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
).

•

Unbiasedness: the expected estimation
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X]
equals to the estimated value
\mathit{ans}
.

•

Moment preserving: for
r\in\mathbb{R}
, an estimator is said to
preserve the
r
th moment
if
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X^{r}]=O(\mathit{ans}^{r})
. An estimator is said to
strongly preserve the
r
th moment
if for every
\ell\geq 0
there exists
C_{\ell}>0
for which
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X^{r}(\log X^{r})^{\ell}]=O(C_{\ell}\cdot\mathit{ans}^{r}\cdot(\log\mathit{ans}^{r})^{\ell})
.

Observe that, by Jensen’s inequality, if a non-negative estimator preserves the
r
th moment, then it strongly preserves every moment
r^{\prime}
which is strictly between
0
and
r
(that is,
0<r^{\prime}<r
or
r<r^{\prime}<0
, depending on the sign of
r
).

3
Non-technical overview

This paper is written in a top-down form. After the preliminaries, this non-technical overview and the technical overview, each section provides full details about its main statement (either an algorithmic module or a lower bound), as well as a full pseudo-code and verifiable proofs. Statements labeled as “technical lemma” are proved in the last subsection of the section in which they appear.

Upper bound

The second-moment analysis results in an algorithm that takes an advice
r
and uses
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+r/\varepsilon^{2})
samples to estimate
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, such that if
r\geq t_{\mu}=\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}/\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}-1
, then the estimation is in the range
(1\pm\varepsilon)\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
with high probability.

For having an instance-optimal sample complexity without any knowledge a-priori about
t_{\mu}
, we have to algorithmically obtain a random variable
r
, representing an advice, such that
r\geq t_{\mu}
with high probability (for correctness) and
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[r]=O(t_{\mu}+\varepsilon/\|\mu\|_{2})
(for complexity).

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon)
, then it suffices to: (1) estimate
\|\mu\|_{2}
within a
(1\pm O(1))
-multiplicative factor, which is not a bottleneck, and (2) estimate
\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}
within an
(1\pm O(1))
-multiplicative factor or determine that
\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}=O(\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}^{3})
. In the first case, we can obtain a fixed-factor estimation of
t_{\mu}+1=\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}/\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}
, but since
\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon)
, this is actually a fixed-factor estimation of
t_{\mu}+O(\varepsilon/\|\mu\|_{2})
. In the second case, we can determine that
t_{\mu}=O(\varepsilon/\|\mu\|_{2})
and avoid a more accurate (and expensive) estimation.

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=\Omega(\varepsilon^{3}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon)))
and also
O(\varepsilon^{3/5}/\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon)))
, then we can estimate
t_{\mu}
directly by its definition, by estimating
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
and
\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}
within a
(1\pm O(\varepsilon/\|\mu\|_{2}))
-multiplicative factor. The cost of this estimation is
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
for this range of “medium”
\|\mu\|_{2}
s. For higher
\|\mu\|_{2}
this resolution is too accurate (and expensive), and for lower
\|\mu\|_{2}
we cannot take advantage of the
\Omega(1)
-accuracy.

Estimating larger
\|\mu\|_{2}
s is more intricate. In particular, it involves reduction to distributions defined over an explicit finite domain.

For distributions over a finite domain of
N
elements, we can estimate
N\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}-1
within a
(1\pm O(1))
-multiplicative factor by iteratively looking for a resolution
\hat{\varepsilon}
for which
(1-\hat{\varepsilon})\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}>(1+\hat{\varepsilon})/N
. If the sequence of resolutions starts with
\Omega(1)
and decreases exponentially, then
\hat{\varepsilon}=\Theta(N\|\mu\|_{2}-1)
for the earliest
\hat{\varepsilon}
for which
(1-\hat{\varepsilon})\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}>(1+\hat{\varepsilon})/N
. Note that this logic fails if
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}=(1+\Omega(1))/N
, but this case is easy to detect and to resolve, since it implies that
N\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}-1=\Theta(N\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
.

We consider a distribution over a finite domain of
N
elements as
friendly
if every element has probability at least
\Omega(1/N)
. If
\mu
is friendly, then we can estimate
t_{\mu}
by learning the mass of every element within a
(1\pm O(1))
-multiplicative factor, at the cost of
(N\log N)
samples, and then embed them in an explicit formula that involves the (estimated) individual masses of
\mu
and the (estimated) value of
N\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}-1
.

If
\|\mu\|_{2}=\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon)))
, then we can algorithmically find a partition of the domain to a set
A
for which
\mu_{A}
is friendly and a set
B
whose total mass is
O(t_{\mu})
, such that
\left|{t_{\mu}-t_{\mu_{A}}}\right|=O(\mu(B))
.

An
\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
lower bound

While tight instance-specific identity testing bounds are already provided by
[
VV13
]
, the collision norm of their hard-to-distinguish input distributions is not necessarily outside the range
(1\pm\Omega(\varepsilon))\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
with sufficiently high probability. Therefore, we use an ad-hoc construction based on their ideas.

We recall a common construction for showing the
\Omega(\sqrt{N}/\varepsilon^{2})
lower bound for uniformity testing over an explicitly given domain (for example,
[
Pan08
]
) and generalize it to non-uniform distributions as a per-instance
\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2})
lower bound. We observe that the collision norm of the constructed distribution is usually in the range
(1\pm\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}))\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, and therefore, we use
\varepsilon^{\prime}\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon}
to obtain an
\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
-sample lower-bound for distinguishing
\mu
from distributions whose collision norm is
(1\pm\Theta(\varepsilon))\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
.

4.1
Elementary tools

Amplification

The success probability of our procedures is a parameter
\eta
. For some procedures, the base success probability is
2/3
and they use a generic amplification patch (which appears in the pseudocode as a preamble declaration rather than an actual code) to match the
1-\eta
bound. For
\eta<1/3
, amplification of the success probability from
1-1/3
to
1-\eta
is done by taking the median of
O(\log(1/\eta))
independent estimations.

Lemma 4.1

(note= , store=lemma:amplify-1/3-to-eta, label=lemma:amplify-1/3-to-eta)
.

If
0<\eta<1
and
q\geq 18\ln\eta^{-1}
is an integer, then
\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(q,2/3)\leq q/2]\leq\eta
. In other words, if a sample is “good” with probability at least
2/3
, then the median of
q
independent samples is “good” with probability at least
1-\eta
.

Lemma 4.2

(note= , store=lemma:median-expected-value, label=lemma:median-expected-value)
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable, and let
Y
be the median of
k\geq 1
independent variables distributing the same as
X
. If
k
is even, then we use the “lower median” (the value of rank
{\left\lfloor{k/2}\right\rfloor}
, where the minimum has rank
1
). In this setting,
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[Y]\leq C\cdot\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X]
, for some constant
C
independent of
X
and
k
.

4.4
Top-level algorithm

Our core task for estimating
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
is finding an advice
r\geq t_{\mu}
where
t_{\mu}=\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}/\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}-1
, and then use the
L_{2}
base-estimator. We analyze three (overlapping) cases:
\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon)
(“small”),
\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon^{3/5}/\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon))),\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon)))
(“medium”) and
\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{poly}(\log(1/\varepsilon)))
(“large”). We overview each case in its own subsection.

Since the expected complexity of the procedure handling each case is suboptimal in other cases (unless the input belongs to an overlapping part), we have to figure out the relevant case with a very high probability. Distinguishing between the first case (small
\|\mu\|_{2}
) and the other cases requires
1-O(\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
success probability. Distinguishing between the medium and the large cases only requires
1-O(\varepsilon^{2})
.

Unfortunately, to test
\|\mu\|_{2}
for being
O(\varepsilon)
versus
\Omega(\varepsilon)
with an
O(\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
-error using our reference estimators, we must draw either
O(\log(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})/\|\mu\|_{2})
or
O(\log(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})/\varepsilon)
samples, which are incompatible with the bi-criteria
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
bound. Instead, we first use an
(1-O(\varepsilon^{2}))
-error estimation to find a “probable case”, and if we take the branch of a medium- or large-
\|\mu\|_{2}
case, then we make another test to verify this choice with success probability
1-O(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
.

The “second-thoughts” test estimates
(1\pm O(1))\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
with a constant success probability, and if the result estimation is smaller than
((3/2)\varepsilon)^{2}
, then it uses additional
O(\log(1/\|\mu\|_{2})/\varepsilon)
samples to make sure, with probability
1-O(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
, that
\|\mu\|_{2}
is indeed small. The pseudocode for
is provided as Algorithm
8
.

Lemma 4.3

(note= , store=lemma:test-L2-magnitude, label=lemma:test-L2-magnitude)
.

Procedure
correctly distinguishes between the case where

\displaystyle\Pr\left[S_{2}/M_{2}>p+\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(M_{2},p)>(p+\varepsilon)M_{2}\right]=\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(M_{2},p)>\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\right)\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[\mathrm{Bin}(M_{2},p)]\right]

Recall that
M_{2}\geq 6\ln\frac{10}{\eta}\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{2}=6\ln\frac{10}{\eta}/\varepsilon
regardless of
S_{1}
. Since
\varepsilon/p\geq 1
, by Chernoff’s bound:

\Pr\left[S_{2}/M_{2}\neq p\pm\varepsilon\right]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{3}(\varepsilon/p)\cdot pM_{2}}=e^{-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon M_{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon\cdot(6\ln(10/\eta)/\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{10}\eta

∎

Rejection sampling

In some procedures we construct a subset
A\subseteq\Omega
and run a subroutine on the conditional distribution
\mu_{A}
. Since our algorithm can only sample
\mu
, it has to simulate an exact sampling of
\mu_{A}
by rejection sampling, which is repeatedly sampling
\mu
until obtaining an element belonging to
A
. If
A
has sufficiently large mass, then the rejection sampling can be done effectively.

Lemma 5.2

(note= , store=lemma:rejection-sampling-concentration, label=lemma:rejection-sampling-concentration)
.

Let
0<\eta\leq 1/3
be an explicit parameter. Let
A
be a subset accessible through the “
i\in^{?}A
” oracle. Assume that we wish to draw an unknown number of independent samples from the conditional distribution
\mu_{A}
by rejection sampling. Consider the following logic: for the
i
th requested sample from
\mu_{A}
, we draw samples from
\mu
until obtaining an
A
-element or until the total number of drawn
\mu
-samples exceeds
4(i+{\left\lceil{12\log(1/\eta)}\right\rceil})
, in which case the rejection-sampler crashes. In this setting, if
\mu(A)>1/2
, then with probability at least
1-\eta
, the rejection sampling does not crash even for infinitely many sample requests.

Proof.

Assume that
\mu(A)>1/2
. For an individual
i\geq 1
, the sampler crashes at the
i
th sample with probability:

\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(4(i+{\left\lceil{12\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil}),1/2)<i\right]

For
i\geq 12\ln(1/\eta)
:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(4(i+{\left\lceil{12\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil}),1/2)<i\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(4i,1/2)<i\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(4i,1/2)<2i-i\right]\leq e^{-2(i)^{2}/(4i)}\leq e^{-i/2}

For
i\leq 12\ln(1/\eta)
:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}(4(i+{\left\lceil{12\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil}),1/2)<i\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}({\left\lceil{48\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil},1/2)<i\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathrm{Bin}({\left\lceil{48\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil},1/2)<\frac{1}{4}\cdot{\left\lceil{48\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rceil}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{12}\cdot 48\ln(1/\eta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-4\ln(1/\eta)}

By the union bound, the probability to crash is bounded by:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{{\left\lfloor{12\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rfloor}}e^{-4\ln(1/\eta)}+\sum_{i={\left\lfloor{12\ln(1/\eta)}\right\rfloor}+1}^{\infty}e^{-i/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 12\ln(1/\eta)\cdot\eta^{4}+e^{-6\ln(1/\eta)}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-i/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 12\ln(1/\eta)\cdot\eta^{4}+\eta^{6}\cdot 3

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(12\eta^{3}\ln(1/\eta)+3\eta^{5})\eta\leq\eta

The last transition is correct for every
0<\eta\leq 1/3
.
∎

Exponential tail

Lemma 5.3

(note= , store=lemma:exponential-tail, label=lemma:exponential-tail)
.

There exist a non-decreasing monotone function
f:\mathbb{N}\times[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the following property: let
X
be any non-negative random variable for which there exists some
a
such that for every integer
\lambda\geq 1
,
\Pr[X\geq\lambda a]\leq(\Pr[X\geq a])^{\lambda}
. If
\Pr[X\geq a]<1
, then for every integer
k\geq 1
,
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X^{k}]\leq a^{k}\cdot f(k,\Pr\left[X\geq a\right])
. In other words, if
k
and
\Pr\left[X\geq a\right]
are considered as constants, then
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X^{k}]=O(a^{k})
.

Proof.

Let
f(k,p)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(i+1)^{k}p^{i}
. For every non-negative random variable
X
with an appropriate
a
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X^{k}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}((i+1)\cdot a)^{k}\Pr\left[i\cdot a\leq X<(i+1)a\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a^{k}+a^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(i+1)^{k}\cdot(\Pr\left[X\geq a\right])^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{k}\cdot\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(i+1)^{k}\cdot(\Pr\left[X\geq a\right])^{i}\right)=a^{k}\cdot f(k,\Pr\left[X\geq a\right])

∎

The unbiased
L_{2}
-estimator

Assume that we draw
m
independent samples from a given distribution
\mu
, and let
S_{m}
be the number of collisions. More explicitly,
S_{m}
is the number of choices of
1\leq i<j\leq m
for which the
i
th sample equals to the
j
th sample. The expected value of
S_{m}
is exactly
\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\binom{m}{2}
, and its variance is bounded by
\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+m^{3}\left(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)
. Note that this variance has a matching lower bound (up to constant factors). The unbiased estimator cannot be used directly, since we must have some knowledge about
\mu
to choose an appropriate number of samples.

Lemma 6.1

(note=Based on
[
BC17
]
, store=lemma:base-algorithm-variance, label=lemma:base-algorithm-variance)
.

Let
\mu
be a discrete distribution over a (possibly infinite) domain. Assume that we draw
m
samples, and let
S_{m}
be the number of collisions. In this setting,
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[S_{m}]=\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathop{{\rm Var}\/}[S_{m}]\leq\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+m^{3}\left(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)
.

Proof.

For every
1\leq i<j\leq m
, let
X_{ij}
be the indicator for a collision between the
i
th sample and the
j
th sample.

\displaystyle E\left[X_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\mu(x))^{2}=\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{ij}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\mu(x))^{2}=\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{ij}X_{ij^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\mu(x))^{3}=\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}

By linearity of expectation,
\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[S_{m}]=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[X_{ij}]=\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
.

For the variance,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[S_{m}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{ij}^{2}\right]+2\sum_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq m\\
j,j^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{ij}X_{ij^{\prime}}\right]+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq m\\
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\neq i,j\end{subarray}}\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{ij}\right]\mathop{{\rm E}\/}\left[X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2m\binom{m-1}{2}\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}+\binom{m}{2}\binom{m-2}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}+\binom{m}{2}\binom{m-2}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}+2\binom{m-2}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}+\left(\binom{m}{2}-(2m-3)\right)\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)

We use
\mathop{{\rm Var}\/}[S_{m}]=\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[S_{m}^{2}]-(\mathop{{\rm E}\/}[S_{m}])^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Var}\/}\left[S_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}+\left(\binom{m}{2}-(2m-3)\right)\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)-\binom{m}{2}^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-(2m-3)\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\left(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+2(m-2)(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{m}{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}+m^{3}(\|\mu\|_{3}^{3}-\|\mu\|_{2}^{4})

∎

Lemma 7.1

(note= , store=lemma:L3-algorithm-variance, label=lemma:L3-algorithm-variance)
.

For every

be the indicator for a collision between the
i

Top-level algorithm

In this section we provide the top-level logic of our estimation algorithm. The algorithm distinguishes between three (overlapping) cases:

•

\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon)
.

•

\|\mu\|_{2}=O(\varepsilon^{3/5}/\log\varepsilon^{-1})
and
\|\mu\|_{2}=\Omega(\varepsilon^{3}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
.

•

\|\mu\|_{2}=\Omega(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon^{-1})
.

While we can distinguish between the cases with probability
1-O(\eta)
at the cost of
O(\log(1/\eta)/\|\mu\|_{2})
samples (for example by Lemma
), we have to make sure that the expected sample complexity is bounded by
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2}+t_{\mu}/\varepsilon^{2})
as well. This requires that, in addition to the
O(\eta)
-error, we must have the following bounds for inputs not belonging to the overlapping ranges:

•

If
\|\mu\|_{2}
belongs to the first case (“small”), then the probability to misclassify it must be
O(\varepsilon^{2}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
.

•

If
\|\mu\|_{2}
belongs to a non-first case (“medium” or “large”), then the probability to misclassify it must be
O(\varepsilon^{2})
.

In a straightforward test, the first constraint would require
\Omega(\log(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})/\max\{\varepsilon,\|\mu\|_{2}\})
samples, which is incompatible with the bi-criteria bound
O(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
. Therefore, we use the intersection of two independent tests: one
O(\varepsilon^{2})
-error classifier and one
O(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})
-verifier to make sure that the first-case is not misclassified.

The verifier

In the verifier
, we use the BC-estimator to have an initial estimation of the magnitude at the cost of
O(\log(1/\eta\varepsilon)/\|\mu\|_{2})
. If
\|\mu\|_{2}
seems to be small then we can accept immediately, but if it seems to be large, then we verify it using additional
O(\log(1/\eta\|\mu\|_{2})/\varepsilon)
samples. As a result, if
\|\mu\|_{2}
is small then we accept with probability
1-\min\{\eta,O(\|\mu\|_{2}^{2})\}
, and if
\|\mu\|_{2}
is large, then we reject with probability at least
1-\eta
, preserving correctness with high probability. The pseudocode for
is provided as Algorithm
8
.

\displaystyle O(\log(32\cdot 3^{k-i}/\eta))\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{i}\|\mu\|_{2}}+\frac{t_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle O((1+k-i)\log(1/\eta))\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{i}\|\mu\|_{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\varepsilon_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\mu(1)\geq\frac{1}{8}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
, then the lower bound is covered by Lemma
. We proceed assuming that
\mu(1)<\frac{1}{8}\|\mu\|_{2}

Let
B_{1}
be the set of odd indexes for which
\mu(i)>\sqrt{2}\mu(i+1)
. Observe that
B_{1}
is exponentially decreasing (if
i_{1}<i_{2}
and both belong to
B_{1}
then
\mu(i_{2})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu(i_{1})

\sum_{i\in B_{1}}(\mu(i))^{2}\leq\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}\max_{i}(\mu(i))^{2}=2\cdot\left(\frac{1}{8}\|\mu\|_{2}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{32}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}
.
\mu(B)\leq\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}2^{-i/2}\max_{i\in B}\mu(B)\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\cdot\frac{1}{8}\|\mu\|_{2}\leq\frac{3}{7}\|\mu\|_{2}
.

Let
B_{2}=\{i:i-1\in B_{1}\}
be the set of even indexes paired with the indexes of
B_{1}
. By definition of
B_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mu(B_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu(i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu(B_{1})\leq\frac{(2+\sqrt{2})/8}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mu\|_{2}\leq\frac{4}{13}\|\mu\|_{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{2}}(\mu(i))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu(i)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{64}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

Let
A=\Omega\setminus(B_{1}\cup B_{2})
, so that
\|\mu_{A}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{i\in A}(\mu(i))^{2}\geq\frac{15}{16}\|\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\mu(B_{1})
and
\mu(B_{2})

\mu(A)\geq 1-\frac{67}{91}\|\mu\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}

We can apply Lemma

\mu_{A}

\varepsilon^{\prime}=16\varepsilon

\mathcal{D}_{A}

A_{1}

\mathcal{D}
over distributions over
\Omega

\nu_{A}

\mathcal{D}_{A}

\nu=\mu(A)\times\nu_{A}+\mu(B_{1}\cup B_{2})\times\mu_{B_{1}\cup B_{2}}

•

When drawing
\nu

\mathcal{D}

3/4

•

Any algorithm that distinguishes between

\nu

Therefore,
\mu

\Omega(1/\varepsilon\|\mu\|_{2})
