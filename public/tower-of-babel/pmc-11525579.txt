Adverse effects of CXCR2 deficiency in mice reared under non-gnotobiotic conditions
Authors: Maximilian J Garcia, Monica S Morales, Tzushan S Yang, Joseph Holden, Olivia L Bossardet, Samuel A Palmer, Marvarakumari Jhala, Stephen Priest, Neeraj Namburu, Nolan Beatty, Sariah E D’Empaire Salomon, Jordan Vancel, Lauren K Wareham, Dolly Ann Padovani-Claudio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe family of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic chemokines including Interleukin-8 (IL-8, aka CXCL8) and its homologues (CXCL1,2,3,5,6, and 7) exhibit promiscuous binding and activation of several G-protein-coupled receptors (i.e., CXCR2, CXCR1, and the atypical chemokine receptor (ACKR1)). A high proportion of their biological activity is attributed to CXCR2 activation, thus many CXCR2 inhibitors are in clinical trials for several chronic diseases. However, CXCR2 inhibition is often only investigated acutely in these trials or in Cxcr2−/− mice grown in gnotobiotic conditions. Since humans do not live in germ-free environments, our first goal is to highlight novel retinal and systemic observations in Cxcr2−/− mice grown in non-gnotobiotic conditions that suggest potential harmful consequences of long-term CXCR2 deficiency or blockade. Beyond confirmation of circulating blood/immune cell-related phenotypes, we report novel findings in Cxcr2−/− mice including: (1) delayed dye transit to the retinal vasculature, (2) alterations in the density and distribution of retinal vessels, astrocytes and microglia, (3) decreased electroretinogram a- and b-wave amplitudes, (4) reduced visual acuity, and (5) increased polymorphonuclear cell accumulation in vascular lumina abutting venular walls in the retina and in vital non-ocular tissues (lung and liver). Furthermore, PheWAS of CXCR2 CXCR1, and ACKR1 gene variants using data from UK Biobank participants suggest clinical associations with both retinal and vascular disease phenotypes. We conclude that chronic CXCR2 deficiency in mice contributes to functional damage to the retina and that the long-term safety of CXCR1/2 inhibitors designed for chronic use in humans should be explored before clinical adoption to safeguard sight and overall vascular health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75532-9.Subject terms: Model vertebrates, Eye manifestations

Introduction

The family of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL7 and CXCL8/IL-8 exhibit promiscuous binding activity with several G-protein-coupled receptors, including the atypical chemokine receptor (ACKR1), CXCR1, and CXCR21–4. Activation of CXCR1 and CXCR2 (CXCR1/2) has been implicated in several human conditions, including cancer, arthritis, and lung disease, amongst others5–9. We and others have shown that impaired CXCR2 signaling in mice by either genetic or pharmacological manipulation affects both development and disease, decreasing pathology in several disease models affecting multiple organ systems10–16. As such, inhibitors designed to block these receptors are in clinical trials for several chronic diseases (e.g., COPD, asthma, diabetes), yet many have failed17–20.

In the eye, upregulation of CXCR1/2 ligands is found in patients with many sight-threatening conditions including diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy, diabetic macular edema, and macular degeneration10,21–24. CXCR1/2 expression has been reported in macrophages, neutrophils, brain astrocytes, microglia, and in retinal neurons and glia in several retinal layers25. Their ligands, most of which preferentially bind CXCR2, are similarly variably expressed25. Further, CXCR2 activation has been found to promote angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo and to regulate myeloid differentiation26–31; these pathogenic mechanisms are implicated in many blinding conditions. Overall, existing data suggests that drugs targeting the CXCR1/2 signaling system may prove useful to preserve vision.

Since Cacalano et al. highlighted the phenotype of mIL-8Rh knockout (Cxcr2−/−) mice32, in addition to research into disease pathophysiology, functions beyond leukocyte trafficking (including regulation of hematopoiesis and myelination and suppression of myelopoiesis), have been explored and reported in these mice14,27,33–35. Yet, eye phenotypes outside those modeling specific condition are largely unexplored. In addition, because the immune phenotype in the Cxcr2−/− mice is alleviated when raised under gnotobiotic conditions32,36, the effects of chronic CXCR2 inhibition outside of pathogen-free environments (as would be encountered by humans) are also largely unexplored. Finally, although intended for long-term use in humans, CXCR1/2 inhibitors have usually been tested acutely in disease models or in short-term human safety trials, which may fail to convey a clear picture of the effects of long-term receptor inhibition on health.

Given that the CXCL: CXCR1/2 chemokine system is so promiscuous, affects so many relevant cells, and is implicated in several sight-threatening ocular conditions, we sought to investigate the long-term effects of impaired CXCR2 signaling on retinal pathology in unperturbed Cxcr2−/− mice reared in sterile cages outside of a gnotobiotic facility. This is felt to be more representative of everyday human conditions that patients with chronic CXCR1/2 inhibition would experience.

In general, we observed a progression of the previously described immune and systemic phenotype of Cxcr2−/− mice. Furthermore, we report novel ocular pathophysiology, including delayed dye delivery to the retina, increased activation of Iba1 positive cells in the retina, impaired visual acuity, and reduced a- and b-wave amplitudes on electroretinograms as these mice age. Finally, we identified phenome wide associations between CXCL8-receptor gene variants (ACKR1, CXCR1, and CXCR2) and retinal and systemic vascular diseases including ischemic diseases in UK Biobank human participant data. Our results suggest that, although promising, the consequences and safety of long-term CXCR2 inhibition should be further explored, especially since many of the current inhibitors are in clinical trials for chronic human conditions.

Results

Delayed retinal perfusion in Cxcr2−/− mice

Since CXCR1/2 ligands are reported to affect tight junction integrity in several organ systems37, we first assessed in vivo retinal blood-barrier function in Cxcr2−/− mice and WT mice using fluorescein angiography (Fig. 1a). No retinal leakage of fluorescein was evident in retinas of Cxcr2−/− or WT mice. However, the transit time of fluorescein into the retina after systemic injection was significantly delayed in the knockouts (KO) (Cxcr2−/−: 18.83 +/- 5.42s vs. WT: 11.11 +/- 3.26s; p = 0.0041; Fig. 1b).

Increased central retinal vessel area in Cxcr2−/− mice

To explore if the delayed fluorescein detection was due to altered retinal vasculature, we used lectin stains and Rapid Editable Analysis of Vessel Elements Routine (REAVER) software to quantify superficial vascular density within matched regions in the proximal (central) and more distal (peripheral) parts of the Cxcr2−/− or WT retinas (Fig. 2a, b). We found that vascular area, represented as the fraction of pixels in each region of interest (ROI) that belongs to blood vessels, was significantly larger in the proximal region of the retinal vasculature in Cxcr2−/− mice compared to WT (Cxcr2−/−: 0.20 +/- 0.02 vs. WT: 0.15 +/- 0.02; p < 0.0001 Fig. 2c). No significant difference was observed between genotypes in the distal retinal vasculature (Fig. 2c). Retinal vessel branching was also significantly greater in the proximal regions of the Cxcr2−/− vs. WT retinas (branchpoints Cxcr2−/−: 262.3 +/- 37.67 vs. WT: 135.4 +/- 38.67; p = 0.0014; Fig. 2d), but not the distal ones. In addition, in Cxcr2−/− mice, the branchpoint density was significantly higher in proximal than in distal regions (Cxcr2−/− branchpoints proximal: 262.3 +/- 37.67 vs. distal 111.5 +/- 47.73; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2d), while no difference between them was detected in WT mice.

Cellular changes in glia, but not retinal ganglion cells, in Cxcr2−/− retina

Given the intimate connection between astrocyte density/distribution and vessel growth during retinal development38, we next estimated total retinal astrocyte coverage in the superficial retina using antibodies to glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Regions of higher GFAP density were more frequently observed in Cxcr2−/− than in WT retinal wholemounts (Fig. 3a), especially in the central retina. This was akin to the relative increase in vessel area and branching observed in the Cxcr2−/− mice (Fig. 2). Although there was a greater frequency of GFAP dense regions in the Cxcr2−/− than WT mice (Fig. 3b, right shift green bars), the overall differences in GFAP coverage between the two mice were not statistically significant (Fig. 3c).

Cxcr2−/− mice exhibit chronic inflammation and infection

Given the previously described reports of systemic immune system alterations and our novel finding of activated microglia in Cxcr2−/− mice, we sought to further characterize systemic pathophysiology our the Cxcr2−/− mice grown in non-gnotobiotic conditions. Inflammatory lesions, which were absent in WT mice, were observed in various sites in all Cxcr2−/− animals. Some lesions observed in Cxcr2−/− mice include rhinitis, pharyngitis, otitis, dermatitis with erosions and ulcerations, neutrophilic infiltration of the leptomeninges, and severe bronchiectasis and bronchiolitis that resulted in complete lung lobe effacement in one animal (Fig. 6). Most inflammatory lesions primarily consisted of polymorphonuclear cells (a mixture of mature and immature neutrophils) occasionally mixed with macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Necrosis within the inflammatory sites was observed in 5 of 6 mice, and all Cxcr2−/− animals had bacteria in at least one inflammatory site, though never in the retina (Table 1).

Hematopoietic system abnormalities in Cxcr2−/− mice

Consistent with previous reports32, the bone marrow on all necropsies of Cxcr2−/− mice showed exuberant granulopoiesis comprised mainly of bands and neutrophils (Fig. 7ai, bi). In addition, Cxcr2−/− mice displayed marked extramedullary hematopoiesis and lymphoid depletion in the spleen (Fig. 7aii, bii), resulting in gross splenomegaly. Extramedullary hematopoiesis was also observed multifocally in the liver (Fig. 7aiii, biii).

Vascular system abnormalities in Cxcr2−/− mice

A higher density of immune cells was evident in the lumen of vessels of different calibers from various organ systems in Cxcr2−/− vs. WT mice (Fig. 8). As illustrated in vessel cross-sections of liver and lung in Fig. 8a, b, intraluminal polymorphonuclear cells were abundant in Cxcr2−/− mice. Consistent with this and with the findings of others44, we observed profound neutrophilia in complete blood counts (CBC) of Cxcr2−/− mice (Cxcr2−/−: 6.16 +/- 4.98 K/uL vs. WT: 1.75 +/- 0.54 K/uL; p = 0.0007; Fig. 8ci). In addition to increased neutrophil percentage, red cell standard distribution (Cxcr2−/−: 8.31 +/- 1.08 fL vs. WT: 6.711+/- 1.18 fL; p = 0.0002; Fig. 8cii) and mean platelet volume (Cxcr2−/−: 5.16 +/- 0.25 fL vs. WT: 4.99 +/- 0.06 fL; p = 0.0093; Fig. 8ciii) were also greater in the CBCs of Cxcr2−/− mice than in those of WT mice. Furthermore, other CBC indices, such as hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, and mean corpuscular volume, were significantly lower in the Cxcr2−/− CBCs (Supplementary Fig. S1). Analysis of other white blood cell (WBC) sub-populations revealed that relative proportions of monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils remained similar between WT and Cxcr2−/− mice while the proportion of neutrophils was increased, and lymphocytes decreased in the Cxcr2−/− mice compared to WT (Fig. 8d).

Intraluminal polymorphonuclear cell accumulation

We investigated the retinal vasculature more in depth and at higher magnification (Fig. 9a), and consistently found a higher density of immune cells was evident in the lumen of veins in lectin-stained retinal flat mounts from Cxcr2-/- vs. WT mice (cells per vein Cxcr2-/-: 35.54 +/- 22.25 vs. WT: 13.29 +/- 9.081; p < 0.0001; Fig. 9b). Given this prominence of polymorphonuclear cells (PMN) in the vascular lumen of retinal veins and the delay in retinal perfusion (Fig. 1) we assessed HIF-1ɑ expression by immunofluorescence in retinal sections from WT and Cxcr2-/- mice. Not only did the retinal sections from Cxcr2-/- mice, reveal increased HIF-1ɑ expression, but they were also significantly thinner than those from WT mice (Fig. 9c).

Structural and functional deficits including reduced retinal thickness, electroretinogram amplitudes, and visual function in Cxcr2-/- mice

Taken together our findings suggest that the combined phenotype of intraluminal PMN accumulation and delayed perfusion may lead to ischemia, hypoxia, and tissue damage. We thus, further compared histological retinal sections from WT and Cxcr2-/- mice to assess the integrity, organization, and thickness of the retina (Fig. 10a). Overall, the individual retinal layers in the H&E sections retained proper organization, however, the total retinal thickness was reduced in Cxcr2-/- compared to WT mice (Cxcr2-/-: 0.85 +/- 0.15 vs. WT: 0.98 +/- 0.18; p = 0.0177; Fig. 10b). Additionally, the inner nuclear layer (INL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and OPL appeared to be significantly thinner in the retinas from Cxcr2-/- compared to WT mice (INL: Cxcr2-/-: 0.85 +/- 0.18 vs. WT: 0.98 +/- 0.16, p = 0.0458; OPL: Cxcr2-/-: 0.83 +/- 0.16 vs. WT: 0.98 +/- 0.17, p = 0.0473; ONL: Cxcr2-/-: 0.85 +/- 0.09 vs. WT: 0.99 +/- 0.07; p = 0.0049; Fig. 10b).

Given the observed phenotype in Cxcr2−/− mice (delayed perfusion, altered retinal vessels density, altered number and distribution of immune and glial cells, thinner retinas) we assessed visual function in these mice. We measured the optokinetic reflex response. Mean spatial frequency was significantly reduced in Cxcr2−/− mice compared to WT animals (Cxcr2−/−: 0.15 +/- 0.07 cd/m2 vs. WT: 0.27 +/-0.06 cd/m2; p = 0.0123; Fig. 11a). Importantly, none of these animals exhibited cataracts, corneal, or media opacities to explain the altered visual responses.

Cxcr2−/− mice are known to have defective myelination and reduced visual evoked potential conduction velocities14 which could conceivably alter vision; however, intrinsic changes in retinal function and changes in visual function had not been previously explored. Thus, we compared electroretinogram (ERG) responses including implicit times and amplitudes of the a- and b-waves in dark-adapted mice. While there was no significant difference in the implicit times between genotypes (Fig. 11b), we observed a significant reduction in the amplitudes of both the a-wave (WT: 209.80 +/- 89.62 µV vs. Cxcr2−/−: 99.32 +/- 73.02 µV; p = 0.0042) and b-wave (WT: 68.13 +/- 25.77 µV vs. Cxcr2−/−: 72.56 +/- 17.24 µV; p = 0.0196) in Cxcr2−/− mice (Fig. 11c, d). Our findings suggest an intrinsic retinal effect independent of the myelin defects reported in the CNS of Cxcr2−/− mice34.

Phenotypic associations of genetic variants in CXCR1, CXCR2, and ACKR1 with retinal and vascular disorders

Because CXCR2 expression is not only altered in the retina, and necropsies suggested similar accumulation of immune cells in the vascular lumina of other organs, we suspected that alterations in the function of CXCR2 and other IL-8 receptors may be associated with both retinal and systemic vascular disorders. To asses this possibility in an unbiased fashion, we used the GeneATLAS47 to search for phenome-wide associations between variants in genes encoding CXCR2 (rs4674258, rs2230054, rs1126579), CXCR1 (rs1008563), and ACKR1 (rs3027012) (Table 2a) and disorders of the retina (Table 2b) and vasculature (Table 2c) using UK Biobank clinical phenotype data. The data suggests associations with disorders of the retina/choroid and with systemic vascular phenotypes, including ischemic heart disease. In addition, it highlights prominent variations in allelic frequencies amongst individuals of different ancestries, particularly in CXCR2 variants where allele frequencies are much higher in patients of African ancestry (Table 2a).

Discussion

Our own work and that of others suggests that CXCR2 inhibition has the potential to decrease retinopathy10,15,16,35,49. CXCR2-specific inhibitors have been and are being investigated in clinical trials for a variety of chronic clinical conditions, including COPD [NCT01233232], asthma [NCT01704495], and head and neck cancer [NCT02499328]. However, the duration of these trials has been limited and the efficacy and safety of interventions has not been well-established. For instance, a phase 2a clinical trial examining the efficacy of Danarixin, a slowly reversible CXCR2 antagonist, in treating acute influenza found that twice-daily doses for five days were well-tolerated but had no therapeutic effect19. Another phase 2b clinical trial using Danarixin twice daily for 24 weeks to treat patients with COPD found that there was no observed improvement compared to placebo, and experimental groups were found to have increased cases of pneumonia and COPD exacerbations17. In addition, although it is recommended that Cxcr2-/- mice, use to model human disease, be raised under gnotobiotic conditions to allow them to thrive, previous research has shown that, under these germ-free conditions, they do not exhibit outward phenotypic changes compared to WT and do not develop the profound neutrophilia that has otherwise been widely reported in Cxcr2-/- mice32,44. This could perhaps provide unrealistic reassurance about the safety of CXCR2 inhibition in humans.

Given that so many CXCR2 inhibitors are in trials for human conditions, and because humans do not live in germ-free environments, our goal was to highlight novel observations of Cxcr2-deficient mice grown in non-gnotobiotic conditions that suggest potential harmful consequences of long-term CXCR2 deficiency or blockade. We observed: (1) delayed fluorescein uptake (representing blood flow) to the retina, (2) alterations in the density and distribution of vessels, astrocytes and microglia in the retina, (3) foci of inflammation in all — and often evidence of bacterial infection in organ systems in nearly all — Cxcr2−/− mouse necropsies, (4) exuberant granulopoiesis in hematopoietic tissues, (5) extramedullary hematopoiesis, (6) neutrophilia in CBCs, blood smears and necropsies, (7) CBC differences indicative of iron-deficiency anemia secondary to chronic inflammation, (8) increased polymorphonuclear cells in vascular lumina abutting the endothelial walls (especially in veins), (9) reduced retinal electrical function evident in blunted electroretinogram a- and b-wave amplitudes, and (10) vision loss in Cxcr2−/− mice. In addition, our research suggests potential associations of genetic variants mapping to CXCR1, CXCR2 and ACKR1 receptor genes with retinal and vascular disorders. Given that changes in vascular tone and distribution can be driven by hypoxia, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction50–52, we speculate that the enhanced intraluminal accumulation of immune cells observed in retinal veins may be interfering with tissue perfusion leading to ischemia and hypoxia and contributing to vascular remodeling.

Overall, the retinas of Cxcr2−/− mice were thinner than those from WT mice, particularly in the INL, ONL, OPL, and photoreceptor layers. Cells of the INL are the major contributors to the scotopic ERG b-wave53, and photoreceptors, which span the photoreceptor layer, ONL, and OPL, are the major contributors to the ERG a-wave, both of which were found to be diminished in our Cxcr2−/− mice. Our novel finding of altered electro-retinal responses with reduced amplitudes of both a- and b-waves suggests that the vision deficits in Cxcr2−/− mice result from improper signaling that affects electrical retinal output. This compromise in intrinsic retinal signaling may additionally be compounded by known delayed axonal conduction velocity from defective myelination14,34.

Although activity of third-order neurons, including RGCs, is reported to contribute to the scotopic ERG b-wave54 and RGCs express CXCR2 with both toxic and protective effects on RGC health41,55, we found no difference between RGC numbers or density in wholemounts of Cxcr2−/− and WT mice. Because RGCs act as a bottleneck of the transmission of visual information through the optic nerve and to the brain, however, we cannot rule out RGC dysfunction rather than RGC loss could be contributing to loss of visual acuity in Cxcr2−/− mice. Finally, retinal astrocytes, whose activation is known to be modulated by CXCR2 signaling14, contact both vascular and neuronal elements, and seemingly small redistributions in astrocyte density have been shown to have impactful effects on blood flow, resource distribution, and neuronal support56. Accordingly, although Cxcr2−/− and WT retinas had similar overall GFAP coverage, specific regions of increased GFAP density were more commonly observed in Cxcr2−/− mice, especially centrally where the vessel density was also higher.

CXCR2 activation can influence microvascular endothelial cell migration, angiogenesis, glial physiology, and neutrophil activities such as chemotaxis, maturation, and phagocytosis, amongst other biological functions26,57. On the other hand, CXCR2 deficiency can impair the downregulation of inflammatory cytokines that could bind and activate alternative receptors such as CXCR1 or ACKR1 with potentially untoward effects44,58. ACKR1 is thought to act as a CXCL-chemokine sink and to enhance CXCL chemotactic gradients to facilitate and regulate neutrophil chemotaxis through the typical receptors CXCR2 and CXCR159,60. While pharmacologic inhibition or genetic modifications in CXCR2 may appear attractive as therapeutic strategies in animal models of diseases including retinopathy10,35, CXCR2 deficiency has also been associated with a variety of unfavorable effects such as neutrophilia, splenomegaly, and delayed wound healing32,61,62. We believe that the shortcomings of existing clinical trials may be partially explained by the pleiotropic effects of CXCR2 activation and by binding of its ligands to alternate receptors such as ACKR1 in vivo.

We suspect that the chemotactic response and regulatory feedback that is normally driven by CXCR2 ligands is impaired in these Cxcr2−/− mice, leading to ligand accumulation and signaling dysregulation32,63. In fact, it has previously been found that CXCR2-deficient mice exhibit drastically increased circulating levels of the high-affinity CXCR2 ligands CXCL1 and CXCL2 in the blood, with a 17-fold increase of CXCL1 and a greater than 100-fold increase of CXCL2, compared to wild type64. Another group has shown significant upregulation of CXCL1 mRNA and gene expression in the microglial cells of mice, as well as of CXCL1 protein in the cortex of male mouse pups, in response to inflammation-sensitized hypoxic-ischemic brain injury65.

In mice, CXCL1 acts as a functional homologue of human IL-8 and preferentially binds CXCR2 with high affinity, and can induce leukocyte recruitment and neutrophil trans-endothelial migration through high-affinity binding to CXCR266–68. At high concentrations, CXCL1 has also been shown to bind and activate CXCR1 and ACKR158,64,69. Although ACKR1 regulates chemokine gradients without direct G-protein activation, it can mediate luminal and sub-endothelial cell crawling of neutrophils when bound to CXCL170. It is thus conceivable that unhampered levels of CXCR2 ligands driven by CXCR2-defective signaling may bind ACKR1 (which is preferentially expressed in veins) leading to CXCR2-independent immune cell recruitment without tissue infiltration, and thereby result in PMN and microglia accumulation near veins, microglial activation, and the delayed perfusion we observed in Cxcr2−/− mice. This is consistent with a recent finding of macular perfusion deficits mainly in the perivenular capillaries in humans with diabetes71, a disease in which CXCR2 and ACKR1 ligands such as IL-8 are highly elevated and correlate with retinopathy development and progression72. In the eye, these ligands correlate with the development and progression of ocular diseases, including retinopathies24,73.

ACKR1 receptors, which are expressed in neutrophils, venous endothelial cells, microglia, and erythrocytes, are often promiscuous and considered atypical because they are unable to activate classic GPCR signaling74,75. In erythrocytes, ACKR1 acts as a receptor for malaria’s Duffy-binding proteins (hence its other name, Duffy Antigen Receptor for Chemokines, DARC) with evolutionary selective pressure for some variants in specific human populations exposed to malaria. Many patients with ACKR1 variants exhibit what is called benign neutropenia76. Similarly, patients with a homozygous frameshift mutation in the Cxcr2 gene exhibit a syndrome called WHIM syndrome 2 (WHIMS2) characterized by recurrent infections, myelokathexis, and neutropenia77,78.

The CBCs of Cxcr2−/− mice had bone marrow effacement by neutrophils, but instead showed neutrophilia, potentially due to ligand binding to an alternate receptor. Cxcr2−/− mice also had increased mean platelet volume (MPV) and a larger variation in red blood cell width (RDW) compared to WT. In humans, elevated MPV and RDW have been associated with increased risk of and worse outcomes of ischemic events79–84. Furthermore, total white blood cell and neutrophil counts have been linked to vessel occlusion in stroke patients80–82,84, which supports our interpretation that the marked neutrophilia in the Cxcr2−/− mice may be contributing to the delayed blood flow to the retinal microvasculature observed in our studies. If true, chronic CXCR2 inhibition could potentially lead to ischemia and long-term tissue damage in humans, thus threatening organ function. Our exploration of PheWAS results suggests potential associations between gene variants in these chemokine receptors and both retinal and systemic vascular and ischemic conditions which should be further investigated.

Natural selection from malaria resistance has resulted in a high prevalence of ACKR1 variants in many patients of African descent who are already disproportionately burdened by stroke, cardiovascular disease, and vaso-occlusive disease, and retinopathies85–87. Given the loss of chemokine scavenging and altered chemokine profiles associated with impaired ACKR1 and CXCR2 signaling and evolutionary pressures favoring certain ACKR1 variants (reflected in marked differences in allelic frequencies by ancestry in Table 2a), we fear that chronic CXCR2 inhibition could affect patients differently depending on their ancestry, which could worsen health disparities.

In humans, spontaneous CXCR2 mutations as observed in WHIM syndrome 2 are rare, and we are thus largely limited to studying CXCR2 manipulation and mutation in animal models. Although this translational approach allows for flexible and in-depth exploration of physiologic alterations in KO mice, these models may not fully replicate the pathophysiology of CXCR2 mutation or inhibition in humans. Therefore, additional investigation of CXCR2 therapies as they relate to humans is recommended. Since small-molecule CXCR2 inhibitors have been administered to human participants in short-term clinical trials, it is important to consider whether long-term pharmacological inhibition of CXCR2 would result in the adverse phenotypes we presented in our current study of genetic-based CXCR2 deficiency. Furthermore, examining conditional CXCR2 knockout in different cell types could help shed light on upstream causes of the pathology we observed in our CXCR2-deficient mice. These suggestions promise to guide more-focused investigation of CXCR2 therapies in both animals and humans.

Conclusion

Findings from many clinical and preclinical studies suggest that acute inhibition of CXCR2 signaling confers protective effects in many organ systems, prompting the development of several pharmacologic CXCR2 inhibitors. Germline-deficient Cxcr2−/− mice grown in non-gnotobiotic environments exhibit not only extramedullary hematopoiesis, a propensity for inflammatory and infected lesions, and hematologic abnormalities including neutrophilia, but also altered distribution and activation state of retinal glia, enhanced concentrations of intraluminal polymorphonuclear cells in retinal veins, delayed vascular flow to the retina, and deficits in electrical retinal responses and visual acuity. The accumulation of intraluminal polymorphonuclear cells in the vasculature of other tissues, including the liver and lungs, raised concerns for potential additional damage to other organ systems (heart, kidneys, brain).

Unlike mice, which can be housed in gnotobiotic conditions, humans do not live in a bubble, and our environment is ridden with microorganisms. We believe that there is a risk that long-term CXCR2 blockade may lead to compromised perfusion and functional damage in the retina and other organs and tissues threatening vision and overall health. As such, we feel a responsibility to raise awareness of the novel phenotype observed in Cxcr2−/− mice and to encourage further exploration of the consequences of chronic CXCR2 inhibition in diverse patient populations before these inhibitors become widely used to manage chronic health conditions.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, approved by the IACUC at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC), and reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Cxcr2 knockout and wild-type littermate mice were obtained by breeding heterozygous B6, 129 S(C)-Cxcr2tm1Mwm/J from Jax (006848) mice32. Ccr2RFPCx3cr1GFP dual-reporter mice, (B6.129(Cg)-Cx3cr1tm1LittCcr2tm2.1lfc/JernJ from Jax (032127)88, were obtained as a gift from the Uddin Laboratory for Ocular Imaging at VUMC. Homozygous Cx3cr1GFP mice were crossed with Cxcr2+/- mice to generate Cxcr2-/- and WT littermate Cx3cr1-GFP reporter mice.

Mice were housed in autoclaved cages containing paper bedding and cardboard huts in level 5 A housing conditions at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. Mice were provided water and fed ad-lib with autoclaved pellets (5L0D, PicoLab® Laboratory Rodent Diet) and were supplemented with 2 oz of 76 A DietGel (Clear H20, catalog #72-07-5022, ME, USA) twice per week. Observers masked to animal genotype, experimental group, and diet performed all data acquisition and analyses described.

Anesthesia & euthanasia

For short sessions of fundus live imaging anesthesia was achieved by isoflurane delivered through a small low profile anesthesia mask (Item #SOMNO-0801, Kent Scientific Co., Torrington, CT, USA). For endpoints that required extended sedation, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a solution containing ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg). All mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation while under isoflurane anesthesia.

Genotyping

Tail tissue snips from littermate pups were collected and each placed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube for DNA extraction. Samples not immediately processed were stored at -20°C. Sigma Red extract-N-Amp Tissue PCR kit (Cat #XNAT2) was used to extract and amplify genomic DNA for genotyping. Samples were incubated with DNA isolation buffer for 10 minutes at room temperature and then for 3 minutes at 95°C on a heat block. PCR amplification was done in PCR tubes using WT forward 16927 (5’-AGA ATT GGG GTC AGG GTA ’A-3’), WT reverse 16,928 (5’-CCC TTA GGC TGC AAA TGA ’C-3’), mutant forward oIMR6916 (5’-CTT GGG TGG AGA GGC TAT ’C-3’), and mutant reverse oIMR6917 (5’-AGG TGA GAT GAC AGG AGA ’C-3’) primers. Samples were programmed in the thermocycler as follows: 94 °C for 3 min, 30 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 62 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 10 min, and then held at 4 °C until gel electrophoresis. Electrophoresis was performed with parameters of 100 V, 0.08 A, and 8 W for 1:15 h. Gel was prepared adding 1.5 g of agarose to 100 mL IX TAE buffer, heating the mixture for 1 min in the microwave to fully dissolve the agarose. SYBR safe (Invitrogen, Cat# s33102) was added for staining and the solution allowed to polymerize at room temperature for ~ 20 min. DNA fragment size was found at 214 bp for WT and at 280 bp for Cxcr2 mutant.

Fundus imaging and fluorescein angiography

Mice were anesthetized as described above and pupils were dilated with 1% tropicamide (Bausch + Lomb, Inc., NDC 24208-585-64). Lubricating ophthalmic ointment (GenTeal, 4R75) was administered for proper focusing and lubrication. Using a commercially available Micron IV retinal imaging system (Phoenix Research Laboratories, Pleasanton, CA), retinal fundus images were obtained in one eye of each mouse.

In examination of Cx3cr1 + cell distribution within the fundus, camera settings were set to green fluorescence with maximum brightness and minimum frame rate, and both still images and videos were captured.

For fluorescein angiography, mice were given an IP injection of sodium fluorescein stock solution (100 mg/ml; 200 uL, Millipore Sigma). The camera settings were set to green fluorescence imaging and both still images and videos were used to record fluorescein flow. Entry time was defined as time of injection to time of fluorescence appearance in retinal arteries (arterial phase start).

Retinal wholemount fresh dissection and immunohistochemistry

Mice were perfused with PBS prior to enucleation for fresh dissection. Retinas were freshly dissected and treated with a solution of collagenase (Worthington Biochemical, LS005273) and hyaluronidase (Worthington Biochemical, LS002592) in Ames’ media (US Biologic, A1372-25) to aid in vitreous removal as previously described56. The tissue was transferred to a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h and then stored in PBS at 4 °C.

Tissue was blocked in a solution of 5% normal donkey serum in 0.1% Triton-X for 3 h, while shaking at room temperature. The tissue was then incubated in a solution of primary antibody in 0.1% Triton-X for 3 days, while shaking at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used include rabbit anti-RBPMS (GeneTex, GTX118619; 1:500), goat anti-GFAP (Abcam, ab53554; 1:500), rabbit anti-Iba1 (Wako, 019-19741; 1:500), and isolectin GS-IB4 (IB4) conjugate (Invitrogen, I21414; 1:200). Following three five-minute washes of the retinas in PBS, tissue was incubated in a solution of secondary antibody for one hour. Secondary antibodies used include donkey anti-rabbit conjugated to Alexa 555 (Invitrogen, A-31572; 1:200), donkey anti-goat conjugated to Alexa 488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 705-545-003; 1:200), donkey anti-rabbit conjugated to Alexa 488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-545-152; 1:200), and streptavidin conjugated to Alexa 647 (Invitrogen, S32357; 1:200). Retinas were again washed three times for five minutes with PBS and then mounted on slides for imaging.

Retinal paraffin section IHC

After euthanasia, eyes were immediately enucleated. Eyes destined for paraffin embedding were fixed for 24 h in paraformaldehyde (16% PFA, Electron Microscopy Science; 15710) diluted to 4% with 1X PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (Sigma; P7059). Whole eyes were embedded in paraffin and sectioned by the Vanderbilt Vision Research Center histology core. Paraffin sections on slides were heated on a slide warmer to 60 °C for 20 min. Slides then underwent deparaffinization and rehydration in a series of solutions, including two rounds of xylene (5 min each), two rounds of 100% ethanol (5 min each), 95% ethanol (5 min), 70% ethanol (5 min), 40% ethanol (5 min), 20% ethanol (5 min), and ddH2O (5 min). For antigen retrieval, slides were then placed in a Coplin jar, submerged in boiling sodium citrate solution (10 mM, pH 6.0), and remained in this solution until cooled down to room temperature. Slides were washed in 1X PBS and incubated in a sodium borohydride solution (0.1% sodium borohydride in 1X PBS) for 30 min at room temperature and then washed 3 times in 1X PBS. Slides were then blocked in a solution containing 5% normal donkey serum in 0.1% Triton X-100/1X PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Slides were covered with primary antibody solution containing antibodies to Iba1 (goat 1:200; Abcam; ab5076) and HIF-1ɑ (mouse 1:35; Novus Biologicals; NB100-105) diluted in 3% normal donkey serum in 0.1% Triton X-100/1X PBS. Slides remained in the primary antibody solution overnight in a staining chamber covered by parafilm at 4 °C. The following day, slides were washed 3 times in 1X PBS and secondary antibody (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-545-152) diluted in 1% normal donkey serum in 0.1% Triton X-100 1X PBS was added to the slides for two hours. After 2 h, slides were washed in 1X PBS 3 times followed by ddH2O. Slides were cover-slipped, mounted with DAPI fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech; 0100 − 20), and sealed with nail polish.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining and thickness quantification in retinal sections

Paraffin section slides were heated on a slide-warmer at 60 °C for 20 min. Slides underwent deparaffinization and rehydration as described above. Slides were placed in Harris-Hematoxylin stain (Sigma, HHS16) for 15 min and washed gently with ddH2O. Slides were then placed for 30 s in 0.1% NaHCO3.ddH2O and washed gently with ddH2O. Slides were then placed in eosin stain (Sigma; HT110116) for 1 min, before washing gently with ddH2O. Slides were then dehydrated in a series of ethanol steps: 70% ethanol (3–10 s), 95% ethanol (3–10 s), 2 rounds of 100% ethanol (3–10 s), and Xylene (1 min). Dehydrated sections were cover-slipped using a mounting solution (Sigma; M1289).

Slides were imaged using a brightfield 20X objective on a Nikon Ni Eclipse microscope. For each retina, 2–3 images were taken across the retina. Measurements were obtained from four locations inside the same sagittal segment of the retinal layers, using independent line intersections drawn in ImageJ to assess the thickness of various layers of the retina including the Ganglion Cell Layer (GCL), Inner Plexiform Layer (IPL), Inner Nuclear Layer (INL), Outer Plexiform Layer (OPL), and Outer Nuclear Layer (ONL), using the measurement tool (ImageJ). These values were combined to generate a total retinal thickness measurement. In order to control for the effects of mouse age and the region of interest quantified (i.e., distance of the measurement from the optic nerve), measurements were made from age-matched adult mice and a paired t-test was conducted on groups of mice such that measurements from the middle and peripheral regions of the retina were compared to similarly-located measurements in similarly-aged mice.

Retinal wholemount and section imaging and microglial analyses

Retinas labeled for IB4 and Iba1 were montage imaged with a 20X objective using a Nikon Ni Eclipse fluorescence microscope to obtain whole retina images. Images of the deep retinal layer were obtained with a 20X objective using a Nikon Spinning Disc confocal microscope. For retinal sections, slides were imaged on the Olympus FV 1000 confocal microscope at 40X magnification with a Z resolution of 1.6103 pixels per micron. All images were collected with the same exposure settings. Relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) excluding the outer segment was measured for Iba1 by creating maximum intensity projections in ImageJ, outlining the region of interest, and measuring integrated density.

Vessel area and branchpoint quantification and immune cell counting

IB4-labled whole-mounted retinas from non-perfused, enucleated, and fixed mouse eyes were montage imaged with a 20X objective on a Nikon Ni Eclipse fluorescence microscope, saved as TIF files and quantified using FIJI-ImageJ (Version 1.54f). To quantify vascular area and branching, rectangular regions of interest of 1600 × 3900 pixels were overlayed on the distal portions of each retinal petal, as well as equally sized proximal regions of interest just inward of the distal sections. The University of Virginia’s REAVER software was used to quantify the vessel area and number of branchpoints of each region as previously described89. For immune cell counting, rectangular regions of interest of 580 × 2200 pixels were placed in line with each radial vein, 415 pixels from the optic nerve head. The selections were opened on an internal clipboard and a manual count of the number of nucleated cells was conducted. Veins were excluded if they tapered off within the bounds of the region of interest or if they had a prominent curved shape.

Retinal ganglion cell and astrocyte density analyses

Retinas labeled for RBPMS and GFAP were montage imaged with a 20X objective on a Nikon Ni Eclipse fluorescence microscope. Local contrast of the resulting images was enhanced using the CLAHE function embedded in Fiji-ImageJ. These enhanced images were passed into the opensource software RGCode for automated RGC counting and isodensity map creation90. GFAP label density was determined using a custom Python script. The threshold for image binarization was set manually. Additionally, a mask was created that removed any damaged regions of the retina from analysis. Binary images were processed by a script that calculated the density of GFAP-positive pixels in an 85 × 85 µm2 region surrounding each pixel in the image. These density values were plotted as a histogram using Matplotlib and mapped to a color in the viridis colormap, which became the pixel value for a resulting heatmap image. In Fig. 3c, Fishers Least Significant Difference (LSD) Test was applied for the GFAP coverage analysis because, although plotted in a single graph, each comparison represents an unpaired t-test (i.e., the overall, proximal, and distal GFAP (%) comparison between WT and Cxcr2−/− retinas were performed individually).

Optokinetic response (OKR)

Individual mice were placed, unrestrained, on an elevated platform inside the OptoMotry equipment (Cerebral Mechanics Inc, Lethbridge, Canada). Spatial acuity was assessed using the optokinetic head-tracking reflex while mice were presented with a sinusoidal-wave gradient in a 3D coordinate space that rotated around the animal. Stimuli with random spatial frequencies ranging between 0.042 and 0.492 cd/m2 were projected by four interfacing Dell LCD monitors around the platform. Speed (12 d/s) and contrast (100%) were held constant. Positive visual tracking, defined as smooth head movements tracked in the same direction and speed as the rotation of the sinusoidal-wave gradient, were graded by two masked observers and averaged. Spatial frequency thresholding was done using a tandem method of low and high spatial frequencies to narrow down to a threshold visual function. Mice were tested twice a week for two weeks (4 measurements per eye per mouse) and responses were averaged to determine spatial acuity.

Retinal function with scotopic full-field electroretinogram (ERG)

Mice were dark-adapted overnight in a lightproof and ventilated cabinet. After dark adaptation, mice were anesthetized as described above. Anesthetic (0.5% proparacaine HCL, Akron Inc, Cat #NDC 17478-263-12) and dilating (tropicamide 1.0%, Akron Inc, Cat #NDC 17478-102-12) eye drops were applied to each eye. Using the heated ColorDome LabCradle (Diagnosys LLC D315, MA, USA) procedure stage, needle electrodes (12 mm, 29 gauge) were placed subcutaneously behind the neck of the mice between their ears and near the base of their tail. Lubricating ophthalmic ointment (Genteal, 4R75) was administered to each eye and a 3 mm gold foil loop electrode (Diagnosys LLC, Cat #D200) was placed on each eye to record electrical impulses from the retina. Bright flashes of 100 cd*s/m2 intensity were used to stimulate the retina. a- and b-wave implicit times (ms) and amplitudes (µV) were documented for each individual eye per animal.

Necropsy

Standard necropsy was performed, and tissues were collected into 10% neutral-buffered formalin (NBF). After fixation, collected tissues were processed routinely and embedded in paraffin by Vanderbilt University Medical Center’s Translational Pathology Shared Resource (TPSR), and 4–5 μm sections were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathology was then evaluated by a board-certified veterinary pathologist (TSY). Selected slides with lesions were subjected to gram stain (Agilent DAKO, Santa Clara, CA) in TPSR according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

CBC counts

Blood samples were collected from the submandibular vein of mice using a 5 mm lancet (Goldenrod, SKU: GR 5MM) to puncture the tissue approximately halfway between the ear and the mandible. A few drops of blood were collected into EDTA Mini collection tubes (Greiner, Cat #K3E K3EDTA). Samples were submitted for same-day CBCs using a Forcyte (Oxford Science) hematology analyzer at VUMC’s TPSR core. Alternatively, blood samples were smeared, dried, stained with H&E, and then examined at 20X magnification capturing 10 random fields per smear for neutrophil quantification.

PheWAS analyses

Allelic frequencies were obtained from Ensemble91. UK Biobank phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) data was obtained through GeneATLAS47. Associations in GeneATLAS are computed using Mixed Linear Models in a large supercomputer that uses DISSECT to avoid having to remove data from large amounts of related individuals in the 452,264 UK Biobank of White British participants. Polygenic effects are corrected using a Leave-One-Chromosome-Out (LOCO) approach for the Linear Mixed Model. Retinal traits with p < 0.05 included Disorders of choroid and retina [8,074 cases with ICD: H300,1,2,8,9; H310,1,2,3,4,8,9; H320,8, H330,1,2,3,4,5; H340,1,2,8,9; H350,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9; H360,8], Other retinal disorders [3,774 cases with ICD: H350,1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9], and Retinal disorders in diseases classified elsewhere [3,774 cases with ICD: H360, 8]. Search terms for retinal and non-retinal vascular phenotypes were: “vasc”, “venou”, “vein”, “art”, “occ”, “eye”, “retin”, “constr”, “dilat”, “isch”, “infar”, “stroke”.

Statistics

Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). We first determined whether datasets formed a normal distribution using Shapiro–Wilk tests, a test of normality appropriate for small datasets. In cases where datasets were normally distributed, we performed parametric statistics (t-tests or ordinary one-way ANOVA). Where data was not normally distributed, we carried out the non-parametric statistics (Mann Whitney test or ANOVA on ranks). Welch’s t-tests were performed since many sample sizes were uneven and the standard deviations between groups were not assumed to be equivalent. Multiple comparisons were accounted for by applying the Bonferroni correction for normally distributed data and the Dunn’s correction for non-normally distributed data. Data are presented using violin plots, which display the data median as well as the first and third quartiles of the data distribution. We defined statistical significance as a p-value of 0.05 or less. The number of measurements and specific p-values are indicated in each figure legend.
