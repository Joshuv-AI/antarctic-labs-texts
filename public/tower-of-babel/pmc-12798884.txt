Agreement Between a Questionnaire-Based Tool and Experienced Pediatric Neurologists in Classifying Seizure Types in Children with Epilepsy
Authors: Puneet Kumar Choudhary, Richa Tiwari, Prabhjot Kaur, Shivam Bansal, Gopal Puri, P Srividya, Satwik Pasani, Gautam Kamila, Prashant Jauhari, Biswaroop Chakrabarty, Rakesh Lodha, Ravindra Mohan Pandey, Vinod Kumar Paul, Sheffali Gulati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Accurate identification of the seizure type and prompt initiation of appropriate treatment are crucial in reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with epilepsy. In this observational study, our objective was to develop a questionnaire-based tool to assist in recognizing seizure types based on the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2017 classification system. Additionally, the study assessed the level of agreement between pediatric neurologists (PNs) and this tool.Methods:Six additional questions were added to the existing, validated AIIMS modified International Clinical Epidemiology Network diagnostic instrument for epilepsy. These questions were designed to encompass all seizure types outlined in the ILAE 2017 classification scheme. Two pediatric postgraduate residents (PGs) and four undergraduate interns were trained to classify seizures using the tool. The classification of seizure semiology by PGs and undergraduate interns was performed verbatim using the tool. PNs used their clinical experience for seizure classification and served as the gold standard for comparison. The seizure classification by the tool was compared with the classification of three pediatric PNs using kappa agreement.Results:A total of 144 children (97 males) with confirmed epilepsy were enrolled. The kappa agreement between PGs and PNs was 0.52 at the first level (broad categorization of seizure type as per the ILAE classification) and 0.28 at the second level (detailed categorization of seizure type as per the ILAE classification). Between interns and PNs, the kappa agreement was 0.45 at the first level and 0.30 at the second level.Conclusions:The tool is appropriate for the broad classification of seizures into generalized, focal, multiple, and unclassified seizure types. However, for detailed seizure classification as per the ILAE 2017 classification, more extensive training may be required.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common health condition among children, and it is estimated that approximately 5.5 million individuals in India are affected by epilepsy, with around 4.1 million residing in rural regions, accounting for approximately 74% of the total cases. About three-quarters of these individuals do not receive appropriate treatment according to existing standards. The burden of epilepsy in the 2–9 years age group in India is 3.5 million.[1]

Correct classification of seizure type is imperative to achieve an appropriate epilepsy diagnosis. Appropriate antiseizure medication cannot be initiated without correct identification of seizure semiology.[2] The operational classification of seizure types by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) is the most widely used scheme for seizure classification, and the latest ILAE classification scheme was published in 2017.[3] Misdiagnosis is not uncommon in pediatric epilepsy, and up to 30% of children referred with an epilepsy diagnosis do not have seizures at all.[4] Hence, there is a need to develop a diagnostic instrument that can be used by primary care physicians in community settings to accurately identify childhood seizures.

The International Clinical Epidemiology Network (INCLEN) developed a tool for diagnosing epilepsy among children aged 2 to 9 years, which has a sensitivity and specificity of 85.8% (95% Confidence interval [CI]: 80.8–90.0) and 95.3% (95% CI: 92.0–97.4), respectively.[5] A modification of this tool, known as the AIIMS modified INCLEN diagnostic instrument for epilepsy, has been validated for children from 1 month to 18 years. It has a sensitivity of 91.5% (95% CI: 84.5–96.1) and specificity of 88.6% (95% CI: 80.0–93.5).[6] However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no tool that has been developed to identify seizure types in children. Therefore, we designed this study to create a tool and assess the agreement between the tool (administered verbatim by postgraduate pediatric residents and undergraduate interns) and pediatric neurologists (PNs), who would base their classification of seizure types on their clinical expertise.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational study was conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India over a period of 2 years. The aim of the study was to develop a questionnaire-based tool as an addendum to the existing AIIMS modified INCLEN diagnostic instrument for epilepsy and to classify seizures based on the ILAE 2017 classification using this tool. Subsequently, the study aimed to examine the agreement between the tool and experienced PNs in classifying seizures. Children with confirmed epilepsy, aged 1 month to 18 years, and accompanied by their primary caregivers were enrolled. Ethical approval was obtained from the institute ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the enrolled subjects.

Procedures/data collection

In the AIIMS modified INCLEN diagnostic instrument for epilepsy, questions 1 and 2 pertain to seizure semiology. An expert group of three PNs added six new questions [Supplementary Table 1] to this tool, encompassing all semiology as per the ILAE 2017 classification. The feasibility of the modified tool was tested in five children to ensure that no semiology was missed and that the questions were unambiguous. Two pediatric postgraduate residents (PGs) and four interns were trained in the use and interpretation of the tool. The construct validity of the modified tool was tested in a pilot study with ten supervised and ten unsupervised cases. This tool was named the AIIMS modified INCLEN diagnostic instrument for epilepsy (Addendum 2017). We utilized two groups of evaluators to administer the tool, namely pediatric PGs and interns, to observe any potential differences attributable to the administrator’s level of clinical experience.

In our study, the gold standard for seizure semiology classification was established through a consensus reached by three experienced PNs based on their clinical judgment. Only those cases in which all three neurologists independently concurred on the seizure semiology were included in the study population. The study was conducted in two parts. In the first phase, each enrolled child was evaluated by one of two PGs, in addition to being assessed by all three PNs. The PGs used the tool verbatim for the categorization of seizure types. In the second phase, assessments were conducted by one of four interns, alongside evaluations by the same three PNs. As in the first phase, interns utilized the tool verbatim to identify seizure types. PGs and interns did not use any home video evidence during the application of the tool. The sequence of evaluation of enrolled children by the two groups of evaluators was randomized, and the outcomes of both evaluations were kept confidential from one another. Statistical analysis

Considering a κ-agreement of 0.8 between the tool and PNs, a precision of 10%, and a confidence level of 95%, the sample size was calculated to be 139. The inter-observer agreement between the two sets of observers, namely between PNs and the tool administered by the PGs and interns, was measured using kappa agreement. Kappa values were categorized as follows: <0 for poor agreement, 0.0–0.2 for slight agreement, 0.21–0.4 for fair agreement, 0.41–0.6 for moderate agreement, 0.61–0.8 for substantial agreement, and 0.81–1.0 for almost perfect agreement.[7] Agreement was compared between the two sets of evaluators at two levels. At the first level of classification, the groups consisted of focal, generalized, unknown onset/unclassified, and multiple semiology. To facilitate comparison, a 4 × 4 table was created. At the second level, the groups consisted of focal aware motor, focal aware nonmotor, focal impaired aware motor, focal impaired aware nonmotor, focal to bilateral tonic-clonic, generalized motor, generalized nonmotor, unknown motor, unknown nonmotor, unclassified, and multiple semiology. Each semiology was assigned a numerical code from 1 to 12, and a 12 × 12 table was prepared for comparison. Agreement, expected agreement, and kappa values were calculated using Strata software, version 14.2 (Stata Corp).

Discussion

Primary care physicians play a crucial role in the identification, treatment, and prompt referral of childhood epilepsy cases. Compared to adults and older children, younger children exhibit fewer clinical trial manifestations, making the identification of semiology challenging.[8] In a study by Hutchison et al., the perspectives of parents and caregivers of children with epilepsy and intellectual disability were assessed. The majority of participants expressed the view that healthcare professionals had difficulty accurately recognizing seizures.[9] Therefore, it is vital to empower primary care physicians by developing a tool that can help in identifying different seizure types in children.

The classification of seizures according to the ILAE 2017 seizure classification scheme is clinical. It is essential not only for selecting appropriate therapy but also for correct epilepsy diagnosis and prognosis. In our study, we classified seizures using a tool based on the ILAE 2017 classification scheme, and the trained PNs served as the gold standard for comparison. The most common seizure semiology identified by the PNs in our study was generalized motor seizures (47%), followed by focal seizures (35%) and multiple semiology seizures (15%). This distribution of different seizure semiologies aligns with the findings of previous studies.[1011] There was moderate agreement (kappa = 0.45–0.52) at the first level of seizure classification, which was better than at the second level of classification (kappa = 0.28–0.30). However, at both levels of classification, agreement levels were below the hypothesized agreement of κ = 0.8. The tool performed relatively well in identifying whether the seizure was generalized or focal. This is significant, as differentiation between focal and generalized seizure is vital for decisions regarding initial antiseizure medications and the diagnostic approach. However, analysis of the agreement at the second level of classification revealed that the agreement was generally unsatisfactory.

Some seizure types, such as nonmotor seizures, pose challenges in identification compared to motor seizures, even for experienced clinicians.[12] Better agreement between neurologists has been observed after excluding seizure types that were difficult to identify or ambiguous.[10] Bergin and colleagues examined the consensus among clinicians in the diagnosis of epilepsy and reported a kappa agreement of only 0.4 for the identification of seizure semiology. The majority of participants in this study were neurologists, epileptologists, or trainees in neurology.[13] Semiology identification is further complicated by the fact that a single seizure event may exhibit two or more semiologies in up to 50% of seizure events.[14] The operational seizure classification system of ILAE 2017, which serves as the foundation for our study, recommends categorizing seizures based on their initial manifestations.[3] However, this approach could pose difficulties when dealing with a variety of symptoms at onset. In our tool, seizure semiology was labelled in accordance with the ILAE, and the initial semiology was considered for final consideration. It is likely that a questionnaire-based tool will miss these intricacies.

Comprehending seizure semiology in children, even among experienced clinicians, can indeed be difficult and prone to errors. Nevertheless, despite these limitations and challenges, good agreement has been reported in semiology identification when experienced PNs are involved.[14] Similarly, the localizing and lateralizing value of semiology, when assessed by experienced neurologists, is high and comparable with scalp Electroencephalography (EEG) and neuroimaging.[15] It is important to note that studies reporting good inter-observer agreement involve trained or experienced neurologists. In view of these findings, the importance of experience in accurately differentiating specific seizure types cannot be underestimated. The impact of experience has been highlighted by various studies in other fields of medicine evaluating inter-observer agreement between two set of observers.[1617] Parents might not always provide detailed descriptions, and targeted questions can facilitate crucial insights into seizure semiology. Achieving this requires comprehensive knowledge and familiarity with a wide range of seizure types. Alongside experience, training also plays a crucial role. Training with clinical videos can significantly enhance the ability to discern diverse seizure types and differentiate them from mimics.[18]

One of the limitations of our study was the unequal distribution of seizure types, with some subtypes being underrepresented. This was primarily due to the limited duration of the study.

Conclusions

The AIIMS modified INCLEN diagnostic instrument for epilepsy (Addendum 2017) is a suitable tool for the identification and broad classification of seizures, which is crucial for selecting the appropriate first-line treatment. However, its use does not replace the need for consultation with a pediatric neurologist when more detailed seizure type evaluation and treatment modification are necessary.
