Study on reasonable sampling interval and range of compressional-torsional structural plane based on undulation angle characteristic parameters
Authors: Jia Jia, Zhijian Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Structural planes within rock masses often control their stability. Characterizing the morphology of structural planes with characteristic parameters and developing shear-strength models for those planes are widely used approaches that play a critical role in stability assessment and reinforcement design. The characterization effect of characteristic parameters is controlled by the sampling interval and range. This study focuses on the compressional-torsional structural planes controlling the stability of high rock slopes in the Zhongyuntai mountain cutting high rock slope in Lianyungang City, Jiangsu Province, China. Surface morphology data of the discontinuities were collected using 3D laser scanning technology to determining the optimal minimum sampling interval and range for 2D and 3D undulation angle feature parameters to enhance the accuracy of roughness characterization. The findings indicate: (1) A sampling interval of less than 1.0 mm is recommended for assessing the roughness of compressional-torsional structure surfaces using undulation angle parameters. (2) The stability of a single sampling line in characterizing the roughness of the entire structure surface using 2D undulation angle parameters is difficult to be stable with the change of sampling range. Using the mean value for characterization exhibit higher stability, and the sampling range should be no less than 500 mm. (3) The 3D undulation angle feature parameters exhibit higher stability across different sampling ranges, suggesting a sampling range exceeding 500 mm, with an optimal range surpassing 700 mm.

Introduction

The failure of rock masses in engineering, such as slopes and caverns, is significantly influenced by the internal structural planes of the rock mass1,2. Numerous studies indicate that rock mass failure is primarily caused by shear activity along these structural planes. Quantitatively describing the roughness of structural surfaces using feature parameters is a common approach. Since Barton3 introduced the roughness coefficient JRC in 1977, extensive research has been conducted by scholars globally on determining the quantitative value of JRC. Among the various studies, analyzing the asperity angle inclination is a prevalent statistical parameter method. In recent years, Ban10 proposed a two-dimensional parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{2d}$$\end{document}, and Hong11 proposed a new index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{c}$$\end{document} for the two-dimensional roughness of rock structural surfaces. Zheng12 used Gaussian process regression to construct a JRC prediction model for eight roughness coefficients, providing new insights for the application of artificial intelligence technology in the study of structural surface roughness. Overall, the undulation angle characterization method has been extensively studied from two-dimensional, three-dimensional, single-parameter and comprehensive-parameter perspectives. As deep learning technology has matured, there has also been development in the direction of combining artificial intelligence technology. Studies of structural surface roughness using feature parameters have shown that smaller sampling intervals provide a more comprehensive representation of the structural surface morphology. However, as the sampling interval increases, smaller undulations are gradually ignored and the resulting morphology represents a transition from the microscopic to the macroscopic13. Based on this, scholars have generally used small sampling intervals for feature parameter research. Sun14 introduced a new structural surface roughness parameter based on fractal dimension theory, exploring its correlation in tensile structural surfaces with sampling intervals of 0.2 mm to 0.8 mm. However, detailed analysis of a stable sampling interval for this parameter was not provided. Song15 examined six morphological parameters based on the characteristics of three weak rock samples and concluded that a stable sampling interval should be between 0.1 mm and 0.5 mm. Huang16 established a relationship between JRC and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{z}_{2}$$\end{document} within a sampling interval of 0.1 mm to 0.5 mm, but this study was limited to two-dimensional profiles and lacked applicability to realistic three-dimensional angular structural surfaces. Kulatilake17,18 conducted an in-depth study on joint roughness quantification. Other scholars19–21 have studied the characteristics of different-sized structural surfaces and found that the characteristic parameter remains stable after the sampling range exceeds a certain threshold. As research on the characteristic parameters of structural planes has deepened, point-cloud data and characteristic parameters have been applied to engineering stability assessment. Pham22 evaluated rock-mass stability at a tunnel face using three-dimensional point-cloud data of structural planes and incorporated deep learning to reduce the subjectivity of manual judgments. M. Sturzenegger23 surveyed The Palliser Rockslide in the Canadian Rocky Mountains and used ground digital photogrammetry to assess the rock-slope stability and its complex failure mechanisms. A. J. Riquelme24 processed three-dimensional point-cloud data from an open-pit coal-mine slope in Spain, extracted geometric parameters such as attitude and roughness, and thereby evaluated slope stability.

The morphologies of rock mass structural planes in nature are intricate and closely linked to their origin, scale, and the lithology of the host rock. Unlike stable, relatively flat torsional structural planes, compressional and compressional-torsional structural planes exhibit mirror-like surfaces, scratches, steps, and reverse steps. In contrast to steeply dipping tensile structural planes, the dip angles of compressional and compressional-torsional structural planes show significant variability. It is common for the dip angle of compressional and compressional-torsional structural surfaces to be shallower than that of the overlying surface, potentially leading to stability issues. Unlike structural surfaces and filled structural surfaces in soft rocks, the shear strength of unfilled compressional-torsional structural surfaces in hard rocks is primarily influenced by the morphological characteristics of the surface. Previous research has demonstrated that these morphological features play a crucial role in determining the shear strength of such structural surfaces25–28. Affected by the irregular distribution of derived structures, compressional-torsional structural planes exhibit multi-scale undulation characteristics on their surfaces. Variations in morphological characteristics and shear strength exist along the same structural plane at different locations. This discrepancy raises concerns about the representativeness of morphological characteristic parameters and shear strength values. Specifically, determining the appropriate sampling range and interval is essential for accurately capturing the morphological characteristics that represent the overall features of structural planes.

Most studies of structural-plane morphology have used either laboratory-fabricated regular zigzag surfaces or Brazilian-splitting tensile specimens. The applicability of the characteristic parameters derived from these methods to compressional-torsional structural planes remains unexplored. Additionally, further investigation is needed to determine the applicability of the stable sampling range and interval of these parameters on compressional-torsional structural planes. This study utilizes the favorable exposure conditions of structural surfaces at the Tianwan Nuclear Power Plant and the Port Area Access Highway Project in Lianyungang, Jiangsu Province, China. Three-dimensional laser scanning technology is employed to investigate optimal sampling intervals and ranges for collecting morphological feature parameters of compressional-torsional structural surfaces.

Study area

The cut slope in the study area has a total height of approximately 200 m with a general strike of N52.5°E. The lithology is primarily composed of hard, metamorphic granulite (Pt2−3yx). Constrained by the existing Yunmen Temple Reservoir and transmission lines of Tianwan Nuclear Power Station, the slope exhibits an overall angle of 42.4°, with 20 berms having bench face angles ranging from 49° to 76°. The cut slope is bordered by disused quarries provides optimal conditions for investigating the surface morphology of compressional-torsional structural discontinuities (Fig. 1a). On the area, there is a compressional-torsional fault (L1) dipping out of the slope at angles ranging from 10° to 73° to the northwest at an inclination of 36° to 45°, forms the potential basal failure surface of an unstable rock mass (Fig. 1b), extends for a distance of 400 m to 1200 m. with well-developed secondary structures, exhibiting striations of 48°–58° pitch and 10°–19° rake. Field observations reveal a notable angular deviation between the actual fault movement direction and its dip direction, indicating pronounced anisotropy in both surface morphology and shear strength characteristics. The passive sliding surface in the rock mass is composed of a chlorite schist weak interlayer (L2) that dips into the slope (Fig. 1b). This interlayer has a thickness varying from 0.1 to 2.0 m, with a schistosity attitude of 20–60°/SE∠20–30°. Additionally, the rock mass features two sets of steeply dipping conjugate shear fractures that act as transverse and longitudinal release surfaces: L3 (312°/NE\SW∠70–88°) and L4 (32°/NW, SE∠74°) (Fig. 1b). These fractures are characterized by a typical spacing of less than 1.0 m but exhibit significant persistence and penetration depth. These structural discontinuities combine unfavorably with exposed faces, leading to numerous local stability problems and posing significant issues to overall slope stability.

Methodology and result

Acquisition of structural surface morphology parameters based on 3D laser scanning

The compressional-torsional structural surfaces in the study area were scanned meticulously with a SCANTECH handheld composite 3D laser scanner (Fig. 2), utilizing a sampling interval of 0.1 mm to capture detailed surface morphology point cloud data. To mitigate scanning inaccuracies stemming from factors like scanner positioning, ambient temperature variations, and minor debris on surfaces, the point cloud data underwent coordinate transformation, clipping, and temperature-based denoising processes. Subsequently, the processed point cloud data were subjected to interpolation using Kriging Interpolation Method29 to calculate normalized point coordinates. These coordinates were then used to reconstruct the vertices and normal vector coordinates of 3D micro-element triangles.

Discussion

This study found that, when using the morphological characteristic parameters of the structural-plane undulation angle method to describe compressional-torsional structural planes, the sampling interval should be less than 1.0 mm and the sampling range should exceed a diameter of 500 mm. Compared with prior studies that generally recommended sampling intervals below 0.5 mm, our result for the sampling-interval threshold can be regarded as a critical value. When the sampling interval exceeded 1.0 mm, the resulting point-cloud morphology was insufficient to represent the morphological features of the compressional-torsional structural plane. Consistent with many other studies, the sampling interval below 0.5 mm is the optimal choice for characterizing the morphology of compressional-torsional structural planes. The sampling range and interval are closely tied to the scale-dependent behavior of rock joint roughness, which often displays fractal characteristics. These characteristics imply that roughness changes with the observation scale. Our study systematically quantifies how the values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{\theta\:}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{-}{\theta\:}}^{+}$$\end{document} change as the sampling range increases. We found that a stable, representative value emerges beyond a certain sampling range, indicating the attainment of a Representative Elementary Area for these parameters under the studied conditions. This scale acts as a threshold where the first-order roughness (or waviness) is sufficiently captured, and further expansion does not significantly alter the parameter values. Similarly, defining an optimal sampling interval establishes the necessary resolution to capture the second-order roughness (or undulation) affecting the parameter’s value. This study investigates the compressional-torsional structural planes of gneiss under shallow geological conditions characterized by low normal stress. The findings are most relevant to the compressional-torsional structural planes of hard metamorphic rocks with distinct anisotropic structures. The results may not apply to soft rocks or to rocks with weak anisotropy because the shear behavior of soft rocks exhibits scale-dependent degradation and is more susceptible to wear of second-order roughness. Consequently, a larger sampling range and a smaller sampling interval may be necessary for such materials. Under high normal stress, second-order undulations are suppressed by the confining pressure during shear, and first-order undulations control the shear strength of structural planes; thus the influence of sampling interval diminishes, but further work is required to determine suitable sampling ranges under these conditions.

Although the applicability of our research findings to structural planes formed by others genesis types and stress conditions remains uncertain, the method employed in this study is still applicable to various rock types and stress conditions. Determining the Representative Elementary Area for roughness characteristic parameters is crucial in rock mechanics. Our approach, which examines how characteristic parameters change with the sampling range and identifies stable values, has proven feasible. We assert that the sampling interval and range we recommend can enhance the reliability of rock mass engineering stability analyses. Furthermore, as artificial intelligence methods advance in engineering, the reliability of training data becomes increasingly important. Our results provide a high-quality standard for constructing training data, thereby establishing a foundation for an intelligent and efficient roughness prediction model.
