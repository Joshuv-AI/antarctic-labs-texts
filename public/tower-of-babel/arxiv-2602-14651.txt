Abstract
. We derive the asymptotic expansion at infinity for embedded ends of uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces with finite total curvature in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , and we establish a maximum principle at infinity. Furthermore, we solve the Dirichlet problem for the uniformly elliptic Weingarten equation in dimension two on strictly convex bounded domains.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an oriented immersed surface.
\Sigma
is said to be a
uniformly elliptic Weingarten surface
if its principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}\geq\kappa_{2}
satisfy

(1.1)

\kappa_{2}=f(\kappa_{1}),

for a certain function
f\in C^{1,1}_{loc}[c,+\infty)
, where

(1.2)

-\frac{1}{\Lambda}\leq f^{\prime}(t)\leq-\Lambda,~\forall~t\geq c,

for some constant
0<\Lambda<1
, and
f(c)=c
. Note that constant mean curvature (CMC) surfaces and minimal surfaces are included in this class. Moreover,
\Sigma
is called of
minimal type
if

(1.3)

f(0)=0.

The global study of uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces has been developed by many authors (see, for instance,
[
1
,
2
,
5
,

,

,

). However, some important problems still remain open.

Observe that the theory of uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces can be regarded as the natural fully nonlinear counterpart of the theory of CMC surfaces. From this perspective, one of the fundamental problems in the theory is to extend to this fully nonlinear setting some of the main global theorems of CMC surface theory. In this regard, it is important to note that, although certain features do extend without essential difficulty—such as invariance under ambient isometries, the validity of a maximum principle, and the existence of basic examples including spheres, cylinders, planes, and rotationally symmetric surfaces—many properties are lost in the passage to the Weingarten setting. For instance, the Weierstrass representation for minimal surfaces, the harmonicity of the Gauss map for CMC surfaces, and, more generally, the variational structure of the associated PDE no longer hold for Weingarten surfaces.

In this paper, we study the asymptotic behavior at infinity of uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces of minimal type. We obtain the expansion at infinity of each embedded end with finite total curvature, whose behavior depends strongly on the value of
f^{\prime}(0)
. Observe that, in the case of minimal surfaces, this problem can be solved using the Weierstrass representation in terms of holomorphic data.
More precisely, it is well known that every embedded end of a minimal surface with finite total curvature is asymptotic to either a plane or a catenoid. Thus, for such a minimal surface, after an isometry, each end can be seen as a graph over a plane outside a ball
B_{R}
, centered at the origin and of radius
R
, of the form

u(x)=d\log|x|+c+o(1),\qquad x\in B_{R}^{c}.

This result is essential for the study of complete embedded minimal surfaces with finite total curvature and constitutes a fundamental tool for various theorems, such as, among many others, Schoen’s classical characterization of rotational minimal surfaces
[
35
]
and the classification of complete embedded minimal surfaces of genus zero with finite total curvature by López and Ros
[
27
]
. It is also used, for instance, to study the moduli space of finite total curvature surfaces, to control the stability, and to analyze the rigidity of their periods.

Consequently, in order to extend some of the fundamental theorems of minimal surface theory to the fully nonlinear context, it is crucial to understand the behavior of finite total curvature ends in this setting. However, a Weierstrass representation is no longer available; therefore, for general uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces, the problem reduces to the study of the asymptotic behavior at infinity of solutions to their associated fully nonlinear (and non-uniformly) elliptic equations over exterior domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Thanks to our description of the asymptotic behavior at infinity of the embedded ends of finite total curvature, we are also able to establish a maximum principle at infinity (cf.
[
21
]
for the case of minimal surfaces).
Our expansion at infinity and the associated maximum principle are used by Espinar and Mesa
[
6
]
to give a Jorge-Meeks formula
[
20
]
for uniformly elliptic Weingarten surfaces of minimal type and also extend the classical Schoen characterization of rotational examples for minimal surfaces
[
35
]
. Additionally, our approach should help, for instance, to solve the exterior Dirichlet problem for
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
, to construct new complete examples and to study the moduli space of the complete embedded examples with finite total curvature.

Let

Observe that a specially important case happens when the Weingarten relation

This enables us to control the asymptotic distance between two disjoint embedded ends of finite total curvature:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let

define two graphs satisfying

1.1
–

1.2
–

1.3

Then there exists a constant

such that

(1.7)

\frac{u(x)-\tilde{u}(x)}{\log|x|}\to c_{0}\quad\mbox{as}~~x\to\infty

Moreover, if

Let

define two graphs satisfying

1.1
–

1.2
–

1.3

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\Sigma
be an oriented immersed surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Gauss map
N:\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
, satisfying
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
. Then, its Gaussian curvature
\mathcal{K}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\leq 0
and

(2.1)

2|\mathcal{K}|\leq\kappa_{1}^{2}+\kappa_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\Lambda}|\mathcal{K}|.

This indicates that
N
is a quasiregular mapping (see
[
13
,
37
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a complete, embedded, uniformly elliptic Weingarten surface of minimal type, possibly with compact boundary. Assume that
\Sigma
has finite total curvature, that is,

\int_{\Sigma}|\mathcal{K}|\,dA<\infty.

Then
\Sigma
has finitely many ends, each of which is properly embedded and possesses a limit unit normal at infinity. Moreover, after possibly truncating an end, it can be represented, up to an isometry, as the graph of a function defined over the complement of a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
has finite total curvature, we have from
[
19
]
that
\Sigma
has a finite number of ends and each of them is conformally equivalent to the puncture unit disk
\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{\ast}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ 0<|z|\leq 1\}
. Thus, fixed an end
E
of
\Sigma
, using
eq.

2.1
, we get from
[
41
]
that
E
is properly embedded and has a limit unit normal at infinity, that is, there exists
N_{\infty}:=\lim_{z\to 0}N(z)
when
E
is conformally parameterized as
\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{\ast}
.

Up to an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
we can assume that
N_{\infty}=(0,0,1)
. Thus, shrinking
E
if necessary, we can assume that the third coordinate of the Gauss map
N_{3}\geq 1/2
in
E
. Hence, the vertical projection on the plane
x_{3}=0
is a local diffeomorphism, that is,
E
can be locally written as a graph
(x_{1},x_{2},u(x_{1},x_{2}))
. Since
E
is properly embedded and the gradient
|Du|\leq 1/2
, a standard topological argument gives us that, shrinking
E
again if necessary, the end is a graph over the exterior of a compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

Hence, to analyze the behavior of embedded ends of finite total curvature, we will concentrate on the study of graphs outside a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Our attention now turns to the fully nonlinear elliptic partial differential equations satisfied by the surfaces under consideration. We start with the definition of uniform ellipticity (see
[
4
, Definition 2.1 and Lemma 2.2]
):

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{2}
be the set of
2\times 2
symmetric matrices. An operator
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is called uniformly elliptic if there exist positive constants
\bar{\lambda}\leq\bar{\Lambda}
such that for any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
M,N\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
,

\displaystyle F(p,M)-F(p,N)\leq\bar{\Lambda}|(M-N)^{+}|-\bar{\lambda}|(M-N)^{-}|,

where
(M-N)^{+}
and
(M-N)^{-}
denote the positive part and negative part of
M-N
respectively. The constants
\bar{\lambda},\bar{\Lambda}
are called ellipticity constants.

We will frequently use the following structure condition, which comprises the Lipschitz continuity of
F
with respect to
p
: there exist positive constants
\bar{\lambda}\leq\bar{\Lambda}
and
\mu
such that for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
M,N\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle F(p,M)-F(q,N)\leq\bar{\Lambda}|(M-N)^{+}|-\bar{\lambda}|(M-N)^{-}|+\mu|p-q|.

We also refer to
\mu
as the ellipticity constant for simplicity.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
eq.

2.2
is equivalent to the following:
F\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})
(i.e., Lipschitz continuous) and there exist positive constants
\bar{\lambda}\leq\bar{\Lambda}
and
\mu
such that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}I\leq F_{M}(p,M)\leq\bar{\Lambda}I,\quad|F_{p}(p,M)|\leq\mu,~~\mbox{for}~~a.e.~~p\in\mathbb{R}^{2},~M\in\mathcal{S}^{2},

where
I
is the unit matrix. As usual,
F_{M}
and
F_{p}
denote the first derivatives of
F
with respect to the matrix
M
and the vector
p
respectively, i.e.,
F_{M}
is a matrix and
F_{p}
is a vector whose elements are given by

(F_{M})_{ij}:=F_{M_{ij}}:=\frac{\partial F}{\partial M_{ij}},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq 2;\quad(F_{p})_{i}:=F_{p_{i}}:=\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{i}},\quad 1\leq i\leq 2.

Indeed, for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
M,N\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, by the Newton-Leibniz formula,

(2.4)

\displaystyle F(p,M)-F(q,N)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{p_{i}}(\xi)(p-q)_{i}+F_{M_{ij}}(\xi)(M-N)_{ij}\right)dt,

where
\xi=\left(q+t(p-q),N+t(M-N)\right).
Thus, it follows from
eq.

2.4
that
eq.

2.2
and
eq.

2.3
are equivalent.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that the uniform ellipticity of a PDE is different from that of a Weingarten surface (i.e.
eq.

1.2
). Indeed,
eq.

2.3
means that the two eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
of
F_{M}
lie between two positive constants. Instead,
eq.

1.2
means that the product of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
lies between two positive constants if we assume the boundedness of
|p|
additionally (see
[
18
, Section 2.4 in Chapter V, pp. 128-129]
). Hence,
eq.

2.3
(or equivalently
eq.

2.2
) is stronger than
eq.

1.2
.

On the other hand, if
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are bounded,
eq.

1.2
immediately implies
eq.

2.3
. That is, we obtain a uniformly elliptic PDE if we have curvature estimates, which is true in fact (see
Section

2
and
[
7
, pp. 1913-1915]
).

We will also need the following interior
C^{2,\alpha}
regularity result (see
[

).

Lemma 2.5

Let

and

Next, we show that if
u
defines a graph satisfying
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
then

is indeed a solution of a fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equation when

is bounded. This has been proved in
[
7
, pp. 1913-1915]
. For the readers’ convenience, we give the proof below. First, we introduce the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
G\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
G\geq 0
. Then for any
r\geq 1
,

(2.6)

|DG|\leq C\sqrt{G}\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{r},

where
C
depends only on
\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r})}
and
\|D^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r})}
.

Proof.

Fix
r\geq 1
and
x\in B_{r}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
|DG(x)|>0
. Denote
K=\|D^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2r})}
. By the Taylor formula and noting
G\geq 0
, for any
h\in\bar{B}_{r}
, there exists
\xi\in B_{r}
such that

\displaystyle 0\leq G(x+h)=G(x)+DG(x)\cdot h+\frac{1}{2}h^{T}D^{2}G(\xi)h\leq G(x)+DG(x)\cdot h+\frac{1}{2}K|h|^{2}.

If
|DG(x)|<Kr
, take
h=-DG(x)/K
. Then

0\leq G(x)-\frac{|DG(x)|^{2}}{K}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{|DG(x)|^{2}}{K}.

Thus,

(2.7)

|DG(x)|\leq\sqrt{2K}\sqrt{G(x)}.

If
|DG(x)|\geq Kr
, take
h=-rDG(x)/|DG(x)|
. Then

0\leq G(x)-r|DG(x)|+\frac{1}{2}Kr^{2}.

Thus,

|DG(x)|\leq\frac{G(x)}{r}+\frac{1}{2}Kr\leq G(x)+\frac{1}{2}|DG(x)|.

That is,

(2.8)

|DG(x)|\leq 2G(x).

By combining
eq.

2.7
and
eq.

2.8
, we arrive at the conclusion. ∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
u
define a solution of

1.1
–

in such a way that

is seen as a solution to a fully nonlinear elliptic equation:

(2.9)

F(Du,D^{2}u)=0\quad\mbox{in}~~\Omega.

Note that
f
may be not defined at

. To ensure that

is well defined for all
(p,M)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}
, we extend the domain of

from

Moreover, if

Proof.

This lemma can be deduced from
[
7

. For the readers’ convenience, we give the proof below. Let us consider the mean curvature and the Gaussian curvature of the graph:

\mathcal{H}=\left(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}\right)/2,\quad\mathcal{K}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}.

Then

1.1

\mathcal{H}-\sqrt{\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K}}-f\left(\mathcal{H}+\sqrt{\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K}}\right)=0.

Hence,

is a solution of the following fully nonlinear equation:

(2.10)

F(Du,D^{2}u)=0\quad\mbox{in}~~\Omega,

where

(2.11)

F(p,M):=\mathcal{H}-\sqrt{\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K}}-f\left(\mathcal{H}+\sqrt{\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K}}\right),

and
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{K}
are functions of

given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(p,M):=\frac{(1+p_{2}^{2})M_{11}-2p_{1}p_{2}M_{12}+(1+p_{1}^{2})M_{22}}{2(1+|p|^{2})^{3/2}},\quad\mathcal{K}(p,M):=\frac{\det M}{(1+|p|^{2})^{2}}.

Obviously,

is a continuous function. The ellipticity of

).

Next, we prove the uniform ellipticity of

. Define for

(2.12)

\mathcal{B}_{r}:=\left\{(p,M)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}:|p|+|M|<r\right\}

and

(2.13)

A_{r}:=\left\{(p,M)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}:\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K}=0\right\}=\left\{(p,M)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}:M=\nu\begin{pmatrix}1+p_{1}^{2}&p_{1}p_{2}\\
p_{1}p_{2}&1+p_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix},\nu\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Note that
A_{r}
is a submanifold of lower dimension. By the definition of
F
, we have
F\in C^{0,1}_{loc}
in
\mathcal{B}_{r}\backslash A_{r}
.

We have known that

3.
Solvability of the Weingarten equation in a bounded convex domain

In this section we obtain the solvability of the Weingarten equation for general strictly convex, bounded domains.

Along this section we search for a function
u
defined in a strictly convex domain
\Omega
whose graph satisfies

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\kappa_{2}=f(\kappa_{1})&&\quad\mbox{in}~~\Omega,\\
&u=\varphi&&\quad\mbox{on}~~\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

for a certain function
f\in C^{0,1}_{loc}[0,+\infty)
satisfying
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
.

The theorem will be proved by means of the method of continuity, which is divided into several steps.

First, we observe that if
u
is a solution of
eq.

3.1
then its graph is saddle, that is, has non positive Gaussian curvature. Thus, from
[
39
,
30
]
(see also
[
13
, p. 311]
) one has:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
be a solution of
eq.

3.1
, where
\Omega
is a strictly convex, bounded,
C^{2}
domain and
\varphi\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Then

(3.2)

\|u\|_{C^{0,1}(\bar{\Omega})}\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
\min\kappa_{\partial\Omega}
and
\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
.

A key ingredient is the following curvature estimate.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
be a solution of
eq.

3.1
. Suppose that
\Omega
is a strictly convex, bounded,
C^{2,\alpha}
domain, and
\varphi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
for some
0<\alpha<1
. Then the second fundamental form of the graph of
u
is bounded, i.e.,

(3.3)

\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})}\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
\Lambda
,
\alpha
,
\max\kappa_{\partial\Omega}
,
\min\kappa_{\partial\Omega}
,
\|\partial\Omega\|_{C^{2,\alpha}}
and
\|\varphi\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}
.

Proof.

The proof is a standard combination of a blow-up process with the classification of entire solutions. We prove the lemma by contradiction.

Suppose that the conclusion is false. Then there exist positive constants
\Lambda
,
0<\alpha<1
,
K_{1}\leq K_{2}
and
K
and sequences of
f_{n},u_{n},\Omega_{n},\varphi_{n}
such that
u_{n}
are solutions of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\kappa_{2}=f_{n}(\kappa_{1})&&\quad\mbox{in}~~\Omega_{n};\\
&u_{n}=\varphi_{n}&&\quad\mbox{on}~~\partial\Omega_{n},\end{aligned}\right.

where
f_{n}
are uniformly elliptic with
\Lambda
; the
\Omega_{n}
are bounded convex domain with

K_{1}\leq\kappa_{\Omega_{n}}\leq K_{2},~\forall~n

and

\|\partial\Omega_{n}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}},~\|\varphi_{n}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{n})}\leq K.

Moreover, there exists a sequence of points
x_{n}\in\bar{\Omega}_{n}
such that

\lambda_{n}:=|\sigma_{n}(x_{n})|=\max_{x\in\bar{\Omega}_{n}}|\sigma_{n}(x)|\geq n,

where
\sigma_{n}
denotes the second fundamental form of the graph of
u_{n}
.

In the following, we divide the proof into two cases:

(i) Up to a subsequence,
d(x_{n},\partial\Omega_{n})\cdot\lambda_{n}\to\infty
.

In this case, we use the following blow-up:

y:=\lambda_{n}(x-x_{n}),\quad v_{n}(y):=\lambda_{n}\left(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(x_{n})\right).

Then, after the rescaling,
v_{n}
is a solution of

(3.4)

\kappa_{2}=g_{n}(\kappa_{1})\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R_{n}},

where
R_{n}=d(x_{n},\partial\Omega_{n})\cdot\lambda_{n}\to\infty
and

g_{n}(t):=\lambda_{n}^{-1}f_{n}(\lambda_{n}t).

Hence,
v_{n}
is a uniformly elliptic Weingarten graph of minimal type as well.

Furthermore, with the aid of the Lipschitz estimate for
u_{n}
, given by
Section

3
, we have

(3.5)

v_{n}(0)=0,\quad|Dv_{n}(y)|=|Du_{n}(x)|\leq C,~\forall~y\in B_{R_{n}}.

Moreover, the second fundamental form of
v_{n}
is bounded. In fact,

|\sigma_{v_{n}}(y)|=\lambda_{n}^{-1}|\sigma_{n}(x)|\leq 1,~\forall~y\in B_{R_{n}}.

Hence,

(3.6)

\|D^{2}v_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R_{n}})}\leq C.

Therefore,
eq.

3.4
is a uniformly elliptic fully nonlinear equation for each
n
(cf.
Section

2
).

By the interior
C^{2,\alpha}
regularity (see
Section

2
), there exists
0<\beta<1
such that

\|v_{n}\|_{C^{2,\beta}(\bar{B}_{R})}\leq C\|v_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R})},~\forall~0<R<R_{n}/2,

where
C
depends on
R
but not on
n
. By
eq.

3.5
,

\|v_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2R})}\leq CR.

Hence,
\|v_{n}\|_{C^{2,\beta}(\bar{B}_{R})}
are uniformly bounded for any fixed
R
. Since
R_{n}\to\infty
, up to a subsequence, there exists
v\in C^{2,\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
v_{n}\to v
in
C^{2}(\bar{B}_{R})
for any
0<R<\infty
.

Since
g_{n}(0)=0
and
|g^{\prime}_{n}|
are uniformly bounded, up to a subsequence, there exists a Lipschitz function
g
such that
g_{n}\to g
uniformly and

-\frac{1}{\Lambda}\leq g^{\prime}(t)\leq-\Lambda,~\forall~t\geq 0.

In addition, from
eq.

3.5
and
eq.

3.6
, we have

(3.7)

v(0)=0,\quad\|Dv\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|D^{2}v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C.

Hence,
v
is a solution of the following uniformly elliptic equation

\kappa_{2}=g(\kappa_{1})\quad\mbox{in}~~\mathbb{R}^{2}.

As in the proof of
Section

2
(see
eq.

2.29
),
v
can be regarded as a solution of a linear uniformly elliptic equation:

(3.8)

a^{ij}u_{ij}=0\quad\mbox{in}~~\mathbb{R}^{2}.

From the interior
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity (see
[
13
, Theorem 12.4]
), there exists
0<\alpha<1
such that

|Dv(x)-Dv(y)|\leq C\frac{\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}}{R^{1+\alpha}}|x-y|^{\alpha},~\forall~x,y\in B_{R/2},~\forall~R>0.

By
eq.

3.7
,
\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\leq CR
. Hence, by letting
R\to\infty
in the above equation, we conclude that
Dv
is a constant vector. That is,

is a linear function.

On the other hand,

|\sigma_{v}(0)|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|\sigma_{v_{n}}(0)|=1,

where
\sigma_{v}
denotes the second fundamental form of
v
. Therefore, we obtain a contradiction.

Next, we consider the case

(ii)
d(x_{n},\partial\Omega_{n})\cdot\lambda_{n}\leq C_{0}
for some positive constant
C_{0}
.

Let
\tilde{x}_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
such that
d(x_{n},\partial\Omega_{n})=|x_{n}-\tilde{x}_{n}|
. Consider a rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with associated orthogonal matrix
A_{n}
such that
A_{n}\nu_{n}=e_{2}=(0,1)
, where
\nu_{n}
is the interior unit normal of
\partial\Omega_{n}
at

4.
The constant sign property

We now consider an end of a uniformly elliptic Weingarten surface of minimal type

c=+\infty
, i.e.,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}{u(x)}=0.

Consider the function
u_{\delta}
which defines the new graph obtained by scaling with factor
0<\delta<1
:

u_{\delta}(x):=\delta\,u\left(\frac{x}{\delta}\right),~\forall~x\in B_{R}^{c}.

Then
u_{\delta}
is a solution of

(6.22)

\kappa_{2}=f_{\delta}(\kappa_{1})\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R}^{c},

where

f_{\delta}(t):=\delta f(\delta^{-1}t),~\forall~t\geq 0.

With respect to this
f_{\delta}
, by
Section

6
, there exists a radial solution
v_{\delta}
of
eq.

6.22
with
v_{\delta}(R)=0
and
v^{\prime}_{\delta}(R)=1
. Moreover (note that
f^{\prime}_{\delta}(0)=f^{\prime}(0)
),

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{v_{\delta}(x)}{u_{\delta}(x)}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}{u_{\delta}(x)}=0.

Hence, there exists
R_{0}>R
such that

v_{\delta}<u_{\delta}\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R_{0}}^{c}.

By the comparison principle, we also have

v_{\delta}<u_{\delta}\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R_{0}}\backslash B_{R}.

That is,

(6.23)

v_{\delta}<u_{\delta}\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R}^{c}.

By
Section

2
,

u_{\delta}(x)=\delta u(\delta^{-1}x)\leq C\delta^{\alpha}|x|^{1-\alpha},~\forall~x\in B_{R}^{c}.

In addition, by the uniform estimates for
v_{\delta}
(see (i) in
Section

6
), up to a subsequence,
v_{\delta}\to v
in
C^{1}_{loc}(B_{R}^{c})
for some
v
. Let
\delta\to 0
in
eq.

6.23
, we have

(6.24)

v\leq 0\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{R}^{c},

which is a contradiction since
v^{\prime}(R)=1>0
.

Next, we consider the case
c=0
. By
Section

6
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive radial solution
v^{\varepsilon}
of
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
in
B_{R}^{c}
with
v^{\varepsilon}(R)=0
,
(v^{\varepsilon})^{\prime}(R)=C_{\varepsilon}
for some
C_{\varepsilon}>0
and

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{v^{\varepsilon}(x)}{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}=\varepsilon.

Hence, by noting
c=0
,

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{u(x)}{v^{\varepsilon}(x)}=0.

Then there exist sequences of
R_{i}\rightarrow\infty
increasingly and
x_{i}\in\partial B_{R_{i}}
such that

u(x_{i})\leq\varepsilon v^{\varepsilon}(x_{i}),\quad~\forall~i\geq 1.

By applying the Harnack inequality to
u
and choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, we obtain

u(x)\leq Cu(x_{i})\leq C\varepsilon v^{\varepsilon}(x_{i})\leq v^{\varepsilon}(x_{i})=v^{\varepsilon}(x),~\forall~x\in\partial B_{R_{i}},~\forall~i\geq 2.

Thus, from the comparison principle,

u\leq v^{\varepsilon}+\max_{\partial B_{R}}u~~\mbox{ in}~~B_{R}^{c}.

By letting
\varepsilon\to 0
(equivalently
C_{\varepsilon}\to 0
), we infer that
v^{\varepsilon}\to 0
and

u\leq\max_{\partial B_{R}}u~~\mbox{ in}~~B_{R}^{c}.

That is,
u
is bounded. Hence, from
Theorem

1.2
, there exists a constant
K
such that
eq.

6.21
holds.

Finally, we consider the case
0<c<+\infty
. By the assumption, we have for the radial solution
v^{\varepsilon}
, with
\varepsilon=c
, that

\liminf_{x\to\infty}\frac{u(x)}{v^{c}(x)}=1.

Then, for any
0<\varepsilon<c
, there exist sequences of
R_{i}\rightarrow\infty
increasingly and
x_{i}\in\partial B_{R_{i}}
such that

v^{c-\varepsilon}\leq u~~\mbox{ in }B^{c}_{R_{1}},\quad u(x_{i})\leq v^{c+\varepsilon}(x_{i}),\quad~\forall~i\geq 1.

Let
w=u-v^{c-\varepsilon}
and then as before (see
eq.

5.12
),
w
is a solution of the following linear uniformly elliptic equation:

a^{ij}w_{ij}+a^{i}w_{i}=0\quad\mbox{in}~~\bar{B}_{R}^{c},

with

|a^{i}(x)|\leq C|x|^{-1}.

Then by applying the Harnack inequality to
w
on
\partial B_{R_{i}}
(
i\geq 2
large enough), we have

\displaystyle u(x)-v^{c-\varepsilon}(x)=

\displaystyle w(x)\leq Cw(x_{i})=C\left(u(x_{i})-v^{c-\varepsilon}(x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(v^{c+\varepsilon}(x_{i})-v^{c-\varepsilon}(x_{i})\right)\leq v^{C\varepsilon}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{C\varepsilon}(x),\quad\forall~x\in\partial B_{R_{i}}.

That is,

v^{c-\varepsilon}\leq u\leq v^{c+C\varepsilon}~~\mbox{ on}~~\partial B_{R_{i}},~\forall~i\geq 2~~\mbox{ large enough}.

From the comparison principle,

v^{c-\varepsilon}\leq u\leq v^{c+C\varepsilon}+\max_{\partial B_{R}}u~~\mbox{ in}~~B_{R}^{c}.

By the definitions of
v^{c-\varepsilon}
and
v^{c+C\varepsilon}
, there exists
R_{0}
(depending on
\varepsilon
) such that

\left|\frac{v^{c-\varepsilon}(x)}{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}-c\right|\leq 2\varepsilon,\quad\left|\frac{v^{c+C\varepsilon}(x)}{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}-c\right|\leq 2C\varepsilon,~\forall~x\in B_{R_{0}}^{c}.

Hence,

\left|\frac{u(x)}{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}-c\right|\leq C\varepsilon,~\forall~x\in B_{R_{0}}^{c}.

That is,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{u(x)}{|x|^{1+f^{\prime}(0)}}=c,

as we wanted to show. ∎

Next, we consider the case
f^{\prime}(0)<-1
. We have

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
u
define a positive solution to
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
–
eq.

1.3
in
B_{R}^{c}
with
f^{\prime}(0)<-1
. Then there exists a constant
K
such that

(6.25)

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}u(x)=K.

Proof.

The proof follows the same ideas as in
Section

6
. Thus, if
\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=+\infty
, it can be shown in an analogous way that
eq.

6.24
holds; that is,

v\leq 0\quad\text{in}~B_{R}^{c},

for some radial function
v
with
v^{\prime}(R)=1
, which again leads to a contradiction. Therefore, by
Theorem

1.2
, we obtain
eq.

6.25
. ∎

7.
Appendix

In
[
7
]
, a series of uniqueness results were established for elliptic Weingarten surfaces in the symmetric case, that is, when
\kappa_{2}=f(\kappa_{1})
where
f\circ f
is the identity. In this section, we show that the techniques developed in this article allow these results to be extended to the general case
\kappa_{2}=f(\kappa_{1})
, with
f(c)=c\in\mathbb{R}
, where no symmetry assumption on
f
is imposed, and even its regularity can be weakened.

We begin by extending Lemma 2.10 in
[
7
]
. More precisely, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an elliptic Weingarten surface satisfying
eq.

1.1
where
f\in C^{0,1}_{loc}[c,a)
for some
a>c\geq 0
and

-\infty<\inf_{t\in[c,a^{\prime}]}f^{\prime}(t)\leq\sup_{t\in[c,a^{\prime}]}f^{\prime}(t)<0,~\forall~a^{\prime}<a.

Assume that
\Sigma
is a multigraph, that is, the third coordinate of its unit normal satisfies
N_{3}\geq 0
.

Then either
N_{3}\equiv 0
, or
N_{3}>0
on
\Sigma
. If
N_{3}\equiv 0
, then
\Sigma
is a portion of a plane or a cylinder.

Proof.

Since the fully nonlinear operator
F
(see
eq.

2.11
) is not smooth, the original proof cannot be applied; in particular, (2.10) in
[
7
]
is no longer available. We provide an alternative proof here.

As in the proof of
[
7
, Lemma 2.10]
, we assume that there exists
q_{0}\in\Sigma
satisfying
N_{3}(q_{0})=0
. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
q_{0}
is the origin and its unit normal is
N(q_{0})=(1,0,0)
. Let
C
denote the vertical plane or the vertical cylinder (depending on whether
c=0
or
c\neq 0
) that is tangent to
\Sigma
at
q_{0}
, with the same orientation. Then
\Sigma
and
C
can be seen around the origin as graphs
x=h^{i}(y,z),i=1,2
over their common tangent plane, and
h^{1},h^{2}
are solutions to the same fully nonlinear elliptic PDE. Note that
h^{2}\equiv 0
(the vertical plane case) or
h^{2}
is independent of
z
(the vertical cylinder case).

Let
v=\partial h^{1}/\partial z
. Then
v
belongs to the Pucci’s class
S(0)
(see for instance the second paragraph of the proof of
[
36
, Lemma 4.1]
). Note that
v\leq 0
(i.e.,
N_{3}\geq 0
) and
v(0)=0
(i.e.,
N_{3}(q_{0})=0
). By the strong maximum principle,
v\equiv 0
. That is,
h^{1}
is independent of
z
as well. Then, the function
h=h^{1}-h^{2}
is a solution of the following linear elliptic equation

a^{ij}h_{ij}+a^{i}h_{i}=0

for some
a^{ij}
and
a^{i}
(depending on
h_{1},h_{2}
). As
h
is independent of
z
, the equation above reduces to

a^{11}h_{11}+a^{1}h_{1}=0.

Since
C
is tangent to
\Sigma
at the origin,
h(0)=0
and
h_{1}(0)=0
. Hence, by the ODE theory,
h\equiv 0
, and
\Sigma
is a piece of the plane or the cylinder. ∎

Theorem 4.2 in
[
7
]
proves a Bernstein theorem for elliptic Weingarten surfaces. Thanks to
Section

7
(instead of Lemma 2.10 in
[
7
]
) this result can be generalized. Specifically, it can be shown that there are no complete elliptic Weingarten multigraphs with
f(c)=c\neq 0
and bounded second fundamental form:

Theorem 7.2
.

There is no complete multigraph with bounded second fundamental form satisfying
eq.

1.1
for some
f\in C^{1}_{loc}[c,a)
, with
a>c>0
and
f^{\prime}<0
.

Proof.

The proof follows verbatim that of
[
7
, Theorem 4.2]
. The only step in which the smoothness of the fully nonlinear elliptic operator
F
(see
eq.

2.11
) is required is in the linearization of the operator (see
[
7
, (4.3), p. 1909]
). Observe that the linearization is performed at the cylinder, where

F
is indeed as smooth as

f
, namely of class
C^{1}
, since the lack of smoothness of

F
occurs only at umbilical points of surfaces. ∎

We now extend Theorem 5.2 in
[
7
]
by establishing the existence of curvature estimates for general functions
f
:

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, possibly with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, and whose Gauss map image
N(\Sigma)
is contained in an open hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Assume that
\Sigma
satisfies a uniformly elliptic Weingarten equation
eq.

1.1
–
eq.

1.2
where
f\in C^{0,1}_{loc}[c,+\infty)
for some
c\geq 0
.

Then, for every
d>0
there exists a constant
C=C(\Lambda,c,d)
such that for each
p\in\Sigma
with
d(p,\partial\Sigma)\geq d
, it holds

|\sigma(p)|\leq C.

Here,
d
and
|\sigma|
denote, respectively, the distance function in
\Sigma
and the norm of
the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
.

In particular, if
\Sigma
is of minimal type, i.e.,
f(0)=0
, we have

(7.1)

|\sigma(p)|\leq\frac{C}{d(p,\partial\Sigma)},

where
C
depends only on
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Again, we can use essentially the same proof as in
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
. So, we will focus only on the parts of the proof that change due to the lack of differentiability of the associated PDE. The condition
eq.

1.1
in
[
7
]
is used to ensure that the fully nonlinear elliptic operator
F
(see
eq.

2.11
) is
C^{1}
. The authors then apply Nirenberg’s
C^{2,\alpha}
estimate. In fact, this estimate remains valid even when the operator
F
is not smooth (see
[
42
, Corollary 1.2]
).

We use the same notation as in the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
and only point out the main differences below. As in the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
, we prove the result by contradiction and take a sequence of blow-ups. In our setting, equation (5.4) in
[
7
]
is replaced by the following:

(7.2)

\kappa_{2}=\tilde{f}_{n}(\kappa_{1}),\quad\tilde{f}_{n}(t):=\lambda_{n}^{-1}f_{n}(\lambda_{n}t),

where
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
. Note that
\tilde{f}_{n}
are uniformly elliptic as well (with the same
\Lambda
), i.e.,

(7.3)

-\frac{1}{\Lambda}\leq\tilde{f}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\Lambda,~\forall~t\geq c_{n},

where
c_{n}=\lambda_{n}^{-1}c
and
c
is the common umbilical constant for
f_{n}
.

In addition, we have the uniform curvature estimate (see
[
7
, p. 1913]
):

|\hat{\sigma}_{n}|\leq 2\quad\mbox{in}~~\hat{D}_{n}.

And by representing the surfaces locally as graphs of
v_{n}
, we have the following uniform estimate:

\|v_{n}\|_{C^{2}(B_{\delta_{0}})}\leq\mu_{0}.

Hence, by
Section

2
,
v_{n}
are solutions of fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations:

F_{n}(Dv_{n},D^{2}v_{n})=0\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{\delta_{0}}.

Furthermore, by the uniform ellipticity of
\tilde{f}_{n}
and noting
f_{n}(c)=c
,

\displaystyle|F_{n}(0,0)|=|\tilde{f}_{n}(0)|=\lambda_{n}^{-1}|f_{n}(0)|=\lambda_{n}^{-1}|f_{n}(c)-f^{\prime}_{n}(\xi_{n})c|=\lambda_{n}^{-1}|c-f^{\prime}_{n}(\xi_{n})c|\leq C\lambda_{n}^{-1},

where
\xi_{n}\in[0,c]
and
C
depends only on
\Lambda
and
c
.

Therefore, by the interior
C^{2,\alpha}
regularity for
v_{n}
(see
Section

2
), we have the same uniform estimate as in
[
7
, (5.13)]
for
v_{n}
:

(7.4)

\|v_{n}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(B_{\delta^{\prime}})}\leq C^{\prime},~\forall~n\geq 1.

Then there exists
v^{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{\delta^{\prime}})
such that (up to a subsequence)

v_{n}\to v^{0}\quad\mbox{in}~~C^{2}(B_{\delta^{\prime}}).

Note that
\tilde{f}_{n}
are Lipschitz continuous with a uniform Lipschitz constant (see
eq.

7.3
). In addition,

\tilde{f}_{n}(c_{n})=\lambda_{n}^{-1}f_{n}(c)=\lambda_{n}^{-1}c=c_{n}

and
c_{n}\to 0
. Hence, up to a subsequence, there exists
f^{0}\in C^{0,1}_{loc}[0,+\infty)
with
f^{0}(0)=0
such that

\tilde{f}_{n}\to f^{0}\quad\mbox{in}~~C_{loc}[0,+\infty).

Then
v^{0}
is a solution of

\kappa_{2}=f^{0}(\kappa_{1})\quad\mbox{in}~~B_{\delta^{\prime}}.

That is, the graph of
v^{0}
is a uniformly elliptic Weingarten surface of minimal type. Thus, its Gauss map is a quasiregular mapping (see
Section

2
). It is then straightforward to verify that all the properties (P1)–(P5) in
[
7
, pp. 1915–1916]
hold. The remaining part of the proof is the same as that of
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
and we arrive at a contradiction.

If
\Sigma
is of minimal type, i.e.,
f(0)=0
, we can obtain
eq.

7.1
by a standard scaling argument. ∎

As a consequence of the two preceding theorems, Theorem 6.2 in
[
7
]
can be generalized, with the same proof, to a more general setting:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a complete multigraph in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that satisfies an elliptic Weingarten equation
eq.

1.1
with
f\in C^{0,1}_{loc}[0,a)
,
f(0)=0
and

-\infty<\inf_{t\in[c,a^{\prime}]}f^{\prime}(t)\leq\sup_{t\in[c,a^{\prime}]}f^{\prime}(t)<0,~\forall~a^{\prime}<a.

Assume

(i)
a\in\mathbb{R}
and

f(t)\to-\infty\quad\mbox{as }~~t\to a.

or

(ii)
a=+\infty
and for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
,

f(t)\to b\quad\mbox{as }~~t\to+\infty.

Then
\Sigma
is a plane.

With the aid of the results above, we conclude that, in addition to not requiring any symmetry assumption on
f
, the main results, Theorems B and C in
[
7
, pp. 1889–1890]
, hold for functions
f\in C^{1}
, and Theorem D in
[
7
, p. 1890]
holds for
f\in C^{0,1}
.
