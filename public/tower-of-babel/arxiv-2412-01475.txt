Planar radial mean bodies are convex
J. Haddad

Abstract
. The radial mean bodies of parameter p > − 1 p>-1 of a convex body K ⊆ ℝ n K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} are radial sets introduced in [ 4 ] by Gardner and Zhang. They are known to be convex for p ≥ 0 p\geq 0 . We prove that if K ⊆ ℝ 2 K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} is a convex body, then its radial mean body of parameter p p is convex for every p ∈ ( − 1 , 0 ) p\in(-1,0) .



1.
Introduction

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body (a compact convex set with non-empty interior).
The radial mean body of
K
of parameter
p>0
is the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the norm defined by

(1)

\|v\|_{{R_{p}K}}=\left(\frac{1}{\left|K\right|_{n}}\int_{K}\varrho_{K}(x,v)^{p}dx\right)^{-1/p},v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
\left|K\right|_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional volume of
K
and
\varrho_{K}(x,v)
is the largest
\lambda>0
such that
x+\lambda v\in K
, this is, the radial function of
K
with respect to
x\in K
in the direction of
v
.

The family of bodies
{R_{p}K}
was introduced and studied by Gardner and Zhang in
[
4
]
where several important properties and inequalities were established. In particular, the results in
[
4
]
include the Zhang inequality on the polar projection body (see
[
9
]
) and the Rogers-Shephard inequality (see
[
6
]
).
Its close relation with the X ray transform, the covariogram function, the convolution bodies and Berwald-type inequalities on logarithmically concave functions makes
{R_{p}K}
a very interesting geometric object.
These subjects were studied further in
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
to cite just a few examples.

The definition of
{R_{p}K}
also makes sense in the range
p\in(-1,0)
, while for
p=0
it is defined via continuity (see
[
4
]
or Proposition
2
below).
Even without knowing if the right-hand side of (
1
) is a norm we still denote it by
\|x\|_{{R_{p}K}}
, and the set
{R_{p}K}
can be defined as its level set

{R_{p}K}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|_{{R_{p}K}}\leq 1\right\},\qquad p>-1

which is a star set with respect to the origin.

The non trivial fact that
{R_{p}K}
is convex was proven in
[
4
, Section 4]
for
p\geq 0
, by generalizing a previous result by K. Ball
[
3
]
, on logarithmically concave functions.
However, the proof breaks down if
p\in(-1,0)
, and the convexity of
{R_{p}K}
remains an open problem.
It seems that there is no hope of adapting the proof given in
[
4
]
to this range, and after more than two decades, little to no progress was made in this problem.
In this paper we prove the convexity of
{R_{p}K}
in the plane.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body, then
{R_{p}K}
is convex.

The proof will be divided in Sections
3
and
4
. In Section
3
we find an interesting formula for the norm (
1
), for a generic family of polygons
K
(Proposition
6
), and prove its convexity in a cone of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Proposition
6
is the core of our result. Then in Section
4
we extend the convexity to a finite set of non-overlapping cones covering the plane, and analyze the behaviour of the norm at the intersection of these cones.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper a
closed cone outside the origin
is a set of the form

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}:\langle x,v_{1}\rangle\geq 0\text{ and }\langle x,v_{2}\rangle\geq 0\}

where
v_{1},v_{2}
are non-zero vectors.
A closed cone outside the origin is a closed subset of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
An
open cone
is defined similarly with strict inequalities, and automatically does not contain the origin so we can avoid the term
outside the origin
.

A non-negative function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
homogeneous of degree
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
if
f(\lambda x)=|\lambda|^{\alpha}f(x)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
the line generated by
x
is denoted by
\langle x\rangle
, and its orthogonal complement, by
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}
.
For
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a convex body and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
X ray of
K
in the direction of
v
going through
y
is the segment
K\cap(y+\langle v\rangle)
. Its length is denoted by
X_{v}K(y)
.
We will use the following well known formula for
\|\cdot\|_{{R_{p}K}}
in terms of the X rays of
K
.

Proposition 2

(Theorem 2.2 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
K
be a convex body and
p>-1
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a unitary vector,

(2)

\|x\|_{{R_{p}K}}=\left((p+1)\left|K\right|_{n}\int_{\langle x\rangle^{\perp}}X_{x}K(y)^{1+p}dy\right)^{-1/p}.

Since we will prove Theorem
1
for polygons first, and then use approximation, we need to show that
{R_{p}K}
is continuous with respect to
K
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
K_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sequence of convex bodies converging to the convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the Hausdorff metric of compact sets.
Then for every
p>-1
,
\|\cdot\|_{R_{p}K_{m}}
converges pointwise to
\|\cdot\|_{{R_{p}K}}
.

Proof.

Fix
x
a unitary vector and
y\in\langle x\rangle^{\perp}
.
If
y+\langle x\rangle
does not intersect
K
, then by the compactness of
K
, the distance from
y+\langle x\rangle
to
K
is strictly positive, and
y+\langle x\rangle
is disjoint from
K_{m}
for sufficiently large
m
.
If
y+\langle x\rangle
intersects the interior of
K
, consider a point
p_{0}\in y+\langle x\rangle
in the interior of
K
.
For
m
large enough,
p_{0}
belongs to
K_{m}
.
The convergence in the Hausdorff metric of convex bodies then implies
\varrho_{K_{m}}(p_{0},\pm x)\to\varrho_{K}(p_{0},\pm x)
.
This means that
X_{x}K_{m}(y)\to X_{x}K(y)
provided that
y+\langle x\rangle
is not a support line of
K
.

Since the orthogonal projection of
K
onto
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}
has a relative boundary of zero
n-1
dimensional measure, we get that
X_{x}K_{m}
converges pointwise to
X_{x}K
in almost every point of
\langle x\rangle^{\perp}
.

The sets
K_{m},K
are uniformly bounded by the convergence and the compactness of
K
.
Then applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem we deduce that
\|x\|_{R_{p}K_{m}}\to\|x\|_{{R_{p}K}}
.
By the homogeneity of
\|\cdot\|_{R_{p}K}
, the same holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the theorem follows.
∎

In several situations it will be easier to show the convexity of sets and functions, locally.
We use the following result by Tietze on “local to global” convexity.

Lemma 4

(Satz 1 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed connected set.
Then
K
is convex if and only if for every
x\in K
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
B(x,\varepsilon)\cap K
is convex.

We say that
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
convex polygon
if it is a convex body (it must have non-empty interior) which is the convex hull of finitely many points
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
ordered in such a way that the segments
[q_{1},q_{2}],\ldots[q_{k-1},q_{k}]
and
[q_{k},q_{1}]
have disjoint relative interiors and their union is the boundary of
K
.
These segments are the
sides
of
K
.
The
vertices
of
K
are the points
q_{i}
, and we do not require them to be extremal points of
K
(three vertices can be aligned).

When no confusion arises, we will identify the sides of
K
with the corresponding vectors
q_{i}-q_{i+1}
.

A pair of
opposite parallel sides
is a pair of sides of
K
that are parallel and are not contained in the same line.

Let us denote by
L,R
(standing for left and right) the
2\times 2
matrices of
90
degrees rotations in the counter-clockwise and clockwise directions, respectively.
Clearly
R=-L=L^{-1}=L^{t}
and
\langle Rx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ly\rangle
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

From now on, until the end of the paper we will assume that
p\in(-1,0)
.

3.
Polygons generated by alternating vectors

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},x
be non-zero vectors in the plane.
We say that the vectors in
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
are
alternating
if
(-1)^{i+1}\langle Lz_{i+1},z_{i}\rangle>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,m-1
, and that they are
oriented
with
x
if
\langle z_{i},Rx\rangle>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
(see Figure
1
).
We say that a convex polygon
K
is
generated
by the set of vectors
Z
in the direction of

if
Z
is alternating and oriented with
x
, and the vertices of
K
are
p_{0}=0
and
p_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}z_{j}
for

(they are not ordered as the

before).
We simply say that
K
is generated by
Z
if it is generated by
Z
in some direction

.
Notice that two consecutive
z_{i}
cannot be parallel.

The purpose of this section is to compute explicitly
\|x\|_{R_{p}K}
in terms of
Z
.
The formula obtained will hold in a cone determined by

, and we will show that
\|\cdot\|_{R_{p}K}
is convex there.

Figure 1.
The convex polygon
K
generated by the vectors
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
.

The sides of the polygon are
z_{1},z_{m}
and

where

(3)

w_{i}=z_{i}+z_{i+1}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m-1.

With this notation the sides
w_{i}
with odd

are in one arc of

connecting
0

p_{m}
, while
z_{1}
and the sides

with even

can belong to either of the two arcs, depending on the parity of

.
Notice that

are ordered in such a way that they alternate between these two arcs (see Figure
1
).

For
i=1,\ldots,m-1
we define the vectors

(4)

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i+1}\langle Lw_{i},z_{i}\rangle^{-1}Lw_{i}

(5)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i+1}\langle Lz_{i+1},z_{i}\rangle^{-1}Lw_{i}

(6)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\langle Lw_{i},z_{i+1}\rangle^{-1}Lw_{i}

(7)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\langle Lz_{i},z_{i+1}\rangle^{-1}Lw_{i},

where the equalities hold thanks to the facts that
w_{i}=z_{i}+z_{i+1}
and
\langle Lw_{i},w_{i}\rangle=0
.
The vector
n_{i}
is orthogonal to
w_{i}
and satisfies
\langle n_{i},x\rangle>0
and
\langle n_{i},z_{i}\rangle(-1)^{i+1}=\langle n_{i},z_{i+1}\rangle(-1)^{i}=1
, as it can be seen from the definition and the fact that

is alternating.

Let

(8)

C_{Z}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}:\langle y,Lz_{i}\rangle\geq 0,\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m\},

and

(9)

C_{Z}^{\prime}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}:\langle y,n_{i}\rangle>0,\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m-1\},

then
C_{Z},C_{Z}^{\prime}
are two cones (closed and open, respectively) outside the origin, clearly containing the vector
x
.
The contention
C_{Z}\subset C_{Z}^{\prime}
is easy to prove. If
y\in C_{Z}
then
\langle y,Lw_{i}\rangle=\langle y,Lz_{i}\rangle+\langle y,Lz_{i+1}\rangle\geq 0
, with equality if and only if both terms are
0
, and this cannot happen because
z_{i},z_{i+1}
are not parallel and
y\neq 0
.
Clearly
Z
is oriented with every direction in the interior of
C_{Z}
.

Let
x\in C_{Z}
be a unit vector.
The line passing through any vertex
p_{i}
parallel to
x
,
1\leq i\leq m-1
, touches the side
w_{i}
on the opposite arc.
The distance between these two points is given by
X_{i}=\langle n_{i},x\rangle^{-1}
for every
i=1,\ldots,m-1
.
This is because
n_{i}
is perpendicular to
w_{i}
, and its norm is the inverse of the distance from
p_{i}
to the line containing the side
w_{i}
.
Thus the X ray in the direction of
v
between the points
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
is an affine function interpolating the values

X_{i}
and
X_{i+1}
. This is still true for
i=0
and
i=m-1
if we set
X_{0}=X_{m}=0
.

Recall that
K
has no pair of opposite parallel sides and
w_{i},w_{i+1}
belong to two different arcs, so
\{n_{i-1},n_{i}\}
is linearly independent for every
i=2,\ldots,m-1
(still,
n_{i}
and
n_{i+2}
are allowed to be parallel).
Define
a_{i},\tilde{a}_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for

(10)

\displaystyle Lz_{i}=a_{i}n_{i-1}+\tilde{a}_{i}n_{i}

(11)

\displaystyle Lz_{i+1}=b_{i}n_{i-1}+c_{i}n_{i}.

Proposition 5
.

For
i=2,\ldots,m-1
the following relations hold:

a)

\tilde{a}_{i}=-a_{i}

b)

b_{i}=-a_{i}

c)

c_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\langle Lz_{i+1},z_{i}\rangle+a_{i}

The first assertion, which is the fact that

For the second and third assertions use (

(12)

\displaystyle Lz_{i+1}

\displaystyle=Lw_{i}-Lz_{i}

(13)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i+1}\langle Lw_{i},z_{i}\rangle n_{i}-a_{i}n_{i-1}+a_{i}n_{i}

(14)

\displaystyle=((-1)^{i+1}\langle Lz_{i+1},z_{i}\rangle+a_{i})n_{i}-a_{i}n_{i-1}

which shows that

.
∎

(15)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=-a_{2}+\langle Lz_{2},z_{1}\rangle,

\displaystyle=b_{i+1}+c_{i},\text{ for }2\leq i\leq m-2

(17)

Proposition 6
.

Let
K
be a polygon with no pair of opposite parallel sides, generated by a set of alternating vectors
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
.
Then for every
x\in C_{Z}
and
p\in(-1,0)

(18)

\|x\|_{R_{p}K}=\left(\frac{p+1}{p+2}\left|K\right|_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\alpha_{i}\langle n_{i},x\rangle^{-p}\right)^{-1/p}

Moreover, the function

(19)

is well defined and
C^{\infty}
in

.

Proof.

The fact that
f_{Z}
is well defined and

C_{Z}^{\prime}
.

Since the expression on the right-hand side of (

By continuity of both sides of (

The X rays are parametrized by

For notational convenience we write

Formula (

The function

\displaystyle\int_{\langle p_{i},Rx\rangle}^{\langle p_{i+1},Rx\rangle}

Proposition 7
.

Let
K
be a polygon with no pair of opposite parallel sides, generated by a set of alternating vectors
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
.
Then there is
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
such that
\alpha_{i_{0}}>0
while
\alpha_{i}\leq 0
for every
i\neq i_{0}
.

Proof.

Here we will define
w_{0}=z_{1}
and
w_{m}=z_{m}
.
With this convention, the sides of
K
are exactly
w_{0},\ldots,w_{m}
.
The convexity of
K
implies the inequality

\langle w_{i+1},(-1)^{i}Lw_{i-1}\rangle\geq 0
for all
i=0,\ldots,m-1
.

By the convexity of
K
and the fact that
K
has no pair of opposite parallel sides, there exists exactly one index
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
for which
\langle w_{i},Lw_{i-1}\rangle
and
\langle w_{i},Lw_{i+1}\rangle
have different signs where these numbers are non-zero for every
i=1,\ldots,m-1
.
We claim that for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, the sign of
\alpha_{i}
is that of
-\langle w_{i},Lw_{i-1}\rangle\langle w_{i},Lw_{i+1}\rangle
, then the result follows.

For two linearly independent vectors

denote

the linear projection with image
\langle v\rangle
and kernel
\langle u\rangle
.
Then clearly
P_{u,v}(x)=v\frac{\langle x,u^{\perp}\rangle}{\langle v,u^{\perp}\rangle}
and
P_{u,v}(x)+P_{v,u}(x)=x
.

Let us prove the claim for
i=1
. By (
10
) and (
3
),

(39)

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{2}Rn_{1}-a_{2}Rn_{2}

(40)

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle=-w_{0}+w_{1}

is also convex.
To see this, take
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, and write

\|\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\|_{R_{p}K_{m}}\leq\lambda\|x\|_{R_{p}K_{m}}+(1-\lambda)\|y\|_{R_{p}K_{m}}.

Taking limits as
m\to\infty
one obtains the same inequality for
{R_{p}K}
, and the proof is now complete.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks

The restriction on the dimension provides a way of generating polytopes where it is possible to compute
f_{Z}
.
Proving an analog of Proposition
6
for higher dimensions, for example
n=3
, seems to be significantly more complicated but not impossible, although probably there are better ways to prove the convexity of
{R_{p}K}
.
For the moment this appears to be out of reach.

The special form of
f_{Z}
in Proposition
6
and the signs of the coefficients given in Proposition
7
show that there is a specific structure that makes
-p
-combinations of linear functions, convex in a cone.
It would be interesting to find general necessary and sufficient conditions on
n_{i},\alpha_{i}
that ensure that a function of the form (
18
) is convex.

A second interesting problem is to analyze the limit of formula (
18
) when the polygon approaches a smooth convex body
K
, and find an analog formula for a smooth convex body based on differential invariants of
K
(tangent plane, curvature, etc).
As can be observed from the proof of Proposition
7
, the formula for
f_{Z}
does not depend on
Z
but rather on
K
, since
\alpha_{i}=0
if
w_{i-1}
and
w_{i+1}
are parallel.

Lastly, we point out that there exists a similar problem regarding convex norms that seems to be related to our work. Consider the following invariant
p
-norm in the space of
n\times n
matrices.

\|A\|_{p}=\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}|A.v|^{p}d\sigma(v)\right)^{1/p},\quad A\in\operatorname{M}_{n,n}(\mathbb{R})

where
\sigma
is the invariant probability measure of the sphere and
|\cdot|
is the euclidean norm.
The norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
interpolates between the operator norm when
p\to\infty
and a multiple of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm when
p=2
.
Also, if
A
is non-singular and
p=-n
, it equals
|\det(A)|^{-1/n}
, while for
p\to-\infty
it recovers the smallest singular value.
The function
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is clearly convex for
p\geq 1
, since
A\mapsto|A.v|^{p}
is convex for every fixed
v\in S^{n-1}
, and thus the level set
\{A\in\operatorname{M}_{n,n}(\mathbb{R}):\int_{S^{n-1}}|A.v|^{p}d\sigma(v)\leq 1\}
is convex.

Since
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is unitarily invariant, it is determined by its restriction to the subspace of diagonal matrices. Identifying this subspace with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has

\|x\|_{p}=\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}v_{i})^{2}\right)^{p/2}d\sigma(v)\right)^{1/p}.

Interestingly enough, numerical simulations suggest that
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is also convex for
p\in(0,1)
.
The case
n=2
shows similarities with formula (
18
), but without negative coefficients.
