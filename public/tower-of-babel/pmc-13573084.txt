ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCancer patients may face elevated mortality not only from their malignancy but also from other factors including multi‐morbidity and the impact of treatment‐related complications. This study analyses mortality from all causes other than the index cancer among cancer patients compared to the general population.MethodsWe analysed EUROCARE‐6 data on 1,894,057 cancers diagnosed in 13 European countries and collected by 17 cancer registries with complete official cause‐of‐death information. Included were first‐primary head and neck (H&N), stomach, colorectal, lung, breast, cervical, endometrial, ovarian, prostatic, kidney, and bladder cancers diagnosed between 1998 and 2013 in patients aged 15–79, with follow‐up until 31st December 2014. We estimated the relative risk (RR) of death from all other causes for patients relative to population mortality rates. Cause‐specific survival (CSS) was compared to relative survival (RS) at 5 and 10 years.ResultsRR were > 2 for H&N and female lung cancers, between 1 and 2 for stomach, male lung, cervix, endometrial, ovary, kidney and bladder, and < 1 for prostate and female breast cancers. Relative risk declined with increasing age for almost all cancer types. Ten‐year CSS exceeded RS by 7%–11% points (pp) in H&N cancers, with smaller differences in bladder (2–6 pp), stomach, lung, cervix, ovary and kidney (2–3 pp). CSS was lower than RS in breast and prostate cancers.ConclusionDifferentiation between deaths due to the index cancer and to all other causes is crucial for patient follow‐up and to improve quality of life. Cause‐of‐death data should be a core component of population‐based cancer registries to inform interpretation of survivorship data.

Introduction

Cancer survivors are a vulnerable population, not only due to the risks associated with the diagnosed cancer (also referred as index cancers for this study), but also to the toxic and immunosuppressive effects of cancer treatments, genetic predisposition to other cancers and risk factors common to other diseases including cardiovascular, digestive or respiratory diseases [1, 2, 3]. Cancer survivors may therefore have, in addition to the specific mortality risk associated with their index cancer, a higher risk of dying from other diseases compared to the general population. Previous evidence on this issue mostly comes from cohort studies [4, 5] and population‐based analyses carried out at national level [6, 7, 8, 9]. No population‐based pan‐European study has examined, to the best of our knowledge, the distribution of causes of death (CoD) in cancer patients. This lack of information is probably due to mortality data not being routinely collected by all European Cancer Registries (CRs) and the lack of quality assurance and in‐depth investigation of this data for cancer patient's population cohorts. In addition, attributing CoD in cancer patients may be difficult when comorbidities are present.

However, disentangling the risk of death attributable to cancer and its progression from that due to other diseases is important for health care planning, tailoring clinical follow‐up and improving quality of life of cancer survivors [1]. It is also valuable when interpreting survival statistics [10]. Relative survival, a common method to compare survival between populations, is estimated in the majority of applications assuming an equal background mortality in cancer patients and in the general population. Testing of this assumption is therefore crucial for correct interpretation of relative survival estimates.

The EUROCARE‐6 study [11] has collected official CoD along with diagnostic and follow‐up data from participating CRs. The last collected database, covering diagnostic years up to 2013 and follow‐up to 2014, has better completeness of CoD information compared to previous datasets and allows a selection of a subset of participating CRs with high completeness. Our study presents results of CoD analysis in this European population‐based sample. We aimed to estimate the relative risk of death from all other causes (AOC) than the index cancer (and therefore including other non‐index cancers) in cancer patients and to compare cause‐specific survival (CSS) with relative survival (RS) on a population‐based setting. Analyses focused on the most common solid cancers, namely head and neck (H&N), stomach, colon and rectum, lung, breast, cervix, endometrium, ovary, prostate, kidney and bladder.

Materials and Methods

We analysed data on 1,894,057 first primary tumours diagnosed in patients aged 15–79, from 1998 to 2013 and followed‐up for vital status to 31/12/2014. Cancer cases were collected by 17 population‐based cancer registries from 13 European countries and provided to the EUROCARE‐6 database. These CRs had more than 89% of cases with complete CoD information: Austria, Cyprus, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Schleswig‐Holstein and Saarland (Germany), Ireland, Lithuania, Malta, Slovenia, Balearic Islands, Girona and Tarragona (Spain), Geneva (Switzerland) and Northern Ireland and Wales (UK) (Table S1). The threshold of 89% was chosen because it marks a gap in completeness distribution, with all other registries having 20% or more cases with missing CoD. The included registries are from all European geographical regions, had similar age and sex distribution compared to the EUROCARE‐6 pool (Table S2) and, apart from Germany, Spain and Switzerland, all included countries had 100% cancer registration coverage.

We selected malignant first primary tumours of head and neck (including larynx and nasal cavity but excluding lip: ICD‐O‐3 C01–C14, C30–C32), stomach (C16), colon and rectum (C18–C20), lung (C34), female breast (C50), cervix (C53), endometrium (C54), ovary (C56), prostate (C61), kidney (C64) and bladder (C67). The number of cases analysed are listed by registry and site in Table S1. Cases detected by autopsy and death certificates only, and those lost to follow‐up at diagnosis were excluded from survival analysis.

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the yearly number of expected deaths in each cohort of patients and follow‐up interval as: Expdi=ai−0.5*di−0.5*wi*1−Sei where a i is the number of patients alive at the start of the i‐th interval, d i , w i , and S ei are respectively the number of patients who had died, those that were withdrawn and the expected population survival during the same time interval.

We derived the number of yearly observed deaths from other causes Othd i from the patients' cancer‐specific life tables: Othdi=di−Candi where Cand i is the number of cancer specific deaths, detected using CoD provided by the CRs. These were defined, in terms of ICD‐10, as follows: C00–C14, C30–C32 for any head and neck subsite, C15–C26 for stomach, C18–C21 for colorectal, C33–C34, C39 for lung, C50 for breast, C53–C55 for cervix and endometrium, C56–C57 for ovary, C61 for prostate, C64–C68 for kidney and bladder cancers. We then calculated the ratio of ∑iOthdi/∑iExpdi for all follow‐up intervals and during the entire period (15 years) to estimate the relative risk (RR) of death from AOC, excluding the index cancer. 95% confidence interval (CI) was obtained using log‐normal transformation.

CSS was calculated considering as events deaths attributed to the specific cancer according to the previous definitions. RS was calculated dividing observed by expected survival, the latter estimated by Ederer 2 method [12] and using country‐year‐sex specific general population life tables. Finally, 15‐year cumulative mortality was estimated summing up, over the 15 follow‐up intervals, expected and observed interval‐specific mortality rates, by age class at diagnosis.

The design of the survival analysis, including cases diagnosed from 1998 to 2013 with follow‐up to the end of 2014, led to inflate the number of cases at risk of death in the first years of diagnosis. To evaluate its impact, we have carried out a sensitivity analysis by weighting the contribution of each interval of follow‐up in order to simulate the behaviour of a virtual cohort with a complete 15‐years observation. We re‐estimated the RRs after weighting the number of observed and expected deaths from AOC in each interval by the ratio between the initial number of cases multiplied by the observed survival up to the start of the interval and the actual number of cases alive at the beginning of the same interval.

We analysed pseudonymised data, collected from 17 population‐based cancer registries, not requiring informed consent. We get approval by the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Institute of Milan (INT 73/16; April 21, 2016). We hold these data in trust from each participating registry for the statistical analyses agreed on in the EUROCARE‐6 protocol, available at http://www.eurocare.it.

Results

A total of 1,894,017 cases were considered in the analyses (Table S1), representing 24% of all the selected cancers in the EUROCARE‐6 database during the same period. After removing DCO, autoptic, and cases censored alive with zero time of follow‐up, survival analysis was done on 1,856,805 patients. Their age and sex distributions were similar, and their RS at 5 and 10 years for the considered cancer sites was roughly comparable to those estimated from the whole EUROCARE‐6 Pool (Table S2). The largest 10‐yr RS differences with respect to the European Pool were found for cervical (−3.4 pp) and bladder cancers (+2.6 pp).

Figures 1 and 2 show, for males and females respectively, the ratio between the number of deaths attributed by death certificates to AOC and of those expected from the general population rates, with their 95% CI, for the patients diagnosed with the selected cancers. The corresponding numbers are reported in Tables S3 and S4. Relative risk of non‐cancer death compared to the general population greater than 2 was found for H&N (2.08 for males and 2.21 for females) and for female lung (2.60). RRs between 1 and 2 were found for stomach (1.34 and 1.49), male lung (1.85), cervix (1.63), ovary (1.60), kidney (1.11 and 1.31) and bladder (1.25 and 1.37). RR for colorectal and endometrial cancers was close to 1. RR lower than 1 was found for prostate (0.77) and female breast (0.89) cancers. With the exception of breast and prostate, all cancer sites showed a steady decreasing gradient of relative risk with increasing age groups (Figures 1 and 2). With the same exceptions, the age increase in 15‐years cumulative mortality after diagnosis was much less steep in cancer patients with respect to the one expected from general population rates (Table S5).

We analysed the pattern of deaths from AOC by time from diagnosis, with Figure 3 showing the RR estimated from 1 to 14 years of follow‐up. The last year was excluded because not all registries had complete follow‐up to 15 years. For most sites, RR showed a decreasing pattern as time from diagnosis increased, with very high values in the first years. RR remained high during all the investigated years for H&N cancer patients and tended to one for the other cancers, slowly for cervical, lung and bladder cancers and within 3–7 years for stomach, ovary and kidney cancers. Exceptions were colorectal, female breast and prostate cancers that presented approximately constant and < 1 RR of death from AOC during all the considered follow‐up period, with the exception of colorectal and endometrial cancers during the first year after diagnosis (RR = 1.69 and 1,27, respectively).

Tables 1 and 2 present 10‐year CSS compared to RS for males and females. Head and neck cancer patients showed the highest differences between the two estimates, with 10‐year CSS being 7%–11% points above RS for both sexes. Lower differences were observed for bladder cancer (2–6 pp), stomach, lung, cervix, endometrium, ovary and kidney (1–3 pp). Colorectal cancer CSS was higher than RS in the youngest but lower in the oldest age group (70–79 years). For most other cancers the difference was higher in the age groups 50–59 and 60–69 years compared to the youngest and oldest age groups. Finally, CSS was lower than RS in breast and prostate cancer patients, particularly for the 70–79 year groups (−4.1 and −11.3 pp., respectively).

Discussion

We analysed CoD reported in death certificates of cancer patients diagnosed with the most common tumour types with data from a number of population‐based cancer registries involved in the EUROCARE‐6 study. We found that patients diagnosed with head and neck, stomach, lung, cervix, endometrium, ovary, kidney, and bladder cancers had higher death rates from AOC compared to the general population of their country or region. Conversely, lower death rates from AOC were estimated for patients diagnosed with breast and prostate cancers. For almost all the cancers studied, the estimated RR of death was greater in patients diagnosed at a younger age and in the first years after index cancer diagnosis. We also observed sex‐related differences: females with cancer appear more fragile than the general female population, and this gap seems greater than the one observed among males.

Our results are consistent with findings from previous North American studies from the SEER database [13, 14] and Canadian registries [15]. These studies showed a higher RR for dying from all other diseases for patients diagnosed with H&N, stomach, lung, cervix, and kidney cancers. A broad agreement was also found [13, 14, 15] regarding the lower risk of death from AOC for prostate and breast cancer patients. This is partially attributed to a “healthier participant” effect in females accessing breast screening programmes and males seeking PSA testing. It should be mentioned that a 10%–35% overestimation of deaths attributed to prostate cancer in official mortality codes has been found in Estonia [16] and in several other countries (references [7, 8, 9, 10, 11] therein) after comparing them with clinical records.

Screening effect could partially explain our results on colorectal cancer patients, for whom we found RR very close to 1 (see Figures 1 and 2) and very similar 10‐year RS and CCS (Tables 1 and 2) as the study period includes the years where most of the countries involved started the screening process. However, previous literature findings for colorectal cancer are less consistent. Conclusions similar to our study were reported from the UK registry data [10], but Chen et al. [8] estimated standardised mortality ratio equal to 2 from US SEER data and found that non‐cancer and second independent cancers become the main causes of death among CRC patients. A study carried out on the same database found higher CSS relative to RS for older colorectal cancer patients only. Similar results were also obtained in a EUROCARE‐6 paper investigating this risk using model based estimation [17].

We found the highest RR for younger patients, diagnosed between 15 and 59 years in both males and females for most cancers. This may be due to their lower general mortality rates, to more aggressive treatments given to relatively young patients, and to the accelerated time to cancer occurrence, resulting in a younger age at onset, associated with high levels of cancer risk factors common with other diseases.

Cancer survival was higher in females compared to males for the majority of cancer sites, except bladder (see Tables 1 and 2). We observed a difference by sex: for all cancers, females had a higher than men RR for death from other diseases, particularly in the youngest age groups. Possible reasons might be found on lower baseline mortality rates and, perhaps, on different side effects of treatments, more effective than in men towards the diagnosed cancer. Further investigation on this issue could be carried out with more disease‐specific data on mortality from other causes.

With the exception of breast and prostate cancers, the RR of death from AOC was particularly high in the first year after cancer diagnosis, and decreased thereafter, very steeply for stomach, colorectal, and kidney and more gradually for the other cancers. This stronger effect appears in a period in which patients' health is intensively monitored. The high RR in the first 2 years after diagnosis perhaps indicates an early side effect of treatment, mainly infectious diseases due to chemotherapy‐induced immune suppression, even if we cannot exclude the exacerbation of comorbidity close to, or because of, diagnosis of index cancer. Also, a frailty effect can be envisaged, making patients more at risk of other diseases to die in the acute phase of cancer, and leaving the more healthy ones to survive in the subsequent years. Moreover, the incidence of second tumours included in our AOC estimation is higher in the first years due to intensive diagnostic testing, mainly among survivors of oral cavity and oropharyngeal cancers [18].

We found substantial differences between 10 year CSS and RS estimates, up to 10% points for H&N (positive) and prostate (negative) patients, with smaller but still important differences, about 2%–3% points, for other cancers considered.

Both indicators (CSS and RS) are routinely used to estimate the survival of patients to the specific risk of death from the cancer being considered. However, both have limitations. Defining exact cause of death for a patient with multiple comorbidities is not easy, as it may be unclear whether the patient has died from cancer, comorbidities or complications related to the cancer treatment. Also, there is often a tendency in many countries to prioritise cancer as the underlying CoD, particularly when automatic algorithms, such as ACME (Automated Classification of Medical Entities), are used to code primary cause from the patients' death records. This could lead to possibly underestimating mortality from AOC.

Relative survival estimates, expressing excess mortality of cancer patients compared to the general population to which they belong, commonly assume expected mortality in the absence of cancer equal to the mortality observed in the general population. If the relative risk of death from AOC is different than one, RS with the general population as a reference gives biased estimates, and use of relative‐risk‐adjusted life tables is recommended. Despite its limitations, RS remains an easy to calculate and interpret survival indicator, comparable between international cohorts of patients with different background mortality, and there are no current, robust alternatives for comparative studies on survival between populations and time periods. An alternative approach [19], developed by some of the authors of this paper, considers cure models to estimate cure fraction including specific parameters to account for excess mortality of cancer patients from causes other than their primary cancer without knowledge of the cause of death.

The main strength of this study is that it reports, for the first time, an analysis of cancer patient mortality for other causes or other cancers from a large population‐based sample, including 13 countries with high CoD completeness from across European regions. For the first time it analyses several solid cancers across Europe, allowing results to be compared with similar international studies, such as those based on the SEER database.

As a pioneer pan‐European and population‐based study using CoD information, this work had to face several limitations, all important for the interpretation of results. First, the study analysed officially coded CoD and did not have access to other clinical information which may have been used to assess possible misclassification. Cause of death is usually not directly coded by population‐based cancer registries, who use the official CoD of the relevant national statistical office, whose accuracy and reliability is not always well known even if, following internationally agreed rules, they assure a high level of standardisation. This is a problem shared with several similar studies based on population‐based routinely collected data and which can hopefully be addressed in future by expanding the use of CoD information in cancer registry data [20]. Accurately determining the cause of death in cancer patients presents challenges. Misclassification can occur, especially when cancer has metastasized. Reporting practices can vary depending on regional healthcare systems, classification standards and the diligence of medical professionals. Cancer patients can have multiple health conditions, making it difficult to identify a single, primary cause of death. Changes in how causes of death are recorded over time can complicate long‐term data consistency.

This study is based on a subset of EUROCARE‐6 cancer registries with good completeness of CoD information. The 17 registries included were from all the European areas, had similar age and sex distributions, and relative survival not far from that of the entire EUROCARE‐6 pool. However, this was not a random sample, and in particular, the UK and Ireland and Southern Europe were under‐represented. Given the heterogeneity in economy, welfare, and health care between and within countries, those presented here cannot be considered as statistically representative estimates of cancer‐specific survival in the whole European population.

Another limitation is the period of incidence (1998–2013) we studied. Using the EUROCARE‐6 data we did not capture changes in cancer treatments (i.e., targeted therapies, immunotherapies and others), healthcare organisation and major public health events, as for example the COVID‐19 pandemic, that occurred after 2013 and could have had an impact on the causes of death [20]. Actually, also WHO introduced specific guidelines for certification and ICD coding of COVID‐19 deaths in 2020, which have affected the attribution of the underlying cause of death in individuals with severe comorbidities, including cancer [21, 22]. Having more up‐to‐date data would certainly be preferable, but these will be an important benchmark for comparison when more recent data will become available.

We analysed all the cases diagnosed and treated from 1998 up to 2013, irrespective of the duration of follow‐up. A classical cohort design, limiting the analysis only to cases with the same length of potential follow‐up, would have considered either obsolete clinical approaches or too short duration of the follow‐up. The disadvantage is to have inflated the number of cases at risk of death in the first years since diagnosis, those with the higher proportion of Observed/Expected deaths from AOC. The results of the sensitivity analysis (Table S6) show that such overweighting of the first years of follow‐up had a minor impact on the estimated RR, to an extent that does not change neither their direction nor the conclusions of the analysis.

A further limitation is the lack of information on individual‐level risk factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and socioeconomic status. These factors are known to influence both cancer outcomes and non‐cancer mortality and may contribute to some of the differences observed across cancer sites and populations. Data on their distribution in the patients and in the general population would allow us to disentangle their effect from that of treatments, but this unfortunately is not possible in the present population‐based data situation.

Finally, this first study did not analyse the specific detail of non‐cancer causes of death. Estimating RR of death for, say, cardiovascular diseases or other specific cancers requires constructing cause‐specific mortality rates by sex, calendar year and age for the general population in the considered countries and regions. This is outside the scope of the present study and remains a possible task for future research.

Conclusions

Cause‐specific survival is a targeted, informative and clinically relevant metric that helps understanding of disease impact, treatment efficacy and long‐term outcomes. Separating survival based on deaths due to the index cancer and/or its progression from those attributable to AOC, including other independent cancers, is vital to inform cancer patient follow‐up and quality of life. Furthermore, CoD is particularly valuable in estimating real‐world statistics, as crude probabilities of death and years of life lost from cancer vs. AOC. Collection, validation, and analysis of cancer patients' cause of death should be integrated into the practice of population‐based cancer registries.
