Over-the-counter but not harmless: Paracetamol abuse in a benzodiazepine user
Authors: Ritwick Tripathi, Rashmi Shukla, Nalin Shukla
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is a widely used over-the-counter analgesic and antipyretic for mild-to-moderate pain and fever, exerting its effect mainly through central inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis in the hypothalamus, with minimal peripheral anti-inflammatory action.[1] Literature regarding PCM dependence is sparse, with no established mechanisms that can lead to physical dependence. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case from India with this presentation.

A 29-year-old male presented with benzodiazepine use for the past 4 years, currently taking up to 10 tablets of alprazolam 0.5 mg/day. This use was associated with an inability to quit due to withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety, lethargy, impaired concentration, and sleep disturbances. Patient was being managed on an outpatient basis with tab chlordiazepoxide. In a follow-up consultation, the patient reported that for the past 4 months, he had been taking around 3–4 tablets/day of paracetamol 650 mg for epigastric pain, initially advised by a local chemist. He noticed that the discomfort subsided rapidly after each dose. What started as one tablet/day gradually increased to 3–4 tablets (1950 to 2600 mg/day) over a period of three months. Extensive blood investigations, including liver function and kidney function tests, as well as abdominal imaging, were unremarkable. The pain recurred 4–5 hours after the last dose and was relieved within minutes of taking Paracetamol (PCM), but not by antacids, antispasmodics, or other analgesics. The patient had taken up always carrying a few tablets of PCM 650 mg in his pockets. At times, the patient would wake up at night and ask his family members to get him the tablet as he was experiencing anxiety and severe pain in his abdomen. The patient also reported feeling more alert and being able to better manage his daily schedule after taking PCM. Any attempts made by the patient’s family members to dissuade him from consuming or carrying the medication were met with resistance from the patient. A detailed evaluation was conducted to explore possible underlying somatoform or anxiety disorders however the patient did not meet the diagnostic criteria for either. The patient was psychoeducated regarding the possibility psychological reliance on paracetamol and the possible long term side effects of regular use. In follow-up visits the patient was gradually able to taper down his paracetamol use to 500 mg every alternate day over a period of 6 weeks.

There is no strong evidence supporting a euphoric effect of PCM, and it lacks dopaminergic reward system activation typically associated with substances that produce euphoria. PCM exerts central effects by acting on: Cannabinoid (CB1) receptors via its metabolite AM404, which inhibits anandamide reuptake, potentially increasing endocannabinoid activity; Serotonergic pathways, which may influence mood; Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and Cyclooxegenase (COX) inhibition in the central nervous system (CNS) involved in pain and temperature regulation.[2] Studies have shown that PCM can help dampen emotional response,[3] and there are case reports highlighting that psychological reliance resembling dependence on paracetamol can occur.[4567] Such abuse can go unnoticed and underreported in the general population because of no overt signs of intoxication or use.

This case underscores the need for awareness of atypical substance abuse involving widely available over-the-counter medications especially among population with psychiatric comorbidities. Further studies are needed on the topic to explore the possibility of Paracetamol dependence and to inform the formulation of regulation and specific management strategies in such cases.
