Early deep response to cilta-cel as 2nd CAR T-Cell therapy after CELMoD-based bridging for advanced multiple myeloma
Authors: Henrik Niklas, Anna Bold, Simon Völkl, Nicola Lang, Marietta Truger, Knut Wendelin, Susanne Strifler, Johannes Gärtner, Stefan Knop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has emerged as an innovative and highly effective treatment option for patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). This effect is based on redirecting modified T-cells towards plasma cells expressing target antigens, specifically B cell maturation antigen (BCMA). Despite high initial response rates, disease relapse remains a major clinical challenge. Therapeutic options after progression following prior BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy are limited, and prognosis is often poor. We report on a 58-year-old male with high-risk IgA kappa multiple myeloma who was heavily pretreated and received idecabtagene vicleucel as sixth-line therapy, achieving a deep response lasting for approximately one year. Following disease progression, the patient received bridging therapy with mezigdomide,carfilzomib, and dexamethasone, followed by a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell infusion using ciltacabtagene autoleucel. Previous whole genome sequencing had confirmed preserved BCMA expression.Early post-infusion assessment showed a rapid and profound response, with marked declines in serum IgA and free kappa light chains, accompanied by robust CAR T-cell expansion. Treatment-related toxicities were manageable, including grade II cytokine release syndrome and prolonged cytopenias, without evidence of neurotoxicity.This case provides additional knowledge that retreatment with BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy may be feasible and clinically effective in heavily pretreated multiple myeloma, particularly after a durable response to initial CAR T-cell therapy. Bridging therapy and the use of an alternative CAR T-cell construct may further improve outcomes. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal patient selection and treatment sequencing in this setting.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma is a clonal plasma cell malignancy that, despite substantial therapeutic progress with proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory agents, monoclonal antibodies, and autologous stem cell transplantation, remains incurable [1, 2]. High-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, including t(4;14), del(17p), and 1q21 gain, are associated with early relapse and poor outcomes under conventional treatment [3].

CAR T-cell therapy targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) has emerged as a highly effective option for heavily pretreated patients. Two constructs are currently approved: idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel), based on a murine-derived scFv, and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel), which uses two llama-derived single-domain antibodies against distinct BCMA epitopes [4, 5]. Despite high initial response rates, most patients eventually relapse, and therapeutic options thereafter remain limited.

Retreatment with a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell infusion is emerging as one such option, particularly after a durable response to the first infusion, though data remain scarce [6, 7]. Cereblon E3 ligase modulators such as mezigdomide represent a further option in this setting, with response rates of 30–50% reported in heavily pretreated patients in early-phase trials [8, 9]. Here, we report a case of successful retreatment with cilta-cel following mezigdomide-based bridging therapy, highlighting the role of target antigen preservation and CAR T-cell expansion as key factors influencing therapeutic efficacy.

Case presentation

Our male patient was diagnosed with ISS stage II IgA kappa multiple myeloma in August 2016 at age 58, with a history of prostate carcinoma (pT2c, pN0) cured by radical prostatectomy in 2015.

Laboratory assessment revealed an M-protein of 42 g/L, anemia (Hb 10.5 g/dL), and normal renal function. Bone marrow biopsy showed 60% clonal plasma cells (CD38⁺, CD138⁺, CD19⁻, CD45low, CD56⁺, kappa-restricted). Cytogenetics revealed del(13q) and t(4;14), consistent with high-risk disease [3]. The patient received VCD induction, HDM/ASCT, and lenalidomide maintenance, followed after serological relapse in 2019 by bortezomib monotherapy and DVd. Subsequent regimens included KRd (discontinued due to COVID-19 infection with progression) and IsaPd, achieving stable disease.

In October 2022, the patient received lymphodepletion followed by ide-cel, complicated by grade I CRS and prolonged cytopenia requiring intermittent G-CSF. Referred to our center in January 2023, he had negative serum immunofixation with unconfirmed CAR T-cell persistence. By April 2023, early polyclonal immune reconstitution was accompanied by severe T-cell depletion (CD4⁺ 105/µL). Low-level serological relapse became detectable in November 2023; PET/CT in January 2024 showed stable osteolytic lesions without metabolic activity. In April 2024, carfilzomib monotherapy was given within the “CAR-CAR program”, aiming to enhance CAR T-cell recognition via increased BCMA expression [10], resulting in stable disease.

By July 2024, disease activity was increasing. WGS and FISH (March 2024) confirmed presence of a clonal population with 1q gain, t(4;14), 13q deletion, and a KRAS missense mutation; BCMA (TNFRSF17) remained unaltered with normal expression, and IGH::NSD2 fusion with NSD2 overexpression was confirmed. The patient was enrolled in an early-phase trial of an experimental antibody-drug conjugate in August 2024. Baseline bone marrow showed 70% plasma cell infiltration with persistent high-risk cytogenetics. He achieved minor remission for two cycles but was withdrawn after cycle 4 due to progression and ≥grade 3 neutropenia/pneumonia; immunohistochemistry at that time confirmed 80% plasma cell infiltration with strong BCMA expression despite prior ide-cel exposure.

In January 2025, mezigdomide/carfilzomib/dexamethasone became available and was initiated as bridging therapy to reduce tumor burden ahead of a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell infusion. Despite treatment interruptions from viral infections and grade 3 neutropenia, the patient achieved a partial response, underscoring the efficacy of mezigdomide-based bridging in this heavily pretreated, high-risk setting.

Following apheresis in July 2025 and continued Kd/mezigdomide bridging until November 2025, bone marrow still showed 50–70% plasma cell infiltration despite serological partial remission; WGS/FISH again confirmed high-risk features with preserved BCMA. PET/CT showed only low metabolic activity in known lesions without extramedullary disease.

After fludarabine/cyclophosphamide lymphodepletion, the patient received cilta-cel in December 2025 (baseline IgA 1,529 mg/dL, free kappa light chains 1,103 mg/L), with robust CAR T-cell expansion confirmed on days + 7 and + 14. An early response followed: by day + 3, a VGPR per IMWG criteria had been achieved, and by April 2026, immunofixation was negative. CAR⁺ T-cell s increased from 3.73% (0.9 cells/µL) at day + 7 to 31.72% (187.1 cells/µL) at day + 14, becoming undetectable (< 0.05%) by day + 57. Toxicity was limited to grade II CRS and prolonged cytopenias, without neurotoxicity. Preserved BCMA expression supported the biological rationale for retreatment.

More than 6 months after the second CAR T-cell therapy, the patient shows no serological evidence of disease and polyclonal plasma cell reconstitution — highlighting the efficacy and potential of repeat BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy in carefully selected patients.

Discussion

Rationale for second CAR T-cell therapy

Relapse of multiple myeloma remains a major challenge after BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy, and treatment options thereafter are limited. Retreatment with a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell product is considered to carry additional risks, including antigen loss, T-cell exhaustion, and cumulative toxicity, with supporting data remaining limited [11, 12]. However, a growing body of evidence indicates that retreatment can still achieve meaningful disease control in selected patients, particularly if the initial CAR T-cell therapy resulted in a response lasting more than six months [12–14]. This criterion was fulfilled in our patient, who achieved a deep and durable response of approximately one year to idecabtagene vicleucel, supporting the rationale for a second infusion with a different product in light of preserved organ function and good overall condition (ECOG 1). A brief attempt to reactivate the persisting ide-cel product via proteasome inhibition (CAR-CAR approach) achieved only stable disease, likely reflecting limited persistence of the initial CAR T-cell product [10]. Based on this rationale and published precedents (Table 2), the decision was made to proceed with cilta-cel as a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell infusion.

Preserved BCMA expression supported the choice of a structurally distinct second product

Ide-cel and cilta-cel differ primarily in their antigen-binding domains: cilta-cel incorporates two BCMA-binding single-domain antibodies, potentially enhancing tumor recognition even with heterogeneous BCMA expression [6]. High-risk cytogenetic features (t(4;14), 1q21 gain) were present at diagnosis [3], yet comprehensive molecular analysis before the second CAR T-cell infusion revealed no genomic alterations in the BCMA-encoding gene (TNFRSF17) and preserved TNFRSF17 expression at RNA level, comparable to CAR T-cell-naïve patients, and confirmed by strong BCMA expression on immunohistochemistry [15, 16]. These findings excluded antigen loss as a potential mechanism of resistance, consistent with reports that preserved BCMA expression is a prerequisite for successful CAR T-cell therapy even in high-risk disease [16–18]. Tumor heterogeneity and the incompletely understood mechanisms underlying BCMA loss mean that confirmed target antigen integrity, while reassuring, cannot yet reliably predict response to a second BCMA-directed CAR T-cell infusion [15, 17]. This is illustrated by preliminary data from Attar et al., who reported outcomes in a cohort of nine patients receiving cilta-cel after prior BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy, with responses in 7/9 patients despite variable antigen status, providing additional albeit still limited evidence in this setting [19]. A structurally distinct second product may additionally help overcome resistance mechanisms such as pre-existing anti-CAR immunity, although clinical evidence for this remains limited [20]. Consistent with this rationale, cilta-cel has been associated with deeper and more durable responses than ide-cel in heavily pretreated patients, albeit potentially at the cost of increased toxicity [6, 21].

Safety of repeat CAR T-cell therapy

In our patient, both CAR T-cell treatments were complicated by low-grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and prolonged cytopenias consistent with prior reports in heavily pretreated patient populations [22, 23].

A key factor enabling repeat CAR T-cell therapy in this case was the preservation of organ function throughout the disease course. This allowed for the safe administration of repeat lymphodepleting chemotherapy followed by CAR T-cell infusion. These observations underscore the critical importance of careful patient selection when considering CAR T-cell retreatment.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that repeat BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy, prepared for by CELMoD-based bridging, a structurally distinct second product, and molecular confirmation of BCMA integrity, can achieve meaningful disease control even in heavily pretreated, high-risk multiple myeloma.
