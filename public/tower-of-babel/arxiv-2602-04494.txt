Abstract
This work contributes to a foundational question in economic theory: how do individual-level cognitive biases interact with collective-choice mechanisms? We study a setting where voters hold intrinsic preference rankings over a set of alternatives but cast approval ballots to determine the collective outcome. The ballots are shaped by an anchoring bias: alternatives are presented sequentially by a social planner, and a voter approves an alternative if and only if it is acceptable and strictly preferred to all alternatives previously encountered. We first analyze which approval-based voting rules are anchor-proof, in the sense that they always select the same winner regardless of the presentation order. We show that this requirement is extremely demanding: only very restrictive rules satisfy it. We then turn to the potential influence of the social planner. On the upside, when the planner has no information about the voters’ intrinsic preferences, she cannot manipulate the outcome.

Sequential presentation of the alternatives is common both in institutional contexts of collective decision-making and in digital-choice architectures, where ordering is an implicit design feature.

In modern online platforms that rely on recommender systems, the ordering presented to users is personalized. On the other hand, electoral contexts usually present alternatives in a fixed order for all voters.
In such environments, anchoring can alter whether a voter approves an alternative by setting the reference point against which new alternatives are evaluated. Consequently, the induced approval ballots may reflect not only voters’ underlying preferences but also the given presentation order. This raises questions about the robustness of standard voting rules and the extent to which social planners can influence outcomes through choice architecture.

Our Contribution.

We study a framework of approval voting, where voters possess intrinsic ordinal preferences over a finite set of alternatives and also hold an acceptability relation over the alternatives. A key feature of our model is that approval ballots are generated from preferences via a simple
anchoring
mechanism: a social planner specifies the order in which a voter encounters the alternatives, and the voter approves an alternative if and only if it is acceptable and strictly preferred to all previously encountered alternatives. Hence, the presentation order determines the mapping from preferences to approval ballots.

Our analysis addresses two central questions.
First, we study the existence of approval-based voting rules that are robust to the anchoring bias, called
anchor-proof
, meaning that the set of selected winners is invariant with respect to the presentation order.
Such rules guarantee that collective outcomes faithfully represent the voters’ underlying preferences, and are immune to the behavior of a social planner that may be driven by personal interests. We find that every non-constant rule violates anchor-proofness in its most demanding form; yet, there do exist reasonable rules that satisfy anchor-proofness for restricted preference structures or particular presentation orders.
Second, for non-robust rules, we study the strategic role of the social planner in more depth. When the planner has her own preference over outcomes, we examine whether she can exploit the presentation order to induce a favorable collective decision.
Our results bring to light cases in which such manipulation is possible and specify how it depends on the rules and the planner’s available information. Notably, we show that although the planner is often able to manipulate even with limited information about the voters’ preferences, complete lack of such information prohibits this endeavor.

Related Literature.

A well-developed strand of the literature on social choice theory analyzes the relationship between voters’ underlying (ordinal) preferences and the approval ballots they cast. The distinction between a voter’s preference and her ballot, as well as the incentives that result to the latter, has been made explicit, e.g., by
14
.
Similarly to the seminal work on approval voting by
10
, much of this literature assumes that a sincere voter with an intrinsic linear preference will form a so-called
consistent
approval ballot where all approved alternatives are preferred over all disapproved ones
(
3
;
15
)
. In particular, this consistency assumption is central on results that compare outcomes of approval-based rules to outcomes of ranking-based rules
(
18
;
27
;
28
)
.
33
perform this comparison exercise from a dual perspective, exploring how to generate ranking ballots from approval preferences, again assuming that approved alternatives are always preferred over disapproved ones.

34
examines related settings where voters face costs of accessing their full intrinsic preferences, and the order in which they encounter the alternatives determines the subset over which the ballots are formed. The input of the aggregation rules in this work consists of partial orders rather than approval ballots, and the focus is on voters’ capacity limits.

Importantly, the behavioral construction proposed in our paper highlights a distinct source of discrepancy between preferences and ballots that is neither strategic (incentive-driven) nor purely capacity-driven.
Moreover, our approach generates approval ballots from underlying linear preferences but does not, in general, satisfy the common consistency requirement of prior models of the literature. Indeed, in the presence of anchoring, a voter may approve an alternative
y
and later reject an alternative
x
even though she prefers

x
to
y
(because for instance, she may encounter
y
early on but then, before seeing
x
, she may encounter a more preferred alternative
z
).

Empirical and experimental evidence support the plausibility of ordering effects in large-scale choice environments, suggesting that early options are more likely to be selected. This resonates with our framework: early options may seem satisfactory precisely because they have not yet been compared against potentially better alternatives.
Eye-tracking experiments demonstrate that users disproportionately trust and select results that appear early in their Google search, even when those results are objectively less relevant
(
25
)
. Research on product listings (notably a randomized variation in Expedia’s rankings) also indicates that higher-ranked products attract significantly more consumer attention and clicks
(
37
)
. These findings motivate modeling presentation order as a potentially designer-controlled feature that impacts expressed choices.

At first glance, our sequential anchoring mechanism may seem structurally related to optimal-stopping selection settings, classically embodied by the secretary problem. The secretary problem, tackled within a long theoretical literature, studies single-agent sequential choice when options arrive in random order and decisions are irrevocable
(
17
, for an overview, see)
.
Our model differs in three key respects:
(i)
voters are influenced by the anchoring bias when forming their ballots, rather than trying to optimize an objective over the sequence of presented alternatives;
(ii)
the presentation order does not need to be random and may be set by a planner who has incentives over the outcome; and
(iii)
our focus is on the collective consequences given a group that engages in approval voting rather than on individual decision making.

Our mechanism is also related to the literature on agenda manipulation, originating by
16
and
7
, which studies how the order of binary comparisons can affect the outcome of collective decision-making processes.
Under complete information,
24
and
1
characterize the set of alternatives that an agenda-setter can induce a committee to select, with subsequent extensions by
2
and
30
.
More recently, settings with incomplete information have been examined by
22
and
19
.
Our framework differs from this line of work along several dimensions. First, the sequence in which alternatives are presented may vary across voters.
Second, voters are shown individual alternatives sequentially rather than engaging in a sequence of pairwise comparisons. Third, voting takes place only after all approval ballots have been generated, rather than at each stage of the process.

Finally, there is growing interest in the new area of
behavioral social choice
, which examines how cognitive constraints shape individual preferences and consequently collective outcomes.
26
argue that standard models of social choice presuppose stable and context-independent evaluations, whereas empirical evidence consistently exhibits context sensitivity and framing effects. They call for theoretical frameworks that incorporate behavioral regularities.
8
shows that even preferences over aggregation procedures are malleable. Using controlled experimental settings of judgment aggregation, he demonstrates that a simple positive or negative framing of the judgment at hand can reverse individuals’ procedural preferences, which can in turn shift the collective outcome.
4
discuss how voting behavior depends on the particular electoral system in the context of French presidential elections.
6
provide complementary field evidence using a natural experiment in California ballot initiatives. They show that framing effects meaningfully alter electoral support for low-salience propositions, while vigorous campaign activity substantially attenuates these effects.

Our main question is thus phrased as follows:

How does the order in which voters examine alternatives influence the outcome of a voting rule?

Intuitively, we examine how the voters’ anchoring bias might influence the outcome, as different approval ballots can be elicited from the same preference, and if this can be exploited by a social planner.
In some extreme cases, the formation of an approval ballot is not that influenced by the presentation order of the alternatives.

\mu(A)=\{\mu(x)\mid x\in A\}

The next three axioms state different degrees of consensus that a voting rule should satisfy.

Definition 4
.

We say that

satisfies

if for every approval profile
\boldsymbol{A}
such that
U=\{x\in X:x\in A_{i}\text{ for all }i\in N\}\neq\emptyset

.

Definition 5
.

We say that
F_{a}
satisfies

if for every approval profile
\boldsymbol{A}
where
A_{i}=X
for all

, it holds that
F_{a}(\boldsymbol{A})=X
.

Definition 6
.

We say that
F_{a}
satisfies

if for every approval profile
\boldsymbol{A}
such that
U=\{x\in X:x\in A_{i}\text{ for all }i\in N\}\neq\emptyset

.

Note that weak unanimity is a weaker axiom than the Pareto condition proposed by
12

Standard approval voting rule (SAV) satisfies unanimity and thus also satisfies weak and total unanimity.
The nomination rule (Nom) satisfies total unanimity but violates weak unanimity.

3
Anchor-proofness

This section investigates questions of
anchor-proofness
—specifically, conditions under which the outcome of a voting rule remains invariant to the presentation order of the alternatives.
Our findings can be organized around six sub-questions (summarized in Figure
1
).
Fixing a voting rule, we ask:

1.

Are the approval winners independent of the presentation order for all ranking profiles?

2.

Does there exist a ranking profile for which the approval winners are independent of the presentation order?

3.

Does there exist a pair of distinct presentation orders that produce the same approval winners for an arbitrary ranking profile?

4.

Given an arbitrary ranking profile, does there exist a pair of different presentation orders that produce the same approval winners?

5.

Given an arbitrary pair of presentation orders, does there exist a ranking profile for which the approval winners coincide?

6.

Does there exist some ranking profile and some pair of distinct presentation orders for which the approval winners coincide?

Together, these six questions form a complete landscape for assessing qualitative aspects of anchor-proofness: any meaningful inquiry into whether a profile’s winners depend on the presentation order can be stated as one of them.
Clearly, the first question is the strongest and most demanding (and indeed has a negative answer for all non-constant rules), while the last question is the weakest and has a trivial positive answer for all rules.
The intermediate questions (2–5) are not logically related.
Note also that questions (4-6) are included mostly for conceptual completeness and are of limited practical interest, since they require information about a concrete ranking profile (resp. a presentation order) to construct a suitable presentation order (resp. ranking profile).
The topic of information needed to influence the voting outcome will be revisited in Section
4
.

3.1
Anchor-Proofness for All Profiles

We start the presentation of our main results with Theorem
1
, showing that anchor-proofness is impossible for non-constant rules within the restricted domain of tolerant profiles. Using the intermediate Lemma
1
, we will then extend this impossibility to the general domain of all preference-approval profiles in Theorem
2

Note that a voting rule that is constant for all tolerant profiles is not necessarily constant for all, possibly non-tolerant, profiles, while the other direction holds. This means that Theorem
1

and
\boldsymbol{q}

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}

3.2
Existence of an Anchor-Proof Profile

Having addressed the first question in our list about anchor-proofness in the general domain of preference-approvals, we move on to the second question that searches, more conservatively, the existence of
some
profile for which anchor-proofness is achievable.
By the second point of Remark
2

Theorem

shows that this ceases to be possible if we look only within the domain of tolerant profiles and apply a rule satisfying the reasonable axioms of weak and total unanimity.
However, the impossibility for the tolerant domain is not generalizable to arbitrary voting rules: Example

illustrates that there exist non-neutral rules that are anchor-proof for tolerant profiles.

3.3
Order Pairs Preserving Outcomes for All Profiles

We continue with the third question in our list, specifying the existence of a pair of orders producing the same winners for all profiles. This is clearly a weaker requirement than anchor-proofness, which spans across all orders.
Proposition

(utilizing Lemma

Lemma 2

Consider two orders
\sigma_{i}

3.4

Lastly, we examine whether for any pair of orders there exists a profile that produces the same winners for both orders.
The answer is trivial if we allow for any profile: the outcomes of intolerant profiles are invariant for all orders (recall Remark

However, if we consider a voting rule satisfying unanimity, the answer becomes negative when we only allow for tolerant profiles (Proposition

This section speaks about scenarios where the presentation orders are fixed and limited, for instance within electoral contexts that are supposed to exhibit the alternatives to voters in a pre-determined way. We ask whether there are specific preference structures that would induce robust outcomes for at least two different fixed presentation orders within groups of tolerant voters, thus restricting the potential influence of the social planner. We find that this — rather weak — requirement is not satisfied as long as the voting rule adheres to unanimity. However, there do exist situations where the social planner has no influence over the outcome. One of them concerns the tolerance of voters: In particular, consider scenarios where projects in participatory budgeting are extreme, in the sense that each project clearly benefits one part of the society while harming all the rest; then, voters can be assumed to accept only those projects from which they benefit and to reject all others, yielding an intolerant preference profile in which the outcome is independent of the presentation order.
The other situation that sets constraints on the influence of the social planner does not necessarily demand intolerant voters, but employs a voting rule that is only meaningful in situations of indirect intolerance: unless each voter only approves a single alternative where simple SAV may be applied, that rule returns a tie between all alternatives and is thus completely undecisive.

4
Strategies for the Social Planner

Previously, we studied anchor-proofness as a robustness property of voting rules with respect to the order in which alternatives are presented to voters. We now delve more deeply into this notion from a strategic perspective.

We consider that a social planner may have her own preferred outcome of a voting process. Then, choosing a presentation order is a strategic decision aimed at influencing that outcome. In this angle, a preference-approval profile is anchor-proof if and only if it is immune to manipulation by any planner: no matter which order is chosen, the resulting outcome remains unchanged. We can thus view anchor-proofness as a benchmark for worst-case robustness against strategic manipulation by the planner. This equivalence specifically assumes that the planner has complete access to voters’ preferences, and is thus able to tailor individual presentation orders. This underlies our characterizations of anchor-proof profiles under
SAV
and
Nom
(Theorems
4
and
5
), as well as our general result for all weakly unanimous rules (Theorem
6
).

We then relax the assumption of full information and study precisely how the planner’s ability to manipulate the outcome depends on the information she has on the preferences of the voters. We first show that, under zero information, manipulation is impossible for any rule satisfying weak and total unanimity, SAV being one of them (Theorem
7
), and also for the nomination rule (Theorem
10
).
We then demonstrate that even limited aggregate information — such as acceptability points or plurality information — can restore manipulability for broad classes of rules (Theorems
8
,
9
,
11
, and
12
).

4.1

Let us start with our results regarding anchor-proof profiles under SAV. Loosely speaking, homogeneity seems to help with anchor-proofness, but to an extent, since profiles with more than one unanimously accepted alternative are not anchor-proof.

4.2
Anchor-proofness under Partial Information

So far we have been able to find only a small number of profiles for which particular rules are anchor-proof.
Crucially, these results rest on a strong assumption about the information available to the planner: anchor-proofness often fails precisely because the planner might be able to select a specific presentation order relying on the intrinsic preferences of the voters.

Next, we turn to settings where the planner lacks full information about the voters’ preferences. Using the model of
29
, we define a notion called ‘information function’
\mathit{info}
that maps each preference profile
\boldsymbol{p}
to a type of information.
We will study the following such functions:

•

Zero:

\mathit{info}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns a constant value, independently of
\boldsymbol{p}
; that is, it provides no useful information.

•

Acceptability points:

\mathit{info}_{acc}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns, for each alternative, the number of voters that consider it acceptable in
\boldsymbol{p}
.

•

Acceptability sets:

\mathit{info}_{\{acc\}}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns, for each alternative, the set of voters that consider it acceptable in
\boldsymbol{p}
.

•

Plurality points:

\mathit{info}_{pl}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns, for each alternative, the number of voters that rank it on top in
\boldsymbol{p}
.

•

Plurality sets:

\mathit{info}_{\{pl\}}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns, for each alternative, the set of voters that rank it on top in
\boldsymbol{p}
.

•

Full:

\mathit{info}_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})
returns precisely the profile
\boldsymbol{p}
.

Each information function gives rise to a set of profiles that the social planner considers possible, consistently with the provided information. Formally, the set of profiles that are considered possible given a certain profile
\boldsymbol{p}\in P(X)^{N}
is defined as follows:

W_{\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p})}=\{\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}\in P(X)^{N}\mid\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p})=\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}})\}

For example,
W_{\mathit{info}_{0}(\boldsymbol{p})}=P(X)^{N}
and
W_{\mathit{info}_{1}(\boldsymbol{p})}=\{\boldsymbol{p}\}
for any profile
\boldsymbol{p}
.
Next, Definition
7
formalizes the comparison between information functions in terms of the degree of information they provide.

Definition 7
.

Let
\mathit{info}
and
\mathit{info}^{\prime}
be two information functions.
We call
\mathit{info}

at least as informative as

\mathit{info}^{\prime}
if for every profile
\boldsymbol{p}
it holds that
W_{\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p})}\subseteq W_{\mathit{info}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{p})}
.

Considering the information functions that we presented,
\mathit{info}_{0}
(respectively,
\mathit{info}_{1}
) is the least (respectively, the most) informative of all;
\mathit{info}_{acc}
is less informative than
\mathit{info}_{\{acc\}}
; and

\mathit{info}_{pl}
is less informative than
\mathit{info}_{\{pl\}}
.
Note that
\mathit{info}_{pl}
may at first appear at least as informative as
\mathit{info}_{acc}
, but
technically this is not true since
\mathit{info}_{pl}
restricts the alternatives that can be on the top of the profile, while
\mathit{info}_{acc}
restricts the acceptance thresholds of the voters.

Now, we assume that the social planner has a preference
\succ_{sp}
over all the possible outcomes of the aggregation process (that is,
\succ_{sp}\in L(2^{X})
).
Thus, she might have the incentive to show the alternatives in a specific order (which we call a
strategy
) to obtain a more preferred outcome.

We ask whether, under partial information, the social planner can manipulate the voting outcome to her advantage.
Given a set of possible profiles
W\subseteq P(X)^{N}
consistent with the information of the social planner and a voting rule, we call a strategy
optimal
for the social planner if
(i)
for every possible profile in
W
it induces an outcome that is at least as good as the outcome of every other strategy, and
(ii)
in some possible profile in
W
it induces an outcome that is strictly better than the outcome of some other strategy.
4
4

4

Case
(ii)
is important to avoid the scenario where all strategies give the same outcome in all possible profiles, meaning that none of them can be considered optimal.
If the social planner has an optimal strategy given her information, then she can manipulate the voting rule. Definition
8
formalizes this idea.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathit{info}
be an information function and
F_{a}
be a voting rule. We say that
F_{a}
is
manipulable under
\boldsymbol{\mathit{info}}
on a profile
\boldsymbol{p}
by a social planner with preference

if there exists a strategy
\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\ast}}\in S(X)
for which the following two conditions hold:

•

for every profile
\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}\in W_{\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p})}
, and for every order
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\ast}}
, it is the case that
F_{a}(\boldsymbol{A_{p^{\prime},\sigma^{\ast}}})\succeq_{sp}F_{a}(\boldsymbol{A_{p^{\prime},\sigma}})
;

•

for some profile
\boldsymbol{p^{\prime}}\in W_{\mathit{info}(\boldsymbol{p})}
, and for some order
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\ast}}
, it is the case that
F_{a}(\boldsymbol{A_{p^{\prime},\sigma^{\ast}}})\succ_{sp}F_{a}(\boldsymbol{A_{p^{\prime},\sigma}})
.

We next define the general notion of manipulability for a voting rule
F_{a}
.

Definition 9
.

Let
\mathit{info}
be an information function and
F_{a}
be a voting rule. We say that
F_{a}
is
manipulable under

\boldsymbol{\mathit{info}}
if it is manipulable on some profile
\boldsymbol{p}
by a social planner with some preference

.

Definition

is a direct analog of the definition of manipulability in voting under partial information by

(

38

The strength of this condition increases the relevance of the manipulability results that we will soon present.
We start with an intuitive observation: an increase on the degree of the planner’s information benefits her ability to manipulate.

Lemma 3
.

Take an information function
\mathit{info}
that is at least as informative as an information function

\mathit{info}
for the same planner’s preference

5
Discussion

In this section, we discuss two extensions of our framework. First, we examine the setting with ranked ballots rather than approval ballots. Second, we analyze a particular class of information functions in which alternatives are indistinguishable to the social planner.

5.1
Ranked Ballots

A natural variation of our setting assumes that voters generate top-truncated ranked ballots rather than approval ballots. A top-truncated ranking constitutes a linear order over some subset of the alternatives, with the implicit assumption that all non-ranked
alternatives are inferior to all ranked alternatives.
5
5

5

Top-truncated rankings are commonly studied in the social choice literature about incomplete preferences
(
5
;
32
)
.
The anchoring process here operates similarly: given an intrinsic preference of a voter that is a complete ranking (possibly endowed with an acceptance threshold), when a presented alternative is less preferred than some alternative already ranked in the voter’s ballot, then it is discarded; otherwise, if it is accepted, it is ranked in accordance with the voter’s intrinsic preference.

To be more precise, a few definitions are in order.
The top-truncated ballot of voter
i
with intrinsic preference
p_{i}
given the presentation order
\sigma_{i}
is denoted
p_{i,\sigma_{i}}
(a linear order over a subset of
X
). For a ranking
p
(truncated or complete),
p^{1}
is its top alternative, meaning the most preferred alternative in that ranking.

We introduce the notion of a
rank-based voting rule

F_{r}
, which takes as input a profile of (possibly truncated) ballots and outputs a subset of winning alternatives.
We say that
F_{r}
satisfies
tops-only
if for every two profiles
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
such that
p^{1}_{i}=q^{1}_{i}
for all
i\in N
, it holds that
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{q})
. For a tops-only rule, the only relevant information lies at the top of each voter’s ranking.
Thus, it is a rule that does not demand the voters too many details about their preferences.
6
6

6

For further motivation regarding this property, we refer the reader to
31
.

The most famous tops-only voting rule is
plurality
, which selects as winners the alternatives that occupy most often the top positions in the voters’ preferences of a given profile.

Our main result characterizes all anchor-proof ranked-based rules. Recall that the presentation order
\boldsymbol{\bar{\sigma}}
shows each voter their top alternative first.

Theorem 13
.

A rank-based voting rule is anchor-proof if and only if it is tops-only.

Proof.

(if)
Let
F_{r}
be a tops-only rule. By the natural extension of Remark
1
to top-truncated ballots, we have that
p^{1}=p^{1}_{\sigma}
for every presentation order
\sigma
. In particular, this holds for every individual preference
p
. Since the rule is tops-only, we then have that
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p_{\sigma}})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p_{\sigma^{\prime}}})
for all pairs of presentation orders
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma^{\prime}}
. Thus,
F_{r}
is anchor-proof.

(only if)
Let
F_{r}
be anchor-proof. We need to show that for any two profiles
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
such that
p^{1}_{i}=q^{1}_{i}
for all
i\in N
, it holds that
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{q})
. By anchor-proofness, we have that
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p_{\bar{\sigma}}})
and
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{q})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{q_{\bar{\sigma}}})
. Since
\boldsymbol{p_{\bar{\sigma}}}=\boldsymbol{q_{\bar{\sigma}}}
, it follows that
F_{r}(\boldsymbol{p})=F_{r}(\boldsymbol{q})
. We conclude that
F_{r}
satisfies tops-only.
∎

At first glance, one might expect that finding anchor-proof rules for ranked ballots would be even more challenging than for approval ballots, given the difficulties we encountered in the approval setting. Theorem
13
reveals the contrary: Under ranked ballots, we can construct anchor-proof voting rules by paying attention to those that use only limited information — it is for such informational limitations that the plurality rule has been largely criticized
(
23
)
, and it is precisely for those that it is deemed desirable with respect to anchor-proofness. Given a tops-only rule, even a fully informed social planner cannot manipulate the outcome.
Thus, an informational trade-off emerges: rules that demand more information provide the social planner with greater leverage for manipulation, while informationally austere rules prove by construction more robust to strategic presentation orders.

5.2
Alternative-Ignorant Information Functions

Note that except for the case of zero information, all information functions described in this Section
4.2
allow us to distinguish the status of the different alternatives in a profile (which is sensible in several contexts of collective decisions, including polls of political elections and preliminary results of participatory budgeting contests).
But in other settings, we can imagine that the provided information does not allow the planner to separate the different alternatives.
The reader might suspect that for reasonable rules such as SAV, manipulability is then avoidable, since the planner has to fix a presentation order of the alternatives in her strategy without being able to exclude any permutation of the alternatives in the profiles she considers possible. However, this conjecture does not hold in general as Example
7
shows.

6
Conclusion

Our analysis of anchor-proof voting rules demonstrates how axiomatic characterizations in social choice can be extended to accommodate systematic deviations from rational preference formation. We have started by considering voters with intrinsic preference rankings who are subject to an anchoring bias, influencing the formation of their approval ballots via the presentation order of the alternatives. Our central characterization result shows that no approval-based voting rule (except constant ones) is robust to the impact of such an anchoring bias. On the positive side, we also find that a social planner who has no information about the voters’ underlying preferences cannot choose the presentation order to safely steer the voting outcome to her advantage.

We have further shown that the picture changes substantially when considering ballots with richer structures, particularly ranked ballots. In such settings, the derived ballots under an anchoring bias correspond to incomplete preference orders over the set of alternatives. Our characterization establishes that a rank-based voting rule is anchor-proof if and only if it is tops-only—that is, if it determines the outcome solely based on each voter’s most preferred alternative. This result reveals an informational trade-off: rules that demand more information provide the social planner with greater leverage for manipulation, while informationally austere rules prove inherently more robust to strategic presentation orders. The plurality rule, as a tops-only rule, is thus anchor-proof in the ranked ballot setting, connecting our work to the broader literature on collective decision-making under incomplete preferences.

A future research direction involves exploring alternative mechanisms for deriving anchor-biased ballots. One possibility is to consider trichotomous ballots, where alternatives are classified as approved, disapproved, or neutral. Under this formulation, an alternative that is accepted by a voter but appears after a more preferred one could be treated as neutral rather than disapproved, offering a more fine-grained representation of cognitive constraints.

Beyond anchoring, developing a systematic taxonomy of cognitive biases in voting could give rise to novel characterizations across different rules, analogously to classical results like Arrow’s theorem but incorporating bounded rationality. In general, different biases may require distinct formal representations of how reported ballots deviate from underlying preferences, suggesting a rich space of models to explore. Framing effects, loss aversion, and social conformity biases represent particularly promising candidates, as they admit tractable mathematical representations while exhibiting substantial empirical prevalence.

The negative results established in our paper motivate yet another research direction: designing voting rules with quantitatively optimal
degrees
of immunity to specific biases. From a computational social choice perspective, understanding which biases can be efficiently detected or corrected through algorithmic pre-processing of preference data, versus which require fundamentally different aggregation mechanisms, has important practical implications for both human and multi-agent collective-decision contexts.
This direction can also be considered for ranked ballots, as even though we show that the family of tops-only rules is anchor-proof, one could be interested in optimal informational trade-offs for more complex voting rules.

Finally, some negative results might be circumvented through randomization mechanisms, for instance where alternatives are displayed in random orders with all orderings equally likely. However, when certain alternatives are objectively superior or inferior (as may be determined through quality assessments or expert evaluations) one might assign greater probability to orders that position such alternatives earlier or later respectively. The formal development of such probabilistic models, potentially incorporating techniques from online learning and mechanism design, may yield further insights into the existence and characterization of bias-robust social choice rules.

All these directions suggest that behavioral social choice, incorporating bounded rationality as a fundamental consideration rather than implementation noise, can give rise to both theoretically richer frameworks and more reliable mechanisms for collective decision-making. Bias-robust rules extend naturally beyond voting, to participatory budgeting, committee selection, peer review, recommendation systems, and multi-agent resource allocation, where systematic cognitive biases similarly threaten the normative properties of aggregation procedures.
