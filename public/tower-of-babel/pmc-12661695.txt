Spinal pain as a public health concern: associations with health anxiety, life satisfaction, sleep quality, and well-being in young adults
Authors: Meryem Buke, Serbay Sekeroz, Ayşe Merve Tat, Necati Muhammed Tat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal pain is a prevalent and emerging public health concern among young adults, and is associated with psychological well-being and overall quality of life. However, its associations with health anxiety, life satisfaction, and sleep quality in this population remain underexplored. This study investigates the associations between spinal pain and these factors, highlighting implications for early public health interventions.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 206 young adults, categorized based on the presence of spinal pain using the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire. Participants completed validated measures assessing health anxiety, life satisfaction, sleep quality, and quality of life. Spearman correlation and regression analyses were performed to evaluate relationships among variables.ResultsSpinal pain was significantly associated with increased health anxiety and reduced life satisfaction and sleep quality. Health anxiety showed a positive relationship with poorer quality of life and was a significant predictor of life satisfaction (β = -0.284, p = 0.0001), sleep quality (β = -0.145, p = 0.039), and quality of life (β = +0.476, p = 0.0001). These findings underscore the psychological burden accompanying spinal pain in young adults.ConclusionsThe study emphasizes the need to address spinal pain not only as a physical condition but also as a psychological and public health issue affecting young adults’ well-being. Integrating mental health assessment and early preventive strategies into public health policies could mitigate the broader impact of spinal pain on this population.

Introduction

Health anxiety is a psychological condition that arises from the belief that individuals are under serious threat to their health, leading to emotional distress symptoms [1, 2]. It has been reported that health anxiety levels are particularly high in young adults, defined in the literature as individuals aged ≥ 18 years, generally up to 30 years, making this group vulnerable to both mental and physical health challenges [3]. In addition, musculoskeletal pain is commonly observed not only in working individuals but also in environments such as universities, where young adults are prevalent [4]. Recent evidence synthesizing young people’s lived and care experiences and characterizing clinical phenotypes highlights reciprocal links between chronic musculoskeletal pain and mental health, underscoring the need for integrated, developmentally appropriate support [5, 6]. Given its frequency and impact [7, 8], spinal pain among young adults represents an emerging public health concern that warrants detailed investigation into its psychological and functional consequences.

In a large number of musculoskeletal pain cases, no organic pathology is found, and the etiology of the pain is typically multifactorial [9, 10]. Pain, intertwined with psychological factors such as anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders, can lead to chronic musculoskeletal pain, posing a significant public health issue [11, 12]. A longitudinal study on individuals aged sixteen years reported that poor health, severe sleep problems, and anxiety contributed to the persistence or development of chronic musculoskeletal pain over time, highlighting the importance of early identification and intervention [13]. Expanding this research focus to young adults is critical, as this population faces unique developmental and social challenges that may influence the pain experience and its consequences.

Pain goes beyond being just a physical symptom and can also affect psychological states. Specifically, it has been shown that pain has significant effects on anxiety, depression, and quality of life among young adults [14, 15]. The psychological responses to pain tend to intensify with greater pain duration and severity, potentially undermining young adults’ overall mental health and daily functioning [16]. During this period, the interaction between pain, psychological factors, and sleep plays a determining role in individuals’ pain management, coping strategies, and overall well-being [17].

Life satisfaction is a reflection of an individual’s emotional and cognitive evaluations of their life and is an important indicator of personal well-being [18]. This satisfaction can vary depending on individual factors, environmental influences, and the level of social support [19, 20]. Similarly, quality of life is directly related to individuals’ health, social relationships, and overall well-being, and these factors can enhance individuals’ resilience in coping with anxiety [21, 22]. However, the interplay between health anxiety and these well-being factors in young adults with spinal pain remains insufficiently explored from a public health perspective.

While previous studies have explored health anxiety or pain-related quality of life in isolation, the comprehensive interaction between spinal pain, health anxiety, life satisfaction, and sleep quality in young adults remains under-researched. This study aims to fill this gap by examining these relationships from a public health perspective. This research primarily aims to answer the question, “To what extent is the presence of spinal pain associated with health anxiety, life satisfaction, sleep quality, and overall quality of life in young adults, and what are the public health implications?”. The secondary objective of the study is to investigate the relationship between health anxiety and its impact on life satisfaction, sleep, and quality of life in young adults.

Methods

A total of 206 participants were included in this cross-sectional descriptive study. Information about the study was shared with university students, and voluntary participation was ensured. The aims and procedures of the study were explained to all participants, and after obtaining informed consent, the study was conducted. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee for Non-Interventional Clinical Researches of Van Yuzuncu Yil University, with decision number 2024/13–20, and was carried out in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

To determine the presence of spinal pain, regions indicated in the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMSQ) were used [23].

Study design and participants

Spinal pain group

Inclusion criteria were being aged 18–30 years, having spinal pain complaints lasting at least 3 months, having pain intensity of 3.5 cm or more on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and no pathology affecting the vestibular or visual systems. Exclusion criteria included previous spinal surgery, rheumatological diseases, shoulder complex pathologies, scoliosis, thoracic outlet syndrome affecting the spinal region, and any clinically diagnosed specific spinal conditions (e.g., disc herniation, spinal stenosis, fractures, or tumors) identified through available medical history, to ensure that the sample reflected non-specific spinal pain.

Healthy group

Participants had no history of spinal surgery, no structural spinal deformities (e.g., scoliosis) or congenital anomalies (e.g., spina bifida), no nerve root defects, and no neck-related diseases or injuries. Additionally, healthy participants had no spinal pain complaints.

Assessments

Before the survey, participants were informed about the research topic and purpose through face-to-face meetings. It was explained that participation was entirely voluntary. Participants were instructed not to write their names on the surveys to ensure anonymity, and it was emphasized that collected data would only be used for this study. Survey completion took approximately 5–10 min.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed using an evaluation form including personal information and clinical status.

Personal information form

Developed by the researcher, this form collected data on age, gender, height, weight, smoking habits, and regular exercise routines.

Pain assessment

The Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire was used to identify the anatomical regions of pain (neck, back, lower back, shoulder, elbow, hand-wrist, hip-thigh, knee, foot-ankle) [23]. Pain intensity in each marked region was then assessed using a 10 cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 = no pain and 10 = unbearable pain [24]. The primary outcome was VAS-based pain intensity.

Life satisfaction scale

This scale, developed to measure life satisfaction, is a valid and reliable one-dimensional instrument consisting of 5 items [25, 26]. The lowest score possible on the scale is 5, while the highest score is 25. A higher score indicates increased life satisfaction, while a lower score indicates lower life satisfaction.

Health anxiety inventory- short version

Developed by Salkovskis and colleagues, this scale consists of 18 items [27]. The first 14 items contain sequential responses related to the patient’s mental state, with four options for each. The remaining 4 items inquire about the possible mental state of the patient assuming they may have a serious illness. Scoring for each item ranges from 0 to 3. A higher score indicates a higher level of health anxiety. The Turkish validity and reliability of the test were conducted by Aydemir et al. [28].

Nottingham Health Profile (NHP)

The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale was established by Küçükdeveci et al. [29]. It assesses perceived health problems and their impact on daily activities through 38 items grouped into six subscales: sleep, emotional reactions, pain, social isolation, energy, and physical abilities. In this study, only the first section of the scale was used. Items are answered as “yes” or “no.” Higher scores indicate poorer health status, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 for each subscale.

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was assessed using a 10 cm Visual Analog Scale. Participants were asked to mark their sleep quality, with 0 representing very poor sleep and 10 representing excellent sleep quality.

Data analysis

Based on the effect size (d = 0.35) obtained from the reference study [30], a power analysis was conducted, which indicated that at least 202 participants should be included in the study to achieve 80% power with a 95% confidence level.

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corp., 2017). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Group comparisons for parametric data were conducted using the Independent Samples t-test, while for non-parametric data, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. Additionally, relationships between continuous variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation analysis and regression analyses were performed using Linear Regression Analysis. The significance level for statistical tests was set at 0.05.

Discussion

This study compared young adults with and without spinal pain in terms of health anxiety, life satisfaction, sleep quality, and overall quality of life. The findings showed that young adults with spinal pain had higher health anxiety, poorer sleep quality, lower life satisfaction, and reduced overall quality of life. These group differences were generally small in effect size, except for overall quality of life, which showed a moderate effect size. Additionally, our study demonstrated that health anxiety is associated with life satisfaction, sleep, and quality of life, highlighting its role as a key psychological factor related to well-being in young adults. Taken together, the pattern suggests that the presence of spinal pain is associated with broader psychosocial outcomes, with the most pronounced difference observed in overall quality of life. These results point to the multidimensional burden of spinal pain on young adults, emphasizing not only the physical but also the psychological and social dimensions of this condition, which has important implications for public health interventions.

Health anxiety is a condition where individuals experience excessive concern about their health, often arising from misinterpretations of bodily sensations or symptoms [31]. This anxiety may lead individuals to perceive normal physiological signs as indications of a serious illness [32, 33]. Health anxiety has been found to be prevalent not only in individuals with physical or psychological conditions but also in the general population [34].

Previous studies have shown that levels of health anxiety vary across different groups. For instance, research conducted among nurses has indicated relatively higher levels of health anxiety compared to medical students [2, 35]. In our study, physiotherapy students demonstrated levels of health anxiety comparable to those of other student groups in health-related fields. These data suggest that even among individuals with health-related education, concerns about personal health can persist. This emphasizes the importance of incorporating psychological support and preventive mental health strategies into the education and training of future health professionals.

In our study, individuals with spinal pain were found to experience higher levels of health anxiety, although this difference corresponded to a small effect size. This could be related to an increased concern about their health as a result of experiencing more intense pain. The persistence of pain may create a continuous perception of health threats, which could trigger health-related anxiety. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, where the average age was higher than in our study [30]. In that study, it was suggested that health anxiety could be re-triggered by past pain experiences, especially during stressful periods like global crises such as COVID-19. These findings suggest that the management of spinal pain should incorporate psychological support mechanisms, especially during periods of heightened societal stress, to reduce the risk of exacerbated health anxiety.

In the literature, there are conflicting findings regarding the relationship between health anxiety and life satisfaction. Some studies suggest that individuals with elevated health anxiety may, under certain conditions, maintain relatively good functioning and social engagement, possibly due to adaptive coping strategies or protective personality traits, though these findings are limited and context-dependent [21, 22, 36]. On the other hand, there are studies that report a decrease in life satisfaction as health anxiety levels increase, indicating a significant negative relationship between these two variables [34, 37]. In our study, we found that health anxiety was associated with lower life satisfaction, corresponding to a small effect size. This suggests that, although the practical magnitude of this relationship may be limited at the individual level, it still reflects a measurable reduction in perceived well-being that warrants attention in preventive strategies. Taken together, these findings highlight the complexity of health anxiety’s effects and point to the need for personalized approaches in public health initiatives aimed at improving life satisfaction in populations affected by chronic pain.

Our findings indicate that individuals with spinal pain have poor sleep quality, with a small between-group difference. Even modest impairments in sleep may compound pain-related disability over time. The strong relationship between pain and sleep disturbances is frequently emphasized in the literature [38, 39]. Additionally, sleep quality in individuals with musculoskeletal pain has been shown to be associated not only with pain intensity but also with psychological and cognitive factors such as anxiety, depression, rumination, fatigue, and impaired attentional performance [40]. Considering these findings together, it can be concluded that spinal pain is associated with decrements in quality of life across multiple domains, involving not only pain intensity but also psychological and behavioral pathways. From a community health perspective, addressing sleep disturbances alongside pain management may improve functional outcomes and reduce the burden of musculoskeletal conditions.

In the present study, when examining the subcategories of quality of life, worse outcomes were observed in young individuals with spinal pain. However, no significant difference was found between the group with spinal pain and the healthy group in the social isolation subcategory. This result suggests that the impact of social isolation may become more pronounced over the long term. Considering the unpleasant nature of social isolation and loneliness, and their negative effects on health and well-being, it is emphasized that interventions are necessary to address this issue [41]. Additionally, the socioeconomic and psychological conditions of individuals should be taken into account, as they may influence this relationship. This insight highlights the importance of longitudinal and multidimensional assessments in public health research and interventions to fully capture and mitigate the social consequences of chronic pain.

The finding that health anxiety significantly predicts life satisfaction, sleep quality, and overall quality of life highlights its relevance in understanding the psychosocial burden of spinal pain. Notably, health anxiety explained 22.7% of the variance in quality of life, indicating a substantial influence. While its effect on sleep and life satisfaction was more modest, these results still suggest that health anxiety should be routinely addressed in clinical evaluations and incorporated into multidisciplinary care plans. Taken together, these insights underscore the importance of preparing future health professionals—such as physiotherapy students—to address the biopsychosocial dimensions of spinal pain through the development of person-centered and psychologically informed care competencies [42].

This study has some limitations. The sample consisted solely of university students, which limits generalizability. Future studies should include diverse populations across age groups and professions. Additionally, sleep quality was assessed using a single-item scale; more comprehensive tools are needed for better accuracy. Finally, between-group differences were generally small, except for the moderate difference in overall quality of life, which highlights clinically relevant signals despite the homogeneity of the sample and the cross-sectional design. Longitudinal studies with objective measures are needed to better inform targeted public health interventions.

Conclusions

Spinal pain in young adults is a significant public health issue with both physical and psychosocial consequences. This study highlights the critical role of health anxiety in mediating life satisfaction, sleep quality, and overall well-being.

Public health interventions should focus on early identification and management of psychosocial risk factors associated with musculoskeletal pain. Multidisciplinary approaches integrating psychological and physical health strategies are essential for improving outcomes in this population. Further research is warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted psychological interventions in reducing the burden of spinal pain among young adults.
