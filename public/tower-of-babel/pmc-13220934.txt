Development and Validation of the Inclusion Body Myositis-Health Index: A Disease-Specific Patient-Reported Outcome Measure for Use in Clinical Trials and Drug-Labeling Claims
Authors: Charlotte Irwin, Spencer Rosero, Jamison Seabury, Jennifer Weinstein, Charlotte Engebrecht, Anika Varma, Christine Zizzi, John Heatwole, Perya Bhagchandani, Cynthia Gibson, Nuran Dilek, Bill Martens, Chad Heatwole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

INTRODUCTION

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is a chronic inflammatory muscle disease that causes progressive asymmetric muscle weakness and degeneration.1 IBM primarily affects the quadriceps and deep finger flexor muscles1; however, other limb and facial muscles may also be affected.2 IBM typically has a symptom onset after 50 years of age and its prevalence is approximately twice as high in men than in women.2 Over time, patients with IBM may experience a variety of symptoms including impaired ambulation, problems using their hands, life-altering dysphagia, and aspiration pneumonia.1

To optimize clinical trial readiness, it is preferred to have an outcome measure that can measure clinically important changes in how a patient feels and functions. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has identified properly validated patient-reported outcome measures as an effective and meaningful way of measuring patient health in clinical trials.3 A multifactorial outcome measure designed to sensitively measure changes in IBM health has the potential to add to existing clinical trial infrastructure and facilitate future therapeutic development.

In this study, we report the results of a large-scale cross-sectional study of patients with IBM that identifies the symptoms of greatest importance to individuals with IBM, as well as the subsequent development and validation of a novel, disease-specific patient-reported outcome measure for IBM that is designed to measure these symptoms.

METHODS

Eligibility Criteria

Participants were recruited through the University of Rochester neuromuscular subspecialty clinics and The Myositis Association (TMA) patient registry4 and included individuals with IBM who were at least 18 years of age and could understand English. Individuals were not eligible for inclusion in the study if they did not meet all of these criteria.

Qualitative Interviews

In a previous study, we conducted qualitative interviews with 10 individuals with IBM.5 During these semistructured interviews, participants were asked which symptoms of IBM most affected their physical, social, emotional, and cognitive health, as well as their overall quality-of-life. These interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and coded using a two-investigator consensus approach.3 Using these data, we developed a survey that inquired about 162 symptoms of potential importance to individuals with IBM.

Cross-Sectional Study

We conducted an online survey with individuals with IBM using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform, a HIPAA-compliant electronic data capture system. Upon accessing the survey, participants were asked to read a patient information letter and residents of the European Union or United Kingdom were provided a General Data Protection Regulation notice. Participants then indicated their informed consent by checking a box before proceeding to a demographic survey and questions about their symptomatic health.

For each symptomatic question, participants were asked “How much does the following impact your life now?” and were provided with the following Likert scale responses: (1) I don't experience this; (2) I experience this but it does not affect my life; (3) It affects my life a little; (4) It affects my life moderately; (5) It affects my life very much; and (6) It affects my life severely. Participants also had an opportunity to write in any symptoms they felt were important but not included in the survey. Participants had the option to decline to answer any demographic or symptom question. Participants were not required to state how they were referred to the study. Minimum data inclusion criteria for the cross-sectional survey required that a participant complete at least one demographic question and one symptom question.

Statistical Analysis

Using data from the cross-sectional survey, we calculated the prevalence and relative impact/importance of each symptom in the survey. The relative impact of each symptom was determined for those participants who reported experiencing the symptom using the following metric: 0= I don't experience this or I experience this, but it does not affect my life; 1 = It affects my life a little; 2 = It affects my life moderately; 3 = It affects my life very much; and 4 = It affects my life severely. A score of 0 indicated the lowest symptom impact, while a score of 4 indicated the highest symptom impact. We have used this methodology in the development of numerous other instruments for neuromuscular and non-neuromuscular diseases.5–22

A population impact score (PIP) was calculated for all symptoms included in the cross-sectional survey by multiplying symptom prevalence by the mean impact score among participants who reported experiencing that symptom. Questions with a PIP <0.40 were considered for exclusion from the instrument because of a low population impact. The selection of symptom questions also excluded those unclear in wording or meaning, above an elementary-school reading level, redundant, potentially abrasive, and those lacking the potential to respond to therapeutic intervention.

Initial Internal Consistency Testing

Questions selected for the first version of the IBM-HI were grouped into symptomatic subscales using a research team consensus approach. The internal consistency of the symptomatic subscales was assessed using factor analysis. Inter-item correlations were used to ensure that symptom questions within each subscale measured similar aspects of IBM health. Questions were moved between subscales through an iterative process to optimize the internal consistency and face validity of each subscale.

Beta Testing

We beta tested the first version of the IBM-HI using participants with IBM recruited through the TMA registry. Participants were asked to complete the first version of the IBM-HI and then provide feedback on the content, relevance, and usability of the instrument in a Zoom audio interview. In compliance with FDA standards, participants were asked to describe what each question meant to them (comprehension probe), discuss how sure they were of their answers (confidence probe), and discuss the time frame of the responses (recall probe).3 They were also asked to remark on wording of each question and theme, comment on the response options, discuss the time needed to complete the instrument, identify any problematic formatting issues, discuss the clarity of the instructions, and identify any symptoms that were not addressed. All participant responses were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using an investigator consensus approach.3 Any questions that were poorly understood by participants were reworded or removed. Any questions that were originally part of the cross-sectional survey and reported by participants as missing, but important, were added back into the instrument.

Test–Retest Reliability

Test–retest evaluation of the second version of the IBM-HI was conducted with participants from the TMA registry. Participants completed the IBM-HI at baseline and again two weeks later. A two-week time frame was used between consecutive administrations of the survey because it is short enough to restrict extensive changes in participant health, but long enough to limit participant recall of earlier responses. The reliability of each of the symptom questions in the instrument was calculated using weighted kappa values. Questions were considered for removal if they had a weighted kappa value <0.40. The reliability of each of subscales was determined using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Final Internal Consistency Testing

After reliability testing, we conducted an interval confirmatory factor analysis on the total instrument and each of the subscales.

Instrument Scoring

Each IBM-HI subscale was scored from 0 to 100, where a score of 0 indicates no disease burden and a score of 100 indicates maximum disease burden. Each question within the subscales was weighted based on its relative importance to participants as determined by the cross-sectional study. Similarly, each of the subscales was weighted to generate a total IBM-HI score (range 0–100). The IBM-HI-Short Form (IBM-HI-SF) was created including 1 representative item from each of the IBM-HI subscales.

Known Groups Validity

Using data from our cross-sectional study, we calculated average IBM-HI total and subscale scores for predefined subgroups. Comparisons were made according to the following demographic and clinical features: (1) fit of ability (moderate/moderately severe/severe disability vs. no disability/slight disability); (2) disease duration (above mean disease duration of 12.8 years vs. below or equal to mean disease duration); (3) disability status (on disability vs. not on disability); (4) walking ability (does not walk independently vs. walks independently); (5) sex (male vs. female); and (6) speech (has speech impairment vs. speaks clearly). Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with two-sided P-values calculated using the t-approximation. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate corrections were applied to adjust for multiple comparisons across all known group analyses. Using a false discovery rate of 0.05 across 90 statistical tests, 78 comparisons remained statistically significant after correction (see Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JCND/A84).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This research conforms to the standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Rochester Review Board.

RESULTS

Cross-Sectional Study

We analyzed data from 569 participants who completed at least one demographic and one survey question. Most participants in the international cross-sectional sample (84.9%) reported living in the United States and more than 70% indicated having a college, master's, or doctoral degree. Participants had a mean age of 71.5 years and were predominantly White (93.9%), male (67.3%), and retired (74.2%). Full demographic information for this population is summarized in Table 1. The cross-sectional survey evaluated 162 symptoms of importance. The most commonly experienced symptomatic themes were “Inability to do activities” (98.1%), “Limitations with mobility or walking” (97.7%), and “Problems with your hands and fingers” (96.0%). The symptomatic themes with the highest average impact were “Limitations with mobility or walking” (2.48), “Problems with your hands or fingers” (2.46), and “Inability to do activities” (2.43). The most commonly experienced symptoms were “Muscle weakness” (99.3%), “Difficulty getting up from the floor or ground” (98.9%), and “Difficulty with stairs” (98.8%). The symptoms rated as most impactful were “Difficulty getting up from the floor or ground (3.42), “Trouble running” (3.40), and “Needing help to get off the floor” (3.33) (Fig. 1). The prevalence and relative impact of all symptom questions are provided in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JCND/A84).

Symptom Item Selection Selection

After analysis of the cross-sectional survey data, we developed the first version of the IBM-HI. Five symptom items from the original survey were not included due to a low PIP value. An additional 66 symptom items were excluded because of the following reasons: redundancy (20 symptom items), vagueness (15 symptom items), not being potentially responsive to future therapeutic interventions (22 symptom items), or a low generalizability (9 symptom items). During an initial factor analysis, 2 symptom items were deleted and 5 symptom items were moved to improve the internal consistency of the individual subscales.

Beta Testing

Fifteen individuals with IBM participated in beta testing (Table 1). Two participants were from Canada, the remainder were from the United States. Participants completed the first version of the IBM-HI and reported they believed the IBM-HI was well suited to measure how they feel and function. Beta testing participants further reported that the instrument would be capable of measuring changes in their IBM health over time, as well as their disease severity. Participants also had the opportunity to report on items they felt should be added, removed, or reworded for clarity. Based on participant recommendations, one item was removed because it was perceived as redundant, one item was removed because of a lack of generalizability, 3 items were removed after being identified as vague, and 1 item was reworded for clarity. Finally, 2 symptom items previously removed after the cross-sectional survey, “Difficulty opening car doors” and “Having to take naps during the day” were added back into the IBM-HI based on participant recommendations.

Test–Retest Reliability

The second version of the IBM-HI was created after implementing changes based on beta testing feedback. We conducted test–retest reliability with the second version of the IBM-HI with 21 participants (Table 1). Participants completed the IBM-HI twice, approximately two weeks apart. The average time in between the first and second survey completion was 14.8 days. Seven symptom items had weighted kappa values <0.40, indicating borderline reliability. However, 4 of the 7 symptoms, “Problems with shoulders or arms,” “Emotional issues,” “Anxiety,” and “Shortness of breath” were kept because of their high importance to the population and the likelihood that changes could occur in these items for a 14-day period. The remaining 3 items were removed from the IBM-HI. On average, the instrument took 11 minutes to complete. At the completion of this step, the third version of the IBM-HI was created. Final Internal Consistency Testing

We conducted a final confirmatory factor analysis on the third version of the IBM-HI, the short form, and each subscale. Cronbach's alpha values for each subscale ranged from 0.88 to 0.99 with an average value of 0.93 (Table 2) and no additional changes were made to the instrument. Known Groups Validity

Known groups analysis using cross-sectional study data from 569 participants indicated that the IBM-HI is capable of distinguishing between subgroups of individuals based on different demographic and clinical characteristics. Statistically significant differences were detected with the following group comparisons: fit of ability (moderate/moderately severe/severe disability vs. no disability/slight disability), disease duration (above mean disease duration vs. below mean disease duration), disability status (on disability vs. not on disability), walking ability (does not walk independently vs. walks independently), sex (male vs. female), and speech status (has speech impairment vs. speaks clearly).

Men indicated significantly higher disease burden than women as measured by the IBM-HI total score, short form, and 6 subscales (Table 3). Individuals who reported a “Moderate”, “Moderately severe”, or “Severe” disability level in comparison with individuals with “No disability” or “Slight disability” demonstrated a higher disease burden on the total score and short form and all 13 subscales (Table 3). Individuals with a longer duration of disease indicated a higher level of disease burden as measured by the total score, short form, and all subscales compared with those with a shorter duration of disease (Table 3).

The IBM-HI detected differences in disease burden based on disability status. Participants who reported being on disability had a higher disease burden as measured by the total score, short form, and 8 out of 13 subscales compared with those not on disability (Table 3). Individuals with IBM who reported not being able to walk independently had a higher disease burden with the total score, short form, and all 13 subscales compared with those who reported walking independently. DISCUSSION

The IBM-HI is a disease-specific patient-reported outcome that measures the areas of symptomatic burden that are most important to individuals with IBM. The IBM-HI was validated to satisfy existing FDA criteria for use in upcoming therapeutic trials and to support regulatory claims.23 In addition, this instrument provides a mechanism for researchers and clinicians to use direct patient input to quantify a patient's point-in-time symptomatic burden in 13 distinct areas. Overall, the instrument reliably differentiates between groups of patients with different levels of disease severity and appears to be an appropriate measure of how patients feel and function.

The IBM-HI is specifically designed to help determine clinically meaningful changes in symptomatic burden in response to treatments during upcoming trials. In this way, it can be administered in conjunction with functional measures and biomarkers to assess the totality of a treatment's effectiveness. Most importantly, the IBM-HI enables patients to offer direct input on a treatment's effectiveness and its meaningful impact on improving their daily lives by addressing symptoms identified by patients with IBM as significant. Unlike many traditional instruments in the neuromuscular space, all of the items in the IBM-HI and each of its subscales were selected based on views of patients with IBM on what symptoms are most important and should be included in an outcome measure for this population.

As part of the IBM-HI validation process, our group conducted one of the largest patient-focused cross-sectional studies to date regarding disease burden in IBM. Our findings revealed that IBM is a multifactorial disease characterized by a wide range of symptoms, each with varying levels of impact on patients' lives. The IBM-HI is uniquely suited to measure the impact of the most significant of these symptoms over time. While many issues, such as mobility impairment, are commonly assessed during clinical trials, other equally burdensome symptoms—such as fatigue, pain, swallowing dysfunction- are less frequently addressed or measured during therapeutic trials. The IBM-HI provides a potential mechanism to track and quantify changes in these important issues in the context of a clinical trial. Indeed, the IBM-HI is currently being used by the research community for this purpose in INSPIRE-IBM: An NIH-funded, two-year, multicenter, observational study in IBM.24

IBM-HI scores demonstrate a strong correlation to disability status, a pattern we have observed with many of our disease-specific instruments for other neurologic diseases.25–34 Notably, the fatigue and pain subscales of the IBM-HI showed the strongest association with disability status. While association does not imply causation, this finding underscores the importance of these two issues in IBM and highlights them as potential symptomatic targets for future clinical trials.

Of note, our study population, like the greater IBM population, was mostly older and male.2 Although the development of this instrument included more than 500 patients from diverse locations, our conclusions and findings are limited to the assumption that this sample is an appropriate representation for the full IBM population. Although we suspect that this is reasonable, our research heavily used the TMA registry and required patients have access to a computer. It is suspected that those with limited access to a computer, those who are not familiar or comfortable with participating in research, and those who have not had sufficient involvement with the TMA registry would have been underrepresented in our sample.

The IBM-HI adds to existing clinical trial infrastructure in IBM. Although many outcomes have historically been used in IBM, many have limitations as markers of disease burden. Measures such as strength testing, 6 minute walk testing, the IBMFRS, and the sIFA can be susceptible to variability, be dependent on cardiorespiratory fitness, lack sensitivity and reliability, and do not address all of the symptomatic components important to IBM.35 The IBM-HI has the potential to be used in conjunction with many of these outcome measures to generate a more robust point-in-time assessment of patient perceived health.

Overall, the findings from this research highlight the construct validity, usability, and performance metrics of a novel disease-specific instrument for IBM. This outcome measure has the potential to enhance the efficiency of therapeutic trials and provide researchers with a valuable tool to explore the clinical meaningfulness and impact of future IBM therapeutics.
