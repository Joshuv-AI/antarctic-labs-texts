Gait and gluteus medius alterations after Trochanteric-Entry intramedullary nailing: A retrospective quantitative analysis
Authors: Anil Agar, Sefa Key, Mustafa Yildirim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrochanteric-entry intramedullary nailing (IMN) is widely used for the treatment of femoral shaft fractures. However, concerns remain regarding potential iatrogenic injury to the gluteus medius (GMed), a key hip abductor muscle essential for gait stability. Limited clinical studies have simultaneously investigated postoperative muscle morphology and gait biomechanics in this context. This retrospective study aimed to quantitatively evaluate gait and gluteus medius muscle alterations following trochanteric-entry intramedullary nailing in patients with healed femoral fractures.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 50 adult patients who underwent trochanteric-entry IMN between January 2020 and January 2024. At the 12th postoperative month, bilateral evaluations of GMed muscle thickness (ultrasonography), plantar pressure distribution, and spatiotemporal gait parameters (step length and cadence) were performed using the WIN-TRACK gait analysis system (Medicapteurs, France). Comparisons between operated and contralateral limbs were conducted with paired tests. Correlation, regression, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to examine associations between morphological and functional measures.ResultsThe operated limbs demonstrated significantly reduced step length (0.553 ± 0.051 m vs. 0.584 ± 0.045 m), cadence (104.6 ± 11.2 vs. 109.9 ± 10.8 steps/min), and GMed thickness (2.09 ± 0.28 cm vs. 2.27 ± 0.29 cm), all with p < 0.001. Plantar pressure variables, including maximum pressure, pressure-time integral, and contact area, were also significantly decreased on the operated side (p < 0.001). Effect size analyses revealed medium differences (Cohen’s d: 0.48–0.63). Although step length showed weak positive correlations with GMed thickness and plantar loading parameters, regression (R² = 0.035, p = 0.643) and ROC analyses (AUC 0.46–0.53) indicated limited predictive accuracy.ConclusionOne year after trochanteric-entry IMN, patients exhibited persistent structural and functional asymmetries, including reduced step length, cadence, GMed thickness, and plantar pressure. Despite bone union and fracture healing, compensatory deficits remained evident. These findings highlight the need for postoperative rehabilitation protocols focusing on hip abductor strengthening, and gait re-education, as well as long-term monitoring of gluteus medius integrity.Keywords: Femoral shaft fracture, Intramedullary nailing, Trochanteric entry, Gait analysis, Gluteus medius, Ultrasonography

Introduction

The frequency of femoral shaft fractures varies between 9.9 and 12.0 per 100,000 person-years [1–3]. Young persons between the ages of 15 and 25 have the highest incidence; 60% of them are male and 40% are female [1–3]. Low-energy trauma in the elderly is another potential cause [4]. Femoral shaft fractures account for a significant proportion of high-energy orthopaedic injuries and often result in compromised gait and muscular function if not treated appropriately [5].

The generally acknowledged gold standard for treating these injuries is intramedullary nailing (IMN), which promotes early mobilization and provides biomechanical stability [6]. Because of its ease of use and lower risk of piriformis fossa damage, the trochanteric-entry approach has become increasingly popular among IMN techniques [7]. Concern over its possible effects on the gluteus medius (GMed), a crucial hip abductor that supports pelvic stability in a single-leg stance, is on the rise [8].

Following trochanteric-entry nailing, GMed dysfunction may occur as Trendelenburg gait and decreased abductor strength, which can have a detrimental impact on rehabilitation and long-term functional results [9, 10]. With an average disruption of the tendinous footprint during reaming of more than 25%, cadaveric studies have consistently shown iatrogenic GMed tendon damage [11–13]. Although the results vary from patient to patient, recent ultrasonographic studies have verified morphological changes in GMed following surgery, including thickness and stiffness [14, 15]. Even in the absence of obvious strength deficiencies, electromyographic assessments also point to possible neuromuscular impairment [16].

Despite these results, there are not many clinical studies that have evaluated GMed muscle morphology and gait biomechanics in patients receiving trochanteric-entry intramedullary nails at the same time. Despite decreased hip abductor strength, one pertinent cohort study found no discernible changes in gait at the mid-term follow-up [16]. A trustworthy, non-invasive method for assessing postoperative tendon integrity and muscle morphology is diagnostic musculoskeletal ultrasonography [14, 17].

Thus, by combining quantitative gait analysis with ultrasonographic evaluation of the GMed muscle in patients who have had trochanteric-entry IMN for femoral shaft fractures, this retrospective study aims to close the current gap in the literature. The study focuses on assessing the distribution of plantar pressure between the operated and non-operated limbs, as well as spatiotemporal gait parameters, such as step length and cadence. Musculoskeletal ultrasonography is also used to analyse variations in GMed muscle thickness from side to side. Correlation analysis is also used to examine the connection between muscle morphology and gait function. Additionally, the diagnostic utility of biomechanical and GMed-related factors in detecting patients with impaired gait performance is investigated. This study attempts to offer more precise insights into postoperative rehabilitation by combining morphological and functional evaluations. We hypothesized that patients treated with trochanteric-entry intramedullary nails would demonstrate persistent side-to-side asymmetries in gait parameters and gluteus medius thickness even after fracture healing.

Material and method

This retrospective observational study was authorized by the Fırat University Clinical Research Ethics Committee on March 21, 2025, with approval number 21.03.2025–33,233. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients operated on for femoral shaft fractures between January 2020 and January 2024 using trochanteric-entry IMN were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were being at least 18 years old, having a unilateral, isolated diaphyseal femur fracture that was treated with trochanteric entrance femoral IM nailing, being available for postoperative follow-up at 12 months and having radiographic evidence of complete union of the fracture. The analysis excluded patients with bilateral lower limb fractures, associated musculoskeletal or neurologic disorders that affect gait, missing data or radiographic evidence of incomplete union of the fracture.

To assess the reliability of ultrasonographic measurements, intra-observer reproducibility was evaluated in 15 randomly selected patients, yielding an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.91 for GMed thickness, indicating excellent reliability. Inter-observer variability was not assessed, which represents a limitation.

At the 12th postoperative month, all evaluations had been performed. Bilateral assessments were made of the GMed muscle thickness, plantar pressure distribution and spatiotemporal gait parameters (step length and cadence). Gait analysis was performed using the WIN-TRACK system (Medicapteurs, France). A validated computerized pressure-sensitive walkway system was used to analyse gait, and musculoskeletal ultrasonography in a standardized lateral decubitus position was used to measure the thickness of the GMed. One experienced musculoskeletal radiologist (M.Y.) who was blind to the patients’ clinical status performed all ultrasonographic measurements (Samsung V7, Samsung Healthcare Global, South Korea). Gluteus medius thickness was measured at a standardized site located 4 cm posterior and 2 cm superior to the greater trochanter, with the probe oriented perpendicular to the muscle fibers at rest.

The contralateral side was used as the within-subject control, and the operated limb was identified using surgical records. During a planned follow-up visit, all measurements were taken on the same day.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28.0.1.1 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies (n, %) for categorical variables. The normality of distributions was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between the operated and contralateral limbs, paired-sample t-tests were applied to normally distributed continuous variables (step length, cadence, GMed thickness, plantar pressure parameters and contact area). For variables with non-normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Associations between muscle morphology and gait parameters were evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). To identify independent predictors of step length, a multiple linear regression model was constructed, including GMed thickness, pressure-time integral (PTI) and maximal plantar pressure as independent variables. The diagnostic accuracy of morphological and biomechanical parameters for detecting patients with impaired gait performance (step length < 0.55 m) was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Additionally, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of the mean differences between the limbs. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 86 patients who underwent trochanteric-entry IMN for femoral shaft fractures between 2020 and 2024 were initially screened for eligibility. After applying exclusion criteria from the initial 86 patients screened, 36 were excluded due to incomplete follow-up data (n = 22), bilateral fractures (n = 8) or associated neuromuscular disorders (n = 6). A total of 50 adult patients were included in the final analysis. A flow diagram has been provided to summarize patient inclusion and exclusion (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study

The mean age of the participants was 45.2 ± 12.8 years (range: 21–68), and 32 patients (64%) were male, while 18 (36%) were female. The right and left sides were the operated limbs in 27 patients (54%) and 23 patients (46%), respectively.

At the 12th postoperative month, all muscle and gait examinations were performed. All assessed variables showed statistically significant changes between the operative and contralateral limbs when spatiotemporal gait characteristics and GMed muscle thickness were compared (Table 1). In particular, with a p-value of less than 0.001, the step length on the operated side was substantially lower (mean: 0.553 ± 0.051 m) than on the non-operated side (mean: 0.584 ± 0.045 m) (Fig. 2). Likewise, the cadence was statistically substantially (p < 0.001) lower on the operated limb (mean: 104.59 ± 11.2 steps/min) than on the contralateral side (mean: 109.85 ± 10.8 steps/min) (Fig. 3). In terms of muscle morphology, the GMed thickness on the operated side was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and smaller (mean: 2.09 ± 0.28 cm) than on the non-operated side (mean: 2.27 ± 0.29 cm), according to ultrasonography (Fig. 4).

In addition to spatiotemporal and morphological aspects, plantar pressure analysis also revealed significant asymmetries. The maximum plantar pressure of the operated side was found to be lower (mean: 278.86 ± 29.3 kPa) than that of the contralateral limb (mean: 298.96 ± 27.4 kPa), with a statistically significant p-value (< 0.001) (Fig. 5). Additionally, the PTI was lower on the operated side (mean: 39.75 ± 5.0 kPa·s; non-operated: 42.97 ± 5.3 kPa·s)(Fig. 6), and the foot contact area was slightly smaller on the operated side (mean: 110.47 ± 13.9 cm²) than on the non-operated side (mean: 114.78 ± 14.1 cm²) (Fig. 7), both of which were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Step length and GMed muscle thickness were found to be positively correlated by Pearson correlation analysis (r = 0.16); however, this correlation did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.26). Step length, PTI and maximal plantar pressure showed similar non-significant positive trends, indicating weak to moderate correlations (Fig. 8).

Correlation matrix of gait and muscle parameters of the study

To further explore the predictive relevance of these biomechanical and morphological parameters, a multiple linear regression model was constructed with step length as the dependent variable and GMed thickness, PTI and maximal plantar pressure as independent variables. The model yielded a non-significant result with a p-value of 0.643 and an R2 value of 0.035, indicating that these parameters alone were not sufficient to account for the variance in step length across the patients in this study.

The predictive efficacy of biomechanical factors for identifying patients with low step length (defined as < 0.55 m) was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. Of all the variables examined, maximum plantar pressure showed the highest discriminative value, with an AUC of 0.53. Next were the thickness of the GMed muscle (AUC of 0.46) and the PTI (AUC of 0.49). All of these findings point to the diagnostic limitations of these individual measures in correctly detecting gait abnormalities (Fig. 9).

ROC curves for predictors of low step length

Effect size analyses were also performed to determine the magnitude of the differences between the operated and contralateral limbs. The standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) for step length was 0.63, 0.48 for cadence and 0.62 for GMed thickness, all reflecting medium effect sizes. These findings support the clinical relevance of the observed statistical differences. Despite statistical significance, the ROC analysis demonstrated poor diagnostic accuracy for individual parameters (AUC 0.46–0.53), suggesting that these measures alone are insufficient to serve as predictive tools for gait dysfunction (Fig. 10).

Furthermore, 95% CIs were calculated for the main comparisons: step length difference: mean difference = −0.031 m (95% CI: −0.045 to−0.017), cadence difference: mean difference =−5.26 steps/min (95% CI: −7.8 to −2.7) and GMed thickness difference: mean difference = −0.18 cm (95% CI: −0.24 to −0.12) (Fig. 11). All intervals excluded zero, reinforcing the statistical significance and clinical importance of these differences.

Mean Differences in Gait and Muscle Parameters

The operative and contralateral limbs continued to differ, as indicated by the asymmetry indices. The average cadence asymmetry was 4.8%, and the average step length asymmetry was 5.6%. With a mean difference of 8.6%, the GMed muscle thickness showed the greatest asymmetry (Fig. 12). These results highlight the fact that, even a year after surgery, there is still a functional and muscle imbalance between the limbs.

Discussion

At 12 months following trochanteric-entry IMN for femoral shaft fractures, we found ongoing structural and functional asymmetry in our retrospective sample of 50 patients. The operated limbs showed significantly decreased step length, cadence, GMed thickness, plantar pressure parameters and contact area (all p < 0.001), despite excellent fracture healing. These findings show that damage persists, even when bone union seems to be successful.

Our results are in line with previous studies that demonstrated that subclinical GMed dysfunction without overt gait abnormalities could be caused by trochanteric entrance IMN. Ergişi et al. evaluated 16 patients and found no statistically significant changes in GMed EMG (electromyography) activity or spatiotemporal gait characteristics; however, functional performance decreased when compared to controls (e.g., hop test p = 0.009, balancing tests p < 0.001) [16]. Although traditional gait measurements may show stability, this discrepancy suggests that basic morphological and loading deficiencies persist. Our study builds on these results by integrating ultrasonographic GMed thickness data with plantar loading data.

Imaging and ultrasound studies that look at GMed integrity after trochanteric invasion support this structural degradation. Research by Çölbe et al. revealed reduced thickness and stiffness in post-IMN GMed, as determined by grayscale ultrasonography and elastography at 6–12 weeks post-op, even though group-level changes occasionally lacked significance [15]. In contrast, our results show statistically significant GMed atrophy (mean difference ~ 0.18 cm), which supports the effect of surgical trauma on morphology.

Even in elderly patients with intertrochanteric fractures, radiological imaging investigations using CT (computed tomography) have also demonstrated decreased GMed cross-sectional area (CSA) post-operatively, with decreases of 11–17% in CSA when compared to the contralateral side [18]. Even in mid-term follow-up, evidence of iatrogenic muscle damage is strengthened by the consistency of these results across CT, elastographic and ultrasonographic modalities.

We found that step length was positively but weakly correlated with peak plantar pressure, PTI and GMed thickness (r = 0.16, p = 0.26). These factors only account for 3.5% of step length variability, according to regression analysis (R2 = 0.035, p = 0.643), and ROC analysis revealed poor diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.46–0.53). In addition to muscle morphology, these findings imply that gait dysfunction following trochanteric-entry IMN is complex and presumably caused by deficiencies in abductor strength, proprioception, pain, limb alignment or neuromuscular coordination. All of these results require further investigation.

The lack of a strong correlation between GMed morphology and gait function in our cohort suggests that compensatory neuromuscular mechanisms, proprioceptive adaptation and recruitment of accessory muscles may play significant roles in preserving functional ambulation despite measurable structural deficits. Additionally, pain levels, hip alignment and rehabilitation adherence—parameters not assessed in this study—may have contributed to the variability in gait outcomes. From a clinical perspective, these results highlight the need for targeted rehabilitation protocols emphasizing hip abductor strengthening, proprioceptive training and gait re-education in patients treated with trochanteric-entry femoral nails. Routine postoperative follow-up incorporating ultrasonographic evaluation of the GMed and quantitative gait analysis could enable early detection of subclinical deficits and guide individualized rehabilitation programs.

Although the mean step length difference was modest (3.1 cm), previous gait analyses suggest that even small asymmetries exceeding 2–3 cm may increase compensatory loading and contribute to long-term gait inefficiency or contralateral limb overuse [19, 20].

Significant side-to-side asymmetries, ranging from 5.6% for step length to 8.6% for GMed thickness, suggest a persistent functional imbalance and may increase the risk of compensatory mechanics or chronic hip disorders, such as Trendelenburg gait or gluteal tendinopathy, in patients. These metrics demonstrate the importance of comprehensive postoperative evaluation and targeted rehabilitation, with a particular focus on hip abductor recovery.

This study has several limitations. Its small sample size (n = 50) and retrospective design limit causal inference. We did not have early (e.g., 3 and 6 months) or longer-term (>24 months) follow-ups to show the course of improvement or deterioration. We did not gather functional outcomes such as hip abduction strength, pain intensity, patient-reported metrics (e.g., WOMAC and HOOS) or radiographic evaluations of alignment or offset. To evaluate muscle integrity, thickness measures were used instead of other modalities that could more thoroughly describe tissue quality, such as EMG, shear-wave elastography or CT imaging. Due to the nature of the operation, blinding the gait assessor was not feasible, even though a blinded radiologist conducted the imaging. The lack of inter-observer reliability assessment for ultrasonographic measures is also acknowledged as a methodological shortcoming. Lastly, since all operations were performed by the same surgical team in the same surgical position, rotational defects that could potentially impair walking, caused by trochanteric femoral nails, were not measured in the study [21].

Our results underline the necessity of surgical methods that reduce GMed trauma and justify the cautious use of trochanteric-entry IMN. According to this study, one year after undergoing trochanteric-entry IMN for femoral shaft fractures, the patients still had morphological and functional asymmetries. Step length, cadence, plantar pressure parameters and GMed muscle thickness were all significantly reduced on the operated side compared to the contralateral limb. Although there were weak correlations between muscle thickness and gait measures, these factors alone were insufficient to predict gait performance, indicating that postoperative functional recovery is complicated. Rehabilitation strategies should prioritize targeted strengthening of the hip abductors and gait symmetry training, given the observed persistence of gluteus medius and step length asymmetries. Although neuromuscular or proprioceptive factors may also play a role, these were not directly assessed in the present study and warrant further investigation. Early screening methods that employ quantitative gait and morphologic assessments could be used to identify patients who are at risk of developing chronic dysfunction.

The findings demonstrate the importance of considering the potential effects of trochanteric entrances on gait symmetry and the integrity of the abductor muscles. Incorporating GMed evaluation and quantitative gait analysis into routine postoperative follow-up may help identify mild impairments that might otherwise go unnoticed. Additionally, early and individualized rehabilitation programs that prioritize hip abductor strength and neuromuscular control may lessen chronic dysfunction.

It is necessary to compare trochanteric and piriformis-entry nails in prospective, randomized studies. Longer-term monitoring and multimodal assessments (such as ultrasound, CT/MRI(magnetic resonance imaging), EMG and patient-reported results) ought to be a part of these investigations. Additionally, studies on targeted treatments (such as abductor-strengthening exercises and neuromuscular training) would clarify how to translate morphological gains into therapeutic benefits. Future studies employing multimodal evaluations, including electromyography, MRI and patient-reported outcomes, are required to gain a better understanding of the relationship between structural muscle changes and functional outcomes following femoral IMN. Future prospective studies should compare trochanteric and piriformis-entry nails to directly evaluate the relative risk of GMed dysfunction. Larger multicentre trials with multimodal assessments (MRI, EMG, shear-wave elastography and patient-reported outcome scores) are needed to elucidate the relationship between structural changes and functional outcomes. Furthermore, randomized trials testing rehabilitation interventions—particularly hip abductor–focused physiotherapy and neuromuscular training—would clarify how targeted strategies can mitigate long-term deficits observed after trochanteric-entry IMN.
