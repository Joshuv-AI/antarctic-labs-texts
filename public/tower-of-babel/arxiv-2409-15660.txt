An Effective Slope Gap Distribution for Lattice Surfaces
Tariq Osman, Josh Southerland, Jane Wang

Abstract
We prove an effective slope gap distribution result first for the square torus and then for general lattice translation surfaces. As a corollary, we obtain a dynamical proof for an effective gap distribution result for the Farey fractions. As an intermediate step, we prove an effective equidistribution result for the intersection points of long horocycles with a particular transversal of the horocycle flow in SL 2 ​ ( ℝ ) / Γ \mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma where Γ \Gamma is a lattice.

Introduction

In dynamical systems, especially ergodic theory, many theorems are qualitative in the sense that they identify when iterates of a dynamical system converge, but fail to quantify the rate of this convergence. In the following paper, we resolve an issue of this type: we provide an effective limit theorem for the gap distribution of the saddle connections on the square torus, which relates to the spacing statistics of Farey fractions, and then generalize the result to lattice translation surfaces. Before introducing the main results, we will describe the main objects studied, and explain what we mean by the gap distribution of saddle connections.

A
translation surface
can be defined geometrically as a finite collection of disjoint polygons in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
with sides identified in parallel opposite pairs by translation. On a translation surface, there is a natural notion of length, area, and direction, inherited from the geometry of
\mathbb{C}
as identified with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A translation surface can alternatively thought of as a pair
(X,\omega)
where
X
is a Riemann surface and
\omega
is a holomorphic one-form. To go from the first definition to the second, we notice that our polygons can inherit the complex structure from
\mathbb{C}
as well as the one-form
dz
. To go from the second definition to the first, one can show that every translation surface
(X,\omega)
can by triangulated (see
[
Wri15
]
for more details).

The geometry on a translation surface locally looks like Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
except at finitely many
cone points
that have cone angle
2\pi n
for some
n\geq 2
. There cone points correspond to the zeros of the one-form
\omega
. A
saddle connection
is a straight line segment connecting two cone points that does not pass through any other cone points. If
\gamma
is an oriented saddle connection, then its
holonomy vector
is
\mathbf{v}_{\gamma}=\int_{\gamma}\omega
, the vector that describes the length and direction of
\gamma
.

The set of saddle connection vectors

\Lambda:=\{\mathbf{v}_{\gamma}:\gamma\text{ is a saddle connection of }(X,\omega)\}

(1.1)

is a discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}.
One can then ask about the growth and randomness of the angles of
\Lambda
.

Results of Masur (
[
Mas88
]
and
[
Mas90
]
) show that for almost every translation surface, the set
\Lambda\cap B(0,R)
of saddle connections of length at most
R
has quadratic upper and lower bounds. By results of Vorobets (
[
Vor05
]
), it is also known that for almost every
(X,\omega)
with respect to a natural measure on the space of translation surfaces called the Masur-Veech volume (see, for example,
[
Zor06
]
for more details), the angles of the saddle connections equidistribute in the circle. That is, as
R\rightarrow\infty
, the proportion of saddle connections with length
\leq R
with angle in an interval
I\subset S^{1}
converges to the length of
I
. Here, we normalize
S^{1}
to have total length
1
.

We note that equidistribution of a given sequence on
S^{1}
is akin to the sequence being generated by a sequence of independent, uniform random variables on
S^{1}
. To gain a deeper understanding as to just how random a given sequence is, we may study the spacing statistics of the sequence.

For dynamical reasons, it is often easier to consider the spacing statistics of slopes of saddle connections, rather than the angles. Following a convention of Athreya, Chaika, and Leliévre (
[
ACL15
]
), we let

\Lambda_{R}:=\{v=(a,b)\in\Lambda:0\leq b\leq a\leq R\}.

(1.2)

That is,
\Lambda_{R}
denotes the vectors in
\Lambda
with a positive
\leq R
horizontal component and slope between
0
and
1
. We will be concerned with the set of slopes of this set as
R\rightarrow\infty
.

To assess the randomness of saddle connection directions on
(X,\omega)
, we wish to understand the
limiting slope gap distribution
. To define the slope gap distribution of
(X,\omega)
, we first order the
N(R)
unique slopes of
\Lambda_{R}
in increasing order:

0\leq s_{R}^{(0)}<s_{R}^{(1)}<\ldots<s_{R}^{N(R)-1}\leq 1.

Here
N(R)
grows quadratically. We then consider the set of
renormalized gaps

\mathbb{G}_{R}:=\{R^{2}(s_{R}^{(i)}-s_{R}^{(i-1)}):1\leq i\leq N(R)\},

(1.3)

where the renormalization by
R^{2}
is to counter that the expected gap size decays like
R^{2}
.

We say that
F:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is the
slope gap distribution
of
(X,\omega)
if, for every
a<b
, we have that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\mathbb{G}_{R}\cap(a,b)|}{N(R)}=\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,dx.

It is known by work of Athreya and Chaika (
[
AC12
]
) that for almost every translation surface
(X,\omega)
with respect to the Masur-Veech volume, the slope gap distribution of
(X,\omega)
exists. If the sequence of slopes of saddle connections ordered by length were independent and uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
, then one would expect the slope gap distribution to be exponentially distributed. It is currently unknown if there exist any translation surfaces whose slope gap distribution is exponential, and the study of slope gap distributions of general translation surfaces is difficult.

Athreya and Cheung (
[
AC14
]
) computed the slope gap distribution of the square torus, which is also the gap distribution of the Farey fractions. Work of Uyanik and Work (
[
UW15
]
) as well as Kumanduri, Sanchez, and Wang (
[
KSW24
]
) gives an algorithm to compute the slope gap distributions of an important subclass of translation surfaces with many symmetries called
lattice surfaces
, also known as
Veech surfaces
. This work will be described in more detail in Section
2
.

While the existence of slope gap distributions is known in a few cases, to the best of our knowledge, there are no known results about how quickly renormalized slope gaps converge to the gap distribution. Our contribution is to make effective the convergence to the gap distribution in these two cases: the square torus and Farey fractions, and then general lattice surfaces.

For the following two theorems, let
N(R)
denote the number of unique slopes of saddle connections in
\Lambda_{R}
(defined in Equation
1.2
) and
\mathbb{G}_{R}
denote the renormalized slope gaps of
\Lambda_{R}
, as defined earlier in the introduction.

Theorem 1.1

(Effective gap distribution (torus and Farey fractions))
.

Let
F(x)
be the slope gap distribution of the square torus with one marked point, which is also the limiting gap distribution of the Farey fractions. Let
\mathbb{G}_{R}
be the renormalized gaps of the slopes
\Lambda_{R}
for the square torus, or the renormalized gaps of the Farey fractions of denominator
\leq R
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
0\leq a<b<\infty
,

\left|\frac{|\mathbb{G}_{R}\cap(a,b)|}{N(R)}-\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,dx\right|\leq C\log(R)R^{-\frac{1}{15}}

.

Remark 1.2
.

We note that renormalized gaps in the sets of Farey fractions

\mathcal{F}(Q):=\{\text{reduced fractions }\frac{p}{q}:(q,p)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},0<q\leq Q\}

are known to converge to a function called
Hall’s distribution
(see
[
AC14
]
and Section
2.2
for more details). The Farey fractions
\mathcal{F}(Q)
are also exactly the slopes
\Lambda_{Q}
of the square torus. In this way, it can be seen that the slope gap distribution of the square torus is the gap distribution of the Farey fractions, and the rate of convergence in Theorem
1.1
also applies to the convergence of the Farey fraction gaps to Hall’s distribution.

Theorem 1.3

(Effective gap distribution (lattice surface))
.

Let
F(x)
be the slope gap distribution of the lattice surface
(X,\omega)
. Then, for any
0\leq a<b<\infty
,

\left|\frac{|\mathbb{G}_{R}\cap(a,b)|}{N(R)}-\int_{a}^{b}F(x)\,dx\right|\leq\begin{cases}C\log(R)R^{-\frac{1}{15}}&\text{if }\Gamma\text{ is tempered}\\
C(s)R^{-\frac{1}{15}(1-s)}&\text{if }\Gamma\text{ is non-tempered.}\end{cases}

where the constant
C
depends on the choice of lattice surface, and if
\Gamma
is not tempered,
s
, where
\frac{1-s^{2}}{4}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
.

For our purposes, a
tempered lattice
is one such that
\frac{1}{4}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
, noninclusive of the
0
, whereas a non-tempered lattice is one such that the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
is strictly less than
\frac{1}{4}
. The spectral parameter
s
in Theorem
1.3
quantifies the spectral gap.

Remark 1.4
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
rely on the effective equidistribution results of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
. As Remark
1.7
indicates, the rate of decay in these latter results is actually better than stated. Consequently, the exponents in Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
are also better than stated. One can change the
\frac{1}{15}
to
\frac{1}{3}
in Theorem
1.1
for the torus. In Theorem
1.3
for general lattice surfaces, when
(X,\omega)
is periodic under the unstable horocycle flow, one can change
\frac{1}{15}
to
\frac{1}{4n}-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, where
n
is the smallest even integer greater than
\frac{1}{1-s}
and
\frac{1+s^{2}}{4}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
. In the case where
(X,\omega)
is not periodic under the unstable horocycle flow, one can change the
\frac{1}{15}
to
\frac{1}{9}
in Theorem
1.3
.

The proof of these theorems rests on the following effective equidistribution results for the intersection points of a family of long horocycles with a particular Poincaré section
\Omega
, which is a transversal to the horocycle flow. The horocycle flow, the transversal
\Omega
, their connection to gap distributions, and the natural measure
m
on
\Omega
will be discussed in Section
2
. Briefly, if
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the group of symmetries of
(X,\omega)
, then
\Omega\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
is the set of translation surfaces in the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
orbit of
(X,\omega)
that has a short (length
\leq 1
) horizontal saddle connection.

Theorem 1.5

(Effective equidistribution on the section (torus))
.

Let
H_{L}
be a the set of points corresponding to a segment of length
L
of an (unstable) periodic horocycle trajectory. Let
H_{L(t)}=g_{t}^{-1}H_{L}
, where
g_{t}
is the geodesic flow for time
t
, and
L(t)
denotes the length of
H_{L(t)}
. Let
\rho_{L(t)}=\frac{1}{\lvert H_{L(t)\cap\Omega}\rvert}\sum_{x\in H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega}\delta_{x}
and let
m
be the measure on the section

. For any compactly supported and bounded

\left|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-m(f)\right|\leq C\|f\|_{2}\log(L(t))L(t)^{-\frac{1}{30}}

where the constant
C

f
and the geometry of the section.

For general lattice surfaces, we have a similar effective equidistribution results with worse bounds.

C(s)\|f\|_{2}(L(t))^{-\frac{1}{30}(1-s)}&\text{if }\Gamma\text{ is non-tempered.}\end{cases}

where the constant

\Omega
, and if

is not tempered,

\frac{1-s^{2}}{4}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma

Remark 1.7
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6

\frac{1}{8n}-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, where
n
is the smallest even integer greater than
\frac{1}{1-s}
and
\frac{1+s^{2}}{4}
is the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
. Moreover, in the case of non-closed horocycles, one can change the
\frac{1}{30}

\frac{1}{18}

Remark 1.8
.

The bounds for lattice surfaces are, in general, worse because for many lattice surfaces,
\Gamma
is not a tempered lattice. Our arguments rely crucially the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
, and as a result, the appearance of a complementary series representation is an obstruction: the
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-action commutes with the Casimir operator on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
, and hence iterates of the action fall prey to the smallest element in the spectrum of the operator. It is well-known that this eigenvalue agrees with the bottom of the spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma
since complementary series representations are spherical.

Related Literature.
In
[
AC14
]

[
ACL15
]
for the golden L, by Uyanik and Work
[
UW15
]
for the regular octagon, and by Berman et al
[
BMMM+23
]
for general
2n
-gons. In
[
UW15
]
, Uyanik and Work also proposed an algorithm for computing the slope gap distributions of arbitrary lattice surfaces. That algorithm was later improved upon by Kumanduri, Sanchez, and Wang in
[
KSW24
]
. In
[
San22
]
, Sanchez proves properties about the gap distributions of double-slit tori, a family of non-lattice surfaces.

The proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
rests on the effective equidistribution horocycle translates, under the action of the geodesic flow. In the case where the horocycle is closed, then the effective result is due to Sarnak
[
Sar81
]
. In
[
Str13
]
, the effective equidistribution of generic horocycle arcs are considered. Theorems

and

, and the “Margulis Thickening Technique”. We recount the proof of Theorem

]

Theorems concerning the equidistribution of expanding translates of horospheres have been shown to hold in very general contexts, see for instance

Structure of the proofs of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
.
Recall that
H_{L(t)}
is a geodesic flow push of a horocycle segment
H_{L}
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
. The main technical step in proving an effective gaps result (Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
) is to understand the rate at which the counting measure
\rho_{L(t)}
of
H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega
converges to Lebesgue measure
m
on the transversal
\Omega\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
over a suitably regular class of test functions. More precisely, for sufficiently regular
f
, we estimate

\left|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-m(f)\right|.

(1.4)

Given a function
f
on
\Omega
, we define a particular ‘thickening’ of
f
to a function
\tilde{f}
, defined on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
.
The function
\tilde{f}
is chosen so that it is constant in unstable horocycle direction. As we will see in Section
2.4
,
\Omega
breaks into finitely many pieces
\Omega_{i}
where there are natural local
(a,b)
coordinates, and the thickening width,
w
, can be chosen depending on
(X,\omega)
so that
w
is less than the minimum horocycle return time on
\Omega
.

We then define

\displaystyle\nu_{L(t)}

\displaystyle:=\text{length measure on }H_{L(t)}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma,\text{ normalized so the total measure is }1

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle:=\text{Haar measure on }\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma,\text{normalised to be a probability measure.}

(1.6)

By multiple applications of the triangle inequality, finding an upper bound for (
1.4
) amounts to finding upper bounds for
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})|
,
|\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})-\mu(\tilde{f})|
, and
|\mu(\tilde{f})-m(f)|
, respectively.

For
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-m(f)|
, we use the relation between the length of
H_{L(t)}
and
|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|
as proven in Section
3
. This relation is derived from results about counting lattice points in certain regions of the plane. An upper bound for
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})|
is obtained using the effective equidistribution of horocycle translates. In order to apply the appropriate theorems, we must first smooth
\tilde{f}
. This procedure requires various technical lemmas, carried out in Section
4
. Finally,
|\mu(\tilde{f})-m(f)|=0
as shown in Proposition
3.3
. We note that there is a tension between when the decay rates of
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-m(f)|
coming from lattice counting methods or the decay rates of
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})|
coming from the effective equidistribution of long horocycles contribute the dominant term in the error rate of our effective gaps theorems. This is commented on more in Section
4
.

Outline of the Paper.

In Section
2
, we summarize the relevant existing work on the relation between slope gap distributions and the horocycle flow and how this can be used to compute the slope gap distribution of the Farey fractions and the square torus. This section also contains the proofs of some key propositions as well as our main effective gaps results, Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, assuming our effective equidistribution results, Theorems
1.5
and

4

1.5
and

, which are effective equidistribution results on the transversal

We note that there are two main goals of this paper: to prove an effective gaps result for the square torus and the Farey fractions, and to prove a more general effective gaps result for all Veech translation surfaces. A reader interested primarily in Farey fraction gaps could skip Sections

,

, and

Background and Effective Gaps

In this section, we introduce the necessary background on slope gap distributions and prove our effective gap distribution theorems. Most of the content in Sections
2.1
through
2.4
is not new, excepts that in Section
2.3
, we note that a key gap distribution proof from Uyanik and Work (
[
UW15
]
) does not work as stated for all lattice surfaces. We comment on why and remedy this. Section
2.5
contains two new propositions needed to prove our main theorems, and Section
2.6
contains the proofs of our main effective gaps theorems.

2.1
From the horocycle flow to slope gap distributions

There is a natural action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the space of translation surface coming from the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
M\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
(X,\omega)
is a translation surface made by gluing the polygons
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
, then
M\cdot(X,\omega)
is the translation surface made by gluing the polygons
MP_{1},\ldots,MP_{k}
in the same gluing pattern. Here,
M
acts on each
P_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
via the linear action of
M
on the plane.

Sometimes,
M(X,\omega)
and
(X,\omega)
are equivalent after cut and paste operations. In this case,
M
is said to be in the
Veech group

\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. That is, the Veech group is the subgroup of all matrices that stabilize
(X,\omega)
. When
\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a lattice (that is, when
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)
has finite volume),
(X,\omega)
is said to be a
lattice surface
. Lattice surfaces are a measure zero set in the space of all translation surfaces, but are important examples of translation surfaces, in part because they have nice dynamical properties. In particular, it is known that the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
orbit of a translation surface
(X,\omega)
is closed in its
stratum
of the moduli space, which includes all translation surfaces with the same cone angle data, if and only if
(X,\omega)
is a lattice surface. This closed orbit property makes computing the slope gap distributions of lattice surfaces more tractable than for general translation surfaces, where the orbit closure is higher dimensional and dynamics on the orbit closure is more complicated. For the rest of this paper, all translation surfaces
(X,\omega)
are assumed to be lattice surfaces.

Athreya and Chaika (
[
AC12
]
) have shown that the slope gap distributions of lattice surfaces have no support near zero and therefore cannot be exponential. However, we now know how to compute the slope gap distribution. An algorithm to compute this limiting gap distribution for lattice surfaces was first proved by Uyanik and Work (
[
UW15
]
), and then was later improved upon by Kumanduri, Sanchez, and Wang (
[
KSW24
]
). This algorithm relies on renormalization techniques that allow us to relate the slope gap distribution of a lattice surface
(X,\omega)
to the horocycle flow on the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
orbit of
(X,\omega)
. These techniques were first exploited by Athreya and Cheung on the torus
[
AC14
]
.

Let
h_{s}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
-s&1\end{bmatrix}
denote the (unstable) horocycle flow and let
g_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{bmatrix}
denote the geodesic flow. Note that
h_{s}
acts on translation surfaces via a vertical shear. Recall that
\Lambda
is the set of holonomy vectors of
(X,\omega)
Then, the space

\Omega:=\{g\Gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma:g\Lambda\text{ contains a short (length $\leq 1$) horizontal vector}\}

(2.1)

is a transversal for the horocycle flow, and
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
is a suspension over
\Omega
where the height above a point is the return time of the
h_{s}
flow to
\Omega
. Another way to think of
\Omega
is as the set of surfaces
g(X,\omega)
in the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
orbit of
(X,\omega)
that have a short horizontal saddle connection.
\Omega
naturally inherits a measure
m
from the Haar measure
\mu
on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
dm\,ds=d\mu
, where
s
is the horocycle direction. We can then define the return time function
R:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
by letting
R(x)
be the first return time of
x\in\Omega
back to
\Omega

Let

to

As shown in the proof of Theorem

For any

be the characteristic function on

3
Horocycle length and intersection point bounds

One of the main steps in the proof of an effective gaps theorem is to prove a result about effective equidistribution of long horocycles on the transversal
\Omega
. The main tool here is a thickening argument. One of the steps of this thickening argument is a comparison between the horocycle hitting measure
\rho_{L(t)}
on
\Omega
and the horocycle length measure
\nu_{L(t)}
on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
, which is the topic of this section.

3.1
Spaces and measures

As described in Section
2.4
, for a lattice surface
(X,\omega)
, the transversal
\Omega\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
breaks up into finitely many pieces that are triangles in local
(a,b)
coordinates. Then,
\Omega
is a transversal to the horocycle flow and
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
can be thought of as a suspension over
\Omega
. Locally, over any piece of
\Omega
with
(a,b)
coordinates,
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
can be parametrized in
(a,b,s)
coordinates where the
s
comes from moving in
h_{s}
direction. The height above each
(a,b)
in this suspension is given by
R(a,b)
, the return time of the
h_{s}
flow starting at
(a,b)
in
\Omega
.

When working in local
(a,b,s)
coordinates, we sometimes refer to
\Delta
as the
parameter space
for
\Omega
and
S
as the
suspension space
for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
. As defined in Equation
2.8
, let
H_{L(t)}
denote a
g_{t}^{-1}
push of length
L
horocycle segment based at
(X,\omega)
. We recall that
H_{L(t)}
has length
L(t)=e^{t}\cdot L
, as commented upon in Remark
2.3
.

Then, on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
we will work with the following two measures, both normalized so that the measure of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
is
1
:

1.

The Haar measure
\mu
. In local
(a,b,s)
coordinates,

\mu=c_{\mu}\,da\,db\,ds

(3.1)

where the constant
c_{\mu}
is chosen so that
\mu(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{R(a,b)}c_{\mu}\,ds\,da\,db=1
.

2.

The horocycle measure
\nu_{L(t)}
, which is the measure supported on the horocycle segment
H_{L}(t)
, normalized so that
\nu_{L(t)}(H_{L(t)})=1
.

On the transversal
\Omega\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
, we have the following two measures, also normalized so that the measure of
\Omega
is
1
:

1.

The Lebesgue measure
m
. In local coordinates,

m=c_{m}\,da\,db,

(3.2)

where
c_{m}
is chosen so that
m(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}c_{m}\,da\,db=1
.

2.

The horocycle counting measure

\rho_{L(t)}=\frac{1}{\lvert H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega\rvert}\sum_{x\in H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega}\delta_{x}.

Remark 3.1
.

For the square torus, one can check (see
[
AC14
]
) that
c_{m}=2
and
c_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}
.

Remark 3.2
.

There is a map from the interior of the suspension space
S
to the a subset of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see Athreya-Cheung for the restriction of this map to the transversal
[
AC14
]
). The map is smooth, and consequently, a function that has been smoothed in the suspension space will pullback to a smooth function in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

In Proposition
2.10
, we saw that there exists a
w>0
such that the return time
R(a,b)>w
for all points
(a,b)
in every piece of
\Omega
. For the torus, the explicit return time function
R(a,b)=\frac{1}{ab}
from Theorem
2.2
and the explicit parametrization of
\Omega
given in Equation
2.4
give that we can take, for example,
w=\frac{1}{2}
.

Given a a measurable function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define a function
\tilde{f}
that is thickened in the direction of the unstable horocycle flow
h_{s}
as follows:

\tilde{f}(a,b,t):=\begin{cases}\tfrac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}\cdot w}f(a,b)\chi_{[0,w]}(t)&if $(a,b)\in\Omega$,\\
0&otherwise\end{cases}.

(3.3)

Here,
\chi_{[0,w]}
denotes the indicator of the interval
[0,w]
. The constant multiplier in the definition of
\tilde{f}(a,b,t)
is chosen so that the following proposition holds.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given a measurable
f
, a
w
so that the return time to
\Omega
is bounded below by
w
,
m=c_{m}\,da\,db
on
\Omega
and
\mu=c_{\mu}\,da\,db\,ds
on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
in local
(a,b,s)
coordinates, and a thickening
\tilde{f}
as defined in Equation
3.3
,
\mu(\tilde{f})=m(f)
.

Proof.

In local
(a,b)
coordinates on the pieces of
\Omega
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\tilde{f})=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{R(a,b)}\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}\cdot w}f(a,b)\chi_{[0,w]}(s)\,c_{\mu}\,ds\,da\,db=\frac{c_{\mu}\cdot w}{c_{m}}\cdot\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}\cdot w}\int_{\Omega}f(a,b)\,c_{m}\,da\,db=m(f),

where the factor of
w
can be taken out of the integral because
R(a,b)>w
for all
(a,b)
in
\Omega
.
∎

The goal of this section is to relate
\rho_{L(t)}(f)
and
\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})
. To do so, we notice that

\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})=\int_{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}\cdot w}f(a,b)\chi_{[0,w]}d\nu_{L(t)}=\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}\cdot w}\cdot w\cdot\frac{1}{L(t)}\sum_{x\in H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega}f(x).

Since
\rho_{L(t)}(f)=\frac{1}{|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|}\sum_{x\in H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega}f(x)
, we have that

\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})=\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}}\frac{|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|}{L(t)}\rho_{L(t)}(f).

(3.4)

To understand the relationship between
\rho_{L(t)}(f)
and
\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})
, we must therefore examine the relationship between
|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|
and
L(t)
. We first look at the case of the square torus in Section
3.2
before moving on to general lattice surfaces in Section
3.3
.

3.2
Comparisons for the Square Torus

We begin with the case where
(X,\omega)
is the area one square torus. In this case, the slopes of the saddle connections are rational and the slope gap distribution of the square torus is exactly the gap distribution of the Farey fractions. The gap distribution of the square torus was studied deeply by Athreya and Cheung in
[
AC14
]
.

In their paper, they consider the Farey fractions of denominator
\leq Q
, given by

\mathcal{F}(Q)=\{\text{reduced fractions }\frac{p}{q}\text{ with }0\leq\frac{p}{q}\leq 1,q\leq Q\}.

When
(X,\omega)
is the unit area square torus, these are exactly the slopes in

\Lambda_{Q}=\{v=(a,b)\in\Lambda:0\leq b\leq a\leq Q\},

where
\Lambda
are the saddle connection vectors of
(X,\omega)
.

For the rest of our discussion on the torus, we will use the general notation of the pushed horocycles
H_{L(t)}
rather than the
Q
of Athreya-Cheung, which was specific to the square torus and Farey fractions. In Section
3.3
, we will use similar notation to do similar comparisons for general lattice surfaces.

The Veech group
\Gamma
of the square torus is
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. In this space, there is a family of closed horocycles corresponding to the single cusp of
X_{2}=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. We let
H_{L}
be the closed horocycle of length
L=1
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The following Theorem of Athreya and Cheung allows us to relate
|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|
and
L(t)=e^{t}
, the length of
H_{L(t)}
. To relate this back to the notation of the paper of Athreya and Cheung, we have that
t=2\log(Q)
,
|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|=P(a,b)
, and
L(t)=s(a,b)
where
(a,b)\in\Omega
is
h_{s}
-periodic with period
Q^{2}
.

Remark 3.4
.

The next few results are asymptotics in terms of
L(t)
. We note that since
L(t)=e^{t}
, these asymptotics could be written in terms of
t
instead. However, we keep the
L(t)
to make explicit the dependence on length.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
AC14
]
Theorem 1.5 therein)
.

Let
H_{L(t)}
be the periodic horocycle in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of period
L(t)=e^{t}
. Then,

|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|=N\left(\lfloor\sqrt{L(t)}\rfloor\right),

(3.5)

where
N(k)
is the number of Farey fractions of denominator
\leq k
.

Using this theorem, we have the following corollary, which is an intermediate step to relating
\rho_{L(t)}(f)
with
\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
H_{L(t)}
be the periodic horocycle in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of length
L(t)=e^{t}
. Then,

|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}L(t)+O\left(\sqrt{L(t)}\log(L(t))\right).

(3.6)

Proof.

Let
\varphi(n)
be Euler’s totient function and
\Phi(k)
be the summatory totient function. Since
\mathcal{F}(Q)=\mathcal{F}(Q-1)+\varphi(Q)
and
\mathcal{F}(1)=|\{\frac{0}{1},\frac{1}{1}\}|=2
, it follows that

N(k)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{k}\varphi(n)=1+\Phi(k).

(3.7)

The summatory totient function is known (for example, see
[
HW08
]
) to have the asymptotic expansion

\Phi(k)=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}k^{2}+O(k\log k).

(3.8)

Putting this together, we have that

\displaystyle|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|

\displaystyle=N\left(\lfloor\sqrt{L(t)}\rfloor\right)=1+\Phi\left(\sqrt{L(t)+O(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}L(t)+O\left(\sqrt{L(t)}\log(L(t))\right).

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
H_{L(t)}
be the periodic horocycle on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with length
L(t)=e^{t}
, let
f
be a bounded, measurable function, and let
\tilde{f}
be
f
thickened by
w=\frac{1}{2}
in the horocycle flow direction. Then, there exists a constant,
K=K(f)
, independent of
t
, such that

|\rho_{L(t)}(f)-\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})|\leq K\left(\frac{\log(L(t))}{\sqrt{L(t)}}\right)

Proof.

We set
C:=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}
and
\mathcal{E}(t):=\sqrt{L(t)}\log(L(t))
.

Then, from Corollary
3.6
, there exists a constant such that, for
t
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|=C\cdot L(t)+O(\mathcal{E}(t)).

(3.9)

From Equation
3.4
, we have that

\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})=\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}}\frac{|H_{L(t)}\cap\Omega|}{L(t)}\rho_{L(t)}(f).

As remarked upon earlier, for the square torus, one can check (see
[
AC14
]
) that
c_{m}=2
and
c_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}
.

It follows then that

\nu_{L(t)}(\tilde{f})=\frac{c_{m}}{c_{\mu}}\cdot\frac{C\cdot L(t)+O(\mathcal{E}(t))}{L(t)}\rho_{L(t)}(f)=\rho_{L(t)}(f)\left(1+O\left(\frac{c_{m}\cdot C}{c_{\mu}}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{E}(t)}{L(t)}\right)\right)

∎

Let
M_{f}=\sup|f|
. Since
\rho_{L(t)}
is normalized so that
\rho_{L}(t)(\Omega)=1
, we have that
|\rho_{L(t)}(f)|\leq M_{f}
. Then, by substituting in
\mathcal{E}(t)=\sqrt{L(t)}\log(L(t))
, the statement of the Lemma follows for

depending on
c_{m}
,

,
C
,

, and the implicit constant in the big-O term coming from Equation

. Thus, the constant

depends on

.

3.3
Comparisons for lattice surfaces

Following the convention of Athreya-Chaika-Leliévre in
[

As defined in Section

Theorem 3.8

\frac{1}{m_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(S_{t})}\int_{S_{t}}f(gg_{t}^{-1}h\Gamma)dm_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(g)=\frac{1}{m_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(S_{0})}\int_{S_{0}}f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\,dm_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(g)\\

Now, observe that the projection of
S_{0}
into
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma
is injective, provided we pick
\mathcal{O}_{P}
sufficiently small. Let
P_{0}=\{g(h\Gamma):g\in S_{0}\}
be the projection. Then,

\frac{1}{m_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(S_{0})}\int_{S_{0}}f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\,dm_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})}(g)=\frac{1}{\mu(P_{0})}\int_{P_{0}}f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\,d\mu(g(h\Gamma))\\

Now, observe that the integral is a matrix coefficient. As
t\to\infty
, we expect exponential decay.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu(P_{0})}\int_{P_{0}}f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\,d\mu(g(h\Gamma))

\displaystyle=\int_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}(g(h\Gamma))}{\mu(P_{0})}\,d\mu(g(h\Gamma))

\displaystyle=\left\langle\pi(g_{t})f,\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}}{\mu(P_{0})}\right\rangle

Let
\chi
denote
\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}}{\mu(P_{0})}
. We cannot directly apply Theorem
A.1
since
\chi
is not
K
-smooth. Let
\psi
be a continuous approximation of
\chi
where for any small
\varepsilon>0
,
\int|\chi-\psi|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Further, let
\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi
be a smooth approximation of
\psi
. Then, by applying Theorem
A.1
, we can deduce the desired result.

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\pi(g_{t})f,\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}}{\mu(P_{0})}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}\left|f(g_{t}^{-1}g(h\Gamma))\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}(g(h\Gamma))}{\mu(P_{0})}\right|\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}\left|\pi(g_{t})f\right|\cdot\left|\chi-\psi\right|\,d\mu+\int_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}\left|\pi(g_{t})f\right|\cdot\left|\psi-\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi\right|\,d\mu

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\int_{SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\Gamma}\left|\pi(g_{t})f\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi\right|\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\mathcal{S}_{K}(\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi)

where
M_{f}
is the maximum of
f
. If
\Gamma
is tempered, then
b(t)
is
te^{-\frac{t}{2}}
and
C
is independent of
s
. If
\Gamma
is non-tempered,
b(t)
is
e^{-\frac{t}{2}(1-s)}
and
C
depends on
s
. It remains to approximate the
K
-Sobolev norm of the function
\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{K}(\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi)^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi\|_{2}^{2}+\|d(\omega)\phi_{\varepsilon}*\psi\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\cdot\sup_{g}\left(d\omega(\phi)\right)\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}

Observe that by an application of Cauchy-Schwarz, and using that
m_{P}^{l}(\mathcal{O}_{P})=\delta
we have

\|\psi\|_{2}\leq\|\chi\|_{2}+\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{\delta L}+\varepsilon,

so we have, for sufficiently small
\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\pi(g_{t})f,\frac{\chi_{P_{0}}}{\mu(P_{0})}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\left(\|\psi\|_{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{1+\sup_{g}d\omega(\phi)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\left((\|\chi\|_{2}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{1+\sup_{g}d\omega(\phi)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\left(\left(\frac{1}{\delta L}+\varepsilon\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{1+\sup_{g}d\omega(\phi)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\left(\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta L}\right)\left(\sqrt{1+\sup_{g}d\omega(\phi)}\right)\right)\text{.}

Hence, combining all of our estimates, we have

\left|\frac{1}{e^{t}L}\int_{0}^{e^{t}L}f(h_{s}g_{t}^{-1}h\Gamma)\,ds\right|\leq\varepsilon M_{f}+Cb(t)\mathcal{S}_{K}(f)\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}\right)+D\sqrt{\delta}

for some constants
C,D>0
. To conclude, pick
\varepsilon=e^{-\frac{1}{10}(1-s)}t
and
\delta=e^{-\frac{1}{5}(1-s)}t
. As
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
, the uniform convergence of this matrix coefficient to the desired integral gives the result.
∎
