Study protocol of the Speech of children After Neonatal Encephalopathy (SANE): a single-centre prospective cohort study
Authors: Dóra Luca Bodócs, Anett Ragó, Nelli Farkas, Andrea Lakatos, Bence Kas, Ágnes Lukács, Ágnes Jermendy, Márk Hernádfői, Emma Lökkös, Norbert Kiss, András Bánvölgyi, János Major, Péter Hegyi, Miklós Szabó, Zsuzsanna Varga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLanguage development is a key determinant of academic achievement and psychosocial outcome. Due to the therapeutic hypothermia procedure, 75% of children diagnosed with moderate to severe neonatal encephalopathy (E) survive without serious neurological sequelae. Several studies have already revealed the overall cognitive deficits of these infants; however, their long-term language developmental specificities and their attendant neonatal risk markers remain under-investigated.Methods and analysesIn our single-centre prospective cohort study (n≈128), we will include only those children with perinatal asphyxia who meet the stepwise criteria of the Total Body Hypothermia (TOBY) study and who survive NE without functional motor deficits and moderate/severe overall cognitive deficits at the second-year follow-up examination. The NE survivors included and cared for in the Level III Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of the Pediatric Center, Semmelweis University, Budapest, Hungary, were born between 2017 and 2022. Clinical, laboratory, electrographic and neuroimaging data will be extracted from their medical records and will be evaluated retrospectively. Patients will be followed up to assess overall cognitive and language development between 4 and 8 years. The association between neonatal biomarkers and developmental language disorder (DLD) will be analysed with binary logistic regression models. Machine learning techniques will be used to develop predictive models for DLD. For modelling the longitudinal changes between early (2 years) and later (4–8 years) cognitive and language status, mixed effects models will be applied.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol was approved by the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy (NNGYK/24844-9/2025) on 21 May 2025. Results of this study will be disseminated through multiple channels. We will publish the findings in international peer-reviewed scientific journals and present them at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT07136636.

Introduction

Neonatal encephalopathy (NE) affects 0.5–3 per 1000 newborns in economically developed countries.1 With the advancement of therapeutic procedures involving moderate whole-body hypothermia, which provides successful neuroprotection,2 more than 75% of neonates with NE survive without severe neurological or cognitive impairments.3 Domain-specific language development has received less attention in infants who survive NE.4 Language abilities play a critical role in learning, social and emotional abilities as well as academic success and are also at the heart of parental concerns in infants born with a perinatal insult.5

Our group has recently described that in NE survivors without cerebral palsy, only 41% had favourable cognitive and language development at the age of 20 months, whereas 25% had early language delay (ELD).6 By examining the long-term stability of this group at 4–8 years of age, we aim to characterise the developmental trajectories of NE survivors and determine the environmental factors that influence these neurocognitive long-term outcomes. This approach will allow us to explore the cases where ELD persists, emerging in developmental language disorder (DLD).

The incidence of DLD is unknown in those infants with HIE who survive without severe cognitive and neurological impairments.6 DLD becomes recognisable from the age of 4 or 5 years and is diagnosed in relation to intellectual development. A typical profile of children with DLD is a significant language deficit with concurrent average cognitive development (IQ≥70), normal nonverbal intelligence, adequate auditory acuity and the absence of gross neurological disabilities.7 In normative samples, the prevalence of DLD is 7%–8% in 5-year-old children.8 9

Previous research has provided valuable insights into language development following NE.4 10–13 However, neither of these studies involved the concept of DLD in their work. The methodological differences across these studies highlight the need for a more comprehensive and systematic assessment, the reasons for which are listed as follows: (1) testing a homogeneous sample regarding the applied hypothermia; (2) using a comprehensive standardised language assessment for defining the language outcomes and the underlying language deficits. To our knowledge, no studies have systematically investigated the structural, content and memory components of language, or the receptive and expressive abilities spanning phonology, lexicon, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics in NE survivors without cerebral palsy (CP) and moderate/severe cognitive delay.

Early predictive marker is essential to enable timely identification of NE survivors with otherwise favourable overall cognitive outcomes who are at risk of DLD.12 This subgroup would likely benefit from early targeted language-specific interventions. According to a recent meta-analysis, the combination of electrographic (amplitude-integrated electroencephalography—aEEG) and neuroimaging (MRI) markers provides the most reliable prediction of early neurodevelopmental outcomes in infants with NE.14 From a neonatal clinical perspective, a key question is which early clinical signs observed in the first days of life best predict long-term language outcomes. White matter microstructural development evaluated at 6 years of age has been correlated with communication abilities among NE survivors without CP.12 However, this study does not answer the question of whether white matter abnormalities already detectable at the acute phase of neonatal encephalopathy can be good predictors of long-term language development.

A diffusion restriction in the splenium part of the corpus callosum (CC) is considered a comprehensive marker of white-matter structural abnormalities, which can be objectively measured by the minimum apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value. In particular, diffusion inhibition in the splenial part of the CC has been recognised as a strong early neuroradiological marker of adverse neurological outcomes.15 16 The overarching goal of our study is therefore to find the predictive markers of DLD, combining neonatal clinical and neuroradiological markers with high specificity and sensitivity. The development of such a predictive model would enable early risk identification and early intervention, mitigating the risk of language and learning disabilities.

Research questions

Our goal is to conduct a single-centre cohort study that integrates a retrospective analysis of clinical data representing HIE severity and previously acquired imaging, and a prospective longitudinal neuropsychological follow-up in moderate to severe NE survivors.

We propose three research questions that we will examine as part of the Speech of children After Neonatal Encephalopathy (SANE) study. Our first research question is: Which neonatal clinical and neuroradiological markers predict the risk of DLD? The second research question is: What are the long-term overall cognitive, language and neurobehavioural outcomes in NE survivors without moderate/severe cognitive delay and without CP? Third research question: How do early (2 years) overall cognitive and language outcomes relate to later (4–8 years) outcomes, and what neonatal and environmental factors influence these developmental trajectories?

Materials and methods

Design and setting

This is a single-centre cohort study combining both the retrospective and prospective aspects of the neurocognitive, and language development of children up to 4–8 years who have been previously treated for moderate to severe HIE with whole body hypothermia. This protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) reporting recommendations where applicable. The completed observational study will be reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement (see online supplemental material S1).

Study participants were born between 2017 and 2022 and cared for at the Level III Neonatal Intensive Care Unit at the Paediatric Centre of Semmelweis University, Budapest, a regional centre offering therapeutic hypothermia for neonates not born at our centre. The estimated sample size is 128. Data recorded as part of standard clinical care—including EEG, clinical, laboratory and neuroimaging data—will be analysed retrospectively. The studies planned for children aged 4–8 years will be recorded prospectively and conducted specifically as a part of the SANE study. It will also include clinical, laboratory, electrographic and neuroimaging data extracted from patient medical records. Figure 1 illustrates the design of the series of examinations of which the SANE study is a part. Data collection for the short-term outcome has been completed and published,6 while participant recruitment and data collection for the long-term outcome within the framework of the SANE study is planned to begin on 1 April 2025, and scheduled to end by 31 December 2026. Written informed parental consent will be obtained for all participants. Families will be contacted by phone and by mail with an invitation to participate in the follow-up assessments. We expect to have our first results in January 2027.

Study population

Infants were eligible if they met the stepwise TOBY study criteria17 and were born between 2017 and 2022. Infants were excluded if they presented with congenital abnormalities, metabolic disorders or had experienced sudden unexpected postnatal collapse and a polylingual language environment. Following hypothermia treatment, newborns with confirmed hearing loss on post-treatment audiological screening were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included the presence of functional motor deficits - diagnosis of CP in line with the recommendation of the Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in Europe Working Group18—and/or moderate to severe cognitive impairment—Bayley-II mental development index ≤70 at 2 years of age.6 Taking into account the inclusion and exclusion criteria between these time points, the expected number of participants is 128.

Variables predicting long-term language developmental outcome

Blood gas values like pH, base deficit, PCO2, HCO3 and lactate were registered as soon as possible after birth, and subsequently every 6 hours until 72 hours of postnatal age. At birth, the extent of deviation of these values from normal is an important marker of the severity of the asphyxia insult; it is also closely associated with the severity of the NE. In addition, Apgar scores and clinical scores assessing the severity of encephalopathy (Thompson score)19 before and during therapeutic hypothermia will also be assessed. The time of onset of hypothermia treatment will also be extracted from the medical records as a potentially relevant variable affecting the outcomes.20

Amplitude-integrated electroencephalography

Cerebral activity was recorded using continuous single-channel (biparietal, P3-P4) aEEG monitoring (Olympic CFM 6000 monitor; Natus Medical or EEG-1200K; Nihon Kohden). Recording started as soon as possible, but always before 6 hours of age, and continued for the duration of therapeutic hypothermia and rewarming, for 84 hours of study in total. The aEEG background activity was scored for each 6-hour interval by two neonatologists blinded to the participants’ clinical history, according to the Hellström–Westas criteria21: continuous normal voltage, discontinuous normal voltage, burst suppression, continuous low voltage and flat trace. Longitudinal analysis of the background activity, in combination with the onset of sleep-wake cycling will be an important predictor of outcomes.22

Brain MRI

MRI scans were acquired between postnatal days 4 and 5 (after completion of therapeutic hypothermia) in all neonates, adhering to institutional sedation and monitoring protocols. Imaging was performed using a Philips Ingenia 3.0T scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) with an 8-channel Sensitivity Encoding (SENSE) head coil and the following sequences: T1-weighted 3D turbo field echo (TR (Repetition Time)≈8 ms, TE (Echo Time)≈4 ms, isotropic voxel 1 mm); axial/coronal T2-weighted turbo spin echo (TR≈5500 ms, TE≈100 ms, slice thickness 3 mm, 0.3 mm interslice gap); axial susceptibility-weighted imaging (TR≈31 ms, TE≈20 ms, slice thickness 2 mm); axial diffusion-weighted imaging (b=0/1000 s/mm²); and single-voxel proton MR spectroscopy via Point Resolved Spectroscopy (PRESS) (TE=144 ms) from the left thalamus.

Weeke MRI score

Our research will apply the recently developed Weeke scoring system to the existing MRI images (retrospective analysis). The Weeke MRI scoring system is a novel scoring system, which includes diffusion weighted images to predict neurodevelopmental outcomes in newborns who have experienced perinatal asphyxia and were treated with therapeutic hypothermia.23 The scoring system identifies three areas of injury, which are the following:

Deep grey matter, including the thalamus, basal ganglia, posterior limb of the internal capsule, brainstem, perirolandic cortex and hippocampus.

Deep grey matter, including the thalamus, basal ganglia, posterior limb of the internal capsule, brainstem, perirolandic cortex and hippocampus.

Cerebral white matter/cortex, which includes the cortex, cerebral white matter, optic radiations, CC, punctate white matter lesions and parenchymal haemorrhage.

Cerebral white matter/cortex, which includes the cortex, cerebral white matter, optic radiations, CC, punctate white matter lesions and parenchymal haemorrhage.

The cerebellum, where the cerebellum itself and cerebellar haemorrhage are scored.

The cerebellum, where the cerebellum itself and cerebellar haemorrhage are scored.

For each area, the presence and severity of the injury are measured. If there is no injury in the given area, 0 points are assigned; if the injury extends to less than 50% of the area, the score is 1, and if it extends beyond 50%, 2 points are given. Furthermore, if the injury is unilateral, 1 point is assigned, while bilateral injuries receive 2 points. The scoring also takes into account the presence of intraventricular or subdural haemorrhages as well as sino-venous thrombosis. A score of 0 points is given if these are absent, and 1 point if any of them are present. As a result, the total score consists of 4 subscores: 23 deep grey matter points, 21 cerebral white matter/cortex points, 8 cerebellum points and the presence of intraventricular or subdural haemorrhages and sino-venous thrombosis with 3 points. The maximum total score is 55, and a higher score indicates more severe brain injury/damage. The neonatal MRI scans will be evaluated by AL, who will be blinded to all clinical and outcome data of the patients. To ensure reliability in our project, we will select a random sample of 20 children whose MRI scans will be independently scored by two radiologists blinded to all clinical and outcome data, inter-rater reliability of the total and subscores will be quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients.

Measurement of the ADC values

In the context of the planned project, we are conducting ADC value measurements using region of interest (ROI) evaluation of the splenium of the CC. Our neuroradiologist (AL) will place regions of interest for ADC extraction. Image postprocessing and analysis will be performed using a Philips IntelliSpace Portal, Philips Concerto V.12.1 with the MR Diffusion Postprocessing Package (Philips Medical Systems). ADC maps will be calculated from diffusion-weighted images acquired with two b-values (b=0/1000 s/mm²). The MR Diffusion package provides a default layout of two viewports: source image and real-time parametric diffusion maps. Colour look-up table (LUT) settings for parametric diffusion maps will use the ASIST (Acute Stroke Imaging Standardization Group) LUT, which ranges from black (minimum ADC) through light blue, green, yellow and orange to red (maximum ADC). ROI analysis of the splenium of the CC will involve at least three elliptical measurements, with the minimum ADC value recorded as the lowest among them (unit: ×10⁻³ mm²/s).

Comprehensive neurodevelopmental outcome at 4–8 years of age

The neurocognitive outcomes of the study cohort will be administered once between 4 and 8 years of age by the following tests and parental questionnaires (see online supplemental material table S1). The assessments will be administered by trained psychologists and certified speech therapists, who will be blinded to the patients’ clinical history. We will administer the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV),24 which measures Full-Scale IQ, verbal IQ and performance IQ from the ages of 4 years to 7 years 3 months. Above this age, we will administer the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Fourth Edition (WISC-IV).25 Furthermore, we will use the Complex Test of Spoken Language Skills (KOBAK).26 The test consists of 16 subtests assessing expressive and receptive language functions (verbal expression and comprehension) at the following levels: phonology, lexical semantics, morphology, syntax and pragmatics; speech motor functions and verbal working memory. The children participating in the study undergo regular developmental neurology follow-ups at our clinic by a multidisciplinary team (neurologist, physiotherapist, psychologist).

In the context of the neurodevelopmental examination, we will also administer several parental questionnaires (1): the Socioeconomic questionnaire for the measurement of the parental educational level, income, etc, (2) the Jaworsky questionnaire, (3) the Social Communication Questionnaire, (4) ScreenQ, (5) ADHD Rating Scale and (6) the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire. Online supplemental material table S1 summarises the subscales and outcomes of interest of these tests.

Statistical analysis

Approximately 128 eligible children are expected to meet the inclusion criteria. Expected attrition rate is 10%. As this study includes all eligible children born between 2017 and 2022 who meet the predefined eligibility criteria, the available sample size is fixed by the source population rather than determined by recruitment targets.

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics. Continuous variables will be described using means and SD or medians and quartiles, while categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation if the proportion of missing values exceeds 5%. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to compare results from imputed and complete-case datasets. Statistical significance will be set at a two-sided p value<0.05, and 95% CIs will be reported for all estimates. All analyses will be performed using R (V.4.2.0).

First research question To assess the association between neonatal biomarkers and DLD, binary logistic regression models will be employed. Based on theoretical consideration, the predictor variables for the prediction of DLD are the following: the Weeke Total Score,23 maternal educational level,27 sex, gestational age,28 aEEG normalisation within 48 hours,29 CC ADC values, first base excess, daily screen time exposure and the vocabulary total score at 2 years of age.27 Interaction terms will be considered to explore combined effects. Model performance will be evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC). To determine an optimal cut-off value of DLD, Youden’s Index will be used, and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) will be reported. To predict DLD status, several supervised machine learning (ML) classification algorithms will be developed and compared, including Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Support Vector Machines (SVM). Hyperparameter optimisation will be performed using grid search, and model performance will be assessed using stratified k-fold cross-validation. The best-performing model will be selected based on overall predictive performance. Evaluation metrics will include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, area under the curve (AUC), receiver operating characteristic (ROC), PPV and NPV. Confusion matrices will be generated to assess classification performance. To improve model interpretability, feature importance measures, including SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values and permutation importance, will be calculated. Partial dependence plots will be used to visualise the effects of individual predictors on model predictions. Algorithms to be tested include logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting machines (eg, XGBoost), and SVM. Model evaluation will be conducted using stratified k-fold cross-validation, and performance metrics will include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and AUC. Confusion matrices will be generated to assess classification performance. Model interpretability will be addressed using feature importance measures such as SHAP values and permutation importance. Partial dependence plots will be used to visualise the effect of individual predictors. Hyperparameter tuning will be performed using grid search.

Second research question Group comparisons between favourable intellectual and language development (FD), delayed language development (DLD), and adverse cognitive and language development (AD) subgroups will be conducted using independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, Mann–Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, depending on the number of subgroups and on the distribution of the data, and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. These variables are the following: sex, gestational age, birth weight, first pH, first base excess, first serum lactate level, Apgar scores at 1, 5 and 10 min, the Thompson score, the timing of hypothermia initiation, the Weeke Total score, the deep grey matter subscore, the cortex/white matter subscore, the cerebellum subscore, maternal educational level and the deprivation index. In addition, all relevant subscales of the WISC-IV, WPPSI-IV and KOBAK assessments will be included in the comparative analyses. The cut-off values used to distinguish between adverse and favourable intellectual outcomes will be 70 points for the WPPSI/WISC scales and 85 points for the KOBAK test.

Third research question For modelling the longitudinal changes between early (2 years) and later (4–8 years) cognitive and language status, mixed effects models will be conducted as predictors. At 2 years of age, we examined whether children who scored above 70 on the Bayley Scales of Infant Development Second (BSID-II) mental development index or the Bayley Scales of Infant Development Third (BSID-III) cognitive composite score, remained within the same range on the later WPPSI-IV and WISC-IV assessment, meaning that their Total IQ scores were higher than 70, or showed a decline. In parallel, we evaluated language trajectories by identifying children whose MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventory (H-CDI-II) expressive vocabulary score fell below the 20th percentile at 2 years, and assessed how their language outcomes evolved on the preschool and early school age KOBAK test, if their performance at the Global Language Skill subscale fell below 85 points or above. ‘Change’ in outcome is defined as a shift in developmental classification between the two assessment points in the two fields separately (overall cognitive and language development). Predictor variables will include socioeconomic variables (eg, maternal education) and neonatal variables like first pH, first serum lactate level and base excess, Thompson score, aEEG normalisation and the Weeke Total score. To conduct predictive models, the above-mentioned ML techniques will be used as well.

Data management

The clinical data of newborns undergoing hypothermia treatment for asphyxia at the Department of Paediatrics, Semmelweis University, are extracted from medical records and stored in a web-based database system for research purposes (iSORT system (Intelligent Structured Online Reporting Tool), according to the biobank sub-programme of licence 5705–1/2016/EKU). Personal data are encrypted and stored within the university firewall, and access to the data is only possible with the appropriate authorisation. Newborns undergo a brain MR imaging scan at the Medical Imaging Clinic on median Day 4 of life. The raw imaging data are stored on university servers and can be viewed via PACS also within a university firewall. Retrospective structured MR scoring is carried out in the iSORT system. For statistical analysis, the de-identified patient data can be extracted from the iSORT system in .csv format. In informed consent, the parents agree to the processing of their children’s data for research purposes.

Ethics and dissemination

We registered our study to ClinicalTrials.gov with the following registration number: NCT07136636 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07136636). The protocol was approved by the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy (NNGYK/24844-9/2025) on 21 May 2025. Results of this study will be disseminated through multiple channels. We will publish the findings in international peer-reviewed scientific journals and present them at national and international conferences. A plain language summary of the results will be prepared and shared with participating families on request. Any important protocol modifications (eg, changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes or analyses) will be documented and communicated to the relevant parties. Amendments will first be submitted for approval to the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy and the Scientific and Research Ethics Committee. Once approved, the trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07136636) will be updated accordingly. Any deviations from the original protocol will be reported in the final manuscript.

Discussion

Among children born with perinatal insults, unfavourable language outcomes are often one of the leading concerns for families. Our recent study demonstrated that one in three infants exhibit ELD in infants who survive NE without functional motor deficits.6 This study will use a comprehensive language assessment tool together with intellectual measurements and environmental indices to characterise the long-term overall cognitive and language outcomes of HIE survivors. Our main aim is to find the predictive markers of DLD.

A major strength of our current study is the availability of a comprehensive neonatal data including clinical and laboratory indicators, electrographic and neuroradiological markers combined with early language and cognitive outcomes measurements (at 2 years of age), and prospective follow-up at 4–8 years of age. This design allows us to examine whether neonatal biomarkers can predict later DLD with clinically meaningful accuracy.

Another key strength of our research is that we plan to include a larger number of cases than can be found in the currently available literature.10–12 We would like to keep the drop-out rate around 10% by regularly travelling to regional hospitals, maintaining close collaboration with public health nurses and having a particular focus on retaining families from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, ensuring their participation.

Based on previous findings, we selected the ADC values of the splenium part of the CC as our primary biomarker. These ADC values of the CC can be measured using diffusion-weighted MRI sequences and easily integrated into routine neonatal imaging protocols with specialised software. If ADC values, perhaps combined with other radiological markers, prove to be early predictors of DLD, they could support earlier identification of children who may benefit from targeted intervention.

Furthermore, understanding the prevalence and nature of language deficits in this high-risk group may inform the refinement of follow-up protocols for NE survivors, highlighting the importance of systematic early and long-term language screening. Finally, the findings may contribute to theoretical models of language development by clarifying how early hypoxic injury relates to later linguistic abilities.
