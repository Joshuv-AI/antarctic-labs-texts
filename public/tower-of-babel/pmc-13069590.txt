The Urgent Need to Harness Public Health Interventions to Tackle the Burden of Cardiovascular Diseases in Africa: Opportunities, Challenges, and Recommendations
Authors: Victor Oluwafemi Femi‐Lawal, Blessed Olaomo, Quoyyum Alabi, Oluwatomisin Olawoye, Ifeoluwa Fashina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). Evidence‐based interventions face implementation challenges such as inadequate healthcare infrastructure, weak policy enforcement, limited financial resources, and cultural barriers. Addressing these obstacles requires a comprehensive, multi‐sectoral public health approach.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive literature search for recent peer‐reviewed studies on scientific databases such as PubMed and Google Scholar using search terms such as “public health”, “cardiovascular disease”, and “Africa.” This article synthesizes evidence from studies examining the key challenges affecting CVD prevention and control in LMICs, including healthcare funding constraints, poor health literacy, policy gaps, and inadequate surveillance systems.ResultsFindings indicate that government commitment and multi‐sectoral collaboration are essential in prioritizing CVD prevention. Integration with Universal Health Coverage (UHC) will be vital to ensure equitable access to preventive and treatment services. Youth engagement and community‐based interventions are also essential to improve awareness and lifestyle modifications. The adoption of digital health solutions, such as mobile applications, wearable devices, and telemedicine, can also enhance healthcare accessibility and data collection. Additionally, strengthening health information systems (HIS) is critical for monitoring disease trends and intervention effectiveness.ConclusionA sustained, multi‐faceted approach is necessary to reduce the CVDs burden in LMICs. By enhancing policy implementation, leveraging technology, and improving healthcare access, LMICs can mitigate CVD morbidity and mortality, contributing to a more resilient healthcare system and economic stability.

Background

While several African countries continue to battle infectious diseases, current evidence shows a growing burden of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), including cancers and CVDs. Between 1990 and 2019, cardiovascular diseases became the second leading cause of death in Sub‐Saharan Africa, from sixth place prior [1, 2]. Low‐and middle‐income countries bear a disproportionate burden of CVDs. This is mostly driven by urbanization, tobacco use, modifications in diet, and increased sedentary lifestyles [3, 4, 5]. Cardiovascular deaths in LMICs are nearly three times the numbers in high‐income regions [6]. This is due to factors such as health system limitations, poor screening, competing health priorities with infectious diseases, and funding constraints [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

These challenges highlight a need for comprehensive interventions. In high‐income contexts, public health initiatives have driven positive behavioural change, addressing risk factors. For example, smoking cessation programmes in the USA led to quit rates of up to 30% [12]. Similarly, a 33% increase in taxes on cigarette sales reduced smoking‐related deaths by up to 16% [13]. Other interventions such as media campaigns, community organization, cardioprotective polypills, community health worker‐led programmes, and digital health solutions have shown potential for improving health outcomes and treatment adherence in various contexts [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. It is therefore important for governments, policymakers, and practitioners to explore these solutions in tackling CVDs in Africa.

In this perspective, we highlight the current state of public health interventions for CVDs in Africa, explore opportunities and challenges, and provide actionable recommendations. We aim to enable practitioners develop and scale interventions to improve cardiovascular health outcomes.

Methods

To identify relevant studies, we conducted a comprehensive literature search of scientific databases such as PubMed and Google Scholar between January and February, 2025. Search terms were related to “public health interventions,” “public health,” “cardiovascular disease,” and “Africa”. The search was enhanced using Boolean operators, as well as MeSH terms and field tags on PubMed. The search yielded a broad pool of articles, from which studies were screened based on relevance to the role of public health interventions in improving CVDs outcomes in Africa. In total, over 100 studies were identified, with a total of 64 studies included in the narrative synthesis. While we aimed to present data from recent studies published between 2010 and 2025, we made exceptions for select articles published before this period where they provided relevant data for our discussion. We prioritized original research such as cross‐sectional studies, randomized controlled trials, quasi‐experimental studies, and cohort studies. We excluded non‐peer‐reviewed articles and studies unrelated to cardiovascular disease. Studies that met the described standards were included in the narrative synthesis.

Given the relative scarcity of African‐based studies in certain domains, evidence from non‐African settings was included where relevant. However, such studies are presented as illustrative of potential intervention effectiveness, and their applicability to African contexts is interpreted with caution. Factors such as differences in healthcare infrastructure, health literacy, workforce capacity, and supply chain systems are critical in determining the feasibility and scalability of these interventions within African settings.

Case Studies of the Utilization of Public Health Interventions in CVDs Prevention and Management

Many countries have implemented public health interventions in preventing and managing CVDs. The COBRA‐BPS study introduced a multi‐component intervention to manage hypertensive patients in rural Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka [21]. The study recruited hypertensive patients, 40 years and older, whose systolic blood pressures were persistently above 140 mmHg and diastolic pressures above 90 mmHg. Key interventions included blood pressure monitoring, home health education, physician training, and the introduction of triage reception desks to reduce hospital wait times. A care coordinator was also assigned to patients with poorly controlled blood pressure. Results of this study showed that the blood pressure control was significantly improved by this multicomponent intervention [21].

Other successful public health interventions include behavioural counseling interventions to promote a healthy diet and physical activity in persons at risk of CVDs [22]. Policies such as restricted unhealthy food advertisements, improving school meal quality, sugar tax, and increasing physical activity in schools can help reduce obesity which is a major CVD risk factor [23]. Countries like Chile, Canada, and the UK have implemented policies limiting the advertisement of unhealthy foods to children, potentially improving health outcomes [24].

While these interventions have been successful in other contexts, adapting and scaling them in Africa requires collaboration between governments, international organizations, and the private sector. According to Capuccio and Miller, despite improved health research in low‐ and middle‐income countries, studies inquiring into the implementation of interventions are scanty [25]. However, studies have shown that patients with early detection, treatment, and control of hypertension show better outcomes and have reduced blood pressure [25]. Similarly, evidence suggests that a reduction in salt intake reduces blood pressure, particularly among Black populations [26].

Although sparse [25], there is evidence of the utility of public health interventions in improving health outcomes in Africa. A 2002 Nigerian study explored the impact of dietary sodium reduction on blood pressure [27]. The study recruited 82 free‐living normotensive adults, who were counseled to reduce sodium intake. This was to be maintained for 2 weeks. At the end of the 2‐week period, there was a significant reduction in the systolic blood pressure of participants. In Africa, the bouillon cube is a major source of sodium. Decreasing the sodium content of the bouillon cube may contribute to a significant reduction in the overall sodium consumed across the population and thus contribute to population‐wide benefits through reduction of hypertension and death from CVDs [28].

Telemedicine has also been explored in Africa. A Cameroonian study investigated telemedicine in hypertension care at Yaoundé General Hospital. The 165‐patient intervention group received telemedicine‐based care and personalized treatment, while the 103‐patient control group received generic drug treatment without telemedicine. By the study's end, 50% of the intervention group achieved target blood pressure levels, compared to 39.1% in the control group, highlighting telemedicine's role in improving treatment adherence and outcomes [29]. These case studies show evidence of how population‐level interventions can improve CVDs outcomes in Africa.

Key Opportunities for Utilizing Public Health Interventions in Reducing the Cardiovascular Disease Burden in Africa

Population‐level interventions are likely to be cost‐effective and efficient in addressing cardiovascular diseases in Africa [30]. Despite significant gaps in awareness, prevention, and treatment strategies, key opportunities exist for harnessing public health interventions to mitigate CVDs burdens. These include:

Public Health Education and Awareness

Many individuals, especially in Africa, lack awareness of the factors contributing to CVDs. While several health awareness programs have been implemented, their effectiveness largely depends on their delivery mechanisms. A 2015 study in China found that interactive workshops were the most effective form of health education, significantly improving medication adherence, salt intake reduction, exercise habits, and blood pressure levels [31]. This study underscores the importance of not only implementing health education initiatives but also ensuring their strategic delivery for maximum impact.

Another promising approach is utilizing school children to influence household health habits, particularly in homes where parents have limited education. A 2016 study in Sri Lanka trained school children to monitor household health behaviors, and this led to reduced BMI and increased physical activity among family members [32]. Similar studies in Brazil [33], China [34], and Japan [35] have confirmed that children can serve as effective change agents in promoting healthier lifestyle choices within their families.

Studies in Africa have shown some impact. The Healthy Heart Africa intervention among 838 individuals in rural Kenya improved knowledge of hypertension risk factors, consequences, and management after 12 months [36]. Similarly, the “Heart School” educational intervention among 45 patients in Dakar, Senegal, recorded improvements in knowledge of coronary artery disease, physical activity, and diet [37]. These successes highlight the potential of larger‐scale public education interventions to enhance cardiovascular health outcomes, as noted by expert panels [38].

Policy‐Based Interventions

Tobacco use is a major modifiable risk factor for CVDs. Government‐imposed smoking restrictions can play a crucial role in reducing CVDs prevalence. A 2014 multi‐intervention study examined the effects of enforcing smoking bans in public areas, demonstrating a slight decrease in smoking prevalence [39]. However, further research is needed to evaluate the long‐term effectiveness of such strategies.

Fiscal policies such as taxation on unhealthy foods can mitigate CVDs risks. Imposing taxes on high‐sodium foods can discourage excessive salt consumption, a known contributor to hypertension. Conversely, subsidizing the prices of fruits, vegetables, and other nutrient‐rich foods can promote healthier dietary habits.

Strengthening Primary Healthcare for Early Disease Detection

Early identification of CVDs risk factors is critical in preventing severe complications. Given that primary healthcare centers often serve as the first point of contact in many countries, training healthcare workers to screen for CVDs risk factors can enhance early diagnosis and intervention. A study in Rwanda demonstrated that community health workers (CHWs) effectively screened individuals for CVDs risk, facilitating timely referrals for management and treatment [40]. Strengthening primary healthcare systems through CHWs training and resource allocation can significantly improve CVDs prevention. However, persistent workforce shortages, inadequate funding, and weak health system infrastructure across many African settings may limit the scalability and sustainability of such interventions.

Leveraging Digital Health and Mobile Health Solutions

Recent research into the prevention of CVDs has increasingly focused on mobile health (mHealth) innovations [41]. The “TEXT ME” study, a 6‐month automated text‐messaging program delivering semi‐personalized messages, was associated with increased physical activity and improved cardiovascular risk factors [42]. Additionally, these innovations have proved useful in bridging the gap between patients and healthcare workers [40]. The effectiveness and scalability of such digital interventions are, however, constrained by factors such as limited internet connectivity, low digital literacy, and erratic power supply, particularly in rural and underserved communities.

Health System Strengthening

Despite rising CVDs burdens, many African countries allocate less than 5% of healthcare budgets to NCDs [43]. Consequently, most African countries lack sustainable healthcare frameworks for effective CVDs control. In Ghana, the Ministry of Health, in collaboration with international partners, established the Ghana Heart Initiative (GHI) to address gaps in CVDs management [43]. The initiative includes developing standardized treatment guidelines, training healthcare professionals, providing medical equipment, establishing a national support center, and improving national CVDs data collection systems. Similar models should be adopted across other African countries to enhance CVDs care. Nevertheless, their successful implementation depends on political commitment, adequate funding, and robust governance structures, which remain inconsistent across the region.

Challenges in Implementing Public Health Interventions to Tackle Cardiovascular Disease in Africa

Despite the promising opportunities, their successful implementation is closely dependent on broader health system capacities. Structural challenges such as weak political commitment, inadequate financing, and limited infrastructure persist. These not only constrain the scale‐up of digital health innovations but also undermine the effectiveness of primary healthcare strengthening and public health education efforts. These interrelated factors highlight the need for integrated strategies that simultaneously address both intervention design and the systemic conditions required for their successful delivery. Key challenges include:

Insufficient Healthcare Funding and Resources

Inadequate healthcare funding is a significant barrier to implementing CVDs interventions. This often leads to a lack of standard healthcare systems, inadequate facilities and medical equipment, and a scarcity of trained healthcare professionals. The scarcity of health practitioners in African countries hinders the delivery of adequate and effective care. Many LMICs struggle to allocate sufficient public funds for NCDs, leading to high out‐of‐pocket costs for individuals [44]. These financial constraints also directly limit the implementation of cost‐effective interventions such as community‐based screenings, workforce training, and digital health programs, all of which require sustained investment for long‐term impact.

Health Literacy and Cultural Barriers

Cultural beliefs and low levels of health literacy are known to hinder the acceptance of public health interventions. In many African countries, traditional medicine is often preferred over modern healthcare, and misconceptions regarding diet and physical activity as CVDs risk factors persist [44]. Public health education is essential to address these cultural barriers and encourage the uptake and adoption of evidence‐based cardiovascular disease interventions.

Policy Enforcement and Lack of Political Commitment

In several African countries, weak policy frameworks and weak governance structures have been major setbacks in the effective implementation of CVDs interventions. Governments often give high preference and funding to addressing communicable diseases, as opposed to NCDs. Evidence from LMICs indicates that salt reduction policies may be useful, with regulations on salt content in food leading to improved knowledge and decreases in salt levels in food, as seen in countries like South Africa and Morocco [45, 46, 47]. This highlights the importance of national prioritization of CVDs prevention through comprehensive health policies and programmes [48]. This lack of prioritization undermines the enforcement and sustainability of policy‐based interventions, thereby reducing their population‐level impact.

Inadequate Surveillance Systems and Data for Monitoring Population Health

Obtaining reliable data on CVDs morbidity and mortality in Africa is challenging due to the absence of comprehensive vital statistics and cause‐of‐death recording [44]. While being responsible for up to 90% of the global CVDs burden, LMICs account for only 10% of global research capacity [49]. Even when such data are available, determining the causes of death remains a technical challenge [50]. This lack of clinical records and consistent reporting prevents early detection of vulnerable populations. Weak surveillance systems hinder the effective planning, targeting, and evaluation of public health interventions, limiting the ability of health interventions to react proactively to the growing CVDs burden. While overseas collaboration has been harnessed to drive research output in Africa, ownership of research and population health surveillance is essential for sustainable outcomes.

Recommendations for Strengthening Public Health Approaches

Public health interventions play a key role in reducing the CVDs burden in Africa. However, several challenges, including inadequate health infrastructure, weak policy implementation, and limited financial resources, hinder their effectiveness [51]. Addressing this burden requires comprehensive public health approaches that are tailored to the challenges faced.

An increased level of commitment from the government is essential to reducing CVDs burden. Governments must prioritize cardiovascular health within national health agendas by allocating sufficient resources to prevention programs. Policies targeting physical inactivity, unhealthy diets, and smoking should be enforced [52]. Multi‐sectoral collaboration is also important, as CVDs risk factors extend beyond healthcare. For instance, agricultural policies can support healthier food production. Partnerships with non‐governmental organizations (NGOs) can also enhance community outreach efforts by ensuring that interventions are culturally sensitive [53].

The integration of CVDs prevention into Universal Health Coverage (UHC) strategies helps to ensure equitable healthcare access. This is in keeping with recent calls and recommendations by key bodies such as the World Heart Federation [54]. UHC strategies, inclusive of CVDs prevention and management, such as coverage of non‐communicable diseases, provision of treatments for chronic conditions, and community outreaches, have been employed by countries within and outside Africa [55, 56]. Key strategies, such as promoting preventive services such as health screenings and lifestyle education, should be integrated into national UHC frameworks [57]. By integrating CVDs prevention within UHC frameworks, African countries can ensure that cardiovascular health is prioritized.

Youths play a key role in shaping societal norms and behaviors, making them key agents for change. Additionally, adolescence is a critical period for developing lifelong habits and behaviours [58]. Government policies should be designed to integrate CVDs prevention education into school curricula, equipping students with lifelong heart‐healthy skills [58]. Beyond that, the role of youths as strong voices for change in their communities, grassroots advocates, co‐creation partners, and policy advocates exemplifies the significance of youth engagement in this objective [59]. A recent review by Raeside and colleagues highlighted the role of young people in co‐creating digital interventions for preventing CVDs, with innovative solutions for weight management, lifestyle modification, and risky alcohol drinking [60]. Similarly, a 2019 randomized controlled trial in Sri Lanka highlighted the role of youths as change agents in promoting healthy lifestyle behaviours, with significant decreases in body weight, increased consumption of fruits, and decreased consumption of snacks [61]. This highlights the potential of similar interventions and strategies in Africa, which has a predominantly youthful population.

The increasing penetration of digital technology in Africa has created new opportunities for enhancing public health approaches. Such tools include mobile applications, wearable devices (smart watches and fitness trackers), and telemedicine [62]. These digital platforms can facilitate data collection on CVDs risk factors, enabling timely responses to emerging trends.

Finally, comprehensive data collection and research are crucial for mapping out evidence‐based CVDs interventions. Many African countries face challenges in obtaining reliable epidemiological data due to weak surveillance systems. To address this, governments should invest in strengthening health information systems (HIS) to improve CVDs data collection, tracking prevalence, risk factors, and intervention outcomes. Collaborations with academic institutions and international organizations would foster research that generates locally relevant evidence to drive policy changes, improving health outcomes [63].

Conclusion

Addressing cardiovascular diseases in Africa requires integrated public health strategies, policy‐driven interventions, and stronger healthcare systems. Effective measures include health education, tobacco and dietary regulations, early detection via primary healthcare, and digital health innovations. Challenges remain, such as limited healthcare funding, poor health literacy, cultural barriers, weak policy enforcement, and inadequate data surveillance. Overcoming these requires political commitment, sustainable funding, and multi‐sectoral collaborations involving governments, international organizations, NGOs, and the private sector. A proactive, evidence‐based approach focusing on prevention, early detection, and accessible treatment is essential. By leveraging innovative public health strategies and fostering supportive policies, African countries can improve cardiovascular health outcomes and reduce the economic and social burden of CVDs.
