Covalently Immobilized Neomycin in Self-Healing Hydrogel for Nonleaching Antibacterial Dressing in Diabetic Wound Healing
Authors: Mingchen Zhang, Lei Jiang, Yu Liu, Ruiting Shen, Yiqiong Sun, Yang Zhou, Jiantao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial infections remain a major challenge in the treatment of chronic diabetic wounds, primarily due to hyperglycemic conditions that favor microbial proliferation. Conventional hydrogels incorporating physically entrapped antibiotics are often limited by burst release and inadequate long-term antibacterial efficacy. To address this, we developed a covalently grafted antibacterial hydrogel system based on neomycin sulfate (N) conjugated to sodium alginate (SA) to form SA-N. This modified polymer was further combined with poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and cross-linked via boric acid to establish an interpenetrating network structure, denoted as PBN hydrogels. Successful covalent conjugation of neomycin was verified by FTIR and 1H NMR spectroscopy. Rheological analysis under alternating strain demonstrated the hydrogel’s rapid self-healing behavior, a critical feature for dynamic wound environments. The PBN hydrogel exhibited strong broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, achieving inhibition rates of 98.03% against Staphylococcus aureus and 93.19% against Escherichia coli. Moreover, in a diabetic mouse wound model, treatment with the PBN hydrogel resulted in accelerated wound closure, reaching 96.59% after 21 days. Collectively, the PBN hydrogel demonstrates excellent biocompatibility, sustained antibacterial performance, and promising therapeutic potential for the management of chronic diabetic wounds.

Introduction

With the aging of the population and the increasing incidence of diseases at younger ages, there has been a significant increase in the prevalence of various metabolic conditions, most notably diabetes mellitus, which in turn has led to a considerable increase in the number of patients suffering from chronic wounds. Chronic diabetic wounds are a common complication in diabetic patients. Intense inflammation and excessive oxidative stress disrupt the delicate wound-healing microenvironment, while persistent hyperglycemia fosters bacterial colonization, synergistically impeding tissue regeneration. These nonhealing wounds are associated with high morbidity, frequent recurrence, and elevated amputation rates, which severely impair patients’ quality of life and impose significant socioeconomic and psychological burdens. Consequently, the development of highly effective therapeutic strategies for managing chronic diabetic wounds constitutes a pressing clinical imperative. ,

As a wet dressing, hydrogel consists of water-soluble polymers cross-linked into a three-dimensional network structure that is highly porous and hydrophilic, facilitating gas exchange and providing a water-retaining environment that maintains fluid balance in the wound area and promotes wound healing. Among hydrogel biomaterials, polysaccharide-based systems are particularly promising for wound management due to their exceptional biocompatibility and intrinsic bioactivity. Sodium alginate (SA), an FDA-approved anionic polysaccharide composed of β-d-mannuronate and α-l-guluronate units, has wide applications in biomedical and engineering fields due to its excellent properties. SA has the potential to activate macrophages and stimulate monocytes to produce interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) when managing chronic wounds. SA’s abundance of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups enables facile chemical modification (e.g., amidation with amine-bearing agents) and ionic cross-linking (e.g., with Ca2+) to form hydrogels. SA hydrogel is biocompatible and highly absorbent, which can create physical protection and maintain a moist environment on the wound surface. Most importantly, SA hydrogels do not cause secondary damage to the wound during removal. Notably, their “lateral wicking” effect, which can localize exudate away from wound margins, is able to protect periwound tissue from maceration.

Despite these regenerative advantages, pristine SA hydrogels lack inherent antimicrobial activity, severely limiting their efficacy in infected chronic wounds. Recent advances in wound dressing design have emphasized the integration of multiple functionalities, including controlled exudate management and potent antibacterial activity. , To achieve this, antibiotic integration remains a prevalent strategy. The loading of antibiotics onto SA hydrogel networks is a commonly used method to impart antimicrobial properties to them. Early approaches physically embedded broad-spectrum agents like silver nanoparticles within SA/chitosan networks or incorporated inorganic antimicrobials (e.g., ZnO NPs) into hybrid systems (e.g., gelatin/oxidized SA), achieving >96% inhibition against common pathogens. Similarly, the aminoglycoside antibiotic neomycin sulfate (Neo)a potent broad-spectrum agent against skin pathogenshas been loaded into polysaccharide hydrogels. , For example, neomycin was added to SA-based hydrogels to enhance the synergistic antimicrobial activity. In addition, different quantities of neomycin sulfate were loaded into poly­(vinyl alcohol)/Xanthan Gum hydrogels to create promising low-class topical antibacterial formulations with enhanced antimicrobial effects. The incorporation of neomycin sulfate endowed the hydrogels with potent antimicrobial activity, making them promising candidates for highly effective topical antibacterial formulations.

However, the burst release of neomycin sulfate, which is physically loaded into the hydrogel network, often results in excessive initial drug concentration, leading to toxic side effects, as well as insufficient drug concentration in the later stages, which promotes biofilm formation. In addition, there may be an increased risk of inducing bacterial resistance. Therefore, it is urgent to develop materials with effective antimicrobial properties and nonleaching activity. , Nonleaching antibacterial surfaces, also known as contact-killing, involves the use of active substances on the surface of materials to disrupt bacterial cell membranes, thereby inhibiting or killing adhered bacteria. Contact-activated antimicrobial mechanisms offer a compelling alternative, providing sustained, nonleaching bactericidal activity with minimal cytotoxicity and resistance risk. − The antimicrobial motifs used in this context range from natural biological molecules such as antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) to synthetic chemical substances such as quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) and heterocyclic compounds. Despite progress in fabrication techniques, such as quaternization-based modifications, current methodologies often involve complex synthetic routes that compromise the synergistic balance between mechanical robustness and biocompatibility in hydrogel systems. , Consequently, the rational design of multifunctional hydrogels integrating optimal biomechanical properties, biocompatibility, and potent contact-mediated antimicrobial activity represents a critical research frontier, particularly for addressing the therapeutic challenges associated with chronic wound management.

Herein, we engineer a multifunctional sodium alginate-based hydrogel through a facile grafting and cross-linking strategy that simultaneously addresses the tripartite challenge of antimicrobial efficacy, biomechanical functionality, and biocompatibility for chronic wound management (Figure ). Capitalizing on covalent amidation to graft neomycin sulfate onto SA backbones, we establish persistent contact-activated antimicrobial activity without drug leaching, effectively eliminating burst-release toxicity and resistance risks. Meanwhile, dynamic borate cross-linking with poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) confers tunable viscoelasticity, shear-thinning injectability, and intrinsic self-healing properties, enabling precise adaptation to irregular wound geometries. , Critically, this rational design retains the inherent biocompatibility, exudate management, and proregenerative cytokine induction of SA while integrating PVA’s biomechanical functionality. The resultant nonleaching, infection-resistant dressing represents a targeted therapeutic advance against the complex pathophysiology of diabetic chronic wounds.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Neomycin sulfate, sodium alginate, poly­(vinyl alcohol) (Type 1788), Borax anhydrous, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, 98%), 1-(3-(Dimethylamino)­propyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Dipotassium Hydrogen Phosphate, Potassium Phosphate, and NaCl were obtained from Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin (PS), and trypsin solution were purchased from Gibco (USA). CCK-8 Kit, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, and Calcein-AM kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). SYTO 9/Propidium iodide kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) and Tryptone Soy Agar (TSA) were purchased from Oxoid (UK). DI water (conductivity of 1 μS/cm) was used for all experiments. The bacterial strains S. aureus (ATCC 6835) and E. coli (ATCC 25922) were obtained from Fenghui Bio Co., Ltd. (China). Mouse fibroblast cells (L929 cell line) were obtained from Haixing Biosciences (China). ICR mice (male, 18–22 g, 4 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were approved by the Guoke Ningbo Life Science and Health Industry Research Institute (GK-2024-XM-1048 and 09-24-2024).

Synthesis of SA-N

SA (0.6 g) and EDC-HCl (0.6 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 6.0) containing 5% (w/v) NaCl. The mixture was activated by stirring at 700 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, NHS (0.15 g) was added to the solution, and the pH was adjusted to 4–6 using dilute HCl or NaOH. The carboxylation reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring at 700 rpm for 6 h at ambient temperature, yielding an activated SA. Following carboxyl group activation, neomycin sulfate (0.36 g, C23H48N6O17S) and NaCl (5 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of PBS (pH 4–6) to prepare the antibiotic solution. The activated SA solution was subjected to high-speed stirring (1500 rpm), and the neomycin sulfate solution (100 mL) was added dropwise at a controlled rate of 20 mL/h. The amide-bonding reaction was maintained under vigorous stirring (1500 rpm) for 20 h at room temperature, resulting in the formation of an antimicrobial peptide polymer (SA-N). The crude SA-N solution was dialyzed against PBS (pH 6.0) by using a regenerated cellulose dialysis membrane (MWCO 3500 Da) until no UV absorption at 335 nm was detected in the dialysate, indicating complete removal of unreacted reagents. The retained solution was lyophilized to obtain purified SA-N polymer, which was stored at −20 °C for subsequent use.

Preparation of PBN Hydrogels

SA-N polymer was dissolved in pH 6.0 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prepare solutions with concentrations of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 1.5% (w/v). Specifically, 1, 5, 10, and 15 mg of SA-N were dissolved in 1 mL of PBS, respectively. Poly­(vinyl alcohol) was dissolved in ultrapure water to achieve a 0.1 g/mL (10% w/v) solution under continuous stirring at 90 °C until complete dissolution. The SA-N solution (1 mL) was combined with the PVA solution (2 mL) at a 1:2 (v/v) ratio and homogenized by vortex mixing for 5 min to ensure uniformity. A borate cross-linking agent (0.2330 g) was dissolved in 10 mL of ultrapure water to prepare a 0.08 M solution. This borate solution was then added to the SA-N/PVA mixture at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio relative to the PVA solution (i.e., 2 mL of borate solution per 2 mL of PVA). The mixture was stirred at 25 °C for 1 h to facilitate the dynamic borate ester bond, ultimately forming the polyborate-network (PBN) hydrogel. PBN hydrogels are named PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 according to the different concentrations of SA-N. PB hydrogels were prepared using the same protocol without SA-N.

Characterization of PBN Hydrogels

The successful grafting of neomycin sulfate onto SA was verified through 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR, Bruker, Avance Neo) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Fisher, IS 50). Rheological properties were quantified using a rheometer (TA, HR-2) with a 25 mm parallel plate geometry. Hydrogel samples (500 μL) were loaded at a fixed gap distance of 0.50 mm. Frequency sweep tests (0.1–10 Hz) within the linear viscoelastic region (1% strain) were performed at 25 °C to determine storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Cross-sectional morphologies of hydrogels with varying SA-N concentrations (0.1–1.5% w/v) were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230). Samples were lyophilized, sputter-coated with 5 nm gold–palladium, and imaged at a 5 kV acceleration voltage. To test the porosity of PBN hydrogels, lyophilized hydrogel cylinders (diameter: 1.5 cm; height: 1.4 cm) with uniform mass (±2% variation) were prepared by using a precision cylindrical mold. Afterward, samples (n = 3 per group) were immersed in 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol within 50 mL centrifuge tubes. Four sequential vacuum cycles (5 min each at 0.1 MPa) were applied to eliminate trapped air, followed by 24 h of saturation at ambient temperature. The mass difference between ethanol-saturated and dry hydrogels was recorded to calculate porosity (ρ) using Porosity(%)=W1−W0ρethanol×100%

where W 0 and W 1 are the weights of the hydrogel before and after absorption of alcohol, respectively, and ρethanol = 0.789 g/cm3.

The in vitro degradation behavior of PBN hydrogels was evaluated by measuring the mass loss over time. Lyophilized PBN hydrogel samples (n = 3 per time point) were weighed (W 0) and immersed in 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (1, 4, and 7 days), samples were removed, gently rinsed with distilled water, lyophilized, and reweighed (W t). The degradation rate was calculated as: Degradationrate(%)=W0−WtW0×100%

In Vitro Cytocompatibility

L929 fibroblasts were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 (160i, Thermo Scientific). PBN hydrogels were sterilized under UV irradiation for 30 min and equilibrated in complete growth medium (1:10 w/v) for 24 h at 37 °C to obtain hydrogel extracts. Cells were trypsinized and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well in 100 μL of complete medium. After 24 h of adhesion, culture medium was replaced with 100 μL of hydrogel extract per well (n = 6 per group), maintaining control wells with fresh medium only. Cell viability was quantified at 24, 48, and 72 h using CCK-8 Assay and Live/Dead Staining. For CCK-8 Assay, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added per well, followed by 2 h of incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x), and the cell viability was calculated by the following formula: Cell viability(%)=Asample−AblankAcontrol−Ablank×100%

where A sample, A control, and A blank are the absorbance of the hydrogel group, the complete growth medium group, and the CCK-8 blank group, respectively.

For Live/Dead Staining, cells were incubated with 2 μM calcein-AM and 4 μM propidium iodide for 15 min and then imaged by fluorescence microscopy (TCS SP8, Leica).

To evaluate the effect of the PBN hydrogel on vascular cell growth, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with 100 μL of PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 hydrogel extract per well (n = 6). Control wells received fresh ECM only. After 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay following the same protocol used for L929 fibroblasts. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm by using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated relative to the control group.

Antibacterial Performance

The antibacterial ability of hydrogels was tested against S. aureus and E. coli. Lyophilized S. aureus and E. coli were resuscitated in 10 mL of TSB culture medium and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18 h. Activated cultures were centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min), washed twice with sterile PBS (pH 7.4), and adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. One mL portion of the diluted bacterial solution was cocultured with 1 g of PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 hydrogel in 40 mL of TSB medium, respectively. Meanwhile, 1 mL of the diluted bacterial solution was cocultured without PBN hydrogel in 40 mL of TSB medium as a positive control. Bacterial survival was measured by determining the OD value of the bacterial solution at 600 nm at fixed time intervals, and the growth curve of PBN over time was plotted. Colony-forming unit test was also conducted to evaluate the antibacterial ability. When the bacteria were cocultured with the hydrogel for 6–8 h, the concentration of live bacteria in the blank group was determined by a turbidimeter. The bacterial solution was diluted to 1 × 104 CFU/mL with PBS, corresponding to the experimental group by the same factor, and 100 μL of each was sprayed on TSA medium (3 plates for each group). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. The number of colonies on the plates was counted by using ImageJ software. In the positive control group, 100 μL of the diluted bacterial solution was sprayed on TSA medium (3 plates for each group).

The antibacterial mechanism of the PBN hydrogel was further investigated using live/dead fluorescent staining. E. coli and S. aureus suspensions (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were incubated with PBN4 hydrogel at 37 °C for 4 h. Bacteria incubated without the hydrogel served as the control. After incubation, bacteria were collected by centrifugation and stained with the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (SYTO 9 and propidium iodide, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained bacteria were observed under a fluorescence microscope. Live bacteria with intact membranes appeared green, while dead bacteria with damaged membranes appeared red.

Hemocompatibility

The hemocompatibility of hydrogels was evaluated through a standardized hemolysis assay: Fresh mouse blood (10 mL, anticoagulated with heparin sodium) was centrifuged at 116 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to isolate erythrocytes, which were subsequently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and resuspended as a 5% (v/v) suspension. Test groups were prepared by incubating 500 μL of hydrogel with 500 μL of erythrocyte suspension in 24-well plates (37 °C, 1 h), while control groups consisted of erythrocytes mixed with 500 μL of PBS (negative control) or deionized water (positive control). Postincubation, all samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and hemoglobin release was quantified by measuring supernatant absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis percentage was calculated as: Hemolysis rate %=A1−A0−A2A3−A2×100% where A 1 represents the absorbance (λ = 540 nm) of the supernatant from 500 μL erythrocyte suspension incubated with 500 μL PBN hydrogel extracts for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by centrifugation (1000 × g, 10 min); A 2 corresponds to the negative control (500 μL erythrocytes + 500 μL PBS) under identical treatment; A 3 denotes the positive control (500 μL erythrocytes + 500 μL ultrapure water) lysed completely; and A 0 (optional background correction) refers to the absorbance of hydrogel extracts mixed with PBS (500 μL + 500 μL) without erythrocytes.

In Vivo Diabetic Wound Healing Performance

A diabetic wound model was established using male ICR mice (18–22 g) through intraperitoneal administration of STZ (50 mg/kg in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 4.5) every 48 h for five consecutive injections, followed by confirmation of diabetes via sustained nonfasting blood glucose levels >16.7 mmol/L over 7 consecutive days using a glucometer. Under isoflurane anesthesia, an 8-mm full-thickness excisional wound was surgically created on the dorsal epidermis using sterile biopsy punches, with animals randomly allocated into three groups: untreated control, commercial hydrogel (DUODERM), and PBN hydrogel treatment. Wound closure kinetics were monitored by serial macroscopic imaging and quantified via ImageJ using standardized grayscale thresholding. At designated endpoints, euthanized specimens were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (24 h, 4 °C), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm thickness) for histological evaluation through hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining with microscopic visualization performed on a confocal system, ensuring triplicate technical replicates per biological sample to validate observations.

Results and Discussion

Neomycin sulfate was covalently grafted onto the carboxyl groups of sodium alginate via amide bond formation through a classical carbodiimide-mediated amidation reaction (Figure a). To confirm the successful chemical conjugation, the structural evolution of sodium alginate before and after modification was characterized by both proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. In the 1H NMR spectrum (Figure b), the appearance of a distinct resonance signal at 2.8 ppm is attributed to the methylene protons adjacent to the secondary amine moieties in the neomycin-modified alginate, consistent with previous reports. The signal from the typical amide bond absorption peak confirms the successful grafting of neomycin sulfate onto sodium alginate. As shown in Figure c, the typical bands of PVA, including C–O single bond stretching band at 1108 cm–1, C–C single bond stretching band at 1459 cm–1, C–H single bond stretching band at 2952 cm–1, and a broad OH single bond stretching band centered in the vicinity of 3360 cm–1 were observed from PB and PBN hydrogel. The absorption peak at 1735 cm–1 is due to CO stretching, which can be observed in PBN and SA-N. For the PB hydrogel, most of the absorption peaks overlap with those of the PBN hydrogel. The difference lies in the characteristic peak at 1651 cm–1 where the PBN hydrogel shows C–N stretching and N–H vibration due to the grafting of SA-N. The characteristic peak at 1335 cm–1 was found in PB and PBN, which is due to the asymmetric stretching relaxation peak of B–O–C, , indicating the formation of borate bonds between the PVA and SA chains.

The internal network structure of the hydrogels was examined by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as depicted in Figure d. Notably, all the hydrogels exhibited well-defined porous structures. The porosity of the PBN4 hydrogel was measured to be 59.22% (Figure e), which was significantly higher than that of PBN1 (24.03%). This disparity can be attributed to the ability of pure poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) to form a uniform and dense network structure facilitated by borate ester bonds. However, as the concentration of SA-N in PBN increases, the rising content of free carboxyl groups interferes with the formation of these chemical bonds. Instead, the carboxyl groups tend to form hydrogen bonds with PVA, which ultimately leads to an increase in porosity.

Strain sweep of (a) PB, (b) PBN1, (c) PBN2, (d) PBN3, and (e) PBN4 hydrogels. (f) Alternative strain sweep of PBN4. (g) Injectability. (h) Self-healing properties.

To quantify the mechanical properties of the PBN hydrogels, their rheological behavior was evaluated. The amplitude sweep test revealed a distinct linear viscoelastic region, with nonlinear deformation occurring beyond the gel–sol transition point (Figure a-e). The storage modulus of the PB hydrogel without SA-N was 4247 Pa, indicating high mechanical strength. After the introduction of SA-N, the storage modulus of PBN decreased to some extent, and the storage modulus of PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 was 1164, 585, 581, and 550 Pa, respectively. The results indicated that the incorporation of SA-N significantly modulated the rigidity of the pure PVA hydrogel. The introduction of SA-N endowed the PBN hydrogels with good viscoelasticity, which resulted in better injectability of the hydrogels. As shown in Figure f, the self-healing behavior of PBN4 was evaluated by applying alternating strains of 1% (30 s) and 1000% (30 s). The hydrogel remained structurally intact under 1% strain, whereas it fractured under 1000% strain. Remarkably, upon returning to 1% strain, the hydrogel effectively restored its original structure. The introduction of SA-N into the PVA network modulates the density and mobility of the borate ester bonds. The carboxyl groups from SA-N compete with PVA hydroxyl groups for borate coordination, leading to a more dynamic, yet slightly less rigid network. This results in faster bond reformation upon fracture, enhancing the self-healing kinetics, as evidenced by the rapid recovery of storage modulus in alternating strain tests (Figure f).

These favorable rheological properties, combined with excellent self-healing ability, allow PBN hydrogels to be extruded through a syringe and adapt to irregular wound surfaces, thereby enhancing their therapeutic potential in wound healing applications. Consistently, PBN hydrogels passed smoothly through a syringe needle (Figure g) and exhibited macroscopic self-healing behavior (Figure h).

The degradation profile of the PBN hydrogel is presented in Figure S1. The hydrogel exhibited a controlled degradation pattern, with mass losses of 12.28%, 47.93%, and 72.69% on days 1, 4, and 7, respectively. This gradual degradation ensures that the hydrogel maintains its structural integrity during the critical early phase of wound healing while gradually resorbing as tissue regeneration progresses.

Although the favorable rheological properties of PBN hydrogels are essential for clinical handling, they necessitate the concurrent assessment of biological safety to advance translational development. Hemocompatibility assessment via standardized hemolysis assays (Figure a) demonstrated negligible erythrocyte damage, with visual congruence to PBS-negative controls and a hemolytic ratio of <2.3% (significantly below the 5% ISO 10993-5 biocompatibility threshold), confirming no significant erythrocyte lysis. Furthermore, the cell viability and proliferation of the PBN hydrogel for L929 fibroblasts by CCK-8 and live cell staining were evaluated. As shown in Figure b, the relative cell viability values of cells cultured for 48 h in PBN hydrogel extracts with varying SA-N contents were greater than 100% compared to the control group. Specifically, at the highest SA-N concentration, PBN4 exhibited a 142.8% increase in cell proliferation after 48 h, demonstrating the excellent cytocompatibility of the PBN hydrogel. The corresponding fluorescence images of cell proliferation are shown in Figure c, where the morphology of the cells treated with the extract was similar to that of the control group, and the cell density was greater than that of the control group. Furthermore, the cytocompatibility of the PBN hydrogel with endothelial cells was assessed using HUVECs. As shown in Figure S2, the PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 hydrogels exhibited excellent compatibility, with cell viability reaching 162.4%, 148.5%, 138.9%, and 121.2% at 24 h, respectively. These results support the in vivo observation of enhanced neovascularization in PBN-treated wounds (Figure a) and suggest that the hydrogel creates a favorable microenvironment for angiogenesis. In addition, to highlight the advantages of neomycin sulfate-binding hydrogels through chemical grafting, we prepared N hydrogels by blending neomycin sulfate directly with PVA hydrogels. Additionally, we prepared SA/N hydrogels with the same composition as PBN hydrogels but without a chemical grafting reaction between SA and N and investigated their biocompatibility. As illustrated in Figure d, N hydrogels and SA/N hydrogels showed strong cytotoxicity, with cell survival rates lower than 50% and 60%, respectively. These findings confirm that the PBN hydrogel exhibits excellent in vivo biocompatibility.

In chronic wounds, bacterial colonization exacerbates the inflammatory reaction and impedes tissue regeneration through persistent pathogen proliferation. Ideal wound dressings should possess antibacterial properties to facilitate wound healing by reducing pathogen load and mitigating the inflammatory response in the wound environment. , The antibacterial efficacy of the PBN hydrogels against E. coli and S. aureus was assessed using the colony-forming unit (CFU) quantification method and growth kinetics monitoring. Quantitative CFU assays demonstrated dose-dependent antimicrobial activity, with PBN4 hydrogels exhibiting superior bactericidal effects against E. coli (98.03% inhibition) compared to S. aureus (93.19% inhibition) (Figure a and b). The slightly higher antibacterial efficacy of PBN4 against E. coli (98.03%) compared to S. aureus (93.19%) may be attributed to structural and compositional differences between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial cell walls. E. coli, as a Gram-negative bacterium, possesses an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and a thin peptidoglycan layer, which may render it more permeable to cationic agents or more susceptible to membrane disruption. In contrast, S. aureus (Gram-positive) possesses a thicker, multilayered peptidoglycan cell wall and teichoic acids, which could provide greater structural rigidity and reduce the accessibility or effectiveness of surface-bound antimicrobial agents.

The antibacterial effects of PBN hydrogels. (a) Bacterial inhibition photographs of the hydrogels against E. coli and S. aureus. (b) The bacteriostatic rate of PBN hydrogels against E. coli and S. aureus. (c) The growth curves of E. coli. (d) The growth curves of S. aureus.

This phenomenon is consistent with findings from recent studies. A chitosan/sodium alginate/neomycin sulfate/hydroxyapatite (Cs/SA/NS/NPHA) scaffold was developed and exhibited higher antibacterial activity against E. coli compared to S. aureus, ascribing this difference to the greater susceptibility of Gram-negative bacteria to membrane-disruptive agents. Similarly, Li constructed tetrahedral framework nucleic acids loaded with neomycin sulfate (tFNAs-NeoS) and observed that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of tFNAs-NeoS against E. coli was lower than that against S. aureus, indicating enhanced sensitivity of E. coli to neomycin-based nanocarriers. Both studies attributed the observed differences to distinct cell envelope architectures and the resulting variations in membrane permeability and drug–target interactions.

In our system, the polycationic nature of covalently grafted neomycin may interact more effectively with the negatively charged LPS on the E. coli surface, leading to enhanced membrane damage and bacterial death. Additionally, differences in surface charge density and membrane composition between the two strains may further influence the extent of contact-mediated antibacterial activity. These factors collectively contribute to the observed variation in the inhibition rates.

In addition, growth kinetic monitoring revealed significant suppression of bacterial proliferation in an SA-N concentration-dependent manner. Figure c and d show the growth curves of E. coli and S. aureus treated with PB, PBN1, PBN2, PBN3, and PBN4 hydrogels, illustrating that antibacterial activity was enhanced with the increasing concentration of SA-N. This trend was consistent with the colony counts observed on the TSA plates. It is worth mentioning that when the content of SA-N reached 1.5% in the PBN hydrogel, the growth of E. coli was almost completely inhibited. To visually confirm the antibacterial mechanism of the PBN hydrogel, live/dead fluorescence staining was performed on E. coli and S. aureus after coincubation with PBN4. As shown in Figure S3, the control group exhibited predominantly green fluorescence, indicating intact bacterial membranes. In contrast, after exposure to the PBN4 hydrogel, both bacterial strains displayed red fluorescence, demonstrating extensive membrane disruption and cell death. These results provide direct evidence for the contact-activated bactericidal mechanism of covalently immobilized neomycin in the PBN hydrogel.

The enhanced contact-killing activity of covalently grafted neomycin in the PBN hydrogel can be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. Neomycin, as an aminoglycoside antibiotic, contains multiple protonated amino groups that remain cationic under physiological conditions. Upon covalent immobilization onto the sodium alginate backbone, these positively charged moieties are presented at a high density on the hydrogel surface, creating a polycationic interface that interacts electrostatically with the negatively charged components of bacterial membranes. This strong electrostatic interaction leads to localized membrane depolarization, pore formation, and increased permeability, resulting in leakage of intracellular contents, as evidenced by the red fluorescence in live/dead staining (Figure S3). Unlike physically loaded antibiotics that diffuse away from the wound site, covalently immobilized neomycin remains confined to the hydrogel surface, creating a sustained, localized high concentration of antimicrobial moieties at the bacteria–hydrogel interface, which maximizes bactericidal efficiency while minimizing systemic exposure and resistance risks. Additionally, the polycationic surface may promote initial bacterial adhesion, bringing pathogens into closer contact with the immobilized neomycin and facilitating more effective killing.

Building upon the demonstrated mechanical robustness, biocompatibility, and bactericidal function of PBN hydrogels, we next evaluated their therapeutic performance in a clinically relevant diabetic chronic wound model. Full-thickness excisional wounds were surgically induced on the dorsal surface of streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice to evaluate the performance of the PBN hydrogels. The wounds were topically treated with PBN4 hydrogel, commercially available DUODERM hydrogel, and a 3M Tegaderm membrane (negative control), respectively. All wounds were monitored daily to track the closure kinetics throughout the 21-day treatment period. Representative images of the wound surface from the different treatment groups at days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21, along with a visualization chart illustrating wound closure, are presented in Figure a. Quantitative analysis revealed time-dependent healing acceleration in the PBN4 group, achieving significantly higher wound closure rates compared with other cohorts (Figure b). On day 7 of treatment, the PBN4-treated group exhibited 63.5% wound contraction, whereas the DUODERM and control groups exhibited 46.9% and 18.3% wound contraction, respectively. The first 7 days of wound healing correspond to the acute inflammatory phase, during which the superior antimicrobial properties of PBN hydrogels likely reduce microorganism adhesion and proliferation at the wound site, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response. By day 21, wounds treated with PBN hydrogels achieved 96.59% closure and complete epithelialization. In contrast, the DUODERM and control groups required extended healing periods, reaching only 76.4% and 58.6% closure, respectively. The enhanced healing kinetics observed in the PBN hydrogel group were attributed to its unique structural advantages, antibacterial activities, and flexibility. Furthermore, the self-healing property of the PBN hydrogel is particularly advantageous in dynamic wound environments. As the wound contracts and expands during healing, the hydrogel can autonomously repair microdamages, maintaining structural integrity and sustained contact with the wound. This ensures continuous antibacterial activity and physical protection, thereby reducing the dressing replacement frequency and minimizing secondary trauma. Moreover, the injectability and shape adaptability derived from self-healing behavior enable complete coverage of irregular wound geometries, facilitating uniform drug delivery and enhancing overall healing outcomes, as observed in the in vivo study. This microenvironment facilitated cell migration and angiogenesis while preventing excessive exudate accumulation, as evidenced by the accelerated wound closure, which promoted cellular growth and migration.

Histological evaluation of epidermal regeneration and granulation tissue formation was performed via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. Comparative analysis revealed that the PBN-treated group exhibited a significantly higher density of organized connective tissue on day 3 of treatment compared to both the blank control and commercial dressing groups (Figure a). By day 7, the PBN group demonstrated substantial epidermal stratification with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration, in contrast to the persistent inflammatory response observed in the commercial dressing group. Although partial epidermal closure was achieved in the commercial dressing group by day 14, histological examination revealed discontinuous epithelial layers and residual inflammatory exudates in areas that had not fully healed. Complete re-epithelialization was observed across all groups by day 21, with the PBN-treated group developing a stratified epidermis that surpassed that of the control groups. Notably, the dermis in the PBN group exhibited enhanced tissue remodeling, characterized by prominent neoangiogenesis, evidenced by thin-walled capillaries and an increased presence of proliferating fibroblasts.

Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated time-dependent collagen deposition in all groups, with the PBN group displaying superior architectural organization (Figure b). On day 14, collagen bundles in PBN-treated wounds were aligned parallel to the epidermal layer, forming densely interwoven structures that were more organized than those observed in the control group.

Conclusion

In this study, a self-healing interpenetrating network hydrogel (PBN) was successfully engineered by chemically grafting neomycin sulfate (N) onto sodium alginate (SA), followed by cross-linking with poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and boric acid. The covalent incorporation of neomycin into the alginate backbone was verified through FTIR and 1H NMR, confirming a stable integration of the antimicrobial agent within the hydrogel matrix. The PBN hydrogel demonstrated pronounced self-healing properties under cyclic strain conditions, which is a critical attribute for maintaining mechanical integrity in dynamic wound environments. Remarkably, the PBN hydrogel exhibited potent antimicrobial efficacy, achieving inhibition rates of 98.03% against S. aureus and 93.19% against E. coli, thereby addressing a major clinical barrier in the treatment of infected chronic diabetic wounds. In vivo experiments further validated its therapeutic potential, achieving a 96.59% wound closure rate within 21 days in a diabetic animal model. These results underscore the hydrogel’s dual advantages of robust biocompatibility and sustained antimicrobial activity, offering a promising alternative to traditional antibiotic-loaded hydrogels prone to burst release.
