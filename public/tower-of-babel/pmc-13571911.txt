Cytotoxicity and Bioactivity of Five Hydraulic Silicate Cements in Human Dental Pulp Cells at Different Setting Stages
Authors: Chanakarn Sinsareekul, Suphalak Phothichailert, Ravipha Suwittayarak, Vorapat Trachoo, Chatvadee Kornsuthisopon, Waleerat Sukarawan, Yoshio Yahata, Thanaphum Osathanon, Sirawut Hiran-us
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsEvidence regarding the biological responses of human dental pulp cells (hDPCs) to freshly mixed hydraulic silicate cements remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the biocompatibility, mineralisation potential, and immunomodulatory effects of five hydraulic silicate cements (ProRoot MTA, Biodentine, TotalFill BC Putty, CeraPutty, and C-Root BP) at different stages of setting on hDPCs.MethodshDPCs were isolated from human third molars. Extraction media were prepared from the different pulp dressing materials at 15 minutes, 24 hours, and 7 days after mixing. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay and live/dead staining. Odonto/osteogenic differentiation was evaluated using Alizarin Red S staining. RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction were conducted to investigate the mRNA expression of inflammatory and immunomodulatory-related genes.ResultsUndiluted extraction media obtained from all five freshly mixed hydraulic silicate cements (15 minutes after mixing) were cytotoxic to hDPCs. In contrast, the extraction media collected after a longer setting period (7 days post-mixing) showed no cytotoxicity, except for C-Root BP. Enhanced mineralisation was observed in cells treated with extraction media derived from ProRoot MTA, Biodentine, and TotalFill BC Putty at all time points. By contrast, cells exposed to extraction media from C-Root BP and CeraPutty at the 24-hour and 7-day setting time points exhibited markedly reduced mineral deposition. Additionally, the extraction medium derived from TotalFill BC Putty downregulated the expression of inflammation-related genes in hDPCs.ConclusionTotalFill BC Putty, Biodentine, and ProRoot MTA demonstrated superior performance in reference to the investigated parameters compared with CeraPutty and C-Root BP, suggesting their suitability as pulp dressing materials for vital pulp therapy procedures.Clinical relevanceThe data may guide clinicians in selecting the most appropriate pulp dressing material based on the investigated biological parameters.

Introduction

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) has been increasingly recognised as a biologically based and conservative alternative to conventional root canal treatment, aiming to preserve the vitality and functionality of the dental pulp following injury caused by caries, trauma, or restorative procedures.1 The success of VPT relies on preserving healthy pulp tissue capable of initiating healing and reparative processes within the dentin–pulp complex.2 Pulp dressing materials play a crucial role in this procedure by stimulating odontoblastic activity, promoting the deposition of reparative dentin, and maintaining pulpal vitality.3

An ideal pulp dressing material should therefore exhibit excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, and the ability to induce mineralised tissue formation while maintaining a favourable environment for endogenous stem cell responses. Mineral trioxide aggregate has been extensively used in vital pulp therapy due to its favourable biological outcomes.4 However, despite its proven clinical efficacy, these materials exhibit certain drawbacks, including long setting time, difficult handling, and potential discolouration.5,6 To address these limitations, new calcium silicate–based materials have been introduced, aiming to optimise handling characteristics, shorten setting time, and enhance biological performance.7 Hydraulic calcium silicate cements from different manufacturers may contain calcium silicate (CaSiO₃) as their main ingredient; however, variations in composition and the presence of additional undisclosed ingredients may influence their properties.

Recently, a strontium silicate–based biomaterial (C-root BP, Dental Medical Devices Co Ltd Beijing, China) has been introduced as an alternative to conventional calcium silicate–based formulations. In contrast to calcium silicate-based materials, this system incorporates strontium silicate (SrSiO₃) in place of CaSiO₃, potentially conferring distinct biological properties. Previous studies have reported that strontium is widely recognised for its ability to enhance hard tissue regeneration and promote angiogenesis by upregulating angiogenic factors.8, 9, 10 However, evidence regarding the biocompatibility and inflammatory responses to this material remains limited, particularly with respect to its effects on human dental pulp cells (hDPCs).

Importantly, the biocompatibility and inflammatory response to calcium silicate–based materials may vary significantly depending on their setting stage.11 During the freshly mixed phase, these materials actively release calcium and hydroxyl ions, contributing to a highly alkaline environment that can influence the early cellular response. In contrast, after setting, the surface chemistry and ion release dynamics change considerably, potentially altering cytocompatibility and the ability to induce mineralisation. However, most in vitro studies have focused on fully set materials,12,13 providing limited information on the immediate biological interactions with freshly mixed formulations. Furthermore, a previous study has shown that freshly mixed and fully set materials yield markedly different results.14

Therefore, understanding how freshly mixed hydraulic silicate cements, which more closely reflect real clinical conditions, affect hDPCs behaviour is essential for predicting their clinical performance and cytotoxicity in vital pulp therapy procedures. Accordingly, this study evaluated the cytotoxicity, mineralisation potential, and immunomodulatory gene expression of five hydraulic silicate cements, with particular emphasis on comparing a novel strontium silicate–based cement with established calcium silicate–based materials across three setting stages, to clarify how the setting stage governs the early biological response of hDPCs in vitro. The null hypothesis was that the setting stage would not affect the biological properties of the tested hydraulic silicate cements.

Materials and methods

Human dental pulp cells isolation and culture

Dental pulp tissue was harvested from impacted third molars with no carious lesion scheduled for surgical removal in accordance with the patient's treatment plan (age 18-44 years). The collection of human tissues was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University (approval No. HREC-DCU 2025-111), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Briefly, the collected teeth were sectioned to obtain the dental pulp. The tissues were collected and minced. Cell isolation was performed using cell explantation methods. The isolated cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), and 100 units/ml of antibiotic and antimycotic (Gibco). Cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the medium was changed every 2-3 days. Subculturing was performed using passages 2 to 5, which were used for all subsequent experiments.

Materials and extraction medium preparation

The commercially available products were chosen for the present study: CeraPutty (Meta Biomed Co, Cheongju, Korea), BiodentineTM (Septodont, Saint Maur des Fossés, France), ProRoot MTA (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), C-Root BP, and TotalFill BC Putty (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland). All tested materials were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The extraction protocol was modified in accordance with ISO 10993-12. Each specimen was prepared as a cylinder measuring 5 mm in diameter and 5 mm in height in sterile conditions, corresponding to a total surface area of approximately 1.18 cm². The specimens were subsequently contacted with 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 minutes, 24 hours, or 7 days to simulate clinical moisture exposure, after which they were removed from the mould. According to these different time points, the freshly mixed group was referred to as the materials that had been allowed to set for 15 minutes prior to immersion in the extraction medium. The 24 hours and 7 days groups were defined as materials that had been allowed to set for 24 hours and 7 days prior to immersion in the extraction medium, respectively. One millilitre of normal culture medium or osteogenic induction medium was added to each material, and the materials were incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The extraction medium was filtered (0.1 μm) and stored at −20 °C until use. Each experiment employed the same batch of extraction medium. The extraction medium was prepared for serial dilution in the growth medium at 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.12%, and 1.5%.

For osteogenic differentiation, all tested materials were prepared in osteogenic medium conditions consisting of ascorbic acid 50 µg/ml (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), beta-glycerophosphate 5 mM (Sigma-Aldrich), and dexamethasone 10 nM (Sigma-Aldrich) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cell viability

To assess viability, cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 48-well plates and cultured in growth medium (GM) for 24 hours. Afterwards, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were treated with the extraction medium. Cells cultured in medium without the material extract served as the negative control. At the determined time points, the cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/ml of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) in serum-free medium (SFM) for 30 minutes at 37 °C. The precipitated formazan crystals produced by the cells’ metabolic activity were dissolved using an eluting buffer composed of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and glycine buffer. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Palo Alto, CA, USA) at 570 nm. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage.

Live/dead assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in a 48-well plates and allowed to reach confluence. The culture medium was removed and replaced with the extraction medium for 24 and 48 hours. After treatment, the cells were incubated with calcein-AM and ethidium homodimer-1 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 30 minutes. As a negative control, cells were treated with 0.1% TritonX-100. The stained cells were visualised using the ApoTome system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Osteogenic differentiation

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in a 24-well plates and maintained with GM until confluence was achieved. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with the material extraction in osteogenic induction medium (OM). The cells were cultured in OM for 14 days at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

To investigate mineral deposition, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in PBS for 10 minutes and then washed with deionised water (DI). The cells were stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS) solution (pH 4.1) (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 minutes at room temperature with gentle agitation and subsequently washed with deionised water. The stained mineral deposits were visualised under a light microscope and subsequently solubilised with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride monohydrate in 10 mM sodium phosphate solution for 30 minutes. The solution eluting mineral deposition of the sample was measured at 570 nm by a microplate reader (ELx800; BIO-TEK®, Winoosk, VT, USA).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Quantitative mRNA expression analysis was performed using real-time polymerase chain (qRT-PCR). The TRIzol reagent (RiboExTM, GeneAll®, Seoul, Korea) was used to extract total RNA, and a reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was utilised to convert 1 µg of total RNA into complementary DNA. A FastStart SYBR Green Master Essential DNA (Roche, MA, USA) was used to conduct the real-time polymerase chain reaction in the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR (Bio-Rad, Hercules, Singapore). The comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCT) was used to quantify relative gene expression after standardising the target gene's mRNA levels to those of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene. Oligonucleotide sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using cells obtained from at least four independent donors. Data were shown as mean ± standard deviation. Each dot in the graph represented each data value. Statistical analysis was performed using One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. When P-values were less than 0.05, the conditions were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study described cytotoxicity, mineralisation potential, and immunomodulatory effects of five hydraulic silicate cements with different setting stages on hDPCs in vitro. As mentioned previously, freshly mixed and fully set materials yield markedly different properties.14 Thus, this study highlights the influence of different setting stages on cellular responses and provides evidence relevant to the clinical context, in which cells are exposed to materials from the early stages of setting. Consistent with this premise, the present study demonstrates that variations in material setting stages differentially affect cytotoxicity, mineral deposition, and the expression of inflammation- and immunomodulation-related genes in hDPCs. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Previous studies15,16 have demonstrated that the biological responses of human dental pulp stem cells (hDPCSs) to bioactive pulp-capping materials may vary with differences in material composition. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that physicochemical properties such as ion release, alkalinity, and surface characteristics undergo substantial changes throughout the setting process, which may subsequently influence cellular behaviour. Given that both material composition and setting stage can influence the biological performance of hydraulic silicate cements. Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in its direct comparison of five commercially available hydraulic silicate materials across different stages of setting under standardised experimental conditions. This controlled in vitro design provides clearer insight into the biological behaviour of the tested materials than indirect comparisons across previous studies, which may differ in cell sources, methodologies, extraction protocols, and experimental conditions.

The three experimental time points were selected to represent clinically and biologically relevant stages of setting. The 15-minute time point represented the freshly mixed stage, reflecting the early period after manipulation and placement when the material may initially contact pulp tissue in clinical conditions. Although an earlier time point may more closely simulate immediate clinical application, 15 minutes allowed standardised preparation and handling of all materials. The 24-hour time point was selected as an intermediate stage commonly used in material testing, allowing comparison with previous studies; however, some materials may still undergo setting or maturation at this stage. The 7-day time point represented the fully set or mature stage, allowing assessment of biological responses after more complete hydration and stabilisation of the materials.

In the present study, the cytotoxicity of all five materials was both concentration- and setting-stage-dependent, decreasing as the setting reaction progressed. A previous study has demonstrated that the cytotoxic effects of hydraulic silicate cements are concentration-dependent, with reduced cell viability observed at higher extract concentrations, particularly during the early setting stages.17 This is consistent with our findings that cell viability decreased following exposure to undiluted extracts (100%), especially for those cells exposed to extracted medium from 15-minute time points after mixing. When allowing materials to ensure a full set for 7 days prior to exposing the extracted medium to hDPCs, all materials, except C-Root BP, exhibited better cell viability in those treated with undiluted extraction medium. During the freshly mixed phase, these materials actively release calcium and hydroxyl ions, contributing to a highly alkaline environment that can influence the early cellular response.18 These observations align with previous reports indicating that freshly mixed hydraulic silicate cements may exert transient cytotoxic effects.17,19 However, as hydration progresses and the material becomes more stable, ion release may become more controlled, thereby improving cytocompatibility and enhancing mineralisation potential.

In contrast, the relatively lower cell viability observed for C-Root BP in the present study appears inconsistent with the findings of Zhou et al20 and Kwan et al21 which reported that C-Root SP did not exhibit cytotoxicity. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in material formulation, extract concentration, and cell types tested. C-Root BP is a strontium silicate-based cement that does not generate calcium hydroxide as a by-product of the hydration reaction. Instead, calcium hydroxide is incorporated as an additive in its formulation, which may contribute to a rapid increase in alkalinity and thereby explain its inferior cytocompatibility. Moreover, a previous study has shown that even minor alterations in strontium levels significantly influence cellular behaviour.22 In the present study, the use of C-Root BP, which may contain higher concentrations of bioactive components than other sealer formulations, could lead to increased strontium-ion release. Consequently, ion concentrations in undiluted extract solutions may exceed optimal thresholds, leading to an imbalance in the ion release profile and subsequent cytotoxic effects that compromise cell viability at the later stage of setting time. This setting-dependent behaviour suggests that the bioactivity of hydraulic silicate cements should be interpreted as a dynamic process rather than a static material property.

In the present study, both TotalFill BC Putty and Biodentine significantly enhanced mineralised nodule formation in hDPCs regardless of the setting stages condition. These findings may be explained by the initiation of the hydration reaction, which promotes ion dissolution from the material and subsequently induces favourable biological responses in hDPCs.11,23,24 Notably, both materials exhibited greater mineralised nodule formation than ProRoot MTA under those conditions when treated with extracted medium from the 15-minute time point after mixing. This difference may be influenced by variations in material composition and setting characteristics, which can affect ion dissolution dynamics. Specifically, increased release of zirconium ion components in TotalFill BC Putty and Biodentine has been suggested to enhance mineralisation potential in hDPCs, potentially through activation of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signalling pathway via receptor-regulated SMAD proteins.25,26

However, less pronounced calcium deposition was observed in CeraPutty compared with the other silicate cements. This finding is consistent with a previous study.27 which reported that Biodentine and endosequence root repair material (ERRM) significantly enhanced mineralisation and osteo/cementogenic marker expression in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) relative to CeraPutty. The reduced mineralisation may be attributed to compositional differences, such as variations in radiopacifier type and the presence of resin or filler components, which could attenuate the osteogenic response despite a similar calcium silicate–based formulation.

In the present study, C-Root BP showed inferior mineralisation potential compared with the other tested materials, which appears to contrast with findings from a previous study,21 which evaluated C-Root SP after complete setting for 3 days, using the material in a sealer formulation and testing its effects on pre-osteoblast cells. In contrast, the present study evaluated the biological responses of hDPCs to material extracts prepared at different stages of the setting process. Therefore, differences in material formulation, setting duration, cell type, and experimental conditions may account for the inconsistent findings.

Currently, direct evidence regarding the effects of C-Root BP on hDPCs remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is among the first to directly evaluate the biocompatibility and mineralisation response of hDPCs to C-Root BP. The observed sustained cytotoxicity and relatively low mineralisation potential suggest that its biological performance is compromised. These findings highlight the need for further comprehensive investigations, including both in vitro and in vivo studies, to substantiate its efficacy prior to widespread clinical application in vital pulp therapy.

Among the five materials, only TotalFill BC Putty consistently downregulated pro-inflammatory gene expression in hDPCs across the early setting stages. The present study evaluated key inflammatory and immunomodulatory gene expression in hDPCs exposed to the extracted medium from hydraulic silicate cements with different hydraulic setting stages. The extraction medium from TotalFill BC Putty significantly downregulated inflammatory-related gene expression (TNFA, IL1B, and IL6) in samples extracted at 15-minute and 24-hour post-mixing. Consistent with a previous study in dogs, TotalFill BC sealer exhibited lower inflammatory responses than MTA Fillapex in periapical lesion models at 1 month post-application, suggesting the facilitation of inflammation resolution.28 In addition, TotalFill BC Putty reduced IL6 expression in hDPCs on day 7 after treatment.29 However, the present study provides important additional evidence that freshly mixed material (15 minutes after mixing) significantly reduced the expression of inflammation-related genes, a finding of particular clinical relevance because cells are exposed to the material before it has fully set.

The extraction medium derived from TotalFill BC Putty also upregulated TGFB1 expression while downregulating IDO expression. TGFB1 also acts as an anti-inflammatory and enhances the immunomodulation properties of dental pulp cells.30 TGF-β1 regulates a wide variety of immune cells, including T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells.31 Thus, an increase in TGFB1 expression in hDPCs treated with TotalFill BC Putty-extracted medium could suggest a role in immunomodulation. IDO expression is upregulated in inflamed human dental pulp tissues.32 It is known to play a critical role in immunomodulation by promoting regulatory T-cell differentiation and directing macrophage polarisation toward the M2 phenotype.33,34 Therefore, the reduced IDO expression observed in hDPCs treated with TotalFill BC Putty extraction medium may compromise their immunomodulatory capacity. Accordingly, the immunomodulatory effects of TotalFill BC Putty on hDPCs warrant further investigation. However, it should be noted that the co-culture of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells with IDO-knockdown M2 macrophages has been reported to increase alkaline phosphatase expression and mineral deposition.34 Therefore, the downregulation of IDO expression in hDPCs treated with TotalFill BC Putty extraction medium may also have contributed to the enhanced mineralisation observed in these cells. Corresponding with the upregulation of TGFB1, a known odonto/osteogenic induction molecule,35,36 these two molecules may exert their effects on both mineralisation and immunomodulation in this situation. This hypothesis warrants further investigation.

Interestingly, the present study observed a marked increase in IL8 mRNA expression when hDPCs were exposed to extracted medium from ProRoot MTA and Biodentine at both 15 minutes and 24 hours after mixing. IL-8 was detected in the inflamed pulp tissues and was upregulated in cultured dental pulp treated with lipopolysaccharide.37 IL-8 functions as a chemoattractant for neutrophils, and increased IL-8 levels have been shown to correlate with enhanced human neutrophil migration.38 Previous study has demonstrated that pulp-capping materials, including calcium hydroxide, adhesive resin, and MTA, stimulate IL-8 secretion and promote neutrophil migration.38 Therefore, the role of IL8 expression in hDPCs exposed to extraction media derived from ProRoot MTA and Biodentine warrants further investigation. Notably, mild and well-regulated inflammation may contribute positively to tissue healing and regeneration.39 Nevertheless, additional studies are needed to clarify the significance of ProRoot MTA- and Biodentine-induced IL-8 expression in hDPCs.

Additionally, the present findings suggest that extracts from the tested hydraulic - silicate-based materials at the 7-day setting stage exhibited a distinct immunomodulatory effect on hDPCs. The marked reduction in IFNG, TGFB1, and IDO expression observed with all materials may indicate attenuation of a pro-inflammatory signalling profile after complete setting of the materials. This finding supports the concept that the biological behaviour of hydraulic - silicate-based materials is time-dependent, with progressive setting associated with reduced release of early-reactive components and a subsequent decline in inflammatory stimulation. This study has several limitations. First, the use of an in vitro cell culture model does not fully replicate the complexity of the clinical environment, where factors such as inflammation, immune responses, and vascularisation may influence cellular behaviour and material biocompatibility. Second, the concentration and exposure conditions of the tested materials may not accurately reflect clinical scenarios, as the quantity and diffusion of materials in vivo differ from those in controlled laboratory settings. Future studies should evaluate the biological effects of these materials on different cell types relevant to endodontic procedures, such as hPDLSCs, to better understand their regenerative potential. A comparative evaluation using an in vivo model of pulp capping or pulpotomy remains warranted. Third, the use of PBS during the hydration step of the prepared materials. Although PBS does not fully replicate serum or other body fluids, it is a commonly recommended extraction medium for in vitro biocompatibility testing. In addition, its buffering capacity may help neutralise pH changes that occur during material setting. While this could reduce biological responses to pH fluctuations, it also suggests that the effects observed in human dental pulp cells in this study may be more directly attributable to substances released from the materials themselves. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Conclusion

Our findings extend this evidence by showing that (i) all types of hydraulic silicate–based materials showed cytotoxicity of the freshly mixed materials; however, with dilution, the cytotoxicity is in the acceptable range, (ii) TotalFill BC Putty, Biodentine, and ProRoot MTA demonstrated superior mineralisation, and (iii) TotalFill BC Putty exhibited a favourable immunomodulatory profile. These differences may be particularly relevant in vital pulp therapy for inflamed teeth, where materials combining low sustained cytotoxicity, strong mineralisation, and controlled inflammation are likely to optimise long-term pulpal healing. Further in vivo studies and randomised clinical trials are warranted to confirm whether the favourable in vitro profile of materials translates into superior clinical outcomes.
