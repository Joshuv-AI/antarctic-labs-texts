From renal biopsy to genetic diagnosis: EYA1 mutation in branchio-oto-renal syndrome with renal insufficiency

Background

Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by pre-auricular pits, hearing loss, branchial fistulae, and renal urinary tract malformations. EYA1, the most common causative gene of BOR syndrome, functions as a transcriptional co-activator that regulates the development of the kidneys, inner ears, and branchial arches during embryogenesis [1–3]. To date, over 290 pathogenic EYA1 variants have been reported worldwide, including missense, frameshift, nonsense, splice-site, and large deletions [4–6]. These pathogenic variants may lead to loss or dysfunction of the EYA1 protein, resulting in multi-system developmental defects. During renal development, EYA1 plays a critical role in the maintenance and differentiation of the metanephric mesenchyme [7].

Smith et al. [8] reported that approximately 90% of patients with BOR syndrome present with hearing loss, 50% demonstrate branchial fistulae, and 65% exhibit renal anomalies, including agenesis, dysplasia, ureteropelvic junction obstruction, and hydronephrosis. Although renal involvement is common in BOR syndrome, systematic studies of its renal pathology remain scarce. The few existing renal biopsy cases primarily show pathological features of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and mesangial proliferation [9, 10]. However, the relationship between these pathological changes and specific genetic mutations has not yet been systematically elucidated.

We report a case of BOR syndrome confirmed by renal biopsy and genetic testing. The patient carries a nonsense mutation in the EYA1 gene, and renal pathology showed mild mesangial proliferation and thinning of the basement membrane.

Case report

An 18-year-old male presented for acne treatment and was found to have renal insufficiency (Stage 2 chronic kidney disease) and proteinuria, with no hematuria or edema. Physical examination showed stable vital signs, body temperature 36.4 °C, blood pressure 115/65 mmHg, height 175 cm, and weight 60 kg. Bilateral preauricular and branchial fistulas were noted, with serous discharge from the right branchial fistula (Fig. 1). The patient was born at term with a birth weight of 2.9 kg and no perinatal abnormalities. The patient had a history of recurrent branchial fistula discharge and had undergone drainage treatment for infection. Hearing loss developed around age 10, and vision loss was noted at age 17. There was no family history of preauricular fistula, branchial fistula, hearing loss, vision loss, or kidney disease.

Bilateral preauricular and branchial fistulas. A: Bilateral preauricular fistulas. B: Branchial fistulas

After admission, laboratory tests revealed serum creatinine at 121µmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate of 81.38 mL/min/1.73 m², albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) of 133.16 mg/g, serum albumin at 48 g/L, and no hematuria. Complete blood count, liver function, serum immunoglobulins, complement levels, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate were normal. Tests for autoimmune antibodies, hepatitis, syphilis, and other infectious diseases were negative (Table 1). Color Doppler ultrasound indicated that both kidneys were small. Left kidney measuring 79 × 32 mm (length and width dimensions below the mean normal values by 2.07 and 3.00 standard deviations, respectively), right kidney measuring 97 × 40 mm (length and width dimensions below the mean normal values by 0.79 and 2.00 standard deviations, respectively). Both kidneys showed normal parenchymal echogenicity and blood flow. Pure-tone audiometry revealed bilateral mixed hearing loss. Tympanometry showed a type Ad pattern in the left ear and a type B pattern in the right ear. Ophthalmic examination suggested refractive error, with no anterior lenticonus, posterior subcapsular cataract, posterior polymorphous dystrophy, or retinal flecks. Bilateral internal auditory canal MRI revealed bilateral submucosal cysts of the maxillary sinuses.

Renal pathology from the right kidney biopsy revealed that among the 15 glomeruli sampled,7 showed glomerular enlargement (180 ~ 280 μm), 4 demonstrated proliferation of mesangial cells and matrix on light microscopy, accompanied tubular dilation and loss of brush border in a few tubules, mild fibrosis (Fig. 2). Electron microscopy demonstrated focal thinning of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) (160 ~ 380 nm) with segmental wrinkling, segmental foot process effacement, and no complex deposition was observed. (Fig. 3). Immunofluorescence showed weak positivity for IgM and C3, while IgA, C4, IgG, C1q, albumin, fibrinogen, kappa, and lambda were negative. Expression of COL4α3, α4, and α5 in the GBM was normal (Supplementary Fig. 1). We performed whole-exome sequencing for genetic analysis and identified no pathogenic variants in the COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5 genes. However, a heterozygous variant (c.1081 C > T p.Arg361*) was detected in the EYA1 gene. Genetic analysis of the parents revealed that neither carried this EYA1 gene variant, suggesting it represents a de novo mutation and that this case is sporadic (Fig. 4). According to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, the EYA1 gene variant (c.1081 C > T p.Arg361*) was classified as pathogenic. Based on the above findings, a definitive diagnosis of BOR syndrome was confirmed.

Regarding treatment, given the patient’s proteinuria, we selected an angiotensin II receptor blocker for renal protection to slow the progression of chronic kidney disease. Due to hyperuricemia, the patient also received uric acid-lowering therapy, with follow-up examinations every six months. Following angiotensin II receptor blocker therapy, the patient’s ACR demonstrated a consistent downward trend, decreasing from 133.16 mg/g at the initial treatment date (July 13, 2022) to 73.23 mg/g at the final follow-up (June 4, 2025). This indicates the treatment has had a positive effect in reducing the patient’s proteinuria. In November 2024, the patient underwent incision and drainage surgery for an abscess at the left preauricular fistula. The last follow-up was on June 4, 2025, with blood pressure stable at 100 ~ 120/65 ~ 75 mmHg. Serum creatinine fluctuated between 109 ~ 130µmol/L, and ACR was 73.23 ~ 92.31 mg/g.

Discussion

BOR syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder. Its diagnostic criteria include major features such as branchial anomalies, deafness, preauricular pits, and renal anomalies, as well as minor features such as external ear anomalies, middle ear anomalies, inner ear anomalies, preauricular tags, facial asymmetry, and palate abnormalities [11]. This report describes a case of BOR syndrome initially presenting with renal insufficiency. The patient exhibited hearing loss, accompanied by preauricular pits, branchial fistulas, and small kidneys with renal insufficiency. Genetic testing revealed a pathogenic heterozygous variant in the EYA1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of BOR syndrome.

The patient exhibited hearing loss, visual loss, mild creatinine elevation, and proteinuria, raising suspicion for Alport syndrome. Although the patient lacked typical hematuria, literature reports indicate that some Alport syndrome patients (such as those with specific mutation types or female carriers) may not present with hematuria [12, 13]. Therefore, we maintained a high suspicion for this disease during initial diagnosis and listed it as the primary differential diagnosis. BOR syndrome is characterized by branchial arch anomalies, which are absent in Alport syndrome [11]. Pathologically, Alport syndrome typically presents with diffuse thickening or thinning of the GBM, along with lamellation and the characteristic basket-weave. In rare instances, GBM rupture is seen. In contrast, although renal pathological studies of BOR syndrome remain limited, they primarily involve FSGS and mesangial proliferation [9, 14, 15]. In terms of genetic patterns, BOR syndrome is caused mainly by autosomal dominant mutations in genes such as EYA1, whereas Alport syndrome results mainly from X-linked or autosomal recessive or dominant mutations in COL4α3/4/5 genes [16–18]. Furthermore, the pathological findings of GBM thinning and enlarged glomeruli also raise the consideration of differential diagnosis from oligomeganephronia. Oligomeganephronia is often associated with low birth weight; however, in this case, the patient had a normal birth weight, and the number of glomeruli observed in the renal biopsy tissue was normal, which is inconsistent with the core pathophysiological mechanism of oligomeganephronia. Additionally, differential diagnosis should also consider thin basement membrane nephropathy, which similarly demonstrates basement membrane thinning on electron microscopy. However, its characteristic features include uniform thinning of the basement membrane (< 250 nm). Clinically, it often presents as isolated hematuria with typically normal renal function, and lack of extrarenal manifestations such as hearing loss or branchial fistulae [19]. This patient initially presented solely with hearing loss without obvious external ear anomalies and exhibited mild renal insufficiency. These atypical manifestations increased the diagnostic challenge. However, genetic testing identified a pathogenic variant in the EYA1 gene, and physical examination revealed characteristic preauricular pits and branchial fistulas, ultimately confirming the diagnosis of BOR syndrome. This diagnostic process suggests that some patients with BOR syndrome may present with atypical clinical manifestations. In particular, the absence of significant ear anomalies may lead to misdiagnosis if detailed physical examination is overlooked. Therefore, clinicians should emphasize comprehensive physical examinations, with particular attention to characteristic features such as preauricular pits and branchial fistulas, and combine these with genetic testing to improve the early diagnosis of BOR syndrome.

Additionally, the ophthalmic examination of this patient revealed refractive error. Although ocular abnormalities are uncommon in BOR syndrome, the literature has documented associated findings such as cataracts and lacrimal duct aplasia [11, 20, 21]. Although refractive error is a nonspecific manifestation, it may still belong to the diverse phenotypic spectrum of BOR syndrome. It is important to note that this differs from the characteristic ocular lesions of Alport syndrome (anterior lenticonus, posterior subcapsular cataract, posterior polymorphous dystrophy, retinal flecks). This nonspecific ocular presentation further highlights the broad phenotypic heterogeneity of BOR syndrome, suggesting that systematic ophthalmologic evaluation of suspected patients may also provide clues for diagnosis and differential diagnosis.

According to published studies, approximately 66% of patients with BOR syndrome exhibit renal developmental abnormalities, with 6% progressing to renal failure [21]. However, currently only about 8.7% of BOR syndrome studies have been published in nephrology journals, indicating that awareness and attention to renal damage associated with this disorder remain insufficient [22]. Some patients are already diagnosed with end-stage renal disease at the time of initial diagnosis, or gradually develop end-stage renal disease over time [21, 23, 24]. In this case, the patient sought medical care for acne and was incidentally found to have proteinuria and elevated serum creatinine, suggesting that renal involvement may be insidious. This is consistent with literature reports that the renal manifestations of BOR syndrome can range from mild renal insufficiency to end-stage renal disease [25–27]. When patients present only with mild or atypical extrarenal features, clinicians may not immediately consider BOR syndrome, potentially leading to omissions in systematic renal function assessments and detailed physical examinations. This lack of clinical vigilance may result in diagnostic delays. Therefore, for patients with ear and branchial arch anomalies, even in the absence of obvious renal symptoms, routine renal function tests and urinary tract imaging should be performed.

To date, seven cases of renal biopsy have been reported worldwide, with the most common pathological findings being FSGS (4 cases), followed by mesangial proliferation (2 cases) and membranous nephropathy (1 case) [9, 10, 15, 28–31]. In this case, the patient presented with mild mesangial proliferation with glomerular enlargement, but did not meet the diagnostic criteria for glomerulosclerosis, suggesting that the renal lesions may be in the early stages of the disease. In terms of immunofluorescence, three previously reported cases showed immune complex deposition [9, 10, 31]. Although IgM and C3 deposition were detected in this case, the intensity was weak, differing from typical immune complex-mediated nephritis. Electron microscopy findings in previous reports included podocyte foot process effacement (2 cases), uneven thickening of the GBM (1 case), and electron-dense deposits (1 case) [10, 15, 28, 31]. In addition to segmental foot process effacement, this case is the first to observe partial thinning of the basement membrane (160–380 nm). Additionally, the uniqueness of this case is reflected in the coexistence of chronic tubulointerstitial lesions and acute tubular injury, indicating potential secondary contributing factors such as hyperuricemia or hemodynamic alterations superimposed on the renal hypoplasia. These findings not only broaden the renal pathological spectrum of BOR syndrome, revealing a disease continuum from mild mesangial proliferation to FSGS, but also propose for the first time that basement membrane thinning may serve as a novel feature of EYA1 gene-related renal impairment.

Genetic testing plays a critical role in the diagnosis of BOR syndrome. The known pathogenic genes primarily include EYA1, SIX1, and SIX5, with EYA1 gene mutations being the most frequently detected, accounting for approximately 40% [11, 32, 33]. Among the seven previously reported cases that underwent kidney biopsy, six completed genetic testing. Except for one negative result, the remaining cases all revealed EYA1 mutations, including partial or whole gene deletions, as well as variants such as c.1475 + 1G >C, c.1748T >C, and c.1319G >A. In this case, a nonsense mutation in the EYA1 (c.1081 C >T p.Arg361*) gene was detected, which was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines [34]. The pathogenic mechanism likely involves premature translation termination, resulting in truncated proteins or nonsense-mediated mRNA decay [35]. Orten et al. [36] reported two patients with the same mutation who exhibited renal impairment, but did not describe their pathological features. Our study is the first to systematically describe the renal pathological changes with this mutation, providing an important supplement to the renal phenotype spectrum of this mutation. In this study, the patient with this pathogenic variant exhibited hearing loss without significant external ear malformations, initially leading to a clinical diagnosis leaning toward Alport syndrome. Subsequent detailed physical examinations identified key findings such as branchial fistulae and preauricular fistulae, thereby correcting the diagnostic direction. The diagnosis was ultimately confirmed as BOR syndrome by genetic testing, further highlighting the necessity of physical examination and genetic testing. It is worth noting that no clear correlation has been established between the type of EYA1 variant and clinical phenotypes. The same mutation may exhibit significant differences in different individuals, suggesting potential modulation by genetic or environmental modifying elements [36]. In cases of kidney biopsy where genetic testing has been completed, all detected cases were associated with EYA1 variants. In contrast, SIX1 variant carriers were less likely to exhibit renal involvement, while the pathogenicity of SIX5 variants remains controversial [32, 37, 38]. These findings confirm the pivotal role of EYA1 mutations in kidney injury associated with BOR syndrome.

This study also has certain limitations. As a single case report, the generalizability and extrapolation of its findings are limited. Although we identified a novel pathological feature—focal thinning of GBM associated with EYA1 gene mutations, the prevalence of this feature among BOR syndrome patients and its exact clinical significance require validation in future larger cohort studies. Additionally, the glomerular hypertrophy observed in this case may be associated with compensatory changes in the right kidney following marked atrophy of the left kidney. Although glomerular hypertrophy has been reported in BOR syndrome [10, 15, 31], the patient in this case presented with marked atrophy of the left kidney, which may have led to a compensatory hyperfiltration state in the right kidney, subsequently resulting in glomerular hypertrophy. Therefore, it remains unclear whether this pathology is a direct manifestation of BOR syndrome or a compensatory secondary change. This also highlights the complexity involved in distinguishing primary damage from secondary adaptive changes in hereditary nephropathies.

In conclusion, this study reports a case of Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome caused by a de novo nonsense mutation (c.1081 C > T p.Arg361*) in the EYA1 gene and describes for the first time a novel renal pathological feature characterized by focal thinning of the GBM. This study emphasizes that definitive diagnosis of BOR syndrome relies on genetic testing, while renal biopsy provides significant auxiliary value in differential diagnosis. The combined application of these two approaches helps reduce misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis. Genetic testing not only provides critical evidence for the precise diagnosis of BOR syndrome but also reveals its genetic heterogeneity and the complexity of clinical phenotypes. In the future, the integration of multi-omics analysis and large-scale cohorts may further elucidate genotype-phenotype relationships, advancing the development of personalized treatments and genetic counseling.
