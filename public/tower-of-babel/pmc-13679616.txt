Rethinking sympathetic regulation in cancer: From uniform signaling to context-dependent precision targeting
Authors: Jun-yi Xu, Guo-zhong Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Sympathetic effects in cancer are determined by biological context, not activity alone.•Receptor subtype, direct target cell, and tissue niche define therapeutic vulnerability.•Circuit-based profiling may enable precision sympathetic-targeted cancer therapy.•Neuromodulation represents a future strategy beyond systemic adrenergic drugs.

The next challenge in cancer neuroscience is how to target sympathetic signaling

For decades, SNS activation has been recognized as a tumor-promoting driver, with adrenergic signaling linked to angiogenesis, metastasis, immune suppression, and therapy resistance [1]. The clinical finding that β-blockers, including propranolol, can enhance the therapeutic efficacy in some tumor types also strongly supports the notion that the SNS promotes tumor progression [2,3].

Concurrently, cancer neuroscience has emerged as a major frontier, revealing that peripheral nerves actively orchestrate tumor initiation, progression, and immune surveillance rather than passively coexist within the microenvironment [4]. This positions neural targeting as a potential therapeutic dimension complementary to cytotoxic, targeted, and immunotherapies.

However, an essential question remains unresolved: how should sympathetic signaling be therapeutically targeted? Current therapeutic strategies are largely based on the assumption that sympathetic activation represents a relatively uniform tumor-promoting pathway and therefore advocate broad inhibition of adrenergic signaling. Recent studies challenge this assumption. Instead of functioning as a single signaling axis, sympathetic regulation consists of multiple adrenergic receptors acting on distinct cellular populations within different tissue environments. We therefore propose that future therapeutic strategies should shift from indiscriminate sympathetic suppression toward context-dependent precision targeting of tumor-specific sympathetic circuits.

First, adrenergic receptor subtype contributes to signaling specificity. Extensive evidence from β1- and β2-adrenergic signaling studies has supported a tumor-promoting role of sympathetic regulation, particularly through impairment of anti-tumor immunity. β1-adrenergic activation has been linked to CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion, whereas β2-adrenergic signaling can disrupt immune surveillance through distinct cellular circuits, providing mechanistic support for the therapeutic exploration of β-adrenergic blockade. In contrast, recent studies have revealed that α2-adrenergic signaling may exert different anti-tumor effects in specific contexts. Pharmacological activation of host α2A-adrenergic receptors was shown to induce immune-mediated tumor rejection, with genetic evidence identifying ADRA2A in host immune cells as the critical mediator. However, this study did not directly manipulate sympathetic nerves and therefore should be distinguished from endogenous sympathetic neurotransmission. Similarly, local sympathetic regulation involving α2-adrenergic signaling has been implicated in melanoma control, although the specific α2 isoform was not genetically resolved. α2 receptors can also function as presynaptic autoreceptors regulating norepinephrine release, and systemic α2 agonism may involve additional peripheral or central effects. Together, these findings highlight that adrenergic receptor subtype represents an important determinant of sympathetic signaling outcomes, while the functional consequences remain dependent on the biological context in which each receptor operates. Furthermore, although norepinephrine serves as the same ligand for adrenergic receptors, individual receptor subtypes differ in ligand affinity, cellular distribution, and downstream signaling properties, suggesting that local neurotransmitter availability and receptor engagement patterns may further shape sympathetic regulation within tumors.

Second, the direct responding cell determines biological outcome. Adrenergic receptors are expressed across multiple compartments of the tumor microenvironment, including immune cells, malignant cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells. Consequently, activation of the same receptor subtype may generate distinct consequences depending on the responding cell population and downstream signaling network. β2-sympathetic signaling in macrophage could suppress anti-tumor immunity directly, while it could also influence immunity indirectly through non-immune cells. For instance, β2-adrenergic signaling in epithelial cells can impair tissue-resident memory T-cell-mediated immunosurveillance through an epithelial–immune circuit rather than direct receptor activation within T cells. Beyond immune regulation, sympathetic signaling could also directly affect malignant cells and other stromal compartments, thereby promoting tumor growth through mechanisms independent of immune modulation.

Third, tissue niche shapes adrenergic circuit behavior. Sympathetic innervation density, immune composition, stromal organization, and local neurotransmitter availability vary substantially across anatomical sites. As a result, adrenergic signaling within different organs, including skin, lung, and pancreas, may engage distinct neural circuits and produce divergent biological outcomes.

Collectively, these observations support a context-dependent model in which receptor subtype, direct target cell, and tissue niche interact to determine the dominant sympathetic circuit within each tumor. Based on this framework, we propose that tumor-associated sympathetic regulation may be conceptually categorized into several circuit patterns, including immune cell–mediated circuits, tumor cell–intrinsic circuits, stromal/epithelial/vascular-mediated circuits, mixed or uncertain multicellular circuits, and cases in which the dominant circuit remains unresolved. This classification is not intended as a fixed biological taxonomy, but rather as a hypothesis-generating framework to guide functional validation and therapeutic stratification. Identifying the dominant sympathetic circuit may therefore provide a conceptual foundation for precision therapeutic intervention.

Toward precision targeting of sympathetic–tumor circuits

This context-dependent framework provides a potential roadmap for translating sympathetic regulation into precision cancer therapy (Fig. 1). Multimodal characterization of individual tumors may be the first step. Rather than relying on adrenergic receptor abundance alone, future patient profiling may integrate blood-based biomarkers, tumor tissue analysis, and imaging approaches. These integrated measurements may enable the identification of the dominant adrenergic circuit within each tumor. Instead of defining tumors simply as adrenergic receptor-high or -low, this framework considers whether sympathetic regulation is primarily mediated through immune cells, tumor cells, stromal components, or multiple interacting circuits. In cases where the dominant circuit remains uncertain, functional validation will be essential before therapeutic intervention.

Circuit-based classification may guide future therapeutic strategies, including β-adrenergic blockade, α2-adrenergic receptor agonism, immunotherapy combination, and neurointervention. Importantly, α2-adrenergic agonism remains supported mainly by preclinical evidence and should be considered a therapeutic hypothesis rather than an established treatment. Neurointervention, including denervation and emerging neuromodulation technologies, represents an additional strategy to directly regulate tumor-associated neural circuits.

Therapeutic optimization will require careful consideration of receptor specificity, delivery strategy, and systemic toxicity. Selective targeting of disease-associated adrenergic circuits, rather than broad suppression of sympathetic function, may maximize anti-tumor effects while preserving physiological autonomic activity.

Future clinical development will depend on establishing reliable biomarkers, patient selection strategies, and prospective clinical trials. In particular, precision neuromodulation offers a promising direction by enabling spatially and temporally controlled manipulation of tumor-associated sympathetic circuits. Although still speculative, this approach may ultimately expand sympathetic targeting beyond pharmacological intervention toward programmable neural regulation as a new dimension of precision oncology.
