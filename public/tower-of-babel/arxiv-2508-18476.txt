Observability and State Estimation for Smooth and Nonsmooth Differential Algebraic Equation Systems
Hesham Abdelfattah, Sameh A. Eisa, Peter Stechlinski

Abstract
In this work, we extend the sensitivity-based rank condition (SERC) test for local observability to another class of systems, namely smooth and nonsmooth differential-algebraic equation (DAE) systems of index-1. The newly introduced test for DAEs, which we call the lexicographic SERC (L-SERC) observability test, utilizes the theory of lexicographic differentiation to compute sensitivity information. Moreover, the newly introduced L-SERC observability test can judge which states are observable and which are not. Additionally, we introduce a novel sensitivity-based extended Kalman filter (S-EKF) algorithm for state estimation, applicable to both smooth and nonsmooth DAE systems. Finally, we apply the newly developed S-EKF to estimate the states of a wind turbine power system model.



I
Introduction

Observability analysis aims to determine if we are able to identify the states of the system via access to the measurements of some output function. In mathematical terms, observability of an input-output dynamical system means that we can uniquely identify the initial conditions of the states via measurements of the output function.
In the literature, a common method to study local observability of ODE systems is the observability
rank condition test, which is based on successive Lie
derivative computations involving the output function
[
1
]
. There are other methods in the literature that study local observability, such as the sensitivity rank condition test (SERC)
[
2
]
. Recently, the authors in
[
3
]
showed that the SERC test for observability (and its closely related property, identifiability) is a more computationally efficient method when compared to Lie derivative-based tests, especially for large-scale systems. For ODE systems,
establishing (or assuming) system observability
has led to a matured literature of designing observers for state estimation, such as but not limited to extended Kalman filtering (EKF)
[
4
, Chapter 8]
; recently, geometric-based EKF was also introduced for control-affine ODEs
[
5
]
. It is important to highlight that all the aforementioned results are for smooth ODE input-output systems. Recently, the authors of this paper introduced a novel nonsmooth analog of the SERC test, the lexicographic SERC (L-SERC) test, which enabled the observability test for nonsmooth ODE systems
[
6
]
.
For piecewise ODE systems (i.e., ones which transitions between different modes), a discontinuous EKF (DEKF) algorithm was introduced in
[
7
]
and its convergence was studied — their approach operates on subintervals within each mode but does not theoretically treat the nonsmoothness in a direct way.

In this paper, we are concerned with another important class of input-output systems, namely, the smooth and nonsmooth DAEs of index-1.
Such systems are widely present in different engineering applications and physical models. For example, different applications where smooth DAEs are utilized are presented in

[
8
,
9
]
, and we see that nonsmooth DAE systems
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
are used in modeling process systems
[
13
]
, multibody mechanical systems
[
14
]
, and power systems
[
15
,
16
]
. In the literature, multiple works have been aimed at studying observability of
smooth
DAEs, such as the study of observability of linear DAEs (see
[
17
,
18
]
), and of nonlinear DAEs (see
[
19
,
20
]
). There have also been studies for designing observers for
smooth
DAEs, such as the
the application of EKF methods to DAEs in
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, sensitivity-based methods, such as SERC, have not been introduced to study observability of DAE systems (smooth or nonsmooth).

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we will use the lexicographic directional derivative (LD-derivative)
[
23
]
as a tool for obtaining derivative information of a given lexicographically smooth (L-smooth)
[
24
]

\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

\mathbf{x}_{0}

PC^{1}

[

The LD-derivative is is denoted by
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{M})
, where
\mathbf{M}=[\mathbf{m}_{1}\quad\cdots\quad\mathbf{m}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is some directions matrix. The LD-derivatives are computationally relevant objects as they satisfy sharp calculus rules, hence providing a practical, theoretically rigorous, numerical toolkit
[
26
]
. In addition, the LD-derivatives theory provides closed-form formulas for widely used nonsmooth functions
such as the absolute-value function and the max/min functions.
For example, given a directions matrix
\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{M}_{1,\bullet},\mathbf{M}_{2,\bullet})=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{1,\bullet}\\
\mathbf{M}_{2,\bullet}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times k}
,

\displaystyle\max{{}^{\prime}}(x_{0},y_{0};\bm{\mathrm{M}})

\displaystyle={\rm\bf slmax}([x_{0}\quad\mathbf{M}_{1,\bullet}],[y_{0}\quad\mathbf{M}_{2,\bullet}])

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\mathbf{M}_{1,\bullet},&\text{if }\mathrm{fsign}\left(v_{0},\mathbf{V}\right)\geq 0,\\
\mathbf{M}_{2,\bullet},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

where
v_{0}=x_{0}-y_{0}
,
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{M}_{1,\bullet}-\mathbf{M}_{2,\bullet}
, and
\mathrm{fsign}
returns the sign of the first nonzero element, or zero otherwise.
The lexicographic derivative (L-derivative)
[
24
]
,
\mathbf{J}_{\rm L}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{M})
,
is a computationally relevant Jacobian-like object, i.e., it can be used in nonsmooth numerical methods
[
27
]
as it can substitute an element of Clarke’s generalized derivative
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\text{conv}\{\mathbf{F}:

\displaystyle\;\exists\mathbf{x}_{j}\to\mathbf{x}_{0},\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{x}_{j}\in S_{\mathbf{f}},\mathbf{J}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\to\mathbf{F}\},

where
S_{\mathbf{f}}
is the full measure subset of
\text{dom}(\mathbf{f})
on which
\mathbf{f}
is differentiable. With L-derivatives being the desirable object to compute, and LD-derivatives enjoying a strong toolkit for calculation, the picture is completed by noting that an L-derivative can be obtained from an LD-derivative when the matrix
\mathbf{M}
has full row rank (and is hence right invertible):

\underbrace{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{M})}_{m\times k}=\underbrace{\mathbf{J}_{\rm L}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{M})}_{m\times n}\underbrace{\mathbf{M}}_{n\times k}.

(2)

Note that if the function is at least
C^{1}
, then it is true that
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{Jf}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathbf{M}
(hence, the L-derivative equals the Jacobian in this case).
Lastly, for later use in discussing nonsmooth DAEs, we recall that the Clarke generalized derivative projection
[
28
]
of
\mathbf{g}
with respect to
\mathbf{w}
at
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{0})
is

\pi_{\mathbf{w}}\partial\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{0}):=\left\{\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{w}}:\exists[\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{x}}\quad\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{w}}]\in\partial\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{0})\right\}.

Remark III.5
.

The L-SERC matrix provides local observability conclusions in the direction

. The significance of the direction here is that for nonsmooth systems/output functions, studying observability depends on the piecewise nature of the system.
For example, for a nonsmooth output function

III-B

Determining Observable and Non-Observable States

The observability L-SERC test presented above is used not only to determine the observability of the smooth/nonsmooth DAE system, but also to determine which of the system’s states are non-observable. That is, it determines the states that are not possible to be identified from the system’s output, hence causing the non-observability of the system.
To make this more precise, we introduce the following definition.

Definition III.8
.

The differential states and algebraic states in

\bm{\chi}_{\rm lno}\subseteq\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n_{x}}\}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{\rm lno}\subseteq\{\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{n_{w}}\}

are
locally partially non-observable
(or simply
partially non-observable
) at
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{*}\in D_{z}
if there exists a neighborhood
N\subseteq D_{z}
of
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{*}
and a connected set
V\subseteq D_{z}
containing
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{*}
such that the states in
\bm{\chi}_{\rm lno}\cup\bm{\alpha}_{\rm lno}
are locally non-observable in
N\cap V
.

As a part of our L-SERC observability algorithm, we determine which states are locally partially non-observable near
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{*}
by analyzing the singular vectors produced from the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the L-SERC output matrix.
We first determine which differential states are non-observable: given the SVD
\textstyle{\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\mathbf{d}}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{\rm T}
,
with left singular vectors matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{y}\times(N+1)n_{y}}
, singular values matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{y}\times n_{x}}
, and right singular vectors matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
, if
{\rm rank}({\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\mathbf{d}})=n_{r}<n_{x}
, then the right nullspace
{\rm Null}({\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\mathbf{d}})\neq\emptyset
. Similar to the procedure outlined in
[
29
, Section III-B]
, we can identify the zero singular values
\sigma_{n_{r}+1}=\cdots=\sigma_{n_{x}}=0
and it follows that the
n_{x}-n_{r}
columns in
\mathbf{V}
associated with these zero singular values
form a basis for
\text{Null}({\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}}_{\mathbf{d}})
. Accordingly, we construct a matrix whose columns are those
n_{x}-n_{r}
singular vectors,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{r}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{(n_{r}+1)}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{(n_{x})}\end{bmatrix},

(11)

from which we can identify the non-observable differential states by placing
\mathbf{V}_{r}
in row echelon form,
\textnormal{rref}(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\rm{T}})
, and identifying pivot columns; if row
j
of
\textnormal{rref}(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\rm{T}})
contains the pivot from pivot column
i
, then
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is partially non-observable.

With non-observable differential states
\bm{\chi}_{\rm lno}
identified, we can shift to identifying non-observable algebraic states
\bm{\alpha}_{\rm lno}
(thanks to generalized differentiation index one — see Remark
III.6
):
For each
\mathbf{w}_{i}
, we construct the matrix

\bm{\Psi}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}:=(\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}^{\rm L}(t_{0}),\dots,\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}^{\rm L}(t_{N})),

(12)

where
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}^{\rm L}(t_{k})=[(\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{w}}^{\rm L}(t_{k}))]_{i,\mathcal{J}}
(i.e.,
i^{\rm th}
row with columns indexed by
\mathcal{J}
) and
\mathcal{J}=\{j:\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\bm{\chi}_{\rm lno}\}
. Then
\bm{\Psi}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}
characterizes to the sensitivity of the algebraic
\mathbf{w}_{i}
with respect to the non-observable differential states and so if
\text{rank}(\bm{\Psi}_{\mathbf{w}_{i}})\neq 0
, then
\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\bm{\alpha}_{\rm lno}
is non-observable.

IV
Sensitivity-based EKF Method

An important application of the observability property is the design of dynamic observers, with EKF and its variations being an important technique. In this section, we present a variation of EKF, the sensitivity-based EKF (S-EKF), that utilizes the sensitivity information from (
7
) to provide accurate estimations of the system’s observable, or even partially-observable, states. Consider the DAE system given by (
3
), but with process noise and measurement noise added to the system, i.e., the DAE system is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}(t),\mathbf{u}(t))+\bm{\Omega}(t),\quad\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0},

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}(t),\mathbf{v}(t)),

(13b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}(t),\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))+\bm{\nu}(t),

(13c)

where
\bm{\Omega}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the process noise, i.e., zero mean, uncorrelated, independent continuous random
variables with covariance
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
and
\bm{\nu}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is a measurement noise, i.e., discrete uncorrelated independent random variables with zero mean and covariance
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{y}}
. The goal of the S-EKF is that, for some model that can be predicted by a DAE system (smooth or nonsmooth), we use the “approximate” DAE model in (
3
) to give accurate estimation of the states of the “true” DAE model in (
13
), with the process noise representing the uncertainty in the modeling and the measurement noise representing sensor limitations.

In the proposed S-EKF, the sensitivity functions are utilized in the calculation of the covariance matrix. In particular, we solve the following “augmented” nonsmooth forward sensitivity system (cf. the non-augmented version in (
7
)) with the covariance matrix,
\bm{P}(t)
, added as an extra state:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*};(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{0})),\quad\mathbf{X}(t_{0})=\mathbf{M},

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{v}^{*};(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{0})),

(14b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{v}^{*};(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})),

(14c)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{P}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{W}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\bm{P}+\bm{P}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{W}\end{smallmatrix}\right]^{\bm{T}}+\bm{Q},\quad\bm{P}(t_{0})=\bm{P}_{0},

(14d)

where
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*})
are the solutions of (
3
) corresponding to
\mathbf{x}_{0}^{*}
, which admits the unique solution
(\mathbf{X}^{*},\mathbf{Y}^{*})
on
[t_{0},t_{f}]
. Here
\mathbf{M}
acts as a “probing matrix” for sensitivity information and should be chosen to be full rank, while the choice of
\bm{P}_{0}
should be made based on the confidence in the accuracy of the initial conditions
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{0}
(see
[
31
, Chapter 5]
for more details).

Example V.1

(Wind Turbine Power System)
In order to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed L-SERC observability test and S-EKF algorithm, we consider a wind turbine power system under a constant wind speed (i.e., with constant active power and time-changing reactive power). This model is represented by the following DAEs
[
32

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{ref}=K_{Q_{i}}(Q_{cmd}-Q)+\Omega(t),

\displaystyle\dot{E}^{\prime\prime}_{q}=K_{V_{i}}(V_{ref}-V),

(18)

\displaystyle 0=V^{4}-[2(PR+QX)+E^{2}]V^{2}+(R^{2}+X^{2})(P^{2}+Q^{2}),

is the algebraic state,

represents the reference terminal voltage,

represents the equivalent voltage controlling the reactive current injection, and

K_{Q_{i}}=0.1

1.

Smooth output function

VI
Conclusion

We provided a novel sensitivity-based method for assessing local observability of smooth and nonsmooth DAE systems, which can also
judge
observable/non-observable states.
In addition, we also introduced a novel sensitivity-based EKF algorithm (S-EKF), which uses the L-SERC observability test and sensitivities of DAE systems (smooth or nonsmooth) to provide accurate estimations of the system’s states from the system’s output. We demonstrated the new methods in a wind turbine power system application.
Lastly, we note that the methods here are amenable to smooth/nonsmooth DAE systems that are non-autonomous and/or have explicit parametric dependence, the latter of which can be dealt with by amending the states with additional variables
x_{\theta_{1}},\ldots,x_{\theta_{n_{p}}}
and augmenting the system with
n_{p}
additional ODEs
\dot{x}_{\theta_{i}}=0
.
