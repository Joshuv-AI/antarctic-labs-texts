Glycemic Patterns Assessed by Continuous Glucose Monitoring in People with Type 2 Diabetes Treated with Insulin Glargine 100 U/mL in Primary Care: The GPDetect Study
Authors: Virginia Bellido, Ana Cebrián, Cristóbal Morales, Alberto S de las Hazas, Alfonso Soto-Gonzalez, Carla Morer-Liñan, José M Borrachero, Gabriel Cuatrecases, Elena L García, Judith López-Fernández, Silvia Lozano-García, Álvaro M Bayón, Cristina M Niño-Azcarate, Oscar Laclaustra, Paulina Fuentealba

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypoglycemia is a common complication in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) treated with basal insulin, yet its true prevalence in real-world primary care remains underexplored. This study used blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) to assess glycemic patterns in patients with T2DM on basal insulin-supported oral therapy (BOT) with insulin glargine 100 U/mL (Gla-100) in Spain.MethodsGPDetect was a prospective, multicenter, observational study in adults with T2DM treated with Gla-100 for ≥ 6 months in primary care. Participants underwent 14 days of blinded CGM (FreeStyle Libre® Pro iQ®). The primary objective of the present study was to estimate the proportion of patients with T2DM treated with Gla-100 whose TBR (glucose < 70 mg/dL) exceeded the internationally recommended threshold of 4%. Secondary endpoints included time in range (TIR), time above range (TAR), episodes of clinically significant hypoglycemia (< 54 mg/dL), cognitive function (MMSE), and fear of hypoglycemia (EsHFS).ResultsAmong 136 patients (mean age 69 years), 16.9% had TBR > 4%, with a higher prevalence at night (23.5%) than during the day (12.5%). Clinically significant hypoglycemia (< 54 mg/dL > 1%) occurred in 8.8% of patients. Hyperglycemia was common: 46.3% spent > 25% of the day between 181–250 mg/dL, and 28.7% spent time > 250 mg/dL. Only 55.9% achieved TIR ≥ 70%. Patients receiving SGLT2i had lower mean glucose and EsHFS scores. Cognitive impairment (MMSE < 23) was associated with longer hypoglycemia duration.ConclusionsBlinded CGM revealed a relevant burden of unrecognized hypoglycemia—particularly nocturnal—as well as daytime hyperglycemia in patients with T2DM treated with Gla-100 in primary care. These results support the clinical value of CGM in informing treatment decisions and improving individualized diabetes management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-026-01903-2.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Continuous glucose monitoring, Hypoglycemia, Time below range, Insulin glargine

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Hypoglycemia is frequent yet often unrecognized in people with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) treated with basal insulin in primary care, and real‑world evidence using blinded continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) to assess its true prevalence is limited.Understanding glycemic patterns, including unreported hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, may help address unmet needs in basal‑insulin-treated patients managed outside specialist settings.What was learned from this study?In people with T2DM treated with insulin glargine 100 U/mL, both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia may remain unrecognized in routine clinical practice. Blinded CGM can uncover these hidden glycemic disturbances and provide actionable information to optimize treatment in primary care.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disease associated with significant morbidity and mortality, largely due to complications arising from poor glycemic control [1, 2]. It is a major public health issue in Spain, with a 31% increase in the registered population with T2DM from 2007 to 2018, accompanied by increases of 6% and 11% in cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), respectively [3, 4]. This increase underscores the urgent need for effective management strategies to mitigate the disease’s impact on public health.

Despite the ample variety of glucose-lowering strategies available, many adults with T2DM eventually require and benefit from insulin therapy. The strategy of using an insulin basal analog as an addition to oral anti-diabetic drugs (OADs) aims to improve glycemic control while minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia and weight gain associated with other existing insulin formulations [5, 6]. Insulin glargine 100 U/mL (Gla-100) was the first basal analog approved in 2000 [5]. It has demonstrated efficacy in improving glycemic control, becoming extensively studied and serving as a reference for comparing newer basal insulins [7, 8]. Its favorable safety profile encouraged earlier initiation of insulin therapy and contributed to long-lasting glycemic control [9, 10 ].

Hypoglycemia remains one of the most concerning complications of basal insulin therapy and its risk increases with intensive glycemic control and the duration of T2DM [8, 11]. Studies with second-generation basal insulins, such as insulin glargine 300 U/mL (Gla-300) and insulin degludec (IDeg), have demonstrated metabolic efficacy comparable to that of Gla-100 but with a lower risk of hypoglycemia [12, 13]. Hypoglycemia is associated with significant short- and long-term consequences. Acute episodes can lead to physical symptoms such as dizziness, confusion, and loss of consciousness, while severe events may result in hospitalizations, cardiovascular complications, or even death [14, 15]. Chronic exposure to hypoglycemia may contribute to impaired cognitive function and to reduced treatment adherence, as fear of hypoglycemia can discourage patients from optimizing insulin doses or adhering to prescribed regimens [16, 17]. These challenges are further exacerbated by the asymptomatic or nocturnal nature of many hypoglycemic events, which often go undetected with conventional self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) methods [18, 19]. Furthermore, tools for assessing hypoglycemia awareness, such as the Clarke questionnaire, have been found neither valid nor reliable in Spanish people with T2DM [20].

In recent years, there has been a shift from relying solely on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels as the primary marker for glycemic control toward the more dynamic and informative use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems [21]. This represents a transformative advance in diabetes care, offering a comprehensive assessment of glucose dynamics that goes beyond the limitations of SMBG. In effect, CGM systems provide continuous real-time data on glucose levels, trends, and variability, enabling the detection of hypoglycemic episodes that might remain unnoticed [22]. One of the key metrics derived from CGM is time below range (TBR), defined as the percentage of time the glucose levels fall below a specified threshold (e.g., < 70 mg/dL) [23]. TBR has emerged as a critical parameter for assessing the risk and burden of hypoglycemia in both clinical and research settings [24]. Despite the increasing use of CGM and new glycemia metrics, most evidence for people with T2DM has been generated in specialist or trial settings, often involving selected populations and structured treatment intensification strategies. In contrast, the majority of people with T2DM treated with basal insulin are managed in primary care, where access to CGM and detailed glucose pattern assessment remains limited [25, 26]. As a result, clinically relevant hypoglycemia (mainly asymptomatic and nocturnal episodes) may be substantially underrecognized in routine practice. Furthermore, reliance on HbA1c as the primary marker of glycemic control may mask clinically important glucose variability and hypoglycemic exposure. Real-world data using blinded CGM in primary care populations are therefore essential to better characterize true glycemic patterns and identify unmet clinical needs in this setting.

Given that most people with T2DM treated with basal insulin are managed in primary care settings, understanding real-world glycemic patterns in this context is particularly relevant for optimizing treatment decisions. This study aims to generate real-world evidence on the prevalence of unrecognized hypoglycemia assessed by blinded CGM in a population with apparently stable glycemic control, a setting that remains underrepresented in literature.

The primary objective of the present study was to estimate the proportion of patients with T2DM treated with Gla-100 whose TBR (glucose < 70 mg/dL) exceeded the internationally recommended threshold of 4%. Additional glycemic parameters, safety and tolerability, insulin regimen patterns, and healthcare providers’ (HCPs) satisfaction with CGM use were also assessed to provide a real-world snapshot of glycemic management in clinical practice [23]. To this end, CGM was used as a tool to characterize glycemic patterns and hypoglycemia, and not to evaluate its impact on clinical outcomes. By identifying gaps and unmet needs in patient care, these findings can help guide HCPs in making more informed therapeutic decisions, ultimately improving T2DM management.

Methods

Study Design

The GPDetect was a prospective, multicenter, observational study, conducted in people with T2DM treated with insulin glargine 100 U/mL (Gla-100) as part of a basal insulin-supported oral therapy (BOT) regimen in routine care, carried out at 22 centers in Spain—both primary care practices and endocrinology departments of public or private hospitals. Although some study procedures were conducted in hospital-based endocrinology departments, all participants were routinely managed and followed in primary care, reflecting real-world clinical practice. The study design intended to fulfill the main objective of evaluating hypoglycemia events in people with T2DM treated with Gla-100 by implementing a blinded CGM system to record glucose data without influencing the participants’ behavior or the treatment decisions made by HCPs. The study comprised four (4) visits scheduled every 14 days such as: baseline visit/visit 1, follow-up visit 2 (14 + 5 days), and follow-up visit 3 (28 + 5 days). The end of study/visit 4 took place on day 120 ± 5.

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and applicable regulatory requirements. The study protocol was approved by the Seville Provincial Drug Research Ethics Committee (CEIm code: 2541-N-21). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Patient Population

The participating investigators enrolled consecutive patients aged ≥ 18 years with T2DM diagnosed at least 3 years prior to study entry, on basal insulin for ≥ 2 years, treated with Gla-100 for ≥ 6 months, and on a stable dose of basal insulin > 0.2 U/kg/day for ≥ 2 months. Eligible patients had HbA1c values between 7% and 8.5% recorded in the last 3 months in their electronic medical records (EMR), a mean fasting plasma glucose (FPG) value between 80 and 140 mg/dL based on three SMBG made in the last week, and the ability to wear the FreeStyle Libre® Pro iQ® CGM System. Exclusion criteria included: hospitalization for > 7 days in the month prior to study inclusion, pathologies or treatments affecting T2DM control (e.g., chemotherapy, immunosuppressants, systemic corticosteroids), pregnancy or breastfeeding, participation in another clinical trial, treatment with rapid-acting or premixed insulin, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) levels < 30 mL/min, and previous or current treatment with second-generation basal insulin (Gla-300 or IDeg).

Study Outcomes

The primary objective of the present study was to estimate the proportion of patients with T2DM treated with Gla-100 whose TBR (glucose < 70 mg/dL) exceeded the internationally recommended threshold of 4%. Secondary objectives aimed to describe the patient characteristics and treatment patterns at visit 1; assess glycemic control, safety, and tolerability profile during the CGM period; evaluate the incidence and severity of hypoglycemic events documented in the EMR at study visits 1 and 3; analyze the monitoring tools and devices used at visit 3; assess patient fear of hypoglycemia and patient cognitive impairment associated with hypoglycemia events; and assess the impact of CGM system data availability on clinical decision-making by HCPs.

Data Collection

This study had three data sources: the patient’s EMR, questionnaires [the Spanish version of the Hypoglycemia Fear Survey (EsHFS) and the HCP ad hoc questionnaire], and the FreeStyle Libre® Pro iQ® CGM device. The CGM was implemented in a blinded fashion to record glycemic variables and hypoglycemic events in patients with T2DM receiving routine follow-up care in primary care settings. Study variables were calculated over the valid 14-day period closest to study visit 3.

The number and average duration of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia events [classified according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) working group] were registered from the EMR in visits 1 and 3 [27]. All adverse events (AEs) were documented and managed according to applicable regulations. This comprised all AEs, including serious AEs (SAEs), regardless of their association to treatment with Gla-100.

Statistical Analysis

The study hypothesis arose from the observation that people with T2DM treated with Gla-100 in primary care may experience unperceived or unreported hypoglycemia. The International Consensus on Time in Range recommends that people with T2DM maintain glucose levels within 70–180 mg/dL for at least 70% of the day and below 70 mg/dL for less than 4%. Due to the lack of previous data, the sample size calculation assumed that 50% of patients would have a TBR ≤ 4%. A sample of 148 patients, including a 10% replacement rate, was estimated to provide a 95% confidence level and ± 8.5 units precision. Consequently, approximately 150 patients were planned.

Quantitative variables were summarized by mean, median, SD (standard deviation), first quartile (Q1) and third quartile (Q3), minimum and maximum, and N; qualitative variables by frequencies. A 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was calculated for the main quantitative variables. Comparisons between independent samples used the t-test or Mann–Whitney test on the basis of normality. The chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess associations between categorical variables.

The primary objective—the percentage of patients with a TBR ≤ 4% over a 24-h period—was calculated using CGM data over 14 days. Glycemic control was assessed using CGM-derived metrics according to international targets for time in range (TIR), TBR level 1 (glucose 54–69 mg/dL ≤ 4%) and level 2 (< 54 mg/dL ≤ 1%), and time above range (TAR) level 1 (glucose 181–250 mg/dL ≤ 25%) and level 2 (> 250 mg/dL ≤ 5%). The mean time (min) in each range was determined for the three periods: night (00:00–05:59), day (06:00- 24:00), and full day (24 h), following international consensus for CGM [28]. Safety and tolerability were analyzed on the basis of number, duration, and severity of hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic episodes, classified by ADA criteria [29, 30]. Treatment-related hospitalizations, emergency visits, and AEs, including injection site reactions and hypersensitivity, were documented. Treatment patterns were characterized using EMR data on insulin and non-insulin drugs, doses, schedules, and concomitant medications. Devices performance was monitored for technical issues with the FreeStyle Libre® Pro iQ®. Fear of hypoglycemia was assessed using the total EsHFS score [31, 32]. The impact of CGM on clinical decision-making by HCPs was evaluated through the score and description of the results from an ad hoc HCP-specific questionnaire.

Cognitive impairment was measured using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; score < 23 suggesting impairment) [33–35]. Binary logistic regression identified factors associated with hypoglycemia, using variables with p < 0.200 from bivariate analysis; model optimization followed the Mallows’ Cp method.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 160 participants were enrolled between 1 April 2022 and 1 February 2023. Of these, 24 were excluded from the final analysis: 20 due to screening failure and 4 due to loss to follow-up. Thus, a total sample of 136 patients were evaluated. All enrolled patients received routine follow-up care in primary care settings.

The patients had a mean age of 69 years, with the majority being male (62.5%) and white (99.3%). The mean age at T2DM diagnosis was 52 years, with an average disease duration of 17.5 years (Table 1). Hypertension and dyslipidemia were the most common comorbidities, affecting 77% and 75% of the patients, respectively. The mean body mass index (BMI) was within the range classified as overweight.

T2DM Treatments

All patients were treated with insulin Gla-100, which was predominantly administered in the evening (58.8%; Table 2). Four patients (2.9%) received insulin twice daily. On average, patients administered 30.5 ± 12.2 units of insulin per day. The most commonly prescribed non-insulin treatments were biguanides, followed by SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), DPP-4 inhibitors, and GLP1 receptor agonists (Table 2). The most frequently administered concomitant medications targeted the cardiovascular system, followed by the gastrointestinal tract, metabolism, and nervous system (Supplementary Table S1).

Glycemic Control

Regarding the main study outcome, 16.9% of patients spent more than 4% of their total day in hypoglycemia (< 70 mg/dL). When stratified by time of day, 12.5% experienced this during the daytime, and 23.5% during nighttime hours (Fig. 1). These corresponded to a mean TBR level 1 of 30.5 min for the full day period, and 25.1 and 48.8 min specifically for day and nighttime, respectively.

In terms of clinically significant hypoglycemia, 8.8% of patients registered glucose levels below 54 mg/dL for more than 1% in the full day period, remaining about 14.4 min per day in this range. This occurred in 5.1% of patients during the day and in 15.4% at night, indicating that approximately 10% more patients experienced hypoglycemia during the nighttime period compared with daytime.

The percentage of patients who maintained their glucose within the target range (70–180 mg/dL) for at least 70% of the day was 55.9% overall, 52.9% during the day, and 64.7% at night.

Concerning hyperglycemia, 46.3% of patients spent more than 25% of the time in TAR (180–250 mg/dL), and 28.7% at higher glucose levels > 250 mg/dL for more than 1% of the time over the full day (Fig. 1). On average, patients spent 318.8 min per day in TAR level 1 and 94.6 min in TAR level 2. Hyperglycemia occurred more frequently during the day, where 53.7% of patients had a TAR (180–250 mg/dL) > 25% (about 340 min) and 31.6% a TAR > 250 mg/dL > 5% (about 104 min). During nighttime, level 1 hyperglycemia was registered in 33.1% of patients for approximately 250 min, while 21.3% reported level 2 hyperglycemia (about 65 min).

The mean glucose over 24 h was 154.9 ± 33.1 mg/dL and 91.9% of the patients registered a CV ≤ 36%. The group of patients treated with SGLT2i has been compared with those not treated with SGLT2i (Supplementary Table S2). Patients treated with SGLT2i have a significantly lower average glucose level (148.0 mg/dL versus 163.7 mg/dL; p = 0.013) and a lower GMI value (6.8 versus 7.2; p = 0.013).

Incidence and Severity of Hypoglycemic and Hyperglycemic Events

A total of 40 patients (29.4%) reported at least one hypoglycemic episode in the 6 months preceding the baseline visit, with half of them resulting in visits to the emergency room (ER) and/or hospitalizations ≤ 7 days (Table 3). Throughout the study period, only five patients reported hypoglycemia in the period from baseline to visit 3, with no need for ER visits or hospitalization.

More episodes of hyperglycemia were reported by patients compared with hypoglycemia, mostly occurring during the day, however, those reported at nighttime had a longer duration. Patients spent an average of 6 h with glucose levels between 180–250 mg/dL and 2 h above 250 mg/dL during the night when considering the whole CGM period (Fig. 2). Patients who administer their basal insulin in the morning were compared with those who administer their basal insulin in the evening (Supplementary Table S3). Patients who administer insulin in the evening had higher average HbA1c levels (7.7%) compared with those who administered it during the day (7.5%; p = 0.002). Other glycemic parameters and the number of hypo- or hyperglycemic events did not differ statistically on the basis of the timing of basal insulin administration (Supplementary Table S3).

Safety and Tolerability During CGM

A total of 61 adverse events (AEs) were recorded in 38 patients (27.9%, Table 4). Five patients experienced six serious adverse events (SAEs). One of them was a level 3 hypoglycemic event associated with basal insulin Gla-100 and resolved on the same onset day. One death was reported due to a cerebrovascular accident (CVA), unrelated to any hypoglycemic event or to the basal insulin Gla-100. The most frequently reported AE was hypoglycemia, followed by hyperglycemia and urinary tract infection. Concerning injection site reactions and hypersensitivity, two patients reported medical device pain and one application site pruritus, all related with the CGM sensor and not related with the basal insulin Gla-100 (Table 4). A total of 25 patients reported technical issues with the CGM device (18.4%) (Supplementary Table S4). The most frequently reported issue was the accidental removal of the sensor.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

The mean (SD) of the total score of the Hypoglycemia Fear Survey in Spanish (EsHFS) assessed at the baseline visit was 56.1 (± 21.7). Patients who used SGLT2i reported lower levels of fear related to hypoglycemia, as indicated by their lower scores on the EsHFS questionnaire (53.0 ± 21.4 versus 60.0 ± 21.6; p = 0.043; Supplementary Table S2).

A total of 131 and 5 patients scored ≥ 23 and < 23 in the Mini-Mental Cognitive Examination (MMSE), respectively (Table 5). The relationship between the patient’s potential cognitive decline was analyzed through a binary logistic regression model according to the score obtained in the MMSE (< 23 points) versus the factors associated with hypoglycemic events. The data collected at the baseline visit revealed that patients with TBR (< 54 mg/dL) > 1% had 16.3 times more possibilities of having MMSE < 23 when compared with the patients with TBR (< 54 mg/dL) ≤ 1% (Table 5). However, the number of patients with MMSE scores < 23 was small, and this finding should therefore be interpreted with caution. This means that patients who spend more time in a hypoglycemic state than recommended by the consensus guidelines may have a higher probability of experiencing cognitive impairment.

Healthcare Provider Satisfaction

After reviewing the CGM data, HCPs reported in their ad hoc questionnaire that only 35.3% of the patients had the expected glycemic control, while 27.9% and 44.1% had more episodes of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia than expected, respectively (Table 6). In general, they found that in the future, CGM will gain more weight in their clinical decision-making.

Discussion

The GPDetect study provides real-world evidence on glycemic control among people with T2DM on a BOT regimen using insulin Gla-100 in Spanish primary care. The main outcome revealed that 16.9% of participants exceeded the recommended TBR > 4%, indicating a substantial hypoglycemia burden, with nighttime hypoglycemia present in about 10% of patients. Overall, the population showed a greater tendency toward hyperglycemia than hypoglycemia. Insulin Gla-100 was well tolerated, with serious adverse events in 3.7% of patients. Cognitive assessment using the MMSE showed a correlation between increased time in hypoglycemia and cognitive impairment. HCPs reported that CGM data significantly influenced their clinical decisions, planning to incorporate it more in future T2DM management.

Among people with T2DM treated in routine practice in Spain, approximately one in five did not achieve the hypoglycemia target established by the International Consensus on TIR [23]. Hypoglycemia remains a concern in individuals treated with insulin for more than 5 years, affecting approximately 25% of them [36]. Studies involving people with T2DM on basal insulin consistently highlight frequent hypoglycemia. In 140 older individuals treated with basal insulin plus OADs, 50% initially had TBR > 4%, and 30.3% still do after 6 weeks of CGM [37]. Bergenstal et al. reported that approximately half of the participants experienced nocturnal hypoglycemia by SMBG (58.3%) or CGM (47.8%) [38]. Wang et al. later found that patients spent around 7% of the day below 70 mg/dL [39], and Conti et al. reported persistent time spent in the 55–70 mg/dL range despite intermittent CGM [40]. Several factors may contribute, including poor adherence, clinical inertia, disease progression, insulin resistance, and insufficient glucose monitoring [41–44]. Achieving TBR < 4% remains challenging for some people with T2DM, emphasizing the need for personalized strategies and careful monitoring.

About 10% of patients experienced more TBR during the night than the day, suggesting prolonged nocturnal hypoglycemia. Although Gla-100, with a 3-h onset, may predispose patients to nocturnal events (58.8% of the patients had the evening schedule) [45, 46], no statistically significant differences were found between morning and evening administration. The EDITION study reported reduced nocturnal hypoglycemia when switching from Gla-100 to Gla-300, including in people with T2DM over 65 years [7, 47], suggesting that second-generation basal insulins may improve control and reduce hypoglycemia. It is important to note that the aim of the present study was not to compare different basal insulin analogues, but rather to describe glycemic patterns detected by blinded CGM in people with T2DM receiving BOT in routine clinical practice. Although exploratory analyses suggested some differences in nocturnal CGM-derived metrics between administration schedules, these findings were not consistent and should be interpreted with caution given the observational design and limited sample size for subgroup comparisons.

CGM identified far more hypoglycemic episodes than reported in clinical records or by patients. While only 3.7% reported hypoglycemia, 16.9% exceeded the TBR target and 8.8% spent > 1% of time with glucose < 54 mg/dL. This discrepancy highlights the diagnostic value of CGM in revealing silent and nocturnal hypoglycemia and guiding therapy. These findings are particularly relevant in primary care, where treatment decisions are often guided by HbA1c and fasting glucose values. The discrepancy between clinically reported hypoglycemia and CGM-detected events suggests that a significant burden of hypoglycemia may remain unrecognized, potentially contributing to therapeutic inertia and suboptimal insulin titration.

Nearly half of the patients (46.3%) spent > 25% of full day with TAR 180–250 mg/dL, rising to 53.7% at night. Fear of hypoglycemia may drive patients to maintain higher glucose levels or over-treat early symptoms [48]. The 3-h onset of Gla-100 could also contribute to daytime hyperglycemia [45, 46]. Hyperglycemia, particularly postprandial, has been linked to cognitive decline and receives growing attention in guidelines [49, 50]. A report from the Netherlands demonstrated that even well-controlled individuals with T2DM experienced hyperglycemia for more than 9 h a day [51]. In the MOBILE study, patients with T2DM using basal insulin had a mean glucose value of 208 ± 47 mg/dL and spent 61% of the day with glucose ≥ 180 mg/dL and 20.9% with glucose ≥ 250 mg/dL [52]. Together with our findings, these results support closer monitoring of hyperglycemia to prevent macro- and microvascular complications, which drive most diabetes-associated comorbidities [53, 54]. Whether people with long-standing T2DM on basal insulin might benefit from stricter glycemic targets remains an open question. Tighter glucose control may reduce microvascular complications [55–57], but must be balanced against hypoglycemia, with individualized goals considering the patient’s overall health status, risk factors, and comorbidities [27]. These findings suggest that CGM may help clinicians identify hidden glycemic disturbances and support individualized adjustments in basal insulin titration, treatment intensification strategies, and timing of administration.

The mean age of 69 years and comorbidity profile in our cohort reflect the typical insulin-treated patients with T2DM in real-world Spanish primary care [2, 58, 59]. Biguanides were the most prescribed non-insulin treatment, followed by SGLT2i and DPP-4 inhibitors, reflecting current guidelines [27]. Patients treated with SGLT2i were more likely to have adequate glucose levels and less fear of hypoglycemia on EsHFS, consistent with evidence that SGLT2i improve glycemic control and reduce fear (through the HFS score) when combined with insulin degludec/aspart [61–63].

Although 55.9% achieved ≥ 70% TIR, hypoglycemia remained the most frequently reported AE (26.2%), followed by hyperglycemia (4.9%). Only two patients reported medical device site pain, and one patient experienced application site pruritus—all attributed to the CGM sensor and not to Gla-100. The data suggest that wearing the CGM device does not pose major inconveniences or discomfort, as previously published [64]. Indeed, only 25 (18.4%) of the patients reported incidences with the sensor—the most often reported being accidental sensor removal. Serious adverse events occurred in 3.7% of patients, with only one level 3 hypoglycemic event associated with Gla-100, resolving the same day. These results support the safety and tolerability of insulin Gla-100 [65].

The mean EsHFS score was 56.1, indicating substantial fear of hypoglycemia, higher than in previous T2DM cohorts [66, 67]. Shi Min Ko et al., in 2022, found mean HFS scores of 39 and 31 in older people with T2DM with and without hypoglycemia, respectively, while Jódar-Gimeno, in 2015, reported scores of 31.1 and 18.8 for similar groups [66, 67]. Our higher scores reflect a mixed population with greater fear of hypoglycemia, which may also explain the observed trend toward hyperglycemia. The inability to predict hypoglycemic episodes is a key factor contributing to this fear, highlighting the importance of CGM in diabetes management. Conversely, Wang et al. reported a mean HFS score of 71.67 in a cross-sectional study with 385 people with T2DM, and Tasende et al. recorded an even higher score of 81 points in patients with T1DM, indicating greater fear levels than those observed in our study [32, 68].

Most patients (96.3%) had normal cognition (MMSE ≥ 23), but those with TBR < 54 mg/dL > 1% showed a markedly higher likelihood of cognitive impairment. The relationship between hypoglycemia and cognitive impairment is complex [69] with some studies linking frequent hypoglycemia to lower MMSE scores [17, 70, 71] or reporting no direct association [72]. Our analysis through the Mallows’ Cp method showed that patients with TBR < 54 mg/dL > 1% had a 16-fold higher likelihood of scoring < 23 on the MMSE, indicating cognitive impairment. Although exploratory due to the small number of patients with MMSE < 23, this finding is clinically relevant and supports growing evidence linking recurrent hypoglycemia with cognitive decline in older adults with T2DM.

HCPs estimated that only 35% of patients had their glucose levels in the recommended range, and 44% showed more hyperglycemia than expected. Although there was no consensus in considering whether TIR, TAR, or TBR is more relevant for clinical decision-making, most HCPs considered that CGM impacted their decisions in the clinical setting and intended to rely more on CGM metrics in future T2DM management.

Sex-specific analyses were not prespecified in this study, which focused on overall glycemic patterns in a real-world population.

Among the study’s limitations, the relatively small sample size may increase variability and reduce the ability to detect rare events. In addition, information regarding work schedules and sleep–wake patterns (e.g., shift work) were not collected. These factors may influence circadian glucose patterns and the occurrence of nocturnal hypoglycemia and should therefore be considered in future studies specifically designed to assess their impact on glycemic variability. Additionally, the study’s inclusion criteria limited participants to those with HbA1c levels between 7% and 8.5%. This exclusion of patients with more severe hyperglycemia or with better glycemic control may limit the generalizability of our findings to the wider population with T2DM. We acknowledge that the study population includes patients treated with different non-insulin antidiabetic therapies, which may influence the risk of hypoglycemia. This heterogeneity reflects routine clinical practice and is inherent to the real-world observational design of the study. Recommended glycemic targets for older subjects with T2DM (> 65 years), as the population of this study, aim to limit time spent with glucose < 70 mg/dL to no more than 1% or 15 min per day, as well as minimizing time in hyperglycemia [23, 27]. An analysis of the study objectives by age groups would have provided relevant information about the management of these patients in clinical practice in Spain. Finally, the absence of a comparator arm limits the ability to draw conclusions on comparative effectiveness between treatments. However, this descriptive, noninterventional design was necessary to evaluate real-world clinical practice patterns without influencing healthcare provider titration or patient behavior, and the study’s strength lies in the diversity of the sample and the broad geographical coverage. The 136 patients included in the study were recruited from 22 centers; this wide-ranging representation suggests that the findings could potentially be generalizable to the broader Spanish population with T2DM on a BOT regimen. Importantly, the GPDetect study highlights how CGM can reveal clinically relevant glycemic disturbances that remain undetected with conventional monitoring approaches in routine primary care.

Conclusions

In this clinical practice cohort, ~1 in 5 patients did not achieve the target of ≤ 4% TBR, with a higher nocturnal burden. The use of masked CGM detected unrecognized events and was associated with therapeutic changes by healthcare professionals. These findings support individualized insulin adjustment strategies and the use of CGM metrics in primary care. Comparative trials could evaluate whether different basal analogs or titration tactics reduce hypoglycemia while maintaining control. While insulin Gla-100 was generally well tolerated and serious adverse events were infrequent, the significant proportion of patients experiencing either hypoglycemia or prolonged periods of hyperglycemia suggests that achieving optimal glycemic control remains challenging in routine care. These findings address the study’s primary and secondary objectives by comprehensively characterizing real-world hypoglycemia occurrence, hidden daytime excursions, patient psychological/cognitive metrics, and subsequent healthcare provider management shifts in primary care. Overall, integrating CGM data into clinical care and promoting personalized treatment adjustments may enhance both the safety and effectiveness of basal insulin therapy in people with T2DM.
