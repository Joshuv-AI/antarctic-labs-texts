Combined cold atmospheric plasma and photobiomodulation is associated with improved early macroscopic and ultrasonographic wound-healing parameters after unilateral mastectomy in female dogs: an exploratory randomized intra-individual controlled clinical study
Authors: Guilherme Ramon Vieira da Silva, Márcio Santos de Oliveira Filho, Samuel Ferreira de França Silvestre, Gabrielle Arruda Carneiro, Rivaldo Bruno Medeiros de Lucena, Clarisse Maria Rodrigues Costa, Pedro Lucas Diogo Facundo, Fernando Lucas Costa Silva, João Marcelo Azevedo de Paula Antunes, Larissa de Castro Demoner, Valéria Veras de Paula, Airton Torres Carvalho, Carlos Augusto Galvão Barboza, Clodomiro Alves Júnior, Moacir Franco de Oliveira, Marcela Maria de Almeida Amorim, Carlos Eduardo Bezerra de Moura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostmastectomy complications remain relevant in veterinary surgery. Although cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and photobiomodulation (PBM) may improve tissue repair, evidence on their combined use is limited. This exploratory pilot study evaluated CAP–PBM therapy in nine female dogs undergoing unilateral mastectomy.MethodsA prospective, randomized, blinded, intra-individual controlled clinical study was conducted in nine female dogs with mammary neoplasia undergoing clinically indicated unilateral mastectomy. In each dog, one half of the surgical wound was randomly assigned to receive sequential CAP and PBM therapy, while the contralateral half remained untreated and served as an intra-individual control. Evaluations were performed on postoperative days 3, 7, and 14 (D3, D7, and D14) using macroscopic scoring systems, ultrasonography, and infrared thermography; histomorphometric analysis was performed only on D7.ResultsTreated regions showed significantly lower Modified Hollander Scar Scale (p < 0.001) and Modified Draize Scoring System (p = 0.001) scores. Ultrasonographic assessment demonstrated lower semiquantitative scores (p = 0.029) and reduced subcutaneous thickness (p = 0.034), especially during the early postoperative period. Thermographic findings did not differ significantly between groups. Histological analysis revealed numerically greater re-epithelialization in treated areas, although without statistical significance (p > 0.05).ConclusionsIn this exploratory pilot study, combined CAP + PBM therapy was associated with improved early macroscopic and ultrasonographic healing parameters after mastectomy in female dogs. These preliminary findings support further investigation of CAP + PBM as a promising non-invasive adjuvant strategy for postoperative wound management, pending confirmation in larger, adequately powered randomized studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05746-8.

Background

Mammary tumors represent one of the most frequent neoplasms in female dogs, accounting for approximately 54% of all tumors diagnosed in females in hospital records from two provinces in Italy, in addition to having an estimated incidence of 1,340.7 cases per 100,000 female dogs/year in animals under veterinary care in the United Kingdom [1, 2]. In female dogs, mammary neoplasms have a high malignancy rate, with carcinomas being the most frequently observed tumors in this species; in these cases, mastectomy remains the primary therapeutic approach, with surgical excision preferably performed using wide margins and combined with lymphadenectomy in extensive or biologically aggressive tumors [3–6].

As observed in humans, radical mastectomy in female dogs can result in significant complications such as seromas, infections, hematomas, necrosis, subcutaneous emphysema, and suture dehiscence, primarily due to the intense vascularization and lymphatic drainage of the mammary region [4, 7–16]. The rate of postoperative complications after mastectomy in female dogs can range between 40.7% [8] and 78% [4], depending on the surgical extent and individual factors such as obesity and anesthetic time. Given this high frequency of complications, improving postoperative management has become a relevant demand in veterinary oncology, particularly through the incorporation of non-pharmacological adjuvant therapies capable of promoting tissue recovery and reducing surgical complications [9, 17]. In this context, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating inflammation, controlling microbial contamination, and stimulating tissue repair have gained prominence in the postoperative management of patients undergoing mastectomy [18].

Among the emerging therapeutic approaches with potential application in the management of surgical wounds, cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and photobiomodulation (PBM) stand out, both with promising effects in accelerating healing and controlling postoperative complications [19, 20]. CAP is a non-thermal plasma that, through the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), ultraviolet radiation, and charged particles, exerts antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and regenerative effects [19, 21, 22]. Its efficacy has been demonstrated against resistant microorganisms, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Klebsiella pneumoniae, in addition to promoting angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and cell proliferation, with no reports of significant adverse effects in controlled protocols [23–28]. Recent studies reinforce its safe and effective use in human surgical wounds, highlighting benefits such as pain reduction, increased tissue resistance, and absence of postoperative infection [29].

PBM, in turn, uses non-ionizing light sources, such as LEDs and lasers in the visible and infrared spectra, to activate cellular processes favorable to healing through interactions with endogenous chromophores [30, 31]. Among its main effects are the increase in mitochondrial ATP production, the modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the regulation of gene expression and inflammatory mediators [32, 33]. Experimental results indicate that PBM stimulates fibroblast proliferation, promotes analgesia, and improves tissue oxygenation, supporting its potential role in cutaneous wound repair [33, 34].

PBM has already demonstrated efficacy in relieving symptoms of breast cancer-related lymphedema, promoting reduction of limb edema and improvement in quality of life [35]. In female dogs undergoing mastectomy, the application of fluorescent light associated with PBM (PBM-FLE) showed significant clinical benefits in the immediate postoperative period, including reduction of exudate, acceleration of healing, and better appearance of surgical incisions [17]. Considering that local inflammation, microbial contamination, edema, pain, and extensive tissue manipulation can compromise healing after mastectomies, CAP and PBM emerge as promising complementary therapies to modulate the surgical wound microenvironment through distinct and potentially integrated biological mechanisms.

Conventional management of mastectomy wounds in female dogs relies mainly on basic wound hygiene, protective bandaging or Elizabethan collars to prevent self-trauma, systemic antimicrobials and analgesics, and, in more severe cases, advanced dressings such as negative-pressure wound therapy [18]. These measures can reduce gross contamination and support patient comfort, but they act primarily on the external wound environment, depend heavily on strict owner compliance at home, and do not directly target the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving inflammation, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization [8, 10]. Consequently, they offer limited additional protection against the seromas, infections, and dehiscence that remain frequent after radical mastectomy [4, 8]. These limitations of conventional care motivated the investigation of non-pharmacological adjuvant therapies such as CAP and PBM, which act directly on the wound microenvironment through antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative mechanisms, offering a complementary strategy to standard wound management rather than a replacement for it.

The therapeutic association between CAP and PBM has garnered increasing interest due to the complementary potential of their biological effects [36]. While CAP primarily acts through the controlled generation of RONS, with antimicrobial effects, modulation of inflammation, and stimulation of tissue regeneration, PBM promotes cellular responses associated with mitochondrial bioenergetics, cell proliferation, analgesia, and repair organization. Thus, the combined application of these modalities may represent a rational strategy for extensive surgical wounds, where multiple biological events such as inflammation, contamination, edema, pain, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix deposition, and re-epithelialization occur simultaneously and interdependently. In vitro and preclinical studies suggest that combining these therapies may enhance antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative effects, promoting a cellular environment more conducive to tissue repair, in addition to demonstrating clinical feasibility and potential for translation to conventional medical use [36, 37]. However, clinical evidence on the integrated use of CAP and PBM in extensive surgical wounds remains scarce, especially in spontaneous and clinically relevant models, such as female dogs undergoing mastectomy. Most available studies remain limited to experimental or in vitro models [38], which have limited representativeness in the face of the biological complexity of extensive surgeries with a high probability of postoperative complications.

Given this, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of combined CAP and PBM therapy on macroscopic, ultrasonographic, and histomorphometric wound-healing parameters in female dogs undergoing unilateral mastectomy, without dissociating the contribution of each modality. We hypothesized that this non-invasive adjuvant approach would accelerate healing and reduce postoperative complications relative to untreated wounds, providing preliminary evidence with translational relevance to human patients undergoing similar procedures.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The research was previously approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of UFERSA (CEUA/UFERSA; protocol no. 27/2025), and all owners signed the Free and Informed Consent Form (FICF).

Experimental design

This study was conducted as a prospective clinical trial, randomized regarding the allocation of the treated area and blinded for the evaluators, carried out in nine adult female dogs, aged between 6 and 12 years, diagnosed with mammary neoplasia and undergoing unilateral mastectomy at the Veterinary Hospital of the Federal Rural University of the Semi-Arid Region (UFERSA).

The schematic design of the study is presented in Fig. 1. Before surgery, the animals underwent clinical, laboratory, and imaging evaluations for tumor staging and preoperative assessment. In each animal, only half of the surgical wound’s extent, cranial or caudal, was subjected to combined therapy with cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and photobiomodulation (PBM), as previously defined by randomization. The opposite half of the same wound was maintained as an intraindividual control, without experimental intervention. Thus, each animal acted as its own control, resulting in nine halves treated with CAP + PBM and nine control halves.

The combined therapy was applied sequentially during the intraoperative period, with the wound still open, and in the immediate postoperative period, after suturing; no additional treatment sessions were performed on the days following surgery. Evaluations were performed on days 3, 7, and 14 after surgery (D3, D7, and D14, respectively) using macroscopic healing scales, ultrasonography, and thermography. The macroscopic assessment included the Modified Hollander Scar Scale and the Modified Draize Scoring System [39, 40]. The ultrasonographic assessment included a semiquantitative score related to skin healing and measurement of epidermis-dermis complex and subcutaneous tissue thickness. Thermography was used to evaluate relative thermal variations between the suture line, perilesional skin, and adjacent normal skin. Tissue collection for histomorphometric analysis was performed on D7, using a 6 mm surgical punch obtained at the midpoint of the length of each wound half (for example, for a 16 cm half, the biopsy was taken 8 cm from the cranial border). The boundary between the treated and control halves was marked with a nylon skin stitch placed in healthy skin, allowing the two regions to be reliably distinguished throughout the study. On D14, the final evaluation was conducted by the surgical team, including suture removal and, when necessary, the implementation of additional therapeutic interventions; the exact biopsy site was excluded from this final macroscopic and ultrasonographic assessment. All data were collected separately for each half of the surgical wound, allowing paired comparison between treated and control areas.

Eligibility criteria

Non-pregnant female dogs aged between 6 and 12 years, regardless of breed or body condition score, with palpable mammary tumors larger than 3 cm or multiple mammary masses consistent with epithelial neoplasms confirmed by cytology were included.

The exclusion criteria comprised impairment of the general health status according to the surgical team’s assessment, use of photosensitizing agents, history of debilitating systemic diseases, including endocrine disorders such as diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and hypercortisolism, as well as renal insufficiency, suspicion or confirmation of inflammatory mammary carcinoma, presence of single lesions smaller than 0.5 cm, and the need for reconstructive procedures using donor tissue. Baseline characteristics of the nine dogs are summarized in Supplementary material 1.

Sample randomization

Randomization was performed exclusively to determine the half of the surgical wound that would receive the experimental intervention, cranial or caudal, since all dogs underwent combined CAP + PBM therapy on only one portion of the wound. For each animal, a simple draw was performed using Microsoft Excel® software, through the RAND function to generate random numbers. Values less than 0.5 indicated treatment of the cranial portion of the wound, while values equal to or greater than 0.5 corresponded to treatment of the caudal portion. The opposite half was maintained as an intraindividual control.

The allocation process was conducted by an independent researcher not involved in postoperative evaluations. In the end, 18 paired experimental units were obtained, corresponding to nine treated halves with CAP + PBM and nine control halves.

Surgical procedure (unilateral mastectomy)

The surgical procedure followed established guidelines [3, 41]. The animals underwent unilateral radical mastectomy under a standardized anesthetic protocol, including premedication with acepromazine (0.03 mg/kg, IM) and methadone (0.3 mg/kg, IM), anesthetic induction with propofol (2 mg/kg, IV), ketamine (1 mg/kg, IV), and fentanyl (3 µg/kg, IV), and maintenance with partial intravenous anesthesia (PIVA), combining vaporized isoflurane delivered through a calibrated vaporizer with continuous intraoperative infusions of fentanyl (5 µg/kg/h) and ketamine (0.6 µg/kg/h). Dogs were pre-oxygenated by face mask for approximately 3 min before induction. Intraoperative fluid therapy consisted of lactated Ringer’s solution administered intravenously at 5 mL/kg/h throughout anesthesia. Heart rate, respiratory rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide, invasive arterial blood pressure, and body temperature were continuously monitored with a multiparameter monitor throughout the surgical procedure. Tumescent local anesthesia was additionally performed prior to the surgical incision, using a solution prepared with 459.5 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution, 40 mL of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride, and 0.5 mL of epinephrine (final lidocaine concentration of 0.16%), administered at 15 mL/kg subcutaneously along the mammary chain with a Klein cannula, providing hydrodissection and supplementary intraoperative analgesia. In the event of intraoperative nociception, identified as an acute increase in heart rate or arterial blood pressure, rescue analgesia was provided with methadone (0.2 mg/kg, IV). All surgeries and anesthetic procedures were performed by the same trained surgical and anesthesia team.

The surgical technique consisted of an elliptical incision with safety margins, removal of the affected mammary chain and inguinal lymph nodes, in addition to resection of fascia or muscle when there was suspicion of tumor invasion. The axillary lymph node was identified with patent blue (Bleu Patente V®) and removed when indicated. The cranial and caudal superficial epigastric vessels were isolated and ligated. After excision of the mammary chain, the wound was irrigated with sterile saline solution (NaCl 0.9%).

The experimental therapy was applied intraoperatively, before wound closure, and repeated in the immediate postoperative period, after wound closure, always in the half previously defined by randomization. The synthesis was performed with approximation sutures, continuous subcutaneous and simple interrupted cutaneous sutures, using appropriate absorbable and non-absorbable threads. The dressing was made with sterile gauze, microporous adhesive tape, and bandaging, maintained for three days.

The postoperative drug protocol included amoxicillin with clavulanate (12.5 mg/kg, PO, BID, for seven days), tramadol (4 mg/kg, PO, TID, for seven to ten days), dipyrone (25 mg/kg, PO, BID, for four days), and meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg, PO, SID, for four days). Starting on the third day, the dressings were changed daily. Clinical follow-up occurred on postoperative days 3, 7, and 14. Postoperative pain was not assessed using a validated pain scale. Instead, the need for additional analgesia was determined empirically by the attending veterinary team, based on clinical evaluation of the animal (e.g., posture, behavior, and reaction to palpation of the surgical site) and on history obtained from the owner, in accordance with the postoperative drug protocol described above.

Generation and application of combined therapy (CAP + PBM)

The female dogs underwent exclusively combined therapy with cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and photobiomodulation (PBM), applied sequentially to half of the wound previously defined by randomization. The application was performed at two time points: intraoperatively, with the wound still open, and immediately postoperatively, after suturing. Half of the calculated treatment time for each therapy was applied intraoperatively, and the remaining half immediately postoperatively.

The CAP was generated using the Hygiaplasma EHF 1714 equipment, developed and adapted by the research group, utilizing dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) with high-purity helium gas (99.99%) at a flow rate of 2 L/min. The application parameters followed previously described protocols [42–44], including a power of 2.4 W, a frequency of 5 Hz, and pulsed mode. The plasma was applied through continuous scanning, maintaining an approximate distance of 1 to 2 cm between the device’s tip and the wound surface, with a total exposure time of 10 s/cm². The device operated at a discharge voltage of 1–5 kV. The plasma jet applicator consisted of a Teflon nozzle with a 10 mm outlet diameter, through which the helium flow passed after excitation by the dielectric barrier discharge generated by the device, with a gas inlet controlled by a valve and a flow meter. The temperature of the plasma jet at the tissue surface, measured with the same thermographic camera and under the same conditions used for the postoperative evaluations, averaged 35.1 °C. Throughout the intraoperative application, vital parameters were continuously monitored using a multiparameter monitor to detect any adverse physiological response.

Subsequently, low-power diode laser PBM (Kondortech – BioWave LLLT Dual) was performed, emitting red light at a wavelength of 660 nm, power of 30 mW, energy density of 0.2 J/cm², and continuous mode. The application was carried out by scanning over the same area previously treated with CAP, with the pen positioned perpendicular to the tissue and maintained at an approximate distance of 1 to 2 cm from the surface. The irradiated area included half of the wound defined by randomization and a margin of 1 to 2 cm beyond its edges. The exposure time was calculated using the relation time (s) = energy (J)/power (W), as recommended for acute lesions [45].

Considering a nominal beam diameter of 2 mm and a reported full-angle divergence of 20° for the laser pen, the estimated irradiation area at the tissue surface was approximately 0.24 cm² at 1 cm and 0.64 cm² at 2 cm from the tissue, corresponding to the working distance range used during application. Based on the nominal power output of 30 mW, the resulting irradiance was approximately 125 mW/cm² at 1 cm and 47 mW/cm² at 2 cm from the tissue surface.

The treated area, calculated individually for each animal as the estimated width of the mammary gland and adjacent perimammary tissue multiplied by half the length of the mammary chain, averaged 134.0 ± 43.6 cm² (range 90.0–230.0 cm²). This corresponded to a mean total CAP application time of 22.3 ± 7.3 min (range 15.0–38.3 min), a mean total PBM application time of 14.9 ± 4.8 min (range 10.0–25.6 min), and a mean total PBM fluence of 26.8 ± 8.7 J (range 18.0–46.0 J) per animal. Individual values for each animal are provided in Supplementary material 2.

The evaluations were conducted by two veterinarians who were blinded to the treatment allocation, using standardized digital photographs of the wounds on postoperative days 0, 3, 7, and 14. Each half of the surgical wound was evaluated separately. The average of the scores assigned by the two evaluators was used as the final score for each assessed area.

Ultrasonographic assessment

Measurement of the epidermis-dermis complex and subcutaneous tissue

Video clips of the wound region in the axial plane were acquired using a Z60 Mindray® device equipped with a 6.5 MHz multifrequency transducer. The images were obtained by gently sliding the transducer over the skin surface with minimal tissue compression, using acoustic gel as a contact medium and the interface between the skin and adipose tissue as an anatomical reference for the measurements.

The thickness of the epidermis-dermis complex and of the subcutaneous tissue was measured at three distinct and random points in each evaluated region, with the average subsequently calculated for each half of the wound. Because the 6.5 MHz transducer used does not provide sufficient axial resolution to reliably isolate the true epidermis, the outer layer was measured and analyzed as a combined epidermis-dermis complex, delimited from the underlying subcutaneous tissue at the interface with the adipose plane. Image acquisition and measurements were performed by a veterinarian specialized in ultrasonography, who was blinded to treatment allocation.

Semiquantitative ultrasonographic score

The semiquantitative ultrasound score was developed to assess morphological changes in the skin during tissue repair after mastectomy. The following parameters were analyzed: dermal echogenicity, presence of a hypoechoic subepidermal band, definition of skin planes, presence of fluid content or edema, and skin surface regularity. Each variable was classified on an ordinal scale from 0 to 3, according to the intensity of the changes, as presented in Table 3.

The evaluations were performed on days 3, 7, and 14 postoperatively, considering separately the control and CAP + PBM-treated halves. Two veterinarians, blinded to treatment allocation, independently assigned the scores. The final score was obtained by averaging the evaluations of the two observers.

Infrared thermography

Thermographic images of the skin surface were captured at a standardized distance of 0.5 m from the animal, with the camera positioned at a 90° angle relative to the surgical wound, using a FLIR SC620 camera (FLIR Systems, Wilsonville, OR, USA; temperature range − 40 to 500 °C, accuracy ± 2%, thermal sensitivity 40 mK, resolution 640 × 480 pixels). Animals were positioned in lateral recumbency during image acquisition. Images were saved in JPG format and subsequently transferred to a computer for analysis using FLIR Tools software. An emissivity value of 0.95 was adopted for all thermographic analyses. Ambient temperature and relative humidity were recorded with a digital thermohygrometer and entered into the software to standardize environmental conditions during image analysis. Hair clipping over the surgical area was performed only once, on the day of surgery, and the clipped area was maintained throughout the postoperative evaluation period. Thermographic image acquisition and analysis were performed by an evaluator blinded to treatment allocation.

For evaluation purposes, the extent of the surgical wound was divided into three distinct regions, based on a previously described methodology [46]: suture line, corresponding to the wound bed; perilesional skin, defined as the area located 2 cm from the wound edge; and adjacent normal skin, located 2 cm beyond the perilesional area. The temperatures of these regions were designated as T1, T2, and T3, respectively.

Thermographic evaluations were performed on days 3, 7, and 14 postoperatively. To reduce interference from factors such as ambient temperature variation and the animals’ body temperature, relative temperature was used as a descriptive measure. This approach involved calculating the thermal differences between the analyzed regions: ΔT1–2 (T1 − T2), representing the difference between the wound bed and perilesional skin; ΔT2–3 (T2 − T3), corresponding to the difference between perilesional skin and normal skin; and ΔT1–3 (T1 − T3), corresponding to the difference between the wound bed and normal skin. These measurements were used to assess the relative thermal evolution of the healing process in the treated and control halves.

Histological processing

Tissue samples from each half of the surgical wound were collected on the seventh day after surgery (D7), using a 6 mm diameter surgical punch. This period was chosen to evaluate possible effects of the experimental therapy during the early proliferative phase of the healing process. The biopsies were obtained separately from the treated and control halves.

The tissue fragments were immediately fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde, subsequently dehydrated in increasing ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin, following routine histological protocols. After embedding, 5 μm thick histological sections were obtained using a rotary microtome, which were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for subsequent histomorphometric analysis.

The histological images were captured using a conventional light microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica DM500; Leica Microsystems, Germany), using the Leica LAS EZ software for documentation and histomorphological analysis.

Histomorphometric analysis

The histomorphometric analysis was performed by two independent and blinded evaluators regarding treatment allocation, based on digital photomicrographs obtained as previously described. Five semi-serial histological sections per sample were analyzed, spaced at intervals of 20 μm. The average of the obtained values was calculated for each sample and used in the statistical analysis.

The volume density of connective tissue (Vvc), inflammatory cells (Vvi), and blood vessels (Vvv) in the dermis was measured at a 400× magnification using the Image-Pro Plus® software and a point counting system with a grid containing 96 points, following established stereological principles [47].

The height of the epithelium was measured in five random fields per section at 100× magnification using ImageJ® software (National Institutes of Health, USA), and the average per sample was subsequently calculated. The re-epithelialization ratio was obtained by measuring, at 40× magnification, the perimeter of the surgical wound surface covered by intact epithelium, divided by the total perimeter of the surgical gap, including ulcerated or non-epithelialized areas, and multiplied by 100 to obtain the final percentage. Histological sections where the epithelium was absent or completely ulcerated were assigned a value of zero for statistical analysis purposes.

Statistical analysis

The data were organized into electronic spreadsheets and analyzed using R software (v4.5.3). The analysis considered an intra-animal paired design, in which each female dog served as her own control, with comparisons made between the half treated with CAP + PBM and the control half of the same surgical wound. For variables evaluated longitudinally on postoperative days 3, 7, and 14 (D3, D7, and D14), mixed linear models for repeated measures were applied, including treatment (treated half vs. control half), time (D3, D7, and D14), and the treatment × time interaction as fixed effects, and animal as a random effect. This approach was used for macroscopic scores, thermographic parameters, ultrasonographic measurements of epidermis-dermis complex and subcutaneous thickness, and the mean semiquantitative ultrasonographic score.

For the macroscopic and ultrasonographic scores, the longitudinal analysis using a mixed model was complemented by paired comparisons at each time point, using the Wilcoxon paired test, with Holm adjustment when applicable. The histomorphometric parameters, obtained exclusively at D7, were compared between the treated half and the control half through a paired approach. Considering the small sample size and the presence of variables with asymmetric distributions or natural limits, the Wilcoxon paired test was adopted as the primary analysis. These data were expressed as median and minimum–maximum range. Longitudinal variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The significance level was set at 5% (p < 0.05).

To further evaluate interobserver reliability and the magnitude of the paired treatment effect, interobserver agreement between the two evaluators for the macroscopic and ultrasonographic semiquantitative scores was estimated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC 2,1; two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement model), with values below 0.50, 0.50–0.75, 0.75–0.90, and above 0.90 interpreted as poor, moderate, good, and excellent agreement, respectively. For these outcomes and for the D7 histomorphometric parameters, paired mean differences (treated minus control half) with 95% confidence intervals and Cohen’s dz effect sizes were calculated, complemented by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction applied within each outcome’s family of repeated comparisons. These analyses are detailed in Supplementary materials 3 and 4.

Considering the exploratory nature of this clinical study, the scarcity of prior data on the combined use of CAP and PBM in surgical wounds of female dogs undergoing mastectomy, and the limited availability of eligible patients during the recruitment period, a formal pre-study sample size calculation was not performed. The sample size was defined by convenience, based on the available eligible cases and the established inclusion and exclusion criteria. The paired intra-individual design, in which each animal acted as its own control, was adopted to reduce inter-individual variability and increase the sensitivity of the comparison between the treated and control halves of the same wound. The results were interpreted with a descriptive emphasis and cautious inferential approach.

Results

Baseline demographic, clinical, and surgical characteristics of the nine dogs included in this study are summarized in Supplementary material 1, and individual CAP and PBM treatment parameters are detailed in Supplementary material 2.

Semiquantitative analyses

The macroscopic evaluation of healing, conducted using the Modified Hollander Scar Scale [39] and the Modified Draize Scoring System [40], revealed consistent differences between the treated half (TH) with CAP + PBM and the control half (CH) throughout the postoperative period (Table 4). In both scales, lower scores indicate a more favorable healing progression and less intensity of macroscopic changes associated with the inflammatory process.

Discussion

The results of this clinical trial indicate that the combined application of cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and photobiomodulation (PBM) was associated with improvement in macroscopic and ultrasonographic parameters of wound healing in unilateral mastectomy wounds in female dogs. The best response observed in the treated halves was evidenced by lower scores on the Hollander and Draize scales, indicating better wound appearance, more uniform approximation of the edges, and lower intensity of macroscopic inflammatory signs, such as erythema, edema, and secretion [17, 39, 40].

The mechanistic considerations discussed below are hypothesis-generating: they are grounded in prior in vitro and preclinical literature rather than in molecular, biochemical, or redox measurements performed in the present study, and should be interpreted as plausible explanations rather than confirmed mechanisms. Considered individually, CAP acts mainly through the controlled generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), together with ultraviolet radiation and charged particles, exerting direct antimicrobial effects on the wound surface and modulating local inflammatory signaling [21, 48]. These RONS can also activate redox-sensitive intracellular pathways, such as NRF2/ARE, inducing an endogenous antioxidant response and stimulating fibroblast and endothelial cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [24, 26, 49]. PBM, in turn, acts predominantly at the intracellular level, through the absorption of visible and near-infrared light by mitochondrial chromophores such as cytochrome c oxidase, which increases mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production [32, 50]. This process is accompanied by a transient, controlled increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which act as secondary messengers capable of activating transcription factors involved in fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix synthesis [50–52].

Although derived from distinct sources, the extracellular RONS generated by CAP and the intracellular ROS modulated by PBM appear to converge on similar redox-dependent signaling mechanisms relevant to wound healing. At the low, transient concentrations typically induced by both therapies, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species act as signaling molecules rather than damaging agents, contributing to antimicrobial defense, activation of growth-factor and hypoxia-related pathways, recruitment of inflammatory and repair cells, and stimulation of angiogenesis and collagen deposition [48–50]. This contrasts with the sustained, high-level oxidative stress characteristic of chronic or poorly healing wounds, which is associated with impaired repair. The combined use of CAP and PBM may therefore favor a more balanced redox and inflammatory microenvironment than either therapy alone, integrating the antimicrobial, extracellular RONS-mediated effects of CAP with the mitochondrial, ROS-mediated stimulation promoted by PBM [36–38]. Beyond their role as redox signaling molecules, RONS generated by CAP have also been associated with local vasodilation and stimulation of angiogenesis, partly mediated by nitric oxide-related pathways and growth factors such as FGF-2, thereby improving microvascular perfusion and oxygen supply to the wound bed [24, 49]. The findings are consistent with the anti-inflammatory and pro-healing effects described in the literature for CAP and PBM when applied individually [33, 48]. In the present study, however, the intervention was designed as an integrated, clinically applicable approach to the management of extensive surgical wounds, rather than as a comparison between isolated therapeutic modalities. Although the underlying molecular mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study, they provide a plausible biological basis for the clinical and ultrasonographic improvements observed in the treated wounds.

Ultrasonographic analysis, used as a non-invasive tool for structural evaluation of the skin and underlying tissues, provided complementary evidence to macroscopic assessment. The significantly lower semiquantitative score in the treated halves suggests less tissue disorganization, with a reduction in findings such as fluid collections, diffuse hypoechogenicity, and loss of definition of cutaneous planes, changes often associated with edema, inflammation, and immature tissue repair [53–55]. These sonographic changes are plausibly related to the biological effects of CAP and PBM discussed above: by limiting RONS/ROS-driven inflammatory signaling and vascular permeability, the combined therapy may have reduced interstitial fluid accumulation and inflammatory cell infiltration, the main determinants of hypoechogenicity and blurred tissue-plane boundaries on ultrasound, while the stimulation of fibroblast proliferation and organized collagen deposition associated with PBM may have favored earlier redefinition of the epidermis-dermis complex and subcutaneous layers [48–50]. The lesser subcutaneous thickness observed in the treated half on D3 may likewise be interpreted as an indirect sonographic correlate of reduced edema and lesser intensity of the initial inflammatory response, in contrast to the subcutaneous thickening expected during the inflammatory phase of repair [53, 55, 56]. Thus, the ultrasonographic findings reinforce the hypothesis that the combined therapy may have favored earlier tissue organization, in agreement with the macroscopic observation of more uniform incisions and less secretion. However, this interpretation should be considered with caution, as ultrasonography provides indirect morphological evidence and does not replace specific cellular or molecular markers of inflammation.

Infrared thermography did not reveal statistically significant differences between the control and treated halves, despite a significant time effect for ΔT2–3 and ΔT1–3. This likely reflects the physiological basis of IRT, which is most informative in wounds with an underlying perfusion deficit [46, 57], a condition not present in this elective mastectomy model with preserved local perfusion in both halves. Species-specific factors, such as the insulating effect of the haircoat, may have further limited sensitivity compared with the hairless human skin on which most IRT literature is based [58, 59]. These findings do not support a direct thermographic effect of CAP + PBM but remain biologically plausible, since persistently reduced local temperature can compromise collagen deposition and fibroblast activity [60, 61].

The histomorphometric findings on D7 revealed that the proportion of reepithelialization was numerically higher in the treated halves, although without statistically significant difference, a result consistent with the limited statistical power of a sample of nine dogs. This finding remains exploratory and non-significant, and should not be taken as evidence of a biological treatment effect, noting that five of the nine treated halves achieved 100% reepithelialization, while only one control half showed complete epithelial closure. Epithelial thickness and the volume density of the inflammatory infiltrate were also numerically lower in the treated halves, without statistical significance, while the densities of connective tissue and blood vessels were similar between the halves. Together, these data suggest a descriptive pattern compatible with more advanced epithelial repair in the treated areas, but do not allow definitive conclusions that the therapy promoted superior histomorphometric changes by D7. The occurrence of partial dehiscence of the wound, associated with local inflammatory changes exclusively in the control halves of two animals, reinforces the potential clinical relevance of the macroscopic findings but should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of cases and the absence of microbiological confirmation of infection. These results are consistent with the antimicrobial properties attributed to CAP, including action against bacteria and biofilms, as well as the effects of PBM on tissue repair [38, 49, 62]. In parallel, these findings align with a previous study in which light-based modalities favored the healing of surgical wounds in female dogs undergoing mastectomy [17].

The integration of macroscopic, ultrasonographic, thermographic, and histomorphometric findings suggests that the combined CAP + PBM therapy may have primarily acted on the initial modulation of the inflammatory response and early organization of scar tissue. The reduced subcutaneous thickness on D3, associated with lower macroscopic and ultrasonographic scores, is consistent with less edema and reduced tissue disorganization in the treated halves. On the other hand, thermographic and histomorphometric findings did not show statistically significant differences between the halves, indicating that the more consistent effects of the intervention were detected by macroscopic and ultrasonographic methods. The higher numerical proportion of re-epithelialization on D7 may represent a complementary manifestation of more organized tissue repair, although this interpretation remains exploratory. However, it is not possible to infer direct mechanistic causality from these findings, as the present study did not evaluate molecular markers of inflammation, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, or extracellular matrix remodeling. Thus, the possible biological complementarity between CAP and PBM should be understood as a hypothesis supported by preliminary clinical and morphological findings, and not as a confirmed mechanism.

From a clinical standpoint, these preliminary findings suggest that combined CAP + PBM therapy may be a feasible, non-invasive adjunct to routine postoperative care after mastectomy, applicable at the time of surgery and in the immediate postoperative period without requiring specialized infrastructure beyond the devices themselves. If confirmed in larger trials, such an approach could offer veterinary surgeons an additional tool to support early wound organization and potentially reduce macroscopic complications, complementing rather than replacing standard surgical and postoperative wound care.

The main limitation of this trial was the small sample size, inherent to the clinical, prospective, and exploratory nature of the study, which reduces the statistical power to detect differences in variables with high biological variability. Additionally, the intra-individual design, although reducing variability between animals, does not completely eliminate the possibility of systemic influence from the therapy applied to one half of the wound on the control half, especially considering the anatomical continuity of the surgical incision. Another important limitation is that the control half remained untreated rather than receiving a sham CAP/PBM procedure; consequently, potential effects related to handling, gas flow, device proximity, transducer scanning, or increased local attention to the treated half cannot be fully distinguished from the specific biological effects of CAP and PBM, and future studies should incorporate sham-treated conditions to address this issue. Interobserver agreement between the two evaluators, reported in Supplementary material 3, was excellent for the Modified Hollander and Modified Draize scales (ICC = 0.945 and 0.966, respectively) but only poor to moderate for the ultrasonographic semiquantitative score (ICC = 0.409), indicating that this specific outcome warrants additional caution during interpretation. The short-term follow-up and histological collection at a single experimental time point (D7) also restrict inferences about the late remodeling phase of the extracellular matrix. As this study was designed to evaluate the clinical applicability of a combined therapeutic strategy, and not to dissociate the individual effects of each modality, it is not possible to determine the specific contribution of CAP and PBM separately. Future randomized studies, with larger sample sizes, prolonged follow-up, and analysis of inflammatory, angiogenic, and redox biomarkers, are necessary to confirm these findings and clarify the mechanisms involved in the tissue response to combined therapy. Additional studies may also compare different application protocols and, when relevant, investigate the isolated effects of each therapeutic modality.

Conclusion

In this exploratory, intra-individual pilot study, combined CAP + PBM therapy was associated with improved macroscopic and ultrasonographic wound-healing parameters after unilateral mastectomy in female dogs, particularly by reducing macroscopic inflammatory scores, subcutaneous thickening, and ultrasonographic signs of tissue disorganization during early repair. Thermographic and histomorphometric parameters did not show statistically significant differences, although treated halves showed a numerically higher proportion of re-epithelialization. These preliminary findings support combined CAP + PBM as a promising non-invasive adjuvant approach for postoperative wound management, while larger, adequately powered randomized studies are required to confirm efficacy and clarify mechanisms.
