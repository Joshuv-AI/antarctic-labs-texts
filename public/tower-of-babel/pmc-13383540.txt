Genetic diversity and population structure of enset (Ensete ventricosum (Welw.) Cheesman) landraces from Gedeo and Wolaita zones, and Sidama regional state, Southern Ethiopia using SSR markers
Authors: Asmare Zerfu Getahun, Mesfin Teshome Russo, Nebiyat Hailemariam Mune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enset (Ensete ventricosum (Welw.) Cheesman) also called Abyssinian banana, a monocarpic perennial plant native to Ethiopia. It is a crucial food source for both humans and livestock, particularly in the southern and southwestern of the country. Although Ethiopia is the center of origin and diversity of enset, its genetic diversity and population structure have not been thoroughly studied in several important growing regions. Therefore, the current study addressed this gap by evaluating the genetic diversity and population structure of 148 enset landraces collected from Gedeo, Wolaita, and Sidama using nine simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified Cetyltrimethylammonium Bromide (CTAB) protocol, and its quality was checked by 1% agarose gel. Nine SSR markers were selected for further genetic diversity and population structure analysis. The study revealed 88 alleles across all markers, with expected heterozygosity ranged from 0.74 to 0.9 and observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.095 to 0.96. The Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) result showed that 4% of genetic variation occurred among populations and 96% of genetic variation occurred within populations with Fst value of 0.042. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) revealed that substantial genetic admixture with no clear separation among populations. Besides, Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) revealed two distinct clusters; however, it failed to show clearly distinct groups across all three populations due to admixture. Unweighted Neighbor-Joining (UNJ) dendrogram revealed a close genetic relationship between Sidama and Gedeo landraces while Wolaita stands an isolated group. All the genetic diversity parameters indicated that the three enset populations have high genetic variation within populations. It provides basis for implementing modern molecular techniques such as genome wide association study (GWAS) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–based genotyping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07835-1.

Introduction

Enset (Ensete ventricosum (Welw.) Cheesman) is a monocarpic perennial, large herbaceous allogamous, a diploid (2n = 18) root crop that belongs to the genus Ensete of the Musaceae family. It is distributed in many parts of Sub-Saharan Africa [10, 34]. Enset (Ensete ventricosum) is the only cultivated member of the genus Ensete [6, 7]. It is widely grown and domesticated in Ethiopia. It has never been cultivated outside the country, despite wild populations of E. ventricosum occurring in eastern Democratic Republic Congo and extending to South Africa [7]. It extensively is distributed throughout southern and southwestern Ethiopia [8].

The national annual production of enset in the 2021 production year was 122,485,695 tons. In southern Ethiopia, nearly 65% of the total crop production was covered by enset. Specifically, over 44,892.50, 8493.95, and 7641.70 tons were harvested from Sidama, Gedeo, and Wolaita respectively [8]. Enset cultivation for human consumption is entirely limited to Ethiopia [5, 14]. In the southern part of the country, enset is used as primary or secondary crop, and more than 20 million people in the country use enset as food [7, 15].

Ethiopia is the primary center of origin of enset, and the southern and southwestern parts of the country are rich in enset germplasm. The presence of genetic diversity in crops is a valuable source of desirable genes for breeding programs, enhancing production, and developing new farming systems and high-quality products [3, 4, 21].

Molecular markers-based techniques have been a powerful tool applied to estimate the extent of genetic diversity between and within populations [9, 12, 22].

Few studies have been conducted using molecular markers such as SSR and RAPD to assess enset genetic diversity [3, 29]. However, these investigations were constrained by specific landrace groups that did not represent the original populations of the enset-growing regions [28], a limited geographical scope [13], or a reliance on markers derived from related species rather than enset-specific primers [18]. Crucially, there is a lack of comparative studies across the regional states exhibiting differ significantly in ethno-botanical practices and cultural management. To our knowledge, this is the only study that attempts to compare the population diversity of three regional states collected from farmers’ fields and enset maintenance center. The genetic diversity of enset has not been adequately studied, and geographically representative data are needed [5]. Consequently, the genetic diversity among these ethnically, geographically, and culturally distinct enset-growing regions has been inadequately studied. Therefore, the present study aimed to provide new insights into the genetic diversity and population structure of enset landraces in these understudied southern enset-growing regions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Young leaves from 148 cultivated E. ventricosum landraces were collected from two administrative zones; 45 samples of the Gedeo population from farmers’ fields and 43 samples of the Wolaita population from the Areka Agricultural Research Center, as well as 60 samples from farmers’ fields in the Sidama Regional State (Fig. 1), with the assistance of knowledgeable enset-growing farmers and agricultural extension workers. These three distinct administrative regions share borders with each other [32]. These regions encompass various traditional agro-climatic classifications including high-altitude climate (Wurch), mid-altitude climate (Dega), sub-tropical climate (Weyna-Dega), and hot tropical climate (Kolla). Leaf samples were collected from individual farms in Sidama and Gedeo, and from Areka Agricultural Research Centre in Wolaita using silica gel.Fig. 1Map of Ethiopia highlighting the three collection sites (Gedeo, Sidama, and Wolaita) where enset leaf samples were collected

DNA extraction and PCR amplifications

Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB protocol [33] with minor modifications at Biotechnology Institute laboratory of the Addis Ababa University, Ethiopia. The quality of extracted genomic DNA was checked by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gel. In addition, DNA concentration and quality was checked by NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (ND-2000, Thermo Scientific USA).

PCR amplification was carried out in a 10 μL volume mixture containing 1 μL of 10 × PCR buffer, 0.25 μL (10 μM) of each forward and reverse primer, 0.25 μL (10 mM) of each dNTPs, 0.2 μL (5 U/μL) Taq DNA polymerase, 1 μL (2.5 mM) MgCl₂, 1 μL of 50 ng/μL genomic DNA, and 6.05 μL PCR-grade nuclease-free water. The amplification program included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 57–60 °C (depending on the primers) for 45 s., and a 2 min extension at 72 °C, with a final extension of 10 min at 72 °C. The amplified DNA fragments (10 μL) were mixed with 2 μL of 6 × loading dye and separated by electrophoresis on a 3% high-resolution MetaPhor agarose gel in 1 × TBE buffer was used. A 100 bp DNA ladder was used to estimate the molecular weight. Electrophoresis was conducted for 3 h at a constant voltage of 100 V. DNA was stained with 5 μL of 10 mg/mL ethidium bromide. Finally, the fragments in the gel were visualized using an AlphaImager 2200 (Alpha Innotech, Santa Clara, CA, USA) under a UV transilluminator and the fragments were scored using Image Lab software [2]. For the SSR analysis, 34 SSR primers initially developed by Olango et al. [29] from those 15 primers were also successfully used by Nuraga et al. [28]. For this study, out of the 15 primers tested, only nine that were polymorphic and showed reliable amplification were selected for further analysis (Table 1). Statistical analysis

The standard indices of genetic diversity and population structure were calculated using various statistical software tools using scored SSR data. Locus-based diversity parameters, such as polymorphic information content (PIC), number of alleles (Na), effective number of alleles (Ne), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), Shannon’s information index (I), unbiased expected heterozygosity (uHe), and pairwise genetic differentiation (Fst), were computed over 999 bootstrap replications for the entire population as well as for each marker using GenAlEx software version 6.501 [30]. Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) and Unweighted Neighbor-Joining (UNJ) trees were constructed based on Nei’s standard genetic distance [27] using PowerMarker version 3.25 [26], with 1000 bootstrap replications [16], and visualized using MEGA software [24]. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was performed using the Multivariate Statistics Package (MVSP) version 3.22 [23]. Bayesian model-based clustering was used to determine the admixture patterns and population structure using STRUCTURE software version 2.3.4 [31]. Software parameter included a 100,000 burn-in period in each run, with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) consisting of 100,000 replications for K = 1–6, using 20 iterations to evaluate the optimal number of population clusters (K). The best K value was determined using the web-based software Structure Selector [25].

Results

The genetic diversity of Ensete ventricosum landraces

Nine SSR primers produced a total of 88 alleles, including 20 rare alleles across all populations. The number of alleles in the overall population varied from 5 for the EVG07 to 13 for the EVG09 marker, while the average was 10, ranging in size from 76 to 226 bp per individual genotype. The observed heterozygosity (Ho) for the overall populations varied from 0.1 for the EVG10 marker to 0.96 for the EVG07 marker, with a mean of 0.47. The expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.75 for the EVG07 marker to 0.90 for the EVG11 marker with a mean value of 0.83, and Shannon’s information index (I) ranged from 1.45 for the EVG07 marker to 2.35 for the EVG11 marker with a mean value of 2.00. The PIC values of the markers varied from 0.70 (EVG09) to 0.82 (EVG07) with mean value of 0.75. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA)

PCoA was analyzed to understand the genetic relationship between 148 E. ventricosum landraces from three locations. The first three principal coordinates explained 60.70% of the total genetic variance, which were 32.02 and 17.02, 11.65% respectively (Fig. 2). Although the landraces were collected from different administrative zones and separated by considerable distances, the PCoA revealed that some individuals exhibited significant genetic admixture with no clear population boundaries. Discussion

Genetic diversity of Ensete ventricosum

The genetic diversity analysis of 148 enset landraces using nine SSR primers produced a total of 88 alleles, including 20 rare alleles across all populations. Similarly, the presence of rare alleles has been reported in enset studies using the same markers [28, 29]. The number of alleles per locus ranged from 5 to 13, with an average of 10 alleles. This high allelic diversity is comparable with the previous studies on enset and banana [11, 17, 19, 20]. The existence of this high genetic diversity is crucial for a species adaptability and resilience to both biotic and abiotic factors.

The observed heterozygosity varied from 0.09 to 0.96 with a mean of 0.47, consistent with the mean of 0.42 reported by Nuraga et al. [28].The expected heterozygosity ranged from 0.74 to 0.90, with a mean value of 0.83. Similarly, moderate and high He values 0.59 and 0.47 were reported on enset by Gerura et al. [17], Olango et al. [29], respectively. On the other hand, Dilebo et al. [13] reported higher observed heterozygosity (0.85). This difference might be attributed to their inclusion of six different populations including wild relatives, which are indicatives of higher genetic diversity.

Shannon's information index with mean value of 2.00 showed high genetic variability within the studied enset populations. Likewise, Dilebo et al. [13], Olango et al. [29] reported Shannon's index values of 2.2 and 0.91, respectively. The high Shannon’s index value indicates the presence of many numbers of different alleles and high diversity, which plays a crucial role in adaptability and productivity [1].

PIC value ranged from 0.70 to 0.82 with a mean value of 0.75. This finding aligns with earlier studies in that Gerura et al. [17] and Hölscher and Schneider [20] also reported high PIC values 0.91 and 0.69, respectively. The high PIC values indicate that the SSR markers were highly informative and revealed significant genetic diversity across all enset landraces. In contrast to the current study, Nuraga et al. [28] reported lower PIC values (average = 0.41), this difference may be come from the smaller number of samples.

Analysis of molecular variance

AMOVA analysis among populations and within populations revealed that 96% of the total genetic variation attributed within populations, whereas 4% attributed among populations. Similarly, Olango et al. [29] reported that 84% and 16% genetic variation within and among populations, respectively. In addition, Birmeta et al. [4] reported that 88% of the genetic variation within populations, while 12% among populations using RAPD markers. This high genetic variation within population implies the importance of on-farm conservation and suggests that valuable genetic potential for improvement programs can be sourced from local populations.

According to the benchmark of Fst values by Wright [35], the pairwise Fst analyses showed low level genetic differentiation among three populations. The degree of genetic differentiation remains relatively low, with Fst values ranging from 0.02 to 0.03 among the Gedeo, Sidama, and Wolaita populations (Table 4). Similarly, Nuraga et al. [28] reported low Fst values of 0.024. This low genetic differentiation among populations may be due to the exchange of seedlings among these regions.

Population structure

The PCoA plot of the first two axes accounted for 60.04% of the total genetic variability, particularly, PC1 accounts for 32.02%, PC2 for 17.02% and PC3 for 11% of the total variance. The present study showed a similar pattern to previous studies by Birmeta et al. [3], Olango et al. [29] using RAPD and SSR markers, respectively. The PCoA scatter plot revealed the pattern of distributions within individuals and among groups (Fig. 2). The PCoA plot showed that the Gedeo and Sidama populations were dispersed, partly mixed, and formed distinct groups. Nevertheless, the Wolaita population failed to show distinct genetic separation. This lack of showing distinctness may be attributed to long-term cultivation and exchange of planting materials that promote genetic admixture.

The observed admixture of individuals across the three populations in the PCoA scatter plot might be explained by different factors. First, the landraces could experience ongoing gene flow among populations, leading to a shared genetic makeup and resulting in overlap on the PCoA plot. Second, their common ancestral genetic origin might be randomly distributed among landraces. Third, enset-growing farmers may exchange seed suckers among the three regions.

The UNJ dendrogram of the enset landraces in the present study grouped into two major clusters, but without clear separation among the predefined populations. Cluster I was composed of seven landraces that were collected from Sidama population. This may revealed that they are genetically alike to each other but separate from the remaining landraces; due to localized farmer selection or limited gene flow within these specified individuals of Sidama populations. Cluster II, however, contained two large subgroups with highly intermixed landraces. This UNJ analysis is consistent with the AMOVA and PCoA results. This pattern suggests shared genetic relation among populations and is consistent with major clonal dispersal and exchange of planting materials [5, 28]. Similar weak geographic structuring was reported in previous molecular studies of Enset [13, 17].

Bayesian model-based STRUCTURE analysis, it was revealed that two major genetic clusters (K = 2) best explain the genetic structure of the 148 enset landraces. The fact that only two major clusters formed, although the samples were collected from three different administrative zones, indicates strong gene flow and extensive exchange of planting material. These anthropogenic influences have likely led to genetic homogenization [13, 28]. Similarly, minimal geographic structuring due to clonal exchange is also reported for enset [13, 29]. Furthermore this population structure is highly congruent with the results of the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) dendrogram and unweighted neighborjoining tree (UNJ).

Limitations of the study

Geographic Coverage; the study was not included all enset-growing regions and wild relatives of southern Ethiopia due to economic and security challengesSingle Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs), one of the most informative and recent molecular markers, were not utilized in this study. We recommend that future research work using SNP markers to provide deeper understanding on genetic diversity and population structure of enset landraces.

Conclusions and recommendation

All the genetic diversity parameters have shown that the three enset populations have high genetic variation within populations. This genetic diversity among enset landraces is vital for conservation and breeding programs to enhance productivity and support food security. It also suggests that more germplasm collections should be made from the rest of the enset-cultivating regions for further conservation and to develop and release potential enset varieties. Furthermore, this study provides a foundation for applying modern breeding techniques like marker-assisted selection (MAS) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP).
