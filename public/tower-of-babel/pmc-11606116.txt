Arcobacter species isolated from human stool samples, animal products, ready-to-eat salad mixes, and ambient water: prevalence, antimicrobial susceptibility, and virulence gene profiles
Authors: Hossein Niyayesh, Ebrahim Rahimi, Amir Shakerian, Faham Khamesipour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionArcobacter species are emerging foodborne pathogens increasingly associated with human illness worldwide. They are commonly found in the gastrointestinal tracts of animals and are frequently isolated from various food sources, including raw meat, poultry, and seafood. The aim of this study is to investigate the antimicrobial resistance patterns of Arcobacter spp. isolated from human stool samples, animal products, ready-to-eat salad mixes, and ambient water, assess the presence of resistance genes, and explore their potential implications for public health.MethodsIn this study, a total of 683 samples were collected from the Shahrekord area over a 12-month period. Samples were obtained from human stool, chicken meat, raw cow milk, RTE salad mixes, and environmental water sources. Two different methods were used to detect Arcobacter, depending on the sample type: bacteriological isolation and identification, and molecular identification. After identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to identify ten putative Arcobacter virulence and resistance genes.FindingsThe results revealed that Arcobacter spp. were present in 26.06% (178 out of 683) of the tested samples, with varying isolation rates across different sample types. A. butzleri being the most commonly isolated species across all sample types, while A. cryaerophilus was restricted to RTE salads, surface waters, and chicken meat. Notably, A. skirrowii was only isolated from chicken meat and environmental water. The differences of Arcobacter spp. in prevalence between the sample types were statistically significant (p < 0.05), and no significant seasonal variation was found across the sampling periods (p > 0.05). PCR analysis for ten putative virulence genes indicated that the cadF gene was present in all Arcobacter isolates. Similarly, 83.33% of the tested strains harbored the ciaB gene, while other genes were less frequently detected. Regarding resistance genes, tet(O) (7.69%) was the most identified gene, followed by blaOXA-61 (4.37%).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study highlights the alarming prevalence of antimicrobial resistance in Arcobacter spp. Monitoring Arcobacter spp. resistance can be achieved through surveillance, risk assessments, antibiotic stewardship in agriculture, public education, research collaborations, rapid diagnostics, and harmonized policies, all aimed at reducing contamination and safeguarding public health effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10256-9.Keywords: Arcobacter spp, Antimicrobial resistance, Food contamination, Human stool

Introduction

Arcobacter spp. are Gram-negative bacteria within the Campylobacteraceae family known to cause zoonotic infections. These bacteria exhibit tolerance to oxygen, allowing them to thrive in both aerobic and microaerophilic conditions, with an optimal growth temperature of approximately 30°C. Most strains of Arcobacter are motile and do not produce hemolysis [1].

Among the identified species, Arcobacter butzleri, Arcobacter cryaerophilus, Arcobacter skirrowii, Arcobacter nitrofigilis, Arcobacter cibarius, and Arcobacter halophilus are the most commonly reported [1–3]. Arcobacter species are increasingly recognized as foodborne pathogens associated with human illness. These bacteria are often present in the gastrointestinal tracts of animals and can be isolated from various food sources, including raw meat, poultry, and seafood, as well as from environmental water sources [4]. Notably, Arcobacter poses a unique risk within the food chain, given its capacity to persist in raw poultry, meats, and water sources, which may result in cross-contamination during food processing [4].

Research indicates that A. butzleri, A. cryaerophilus, and A. skirrowii are associated with human and animal infections, causing conditions such as bacteremia, endocarditis, peritonitis, and diarrhea [5, 6]. According to EFSA, Arcobacter spp. isn’t widely monitored in humans due to limited testing, though evidence increasingly links it to gastrointestinal illness. Prevalence varies across studies, generally between 0.5%−2% in human cases in Europe. The higher rates in raw poultry underscore the need for systematic monitoring in food sources [7, 8]. In a recent investigation conducted in China, the prevalence of Arcobacter was found to be 1.3% among individuals with diarrhea and 26.7% in raw chickens. In contrast, Germany reports Arcobacter as the second most common bacterial pathogen identified in human stool samples, while in Belgium, it ranks as the fourth most common [7, 8].

From a human health perspective, Arcobacter infections can cause symptoms ranging from mild gastrointestinal upset to severe diarrhea, especially in vulnerable populations. Furthermore, Arcobacter has shown multidrug-resistant profiles, complicated treatment and raising public health concerns [9–14]. In environmental health, the presence of Arcobacter in water sources signals potential fecal contamination, highlighting issues in water safety and sanitation. This pathogen's adaptability across such domains emphasizes the critical need for integrated surveillance and control strategies in food safety and public health to mitigate its impact effectively [9–14].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing has demonstrated that Arcobacter species are generally susceptible to macrolides, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides; however, resistance to fluoroquinolones and certain β-lactams has been documented [15]. The primary transmission routes for Arcobacter species are through contaminated food and water sources, drawing significant global research interest since the early 2000s [3]. Arcobacter species are found globally in diverse sources, including raw milk, unpasteurized cheese, poultry, pork, beef, surface and drinking water, sewage, and seafood, posing significant public health and food safety risks [3].

Virulence gene profiling has been utilized to identify specific virulence factors in different Arcobacter species. The virulence genes cell adhesion gene F (cadF), cell invasion gene B (ciaB), toxin A (tlyA), and phospholipase D A (pldA) in Arcobacter species enhance pathogenicity by facilitating adhesion, invasion, and cytotoxicity. cadF helps the bacteria attach to host cells, while ciaB promotes intracellular invasion. tlyA encodes a toxin that causes tissue damage and inflammation, and pldA encodes a phospholipase that disrupts host cell membranes. Together, these genes enable Arcobacter to effectively colonize hosts and evade immune responses. These genes contribute to the bacteria's ability to adhere to and invade host cells and are associated with the production of toxins and hemolysins [16, 17].

This study aims to assess the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and virulence gene characteristics of Arcobacter species isolated from human stool samples, animal-origin foods, ready-to-eat (RTE) salad mixes, and environmental water sources, providing essential data to inform disease prevention and control efforts related to these emerging pathogens.

Methods

Sample collection

The study was approved by the Ethical Council of Research of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Shahrekord Branch, Islamic Azad University, Shahrekord, Iran. In the framework of this study, a total of 683 samples were gathered from the Shahrekord area. Throughout the year 2022, samples were collected from human stool, chicken meat, raw cow milk, RTE salad mixes, and environmental water sources. The samples are collected in four time in the year: Spring, Summer, Fall and Winter. Specifically, 120 human stool samples were collected at Hajar Hospital of Shahrekord from inpatients exhibiting symptoms of gastroenteritis before undergoing antimicrobial treatment. Concurrently, chicken meat including drumsticks and wings (n = 231), raw cow milk (n = 104), and ready-to-eat (RTE) salad mixes including mixed greens, coleslaw, pasta salad (n = 100) was obtained from various randomly selected retail stores. Additionally, surface water samples (n = 128) sourced from public beach sites, including river and lake water, were gathered using sterile 50 ml conical tubes. All food and water samples were transported to the laboratory and processed within 2 to 4 h after collection. The samples were collected from a mix of supermarkets, local grocery stores, and open-air markets to capture a representative range of retail sources in Shahrekord. For each sample type, specific selection criteria were used: samples were randomly chosen from different outlets to avoid sampling bias and reflect typical consumer options. Chicken meat, raw cow milk, and RTE salads were handled according to strict aseptic techniques, with samples placed in sterile containers immediately upon selection. All samples were then rapidly transported to the laboratory, where processing began within 2–4 h to ensure optimal conditions for accurate microbial analysis.

Isolation and identification of Arcobacter spp

Two different methods were employed to detect Arcobacter, depending on the sample type. For human stool samples, the selective enrichment method described by Uljanovas et al. (2021) [7] was utilized to isolate and identify Arcobacter spp. In this method, 1 g of feces per sample was mixed with selective enrichment broth containing various compounds, including Arcobacter broth (Oxoid, Wesel, Germany) (24 g/l) supplemented with 5% lysed horse blood, 5´-fluorouracil (100 mg/l), amphotericin B (10 mg/L), novobiocin (32 mg/l), cefoperazone (16 mg/l), and trimethoprim (64 mg/l) (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany). After incubation for 72 h under microaerobic conditions at 30°C, 50 µl of the broth was streaked onto Arcobacter selective agar plates (Oxoid, Wesel, Germany), characterized by small round white or grey colonies, which were then incubated for 48 h at 30°C under microaerobic conditions.

The cultures were incubated under microaerobic conditions, which were achieved using a gas mixture of 5% oxygen (O₂), 10% carbon dioxide (CO₂), and 85% nitrogen (N₂). This environment was maintained using a GasPak system, ensuring the appropriate conditions for the growth of Arcobacter species. Typical Arcobacter colonies were subcultured onto Mueller Hinton agar plates supplemented with defibrinated sheep blood and incubated for 48 h under microaerobic conditions at 30°C.

For food products and water samples, the membrane filtration method described by Brückner et al. (2020) [11] was employed. Before enrichment, water samples were centrifuged, and pellets were resuspended in Arcobacter broth with a selective supplement containing cefoperazone, amphotericin B, and teicoplanin. Each food sample was added to the broth at a 1:10 ratio and incubated for 48 h under microaerobic conditions at 30°C in incubator. After incubation, enriched samples were transferred onto a 0.45 μm pore size mixed cellulose ester membrane filter placed on the surface of MHB agar. The filters were left to passively filter for 1 h at 30°C under aerobic conditions. Subsequently, the filters were removed, and plates were incubated for up to 7 days at 30°C under microaerobic conditions. The plates were checked every 24 h for the presence of typical Arcobacter colonies (i.e., small round white or grey colonies), and five suspected colonies were subcultured onto MHB plates for 48 h under microaerobic conditions at 30°C in incubator.

Molecular identification and verification of Arcobacter isolates

For the molecular detection of species-specific genes in Arcobacter spp., we used 16S rRNA sequencing, which is the gold standard for accurate identification at the species level [7, 11]. This method allows for comprehensive characterization of bacterial populations and provides reliable differentiation among closely related species. In our study, we implemented 16S rRNA sequencing to confirm the identity of the isolates, followed by analysis of specific virulence and resistance genes associated with Arcobacter pathogenesis [7, 11]. The primers used for the simultaneous identification of Arcobacter species are shown in Table 1. Each sample underwent an initial denaturation phase at 94°C for 2 min prior to cycling. 32 PCR cycles, comprising of denaturation at 94°C for 45 s, annealing at 61°C for 45 s, extension at 72°C for 30 s, and a final elongation step at 72°C for 5 min, were performed after this step. Positive controls included the DNA of A. butzleri (ATCC 49616), A. cryaerophilus (ATCC 43158), and A. skirrowii (ATCC 51132), while negative controls included molecular grade water (Termo Fisher Scientific). Utilizing horizontal electrophoresis in 2% agarose in 1xTris-Borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer, amplifcation products were separated. Under UV light, the gels were seen after being stained with ethidium bromide. By using the previously described rpo B gene sequencing to confirm the isolates that had been found by Korczak et al. (2006) [18].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Using the gradient strip diffusion method (E-testTM, bioMérieux, Nürtingen, Germany), all confirmed isolates were submitted to susceptibility testing for 22 antimicrobial drugs (vancomycin, polymyxin-B, colistin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, sulphamethizole, gentamicin, kanamycin, streptomycin, erythromycin, cephalothin, cefoxitin, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid, norfloxacin, ampicillin, amoxicillin + clauvunic acid, ertapenem, meropenem, imipenem). With a few minor adjustments, the manufacturer's instructions were followed. Arcobacter isolates were grown for 48 h in a microaerobic environment at 30°C on MHB agar plates. Each plate's colony material was transferred in small amounts to 2 ml tubes of Brucella broth (BB) (Biolife, Milan, Italy) and cultured at 30°C overnight in a microaerobic environment. An inoculum containing approximately 1 × 108 colony forming units (CFU) per milliliter was made using these cultures. Three overnight cultures per isolate were created because A. cryaerophilus isolates had a slow rate of development. To achieve comparable inoculum concentrations, all cultures from one isolate were mixed (6 ml), centrifuged (16,000 × g for 5 min), and then resuspended in 0.6 ml of BB. Each test run included a quality control run where Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was precultured overnight in BB at 37°C in an aerobic environment before being cultivated on MHB for 48 h. Test strips were used after inoculating the MHB agar plates with 100 l of an overnight culture. After 48 h of incubation at 30°C in a microaerobic environment (37°C in an aerobic environment for the E. coli reference strain), minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were calculated. The only plates that were examined had a full bacterial lawn [7, 11].

Detection of virulence and resistance genes

PCR was conducted to identify ten putative Arcobacter virulence genes. The PCR protocol for partial amplification of collagen adherence factor (cadF), Campylobacter jejuni 1349 (cj1349), Campylobacter invasion activator B (ciaB), Membrane viscosity inducible N (mviN), Phospholipase D A (pldA), Toxin like Yield A (tlyA), Iron regulated gene A (irgA), Helicobacter enterotoxin Containing A (hecA), Iron regulated Operon E (iroE), and Helicobacter enterotoxin Containing B (hecB) was performed as described by Lameei et al. (2022) [4], and the primers used are listed in Table 2. A 25µl volume reaction mixture containing 2µl template DNA, 12.5µl of DreamTaq™ Green PCR Master Mix (2x), and 0.1µM of each forward and reverse primer was used for the assay. The PCR process consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 4 min, 30 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 56°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s), and a final elongation at 72°C for 5 min. The amplification products were separated using horizontal electrophoresis in 2% agarose in 1xTBE buffer, and the gels were stained with ethidium bromide. The presence of fragments was verified using a UV trans-illuminator. A positive control was used for each bacterial species. The resistance genes were: tet(O), cmeB, blaOXA-61, aphA-3–1 and aadE1. The same protocol was used for the preparation of the reaction medium and the PCR conditions used were: initial denaturation at 92°C for 5 min, 30 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 92°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s), and a final elongation at 72°C for 5 min.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Office Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, US) and IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software package (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, US) were utilized for data analysis. Data analysis was performed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables, with post-hoc comparisons where applicable. Differences were considered significant if the P-value was less than 0.05. Differences were considered significant if the P-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of Arcobacter spp. in the examined samples

According to Table 3, Arcobacter spp. were detected in 26.06% (178/683) of the samples, with varying isolation rates across different matrices. The highest prevalence was observed in environmental water 36.72% (47/128), followed by raw cow milk 32.69% (32/104), chicken meat (29.44% (69/231), ready-to-eat (RTE) salad mixes 22.00% (22/100), and human stool 5.83% (7/120). Species identification was conducted using multiplex PCR and rpoB sequencing, with A. butzleri being the most commonly isolated species across all sample types, while A. cryaerophilus was restricted to RTE salads, surface waters, and chicken meat. Notably, A. skirrowii was only isolated from chicken meat and environmental water. The differences in prevalence between the sample types were statistically significant (p < 0.05), suggesting a strong association between the source and the likelihood of contamination with Arcobacter spp. However, no significant seasonal variation was found across the sampling periods (p > 0.05). According to Table 3, which shows the higher prevalence during warmer seasons, particularly spring and summer. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences between seasons in sample types, such as chicken meat, environmental water, and raw cow milk, with a general trend of higher detection rates in spring and summer (p < 0.05), while differences were not significant (p > 0.05).

Antimicrobial susceptibility

Table 4 summarizes the MIC distributions for six antimicrobial agents tested against 178 Arcobacter isolates, including A. butzleri (n = 122), A. cryaerophilus (n = 12), and A. skirrowii (n = 4). The table presents the percentage of isolates within three MIC ranges: ≤ 0.5 µg/ml (susceptible), 1–16 µg/ml (intermediate), and > 16 µg/ml (resistant), for each antimicrobial agent: ampicillin, azithromycin, gentamicin, tetracycline, erythromycin, and ciprofloxacin. The data reveal a high prevalence of resistance to ciprofloxacin and ampicillin, particularly among A. butzleri and A. skirrowii. This table complements the detailed MIC distributions provided in Supplementary File S1, offering insights into antimicrobial resistance patterns across these clinically significant Arcobacter species.

Antimicrobial susceptibilities of Arcobacter butzleri isolates used in this study

Phenotypical resistance was observed among Arcobacter cryaerophilus isolates from various sources, with 100% resistance to vancomycin across all isolates. Sulphamethizole resistance ranged from 66.7% to 100% depending on the source. Similarly, cefoxitin resistance was universal (100%). For ciprofloxacin, resistance varied, being highest (100%) in environmental water and lowest (60%) in chicken meat isolates. Ampicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid showed high resistance (80%−100%) across all isolates. Resistance profiles of Arcobacter skirrowii were similar, with 100% resistance to vancomycin, sulphamethizole, cefoxitin, ciprofloxacin, ampicillin, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid observed in isolates from all sources. The detailed antimicrobial susceptibilities of Arcobacter cryaerophilus and Arcobacter skirrowii isolates used in this study are provided in Supplementary File S2 and S3.

Distribution of resistance genes in Arcobacter isolates from various sources

The isolation source significantly influenced the distribution of resistance genes and virulence factors among Arcobacter isolates. For Arcobacter butzleri, a majority of isolates from chicken meat (100%) exhibited various virulence-associated gene amplicons, with high frequencies for cadF (85.25%) and mviN (98.36%). In contrast, A. cryaerophilus showed lower prevalence of these genes, particularly in environmental water and raw cow milk. Only 50% of the isolates from chicken meat demonstrated cadF positivity, indicating a notable decrease in virulence potential compared to A. butzleri. Arcobacter skirrowii isolates exhibited a more uniform expression of virulence factors in chicken meat, but fewer genes were detected overall, reflecting a lower potential for virulence. The environmental water isolates of A. butzleri maintained a high prevalence of virulence factors (around 100%), while A. cryaerophilus and A. skirrowii showed significantly reduced expression across all sources.

Discussion

In our study, we implemented 16S rRNA sequencing to confirm the identity of the isolates, followed by analysis of specific virulence and resistance genes associated with Arcobacter pathogenesis. This approach enhanced the accuracy of our findings and ensured that the data obtained reflect the true prevalence and genetic diversity of Arcobacter spp. in the samples analyzed [7, 11]. Research has demonstrated that A. butzleri and A. cryaerophilus pose significant hazards to human health, a classification endorsed by the International Commission on Microbiological Specifications for Foods [19]. The present study revealed that Arcobacter spp. were detected in 26.06% (178 out of 683) of the examined samples, with the highest prevalence observed in environmental water (36.72%), followed by raw cow milk (32.69%), chicken meat (29.44%), RTE salad mixes (22.00%), and human stool samples (5.83%). A. butzleri was the most commonly isolated species across all sample types, while A. cryaerophilus and A. skirrowii were found in specific samples. This highlights the potential risk posed by different sources, particularly environmental water and raw food products, as reservoirs for Arcobacter contamination [8, 19, 20].

The detection rates were significantly higher during spring and summer, possibly due to warmer temperatures promoting bacterial growth [8, 20]. The high prevalence in environmental water and raw food products, such as cow milk and chicken meat, underscores the importance of proper hygiene and sanitation measures to minimize contamination and protect public health.

Compared to other countries, the prevalence of Arcobacter spp. in Iran aligns with global trends, where contaminated water and food products have been reported as major sources of Arcobacter infections [7, 9, 11, 21–24]. However, the relatively low prevalence in human stool samples suggests that while contamination is widespread, the direct impact on human health may vary depending on exposure and immune response [7, 9, 11, 21–24].

Differences in Arcobacter spp. prevalence across countries are influenced by several factors. Geographical and environmental conditions play a key role, with areas having poor water sanitation and agricultural runoff showing higher contamination, especially in environmental water. Food handling practices, particularly in animal farming and raw food consumption, also contribute; stricter hygiene regulations in some countries lead to lower contamination in chicken meat and raw milk. Cultural dietary habits, such as consuming raw salads, can increase risk, while detection methods and study designs affect prevalence estimates. Lastly, warmer climates, like Iran’s, support bacterial growth, explaining seasonal and regional variations [8, 19, 25, 26].

The documented prevalence of Arcobacter spp. in foods exhibits considerable variability across studies. However, there is a general consensus that poultry meat tends to have higher contamination rates compared to red meat, raw cow milk, and vegetables [27, 28]. According to Hsu and Lee, (2015) [14] review, Arcobacter spp. are more commonly present in food products of animal origin, with the highest weighted mean prevalence observed in chicken meat (45.2%), followed by dairy products (36.4%), pork (36.3%), seafood (32.3%), beef (31.2%), and vegetables (14%). However, certain results diverge from studies conducted in Malaysia and Italy, where Arcobacter was detected in 39% (48/123) of chicken meat and in 21.6% (8/37) of raw cow milk samples [7]. Other authors have reported varying prevalence rates of Arcobacter spp. in chicken meat and raw cow milk, ranging from 12% to 85.7% and from 4.1% to 46%, respectively [7, 21]. The isolation rate of Arcobacter in ready-to-eat (RTE) salad mixes was lower than in studies conducted in Italy and Portugal (ranging from 27.5% to 47.6%). However, it was higher than the reported contamination of leafy green vegetables (4.4%, 4/90) in a study conducted in South Korea [29, 30]. A. butzleri was the predominant species detected in the samples, followed by A. cryaerophilus and A. skirrowii, which is consistent with previous studies [29–31].

In other studies, the prevalence of Arcobacter in environmental waters has been reported to range from 20.8% to 58.6% [32]. Reported Arcobacter prevalence rates in different sources can vary widely due to factors such as sample size, geographic and seasonal variation, hygiene and sanitation protocols in farms and food processing facilities, patient populations, and the sensitivity and specificity of detection methods. As there is currently no standard protocol for isolation and cultural identification, the latter aspect is particularly important to consider.

Due to the limited research on the antimicrobial resistance of Arcobacter spp., we compared our findings with the more extensively studied resistance patterns of Campylobacter spp., which shares several biological and ecological similarities with Arcobacter. The antimicrobial resistance patterns of Arcobacter spp., particularly A. butzleri and A. skirrowii, exhibit significant concern, especially with high levels of resistance to ciprofloxacin and ampicillin. These findings parallel reports on Campylobacter spp., another Campylobacteraceae family member, known for its rising resistance to fluoroquinolones and β-lactam antibiotics, especially from foodborne sources. Resistance to β-lactams, particularly ampicillin, was notable in A. butzleri isolates, with 25% showing high MIC values. Ciprofloxacin resistance was similarly widespread, indicating the potential challenge of using fluoroquinolones in clinical treatments [33, 34]. Typically, aminoglycosides (such as gentamicin, kanamycin, and streptomycin) are potent against Arcobacter spp. and are suggested for treating severe infections [7].

Comparing Arcobacter to Campylobacter, resistance to macrolides, such as erythromycin, was observed in both genera, though Arcobacter showed a higher variation in susceptibility depending on the species and source. This suggests that Arcobacter may have a different resistance mechanism or genetic profile influencing its susceptibility to these drugs [35]. The significant resistance to polymyxin-B in environmental isolates also highlights the environmental reservoirs' role in sustaining antimicrobial resistance, necessitating a One Health approach to managing resistance [36]. These findings underscore the need for continuous monitoring of Arcobacter resistance, given its zoonotic potential and increasing association with human infections, similar to the well-studied Campylobacter species.

The identification of resistance genes in Arcobacter spp. highlights important mechanisms driving antimicrobial resistance in this genus [37, 38]. The tet(O) gene, which confers resistance to tetracyclines by protecting ribosomes from the drug’s inhibitory action, was the most prevalent gene detected (7.69%), especially in Arcobacter butzleri. This is consistent with findings in related bacteria like Campylobacter spp., where tet(O) is also a major contributor to tetracycline resistance [39, 40]. The blaOXA-61 gene, another commonly identified resistance gene (4.37%), primarily in A. butzleri, confers resistance to β-lactams by producing an oxacillinase enzyme that deactivates β-lactam antibiotics. These genes’ presence is linked to phenotypic resistance, particularly in chicken meat and environmental water samples, where resistance was more prevalent [36].

Arcobacter cryaerophilus showed lower resistance, with only tet(O) detected in chicken meat. Notably, A. skirrowii exhibited no resistance genes, indicating potential species-specific differences in resistance profiles. This variation among Arcobacter species suggests environmental and host-specific factors influencing resistance gene acquisition. The detection of these genes in food and environmental sources raises significant public health concerns, as resistant Arcobacter strains may contaminate the food chain, posing risks of treatment failures in human infections. Understanding these resistance mechanisms is crucial for monitoring antimicrobial resistance trends in emerging foodborne pathogens like Arcobacter.

Although A. butzleri and A. cryaerophilus are considered as emerging zoonotic pathogens, data on virulence and pathogenic mechanisms is still limited [24]. The cadF gene was present in all Arcobacter isolates regardless of species. In addition, the ciaB gene was detected in 83.33% of the tested strains, while other genes were less frequently detected. Overall, all putative virulence-associated genes were present in the analyzed Arcobacter spp. strains, except for hecA, hecB, iroE, and irgA, which were absent in A. skirrowii, and hecB, iroE, and irgA, which were absent in A. cryaerophilus. Regarding resistance genes, tet(O) (7.69%) was the most commonly identified gene, followed by blaOXA-61 (4.37%). Previous studies also reported a high occurrence of these genes after testing A. butzleri isolates from human stool, food products, and in-line milk filters of cow dairy farms [2, 41]. The protein required for peptidoglycan biosynthesis is encoded by the mviN gene. Two outer membrane proteins, encoded by cadF and cj1349, play a crucial role in facilitating the binding of bacteria to intestinal epithelial cells. Additionally, Campylobacter invasive antigen B (CiaB) is responsible for promoting the invasion of host cells by the bacteria. The hecA gene encodes an adhesin that belongs to the filamentous hemagglutinin family. Three other genes, namely pldA (encoding outer membrane phospholipase A), hecB (encoding hemolysin activation protein), and tlyA (encoding hemolysin), are associated with the lysis of erythrocytes. Lastly, irgA and iroE genes encode functional components of the iron acquisition system, namely iron-regulated outer membrane protein and periplasmic enzyme, respectively, which are necessary for the establishment and maintenance of infections [42].

This study identified several antimicrobial resistance genes in Arcobacter spp., with tet(O) being notably associated with tetracycline resistance through a ribosomal protection mechanism. The prevalence of tet(O) in Arcobacter spp. aligns with findings in related species like Campylobacter, suggesting potential horizontal gene transfer. This underscores the interconnectedness of resistance mechanisms across genera and the need for ongoing surveillance to mitigate public health risks. The study also highlights the significant prevalence of Arcobacter spp. across various sample sources. While no seasonal variation was observed in this study, existing literature suggests that environmental factors may influence Arcobacter prevalence, warranting further investigation into seasonal patterns for risk assessment and public health strategies.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides valuable insights into the antimicrobial resistance profiles of Arcobacter spp., several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the sample size may not fully represent the diversity of Arcobacter strains present in different environments or geographical regions, which could influence the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the focus on specific resistance genes does not encompass the entire genomic landscape of resistance in Arcobacter, potentially overlooking other relevant genes and mechanisms. Furthermore, the study does not establish a causal relationship between the presence of resistance genes and clinical outcomes, limiting our ability to infer the direct implications for human health. Lastly, the methodologies employed may not capture the dynamic nature of resistance development, necessitating further longitudinal studies to monitor trends over time. Acknowledging these limitations is crucial for accurately interpreting the results and guiding future research directions.

Conclusions

The findings of this study underscore the concerning rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Arcobacter species, particularly the emergence of resistance to newer generations of antibiotics, which were previously effective. This rapid evolution highlights the critical need for continuous surveillance and more targeted antimicrobial stewardship strategies to combat the growing threat posed by Arcobacter and other emerging pathogens. Failure to address this trend could limit future treatment options and exacerbate the public health challenge posed by these bacteria.
