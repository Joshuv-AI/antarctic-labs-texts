Environmental selection shapes the ecological cascade of biofilm assembly and functional gene abundance in sandstone weathering
Authors: Puchao Jia, Wei Zhang, Gaosen Zhang, Wansheng Pei, Fasi Wu, Zhenzi He, Tuo Chen, Guangxiu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microorganisms are pivotal agents in the process of sandstone weathering; nevertheless, the ecological mechanisms that govern their transition from mere colonization to sustained weathering activity remain ambiguous. This study systematically elucidated microbe-mediated weathering mechanisms through amplicon and metagenomic sequencing of bacteria, fungi, and archaea across a sandstone weathering sequence—from original unweathered sandstone (OS), biofilm-covered sandstone (BS), to weathered sandstone (WS). The findings indicate that microbial communities undergo associations across a weathering gradient, with biofilms constituting a unique transitional state. Community assembly mechanisms undergo a transition from stochastic processes in original sandstone to deterministic processes during the processes of biofilm formation and weathering. Biofilm communities formed modular, tightly interconnected putative association networks enriched with keystone taxa. Metagenomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of functional pathways related to iron acquisition, organic acid metabolism, and sulfur cycling during weathering, with functional annotation directly linking these traits to pivotal microbial groups. The findings of this study, as suggested by partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM), indicate that environmental changes are associated with deterministic processes and with increased microbial richness. These factors are further linked to the composition of putative keystone taxa along the weathering gradient. These pivotal groups subsequently influence the abundance of weathering-related functional genes, directly accelerating weathering processes. This finding unveils a distinct ecological cascade pathway, commencing with environmental selection and culminating in the enrichment of functional gene potentials. The present study proposes a universal framework demonstrating that sandstone weathering is associated with deterministic processes, putative keystone taxa, and synergistic gene networks. This mechanism is not only applicable to sandstone systems, but also offers novel insights into the understanding of microbially mediated mineral weathering in terrestrial environments. This process is fundamental in influencing global biogeochemical cycles, soil formation, and the preservation of geological and cultural heritage.

Introduction

Global climate change and intensified human activities exert profound impacts on lithospheric ecosystems, particularly by accelerating geological weathering processes through temperature fluctuations, moisture variations, and extreme weather events, thereby threatening the long-term preservation of geological landmarks and stone cultural heritage [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Sandstone, one of Earth's most widely distributed rocks, has been extensively used in architecture and monumental structures since ancient times. From medieval cathedrals in Europe to cave temples in Asia, sandstone cultural heritage sites span the globe, possessing irreplaceable historical, artistic, and scientific value [5,6]. Microorganisms, as key drivers of this process, participate in the dissolution and transformation of minerals through various biogeochemical mechanisms, such as organic acid production (e.g., citric, oxalic, and gluconic acids), siderophore-mediated iron chelation, and extracellular polymeric substance (EPS)-mediated mineral surface alteration [[7], [8], [9]]. Among these, biofilms—as the primary form of microbial life on sandstone surfaces—play a central role in coordinating interspecies interactions and regulating weathering rates, serving as a key interface for understanding microbe-mineral interactions [10,11].

Sandstone primarily consists of silicate and aluminosilicate skeletal grains (such as quartz) with strong weathering resistance, but its cementing and matrix minerals (such as iron oxides, clay minerals, and carbonates) are typically more susceptible to weathering [5]. This mineralogical heterogeneity makes sandstone an ideal model for studying selective weathering: microorganisms can selectively attack cementation, causing skeletal particles to detach and accelerating overall degradation [5]. Significant progress has been made in understanding microbial weathering mechanisms. Diverse microbial communities colonize both the surface and interior of sandstone, including bacteria, fungi, algae, and lichens. Studies indicate that the phyla Pseudomonadota and Actinobacteria constitute the primary components of sandstone bacterial communities [12]. In arid and semi-arid environments, cyanobacteria act as pioneer organisms, forming biofilms through the secretion of extracellular polymers (EPS) that provide nutrients for other species and moderate extreme conditions [13]. Studies indicate that specific microbial groups, such as actinomycetes and cyanobacteria, can significantly accelerate the decomposition of silicate minerals by producing metabolites including organic acids, iron carriers, and extracellular polymers [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Fungi also play a crucial role in sandstone weathering by accelerating mineral dissolution through hyphal penetration and organic acid secretion [6]. However, recent studies reveal that under sustained environmental stress, a potential decoupling may occur between microbial community α-diversity and weathering function [[20], [21], [22]]. However, whether diversity per se directly promotes weathering remains debated, and our study aims to explore the relationship between community structure and functional potential along the weathering gradient. Therefore, a more comprehensive ecological perspective encompassing community assembly, network structure, and functional traits is needed to unravel the complex mechanisms of microbial weathering.

From a theoretical framework perspective, the assembly of microbial communities on sandstone surfaces is jointly influenced by deterministic selection and stochastic processes. Niche theory emphasizes the role of environmental filtering and interspecific competition in shaping community structure, while neutral theory highlights the importance of random dispersal and ecological drift [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. The relative importance of these two mechanisms dynamically adjusts along environmental gradients, collectively determining the functional output of communities [27,28]. However, most existing studies focus on single microbial groups (e.g., bacteria) or isolated functional processes, with limited research on the synergistic interactions among multi-domain organisms (bacteria, fungi, archaea) and their impact on ecosystem multifunctionality. This research gap may lead to biased interpretations of microbial weathering mechanisms.

Against this backdrop, this study employs a sandstone weathering system as a model. Integrating metagenomics, three-domain (bacteria, archaea, fungi) amplicon sequencing, and geochemical analysis methods, it conducts a multi-scale, multi-omics integrated investigation to address three core questions: (1) Biofilms represent a unique transitional phase along the weathering gradient—they are not merely a passive response of community structure to environmental gradients, but actively reshape microenvironments through niche construction (2) Deterministic processes (e.g., environmental filtering); dominate over stochastic processes in community assembly along the weathering gradient, and specific environmental factors (e.g., pH and electrical conductivity) are the key drivers of this deterministic selection; (3) Structural features of microbial putative association networks (e.g., network complexity and the abundance of putative keystone taxa) are correlated with weathering-related functional gene abundance, thereby linking community organization to weathering processes. This research aims to systematically reveal the potential ecological chain from environmental screening and community assembly to functional execution. Its findings will not only deepen our understanding of the intrinsic mechanisms underlying microbial-mediated sandstone weathering—a fundamental geoprocess—but also provide critical scientific basis for preventing biological corrosion and ecologically restoring stone cultural heritage.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Samples were collected in April 2024 from the surrounding environment of the Matisi Grottoes in Zhangye City, Gansu Province, China. Three types of samples with varying degrees of weathering were collected: Original unweathered sandstone (OS), biofilm-covered sandstone (BS), and weathered sandstone (WS) (Fig. S1; Table S1). In this study, weathering degree was quantified based on field observations and macroscopic features documented during sampling, including: (1) color changes (from reddish-brown to grayish or darker tones); (2) surface roughness (smooth vs. rough/uneven surfaces); (3) presence and density of visible cracks; (4) relative hardness assessed by field hammer testing; and (5) visible biofilm coverage (presence or absence of patina/layers). Based on these combined criteria, samples were classified into three distinct categories: OS (original sandstone) – fresh, unweathered surfaces with no visible cracks or biofilm; BS (biofilm-covered sandstone) – surfaces with visible biofilm; and WS (weathered sandstone) – surfaces showing pronounced discoloration, extensive cracking, increased roughness, and noticeable granular disintegration. In addition, we combined physical and chemical properties (pH, EC) to determine the three degrees of weathering of sandstone samples (Table S1)The total mass of each mixed sample was maintained around 100 g to ensure sample representativeness and sufficient experimental quantity. During collection, to eliminate the influence of variations in biofilm coverage density on weathering intensity analysis, the ratio of biofilm to underlying sandstone was standardized using the following method: A sampling frame (5 cm × 5 cm) was used to define the sampling area. All visible biofilm within the frame was scraped off and weighed, while a 5-mm-thick layer of overlying sandstone was collected simultaneously. Eight replicates were obtained for each type (three samples mixed into one composite sample), resulting in a total of 24 composite samples. Immediately after collection, samples were placed in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory, stored in a −80°C freezer, and promptly sent to Shanghai Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For library preparation and sequencing.

DNA extraction, amplicon sequencing and OTU pipeline

The analysis of archaeal, bacterial, and fungal communities in samples is to be conducted using amplicon sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted from sandstone samples using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biochemicals, OH, USA). For amplicon sequencing, microbial communities were analyzed by targeting the 16S rRNA gene for bacteria and archaea, and the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region for fungi. The primer pairs used were: 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAG-GCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), those for fungi were ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′), and those for archaea were 524F10extF (5′-TGYCAGCCGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and Arch958RmodR (5′-YCCGGCGTTGA-VTCCAATT-3′) [[29], [30], [31]]. PCR reactions were performed in triplicate. Amplicons were purified, quantified, and pooled in equimolar concentrations. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) with paired-end reads. Bacteria: initial denaturation 95°C (3 min); 30 cycles of 95°C (30 s), 52°C (30 s), 72°C (45 s); final extension 72°C (5 min), Fungi: 32 cycles, 55°C annealing, Archaea: 32 cycles, 56°C annealing.

Raw paired-end reads were quality-filtered using Fastp v0.23.0 [32] with the following parameters: reads with a quality score below Q20 in a sliding window of 50 bp were trimmed; reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming were discarded; and reads containing ambiguous bases (N) were removed. Based on the overlap between paired-end reads, sequences were merged using FLASH v1.2.11 [33] with a minimum overlap length of 10 bp and a maximum mismatch ratio of 0.2 in the overlapping region. Samples were demultiplexed according to their barcode and primer sequences, allowing zero mismatches for barcodes and up to two mismatches for primers. OTU clustering was performed using UPARSE v7.1 [34] at a 97% similarity threshold. Chimeric sequences were detected and removed using the UCHIME algorithm [35] implemented in USEARCH. OTUs with fewer than two sequences across all samples were discarded. Representative sequences were taxonomically assigned using the RDP Classifier v2.11 [36] against the SILVA database v138 [37] for bacteria and archaea, and against the UNITE + INSD database [38] for fungi, with a confidence threshold of 80%.

Metagenomic library preparation, sequencing, and bioinformatics

Metagenomic libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs), following the manufacturer's protocol. For metagenomic sequencing, qualified DNA (total amount ≥ 1 μg, concentration ≥ 20 ng/μL) was randomly sheared into fragments of approximately 300–400 bp using a Covaris M220 ultrasonic homogenizer. Following end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification, fragments were purified using AMPure XP Magnetic Beads (Beckman Coulter, OH, USA) to construct a metagenomic library with an average insert size of approximately 500 bp. Library quality was assessed and quantified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and real-time quantitative PCR. Libraries meeting quality standards underwent paired-end sequencing (PE150) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, targeting 10 Gb of raw data per sample with a minimum sequence quality of Q30 ≥ 85%.

Raw metagenomic reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using Fastp v0.23.0 [32] with default parameters for metagenomic data (quality threshold Q20, minimum length 50 bp). High-quality reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [39] with k-mer parameters set to 21, 41, 61, 81, and 101; only contigs ≥ 300 bp were retained for subsequent analysis. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from assembled contigs using Prodigal v2.6.3 [40] with the -meta parameter, and predicted genes with sequence lengths ≥ 100 bp were retained as candidate gene sets. To construct a non-redundant gene catalogue, all predicted genes were clustered using CD-HIT v4.6.1 [41] at the nucleotide level with 95% identity and 90% coverage thresholds. Gene abundance was calculated by aligning clean reads from each sample against the non-redundant gene set using Bowtie2 [42]. For functional annotation, performed at the amino-acid level using DIAMOND BLASTX (e-value ≤ 1e-5) against the KEGG GENES database, followed by pathway mapping via KEGG Mapper [43].

Data analysis

Chloroplast and mitochondrial sequences were removed from all samples to minimize bias. To mitigate the impact of sequencing depth on subsequent α-diversity and β-diversity analyses, all sample sequences were normalized to the minimum sequencing depth. Principal Co-ordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on bray–curtis distances was performed using the vegan package to illustrate the β-diversity of microorganisms across different weathering levels. To quantitatively analyze the ecological assembly process of microbial communities, this study first performed multiple sequence alignment using MAFFT (v 7.505) based on representative sequences from highly variable regions obtained through amplicon sequencing of bacteria, fungi, and archaea. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree (v2.1.11) under the GTR + GAMMA model. Phylosignal functionality within the picante package was employed to test for phylogenetic signal presence (PageI's λ > 0 and P < 0.05), confirming the tree's suitability for β-Nearest Taxon Index (βNTI) calculations. Further distinguish specific types of random processes using the Raup-Crick index (RCbray) based on Bray-Curtis distance. RCbray > +0.95: Dispersal limitation. RCbray < −0.95: Homogenizing dispersal. |RCbray| ≤ 0.95: Undominated processes, primarily driven by ecological drift and similar factors. Bacterial, archaeal, and fungal OTUs were used to construct respective bacterial, archaeal, and fungal networks. Co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for each sample group (OS, BS, and WS) based on the abundance profiles of OTUs across the 8 biological replicates within each group. Bacterial, archaeal, and fungal OTUs with mean relative abundances below 0.01% were removed from each OTU table. Spearman correlation analysis was performed on all bacterial, archaeal, and fungal OTU pairs, with P-values adjusted using the FDR method. To ensure comparability between networks, identical similarity thresholds P (P < 0.01) and r (|r| ≥ 0.8) were applied during network construction. Network descriptors and topological properties—including total nodes and edges, modularity, average degree, average clustering coefficient, and average path distance—were computed using the igraph package in R software. The cross-domain network was constructed using the “corBionetwork.st” function in ggClusterNet package(https://github.com/taowen-micro/ggClusterNet). OTU abundance matrices for bacteria, fungi, and archaea were aligned by sample and merged as input data. Spearman correlation was employed to calculate correlations, with significant correlations (P < 0.01) filtered and only edges with |r| ≥ 0.8 retained. Finally, Gephi 0.10.1 software was used to visualize the networks. Random networks with the same number of nodes and edges as the empirical networks were generated. Based on this model, topological indices were summarized through 999 iterations to determine whether network properties were prone to error. Modularity, a measure of the degree to which a network is partitioned into modules, was used as the threshold for defining modular structure (modularity > 0.4). Each node's connectivity is determined by intra-module connectivity (Zi) and inter-module connectivity (Pi). Nodes are classified based on their topological roles in the network using Zi and Pi values. Nodes are categorized into four types: module centers (Zi > 2.5, Pi < 0.62), network centers (Zi > 2.5, Pi > 0.62), connectors (Zi < 2.5, Pi > 0.62), and peripherals (Zi < 2.5, Pi < 0.62). Modular hubs, network hubs, and connectors typically serve as critical nodes within the network structure.

Bacterial α-diversity (Chao1 index and Shannon index) in both BS and WS samples exceeded that in OS samples (Fig. 1A; Fig. S2). These findings suggest that sandstone weathering may be associated with microbial colonization. Bacterial communities in samples from the three weathering stages were primarily composed of Actinomycetota, Acidobacteriota, and Pseudomonadota (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, the relative abundance of Pseudomonadota in the BS and WS samples were significantly higher than that in the OS samples, while the relative abundances of Actinomycetota and Acidobacteriota were significantly lower than in the OS samples (Fig. 1C). Fungal communities in the BS and WS samples were dominated by Ascomycota, whereas the OS samples were primarily composed of Ascomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Basidiomycota (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate that sandstone weathering alters the characteristic composition of microbial communities. Core microbial taxa comprised 56 bacterial genera, 40 fungal genera, and 2 archaeal genera (Fig. 1D). Compared to the OS samples, Rubellimicrobium and Methylobacterium were enriched in both BS and WS samples (Fig. 1D). Gp16, Rubrobacter, and Soilrubrobacter were predominantly enriched in the OS samples, Rhinocladiella in the WS samples, and Microvirga and Knufia in the BS samples (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these findings reveal that samples from different weathering stages share major microbial taxa but exhibit significant differences in abundance. These variations may underpin the fundamental functions of microbial communities under varying weathering conditions.

Heterogeneous selection intensifies during weathering processes

To quantify the relative contributions of deterministic and stochastic processes to microbial community assembly, we performed zero-model analysis on bacterial, archaeal, and fungal communities from three sample types (OS, BS, WS) based on the βNTI. The frequency distribution of |βNTI| values revealed distinct assembly mechanisms (Fig. 2A, B, 2C). In OS samples, |βNTI| values across all three domains were generally below 2, indicating that stochastic processes were the primary force shaping the initial community. A significant shift occurred in BS and WS samples, where the distribution of |βNTI| values shifted markedly toward values greater than 2 (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001). We further quantified the relative contributions of different ecological processes (Fig. 2D, E, 2F). As weathering progressed, deterministic processes (heterogeneous selection) gradually dominated bacterial communities. Fungal and archaeal communities also transitioned toward deterministic processes, yet stochastic processes (undominant processes, homogeneous dispersal, dispersal limitation) remained dominant. The divergent assembly patterns among bacteria, fungi, and archaea indicate that these three domains respond differentially to intense environmental selection pressures during the transition from unweathered to actively weathered sandstones.

To identify putative keystone taxa within these modules, we calculated the Zi (intramodular connectivity) and Pi (intermodular connectivity) values for each node across all networks. Analysis of the Zi-Pi scatterplot (Fig. 3) revealed clusters of different ecological roles: Module hubs (Zi > 2.5, Pi ≤ 0.62): exhibit high connectivity centrality within their own modules, playing a crucial role in maintaining module stability. Connectors (Pi > 0.62, Zi ≤ 2.5): link different modules, serving as bridges for the exchange of energy, matter, or information between modules. Notably, in the bacterial, fungal, and three-domain integrated networks of BS, both the number and proportion of Network hubs and Module hubs were significantly higher than in OS and WS networks (Fig. 3). The abundant emergence of these hub groups during the BS stage indicates that a cohort of microorganisms playing crucial roles in maintaining network structural and functional stability emerged during biofilm formation (Tables S3–S5).

Potential mechanisms of microbe associated with sandstone weathering

To integrate multi-level data spanning environmental physicochemical properties, microbial community dynamics, and functional outputs, we constructed a partial least squares path model. This model aims to quantitatively validate our core hypothesis: environmental changes induced by sandstone weathering (e.g., pH and EC) shape microbial community structure and assembly processes, thereby regulating the abundance of weathering-related functional genes and ultimately driving weathering processes.

The PLS-PM analysis revealed that our theoretical model effectively explains the observed data (Fig. 5A–C). Specifically, environmental screening drives community change and environmental physicochemical properties (primarily driven by the pH and EC) exert strong direct effects on microbial community characteristics. The pH directly influenced species richness (path coefficients = 0.659, 0.596, 0.792), while EC affected community assembly processes (path coefficients = −0.729, −0.590, −0.503). Community dynamics associated with the emergence of key groups. Increased species richness coupled with enhanced deterministic assembly processes collectively create a highly selective community environment. This environment directly determines the composition of putative keystone taxa. Specifically, a complex species pool (high richness) and intense environmental selection create conditions for the colonization and proliferation of groups possessing specific functional advantages. Putative keystone taxa serve as carriers and executors of functional genes. The model reveals that changes in putative keystone taxa composition are the most direct predictors of weathering-related gene abundance (path coefficients = 0.429, 0.672, 0.530). In contrast, overall species richness shows no significant direct contribution to functional gene abundance. This indicates that functions are not randomly distributed across the entire community but are highly concentrated in key groups that have prevailed in environmental selection. Functional genes linkes to weathering outcomes. Discussion

Successional patterns of microbial communities during weathering processes

The patterns of microbial α-diversity variation indicate that biofilm formation and weathering processes significantly promote microbial community diversification. Biofilms serve as hotspots of microbial diversity [20,44,45]. The three-dimensional structure of biofilms provides diverse ecological niches for different microbial groups, while the dissolution and transformation of minerals during weathering further release new niche resources, thereby supporting higher levels of species coexistence [46]. The significant separation of community structures revealed by PCoA analysis further confirms the strong influence of the weathering gradient on microbial community assembly. This segregation pattern reflects the crucial role of environmental filtering in shaping community structure, where variations in the physicochemical properties of sandstone surfaces (Table S3) may select microbial groups with specific functional traits through environmental filtering mechanisms [47,48]. Notably, the community structure at the biofilm stage exhibits distinct transitional characteristics, supporting its significance as an intermediate stage in ecological succession [13,49].

The compositional shifts observed at the phylum level reflect distinct ecological strategies among microbial groups along the weathering gradient. Pseudomonadota are typically considered r-strategists, capable of rapidly utilizing biofilm substrates and nutrients released during weathering processes [50,51]. Conversely, Actinomycetota and Acidobacteriota tend to adopt K-strategies, adapting to the stable yet nutrient-poor conditions within unweathered sandstone [51]. This shift in community composition indicates that weathering processes favor microbial groups possessing rapid growth capabilities and strong metabolic activity. Core microbial analysis revealed key functional groups across different weathering stages. The persistent enrichment of Rubellimicrobium and Methylobacterium in biofilms and weathered samples suggests these groups may play crucial roles in sustaining community function. Methylobacterium, in particular, is known for its diverse organic acid secretion capabilities and may directly participate in silicate mineral weathering processes [52]. Meanwhile, Gp16, Rubrobacter, and Soilrubrobacter, enriched in unweathered samples, likely represent the primitive community within the sandstone, with these groups typically associated with adaptations to oligotrophic environments [53]. Notably, the pattern of shared major microbial groups with differing abundances across samples of varying weathering levels may reflect functional redundancy within microbial communities. This redundancy contributes to maintaining ecosystem stability, ensuring that fundamental biogeochemical processes persist even under changing environmental conditions.

Ecological succession of community assembly mechanisms and their environmental associations

The transition from stochastic to deterministic assembly along the weathering gradient suggests a fundamental shift in the ecological processes structuring sandstone microbial communities. In unweathered sandstone, the dominance of random processes reflects the oligotrophic, low-biomass nature of the habitat, where dispersal limitation and stochastic colonization are expected to prevail (Li et al., 2024,). However, when microorganisms form biofilms on sandstone surfaces, the community assembly mechanism undergoes a significant shift, with deterministic selection becoming the dominant force. This finding supports the theory of biofilms acting as ecological filters, exerting strong selective pressures on microbial communities by creating unique microenvironments [[54], [55], [56]]. Notably, the relative contribution of deterministic selection continues to increase throughout the biofilm and weathering stages, where variable selection remains the most important assembly process. This indicates that as weathering progresses, environmental heterogeneity increases, thereby screening for functional groups capable of adapting to specific microenvironments. This shift in assembly patterns reflects the ecological process of microbial communities evolving from a “first-arrived” random colonization phase to a “best-suited” environmental adaptation phase.

The three microbial domains exhibited differential responses to deterministic selection, consistent with their distinct life-history strategies and functional traits. Bacteria, with short generation times and high metabolic plasticity, are expected to respond rapidly to environmental filtering, whereas fungi may leverage their mycelial networks for enhanced spatial exploration and resource acquisition [57]. Archaea, often adapted to extreme or fluctuating conditions, may possess distinctive tolerance mechanisms that influence their response to microenvironmental changes during biofilm initiation. These domain-specific patterns suggest that predicting community responses to environmental change requires consideration of the diverse ecological strategies and physiological constraints across phylogenetic groups.

The complex structure and functional synergy of biofilm networks

Compared to the OS and WS samples, the microbial network in the BS samples exhibited intermediate complexity. This finding supports the hypothesis that biofilms serve as transitional stages in weathering processes. The three-dimensional matrix structure of biofilms not only provides habitats but also promotes close interactions among microorganisms [[58], [59], [60]]. The presence of EPS within biofilms may enhance network complexity by creating chemical gradients and microenvironmental heterogeneity, thereby offering diverse ecological niches for different microbial types (Fig. S3) [61,62]. Notably, the high modularity of biofilm networks holds significant ecological implications. Modular structures reflect functional division within microbial communities, with distinct modules potentially responsible for specific ecological functions such as organic acid production, iron carrier synthesis, or environmental sensing. This “division of labor” pattern may significantly enhance the functional efficiency of the community, enabling it to perform complex weathering tasks in a synergistic manner. In contrast, the network structure at the unweathered stage is relatively simple, reflecting limited microbial interactions in a barren environment. The highest network complexity observed at the highly weathered stage may result from the porous structure provided by weathered sandstone and the accumulation of abundant nutrients during the biofilm stage.

A key finding of this study is the identification of numerous hub species in the biofilm stage through Zi-Pi analysis. These hub species (primarily bacteria, with fewer fungi and archaea; Tables S3–S5), encompassing both module hubs and network hubs, likely play a central role in coordinating community functions and maintaining network stability (Fig. S4) (Chen and Wen., 2023; [19,20,63]). Module hubs ensure stable execution of specific functions by maintaining internal connectivity within their respective modules, while network hubs facilitate synergistic coordination between functional modules by linking disparate modules. Notably, the abundant emergence of these hub species during biofilm formation indicates that biofilm development is not merely a simple aggregation of microorganisms but a highly organized ecological process. These hub species may influence community function through multiple mechanisms, such as secreting common products (e.g., EPS), initiating quorum sensing, or executing key metabolic reactions. Their emergence likely significantly enhances the ecological efficiency of microbial communities, enabling them to respond more effectively to environmental stresses on sandstone surfaces.

The LEfSe analysis revealed that the biofilm stage (BS) and weathering stage (WS) exhibited distinctly different functional profiles, a finding of significant ecological importance. The significant enrichment of quorum sensing and two-component signaling pathways in the biofilm stage reflects the high dependence of microorganisms on environmental perception and intercellular communication during colonization of sandstone surfaces [64,65]. This indicates that microorganisms require the establishment of efficient information exchange networks to coordinate collective behavior during the initial stages of interface colonization. Notably, as a transitional stage from unweathered to highly weathered conditions, the biofilm stage exhibits distinct transitional functional characteristics. It retains some fundamental metabolic functions while preliminarily acquiring specialized weathering capabilities (Fig. 4B). The functional spectrum of the weathering stage (WS) is characterized by extreme enrichment of metabolic pathways directly linked to mineral dissolution, including iron carrier biosynthesis, organic acid metabolism, and sulfur metabolism. This spectrum delineates a highly specialized weathering arsenal, where different pathways may synergistically accelerate mineral breakdown (Fig. 4A; 4C). For instance, organic acids disrupt silicate lattices through acidolysis, while iron carriers dissolve iron-bearing minerals via chelation. This multi-pathway synergistic weathering mechanism significantly enhances efficiency [66]. Notably, the enrichment of sulfur metabolism pathways suggests that biosynthetic sulfate production may play a significant role in sandstone weathering [67], aligning with theories proposing microbial sulfur cycling contributes to geological weathering [[20], [21], [22],68].

Functional contributions of key taxonomic groups

The Phylogenetic tracing analysis conducted via the MetaPhlAn workflow provides new insights into understanding microbial functional contributions. The module hubs and network hubs primarily comprise Sphingomonas, Rubellimicrobium, Microlunatus, Blastococcus, Microvirga, Nocardioides, and Rubrobacter. These align closely with the microbial taxa traced to the genus level in the significantly enriched pathways depicted in the Sankey diagram (Fig. 4B). These microbial taxa maintain community stability while being major contributors to weathering-related metabolic pathways. The Sankey diagram clearly shows Rubrobacter as a primary contributor to multiple core weathering functions, a finding highly consistent with its ecological niche in sandstone environments. Previous studies indicate Rubrobacter possesses strong environmental adaptability and metabolic diversity, capable of producing various organic acids and iron carriers involved in mineral weathering [14,69]. Notably, this genus maintains high functional contributions in both biofilm and weathering stages, indicating its role as a putative keystone taxa within the weathering microbiome and potentially a key factor in maintaining community functional stability. Beyond Rubrobacter, other taxa such as Streptomyces and Acidithiobacillus also exhibit significant contributions within specific functional modules. This functional division reflects an ecological strategy where microbial communities optimize functionality through niche differentiation and resource allocation. Different taxa play complementary roles during weathering, forming complex functional collaboration networks consistent with the theory of functional redundancy and complementarity in microbial communities proposed.

Synergy among different functions during weathering processes

The Co-occurrence network analysis reveals the highly organized nature of microbial weathering functions. The network's modular structure indicates that distinct functional processes are efficiently organized into relatively independent yet interconnected functional modules. This modular architecture holds significant ecological implications: on one hand, it enhances metabolic efficiency through functional division of labor; on the other, connections between modules ensure coordinated integration of diverse functional processes [70]. Particularly noteworthy is the tight linkage between the environmental sensing and regulation module, the iron acquisition and metal resistance, and organic acid production and central carbon metabolism modules. This connectivity pattern indicates that microbial communities establish an efficient “perception-response” mechanism, enabling precise regulation of weathering function expression in response to environmental signals. For instance, upon detecting iron limitation signals, microbes may activate iron transporter synthesis pathways via a two-component system. Conversely, when environmental pH shifts, quorum sensing systems may coordinate organic acid production and secretion [71,72]. This fine-tuned regulatory mechanism enables microbial communities to allocate metabolic resources optimally, maximizing weathering efficiency.

Validation and implications of a hierarchical ecological model

The PLS-PM constructed by this research successfully quantifies and validates the complete causal chain from environmental gradients to microbial functional execution, revealing a clear hierarchical ecological action model (Fig. 5). This model is not only statistically significant but, more importantly, its path coefficients clearly delineate the ecological logic of sandstone weathering: environmental selection acts as the top-level driver [73], shaping community structure and key taxa, and consequently modulating the abundance of functional genes associated with weathering, thereby enhancing the community's overall functional potential for mineral dissolution.

First, the model confirms the primary driving role of environmental change. Geochemical properties (pH and EC) exert the strongest direct effects on microbial communities, highlighting that the environment constitutes a fundamental selective pressure within the sandstone-microbe interaction system. pH primarily influences species richness, while EC more directly regulates the deterministic assembly process of communities [20,45,74,75]. This finding quantitatively bridges traditional environmental factor studies with emerging community assembly theory, demonstrating that environmental change does not merely alter species lists but fundamentally reshapes the ecological rules governing community construction—shifting from randomness-dominated to deterministic selection-dominated processes [76].

Second, the model reveals the pivotal role of putative keystone taxa in linking community structure and function. The most critical finding is that overall species richness does not directly contribute to functional gene abundance, whereas changes in putative keystone taxa composition emerge as the strongest predictor. This strongly challenges the limitations of the functional redundancy hypothesis in the specific context of sandstone weathering, instead emphasizing the importance of functional specialization and niche optimization [77]. Under intense environmental selection, successful colonizers are not random species but those possessing specific metabolic functions (e.g., organic acid secretion, iron transporter synthesis). These groups become critical nodes in the network through ecological interactions, concentrating and driving core biogenic weathering functions [78]. This provides micro-mechanistic evidence for understanding how specific groups within complex communities associated with ecosystem functions.

Finally, the model clearly delineates an execution pathway from genes to geological outcomes. The exceptionally high direct effect of weathering-functional gene abundance on weathering intensity (path coefficient = 0.970) nearly perfectly demonstrates the direct geological efficacy of microbial metabolic activity. This establishes a robust causal link between molecular biological evidence and macroscopic geological phenomena, bridging a critical gap between gene identification and functional validation.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the ecological succession mechanisms and functional regulatory networks of microbial communities during sandstone weathering by integrating multi-omics and geochemical analyses. The findings reveal that biofilm formation serves as a critical transitional phase in the weathering process, driving the shift from random to deterministic selection in community assembly mechanisms. This process constructs a highly modular interspecies putative association networks enriched with functionally specialized putative keystone taxa. The tight coupling between the environmental signal perception module and the weathering metabolic function module enables synergistic regulation from external stimuli to internal metabolic responses, significantly enhancing the community's sandstone weathering capacity. Through quantitative validation using PLS-PM, this study further proposes and validates a theoretical framework of “environmental screening–network organization–functional synergy.” This framework demonstrates that sandstone weathering is not a singular physicochemical process or a passive outcome of microbial activity, but rather a highly ordered ecological cascade initiated by environmental change, deterministically assembling and selecting key groups, and ultimately executed through functional gene network synergy. This predictive mechanistic model not only deepens our understanding of microorganisms as geological engineers but also provides a universal theoretical reference for interpreting microbially associated with biogeochemical processes at other mineral interfaces—such as soil formation, marine crust weathering. Simultaneously, the findings provide crucial scientific basis for preventing biological corrosion and ecologically restoring stone cultural relics, offering significant practical guidance for long-term heritage preservation and geo-ecological management.
