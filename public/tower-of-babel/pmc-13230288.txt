Gain-of-function Modulation of TREM2 and its Impact on Amyloid-β and Tau Pathologies in Alzheimer’s Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Animal Studies
Authors: Yingdi Liao, Sirong Ye, Lianna Zhao, Muyan Zhou, Yuanyuan Wen, Youyang Zhu, Jinqing Huang, Luoqing Zhang, Xinjue Zhang, Peixin Guo, Yuhuan Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, hyperphosphorylated Tau accumulation, and chronic neuroinflammation, with microglial function playing a crucial role in modulating these pathological cascades. The microglial receptor TREM2 has emerged as a key regulator of microglial responses to AD-related pathology, and gain-of-function strategies targeting TREM2 have shown substantial therapeutic potential in preclinical models. However, outcomes of these strategies on Aβ and Tau pathologies have exhibited marked heterogeneity across studies, and no systematic integration of the relevant evidence so far. This deficiency has substantially hindered the rational development and clinical translation of TREM2-targeted therapeutic strategies. The objective of this study is to evaluate the effects of TREM2-targeted interventions on Aβ and Tau pathologies in AD animal models. A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library identified studies using AD animal models with TREM2-targeted interventions and reporting Aβ or Tau outcomes. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251131147). A total of 12 studies were included, with overall moderate risk of bias, mainly due to inadequate randomization and blinding. In APP/PS1 mice, TREM2 overexpression significantly reduced Aβ plaque number (SMD = − 0.87; 95% CI, − 1.28 to − 0.47) and plaque area (SMD = − 0.98; 95% CI, − 1.46 to − 0.50), with more pronounced effects observed in younger mice (≤ 7 months), where reductions in insoluble Aβ42 were also observed; phosphorylated Tau levels decreased as well. TREM2 agonist antibodies also reduced Aβ plaque number (SMD = − 2.03; 95% CI, − 2.83 to − 1.24). However, effects on Aβ plaque area, insoluble and soluble Aβ isoforms levels were inconsistent. Antibody treatment also attenuated Tau pathology and Aβ pathology in certain models, including 5XFAD mice or Tau transgenic mice. In addition, both TREM2 overexpression and agonist antibodies could reverse the cognitive impairment of AD animal models. This systematic review and meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive synthesis of preclinical evidence supporting TREM2 gain-of-function modulation as an AD therapeutic strategy targeting both Aβ and Tau pathologies. Our findings reveal that therapeutic efficacy is governed by disease stage, model pathological complexity, and intervention mode. These insights highlight the need for developing stage-specific and pathology-stratified strategies in translational research, establishing TREM2 as a condition-dependent yet promising immunotherapeutic target for AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-026-05925-6.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, TREM2, Gain-of-function modulation, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder globally, characterized by insidious onset and progressive cognitive decline [1]. The pathological hallmarks of AD include the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) oligomers and insoluble Aβ plaques, and the intracellular formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of phosphorylated Tau protein [2]. This pathological milieu potently activates microglia, inducing a state of chronic neuroinflammation that is now recognized as a key driver of AD pathology [3]. Activated microglia cluster around Aβ plaques to facilitate phagocytic activity [4]. Notably, recent studies have identified a specialized disease-associated microglia (DAM) phenotype, characterized by enhanced phagocytic capacity and a transcriptional program critically dependent on triggering receptors expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) [5].

TREM2 is a single-pass membrane receptor predominantly expressed in microglia within the central nervous system (CNS), but it is also expressed in peripheral dendritic cells, macrophages, and osteoclasts [6]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified TREM2 mutations, most notably the R47H variant, as robust risk factors for AD pathogenesis [7, 8]. Functionally, TREM2 plays a critical role in Aβ clearance and the microglial response to brain injury and neuroinflammation. Impaired TREM2 function, observed in loss-of-function models or carriers of the R47H variant, compromises microglial recruitment to and engagement with Aβ plaques, accelerates the seeding and spread of Tau pathology, and exacerbates neuronal damage [9, 10]. This notion is experimentally validated by findings that intracerebral injection of AD-derived pathological Tau into amyloid-depositing mice with defective TREM2 function results in significantly enhanced Tau seeding, spreading, and cerebral atrophy [11]. Consequently, TREM2 is hypothesized to exert a protective effect during AD progression by limiting Aβ-facilitated propagation of pathological tau [12]. Consistent with this protective role, TREM2 overexpression in APP/PS1 mice ameliorates AD-related pathological features and alleviates spatial cognitive deficits [13], while targeted enhancement of TREM2 function promotes microglial phagocytic activity [14]. Therapeutically, TREM2 agonist antibodies have recently demonstrated promising efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo AD models [15–17].

However, a comprehensive and unambiguous understanding of TREM2’s role in mediating the clearance or accumulation of Aβ and Tau is still lacking. Existing studies have shown that chronic and sustained TREM2 activation may fail to reduce Aβ plaque burden or modify plaque conformation; instead, it can elevate microglial activation to a neurotoxic inflammatory state, ultimately contributing to neuritic dystrophy [18]. This functional duality further highlights the inadequacy of our current understanding of the effects of its activation or upregulation, which will significantly hinder the rational development and clinical translation of TREM2-targeted therapeutic regimens. Therefore, this review and meta-analysis intends to evaluate the potential effects of TREM2 gain-of-function modulation on Aβ and Tau pathology in AD through a systematic review.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251131147).

Eligibility Criteria and Screening

The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases for articles published from inception to March 23, 2025, were searched, using specific search terms related to Alzheimer’s disease and TREM2. The search keywords include “Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2,” “TREM2,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” “antibody,” “therapy.” The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in the supplementary materials.

Studies were included if they employed animal models of AD, such as transgenic mice or induced models, applied direct pharmacological interventions targeting TREM2, including TREM2-specific agonists, anti-TREM2 antibodies, or TREM2 overexpression/upregulation, and included at least one appropriate control group. Eligible studies were required to report primary outcomes related to Aβ pathology and/or Tau pathology. Meanwhile, cognitive function outcomes from the Morris water maze, Y-maze, and novel object recognition tests from included studies were pooled for analysis. Studies were excluded if they were non-animal investigations, did not directly target TREM2 or lacked mechanistic evaluation, were duplicate publications or lacked suitable control groups, or did not report relevant outcomes.

Study Selection and Data Collection

Two authors (YL and SY) independently screened the articles by reviewing titles and abstracts after duplicates removal. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then retrieved and assessed against the inclusion criteria, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.

Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers (YL and SY) using predefined forms. Extracted information included the first author’s name, year of publication, animal species and strain, AD modeling methods, laboratory technique, and relevant outcome data for meta-analysis. GetData software (Version 2.25.0.32) was used to obtain the mean, standard error of the mean (SEM) or standard deviations (SD) of Aβ or Tau levels, as well as other behavioral assessments of cognition, across the different groups. When necessary, subgroup data were merged and continuous variables were calculated using standard formulas [20]. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (YX) until consensus was reached.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risks of bias among the included studies were assessed using a checklist developed by the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) [21]. The evaluation domains comprised: random sequence generation (selection bias), baseline characteristics (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), randomized housing (performance bias), blinding of caregivers and investigators (performance bias), random outcome assessment (measurement bias), blinding of outcome assessment (measurement bias), incomplete outcome data (loss of follow-up bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases. Each item is judged by “yes” (low risk), “no” (high risk), and “unclear” (unclear information).

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3. When data were extracted as mean and SEM, the SD was calculated as SD = SEM × √n. The effect size was estimated using the standardized mean difference (SMD) and its 95% confidence interval (CI), and the hypothesis test level was set at α = 0.10. The I2 statistic was calculated to evaluate inter-study heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the Chi2 test and quantified by the I2 statistic. An I2 value greater than 50% was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity, in which case a random-effects model was adopted; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used.

To assess the robustness and reliability of the results, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled effect size. In addition, pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, stratified by animal age, treatment duration, and exposure duration. Where sufficient data were available, meta-regression analyses were considered to further examine the impact of study-level covariates on effect sizes. To evaluate small-study effects and publication bias, funnel plots were generated when at least 10 studies were included in a pooled analysis. Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test were additionally planned for quantitative assessment of publication bias.

Results

Literature Research Finding

After literature retrieval, a total of 1820 studies were identified. Following screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts, 12 studies [13, 15, 18, 22–30] published between 2014 and 2025 were finally included. The screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of the Included Studies and Risk of Bias Assessment

Among the 12 included studies, four employed the 5XFAD model [18, 23, 26, 29], four used the APP/PS1 model [13, 24, 25, 27], one adopted the APP-NL-G-F transgenic model [28], two investigated a novel transgenic mouse expressing mutant Tau [15, 30], and one applied the TauPS2APP model simultaneously expressing human mutant Tau, presenilin 2 (PS2), and amyloid precursor protein (APP) developed to simulate the two core pathologies of AD [22]. One study used 1.5-month-old mice [30], one study simultaneously examined 2-month-old and 10-month-old mice [15], another investigated 18-month-old AD model mice [24], while the remaining studies used mice aged 4–7 months [13, 18, 22, 23, 25–29]. Regarding intervention strategies, five studies employed lentivirus-mediated TREM2 overexpression [13, 24, 25, 27, 30], whereas the other seven used TREM2 agonist antibodies [15, 18, 22, 23, 26, 28, 29]. Only one study implemented a short-term multiple-dose antibody regimen [28], whereas the others applied long-term interventions administered weekly or biweekly [13, 15, 18, 22–27, 29]. Of these, nine studies reported pathological outcomes related to Aβ [13, 18, 22–24, 26–29], and six reported pathological indicators of Tau [15, 18, 22, 25, 29, 30]. Assessment of Methodological Quality

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool. The results showed that six studies had a high risk of bias in random sequence generation [13, 15, 18, 22, 23, 30], while six studies mentioned “randomization” without providing details [24–29] and were therefore judged as “unclear.” Regarding baseline characteristics, two studies exhibited a low risk of bias [25, 27]. Due to insufficient information, all studies were rated as “unclear” for allocation concealment. Nine studies [15, 18, 22–25, 27, 28, 30] reported consistent animal housing conditions and environments, thus assessed as low risk. Only four studies explicitly reported blinding [13, 22, 26, 29], whereas the remainder were judged as “unclear”. For random outcome assessment, one study was rated as high risk [15], while the others either mentioned random selection without further details or were considered “unclear.” With respect to incomplete outcome data, four studies had complete datasets [23, 25, 28, 29], one reported animal deaths without sufficient explanation [15], and the others were rated as “unclear.” All studies were judged as low risk for selective outcome reporting, and most were also rated as low risk for other sources of bias [13, 18, 22–29]. Meta-analysis

Impact of TREM2 Overexpression on AD Pathology and Cognition

Effects on Aβ Burden and Levels

Four studies [13, 24, 25, 27] using a lentiviral strategy to overexpress TREM2 in APP/PS1 mice demonstrated significant reductions in both the number of Aβ plaques (SMD = −0.87; 95% CI, −1.28 to −0.47; P < 0.0001; I2 = 9%; Fig. 2) and the area of Aβ plaques (SMD = −0.98; 95% CI, −1.43 to −0.52; P < 0.0001; I2 = 10%; Fig. 3) in the brain.

Meta-analysis of overexpression of TREM2 on Aβ plaque area

Subgroup analyses by age showed that TREM2 overexpression significantly reduced plaque numbers in animals younger within 7 months (SMD = −1.1; 95% CI, −1.63 to −0.57; P < 0.0001), but not at 18 months (SMD = −0.55; 95% CI, −1.18 to 0.09; P = 0.09; Table 3). For Aβ plaque area, significant reductions were observed in animals within 7 months (SMD = −1.03; 95% CI, −1.65 to −0.40; P = 0.001; Table 3) and at 18 months of age (SMD = −0.92; 95% CI, −1.58 to −0.26; P = 0.006; Table 3). Region-specific analysis showed a significant decrease in both the hippocampus and cerebral cortex (Table 3).

Pooled analysis of three studies [13, 24, 27] showed that TREM2 overexpression could significantly reduce the levels of soluble Aβ42 (SMD = −1.65; 95% CI, −3.27 to −0.03; P = 0.05; I2 = 84%) and insoluble Aβ42 (SMD = −1.05; 95% CI, −2.03 to −0.08; P = 0.03; I2 = 64%) in the brain (Fig. 4). Age-stratified analysis showed reduced heterogeneity in younger mice, where insoluble Aβ42 was markedly decreased at ≤ 7 months (SMD = −1.5; 95% CI, −2.27 to −0.73; P = 0.0001; I2 = 15%; Table 3), but not in older animals (SMD = −0.26; 95% CI, −1.14 to 0.62; P = 0.56; Table 3).

Meta-analysis of overexpression of TREM2 on Aβ levels

Analysis on Tau Pathology

One study [25] investigated the effect of TREM2 upregulation on phosphorylated Tau levels in APP/PS1 mice. Compared with the control group, TREM2 overexpression significantly inhibited the expression of phosphorylated Tau at Ser199, Ser396, and Thr205 in both the cortex and hippocampus (P < 0.05). Chen et al. [30] overexpressed TREM2 in the PS19 Tau protein pathological model and found that it could reduce the level of soluble phosphorylated Tau (P < 0.05).

Assessment of Cognitive Function

Two studies [25, 27] employed the Morris water maze to assess learning and memory, and the pooled results demonstrated that TREM2 overexpression in APP/PS1 mice significantly improved spatial memory, evidenced by increased time spent in the target quadrant (SMD = −3.57; 95% CI, 1.29 to 5.85; P = 0.002; I2 = 76%; Fig. 5) and a greater number of crossings over the platform (SMD = 2.65; 95% CI, 1.75 to 3.55; P < 0.01; I2 = 0%; Fig. 5). Peng et al. [25] utilized both the Y-maze and novel object recognition tests, reporting that TREM2 overexpression also ameliorated deficits in spatial working memory and recognition memory. Collectively, these results indicate a broad positive impact of TREM2 overexpression on cognitive function in this AD model.

Effects of TREM2 Agonistic Antibodies on AD Pathology and Cognition

Analysis of Aβ Pathology

Six studies have investigated the impact of TREM2 antibodies on Aβ pathology [18, 22, 23, 26, 28, 29]. A pooled analysis of two studies [23, 29] in 5XFAD mice demonstrated that TREM2 antibodies significantly reduced the number of cerebral Aβ plaques following a 3-month treatment period (SMD = − 2.03; 95% CI, −2.83 to −1.24; P < 0.0001; I2 = 0%; Fig. 6). Subgroup analysis by brain region revealed significant reductions in both the cortex (SMD = −1.66; 95% CI, −2.53 to −0.79; P = 0.0002; I2 = 0%; Table 4) and hippocampus (SMD = −10.08; 95% CI, −15.89 to −4.27; P = 0.0007; Table 4) following TREM2 agonistic antibody treatment.

Meta-analysis of TREM2 agonistic antibodies on Aβ levels

One additional study [26], which did not specify the exact sample size and was therefore not included in the pooled analysis, was narratively summarized. In 5XFAD mice, treatment with the TREM2 antibody AL002a significantly reduced total Aβ deposition in the hippocampus and frontal cortex compared with controls. While no differences were observed in soluble Aβ38, Aβ40, and Aβ42, AL002a markedly reduced insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 in the frontal cortex (P < 0.05).

Analysis of Tau Pathology

Three studies [15, 22, 29] evaluated the effects of TREM2 antibodies on Tau pathology. In hTau transgenic mice, compared with IgG-treated animals, treatment with Ab-T1 significantly reduced the number of neurofibrillary tangles in the hippocampal region [15]. The pooled results showed that TREM2 agonist antibodies have no significant effect on phosphorylated Tau area in the hippocampus (SMD = −1.83; 95% CI, −5.68 to 2.02; P = 0.35; Fig. 9) [22, 29]. Considering the heterogeneity of the model, it is analyzed separately. In 5XFAD mice, administration of Ab18 led to a 50% reduction in phosphorylated Tau staining intensity in both the cortex and hippocampus compared with isotype control antibody treatment [29] (P < 0.05). One of the studies found that in animals transgenic with TauPS2APP combined with hTREM2, the TREM2 agonist antibody had no significant effect on phosphorylated Tau [22]. Based on the dual background of Aβ and Tau pathology in adult mice, Jain et al. [18] investigated the effect of TREM2 antibody on Aβ-induced neuroinflammatory plaque-related Tau (NP-Tau) pathology. The results suggested that long-term administration of TREM2 agonists would increase NP-tau pathology.

Cognitive Function Assessment

Conflicting results were reported regarding the efficacy of TREM2-targeting antibodies in improving cognitive function in AD mice. Supportive evidence comes from studies where Ab-T1 improved Morris water maze performance [15, 23] and AL002a enhanced novel object exploration [26]. However, these positive findings could not be quantitatively synthesized in a meta-analysis due to a lack of clear sample size data. Furthermore, a contrasting study [22] observed no significant therapeutic effects on a battery of behavioral tests, including nest building and spatial alternation in the Y-maze.

Discussion

Summary of Findings

This first systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively evaluates the therapeutic potential of TREM2 gain-of-function modulation for mitigating core pathological features of AD, focusing on two primary strategies, lentivirus-mediated TREM2 overexpression (or knock-in) and treatment with TREM2 agonist antibodies. Our findings demonstrate that TREM2 gain-of-function modulation may effectively engage microglia to ameliorate Aβ and Tau pathologies and to rescue cognitive deficits in preclinical models. However, the efficacy of this strategy is highly condition-dependent, being most pronounced in early disease stages and modulated by the complexity of the underlying pathology.

In the APP/PS1 mouse model, TREM2 overexpression markedly reduced Aβ burden, as evidenced by decreased plaque number and area. These effects were age dependent, with a more substantial reduction in insoluble Aβ42 levels observed in younger animals (≤ 7 months) compared to older counterparts [24], suggesting a critical therapeutic window for intervention. Beyond Aβ pathology, TREM2 overexpression suppressed the accumulation of phosphorylated Tau in cortical and hippocampal regions [25].

Similarly, antibody-based TREM2 activation showed therapeutic promise. TREM2 agonist antibodies reduced plaque numbers in the cortex and hippocampus, consistent with enhanced microglial phagocytic activity. However, their effects on plaque area, soluble and insoluble Aβ isoforms were variable, implying the involvement of additional modulatory mechanisms. Notably, these antibodies also mitigated neurofibrillary tangle formation and reduced phosphorylated Tau deposition. The efficacy of antibody interventions varied across AD models, with improvements in both Aβ and Tau pathology observed in 5XFAD and pure Tau transgenic mice [15, 29], whereas no benefit was detected in TauPS2APP triple-transgenic mice [22]. This suggests that complex pathological contexts, such as coexisting Aβ and Tau pathologies, may influence the therapeutic outcomes of TREM2 modulation.

Taken together, this review provides evidence-based support for TREM2 as a key therapeutic target in AD and offers a theoretical foundation for developing precision interventions tailored to different disease stages and pathological subtypes. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the efficacy of TREM2 agonism in pure Aβ or Tau models; however, the current evidence remains insufficient to validate the cognitive benefits and comprehensive pathological improvements of TREM2 modulation in disease contexts characterized by complex, mixed Aβ and Tau pathologies.

Mechanism Exploration and Explanation

TREM2 as a Master Regulator of Microglial Homeostasis

TREM2 is a pivotal regulator of microglial function. Upon binding to ligands such as Aβ, apolipoprotein E, lipoprotein, and interleukin 4, TREM2 transduces signals via the adaptor DNAX-activating protein of 12 kDa (DAP12), activating downstream pathways that enhance phagocytosis, promote proliferation, regulate lipid metabolism, and maintain inflammatory response equilibrium [7, 31]. Rather than functioning as a simple binary switch, TREM2 acts as a homeostatic rheostat. It guides microglia toward the DAM phenotype, ensuring energy supply and chemotactic migration toward Aβ plaques to maintain equilibrium in the brain’s immune environment [32]. Elevated expression of TREM2 helps suppress neuroinflammation, whereas its dysfunction or deficiency can intensify the inflammatory response [33]. While the impact of TREM2 on AD has long been attributed primarily to its role in promoting Aβ clearance and regulating microglia-mediated neuroinflammation, recent evidence highlights a complex, stage-dependent dichotomy in its function.

A Double-edged Sword of TREM2 Signaling on Aβ Pathology

Firstly, the influence of TREM2 deficiency on seeding and expansion of Aβ plaques reveals two faces. In part, it promotes microglial migration toward plaques and enhances the clearance of both oligomeric and fibrillar Aβ [29]. In line with this, TREM2 deficiency exacerbates Aβ pathology: loss of a single copy of TREM2 reduces plaque-associated microglia [34], the variation of the TREM2 gene impairs microglial clustering around plaques, worsens neuritic dystrophy, and increases diffuse plaque morphology [10]. Furthermore, TREM2-deficient brains exhibit elevated Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios and increased soluble fibrillar oligomers, underscoring impaired Aβ clearance [35]. However, there are contradictory studies suggesting TREM2 deficiency reduces the accumulation of myeloid cells around plaques to relieve inflammation and ameliorates amyloid and tau pathologies [36]. Evidence also suggested that the impact of TREM2 deficiency is also highly dependent on disease progression [37]. During the early seeding stage (in 2-month-old models), TREM2 deficiency paradoxically reduces Aβ plaque deposition, suggesting that early microglial recruitment to Aβ may inadvertently promote plaque seeding or nucleation. In contrast, during the advanced plaque expansion stage (in 8-month-old models), TREM2 deficiency significantly increases plaque area, likely because the loss of plaque-associated microglia disrupts the protective microglial barrier, thereby permitting uncontrolled plaque growth and the spread of neurotoxic Aβ species.

Besides, TREM2 activation is not a guaranteed benefit. In AD model mice, high TREM2 expression drives the protective DAM phenotype, yet it is also associated with an increase in senescent microglia, which contribute to chronic inflammation [36]. Specifically, the activation of TREM2 may increase beneficial DAM, but it will also increase harmful senescent cells.

In early disease, TREM2-mediated recruitment of microglia is essential for initiating Aβ clearance and limiting plaque toxicity [29, 33], and at this time, TREM2 acts as a “friend”. Our subgroup analyses also suggested that the upregulation of TREM2 has a potential protective effect in the early stage of the disease, supporting the existence of a time window for its treatment. However, in the later stage of AD, microglia enter an aging state, rendering their activation ineffective against Aβ pathology. Moreover, the surface of microglia expresses pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns and damage-associated molecular patterns, initiating a strong pro-inflammatory response. At this stage, the brain is dominated by chronic TLR-driven inflammation and metabolic exhaustion. These potent pro-inflammatory signals may override TREM2’s anti-inflammatory effects, rendering even high levels of TREM2 insufficient to restore Aβ clearance [38]. Consequently, even when TREM2 expression is upregulated, the brain microenvironment is dominated by TLR-driven inflammation and metabolic stress, leaving microglia trapped in a dysfunctional, aged state. It seems that the activation of TREM2 is like a “foe”.

Divergent Roles of TREM2 in Tau Proteotoxicity

Unlike Aβ, Tau does not directly bind to TREM2, making their relationship more complex than a simple protective or detrimental one, with specific effects depending on the animal model and disease pathological stage. Clinical observations indicate that TREM2 mRNA levels positively correlate with Tau burden in AD patients compared with healthy control [39], and AD patients carrying the R47H variant exhibit higher cerebrospinal fluid Tau levels than noncarriers [40], suggesting that impaired TREM2 function may facilitate Tau aggregation and seeding. Our meta-analysis reinforces this protective narrative, showing that TREM2 agonist antibodies or upregulation can inhibit Tau hyperphosphorylation and rescue cognitive deficits in pure Tau models or APP/PS1 animal models.

However, the interplay between TREM2 and Tau is not strictly unidirectional. Paradoxically, several studies demonstrate that the complete deletion of TREM2 may attenuate neuroinflammation and diminish Tau spreading in specific tauopathy models [41, 42]. This counterintuitive finding suggests that dysfunctional TREM2 signaling (rather than the complete absence of signaling) may be the driver that facilitates Tau propagation. Further supporting this notion, excessive activation of TREM2 downstream signaling, including NF-κB pathway, can exacerbate Tau seeding, propagation, and toxicity [43]. Thus, complete loss of TREM2 may paradoxically confer protective effects under certain conditions.

More concerning is that haploinsufficiency may be more detrimental than complete loss, trapping microglia in a dysfunctional “intermediate state” that fails to provide protection yet sustains chronic neuroinflammation, thereby accelerating Tau aggregation, spread, and neurodegeneration [44]. To reconcile these seemingly conflicting results, a recent study proposes that the impact of TREM2 on Tau pathology is highly dependent on both the type of pathology and the disease stage [45].

In our study, TREM2 agonist antibodies have shown limited therapeutic benefit in dual-pathology models where Aβ and Tau coexist. In some cases, they not only failed to improve Tau pathology, Aβ burden, or cognitive performance, but even exacerbated NP-Tau. These findings suggest that, in the complex inflammatory and metabolic environment created by concurrent Aβ and Tau pathology, enhancing TREM2 signaling may be insufficient to restore microglial homeostasis and may instead amplify detrimental inflammatory pathways. This discrepancy highlights the context-dependent nature of TREM2–microglia–AD interactions, which may vary with disease stage [46]. Overall, TREM2 signaling must be precisely tuned rather than simply amplified.

The Regulatory Role of Soluble TREM2 (sTREM2)

The functional landscape of TREM2 is further complicated by its proteolytic cleavage. Beyond membrane-bound TREM2, its soluble form sTREM2, has emerged not merely as a metabolic byproduct of ectodomain shedding, but as a bioactive ligand that critically modulates microglial function with distinct and sometimes opposing effects in a stage-specific manner [47]. The level of sTREM2 in CSF is closely related to the pathological progression of AD. The study found that sTREM2 significantly increased at the stage of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and compared with Aβ lesions, it was more closely associated with neurodegenerative lesions related to Tau protein [48, 49]. In summary, the early increase in sTREM2 indicates the activation of microglia. As AD advances from amyloidosis to tauopathy, the accelerated shedding of TREM2 may reflect a desperate but ultimately dysfunctional compensatory attempt by microglia to combat escalating proteotoxicity.

Mechanistically, sTREM2 exerts multifaceted effects that mirror the complexity of its membrane-bound counterpart [50]. On the one hand, sTREM2 is neuroprotective in early stages: it promotes Aβ clearance via the PI3K/Akt pathway [51] and mitigates Tau hyperphosphorylation by inhibiting the RhoA-ROCK-GSK3β axis [52]. On the other hand, sustained elevation of sTREM2 may exacerbate chronic neuroinflammation via NF-κB activation [53]. Despite ongoing debates regarding its neuroprotective versus neurotoxic effects, sTREM2, alongside TREM2, remains a highly promising candidate for clinical translation and therapeutic innovation in AD.

Limitations and Implication

Despite accumulating evidence supporting the potential effects of TREM2 gain-of-function modulation in AD, several critical limitations must be addressed to bridge the gap between preclinical success and clinical reality.

First, the predominant reliance on Aβ-centric models, such as APP/PS1 or 5XFAD models, oversimplifies the multifactorial nature of human AD. The relative underrepresentation of tauopathy and mixed-pathology models limits the generalizability of findings. Second, although many studies assessed pathological outcomes, only a few systematically evaluated long-term behavioral improvements or the dynamic changes in neuroinflammation. Furthermore, our analysis does not fully account for the nuanced impacts of specific TREM2 variants (R47H or H157Y) on disease phenotypes. Additionally, methodological heterogeneity also poses a significant challenge. Strategies for TREM2 modulation vary markedly, ranging from passive immunization with agonist antibodies to viral vector-mediated overexpression, with substantial disparities in dosage, administration timing, and intervention duration. These inconsistencies are further compounded by fragmented outcome measures, where some studies prioritize plaque density while others focus on soluble oligomer levels, ultimately complicating cross-study comparisons.

The complexity of translating TREM2-targeted therapies is starkly highlighted by the recent top-line results from the Phase-2 randomized trial of TREM2 agonist antibody AL002 in early AD. Despite showing favorable safety and tolerability profiles in preclinical studies and a Phase I clinical trial [16], AL002 failed to meet its primary and secondary endpoints [54]. It indicates that the coexistence of Aβ/Tau in the environment reduces the sensitivity of microglia to TREM2 activation, narrowing the clinical intervention window. Therefore, future immunotherapy should go beyond the single receptor activation model and instead explore combined intervention targeting membrane-bound TREM2 and sTREM2, which may represent a more promising direction for AD immunotherapy.

Conclusion

This review is the first to systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TREM2 gain-of-function modulation across distinct disease stages and models of varying pathological complexity. Our findings validate an early therapeutic window for TREM2-targeted interventions, which demonstrate robust efficacy in single-pathology models but yield limited benefits in mixed-pathology contexts. These findings provide a theoretical framework for stage-specific and precision-based intervention strategies. Future research should aim to define the optimal intervention timing and investigate potential synergistic effects with established disease-modifying therapies to enhance clinical translatability.
