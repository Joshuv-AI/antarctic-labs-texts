BMSC-targeting aptamer conjugation enhances bone delivery of a rice bran peptide to mitigate osteoporosis via eIF3L
Authors: Hao Yin, Teng-Fei Wan, Shan-Shan Rao, Ze-Hui He, Zheng-Zhao Liu, Zhen-Xing Wang, Zhi-Rou Lin, Yi-Yi Wang, Ling Jin, Yi-Wei Liu, Guo-Qiang Zhu, Jiang-Shan Gong, Jia Cao, Zun Wang, Yi-Juan Tan, Yu-Xuan Qian, Xin Wang, Qin-Lu Lin, Ying Liang, Chun-Yuan Chen, Hui Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis is frequently accompanied by a shift in the fate of bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) from osteogenesis toward adipogenesis, contributing to bone-fat imbalance and fragility fractures. Here, we evaluated a naturally occurring eight-amino acid rice bran-derived peptide, RBAP (KHNRGDEF), together with an aptamer-RBAP conjugate designed to improve bone accumulation via a previously identified BMSC-targeting nucleic acid aptamer. In ovariectomized mice, RBAP administered either preventively (early after ovariectomy) or therapeutically (after osteoporotic phenotypes had developed) preserved trabecular microarchitecture, improved mechanical properties, reduced marrow adiposity, and accelerated osteoporotic fracture healing. RBAP also conferred bone-protective effects in models of natural aging and disuse osteoporosis. In vitro, RBAP promoted osteogenic differentiation and suppressed adipogenic differentiation of primary BMSCs without detectably affecting osteoclastogenesis. Proteomic pull-down and functional screening identified eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit L (eIF3L) as a key RBAP-interacting protein, and genetic suppression of eIF3l attenuated RBAP-induced osteogenesis and blunted its bone protective capacity in vivo. Conjugation of RBAP to the BMSC-targeting aptamer increased BMSC uptake and bone tissue accumulation, thereby enhancing its pro-osteogenic and anti-osteoporotic effects at the same administered dose. Our study identifies RBAP as a bioactive peptide candidate for correcting bone-fat imbalance in osteoporosis and support aptamer conjugation as a modular strategy to improve bone delivery.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a worldwide epidemic characterized by systemic bone loss, reduced bone strength, and microarchitectural deterioration arising from uncoupled osteoclastic resorption and osteoblastic bone formation, which increases the risk of fragility fractures [1,2]. This disease primarily affects older adults, especially postmenopausal women, and also occurs under conditions such as skeletal unloading (disuse osteoporosis), excess glucocorticoid use, and inflammatory diseases [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Dysregulated lineage allocation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (BMSCs)—enhanced adipogenesis and/or reduced osteogenesis—contributes to bone–fat imbalance and the development of senile, postmenopausal, and disuse osteoporosis [[7], [8], [9]]. Accordingly, strategies that redirect BMSC fate toward osteoblasts rather than adipocytes show promise for osteoporosis prevention and therapy [[7], [8], [9]].

Bioactive peptides (BPs), short amino-acid chains typically consisting of 2–20 residues, are derived from precursor proteins or generated during food processing and gastrointestinal digestion [[10], [11], [12]]. Due to their small size and sequence-defined binding properties, BPs can interact with target proteins in recipient tissues with high affinity and specificity [13,14]. Numerous BPs have been reported to exert immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, metabolic regulatory, and antioxidant activities, and several endogenous- or food-derived peptides have been linked to bone health [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. We previously identified an eight–amino acid rice bran-derived active peptide, RBAP (also termed KF-8; KHNRGDEF), which attenuates oxidative injury by binding to and inhibiting Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [18,19]. Whether RBAP can remodel BMSC fate and confer benefits in osteoporotic bone remains unknown.

Nucleic acid aptamers (“chemical antibodies”) are single-stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides generated through systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) and capable of binding cellular or molecular targets with high specificity [20,21]. Their small size, low immunogenicity, and chemical versatility make aptamers attractive ligands for targeted delivery of drugs and biomaterials [22]. Our previous studies have identified a BMSC-targeting aptamer that efficiently delivers extracellular vesicles or antagomiRs to BMSCs and enhances bone formation in ovariectomy (OVX)- and aging-induced osteoporosis models [23,24]. These findings provide a rationale for using aptamer conjugation to improve the delivery of short therapeutic peptides to bone marrow BMSCs.

In this study, we report that treatment with RBAP by oral gavage or intravenous route in the early stage can prevent OVX-induced osteoporosis (an animal model of postmenopausal osteoporosis) in mice. We have also determined that RBAP can rescue the osteoporotic phenotypes and accelerate the fracture healing process in mice that estrogen deficiency-related osteoporosis has occurred. Moreover, we have found that RBAP also exerts significant bone-protective effects in mouse models of senile and disuse osteoporosis. Mechanistic studies indicate that RBAP alleviates bone-fat imbalance in osteoporotic mice associated with its direct stimulation of osteogenesis and inhibition of adipogenesis in BMSCs primarily by binding and activation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 subunit L (eIF3L). The conjugation of RBAP with a BMSC-targeting aptamer further increases the capacity of RBAP to target BMSCs and bone tissues, increase bone mass and bone quality, and reverse bone-fat imbalance. Our study suggests the potential of RBAP and BMSC-targeting aptamer-conjugated RBAP (Apt-RBAP) as efficient preventative or therapeutic agents for osteoporosis and osteoporotic fracture.

While previous studies have examined bone-anabolic peptides or BMSC-targeting delivery strategies [[24], [25], [26], [27]], our work is distinguished by the combination of three features: first, we identify RBAP as a minimal food-derived peptide with broad bone-protective activity in multiple osteoporotic and fracture-healing models; second, we define eIF3L as a key intracellular mediator of RBAP-induced regulation of BMSC osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation; and third, we show that conjugation with a BMSC-targeting aptamer further improves RBAP delivery to BMSCs and bone tissue, thereby enhancing its anti-osteoporotic efficacy.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and conjugation of ligands to RBAP

All short-chain peptides used in this study were purchased from GenScript (Nanjing, China), and all nucleotide sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). To enhance the ability of RBAP to target BMSCs, we synthesized a previously screened BMSC-targeting nucleotide aptamer (5′-ACGACGGTGATATGTCAAGGTCG TATGCACGAGTCAGAGG-3′) and conjugated RBAP to this aptamer to obtain Apt-RBAP. RBAP conjugated with a random nucleotide sequence (Rd-RBAP) was prepared as a control. Briefly, the 5′ end of the BMSC-targeting aptamer and the random sequence were modified with reactive DBCO, and the C terminus of RBAP was modified with azide. The DBCO-conjugated aptamer (DBCO-Apt) or random sequence (DBCO-Rd) was linked to N3-RBAP via copper-free click chemistry in three steps. Step 1 (activation of DBCO-Apt/Rd): DBCO-Apt/Rd was dissolved in PBS to 18 nmol mL−1, boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, and then equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min. Step 2 (reaction): N3-RBAP was dissolved in PBS to 12 nmol mL−1, mixed with activated DBCO-Apt/Rd at a 1:1 ratio, and allowed to react on a rotating mixer at 4 °C for 12 h. Step 3 (storage): Apt/Rd-RBAP was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments. To trace the distribution of RBAP and Apt-RBAP in vitro and in vivo, the N-terminus of RBAP was conjugated with Cy7 or FITC and then, with or without the BMSC-targeting aptamer, to obtain Cy7/FITC-Apt-RBAP and Cy7/FITC-RBAP. All reactions were performed at pH 7.4.

Cell culture

Primary BMSCs and BMMs were isolated from the bone marrow of 3-week-old C57BL/6 mice as described in our previous study [9]. BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Solarbio). BMMs were cultured in α-MEM with 10% FBS; Gibco, 1% PS and 50 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; Proteintech). MC3T3-E1 cells (Procell, Wuhan, China) were incubated in high glucose DMEM (Gibco) with 1% PS and 10% FBS. HMECs (ATCC CRL-3243, Rockville, USA) were cultured in MCDB131 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 1% glutamine, 10 ng mL−1 epidermal growth factor (EGF; Peprotech, Rocky Hill, USA), 1% PS, and 10% FBS. LO2 cells (Procell) were incubated in RPMI 1640 medium (Biological Industries, Beit Haemek, Israel) with 10% FBS. MLO-Y4 cells (Procell) were maintained in α-MEM (Hyclone, Logan, USA) with 2.5% FBS, 2.5% calf serum (Gibco), and 1% PS. RAW264.7 cells (ATCC, TIB-71) were incubated in high glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% PS. Cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Verification of aptamer affinity

To validate that the Apt–RBAP conjugates could preferentially target BMSCs, FITC-RBAP or FITC-Apt-RBAP at equimolar RBAP concentrations (100 nM) were incubated with BMSCs at 37 °C for 3 h, respectively. The fluorescence signals of conjugated RBAP were detected using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DM5000B). The suspended BMSCs, MC3T3-E1, HMECs, LO2, MLO-Y4, and RAW264.7 cells were incubated with FITC-RBAP or FITC-Apt-RBAP at equimolar RBAP concentrations (100 nM) to confirm that the aptamers could enhance the ability of RBAP to target BMSCs. Excess conjugates were removed using a 300 × g centrifuge for 5 min. The cells were then examined using a FACSCANTO II (BD Biosciences), and the proportion of fluorescence-positive cells was determined using FlowJo 10.0 software.

Animals and treatments

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (see Ethics statement). Unless otherwise specified, RBAP was administered at 30 mg kg−1 body weight in 200 μL saline according to the schedules described below. The dosing regimen used in the present study was selected based on our previously published RBAP-related work, in which intragastric administration at 30 mg/kg twice per week was used [28]. We included oral administration because RBAP is a food-derived peptide and oral delivery is therefore highly relevant for translational application. Intravenous administration was additionally used as a reference route to assess the efficacy of RBAP under conditions of systemic exposure.

OVX prevention model: Ten-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (22–25 g) underwent bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) or sham surgery as described previously [24,29]. One week after surgery, OVX and sham mice received vehicle (saline) or RBAP by intragastric (i.g.) gavage or intravenous (i.v.) injection twice weekly for 8 weeks. Therapeutic OVX model: Ten-week-old female C57BL/6 mice underwent OVX and were left untreated for 8 weeks to allow osteoporotic phenotypes to develop. Mice then received RBAP by i.g. gavage or i.v. injection twice weekly for 8 weeks. Vehicle-treated sham and OVX mice served as controls. Sequence specificity control: To assess sequence specificity, a random peptide sequence (RPS) with the same amino-acid composition as RBAP but a scrambled sequence was administered intravenously at an equimolar dose, with all other experimental conditions kept identical. Recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH) was administered as a positive control at a dose of 40 μg/kg/day via subcutaneous injection (s.c.) [29]. Osteoporotic fracture healing: Eight weeks after OVX, a standardized femoral midshaft fracture was created (Section 2.5). One week after fracture, mice received vehicle or RBAP twice weekly for 8 weeks via intragastric gavage or intravenous injection. For analyses at defined stages of fracture healing, bone tissues were collected at 2 and 6 weeks after treatment initiation.

Natural aging model: Fifteen-month-old male C57BL/6 mice received vehicle or RBAP via intragastric gavage or intravenous injection twice weekly for 8 weeks. Disuse osteoporosis model: Ten-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were subjected to hindlimb unloading by tail suspension (TS) or hindlimb loading (control) as described previously [30] and received vehicle or RBAP via intragastric gavage or intravenous injection five times per week for 21 days. In the tail suspension model, a higher dosing frequency (five times per week for 21 days) was adopted to match the relatively short duration of the observation period.

Genetic and mechanistic models: To assess whether RBAP exerts bone protection independently of TLR4, 8–10-week-old female Tlr4-knockout mice that had undergone OVX or sham surgery 1 week earlier were treated with saline or RBAP via intravenous injection twice weekly for 8 weeks. To test the role of eIF3L in vivo, an rAAV-mediated inhibition approach was employed. An rAAV9 vector carrying shRNA targeting eIF3l (AAV9-sh-eIF3l #1) was injected into the femur of OVX mice by intramedullary route. One week later, mice received vehicle or RBAP via intravenously twice weekly for 8 weeks.

Apt-RBAP efficacy study: One week after OVX, 10-week-old female C57BL/6 mice received intravenous administration of equimolar aptamer (6 mM; 200 μL), RBAP (6 mM; 200 μL), Rd-RBAP (6 mM; 200 μL), Apt-RBAP (6 mM; 200 μL), or saline (200 μL) twice weekly for 2 months. Sham-operated mice received saline. At the end of treatment, mice were euthanized, and serum and tissues were collected for downstream analyses. Uteri were excised and weighed to confirm OVX success.

All animal procedures conformed to the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0 and ethical standards of Materials Today Bio, with the protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. To minimize selection bias, mice in all models were randomly divided into experimental and control groups using a simple randomization procedure. No pre-specified exclusion criteria were applied, and no animals were excluded from the final analysis. Sample sizes were determined according to well-established experimental paradigms for osteoporosis and fracture models in bone biology, ensuring sufficient biological replicates to support robust statistical analysis and reliable identification of phenotypic and mechanistic changes. Blinding was not applicable during outcome assessment because of distinct group interventions. All quantitative analyses were conducted using standardized protocols to guarantee data objectivity and reproducibility.

Femoral midshaft fracture mouse model

A femoral midshaft fracture model was used to assess the effects of RBAP on osteoporotic fracture repair. Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (0.1 mg/g) and xylazine (0.01 mg/g), which provided approximately 20 min of deep sedation. Analgesia was provided by subcutaneous administration of marcaine (0.002 mg/g) and buprenorphine (0.1 μg/g). The right leg was then shaved and scrubbed with betadine followed by ethanol [31]. Briefly, fallowing skin preparation and sterilization, a ∼5-mm incision was made anterolateral to the patella. The patella was dislocated laterally to expose the joint space and distal femur. A 23-gauge needle was inserted longitudinally through the patellofemoral groove into the medullary canal to serve as an intramedullary pin. A transverse diaphyseal fracture was created at the midshaft using fine scissors. The protruding needle was trimmed and positioned beneath the femoral condyle to maintain patellofemoral joint mobility. The fracture was verified to be neither partial nor comminuted, and the subcutaneous tissue and skin were closed with 4-0 silk sutures. Post-operative analgesia was provided with buprenorphine.

Tissue distribution of RBAP and Apt-RBAP

To determine the tissue distribution of RBAP and the bone-targeting ability of Apt-RBAP in mice after intravenous injection, FITC/cy7-RBAP and FITC/cy7-Apt-RBAP were intravenously injected into the fasted OVX mice. The control mice were treated with 100 μL vehicle (Saline). All mice were killed 4 h after injection. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging (FMT-4000, PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) was performed on the brain, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and lower limb bones (including the femurs and tibias) of mice treated with vehicle, cy7-RBAP, or cy7-Apt-RBAP. The brain, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and femurs from the vehicle-, FITC-RBAP- or FICT-Apt-RBAP-treated mice were fixed for 48 h with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The femurs were decalcified for 72 h with 0.5 M EDTA. Other organs were dehydrated in 30% sucrose and then embedded in OCT. The samples were sectioned to a thickness of 14 μm and stained for nuclei with DAPI.

To examine the dynamic changes of RBAP and Apt-RBAP distribution in bone after administration, the Cy7-labeled RBAP or Apt-RBAP was intravenously into the mice.12 h, 24 h, 3 d, 5 d, and 7 d later, the mice were killed to collect the femurs and tibias, followed by analysis using a near-infrared fluorescence imaging system to measure the radiant efficiency of the Cy7-labeled RBAP or Apt-RBAP. Identical threshold settings and a uniform quantification formula were applied across all groups to calculate the radiant efficiency of each organ, thereby ensuring consistency and comparability of the fluorescence-based measurements. The resulting data were independently verified to improve rigor and reproducibility.

To evaluate the uptake of BMSCs of RBAP and Apt-RBAP under in vivo conditions, the FITC-labeled RBAP and Apt-RBAP were intravenously administered into the Prrx1Cre/+; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato/+ mice, which will express the tdTomato fluorescent protein in cells that have previously or are currently expressing Prrx1. Six hours after the intervention, femurs were collected and subjected to decalcification and sectioning, followed by observation under a fluorescence microscope. Then, the ratio of the tdTomato (Prrx1)- and FITC-positive area within the overall tdTomato-positive area was measured. The images were collected using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Jena, Germany). Image-Pro Plus 6.0 was used to measure the mean intensity.

μCT analysis

The femur samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 days and then scanned by vivaCT80 (SCANCO Medical AG, Bruettisellen, Switzerland) at 70 kV voltage, 400 μA current, 55 kVp X-ray tube potential, 400 ms integration time, and 11.4 μm voxel size. These scanning parameters were kept consistent and standardized across all samples to ensure quantitative reproducibility. Images were reconstructed with the NRecon and displayed with the CTVol v2.3.2. To assess trabecular bone parameters, including Tb. BV/TV, Tb. Th, Tb. N, and Tb. Sp, the regions between 0.45 and 0.90 mm proximal to the growth plate of the distal femurs were chosen. This ROI was consistently applied to all specimens to avoid analytical bias. To evaluate cortical bone parameters, including Ps. Pm, Es. Pm, Cs. Th and closed porosity of cortical bone, the regions comprising 5% of the femoral length in the mid-diaphysis were selected. Uniform ROI positioning was performed for all samples. For fracture callus analysis, μCT scanning was performed under the same conditions, and 600 slices centered on the fracture line were acquired. A fixed threshold of ≥70 was applied to all samples to segment mineralized tissue within the callus. TV, BV, and BV/TV in the callus were then calculated. All analyses were performed using standardized acquisition and quantification procedures, and the results were independently verified to improve rigor and reproducibility.

Measurement of femur biomechanical parameters

Osteoporotic bone strength was assessed by the three-point bending test with the Instron 3343 (Instron, Canton, USA). The femurs have a central loading point, and two fulcrums positioned 8 mm apart. The specimens were loaded at a constant rate of 5 mm min−1 until failure. The ultimate load value (N) was calculated from the load-deformation curves. The fracture-healing quality of the femora was measured by the four-point bending test, as previously described [32]. Briefly, the femora were positioned anterior-to-posterior and 9 mm apart on the lower supporting bars. The distance between the upper bars was 5.5 mm. A steady compression force was applied vertically at a 3 mm min−1 rate until failure occurred. Maximum bending load (N) data were automatically recorded by a computer.

Histological, immunohistochemical and histomorphometric analyses

Femora were dissected, fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, decalcified in 18% EDTA (pH = 7.4) for one week, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. As mentioned previously [30], Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned longitudinally into 5 μm-thick slices for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. OCN and TRAP staining were used to quantify osteoblast and osteoclast numbers on the trabecular bone surface (BS), respectively. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to evaluate eIF3L expression in bone tissues. Perilipin immunofluorescence was applied to assess adipocyte numbers. Positively stained cells were quantified in three random visual fields per section and 4-6 mouse femur samples for each sample. The numbers of osteoblasts (N. OBs/BS/mm) and osteoclasts (N. OCs/BS/mm) per BS and the numbers of adipocytes per area (N. AdCs/Ar/mm2) were calculated. The mean intensity of eIF3L in the bone was measured. Anti-OCN and all secondary antibodies were acquired from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Anti-eIF3L was purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). Anti-perilipin and TRAP staining kit were purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was utilized to assess the morphological changes in bone tissues of each group, while Masson's trichrome and Alcian Blue staining were applied to evaluate collagen deposition and cartilage formation in the fracture tissues, respectively.

To assess dynamic bone formation, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 0.1% calcein (10 mg/kg body weight; Sigma) in PBS at 10 and 3 days before euthanasia. Femora were collected and fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h and then dehydrated in ethanol solutions. The uncalcified bones were embedded using methyl methacrylate. The femur was sectioned into 60 μm slices using a microtome and detected under a Zeiss ApoTome microscope. Three random visual fields per slice and 3-5 femur samples per group were analyzed to determine the label perimeter and inter-label width. Bone formation rate (BFR/BS, mm year−1) and mineral apposition rate (MAR, μm d−1) were measured. The OCN and TRAP staining images were acquired with a digital slice scanner (KF-PRO-040-HI; KFBIO, Zhejiang, China).

ELISA

Serum samples were collected and kept at −80 °C until thawing for analysis. The serum concentrations of OCN and CTX-1 were measured using ELISA kits from Elabscience (Wuhan, China).

Osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation assay

To determine whether RBAP could affect osteogenesis and adipogenesis, primary BMSCs were seeded into 48-well plates at a density of 1-2 × 105 cells per well for osteogenic and adipogenic induction. The culture medium was replaced with fresh osteogenic or adipogenic medium (Cyagen) supplemented with 100 or 300 μg mL−1 of RBAP or vehicle. To compare the effects of RBAP conjugated with either the BMSC-targeting aptamer or a random nucleotide sequence on osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, RBAP, Rd-RBAP, and Apt-RBAP were prepared from the same RBAP stock solution and administered at the same RBAP-equivalent molar concentration (300 μM) across all groups.

The 300 μM free RBAP was approximately equivalent to 300 μg mL−1RBAP, which was comparable to the concentrations used for the other treatment groups in vitro. To confirm the underlying mechanism of the pro-osteogenic and anti-adipogenic effects of RBAP, this peptide was used at the dose of 300 μg mL−1. The induction medium was replaced every other day. After 3 days of differentiation, total RNA was collected from differentiated BMSCs. qRT-PCR was used to determine the expression of a class of genes associated with osteogenesis or adipogenesis. After 7 days of differentiation, the cells were stained with ARS solution (Solarbio) to evaluate matrix mineralization or with ORO solution (Solarbio) to identify the generation of lipid droplets. The images were captured by an inverted microscopy (Nikon) and the ARS+ and ORO+ areas were measured.

Osteoclastic differentiation assay

To determine whether RBAP could impact osteoclastogenesis, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 104 cells per well) or BMMs (8 × 103 cells per well) were used and supplemented with 0, 100, or 300 μg mL−1 RBAP. The culture medium was replaced every two days. After 8 days of differentiation, the cells were stained with TRAP reagents to determine the production of osteoclasts. Osteoclasts were identified as TRAP-positive multinucleated cells with more than three nuclei. The number of osteoclasts in each field was determined.

RNA interference

Tlr4 siRNAs (si-Tlr4 #1, 2, and 3), Tnks1bp1 siRNAs (si-Tnks1bp1 #1, 2, and 3), eIF3b siRNAs (si-eIF3b #1, 2, and 3), Tpm2 siRNAs (si-Tpm2 #1, 2, and 3), eIF3j1 siRNAs (si-eIF3j1 #1, 2, and 3), and eIF3l siRNAs (si-eIF3l #1, 2, and 3) were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) and utilized to interfere with the expression of Tlr4, Tnks1bp1, eIF3b, Tpm2, eIF3j1, and eIF3l in BMSCs, respectively. The universal siRNAs (si-Cont)-transfected cells served as the controls. After 36 h of transfection, total RNA of these cells was collected and processed for qRT-PCR. The siRNAs with the highest inhibitory effects were chosen for further assays. The target sequences of these siRNAs are shown in Table S1.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Fermentas, Burlington, Canada). cDNA was then amplified using an ABI PRISM® 7900HT System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, United States) with FastStart Universal SYBR Premix ExTaqTM II (Takara Biotechnology, Japan). The 2–ΔΔCT method was used to measure relative gene expression and GAPDH was used as a standard internal gene. The qRT-PCR primer sequences are presented in Table S2.

Proteomic analysis

BMSCs pellets in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 1 μg ml−1 leupeptin, 1 μg ml−1 aprotinin, 1 mM Na3PO4,1 mM PMSF, 5 mM NaF, 3 mM Na4P2O4, pH 7.5) were Dounce homogenized and centrifuged at 12000 g and at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was obtained for measuring the protein concentration with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cell lysates containing about 1 mg total protein were incubated with the beads-RBAP complex or beads only at 4 °C overnight and then washed with lysis buffer 3 times. After washing, 100 μL loading buffer was added to the peptides and beads mixtures. Subsequently, the reaction was stopped by heating at 98 °C for 10 min and then the supernatant was collected for western blotting and proteomic analysis. The label-free proteomic analysis was processed by Hangzhou Jingjie PTM BioLab (China) and all analysis procedures were described in our previous study [33]. Briefly, the conjugated proteins by the beads-RBAP complex or beads only were digested, dissolved, nanospray ionized, and then analyzed using tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). The generated MS/MS data were processed using Maxquant search engine (v.1.5.2.8). At the protein and peptide-to-spectrum match (PSM) levels, the false discovery rate was adjusted to < 1%. To annotate all the identified proteins, GO analysis was performed. To further understand the function of the differentially expressed proteins between the RBAP + beads and beads group, functional enrichment analyses based on GO and KEGG pathways were performed.

Western blotting

The protein extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE and deposited to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Immobilon P, Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% milk for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, the membrane was treated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. The protein bands were observed with an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and photographed using the ChemiDoc XRS Plus luminescent image analyzer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). eIF3L antibody was purchased from Bethy Laboratories (A304-753A-T; Montgomery, US). The secondary antibody (1:5000) was acquired from Servicebio.

Predicting the interaction between eIF3L and RBAP

The RBAP-binding region within eIF3L protein was predicted using the PEP-FOLD3 website (http://bioserv.rpbs.univ-paris-diderot.fr/services/PEP-FOLD3).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses of continuous outcome variables were performed using standard parametric tests, including unpaired two-tailed Student's t tests for two-group comparisons and one- or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple-group comparisons. To assess the robustness of the findings, nonparametric sensitivity analyses were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests for two-group comparisons and Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by Dunn's post hoc tests for multi-group comparisons; these analyses yielded consistent results. Formal testing for normality and variance homogeneity was not conducted for all datasets and is noted as a limitation of the study. No data points were excluded unless predefined technical exclusion criteria were met and explicitly stated. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. The symbols "∗", "∗∗", and "∗∗∗" indicated P < 0.05, <0.01, and <0.001, respectively. Detailed sample sizes and statistical tests for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

RBAP protects against estrogen deficiency-induced osteoporosis and stimulates osteoporotic fracture healing

To assess whether RBAP treatment by oral gavage or intravenous route can prevent osteoporosis, 10-week-old female mice were subjected to OVX or Sham operation and then left for one week after surgery, followed by treatment with vehicle or RBAP twice a week for 8 weeks by intragastric (i.g.) or intravenous (i.v.) administration (Fig. 1A). The marked decreases of uterus sizes and weights in OVX + Vehicle (Veh) group compared with Sham + Veh group indicated the success of the surgery (Fig. S1A and S1B). Either intragastric or intravenous administration of RBAP had no notable effects on OVX-induced reductions of uterus sizes and weights (Fig. S1A and S1B). Microcomputed tomography (μCT) analysis revealed that the administration of RBAP by oral route from an early stage markedly blocked trabecular bone loss induced by OVX, as shown by the representative μCT images of distal femurs (Fig. 1B) and the much higher values of trabecular bone volume fraction (Tb. BV/TV) and trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) in OVX + RBAP (i.g.) group compared with OVX + Veh group (Fig. 1C). Treatment with RBAP by intravenous injection not only significantly increased Tb. BV/TV, trabecular number (Tb. N) and Tb. Th in OVX-induced osteoporotic mice, but also resulted in much higher Tb. BV/TV, Tb. N, and periosteal perimeter (Ps. Pm) in sham-operated healthy control mice (Fig. 1B and C). Meanwhile, intravenous RBAP administration, but not oral RBAP administration, notably decreased trabecular separation (Tb. Sp). However, neither oral nor intravenous RBAP administration induced statistically significant changes in endosteal perimeter (Es. Pm) in sham or OVX mice (Fig. 1B and C). Three-point bending test showed that both oral and intravenous administration of RBAP enhanced the femur ultimate load to failure in both Sham and OVX mice, Nevertheless, only intravenous RBAP treatment led to a statistically significant improvement in this bone strength parameter in OVX mice (Fig. 1D). To carefully evaluate the specific bone-protective effects of RBAP, a random peptide sequence (RPS; control) with a same length as RBAP was synthesized, featuring an amino acid sequence randomly arranged and mirroring the quantity and overall composition of RBAP. This was intended to exclude any potential nonspecific effects attributable to random amino acid configurations. In parallel, OVX mice receiving recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH) treatments were established as a positive control group. As indicated in Fig. S2A, a significant increase in body weight was observed in the vehicle-treated OVX mice compared with vehicle-treated Sham mice, while treatment with RBAP, RPS, or rhPTH did not impact the body weight of OVX mice. Consistently, RBAP significantly enhanced Tb. BV/TV, Tb. N, and Tb. Th in the OVX-induced osteoporotic mouse model (Fig. S2B and S2C). However, RPS treatment did not elicit any significant alterations in these parameters in comparison to the vehicle-treated OVX group, as determined by μCT (Fig. S2B and S2C). Additionally, treatment with rhPTH in OVX mice substantially ameliorated both bone mass and microarchitectural parameters, as shown in Fig. S2B and S2C. Three-point bending tests indicated that both RBAP and rhPTH were effective in reversing the compromised bone strength in OVX-induced osteoporotic mice, whereas the mice treated with RPS did not exhibit any noticeable improvement in bone strength, as evidenced in Fig. S2D. H&E staining images revealed that both RBAP and rhPTH significantly increased bone tissue, in contrast to RPS, which had no such effect (Fig. S2E). Collectively, these findings further demonstrate the bone-protective efficacy of RBAP against estrogen deficiency-induced osteoporosis, whereas the random peptide control does not produce detectable protective effects.

Next, we established OVX mouse models and left these mice for 8 weeks. We then tested whether the intragastric or intravenous administration of RBAP two times a week for 8 weeks could induce therapeutic effects in these mice that estrogen deficiency-induced osteoporosis had occurred (Fig. 1E). No significant changes were observed in uterus sizes and weights of OVX mice after RBAP administration (Fig. S1C and S1D). As shown by the μCT images of distal femurs and the quantitative parameters, marked bone loss was observed in the vehicle-treated OVX mice compared with the vehicle-treated Sham mice, while the OVX-induced reductions of Tb. BV/TV and Tb. N were markedly reversed with the intravenous injection of RBAP (Fig. 1F and G). Treatment with RBAP by oral route enhanced Tb. Th and reduced Tb. Sp in OVX mice, but only by trend (Fig. 1F and G). Three-point bending test showed that RBAP induced a marked increase of femur ultimate load once given to OVX mice by intravenous injection but not by oral route (Fig. 1H).

We further assessed the effects of RBAP on fracture healing in OVX-induced osteoporotic mice. 10-week-old female mice received OVX or Sham surgery and then left for 8 weeks before femur fracture (Fig. 1I). One week after fracture, the mice were treated with RBAP twice a week for 6 weeks by intragastric or intravenous route (Fig. 1I). μCT analysis and four-point bending test, respectively, revealed that the intravenous injection of RBAP to these mice remarkably increased the values of callus BV, BV/TV, and the failure load of the femurs, whereas the oral administration of RBAP just induced trend of increases of these three parameters (Fig. 1J–N). H&E staining and μCT analysis confirmed RBAP notably increased bone tissue at the distal femoral epiphysis or callus in various osteoporosis models, but did not show statistically significant differences of cortical thickness (Cs. Th) and closed porosity in both Sham and OVX mice (Fig. S3). Further analysis in different post-fracture stages indicated that OVX reduced failure load and callus BV/TV, which RBAP significantly reversed these changes (Fig. S4A–S4C). Meanwhile, RBAP also improved bone continuity and the deposition of collagen in OVX mice with fracture, and promoted early cartilage formation without impacting later stages, as shown by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E; Fig. S4E), Masson's trichrome (Fig. S4F), and Alcian Blue staining (Fig. S4G–S4I), respectively. These findings, together the results in mice that OVX-induced osteoporosis had occurred, indicate that RBAP at the current dose can rescue osteoporosis and accelerate osteoporotic fracture healing in estrogen-deficient mice after administration by systemic route, but not by oral gavage.

In vitro, RBAP did not affect BMSC proliferation (Fig. 2O). Upon treated with varying concentrations of RBAP in osteogenic or adipogenic induction medium, Alizarin Red S (ARS) and Oil Red O (ORO) staining revealed that RBAP induced a dose-dependent increase of mineralized nodule formation and a decrease of adipocyte formation in BMSCs under respective induction conditions, respectively (Fig. 2P–S). The effects of RBAP on osteoclast formation and resorptive activity were assessed by tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining and measurement of serum C-terminal telopeptides of type I collagen (CTX-1), respectively. As shown in Fig. S7A–S7C, OVX markelly increases osteoclast numbers in the distal femur and serum CTX-1 compared with the Sham group, whereas RBAP treatment either by intragastric or intravenous route exerted no significant effects on these parameters in either Sham or OVX mice. We further assessed the anti-osteoporotic effects of RBAP in mouse models of hind-limb disuse osteoporosis. 10-week-old male mice were subjected to hind-limb unloading by tail suspension (TS) or hind-limb loading (Control) and then treated with RBAP or vehicle five times a week for 21 days (Fig. 3K). N, and the failure load of the femurs (Fig. 3L–N), and a prominent stimulation of increases in the number of osteoblasts (Fig. 3O and Fig. S8B), the serum level of OCN (Fig. 3P), and the values of BFR/BS and MAR (Fig. 3R and S) in TS-treated mice. The proteins specifically detected in RBAP + beads group were then subjected to further bioinformatic analyses. Among them, subcellular localization based on Gene Ontology (GO) annotation predicted the location of 951 proteins (Fig. 5D). Most of them were from nucleus, followed by proteins from cytoplasm. A minority of these proteins were from mitochondria, extracellular, cytoplasm-nucleus, plasma membrane, and others (Fig. 5D). The functions of these RBAP-interacting proteins were annotated by three GO terms. In terms of biological processes, they were mainly involved in cellular process, biological regulation, metabolic process, etc. (Fig. 5E). For cellular component terms, they were the components of cell, intracellular structures, or protein-containing complexes (Fig. 5E). For molecular function terms, most of these proteins showed binding capacities, and then catalytic activity (Fig. 5E). A certain number of proteins were regulators of transcription and molecular function, and some others showed transporter activity, structural molecule activity, or other functions (Fig. 5E). GO biological process enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were mainly involved in the regulation of gene expression, nucleic acid metabolic process, cellular macromolecule biosynthetic process, protein-containing complex assembly, and others (Fig. 5F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these proteins were enriched in pathways including Spliceosome, Ribosome, RNA transport, and others (Fig. 5G). Molecular function enrichment analysis showed that many of these RBAP-interacting proteins were involved in binding to various cellular components (organic cyclic compound, enzyme, RNA, protein-containing complex, etc.) and initiating protein translation (Fig. 5H). These results suggest the wide-ranging influences of RBAP on biological activities of recipient cells. The top 10 most abundant proteins, specifically in RBAP + beads group, were shown in Fig. 5I, among which four in ten proteins were translation initiation factors, suggesting that RBAP may function through these proteins to stimulate or inhibit osteogenesis- or adipogenesis-related protein translation and thereby modulate BMSC differentiation. We next focused on the role of eIF3L in the in vivo anti-osteoporotic effects of RBAP. We first examined eIF3L expression in bone tissues from OVX-, natural aging-, and tail suspension-induced osteoporotic mouse models. Discussion

Current anti-osteoporotic drugs, such as bisphosphonates, teriparatide, romosozumab, denosumab, and raloxifene, mainly exert their effects by inhibiting osteoclastic bone resorption and/or promoting osteoblastic bone formation [30,36]. Although these agents are clinically effective, their long-term use may be limited by adverse effects [30,36], highlighting the need for additional therapeutic options. In recent years, accumulating evidence indicates that aberrant BMSC lineage commitment is a key contributor to osteoporosis [7,24,[37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. In this study, we show that RBAP, a short rice bran-derived peptide, prevents bone loss and improves fracture healing across multiple mouse models of osteoporosis, while primarily promoting osteogenesis and suppressing adipogenesis in BMSCs, with no apparent direct effect on osteoclastogenesis under the tested conditions. Moreover, RBAP conjugated with a BMSC-targeting aptamer (Apt-RBAP) exhibits enhanced bone accumulation and more pronounced bone-protective effects. These findings suggest that RBAP may offer a mechanistically complementary strategy to existing therapies by rebalancing BMSC lineage commitment rather than directly inhibiting bone resorption, and thus may hold potential for combination use with current anti-resorptive or anabolic agents. Longer-term outcomes following RBAP or Apt-RBAP administration—such as bone remodeling quality, re-fracture risk, and maintenance of mechanical properties—remain to be assessed in future studies.

Rice is one of the most widely produced and consumed crops worldwide. Rice bran is the outer layer of brown rice and a major byproduct of rice milling [42]. Researchers in our group has previously identified RBAP, a short BPs derived from rice bran, as a functional component with anti-oxidative and anti-aging properties [18,19]. Compared with traditional medicines, short BPs generally exert more specialized function in the target tissues and are easily utilized and excreted by the body [11]. These features, along with the fact that RBAP is naturally present in rice bran, suggest that it may represent a potentially safer and more biocompatible anti-osteoporotic candidate than current drugs, although this will require further validation. Aptamers represent an attractive drug-delivery strategy, and both aptamers and aptamer-functionalized systems have been widely used to deliver therapeutic agents in various diseases [22,27,43,44]. In this study, we successfully developed a BMSC-targeting aptamer-conjugated RBAP system (Apt-RBAP) and showed that this design enhances RBAP uptake by BMSCs and improves its ability to reverse bone-fat imbalance in osteoporotic mice. The aptamer likely enhances the local interaction between Apt-RBAP and target cells by facilitating BMSC recognition and internalization, rather than serving as a classical tissue-targeting moiety in vivo. This increased binding and uptake may account for the stronger osteogenic and anti-adipogenic effects of Apt-RABP observed in vitro, and may also contribute to its greater bone accumulation and improved anti-osteoporotic efficacy seen in vivo. Of note, RBAP, Rd-RBAP, and Apt-RBAP were administered at equimolar RBAP concentrations. A limitation of this approach is that, due to the conjugation process and the difference in molecular weight between free RBAP and the conjugated constructs, the total mass concentrations of the final formulations were not identical, which should be considered when interpreting the relative efficacy of RBAP versus its conjugated derivatives.

From a translational perspective, several challenges remain before RBAP and Apt-RBAP can advance toward clinical application. Although our findings showed no notable histopathological abnormalities in major organs or obvious changes in hematological, hepatic, or renal parameters, immunogenicity and long-term toxicity were not systematically evaluated in the present study; dedicated long-term safety studies will therefore be necessary. Route of administration is another important translational consideration. We found that orally administered RBAP was less effective than intravenous RBAP at the same administered dose in protecting against osteoporosis, which might be attributable to the well-known limitations of oral peptide delivery, such as gastrointestinal degradation, limited intestinal absorption, and first-pass hepatic metabolism [[45], [46], [47]]. Given the convenience and safety of oral administration, future studies should explore strategies to improve RBAP oral bioavailability, such as dose optimization, enteric coating, permeation enhancers, nanoparticle encapsulation, or chemical modification. Nevertheless, parenteral administration remains a clinically feasible option for selected patient populations. In our study, intravenous RBAP produced significant bone-protective effects without obvious adverse effects under the tested conditions; for patients with poor oral adherence or gastrointestinal conditions that limit oral absorption, injectable administration may therefore represent a practical alternative. Clarifying the biodistribution and pharmacokinetic behavior of RBAP will be essential for optimizing its dosing and delivery strategy. As a peptide therapeutic, RBAP may be susceptible to proteolytic degradation and suboptimal pharmacokinetics, which could limit systemic exposure and in vivo efficacy. Future investigations should systematically evaluate dosing regimens across different routes, pharmacokinetic profiles, repeated-dose safety, metabolic fate, biodistribution, and comparative efficacy in larger animal models. For Apt-RBAP, the modular aptamer–peptide design offers a flexible targeting platform; however, manufacturability, batch-to-batch consistency, and large-scale production will remain important practical considerations.

Translation is primarily regulated at the initiation step, which is mediated by the eukaryotic translation initiation factors (eIFs) and is critical for controlling protein synthesis [[48], [49], [50]]. The eIF3 complex is the largest eIF complex and plays an essential role in 48S initiation complex assembly [49,50]. Beyond its canonical role in translation initiation, eIF3 has also been implicated in the selective activation or repression of specific mRNA translation [[51], [52], [53]] and in diverse biological processes, such as stem cell maintenance and differentiation [49,54,55]. Mechanistically, our study showed that RBAP could interact with eIF3L protein to promote osteogenesis and inhibit adipogenesis in BMSCs, as eIF3l knockdown profoundly blocked the RBAP-induced pro-osteogenic and anti-adipogenic effects on BMSCs. These findings suggest that eIF3L may mediate the translational regulation of lineage-associated factors downstream of RBAP. Since protein translation is crucial for controlling gene expression [49,56], the regulation of osteogenesis- and adipogenesis-related gene expression may also contribute to the regulatory effects of RBAP on BMSC differentiation fate and bone-fat imbalance. Wnt/β-catenin, BMP, and mTOR signaling are well-established pathways governing osteogenic and adipogenic fate decisions in MSCs [[57], [58], [59]]. Wnt signaling promotes osteogenesis and antagonizes adipogenesis, whereas BMP signaling supports osteoblast differentiation; mTOR signaling also integrates anabolic cues and contributes to osteogenic commitment [[57], [58], [59]]. Thus, it is plausible that RBAP exerts its effects through eIF3L-dependent translational control of components within these signaling networks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that RBAP exerts broad anti-osteoporotic effects by promoting osteogenesis and suppressing adipogenesis in BMSCs, thereby improving bone mass, bone quality, and fracture healing in multiple mouse models of osteoporosis. We further show that Apt-RBAP enhances bone and BMSC accumulation and produces more pronounced skeletal benefits than RBAP alone. However, although eIF3L is identified as a key mediator of RBAP action, the downstream eIF3L-regulated translational and lineage-specific pathways remain to be fully elucidated in future studies.
