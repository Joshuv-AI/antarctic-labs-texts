Carbon dots derived from natural medicines: Therapeutic advances and challenges
Authors: Ao Zhang, Yichen Sun, Meng Li, Foxing Tan, Fuqiong Zhou, Jie Ruan, Shijie Zhang, Kang Ding, Tao Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon dots (CDs) are a novel nanomaterial exhibiting exceptional biocompatibility, tunable fluorescence, and versatile functionality, making them a promising candidate in biomedicine. Natural medicines, including plants, animals, minerals, and their extracts, represent ideal precursors for CD synthesis due to their abundance, renewability, and eco-friendliness. This addresses sustainability and environmental concerns associated with conventional chemical precursors. Natural medicine-derived CDs (NM-CDs) feature quasi-spherical core-shell structures, ranging from 1 to 10 nm, with surface hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups enhancing their hydrophilicity and functionality. Their properties can be further modulated by synthesis temperature and heteroatom doping. Critically, NM-CDs combine the pharmacological activities of natural medicines with the unique nanoscale properties of CDs, bridging traditional medicine and modern nanotechnology. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the development of NM-CDs, including green natural precursors, formation mechanisms, and synthesis strategies. It systematically analyzes the structure and properties of NM-CDs, categorizes their therapeutic applications into seven major disease types, and offers a detailed explanation of their fundamental pharmacological mechanisms. Finally, ten key opportunities and challenges for the sustainable development of NM-CDs are highlighted. By integrating efficient resource utilization, green chemistry principles, and clinical demands, NM-CDs exhibit strong potential to advance bio-based nanotherapeutics and enhance the value of natural medicines through modern scientific approaches.Keywords: Natural medicine, Carbon dots, Nanomedicine, Nanotherapeutics, Green chemistry

Introduction

Carbon dots (CDs) are a class of zero-dimensional (0D) carbon-based nanomaterials with diameters less than 10 nm, first discovered by Xu et al. [1] in 2004 during the arc-discharge synthesis of single-walled carbon nanotubes [2,3]. Structurally, CDs typically consist of a core composed of sp2/sp3-hybridized amorphous carbon atoms, surrounded by a shell rich in surface functional groups or polymer chains [4]. Their unique optical properties, bioimaging and biosensing capabilities, size-dependent drug delivery performance, and targeting potential, render them ideal candidates for precision therapy and real-time diagnosis [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. These properties have attracted considerable attention in the biomedical field [9]. However, early CDs were predominantly synthesized using chemical precursors such as citric acid, aniline, and ethanol, alongside other organic solvents [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Such chemically driven syntheses often generate toxic byproducts, thereby limiting the biomedical applicability of these CDs [14]. This limitation has prompted increasing interest in research on green precursors [15,16]. In contrast, CDs synthesized from natural medicines offer an environmentally friendly and sustainable alternative [17].

Natural medicines, comprising plants, animals, minerals, and their extracts, serve as abundant and renewable precursors, imparting enhanced functionality and biocompatibility to CDs [[18], [19], [20]]. In the traditional processing of natural medicines, carbonization is commonly employed to alter the chemical composition and pharmacological properties of raw materials. Studies have shown that carbonization can significantly modify the chemical structure and biological activity of natural medicines, providing a theoretical basis for the synthesis of CDs [21,22]. Nevertheless, carbonized materials and their bioactive constituents remain inadequately characterized, and carbonization practices are still predominantly guided by empirical criteria, without standardized preparation protocols [23]. Accordingly, studying CDs derived from natural medicines as precursors can both refine the theoretical foundation of traditional carbonization processes and expand the exploration of their biomedical applications.

Natural medicine-derived CDs (NM-CDs) combine the pharmacological activities of natural precursors with the physicochemical advantages of nanoscale carbon materials. They exhibit high biocompatibility and low cytotoxicity, and the inherent abundance of bioactive compounds in natural medicines provides a diverse pool of heteroatom doping pathways [24]. Moreover, through π–π stacking or electrostatic interactions, NM-CDs can enhance the water solubility of poorly soluble compounds [25]. During synthesis, NM-CDs can retain the original pharmacological groups while introducing new ones, thus expanding their therapeutic applications [26]. Furthermore, their intrinsic optical properties facilitate the integration of therapy and diagnosis, as well as the real-time visualization of pharmacological mechanisms [27].

Natural medicines represent a vast reservoir of pharmacological resources, yet only a small fraction of their active components has been identified and characterized to date. NM-CDs have recently emerged as a novel class of bioactive components in the context of modern nanomedicine. Thus, integrating fragmented evidence on NM-CDs including precursors, formation mechanisms, synthesis strategies, structural features, physicochemical properties, and applications is essential. This consolidation will help clarify their structure-function relationships, bioactivity, and translational potential. Ultimately, NM-CDs form a critical bridge between natural medicines and nanotechnology, offering a promising pathway for bio-based nanotherapeutics.

Precursors

As the origin of NM-CDs, natural medicine precursors play a fundamental role in governing subsequent formation mechanisms, synthesis strategies, and downstream structural and functional characteristics. Based on whether natural medicines undergo carbonization, NM-CD precursors can be categorized into pre-carbonized precursors and post-carbonized precursors (raw medicines). Pre-carbonized precursors refer to natural medicines that have been subjected to traditional carbonization prior to NM-CD synthesis, whereas post-carbonized precursors are processed with carbonization during the synthesis of NM-CDs. Empirically, pre-carbonized precursors typically exhibit hemostatic effects. In contrast, post-carbonized precursors have a broader spectrum of clinical applications and are used to treat a variety of systemic diseases such as cough, hypertension, and diarrhea, extending far beyond trauma-related hemostasis [[28], [29], [30]].

To date, among documented natural medicines, many of which are cataloged in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), only 105 have been developed into CDs for biomedical applications. Of these, 33 NM-CDs derived from pre-carbonized precursors have been reported, including carbonized ginger, Salvia miltiorrhiza Carbonisata, Zingiberis Rhizoma Carbonisatum (ZRC), and Scutellariae Radix Carbonisatum [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. These NM-CDs not only retain hemostatic properties but also demonstrate efficacy in treating acute organ injury, inflammatory diseases, and autoimmune disorders [[36], [37], [38]] (Table S1). In parallel, 72 NM-CDs derived from raw medicines have been developed, including Rhei Radix et Rhizoma and Glycyrrhizae Radix et Rhizoma (GRR) [39,40]. Reflecting the broad pharmacological activities of their natural medicine precursors, these NM-CDs exhibit therapeutic potential across a wide range of acute and chronic diseases, including infectious and neuropsychiatric disorders [41,42] (Table S1). Although NM-CDs show promise in elucidating the material basis of efficacy and expanding clinical applications, current strategies for precursor selection and functional optimization are largely based on empirical trial-and-error approaches. Artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly recognized as a potential auxiliary tool for precursor screening and rational design [43]. For example, intelligent decoction systems can optimize the decoction process based on the types and dosages of natural medicinal ingredients specified in traditional prescriptions, as well as specific decoction requirements. This ensures optimal preparation of different natural medicines, thereby potentially enhancing the yield of NM-CDs. Additionally, the integration of AI with network pharmacology can facilitate the quantitative evaluation of NM-CD efficacy and the identification of key therapeutic targets and pathways. In the future, the integration of AI with cutting-edge technologies (e.g., single-cell omics, spatial omics, and organoid models) and the development of methodologies aligned with TCM principles will create significant opportunities for the modernization of TCM. This approach is expected to facilitate the transition from empirical medicine to precision medicine. However, the systematic application of AI in synthesis optimization and translational evaluation remains at an early stage and warrants further exploration.

These observations at the precursor level suggest that a comprehensive understanding of NM-CDs requires a systematic examination of the transformation processes occurring during their formation and synthesis. Accordingly, the following section examines the structural construction of NM-CDs from the perspectives of formation mechanisms, synthesis strategies, and structural characteristics.

Formation mechanism, synthesis, and structure

Formation mechanism

The composition of NM-CDs primarily comprises a carbon-based core, heteroatom dopants, surface functional groups, and naturally derived bioactive residues. The carbon core is mainly composed of sp2/sp3-hybridized or amorphous carbon structures formed through the dehydration and aromatization of polysaccharides, flavonoids, and other compounds in natural medicines under high temperatures [26,27]. Endogenous heteroatoms, such as nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus, originating from alkaloids, amino acids, or sulfur-containing compounds, are incorporated into the carbon framework, generating doped states that modulate the electronic structure [8,44]. The surface is enriched with hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and amino (–NH2) groups, formed from incomplete deoxygenation or deamination during carbonization, thereby ensuring good water dispersibility and functionalizability. Some thermally stable natural bioactive compounds, such as saponins and polyphenols, may be physically adsorbed, π–π stacked, or coordinated with metal ions on or within the CDs, retaining intrinsic properties [45].

Regarding the formation mechanism, lower temperatures (usually below 300 °C) favor the fragmentation of natural medicines and condensation into amorphous carbon cores, typically emitting blue fluorescence [46]. In contrast, higher temperatures promote graphitization of the carbon core, inducing red-shifted fluorescence through bandgap narrowing [47]. Endogenous metal ions may further catalyze cross-linking and enhance structural stability [48]. Ultimately, NM-CDs with uniform size, tunable fluorescence, and retained pharmacological activity are obtained, thereby laying a foundation for integrating structure, composition, and function in a wide range of biomedical applications.

Synthesis

Traditional pyrolysis, as recorded in historical sources, generally refers to the empirical carbonization of raw natural medicines through direct thermal decomposition under high temperatures and limited oxygen conditions. However, methods and standards varied across different texts, limiting mechanistic investigation and industrial standardization [49]. The discovery of CDs in 2004 [1] and the subsequent exploration of NM-CDs have catalyzed the development of modern synthesis strategies, enabling more standardized and mechanistically guided preparation approaches.

Modern synthesis of CDs is generally categorized into top-down and bottom-up strategies [50]. Top-down methods involve breaking down larger carbon structures via laser ablation and arc discharge; however, they often result in heterogeneous particle sizes and the generation of undesired by-products. The use of strong acids for surface passivation further limits their biomedical applicability due to environmental and safety concerns [51]. Consequently, electrochemical synthesis has gradually emerged as an alternative to traditional top-down techniques [52] (Fig. 1). In contrast, bottom-up methods, which are more suitable for NM-CDs, have gained prominence. These methods utilize natural medicine precursors enriched with hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups that, under thermal treatment, undergo dehydration and carbonization to form uniform, biocompatible, and stable CDs [26,53]. Among these, high-temperature pyrolysis conceptually corresponds to the carbonization processing of natural medicines, typically applying brief but intense heating under anaerobic conditions [54] (Fig. 1). However, this method often results in low quantum yield (QY) and requires harsh reaction conditions. In comparison, hydrothermal synthesis uses water as the reaction medium, thus eliminating the need for further surface passivation [55] (Fig. 1). It offers higher QY and lower toxicity, making it the most widely adopted and environmentally friendly method, despite its relatively long reaction time and limited control over particle size [27]. The solvothermal method, using organic solvents such as formamide, offers simplicity and low cost but requires extensive purification to remove toxic residues (Fig. 1). Microwave-assisted synthesis enables rapid carbonization within minutes by disrupting chemical bonds using microwave radiation [56] (Fig. 1). It has been successfully employed to prepare CDs from herbal sources, such as Gardenia jasminoides [57]. Although the particle size produced by this method may be less uniform, this limitation can be mitigated through post-processing techniques such as dialysis or chromatography [58,59]. Due to its high efficiency and good reproducibility, microwave-assisted synthesis is considered as a promising approach for the industrial-scale production of NM-CDs. Overall, synthesis strategies and their parameters largely determine the particle size distribution, morphology, and structural uniformity of NM-CDs, thereby influencing their overall structural characteristics.

Synthesis and structure of natural medicine-derived carbon dots (NM-CDs).

Structure

NM-CDs generally exhibit particle sizes ranging from 1 to 10 nm, with the smallest reported size reaching 0.76 nm [[60], [61], [62]]. These nanoparticles display a uniform distribution and a narrow size range, forming spherical or near-spherical nanostructures [63]. Morphologically, NM-CDs possess a core-shell structure consisting of a graphitic carbon nucleus and an amorphous carbon region, though the boundary between these regions is often indistinct [64] (Fig. 1). Through precursor design and controlled synthesis processes, morphologies such as triangular shapes, ribbons, and belts can also be obtained; however, size-dependent quasi-spherical structures remain predominant [[65], [66], [67], [68]]. The particle size and structural characteristics of NM-CDs are typically determined by various physical characterization techniques. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) are commonly employed to directly observe particle size and morphology. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) are utilized to further characterize the crystalline structure, such as lattice parameters. TEM and HRTEM are commonly used to observe particle size, morphology, and lattice fringes, providing evidence of graphitized carbon structures. Core-shell features and surface disorder are often further confirmed by Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Particle size strongly influences bioactivity and barrier penetration, and antibacterial activity generally increases as particle size decreases [69]. In a Taraxacum-derived pyrolysis system, raising the pyrolysis temperature further reduces particle size, reaching a minimum at 400 °C [70]. These CDs also demonstrated the most pronounced reductions in blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cre) concentrations in acute kidney injury (AKI) models. Additionally, small-sized NM-CDs are considered promising for crossing biological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Although studies on the membrane penetration ability of NM-CDs remain limited, several CDs synthesized from chemical precursors have been shown to cross the BBB [71]. For example, CDs synthesized from citric acid and o-phenylenediamine (yellow-emissive CDs (Y-CDs)) can cross the BBB via passive diffusion, owing to their small size (approximately 3 nm) and amphiphilic nature [72]. Other studies have indicated that CDs may conjugate with human transferrin, enabling transcytosis-mediated transport across the BBB [73].

Beyond morphology and size, maintaining the core-shell structure is crucial for the functionality of NM-CDs. This is because the amorphous carbon region of the core-shell structure hosts a variety of surface functional groups that endow NM-CDs with their unique properties [74]. However, under excessively high temperatures or inappropriate synthesis conditions, these functional groups may undergo complete carbonization, resulting in the loss of biological activity. These moieties, typically composed of carbon and oxygen elements, are closely associated with the raw medicines and exhibit chemical inheritance. The abundance and diversity of surface functional groups contribute significantly to the distinct behaviors of NM-CDs [75,76]. The most commonly observed surface functional groups are hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and amino (–NH2) groups [77] (Fig. 1). Hydroxyl groups can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, conferring strong hydrophilicity, which enhances their dispersion in aqueous media and increases the likelihood of interaction with cells, ultimately improving pharmacological efficacy. Carboxyl groups contribute to the water solubility of NM-CDs and enable specific chemical interactions with targeted molecules, expanding their utility as high-sensitivity sensing probes. Amino groups facilitate drug conjugation, enabling targeted drug delivery applications. In addition to these major functional groups, natural medicines rich in proteins, such as human hair, egg yolk, and silkworm cocoons, may introduce heteroatoms (e.g., S and N) during carbonization [[78], [79], [80]]. However, the detailed characterization and functional understanding of these surface groups remain to be further clarified. These structural features form the basis of the physicochemical properties of NM-CDs, enabling them to exhibit distinct optical properties, antioxidant activity, and surface modifiability.

Properties

Optical properties

The optical properties of NM-CDs encompass key parameters such as absorbance, fluorescence, and QY. Most CDs exhibit strong optical absorbance in the ultraviolet (UV) region, ranging from 230 to 320 nm [4]. The absorption peak near 230 nm is typically attributed to π–π∗ transitions of C–C bonds, whereas the peak around 300 nm is ascribed to n–π∗ transitions arising from lone pair electrons or specific surface functional groups [81]. In general, the absorbance of NM-CDs can be modulated by tailoring surface functional groups, tuning internal structural stability, and controlling synthesis temperature [82]. Fluorescence, which varies in intensity and wavelength, is particularly diverse in NM-CDs due to the abundance of surface functional groups that enable multiple emission modes [83]. For example, Poria cocos-derived CDs emit bright blue fluorescence under excitation at 400 nm [84]. In contrast, N-doped CDs (N-CDs) from unripe peach exhibit maximum fluorescence emission at 404 nm under 325 nm excitation [85]. QY is a key parameter for evaluating fluorescence efficiency and plays an essential role in determining the in vivo tracking potential of CDs. CDs synthesized via pyrolysis generally exhibit low QY values, typically below 10% [86]. However, protein-rich natural medicines are particularly effective at enhancing QY [87]. Therefore, protein-rich animal-derived natural medicines may serve as highly promising precursors for the synthesis of high QY NM-CDs in future biomedical applications.

Bioimaging

NM-CDs, owing to their nanoscale size, strong fluorescence, low toxicity, and excellent biocompatibility, have been utilized in both in vitro and in vivo imaging (Fig. 2). Current studies on in vitro imaging mainly focus on cellular imaging. Eucommia ulmoides fruit-derived N-CDs exhibit uniformly distributed intracellular fluorescence with high multicolor emission under blue, green, and red excitation channels [88]. Similarly, MC38 cells co-incubated with lotus seed plumules-derived CDs showed a concentration dependent increase in fluorescence intensity over a range of 50–200 μg/mL [62]. Beyond mammalian cells, NM-CDs have also been employed for microbial imaging. Thota et al. [89] discovered that CDs derived from groundnut shells can penetrate Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BY4742 within 10 min of incubation at room temperature, producing strong fluorescence signals and demonstrating excellent photostability.

Biosensing and bioimaging of natural medicine-derived carbon dots (NM-CDs).

In the field of in vivo imaging, Xue et al. [90] synthesized N-CDs from lychee exocarp and further functionalized their surfaces with transferrin and a photosensitizer. These N-CDs exhibited targeted near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging capabilities, enabling deeper tissue penetration in tumor models. In another study, Zhang et al. [91] utilized hair-derived CDs (HaCDs) and skin-derived CDs (SCDs) for long-term fluorescence imaging in zebrafish, for up to 15 days. Longitudinal zebrafish tracking and quenching assays involving adenosine triphosphate (ATP), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), and Fe3+ suggest that the fluorescence decay of NM-CDs results from metabolic clearance or interactions with endogenous quenchers. Despite these promising advances, the current bioimaging applications of NM-CDs remain largely limited to in vitro cellular imaging. Their limited applications in other biological imaging scenarios highlight significant opportunities for further exploration and development in this field (Fig. 2).

Biosensing

Under the synergistic influence of optical properties and surface functional groups, NM-CDs, in addition to their role in bioimaging, demonstrate broad applicability in biosensing (Fig. 2). Their fluorescence signals can be specifically modulated upon interaction with target molecules, and the presence of surface-bound functional groups enables direct recognition of various analytes without additional chemical modification. This enables NM-CDs to achieve sensitive and selective detection. Overall, NM-CDs have been applied in the detection of biomolecules, pH, pharmaceuticals, and ions. CDs derived from lotus seed plumules have been designed for the selective detection of folic acid [62]. Researchers evaluated their selectivity using a variety of ions, amino acids, and compounds from the vitamin B family. Among these, only folic acid significantly quenched the fluorescence of the CDs, highlighting the remarkable selectivity of NM-CDs in biosensing applications. Poria cocos-derived CDs exhibited a linear fluorescence-pH correlation over the range of pH 4.0−9.0 with stable reversibility over six acidification-deacidification cycles [84]. Amin et al. [92] synthesized N-CDs using date kernel as the precursor. Due to the stronger binding affinity of Fe3+ to the surface functional groups of zoledronic acid compared to those on N-CDs, Fe3+ was displaced from the N-CD surface, thereby switching the fluorescence sensor to “on” for zoledronic acid detection in serum, with a detection limit as low as 0.04 mM. Zhang et al. [93] synthesized boron-doped CDs (B@HRCDs) from herbal residues, achieving high Fe3+ selectivity and sensitivity with a detection limit of 1.08 μM for intracellular applications. Additionally, B@HRCDs demonstrated potential applications in anti-counterfeiting and photocatalytic degradation, contributing to the reutilization of herbal waste resources.

Anti-oxidation

Oxidative stress refers to an imbalance between the oxidative and antioxidant systems in organisms, leading to the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during metabolic processes [94]. At physiological levels, ROS play essential roles in cellular signaling and maintaining homeostasis [95]. However, excessive ROS production disrupts the intrinsic antioxidant defense systems and causes oxidative damage to biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, thereby contributing to inflammation and the progression of various human diseases [96,97]. As carbon-based nanomaterials, NM-CDs have been widely reported to possess strong antioxidant capacity. Specifically, they exhibit enzyme-mimetic activities analogous to those of oxidase (OXD), glucose OXD (GOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [98,99]. Structurally, these antioxidant properties originate from conjugated carbon cores, heteroatom-doped electronic structures, and abundant surface functional groups of NM-CDs, which collectively facilitate electron transfer and efficient ROS scavenging. Numerous studies have experimentally validated the antioxidant activity of NM-CDs. For instance, Chen et al. [100] synthesized CDs from Gardenia fructus, which exhibited potent free radical scavenging activity. Jiang et al. [101] prepared CDs from purple sweet potato (CPP-CDs), which effectively scavenged ROS in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophages. By inhibiting ROS accumulation, CPP-CDs reduced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suppressed pyroptosis, and promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. In an LPS-induced acute inflammatory mouse model, CPP-CDs alleviated tissue damage and improved the survival rate. The underlying mechanisms were associated with the coordinated inhibition of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) signaling pathways, as well as the maintenance of calcium homeostasis. Further evidence was provided by Dong et al. [102], who synthesized enzyme-like CDs (e-CDs) from carmine cochineal. These e-CDs demonstrated strong scavenging activity against multiple free radicals, including hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anion radicals, and 2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate) radical cation (ABTS+·) in vitro, thereby reducing apoptosis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). In vivo, e-CDs decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the serum and tissues of D-galactose-induced aging mice, while enhancing the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, such as glutathione POD (GSH-Px), CAT, and SOD. Consequently, they downregulated the expression of apoptosis-related proteins in the hippocampus and liver, and increased the content of elastic and collagen fibers in the skin.

Easy modification

The synthesis methods of NM-CDs are diverse and preserve the bioactive components and functional groups from precursor materials, facilitating both the synthesis process and subsequent modifications [103,104] (Fig. 1). In general, the modification of CDs can be categorized into three strategies: heteroatom doping, non-covalent coupling, and covalent coupling [58,105]. Currently, research on NM-CD modification primarily focuses on heteroatom doping. This approach can endow CDs with tunable properties and help overcome various limitations in biomedical applications. For instance, Wu et al. [106] employed a one-pot method using Magnetitum and Medicated Leaven, incorporating Fe ions from Magnetitum onto the surface of Medicated Leaven-derived CDs. This modification enhanced the ROS scavenging capacity and exhibited high gastrointestinal stability due to minimal changes in electrochemical potential in digestive fluids. Other studies have demonstrated that Cu doping on the surface of NM-CDs imparts cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects against tumor cells [107]. The non-covalent coupling strategy causes relatively little disruption to the structure of NM-CDs and primarily utilizes non-specific forces such as electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic effects, and hydrogen bonding for surface modification. Covalent coupling involves the formation of covalent bonds between the abundant surface functional groups of NM-CDs and various organic/inorganic molecules, polymers, or nucleic acids. This property can significantly enhance the biocompatibility of NM-CDs and broaden their application scope. Currently, drug delivery systems face several challenges, including in vivo degradation and immune rejection. Surface modification of NM-CDs may provide potential solutions, paving the way for their future applications in drug delivery and targeted therapy.

In addition to molecular and ionic modification strategies, recent studies have explored the hybridization of CDs with porous framework materials such as metal organic frameworks (MOFs), leading to the construction of MOF-CD hybrid systems [108]. In such systems, CDs are typically incorporated into MOF matrices through physical confinement or surface interactions, thereby integrating the photoluminescent properties of CDs with the high surface area and tunable pore structures of MOFs [109]. For example, orange peel-derived CDs have been combined with Cu-MOFs to form hybrid nanomaterials for the fluorescent detection of ascorbic acid, with enhanced sensing performance [110]. Overall, current MOF-CD-related studies remain largely focused on material applications such as sensing and catalysis, while their therapeutic relevance in biomedical applications, particularly for NM-CDs, is still at an exploratory stage.

Precise treatments of NM-CDs in diseases

Building on the preceding discussion of precursors, formation mechanisms, synthesis strategies, structural characteristics, and physicochemical properties, the therapeutic effects of NM-CDs can be more comprehensively understood. In the following section, the therapeutic applications of NM-CDs are summarized across different disease contexts.

Acute organ injury

Acute organ injury remains a major cause of mortality, primarily driven by dysregulated inflammatory responses and inadequate tissue perfusion [111]. Although inflammatory processes are commonly involved, acute organ injury is discussed here as a distinct category based on the primary focus on acute tissue damage and organ dysfunction. It encompasses a range of clinical conditions, including myocardial ischemia, AKI, liver dysfunction, and respiratory failure. NM-CDs have shown unique advantages in the treatment of acute organ injury, offering new avenues for clinical intervention.

Salvia miltiorrhiza-derived CDs (SMC-CDs) exhibit significant efficacy in treating acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [31]. In an isoproterenol-induced AMI model, SMC-CDs enhanced impaired cardiac function, as evidenced by the normalization of the ST segment and improvements in echocardiographic parameters, such as ejection fraction and fractional shortening. Their cardioprotective effects were further demonstrated by reductions in serum biomarkers, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatine kinase (CK), along with a marked decrease in infarct size and myocardial apoptosis. Additionally, SMC-CDs exhibited pronounced antioxidant capacity, evidenced by increased activities of SOD, GSH, CAT, and ATPase, compared with the model group (Fig. 3A) [31]. The same group also reported that CDs derived from Curcumae Radix exhibited similar cardioprotective effects [112].

In addition to alleviating myocardial ischemia, NM-CDs have demonstrated therapeutic potential in ischemic stroke. Zhang et al. [37] isolated CDs from Crinis Carbonisatus (CrCi-CDs) and evaluated their effects in a middle cerebral artery occlusion model. Pretreatment with CrCi-CDs significantly reduced infarct volume and improved neurological deficits, while also decreasing BBB permeability and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). The underlying mechanisms were associated with the inhibition of excitatory neurotransmitters, such as glutamate and aspartate, increased 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) levels, and modulation of excitotoxicity, all while maintaining good biocompatibility. Traumatic brain injury (TBI), another form of severe cerebral damage, often disrupts the tight junctions of the BBB. Inspired by TCM herb pair Taoren and Honghua, Luo et al. [113] synthesized negatively charged CDs (TH-CDs) containing two active medicinal components. In a TBI mouse model, intravenous TH-CDs improved neurological function, reduced cerebral edema and neuronal damage, decreased BBB permeability, and upregulated tight junction proteins, including claudin-5 and zona occludens 1 (ZO-1). These effects may result from electrostatic interactions between TH-CDs and claudin-5, enhancing binding and promoting its expression (Fig. 3B) [113].

In the context of AKI treatment, Wang et al. [70] designed a novel CD derived from Pollen Typhae (PT-CDs), characterized by its ultra-small size, enabling it to cross the glomerular filtration barrier. In a rhabdomyolysis (RM)-induced AKI rat model, PT-CDs significantly alleviated oxidative stress and effectively reduced elevated inflammatory levels resulting from renal parenchymal damage by inhibiting the synthesis and release of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Consequently, BUN and Cre levels in treated rats showed a clear dose-dependent reduction (Fig. 3C) [70]. Moreover, CDs derived from Ziziphi Spinosae Semen demonstrated therapeutic efficacy against sepsis-associated AKI [114]. Their dual protective role involves targeted scavenging of mitochondrial ROS and inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing the release of various inflammatory mediators. CDs derived from Phellodendri Chinensis Cortex (PCC) were also reported to mitigate Deinagkistrodon acutus venom-induced AKI, restoring multiple urinary and serum biochemical parameters and reversing histopathological damage, further expanding the application potential of NM-CDs in detoxification scenarios [115].

Research on NM-CDs in liver and lung injury remains limited, yet promising. CDs derived from Paeoniae Radix Alba exerted hepatoprotective effects by reducing alanine aminotransferase (ALT), AST, total bile acid (TBA), and total bilirubin (TBIL) levels in liver injury models through antioxidative mechanisms [116]. Similarly, Platycodon grandiflorum-derived CDs (PGC-CDs) were proposed to act via combined antioxidative and anti-inflammatory pathways to treat hyperbilirubinemia and prevent hepatic injury (Fig. 3D) [117]. In acute lung injury (ALI), Armeniacae Semen Amarum Carbonisata-derived CDs alleviated LPS-induced pathology by reducing inflammatory infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokines, myeloperoxidase (MPO), and MDA (Fig. 3E) [118]. They also increased SOD and GSH activities, promoting the restoration of alveolar structure.

Overall, NM-CDs exhibit a shared therapeutic mechanism in acute organ injury that centers on the regulation of ROS homeostasis and inflammatory responses (Fig. 3) [31,70,113,117,118]. Across different organ injury models, this pathway further displays distinct characteristics. In myocardial ischemia and AKI models, NM-CDs primarily alleviate cellular damage by mitigating oxidative stress and improving energy metabolism. In models of cerebral ischemia and traumatic brain injury, their therapeutic effects are more closely associated with maintaining BBB integrity and modulating neurotransmitter homeostasis. In contrast, in liver and lung injury models, NM-CDs mainly reduce inflammatory cell infiltration and promote structural tissue recovery. Nevertheless, current studies remain largely focused on the protective effects of NM-CDs in individual organs. Given the close functional interconnections among organs, further exploration of the cross-organ protective mechanisms of NM-CDs is of considerable research and translational value [119].

Endocrine and metabolic disorders

Endocrine and metabolic coordination plays a crucial role in maintaining physiological homeostasis, sustaining energy balance, and regulating key metabolic processes. However, under pathological conditions, endocrine dysregulation can lead to dysfunction of endocrine glands. Such dysfunctions have been shown to affect multiple physiological systems, including reproductive, neurological, and metabolic processes, as well as functional activities, thereby triggering a broad spectrum of endocrine and metabolic diseases [120].

Diabetes

Given the rapidly rising global prevalence of diabetes and the limitations of current therapies, the exploration of NM-CDs as alternative or complementary nanotherapeutic agents has attracted growing attention. Sun et al. [121] were the first to report the hypoglycemic activity of NM-CDs. Jiaosanxian-derived CDs (JSX-CDs) exhibited robust glucose-lowering effects in mice subjected to varying doses of glucose, with a more pronounced hypoglycemic response observed at higher JSX-CD concentrations. Building upon these findings, Lu et al. [122] further explored the underlying mechanisms of glucose reduction induced by Fructus crataegi-derived CDs (CFC-CDs). Lineweaver-Burk analysis showed that CFC-CDs induced an unfolding change in sucrase and increased the α-helix and β-sheet contents of maltase, suggesting non-competitive inhibition of both enzymes. In vivo disaccharide load experiments also confirmed this effect, as CFC-CDs treatment significantly reduced postprandial peak blood glucose levels and the area under the curve (AUC) of blood glucose (Fig. 4A) [122].

Gout

Gout, a form of arthritis characterized by elevated uric acid levels, urate crystal deposition, and inflammation, is closely associated with xanthine OXD (XOD) activity. Puerariae lobatae Radix-derived CDs (PLR-CDs), designed by Wang et al. [123], were shown to reduce XOD activity in vivo, alleviating joint swelling in rats without significantly affecting cell viability. Similarly, Aurantii fructus immaturus carbonisata-derived CDs (AFIC-CDs) exhibited comparable therapeutic effects in animal models (Fig. 4B) [124]. Notably, AFIC-CDs significantly reduced XOD activity in both serum and liver, providing new insights into their underlying mechanisms.

Others

In obesity and lipid metabolism, NM-CDs exhibit multi-pathway regulatory potential. Hawthorn-derived CDs (HCDs) reduced body weight, suppressed hepatic lipid accumulation, and improved glucose tolerance in mice by attenuating systemic inflammation, alleviating oxidative stress, and modulating gut microbiota (Fig. 4C) [125]. Gut microbial profiling further revealed that oral administration of HCDs altered various metabolic pathways associated with obesity and endocrine disorders, suggesting a broad-spectrum regulatory effect.

Although the mechanisms remain unclear, Zingiberis Rhizoma-derived CDs (ZR-CDs) may exert regulatory effects on the reproductive system (Fig. 4D) [126]. Chen et al. [126] reported that ZR-CDs exhibited bidirectional modulation of serum estradiol levels. In a mouse model of exogenous estradiol intervention, ZR-CDs initially accelerated estradiol metabolism in the short term; however, by day 21, ZR-CDs promoted endogenous estradiol production, accompanied by significant increases in follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels. Histopathological analysis ultimately confirmed that ZR-CDs reversed endometrial hyperplasia induced by abnormal exogenous estradiol, offering a novel therapy for endocrine disorders such as hormonal dysregulation and anovulatory menstruation.

Overall, despite differences in regulatory modes across disease models, the therapeutic effects of NM-CDs in endocrine and metabolic disorders are primarily associated with pathways related to glucose metabolism, purine metabolism, and lipid metabolism. In addition, some studies indicate that NM-CDs can participate in hormone regulation and ameliorate corresponding pathological manifestations. These shared functional characteristics provide a foundation for the further investigation and application of NM-CDs in complex metabolic diseases.

Inflammatory and autoimmune diseases

Autoimmune diseases arise from excessive immune responses against self-antigens, leading to tissue damage [127]. Due to their heterogeneous pathogenesis and complex clinical manifestations, the treatment of autoimmune diseases is often palliative [128]. Inflammation, a key driver of both physiological and pathological processes, contributes to immune dysregulation and tissue injury, and is closely associated with the onset and progression of autoimmune diseases [[129], [130], [131]]. NM-CDs have demonstrated unique therapeutic potential in alleviating inflammatory and autoimmune diseases through multiple mechanisms.

Six types of NM-CDs have been reported in the context of inflammatory diseases of the digestive system (e.g., alcoholic gastric ulcers, intestinal inflammation, and hepatic fibrosis). These include: Nelumbinis Rhizomatis Nodus Carbonisata-derived CDs (NRNC-CDs), Radix Sophorae Flavescentis carbonisata-derived CDs (RSFC-CDs), GRR-derived CDs (GRR-CDs), Fuligo Plantae-derived CDs (FP-CDs), charred Atractylodes macrocephala-derived CDs (CAM-CDs), and Curcumae Radix Carbonisata-derived CDs (CRC-CDs) [33,39,[132], [133], [134], [135]]. These NM-CDs suppress inflammatory factors (IFs) and counteract oxidative stress. For instance, NRNC-CDs reduce IL-6 and TNF-α levels, while enhancing SOD, CAT, GSH, and GSH-Px, thereby protecting against alcoholic gastric ulcers. In an alcohol-induced mouse model, FP-CDs alleviated gastric mucosal hemorrhage by activating the extrinsic coagulation pathway and restoring gut microbiota balance. These findings suggest that NM-CDs may overcome the gut microbiota disruption often associated with first-line gastric ulcer therapies. Building on these findings, CAM-CDs showed dual protective mechanisms: they suppressed NF-κB/NLRP3-mediated inflammation, oxidative stress, and gastric acid secretion, while enhancing prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and mucin secretion (Fig. 5A) [135]. Furthermore, NM-CDs also demonstrated protective effects beyond gastric tissues. CRC-CDs alleviated hepatic fibrosis-induced degeneration, necrosis, and inflammatory infiltration in liver tissue. They also significantly reduced serum ALT, AST, and TBIL levels, key markers of liver function. Collectively, these findings indicate that NM-CDs possess restorative capabilities for inflammation-related damage across multiple digestive organs.

Importantly, the anti-inflammatory potential of NM-CDs is not confined to the gastrointestinal system but extends to other non-infectious inflammatory conditions. Wang et al. [78] discovered that mulberry silkworm cocoon-derived CDs (MSC-CDs) exhibited broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory activity. In auricular edema and peritoneal vascular permeability assays, MSC-CDs reduced ear swelling and inhibited Evans blue extravasation in mice. In an LPS-induced sepsis model, MSC-CDs further reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels and alleviated pathological injury in the lungs, intestines, and spleen (Fig. 5B) [78]. Another study showed that NM-CDs could also normalize fever and hypothermia in LPS-challenged mice [136]. Furthermore, Artemisiae Argyi Folium Carbonisata-derived CDs were found to mitigate cold-induced inflammation by reducing IL-1β and TNF-α levels, thereby protecting tissues from damage and preventing frostbite in mouse ears [38].

Ulcerative colitis (UC), as an intersection of gastrointestinal inflammation and autoimmune disorders, is a classic autoimmune disease characterized by chronic and persistent inflammation of the colonic mucosa. CDs extracted from scrambled Coptidis Rhizoma (SCR-CDs) significantly alleviated dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced UC symptoms in mice, including elevated disease activity index (DAI), colon shortening, and histological damage [137]. These protective effects were attributed to their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, modulation of gut microbiota, and upregulation of intestinal tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1. Moreover, hydrophilic groups (e.g., –NH2) in SCR-CDs enhanced their water-binding capacity, contributing to reduced diarrhea symptoms. Zhang et al. [40] found that NM-CDs prepared from Rhei Radix et Rhizoma ameliorated UC symptoms through hemostatic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects. Xia et al. [138] further expanded upon these findings by synthesizing CDs through the co-doping of rhein, extracted from Rhei Radix et Rhizoma and L-arginine (RA-CDs). The resulting RA-CDs exhibited strong red fluorescence emission, enabling simultaneous therapeutic and imaging applications. In vitro, RA-CDs effectively scavenged excessive ROS and enabled fluorescent visualization of inflamed colonic tissue. In vivo, both delayed and preventive treatments of RA-CDs significantly alleviated the severity of UC by reducing intestinal inflammation and restoring intestinal barrier function (Fig. 5C) [138]. Wu et al. [106] also made innovative advances in NM-CD synthesis. By combining magnetite and Medicated Leaven, they fabricated iron-ion-modified CDs (MML-CDs), demonstrating improved gastrointestinal stability and resistance to pH-mediated loss of antioxidant activity.

Beyond UC, NM-CDs exhibit therapeutic potential for a broad range of autoimmune diseases. Allergic rhinitis, a highly prevalent autoimmune condition driven by a type 2 immune response, was shown to be alleviated by ZRC-derived CDs (ZRC-CDs) [32]. Zhou et al. [32] observed reductions in IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and histamine, indicating suppression of type 2 immunity. ZRC-CDs also modulated lipid metabolism and inhibited the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathways, contributing to decreased inflammatory responses. Qiang et al. [139] developed two NM-CDs: safflower-derived CDs (SCQDs) and Angelica sinensis-derived CDs (ACQDs). Both SCQDs and ACQDs improved joint lubrication in rheumatoid arthritis models due to their excellent hydrophilic properties and spherical structures. They also reduced hind limb swelling and levels of IL-1, IL-6, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in rat models (Fig. 5D) [139]. For psoriasis, Zhang et al. [140] synthesized PCC-derived CDs (PCC-CDs), which significantly improved skin appearance, Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scores, and histopathology in an imiquimod-induced mouse model. The therapeutic effect was attributed to inhibition of macrophage M1 polarization and promotion of M2 polarization.

In multiple inflammatory models, NM-CDs alleviate tissue injury by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, limiting inflammatory cell infiltration, and mitigating oxidative stress, with their effects primarily acting at the inflammatory effector stage dominated by innate immune responses. In contrast, in autoimmune diseases such as UC and allergic rhinitis, the regulatory effects of NM-CDs are further directed toward disease specific immune mechanisms, thereby contributing to therapeutic outcomes. Overall, modulating inflammatory signaling and correcting immune imbalance serve as the common mechanistic basis for the therapeutic effects of NM-CDs in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases (Fig. 5) [78,135,138,139].

Cancers

Cancer is a major global public health concern, with nearly 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths reported worldwide in 2022 [141]. Natural medicines have shown potential in the treatment of various malignancies, including lung and ovarian cancers [142,143]. Their applications span prevention, therapy, and the mitigation of chemotherapy and radiotherapy side effects. As a novel form derived from natural medicines, NM-CDs possess a small particle size, abundant surface functional groups, targeted delivery capacity, and drug-loading capability, offering a promising strategy for cancer treatment.

NM-CDs synthesized from raw medicines have demonstrated the ability to induce cancer cell apoptosis through multiple mechanisms. Arkan et al. [144] reported that CDs derived from walnut oil exhibited significant cytotoxicity toward MCF-7 and PC-3 cells, and induced apoptosis by increasing caspase-3 activity. Ginger-derived CDs selectively inhibited HepG2 cells, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.35 mg/mL (Fig. 6A) [145]. In contrast, they exerted low toxicity toward normal mammary epithelial cells and hepatocytes, and exhibited only weak inhibition of other cancer cell lines such as A549, MDA-MB-231, and HeLa. This selectivity may be attributed to enhanced uptake of CDs by HepG2 cells, leading to elevated ROS production and upregulation of p53 expression (Fig. 6A) [145]. In addition to their intrinsic cytotoxicity, NM-CDs can enhance cancer therapy through their surface modifiability and excellent drug-loading capacity. For example, Nemati et al. [107] synthesized Cu,N-doped CDs (Cu, N-CDs) from saffron via a hydrothermal method. Due to Cu-mediated ROS generation through Fenton-like reactions, Cu, N-CDs exhibited greater cytotoxicity than N-CDs or saffron extract. They inhibited HCT-116 and HT-29 cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner, enhanced intracellular ROS accumulation, induced lipotoxicity, and downregulated microRNAs involved in apoptosis suppression (Fig. 6B) [107]. Shao et al. [146] synthesized lycorine-loaded CDs by utilizing mulberry leaves (Morus alba L.) residues and loading the anticancer agent lycorine. These CDs exhibited excellent cytoplasmic imaging capabilities and potent tumoricidal activity in human hepatocellular carcinoma cells.

Moreover, NM-CDs can also alleviate side effects caused by conventional cancer therapies and tumor-associated tissue damage. Radiotherapy is a common treatment modality for head and neck cancers, but osteoradionecrosis is one of its most prevalent complications. Guo et al. [147] synthesized CDs from Lycium barbarum (L. barbarum) via hydrothermal methods. These CDs suppressed cellular senescence through the methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)/CAP-Gly domain-containing linker protein 3 (Clip3) axis in an N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-dependent manner. In vitro, the CDs ameliorated radiation-induced damage and senescence in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), promoted osteogenic differentiation, and inhibited adipogenic differentiation. In vivo, these CDs effectively prevented radiation-induced bone necrosis in rats and enhanced osseointegration of titanium implants (Fig. 6C) [147]. Xu et al. [148] prepared CDs from Ziziphus jujuba (J-CDs), traditionally used for anemia treatment. Similar to erythropoietin (EPO), J-CDs promoted the proliferation of human erythroid progenitors without affecting terminal differentiation and enhanced erythropoiesis in bone marrow and spleen in vivo. Given the known risk of EPO in promoting tumor growth and metastasis in cancer patients, Xu et al. further evaluated J-CDs in vitro and in vivo. The results showed that J-CDs had no significant effects on cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, survival, or STAT3 phosphorylation in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6D) [148].

In addition to NM-CDs synthesized from natural medicines, numerous studies have focused on the preparation of CDs from natural product extracts [149]. Jiang et al. [150] synthesized ginsenoside-based CDs (GS-CDs) and applied them in neuroblastoma models. In vitro, GS-CDs suppressed B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) upregulation, activated the caspase-dependent apoptosis pathway, and increased caspase-3 and caspase-10 expressions. In vivo, treatment with GS-CDs reduced tumor volume, slowed tumor growth, and improved overall vitality (Fig. 6E) [150]. Beyond the caspase pathway, NM-CDs can also target mitochondria. Ben-Zichri et al. [151] reported that resveratrol-derived CDs selectively inhibited cancer cells and colocalized with MitoTracker Orange in SH-SY5Y cells. Further studies showed that resveratrol CDs increased intracellular calcium release, disrupted cytochrome c OXD (COX) activity, and strongly interacted with the mitochondrial membrane, facilitating their uptake. To address the frequent mutation of the switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI/SNF) complex subunit AT-rich interactive domain-containing protein 1A (ARID1A) in ovarian cancer, Kousar et al. [152] first demonstrated that triptolide significantly reduced myelocytomatosis oncogene (MYC) protein levels in ARID1A-deficient mouse models, thereby enhancing drug sensitivity. They subsequently synthesized triptolide-CDs and compared them with free triptolide both in vitro and in vivo. The triptolide-CDs group exhibited markedly reduced cell proliferation and viability, and in vivo imaging revealed significant tumor suppression. In addition to their intrinsic anticancer properties, NM-CDs can act as targeted drug delivery vehicles. Methotrexate (MTX) is a common chemotherapeutic agent, but its cytotoxicity is non-specific. Hsin et al. [153] designed Ligusticum striatum-derived CDs, which formed non-covalent MTX/CD complexes. The drug loading efficiency reached 85.9%, and the release was pH-responsive, with 69% of MTX released at pH 5.0, mimicking the acidic tumor microenvironment. In cellular experiments, MTX/CDs enhanced cellular uptake via improved binding to folate receptors on the cell membrane, showing significantly greater cytotoxicity toward cancer cells than free MTX (Fig. 6F) [153].

NM-CDs have demonstrated diverse therapeutic effects in oncological studies, with their shared mechanisms primarily centered on selective tumor cell damage and modulation of the tumor-associated microenvironment. Across different studies, NM-CDs induce tumor cell apoptosis by interfering with key intracellular signaling pathways and enhance the targeting efficiency of anticancer agents as drug delivery carriers. In addition, some NM-CDs can alleviate tissue damage caused by conventional cancer therapies, thereby expanding their potential applications in comprehensive cancer treatment strategies (Fig. 6) [107,145,147,148,150,153].

Tissue repair and coagulopathies

Carbonized medicines have been extensively used for hemostasis in traditional practice, inspiring studies on the hemostatic properties of NM-CDs. Carbonization often yields porous NM-CD surfaces that physically promote wound adsorption and accelerate hemostasis. In parallel, surface functional groups on NM-CDs can activate plasma coagulation factors and promote the release of pro-coagulant mediators. They exert hemostatic effects through the intrinsic, extrinsic, and common pathways, as well as the fibrinogen system.

NM-CDs synthesized from precursors such as Schizonepetae Herba Carbonisata, Selaginella tamariscina Carbonisata, Cirsii Japonici Herba Carbonisata, Crataegi Fructus, carbonized ginger, egg yolk oil, Phellodendri Cortex Carbonisatus, Pollen Typhae Carbonisata, Cirsium setosum Carbonisata, and Junci Medulla Carbonisata significantly reduced bleeding time in tail or liver hemorrhage models and improved coagulation parameters, including activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), and thrombin time (TT) [34,79,122,[154], [155], [156], [157], [158], [159], [160]]. These CDs activate the fibrinogen system and enhance platelet aggregation, producing a synergistic hemostatic effect through multiple coagulation pathways. Notably, Zhang et al. [35] synthesized Scutellariae Radix Carbonisata-derived CDs (SRC-CDs). In rodent models of blood-heat hemorrhage, SRC-CDs significantly reduced rectal temperature, activated the intrinsic coagulation and fibrinogen systems, and improved coagulation indices, including APTT. Additionally, SRC-CDs inhibited the myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)/NF-κB signaling pathway, resulting in decreased expression of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. CDs derived from carbonized Platycladus orientalis (CPO-CDs) also demonstrated notable hemostatic effects in a blood-heat hemorrhage model [161]. CPO-CDs promoted platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation via sarcoma proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase (Src)/spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) signaling and modulated coagulation pathways by regulating metabolites such as phenylalanine (Fig. 7A) [161]. Sun et al. [162] evaluated the hemostatic efficacy of Schizonepetae Spica Carbonisata-derived CDs (SSC-CDs) in conventional mechanical-injury models and in a Deinagkistrodon acutus venom model. Compared with the venom-only group, SSC-CDs significantly increased platelet counts and decreased hemorrhagic activity (Fig. 7B) [162].

Beyond hemostasis, NM-CDs also demonstrate capabilities in wound repair and bone tissue regeneration. Qi et al. [48] synthesized Fe-doped CDs (Fe-CDs) from Eucommia ulmoides that, together with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), promoted cell migration, tissue repair, and angiogenesis. In vivo, Fe-CDs reduced relative wound area, enhanced fibroblast proliferation, and facilitated collagen deposition. Likewise, Mg-doped CDs (Mg-CDs) prepared from mulberry promoted osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3 cells in a dose-dependent manner [163]. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity increased up to 10-fold compared with the control group, with upregulation of osteogenic genes such as Alp and Runx2 (Fig. 7C) [163]. Chen et al. [46] developed Yam-derived CDs (YAM-CDs), which promoted osteogenesis of BMSCs under LPS-induced inflammatory conditions via upregulation of KDM4B. In a murine cranial defect model, YAM-CDs suppressed TNF-α expression, increased KDM4B and osteocalcin levels, and accelerated bone regeneration, suggesting a promising approach for inflammatory bone disorders (Fig. 7D) [46].

To improve reproducibility and stability, some researchers have synthesized NM-CDs from single bioactive compounds. Zhu et al. [164] prepared tetramethylpyrazine (TMP)-derived CDs (TMP-CDs), which activated coagulation factor VIII, promoted plasminogen activator activity, and enhanced epithelial and fibroblast migration (Fig. 7E) [164]. Fu et al. [165] developed a novel wound dressing composed of glycyrrhizic acid and tryptophan-sorbitol CDs (WS-CDs). This composite reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 expression, accelerated the healing of multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)-infected wounds, and showed no significant systemic toxicity.

Overall, the mechanisms underlying the roles of NM-CDs in hemostasis, wound repair, and tissue regeneration primarily involve the regulation of coagulation, attenuation of inflammatory responses, and promotion of regenerative processes. Specifically, NM-CDs activate the intrinsic, extrinsic, and common coagulation pathways, enhance fibrinogen system activity, and promote platelet aggregation, thereby producing a synergistic hemostatic effect. In addition, their surface functional groups and doped elements confer the capacity to regulate cell migration, osteogenic differentiation, and angiogenesis (Fig. 7) [46,[161], [162], [163], [164]].

Neuropsychiatric disorders

Neuropsychiatric disorders are increasingly prevalent and remain inadequately addressed, and natural medicines have long been documented to exert antidepressant effects [[166], [167], [168]]. Overall, NM-CDs exert therapeutic effects primarily by modulating neurotransmitter levels, attenuating neuroinflammatory responses, and regulating neuroendocrine axis activity. AFIC-CDs alleviated depression behaviors of mice by increasing 5-HT, dopamine (DA), and norepinephrine (NE) (Fig. 8A) [169]. Similar outcomes were observed in the pain-depression dyad model, where AFIC-CDs not only elevated the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (Tph2) messenger RNA (mRNA), but also decreased IL-1β and TNF-α, alleviating inflammation-induced neuronal damage (Fig. 8A) [169]. Chen et al. [41] further demonstrated that Os Draconis-derived CDs (OD-CDs) alleviated anxiety-like behaviors, accompanied by increased locomotor activity in mice. Furthermore, serum 5-HT and NE were elevated, whereas corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and corticosterone (CORT) decreased, indicating modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Fig. 8B) [41]. Similarly, Cui et al. [170] demonstrated that Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat carbonisata-derived CDs (CMRC-CDs) relieved anxiety-like behaviors via HPA axis modulation. Additionally, CMRC-CDs increased γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and concurrently decreased glutamate (Glu), helping restore neurotransmitter balance and regulate synaptic signaling (Fig. 8C) [170]. ZR-CDs further exhibited significant analgesic effects against thermal and chemical nociceptive stimuli, likely via activation of opioid pathways and enhanced neurotransmitter release (Fig. 8D) [171].

In the field of neurodegenerative diseases, several studies have reported CDs synthesized from active constituents of natural medicines and investigated their effects in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Tak et al. [172] reported the synthesis of CDs derived from Clitoria ternatea extract (CT-GQDs), and systematically evaluated their potential therapeutic mechanisms in AD models. The aromatic domains and functional groups on the surface of CT-GQDs enable interactions with β-amyloid (Aβ) monomers and oligomers through hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, thereby interfering with their conformational transition toward β-sheet structures. As a result, CT-GQDs significantly inhibited Aβ aggregation and demonstrated the ability to disassemble preformed Aβ fibrils, effectively reducing neurotoxicity. In addition, CT-GQDs demonstrated notable ROS scavenging activity, which alleviated Aβ-induced oxidative stress and mitigated neuronal damage. Building upon these findings, subsequent studies further explored the construction of CDs from single natural bioactive components for AD intervention. Lim et al. [173] prepared curcumin-derived CDs, which simultaneously suppressed Aβ aggregation and reduced tau hyperphosphorylation, indicating a coordinated regulation of two core pathological pathways in AD. Similarly, Wei et al. [174] reported quercetin-derived CDs that efficiently inhibited Aβ fibrillization, promoted rapid fibril disassembly, scavenged excessive ROS, and enabled fluorescence imaging of Aβ plaques. Notably, this multifunctional nanoplatform significantly extended lifespan in a Caenorhabditis elegans AD model, highlighting its therapeutic and diagnostic potential.

In summary, the therapeutic effects of NM-CDs in neuropsychiatric disorders are reflected in their coordinated regulation of multiple key processes within the central nervous system. These effects primarily involve modulation of neurotransmitter homeostasis, attenuation of neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress responses, regulation of neuroendocrine axis activity, and interference with pathological protein aggregation. Through this integrated mode of action, NM-CDs provide new perspectives for the intervention of complex central nervous system disorders.

Infectious diseases

Infectious diseases caused by bacteria and viruses remain major global health threats. Although antibiotics have substantially reduced bacterial infections, the emergence of antibiotic resistance poses a serious challenge [175]. Meanwhile, highly pathogenic viruses continue to emerge, and novel therapeutics capable of broad-spectrum, multi-target viral inhibition remain limited. Owing to structural features distinct from those of conventional small-molecule drugs, NM-CDs are considered as a promising platform for combating bacterial and viral infections.

Eucommia ulmoides-derived CDs (Fe-CDs) showed POD-like activity, generating hydroxyl radicals to enhance antibacterial and wound healing efficacy [48]. In vitro, the combination of Fe-CDs and H2O2 reduced Escherichia coli (E. coli) viability to below 10%. In vivo, Fe-CDs reduced wound infection, promoted cell migration and angiogenesis, and outperformed vancomycin in accelerating wound healing (Fig. 9A) [48]. To address biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance, Chen et al. [42] developed photodynamic antibacterial CDs (B-M-CDs). These CDs enabled precise targeting of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) biofilms. Additionally, B-M-CDs exhibited a light-triggered “on–off” effect, generating ROS under 420 nm irradiation. Metabolomic analyses indicated that B-M-CDs perturbed amino acid and purine metabolism, as well as electron transport and energy metabolism, which may underlie their antimicrobial activity (Fig. 9B) [42].

Studies on CDs synthesized from active constituents of natural medicines are broader and more in-depth. Lin et al. [176] fabricated curcumin-derived CDs (CPDs) that enhanced the water solubility and biocompatibility of curcumin. CPDs promoted neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation to trap and eliminate MRSA. Preserved functional groups enhanced ROS production, activated tissue factor (TF)/activated factor IIa (FVIIa), and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), effectively controlling MRSA-induced sepsis in mice (Fig. 9C) [176]. Beyond antibacterial effects, NM-CDs have demonstrated strong antiviral properties. Curcumin-derived CDs (Cur-CDs) showed potent activity against enterovirus 71 (EV71), with a half-maximal cytotoxic concentration of <13 μg/mL, representing a 1000-fold improvement over native curcumin (Fig. 9D) [177]. Tong et al. [178] synthesized glycyrrhizic acid-derived CDs (Gly-CDs) effectively inhibited porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), pseudorabies virus (PRV), and porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV). Antiviral activity was attributed to multisite binding to viral surface proteins that blocked entry and replication, together with host-directed effects such as IFN-gene upregulation, ROS suppression, and modulation of restriction factors. Li et al. [179] further showed that Gly-CDs reduced mortality and lung viral titers in influenza A virus-infected mice by inhibiting viral internalization, genomic replication, neuraminidase activity, and associated inflammatory responses (Fig. 9E) [179]. Like influenza viruses, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) is a respiratory pathogen. Triptolide-CDs blocked the interaction between the viral spike protein and the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, inhibiting viral entry at nanomolar concentrations [152].

In antibacterial studies, NM-CDs effectively suppress drug-resistant bacteria and complex infections by generating ROS, disrupting bacterial biofilms, or interfering with key metabolic pathways. In parallel, in the context of viral infections, NM-CDs exhibit antiviral potential by blocking viral entry, inhibiting replication processes, and coordinately modulating host antiviral immune responses. Despite differences in antibacterial and antiviral actions, intervention strategies based on the coordinated regulation of both pathogens and host responses represent a shared mechanistic basis for the anti-infective functions of NM-CDs (Fig. 9) [42,48,176,177,179].

Summary and outlook

Summary

NM-CDs embody a revolutionary integration of sustainable natural medicines and advanced nanotechnology. This review systematically summarizes the historical legacy and modern evolution of NM-CDs, which have diversified from traditional precursors to 105 natural medicines now utilized for NM-CDs. In modern synthesis, hydrothermal processing and microwave-assisted synthesis have replaced empirical carbonization methods, thus enabling tighter particle-size control and improved QY. At the same time, these green and sustainable synthesis routes minimize energy consumption, avoid toxic solvents, and utilize renewable natural medicines. Compared with chemically synthesized CDs, NM-CDs inherently address toxicity limitations of chemically synthesized CDs through green precursors, while their hydrophilic surface groups further improve aqueous dispersibility and biocompatibility. More importantly, the integration of therapeutic functionalities from natural medicines endows NM-CDs with inherent pharmacological potential, bridging material innovation and biological efficacy.

Structurally, NM-CDs typically exhibit quasi-spherical core–shell nanostructures with graphitic carbon cores surrounded by amorphous carbon shells, featuring particle sizes of 1 to 10 nm. Particle size critically influences bioactivity and penetration of biological barriers. Smaller particles (below 3 nm) demonstrate enhanced antibacterial efficacy and improved BBB crossing via passive diffusion [73]. Dedicated studies are needed to validate NM-CD-specific transport pathways. The amorphous shell hosts essential surface functional groups including hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups inherited from natural medicines precursors [25,77]. These oxygen-rich moieties endow themselves with good hydrophilicity through hydrogen bonding, significantly improving aqueous dispersibility and cellular interaction capacity. Carboxyl groups additionally enable targeted molecular sensing, while amino groups support drug conjugation for therapeutic delivery. Synthesis temperature critically impacts functionality, as excessive heat causes surface group carbonization that eliminates bioactivity. Protein rich precursors such as human hair or egg yolk incorporate nitrogen and sulfur heteroatoms during their carbonization process [[78], [79], [80]]. The specific functions of these heteroatoms need further research. Importantly, key parameters such as reaction temperature and carbonization degree critically influence the particle size distribution and surface functional group retention. These structural features, in turn, govern their physicochemical properties, including water solubility, redox activity, surface modifiability, and biological interactions, thereby establishing a direct link between synthesis strategies and functional performance.

Functionally, NM-CDs manifest characteristic optical properties, strong antioxidant capacities, and facile surface modification. They exhibit excellent biocompatibility, supporting cellular and microbial bioimaging as well as in vivo tracking. Their biosensing functionality achieves selective molecular detection and quantitative ion analysis through fluorescence modulation. As carbon-based nanozymes, NM-CDs demonstrate potent free radical scavenging capacity and enzyme-mimetic activities (OXD, GOD, POD, CAT, and SOD) that regulate oxidative stress pathways [98,99]. This anti-oxidative functionality mitigates biomolecular damage and suppresses inflammatory cascades. Concurrently, surface modifications can enhance curative effect through heteroatom doping and enable drug delivery via covalent bonding strategy or electrostatic interaction [107]. However, their applications in targeted drug delivery remain insufficiently explored. Cumulatively, these physicochemical characteristics establish NM-CDs as multifunctional nanomedicine platforms integrating bioimaging, biosensing, antioxidation, and drug delivery.

Therapeutically, NM-CDs demonstrate broad-spectrum efficacy across seven major disease categories: acute organ injury, endocrine/metabolic disorders, inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, cancers, tissue repair and coagulopathies, neuropsychiatric disorders, and infectious diseases. i) NM-CDs protect against acute multi-organ damage through three core mechanisms: enhancing antioxidant defenses, inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines, and repairing tissue barriers [37,70,[113], [114], [115], [116]]. Future studies should shift focus from single-organ protection to in-depth investigation of multi-organ crosstalk mechanisms, optimizing systemic therapeutic strategies. ii) For endocrine/metabolic disorders, NM-CDs therapeutically modulate critical targets including sucrase/maltase for diabetes, XOD for gout, gut microbiota for obesity, and estradiol pathways for reproductive disorders [120,125,180,181]. However, their underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, and applicable disease types are limited. iii) NM-CDs predominantly share common therapeutic mechanisms for both inflammatory/autoimmune diseases and acute organ injury [33,39,[132], [133], [134], [135]]. Specifically, NM-CDs suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, inhibit key signaling pathways (NF-κB/NLRP3/JAK-STAT), and enhance tissue barrier integrity. Optimizing durable therapeutic efficacy of NM-CDs represents a critical innovation frontier in autoimmune disease management. v) Despite significant research attention on cancer therapeutics, direct applications of NM-CDs in tumor treatment remain limited. NM-CDs combat cancers through multiple strategies by directly inducing tumor-selective apoptosis via caspase activation, ROS elevation, and enabling high-capacity drug loading coupled with tumor-targeted release [107,[145], [146], [147]]. vi) Regarding tissue repair and coagulopathies, this stands a hallmark therapeutic domain for NM-CDs, embodying potential clinical applicability. NM-CDs activate coagulation pathways and fibrinogen system to reduce bleeding, while accelerating wound repair and bone regeneration [34,79,122,[154], [155], [156], [157], [158], [159], [160]]. However, from the perspective of clinical translation, the long-term biosafety of NM-CDs requires systematic investigation. vii) Neuropsychiatric disorders represent a growing global health burden with unmet therapeutic needs. NM-CDs have shown efficacy in models of depression, anxiety, and pain through multi-target neuromodulation. They elevate the levels of 5-HT/DA/NE, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, modulate HPA axis, and promote endogenous opioid peptide release [[169], [170], [171]]. Whether the pharmacological mechanisms of NM-CDs and natural medicines in neuropsychiatric disorders are consistent requires further discussion. vii) Antimicrobial activity represents an emerging use of NM-CDs. The antimicrobial activity includes biofilm penetration and ROS generation, alongside viral replication inhibition. Additionally, amino acid and purine metabolism, as well as electron transfer and energy metabolism, may be potential antimicrobial mechanisms [48,[176], [177], [178], [179]]. Future research should emphasize their sustained efficacy against drug-resistant pathogens.

Outlook

Endowed with unique optical properties, favorable biocompatibility, and pharmacologically active molecules enriched on their surface, NM-CDs hold great promise in bioimaging, biosensing, disease treatment, and drug delivery. Nevertheless, their future development path is still full of opportunities and challenges. We have summarized ten future development directions as follows.

i)Expanding the precursor sources of NM-CDs. Reducing production costs and achieving efficient utilization of biological resources are key to green and sustainable development. To further promote environmental sustainability, future research should emphasize the use of renewable, low-impact, and biodegradable natural materials as carbon sources, thereby minimizing ecological footprints during synthesis. Converting natural medicines residues, as well as plants with low pharmacological effects or toxicity, into therapeutic NM-CDs will significantly improve the utilization rate of precursors and create enormous value for society.

ii)Elucidating the structure and photoluminescence mechanism. The multi-component nature of natural medicines leads to complex reactions during synthesis, rendering NM-CDs highly structurally heterogeneous. Their carbon cores and surface functional groups vary substantially with parameters such as temperature and reaction time. Current characterization techniques (e.g., TEM, AFM, and XPS) are inadequate for capturing such fine structural details, resulting in “structural ambiguity”. As photoluminescence strongly depends on structural characteristics, precise regulation of QY and optical properties remains a major challenge.

Cutting-edge techniques (e.g., high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)) are therefore essential for precise identification and quantification of active molecules, which constitute the cornerstone of pharmacological research.

iv)Standardizing the preparation process and focusing on yield improvement. Variations in origin and climate cause quality differences in natural medicines, resulting in significant batch-to-batch variability of NM-CDs when used as precursors. Meanwhile, temperature and reaction time are key factors determining the properties and yield of NM-CDs. Therefore, establishing a quality evaluation system and determining preparation process parameters are essential for industrial production. More importantly, the current lack of a standardized evaluation system, cross-model validation, and unified assessment criteria highlights an urgent need for more rigorous and reproducible frameworks to support the future clinical translation of NM-CDs.

v)Focusing on the in vivo metabolism, distribution, and long-term biosafety of NM-CDs. NM-CDs prepared from different precursors show variations in in vivo metabolism and distribution, and identifying their common patterns across formulation and individuals remains a key objective. Moreover, while NM-CDs have shown good biocompatibility in current short-term cell and animal models, the existing safety evaluations are still insufficient. Their long-term biosafety and potential genotoxicity risks require further research. Additionally, the influence of factors related to synthesis, such as residual organic solvents or carbonization by-products, on long-term biosafety should be carefully assessed.

vi)Improving the material theoretical basis and pharmacological mechanism. In multi-herb formulations containing carbonized medicines, the interactions between NM-CDs and other bioactive components remain to be clarified. When multiple carbonized medicines are included, each may generate distinct CDs with potentially synergistic or antagonistic effects that warrant deeper study. More importantly, how NM-CDs, as newly discovered nano-components derived from TCM, correspond to TCM theories is a new direction for exploring the material theoretical basis and pharmacological mechanism of TCM.

vii)Developing drug-loading tools based on NM-CDs. Currently, research on NM-CDs as drug-loading tools remains limited, possibly owing to challenges in controlling their size uniformity, the complexity of their surface-active components, and their relatively low drug-loading efficiency. However, NM-CDs are easy to prepare, possess inherent pharmacological effects, and exhibit good biocompatibility, making them rare and valuable drug-loading tools. Therefore, improving and enhancing the drug-loading efficiency of NM-CDs is a key challenge that modern nanotechnology needs to overcome.

viii)Preparing NIR-emitting NM-CDs. NIR light can penetrate tissues and is less susceptible to interference. Developing NIR-emitting NM-CDs enables real-time tissue imaging. Given the biological activities of natural medicines and drug-loading capacity of NM-CDs, the fabrication of theranostic nanoformulations based on such materials holds high feasibility. The difficulty in preparing NIR-emitting NM-CDs lies in their rich hydroxyl, carboxyl, and other groups. These groups result in strong hydrophilicity, making it hard to form large conjugated structures. Thus, the emission wavelength is difficult to exceed 650 nm. Currently, introducing heteroatoms (N, S) or surface modification of NM-CDs are common methods. However, such methods must be used on the premise of ensuring low toxicity.

ix)Replacing clinically used carbonized medicines with NM-CDs. Carbonized medicines contain some redundant, ineffective, and even toxic substances. How to discard the dross and retain the essence has always been a focus of current research. NM-CDs are a single nano-component, and most of the ineffective components in their precursors are carbonized during the synthesis process. Moreover, the preparation of carbonized medicines requires a large amount of natural medicines, far exceeding the dosage of NM-CDs. Therefore, synthesizing NM-CDs that retain the active molecules and biological functions of carbonized medicines to the greatest extent for replacing clinically used carbonized medicines represents a leap from ambiguity to precision medicine.

x)AI will transform empirical trial-and-error approaches for NM-CDs by constructing a specialized structure-property-activity-efficacy network. At present, AI-assisted research on CDs remains an emerging and not yet fully mature field, but several research directions have begun to take shape. Existing studies indicate that machine learning can establish correlations between synthesis conditions and QY, thereby partially reducing reliance on empirical trial-and-error approaches [182,183]. In addition, AI has been applied to construct the relationships between structure and property, enabling the prediction of optical behaviors of CDs after synthesis, such as emission color and wavelength [184]. Machine learning methods can also improve the efficiency and accuracy of analyzing the large volumes of dispersed experimental data generated during CDs studies [185]. However, in NM-CDs, the complex composition of precursors, difficulties in effective encoding, limited data standardization, and pronounced batch-to-batch heterogeneity pose challenges for AI-based modeling and analysis [186]. Nevertheless, this direction remains a promising frontier. With the aid of machine learning, the integration of multidimensional experimental and characterization data may facilitate deeper understanding of the relationships among pharmacologically active components, the physicochemical properties of NM-CDs, their bioactivities, and potential clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review establishes NM-CDs as transformative nanotheranostic agents bridging natural medicines and nanomedicine within a green and sustainable framework. We systematically summarized the development of NM-CDs from precursor selection to formation mechanisms and synthesis strategies, and further analyzed their structural features, physicochemical properties, and therapeutic applications across multiple disease domains.

Overall, NM-CDs offer a novel nanoscale perspective that integrates material science with pharmacological activity, breathing new life into traditional therapeutic practices while advancing the frontier of green nanomedicine. Despite these promising advances, further efforts are required to address challenges related to structural heterogeneity, mechanistic elucidation, standardization of preparation, and long-term biosafety. Continued interdisciplinary research will be essential to promote the rational design and future clinical translation of NM-CDs.
