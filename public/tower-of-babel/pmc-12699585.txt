Intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system (ScreenTalk) to improve colorectal cancer screening uptake among first-degree relatives of colorectal cancer patients: study protocol for a cluster-randomised type I hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial
Authors: Shuya Lin, Baojia Luo, Lei Cai, Liangzhi Qiu, Haixia Li, Weiyan Liu, Yichao Luo, Yiwei Yuan, Zhenhui Peng, Yang Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFirst-degree relatives of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients have a twofold to fourfold increased risk of CRC. Tailored communication interventions have shown efficacy in improving their risk awareness and screening participation. While computer-based tailoring systems offer convenience, they often overlook the integration of healthcare professionals’ verbal input, potentially limiting effectiveness and long-term impact. To address this gap, we developed ScreenTalk, an intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system that employs intelligent speech interaction to automate the tailoring process, enhance message credibility and improve scalability within CRC screening workflows.Methods and analysisThis study is a two-arm, cluster-randomised controlled trial with a hybrid type I design involving 314 participants across three tertiary general hospitals in Guangzhou, China. Participants in both groups will receive usual care. Additionally, the intervention group will receive a 1-month tailored intelligent voice-interactive intervention, whereas the control group will receive unrelated health education to control for attention. Screening uptake (primary outcomes) and health beliefs (secondary outcomes) are measured at baseline and at 3 months, 6 months, 9 months and 12 month post the intervention. Implementation outcomes including reach, implementation, adoption and maintenance will be assessed through questionnaire, qualitative interviews and tailored system record.Ethics and disseminationThe trial has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sun Yat-sen University (IRB No. L2024SYSU-HL-054). Information on the purpose and process of this study will be provided to the participants before recruitment, and written signatures will be collected from all participants to ensure their voluntary participation and protection of their rights and privacy.Trial registrationNCT06710860 on ClinicalTrials.gov. Introduction

Among the new cases of malignant tumours in the world in 2020, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranked third (9.6%) in the incidence of all cases and second (9.3%) in the mortality rate.1 CRC poses a significant public health burden in China. According to data released by the National Cancer Center, in 2022, China reported 517 100 new CRC cases (10.72%) and 240000 CRC-related deaths (9.32%).2 Multiple meta-analyses based on case-control and cohort studies have shown that the first-degree relatives (FDRs) of patients with CRC (parents, children or their siblings) have a twofold to fourfold increased risk of developing CRC than the general population.3,6 Early screening in such increased risk groups represents one of the most effective strategies for the prevention and control of CRC. According to the recommendations of international authoritative guidelines,7,10 FDRs of individuals diagnosed with CRC should undergo a colonoscopy at age 40 (or 10 years earlier than the youngest confirmed patient in the family).

Although the colonoscopy has been found to be effective in reducing CRC incidence by 46%–90% and mortality by 46%–68%,711,18 global participation in colonoscopy screening remains low. Only 14%–40% of individuals with at least one FDR diagnosed with CRC are receiving colonoscopy screening.19 In China, the participation rate is even lower. According to the data of CRC screening programmes implemented across various cities, the current colonoscopy screening rate among FDRs of CRC patients in China ranges from 15.6% to 23.55%.20,25 This highlights the urgent need to improve colonoscopy screening uptake among FDRs of CRC patients, which is crucial for the early detection and efficient prevention of CRC.

Tailored communication intervention has been proven as one of the most effective interventions to improve screening uptake.26,32 A key distinction between tailored communication and traditional health education lies in the extensive application of behavioural change theories to guide content personalisation. Among these, the most commonly used theoretical frameworks include the Health Belief Model, Extended Parallel Process Model (EPPM) and Transtheoretical Model.26 33 34 Guided by the theories, tailored intervention customises health information by leveraging a structured framework to identify and address psychosocial determinants that are critical to health behaviour change, such as perceived susceptibility and barriers.35 36 Compared with traditional health education such as mass media campaigns that deliver generalised messages at the group level, the personally relevant messages provided through tailored communication are more likely to be read and cognitively processed, and ultimately enhance the potential for behaviour change.37 38

Tailored communication is a time-intensive and resource-intensive process that involves assessing individual characteristics, selecting characteristic-matched health information, refining messages through tailoring strategies and delivering them via suitable channels.27 36 Although tailored communication has proven effective in encouraging health behaviour change, its widespread implementation remains limited due to operational complexity. With advances in technology, traditional manual tailoring procedures have increasingly been replaced by automated, computer-based systems.39 40 However, these systems often overlook the role of healthcare providers’ voice-based message, which, as demonstrated in our systematic review, is a critical component of effective tailored communication.26 This has led to relatively small effect sizes and unclear long-term outcomes. Therefore, a key challenge is to enhance the credibility and authority of tailored messages through the integration of provider voice cues, while also reducing reliance on labour-intensive interventions. With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), intelligent speech interaction (ISI) technology has emerged as a novel delivery channel for health communication. As one of the most mature and widely adopted applications of AI, voice interaction enables systems to interpret user input through spoken language and provide real-time, context-aware responses.41 42 In the healthcare domain, such technologies have been increasingly used in remote medical consultations, hospital follow-ups, intelligent health management services and innovative elderly care models.4143,49

By employing ISI technology as the delivery channel for tailored communication, the tailored communication intervention system can simulate conversations with screening participants—effectively replacing human providers in conducting individual assessments, tailoring content and disseminating tailored information. This AI-driven approach enables the automatic and rapid matching of risk information based on individual measurement results and preset tailoring algorithms, significantly enhancing the efficiency, scalability and accuracy of personalisation compared with the manual processes employed in prior studies.27 28 Furthermore, using dialogues featuring healthcare professional images, in contrast to simple text-based interfaces, may enhance the perceived authority and trustworthiness of the information source. As a result, it facilitates the broader adoption and implementation of data-informed, user-centred intervention models in cancer screening settings. Building on these advancements, the study developed ScreenTalk, an intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system whose clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes will be evaluated using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework50 51, with the aim of accelerating implementation and promoting broader adoption of ScreenTalk in routine hospital and community screening workflows. Following the evaluation, an implementation strategy for the evaluation phase will be established to prepare for long-term implementation.

Methods

Study design

This study will adopt a hybrid type I effectiveness-implementation trial design, which is appropriate when the clinical effectiveness of an intervention has not yet been established. In this design, the primary focus is on determining whether the intervention produces the intended clinical outcomes, while simultaneously gathering prospective data on contextual factors that may influence future implementation.52 53 A cluster-randomised controlled trial (C-RCT) will be conducted to examine the effectiveness of the intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system (figure 1), while both qualitative and quantitative methods will be employed to evaluate implementation outcomes and determine the factors that may influence implementation process. The study protocol was pre-registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06710860) on 26 November 2024. The recruitment of this study was started on 1 January 2025 and the tentative end date of the recruitment is 30 June 2026. Data collection is expected to be completed in June 2027. This protocol was written according to the Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) statement,54 the SPIRIT-AI extension55 and the Reporting Standards of Tailored Interventions.56 The schedule of enrolment, interventions and assessments for SPIRIT is presented in figure 2.

Study settings

This study will be conducted in the departments of colorectal and gastrointestinal surgery of three tertiary public hospitals in Guangzhou, China, comprising the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, from January 2025 to June 2026 (Registration No. NCT06710860). The participating hospitals, all specialising in gastrointestinal tumours, host approximately 150 inpatient beds within the study departments, configured in wards ranging from single to six beds.

Participants

FDRs of patients with CRC will be recruited by using cluster sampling, with the hospital ward serving as the unit of randomisation. All FDRs of CRC patients hospitalised within the same ward will be allocated collectively to either the intervention or control group, thereby preventing contamination. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) age 40–75 years or 10 years before the age at which the relative was diagnosed, (2) have not had a colonoscopy within 5 years, (3) access to digital devices themselves or through close family members living in the same household and (4) able to read and speak Chinese. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) have a history of cancer or inflammatory bowel disease, (2) have a history of hereditary syndromes (Lynch syndrome or familial adenomatous polyposis), (3) recently participate in another study or previously participated in cancer risk counselling or study and (4) with doctor-diagnosed psychiatric illness. The medical history information related to exclusion criteria of the potential participants is mainly obtained through their self-reporting.

Sample size

The sample size estimation was derived from a C-RCT design,57 incorporating an intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a corresponding individually randomised trial.58 The sample size was calculated at the ward level to ensure sufficient power for the primary outcome of CRC screening uptake. Based on the previous study,28 the required total sample size of 254 was calculated under individual randomisation. An ICC of 0.013, calculated from data on FDRs (N=85) across the study settings, was used to account for clustering effects. The ICC was calculated using the formula for binary outcomes (ICC = σ²u / (σ²u + π²/3)),59 where the between-cluster variance (σ²u) was derived from a generalised linear mixed-effects model. The average cluster size, defined as the number of FDRs accessible within each ward, was approximately three per ward. Using the sample size calculation formulas,58 at least 105 clusters are needed, across 314 participants, to be recruited to allow for a 20% loss to follow-up, with a power of 80% and an alpha of 0.05.

Randomisation and blinding

This study will employ a stratified block randomisation design, with FDRs from the same ward assigned to the same group to minimise the risk of contamination. Stratification will be based on the proportion of single-person, double-person and multi-person wards. Within each stratum, wards will be randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio, with block sizes ranging from four to eight to prevent prediction of allocation. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician, who is not involved in the trial. Rerandomisation will occur once all patients in a ward are discharged. A ward will be excluded from rerandomisation if, during the recruitment process, it contains no eligible patients (eg, patients with gastric conditions) or all eligible FDRs of CRC patients decline participation. Under these circumstances, the ward will maintain its original group assignment until new eligible participants are recruited. Allocation will be concealed in opaque, sealed envelopes, sequentially numbered and randomly selected by researchers before recruitment. The original randomisation sequences will be kept by an independent researcher and remain inaccessible to investigators. To avoid a biased estimate of the treatment effect, an assessor-blinded approach will be implemented for outcome collection. An online survey will be conducted, and participants will submit the online questionnaires to the web server directly when they finish the assessment and the researchers will not influence the assessment. Following data collection, all identifying information linked to group assignment will be removed from the dataset before it is provided to an independent statistician to ensure the analyst will remain blinded to group allocation.

Intervention

The intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system (ScreenTalk) was developed as the platform for intervention implementation and follow-up. The system comprises two portals, with the FDRs’ portal incorporating three core modules (figure 3): (1) a measurement module, assessing tailoring variables and grouping FDRs according to decision rules; (2) an algorithmic tailoring module, providing customised information processed by communication appeals and tailored strategies based on different behaviour change techniques (BCTs); (3) an open dialogue module, supporting FDRs to have free dialogue with voice assistants. All participants will be required to complete the three modules within 1 month. If the intervention group does not undergo CRC screening at the follow-up time point, the system will repeatedly provide tailored communication intervention to further enhance the CRC risk perception of FDRs based on the current evaluation results. Built on the ISI, ScreenTalk employs speech synthesis and recognition to deliver tailored messages and facilitate user interaction. ISI supports real-time text-to-speech conversion with customisable dialects (Cantonese and Mandarin) and speech rates, while also enabling voice input in the open dialogue module. A printed manual guiding participants on how to use ScreenTalk will be provided to the intervention group. The main user interface of the ScreenTalk is shown in figure 4.

Guided by the EPPM,60 the measurement and message tailoring components of ScreenTalk assess and respond to FDRs’ perceived threat and efficacy levels. EPPM posits that individuals evaluate fear-based messages through dual risk appraisals—threat and efficacy—which together determine whether they engage in protective or avoidant responses.61 By categorising FDRs into four risk response categories based on the perception of threat and efficacy, the system delivers targeted messages to either elevate risk perception or strengthen efficacy beliefs, aiming to shift individuals toward a high-threat, high-efficacy state that is most conducive to behaviour change. Additionally, perceived barriers were incorporated to address intention-behaviour gaps and identify actionable obstacles to CRC screening uptake.2634 62,64 The theoretical framework guiding the intervention design is illustrated in figure 5.

Measurement module

Perceived threat, perceived efficacy and perceived barriers of FDRs to CRC and CRC screening will be measured by the Colorectal Cancer and Colonoscopy Screening Health Beliefs Scale for First-Degree Relatives (CCHBS-FDR) instrument,65 the Four-item Simplified Chinese Version of the Self-efficacy Questionnaire66 and the Cue to Action for CRC Screening Questionnaire,28 respectively. Participants can select either audio playback or text reading, with the system matching tailored messages to their score upon completion. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1=Strongly Disagree to 5=Strongly Agree) using a positive questioning format, on which basis scores below 3 indicated disagreement with the item and a low level of perception, whereas scores above 3 indicated agreement and a high level of perception. This tailoring algorithm was constructed through a systematic review26 and validated in previous studies.27 Perceived threat and perceived efficacy will be calculated as the average scores of their respective dimensions. Therefore, decision rules which used a threshold of 3 will be applied to categorise FDRs into low (≤3) or high (>3) groups: (1) activated intenders (high threat and high efficacy), (2) refusers (high threat and low efficacy), (3) relaxed intenders (low threat and high efficacy) and (4) no response (low threat and low efficacy). As perceived barriers vary among individuals, the decision rules will tailor the top three barrier items based on participant selection.

Algorithmic tailoring module

The algorithmic tailoring module customises messages for FDRs based on their assessment results, following authoritative guidelines.78 10 13,15 67 In constructing the messages, a combination of behaviour change techniques, communication appeals and tailoring strategies was employed, supported by two rounds of expert consultation (N=10). First, the risk appraisal characteristics of FDRs—grouping results of the measurement module—determine which BCTs are adopted.76 Based on this, different types of information are selected to address various communication appeals. These include logical information, which provides objective facts and data from reliable sources,77 78 and emotional information, which uses vivid, personal content to evoke emotional responses.79 All professional information is then processed through tailored strategies79 to enhance its relevance to individuals and increase the likelihood of message acceptance. The methods applied to process the tailored messages received by each group are shown in online supplemental appendix A. In addition to textual content, FDRs can choose audio playback for all information and will also receive a picture summary, which includes a review of their family history, screening recommendations and tailored messages.

Open dialogue module

The open dialogue module allows FDRs to ask additional questions beyond the tailored messages, either through voice input or text entry. The initial Q&A database was created from three sources: (1) a questionnaire survey (N=68) from Guangzhou Disease Control and Prevention Center’s CRC screening programme, identifying 16 common questions; (2) 100 internet consultation records from a professional medical health platform (Dingxiang Doctor), generating 14 questions; and (3) ChatGPT-4o simulation, adding one question based on a prompt about FDRs’ concerns regarding CRC and colonoscopy. After eliminating duplicates, the final database included 9 questions from the survey, 7 from the consultations and 1 from ChatGPT, totalling 24 questions. Responses were based on clinical guidelines and research,713 68 70 72 74 80,83 and the database was reviewed by a panel of experts for relevance, accuracy, readability and significance.

The system uses Retrieval Augmented Generation with a large language model and a Q&A database. Due to WeChat restrictions, the Baichuan2-Turbo model is used for real-time Q&A. The Q&A database was refined in several ways: (1) adding emotional support information to the response ensures the message’s emotional appeal79 (eg, “You can definitely do these things, and you can better protect your health”) and (2) responses were improved with the GPT-4o model for clarity and the database was expanded by splitting questions and increasing phrasing variation, growing from 24 to 52 Q&A pairs. Baichuan Text Embeddings were used to convert questions into digital representations for comparison. When a user asks a question, it is embedded and compared with the database, with a 90% similarity threshold for a match. If no match is found, the top three most similar Q&A pairs are used as prompts for the model to generate a response. Critically, all AI-generated responses will be prioritised in conjunction with authoritative guidelines, structured databases and Q&A databases uploaded in advance by the research team, thereby reducing the possibility of transmitting incorrect information. A manual oversight procedure will be implemented by the research team to monitor the AI-driven communication. For successfully completed conversations, a periodic review of the chat records will be conducted. If the voice assistant fails to resolve the user’s query, the manual consultation function will be activated, and the healthcare professionals will provide accurate and relevant responses through the healthcare professionals’ portal. Any instances of misinformation or new questions identified will be used to update and expand the Q&A database. In this module, the original voice tone is an electronically synthesised Mandarin female voice, and users can adjust the speech rate (0.5×, 1.0×, 1.5×, 2.0×) and language (Cantonese/Mandarin).

The healthcare professional portal

To facilitate remote intervention, we developed a healthcare professional portal in ScreenTalk for progress monitoring. Key features include viewing and exporting participant data and responding to manual consultations. Researchers can monitor the intervention status of each participant in real time and intervene promptly on any unfinished tasks to ensure the continued effectiveness of the intervention. The manual consultation reply function will allow researchers to review and respond to questions that the open dialogue module is unable to address. The replies generated by the research team will be provided to participants after clinical professionals assess their accuracy.

Attention control group

Participants in both groups will receive standard usual care, which consists of an e-brochure provided through WeChat, containing information on CRC and CRC screening. The standard information provided in the e-brochure included the following: (1) CRC-related knowledge (risk factors, causes, symptoms) and (2) CRC screening-related knowledge (importance of screening, screening options, screening recommendations). To control for the benefits of attention that may come from behavioural interventions, participants in the attention control groups will be provided with extra health education material, ensuring they receive social attention equivalent in dose and timing to that provided by the intervention.84 This extra health education material focused specifically on how to care for CRC patients, including preoperative physical and respiratory exercises, postoperative early activity guidance and dietary guidance.

Quality control of the intervention

To facilitate the implementation and management of this complex intervention, the study team will conduct a unified training for the intervention implementers from three hospitals before the start of the intervention. Standardised communication templates for each step (including recruitment, reminders for completion and follow-up) will be provided, along with ScreenTalk operation manual and recruitment support materials, to ensure protocol fidelity. The intervention workflow, target population, implementation timeline, follow-up plan and structured qualitative interview outlines are summarised in a printed document to ensure all healthcare providers strictly adhere to the intervention protocol during implementation. Our team will weekly monitor the delivery of the intervention through the tailored communication system record and provide timely feedback to support continuous quality improvement.

Data collection

Participants’ data will be collected at five time points: baseline (T0), 3 months post intervention (T1), 6 months post intervention (T2), 9 months post intervention (T3) and 12 months post intervention (T4). After obtaining written informed consent, participants will be asked to complete the CCHBS-FDR, the Self-Efficacy Questionnaire, the Cues to Action Questionnaire and the General Information Questionnaire through WeChat (T0). The intervention group will use ScreenTalk’s measurement module, whereas the control group will complete the assessment using an online tool (WenJuanXing). To distinguish participant data, each FDR in the intervention group will log in to ScreenTalk with a unique research identifer (ID) provided by the investigator. At T1, T2, T3 and T4, both groups will be required to complete the CCHBS-FDR, the Self-Efficacy Questionnaire and the Cues to Action Questionnaires, and the uptake of CRC screening will be measured by collecting verified medical records at each follow-up time point. Any participant who has not completed the questionnaire within 1 week will receive a text reminder via WeChat from the researcher. If the questionnaire remains incomplete after 3 days, the researcher will follow up with a phone call to remind the participant to finish it. All data from the intervention group—including personal information, intervention completion status, evaluation results and usage records—will be encrypted, with access strictly limited to authorised research personnel. During transmission, all data will be protected by the Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS) and WebSocket Secere (WSS) encryption protocols. For storage, sensitive files such as CRC screening medical records will be securely maintained in an independent cloud-based Object Storage Service. To further safeguard privacy, each user will be assigned a unique and time-limited identification token, and strict access control mechanisms will be enforced to ensure users can only access their own data, thereby minimising the risk of data leakage. We strictly adhere to the provisions of China’s Personal Information Protection Law, ensuring that all data processing activities comply with China’s personal information protection and cybersecurity requirements.85 Data from the control group is available on the WenJuanXing platform. All data will be processed anonymously. Data from the ScreenTalk and WenJuanXing platforms will be accessible only to researchers with specific login credentials. Paper records, including consent forms, will be stored in locked filing cabinets, accessible only to authorised researchers.

To measure implementation outcomes, we refer to the RE-AIM framework and draw on qualitative interviews and establish an implementation logbook, which records detailed information on the recruitment, the completion status of each module and the usage frequency of ScreenTalk.

Outcomes

Participant characteristics

A general information questionnaire developed by the research team will be used to gather information about age, sex, marital status, educational level, monthly household income, religion and CRC family history.

Primary outcome

At 3 months post intervention (T1), 6 months post intervention (T2), 9 months post intervention (T3) and 12 months post intervention (T4), the participants will be asked if they had undergone a colonoscopy or faecal test. Medical records will be collected from those who had undergone colonoscopy or faecal test. Only the examination records that are verified by medical records will be counted and involved in the data analysis. Participants who self-reported completion but lacked medical records will be considered non-adherent in the data analysis.

We will measure the following health beliefs in both intervention and control groups:

Perceived beliefs of CRC and colonoscopy screening: the CCHBS-FDR,65 with six dimensions, will be used to measure perceived beliefs of CRC and colonoscopy screening, namely perceived susceptibility of CRC, perceived severity of CRC, perceived barriers (priority, concerns and knowledge) and perceived benefits at T0, T1, T2, T3 and T4. Each item is rated on a 5-point (1–5) Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), with scores ranging from 36 to 180. Higher scores on each dimension indicate higher levels of perception. This 36-item instrument has been validated in a sample of FDRs of patients with CRC and it demonstrated acceptable reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.689 to 0.939.

Implementation outcome

Implementation outcomes will be evaluated based on the RE-AIM framework.50 51 The evaluation plan includes both quantitative and qualitative measures, as shown in online supplemental appendix B.

Pilot study

A pilot study (N=40) will be conducted before the implementation of the full-scale study to examine the feasibility of the intervention, ScreenTalk’s technical performance, user engagement and user experience at 1 month post intervention. The technical performance will be evaluated based on response time, voice recognition accuracy and system stability, using WeChat records and user feedback questionnaires. User engagement will be measured by frequency and duration, while user experience will include usability, emotional responses, satisfaction and overall impressions. User satisfaction with ScreenTalk’s interface, content and functionality will be assessed via the User Feedback Questionnaire. Feedback on technical issues and suggestions for improvements will be gathered through in-depth reviews and focus groups.

Data analysis

Data analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS statistics (V.25), with a test level of α=0.05 (two-sided test), and p<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The data analysis process will follow the intention-to-treat analysis principle. This study employed a C-RCT design, where observations within the same cluster may be correlated. If standard statistical methods for individual randomisation are applied, the significance of the intervention effect could be exaggerated.87 Therefore, the statistical analysis methods used in C-RCTs need to take into account the intracluster correlation coefficient. Studies have shown that when the number of clusters ≥15, reliable results can be obtained by using individual-level statistical analysis.57 It was calculated that the total number of clusters required in this study was 105, so this study intends to use individual-level statistical methods to complete data analysis.

Continuous data will be described using mean and SD, while categorical data will be expressed as frequencies and percentages. Independent t-tests will be used for continuous data, with non-normally distributed continuous data analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data will be analysed using the χ2 test. A p value of 0.10 will be used to identify covariates from the baseline difference. If unbalanced results are found in baseline between intervention and control groups, binary logistic regression will be applied to control the covariate. To examine the impact of the intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system interventions at different time points on the psychosocial variables of FDRs (perceived threat, perceived efficacy, perceived barriers and cues to action), a generalised estimating equation model will be employed. The qualitative data will be analysed by an inductive thematic analysis.88 All interviews will be recorded and transcribed truthfully after the respondents are informed, and the relevant meeting recordings and documents will be regarded as original data.

Ethics and dissemination

Data collection commenced after obtaining ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Sun Yat-sen University (IRB No. L2024SYSU-HL-054). Information on the purpose and process of this study will be provided to the participants before recruitment. Data collection will be conducted through ScreenTalk or an online platform (WenJuanXing), and written signatures will be collected from all participants to ensure their voluntary participation and protection of their rights and privacy. The individuals invited to participate in the study will assure that their participation is voluntary and they could withdraw from the study at any time. As approved by the Ethics Committee, to minimise loss to follow-up, after participants finished the online questionnaire assessment at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months after the intervention, they can receive a total of CNY 140 in transportation and data subsidies. Participants will receive CNY 10, 20, 30 and 40 upon completion of each follow-up, respectively. Furthermore, CNY 40 will be provided as a bonus to those who complete the CRC screening. This incremental compensation is expected to have a positive effect on reducing the drop-out rate. Given that the amount is low relative to the cost of the colonoscopy (approximately CNY 1000), it is not expected to be a major factor in a participant’s decision. The Ethics Committee considered the incentive level reasonable and not mandatory. Participants will be assured of the confidentiality of personal information and the security of data. Results of the study will be disseminated via presentations at conferences and publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

Ensuring adherence to CRC screening among increased-risk populations is an essential prerequisite for improving screening effectiveness and efficiency. FDRs of CRC patients, identified as individuals at increased risk, have been targeted in numerous intervention studies aiming to enhance their screening behaviours.2628,30 32 Despite these efforts, adherence and sustained compliance among this population remain suboptimal,19,25 highlighting the need for more effective intervention strategies.

Tailored health communication—an approach that customises health messages based on individuals’ unique characteristics—has shown promise as an effective pathway to increase screening compliance,2633 89,91 including colonoscopy adherence. However, traditional methods for delivering tailored interventions, such as printed materials or telephone follow-up, often require significant resources, extensive staff involvement and complex operational procedures (eg, targeted assessment, selection of appropriate information and tailored message formulation). Moreover, the important role of voice information from healthcare providers in improving the authority and reliability of risk information was frequently underestimated and overlooked. These limitations hinder the scalability and real-world implementation of tailored interventions.

To overcome these challenges, this study proposes the development and assessment of an intelligent voice-interactive tailored communication system, in which virtual healthcare providers can efficiently assess individual characteristics, select suitable information and deliver customised health messages without intensive human intervention. This approach not only enhances the authoritative credibility of the information source but also enables more efficient and precise customisation of health-related messages. Nevertheless, empirical evidence regarding the effectiveness of intelligent speech interaction system in tailored health communication remains scarce. Therefore, this study aims to provide robust evidence on whether intelligent speech-based tailored communication can effectively promote long-term adherence to CRC screening among FDRs of CRC patients.

Beyond evaluating effectiveness—including both short-term outcomes and long-term sustainability—the successful implementation and integration of evidence-based screening interventions into routine clinical practice remain crucial. To address this, the current study adopts an effectiveness-implementation hybrid type I design. This innovative approach simultaneously evaluates the clinical effectiveness of the intelligent speech intervention and systematically identifies key implementation outcomes influencing its adoption, integration and sustainability in real-world healthcare settings.52 By integrating implementation science methodologies with effectiveness evaluation, this hybrid design helps ensure that the proposed intervention maintains its efficacy when scaled up and facilitates its translation into everyday clinical practice.

Ultimately, this research will contribute essential evidence to the literature on innovative health communication strategies, particularly those employing ISI technology. Its findings will inform future endeavours in optimising and successfully implementing tailored screening interventions for CRC and potentially other CRC programmes targeting high-risk populations.
