Correlation between optic nerve sheath diameter measured by bedside ultrasound and intracranial pressure in neurologically ill patients in a Chinese population
Authors: Xiuli Zhang, Dandan Ma, Wenqiang Li, Jinluan Ma, Kexia Bi, Yuling Qiao, Zhen Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe assessed the correlation between optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) values measured by bedside ultrasound and intracranial pressure (ICP) changes among patients under neurocritical care and evaluated the diagnostic performance of ONSD for increased ICP.MethodsSixty-seven neurologically critical patients who were hospitalised in the intensive care unit (ICU) of Jining No.1 People’s Hospital between September 2023 and March 2024 and underwent lumbar puncture were included. The ONSD was measured and recorded using bedside ultrasound before the lumbar puncture. Patients were divided into normal and increased ICP groups on the basis of the initial lumbar puncture pressure on admission, and both groups were compared. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for evaluating the correlation between ONSD values and ICP. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed for evaluating the diagnostic performance of ONSD for increased ICP.ResultAt admission, the Glasgow Coma Scale scores of patients in the increased ICP group were significantly lower than those of patients in the normal ICP group (P < 0.05). The ONSD level of patients in the increased ICP group was significantly higher than that of patients in the normal ICP group (P < 0.05). Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that ONSD positively correlated with ICP among patients with severe neurological diseases (r = 0.777, P < 0.001). The area under the ROC curve when using ONSD for diagnosing lumbar puncture opening pressure ≥ 200 mmH2O was 0.896 (95% confidence interval, 0.817–0.974). When using ONSD ≥ 4.74 mm as the threshold for diagnosing lumbar puncture opening pressure ≥ 200 mmH2O, the sensitivity and specificity were 0.909 and 0.765, respectively.ConclusionIn patients with critical neurological illness, ONSD measured using bedside ultrasound positively correlated with ICP. Increased ICP can be diagnosed for ONSD ≥ 4.74 mm. The ONSD value measured by bedside ultrasound can be used for evaluating ICP among patients with critical neurological illness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-024-03961-0.

Introduction

Neurologically critical illnesses are common across critical care medicine departments. The common conditions include cerebral haemorrhage, cerebral infarction, subarachnoid haemorrhage, intracranial infection, tumours, craniocerebral trauma, and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Typically, these serious conditions present with sudden onset and rapid progression and are characterised by extremely high mortality and disability rates. Early brain injury among critically ill patients is closely associated with increased intracranial pressure (ICP), which can cause coma, induce brain herniation, and lead to cerebral blood perfusion disorders and brain damage that are detrimental to patient survival and prognosis [1]. Hence, identifying the risk factors that influence increased ICP, early detection, and timely monitoring of intracranial hypertension are of great importance for improving the prognosis of neurologically critical patients [2].

Lumbar puncture and intraventricular manometry are common methods for monitoring ICP. Both are traumatic, difficult to perform, and may cause complications such as infection and bleeding [3, 4]. As a result, the clinical applications are restricted. Therefore, there is a critical need for a non-invasive, effective, and easy-to-operate method for detecting ICP in clinical practice. The optic nerve is an important component of the central nervous system. It is surrounded by the optic nerve sheath, which comprises the dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater, and is connected to the subarachnoid space. For patients with intracranial hypertension, cerebrospinal fluid flows into the subarachnoid space around the optic nerve along the pressure gradient difference, causing optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) expansion [5, 6]. A significant linear correlation has been reported between increases in ONSD and ICP [7]. Currently, bedside ultrasound measurements of ONSD are utilised in clinical studies for identifying patients with intracranial hypertension [8]. The ultrasound measurement of ONSD is a non-invasive method for monitoring ICP and has the advantages of portability and high repeatability [9]. Currently, few studies exist on the correlation between bedside ultrasound monitoring of ONSD and ICP among critically ill patients in critical care medicine departments in Chinese hospitals, and the critical value for estimating ICP increase remains unclear. The primary objectives of this study are as follows: (1) to use bedside ultrasound to monitor the ONSD and analyse its correlation with ICP; and (2) to establish the optimal ONSD threshold for predicting elevated ICP in adults in China.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was conducted in the intensive care unit (ICU) of Jining No.1 People’s Hospital. We recruited consecutive patients who were neurocritically ill and underwent lumbar puncture for diagnostic and treatment purposes between September 2023 and March 2024 (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (KYLL-202308-150), and informed consent was obtained from either the patient or their adult caregivers prior to enrolment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of lumbar puncture for diagnostic and treatment purposes; (2) duration of ICU stay ≥ 48 h; (3) age ≥ 14 years; and (4) consent provided by the patient or their adult caregivers.

The exclusion criteria were (1) eye injury, glaucoma, optic nerve tumours, or other eye diseases; and (2) non-cooperation with this study.

Instruments and method

Instrument

A SonoScape ST-190 (Guangdong, China) colour Doppler ultrasonic diagnostic instrument with linear array probe and frequency 5–10 MHz was used.

ONSD measurement and ICP detection

The patient lay in the supine position with eyes closed, and a sterile patch was applied. A coupling agent was applied to the probe and placed lightly on the eyelids to prevent pressure on the eyeballs. The mechanical index (MI < 0.23) was lowered [10], probe was tilted slightly, and scanning direction was divided into transverse and sagittal planes. During the transverse examination, the ultrasound probe was placed horizontally on the patient’s closed upper eyelid. For the sagittal examination, the probe was positioned vertically on the patient’s closed upper eyelid to obtain the optimal image of the optic nerve sheath (Fig. 2). The ONSD was measured 3 mm posterior to the optic disc on both sides [11]. Two measurements were taken for each eye, one horizontally and one vertically, resulting in four values per patient. To minimise variability, a second physician repeated the measurements for each eye, yielding a total of eight values. The final ONSD measurement for each patient was calculated as the average of these eight values [8]. To reduce the inter-examiner variability, all ONSD measurements were only performed by a total of three ICU physicians experienced in the use of bedside ultrasound devices and trained in ultrasound techniques. For each examination, the measurements were performed by two of them [12]. The ONSD measurements were performed prior to the lumbar puncture. The interval between the two examinations was less than 20 min. Lumbar puncture was performed by a critical care physician in strict accordance with hospital standard methods, and the ICP of each patient was recorded.

Grouping and data collection

Based on previous research [13], patients with lumbar puncture opening pressure of ≥ 200 mmH2O and 80–200 mmH2O were included in the increased and normal ICP groups, respectively. Factors that may be associated with an increased ICP include sex, age, Glasgow coma scale (GCS) [14], Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score [15], ONSD value, duration of ICU stay, mechanical ventilation time, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, smoking history, and drinking history.

Statistical methods

SPSS27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software was used for data processing. Quantitative data for normal distributions were expressed as x̄ ± s. A paired t-test was conducted for comparisons between groups. Non-normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as M(P25,P75). Non-parametric Mann-Whitney test was used for comparison between groups. Noncontinuous variables were expressed as n (%), and chi-square tests were performed. Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to perform correlation analysis between ONSD and ICP, with adjustment for covariates (sex, age, GCS, APACHE II score, and other factors). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed for evaluating the diagnostic performance of ONSD for increased ICP, and the Youden index (YI) was calculated to determine the optimal critical value of ONSD for increased ICP. For all statistical analysis results, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of clinical data between the two groups of patients

We enrolled 67 patients (43 men and 24 women), including 28 cases of cerebral haemorrhage, 7 cases of subarachnoid haemorrhage, 5 cases of cerebral infarction, 14 cases of craniocerebral trauma, and 10 cases of intracranial infection.

Among these, 34 patients (21 men and 13 women) were classified into the normal ICP group. Their mean lumbar puncture opening pressure was 138.38 ± 34.44 mmH2O. The remaining 33 patients (22 men and 11 women) were classified into the increased ICP group. Their mean lumbar puncture opening pressure was 264.39 ± 44.44 mmH2O. The comparison of general data between these two groups is summarised in Table 1. Significant differences were observed in the ONSD and GCS scores. The ONSD in the increased ICP group (5.24 ± 0.47 mm) was significantly greater than that in the normal ICP group (4.58 ± 0.24 mm, P < 0.001).

Correlation between ONSD and ICP

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that ONSD values were positively correlated to ICP in patients who were neurocritically ill (r = 0.777, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Efficiency of ONSD in diagnosing increased ICP

In this study, the lumbar puncture opening pressure ≥ 200 mmH2O was used as the state variable for increased ICP, and the ONSD value was used as the test variable. ROC curve analysis was conducted, and the YI was calculated. The maximum YI value was 0.6738, and it was concluded that the best critical value for ONSD for evaluating ICP increases was 4.74 mm. The area under the ROC curve was 0.896 (95% confidence interval, 0.817–0.974), sensitivity was 90.9%, and specificity was 76.5% (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated a positive correlation between ONSD and ICP, indicating that ONSD measurement via bedside ultrasound can be a reliable method for predicting elevated ICP and assessing ICP levels at an early stage. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [16, 17]. Early and timely detection of increased ICP is crucial, as it enables clinicians to accurately evaluate the patient’s condition and pursue more proactive surgical interventions when necessary, potentially reducing the duration of ICU stay and mechanical ventilation and improving patient outcomes [18].

Although invasive ICP monitoring remains the gold standard for diagnosing elevated ICP, its clinical application is limited owing to its complexity, invasiveness, and high cost. In this study, we employed transorbital ultrasound, a non-invasive ICP monitoring technique, for ONSD measurement. This method is easy to operate, cost-effective, and highly reproducible, making it increasingly popular in clinical research and practice in recent years [19, 20].

The results of previous studies varied with respect to the optimal critical ONSD value for identifying increased ICP. Most studies in Western countries recommended an ONSD of > 5.0 mm as the standard for increased ICP [21]. In a Chinese study, Wang et al. [8] indicated that the optimal critical value was an ONSD of > 4.1 mm. The differences may be attributed to the differences in criticality, race, and measurement time of the enrolled patients. Our results indicate that the optimal critical value for ONSD for evaluating increased ICP is 4.74 mm, with a sensitivity of 90.9% and specificity of 76.5%. Critical ONSD values can be used clinically to assist doctors in quick and real-time assessment of patients’ ICP, providing a basis for formulating treatment plans.

This study also has the following limitations: (1) The sample size was small. (2) ONSD and lumbar puncture measurements of ICP were studied and analysed, but continuous ICP monitoring methods, such as ICP monitors, were not included for comparative analysis. (3) We did not consider the impact of pathology on ICP. In future research, it is necessary to increase the number of patients and incorporate multiple factors for further analysis.

Conclusions

Bedside ultrasound measurement of the ONSD is not only convenient and fast but can also provide real-time assessment of increased ICP in critically ill patients. Our results showed a high correlation between ONSD and ICP. The optimal critical value of ONSD ultrasound measurements for evaluating increased ICP was 4.74 mm. It is important to note that ONSD does not substitute invasive ICP monitoring. However, it may be used to guide the decision to monitor ICP invasively and, whenever invasive monitoring is not possible, for example due to lack of resources in middle-to-low-income countries, ONSD could act as a surrogate measure.
