A Multidisciplinary, Digitally-Integrated Life-Cycle Management Model for Early Gastrointestinal Cancer: Enhancing Care Coordination and Continuity
Authors: Rui Yuan, Hui Gong, Chuanjun Tang, Bing Hu, Jinhang Gao, Hao Wu, Wenjuan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe a multidisciplinary, digitally integrated life-cycle management framework for early gastrointestinal (GI) cancer and present its development and preliminary implementation experience within a high-volume tertiary center.MethodsThis narrative review synthesizes published evidence, clinical guidelines, policy documents, and preliminary institutional implementation experience related to multidisciplinary coordination, digital infrastructure, risk stratification, and longitudinal follow-up.ResultsThe proposed framework integrates AI-driven risk stratification, standardized endoscopic treatment, follow-up and data-driven monitoring through a coordinated Physician–Nurse–Administrator team and a “One-System, Three-Platforms” infrastructure. Preliminary implementation experience from colorectal polyp management suggested improved operational indicators, including patient preparation, follow-up continuity, resource utilization, and satisfaction.ConclusionThis framework offers a practical approach to coordinating screening, treatment, and longitudinal follow-up in early GI cancer care. Its clinical effectiveness, long-term impact, and scalability require prospective multicenter evaluation.Keywords: early gastrointestinal cancer, life-cycle management, physician-nurse-administrator integration, multidisciplinary collaboration, digital health

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers represent a considerable global health burden, approximately 26% of all cancer cases and 35% of all cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 By 2025, the United States is projected to record 362,200 new cases of GI cancer and 174,520 related deaths. East Asia bears a particularly high burden of gastrointestinal cancers, especially stomach, liver, oesophageal, and gallbladder cancers, compared with Western regions.2,3 Projections suggest that by 2040, the worldwide incidence of GI malignancies will escalate to 7.5 million new cases, resulting in around 5.6 million fatalities.3 Despite the availability of effective diagnostic tools such as endoscopic chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies,4 a large proportion of GI cancers, especially in high-burden regions like China, continue to be diagnosed at advanced stages, where prognosis is poor.5

While early-stage tumors exhibit five-year survival rates of 70–90%, this figure declines sharply to ≤20% in advanced disease.6 In contrast, endoscopy-assisted early diagnosis and treatment can improve five-year survival to 80–95%.7 This striking survival gap highlights that early detection and timely intervention, rather than escalation of late-stage therapy, remain the most effective strategies for improving outcomes at the population level.

Early-stage GI cancers, however, are often asymptomatic or present with nonspecific symptoms, resulting in a high likelihood of missed diagnosis.8 Consequently, over 80% of gastric cancer (GC) cases in China—accounting for 42% of the global burden—are diagnosed at an advanced stage.3 Importantly, the persistence of late-stage diagnosis cannot be fully explained by limitations in diagnostic technology alone. This disparity highlights a systemic failure beyond technological limitations, rooted in the fragmented, episodic, and reactive nature of conventional care models that lack continuity from risk assessment through long-term surveillance.

In response, China’s national policies, as outlined by the State Council and the National Health Commission (NHC), advocate for a comprehensive, whole-cycle healthcare system that emphasizes shifting from disease-centered to people-centered care and establishing a continuous healthcare service system.9 Similar concepts have also been increasingly emphasized in international health system reforms, particularly for chronic and oncologic diseases that require long-term management rather than isolated clinical encounters. Synthesizing these concepts, this review proposes a novel Integrated Life-Cycle Management Framework for early GI cancer.

Rather than focusing on isolated diagnostic or therapeutic advances, this framework conceptualizes early GI cancer as a longitudinal condition that requires coordinated management across the entire care continuum. We delineate its foundational pillars—a synergistic “Multidisciplinary Team Plus” (MDT+) model and a unified digital health infrastructure—and illustrate its practical implementation. Drawing on published literature, clinical guidelines, and real-world practice from a high-volume tertiary center in China, this narrative review aims to synthesize existing evidence and implementation experience, with the goal of preventing malignant progression at its earliest stage and advancing toward population-wide, life-cycle GI cancer management.

Methodology

This review adopts a narrative synthesis approach to comprehensively examine the development and implementation of an integrated life-cycle management model for early GI cancer. Literature identification was guided by the authors’ direct clinical and administrative experience at West China Hospital, combined with expertise in GI oncology and healthcare delivery innovation. We conducted iterative, exploratory searches across multiple databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, and CNKI, and consulted key clinical guidelines from international endoscopy societies (eg, ESGE) as well as policy documents from the Chinese National Health Commission. Emphasis was placed on frequently cited and high-impact studies in early GI cancer detection, endoscopic treatment, multidisciplinary care coordination, digital health integration, and patient-centered outcomes, supplemented by recent literature in implementation science and health systems research. While this non-systematic methodology may introduce selection bias and does not purport to exhaustively capture all relevant evidence, it affords the flexibility to integrate diverse sources of data, including clinical pathways, hospital-based implementation experiences, technological innovations (eg, AI in screening and follow-up), and health system policy documents. The narrative format is particularly suited to integrating insights across clinical, operational, technological, and policy domains, thereby facilitating a holistic perspective aimed at clinicians, health administrators, and policy stakeholders. Furthermore, this approach enables critical interpretation of findings, identification of translational and implementation gaps, and the proposal of actionable models and research priorities that might not be readily derived through strictly systematic review methodologies.

The Burden and the Gaps

Early GC is defined as carcinoma confined to the mucosa or submucosa (T1), irrespective of lymph-node status. Advanced GC refers to tumors invading the muscularis propria or deeper (T2–T4).10 GC, which accounts for over 95% of all gastric malignancies and ranks as the fifth most common cancer worldwide,11 primarily affects individuals over 50 years of age and is more prevalent in males.12 In 2022, 968,784 new cases of stomach cancer were reported, with 660,175 deaths globally. GC accounted for 7% of all diagnosed cancer cases and 9% of all deaths annually.13 According to data from National Cancer Information Database, the diagnostic rate of early-stage (I–II) stomach cancer rose to 35.63% of all cancer cases between 2017 and 2023.15 The prognosis of GC hinges significantly on the timing of diagnosis, patients in early stages exhibit favorable prognoses following standardized treatment, whereas the five-year survival rate for those in advanced stages is still less than 30%.16 Nevertheless, the rate of diagnosis and treatment of early GI cancer in China remains below 10%, well behind Japan (70%) and South Korea (50%).17,18

Early esophageal cancer (EC) is defined clinically as cT1N0M0 disease, with tumor invasion limited to the mucosa or submucosa. Locally advanced EC includes cT2–T4 and/or regional node-positive, non-metastatic disease, whereas M1 disease is classified as metastatic.19 EC ranks 11th globally in cancer incidence and 7th in cancer-related mortality, representing 2.6% of new cancer cases and 4.6% of cancer deaths.14 According to the latest data from the GLOBOCAN 2022 database, an estimated 511,054 individuals worldwide were diagnosed with EC in 2022, resulting in 445,391 deaths from the disease.20 The number of new cases of EC worldwide is expected to climb by about 80.5% by 2050, with an 85.4% increase in deaths.20 China accounted for 43.8% of new cases and 42.1% of deaths worldwide in 2022.20 Projections for 2050 indicate a rise in China’s new EC cases to 403,627 (an 80.2% increase) and deaths to 371,521 (a 98.2% increase).20 EC often exhibits with vague and non-specific early symptoms, contributing to late-stage diagnosis and a poor overall prognosis, with a five-year survival rate of merely 10–30%.21 After systematic therapy, the 5-year survival rate for patients with early diagnoses can reach 95%.22 The incidence and death of EC can be reduced by screening high-risk populations, as numerous studies have shown.23

Early colorectal cancer (CRC) generally refers to T1 carcinoma that invades the submucosa but does not extend into the muscularis propria. Advanced CRC is defined as T2–T4 disease and/or regional lymph-node or distant metastasis.24 CRC is the third most common cancer worldwide, accounting for 9.6% of all cancer cases, and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, accounting for 9.3% of all fatalities according to the global cancer statistics of 2022.11 Ranked among the top five malignancies in China, CRC imposes a considerable burden, with 555,477 new cases and 286,162 deaths reported nationally.11 Most CRCs follow the “adenoma-cancer” progression model. A precancerous lesion typically develops into aggressive cancer over a period of 5–10 years.25 This extended progression period offers a critical opportunity for early diagnosis and clinical intervention. The prognosis for CRC is highly related to the stage at which it is detected and the 5-year relative survival rate for stage I patients can reach 90%, whereas, upon progression to stage IV with distant metastasis, the 5-year relative survival rate drops considerably to 14%.20,26

Effective early detection hinges on precise risk stratification. Risk factors are categorized into non-modifiable factors such as age (>50 years), male gender, and Genetic susceptibility (eg Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, and Li-Fraumeni syndrome).26,27 Common modifiable risk factors include metabolic status, lifestyle, and infection. Helicobacter pylori infection is a major risk factor for GC.28 While tobacco use and alcohol consumption significantly increase the risk of multiple GI malignancies.29,30 Bad eating habits, such as pickled and high-salt foods,4 red meat, and processed meat, low dietary fiber, also contribute to risk.31,32 Additionally, obesity, physical inactivity,33 and metabolic disorders have been linked to a higher likelihood of developing CRC, pancreatic cancer, and esophageal adenocarcinoma.34 Barrett’s esophagus (BE) is a well-established precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma.35 Chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cystic lesions elevate pancreatic cancer risk.36 Gastrointestinal metaplasia (GIM) is associated with gastric adenocarcinoma;37 Chronic inflammatory diseases such as primary sclerosing cholangitis and inflammatory bowel disease increase CRC risk.38

A hierarchical weighted system that takes persistent infection status and a robust genetic background into account as high-risk thresholds should be produced by an ideal prediction model. In addition to including regional epidemiological features for calibration, this model should incorporate quantitative behavioral elements to create a continuous risk gradient. Ultimately, it should develop an intelligent prediction system capable of dynamically assessing individual risks and guiding precise screening.

Nevertheless, China’s early cancer screening system faces significant implementation challenges that hinder progress in prevention and treatment outcomes, which can be summarized as three critical gaps (Figure 1).

Low Detection Rate

Early-stage GI cancer often presents with minimal or vague symptoms, failing to trigger timely medical visits. High-sensitivity diagnostic techniques (such as high-definition endoscopy, NBI, and virtual chromoendoscopy) are widely applied in urban areas, while rural and primary care settings face shortages in both equipment and pathological expertise. Systematic protocols for managing key risk factors—such as adenoma history and H. pylori infection—remain underdeveloped. Furthermore, low public awareness and apprehension regarding invasive endoscopic procedures continue to suppress screening participation, even when services are accessible.

Low Intervention Rate

Once identified, timely treatment is often obstructed by multiple barriers. Patients may minimize the significance of precancerous or early-stage lesions or express concerns regarding procedural risks and costs. Inefficiencies in primary care referrals and fragmented interdisciplinary coordination further delay management, while the traditional reliance on inpatient admission for endoscopic interventions introduces logistical bottlenecks that discourage or postpone care.

Low Follow-Up Rate

Sustained surveillance is frequently compromised by significant loss to follow-up. Patients may perceive symptom resolution as cure, and adherence is weakened by the cumulative burden of repeated examinations and associated expenses. The widespread lack of intelligent follow-up systems and structured monitoring protocols—exacerbated by limited primary care capacity and persistent interoperability barriers between health institutions—results in discontinuous and uncoordinated long-term care.

Compounding this, underdeveloped regional health information platforms and persistent data-sharing barriers between facilities impede the seamless integration of patient management across the care continuum.

An Integrated Life-Cycle Management Model for Early GI Cancer

Framework for the Integrated Care Model

A collaborative team, coordinated by specialists in the field of GI tumors, has been established to integrate “Physician-Nurse-Administrator”. As shown in Figure 2, the “Physician” serves as the core of this endeavor, directed by the Digestive Endoscopy Center and incorporating expertise from GI surgery, oncology, pathology, anesthesiology, and medical imaging. The core physician team includes two chief physicians, eight associate chief physicians, and three attending physicians. They are jointly responsible for developing and implementing screening strategies for high-risk groups, operating procedures for endoscopic detailed examination, minimally invasive endoscopic treatments, and multidisciplinary collaborative diagnosis and treatment (MDT) plans for complicated cases. The ten-staff nursing team is comprised of associate chief nurses, charge nurses, an advanced practice nurse, and several registered nurses. They are held accountable for administering health education, implementing evaluation scales, and providing perioperative care.

A Framework for the Integrated Care Delivery Model. The operational framework of the early GI cancer Center is underpinned by two core components: a “One System, Three Platforms” digital health infrastructure and a dedicated Life-cycle Management Center operated by a “Physician-Nurse-Administrator” integrated team. The red text highlights the Digestive Endoscopy Center, which serves as the coordinating hub for early GI cancer screening, diagnosis, and endoscopic management within the multidisciplinary team.

Additionally, the “Administration” function was operationalized through the Life-cycle Management Center, which acted as a pivotal bridge between clinicians and patients. It was staffed with three dedicated roles:

The Disease Manager, serving as the first point of contact and dedicated companion for patients, is tasked with performing education and obtaining informed consent regarding inclusion criteria, signing contracts, and setting up files. They also coordinate endoscopic examinations and treatments, manage comprehensive medical plans, give health education, enable doctor-patient contact, provide 24-hour online Q&A support, and manage follow-up care. Furthermore, the Disease Manager acts as the “Interprofessional Glue” in this model. Beyond administrative tasks, the DM facilitates closed-loop communication between endoscopists, pathologists, and nurses, ensuring that critical diagnostic information triggers immediate and appropriate care transitions. These efforts are aimed at alleviating the administrative workload on medical personnel, improving patient satisfaction, and promoting adherence.

The Database Manager is responsible for creating and maintaining a structured and specialized database for early GI cancer. Its work covers the establishment of data models, the integration, cleaning, quality control and in-depth mining of multi-source data both inside and outside the hospital (clinical, endoscopic images, pathology, follow-up), providing high-quality data support for clinical research, therapeutic effect evaluation and prognosis prediction. Patient data are managed in accordance with relevant Chinese laws and institutional policies, including the Personal Information Protection Law, the Data Security Law, and the Cybersecurity Law. Medical and health information is treated as sensitive personal information and protected throughout its collection, storage, use, and sharing.

Health Promoter could effectively employ multi-media and digital technologies to transform medical and health information into accessible video and graphic formats through the utilization of a Cloud-Based Platform for Disease-Specific Educational Video Data, the disease promotion specialist, with the primary aim of delivering personalized health information accurately.

An Implementation Roadmap

We establish a Life-Cycle Management Model that integrates intelligent risk identification, standardized diagnosis and treatment, systematic follow-up, and data-driven optimization. Rather than functioning as a series of isolated clinical steps, the model is structured as a coordinated workflow spanning risk detection to post-intervention surveillance.

This framework is supported by a “One-System, Three-Platform” infrastructure—including the Hospital Information System (HIS), the Chronic Care Platform, AI Follow-up Platform, the Database Platform—and implemented through a coordinated Physician–Nurse–Administrator team. Each component operates within an integrated digital environment to ensure data continuity and operational alignment. The detailed workflow is illustrated in Figure 3.

Intelligent Screening and Risk Stratification for High-Risk Populations

The implementation process begins with the integration of data from the HIS, electronic medical records, physical examination systems, and regional health information platforms. Based on established evidence-based risk factors, a structured algorithm is applied to identify individuals at elevated risk for GI cancer. Rather than relying solely on opportunistic screening, the system enables proactive identification of high-risk populations and structured engagement. Initial Risk Screening and Target Population Identification

The system automatically performs real-time scanning and calculation on the integrated data inputted into the database, capturing the established risk indicators and categorizing the population accordingly, generating a list of individuals for subsequent follow-up. This automated pre-screening mechanism is designed to improve efficiency while reducing manual workload in large populations.

Intelligent Reach and Initial Interaction

The intelligent follow-up system initiates the first round of automated outreach within 48 hours of generating the target list. Using voice interaction supported by natural language processing, it conducts preliminary symptom inquiry and provides standardized educational information regarding early GI cancer screening. The purpose of this step is not to replace clinical consultation, but to facilitate early engagement and assess willingness to participate in further evaluation.

Manual Intervention and in-Depth Assessment

For individuals identified as high-risk or expressing clear intent to participate, the health promoter conducts personalized follow-up through telephone or online communication platforms. This interaction includes explanation of screening indications, procedural details of gastroscopy and colonoscopy, and assessment of overall health status and readiness for examination. Human involvement at this stage ensures individualized clarification and supports informed decision-making.

Informed Consent and Cohort Establishment

Upon confirmation of participation, the patient completes a standardized electronic informed consent procedures and is formally enrolled in the early GI cancer Life-cycle management cohort. Enrollment marks the transition from risk identification to structured clinical pathway management.

One-Stop Reservation and Service Integration

For enrolled participants, the health promoter arranges comprehensive “one-stop” scheduling within the HIS system, including endoscopic examination, anesthesia evaluation, and necessary preoperative testing (eg, laboratory investigations and electrocardiography). Examination appointments are aligned within adjacent time slots to minimize logistical burden. By consolidating multiple procedures into a coordinated schedule, this step aims to reduce time costs, improve compliance, and streamline the diagnostic process.

Periprocedural Management in Digestive Endoscopy

Periprocedural management was organized within a standardized modular framework that delineates responsibilities across the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases. The primary objective of this structure is to reduce procedural variability, enhance diagnostic precision, and ensure continuity of care within the life-cycle management pathway.

Preoperative Standardization

During the preoperative stage, treatment strategies are determined promptly following confirmation of clinical indications. Physicians are responsible for procedural planning, including selection of the appropriate endoscopic modality and preparation of contingency strategies based on lesion characteristics and patient condition. Nursing staff perform structured preprocedural assessments encompassing nutritional status, fall risk, and psychological readiness. Bowel preparation protocols incorporating simethicone and pronase are implemented to optimize mucosal visualization and improve lesion detection quality. The health promoter is responsible for coordinating surgical resources to ensure that the procedure is finished within five working days. Such standardization seeks to enhance examination completeness and minimize preventable technical limitations.

Intraoperative Quality Control

Surgical procedures were meticulously conducted in accordance with the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) Guideline.39 A surgeon-led team coordination mechanism clarifies intraoperative responsibilities and facilitates efficient collaboration. High-quality white-light endoscopy is systematically combined with chromoendoscopy, magnification, and image-enhanced techniques to improve early lesion detection. For suspicious lesions, structured assessment of lesion boundaries, invasion depth, and predicted histology is performed to ensure appropriate therapeutic decision-making. The integration of magnifying endoscopy with image-enhanced modalities strengthens clinicopathological correlation and supports accurate preoperative evaluation. Therapeutic procedures, including endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), are performed following standardized technical principles, with emphasis on maintenance of proper dissection planes, vascular management, and controlled traction. Team members are trained to manage intraoperative risks such as bleeding or anatomical uncertainty. Predefined emergency protocols allow timely multidisciplinary intervention when complications arise, thereby reinforcing procedural safety.

Postoperative Care

Postoperative management focuses on early recovery monitoring and complication control. The physician team oversees postoperative assessment, interpretation of pathological findings, and formulation of immediate treatment and surveillance plans. Simultaneously, the nursing team monitors potential complications, provide individualized education, and document follow-up information within the centralized database. To eliminate care fragmentation, the disease-specific health promoter (manager)—acting as the administrative hub—utilizes the digital platform to automatically ensure timely scheduling of follow-up visits and facilitate communication between patients and the clinical team. This phase leverages digital orchestration to transition the patient from acute treatment to long-term surveillance, ensuring they are securely embedded in a multidisciplinary safety net before hospital discharge. This integrated approach emphasizes short-term safety, complication prevention, and the stabilization of the postoperative course.

Systematic Longitudinal Follow-up

Upon discharge, patients transition into a structured long-term surveillance pathway supported by a chronic disease management platform. Individualized follow-up plans are generated based on pathological findings and risk stratification, incorporating defined intervals for re-examination and disease monitoring.

Patients with non-curative resection are referred to a multidisciplinary outpatient clinic, where oncology, surgery, and gastroenterology teams coordinate subsequent treatment decisions. All follow-up data are incorporated into a disease-specific database to facilitate periodic quality evaluation and pathway refinement every quarter.

Automated reminders through text messaging and AI-assisted voice systems promote adherence to scheduled visits. When predefined non-response patterns or symptom alerts are detected, escalation protocols enable timely clinician review and intervention. This tiered monitoring mechanism aims to reduce attrition and ensure early identification of potential recurrence or complications.

At the regional level, a collaborative network links tertiary specialists with primary and secondary institutions. Tertiary centers provide remote multidisciplinary consultation, endoscopic and pathological review, standardized protocols, and professional training, while local institutions undertake risk screening, health education, routine surveillance, and follow-up. Suspected or complex cases are referred to tertiary centers, whereas clinically stable patients return to local care, thereby extending specialist support and continuity of care to underserved areas.

The early GI cancer Life-cycle Management Center supports both online and offline services, including report interpretation, appointment coordination, and follow-up scheduling. Role-specific access for physicians, nursing staff and specific disease managers ensures shared visibility of patient trajectories and management data. By consolidating digital tools with in-person consultation, the model seeks to maintain centralized oversight while improving accessibility and continuity of care.

Integrated, Data-Driven Care Model

The model converts multi-source clinical data into practical decision support. By applying a structured algorithm to integrated HIS and regional health data, the system generates real-time risk alerts. This advancement allows the MDT team to transition from opportunistic screening to proactive, evidence-based interventions, thereby ensuring that high-risk individuals are identified and prioritized for endoscopic evaluation without the need for manual oversight.

Multi-Channel Communication for Doctor-Patient Interaction

Patients interact with the medical team through offline and online channels, including WeChat official accounts, mini-programs, online consultations, and telephone. Educational content—videos, graphics, and lectures—is tailored to disease stage, risk profile, and lifestyle, empowering patients to actively participate in self-management.

Intelligent Voice Follow-Up System

The intelligent voice follow-up system supports automated patient contact, structured information collection, and the identification of potential postoperative concerns. When predefined alerts or “red flag” symptoms are detected, the system prompts review and intervention by the Disease Manager and clinical team.

Discussion

The shift from fragmented care to digitally integrated MDT models represents a strategic approach to addressing the increasing global burden of GI cancer. Conventional physician-centered approaches frequently yield disjointed care pathways, treating risk assessment, endoscopy, and follow-up as separate occurrences. This fragmentation gives rise to “care gaps” resulting in significant patient attrition during referrals and the omission of crucial diagnostic information. In contrast, digital MDT models reconceptualize cancer care as an ongoing, integrated process, incorporating endoscopists, oncologists, nurses, and disease managers within a unified, collaborative structure.

A prospective study on GI cancer MDT revealed that the model corrected referral diagnoses in 21.8% of cases, enhancing diagnostic accuracy to 93.4% and achieving a decision execution rate of 94.4%.40 Moreover, a systematic review of 16 studies indicated that MDTs altered 18.4–26.9% of initial diagnoses and 23.0–41.7% of treatment plans, with execution rates nearing 100%.41 These results suggest that digitally-supported MDTs not only improve diagnostic accuracy but also bolster patient adherence by providing a continuous “safety net” mechanism. Japan and South Korea have achieved high rates of early detection by implementing government-led screening programs that incorporate MDT protocols. In Japan, endoscopy specialists play a crucial role in standardizing minimally invasive techniques such as endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).42 In contrast, despite having established MDTs, the US model (NCCN) exhibits lower survival rates attributed to the absence of nationwide screening initiatives.43 This underscores the notion that sophisticated MDT systems offer minimal advantages in the absence of timely detection.

China is currently undergoing a cancer transition characterized by a high burden of upper GI cancers and a rising incidence of CRC. Despite having a lower incidence compared to Japan or Korea, China experiences high mortality rates and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).44 This situation is primarily due to a structural imbalance between well-equipped urban healthcare facilities and insufficient primary screening services. Therefore, there is an urgent need for an integrated approach that encompasses screening, diagnosis, MDT collaboration, and post-treatment monitoring. To address this challenge, we have developed a comprehensive model based on West China Hospital’s “one system, three platforms” framework, which revolves around a core team consisting of physicians, nurses, and managers. This model ensures a seamless process from opportunistic screening and remote MDT consultations to standardized treatment and proactive follow-up care. By replacing the traditional fragmented care led by physicians with a digitally coordinated integrated system, our model provides a potentially adaptable framework for enhancing GI cancer management in China.

Although developed in a high-volume tertiary center, this model should be viewed as a modular framework adaptable to local infrastructure and workforce capacity. Its core components include standardized pathways, multidisciplinary roles, risk stratification, structured follow-up, and data feedback. The model may allow tertiary centers to provide remote MDT support and specialist training, while primary and secondary institutions undertake screening, education, and routine surveillance. In settings with limited digital resources, simplified registries, telephone calls, or text-message reminders can serve as viable alternatives. Prospective multicenter validation is essential to assess its transferability and effectiveness.

Despite its potential value, the life-cycle management model for early GI cancer remains exploratory and encounters various challenges. Technical obstacles, particularly the incorporation of diverse HIS systems and resistance to data sharing on cultural grounds, can obstruct smooth information exchange. Additionally, investigating the acceptance of the AI-voice system among elderly patients with limited digital literacy is essential to guarantee fairness. Importantly, standardizing risk prediction models, diagnostic criteria, and intelligent follow-up systems is crucial to ensure their reliability and seamless integration into clinical processes. Lastly, validating the model’s applicability beyond high-volume tertiary centers is necessary. Accordingly, future research should prioritize prospective multicenter implementation studies involving patients with early GI neoplasia to evaluate the model’s feasibility, care continuity, effectiveness, transferability, and sustainability using standardized outcome measures.

Conclusions

This review proposes a digitally integrated, multidisciplinary life-cycle management framework for early GI cancer. By integrating risk stratification, standardized care pathways, and longitudinal follow-up, the framework provides a practical approach to reducing care fragmentation. Prospective multicenter studies are required to validate its effectiveness and scalability.
