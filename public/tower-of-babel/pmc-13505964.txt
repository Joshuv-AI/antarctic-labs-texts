Male and female mice scent mark during social communication regardless of sexual motivation or partner identity
Authors: Sourish Mukhopadhyay, Natalie Cole, Pansée ElGhayati, Anna Pallé, Caitlin H. Miller, David Melendez-Perdomo, Jason A. Keller, John P. Crimaldi, Bard Ermentrout, Lisa Stowers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMany species communicate via pheromone signals that orchestrate key social and survival behaviors. In laboratory assays, only male mice are observed to broadcast pheromones through urine scent marking. This has contributed to the view that males have specialized circuits and anatomy to intentionally transmit urine pheromones and actively participate in socio-chemical communication, while females remain mute recipients in laboratory settings. Here, we find that simple modifications to standard assays reveal exuberant urine marking by laboratory females, enabling reexamination of its dimorphic features. Chemogenetic inhibition of estrogen receptor-1-expressing (Esr1) neurons in Barrington’s nucleus confirms that both sexes rely on a similar mechanistic strategy necessary for urine marking. Moreover, we find that males and females mark toward a broad set of receivers, including reproductively incapable partners. Sex parity in urine marking reframes the understanding of voluntary chemo-communication function beyond a male-specific behavior to a broader social communication strategy engaged independent of sex or reproductive status.

Standard laboratory assays have found urine marking to be a male-specific behavior

Conveying information to others underlies species’ survival and fitness. While humans use various sensory modalities to coordinate behavior within and between species, mice largely communicate via chemical olfactory cues.1–15 Mouse urine from both sexes contains a variety of volatile and non-volatile compounds that convey information about the individual’s identity, sex, social rank, and reproductive status.1–3,6,7,10,16–21 Both sexes detect and respond to urine pheromones from same- and opposite-sex senders to trigger changes in physiology and social behavior.22–30 However, in mice, how urine is excreted to broadcast social pheromones is only understood in males. When the bladder is full, both sexes completely void in one emission event, often away from the sleeping area (latrine behavior).31 Urine (or scent) marking is a specialized mode of urination that occurs irrespective of bladder urgency, in which mice determine when and where to deposit very small droplets of urine in socially important environments, creating a pheromone scent map for other mice to encounter and sample.32–37 Although the behavior has largely been studied in males, previous studies have reported wild or inbred females to increase urination under limited contexts; however, they conflict in identifying the causal situations (pregnancy, female partners, or occupancy near social stimuli), and the urination is limited compared to males (in amount of urine, number of deposits, and number of females that increase urination).38–42 In the laboratory, adult males rapidly mark odorless novel territory (territory marking) or known territory containing male or female social odor (scent marking). There is no known protocol to stimulate female urine marking in a laboratory environment35,38,43–46; therefore, females have been excluded when characterizing the behavioral significance and neural circuits underlying these behaviors.47,48 Urine marking has been cast as a male action because it is thought to be a high-risk action, alerting foes and attracting predators; is metabolically costly; and is thought to require the long urethra and presumed superior motor control of the male penis to produce characteristic small urine droplets, entirely incompatible with female anatomy.45,49–51 Moreover, studies with laboratory-bred mice have shown that male-specific testosterone signaling in early postnatal life is necessary for urine-marking displays in adulthood (circuit organization), while circulating sex hormones post-puberty drive the maintenance and extent of the behavioral performance subsequently (circuit activation), and females masculinized by exogenous testosterone treatment at birth transform their behavior to display moderate scent marking.39,52–54 These findings and assumptions have advanced the narrative that the behavior is a high-cost trade-off only males pay in their quest for reproductive fitness37,50,51 and have stifled the study of female urine marking. Therefore, while female urine is known to contain socially relevant pheromones,28,30,55 females are not thought to actively share them with the social group and are considered to be passive receivers in these chemical conversations.

Male urine marking has traditionally been studied as a territorial action in the presence of another freely behaving male (same strain, same-sex partner), providing rich, dynamic, bidirectional release of stimuli and behavioral actions from both the sender and receiver. For experimental convenience, partners are separated by a perforated wall that enables transmission of auditory, visual, and olfactory cues but prevents partners from accessing the other’s territory to easily assign urine marks to each individual (Figure 1A).35,37 We and others have found that males paired and freely behaving with another male for 120′ deposit numerous scent marks enriched at the territorial boundary conveying that he is able to establish, protect, and maintain his resources37,56 (same strain, same-sex partner; Figures 1B, 1D, and 1E). Consistent with urine marking acting as a male-specific behavior, females fail to similarly urinate in the presence of an intact behaving female (same strain, same-sex partner; Figures 1C–1E), occasionally performing voiding behavior as a physiological need during the long assay.

Urine marks persist in the environment, enabling lone individuals to encounter unmanned deposits, sense the sender’s odors, and, if motivated, deposit their own urine marks in response, a form of unidirectional communication. This can be assayed in the laboratory by successively presenting a control odor and a stimulus social odor (but not novel non-social odors; see Figures 1M and S1C–S1E) from an opposite-sex partner of a different strain (2′ each). This is sufficient to trigger male scent marking, reflecting his courtship drive (Figures 1F, 1G, 1I, 1J, and 1L).47,48 Laboratory females or castrated males assayed similarly do not urine mark (Figures 1H, 1I, 1K, 1L, S1A, and S1B).57

To further gain insight into the function of urine marking, we generated a simple diffusion model and found that urine excretion patterns alter the plume distribution of volatile urine odorants. Urine distributed in multiple small spots expands the spread of volatile ligands compared to the same total volume of urine deposited as a single large void (Figure 1N). Together, these analyses confirm that urine marking involves specific behavioral actions in response to social odors that increase the chance of receivers encountering the ligands, thereby facilitating effective social communication. However, the behavior also comes with peril by amplifying unintended signaling to predators—thought to be a cost that females do not need to bear.51 Together, studies done by us and others have entrenched the assumption that only males have the neural circuits and/or the urinary anatomy to perform both kinds of excretion, voiding for physiological need and marking for reproductive fitness.

Laboratory conditions to promote robust female urine-marking behavior

With clear behavioral sexual dimorphism observed across two urine-marking behavioral assays, we set out to study the mechanisms that enable males to perform this sex-specific behavior. As a control, we subjected females to the same experimental conditions as males in both the bidirectional and unidirectional urine-marking assays. Like their vigorous response to same-sex partners in the bidirectional assay, males also deposit copious urine marks concentrated at the social interface of the arena in the presence of a female partner (same strain, opposite sex; Figure 2A). To our surprise, the wild-type virgin, male-odor naive, female partners simultaneously and robustly deposited their own numerous urine marks (Figure 2A). Just like socially naive males, first exposure to an opposite-sex social and sensory environment without any reinforcing mating behavior was sufficient for a significant population of females to readily display exuberant innate urine marking (Figures 2B and 2C). Moreover, the distributed patterns of enrichment at the social interface were qualitatively similar to those deposited by her male partner (Figure 2B). The innate ability to robustly mark to males occurred in the same females that had failed to mark toward same-sex partners (Figures 1C–1E and 2D), indicating social sensory selectivity in decision-making to control their marking response. This opposite-sex bidirectional assay reveals that normal laboratory-housed females, without hormonal manipulations, mating experience, or any social interactions with males following weaning, can innately scent mark in relevant social and sensory contexts.

Our battery of experiments included the unidirectional assay and revealed a second condition that motivates females to urine mark. Here, we began by characterizing the development of male innate marking behavior throughout puberty and adolescence in response to female urine (like Figures 1F and S2A–i), except the unidirectional assay was run repeatedly, once each week after weaning until adulthood (4–8 weeks of age; Figures 2E and S2A-ii). We similarly assayed females as a negative control for urine-marking behavior, and, unexpectedly, we observed these wild-type adolescent females robustly displaying unidirectional urine marking to the novel female odor (different strain, same sex; Figures 2G and S2A-ii). Study of three independent cohorts revealed this protocol to be a simple means to reliably promote laboratory females to perform unidirectional urine marking at rates similar to males (75%–80%) by sexual maturity (Figures 2F and 2H). Of those individuals that scent mark, the behavior emerged in females at the same developmental rate as males, with approximately half the individuals scent marking by 6 weeks of age and the maximum number maturing to scent mark by 8 weeks of age (Figures 2F and 2H). Social isolation potentiates male-specific innate behaviors such as territorial aggression, copulation, and scent marking in the laboratory.47,57,58 The stress of individual housing partially drives this facilitation of male-specific behaviors.58,59 Our female-enabling marking assay subjects individuals to two unique variables: (1) social isolation stress and (2) experience with novel female odor (Figure S2A-ii). We manipulated both conditions (Figures S2A–i-iv) and found that adolescent exposure to novel social odors had the greatest impact on induction of female scent marking in this assay (Figures S2A–S2H). While the exposure of mice to novel female odors is a form of sensory enrichment in the laboratory, it is almost certainly the norm in the wild. These findings in laboratory mice offer a mechanistic explanation for why observations of female urine marking have been reported primarily in wild or outbred mice,39,40,45 where every individual emits a unique odor profile rather than under standard laboratory conditions, where genetically identical inbred mice emit similar social odors.

Identifying that females can be motivated to mark toward female urine odor prompted us to test whether the subset of unidirectional marking females were similarly motivated to mark in the bidirectional assay with female partners. Indeed, in contrast to females that had not experienced novel female odor during adolescence (Figures 1C–1E), adult wild-type females that had briefly and repeatedly experienced novel female odor throughout adolescence robustly urine marked toward socially behaving, freely moving females (same strain, same-sex partner; Figure 2I). Like the pattern of urine marks that occurs between socially interacting males, female-to-female marks are numerous, distributed, and highly enriched proximal to the partner’s shared wall (Figure 2J). In the laboratory, males also urine mark when placed in a clean, novel territory (territory marking), but we and others have not observed females to similarly mark (Figures 2K and 2L). However, we found those females that display urine marking following adolescent exposure to novel female urine to additionally display territory marking in the novel environment without added female odor (Figures 2M and 2N). Previous studies in female mice in the laboratory have described various manipulations to permanently masculinize females (postnatal estrogen/testosterone administration), which leads to female urine marking.39,52–54,60–62 Analysis of anatomy (Figures S3A–S3C), sex hormone levels (Figures S3D and S3E), and estrus cycles (Figures S3F–S3J) revealed that the females that showed scent marking following adolescent social odor exposure showed no signs of spontaneous masculinization. Further, there were no defects in mating and maternal behaviors, and they did not show territorial aggression toward males (N = 8; data not shown), suggesting that they were socially perceived as females by conspecifics.

Together, we have identified four new experimental assays that promote wild-type females to innately mark in a laboratory environment: (1) the bidirectional assay when she is paired with a behaving male; and, after experiencing brief 2′ periods of novel social odor exposure during adolescence, (2) the unidirectional assay toward female odor, (3) the bidirectional assay when paired with behaving females, and (4) territory marking in novel clean arenas. These assay environments are sufficient to robustly motivate wild-type laboratory females to innately urine mark toward a variety of social partners, social odors, and novel territories—the entire repertoire of environments that are known to promote male urine marking.

Female scent marks and actions are similar to male behavior

Having established assays to reliably elicit and study female urine marking, we next asked if female scent marking differs from the canonical male behavior. The size and pattern of urination differ during voiding (Figure S4A) and marking behavior (Figure S4B) and can be quantifiably measured.31,35–37,47,48,57,63 Qualitatively, we found it difficult to blindly distinguish the urine-marking patterns produced by males or females (Figure S4B). To quantify the unidirectional assay, we defined 18 metrics (Figures S4C–S4T) to formalize the extent and pattern of urination (12 measures) and the dynamics and timing of urine release (6 measures) in response to novel female odor. While we do see population differences in some of the measures between the sexes (7/18 measures), the range of the female behavior largely falls within the variability of the male behavior. To consider all our metrics together, we performed principal-component analysis (PCA, Figure S5A). Analyzing the data by the first two principal components (explaining 55% of the variance) revealed marking metrics from both sexes to be co-distributed (Figures S5B and S5C). Euclidean distances of points representing males and females as groups to centroids representing either sex revealed no significant differences (Figure S5D). When each individual was analyzed, we find them to be equidistant from centroids of their own-sex compared to opposite-sex individuals in the PC1-PC2 plane (Figure S5E). Together, we find the scent-marking kinetics and patterns as well as the behavioral responses including investigation and locomotion (Figures S4U–S4X) to be qualitatively and quantitatively similar between males and females.

We additionally compared male and female urine marking in the bidirectional assay (Figures S6A–S6C). A similar high proportion of their urine marks were found to be proximal (within a body length, ~5 cm) to the partner’s shared wall (Figures S6D and S6E). Although males deposited significantly more urine marks in response to male and female partners than females (Figure S6F), the total area covered and the variability in the mark sizes were not distinguishable, suggesting sophisticated motor control over urine marking across both sexes (Figures S6G and S6H). Together, we find that by most quantifiable measures, in both the unidirectional and bidirectional laboratory assays, female urine marking is not performed in a sexually dimorphic manner; instead, it is qualitatively and quantitatively similar to that of males.

BarEsr1 neural activity is necessary for female urine marking

Given the behavioral parallels between male and female urine-marking behavior, we next asked whether central control of urine marking utilizes sex-specific neural mechanisms. The similarities in male and female scent-marking actions (Figure S6) may be mediated by the same circuits in both sexes or separate but parallel unknown neural circuits in the female. Urine marking in males requires precise top-down control over the external urethral sphincter (EUS), mediated by a subpopulation of estrogen receptor-1-expressing neurons in Barrington’s nucleus (BarEsr1).47 Central circuits that govern female urine marking have not been reported. Using immunohistochemistry, we find that females also express Esr1 in a subset of Bar neurons with an anatomical distribution pattern similar to that observed in the male brain (Figures S7A–S7D). To investigate whether the function is conserved in the female brain, we employed a virus-mediated chemogenetic inhibition strategy (AAV-DIO-hM4Di in Esr1-Cre mice) to acutely and specifically inhibit female BarEsr1 neurons64 (Figures 3A, 3B, 3G, and S7I). BarEsr1-Gi-expressing individuals of both sexes were tested for urine-marking behavior in the bidirectional assay with opposite-sex partners (like Figures 2A and S6A–S6C). Inhibition of BarEsr1 neural activity by administration of either the DREADD agonist C21 (Figures 3D and 3F) or CNO (Figures S7F and S7H) significantly reduced urine-marking behavior (Figures 3I, 3J, 3L, 3M, S7J, S7K, S7M, and S7O), though occasional urination events producing large-diameter spots were recorded, consistent with latrine behavior (Figures 3K, 3N, S6N, and S6P). As controls, bidirectional urine marking was reliably observed in the same individuals following saline (vehicle) treatment (Figures 3C, 3E, S7E, and S7G), and Esr1-Cre individuals of both sexes without AAV-DIO-hM4Di injections continued marking when treated with saline, C21, or CNO, confirming specificity to the BarEsr1 population (Figures S7Q–S7V). Inhibiting BarEsr1 cells equally impaired urine-marking behavior in males and females (Figures 3H and S7L). Our results show that the critical subset of BarEsr1 neurons that gate the urine-marking motor control pathways in males is present and functional in female mice.

In males, BarEsr1 neurons project to the spinal cord, which sends relay afferents to relax the EUS to promote urination.47 We have recently reported EMG recordings of the female EUS during increasing bladder pressure and have found coordinated, bursting muscle contractions crucial for efficient urine voiding in females like the typical action in males,47,65 indicating that peripheral circuits also function similarly between the sexes. Together, we do not find any evidence for the known neural mechanisms that control urine marking to be sexually dimorphic.

Marking behavior is not altered by sender’s reproductive physiology

Understanding that laboratory females also innately scent mark prompted us to revisit assumptions that males risk safety to advertise and gain the attention of specific receivers to support reproductive fitness. Unlike males who are constitutively reproductively ready, females cycle between fertile and non-fertile estrus states (Figure S3F), providing a platform to test the extent to which reproductive status dictates marking behavior. If urine marking toward the opposite sex is a sign of reproductive readiness in females, as it is thought to be in males, we would expect its display to be altered (elevated or depressed) during fertile epochs as a sign to communicate her internal reproductive state. To investigate this function, we analyzed the correlation between the estrus cycle and scent marking toward either same-sex or opposite-sex stimuli. Irrespective of the sensory stimuli, females marked across all stages of the estrus cycle (Figures 4A–4C). In the bidirectional assay, the pattern of distribution proximal to the male partner’s shared wall and production of marks did not vary or correlate with the estrus stage (Figures 4A and 4B). Similarly, in the unidirectional assay, the number of marks emitted to novel female odor was similar in her fertile or non-fertile internal states (Figure 4C). We find that not only do females urine mark across the reproductive cycle but they also do so in a similar manner irrespective of their own internal reproductive estrus state. This suggests that the action of depositing urine marks by females is unlikely to serve strictly as a social signal representing sexual motivation.

Multiple lines of evidence show that maintenance of adult-level circulating sex hormone concentrations is crucial for male- and female-specific behaviors.66,67 Lack of gonad sex hormones abolishes the mating drive in both sexes.68,69 Aligning with this, when we tested the scent-marking behavior of gonadectomized animals in the unidirectional assay, they did not deposit any urine marks in response to the female urine stimulus (Figures S1A and S1B). However, to our surprise, when we tested the same castrated males in the bidirectional assay with freely moving hormonally intact social partners, they innately deposited numerous, small, distributed urine marks enriched at the social interface to either male or female partners (Figures 4D, 4E, 4G, and 4H). While we observed a reduction in the total number of urine spots deposited (Figures 4M and 4N), the distribution and pattern of urine marks by castrated males proximal to the shared wall were statistically similar to that by intact males (Figures 4F and 4I). These actions by impotent individuals are at odds with scent marking solely underlying reproductive motivation.

After observing no overt phenotype across the female estrus cycle, we next ovariectomized females to create a more dramatic and controlled impact on internal physiology. Like castrated males (Figures 4D–4I), even this manipulation had no effect on urine marking. We found ovariectomized females to continue marking with male partners in a pattern and overall abundance that was like hormonally intact females (Figures 4J–L and 4O). Overall, across natural variations in sex hormones or extreme conditions of gonad removal, we find both sexes to continue to perform urine-marking behavior independent of their internal hormonal state. These findings suggest that both sexes equally risk unwanted attention from foes and predators to urine mark irrespective of their ability to engage in sexual reproduction.

Urine-marking behavior is not impacted by partner’s sex, physiology, or age

Functional social behavior relies on a dynamic exchange between the signaler and receiver. In addition to one’s own internal state, partner signals are equally key to advancing social and mating behavior. Therefore, we focused on the sender’s urine-marking behavior while varying the partner’s (signal receivers’) sex hormone physiology (Figure 5A). We used the bidirectional assay to first ask whether the sex of the receiver influenced or altered the marking patterns of males or females (Figures 5B–5E). We found that regardless of the sex of the partner animal, both males and females deposit numerous urine marks, and there was no statistical difference in the number of spots deposited when paired with either the same- or opposite-sex partners (Figures 5F and 5G). Interestingly, marking by males or females did not appear to correlate with the exuberance of their social partners’ marking behavior in the bidirectional assay (R2 combined = 0.01, R2 males = 0.15, R2 females = 0.08, all slopes non-significantly divergent from 0; Figure 5H). This suggests that urine marking is an autonomous, self-motivated social behavior independent of the sex and marking response of others, further challenging its service to specifically facilitate mate attraction.

We continued to use the bidirectional assay to define the boundaries of the relevant social signals that motivate a sender to urine mark. Male mice use odor cues to easily identify and distinguish the reproductive status of estrus females that are physiologically ready to engage in mating from those that are behaviorally disinterested during their diestrus phase.70,71 If urine marking serves to influence courtship and mating, we would expect to observe differences in his amount of urine marking toward estrus and diestrus partners. However, we instead found that irrespective of their partner’s estrus state, males maintained the stereotypical extent and pattern of distributing their urine in small marks with a spatial bias proximal to the shared wall (Figures 5I–5K). This indicates that the reproductive status of the female partner does not influence whether and how a male urine marks.

We next returned to the artificial and more severe hormonal manipulation by surgically gonadectomizing the partner animals. Pheromones that indicate sex and reproductive status are honest signals because they are either transcriptionally initiated or are metabolic derivatives of sex hormones themselves.21,70,72–76 The lack of gonadal sex hormones ceases one’s production of sex-specific pheromones, which in turn blinds receivers to sex chemoinformation and halts sexual advances by others.70,71 We paired reproductively intact animals with gonadectomized partners and quantified the behavior of the intact partner. We expected the lack of reproductive opportunity to extinguish the intact partner’s urine-marking behavior. Contrary to courtship-centered expectations, we found that intact adult males responded to ovariectomized female partners by depositing numerous scent marks statistically indistinguishable from those deposited toward hormonally intact females (Figures 5L and 5N). We did however observe a deficit in the marking by intact males toward castrated male partners, indicating a reduction of motivation for male-male communication (Figures 5O and 5Q). In parallel, intact females did not reduce marking behavior when their male partner was castrated and no longer reproductively available, nor did they modulate marking when their female partners were ovariectomized (Figures 5R, 5T, 5U, and 5W).

Finally, we leveraged juveniles as natural social partners that do not elicit sexual behavior from wild-type adults. We analyzed marking behavior of male or female adults as senders in the bidirectional assay with pre-weaning (P17–20), pre-pubescent, reproductively immature female or male partners (Figures 5M, 5P, 5S, and 5V). We found that adults marked very strongly to juvenile partners (Figures 5N, 5Q, 5T, and 5W), underscoring the fact that the age or reproductive status of the partner animal was not a factor contributing to the pheromonal communicative drive of male or female mice. Together, our results do not support urine marking as a sexually motivated behavior. Although it is primarily driven by the presence of social cues in the environment, the act of engaging in urine marking is largely self-determined and insensitive to the partner’s marking response, sex, reproductive status, circulating hormone levels, or age.

DISCUSSION

The association of the male sex with urine marking has been entrenched in collective assumptions about innate social behavior. Therefore, we did not expect to find that female laboratory mice, without any hormonal manipulations or signs of masculinization, can readily display and use urine marking just like males. While some of our new assays first require females to briefly experience female urine odor from unfamiliar females weekly throughout adolescence, this is undoubtedly the more ethologically relevant situation—where every female they encounter in the wild is genetically unique unlike the laboratory where genetically identical individuals are sex separated at weaning. The other new assay, bidirectional marking to behaving male partners, was displayed by all our wild-type females without any special social experience. The phenotype is highly robust, and it is surprising that it was not previously reported. This is likely because most mouse studies utilize the C57BL6 laboratory strain. Males of this line are notoriously poor urine markers (Figures S8A and S8C–S8G), and we now observe this depression of marking behaviors in C57BL6 females as well (Figures S8B–S8E). Instead, we and others routinely use BALB/c males to study voluntary urination, which scent mark at much higher rates. While the results reveal a fundamental behavioral alignment between males and females, these assays now also enable mechanistic investigation of the neural bases of urinary dysfunction and pathologies in both sexes in parallel, to understand, aid, and design potential therapeutic interventions.77–80 Our assays overcome the combination of previous male-biased assumptions, laboratory husbandry, and the happenstance of strain selection to reveal a repertoire of female marking environments like males.

It has been well appreciated that female urine contains a variety of sex-specific pheromones,30,55 but prior to this work, it was believed that she had no way to actively broadcast these signals voluntarily to the rest of her society. Our data show that the female urinary anatomy and physiology is built to flexibly switch between urine voiding in a large puddle and urine marking that distributes numerous small droplets throughout social environments. Social behavior is more than just sex. It occurs among all individuals, during every encounter, at every life stage, and irrespective of one’s different internal state variables. Motivated communication to facilitate social behavior is useful for both males and females to maintain social group cohesion across and within the sexes. Previous assumptions that only males have the hormonal, circuit, and anatomical means to actively emit and converse via pheromones implicitly deny females the agency to impact their social group. Instead, our data support interpreting scent marking as a general means of active chemical communication and reveal that females can and do widely participate in social chemical conversations. These findings broaden the utility of urine marking from a male-specific action serving to advance sexual reproduction to a general form of social communication, useful and used by both sexes.

Limitations of the study

We limited the study to inbred laboratory mice to control and replicate analysis between sex, age, experience, and internal hormone differences and provide a platform for subsequent mechanistic neuroscience study. Our findings cannot illuminate or extend understanding to the ethological basis of scent marking. The study of scent marking originated last century using wild mice, often in naturalistic conditions with low behavioral resolution. We expect the clear, robust marking behavior of laboratory females to reignite the study of the display of social chemo-communication in wild individuals of both sexes, where the impact on natural societies can be measured using modern high-dimensional approaches. Our study is also limited by its focus on the marking action that releases urine cues, omitting the effects of diverse male- and female-specific chemosignals on the receiver, which are likely to be differentially acted upon depending on one’s sex. We are still largely blind to the identity and meaning of the repertoire of urine ligands. Comprehensive identification of these signals and their influences on the receiver’s response will be essential to ultimately identify the motivation of the sender in creating scent marks and the extent to which marking shapes social behavior. Finally, although we find that BarEsr1, the known neural circuit and cell type that promotes urine marking in males, is present and similarly necessary for urine marking in females, our data indicating that males and females mark similarly are limited to the action of depositing scent marks. Indeed, in this study, we present contexts that differentially motivate males and females to initiate marking. Both sexes can now equally be used to further understand how shared premotor mechanisms are influenced by sexually dimorphic neural nodes.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at The Scripps Research Institute. Mice (male and female) were either group housed or single housed at weaning (P21–22, <6 per cage) into adulthood (P56) and maintained on a 12/12hr light/dark cycle with food and water available ad libitum. The number of mice used for each experiment is listed below where applicable and in the figure legends. All experiments (except Figure S8) were carried out using either BALB/cByJ strain (The Jackson Laboratory stock #: 000651) or BALB/cByJ background (backcrossed at least 5 generations). Esr1-Cre transgenic mice (Jackson Laboratory stock # 017911; backcrossed to BALB/cByJ) were used for BarEsr1 chemogenetic inhibition experiments. Novel female urine odor stimulus was collected from C57BL6 mice (Jackson Laboratory stock# 000664). For experiments in Figure S8, C57BL6 mice were used and were presented with C57BL6 opponent mice (bidirectional assays) or freshly collected female BALB/cByJ urine (unidirectional assays).

METHOD DETAILS

Social and odor experiences

For adulthood unidirectional marking assays (Figure S2A i), mice were group housed at weaning and then single housed in adulthood for at least 1 week before experiments. To test the effects of novel female odor-evoked scent marking in adolescence, mice were either single housed, or group housed at weaning (depending on assay design described in Figure S2A i-iv), and assayed for scent marking response to female urine weekly (5 times total) from p28-p56 (Figures S2A ii, S2A iv). To similarly assay novel female odor-evoked scent marking across 5 weeks in adulthood (Figure S2A iii), mice were group housed through adolescence, single housed as adults for 5 weeks, and weekly assays were carried out beginning 1 week after single housing. To assay social behavior with a conspecific separated by a mesh barrier, males and females that performed odor-evoked scent marking were socially isolated for at least 1 week prior to testing. Non marking females used in the mesh assays had been group housed with other females from weaning and were tested without single housing experience or any prior social or odor exposure to males.

Odor-motivated unidirectional scent marking assay

All novel female odor-evoked unidirectional scent marking assays were carried out as described previously.47 To inhibit novel-territory marking, mice were habituated to behavioral analysis room/assay chamber without female odor on 3 consecutive days, for 20/8/4 minute durations on consecutive days. For assays involving repeated weekly exposures, animals were habituated every week. On day 3, control stimulus (100uL tonic water, used because it also serves as a visual control since it fluoresces like urine under UV illumination) was pipetted from above at 0 min. and 2 min., and the baseline response was recorded. On the test day (Day 4), a 4 min. assay was performed, with 100uL tonic water delivered at 0 minutes and 100uL novel female urine delivered at 2 min. Novel female urine was collected from group housed virgin C57BL6 female mice. For all assays, pooled urine from at least 10 female mice belonging to at least 3 separate cages was used. For odor controls, animals were similarly habituated for 3 days and then exposed to tonic water (100uL, 2 minutes) and then Ethyl Acetate (diluted 50% in mineral oil), Peanut Oil (diluted 50% in mineral oil) or 2-Phenylethanol (diluted 50% in mineral oil) for 2 minutes (each odor tested on different days).

All behavior was conducted during light hours under dim red light, and 70% ethanol was used to clean equipment between trials. The recording box for unidirectional scent marking assays consisted of a UV-opaque acrylic home cage with the bottom cut out, placed on top of 0.35mm chromatography paper (Fisher Scientific 05–714-4) resting on clear glass. Two wide angle cameras (Logitech C930e), one above on a modified cage top, and one below the bottom glass, streamed video to a laptop computer at 15 frames per second, 640×360 pixel resolution. An analog pulse controlled LEDs in each camera field of view in order to synchronize cameras. Two UV fluorescent tube lights (American DJ Black-24BLB) surrounded by foil walls were used to evenly illuminate the chromatography paper from below. Videos were cut using Adobe After Effects and subsequently analyzed for urine marks using a custom MATLAB code (available at github.com/stowerslab/smuf). The red and green channels of the RGB camera frames were used for urine detection, and the blue channel for mouse tracking. An output video with urine detection overlay was generated to manually verify automatic spot detection.

Conspecific-motivated bidirectional scent marking assay

The bidirectional scent marking assay arena consists of a single acrylic bottomless cage (20cm × 40cm) with a wire mesh in the middle, dividing the arena into two 20cm × 20cm individual arenas. A sheet of 0.35mm chromatography paper (Fisher Assays Scientific 05–714-4) was placed underneath the arena. Assays were carried out for 2 hours under red light. No prior habituation to the arena was carried out. One mouse was placed in each arena, and the paper was collected after the assay ended. The chromatography paper was imaged under UV light following the assay using a wide-angle camera (Logitech 920e) with clear markers indicating each animal’s arena. Analysis was carried out by thresholding the green channel of the images (cropped to include only the part of the sheets under the arena) using FIJI and using ‘Count Particles’ and ‘Plot Profile’ commands. Orientation of images of individual arenas were adjusted to allow for uni-directional axes. Analyses to calculate total urine marks, area covered by urine marks, average urine spot size, largest urine spot size, standard deviation of urine spots deposited by an animal, and percentage of proximal urine spots (total urine pixel intensity 0–5cms from mesh divided by total urine pixel intensity across arena represented as percentage) were carried out in Excel and Prism. Each metric was calculated for every individual animal. Analyses to compare bidirectional scent marking metrics across sexes (Figure S6) were carried out on adult females (naïve and adolescent experience combined) and adult males.

Territory marking assay

Animals were placed on thin chromatography paper (0.19mm thickness, Fisher Scientific 05–714-1) with a bottomless home cage (15cm×25cm) serving as the arena. No social features were present in the arena. The paper was illuminated by a UV light lamp from underneath, and the assay was carried out under red light conditions. Animals were allowed to explore for 20 minutes, and a picture of the paper with urine was taken using Logitech C930e camera. Data were analyzed by thresholding (adjusted for illumination conditions) the green channel of the images using FIJI, visually verifying the accuracy of spot detection, and using ‘Count Particles’ command.

Behavioral analysis

For odor-motivated unidirectional urination assays, Noldus Ethovision XT was used to automatically track mice and determine distance traveled, speed of animal (in intervals of seconds) and odor sniffing periods, defined as when the nose point was visually in contact with the female urine stimulus. Statistical analysis and data plotting was performed using Prism. Analysis of urine blots from the odor motivated unidirectional assays was carried out by using a custom MATLAB script that detects each urine spot deposited by the animals based on a user-guided threshold (adjusted to background illumination) and records the time stamp and size (in pixels) of each detected urine spot. Visual verification confirmed analysis, and spots that were less than 2 pixels in size were excluded from analysis since they were found to be artefacts due to rare illumination fluctuations. Animals that were deemed to be ‘marking males’ or ‘marking females’ in experiments described in Figures 1 and 2 (≥3 urine spots in the 2′ epoch post FMU presentation) were subsequently selected for behavioral (Figure S4 and S5; n=19 females, 28 males), anatomical and hormonal analyses (Figure S3; n=8 per group). Analyses to calculate metrics (total, minimum, maximum, average, median, standard deviation, largest and mean normalized to number of pixels) associated with inter-mark intervals (Tn+1th spot – Tnth spot), duration of scent marking (Tlast deposited spot – Tfirst deposited spot), and the number of spots and urine pixels were carried out in Excel and Prism. Each metric was calculated for every individual animal. All of the calculated metrics were used as inputs for performing the Principal Component Analysis using Graphpad Prism.

Morphological measurements

Anogenital (AGD) distances for males and females were measured using calipers and images were collected for all individuals to determine genital morphology across cohorts. All morphological measurements were performed on the same day.

Fecal collection & hormone analysis

Fecal matter was collected to examine fecal estradiol and testosterone levels. To collect sufficient fecal weight for analysis, individuals were housed overnight (12 hours) in a bedding-free, home cage during their dark cycle (active phase). All fecal boluses were collected the following morning and stored at −20°C until assayed. Estrus state of the females was assessed by vaginal lavage prior to setting up for overnight collection and immediately after collection. Samples were not processed if the estrus phase had shifted overnight. Females were collected from twice, once during estrus phase, and once during their diestrus phase, to control for estrus cycle variation in hormone levels. Males were collected from once. All fecal collection was performed within a two-week span prior to mating trials. Fecal metabolites of estradiol and testosterone were measured using the Arbor Assays ELISA kits (Arbor Assays, Ann Arbor, MI). Hormone extraction from fecal samples was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Fecal samples were allowed to thaw and then gently oven-dried at 60°C for 30 minutes. The dried fecal boluses were then ground to a powder using a mortar and pestle. Fecal matter was weighed, and 0.2 grams of the ground feces was placed in a 5 mL tube, followed by the addition of 2 mL of 100% ethanol. The samples were vigorously mixed for 30 minutes by shaking at 300 rpm and vortexing all samples thoroughly. All samples were then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a clean tube, and the ethanol was removed through forced air evaporation using a Speed-Vac. The desiccated samples were stored at −20°C. To prepare the samples for assay, they were reconstituted with 100μL of ethanol and 400μL of assay buffer. The same sample was used for both hormone ELISA kits. All samples and standards were assayed in duplicate according to kit instructions.

Reproductive cycle tracking

Estrus cycles were tracked using vaginal cytology for three consecutive weeks for all females. Cytological swabs were collected daily between 10:00–11:00 AM. Mice were handled gently to minimize stress. Approximately 25 uL of PBS was gentled flushed into the vaginal opening several times (or until the liquid was cloudy) and then deposited onto a glass slide to dry. Cytological swabs were uniformly stained using JorVet DipQuick Stain Kit, and subsequently imaged at 40X under a microscope. Representative images for each slide were collected. Cytological swabs were examined for cell type and proportion to determine estrus stage. All females exhibited estrus cycles, and between 2–4 full cycles were tracked for each individual. Proestrus and estrus phases were grouped together (referred to here as the ‘estrus’ or fertile phase) to prevent bias in the scoring the images and corresponds to when females are close to ovulation and estrogen levels are high. Similarly, the metestrus and diestrus were grouped together (referred to as the ‘diestrus’ or non-fertile phase).

Odor plume simulations

Numerical simulations were performed via finite element discretization of the 3D diffusion equation. The COMSOL Multiphysics package was used to generate the mesh in the 3D model domain equations resulting from the weak (integral) form discretization of the governing equations. Urine spots had a fixed concentration boundary condition, with no-flux boundary conditions elsewhere.

General surgical procedures

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% induction, 1–2% maintenance, Kent Scientific SomnoSuite). They were placed on a heating pad ventral side up for gonadectomy surgeries and positioned in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments model 962) for virus injections. For all procedures, ophthalmic ointment (Puralube) was applied to eyes, buprenorphine (Buprenex, 0.15 mg/kg) was administered intramuscularly at the beginning of the procedure, and carprofen (Rimadyl, 5 mg/kg) and enrofloxacin (Baytril, 5 mg/kg) were administered subcutaneously towards the end of the procedure. Mice were monitored daily and given at least 21 days to recover from gonadectomy surgeries and 14 days for recovery from virus injection surgeries before behavioral testing. All surgeries were carried out on adult animals.

Gonadectomy surgeries

Castrations were carried out by making a single 1–3cm midline incision along lower abdomen after hair removal. Testes were gently exposed and separated from surrounding fat pads. Hemostats were used to clamp down on testicular blood vessels before cauterization to remove each testicle. After confirming hemostasis, fat pads were gently placed back in abdominal cavity and incision was closed with tissue glue and surgical staples. For ovariectomy surgeries, two separate incisions were made on lateral aspects of the abdomen just below the kidney. Ovaries were exposed, and hemostats were applied to the uterine horn proximal to the ovaries. Ovarian tissue was cauterized, and the uterus was placed back into the abdominal cavity after confirmation of hemostasis. Incisions were closed with tissue glue and surgical staples. All animals were allowed to recover on a heating pad for at least 30′ or until mobile.

Stereotactic surgeries

For chemogenetic inhibition of the BarEsr1 cells, AAVdj-CAG-FLEX-hM4Di-GFP (Addgene plasmid no. 52536, a gift from Scott Sternson) was produced by the Salk Institute Gene Transfer Targeting and Therapeutics Core (GT3) and injected bilaterally at 8 × 1012 GC/mL. In brief, following induction of anesthesia and placement in stereotax, the skulls of animals were exposed and levelled. Injections were carried out using pulled glass pipettes (inner diameter of 10–20 μm) and a Picospritzer at 25–100 nL/min. Craniotomies were made at AP-5.3mm, RC 2.5mm (linearly adjusted if Bregma-Lambda separation differed from 4.2mm) and the glass pipette was lowered to a depth of 3.2mm from the dura at a 33 mediolateral angle. 140–200nL of viral solution were injected each side, and pipettes were left in place for 10 minutes before retracting. Craniotomies were sealed with bonewax, and the skull was covered with dental cement following injections.

Chemogenetic inhibition

For chemogenetic inhibition studies, animals were habituated to injections with 0.9% sterile saline injection for 2 days prior to testing. CNO and Compound 21 solutions were prepared, aliquoted and stored at −20C prior to the experiment. On the day of the experiment, animals were injected with 140uL of sterile saline, 5mg/kg CNO (Hellobio HB6149), or 3mg/kg Compound 21 (Hellobio HB6124) 40–45 minutes before start of the assay. Assays were carried out as described previously using WT opposite sex partners. Inhibition index was calculated by dividing the number of spots deposited by an animal following C21 or CNO administration by the number of spots deposited by the same animal following vehicle administration. Following histological analysis, extent of AAV-FLEX-hM4Di infection was assessed. Animals with unilateral or suboptimal (<100 infected cells in Bar) infection were excluded from final analysis.

Immunostaining and histology

Animals used for anatomical characterization of BarEsr1 cells and chemogenetic inhibition experiments were transcardially perfused with cold PBS followed by 4% PFA solution. Brains were dissected and fixed in 4% PFA before obtaining coronal sections using a vibratome (50um sections for ESR1 staining, 100um sections for GFP staining). Sections were blocked with 1% BSA in 1% PBST for 3 hours before overnight RT incubation with Rabbit anti-ESR1 (Santa Cruz sc-542; diluted 1:500 in 1% BSA, 0.3%PBST solution) or Rabbit anti-GFP antibody (Invitrogen A11122; diluted 1:1000 in 1% BSA, 0.3% PBST solution). Sections were washed 3 times with 1% PBST and incubated for 3 hours with secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488 Goat anti-Rabbit; Invitrogen A11008) + Nissl (ThermoFisher NeuroTrace Blue/Deep Red diluted 1:200). Sections were washed thrice with PBST before mounting on glass slides using Prolong Glass (Thermofisher).

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Images were captured with Nikon A1 confocal microscope with a 10× air, or 20× air objective. Nikon Elements software settings were optimized for each experiment to maximize signal range, and z-stack maximum projections were used for representative images and analysis of anatomical localization of transfected cells. Sections containing the Barringtons Nucleus were identified by characteristic presence of Nissl boundaries lateral to the Locus Ceruleus and the dense ovoid shaped Nissl structure medial to the Locus Ceruleus. GFP+ cells in the Bar identified in three or six consecutive sections (100um or 50um apart) centered on −5.34 mm caudal to Bregma (Paxinos Mouse Brain Atlas) were counted using ImageJ (Cell Counter function).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistics were performed on Graphpad Prism. Unless otherwise noted in legends, equal variances and normal distributions for datasets was not assumed, and non-parametric versions of the Students t test (Mann-Whitney U-test) and 1-Way ANOVA (Kruskal-Wallis test) were used. Kruskal-Wallis tests were followed by Dunns multiple comparisons test to find pairwise differences. For categorical data analysis, Fishers Exact tests were used on raw numbers in each group. Principal Component Analysis was carried out following standardizing data for each metric across all animals in the analysis. Specific tests used for each analysis, sample sizes and p values are indicated in associated figure legends. Detailed statistical measures are listed in Table S1. No statistical predetermination of sample sizes was carried out, and sample sizes were determined by animal availability and numbers used in previous studies.47,48,57 Experimenters were not blinded to sex or marking status of experimental animals but estrus states where applicable were determined post experiment completion. Analysis of scent marking data and behavioral data was automated and therefore not subject to bias.
