Resolving the phase of a Dirac topological state via interferometric photoemission
Shiri Gvishi, Ittai Sidilkover, Shaked Rosenstein, Nir Hen Levin, Adi Peled, Omer Pasternak, Costel R. Rotundu, Ido Biran, Semën Gorfman, Naaman Amer, Hadas Soifer

Abstract
The electronic wavefunction is at the heart of physical phenomena, defining the frontiers of quantum materials research. While the amplitude of the electron wavefunction in crystals can be measured with state-of-the-art probes in unprecedented resolution, its phase has remained largely inaccessible, obscuring rich electronic information. Here we develop a quantum-path electron interferometer based on time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy, that enables the reconstruction of the phase of electronic states in quantum materials – with energy and momentum resolution. We demonstrate the scheme by resolving the phase along the Dirac electronic band of a prototypical topological insulator and observe a resonance-associated phase jump as well as a momentum and phase synchronized inversion revealing the helicity of the Dirac cone. We show the interferometer can be optically controlled by the polarization of the absorbed light, allowing a differential measurement of the phase – a crucial component for extracting phase information from an interferogram. This photo-electron-interferometer is a purely experimental scheme and does not rely on any specific theoretical model. It can be extended to a variety of materials, opening up the phase dimension in quantum materials research.



Retrieving the phase information of a quantum wave-function has been a long-standing challenge since the early days of quantum mechanics. While the phase of light has long been accessible through interferometry, unveiling the wave nature of photons and driving revolutions in optics, physics, and technology, its role in matter has remained far more elusive. Yet it is the phase of the electronic wavefunction that encodes the internal coherence, correlations, and topology that define the behavior of quantum materials
Keimer and Moore 2017
. From the Michelson–Morley experiment
Michelson and Morley 1887
, through electron double-slit interference
THOMSON and REID 1927
, attosecond interferometry
Azoury et al. 2019
, and gravitational-wave detection
Bailes et al. 2021
, the pursuit of phase has continually redefined the limits of measurement. Today, the frontier lies in retrieving the phase of electronic states within quantum materials, particularly near singularities such as the Dirac point, where the quantum geometry of the wavefunction gives rise to nontrivial topology and exotic responses
Yan and Zhang 2012
. Conventional probes such as STM (scanning tunneling microscopy
Binnig and Rohrer 1987
) and ARPES (angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy
Damascelli 2004
;
Lv et al. 2019
) provide access to amplitude information, revealing where electrons reside and how they move, but leave the phase, and with it the full complexity of the quantum state, experimentally unresolved. Revealing this hidden phase is therefore essential for capturing the full complexity of quantum matter and for uncovering the mechanisms by which topology, symmetry breaking, and coherence interplay at the most fundamental level.

Phase and temporal dynamics are inherently linked: the evolution of a quantum state is encoded in its time-dependent phase, and coherent light-matter interactions can be harnessed to resolve the electronic wave.
Time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (trARPES) is a natural candidate to access light-matter interactions in quantum materials. It incorporates the ultrafast pump-probe scheme – providing femtosecond temporal resolution – with ARPES – a cornerstone technique in quantum material research that maps the band structure of crystalline solids by photoemission
Boschini 2024
;
Gedik and Vishik 2017
. However, despite its ability to access energy-, momentum-, and time-resolved dynamics, trARPES typically resolves only incoherent population dynamics, where the phase information of the electronic wavefunction still remains inaccessible.

Retrieving this electronic phase requires an interferometric approach. However, unlike optical fields, constructing an interferometer for matter waves with full energy and momentum resolution poses extreme challenges, particularly under the cryogenic, ultrahigh-vacuum, and high-resolution conditions required for quantum material studies.
Instead of constructing a conventional interferometer where spatially separated beams interfere, we exploit
quantum-path (QP) interference between indistinguishable excitation pathways - to construct a nonlinear interferometer for electronic wavefunctions. Under suitable excitation conditions, two-photon photoemission (2PPE), or trARPES in its nonlinear regime, naturally supports multiple absorption pathways that can interfere coherently, analogous to other nonlinear-optical processes. Signatures of such QP interference have been observed in earlier trARPES and 2PPE studies
Eickhoff et al. 2011
;
Chan et al. 2011
, including interference between Floquet and Volkov states in strong-field-modulated bands
Mahmood et al. 2016
;
Bao et al. 2025
;
Choi et al. 2025
;
Merboldt et al. 2025
;
Fragkos et al. 2025
. Yet the momentum-dependent phase of the electronic states has remained unresolved, primarily due to the absence of controllable phase calibration. Coherent trARPES with phase-locked pump and probe pulses has provided access to dephasing and coherence times
Petek 2012
;
Tao et al. 2016
;
Reutzel et al. 2020
;
Reutzel et al. 2023
, but not to the angular phase structure that encodes the hidden geometry of the states.

In this manuscript, we introduce an interferometric scheme that enables the reconstruction of the phase of electronic states in quantum materials with simultaneous energy and momentum resolution. The interferometer is realized in trARPES in a weak-field (perturbative) regime, with degrees of control enabling the phase reconstruction.
We implement the interferometric scheme with
\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Se}_{3}
, which is one of the prototypical topological insulators
Xia et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
, providing a test bed for exploring the phase of topological states. We take advantage of the strong spin-orbit coupling in this material, which leads to strong and rich pump-polarization-induced dichroism
Ketterl et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2021
and helps us identify and isolate the contribution of interference to the overall signal. We focus on the second Dirac cone – a topological surface state in the unoccupied bands
Sobota et al. 2013
;
Soifer et al. 2019
–
and reconstruct its energy- and momentum-dependent phase, showing the phase modulation near the singular Dirac point. By combining femtosecond resolution with interferometric phase sensitivity, our approach captures not only electronic band structures and populations, but also the hidden quantum geometry that governs them.

.1
Concept of controllable quantum-path interferometer in two-photon photoemission

We start by introducing the concept of controllable QP interference in a 2PPE process excited by two pulses of different photon energies: one low-photon-energy (LE) pulse and one high-photon-energy (HE) pulse.
Generally, in a two-color two-photon absorption process, there are two possible pathways, depending on the order of photon absorption (see Fig.
1
(a)).
The first quantum-path (QP1) we consider is the one commonly observed in trARPES experiments: the LE pulse excites an electron from a populated initial state to an intermediate (unoccupied) state, and the HE pulse further excites this electron to a free electron final state, leading to photoemission. The second quantum-path (QP2) is the opposite excitation pathway: the HE pulse excites an electron to a high-energy intermediate state, and the LE pulse consequently photoemits the electron from this highly excited state to a free electron final state. This second pathway is typically absent in trARPES, and is only ”active” in specific conditions that will be discussed below.

Temporally, both pathways can occur when the two pulses overlap in time.
Energetically, when exciting from the same initial state the two pathways will lead to the same final electron (kinetic) energy, as long as the electron has not scattered between the first and second excitation steps. Therefore, the two QPs lead to the same final free electron state (Fig.
1
(a)) where they interfere.
Since the QPs start and end at the same electronic state, the phase difference between them is determined only by the intermediate states. Importantly, each of the QPs includes one photon from each of the two pulses – therefore any phase jitter between the pulses will be identical for the two pathways, and will cancel out – hence phase stabilization of the pulses is not needed in order for QP interference to be resolved.

The observation of an interference signature in itself is not enough for resolving phase. For phase calibration, it is necessary to have some degree of control, such as changing the length of one of the interferometer’s arms, or removing and inserting into the interferometer the element with the unknown phase.
Such control can be hard to achieve with a QP electron interferometer as opposed to a spatial optical interferometer. For example, this has been a major challenge in attosecond science, where much of the temporal information is hidden in the phase of the emitted XUV radiation, and many strategies have been developed for extracting this phase
Uzan et al. 2020
;
Orfanos et al. 2019
.
In order to achieve the required controllability in a trARPES setup, we aim for the interferometer scheme outlined in Fig.
1
(c), where the phase of interest is that of QP1, while QP2 serves as a reference arm with near-constant phase and amplitude. In QP1, an intermediate state is resonantly excited by the LE pulse, before photoemission by the HE pulse. For the second path, which serves as the reference arm, we have a few requirements: The phase of this path needs to have as little structure as possible (in energy and momentum), and should therefore be a non-resonant 2PPE pathway, i.e., have a virtual intermediate state as in Fig.
1
(a). However, in order for this non-resonant transition to have a non-negligible amplitude, it needs to be energetically close to some bound state. Here we will use the polarization selection-rules of such a close-by bound state in order to turn ON and OFF the interfering arm, therefore providing all the information needed for phase reconstruction.

.2
Quantum-path interferometer in a topological insulator

We implement this electron-QP-interferometer scheme in
\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Se}_{3}
, with LE and HE pulses of 3 eV and 6 eV photon energy, respectively, in the experimental geometry as described in Fig.
1
(b) (see Methods for further details).

The spectrum of QP1 is the standard trARPES pump-probe spectrum
Sobota et al. 2012
;
Sobota et al. 2013
;
Ketterl et al. 2018
;
Soifer et al. 2019
, with the range of relevant initial and intermediate states sketched in Fig.
1
(d) bottom.
The second path is often absent in trARPES, both because of pulse-energy considerations (see Methods), and because it requires the presence of high-energy bound states close to the vacuum level, where the band density is typically lower. In
\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Se}_{3}
, such a high-lying state exists about 4.8 eV above the Dirac point (0.63 eV below the vacuum level, see arrow in panel (d)). This is the Image Potential State (IPS), which is a generic surface state typical to metals
Dose 1987
;
Aeschlimann et al. 2025
, and has been well characterized in
\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Se}_{3}
Sobota et al. 2012
;
Niesner et al. 2014
;
Bugini et al. 2017
.
Therefore, QP2 involves excitation with a HE photon into or close to the IPS, and photoemission via a LE photon (Fig.
1
(d)). The experimental signature of the second path – when it does go through a resonance – is a time-reversed trARPES signal which can overlap on the detector with much lower-lying states (Fig.
1
(d) top). The overlap on the detector of these two paths is well known
Reutzel et al. 2020
;
Biedermann et al. 2012
;
Bugini et al. 2017
; however, it is not always appreciated that it is not just the signal intensity summing up on the detector, but a coherent sum of the two signals, which can then lead to interference – even though the intermediate states are at very different energies.

Importantly, the coupling to the IPS in
\mathrm{Bi}_{2}\mathrm{Se}_{3}
is strongly dependent on polarization
Wolf et al. 1999
: with S-polarized excitation there is close to zero coupling to the state, whereas with P-polarization the coupling is strong (see Methods). This strong polarization dependence provides us with an ON/OFF switch for the interference: when the HE beam is S-polarized, there is no (or very weak) coupling to the IPS, QP2 is disabled, and only the intensity of QP1 is measured on the detector. With a P-polarized HE beam, there is a strong coupling to this state, QP2 is activated, and the signal on the detector comprises of the resulting intensity of the two interfering paths:
|
QP1+QP2
|^{2}
(Fig.
1
(d) top). It is important to note that even when the HE excitation is not resonant with the transition into the IPS, the coupling to the IPS still significantly increases the probability of this two-photon transition, and therefore the second path is active in P-polarization and inactive in S, even in far-from-resonance scenarios.

This system can therefore be used as an electronic interferometer as presented schematically in Fig.
1
(c), where the input state is an electron in an occupied band. This electronic wavefunction is split into two paths by the optical excitation: LE excitation to one arm, and HE to the other, with the polarization of HE determining the splitting ratio between the two paths. The electron on the HE side (QP2) undergoes a non-resonant transition, providing the reference phase. On the LE side (QP1), it crosses a resonant state such that the phase of this state is imprinted on the electronic wavefunction. Then absorption of a HE photon brings it to the same electronic final state as the reference arm, imprinting the interference between the two arms on the intensity that is finally measured on the detector.
In analogy to optical imaging interferometers which can spatially resolve the phase for different image locations, here we resolve the electronic phase in energy-momentum space.

.3
Identifying interference in trARPES data

We begin by characterizing our energy-momentum resolved interferometer. The spectrum presented in Fig.
1
(d) top, includes contributions from QP1 – red dashed lines – as well as contributions from QP2 – green dashed line, however it is unclear how far from its (IPS) resonance QP2 could influence the signal. Moreover, we expect the interference to be visible only in energy-momentum regions where QP1 is resonant, since other non-resonantly excited states, i.e. states into which electrons have scattered, will not maintain the electron’s initial phase and will not contribute to the interference. Ideally, the subtraction of the reference spectrum – where QP2 is turned off by using S-polarized HE pulse – would highlight all the regions that are affected by the interference. However, one needs to be more careful due to additional matrix element effects in photoemission: when using S- vs P- polarization in photoemission, different bands may be highlighted depending on their orbital character
Moser 2017
;
Moser 2023
;
Sidilkover et al. 2025
.

\sigma_{+}

I^{(\pm,S/P)}(k_{x},E)

k_{x}
and kinetic energy

|M^{\pm}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}

\alpha

\sigma_{\pm}

|M^{S/P}_{\alpha}(k_{x},E)|^{2}

I^{(\pm,S/P)}(k_{x},E)\propto|M^{\pm}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}\cdot|M^{S/P}_{\alpha}(k_{x},E)|^{2}.

(1)

However, the contributions of the photoemission matrix element
M^{S/P}_{\alpha}(k_{x},E)

I_{\mathrm{CD_{norm}}}^{(S/P)}(k_{x},E)=\frac{I^{(-,S/P)}(k_{x},E)-I^{(+,S/P)}(k_{x},E)}{I^{(-,S/P)}(k_{x},E)+I^{(+,S/P)}(k_{x},E)}\propto\frac{|M^{-}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}-|M^{+}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}}{|M^{-}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}+|M^{+}_{i,\alpha}(k_{x})|^{2}}.

(2)

Therefore, if just one QP is present and there are no interference effects,
I_{\mathrm{CD_{norm}}}(k_{x},E)

The normalized-CD spectra for both S- and P-polarization of the HE pulse are shown in Fig.
2
(a,b). Differences between the two spectra are clearly visible, demonstrating the contributions of interfering paths to the photoemission signal. To highlight the areas of the spectrum where the interference is significant we plot in panel (c) the k-symmetrized difference between these CD-spectra:
|I_{\mathrm{CD_{norm}}}^{(S)}(k_{x},E)-I_{\mathrm{CD_{norm}}}^{(P)}(k_{x},E)|
. Here, the non-white areas represent the spectral regions that are influenced by QP interference, where the three most prominent features are marked by colored rectangles. These three regions all represent resonant transitions of QP1 as seen by the overlap of initial states (upshifted by LE photon energy, marked by dashed lines) and intermediate states (solid black lines). However, the excitation the electron undergoes in QP2 is quite different in each of the three regions. We consider each of them, from top to bottom.

In region 1 (blue square in Fig.
2
(c)), the initial state is only
\sim 0.5
eV below the Fermi level, and 5.94 eV below the vacuum level. This means that the HE photon (
\hbar\omega=5.96
eV) in QP2 excites the electron above
E_{\mathrm{vac}}
into the free electron continuum, see excitation diagram #1 in Fig.
2
(d). Consequently, QP2 could not serve as a clean reference, mainly because of the different polarization dependence: since the excitation into the free electron continuum does not depend on the IPS matrix element, we no longer have the ability to turn the interfering path QP2 on and off. Indeed, we see interference signatures in this spectral region for both S- and P- polarizations of the HE.

The second region (magenta square) reveals a very strong interference effect, involving two neighboring intermediate states (black solid lines) and several possible initial states (dashed lines). Moreover, the IPS (in green) also overlaps with the same bands, meaning that QP2 also undergoes a resonant two-photon process, with the HE pulse directly exciting the electron from the initial state into the IPS (diagram #2 in panel (d)). This is therefore the region where we expect the largest contribution of QP2, as indeed is visible in the strong interference signature in panel (c), but also when looking at the raw 2PPE intensity data (Fig.
2
(e)). The top panel shows the intensity measured with S-polarized HE pulse, and the bottom panel with P-polarized HE, where a minimum in the photoemission intensity is clearly visible – indicating destructive interference.
This result is a strong signature for two-path interference, however, it occurs in conditions that are not ideal for interferometric phase reconstruction. First, because the interference is too strong, with the destructive interference obliterating much of the relevant signal. And second, QP2 here is also resonant, which means it does not provide a constant phase-reference for QP1.

We now consider the lowest interference region (red rectangle in (c) and diagram #3 in (d)). Here we have in QP1 exactly one initial state resonantly excited into one intermediate state – which is moreover a Dirac topological surface state
Sobota et al. 2013
. QP2 here is far from resonance, therefore the phase accumulated along this path should have negligible energy or momentum dependence. Here, QP2 can work well as the reference path for phase reconstruction of QP1, and the rest of this paper focuses on this region.

We now proceed to explore more closely the CD of the signal in this region in both cases. In the one-path case (S-pol HE, Fig.
3
(c)), the LE CD is attributed to asymmetric excitation matrix elements due to the orbital angular momentum texture in the topological state
Soifer et al. 2019
. The dichroic signal is maximal at the resonance, where most of the electrons originate from direct optical coupling rather than from secondary scattering processes, thereby conserving their momentum polarization.
When examining the CD-spectrum of the two-path case (Fig.
3
(d)), a drastic difference is observed: two additional sign changes appear, exactly on the centers of the bands (see also comparison between energy distribution curves (EDCs) in panels (e-f)). As discussed in Fig.
2
, matrix-element effects cannot account for differences in CD structure between S- and P-polarization spectra. Here this is also evident in the P-pol CD spectrum alone: matrix elements can differ between bands, or in some cases within a band when it changes character at different k-regions, but can never cause such a sharp change across the band maximum.

How then could interference between two QPs create such a signal, in particular when QP2 should be relatively constant? The sharp sign change across the band maximum is reminiscent of Fano-type signals, where an interference between a resonant transition and a continuum creates strong intensity modulation around the resonance energy
Fano 1961
;
Litvinenko et al. 2021
. Similarly, in our case, there is one resonant path (QP1) and a second path with a flat-phase contribution. The intensity modulation in such cases stems from a
\pi
-phase-step, which is expected when crossing a resonance
Hazi 1979
;
Dudovich et al. 2001
, leading to a switch between constructive to destructive interference below and above the resonance. The phase-step is present in QP1 with or without the interference with QP2,
however, it does not influence the measured intensity without the second path to interfere with.
This data is therefore a conclusive indication that our QP interferometer is sensitive to the phase accumulated in the resonant transition between the initial state and the unoccupied Dirac cone.

To further highlight the connection between the resonant transition in QP1 to the sign change across the band in the CD of the two-path case, we tune the photon energy of the LE pulse from 3.08 to 3.53 eV. Fig.
3
(g) shows the expected shift of the resonance both in energy and momentum, according to the dispersions of both initial and intermediate states. The one-path case (S-pol HE, Fig.
3
(h)) displays a strong CD signal which has shifted as expected in comparison to panel (c). And indeed the two-path case (P-pol HE, panel (i)) displays a sign flip across the band maximum, at the new energy-momentum position of the resonance. This demonstrates that not only can we turn ON and OFF the interference by switching the HE pulse polarization from P to S, but we can also
control
the energy-momentum position of the interference by tuning the LE resonance wavelength.

.5
Phase reconstruction

We now proceed to use our interferometer to reconstruct the phase accumulated in QP1.
We denote by
\psi_{1}
(
\psi_{2}
) the electronic wavefunction at the output of QP1 (QP2), and by
\Delta\phi
the phase difference between them. The intensity we measure for the two HE polarizations is therefore:

I_{S}=|A\cdot\psi_{1}|^{2}

I_{P}=|\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}|^{2}=|\psi_{1}|^{2}+|\psi_{2}|^{2}+2|\psi_{1}||\psi_{2}|\cos(\Delta\phi),

where
|A|
is the matrix-element ratio between photoemission with S- and P-polarized HE pulses.
Using these two equations, we can extract the phase difference
\Delta\phi
:

\cos(\Delta\phi)=\frac{I_{p}-|\psi_{1}|^{2}-|\psi_{2}|^{2}}{2|\psi_{1}||\psi_{2}|}=\frac{I_{p}-\left|\frac{1}{A}\right|^{2}I_{s}-|\psi_{2}|^{2}}{2\left|\frac{1}{A}\right|\sqrt{I_{s}}|\psi_{2}|},

(3)

where

|\psi_{2}|^{2}\sim 0.3
is the intensity contribution of QP2 and
\left|1/A\right|^{2}\sim 1.75
(see Methods).
In order to reconstruct
\cos(\Delta\phi)
, both the one-path and the two-path measurements are needed, highlighting the importance of the ability to turn ON/OFF the interference.

This is not a straightforward process since for every value of

I
Discussion

We have directly resolved the cosine of the phase of a Dirac topological state in energy and momentum for two different polarizations, and used this measurement to reconstruct the phase, which reveals three contributions.

The first component is a
\pi
-phase shift which follows the Dirac band dispersion and is associated with the resonant optical transition. This phase shift leads to a Fano-like lineshape, which is clearly visible in the CD spectrum. Fully resolving Fano resonances in various systems is an ongoing effort in photonics research
Heeg et al. 2015
;
Limonov et al. 2017
;
Litvinenko et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2025
, and our observation opens up a new avenue for their detection and control in condensed matter systems. This is an example of the rich information about the electronic wavefunction revealed by the use of CD in ARPES. And indeed, in recent seminal works, CD-ARPES and related measures have been used to reveal orbital information in TMDCs
Beaulieu et al. 2020
;
Schüler 2022
;
Beaulieu et al. 2024
;
Schusser et al. 2024
. In our work, the strong spin-orbit coupling and resulting CD signal were helpful for delineating interference and matrix element effects, but were not a crucial part of the interferometer scheme. Indeed, we have reconstructed the phase for each LE polarization independently.

The second phase component we resolve is
a sharp sign inversion
at the Dirac point, which can be associated with the spin and orbital angular-momentum texture characteristic of the helical Dirac cone. The unique phase structure could be used to identify topological states in new materials. It is interesting to note that due to the sharp phase changes in momentum and energy, any optical interference measurement (or other momentum-integrating methods) on this state would supress the signal, and would not reveal any of the structure. In addition to these two energy-momentum varying features, we also resolve a global
\pi/2
-phase shift between the two LE circular polarizations. Such a global phase shift could originate either from QP1 or QP2, whereas the energy-momentum dependent phase changes could only originate from the resonant QP1, as highlighted by the clear linear structure following the Dirac band dispersion.

This interferometric scheme is applicable to many material systems. The IPS state is a generic surface state that appears in many materials
Echenique et al. 2002
;
Borca and Zandvliet 2024
, and can therefore be used widely. The excitation itself is perturbative, requiring low laser intensities and frequencies in the visible to UV ranges, available in many experimental labs.
Importantly, the phase reconstruction is purely experimental, and does not rely on a model of the underlying electronic bands. Therefore, energy-momentum-resolved electron interferometry can now become another widespread tool in the study of quantum materials, opening a new dimension for resolving their complex electronic states.

The requirement that QP2 is non-resonant with the intermediate state means that the energy of the initial state plus one HE photon should still be within the energy range of the material’s bound states (
E_{inter}=E_{\mathrm{init}}+\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{HE}}<E_{vac}

E_{vac}

E_{\mathrm{inter}}>E_{\mathrm{F}}

\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{HE}}-\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LE}}<E_{\mathrm{vac}}-E_{\mathrm{F}}
, where
\Phi=E_{\mathrm{vac}}-E_{\mathrm{F}}
is the work-function of the material.
The work-function of the samples used in our experiments is
\Phi=5.44
eV, and therefore the energy difference of
\sim 3
eV between the pulses fits the requirement.
