Advancements in sagittal alignment parameters and management of adult spine deformities
Authors: Jordan A Bauer, Rohan Rajan, Rajendra Singh, Marissa Gedman, Alexander K Hahn, Jason Dacruz, Hanbing Zhou, Cameron Kia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdult spinal deformity (ASD) is increasingly recognized as a condition in which sagittal alignment plays a role in pain, disability, and mechanical failure. Historically, sagittal correction strategies relied on global radiographic parameters such as sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and pelvic incidence-lumbar lordosis (PI-LL) mismatch. While these measures demonstrated important associations with health-related quality of life, they incompletely account for compensated deformities, segmental mechanics, and directionality of correction. Over the past decade, alignment planning has evolved toward more individualized frameworks that incorporate pelvic morphology, age, segmental lordosis distribution, and direction-sensitive metrics. Outcome-derived strategies, such as age-adjusted alignment targets, have contributed valuable insights but remain controversial and are not universally accepted as definitive sagittal goals. Radiographic parameters, including the L1 pelvic angle (L1PA) and T4–L1-hip axis, provide reproducible, intraoperatively modifiable feedback and improved prediction of mechanical failure risk when applied with appropriate clinical context. This review synthesizes the evolution of sagittal alignment strategies in ASD, highlighting the strengths and limitations of historical global metrics, age-adjusted frameworks, and emerging vector-based and segmental alignment concepts. Emphasis is placed on integrated, patient-specific approaches that balance global, segmental, and directional parameters to optimize outcomes.Keywords: Adult spinal deformity (ASD), sagittal alignment, pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK)

Introduction

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) encompasses a heterogeneous group of degenerative, idiopathic, and iatrogenic conditions that result in pain, disability, impaired function, and reduced quality of life (1-3). Evidence has shown that positive sagittal balance [increased sagittal vertical axis (SVA)] is correlated with pain, disability, gait dysfunction, and reduced global compensation capacity in ASD. Glassman et al. demonstrated that increasing positive sagittal balance is linearly associated with worsening pain and disability scores, as measured by the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) and Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) questionnaires (4). Other studies support that greater sagittal malalignment is associated with higher rates of low back pain, impaired physical performance, and diminished ability to compensate for spinal imbalance (5-7). However, it is important to note that patients with normal or hypernormal SVAs often have sagittal imbalance and this measurement does not consider compensated deformities.

While early classification systems emphasized coronal deformity, it is now recognized that sagittal alignment is the primary determinant of health-related quality of life (HRQoL), functional decline, and mechanical failure in ASD. Traditional sagittal alignment assessment relied on global parameters such as SVA, pelvic tilt (PT), and pelvic incidence-lumbar lordosis (PI-LL) mismatch, which have demonstrated strong associations with pain and disability (1,2,8-11). These formed the basis for widely adopted classification systems, including the SRS-Schwab classification (8-11). However, these global measurements incompletely capture compensated deformities and neither account for segmental lordosis distribution nor provide direction-sensitive guidance for intraoperative correction (2,12,13).

Over the past decade, the field has transitioned away from uniform alignment thresholds towards more individualized strategies that incorporate pelvic morphology, age, and increasingly, segmental and vector-based alignment principles (14). Outcome-driven frameworks such as age-adjusted alignment targets sought to explain variability in postoperative outcomes, specifically proximal junctional kyphosis and failure (PJK/PJF), but have also introduced controversy due to their reliance on global parameters and potential for under- or overcorrection (14-17). More recently, attention has shifted towards direction-sensitive, intraoperatively modifiable parameters, such as L1 pelvic angle (L1PA) and T4–L1-hip axis, that better intergrade spinal and pelvic orientation and align more closely with segmental alignment principles (18).

Additionally, machine learning approaches have refined morphological classification and PI-based correction criteria, further individualizing surgical planning (19). These advances collectively support a modern, patient-specific approach to sagittal realignment in ASD, integrating PI, age, and multiple radiographic parameters to optimize outcomes and minimize complications (2,18,20).

The purpose of this review is to synthesize the evolution of sagittal alignment strategies in ASD, highlighting the strengths and limitations of historical, outcome-derived, and emerging alignment philosophies. Emphasis is placed on the growing role of segmental alignment and vector-based parameters, the ongoing controversy surrounding age-adjusted targets, and how technological advances have improved the reproducibility and alignment execution rather than redefining alignment goals themselves.

Foundational concepts in sagittal alignment

Spinopelvic parameters

Pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), PI-LL mismatch, PT, sacral slope (SS), SVA, and T1 pelvic angle (TPA) are foundational concepts in sagittal alignment for adult spinal deformity and are essential elements in preoperative planning. PI is measured by the sum of PT and SS and is a fixed anatomical parameter that determines the amount of LL required for balanced alignment (Figure 1). The relationship between PI and LL (PI-LL mismatch) has historically been used to guide sagittal correction; however, it has limitations that will be elucidated in this review. A mismatch greater than 10° is associated with sagittal malalignment and worse clinical outcomes. PT and SS are dynamic compensatory parameters that reflect the pelvis’s ability to maintain upright posture when the spine loses lordosis. SVA is a global measure of sagittal balance, with increased SVA correlating with pain and disability. TPA integrates both spinal and pelvic compensation and is increasingly used to guide surgical correction, with thresholds for correction linked to improved outcomes (2,14,21-26). These parameters are measured on standing full-spine radiographs and are strongly correlated with HRQoL, overall disability, and surgical outcomes (2,25,27). Modern classification systems, such as the SRS-Schwab classification, incorporate these spinopelvic parameters to guide individualized surgical planning and realignment strategies (2,25). Multiple prospective studies and meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that surgical correction of sagittal malalignment in adult spinal deformity leads to significant improvements in HRQoL and functional outcomes, especially when alignment targets are appropriately individualized. Smith et al. (2016) found that, at two years, operative patients had significantly better ODI, SRS-22r, and SF-36 scores than matched patients who were treated nonoperatively, with improvements strongly associated with correction of sagittal parameters such as PI-LL mismatch and SVA (28). Scheer et al. (2018) further quantified this improvement, showing that surgery resulted in significantly greater quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained at 1, 2, and 3 years postoperatively compared to nonoperative management (29).

Prior studies reinforce these findings and have shown that deformity-correction surgery for primary degenerative sagittal imbalance produces clinically meaningful improvements in pain and disability, with the degree of LL restoration directly correlating with ODI improvements (4,10,11,26). For severe deformities, osteotomies have been shown to be useful modality in alignment correction. Tarawneh et al. (2020) found that pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) for severe sagittal deformity led to significant improvements in both ODI and SRS scores, achieving minimal clinically important differences (30).

Emerging parameters: global vs. segmental alignment concepts

Over the past decade, recent studies have attempted to define the impact of different alignment targets. Scheer et al. (2018) and Park et al. (2025) demonstrated that matching age-adjusted PI-LL and SVA targets yields improved HRQoL outcomes, while undercorrection leads to worse results and overcorrection increases complication risk without additional benefit (15,16). Williamson et al. (2023) identified radiographic thresholds (e.g., PI-LL >10°, TPA >20°) above which sagittal correction is most likely to produce substantial clinical improvement (26). Traditional sagittal alignment goals (PI-LL within ±10°, SVA <5 cm) are being brought into question and some literature supports using individualized, age-adjusted targets, based on evidence demonstrating that older patients tolerate greater residual malalignment and that overcorrection increases the risk of PJK and PJF. Lafage et al. (2016) proposed that ideal spinopelvic parameters (PI-LL, PT, SVA) should be less strict with increasing age, as older patients have greater compensation and tolerate more malalignment without worse outcomes (31). Other studies have supported this, showing that alignment targets can be individualized by age and PI, and that correction to age-appropriate functional alignment reduces PJK and PJF rates without sacrificing HRQoL (14,32). Large cohort analyses and systematic reviews demonstrate associations between postoperative alignment within age-adjusted thresholds and improved HRQoL and reduced mechanical complications; however, these findings derive from global, outcome-based frameworks with limitations. Overcorrection relative to age-adjusted PI-LL has been shown to increase PJK and PJF risk, and be associated with worse clinical outcomes, while matched correction yields the best long-term ODI and SRS-22 scores (16,17,33,34). Zhang et al. (2024) showed that optimal PI-LL targets should be adjusted upward with age, and that the relationship between PI-LL and HRQoL is non-linear and age-dependent (35). Ani et al. (2024) and Protopsaltis et al. (2023) demonstrated that patient-specific, age-adjusted alignment targets minimize mechanical failures and optimize functional outcomes (32,36). A systematic review by Quarto et al. (2024) synthesized these findings, showing that matched age-adjusted alignment reduces mechanical complications (especially PJK) and achieves similar HRQoL compared to traditional targets, with overcorrection leading to higher complication rates (37). Ultimately, existing literature suggests that age-adjusted PI-LL targets may reduce mechanical complication risk in select populations; however, the lack of directionality in this framework limits its applicability (16,17,38-40).

In contrast, sagittal alignment parameters, such as L1PA and T4–L1-hip axis, are measurements demonstrating that both undercorrection and overcorrection of sagittal alignment lead to worse clinical outcomes (15,16,18); whereas age-adjusted alignment targets encourage less aggressive correction in older patients, based on the premise that alignment goals should match age-specific normative values (14). Hills et al. (2024) demonstrated that realignment to a normal L1PA (PI × 0.5 − 19°) and minimizing T4–L1PA mismatch (–3° to +1°) significantly reduced mechanical failure risk, including PJK, rod fracture, and reoperation, in long-construct ASD surgery. These parameters provide feedback on whether sagittal goals have been achieved intraoperatively, and under- and overcorrection were independently associated with increased mechanical complications, regardless of age. This study also showed a correlation between changes in T4PA and global sagittal alignment, and that higher postoperative C2 pelvic angle (a related global measure) was associated with worse patient-reported outcomes (18).

Other studies support these findings. Joseph et al. (2025) further validated the T4–L1-hip axis error (HAE) as a direction-sensitive predictor of mechanical failure, outperforming L1PA error alone. HAE remained predictive across both long and short segment fusions, and its measurement was recommended for routine intraoperative use to minimize postoperative complications (41). Chanbour et al. (2023) found that achieving ideal L1PA was associated with lower rates of rod fracture and pseudarthrosis, supporting its utility as a practical intraoperative target. L1PA was also modestly correlated with SVA and T1PA, reinforcing its role in integrating spinal and pelvic orientation (42). Another study by Hills et al. (2025) showed that the T4–L1-hip axis objectifies the Roussouly classification using continuous measures, improving precision and accuracy in alignment planning compared to categorical systems (43). Finally, Vaynrub et al. (2018) showed that intraoperative measurement of L1PA and thoracic pelvic angles is more reliable than traditional LL measures, with excellent interobserver and intraobserver reliability, making these parameters practical for real-time surgical correction (44). Collectively, these sources support the use of L1PA and the T4–L1-hip axis as sensitive, reproducible, and clinically relevant measures for guiding sagittal realignment and reducing mechanical failure risk in ASD surgery. Despite some literature supporting improved outcomes associated with age-adjusted alignment, this strategy remains a global, outcome-derived framework and does not account for directionality or lordosis distribution (14,31,45). However, segmental alignment guidelines emphasize the importance of lordosis distribution (46). Smaller preoperative and postoperative lower lordosis arcs (L4–S1) were independently associated with mechanical complications requiring reoperation (OR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.99) (47). The lower lordosis arc has been suggested to be more critical than the upper arc for avoiding mechanical complications (47). Compensating for inadequate lower arc lordosis at proximal levels (T10–L2) resulted in higher rates of PJF (32.8% vs. 16.0% in undercorrected, P<0.001), while undercorrection at these levels led to increased implant failure requiring revision (14.2% vs. 6.4% in overcorrected, P=0.025) (48). Additionally, cranial shifts in lordosis apex were associated with smaller L4–S1 lordosis and greater 2-year back pain (46).

Ultimately, there is support and controversy within the literature surrounding global and segmental alignment parameters. Age-adjusted alignment and L1PA/T4–L1-hip axis aim to avoid overcorrection at a population level and identify direction-specific misalignment on an individual level, respectively. These different strategies are not interchangeable and may generate different correction targets. The optimal approach to sagittal alignment parameters likely involves integrating both frameworks, using age-adjusted targets to avoid overcorrection while ensuring adequate segmental lordosis distribution, particularly in the lower arc. In summary, prospective studies and meta-analyses demonstrate that surgical correction of sagittal malalignment in ASD significantly improves pain, disability, and overall quality of life, with optimal outcomes achieved when individualized alignment targets are met (2,21-23,27).

Surgical approaches for achieving optimal alignment

Combined anterior-posterior approaches

Combined anterior-posterior approaches, specifically anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) at the L5–S1 level, is associated with improved restoration of distal LL and mitigates the risk of PJK in ASD. A large multicenter study by Passias et al. found that combined approaches, particularly those incorporating L5–S1 ALIF, were most effective at achieving complex realignment goals, including optimal lumbar shape and proportion. Notably, patients who underwent combined anterior-posterior surgery with L5–S1 ALIF had higher rates of meeting clinical improvement thresholds and significantly lower rates of PJF compared to posterior-only approaches (odds ratio for PJF: 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2–0.8) (49).

Systematic reviews and comparative studies further support that L5–S1 ALIF provides greater restoration of segmental and global LL than posterior only techniques, with a more physiologic distribution of lordosis and improved spinopelvic parameters (50-54). This restoration of distal lordosis is critical for achieving age and PI-adjusted alignment targets and reducing compensatory stresses at the proximal junction, which are significantly implicated in the development of junctional kyphosis (50-54). A recent study also demonstrates that L5–S1 ALIF, when used as part of a combined approach, can achieve lordosis correction comparable to more invasive osteotomies but with fewer intraoperative and perioperative complications (55). This literature supports that combined anterior-posterior approaches, especially with L5–S1 ALIF, effectively restores distal LL and reduces the risk of PJK and PJF in patients with ASD.

Osteotomies

Rigid adult spinal deformity is characterized by limited flexibility, often due to ankylosis, prior fusion, or severe degenerative changes. In these cases, osteotomies are critical for restoring sagittal and coronal balance when less invasive techniques are insufficient (56-58). Osteotomies remain an important tool for the management of rigid spinal deformities, with the choice of technique predicated on the desired angular correction required and the flexibility of adjacent spinal segments (56-58). Both Smith-Peterson Osteotomy (SPO) and PSO are established methods for achieving realignment in severe, fixed deformities.

SPO is a posterior column osteotomy that relies on mobility of the anterior disc and typically provides 10–15° of correction per level. It is best suited for patients with residual anterior column flexibility and can be performed at multiple levels to achieve cumulative correction (57,59-62). PSO, in contrast, is a three-column osteotomy that removes the posterior elements and vertebral body, offering 30–40° of correction per level (57,59-62). PSO is indicated for more severe, focal deformities, especially when the anterior column is fused or ankylosed, such as in ankylosing spondylitis or after prior fusion (57-61,63,64).

Comparative studies and meta-analyses show that both SPO and PSO can achieve significant radiographic correction with similar complication rates, however, PSO is associated with greater blood loss and operative time (58,61-63,65). In select cases, combining SPO and PSO can achieve even greater correction, particularly in severe deformities requiring >60° of angular change (66). Ultimately SPO and PSO remain to be effective options within the armamentarium of contemporary management of rigid and severe adult spinal deformities.

Minimally invasive approaches

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) for ASD has been shown to have benefits, such as reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and lower complication rates. However, achieving large sagittal corrections remains a challenge with MIS alone, especially in rigid deformity cases. Hybrid techniques, combining MIS interbody fusion with open osteotomies or posterior spinal fusion (PSF), has been shown to be a promising compromise that improves sagittal alignment correction while retaining some advantages of MIS.

Multiple studies support these points. MIS techniques are associated with less blood loss, faster recovery, and fewer major complications compared to open surgery, but open or hybrid approaches generally achieve greater improvements in LL and pelvic parameters, especially in severe deformity or when large sagittal corrections are needed (67-70). For example, a systematic review found that while MIS reduced morbidity, open surgery provided greater correction of LL and PT, and hybrid approaches offered intermediate results (68,69).

Hybrid strategies, such as combining lateral or anterior interbody fusion with open posterior column osteotomies or fixation, can achieve sagittal correction comparable to open techniques, with less blood loss and lower complication rates than open surgery alone (71-76). Lee et al. demonstrated that multilevel minimally invasive interbody fusion with a posterior column osteotomy could achieve similar sagittal balance and lordosis correction as PSO, but with less blood loss and fewer pseudarthroses (72). Strom et al. found that combining lateral interbody fusion with open posterior surgery resulted in faster recovery, fewer complications, and greater pain relief than open surgery alone, with intermediate lordosis correction (76). Overall, hybrid techniques afford the advantage of achieving large sagittal correction while balancing the benefits of MIS with the corrective power of open approaches.

Technological innovations enhancing alignment precision

Advanced imaging and patient-specific preoperative planning

Patient-specific preoperative planning software that integrates EOS imaging and three-dimensional (3D) models enables personalized adjustment of lordosis distribution and facilitates improved predictions of postoperative alignment in adult spinal deformity surgery (Figure 3). Recent studies demonstrate that virtual surgical planning software (VSPS) using calibrated radiographs and machine learning models can simulate postoperative spinopelvic parameters, including lordosis distribution, and predict compensatory changes. In a comparative analysis, VSPS accurately predicted achieved spinal alignment at 3 months, particularly for LL and TPA, and independently predicted achievement of age-adjusted alignment targets, supporting its utility for individualized surgical planning and alignment optimization (77). Similarly, patient-specific musculoskeletal modeling frameworks have shown high accuracy (correlation coefficients 0.86–0.95) in predicting postoperative sagittal alignment, further validating the clinical value of these technologies for preoperative planning and outcome prediction (78).

Advanced imaging techniques, including full-body EOS images, dynamic evaluation, and 3D modeling, offer superior assessment of compensatory mechanisms and postural changes. EOS bi-planar imaging provides low-dose, full-body, weight-bearing 3D reconstructions, allowing for precise measurement of segmental and global spinal parameters, pelvic orientation, and compensatory mechanisms such as pelvic retroversion, hip extension, and knee flexion (2,79). These imaging modalities enable visualization and quantification of how patients recruit extra-spinal compensations in response to sagittal malalignment, which is critical for comprehensive assessment and surgical planning (2). Dynamic and positional imaging (e.g., supine vs. standing EOS) further elucidates curve flexibility and postural adaptation, informing the need for osteotomies or less invasive correction (2,80). Intraoperative navigation and robotics

Robotic assistance and navigation systems have been shown to significantly increase the accuracy of pedicle screw placement compared to traditional freehand and fluoroscopic techniques, with meta-analyses reporting accuracy rates consistently above 94–98% and lower rates of intraoperative revision and reoperation (81-85). These technologies also reduce the risk of screw malposition, vascular and nerve injury, and perioperative complications, which are critical for maintaining construct integrity and optimal alignment (81,82,84,85). Navigation and robotics facilitate precise interbody device placement, allowing for optimal restoration of disc height and segmental lordosis, which is essential for achieving targeted lumbar alignment and global sagittal balance (86,87). Advanced imaging and real-time guidance enable individualized planning and execution, improving the reproducibility of lordosis distribution across lumbar segments and reducing the risk of junctional complications (20,86,87).

Recent reviews and case series confirm that these technologies enhance technical precision, minimize radiation exposure, and support consistent achievement of alignment goals in complex deformity cases (86,88,89). The integration of robotics and navigation into surgical workflows are associated with improved perioperative efficiency and patient-centered outcomes, especially in cases requiring multi-level instrumentation and deformity correction (81,82,86,87). The use of intraoperative navigation and robotics in ASD surgery is strongly supported, as improved device placement, screw accuracy, and LL distribution, all of which are directly linked to optimal final spinal alignment and reduced complication rates.

Remaining challenges and future directions

There are several ongoing challenges and future directions in ASD; specifically, alignment planning in frail or osteoporotic patients, optimization of junctional protection, and precise prediction of postoperative compensation are well supported in recent literature, as well as emerging strategies involving machine learning, lordosis distribution, and biologics/novel implants.

Recent studies emphasize that frailty and osteoporosis significantly increase the risk of mechanical complications, including PJK/PJF, and hardware failure. Modifying alignment goals to account for frailty (using frailty-adjusted scores) and bone quality can reduce complications and improve outcomes. For example, studies have shown that frailty-adjusted realignment scores outperform traditional alignment targets in predicting complications and optimizing outcomes in frail patients (90-92). Medical optimization with anabolic agents (e.g., teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab) and antiresorptives (e.g., denosumab) are recommended to improve bone quality and fusion rates, while surgical adaptations such as cement augmentation, expandable screws, and age-adjusted alignment further mitigate risks of complications and poor outcomes (93,94).

PJK and PJF remain the leading causes for revision in patients who undergo corrective surgery for ASD (95,96). Effective strategies to prevent these complications include combining surgical implant prophylaxis (e.g., transverse process hooks, tethers, cement augmentation) with avoidance of sagittal under and overcorrection, especially in older or frail patients (97-102). Recent literature has suggested that matching age-adjusted alignment and using junctional prophylaxis together most effectively reduces PJF rates (103). Risk factors for bony versus ligamentous PJF differ, with osteoporosis and overcorrection increasing bony PJF, and lack of TP hook use increasing ligamentous PJF (99). Biomechanical studies support semi-rigid fixation and gradual transition constructs to reduce junctional stress (101,102). Optimization of lordosis distribution (not just total LL) is gaining attention. Recent studies show that segmental lordosis distribution, especially at L4–S1 and T10–L2, impacts both functional outcomes and mechanical complication rates, with overcorrection at proximal segments increasing PJF risk (48,49,104).

Predicting how patients will compensate after surgery is complex. Machine learning models now incorporate demographic, radiographic, and clinical variables (including frailty, bone health, and alignment) to predict outcomes such as complications, reoperation, and achievement of ideal surgical results. These are being validated for outcome prediction and decision support, with high accuracy in identifying patients at risk for complications or suboptimal outcomes (96,105-107).

The literature supports a shift toward individualized, frailty- and bone quality-adjusted alignment planning, combined mechanical and biologic junctional protection, and the use of advanced predictive analytics and segmental alignment strategies to improve outcomes and reduce complications in ASD surgery. These areas remain active fields for future research and clinical innovation.

Conclusions

Sagittal alignment is a critical determinant of outcomes in ASD. Age and PI-adjusted targets, along with emerging vector-based and segmental metrics, and advanced technologies have improved sagittal alignment strategies. However, optimal application of these frameworks remains patient-specific and an area of continuing evolution. Future studies should emphasize individualized, data-driven alignment strategies supported by predictive modeling.
