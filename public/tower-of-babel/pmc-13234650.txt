Effect of Thermal Imidization Conditions on Polyimide Webs Derived from Poly(amic acid)/Lignin Nanofiber
Authors: Sa Rang Choi, Min Ho Lee, Jung Myoung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of lignin incorporation on the morphology and thermal imidization behavior of electrospun polyimide nanofibers. Blending lignin with poly­(amic acid) significantly improved fiber uniformity, reducing the average fiber diameter by 46% and decreasing diameter deviation by 73% compared to neat PAA nanofibers. Both neat and lignin-containing nanofibers achieved high degrees of imidization (DOI > 90%) at 230 °C and approached 99% at 280 °C while maintaining their porous structures. Notably, lignin-containing fibers showed higher degrees of imidization than neat PAA under comparable thermal conditions. Although rapid thermal ramping led to greater optical darkening, spectroscopic and thermal analyses confirmed that the polymer backbone remained chemically stable. These findings suggest a potential approach to lignin valorization and provide a basis for fabricating polyimide nanofibers with controlled structure and thermal properties.

Introduction

Polyimide (PI) is a high-performance polymer based on aromatic imide linkages, known for its outstanding thermal stability, chemical resistance, mechanical strength, and high polarity. These properties widen the applicability of PI in various electrical and electronic fields, including flexible circuit boards, interlayer insulation films, and alignment layers for liquid crystal displays. − Particularly, the physical and chemical stabilities of PI are retained under high temperatures and extreme conditions, highlighting its potential for use as a next-generation advanced material. Depending on the type of monomers used, PIs can be classified into aliphatic, semiaromatic, and wholly aromatic and are generally synthesized via a polycondensation reaction between diamines and dianhydrides. , During the synthesis process, poly­(amic acid) (PAA) is formed as an intermediate, which is subsequently converted into the final PI structure via thermal treatment or annealing, yielding a complete imide ring structure.

However, conventional petrochemical-based PI synthesis processes require high-boiling-point organic solvents such as m-cresol and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone and toxic catalysts. Moreover, the moisture sensitivity of PAA solutions hinders storage stability. With the increasing emphasis on sustainable development and the strictness of environmental regulations, developing biomass-derived PI materials to replace petroleum-based polymers has become a priority.

Lignin, along with cellulose and hemicellulose, is a major component of lignocellulosic biomass, accounting for approximately 20–30% of the total weight of wood. Lignin is a naturally occurring phenolic polymer, predominantly found in black liquor, and is a byproduct of pulping and paper-making processes. Globally, the pulp and biorefinery industries annually generate an estimated 50 million tons of lignin as a byproduct. However, approximately 98–99% of the lignin produced is currently incinerated, owing to its complex and heterogeneous chemical structure, to generate heat and steam, with only a limited fraction of lignin used in value-added industrial applications.

Although the chemical composition of lignin varies depending on the feedstock and extraction method, lignin typically exhibits advantageous properties such as high thermal stability, ultraviolet shielding ability, antioxidant activity, and mechanical strength. Lignin, owing to these characteristics, has garnered considerable attention as a functional material for use in diverse applications, including composites, bioplastics, carbon fibers, functional coatings, cosmetics, adhesives, rechargeable batteries, and supercapacitors. − In particular, this material exhibits significant potential for use as a renewable, low-cost carbon-based resource for replacing petroleum-derived polymers, as over 60% of the lignin structure consists of carbon. ,,

Electrospinning is a versatile technique for fabricating micro- and nanofibers with uniform and controllable diameters by applying a strong electric field. This method yields high surface areas and porosities and is widely used for fabricating membranes, adsorbents, and separators in electrical and electronic applications. − PAA can be electrospun into nanofibrous webs, which can be converted into PI nanofibers via thermal imidization. Such post-treatment retains the fibrous morphology while imparting the inherent thermal and chemical stability of PI to the webs. Wang et al. synthesized PAA via the polycondensation of pyromellitic dianhydride and 4,4′-oxidianiline (ODA) in dimethylacetamide, and electrospun this PAA under a tip-to-collector distance (TCD) of 25 cm and an applied voltage of 21 kV to produce a porous fibrous mat. The nanofibers were thermally imidized via stepwise heat treatment at 100, 200, and 300 °C, for 2 h each, yielding PI nanofibrous membranes. The resulting PI fibers possessed a mean diameter of 800 nm, a porosity of 65.9%, and electrolyte uptake of 559%, demonstrating excellent potential for use in battery separator membranes.

When used alone, lignin exhibits poor spinnability owing to its amorphous and chemically heterogeneous structure and relatively low molecular weight, limiting its ability to form stable nanofibers via electrospinning. To address this limitation, a previous study prepared electrospun PAA/lignin composite nanofibers by blending hardwood kraft lignin with PAA in a 9:1 (w/w) ratio using PAA as a polymer matrix. The solution, prepared with 11 wt % total solids in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), possessed a viscosity greater than 10,000 cP, suitable for electrospinning, indicating that PAA effectively served as a matrix for fiber formation. Additionally, the PAA/lignin electrospun fibers produced at a tip-to-collector distance (TCD) of 18 cm and an applied voltage of 16 kV exhibited relatively fine and uniform morphologies, with a mean diameter of 319 ± 59 nm and a coefficient of variation (CV) of 18.4%.

For stable electrospinning, maintaining a sufficiently high solid content (typically above 10 wt %) is desirable to ensure adequate chain entanglement. However, BPDA-based PAA, which contains rigid biphenyl structures, inherently exhibits high solution viscosity compared to PAA derived from less rigid dianhydrides. As a result, the electrospinning of BPDA-PAA often requires relatively lower solid concentrations to achieve a spinnable viscosity range. PAA solutions are susceptible to hydrolysis under diluted conditions in air or in polar solvents such as DMF, which may compromise solution stability. Lignin, owing to its relatively low molecular weight and heterogeneous, spherical dispersion behavior in DMF, can interfere with interchain interactions of high-molecular-weight polymers and effectively reduce solution viscosity, thereby facilitating stable electrospinning at relatively high solid contents. However, beyond its role in solution rheology, lignin is also expected to influence the subsequent thermal imidization process, a critical step that governs both the chemical conversion of PAA to PI and the preservation of the porous, fibrous morphology. In lignin-containing systems, this issue becomes particularly important because lignin may simultaneously promote dehydration and imidization through intermolecular interactions, while also inducing fiber fusion or pore collapse at elevated temperatures due to its thermoplastic softening behavior. However, the influence of lignin incorporation on the thermal imidization behavior of PAA has not yet been systematically investigated. Therefore, we hypothesized that incorporating lignin into BPDA-based PAA would modulate chain entanglement and decrease viscosity, enabling stable electrospinning even at relatively high solid contents, while potentially influencing the subsequent thermal imidization behavior.

Building on previous work, this study fabricated nanofibers from PAA/lignin blended solutions using optimized conditions (a TCD of 18 cm and a voltage of 16 kV), followed by thermal treatment at various temperatures (180–280 °C) to induce imidization. Subsequently, the morphological changes (fiber diameter and porosity) and imidization behaviors of the thermally treated films were evaluated to assess the performance of PAA/lignin-based nanofibrous webs as precursors for the preparation of sustainable PI films. Specifically, this study aimed to investigate the impact of lignin incorporation and imidization conditions on the degree of imidization, fiber morphology, structural integrity, and thermal properties.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The PAA used as a precursor for electrospinning was synthesized using 3,4,3′,4′-biphenyltetracarboxylic dianhydride (BPDA) and ODA, purchased from PI Solution Tech (Daejeon, South Korea). The hardwood kraft black liquor used for lignin extraction was supplied by Moorim P&P (Ulsan, South Korea). H2SO4 (95.0%) was purchased from Daejung Chemical & Materials (Republic of Korea). To dissolve the PAA/lignin blend, DMF (99.9%, special grade) was used as the solvent, obtained from SAMCHUN Chemical (Republic of Korea).

Lignin Extraction

The lignin used for electrospinning was directly extracted in our laboratory from black liquor, a byproduct of the pulp and paper process. Specifically, hardwood kraft black liquor (1 L) with an initial pH of approximately 13 was acidified by the gradual addition of 95% H2SO4 until the pH reached 3. The acidified liquor was stirred at 70 °C for 1 h in a reactor to induce lignin precipitation. The precipitated lignin was collected by vacuum filtration using Advantec filter paper (100 circles, No. 2, 185 mm) and thoroughly washed with distilled water until the filtrate reached a neutral pH. The obtained hardwood kraft lignin exhibited an ash content of 0.5 ± 0.02% and a purity of 90.0 ± 0.47%, indicating relatively low inorganic residue and high lignin content suitable for electrospinning applications. Preparation of the Electrospinning Solution and Fiber Spinning

To prepare the electrospinning solution, PAA and lignin were each dissolved in DMF at a solid concentration of 11 wt % under an N2 atmosphere inside a glovebox (LK Lab Korea Co., Ltd., Korea). The two solutions were subsequently mixed according to the composition ratios listed in Table . The resulting mixture was sealed in a vial in an N2 atmosphere to prevent O2 exposure and stirred at 600 rpm for 2 h to ensure homogeneity. A drum collector wrapped with release paper was employed to collect the nanofibers obtained during electrospinning, with the syringe tip and collector aligned horizontally. The PAA/lignin solution was loaded into a 12 mL syringe equipped with a 23 G metal needle, and electrospinning was implemented under controlled environmental conditions (23 °C and 30% relative humidity) at a flow rate of 1 mL/h for 6 h using a syringe pump. The electrospinning parameters were fixed at a TCD of 18 cm and an applied voltage of 18 kV.

Measurements

Characterizing the Properties of the Electrospinning Solution

To evaluate the effect of the lignin-to-PAA blending ratio on solution viscosity, rheological measurements were conducted using a rotational rheometer (Anton-Paar MCR 102e, Austria) equipped with a 25 mm parallel plate. Moreover, viscosities (cP) were analyzed at a constant shear rate of 10 s–1. The measurements were repeated three times. To quantitatively analyze the jet behavior during electrospinning, the whipping angle was measured from captured jet images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The whipping angle was defined as the divergence angle of the whipping region relative to the vertical jet axis. For each sample, ten independent images were analyzed.

Characterization of Fiber Morphology

The surface morphologies of the electrospun fiber sheets were observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Hitachi SU8220, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV. SEM images were acquired at magnifications of 1500 × and 5000 ×. The pore area fraction of the imidized nanofiber webs was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). SEM micrographs acquired at 1,500× magnification were converted to 8-bit grayscale images prior to analysis. A consistent threshold range was applied to all samples using the ImageJ threshold segmentation function to distinguish pore regions from fiber regions. The threshold values were adjusted to isolate the dark pore areas from the brighter fiber matrix, and the segmented pore regions were highlighted in red for visualization. The pore area fraction (%) was calculated as the ratio of the segmented pore area to the total image area. The same threshold parameters were applied to all images to ensure consistent comparison between samples. The average diameter and corresponding standard deviation for each sample were calculated from approximately 100 individual fibers measured from SEM images acquired at 5,000× magnification. Statistical differences among the imidization conditions were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test using Origin software (OriginPro, OriginLab Corporation, USA). A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied. Additionally, the uniformity of the fiber diameter was evaluated based on the following eq : 1 CV(%)=SD/AD×100 where CV denotes the coefficient of variation, SD represents the standard deviation, and AD corresponds to the average fiber diameter.

The pore structure of the imidized nanofiber webs was additionally evaluated using a capillary flow porometer (CFP-110AE, PMI, USA). Circular specimens (3 cm in diameter) were prepared for the measurements. The dry flow rate was measured at a flow rate setting of 10 cc/min under an applied pressure of 10 PSI. The average pore diameter was automatically calculated by the instrument software. The measurements were repeated three times.

Characterization of the Imidized Film Properties

The degrees of imidization (DOIs) of the PAA nanofibers after thermal treatment were determined using an attenuated total reflectance-infrared spectrometer (Bruker Optics, Germany). Spectra were acquired in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm–1, with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm–1. The DOIs were calculated based on the method reported by Kim et al. using the absorbance peak value ratio between the C–N stretching vibration of the imide ring at 1340 cm–1 and the C = C stretching vibration of the benzene ring at 1510 cm–1 as an internal reference. Peak heights were used for quantification rather than integrated peak areas. The absorbance ratio (A1340/A1510) was defined as the imidization index. The sample treated at 390 °C was used as the reference for full imidization (DOI = 100%), as no further increase in the imidization index was observed beyond this temperature, suggesting that the reaction had reached a plateau. The DOIs of the samples treated at other temperatures were calculated as relative percentages according to the following eq : 2 DOI(%)=[(A1340/A1510)t/(A1340/A1510)r]×100 where (A1340/A1510)t represents the absorbance ratio of the sample treated at temperature T, and (A1340/A1510)r represents that of the reference sample treated at 390 °C. Each sample was measured three times independently, and the reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation.

The color coordinates of the electrospun PAA and PAA/L nanofiber films before and after thermal treatment were measured using a colorimeter (TS7036, Sanhe 3nh Technology Co., Ltd., China) based on the CIE L*a*b* color system. Measurements were conducted under a standard illuminant D65 with a 10° observer angle, and the instrument was calibrated using a standard white calibration plate prior to measurement. The L* value represents lightness (0 = black, 100 = white), while a* and b* represent the red–green and yellow–blue chromatic coordinates, respectively. For each sample, measurements were performed at three different positions on the film surface, and the average values were reported.

Thermal analyses were performed using TGA (Q500, TA Instruments, USA) and DSC (TA Instruments, USA) under a nitrogen atmosphere. The TGA was conducted in the temperature range of 25–600 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min–1, while the DSC measurements were carried out from 25 to 375 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C min–1.

Results and Discussion

Properties of the Electrospinning Solutions

The structures and morphologies of nanofibers produced via electrospinning are strongly influenced by several physicochemical properties of the polymer solution, including molecular weight, surface tension, conductivity, solid content, and viscosity. Among these, viscosity is a key factor determining the degree of polymer chain entanglement, which is essential for forming a stable jet and uniform fibers via electrospinning. If the viscosity of the spinning solution is below a certain threshold, the jet becomes unstable, often generating defects such as beads or fiber breakage. The data in Table reveal that when the solid content is fixed at 11 wt %, the measured viscosity of the pure PAA solution is high, i.e., 35445 ± 49 cP. In contrast, the viscosity of the PAA/L blend solution, containing 10 wt % lignin, was reduced to 17917 ± 69 cP, approximately 50% of the viscosity of the pure PAA solution. According to Dong and Fricke, lignin tends to exist as spherical particles in DMF, and this morphology reduces friction between polymer chains in the solution. Therefore, the inherent dispersion characteristics of lignin in solution could influence intermolecular interactions within the PAA matrix, contributing to the lower viscosity of the PAA/L blend solution.

Electrospinning Behavior

The jet morphology formed during electrospinning, shown in Figure , clearly illustrates the influence of solution viscosity on jet behavior. The Taylor cone is a conical structure formed at the liquid–air interface of a polymer solution under an applied electric field. This cone forms due to the balance between the electrostatic force acting on it and the surface tension of the solution, and the cone’s geometry, such as the angle and length, is strongly dependent on the physicochemical properties of the solution. The jet profiles of the pure PAA and PAA/L blend solutions exhibited distinct differences. The high-viscosity PAA solution produced a relatively narrow and straight jet, whereas the viscosity of the PAA/L blend solution was suitable for electrospinning, forming a smooth and continuous jet under the electric field, and a stable cone-jet structure, characterized by steady elongation, was observed. In addition to visual observation, the whipping angle was quantitatively measured to distinguish between jet behaviors (Table ). As summarized in Table , the pure PAA solution exhibited a whipping angle of 53.7° ± 5.4°, while the PAA/L blend solution showed a significantly larger whipping angle of 116.9° ± 8.1°. The substantial increase in whipping angle indicates enhanced bending instability and broader jet dispersion in the PAA/L system.

This observation is consistent with previous studies, which reported that highly viscous solutions yield extended Taylor cones and long jet segments owing to surface shear stress at the cone apex. , Conversely, a reduction in viscosity may decrease the resistance to electrostatic stretching forces. Accordingly, the lower viscosity of the PAA/L solution may contribute to increased responsiveness to the applied electric field, which is consistent with the observed amplified whipping motion and wider jet trajectory. Despite increased whipping, the PAA/L solution maintained a stable cone–jet structure, producing smooth, continuous fibers. These results indicate that PAA served as the primary polymer matrix, whereas lignin acted as a viscosity-modifying additive, potentially influencing the solution’s flow characteristics and jet behavior during electrospinning. However, it should be noted that viscosity alone does not fully determine electrospinning behavior; other factors, such as conductivity and surface tension, may also contribute.

Properties of Imidized Films

Electrospun PAA and PAA/L composite nanofibers can be thermally converted into PI structures via a subsequent imidization process. The ability to maintain the morphology and porous architecture of the fibers during thermal treatment determines the functionality of the final material. As listed in Table , thermal imidization is conducted via two- or three-step temperature elevation protocols, with final curing temperatures of 180, 230, and 280 °C. Additionally, the ramping rate and holding time were varied at each stage. The microstructural changes before and after imidization were examined using SEM images (Figure ). In the untreated control samples, the high-viscosity pure PAA fibers possessed larger diameters than the PAA/L composite fibers. However, both fiber types possessed smooth, bead-free surfaces and independent nanofibrous structures with no signs of fiber fusion, indicating that even 10 wt % lignin addition yielded uniform nanofiber webs via electrospinning. Upon thermal treatment, structural changes occurred in the nanofiber networks. At a relatively low curing temperature of 180 °C, both the PAA and PAA/L nanofibers retained their original morphologies without noticeable structural collapse or fusion. However, at 230 °C, slight fiber thickening and localized fusing were observed, attributable to polymer chain rearrangement or partial viscoelastic flow at high temperatures. The most significant changes occurred at 280 °C, at which pore collapse owing to fiber fusion was prominently observed. In particular, the pore structure of the PAA/L nanofibers degraded more severely than that of the pure PAA fibers. No major differences in fiber structure were observed under different temperature ramping protocols (110–230 °C and 140–230 °C), suggesting that the final curing temperature has a stronger influence on the structural stability of the fiber than the temperature increase rate.

SEM images of electrospun PAA and PAA/L nanofiber webs before and after thermal imidization.

To quantitatively compare the porous structures of the samples imidized at 280 °C, image analysis was performed using ImageJ threshold segmentation of the SEM micrographs. The red-highlighted regions correspond to the pore areas extracted from grayscale images (Figure ). The neat PAA sample exhibited a pore area fraction of 28.04% (Figure a), whereas the PAA/L composite showed a significantly lower value of 10.81% (Figure b), indicating more pronounced structural densification in the lignin-containing system. The enhanced pore collapse observed in the PAA/L web can be attributed to the thermoplastic nature of lignin. Lignin becomes flowable above 190 °C, which may promote interfiber adhesion and morphological distortion during high-temperature treatment. Under severe curing conditions, such softening may accelerate fiber–fiber fusion within the composite network. Although lignin incorporation improved spinnability and fiber uniformity prior to imidization, its thermal softening behavior appears to compromise pore retention under excessive heat treatment. These findings suggest that while lignin may improve electrospinning performance and imidization behavior, careful optimization of curing temperature is required to balance chemical conversion and structural stability.

The capillary flow porometry results are summarized in Table . The PAA 110–280 sample exhibited an average pore diameter of 2.30 μm, whereas the PAA/L 110–280 sample showed a markedly smaller average pore diameter of 0.13 μm. According to the capillary flow principle, a higher applied pressure is required to open smaller pores. Therefore, the significantly higher pressure (49.81 psi) observed for the PAA/L sample indicates the presence of finer and more constricted pore structures. This behavior is likely due to partial lignin melting, which promotes fiber–fiber fusion. These results are consistent with the ImageJ threshold analysis, confirming that lignin incorporation led to greater pore shrinkage after thermal treatment at 280 °C.

The nanofiber diameters are measured from SEM images, and the diameter distributions are displayed as histograms in Figure . The as-spun PAA nanofibers possessed an average diameter of 620 nm with a standard deviation of 222 nm, indicating the presence of relatively large fibers with a broad diameter distribution. In contrast, the PAA/L composite fibers showed a considerably reduced average diameter of 335 nm, approximately a 46% decrease with respect to that of pure PAA fibers. Additionally, the standard deviation decreased by approximately 73%, suggesting a relatively narrow, uniform distribution of fiber diameters. This result suggests that lignin addition was associated with thin and uniform nanofibers, likely through changes in solution viscosity and jet behavior. The imidized PAA and PAA/L samples that were thermally treated at relatively low temperatures (110–180 °C) possessed slightly larger fiber diameters, with average values of 657 and 345 nm, respectively, compared to the corresponding untreated controls. However, the differences in diameter and standard deviation were insubstantial, suggesting that the nanofiber structures remained stable under these mild thermal conditions. In contrast, at a high temperature of 280 °C, the average diameters of the PAA and PAA/L fibers and the standard deviations markedly increased. This phenomenon is attributable to the combined effects of fiber fusion, melting, and thermal shrinkage at high temperatures. Furthermore, comparing the samples with the same final curing temperature (230 °C) but different heating ramp rates (110–230 and 140–230 °C) revealed that the fibers subjected to fast ramping from a high initial temperature exhibited larger increases and greater nonuniformity in diameter than those subjected to slow ramping. This suggests that a gradual heating profile is favorable for maintaining the structural stability of the nanofibers during imidization.

Diameter distributions of the electrospun PAA and PAA/L nanofibers under varying imidization temperature profiles (110–180, 110–230, 110–280, and 140–230 °C).

Figure compares the average fiber diameters and diameter change rates of the electrospun PAA and PAA/L nanofibers fabricated under various thermal treatment conditions. The diameter change rate was calculated as the relative percentage difference between the average fiber diameter after thermal treatment and that of the untreated control sample. As shown in Figure a, although a statistically significant difference was observed between the 110–230 and 110–280 samples (p < 0.05), no significant differences were observed between the other conditions. The as-spun PAA fibers exhibited relatively large diameters with a broad distribution, likely due to the high solution viscosity and limited spinnability. This large deviation may have obscured statistical differences between the thermally treated samples. Nevertheless, the sample treated at 280 °C exhibited the largest average fiber diameter and the highest diameter change rate among all conditions, indicating that excessive thermal treatment leads to pronounced fiber thickening, likely due to fiber fusion and structural deformation. Although the difference was not statistically significant, the slightly larger diameter observed under the rapid heating condition (140–230) may indicate a tendency for fiber thickening. This trend may be associated with phenomena reported in previous studies, in which elevated temperatures and rapid heating have been linked to fiber fusion and compromised shape retention. Meanwhile, for the PAA/L composite fibers (Figure b), the as-spun fibers had relatively small diameters and a narrow distribution, enabling clearer statistical differentiation between the thermally treated samples. The average diameters of the fibers treated at 110–180 and 110–230, as well as under rapid heating (140–230), did not show statistically significant differences, indicating that the fiber morphology was largely preserved regardless of the heating rate within this temperature range. In contrast, the sample treated at 280 °C showed a pronounced, statistically significant increase in fiber diameter. This behavior was more pronounced in the PAA/L system, which may be related to the thermoplastic softening of lignin at elevated temperatures, as discussed earlier, potentially promoting interfiber adhesion and structural deformation. The SEM images (Figure ) further confirmed substantial fiber fusion and partial collapse of the porous microstructure under this high-temperature condition. These results indicate that moderate thermal treatment preserves fiber morphology better, whereas excessive thermal exposure induces structural deformation and diameter expansion.

The CVs of the fiber diameters derived from SEM-based measurements were quantitatively analyzed to evaluate the morphological uniformity of the fibers subjected to various imidization conditions (Figure ). The CV of the PAA nanofibers before thermal treatment was approximately 36%, whereas that of the PAA/L composite fibers was notably lower at approximately 18%, indicating high fiber uniformity owing to the presence of lignin. Upon stepwise thermal treatment at relatively low temperatures (110–180 °C), the CV values remained similar to those of the untreated fibers, i.e., 36% for PAA and 17% for PAA/L. However, when the treatment temperature exceeded 230 °C, the CV of the PAA fibers increased to the range of 40–42%, suggesting a decline in fiber uniformity. Thermal treatment at 280 °C of the PAA/L fibers significantly increased the corresponding CV, indicating a substantial degradation in fiber uniformity owing to thermal stress. Furthermore, a comparison between the PAA/L samples treated between 110–230 and 140–230 °C revealed CV values of 26% and 33%, respectively. This suggests that low ramping rates during thermal curing are beneficial for maintaining fiber uniformity and structural integrity.

The colors of the PAA samples were slightly darker or similar to those of the control, depending on the temperature. In contrast, the colors of the PAA/L samples remained similar to those of the control at low temperatures (110–180 °C) but gradually turned dark brown as the final treatment temperature increased.

Importantly, such optical variations originate from electronic interactions within the imide structure rather than from chemical degradation or chain scission. The color change observed after thermal treatment is consistent with the well-known optical behavior of thermally imidized polyimide films. It is widely reported that polyimides inherently exhibit a yellow to orange coloration due to the formation of charge-transfer complexes (CTCs) between electron-donating and electron-accepting segments along the polymer backbone. These intermolecular electronic interactions enhance conjugation and increase optical absorption in the visible region, leading to progressive darkening as the imidization temperature increases. ,

This tendency was quantitatively supported by the CIE color coordinates summarized in Table . As the curing temperature increased, the L* value (lightness) generally decreased, indicating progressive darkening of the samples. Simultaneously, changes in the a* and b* coordinates reflected a shift toward red–yellow chromaticity, consistent with enhanced visible light absorption. In particular, the PAA/L samples treated at higher temperatures exhibited lower L* values and increased b* values compared to neat PAA, confirming more pronounced browning behavior in the lignin-containing system.

Therefore, the L* value of the rapidly heated sample was lower, indicating that the heating rate affects the formation of chromophores. This effect may be more plausibly associated with intensified chromophore formation and enhanced charge-transfer complex interactions arising from rapid thermal exposure, rather than unequivocal backbone degradation. In lignin-containing systems, accelerated heating could further contribute to localized thermal darkening within aromatic lignin domains. Previous studies , on thermally treated lignin and lignin-rich materials have suggested that heat exposure can induce demethylation, condensation reactions, and the formation of conjugated chromophoric structures, including quinone-like moieties and expanded aromatic systems. Such structural modifications are known to increase optical absorption in the visible region and are often correlated with progressively darker coloration under more severe thermal conditions. Accordingly, the darker appearance observed under rapid heating conditions is considered to be attributable to heating-rate-dependent chromophore development in lignin. These observations suggest that both lignin incorporation and the thermal ramp profile may influence the visual appearance and color uniformity of the nanofiber mats during thermal curing. Therefore, careful control of the curing profile may be important for achieving consistent aesthetic and optical characteristics in the resulting materials.

In the present study, the slightly higher DOI values observed in the PAA/L suggest that lignin incorporation may influence the local dehydration environment during thermal treatment. In particular, the DOI of the PAA/L sample treated between 110–280 °C approached 99%, suggesting a nearly complete conversion to the imide structure. It is possible that intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonding between lignin hydroxyl groups and amic acid moieties, could affect chain mobility or water diffusion during cyclodehydration. These findings highlight the potential of converting PAA/L composite systems into high-performance PI materials under relatively mild thermal conditions. Nevertheless, further investigations would be required to conclusively clarify the specific role of lignin functional groups in the imidization mechanism. Meanwhile, under rapid heating conditions (140–230), the DOIs of the PAA and PAA/L fibers were 89.1% and 94.4%, respectively, which were slightly higher than those obtained under the 110–230 °C process. These results indicate that, despite the relatively short dwell time during rapid heating, the PAA/L sample achieved a higher degree of imidization than neat PAA. This suggests that lignin incorporation did not hinder imidization and may be associated with slightly higher DOI values under accelerated thermal conditions.

To further verify the imidization behavior and thermal stability differences observed in the DOI results, TGA and DSC analyses were performed for the PAA and PAA/L nanofibers before and after imidization under different heating conditions (110–230 °C and 140–230 °C), as shown in Figure . Figure a and b show the TGA and DTG curves of the samples. The detailed comparison of weight loss behavior is provided in Figure c. The control (nonimidized) PAA and PAA/L samples exhibited relatively high total weight losses of 30.9% and 33.2%, respectively, mainly due to the evaporation of solvent and water and the decomposition of unreacted poly­(amic acid) segments. After thermal imidization, the total weight change significantly decreased, confirming enhanced thermal stability. Notably, the PAA/L samples showed lower overall mass loss than neat PAA under both heating conditions (110–230 °C and 140–230 °C). This finding suggests that lignin incorporation is associated with improved thermal resistance of the fibers in the practically relevant temperature range. The enhanced stability is likely related to aromatic reinforcement from lignin and additional intermolecular interactions between lignin’s phenolic groups and the evolving imide matrix, which together suppress early stage decomposition. Comparing the heating rates, the samples treated under the rapid heating condition (140–230 °C) exhibited slightly higher residual weight than those imidized at 110–230 °C, suggesting that accelerated heating did not adversely affect imidization or thermal integrity. In particular, the PAA/L 140–230 °C sample maintained a total weight change of 20.5%, close to that of the PAA/L 110–230 °C (23.8%), supporting that effective imidization was achieved even under shorter dwell time.

As shown in Figure d, the DSC thermograms provide complementary evidence of this behavior. The nonimidized PAA and PAA/L controls displayed distinct endothermic peaks around 150–250 °C, corresponding to the dehydration and cyclization of the amic acid groups. These peaks were markedly diminished or disappeared in all imidized samples, verifying the completion of imidization during thermal treatment.

Overall, the TGA and DSC results confirm that the addition of lignin enhances both the imidization kinetics and the resulting thermal stability of the PAA matrix. Furthermore, the thermal degradation profiles and heat flow behaviors showed no noticeable differences between the 110–230 °C and 140–230 °C treatments, suggesting that the heating rate had only a minor effect on the overall thermal characteristics of the PAA and PAA/L fibers. However, the structural analyses reveal an important trade-off. While rapid heating offers benefits in terms of thermal stability, it simultaneously induces slight fiber deformation and partial discoloration (browning). In contrast, the milder heating condition provides a more favorable balance, ensuring effective imidization while better preserving both the nanofibrous morphology and the visual uniformity of the fibers. Taken together, the inherent thermal stability and porous architecture of the electrospun PAA/lignin nanofibers may indicate potential relevance to applications requiring thermally stable porous materials. However, further investigation, including functional performance evaluation under practical conditions, is required to assess their suitability for specific applications such as battery thermal management.

Conclusions

In this study, electrospun nanofiber webs were fabricated from a blended solution of PAA and hardwood kraft lignin, and the potential for producing eco-friendly PI films via thermal imidization under various heat-treatment conditions was evaluated. The PAA/L solution containing 10 wt % lignin enabled stable jet formation and afforded a uniform fiber morphology, yielding nanofibers with an average diameter approximately 46% lower and improved diameter uniformity compared to those derived from neat PAA. Analysis of fiber morphologies and DOIs revealed that heat treatment at 230 °C maintained the porous structure while achieving a DOI of over 90%. Furthermore, at 280 °C, the PAA/L sample achieved a DOI of 98.7%, approaching complete imidization. However, excessive thermal treatment led to fiber fusion and pore collapse, while rapid heating caused noticeable discoloration and an increase in fiber diameter. Although the rapid heating rate had only a minor influence on the imidization degree and thermal stability, it induced visible color changes and increased the variation in the imidized fibers. Nevertheless, lignin incorporation was associated with slightly higher degrees of imidization compared to neat PAA under comparable thermal conditions. These results suggest that lignin-containing systems can achieve high degrees of imidization under controlled thermal treatment while maintaining structural integrity under relatively mild curing conditions.
