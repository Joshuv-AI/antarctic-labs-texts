Factors associated with psychological well-being among older adults attending daycare centers of Kathmandu, Nepal: a cross-sectional path-analytical study
Authors: Rekha Timalsina, Madhusudan Subedi, Nima Maharjan, Bigya Shah, Pabitra Adhikari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs individuals age, their mental health becomes critical to overall well-being. Identifying the various factors influencing psychological well-being (PWB), including social, spiritual, psychological, and individual aspects, is essential. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the factors associated with PWB among older adults visiting daycare centers in Kathmandu.MethodsA cross-sectional path-analytical study was conducted among 300 older adults selected via a quota sampling technique (i.e., based on daycare centers). Data were collected by face-to-face interviews using seven sets of structured, valid, and reliable questions, i.e., (1) General Self-Efficacy Scale, (2) Connor and Davidson Resilience Scale, (3) Multi-Dimensional Perceived Social Support Scale, (4) Daily Spiritual Experiences Scale, (5) UCLA Loneliness Scale, (6) Lubben Social Network Scale, and (7) Psychological Wellbeing Scale, including (8) socio-demographic and comorbidity-related characteristics. Double data entry, validation, and cleaning were done using Epi-data software. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 16 software and AMOS version 21 software. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and different assumption testing for the inferential statistics were employed using SPSS software. Then, the data were tested for the model fit and the path diagram using AMOS version 21 software based on 283 samples after excluding 17 multivariate influential outliers.ResultsThe respondents predominantly had a moderate level (66.3%), followed by a high level (17.7%), and a low level (16.0%) of PWB. The hypothesized model of PWB demonstrated a good fit with the study data, explaining 60% of the variance in PWB through six key factors: (1) self-efficacy, (2) resilience, (3) perceived social support, (4) social connectedness, (5) spirituality, and (6) loneliness, (Adjusted R² = 0.60). Furthermore, the significant direct and positive effects of self-efficacy, resilience, and social support on PWB was evident. Conversely, loneliness had a significant direct and negative effect on PWB. Additionally, self-efficacy and social connectedness exhibited significant indirect and positive effects on PWB, both mediated through resilience. However, the direct effects of social connectedness and spirituality on PWB and the indirect effects of loneliness on PWB through resilience were statistically nonsignificant.ConclusionResilience, self-efficacy, social support, and social connectedness are key positive factors associated with PWB among older adults, while loneliness negatively impacts it. Thus, these findings highlight the need for targeted interventions that enhance resilience, foster self-efficacy, and strengthen social support and social connectedness to reduce loneliness and improve PWB in aging populations.Trial registrationNot applicable. The authors wish to clarify that the study design is cross-sectional, with data collected and analyzed at a single point in time. Background

The number of older adults across the globe is expected to rise from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. Similarly, Nepal is experiencing an upward demographic trend [2]. This shift is primarily attributed to increased life expectancy at birth, declining fertility and mortality rates, and the migration of younger populations [2]. In line with this trend, 8.52% of the total population in Kathmandu comprises older adults [2]. With the growing proportion of older adults, concerns about their mental health have become increasingly prominent. Older adults are particularly vulnerable to mental illnesses due to the loss of loved ones, chronic illnesses, and challenges in adapting to life transitions [3]. In addition, a decline in cognitive abilities, reduced autonomy in personal and healthcare decisions, limited mobility, frailty, disability, and dependency on caregivers, combined with financial constraints and life events associated with aging, significantly contribute to psychological burdens among the geriatric population [4].

Amid these challenges, psychological well-being [PWB] plays an important role in the overall health of older adults. According to the World Health Organization [WHO] [5], PWB is defined as a state in which individuals recognize their abilities, can adapt to life stressors, are productive in their work, and contribute to their society. Interestingly, PWB supports healthy ageing by decreasing harmful behaviors and encouraging restorative ones [6]. Additionally, a positive mental outlook enhances physical health, cognitive function, and social connections [6]. Likewise, a systematic review by Kang and Kim [7] highlighted that older individuals with higher PWB are less likely to experience negative emotions or suffer from the impacts of ageism. They are also more adaptable in setting meaningful goals, exhibit greater self-confidence, and have a more positive outlook on aging and the future [7]. Consistent with this, a study from Iraq revealed that 18.33% of older adults demonstrated a high level of PWB [8]. Furthermore, Bratman et al. [9] identified several key components of PWB, including happiness (both hedonic and eudaimonic), self-actualization, resilience, and healthy relationships. Eastern philosophy, particularly the eudaimonic perspective [10], aligns with Ryff’s concept of PWB [11], which incorporates both interpersonal and intrapersonal dimensions of positive functioning, including meaningful relationships, self-acceptance, mastery, and personal growth [12].

Various cross-sectional studies conducted across different countries and populations have explored the correlates of PWB among older adults. These studies have reported a statistically significant association of factors such as resilience [13–17], self-efficacy [18–20], social support [19, 21–24], optimism [13, 15, 24], self-compassion [25], and spirituality [24, 26–28], with PWB among older adults. In contrast, previous studies among older adults in various countries have reported a negative association between loneliness and PWB [29–32]. Conversely, social connectedness had a positive relationship with mental well-being among adults in Azerbaijan [33], whereas social isolation has been linked to increased psychological distress among older adults [31, 34]. The cross-sectional path-analytical and structural equation modeling studies among different age group populations also revealed the indirect effect of different factors on PWB/mental health through resilience. For instance, general self-efficacy had an indirect relationship with mental health through resilience among college students in China [35]. Similarly, social connectedness [33], social isolation [27, 34], and loneliness [36] had an indirect relationship with PWB through resilience. Despite this growing body of international literature, research in the Nepali context remains limited. A review of online databases, including ProQuest, CINAHL, JAMA Network, Scopus, Ovid, PubMed, and Google Scholar, as well as printed resources, indicates that studies in Nepal primarily focus on anxiety, depression, loneliness, and life satisfaction. However, there is a noticeable gap in comprehensive studies that incorporate all major influencing factors into a path-analytical model of PWB from a eudaimonic perspective.

Given these gaps, researchers have emphasized the need to assess the relationship between resilience and PWB [13] and the mediating factors of PWB among older adults [7]. Moreover, culturally contextualized studies, particularly among older Nepalese adults, are essential for generating relevant evidence [37]. In light of this, the current study developed a hypothesized model of PWB based on existing empirical findings (See Fig. 1) and aimed to test it. Ultimately, the findings are expected to inform nurses, geriatric mental health practitioners, healthcare professionals, psychologists, psychiatrists, and social workers about the PWB and its associated factors. Furthermore, the insights gained will support the development of culturally appropriate, sustainable, accessible, acceptable, cost-effective, and age-friendly intervention guidelines to enhance PWB in Nepal’s aging population.

Methods

Aim and objectives

This study aimed to identify the factors associated with PWB among older adults attending daycare centers of Kathmandu, Nepal: A cross-sectional path-analytical study. The objectives of this study were: (1) to identify the level of PWB, and other associated factors (i.e., resilience, self-efficacy, social support, spirituality, social connectedness, and loneliness) among older adults; (2) to examine the fitness of the model of PWB with the empirical data; (3) to identify the direct, and positive effects of resilience, self-efficacy, social support, spirituality, and social connectedness, and negative effects of loneliness, on PWB; (4) to examine indirect, and positive effects of self-efficacy and social connectedness, and negative effects of loneliness, on PWB through resilience (See Fig. 1).

Research design

A cross-sectional path-analytical study design was adopted for this study. Data were collected at a single point in time from older adults, and various aspects of exogenous and endogenous variables were analyzed. Additionally, this study examined the associations between multiple independent variables and a dependent variable, justifying its analytical design. This study specifically examined the direct and indirect relationships between five exogenous variables: (1) self-efficacy, (2) social support, (3) spirituality, (4) social connectedness, and (5) loneliness; and two endogenous variables: (1) resilience, and (2) PWB. Given the focus on analyzing these complex relationships using path analysis, the study design is characterized as a cross-sectional path-analytical study.

Study setting and study population

Kathmandu, the capital of Nepal, serves as the political and administrative center of the federal democratic republic. It attracts individuals from across the country in search of better opportunities, which has contributed to a noticeable rise in the geriatric population. Although there may be more daycare centers in Kathmandu, Nepal, only 14 were identified through personal communication with the administrative authority of Ageing Nepal. This limitation might be due to the lack of updated, proper documentation and registration of these centers. Collectively, these 14 centers are providing daycare services to 1,737 older adults. This study aimed to examine the PWB and its associated factors among older adults attending these daycare centers. The rationale for selecting these centers is based on the realities of busy urban lifestyles, where family members of the productive age group are often occupied with economic activities, leaving older adults alone at home. Daycare centers serve as a crucial support system in such situations, offering supervised care and ensuring the safety and well-being of older adults who are isolated or living alone. These centers provide a variety of care activities facilitated by trained professionals, promoting not only physical and emotional well-being but also encouraging social interaction. Furthermore, they offer opportunities for older adults to engage in recreational and therapeutic activities, fostering enjoyment and a sense of belonging.

The study population consisted of all older adults (N = 1737) attending daycare centers in Kathmandu. Older adults aged 60 years or above who visited daycare centers, were willing to participate and able to communicate, and could recall and discuss issues related to their PWB and other relevant factors were the inclusion criteria of this study. Additionally, older adults with serious comorbidities, psychiatric disorders, or those under medication for mental illnesses were the exclusion criteria. Since daycare centers in Nepal primarily serve as venues for recreational and spiritual activities for older adults, and medical records were typically unavailable, the research team relied on older adults’ self-reports and confirmation from center authorities. Older adults who were unwilling to continue participation at any stage were excluded from the study. Additionally, older adults with cognitive impairments, identified using the WHO Cognitive Decline Screening Tool [38] before data collection or reported by daycare center authorities, were excluded. Research assistants (RAs) approached a total of 316 older adults at the selected daycare centers. However, 16 older adults refused to participate due to a lack of interest and time.

Sample size

Although no prior studies reported the effect size of the six predictors (self-efficacy, perceived social support, spirituality, loneliness, social connectedness, and resilience) on psychological well-being (PWB), the research team adopted a conventional medium effect size (f² = 0.15) based on Cohen [39] for the a priori power analysis. Using G*Power 3.1 with α = 0.05, power (1–β) = 0.80, and six predictors, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 98 participants would be required. However, as this study aimed to identify factors associated with PWB among older adults using multiple variables and a non-probability sampling method, a sample size of 300 was selected to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability. This decision was guided by Bujang et al. [40], who suggested a minimum sample size of 300 for multivariable analyses involving non-random sampling and potential confounders, to ensure accurate parameter estimation.

Sampling technique

Considering the time, cost, and effort involved in reaching all 14 daycare centers in Kathmandu, a one-stage cluster sampling technique combined with a quota sampling approach (See Fig. 2) was used to select the samples for this study. In the first step, six daycare centers were randomly selected from the total of 14 using a random number generator. This random selection helped ensure fair representation of the daycare environments across Kathmandu while reducing logistical burdens. In the second step, a quota sampling method was applied, with strata based on the six selected centers, to ensure a balanced number of respondents from each center and contribute to the representativeness of the sample. Finally, within each selected center, convenience sampling was used to recruit older adults based on their availability during the data collection period and willingness to participate in the study.

Data collection instruments

The instruments used in this study consisted of eight sets related to the exogenous and endogenous variables of the PWB model and socio-demographic and comorbidity-related information.

Questions related to socio-demographic and comorbidity-related characteristics

This part consists of questions related to (1) age, (2) sex, (3) marital status, (4) having offspring, (5) living with spouse, (6) educational level, (7) type of family, and (8) presence of chronic diseases.

Connor and Davidson resilience scale [CD-RISC

CD-RISC-10 [41] was used for assessing resilience among older adults. It consists of 10 items with a response range between 0 and 4 [0 (not true at all), 1 (rarely true), 2 (sometimes true), 3 (often true), and 4 (true nearly at all the time)]. The total scores range between 0 and 40. An example of one of the items is “I try to see the humorous side of things when I am faced with problems.” The respondents were asked to provide a rating based on their feelings about confronting the past month. Higher scores mean greater resilience [42]. The scores were categorized based on median and quartile scores, where (1) quartile 1 st = less resilient, (2) quartile 2nd and 3rd = intermediate level of resilient, and (3) quartile 4th = highest or most resilient, as recommended by Davidson [43]. Permission was given by Professor Jonathan Davidson for using the CD-RISC-10 Nepali version [44] via email.

General self-efficacy scale-6 [GSE-6

GSE-6 [45] was used for assessing self-efficacy among older adults. It consists of 4-point rating scales that range from 1 (not at all true) to 4 (exactly true). An example of one of the items is “No matter what comes my way, I’m usually able to handle it.” The total score is obtained by summing up the scores of all items. The score ranges between 6 and 24. The higher score represented the higher level of self-efficacy. However, the level of self-efficacy was categorized using the median cutoff point of the current samples based on the recommendations of Schwarzer [46]. GSE-6 is a public domain tool and can be used for academic purposes [46].

Multi-dimensional perceived social support scale - Nepali version [MPSS-N

Social support was assessed using well-translated and validated Nepali versions of 12 items from the MPSS [47]. MPSS was developed by Zimet et al. [48]. The respondents were requested to provide a rating on each item of a 7-point Likert scale that ranges between very strongly disagree (1) and very strongly agree (7). An example of one of the items is “I get the emotional help and support I need from my family.” The total scores range from 12 to 84. The higher scores reflect a high level of perceived social support. The scores were categorized as follows: 12–35: low, 36–60: medium, and 61–84: high level of perceived social support [49]. MPSS has three subdomains, i.e., family (3, 4, 8, 11), friends (6, 7, 9, 12), and significant others (1, 2, 5, 10). The family denotes grandparents, parents, siblings, or children. Significant others signify neighbors, healthcare providers, or any person besides family members or friends [48].

Daily spiritual experiences scale [DSES

Spirituality was assessed employing 16 items from the DSES developed by Underwood and Teresi [50]. DSES includes 6-point Likert responses ranging between never (1) and many times a day (6) in 15 items. An example of one of the items is “I feel God’s love (or divine love) for me directly.” Additionally, one item (item 16) is focused on “how close do you feel to God (or the divine)?” which includes 4 possible responses: 1 (not close), 3 (somewhat close), 5 (very close), and 6 (as close as possible). The total scores are obtained by summing up all the items. The scores range from 16 to 96 [51]. The interpretation of the scores was as follows: total scores below the median value (low level), median value scores (medium level), and scores higher than the median value (high level). Permission to use this scale was obtained from Professor Dr. Lynn Underwood.

Loneliness scale (UCLA loneliness scale, 2004) [52]

A well-validated and reliable three-item loneliness scale [53] was used to measure the loneliness of older adults. It consists of 3 items with three response categories from 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (often). An example of one of the items is: “How often do you feel left out?” This scale measures social and relational connectedness as well as self-perceived isolation. The scores range from 3 to 9, where 3–5 = not lonely, and 6–9 = lonely [52]. This instrument is free to use [54].

Lubben social network scale [55]

Six items from the 6-point Likert scale (i.e., 0 = none, 1 = one, 2 = two, 3 = three or four, 4 = five through eight, and 5 = nine or more) of the Lubben Social Network Scale measure social engagement, including family and friends. An example of one of the items is “How many relatives do you see or hear from at least once a month?” The scores ranged from 0 to 30, with a higher score reflecting more social engagement, where mean scores 0–2: less social engagement, and 3–5 more social engagement. Items no. 1 to 3 are questions related to family, and Items no. 4 to 6 are related to friendships. This scale is a widely used tool in different countries in diverse settings, including the community, hospitals, adult day care centers, etc., primarily with older adults. This scale is permitted to be used for research.

Psychological wellbeing scale [56]

The PWB scale was used to assess the PWB of older adults. This scale consists of 42 items rated on a six-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). It encompasses six dimensions: (1) Autonomy: Items 1, 7, 13, 19, 25, 31, 37; (2) Environmental Mastery: Items 2, 8, 14, 20, 26, 32, 38; (3) Personal Growth: Items 3, 9, 15, 21, 27, 33, 39; (4) Positive Relations with Others: Items 4, 10, 16, 22, 28, 34, 40; (5) Purpose in Life: Items 5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 35, 41; and (6) Self-Acceptance: Items 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42. An example item from the scale is: “When I look at the story of my life, I am pleased with how things have turned out so far. Items 3, 5, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 23, 26, 27, 30, 31, 32, 34, 36, 39, and 41 are negatively worded and were reverse-scored to calculate the aggregate score. The total score ranges from 42 to 252, with higher scores indicating greater PWB. However, PWB was categorized as Low: < Mean − 1SD, Moderate: Mean ± 1SD, and High: >Mean + 1SD. The PWB scale has demonstrated reliability among older adults in the United States [26] and Iran [28].

CD-RISC-10, GSE-6, MPSS-N, DSES, and Loneliness Scale had already been translated into the Nepali language by Timalsina et al. [57] following the guidelines of Borsa et al. [58] and were found reliable and used among Nepalese older adults experiencing disaster [57]. The other tools, such as the Lubben Social Network Scale [55] and the PWB Scale [56] in the current study, were translated into the Nepali language following the six-step guidelines of Borsa et al. [58]. Following the recommendation of Perneger et al. [59], the translated versions of the instruments were pretested among 43 older adults at randomly selected two daycare centers, ‘G’ and ‘H’ of Kathmandu, that were designated for daycare services for older adults. During pretesting, RAs conducted cognitive interviews with individual debriefing to understand how older adults interpreted and made sense of each scale item. At the same time, RAs used behavioral coding to carefully observe their reactions, pauses, questions, or any signs of difficulty while responding. Together, these approaches demonstrated that all items were clear, easy to understand, and required no revisions. The reliability coefficient of these instruments was: CD-RISC-10 (α = 0.70), GSE-6 (α = 0.81), MPSS-N (α = 0.83), DSES (α = 0.80), Loneliness Scale (α = 0.78), Lubben Social Network Scale (α = 0.70), and PWBS (α = 0.71) by using the data of 43 older adults obtained from pretesting. These pretesting data were excluded from the main study. Subsequently, the reliability coefficients were calculated using the data from the 300 older adults in the actual study, resulting in slightly higher values: CD-RISC-10 (α = 0.76), GSE-6 (α = 0.86), MPSS-N (α = 0.89), DSES (α = 0.78), Loneliness Scale (α = 0.85), Lubben Social Network Scale (α = 0.70), and PWB Scale (α = 0.72).

Data collection techniques

Data were collected from September to October 2024. Before data collection, the research team conducted a formal discussion session to finalize the tools, administration methods, and instructions for the respondents. Two research assistants (RAs) were recruited, each holding a bachelor’s degree and having prior experience in research and data collection. The involvement of RAs helped maintain respondents’ anonymity from the core research team and fostered a comfortable environment that encouraged honest responses. The RAs underwent training conducted by the research team. This included a face-to-face session that outlined data collection guidelines and provided a detailed checklist of procedures. The training covered rapport-building techniques, informed consent protocols, familiarization with data collection instruments and techniques, and strategies for conducting patience, unhurried interviews. It also emphasized effective communication and interpersonal skills.

Before beginning data collection, RT and PA evaluated the interviewing skills of each RA, offering feedback and suggestions for improvement where needed. Following training, the RAs visited six designated daycare centers labeled ‘A’ through ‘F’ on dates coordinated with each center’s administrative authority. Adhering to all ethical guidelines, the RAs conducted face-to-face interviews using eight sets of Nepali-version questions. Each RA typically conducted five to six interviews per day, occasionally more depending on respondent availability. They were encouraged to listen attentively and create a space where respondents felt free to express their feelings, experiences, and any information they wished to share. Interviews were flexible in length, generally lasting between 45 and 60 min, with no strict time limits imposed. Despite involving eight sets of questions, including socio-demographic and personal characteristics, older adults were neither reluctant nor fatigued and were able to complete the interviews in one continuous session without requiring breaks or follow-up appointments. Moreover, at the end of each interview, the RAs carefully reviewed the completed interview schedule with the respondent to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Data management and analysis

The research team also conducted central editing of each interview schedule to ensure completeness and consistency. Then, the obtained data were classified, coded manually, and double-checked. Double data entry and data cleaning were done using Epi Data software. Then, the data were analyzed using SPSS software version 16 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Ill., USA) and AMOS version 21 software (AMOS Development Corporation, USA). Descriptive statistics (i.e., frequency, percentage, median/inter-quartile range, mean/standard deviation) were used to describe the sample characteristics and level of resilience, self-efficacy, social support, spirituality, social connectedness, loneliness, and PWB among older adults. All the assumptions for the Pearson product-moment correlation, such as level of measurement (i.e., all variables in the hypothesized model were interval level of measurement), related pairs, absence of outliers, and linearity, were achieved. Seventeen cases with multivariate influential outliers were removed based on Cook’s Distance and standardized residual. Furthermore, all the assumptions of multiple regression analysis (i.e., no missing data, no outliers, normality, linearity, no multicollinearity, homoscedasticity, and no autocorrelation) were achieved before path analysis (See Tables 4 and 5). Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the data in this study were not affected by the common method bias [CMB] associated with using a single data collection method. Additionally, partial correlation analysis was also conducted to further examine the influence of CMB. The findings and their overall effects are reported in the Supplementary file (See Table S10). Finally, path analysis was conducted on data from 283 samples.

Discussion

This study employed a descriptive analysis among 300 older adults and used a path analytical approach to develop and validate a model of PWB among 283 older adults in Nepal who were visiting daycare centers. The discussion centers on the levels of PWB, the associated variables, and the relationships between various influencing factors and PWB. Furthermore, a comparative analysis with previous studies was conducted to identify similarities and differences, offering insights into how the findings align with or diverge from existing literature, particularly within Nepal’s unique socio-cultural context.

The present study reveals that the majority of older adults exhibited a moderate level of PWB. This finding differs from Timalsina et al. [57], who reported low levels of positive mental health among Nepalese older adults surviving a disaster. Nevertheless, the finding of the present study is consistent with a cross-sectional study conducted in Iraq, where the highest proportion of older adults also reported moderate levels of PWB [8]. In addition, the older adults in this study primarily demonstrated an intermediate level of resilience, a high level of self-efficacy, and varying levels of spirituality ranging from low to high. These findings are in line with those of Timalsina et al. [57]. The finding in this study is largely attributable to the role of adult day care centers, as highlighted by Orellana et al. [64], Osterholm et al. [65], and Sadarangani et al. [66]. These centers provide structured opportunities for socialization, helping to reduce isolation and loneliness [64–66] and offer activities such as meals, exercise, recreational programs, cognitive stimulation, and social or religious events that support well-being and engagement [66]. Additionally, attendance at a day care center provides older adults with regular health monitoring, fosters confidence and stimulation, builds supportive networks, and creates a sense of belonging, sometimes acting as a ‘second home’ and compensating for limited family interactions [66]. Consequently, these centers are valued for building relationships, combating boredom, and facilitating meaningful interactions [65], which collectively enhance mental health and quality of life, including reduced depression and anxiety, and increased resilience, life satisfaction, self-esteem, and sense of control [64]. Likewise, Sharma [67] emphasized the role of variables such as marital status, literacy, decision-making authority, financial security, and family dynamics in shaping the psychosocial experiences of older adults in Nepal.

The hypothesized model of PWB demonstrated a good fit with the empirical data collected from Nepalese older adults, likely due to the inclusion of relevant factors that reflect both intrinsic and extrinsic dimensions of well-being. Notably, the finding of this study indicates a statistically significant direct relationship between self-efficacy and the PWB of older adults. This result is consistent with previous studies conducted among older adults in Nigeria [18], South Korea [19], and China [20]. In addition to the direct relationship, self-efficacy had a statistically significant indirect positive relationship with PWB through resilience. This finding aligns with a prior study in China, which similarly identified an indirect positive association between self-efficacy and mental health via resilience [35]. There are several plausible explanations for these relationships. Self-efficacy, the belief in one’s ability to manage various challenges, plays a key role in shaping emotions, thoughts, and actions [68]. It is evaluated based on how individuals perceive their capacity to meet specific goals and demands. Similarly, individuals with high self-efficacy tend to maintain a positive outlook, actively pursue challenging goals, and confidently address problems [68]. This proactive and optimistic approach fosters greater engagement and satisfaction in life, even in the face of adversity [68].

Additionally, this study identifies a statistically significant association between resilience and PWB of older adults. This result is comparable to findings from previous cross-sectional studies across diverse cultural contexts, including Europe [13], Spain [14], Thailand [15], Taiwan [16], and Pakistan [17], all of which reported a significant relationship between resilience and PWB. There are several possible explanations for this association. Individuals with high levels of resilience adapt more effectively to challenges, maintain a positive outlook despite adversity, and manage stress and difficult situations efficiently [69]. These qualities, along with a clear sense of purpose and confidence in overcoming obstacles, are closely linked to reduced anxiety and heightened PWB [69].

Similarly, the present study reveals that perceived social support had a statistically significant positive relationship with PWB. This finding aligns with previous studies among older adults in Nigeria [21], Iran [23, 24], and South Korea [19]. Several plausible explanations support this relationship. When individuals feel connected and supported, they are more resilient, experience lower levels of stress, and tend to enjoy better physical and mental health outcomes [70]. Moreover, social support is widely recognized as a fundamental component of strong interpersonal relationships and sound psychological health [70]. Conversely, a lack of social connections has been linked to depression, loneliness, impaired brain function, and increased risks of alcohol use, cardiovascular disease, and suicide [70]. Furthermore, social support encompassing emotional, instrumental, and informational dimensions plays an essential role in promoting mental well-being by meeting diverse human needs [71]. It offers empathy, provides practical assistance, and delivers guidance that helps individuals cope with stress, thereby reducing negative mental health outcomes [71]. Collectively, these insights emphasize the critical role of social support in enhancing PWB, highlighting its importance in maintaining both mental and physical health.

Conversely, loneliness exhibited a statistically significant direct negative relationship with PWB among older adults in the present study. This result aligns with previous studies among older adults in Canada [29], Ukraine [30], India [31], and Pakistan [32]. In contrast, social connectedness did not show a significant direct relationship with PWB, which differs from studies in Azerbaijan [33] and India [31]. Nevertheless, social connectedness contributed to PWB indirectly through its positive influence on resilience. This indirect relationship is supported by prior studies among adults in the USA [34], Iran [27], and Azerbaijan [33]. Factors such as retirement, declining physical health, loss of loved ones, and geographic relocation can contribute to loneliness in later life, intensifying feelings of helplessness and impairing cognitive function [72]. Chronic loneliness can also activate stress responses and negatively affect health [72], offering a plausible explanation for the observed associations in the present study.

Finally, the present study highlights that spirituality did not exhibit a statistically significant direct relationship with PWB. This finding contrasts with previous studies in the USA [26] and Iran [24, 27, 28], which reported significant positive associations. A probable explanation for this non-significant relationship is that, although older adults attending daycare centers frequently engage in spiritual activities, the normative and pervasive nature of spirituality may render it a background factor, thereby diminishing its distinct predictive power in statistical analyses. Furthermore, factors such as social support, loneliness, social connectedness, resilience, and self-efficacy could overshadow the influence of spirituality on PWB. Therefore, further research is warranted to explore the potential mediating or moderating pathways that may reveal a statistically significant relationship between spirituality and PWB.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The research team implemented rigorous training for research assistants, emphasizing effective and neutral questioning techniques to ensure consistency in data collection. To further enhance data accuracy, a cross-checking process was incorporated, whereby older adults’ responses were verified during the interviews. Although Harman’s single-factor test indicated no evidence of common method bias, reliance on a single data collection method may have introduced social desirability and response biases. However, partial correlation analysis revealed that resilience, self-efficacy, social support, and loneliness had genuine effects on psychological well-being, whereas the associations of spirituality and social connectedness were largely attributable to shared variance, thereby limiting confidence in their independent contributions. To address these issues, future studies could employ methodological separation (e.g., using different data collection methods for various variables, e.g., questionnaires, observations, or informant reports) and temporal separation by assessing independent and dependent variables at different time points. Additionally, interviewer bias cannot be ruled out and, combined with the extensive number of questions, may increase burden and survey fatigue among older adults. Thus, to mitigate these risks and improve data quality, future research should consider using shorter instruments, incorporating breaks during interviews, or conducting multiple sessions.

While this study offers valuable insights into gerontology, its cross-sectional design and reliance on face-to-face interviews at a single time point limit the ability to draw causal conclusions, resulting in temporal ambiguity. Accordingly, mediation analysis was applied as an exploratory tool to examine potential mechanisms among older adults rather than to establish definitive causal pathways. In addition, the study included only older adults who attended daycare centers, thereby excluding individuals who were unable to access these services due to limited physical activity, mobility challenges, cognitive decline, or other related factors. Furthermore, while the study identified significant factors associated with psychological well-being among older adults, the use of cluster, quota, and convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, limiting the representativeness of the samples. Older adults attending daycare centers may differ from other older adults in terms of health, social activity, cognitive function, socioeconomic status, or geographic diversity. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and confirmed through future longitudinal or experimental research. Moreover, future research should employ probability sampling techniques across diverse settings, such as community centers, religious organizations, and households, to enhance representativeness and strengthen the generalizability of the results.

Although the Nepali versions of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale and the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale have been validated among older adults in Nepal, Nepali versions of the Psychological Well-being Scale, the Daily Spiritual Experiences Scale, the UCLA Loneliness Scale, the Lubben Social Network Scale, and the General Self-Efficacy Scale have not yet been validated in the Nepalese context. Hence, future studies should prioritize the construct validation of these translated instruments within the Nepalese population and consider employing structural equation modeling to strengthen research design and interpretation. Although the research team excluded older adults with diagnosed psychiatric disorders based on self-report or confirmation from daycare authorities, this study did not assess other key mental health variables, such as undiagnosed psychiatric conditions or personality traits, which may have influenced older adults’ responses and overall psychological well-being. In addition, the effects of substance use, sociodemographic factors (including gender, ethnicity, income, education, household composition, and living conditions), and comorbidities were not examined. Future research should include these variables to better understand determinants of psychological well-being and identify at-risk groups for targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The majority of older adults demonstrated a moderate level of PWB, intermediate levels of resilience, high self-efficacy, evenly distributed low and high levels of spirituality, low levels of loneliness, and more social engagement. Furthermore, the model of PWB in older adults was well-supported by the empirical data, revealing statistically significant direct positive effects of self-efficacy, resilience, and perceived social support on PWB, while loneliness had a significant negative effect. In addition, resilience played a mediating role, with self-efficacy and social connectedness exerting indirect positive effects on PWB. Therefore, it is recommended to focus on enhancing self-efficacy, resilience, and social connectedness in interventions to improve PWB among older adults while addressing loneliness through supportive social engagement. Moreover, the role of spirituality could be further explored to understand its potential impact on well-being.

Implications

The PWB model could serve as a valuable framework for healthcare professionals to design targeted interventions aimed at enhancing PWB among older adults. These interventions would focus on key factors such as self-efficacy, resilience, social support, and social connectedness while addressing loneliness to prevent its negative impact. The findings from this study may help policymakers become aware of the factors associated with PWB and consider them when developing policies and protocols aimed at alleviating loneliness and strengthening social networks, ultimately contributing to improved mental health outcomes among older adults.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with applicable national regulations. Formal written administrative approval was obtained from the Social Security Department of Kathmandu Municipality and the organizational authority of each daycare center. Ethical approval was then secured from the Nepal Health Research Council (Ethical Approval No: NHRC 313/2024). Data were collected only after obtaining written informed consent from each respondent using an informed consent form. One signed copy of the form was retained by the researcher, while another copy was provided to each respondent for their future reference. Respondents’ privacy was ensured by conducting one-to-one interviews in a separate room within the daycare centers, away from other older adults, staff, and authorities. This approach encouraged respondents to express their inner feelings and life experiences, promoting PWB. Confidentiality was maintained by keeping the collected information secure and by analyzing and reporting data in an aggregate form. Respondents were assured that their identities would only be accessible to the research team and that the information gathered would be used solely for research purposes. RAs were instructed to ensure respondents’ anonymity by not collecting the identifiable information, assigning code numbers to interview schedules, and keeping the list of codes and consent forms separate from the interview schedule. All study materials were securely stored under double locks in the principal investigator’s office.

When respondents experienced emotional distress during the interview while recalling and sharing their life experiences, RAs paused the interview, provided emotional support, and stayed with them until they felt comfortable. The interview resumed when the respondents consented to continue. Respondents were informed of the potential benefits of sharing their experiences, including the possibility of identifying psychological distress. The authorities of the centers were informed when further professional help was required for some respondents. Additionally, given the increased risk of comorbidities with advancing age, respondents were provided with informational booklets covering essential topics such as osteoarthritis, diabetes, sleep disturbances, sleep promotion activities, hypertension, dementia, and stress-relief breathing exercises. Respondents were also encouraged to express any feelings of discomfort or dissatisfaction during the interview, which were acknowledged and respected. Throughout the study, respondents’ rights were protected, and they retained the option to withdraw or decline participation at any time, even if they had initially provided consent.
