As noted on its website, (The Pandemic EVIDENCE Collaboration - Kellogg College), The Pandemic EVIDENCE Collaboration was “founded in 2023, with its official launch taking place in April 2024, funded by the McCall MacBain Foundation. It was a collaborative initiative launched by independent academics and scholars from top-tier institutions around the world. Participants included Bond University in Australia, the University of Calgary and McMaster University in Canada, Oslo Metropolitan University, and the University of Oxford in the UK. These experts united with a shared mission: to identify, develop, and implement strategies for generating high-quality evidence on Non Pharmacological Interventions (NPIs) during public health emergencies, with the aim of better informing policy and care decisions.”

After more than a year of planning and agenda development, the Pandemic EVIDENCE Collaboration 2025 International Conference, entitled “Looking at the Pandemic in the Rearview Mirror: Successes, Failures and Unintended Consequences” was held May 14–16, 2025 at the Banff Centre for Arts and Creativity, in Banff, Canada in the heart of the Canadian Rockies. The themes of the Conference were aptly described in its title and was one of the first major international Conferences to systematically look back at the recent COVID-19 pandemic. The major aims of the Conference were to identify, develop and implement strategies to generate high-quality evidence for non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) during public health emergencies to inform policy and care decisions. As described in the Annual Report (PEC-McCall-MacBain-Report-2024-2551.pdf), “the three-day conference featured daily themes aligned with the Pandemic Evidence Collaboration’s three pillars. Over 120 attendees from 13 countries were able to meet with and learn from high-profile international speakers in the field, and members of the collaboration were building connections [sic] and setting out potential future alliances.”

1 Effectiveness of non-pharmaceutical interventions as implemented in the UK during the COVID-19 pandemic: a rapid review of experimental, epidemiological and mathematical modelling studies

Thulani Ashcroft1, Emilie McSwiggan1, Daphne Duval2, Serena Carville2, Stefano Brini2, Jose M. Ordonez-Mena3, Simon De Lusignan3, Aziz Sheikh3, Ruth McQuillan1, Evropi Theodoratou1

1University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, UK; 2UKHSA, London, UK; 3University of Oxford, Oxford, UK

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):1

Background

During the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, NPIs were the only preventive methods available to governments, health systems and populations. They were mainly implemented as a combination of measures although the types of NPIs as well as how and when they were implemented varied across countries which makes the assessment of the evidence of individual NPIs challenging. We conducted a rapid review to assess the evidence on NPIs as implemented in the UK, to support policy-makers to prepare for future pandemics.

Methods

We systematically searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, NIH Covid Portfolio and Corona Central for all UK interventional, observational, and mathematical modelling studies published between March 2020 and January 2024 reporting on NPI effectiveness (as measured by COVID-19 cases, transmission, hospitalisation, and mortality). A modified synthesis without meta-analysis approach was taken to analyse the following domains for each NPI and outcome: methodological quality, study relevance, consistency of results across studies, and assessment of precision.

Results

Ninety-seven (85 modelling, nine observational, and three interventional) studies reported outcomes across 20 NPI categories. Modelling studies were limited by multiple quality issues, while only half of the non-modelling studies were high quality. Because of study heterogeneity, we synthesised the evidence on the direction, but not the size of effect. The evidence for test and release strategies for the contacts of positive cases (moderate certainty of evidence), school related NPIs (low certainty of evidence) and universal lockdown (low certainty of evidence) was suggestive of a protective effect. For the remaining NPI categories there was very low or inconclusive certainty of the evidence to support their effectiveness.

Conclusions

Our study was the first to assess the available evidence on the effectiveness of NPIs specifically as implemented in the UK. We found few studies with high internal validity, the rest were highly heterogeneous with most NPI categories providing very low certainty or inconclusive evidence of a protective effect. These results do not necessarily reflect a lack of effectiveness of packages of NPIs implemented in the UK but highlight the need to strengthen evaluation of public health interventions. We suggest strategies for improving evidence generation to support future pandemic decision-making.

2 Randomized trials of public health and social measures for controlling pandemics: statistical challenges and opportunities

Christopher James Rose, Unni Gopinathan, Runar Solberg

Norwegian Institute of Public Health, Oslo, Norway

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):2

Before vaccines became available, public health and social measures (PHSM) such as mask mandates, school closures, social distancing, and restrictions on movement were the primary tools for managing the COVID-19 pandemic. However, these were implemented with limited high-quality evidence on their effects and interactions with respect to benefits and harms. To better prepare for future pandemics, there is a critical need for randomized PHSM trials to provide high-quality evidence for decision-making.

PHSM trials face unique challenges compared to clinical trials for pharmaceuticals, vaccines, or other public health interventions. Parallel designs may not always satisfy ethics committees. Simple designs may be inefficient, risk exacerbating trial fatigue and recruitment difficulties, and can fail to represent diverse settings. Furthermore, trials of suboptimal dosing or implementation strategies may lead to misleading conclusions about PHSM safety and effectiveness. Common outcomes and effect measures, such as dichotomous events and risk ratios, may fail to capture the dynamic nature of infections and symptoms, limiting their utility to decision-makers.

While PHSM trials should probably be pragmatic, their credibility can be undermined by nonadherence that differs from what would be expected during a pandemic, and missing outcome data. Addressing these issues could improve both the quality of the evidence and its broader acceptance. Misalignment between research questions and trial results could further limit the applicability of PHSM trials to future pandemics. Traditional reporting approaches, such as publishing a single effect estimate in a journal, may limit the utility of trial results for future pandemics and populations that differ from trial settings.

This presentation will explore methodological options and innovations in trial design and powering, outcome selection and development, analysis and effect measures, and reporting, to address these challenges. It will also highlight research opportunities to advance PHSM trial methodology, ensuring the delivery of high-quality, translatable evidence that equips decision-makers to manage future pandemics effectively.

3 Side-effects of using lockdown as public health measure: impact of COVID-19 restrictions on violence against young girls and their mental health in Bhopal, Madhya Pradesh

Rajnish Prasad

IIHMR University, Jaipur, India

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):3

Background

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, governments worldwide implemented strict lockdown measures to control the spread of the COVID19. While considered important from public health perspective, these restrictions disproportionately impacted vulnerable populations, particularly in developing countries like India where social and gender norms increases the vulnerabilities faced by young girls. This study explores the compounded effects of the lockdown on violence against adolescent girls and mental health among adolescent girls in urban areas of Bhopal, India.

Methods

A mixed-methods approach was used, combining structured surveys of 256 adolescent girls aged 15–24 with qualitative insights from focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews with 30 participants.

Results

The findings from study show that that 47% of girls faced some form of violence, with family’s income loss and movement restrictions as key drivers. Among young girls with partner, 69% girls reported increase in intimate partner violence, as social isolation exacerbated vulnerabilities by limiting access to support networks. Social isolation and movement restrictions also contributed in worsening of mental health among young girls, with 72% reported anxiety symptoms, 52% shown signs of depression, and 43% experienced sleep disturbances. Additional stressors, such as interrupted education, increased caregiving responsibilities, and the lack of psychosocial support, further compounded these challenges. During FGDs and in-depth interviews, girls shared that lockdowns have increased their care responsibilities and time spent on household work. Due to existing digital inequities, girls have much lesser access to mobile phone and internet which means they were not able to communicate with their friends and their education was also affected.

Conclusions

The findings highlight the lockdown’s role in exacerbating pre-existing gender inequalities, creating a “shadow pandemic” of violence and mental health crises. Immediate and long-term policy measures are necessary to address these compounded impacts. Further, while designing and implementing non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) during public health emergencies it is very important to understand how these interventions will interact with existing inequities and vulnerabilities. The findings from the study underscores the critical need for an intersectional approach to policymaking that prioritizes the safety, mental health, and well-being of vulnerable groups including young girls, fostering resilience and equity. By addressing these vulnerabilities, policymakers can contribute to effective response to public health emergencies, ensuring that girls and women are not left behind in the wake of public health crises.

5 Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on adverse event incidence rates in Canadian hospitals: a retrospective chart review study

Guosong Wu

University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):5

Objectives

Tremendous efforts have been made to improve the quality and safety of healthcare in Canada. Yet, progress in addressing hospital adverse events has been varied and lacks comprehensive assessment. The study aimed to evaluate the incidence rates and trends of AEs, assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on AE rates, and analyze differences in patient outcomes.

Methods

This retrospective electronic chart review included a random sample of adult patients admitted to acute care hospitals in Calgary between 2017 and 2022. Eighteen Aes and patient information was extracted. We calculated the observed adverse event incidence rates and risk-standardized adverse event rates (RSAER). Interrupted time series analysis was employed to determine the impact of COVID-19 on AE trends. Outcome differences were evaluated using mixed-effects logistic regression and negative binomial models.

Results

Among 10,673 patient admissions, 2,310 Aes were identified, resulting in an incidence rate of 21.64 (95% CI: 20.77–22.54) per 100 patient admissions, or 30.65 (95% CI: 25.77–27.97) per 1,000 patient days. Although a modest upward trend in AE rates was noted during the pandemic, statistical significance was not reached (IRR=1.13, 95% CI:0.74–1.75) after adjusting for patient characteristics, seasonal variations, and overall trends. In multivariable mixed-effect models, patients with Aes had significantly longer hospital stays (IRR=3.13, 95% CI: 2.97–3.30), increased odds of experiencing 30-day readmission (OR=1.4, 95% CI: 1.17–1.68) and in-hospital death (OR=1.72, 95% CI: 1.43–2.08).

Conclusions

The incidence of Aes remains high in acute healthcare settings. Despite the challenges posed by COVID-19, our study suggests that AE rates did not significantly increase. Future initiatives are needed to implement strategies and interventions to reduce adverse events in the post-pandemic era.

6 Evaluation of the Norwegian national tool for observation of infection prevention measures in healthcare (NOST) – a cluster randomized trial

Mette Fagernes1, Eriksen-Volle Hanne-Merete1, Christine Holst2, Christopher James Rose2,3, Runar Barstad Solberg2, Petter Elstrøm2

1Department of Infection control and Preparedness, Norwegian Institute of Public Health, Oslo, Norway; 2Centre for Epidemic Interventions Research, Norwegian Institute of Public Health, Oslo, Norway; 3Cluster for Reviews and Health Technology Assessments, Norwegian Institute of Public Health, Oslo, Norway

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):6

Introduction

Hand hygiene is a crucial preventive measure in managing pandemics. Effective infection prevention and control (IPC) is essential for ensuring high-quality healthcare services. Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) can disrupt essential medical treatments1. To prevent HAIs, healthcare personnel must adhere to standard IPC measures during patient care, including hand hygiene and the use of personal protective equipment. Although healthcare personnel may have high knowledge of proper hand hygiene practices, compliance can vary significantly between healthcare services and professional groups2. The Norwegian Institute of Public Health (NIPH) has introduced a new electronic tool and national template for direct observation of compliance with recommended IPC measures in healthcare (NOST). This abstract describes an ongoing cluster-randomized trial3 that aims to measure if the implementation of NOST leads to: 1) increased compliance with hand hygiene recommendations, and 2) impacts the epidemiology of HAIs in healthcare institutions and the length of hospital stays.

Methods

Eligible hospital wards were randomly allocated 1:1 to intervention or control. The intervention involves implementing NOST, which includes observing and recording compliance with hand hygiene recommendations, reporting the results back to the wards, identifying areas for improvement, followed by training and other measures to improve IPC. Implementation of NOST in control wards was delayed for one year, until after data collection had been completed. The primary outcome is compliance with hand hygiene recommendations by hospital staff performing patient-related work. Secondary outcomes include HAIs and length of hospital stays. Data were obtained from the national NOST database and Norwegian health registers. Treatment effects will be estimated as risk ratios or mean differences, depending on whether the outcome variables are binomial or continuous4.

Results

Data, including more than 3000 hand hygiene observations from 106 wards across 16 hospitals, were collected in November 2024 and will be analysed in the following months. We will present final or preliminary results at the Pandemic Evidence Collaboration conference in May 2025.

Conclusions

Ensuring high compliance with hand hygiene among healthcare personnel is crucial for maintaining the quality of healthcare services. During outbreaks and pandemics, maintaining good hand hygiene practices is essential. Our trial evaluates the effect of a tool for improving compliance with hand hygiene recommendations.

7 Use of technology in pandemic’s response – benefits and limitations: some insights from adaptation of CoWIN platform for COVID19 vaccination in India

Rajnish Prasad

IIHMR University, Jaipur, India

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):7

Objectives

The global COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical role of digital technologies in enabling large-scale public health responses. India’s CoWIN platform, a cornerstone of the world’s largest vaccination program, demonstrated how digital tools can streamline logistics, monitor coverage, and enhance transparency. By facilitating over 2 billion vaccinations, CoWIN highlighted the potential of technology to address complex public health challenges. However, it also revealed significant inequities, particularly in resource-limited settings. This paper examines the dual role of the use of CoWIN platform in advancing vaccine access while inadvertently amplifying existing disparities in health equity.

Methods

A comprehensive review was conducted using data available on CoWIN dashboard to analyze the uptake of COVID19 vaccination. Further, 26 in-depth interviews were conducted, to understand views of different stakeholders and beneficiaries.

Results

While the CoWIN app improved logistics in urban centers, rural areas with limited digital literacy and internet penetration were disproportionately affected. By May 2021, urban areas received 30 vaccine doses for every 12.7 doses administered in rural India, underscoring the digital divide in healthcare delivery. The exclusionary impact of digital-only channels is further amplified for populations without smartphones, stable internet connections, or the digital literacy needed to navigate such platforms. Gender inequities were particularly stark, with over 100 million women failing to receive their second vaccine dose by mid-2022. Besides that, linguistic barriers, inadequate offline systems, and limited health infrastructure emerged as significant obstacles to equitable vaccine distribution.

Conclusions

The findings from the study show that while digital tools can improve efficiency and scalability, its success depends on addressing structural inequities. Policymakers must prioritize inclusive design, multi-modal delivery, and proactive measures to bridge the digital divide. These principles are crucial to ensure that public health technologies promote equity and resilience in global health systems, particularly in the context of pandemic preparedness and response.

8 Emergency preparedness and integrated surveillance potential of a rapid COVID-19 and TB diagnostic in Bangladesh: an adaptive response in health system governance

Farzana Zaman1,2, Muhammad Asaduzzaman3

1Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), Dhaka, Bangladesh; 2University of Pretoria, Pretoria, South Africa; 3University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):8

Background

The impact of the COVID-19 situation, coupled with the continual emergence of new variants, had significantly disrupted not only the local health system but also the aviation system and global public health. Subsequently, many unprecedented challenges have been observed at the health system management of resource poor settings, notably inadequate airport quarantine facilities and lack of highly sensitive yet rapid and cost-effective testing. Therefore, we aimed to present a cost-effective and timely alternative diagnostic and quarantine facility for the incoming travelers in such settings.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional serosurvey from December 15, 2020 to November 30, 2021 for inbound travelers to Bangladesh from different countries. These impoverished travelers (unable to afford hotel quarantine) were quarantined in Hajj camp (a government facility near the airport reserved for Muslim pilgrims) with full coverage of food and lodging at subsidized cost nd free testing by GeneXpert, which is capable of Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (NAAT) for both COVID-19 and TB.

Results

The majority of our study participants were male (74.32%), mainly in the 20–40 year age group. Among 1328 participants, 106 (7.98%) tested positive for SARS-COV-2, with 72 being male. The highest infection rate was observed in travelers from Singapore (22.53%, n=16/71), followed by USA and Malaysia. The NAAT result processing time was only 1 h, enabling 92% (1222 participants) to travel to their destinations on the same day. The positive cases were kept in quarantine for further assessment.

Conclusions

Our study findings endorse the equitable access to COVID-19 diagnostics for disadvantaged groups and optimizing the effective utilization of limited health system resources. Due to high sensitivity and specificity, NAAT is also recommended by WHO for diagnosis of acute COVID-19 cases. Therefore, our research also emphasizes on the potential to equip hard to reach laboratory facilities by GeneXpert for both TB and COVID-19 diagnostics to ensure fair and sustainable health systems, particularly in regions facing the dual burden.

9 Antibiotic consumption and its link to the exaggeration of COVID-19

Muhammad Asaduzzaman1, Farzana Zaman2

1University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway; 2Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), Dhaka, Bangladesh

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):9

When we observe the history of the deadliest pandemics on earth from Antonine Plague to current COVID-19(Forum, 2020), no bacterial pandemic has occurred since the discovery of Penicillin (Lobanovska & Pilla, 2017). The misuse of antibiotics in respiratory viral infections is well established (Li et al., 2016). We hypothesize that use of antibiotics is linked with the exacerbation of viral infections like COVID-19 on the basis of epidemiological and immunological evidence.

Eili Y. Klein et al (2018) shows that United States, France, and Italy consumed the highest quantity of antibiotics among high income countries in 2015, while India, China, and Pakistan did it among lower middle-income countries. Surprisingly, these countries are also burdened with high COVID-19 cases (ECDC, 2020). Turkey, Tunisia, Spain, Greece, Algeria, Romania, Belgium, France, New Zealand and Ireland were the leading 10 countries for antibiotic use in total (Klein et al., 2018) and they are the countries with high disease prevalence (ECDC, 2020). Brazil is the leading country for antibiotic consumption (Klein et al., 2018) and same for COVID-19 cases (ECDC, 2020) in Latin America. The total antibiotic consumption (in metric tons) was higher in Brazil, Turkey, Iran, Russia, France, Tanzania, Sudan, Italy, UK and South Korea chronologically (WHO, 2018). We all are aware of the deadliest attack of current pandemic in these countries. In the ECDC report, the most COVID prevalent Spain, France, Italy, Ireland, Belgium, Portugal, Poland, UK are also listed as high antibiotic users in Europe (ECDC, 2019, 2020).

Thackray et al (2018) have identified impaired T cell response and enhanced viral burden in response to flavi viruses (Dengue, Zika and West Nile virus) in mice after giving oral antibiotics including the ability of oral antibiotics (e.g. Ampicillin) to increase disease severity just with 3 days’ dose.

Morbid COVID-19 patients develop a hyper inflammatory state alled the “Cytokine storm syndrome” with the increased production of proinflammatory cytokines like IL1β, IFNγ, TNF-α (Huang et al., 2020). We consider this type of immune response can be associated with antibiotic therapy. Antibiotics like Linezolid and Vancomycin can augment cytokine production (TNFα, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10) through activating Toll-like receptors (Bode et al., 2015). Again, gut microbiota can halt the respiratory RNA virus replication in the lung epithelia through increased type 1 interferon signaling which can be delayed or severely hindered by antibiotics through reducing interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in lung stroma (Bradley et al., 2019). Our hypothesis is further supported by a recently published article (Hantoushzadeh & Norooznezhad,2020) where the authors have described the possible pathophysiology of antibiotic induced septic shock in COVID-19 patients. In the absence of bacterial infection if antibiotics are administered, the cytokine storm may initiate with unregulated release of Toll-like receptors (TLRs), IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and gut endotoxins (Hantoushzadeh & Norooznezhad, 2020).

Despite having several other factors, the fatality in SARS-CoV-2 infection is higher in European and high income countries where antibiotic consumption is higher. We recommend the containment of irrational use of antibiotics especially for respiratory symptoms in this pandemic era.

Objectives

When we observe the history of the deadliest pandemics on earth from Antonine Plague to current COVID-19, no bacterial pandemic has occurred since the discovery of Penicillin. The misuse of antibiotics in respiratory viral infections is well established. Therefore, we need to know whether the use of antibiotics is linked with the exacerbation of viral infections like COVID-19 on the basis of epidemiological and immunological evidence.

Methods

We conducted several biomedical resources including literature to identify the link between antibiotic consumption and COVID-19 mortality and morbidity.

Results

Impaired T cell response and viral burden is enhanced in mice after giving oral antibiotics including the ability of oral antibiotics to increase disease severity just with 3 days’ dose. Morbid COVID-19 patients develop a hyper inflammatory state called the “Cytokine storm syndrome” with the increased production of proinflammatory cytokines like IL1β, IFNγ, TNF-α. We consider this type of immune response can be associated with antibiotic therapy. Antibiotics like Linezolid and Vancomycin can augment cytokine production (TNFα, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10) through activating Toll-like receptors. Again, gut microbiota can halt the respiratory RNA virus replication in the lung epithelia through increased type 1 interferon signaling which can be delayed or severely hindered by antibiotics through reducing interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in lung stroma. In the absence of bacterial infection if antibiotics are administered, the cytokine storm may initiate with unregulated release of Toll-like receptors (TLRs), IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and gut endotoxins.

Conclusions

Despite having several other factors, the fatality in SARS-CoV-2 infection is higher in European and high income countries where antibiotic consumption is higher. We recommend the containment of irrational use of antibiotics especially for respiratory symptoms in this pandemic era.

10 Exploring Norwegian public attitudes towards public health policy experiments in and outside of public health crises

Ingeborg Hess Elgersma1, Unni Gopinathan1, Heather Munthe-Kaas1, Christine Holst1, Atle Fretheim1,2

1Norwegian Institute of Public Health, Oslo, Norway; 2Oslo Metropolitan University, Oslo, Norway

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):10

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic extensive non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) were enforced that heavily impacted people’s lives. While the effectiveness of these interventions was often uncertain, authorities rarely initiated randomised implementation of these interventions (“policy experiments”). Public acceptance of public health policy experiments may be a barrier to generating high-quality evidence for NPIs.

Objectives

The objective of this study is to better understand Norwegian adults’ preferences regarding government-initiated public health policy experiments.The specific aims are to (1) Assess the general level of acceptability for public health policy experimentation among Norwegian adults; (2) Examine whether acceptance differs between trials (a) focused on different type of public health policies and (b) between trials conducted during and outside a pandemic; (3)Identify the conditions that increase or decrease public support for policy experiments during a pandemic and (4) Examine whether the level of acceptance differs between different types of trial design.

Methods

30 Exploring the importance of perspective in study design to assess the effects or impacts of non-pharmacological interventions during public health emergencies

Tara Schuller

Institute of Health Economics, Edmonton, Canada

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):30

Seminar

Objectives

The evaluation of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) requires an appropriate study design to produce valid and robust results within the resource constraints ubiquitous in research. Those seeking to study the impacts of NPIs must determine which successes, failures, and unintended consequences will be assessed. Such decisions inform the study scope and reveal which impacts, and whose experiences or perspectives, count. For this reason, it is good practice to define in advance the study perspective (from which point of view the investigation is conducted). The perspective frames or scopes the study, and informs the selection of the effects to be measured.

For the assessment of NPIs with broad impact in particular, such as those implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic, embedding contextual factors into impact studies can optimize results for local decision making, but it can make results difficult to compile, compare, and translate to other jurisdictions around the globe.

Methods

Selecting the appropriate perspective is important to generate high-quality evidence for non-pharmacological interventions during public health emergencies. Drawing on examples from the domain of health technology assessment, this seminar will engage participants in the consideration of two common perspectives taken in the evaluation of healthcare interventions: the healthcare payer perspective and the societal perspective.

In the healthcare payer perspective, only those costs and benefits that affect the healthcare system are included, for example patient health outcomes, wait times, access to care, or clinician satisfaction. Costs or benefits that accrue outside the health system, such as to worker absenteeism, educational delays in children, or social isolation of seniors (etc.) are not assessed. These kinds of broader impacts would instead be captured in the societal perspective, along with potentially many other kinds of costs and benefits spanning socioeconomic, psychosocial, and environmental factors that affect different stakeholders in different settings.

Results

Through the short presentation of definitions, theoretical background, and practical examples, the audience will be engaged in facilitated discussion to ways to improve the quality of research on NPIs and the transferability of findings for global knowledge sharing. The results will be to co-create knowledge among participants about the importance of study perspective in assessing the impacts of NPIs implemented during public health emergencies. Subthemes such as weighting as well as the consideration of short- and long-term consequences will be explored. Possible solutions will be postulated to address challenges raised. For example, to improve the comparability of results of NPIs research, it may be useful to differentiate the technical evaluation of NPIs from the appraisal or decision-making step which follows (at which point weighting or other factors could be brought to bear on the evidence in a deliberative process).

Conclusions

As the domain of NPI evaluation advances to generate a corpus of high-quality evidence and to support efficient knowledge exchange globally, the implications of different perspectives and weightings of results may need to be considered. Different study design elements like perspective and weightings present opportunities to tailor evidence to more appropriately inform local decision making, but they can also introduce barriers to the comparative potential of results and their transferability to jurisdictions across the globe.

Objectives

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a detrimental impact on the health and well-being of individuals globally. Public health interventions aimed at mitigating the pandemic’s spread, such as social distancing, school closures, and working from home, have been linked to increased feelings of loneliness. Job loss and business closure exacerbated the issue. Loneliness refers to self-perceived isolation and dissatisfaction with the quality of social connections, and it may be a risk factor for mental ill health.

Northwest Territories (NWT), a northern territory in Canada, spans 1.2 million square kilometres and has a population of 41,135, with Indigenous peoples comprising 51% of the population. Due to its vast area, geographic remoteness, and transportation challenges, social isolation has significantly impacted the quality of life for its residents. This project investigated the lived experiences of loneliness during the COVID-19 pandemic and social distancing measures among Indigenous communities in the NWT.

Methods

This project was conducted in ten NWT communities: three large (> 1000 people), three medium-sized (300–1000 people), and four small (< 300 people). Some communities are isolated with limited road access and health cabins, while others have road access, community health centres, and one large community has a hospital.

This project employed a cross-sectional, mixed-method design and convenience sampling to recruit Indigenous individuals (≥ 18 years). The community-based participatory research model was used, with a community advisory board comprising Elders, healthcare professionals, and government officials.

Data were collected via semi-structured one-on-one interviews, with closed and open-ended questions covering COVID-19’s impacts on physical and mental health, health behaviours, healthcare access, and people’s lived experiences. The questionnaire included one close-ended question regarding loneliness experience: “How has the pandemic affected your feelings of loneliness?” which was followed by an open-ended question for further explanation. Descriptive statistics and deductive thematic analysis were used for data analysis.

Results

Of the participants (n=287), 66.55% were women, 53.00% were 30 to 49 years old, and 72.47% identified as First Nations and 21.60% as Inuit. Most participants (74.48%) reported very good perceived health status, 34.86% reported no change in feelings of loneliness compared to before the pandemic, and 33.45% reported feeling more or much more lonely. Six themes emerged from the qualitative data: pre-pandemic social isolation, associated factors and aspects of social isolation, feelings of loneliness, outcomes of loneliness and coping with loneliness. Lockdown measures, voluntary social avoidance, and limited social gatherings and connections were reported as factors potentially contributing to loneliness, with depression and anxiety reported as outcomes. Participants utilized several strategies to cope with harmful impacts of the pandemic and social distancing and isolation, including social media, engaging with family at home, self-reflecting, and going to the land.

Conclusions

Public health measures and individual responses to perceived COVID-19 risks may have exacerbated isolation and loneliness among Indigenous communities in NWT, potentially harming mental health. Consequently, public health emergency measures in remote areas, including Indigenous communities in NWT and globally, should facilitate coping strategies with social isolation and develop interventions to sustain individuals’ connections to community and culture.

34 Challenges faced by health policymakers in responding to the COVID-19 pandemic in Northwest Territories, Canada

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):34

Objectives

COVID-19 exposed the fragmented nature of health systems in many countries, including Canada. While Canada’s multilevel COVID-19 response, involving national, provincial/territorial, and municipal authorities, effectively controlled the pandemic, there existed regional operational challenges. Many Indigenous community members in Northwest Territories (NWT) in Canada live in remote and isolated communities with accompanying disproportionate socioeconomic, healthcare, and transportation challenges, which may negatively affect the pandemic response.

NWT implemented non-pharmaceutical interventions including closing schools and non-essential businesses, restricting outdoor and indoor gatherings, and mandating self-isolation after returning from out-of-territory travel. Although the response was strongly adhered to and successfully reduced the spread of COVID-19, it was not without challenges. Understanding these challenges is essential for enhancing future public health responses Indigenous communities in NWT and across Canada. This study explored the perspectives of NWT health policymakers on these challenges to identify strategies to strengthen public health preparedness and response.

Methods

This study used a qualitative descriptive design to uncover explicit themes. A community-based participatory research approach was followed, involving collaboration among researchers, Indigenous organizations, and community members. A community advisory board played a key role in planning and implementation. Policymakers from various healthcare organizations in NWT were recruited via convenience sampling methods. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research were followed. Data were collected using semi-structured interviews and covered demographics, perspectives on COVID-19 response, challenges, and future strategies. Interviews averaged 30 min. Participants provided verbal or written consent, and interviews were audio-recorded with permission. Responses were recorded in REDCap. Deductive thematic analysis was conducted, informed by the World Health Organization’s Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management framework. Two authors independently coded the data, with a third resolving discrepancies. NVivo Pro V12 was used for qualitative data analysis.

Results

The participants interviewed (n=56) held several managerial roles, including public health (64.3%) and community service management (26.8%). Participants worked in territorial organizations (71.7%), regional organizations (14.3%), and community organizations (14.3%). Qualitative data revealed four key themes representing the challenges faced by policymakers: Governance challenges included unclear roles and responsibilities, policy lagging, gaps between policy and frontline operations, and limited community consultation. Risk communication challenges included the complexity of information and language barriers. Community-level challenges included people’s compliance level with public health measures, the communities’ distrust of the health system, and geographic barriers. Health system challenges included limited human resource capacity, information technology challenges, the absence of robust information systems, and limited material resources.

Conclusions

Health policymakers in NWT strived to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic addressing challenges related to governance, communication, community, and the health system. Building a resilient health system capable of predicting, adapting to, and responding to future public health pandemics, while maintaining essential health services, requires several key actions. These include clearly defining roles and responsibilities during public health emergencies, fostering collaboration among policymakers, frontline healthcare providers, and community leaders, developing efficient and effective data infrastructure, and enhancing both infrastructural and human resource capacities within the health system. Equally critical is ensuring effective communication with communities and rebuilding trust in the health system among community members.

35 Modelled evidence addressing non-pharmacological interventions for viral respiratory infections: a scoping review protocol

Jessica J. Bartoszko1,2, Adam S. Komorowski1,2,3,4,5, Jenine Leal2,6,7,8,9, Yuan Zhang1,2,10, Mark Loeb1,2,3,5

1Department of Health Research Methods, Evidence and Impact, McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada; 2Pandemic EVIDENCE Collaboration, Oxford University, Oxford, UK; 3Department of Pathology and Molecular Medicine, McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada; 4Microbiology Department, Hamilton Regional Laboratory Medicine Program, Hamilton, Canada; 5Michael G. DeGroote Institute for Infectious Disease Research, McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada; 6Department of Community Health Sciences, Cumming School of Medicine, University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada; 7Department of Microbiology, Immunology, and Infectious Diseases, Cumming School of Medicine, University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada; 8O’Brien Institute of Public Health, Cumming School of Medicine, University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada; 9Infection Prevention & Control, Alberta Health Services, Edmonton, Canada; 10Department of Health Sciences, Brock University, St. Catherines, Canada

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):35

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic, decision-makers relied on modelled evidence to inform policy and action on non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs). The objectives of this scoping review are to summarize the methodological characteristics and quality of epidemiologic modelling studies addressing NPI effectiveness against viral respiratory infection transmission.

Methods

MEDLINE, Embase, MathSciNet and the Living Overview of Evidence online platform, along with citations of included studies, will be searched from inception for studies published as peer-reviewed articles that predict the effect of NPIs on viral respiratory infection transmission. Eligible NPIs may include masking, physical distancing, quarantine upon arrival from travel and school closures. Viral respiratory infections caused by World Health Organization priority pathogens for pandemic preparedness (e.g., SARS-CoV-2, influenza A H5N1) may be eligible. Transmission outcomes may include the reproductive number and rate of incident infections. Studies of statistical models used to estimate the associations between measured variables (i.e., regression models) will be excluded. Screening and data extraction will be completed independently and in duplicate. Collected data will include bibliographic (e.g., year of study publication) and methodological characteristics (e.g., model inputs). Reviewers will use Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guidance conceptualized for modelled evidence to assess the certainty of evidence from included studies. The GRADE assessment will include considerations for risk of bias (e.g., confidence in model inputs), inconsistency (e.g., consistency of model inputs ascertained from the literature), imprecision (e.g., whether or not the modeling study included information about the output variability), indirectness (e.g., degree to which the model output was discrepant with the decision question in terms of the intended population, intervention, time horizon and outcome), and publication bias (e.g., if the single model was not constructed de novo, likelihood that relevant models have been constructed but were not published or made publicly available). We will report results as total numbers, frequencies, and percentages for categorical variables and medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables that are not normally distributed.

Discussion

This study has several strengths compared to previous reviews: inclusion of viral respiratory infections beyond COVID-19; a comprehensive search strategy; no time restriction to the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic; and application of novel GRADE guidance for modelled evidence. This overview of the current landscape may help standardize methods for the conduct, reporting and quality assessment of transmission modelling studies, and inform future generation of high-quality modelled evidence for NPIs and emerging pathogens.

1Department of Medical Microbiology and Immunology, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Canada; 2Department of Medicine, University of Calgary, Calgary, Canada

Introduction/Objectives

Recent data (Bruce et al 2021) have provided valuable insights into how diagnostic PCR-based tests might be used to predict the SARS-CoV-2 infectiousness, reporting that neither a previously published virus negative-strand RNA assay nor a virus subgenomic RNA assay offered any advantages. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrated that the best practical tool used quantitative RT-PCR, E-gene primers, and a Cycle threshold (Ct) cutoff of 31.0 [95% True Positive Rate (TPR) and 61% Positive Predictive Value (PPV)]. The utilized E gene RNA, negative‐strand RNA, and two subgenomic RNA primer‐probe sets may not be as widely used as the N-gene primers designed by the CDC for their SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR assay (Anon. 2020). We therefore sought to assess how well infectivity from clinical samples on cell culture can be predicted using the CDC N-gene primers.

Methods

We have re-evaluated a data set we derived from > 400 clinical specimens, the vast majority of which were collected within a 7-to-8 day window following the onset of symptoms (Lin et al, 2022). The Ct was determined using a commercial RT-PCR kit (IDT Cat#10006713) and unlabelled N-gene primers with internal controls, and the virus titers (PFU/mL) were determined by plating dilutions on Vero (ATCC #CCL-81) or Vero E6/TMPRSS2 (JCRB cell bank 1819) cells. The limit of detection of the plaque assay was ~5 PFU/mL. We generated a ROC plot using GraphPad Prism (v 9.3.1) to compare cultivatability versus the N-gene Ct in a manner analogous to what was described by Bruce et al.

Results

We generated a ROC curve from our data (Figure 1) and found a Ct value ≤ 28.8 would predict that a specimen contained infectious virus in 95% of the infected samples (dotted line), while returning a false positive rate of 35% in uninfected specimens. Judging from DNA-templated standard curves, a Ct of 28.8 in this quantitative PCR assay is approximately 700 target copies per PCR reaction. The 95% positive predictive agreement was very similar to that observed using the total E-gene based primer gene assay (Bruce et al). The determination of Ct thresholds may be a useful surrogate to predict transmission risk and allow removal of isolation in healthcare settings. A limitation is that we assumed that the ability to culture virus on Vero E6 or Vero TMPRSS2 cells was correlated with the ability to be infectious to humans.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that the CDC N gene assay would also be a valuable PCR assay, but with a different Ct cut-off and somewhat higher PPV, when judging the risk of finding cultivatable SARS-CoV-2 in specimens from acutely infected and convalescent patients. We recognize that Ct values are dependent upon the primer sets used, platform used and the laboratory in which it is performed which may be a limitation of our findings.

Posited as a solution to this problem, healthcare policy widely advocated for non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) in the form of digital health technologies (DHTs); accordingly, many healthcare systems accelerated DHT implementation, often with little evidence around their effectiveness. The findings presented here are a truncated part of a larger study which comprised a realist evaluation of coeliac disease (CD) DHTs.

The objectives of this abstract are to:Present a novel understanding of the extent to which rapid implementation of NPIs, in the form of DHTs for coeliac disease, may result in successes, failures and unintended consequences; and Provide evidence which can be used to inform policy and care decisions around use of DHTs during public health emergencies.

Methods

To meet the above objectives, we employed a realist evaluation approach. A strength of this methodology means that the results have the potential to be applicable beyond CD to DHTs for many chronic conditions. Thus, the implications of this study may extend more widely to implementation of a range of NPIs in the form of DHTs in a healthcare emergency.

As part of a realist evaluation, realist interviews were conducted with 40 seldom-heard individuals with CD around the use of DHTs. Participants were recruited purposefully and comprised individuals who were; digitally excluded; living with multimorbidity; from low socioeconomic status and from black and minority ethnic communities.

Results

Successes: Our data analysis found that many individuals with CD did not receive post-diagnosis care. This may have resulted from the need for physical distancing during the pandemic. In this way, CD DHTs may be able to improve access to healthcare, which may be of even greater importance during a health emergency. However, our findings suggest DHTs may widen existing inequalities and generate new inequalities. Our analysis suggests that individuals with CD and multimorbidity, or from black and minority ethnic communities are particularly at risk of this. We found using observable characteristics e.g. low socioeconomic status, ethnic minority, older age, poor digital literacy are not helpful in trying to predict for whom DHTs won’t work. Instead, characteristics of compatibility, relative advantage and complexity from Everett Rogers’ theory of the Diffusion of Innovations may be more useful ways to determine if a DHT is likely to be accessed and adopted.

Conclusions

To reduce future disruption, the pandemic highlighted the importance of developing and integrating digital healthcare. We found DHTs may have been successful in providing access to healthcare in a way that met social distancing requirements, however failures and unintended consequences may result in the form of widening existing inequalities and generating new inequalities. Our findings suggest that when developing and implementing DHTs, considering Rogers’ characteristics of compatibility, relative advantage and ease-of-use may be most useful in reducing failures and unintended consequences linked to inequalities. In other words, to reduce digital healthcare inequalities, our findings suggest consideration of these three characteristics may improve access and adoption, thus reducing digital healthcare inequalities.

50 Benefits and harms of remdesivir for COVID-19 in adults: early steps in a systematic review with meta-analysis of pivotal clinical trials

Marc Chrétien, Igho Onakpoya

University of Oxford, Oxford, UK

BMC Proceedings 2026, 20(29):50

Objectives

Remdesivir, a nucleotide inhibitor, is widely used for treatment of COVID-19 infection. Pivotal phase III trials have been used by drug manufacturers to gain marketing approval for remdesivir. Several reviews have been published evaluating the effectiveness of remdesivir. However, there have been no reviews of the benefits and harms of remdesivir based on information in pivotal trials. In this project I aim to critically appraise and summarize the evidence for benefits and harms of remdesivir therapy for COVID-19 illness in adults using evidence from pivotal clinical trials. Primary outcomes will include changes in mortality and serious adverse events. Secondary outcomes will include length of hospital stay, time to death hospitalizations, and overall adverse events.

Methods

We conducted searches across drug regulatory websites including European Medicines Agency (EMA), Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Medicines & Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), Health Canada (HC), Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) and bibliographic databases (PubMed, Embase). We explored grey literature, backward and forward reference searching, and contacted corresponding authors of journal publications for additional data when needed.

Henceforth we will extract primary and secondary data from included trials according to study type, and setting, participant characteristic(s), study duration, and effects of interventions and will assess the risk of bias using the Cochrane criteria and RoB 2 tool. Randomized participants who received at least one dose of medication will be included in a meta-analysis. I2 values will be used to assess heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses will be used to test the robustness of the overall analyses.

Results

We designed a search protocol and published on Open Science Framework. Searches across drug regulatory websites showed 4, 3, 5, 4, 6, and 2 studies from EMA, FDA, MHRA, HC, TGA, and PMDA drug regulatory websites, respectively. In total there were seven independent studies. We will search each study in PubMed and Embase for related publications, screen for inclusion/exclusion criteria, and extract data for meta-analysis.

Conclusions

Results from this systematic review with meta-analysis will provide an evidence-based summary to clinicians, public policy experts, and health economists on the benefits and harms of remdesivir therapy in COVID-19 positive adults from pivotal trials.
