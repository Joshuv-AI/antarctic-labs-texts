Successful use of levosimendan in managing refractory coronary vasospasm leading to recurrent cardiac arrests: a case report
Authors: Kristoffer Ken Ralota, Wendy Wang, Jamie Layland, Josip Andelo Borovac, Doralisa Morrone, Eitaro Kodani, Dzhem Farandzha, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefractory coronary artery vasospasm is a challenging condition unresponsive to standard vasodilator therapy. It can be life-threatening and precipitate arrhythmias and cardiac arrest. Levosimendan, a calcium-sensitizer and ATP-sensitive potassium channel opener, is an inodilator in acute heart failure and may offer therapeutic benefit in this setting.Case presentationA 57-year-old man with an established diagnosis of coronary vasospasm presented with ST-elevation in the inferior leads and suffered multiple cardiac arrests, initially treated with electrical defibrillation and advanced life support measures. Angiography showed mild coronary artery disease, unchanged from the previous ones. In the intensive care unit, recurrent episodes of vasospasm persisted despite maximally tolerated medical therapy. Levosimendan was initiated at 0.2 mcg/kg/min for 24 h, leading to stabilization with no further episodes of ST-elevation and arrhythmias, and he was eventually discharged from the hospital.ConclusionsLevosimendan may be an effective rescue therapy in refractory coronary vasospasm, particularly when conventional vasodilators fail and haemodynamic instability limits escalation. Its dual action improves cardiac contractility and induces significant vasodilation, targeting key pathophysiologic processes. The presented case highlights the potential of levosimendan in stabilizing patients with refractory coronary vasospasm when conventional therapies fail. Further research and clinical trials are warranted to establish its efficacy and safety profile in a broader patient population.Keywords: Levosimendan, Coronary epicardial vasospasm, Arrhythmia, Cardiac arrest, Case report

Introduction

Coronary artery vasospasm can present with recurrent and sometimes refractory angina and may be life-threatening.1 Refractory coronary vasospasm is generally defined as vasospasm unresponsive to two types of coronary vasodilators and affects about 10% of patients and is a challenging condition that can lead to sudden cardiac death.2 Levosimendan, a calcium sensitizer and ATP-sensitive potassium channel opener, is approved for acute decompensated heart failure and cardiogenic shock and exerts both inotropic and vasodilatory effects.3 This report describes levosimendan use in a patient with refractory coronary artery vasospasm, resulting in repeated ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) arrests and highlights its possible therapeutic benefit.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old Caucasian man with known coronary vasospasm had recurrent emergency presentations for chest pain with inferior ST-elevation. Risk factors included hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, hypothyroidism, variable adherence to medications, and ongoing smoking (5–10 cigarettes/day). There was no family history of ischaemic heart disease or coronary vasospasm. His usual therapy comprised atorvastatin 20 mg daily, isosorbide mononitrate 120 mg daily, nicorandil 5 mg twice daily, levothyroxine 50 µg daily, and diltiazem 180 mg daily.

Six months after the index diagnosis, he re-presented to the emergency with severe chest pain. ECG showed refractory ST-elevation (Figure 1) despite intravenous glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) and calcium channel blockers (CCB). He developed VF and was successfully defibrillated. Shortly afterward, he had recurrent inferior ST-elevation with complete heart block (Figure 2) and progressed into PEA arrest. He was intubated, ventilated, and received appropriate advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) treatment with chest compressions and epinephrine, leading to the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) within 4 min. The patient was transferred to the angiography suite, where he maintained sinus rhythm and did not experience any recurrence of ST-elevation throughout the procedure. Coronary angiography demonstrated only mild nonobstructive disease, unchanged from previous studies (Figure 3). Given clinical instability, acetylcholine provocation testing was not performed.

He was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) on a GTN infusion, but hypotension limited uptitration (maximum 10 µg/min) despite noradrenaline support. He was also continued on nicorandil 10 mg twice daily and diltiazem 60 mg thrice daily via nasogastric tube. Despite maximally tolerated intravenous vasodilator therapy, he developed further episodes of inferior ST-elevation with hypotension and transient complete heart block, culminating in another PEA arrest with ROSC achieved within 4 min of ACLS.

Given refractory vasospasm, we decided to try levosimendan infusion at 0.2 mcg/kg/min for 24 h. The infusion was started within 3 h after ROSC and led to resolution and no further recurrence of ST-elevation or arrhythmias. Mean arterial pressure improved from 60–70 mmHg post-ROSC to 70–80 mmHg with gradual weaning and discontinuation of vasopressor support within 24 h. The patient was extubated less than 48 h after starting levosimendan, and the remainder of his hospital stay was uncomplicated. He received a secondary-prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) prior to discharge on day 13.

During follow-up, medical therapy was optimized to atorvastatin 20 mg daily, amlodipine 10 mg daily, diltiazem 120 mg 3 times daily, cyproheptadine 4 mg daily, isosorbide mononitrate 120 mg daily, nicorandil 20 mg daily, and as needed GTN sublingual spray. His levothyroxine was also optimized and increased from 50 µg to 150 µg. Acetylcholine provocation testing later confirmed vasospasm of the right coronary artery. Despite continued smoking, he has remained stable, with occasional chest discomfort responsive to GTN. Since discharge, he was admitted once for chest pain with transient inferior ST-elevation after missing his medications during a holiday stay but has otherwise remained clinically stable.

Discussion and conclusion

Coronary epicardial vasospasm is a relatively common problem with an incidence of approximately 40%4 with a spectrum of clinical presentation from short-lived angina, myocardial infarction, fatal arrhythmias, and sudden death.2,5 It may involve the epicardial arteries, microvasculature, or both.6 The primary mechanism is vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) hyperreactivity to vasoconstrictors, exacerbated by endothelial dysfunction and reduced nitric oxide. Genetic factors, inflammation, oxidative stress, autonomic imbalance, and conditions, such as hypothyroidism also contribute.7,8

Guideline-directed management primarily focuses on nitrates and CCBs, along with targeted therapies (e.g. statin/smoking cessation) to improve endothelial function. However, some cases are refractory to optimal vasodilator therapy.1 Additional pharmacological strategies include combining different CCBs, nicorandil, serotonin or histamine antagonists such as cyproheptadine, phosphodiesterase-3 inhibition with cilostazol, and Rho-kinase inhibition with fasudil.8 Invasive approaches such as coronary stenting may be considered when there is focal spasm; however, spasm frequently recurs outside the stented segment, and stenting is generally not useful in diffuse disease.2,9

Cardiac sympathetic denervation, including stellate ganglion blockade or thoracoscopic sympathectomy, may be an option for refractory vasospasm,2 but these are very invasive procedures and carry significant peri-procedural risk in a haemodynamically unstable patient and were not readily available at our institution.

Levosimendan is an inodilator that increases myocardial contractility via calcium sensitization of troponin-C and promotes vasodilation through ATP-sensitive potassium channels in VSMCs. Together, these effects improve cardiac output, reduce preload and afterload and may enhance both epicardial and microvascular perfusion without substantially increasing oxygen demand.3 Levosimendan has also shown benefit in cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage, reducing delayed cerebral ischaemia and improving cerebral perfusion.10 Levosimendan is generally administered as an intravenous infusion. Although an oral formulation exists, it is not currently available in our setting.

Given our patient’s instability and the unavailability of certain medication, including fasudil, an infusion with levosimendan was chosen as vasospasm persisted despite maximal tolerated medical therapy, limiting further dose escalation. Following levosimendan infusion, ST-segment elevation and conduction disturbances resolved, and haemodynamics stabilized with rapid weaning of vasopressor support and remained stable throughout the rest of his admission. The prolonged activity of the active metabolite OR-1896, with a half-life of approximately 80 h, may partly explain the sustained clinical stability over subsequent days.11

Levosimendan offers a mechanistically rational option for refractory coronary vasospasm. Although the temporal association strongly suggests a therapeutic effect, we cannot exclude contributions from concurrent vasodilators or spontaneous resolution of spasm. In addition, a prior case report has also demonstrated the successful use of levosimendan in a patient with refractory vasospasm complicated by pulmonary aedema and cardiogenic shock.12

Although levosimendan is costlier than conventional vasodilators, its use in this case was a last resort after standard therapies failed. In rare, refractory scenarios, the cost of a brief trial is minimal relative to overall ICU care and justified by its potential to avert fatal outcomes. While routine use may have cost implications, selective administration in life-threatening vasospasm is clinically and economically reasonable.

The decision to implant an ICD was based on episodes of VF arrests. Observational data indicate a risk of recurrent malignant arrhythmias in such patients. Current practice generally supports ICD implantation for secondary prevention in survivors of vasospasm-related VF.2,13

This case report adds to the growing body of evidence supporting its use as rescue therapy in this challenging clinical setting. However, further research and clinical trials are necessary to establish its efficacy and safety profile in a broader patient population.
