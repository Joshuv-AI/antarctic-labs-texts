Pacemaker implantation after concomitant maze procedure and mitral valve repair in atrial functional mitral regurgitation
Authors: Jung Uk Woo, Sungsil Yoon, Woo Sung Jang, Yun Seok Kim, YoHan Bae, Hee Jeong Lee, Jonghoon Yoo, Kyungsub Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) is caused by atrial enlargement and mitral annular dilation due to atrial fibrillation (AF). Although the outcomes of mitral valve surgery in AFMR have been reported, concomitant Maze procedure outcomes in AFMR remain poorly understood. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and rhythm outcomes of concomitant mitral valve plasty and the Maze procedure in patients with AFMR.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 122 patients who underwent the mitral valve plasty (repair) concomitant with Maze procedure between August 2010 and June 2025. The cohort was divided into an AFMR group (n=50) and a non-AFMR group (n=72). The cumulative incidence of permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation was analyzed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model with death as a competing risk. Postoperative rhythm dynamics were evaluated using a continuous-time multi-state Markov model.ResultsThe overall cumulative incidence of PPM implantation was significantly higher in the AFMR group than in the non-AFMR group (P<0.001). While early (≤30 days) PPM implantation rates did not differ (P=0.53), the AFMR group showed a higher risk of late (>30 days) PPM implantation (P<0.001). Multivariable analysis identified AFMR as an independent risk factor of late PPM implantation [subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) 14.963, P=0.02]. Furthermore, the AFMR group demonstrated a higher rate of postoperative junctional rhythm compared to the non-AFMR group.ConclusionsAFMR was not associated with a statistically significant difference in overall survival and the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events following the Maze procedure compared to the non-AFMR group. However, AFMR was associated with a higher risk of transition to junctional rhythm following the Maze procedure, which was associated with an increased rate of PPM implantation. Notably, the risk of pacemaker implantation was predominantly late-onset, which may be due to a progressive decline in sinus node function. Therefore, close long-term rhythm monitoring and follow-up are warranted.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation (AF), atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR), Maze procedure, pacemaker

Highlight box

Key findings

• Atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) was associated with a significantly higher cumulative incidence of permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation following the concomitant Maze procedure and mitral valve repair compared to non-AFMR patients.

• This increased risk was predominantly observed in the late postoperative period (>30 days), whereas early PPM implantation (≤30 days) did not differ between groups.

• Postoperative rhythm dynamics revealed that AFMR patients had a higher incidence of junctional rhythm as well as a greater probability of transitioning to it, suggesting a potential association with progressive sinus node dysfunction.

What is known and what is new?

• AFMR is a distinct clinical entity caused by atrial fibrillation (AF)-induced chronic left atrial (LA) remodeling and mitral annular dilation. Multiple meta-analyses have shown that smaller LA size strongly predicts successful sinus rhythm restoration after Maze surgery. Chronic atrial remodeling due to AF is also a known contributor to sinus node dysfunction.

• AFMR is associated with late PPM implantation after Maze procedure.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Given the observed association with late-onset pacemaker implantation, close long-term rhythm monitoring is warranted for patients with AFMR to detect potential sinus node dysfunction.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent arrhythmia worldwide and is associated with elevated risks of stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality (1). AF contributes to left atrial (LA) remodeling, causing LA enlargement and mitral valve annular dilation (2,3). This process can progressively cause atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR).

Secondary mitral regurgitation (MR) has garnered increasing attention (2-4). AFMR, a type of secondary mitral valve regurgitation, is characterized by MR caused by mitral annular dilation resulting from LA enlargement. Pathophysiologically, AFMR is interconnected with chronic AF and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Each condition aggravates the others by promoting LA remodeling, worsening left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction, and causing mitral valve annular dilation, creating a vicious cycle (5). Effectively managing AF in AFMR improves outcomes and may slow down heart failure with preserved ejection fraction progression (6,7). Surgical outcomes for AFMR are not well documented (8-10), and Maze procedure outcomes in these patients remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the results of the Maze procedure performed concomitantly with mitral valve plasty in patients with AFMR, including permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation, transition probability of rhythm outcomes, all-cause mortality, and the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2790/rc).

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center, Keimyung University School of Medicine approved this study (IRB File No. 2023-04-042, April 25, 2023). The Medical Ethics Commitee of Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center, Keimyung University School of Medicine waived the requirement of written informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study cohort

This study encompassed patients who underwent Maze surgery due to persistent AF or paroxysmal AF at Keimyung University Dongsan Medical Center between August 2010 and June 2025. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who had PPM implantation before the Maze surgery, those who had infective endocarditis regardless of its timing, those aged <18 years, those with congenital heart disease, those whose primary surgical indication was not MR, and those who underwent concomitant mitral valve replacement. Following these exclusions, the final study cohort consisted of 122 patients who underwent mitral valve plasty due to MR with concomitant Maze procedure. Other concomitant cardiac procedures, including aortic valve surgery, tricuspid valve surgery, or coronary artery bypass grafting, were performed when clinically indicated based on standard guidelines. These patients were then categorized into the AFMR group (n=50) and the non-AFMR group (n=72) (Figure 1).

AFMR criteria

In accordance with previously established diagnostic criteria, we evaluated preoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), transesophageal echocardiography, and intraoperative findings of the patients (5). AFMR was defined on the basis of the following criteria:

Normal LV size, geometry, and shape, with an LV end-diastolic volume of ≤85 mL/m2 in males and ≤78 mL/m2 in females;

Normal LV size, geometry, and shape, with an LV end-diastolic volume of ≤85 mL/m2 in males and ≤78 mL/m2 in females;

Preserved regional and global LV systolic function with LV ejection fraction ≥50% and a preserved or only mildly reduced peak LV global longitudinal strain;

Preserved regional and global LV systolic function with LV ejection fraction ≥50% and a preserved or only mildly reduced peak LV global longitudinal strain;

LA enlargement, defined as an LA volume index (LAVI) of ≥40 mL/m2 and an LA diameter of ≥40 mm (measured at end-systole using M-mode in the anteroposterior axis);

LA enlargement, defined as an LA volume index (LAVI) of ≥40 mL/m2 and an LA diameter of ≥40 mm (measured at end-systole using M-mode in the anteroposterior axis);

Mitral annular dilation and flattening with normal leaflet motion, consistent with Carpentier type I MR;

The severity of MR was assessed using quantitative echocardiographic parameters, including effective regurgitant orifice area and regurgitant volume, in accordance with the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines (11).

To minimize inter-observer variability and address potential misclassification bias regarding AFMR diagnosis, all echocardiographic parameters were measured by experienced sonographers and validated by certified cardiologists in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines. Furthermore, the diagnosis of AFMR was not based solely on imaging; it was corroborated by intra-operative surgical inspection confirming mitral annular dilation with normal leaflet motion (Carpentier Type I).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the rate of PPM implantation and postoperative rhythm outcomes. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality and MACE.

Data collection and definition of terms

All patient and outcome data were extracted from the electronic medical record. The rhythm outcome of the study was evaluated using electrocardiography (ECG). ECG monitoring was performed daily during hospitalization in the preoperative and postoperative periods. Following discharge, ECG data were obtained at outpatient visits. The rhythm observed at the first outpatient visit following discharge was recorded as the initial postoperative rhythm. Follow-up ECGs were scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively and at 3-month intervals thereafter. Fine AF was defined as having an amplitude of <0.1 mV of fibrillatory wave across all ECG leads.

Cardiac rhythm outcomes were classified into the following three categories: AF, normal sinus rhythm (NSR), and junctional rhythm. NSR was defined as a sinus rhythm detected in two or more consecutive ECGs at the postoperative follow-up.

We collected echocardiography data at the following three points: preoperative, postoperative, and 1-year follow-up. Preoperative TTE was the most recent TTE before the Maze procedure. Postoperative TTE was routinely performed before hospital discharge, and additional follow-up TTE was conducted at 1 year following the procedure.

MACE was defined as stroke, readmission due to heart failure, and cardiac death. Early mortality was defined as death that occurred within 30 days postoperatively, and chronic kidney disease was defined as the presence of kidney damage or an estimated glomerular filtration rate of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 persisting for at least 3 months, regardless of the preoperative etiology.

Early and late PPM implantations were defined as implantations performed within and after 30 days following the Maze procedure, respectively.

Junctional rhythm definition

Junctional rhythm was defined as a regular rhythm characterized by narrow QRS complexes with absent or retrograde P waves on a standard 12-lead ECG, accompanied by symptomatic bradycardia. To differentiate clinically significant rhythm dysfunction from transient physiological responses, we specifically identified cases where the rhythm persisted despite the discontinuation of negative chronotropic agents.

Postoperative pharmacologic management

Prophylactic continuous intravenous amiodarone was not routinely administered immediately following the Maze procedure at our institution. For both patients who remained AF and those who successfully converted to NSR after surgery, rate-control strategy using beta-blockers was primarily employed. The use of amiodarone for pharmacological rhythm control was reserved for patients who initially achieved NSR but experienced an early recurrence of AF prior to discharge. Following discharge, rhythm-control and rate-control medications were individually adjusted based on the patient’s rhythm during regular outpatient visits.

Indication of PPM implantation

Following the Maze procedure, all patients routinely received temporary epicardial pacing wires. Decisions regarding PPM implantation were made in accordance with the ACC/AHA/Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) guidelines (12). To avoid premature PPM implantation in the early postoperative period, patients exhibiting symptomatic bradycardia, high-grade atrioventricular block, or sinus node dysfunction were initially managed with temporary pacing up to two weeks. The primary indication for PPM was the persistence of these conditions for more than 7–14 days postoperatively, despite the withdrawal of negative chronotropic agents. To ensure uniformity across the study period and among surgeons, the final decision for PPM implantation was never made unilaterally by the surgical team; rather, it was determined through a multidisciplinary consultation with board-certified electrophysiologists. When implantation was required, dual-chamber pacing was preferentially selected to preserve atrioventricular synchrony. Furthermore, subsequent pacemaker dependency was routinely evaluated by the cardiology department during outpatient follow-ups.

Operative techniques of Maze surgery

All patients underwent a biatrial modified Cox Maze procedure using cryoablation, following the principles described in previous studies (8,13,14). The surgical approach changed over the study period. Historically, all procedures were performed via a conventional median sternotomy. More recently, a minimally invasive approach has been adopted; a right thoracotomy is currently the standard approach for patients undergoing mitral valve plasty and a concomitant Maze procedure, with or without concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty. Conventional median sternotomy remains the standard approach for patients requiring additional concomitant procedures including the aortic valve or coronary artery bypass grafting. In our study cohort, 86 patients underwent the procedure via a median sternotomy, while 36 patients were treated using a minimally invasive right thoracotomy.

The lesion set in the left atrium comprised three ablation lines as follows: two lines extending from the superior and inferior margins of the left atriotomy toward the LA appendage (LAA) to isolate the pulmonary veins, and a mitral isthmus line from the atriotomy to the posterior mitral annulus. For the right atrium, three lines were created, including one connecting the superior and inferior vena cava, a “T” lesion from the intercaval lesion to the tricuspid valve annulus, and a line from the right atriotomy to the tricuspid annulus, including the right atrial appendage. Depending on the surgical setting, the procedure was performed under either antegrade or retrograde cardioplegia.

LAA obliteration was indicated in patients with a history of cerebral infarction or documented cardiac thrombus. For other patients, the decision to perform concomitant LAA obliteration was individualized based on the patient’s stroke risk, LAA morphology, and LAA dimensions. Because this study includes a long period that preceded the universal guideline recommendation for routine LAA closure in AF patients, the procedure was not systematically adopted for the entire cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the independent t-test and were presented as means ± standard deviations. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-sided, and a P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To accurately compare the difference of PPM implantation, we employed the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model, considering death as a competing risk. The cumulative incidence functions for PPM implantation were plotted and compared between the two groups using Gray’s test. This analysis was performed from three perspectives: early implantation (within 30 days postoperatively), late implantation (beyond 30 days postoperatively), and overall implantation (up to 60 months of follow-up). For the analysis of late pacemaker implantation, a 30-day landmark analysis was performed, including only patients who were alive and free from pacemaker implantation at 30 days postoperatively.

Multivariable Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors for outcomes showing significant intergroup differences. The results were expressed as subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Because the number of PPM events was limited, we prespecified a hierarchical modeling strategy to reduce overfitting. A parsimonious primary Fine-Gray model was constructed including clinically essential covariates (age, sex, preoperative LAVI) together with the exposure of interest (AFMR), irrespective of statistical significance. The variables were selected based on prior evidence and clinical relevance. To evaluate robustness to additional adjustment, we fitted an expanded clinical model including additional candidate covariates (persistent AF and tricuspid annuloplasty). The expanded model was considered a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary inferential model due to the limited events-per-variable ratio. Full coefficient tables for both models are provided.

Missing data were handled using a complete case analysis to maintain the integrity of the multivariable Fine-Gray regression model. Specifically, preoperative LAVI data were unavailable for 2 patients (2.8%) in the non-AFMR group.

Postoperative rhythm dynamics were analyzed using a continuous-time Markov model for longitudinal data. The model was defined by three rhythm states and one absorbing state (death). We estimated the transition intensities between states and calculated the transition probabilities at 12 months postoperatively to assess the likelihood of rhythm changes over time.

Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test was employed for estimating and comparing the incidence of the secondary outcomes, including all-cause mortality and MACE. Statistical data were analyzed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient characteristics

Our cohort comprised 122 patients, with 50 and 72 patients assigned to the AFMR and non-AFMR groups, respectively. The median follow-up duration was 52 months [interquartile range (IQR) 25.9–89.1 months] overall, 49 months (IQR 26.5–89.5 months) in the AFMR group, and 55 months (IQR 26.1–88.0 months) in the non-AFMR group. The AFMR group was older (66.9±9.1 vs. 63.6±10.0 years, P=0.058), with a significantly lower proportion of male patients (34.0% vs. 69.4%, P<0.001), than the non-AFMR group. The rate of preoperative persistent AF (80.0% vs. 79.2%, P>0.99) and paroxysmal AF (20.0% vs. 20.8%, P>0.99) between the AFMR and non-AFMR groups showed no significant difference. However, the AFMR group exhibited significantly more prominent fine fibrillatory waves (74.0% vs. 52.8%, P=0.02). No significant differences in most concomitant procedures were noted between the two groups; however, tricuspid annuloplasty (76.0% vs. 58.3%, P=0.053) was more frequently performed in the AFMR group than in the non-AFMR group. Regarding comorbidities, no significant difference was noted between the two groups (Table 1). Results of PPM implantation

The cumulative incidence of PPM implantation was evaluated using the Fine-Gray method, treating death as a competing risk. The results for overall, early, and late PPM implantations are presented in Figure 2. The overall cumulative incidence of PPM implantation was significantly higher in the AFMR group compared to the non-AFMR group (Figure 2A, P<0.001). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year cumulative incidence rates were 8.0%, 16.7%, and 19.3% in the AFMR group, whereas they remained stable at 2.9% across all time points in the non-AFMR group.

Cumulative incidence of PPM implantation. (A) Overall PPM implantation, regardless of timing following the Maze procedure. (B) Early PPM implantation, within 30 days following the Maze procedure. (C) Late PPM implantation, 30 days after the Maze procedure. AFMR, atrial functional mitral regurgitation; PPM, permanent pacemaker.

To provide a detailed temporal profile of conduction events, we analyzed the specific timing of PPM implantations. In the AFMR group (n=50), events were distributed throughout the follow-up period: 2 events occurred within 30 days, 2 between 1 month and 1 year, 4 between 1 and 2 years, and 1 between 3 and 4 years. Notably, 3 additional patients underwent PPM implantation after 5 years, indicating a persistent risk in this population. In contrast, the non-AFMR group (n=72) showed a limited risk window: 1 event occurred within 30 days and 1 event at 12 months, with no further implantations observed thereafter.

Regarding early PPM implantation (within 30 days postoperatively), there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Figure 2B, P=0.53). Early pacemaker implantation was infrequent and occurred in 2 patients in AFMR group and 1 patient in non-AFMR group (4.0% vs. 1.4%). Conversely, the late PPM implantation rate demonstrated a significant intergroup difference (Figure 2C, P<0.001). The cumulative incidence rates for late PPM at 1, 3, and 5 years were 4.5%, 14.1%, and 17.1% in the AFMR group, compared to a consistently low rate of 1.5% in the non-AFMR group.

Risk factor analysis for PPM implantation

Given the significant differences observed in overall and late PPM implantations, multivariable Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors. Prior to finalizing the model, we assessed the potential impact of temporal changes in surgical practice by evaluating the “Surgical Era” (Early [2010–2017] vs. Late [2018–2025]). However, it was not significantly associated with overall PPM implantation in the univariate analysis [sHR 1.13, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.38–3.31, P=0.83] or late PPM implantation (sHR 1.59, 95% CI: 0.45–5.56, P=0.47). Furthermore, the distribution of surgical era did not differ between the AFMR and non-AFMR groups (P=0.85; Table 1).

In the multivariable analysis for overall PPM implantation, male sex (sHR 2.75, P=0.04), preoperative LAVI (sHR 1.01, P=0.001), and AFMR (sHR 11.40, P=0.005) were significantly associated with an increased risk (Table 2). Similarly, for late PPM implantation, preoperative LAVI (sHR 1.01, P=0.001) and AFMR (sHR 14.96, P=0.02) remained significantly associated with an increased risk (Table 3). When additional clinically relevant covariates (persistent AF and concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty) were included in the expanded model, the direction of the associations was preserved. Detailed results can be found in the supplementary appendix online (Tables S2,S3). In this sensitivity analysis, AFMR remained associated with overall PPM implantation (sHR 12.45, P=0.009) and late PPM implantation (sHR 14.66, P=0.03), while LAVI also showed consistent association across models.

Rhythm outcomes following the Maze procedure

The AFMR group demonstrated lower restoration of NSR and higher incidence of junctional rhythm throughout the follow-up period compared to the non-AFMR group. At discharge, the NSR rate was significantly lower in the AFMR group (52.0% vs. 76.4%; P=0.006), whereas the junctional rhythm rate was higher (18.0% vs. 4.2%; P=0.02). At 1-year follow-up, this trend persisted, with the AFMR group showing a lower NSR rate (58.1% vs. 76.9%; P=0.054) and a significantly higher junctional rhythm rate (16.3% vs. 3.1%; P=0.03). At 3-years follow-up, the junctional rhythm rate remained significantly higher in the AFMR group (21.1% vs. 5.6%; P=0.046), while the NSR rate was 60.5% vs. 75.9% in the non-AFMR group (P=0.17).

Rhythm dynamic following the Maze procedure

Postoperative rhythm dynamics were evaluated using a continuous-time multi-state Markov model, defined by three transient states (NSR, AF, and junctional rhythm) and one absorbing state (death). The estimated transition intensities between these states are summarized in Table 4. The non-AFMR group demonstrated higher stability in maintaining sinus rhythm. The estimated probability of a patient remaining in NSR at 12 months postoperatively was 92.2% in the non-AFMR group, compared to 87.1% in the AFMR group (Table 5). In terms of AF recurrence, the AFMR group exhibited a higher susceptibility compared to the non-AFMR group. The estimated 12-month probability of transitioning from NSR to AF was 8.8% in the AFMR group, which was higher than the 4.3% in the non-AFMR group. Similarly, for patients in a junctional rhythm, the probability of reverting to AF at 12 months was higher in the AFMR group (11.7%) compared to the non-AFMR group (10.8%). Regarding transitions to junctional rhythm, direct transitions from NSR to junctional rhythm were rare in both groups, with 12-month probabilities of 1.9% in the AFMR group and 0.3% in the non-AFMR group. Transitions from an AF state to junctional rhythm were more frequent, particularly in the AFMR group than the non-AFMR group (15.5% vs. 10.3%) (Table 5). However, despite the differences observed in transition intensities, they were not statistically significant between the two groups across any of the evaluated pathways (all P>0.05, Table 6).

Results of all-cause mortality and MACE

Early mortality was 6% in the AFMR group and 1.4% in the non-AFMR group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.30). Early mortality in the AFMR group was attributed to worsening heart failure in 1 patient, septic shock in 1 patient, and multiorgan failure resulting from a thyrotoxic storm associated with Graves’ disease in 1 patient. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) rates were 92.0%, 89.7%, and 80.0% in the AFMR group, and 94.2%, 90.9%, and 88.9% in the non-AFMR group, also without a significant difference (P=0.45, Figure 3).

Furthermore, the MACE-free rates at 1, 3, and 5 years postoperatively were 89.9%, 87.4%, and 74.4% in the AFMR group, and 94.2%, 88.3%, and 78.1% in the non-AFMR group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.68, Figure 4A). The individual MACE components—stroke, heart failure readmission, and cardiac death—also did not differ significantly between the two groups (P=0.94, P=0.83, and P=0.36, respectively; Figure 4B-4D).

Discussion

This study presents two important points. First, patients with AFMR were associated with a higher risk of requiring PPM implantation following the Maze procedure compared to those without AFMR. This risk became more evident over time, suggesting that sinus node dysfunction may gradually progress during follow-up. Second, despite these unfavorable conduction outcomes, patients with AFMR did not exhibit significant differences in mortality and MACE following the Maze procedure compared to those without AFMR.

Our study revealed that patients with AFMR exhibit distinct postoperative rhythm dynamics, specifically characterized by increased transitions from AF or NSR to junctional rhythm as identified by our multi-state Markov model. This pattern likely reflects the advanced LA enlargement and remodeling associated with AFMR, consistent with several meta-analyses identifying smaller LA size as a key predictor of sinus rhythm restoration following Maze surgery (15-20). Furthermore, the observed propensity for junctional rhythm aligns with prior evidence that chronic AF and atrial remodeling contribute to intrinsic sinus node dysfunction (21-24). This underlying sinus node dysfunction is often clinically masked by the continuous, rapid fibrillatory electrical signals from the atria. When a Maze procedure successfully eliminates these fibrillatory waves, this underlying nodal dysfunction is unmasked, becoming clinically apparent as a prolonged junctional rhythm.

The higher incidence of junctional rhythm in the AFMR group appears to be related to an increased rate of PPM implantation. Notably, the risk of PPM implantation in the AFMR group is more pronounced for late rather than early PPM implantation. Previous studies have reported that a longer history of AF before undergoing radiofrequency catheter ablation or Maze procedures is associated with progressive structural remodeling of the atria, including atrial fibrosis, which in turn leads to conduction abnormalities and altered electrophysiologic properties even after ablation therapy (25-28). This ongoing remodeling process elevates the risk of sinus node dysfunction, potentially increasing the likelihood of late PPM implantation. Similarly, in patients with AFMR, the tendency for late PPM implantation is more pronounced, suggesting that nodal dysfunction might gradually progress following the Maze procedure.

The probability of all-cause mortality and MACE at 5 years postoperatively showed no significant difference in patients with AFMR compared to those without AFMR. Moreover, the three components of MACE, including stroke, heart failure readmission, and cardiac death, exhibited no significant differences between patients with and without AFMR. Recently, Shin et al. (29) and Wagner et al. (10) reported promising 5-year survival rates (86% and 74%, respectively) following mitral valve surgery in patients with AFMR. Our current results align with these findings, demonstrating a comparable 5-year survival rate of 80%. Additionally, although patients with the AFMR phenotype face an elevated risk of requiring postoperative PPM implantation, recent evidence suggests that this complication does not negatively impact long-term survival. Millar et al. demonstrated that while patients requiring a PPM after mitral valve surgery often present with worse baseline clinical profiles and experience longer intensive care unit stays, PPM implantation itself is not independently associated with worse long-term survival (30). Furthermore, in a nationwide study focusing on patients undergoing mitral valve surgery combined with the Maze procedure, Lee et al. reported that PPM implantation did not significantly affect overall mortality or the risk of ischemic stroke (31). Therefore, while the potential need for a PPM requires careful perioperative planning, it does not translate into compromised long-term mortality. Collectively, these findings support that performing mitral valve surgery and the Maze procedure is safe for patients with AFMR.

This study had several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, retrospective study spanning a 15-year period. Although this design limits causal inference, it offers valuable observational insights. Furthermore, a post-hoc power analysis revealed that while our cohort was adequately powered (97.8%) for the primary endpoint of PPM implantation, it was significantly underpowered (10.7%) to detect differences in OS. Consequently, future studies utilizing larger cohorts are necessary to definitively evaluate long-term survival outcomes. Second, propensity score matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting were not performed because the significant baseline differences between the groups—such as older age and a higher proportion of females—reflect the inherent clinical phenotype of AFMR. Forcing a match resulted in the exclusion of a significant number of representative AFMR patients due to a lack of common support. Furthermore, applying inverse probability of treatment weighting in the presence of such fundamentally different baseline distributions generated extreme weights for certain individuals. These extreme weights severely inflated the variance and led to statistical distortion of the outcomes. Therefore, we instead conducted multivariable Fine-Gray regression analyses to adjust for these potential confounders and validate statistical significance. However, we acknowledge that variations in surgical strategies—such as the application of LAA obliteration and differences in the performance of other concomitant surgeries—serve as confounders. Notably, concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty was performed significantly more frequently in the AFMR group. Although efforts were made to adjust for confounders within our multivariable regression models, the potential for residual confounding cannot be entirely eliminated, and these factors might have influenced the observed outcomes. Third, rhythm follow-up relied on serial 12-lead ECGs obtained during outpatient visits rather than on continuous monitoring (e.g., Holter or implantable loop recorders), as recommended by the expert consensus statement (32). Consequently, asymptomatic or intermittent paroxysmal AF episodes may have gone undetected, and conventional ablation efficacy endpoints could not be reliably determined. Therefore, rhythm findings should be interpreted as descriptive patterns rather than definitive measures of procedural success. In addition, detailed information regarding AF duration, baseline conduction abnormalities, and preoperative antiarrhythmic drug exposure was not consistently available. The unmeasured differences in underlying conduction vulnerability may have influenced pacemaker implantation risk. Finally, the decision to perform LAA obliteration was not standardized but was left to the discretion of the operating surgeon based on LAA morphology, patient comorbidities and the surgical era. Given that LAA contributes to rhythm control via electrical isolation and serves as prophylaxis against thromboembolic events, this non-uniform application may have introduced selection bias regarding rhythm outcomes and stroke prevention.

Conclusions

AFMR was not associated with a statistically significant difference in OS and the incidence of MACEs following the Maze procedure compared to the non-AFMR group. However, AFMR was associated with a higher risk of transition to junctional rhythm following the Maze procedure, which was associated with an increased rate of PPM implantation. Notably, the risk of pacemaker implantation was predominantly late-onset, which may be due to a progressive decline in sinus node function. Therefore, close long-term rhythm monitoring and follow-up are warranted.
