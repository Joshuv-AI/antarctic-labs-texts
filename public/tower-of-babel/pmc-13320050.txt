Missed achilles tendon injury in superficial laceration: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Jeffrey Byrd, Sreenivasulu Metikala, Madana Vallem, Khalid Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A thorough exploration of traumatic wound is critical to accurately assess the severity of the injury. The authors report a case of a missed achilles tendon injury diagnosed as a superficial laceration in the emergency department. The purpose of this case report is to highlight the importance of physical examination and history taking resulting in having a high clinical index of suspicion for diagnosing specific injuries, in this case achilles tendon injury, which will result in prompt orthopedic referral and specialized patient care.

Introduction

This article highlights a missed achilles tendon injury caused by sharp nail off a dry erase board during initial emergency department (ED) evaluation in a young female. Further provided is a management plan for this subacute presentation due to misdiagnosis and proposes effective strategies for preventing such occurrences in the ED.

Case

A 35-year-old female presented to the Orthopedic clinic for evaluation of a 3-week-old laceration on the posterior aspect of her left heel. The injury occurred when a plastic piece with a protruding nail detached from a dry-erase board and cut into the back of her heel. Shortly after the incident, she visited the emergency department (ED) with a bleeding open wound. Initial assessment, including plain radiographs and direct examination, revealed no retained foreign bodies or materials. Following thorough irrigation, the ED provider closed the wound using 4-0 nylon sutures in a simple interrupted repair technique. The patient was discharged with oral antibiotics and pain medication.

At the time of her clinic visit, the patient reported persistent pain and weakness in the ankle. The sutured wound had healed with no signs of infection (Fig. 1), but there was localized tenderness and severe weakness of ankle plantar flexion (Fig. 2). Concerned about possible underlying tendon injury, an MRI was ordered, which revealed a high-grade signal rent involving the posterior three-quarters of the distal Achilles tendon, just proximal to its calcaneal insertion (Fig. 3). Operative technique

Incision was made over the healed scar over the distal aspect of the achilles tendon. Careful subcutaneous dissection was performed. Paratenon was opened. Achilles tendon was identified. Once the scar tissues was removed, near complete rupture of the Achilles tendon was noted (Fig. 4). The rupture was close to its insertion with only about 1 cm of distal stump. The tendon was connected with only small lateral threads with more than 3/4th of the tendon ruptured. After excising the scar tissue, we now placed two Fibertak anchors into the calcaneus under direct fluoroscopic guidance. Next, we passed the sutures inside out through the distal stump. Next, we passed all the four strands through the proximal stump from outside to inside and peripheral to central direction (Fig. 5a–b). The ankle was placed in about 15 degrees of plantar flexion and good clinical apposition of tendon edges was noted. We next tied the knots in this position. We stressed the ankle with dorsiflexion and found the repair stable. We reinforced our repair with Vicryl sutures in pants-over-vest manner (Fig. 6). Wound wash was done. Tourniquet was released, hemostasis was obtained. Closure was done in layers using Nylon sutures for skin approximation. Postoperative follow-up

This patient post-operative course was uneventful with woundcheck follow up appointments scheduled weekly for the first 3 weeks at which point suture were removed and the incision completed healed. Following, this patient went into a controlled ankle motion (CAM) Boot with a heel wedge for 4 weeks and remained nonweightbearing at this time. Patient then graduated to protected weightbearing and to full weightbearing while working with physical therapy. At the six month follow-up appointment the patient demonstrated pain-free, plantigrade foot with full strength (5/5) in ankle plantar flexion and the range of motion comparable to the contralateral side (Fig. 7a–b).Fig. 7ab. 6 month post-op with well healed incisional scar (a) and full ankle plantar flexion strength, equivalent to the contralateral side (b).Fig. 7

Discussion

A prompt diagnosis of an Achilles tendon rupture is important for the initiation of proper treatment. The delay of treatment often requires more extensive surgery and longer rehabilitation with potentially residual disability [1]. Regrettably an Achilles tendon tear is a commonly overlooked injury in the emergency department with historically 25% missed when they first occur due to an unusual history in the mechanism of injury [2]. A paucity of research exists on Achilles tendon tear due to a glass injury presenting for an emergency room visit. This case report attempts to highlight the effective measure for prompt diagnosis and surgical approach for delayed presentation.

Achilles tendon Rupture presentation can vary from sports related injury in younger patients to lower energy injuries in older adults. Sports injuries generally occur in patients between the age of 25 and 40 years and low energy or spontaneous ruptures occur in those over 60 years of age [3]. When evaluating for an Achilles tendon tear history and physical exam remain the most important tools for making the accurate diagnosis. Patient presentation may include the traditional history of a snap in the back of the calf with the inability to walk but they may also present with symptoms of a sprained ankle or with no injury [4]. The physical exam should consist of initially the palpation of the Achilles tendon, looking for a visible defect or swelling in the Achilles tendon [5]. Further, performing diagnostic test such as the Thompson's test and the Matles test may improve the accuracy of diagnosing an Achilles tendon tear due to their high sensitivity and specificity [6]. Additionally, in an acute emergency setting using ultrasound on patients with suspected Achilles tendon tear can aid in solidifying the diagnosis and prevent a missed Achilles tendon injury [7].

Chronic Achilles tendon tear can have a detrimental effect on the patient's lifestyle and surgical repair is required when there is residual weakness involved. Patients undergoing a chronic Achilles tendon repair that must use a graft are twice as likely to develop complications compared to those who don't require a graft. This reinforces the early treatment and diagnosis of an Achilles tendon tear, as well as, using the primary Achilles tendon repair, when possible, to minimize complications [8].

The superficial nature of the Achilles tendon puts it at risk with any penetrating injury and properly evaluating the patient can decrease the missed diagnosis. This awareness of the potential injury coupled with a good exam can decrease the risk of a missed diagnosis. This can give the injured patient the best chance of a complete recovery. This cast study is an example of delayed diagnosis due to a missed Achilles tendon diagnosis. The consideration of possible laceration with Achilles tendon tear should be considered when evaluating these types of scenarios.

Conclusion

The article aims to raise awareness about potential tendon injuries in lacerations near the ankle. An early diagnosis facilitates primary tendon repair and achieves optimal outcomes. In situations of metal or plastic injuries, the detection of a foreign body typically takes precedence over assessing any potential damage to the underlying tissues. In this case report we are reminded of the importance of a history and physical exam when evaluating a patient with an atypical presentation of Achilles tendon injury. Diagnosing and treatment an Achilles tendon tear in the acute phase can facilitate patient healing, resulting in less complications, and a faster recovery rate. The article also describes the delayed primary repair technique of insertional achilles tendon tear and discusses best practices in the ED to prevent such diagnostic pitfalls.
