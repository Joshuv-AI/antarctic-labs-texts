Low-dose l-carnitine supplementation attenuates hepatic lipid accumulation in chow-fed mice: Insights from integrated multi-omics analysis
Authors: Zhen Zhang, Baojun Ding, Li Zhang, Luyao Zhang, Yizhong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost studies on l-carnitine and hepatic lipid metabolism have focused on deficiency or HFD-associated metabolic disorders, whereas its low-dose nutritional effects under standard chow-fed conditions remain unclear. In this study, standard chow-fed C57BL/6 J mice were orally administered low doses of l-carnitine for 35 days, and hepatic responses were evaluated using phenotypic, histological, metabolomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses. l-carnitine supplementation reduced liver weight and hepatic lipid accumulation in a dose-associated manner, accompanied by decreased hepatocyte area and reduced H&E-based hepatocellular vacuolation. Multi-omics integration suggests attenuation of canonical fatty acid oxidation marker activation. Instead, l-carnitine treatment was associated with attenuation of lipogenesis-related signatures, including reduced xylulose-5-phosphate abundance, decreased PPP2CB protein abundance, and lower Acaca and Fasn levels, providing evidence for a potential suppression of a Xu-5P/PP2A/ChREBP-associated lipogenic signature. Concurrent remodeling of amino acid-, carbohydrate-, pyrimidine-, and ascorbate/aldarate-related pathways further indicated coordinated regulation of substrate metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and detoxification-associated processes. Together, these findings highlight a previously underexplored nutritional dimension of l-carnitine action, showing that low-dose supplementation can reshape hepatic lipid-associated metabolic networks even under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions. This study extends the current understanding of l-carnitine from a classical fatty acid transport cofactor to a dietary factor involved in the network-level regulation of hepatic lipid homeostasis.Keywords: l-carnitine, Lipid metabolism, Metabolomics, Transcriptomics, Proteomics, Multi-omics analysis

Introduction

The liver is the central organ regulating systemic metabolism and whole-body lipid homeostasis through coordinated control of fatty acid synthesis, oxidation, transport, and storage(Li et al., 2025; Zhou, Zhang, et al., 2023). However, under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions, the molecular mechanisms by which the liver responds to exogenous nutrients and modulates hepatic lipid metabolism remain incompletely understood(Georgila et al., 2020; Zhang, Chen, et al., 2023). Elucidating these responses is important for understanding nutrient-mediated regulation of hepatic lipid homeostasis under baseline nutritional conditions.

l-carnitine is essential for mitochondrial β-oxidation by transporting long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for energy production(Virmani & Cirulli, 2022; Zhou, Huang, et al., 2023). Previous studies have primarily examined l-carnitine in pathological models, including NAFLD, high-fat diet-induced obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome(Sun et al., 2023), where hepatic lipid metabolism and mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation are often impaired. In these contexts, l-carnitine has been reported to alleviate hepatic lipid accumulation and improve lipid metabolic abnormalities(Li et al., 2023). However, l-carnitine is also widely consumed as a dietary nutrient or nutritional supplement by individuals without overt metabolic disease. Therefore, investigating the hepatic effects of l-carnitine supplementation under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions is important for understanding its nutritional regulatory role in hepatic lipid homeostasis.

In addition to its canonical role in mitochondrial fatty acid transport, accumulating evidence suggests that l-carnitine may also participate in broader metabolic regulatory processes. For example, in a circadian disruption model, l-carnitine reversed lipid metabolic dysregulation and altered clock gene expression, suggesting its involvement in the crosstalk between physiological rhythms and metabolism(Sun et al., 2019). Furthermore, in vitro studies show that l-carnitine regulates nuclear receptor signaling, thereby coordinating glucose and lipid metabolism(Förster et al., 2021; Wang, Guo, et al., 2024). These findings suggest that the metabolic actions of l-carnitine may vary depending on nutritional status, disease context, and tissue-specific regulatory networks. However, systematic multi-omics studies evaluating the hepatic response to l-carnitine supplementation under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions remain limited(Alhasaniah, 2023; Chen et al., 2026; Li & Zhao, 2021; Wu et al., 2020).

To evaluate the effects of l-carnitine on hepatic lipid metabolism under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions, we administered different oral doses to C57BL/6 J mice and performed integrated multi-omics analyses. By combining metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, this study characterized hepatic lipid accumulation and associated molecular responses to l-carnitine supplementation. These findings provide new insights into nutrient-mediated regulation of hepatic lipid homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental treatments

Forty male C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from Sipeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SCXK[Beijing]2024–0001) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22–25 °C, 35–65% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to chow and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10): control (0 mg/mouse/day), low-dose (0.5 mg/mouse/day), medium-dose (1 mg/mouse/day), and high-dose (1.5 mg/mouse/day). Based on an average body weight of approximately 20 g, these doses correspond to approximately 0, 25, 50, and 75 mg/kg/day, respectively. The corresponding human-equivalent doses (HEDs) were calculated using body surface area normalization according to the formula: HED (mg/kg/day) = animal dose (mg/kg/day) × Km _ mouse/Km _ human, where Km _ mouse = 3 and Km _ human = 37. Thus, the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups correspond to HEDs of approximately 2.0, 4.1, and 6.1 mg/kg/day, equivalent to approximately 120, 240, and 365 mg/day for a 60-kg adult(Nair & Jacob, 2016). These HEDs span approximately 120–365 mg/day: the lowest dose is near the upper end of the estimated intake from a mixed diet (approximately 60–180 mg/day), whereas the medium and high doses exceed typical dietary intake but remain below common supplemental amounts (approximately 500–3000 mg/day)(Demarquoy, 2024). The dose rationale and comparison with l-carnitine doses used in previous rodent studies are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.l-carnitine (≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich) was administered by oral gavage once daily for 35 days. The control group received an equal volume of sterile water by oral gavage. Following a 12 h fast, mice were weighed, anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and euthanized. Livers were excised, weighed to calculate the liver index, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

All 40 mice were included in the measurements of body weight, liver weight, and liver index. For histological assessment and multi-omics profiling, six mice with comparable body weights and liver weights within each group were selected, resulting in 24 mice in total. The same set of 24 matched liver samples was used for histological assessment, metabolomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses to support integrated multi-omics analysis.

All animal experiments in this study were performed in strict compliance with the ethical guidelines and procedures approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Welfare Ethics Committee of Guilin Medical University, Guilin, China (Approval No. GLMC202405248).

Histopathological analysis

Liver tissues (∼1 cm3) from the left lobe were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, cleared, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm (Leica RM2235), and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following standard protocols. Stained sections were examined using an Olympus BX53 light microscope, and representative images were captured.

Histological quantification was performed using Case Viewer software (3DHISTECH) at 100× magnification. For each section, four randomly selected fields containing the central vein (central area) and four fields adjacent to the portal triad (peripheral area) were analyzed, excluding regions with artifacts. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software. Hepatocyte area was determined by manually outlining ∼20 randomly selected hepatocytes per field. Lipid density was calculated as the ratio of lipid vacuole area to total field area after threshold segmentation. Binucleated hepatocytes were counted independently by two investigators and expressed as a percentage of total cells. Mean values from all fields were used for statistical analysis.

Metabolite extraction and data analysis

Frozen liver tissues (20 mg) were homogenized and extracted with 400 μL methanol–water (7:3, v/v) containing internal standards according to a previously reported metabolite extraction method(Liu et al., 2021). After centrifugation, the supernatant was clarified by a second centrifugation and subjected to LC–MS analysis.

All samples were analyzed using three LC–MS methods, including reversed-phase LC in positive and negative ion modes and hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC). Reversed-phase separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column using water and acetonitrile (both containing 0.1% formic acid) with gradient elution. HILIC separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH HILIC column using ammonium formate–buffered mobile phases. Detailed chromatographic conditions are provided in the Supporting Information. Data were acquired in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode using an AB Sciex QTRAP® LC–MS/MS system controlled by Analyst TF 1.7.1 software (Sciex)(Chen et al., 2009). Metabolite identification and relative quantification were conducted using a local database based on MRM data acquired in QQQ mode and processed with Analyst 1.6.3 software. Relative metabolite abundance was represented by integrated peak areas. Data were preprocessed by missing value imputation and QC-based filtering. Metabolites with CV < 0.3 in QC samples were retained, and analytical stability was verified using internal standard CVs and QC TIC overlays. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R software after unit variance scaling. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the MetaboAnalystR package with log₂-transformed and mean-centered data, and model robustness was evaluated by 200 permutation tests(Chong & Xia, 2018). Differential metabolites were identified based on VIP > 1.0 and P < 0.05 (Student's t-test), annotated using the KEGG Compound database, and mapped to KEGG pathways for pathway analysis(Kanehisa & Goto, 2000).

RNA extraction and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and RNA quantity and quality were assessed using NanoDrop 2000, agarose gel electrophoresis, and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Poly(A) + mRNA was enriched, fragmented, reverse-transcribed, and used for cDNA library construction following standard Illumina protocols. Libraries were sequenced on DNBSEQ-T7 platforms. Raw sequencing data were quality filtered using fastp to obtain clean reads(Chen et al., 2018), which were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2(Kim et al., 2019). Gene and transcript expression levels were quantified with StringTie(Pertea et al., 2015). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2(Anders & Huber, 2010), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as FDR(false discovery rate) < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway databases(Klopfenstein et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2011).

Protein extraction and proteomic analysis

Mouse liver tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Proteins were processed using the iST sample preparation kit (PreOmics), including denaturation, tryptic digestion, peptide desalting, and elution according to the manufacturer's instructions. Peptides were separated on a nanoElute 2 system and analyzed on a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer operating in diaPASEF mode for data-independent acquisition (DIA). Raw data were processed using Spectronaut 19 with BGS Factory Settings against the UniProt–Mouse database (version 2025). Trypsin was specified as the digestion enzyme, carbamidomethylation (C) as a fixed modification, and oxidation (M) and protein N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications. Protein identification was performed with a FDR of 1% at the precursor, peptide, and protein levels. Protein quantification was conducted using the MaxLFQ algorithm with local normalization. Differentially expressed proteins were identified based on FC > 1.2 and P < 0.05. PCA was used to assess sample reproducibility, and functional annotation was performed using GO and KEGG pathway analyses.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed separately on transcriptomic and proteomic datasets using the WGCNA package (v1.72) via the ImageGP2 platform(Chen, Liu, et al., 2024). The soft-thresholding power was set to 16 for transcriptomic data and 6 for proteomic data, with minimum module sizes of 50 genes and 250 proteins, respectively. Module eigengenes were correlated with phenotypic indices to identify modules associated with hepatic lipid deposition.

Integrated multi-omics analysis

For transcriptome–metabolome integration, genes overlapping between significant WGCNA modules and DEGs were selected and correlated with differentially abundant metabolites using the CNSknowall platform (v2.0) (https://cnsknowall.com/#/HomePage), with |R| > 0.6 and P < 0.05 considered significant. For proteome–metabolome integration, proteins from WGCNA modules significantly associated with l-carnitine treatment were intersected with differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) to identify core DEPs. Pearson correlation analysis was subsequently performed between the core DEPs and differential metabolites to construct interaction networks. For three-omics integration, shared significantly enriched KEGG pathways in the high-dose group were identified, and correlations among genes, proteins, metabolites, and phenotypic indices were visualized using correlation heatmaps.

All statistical analyses and figures were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Dose-associated effects of l-carnitine on hepatic morphology and lipid deposition

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of oral administration of l-carnitine at different doses (0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5 mg/d) for 35 days on the liver of standard chow-fed mice (n = 10 per group). Body weight, liver weight, and liver index were assessed in all 40 mice. Based on body weight and liver weight, six mice were selected from each group (24 mice in total) for histological assessment and subsequent multi-omics analyses. The body weight, liver weight, and liver index of the selected 24 mice showed changes consistent with those observed in the full cohort of 40 mice, supporting the representativeness of the selected samples. The same set of 24 matched liver samples was used for metabolomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses. Finally, an integrated multi-omics analysis was performed involving the correlation of metabolites, proteins, transcripts, and phenotypes to deeply investigate the underlying mechanisms of l-carnitine action on the liver (Fig. 1).

Experimental design and multi-omics strategy for evaluating the effects of oral l-carnitine on mouse liver under normal physiological conditions. Mice were orally administered l-carnitine at doses of 0, 0.5, 1, or 1.5 mg/day for 35 days (n = 10 per group). (1) Pharmacological effect assessment included measurements of body weight, liver weight and liver index, as well as histological evaluation by H&E staining to assess hepatic morphology and pathological alterations. (2) Omics analyses were performed using liver samples (n = 24) for metabolomics (LC–MS-based profiling), transcriptomics (RNA sequencing), and proteomics (DIA-based LC–MS analysis) to characterize molecular alterations at multiple regulatory levels. (3) Integrated analysis combined metabolomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and phenotypic data through multi-omics integration and correlation analyses to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the effects of l-carnitine on hepatic metabolism and function.

The results showed that treatment with three doses of l-carnitine had a relatively limited effect on mouse body weight, but significantly reduced liver weight and the liver-to-body weight ratio in a clear dose-associated manner (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Routine hematological analysis was further performed as supplementary systemic phenotyping. Erythrocyte-related parameters, including HCT, HGB, and RBC, remained largely unchanged among groups, suggesting that l-carnitine supplementation did not induce overt erythrocyte-related abnormalities. In contrast, WBC and absolute lymphocyte counts were reduced in the medium- and high-dose groups, whereas PLT counts were increased, while lymphocyte percentage showed no significant difference among groups (Fig. S1). Further showed by histopathological H&E staining revealed that l-carnitine treatment significantly improved hepatocyte morphology and reduced hepatocellular vacuolation. In the control group, hepatocytes were large and loosely arranged, with distinct cytoplasmic vacuole-like spaces. Conversely, with increasing l-carnitine doses, the vacuoles within hepatocytes in the low, medium, and high-dose groups gradually decreased, cell arrangement tended to be more regular, and cell structure became tighter. The liver tissue structure in the high-dose group was the most intact, with only a few mildly vacuolated cells observed (Fig. 2B). Quantitative analysis further showed that l-carnitine supplementation significantly reduced hepatocyte area and H&E-based lipid density in both the central and peripheral hepatic zones. Specifically, the average hepatocyte area in the control group was 431.52 ± 15.08 μm2, which decreased to 337.49 ± 20.92 μm2, 241.42 ± 9.32 μm2, and 205.64 ± 14.12 μm2 in the low, medium, and high-dose l-carnitine groups, respectively (P < 0.05). Lipid density decreased from 40.92% in the control group to 37.52%, 33.84%, and 30.32% in the respective dose groups (P < 0.05). In the peripheral zone, the average hepatocyte area was 535.48 ± 36.01 μm2, which decreased to 424.36 ± 18.90 μm2, 321.11 ± 18.73 μm2, and 240.45 ± 11.31 μm2 in the low, medium, and high-dose groups, respectively (P < 0.05), while lipid density decreased from 49.30% in the control group to 44.10%, 40.11%, and 35.95% in the respective dose groups (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the proportion of binucleated hepatocytes in all l-carnitine treatment groups was significantly higher than that in the control group, increasing from 14.42% to 19.09% (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that under standard chow-fed conditions, l-carnitine supplementation was associated with reduced hepatocellular vacuolation, decreased hepatocyte area, and an increased proportion of binucleated hepatocytes in a dose-associated manner.

l-carnitine alters the hepatic metabolic profiles in healthy mice

To investigate the potential metabolic mechanisms underlying the amelioration of liver phenotypes by l-carnitine, we performed a comprehensive analysis of liver tissue samples using untargeted metabolomics (Fig. 3). In positive and negative ion modes, 860 and 1203 metabolites were identified, respectively, primarily covering amino acids, lipids, and organic acids (Supplementary Table S2). Statistical classification of metabolites showed that amino acids and their metabolites accounted for the highest proportion of total metabolites (24.38%), followed by organic acids and their derivatives (13.48%) and fatty acids (10.42%) (Fig. 3A). Further class-level chemical classification revealed that small peptides and organic acids and derivatives accounted for 16.73% and 11.56% of the total identified metabolites, respectively (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that the metabolic composition of the normal liver possesses high chemical diversity and pathway complexity, providing a potential molecular basis for l-carnitine-mediated metabolic regulation.

PCA results showed that samples from the l-carnitine treatment groups and the control group were clearly separated in terms of overall hepatic metabolic composition, and this separation trend gradually intensified with increasing doses (Low dose vs. Control: the first two principal components explained 25.68% and 12.92% of the variance, respectively; Medium dose vs. Control: 26.38% and 13.47%; High dose vs. Control: 30.08% and 13.23%) (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that l-carnitine can reshape the hepatic metabolic profile in a dose-dependent manner under normal physiological conditions. To screen for key differential metabolites, we employed orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models for pairwise comparisons. The results demonstrated that all models exhibited good explanatory power (R2Y > 0.9) and predictive ability (Q2 > 0.5), with score plots showing distinct separation between groups (Fig. 3D). To identify differential metabolites, differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened based on VIP values (VIP > 1) combined with statistical significance (P < 0.05). In the comparison between the low-dose group and the control group, a total of 185 DAMs were identified (107 upregulated, 78 downregulated); in the medium-dose group, 424 DAMs were identified (376 upregulated, 48 downregulated); and in the high-dose group, 534 DAMs were identified (421 upregulated, 113 downregulated) (Figs. 3E; Supplementary Table S3-S5). Notably, the upregulated metabolites were predominantly amino acids and their derivatives, whereas the downregulated metabolites were mainly carbohydrates and their associated metabolites (Fig. S2A—F). All identified differential metabolites enabled robust clustering of samples within the same group in the hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. S2G—I), further confirming the reliability and stability of the experimental data. The top 20 metabolites ranked by variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were selected and visualized to highlight the pronounced differences among groups (Fig. S3A—C). In addition, representative carnitine-associated metabolites, including Dl-carnitine and multiple acylcarnitine species, showed dose-associated increases in relative abundance (Fig. S3D).

Venn diagram analysis revealed that 59 significantly differential metabolites were shared among the three dose groups and the control group (Fig. 3F). Heatmap analysis indicated that these shared metabolites primarily exhibited an upregulation trend in the l-carnitine treatment groups, particularly amino acid metabolites (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that l-carnitine may regulate hepatic metabolic activity primarily by promoting amino acid metabolism.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that in the low-dose group, significantly enriched pathways included nucleotide sugar biosynthesis and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Table S6). In the medium-dose group, significantly enriched pathways expanded to include glycerophospholipid metabolism and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 3I; Supplementary Table S7). The high-dose group was primarily involved in pyrimidine metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, and galactose metabolism (Fig. 3J; Supplementary Table S8). Notably, the D-amino acid metabolism pathway was significantly enriched in both the medium-dose and high-dose groups, suggesting that this pathway may play an important role in the overall metabolic remodeling of the liver induced by l-carnitine.

l-carnitine dose-associated regulates hepatic gene transcription

Subsequently, transcriptomics was employed to analyze the regulatory effects of l-carnitine on hepatic gene expression. PCA results demonstrated that samples from the different treatment groups and the control group exhibited a dose-dependent separation trend in global transcriptional profiles: separation between the low-dose group and the control group was relatively weak, whereas the medium- and high-dose groups showed distinct separation (Fig. 4A).

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis identified 241 DEGs in the comparison between the low-dose group and the control group, including 113 upregulated and 128 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S9). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis showed that these genes were primarily enriched in functions related to circadian rhythm regulation and transcription factor binding (Fig. S4A; Supplementary Table S12). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis involved a total of 38 pathways (P < 0.05), with the top five pathways including circadian rhythm, cell cycle, and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Table S15). This suggests that even under low-dose conditions, l-carnitine begins to influence the expression of circadian clock genes in the liver.

In the comparison between the medium-dose group and the control group, the number of DEGs increased significantly to 583 (308 upregulated and 275 downregulated) (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Table S10). GO analysis revealed that these genes were enriched in processes such as response to xenobiotic stimulus, organic hydroxy compound metabolism, and steroid and bile acid biosynthesis (Fig. S4B; Supplementary Table S13). KEGG pathway enrichment identified 62 significant pathways, with the top five including circadian rhythm, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 4E; Supplementary Table S16).

In the comparison between the high-dose group and the control group, a total of 667 differentially expressed genes were identified (321 upregulated and 346 downregulated) (Fig. 4F; Supplementary Table S11). GO analysis showed that these genes were mainly enriched in processes such as response to xenobiotic stimulus, circadian rhythm, small molecule biosynthesis, and regulation of circadian rhythm gene expression (Fig. S4C; Supplementary Table S14). KEGG analysis identified 65 significant pathways, with the top five including circadian rhythm, glutathione metabolism, circadian rhythm-fly, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and drug metabolism (Fig. 4G; Supplementary Table S17).

Further pathway analysis of the 119 shared differentially expressed genes across the three dose groups (Fig. 4H and S4D) revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to circadian rhythm, amino acid metabolism, and lipid synthesis (Fig. 4I). Notably, the core circadian rhythm transcription factors, Bmal1 and Clock, were significantly downregulated in all dose groups, with significant differences (Fig. 4J). To further examine whether the hepatic response to l-carnitine was accompanied by activation of the classical fatty acid oxidation (FAO) program, we evaluated the expression patterns of canonical FAO-related genes, including Cpt1a, Cpt2, Acox1, Ppara, Acadl, Acadm, Hadha, Hadhb, and Ehhadh. These genes were detectable in the transcriptomic dataset but were not identified as differentially expressed genes under the predefined DEG criteria (Fig. 4K;Supplementary Table S18). This suggests that, under standard chow-fed conditions, the transcriptional response to low-dose l-carnitine supplementation was not primarily characterized by broad activation of the canonical FAO pathway.

Subsequently, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to integrate the transcriptome and metabolome to uncover the transcriptional and metabolic pathways through which l-carnitine reduces hepatic lipid accumulation. The results showed that the turquoise module was positively correlated with the phenotype (|R| > 0.8, P < 0.05), while the blue module was negatively correlated with the phenotype (|R| > 0.7, P < 0.05) (Fig. S4E). By intersecting module genes with differentially expressed genes, we identified 25 genes in the turquoise module and 47 genes in the blue module (Fig. 4L). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between these intersected genes and 59 shared differential metabolites from the metabolome (|R| > 0.6, P < 0.05), and a correlation network was constructed (Fig. S4F). Network analysis revealed that the metabolites with the highest degrees included acarbose (Degree = 48), maltopentaose (Degree = 48), and stachyose (Degree = 46), which were closely associated with numerous significantly different hepatic phenotypic genes. This suggests that these key metabolites may play important roles in the regulation of gene modules and the formation of hepatic phenotypes.

l-carnitine induces systemic remodeling of hepatic protein expression

Using 4D-DIA proteomics, an average of 60,395 peptides were identified per sample, enabling quantitative coverage of 6612 proteins. PCA demonstrated that samples from each treatment group exhibited a separation trend ranging from weak to strong in two-dimensional space: the low-dose group slightly overlapped with the control group, the medium-dose group showed distinct separation, and the high-dose group was almost completely distinguished (Fig. 5A). The first two principal components explained the major variations in the proteomics data, with explanation ratios of 64.24%, 68.43%, and 67.25% for the low-dose vs. control, medium-dose vs. control, and high-dose vs. control comparisons, respectively.

Differential protein analysis employed screening criteria: P < 0.05 and FC > 1.2. In the comparison of the low-dose group vs the control group, 594 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified (366 upregulated and 228 downregulated) (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table S19). GO enrichment analysis showed that these proteins were primarily involved in processes such as regulation of cell population proliferation and COPII-coated vesicle cargo loading (Fig. S5A; Supplementary Table S22). KEGG pathways mainly involved cofactor biosynthesis, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and lysosomes (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table S25). In the medium-dose group, a total of 825 DEPs were identified (242 upregulated and 583 downregulated) (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S20). GO analysis indicated primary enrichment in cytoplasmic translation, intrauterine embryonic development, and peroxisomes (Fig. S5B; Supplementary Table S23). KEGG analysis identified 74 significantly enriched pathways, with the top five including cofactor biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and peroxisomes (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Table S26). In the high-dose group, 1668 DEPs were identified (727 upregulated and 941 downregulated) (Fig. 5F; Supplementary Table S21). GO analysis showed that these DEPs were enriched in processes such as cytoplasmic translation, peroxisomes, and postsynaptic density (Fig. S5C; Supplementary Table S24). KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways including peroxisomes, cofactor biosynthesis, and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Fig. 5G; Supplementary Table S27).

Joint pathway analysis of the proteomes across the three dose groups revealed that a total of 31 pathways were significantly enriched, including multiple pathways related to glucose metabolism, such as the pentose phosphate pathway, galactose metabolism, and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism (Fig. 5H). To further evaluate whether l-carnitine supplementation activated the classical fatty acid oxidation (FAO) program at the protein level, we examined canonical FAO-related proteins, including CPT1A, CPT2, ACOX1, PPARα, ACADL, ACADM, HADHA, HADHB, and EHHADH. These proteins did not show consistent dose-associated upregulation across the l-carnitine-treated groups (Fig. 5I;Supplementary Table S28). These results suggest that, under standard chow-fed conditions, the hepatic proteomic response to low-dose l-carnitine supplementation was not primarily characterized by broad activation of the canonical FAO pathway.

Subsequently, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to integratively analyze the metabolomic and proteomic datasets. The turquoise module showed a significant positive correlation with the phenotype (P < 0.05, |R| > 0.6), whereas the blue module was negatively correlated with the phenotype (P < 0.05, |R| > 0.5) (Fig. S5D). By integrating module proteins with differentially expressed proteins, a total of 98 core differentially expressed DEPs significantly associated with the phenotype were identified (Fig. 5J), and their correlations with metabolites were further analyzed (Fig. S5E). The results revealed that xylulose-5-phosphate (Xu-5P) exhibited the highest connectivity within the network and showed significant correlations with multiple phenotype-associated proteins. Further analysis demonstrated that, compared with the control group, the relative abundance of Xu-5P was significantly reduced in the experimental group. In parallel, the protein abundance of PPP2CB (the catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 2 A) as well as key fatty acid synthesis–related proteins, including Acaca and Fasn, also displayed a decreasing trend (Fig. 5K-L). Together, these findings suggest a potential association between reduced Xu-5P abundance, altered PP2A-related protein signatures, and attenuation of hepatic fatty acid synthesis-related protein features in response to L-carnitine supplementation.

Multi-omics analysis reveals the mechanism of l-carnitine in regulating hepatic lipid homeostasis

This study explored the mechanism by which l-carnitine reduces hepatic lipid accumulation through multi-omics analysis. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic data revealed that the number of consistently differentially expressed genes increased significantly with the dose. In the low-dose group, four genes were upregulated in both omics' layers; in the medium-dose group, eight were upregulated and 12 downregulated; and in the high-dose group, 13 were upregulated and 20 downregulated (Fig. S6 A-C). Across the three dose groups, Upp2, Tef, and Klf13 were identified as genes upregulated in both the transcriptome and proteome, while Hacl1, Gldc, Acnat1, and Acnat2, which were downregulated in the medium- and high-dose groups, were enriched in lipid metabolism-related pathways. These findings suggest that these genes may play key roles in the improvement of hepatic lipid metabolism by l-carnitine.

The KEGG results for the three omics layers indicated that when comparing the low-dose and medium-dose groups with the control group, no commonly enriched pathways were observed among the transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome. In contrast, in the comparison of the high-dose group vs. the control group, two shared pathways were identified across the three omics: pyrimidine metabolism and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate that high-dose treatment induces consistent pathway responses across multiple omics levels, suggesting that these two pathways may play critical roles in overall metabolic remodeling and phenotypic changes.

We performed correlation analyses between differentially expressed metabolites, proteins, and genes, and phenotypes related to hepatic lipid deposition. The results showed that most metabolites, genes, and proteins in the pyrimidine metabolism pathway were significantly correlated with phenotypes related to hepatic lipid deposition (Fig. 6B). Key metabolites included uracil, orotate, and β-alanine, while corresponding genes and proteins included Cad, Ctps2, Nt5c2, Dpyd, Dpys, and Nme3. These enzymes and metabolites are involved in pyrimidine de novo synthesis, nucleotide utilization, and degradation and recycling processes. Previous studies have confirmed that pyrimidine metabolism is closely related to hepatic lipid metabolism. Correlation analysis revealed that most metabolites were significantly negatively correlated with the phenotypes, whereas most genes and proteins were significantly positively correlated (P < 0.05, |R| > 0.6). Analysis of key genes related to pyrimidine metabolism showed significant differences in the relative abundance of Dpyd, Upp2, Rrm2, and uracil among the groups (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, correlation analysis of the ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway under high-dose l-carnitine intervention indicated that most metabolites, genes, and proteins in this pathway were significantly positively correlated with hepatic lipid deposition phenotypes (Fig. 6D) (P < 0.05, |R| > 0.6). Notably, low expression of the Ugt gene family is associated with protective effects against hepatic lipid deposition and fibrosis. Bar chart analysis showed that the relative abundance of Ugt1a6 and Ugt2a3 was significantly downregulated in the l-carnitine group (Fig. 6E). This suggests that l-carnitine may alleviate hepatic lipid deposition by downregulating Ugt family genes. Overall, various metabolites, genes, and proteins in the pyrimidine metabolism and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathways exhibited changes associated with hepatic lipid deposition under intervention with different doses of l-carnitine.

Discussion

l-carnitine has long been recognized as a mitochondrial cofactor that facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation. Most previous studies have examined its lipid-lowering effects in pathological contexts, such as NAFLD, obesity, or metabolic syndrome, where mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation is often impaired(Li et al., 2021; Virmani & Cirulli, 2022). However, the hepatic response to low-dose l-carnitine supplementation under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions remains less well defined. This knowledge gap is important from the perspective of nutritional science, because dietary l-carnitine, which is abundant in red meat and dairy products, is routinely consumed by individuals without overt metabolic disease. Therefore, the present study employed an integrated multi-omics approach, combining metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, to investigate the hepatic effects of low-dose l-carnitine supplementation in chow-fed mice. Although l-carnitine is classically linked to mitochondrial fatty acid transport and β-oxidation, canonical fatty acid oxidation (FAO)-related markers were not significantly activated in the present standard chow-fed model. At the transcriptomic level, several FAO-related genes, including Cpt1a, Cpt2, Acox1, Ppara, Acadl, Acadm, Hadha, Hadhb, and Ehhadh, showed upward trends but did not reach statistical significance under the predefined DEG criteria. The weak or subthreshold FAO-related transcriptional response may have been influenced by the 12 h fasting period before sacrifice, because fasting can reshape the hepatic transcriptional state and induce PPARα-related metabolic adaptation, and by the use of a single endpoint after 35 days, which may have missed a transient FAO response occurring earlier during supplementation(Wang, Xu, et al., 2024). The reduction in hepatic lipid accumulation observed in this study may not be primarily explained by significant activation of classical FAO. Instead, the multi-omics results suggest that low-dose l-carnitine supplementation was more closely associated with attenuation of lipogenesis-related signatures and coordinated remodeling of carbohydrate, nucleotide, and detoxification-related metabolic pathways. Future studies using more refined dose gradients, including very low, intermediate, and higher doses, together with direct β-oxidation flux measurements, are needed to determine whether stronger or different FAO responses can be induced under standard chow-fed conditions.

Routine hematological analysis provided supplementary information on systemic blood cell parameters after l-carnitine supplementation. Erythrocyte-related parameters, including HCT, HGB, and RBC, remained largely unchanged among groups, suggesting that low-dose l-carnitine supplementation did not induce overt erythrocyte-related abnormalities. However, WBC and absolute lymphocyte counts were reduced in the medium- and high-dose groups, whereas PLT counts were increased. These hematological changes should be considered preliminary findings, and their biological significance requires further investigation.

Histopathological analyses showed that l-carnitine supplementation was associated with improved hepatic morphological features under standard chow-fed conditions. Oral supplementation significantly reduced histological vacuolation density and cross-sectional area across both periportal and centrilobular zones, without altering systemic parameters such as body weight, indicating a liver-autonomous mechanism(Fujisawa et al., 2017; Nofal et al., 2024). Notably, we observed an increased proportion of binucleated hepatocytes, a morphological hallmark of mature, metabolically stable cells(Gentric et al., 2012; Kreutz et al., 2017). This observation further supports the view that low-dose l-carnitine supplementation may influence hepatic structural remodeling under standard chow-fed conditions. Together with the reductions in hepatocellular vacuolation and hepatocyte area, the increased proportion of binucleated hepatocytes suggests that dietary l-carnitine may be associated with adaptive changes in hepatocyte organization during the regulation of hepatic lipid homeostasis(Liu et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). Across the three administered doses, hepatocyte area and H&E-based lipid density decreased in both hepatic zones, indicating a monotonic dose-associated histological response. However, the magnitude of change was not uniform across all histological indices, and continued improvement at 1.5 mg/day suggests that a clear plateau was not reached within the tested dose range. These findings do not support a human nutritional recommendation at this stage; future studies using finer dose increments and additional higher-dose groups are needed to define the minimum effective dose, response slope, and plateau.

At the metabolic level, l-carnitine induced a profound reprogramming of hepatic substrate utilization. Metabolomic profiling revealed a coordinated shift: amino acid derivatives, particularly N-acetyl-L-leucine, a branched-chain amino acid metabolite associated with improved lipid handling, were markedly elevated, while carbohydrate-derived intermediates, including UDP-glucose (a critical donor for glycogen and glycolipid synthesis) were suppressed(Chen et al., 2021; Chen, Zhou, et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). This pattern suggests that l-carnitine supplementation may shift hepatic metabolic signatures away from carbohydrate-associated lipogenic substrate availability and toward amino acid-related metabolic remodeling. In addition, Dl-carnitine and several acylcarnitine species showed increased relative abundance, particularly in the high-dose group, indicating that oral l-carnitine supplementation induced detectable changes in hepatic carnitine-associated metabolism. The data suggest that under standard chow-fed conditions, the metabolomic response to low-dose l-carnitine supplementation was not primarily characterized by broad activation of classical fatty acid oxidation, but rather by coordinated changes in amino acid derivatives, carbohydrate-related intermediates, and carnitine-associated metabolites(TeSlaa et al., 2023; Yang & Li, 2024; Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2023).

Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed dose-associated regulatory changes. At the low dose, the significant downregulation of Bmal1 and Clock likely represents part of the broader metabolic remodeling associated with l-carnitine supplementation and reflects lower absolute transcript levels at the terminal sampling time. Hepatocyte-specific Bmal1 loss has been shown to disrupt rhythmic hepatic transcription and exacerbate cholesterol accumulation, inflammation, and fibrosis, whereas PPARα-dependent hepatic Clock downregulation has been associated with reduced lipid accumulation in an NAFLD model(de Assis et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2023). In the present study, the downregulation of Bmal1 and Clock occurred together with reduced hepatic lipid accumulation, suggesting that these clock-gene changes may be associated with the hepatic lipid response to l-carnitine supplementation. As dosage increased, the regulatory scope expanded to encompass core metabolic pathways, including unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, primary bile acid metabolism, and xenobiotic detoxification. The concomitant downregulation of peroxisomal enzymes (e.g., Hsd17b4) and bile acid–synthesizing proteins (e.g., Cyp7a1) reflects an adaptive negative feedback loop: with reduced lipid and cholesterol burden, the liver attenuates energetically costly anabolic and detoxification processes, thereby promoting metabolic efficiency.

An important observation of this study was the coordinated alteration of Xu-5P abundance, Ppp2cb expression, and lipogenesis-related genes in response to l-carnitine supplementation. Xylulose-5-phosphate (Xu-5P), a pentose phosphate pathway intermediate, acts as a nutrient-sensitive signal that activates protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), leading to dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of the lipogenic transcription factor ChREBP(Kabashima et al., 2003). In the present study, l-carnitine-treated mice showed reduced Xu-5P abundance, lower expression of Ppp2cb, and decreased expression of downstream lipogenesis-related proteins, including Acaca and Fasn. These coordinated changes are consistent with attenuation of the Xu-5P/ChREBP-related lipogenic program and may partly explain the observed reduction in hepatic lipid accumulation.

In addition to lipogenesis-related signatures, the integrated multi-omics analysis also identified alterations in pyrimidine metabolism and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism. In pyrimidine metabolism, we observed a striking “substrate–enzyme decoupling”: while uracil and orotic acid accumulated, key biosynthetic (Cad) and catabolic (Dpyd) enzymes were transcriptionally suppressed. This pattern suggests the emergence of a metabolite-driven feedback loop, wherein elevated uracil, which is known to inhibit SREBP-1c and reduce lipogenesis, reinforces the anti-lipogenic phenotype(Liu et al., 2025). Concurrently, the downregulation of UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (Ugt1a1, Ugt2a3)—enzymes that utilize UDP-glucuronic acid derived from ascorbate/aldarate metabolism for phase II detoxification—may reflect reduced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. Critically, UGT overexpression has been causally linked to hepatic steatosis in both rodents and humans(Bates et al., 2023; Liang & Song, 2024); thus, their suppression by l-carnitine represents a previously unrecognized layer of hepatoprotection rooted in detoxification biology (Fig. 7).

Collectively, these findings suggest that low-dose l-carnitine supplementation under standard chow-fed conditions was associated with reduced hepatic lipid accumulation and coordinated molecular changes across four major aspects: (1) attenuation of lipogenesis-related signatures potentially associated with Xu-5P/ChREBP-related regulation; (2) remodeling of amino acid- and carbohydrate-related metabolites; (3) alterations in hepatic circadian transcriptional signatures; and (4) changes in nucleotide- and detoxification-related pathways. These integrated multi-omics findings provide a molecular framework for understanding how dietary l-carnitine may participate in the nutritional regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism.

From a nutritional perspective, this study provides preliminary multi-omics evidence that low-dose l-carnitine supplementation may participate in the regulation of hepatic lipid-associated metabolic responses under standard chow-fed, non-HFD conditions. The observed changes in hepatic lipid accumulation, lipogenesis-related signatures, amino acid- and carbohydrate-related metabolites, and nucleotide- and detoxification-related pathways suggest that dietary l-carnitine may act as a nutritional factor involved in hepatic lipid metabolism. These findings provide an integrated molecular framework for understanding the nutritional regulation of hepatic lipid homeostasis by l-carnitine. Future studies should prioritize genetic (e.g., ChREBP knockdown) or pharmacological interventions to prove the causality of the Xu-5P/PP2A/ChREBP pathway in the effects of L-carnitine, together with direct measurements of fatty acid synthesis flux (e.g., using deuterated water) to provide powerful evidence. More broadly, refined dose gradients, targeted biochemical phenotyping, direct β-oxidation flux measurements, and functional validation are needed to clarify its dose-response characteristics and underlying mechanisms.
