The ZFP36-FTO axis is a key regulator of macrophage activation and a druggable therapeutic target of sepsis
Authors: Rajesh Sarkar, Lei Wang, Jie Du, Perry J. Blackshear, Yan Chun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe have reported that ZFP36 promotes Fto mRNA degradation to regulate N6-adenomethylation (m6A) status in macrophages. Here we showed that Zfp36 deletion from macrophages triggered excessive systemic inflammation, whereas Fto ablation suppressed systemic inflammation in sepsis models. Consistently, Zfp36−/− bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) exhibited nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) overactivation and excessive cytokine induction upon lipopolysaccharide (LPS) challenge, whereas NF-κB signaling and cytokine production were suppressed in Fto−/− BMDMs. LPS failed to induce SOCS1 to a proper level in Zfp36−/− BMDMs because Socs1 transcript was under-methylated, whereas this transcript was hyper-methylated in Fto−/− BMDMs leading to SOCS1 over-induction, indicating the ZFP36-FTO axis controls Socs1 m6A status to regulate macrophage activation. Zfp36 deletion abrogated LPS-induced Fto down-regulation, whereas Fto knockdown rescued Zfp36−/− BMDMs from overactivation, confirming FTO acts downstream of ZFP36 to promote macrophage septic response. FTO inhibitors effectively suppressed macrophage cytokine production and curbed systemic inflammation, demonstrating FTO is a druggable target for sepsis treatment.

Introduction

Macrophages play a central role in host defense against infection.1 As the first line of defense, macrophages respond to microorganism infection by rapidly releasing a plethora of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that form a cytokine storm. While the purpose of the cytokine storm is to eliminate the infectious agents, dysregulated cytokine release can trigger systemic inflammation that causes organ dysfunction and even death.2 For example, sepsis is a leading cause of death in bacterial infection that results from excessive and non-resolving systemic inflammatory response and multi-organ failure.3,4 Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), play key roles in the development of sepsis.5 To prevent excessive inflammation, negative feedback mechanisms are in place to control the duration and intensity of the cytokine response. For instance, the SOCS family of proteins is such a group of negative feedback regulators that is co-induced during inflammation to inhibit intracellular cytokine signaling.6,7

N6-adenomethylation (m6A) is the most abundant internal post-transcriptional modification in eukaryotic mRNAs.8 m6A is dynamically regulated by writers (m6A methyltransferases), erasers (m6A demethylases), and readers (m6A-binding proteins), and m6A modification itself is involved in all fundamental aspects of mRNA metabolism, including mRNA processing, stability, and translation.9 As such, m6A plays broad roles in physiology and pathology, ranging from stem cells, immunity, and memory to cancer.10,11

Our recent work demonstrated that m6A is critically involved in the regulation of macrophage activation in acute inflammatory response to bacterial infection.12 We reported that depletion of METTL14, a key component of the m6A methyltransferase, from macrophages triggers excessive and prolonged pro-inflammatory cytokine production in response to bacterial infection, leading to multi-organ damage and high mortality in the mutant mice, and this excessive and sustained inflammatory reaction is mainly due to insufficient induction of SOCS1 in macrophages. We further demonstrated that the molecular basis for SOCS1 under-induction is m6A depletion on the Socs1 transcript, which leads to increased Socs1 mRNA degradation, as m6A is required to stabilize Socs1 mRNA. Importantly, we showed that ZFP36, an AU-rich element (ARE)-binding protein, and FTO, a m6A demethylase, are also involved in the control of Socs1 m6A status.12 Bacterial infection induces ZFP36 expression in macrophages, and ZFP36 protein binds to the ARE on the Fto transcript to promote Fto mRNA degradation, resulting in reduced FTO protein translation. The down-regulation of FTO demethylase is expected to increase m6A status on the Socs1 transcript leading to SOCS1 up-regulation. We speculated that the ZFP36-FTO axis is a crucial upstream regulator of SOCS1 that is required to maintain Socs1 m6A status; as such, this axis is a critical component of the SOCS1-mediated negative feedback loop in bacterial infection. The goal of this study is to confirm this hypothesis, and, based on this hypothesis, to explore further whether FTO is a druggable therapeutic target to curb systemic inflammation in sepsis.

Results

Mice ablated of Zfp36 or Fto in macrophages show opposite inflammatory responses to bacterial infection

To confirm the regulatory function of the ZFP36-FTO axis in the macrophage inflammatory response to bacterial infection, we generated Zfp36flox/flox;LysM-Cre (designated as mZfp36−/−) mice and Ftoflox/flox;LysM-Cre (designated as mFto−/−) mice. We and others have confirmed that LysM-Cre deletes genes mainly in monocytes and macrophages.12,13,14 The mZfp36−/− and mFto−/− mice appeared phenotypically normal at baseline. We examined the macrophage response to bacterial infection in these mice using the cecum ligation and puncture (CLP) model,15 a model of polymicrobial infection that mimics many aspects of human sepsis,16 as well as the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced sepsis model.

Following CLP surgery, mZfp36−/− mice developed much more severe systemic inflammation compared with Zfp36f/f control mice, manifested by a more severe hypothermic response (a drop in the core body temperature typical of bacterial infection in rodents) (Figure 1A) and higher mortality (70% vs. 30%) (Figure 1B) over 120 h. Histological examination revealed that mZfp36−/− mice suffered more severe lung injury (with more immune cell infiltration) (Figure 1C). Similar results were seen in these mice treated with LPS. Peritoneal administration of LPS led to more severe hypothermia and higher mortality (60% vs. 0%) in mZfp36−/− mice compared with Zfp36f/f controls over time (Figures S1A and B). LPS administration triggered a higher expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnfa, Il6, Il1b, and Ifng) in the lung and liver of mZfp36−/− mice compared with Zfp36f/f controls (Figures S1C and D). The induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines in these organs correlated with the severity of organ injury. These observations are consistent with previous studies,17 and confirm that Zfp36 deletion from macrophages triggers excessive and non-resolving systemic inflammation.

In contrast to Zfp36 ablation, deletion of Fto from macrophages markedly mitigated systemic inflammation in both CLP and LPS sepsis models. Following CLP surgery, mFto−/− mice developed less severe hypothermia (Figure 2A) and showed lower mortality (20% vs. 40%) (Figure 2B) compared with Ftof/f control mice. Consistently, mFto−/− mice developed less severe lung injury (Figure 2C) and had smaller inductions in serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IFN-γ) following CLP surgery compared with Ftof/f mice (Figure 2D). Peritoneal macrophages isolated from mFto−/− mice showed less inductions of these cytokine transcripts (Tnfa, Il6, Il1b, and Ifng) compared with Ftof/f mice after CLP (Figure 2E). Similar results were seen in mFto−/− and Ftof/f mice treated with LPS (Figures S2A–E). Differential effects of Zfp36 and Fto depletion on NF-κB signaling pathway

To understand the molecular mechanism whereby the ZFP36-FTO axis regulates macrophage activation, we studied bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) in cultures. Consistent with the animal data, treatment with LPS induced more robust and prolonged expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnfa, Il1b, and Il6) over time in mZfp36−/− BMDMs compared with control BMDMs (Figure 3A). Similar observations were seen at the protein level, as western blotting revealed a higher induction of TNF-α and IL-6 in mZfp36−/− BMDMs compared with Zfp36f/f BMDMs following LPS treatment (Figure 3B). The nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway plays an essential role in macrophage activation.18 LPS binding to TLR4 receptor rapidly activates the NF-κB signaling pathway to drive inflammatory cytokine expression. We found that, in the course of NF-κB activation, LPS treatment induced much stronger activation of NF-κB signaling in mZfp36−/− BMDMs compared with Zfp36f/f BMDMs, with stronger stimulation and longer lasting of IKKα/β and p65 phosphorylation, and more severe degradation of IκBα (Figure 3D). In contrast, the induction of NF-κB signaling activation was less intense in mFto−/− BMDMs compared with Ftof/f BMDMs following LPS treatment (Figure 3E). Therefore, the differential effects of Zfp36 and Fto ablation on NF-κB signaling activation account for the differential expression profiles of pro-inflammatory cytokines in these macrophages. Following LPS stimulation, ZFP36 was induced, whereas FTO was down-regulated, in a time-dependent manner in wild-type BMDMs (Figure 3F). This inverse relationship between ZFP36 and FTO in response to LPS treatment is consistent with our previous observation that ZFP36 promotes Fto mRNA degradation.12

The ZFP36-FTO axis regulates macrophage activation via modulating the m6A status of Socs1 transcript

SOCS1 is a key negative feedback regulator that is rapidly induced by LPS or bacterial infection to contain inflammatory reaction.7,19 SOCS1 directly limits the activation of the NF-κB signal cascade.20 We found that, in the course of LPS-induced macrophage activation, Scos1 mRNA transcript was under-induced in mZfp36−/− BMDMs compared with Zfp36f/f BMDMs (Figure 4A), so was Socs1 protein (Figure 4B), which is consistent with the hyper-stimulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines seen in mZfp36−/− BMDMs. In contrast, SOCS1 was over-induced at both the mRNA and protein levels in mFto−/− BMDMs compared with control BMDMs (Figures 4A and 4C), which explains the suppression of cytokine expression in mFto−/− BMDMs. These data indicate that SOCS1 is a common target of ZFP36 and FTO in the regulation of macrophage activation, and the differential regulation of SOCS1 by ZFP36 and FTO is reflected by the differential inflammatory status of mZfp36−/− and mFto−/− BMDMs under LPS stimulation.Figure 4The ZFP36-FTO axis regulates macrophage activation via modulating the m6A status of Socs1 transcript(A) RT-qPCR quantitation of Socs1 transcript expression in control (Ctrl), mZfp36−/−, and mFto−/− BMDMs over 24 h following LPS stimulation. n = 3 at each time point in each group.(B) Western blot analysis of SOCS1 protein expression in Zfp36f/f and mZfp36−/− BMDMs at 6 h after LPS stimulation.(C) Western blot analysis of SOCS1 protein expression in Ftof/f and mFto−/− BMDMs at 6 h after LPS stimulation.(D) Read density on Socs1 transcript in wild-type BMDMs treated with PBS (Ctrl) or LPS. As m6A regulates Socs1 mRNA stability and Socs1 m6A is dramatically induced by LPS,12 we examined the m6A status of Socs1 transcript in mZfp36−/− and mFto−/− BMDMs by m6A-immunoprecipitation-qPCR. We have reported that LPS induces 4 major m6A peaks throughout the Socs1 transcript, one in 3′ UTR (site 1), one near the stop codon (site 2), and two in the coding region (sites 3 and 6) (Figure 4D). Here we found that the induction of these m6A peaks on Socs1 transcript by LPS treatment was less robust in mZfp36−/− BMDMs compared with Zfp36f/f BMDMs (Figure 4E), whereas LPS treatment caused a much stronger induction of Socs1 m6A on these sites in mFto−/− BMDMs than in Fto+/+ BMDMs (Figure 4F). These observations are consistent with the notion that ZFP36 indirectly targets SOCS1 via FTO, indicating that the ZFP36-FTO axis is the upstream regulator of SOCS1 to control macrophage activation.

Previous studies reported that ZFP36 feedback regulates macrophage activation by promoting mRNA degradation of a number of NF-κB negative regulators (such as Ier3, Tnfaip3, Nfkbid, Nfkbie, and Nfbkiz).21,22 In agreement with these prior reports, we found that these mRNA transcripts, as well as those of Tnfa and Il1b, were induced more in mZfp36−/− BMDMs than in Zfp36f/f BMDMs following LPS treatment (Figures S3A and B). The observation of more robust induction of NF-κB negative regulators, however, is inconsistent with the fact that mZfp36−/− BMDMs are more inflammatory with stronger activation of NF-κB signaling upon LPS stimulation, suggesting that these negative regulators are not responsible for the inflammatory phenotype of mZfp36−/− BMDMs.

FTO acts downstream of ZFP36 to regulate macrophage activation

We have reported that ZFP36 binds to the 3′ ARE of Fto transcript to induce Fto mRNA degradation.12 Consistent with this observation, LPS treatment induced ZFP36 protein, which down-regulated FTO protein in both Zfp36f/f and Ftof/f BMDMs (both are equivalent to wild-type BMDMs) (Figures 5A and 5B). In mZfp36−/− BMDMs FTO protein remained at the baseline level under LPS treatment because of Zfp36 deletion (Figure 5A), whereas in mFto−/− BMDMs ZFP36 was induced in the absence of FTO expression (Figure 5B). To prove functionally that FTO acts downstream of ZFP36 to regulate macrophage activation, we knocked down FTO using two different Fto-specific shRNAs (shRNA-1 and -2), as well as over-expressed FTO, in mZfp36−/− BMDMs (Figure 5C), utilizing a lentiviral transduction approach established in our previous studies,12,23 and then assessed macrophage inflammatory response following LPS stimulation. As expected, LPS drastically induced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnfa, Il6, and Il1b) in control lentivirus-transduced mZfp36−/− BMDMs, but the induction of these cytokines was markedly attenuated in Fto-shRNA-lentivirus-transduced mZfp36−/− BMDMs (Figure 5D); in contrast, mZfp36−/− BMDMs over-expressing FTO showed the highest induction of these cytokines following LPS stimulation (Figure 5D). These observations demonstrate that FTO is a strong promoter of macrophage inflammatory activation that acts downstream of ZFP36.

FTO is a druggable therapeutic target to curb systemic inflammation in sepsis

Antibiotics are the most commonly used treatment for sepsis,24 but at present there are no effective medicines that can directly suppress systemic inflammation in sepsis. The important role of the ZFP36-FTO axis in the regulation of macrophage activation prompted us to ask whether FTO is a therapeutic target for sepsis treatment. As mZfp36−/− mice elicited a much stronger cytokine storm than wild-type control mice in response to bacterial infection, we further tested the anti-sepsis efficacy of FB23-2 in these mice. Again, a single dose of FB23-2 administration attenuated hypothermic reaction (Figure S4A), reduced mortality rate from 60% to 10% (Figure S4B), and lowered the induction of serum pro-inflammatory cytokines to levels less than those seen in LPS-treated control Zfp36f/f mice (Figure S4C). LPS caused very severe lung injury in mZfp36−/− mice, and FB23-2 effectively mitigated the lung injury (Figure S4D) and reduced the production of pro-inflammatory cytokine in the lung and liver of LPS-treated mZfp36−/− mice (Figures S4E and S4F). Together, these data demonstrate that FTO is a druggable therapeutic target to control macrophage cytokine storm, and FTO inhibitors can be used to curb the systemic inflammation in the management of sepsis.

Discussion

Sepsis is a life-threatening clinical problem characterized by overwhelming systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction as a result of dysregulated host responses to infection.3 After infection, pathogen-associated molecular patterns interact with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs),26 to trigger the activation of downstream signaling pathways that are responsible for a burst of production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. The NF-κB cascade is a common signaling pathway activated by TLRs to stimulate the transcription of numerous pro-inflammatory mediators.27 This cytokine storm recruits leukocytes and activates the complement and coagulation systems to eliminate the pathogens.2,28 In sepsis, however, an excessive and un-resolved cytokine storm causes cellular injury that results in the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which further activate PRRs to initiate a vicious cycle, leading eventually to organ damage, catabolism, and death.28

Macrophages as a first line of innate defense are a major source of cytokine storm.2 We have reported that Socs1 m6A status plays an essential role in the regulation of macrophage inflammatory response to bacterial infection.12 As a central negative regulator of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, SOCS1 is rapidly induced upon infection to maintain a balanced septic response and prevent excessive harm to the host caused by the cytokine storm.29,30 Our previous studies showed that bacterial infection increases m6A on the Socs1 transcript, which maintains its mRNA stability to ensure SOCS1 up-regulation.12 In the current study we extended the investigation to demonstrate that the ZFP36-FTO axis regulates the Socs1 m6A status to control macrophage activation. This finding led to the discovery that FTO is a druggable therapeutic target to curb systemic inflammation in sepsis.

We addressed the biological role of the ZFP36-FTO axis using mouse genetic models with Zfp36 or Fto ablation in macrophages. We showed that mZfp36−/− mice developed much more severe cytokine storm and a higher mortality rate compared with control mice in both CLP- and LPS-induced sepsis models, whereas the systemic inflammation occurred in a much milder magnitude in mFto−/− mice relative to the control. These animal phenotypes are caused by altered macrophage activities, respectively. mZfp36−/− macrophages were hyper-inflammatory upon LPS stimulation, with heightened induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhanced activation of NF-κB signaling, and impaired induction of SOCS1, whereas mFto−/− macrophages showed completely opposite phenotypes. Our data indicate that the ZFP36-FTO axis is an upstream regulator of SOCS1 and acts to determine the m6A status of Socs1 mRNA. We found that LPS induced ZFP36 while down-regulating FTO in macrophages, whereas Zfp36 ablation dramatically relieved the suppression of FTO, which confirms that ZFP36 directly inhibits FTO. We also demonstrated that lentivirus-mediated knockdown of FTO rescued the hyper-inflammatory phenotype of mZfp36−/− BMDMs, confirming that FTO functionally acts downstream of ZFP36 to control macrophage inflammatory reaction. Collectively, these data lead to a conclusion that ZFP36 is induced in macrophage response to infection to suppress FTO expression, so as to raise the Socs1 m6A status that up-regulates SOCS1 to feedback control the magnitude of macrophage inflammatory reaction (Figure S5). Future studies are needed to validate this conclusion using additional animal models that closely resemble human sepsis. The critical role of FTO in macrophage activation suggests that FTO may be a useful therapeutic target to inhibit systemic inflammation in the development of sepsis. Indeed, we found that a small molecule FTO inhibitor has a strong anti-sepsis activity. Given the limited therapeutic options in sepsis treatment,31 this result has very valuable translational potentials.

ZFP36 is an RNA-binding protein that has a broad range of activities including regulation of cellular metabolism, T cell activation, and macrophage activation.32,33,34 Global Zfp36 knockout leads to cachexia, arthritis, and autoimmune problems, suggesting that ZFP36 is an anti-inflammatory protein,35,36,37 but its downstream targets are unclear. Previous studies also suggest that ZFP36 is a global post-transcriptional regulator of feedback control in macrophage inflammatory reaction, as ZFP36 targets and suppresses a number of NF-κB negative regulators in LPS-induced macrophage to allow macrophage activation to proceed.21,22 We confirmed the suppression of these negative regulators by ZFP36, as many of them were over-stimulated by LPS in mZfp36−/− BMDMs (Figure S3). However, these data are inconsistent with the fact that mZfp36−/− BMDMs are hyper-inflammatory with stronger activation of NF-κB signaling, because over-stimulation of these negative regulators would have suppressed macrophage activation. In fact, we showed that SOCS1 is under-induced in LPS-treated mZfp36−/− BMDMs relative to the control (Figure 4), which is consistent with the hyper-inflammatory phenotype of these cells. Therefore, SOCS1 is a biologically relevant target of ZFP36 in macrophages, and the anti-inflammatory activity of ZFP36 is achieved at least in part by up-regulating SOCS1 via the ZFP36-FTO axis. In addition, early work has revealed ZFP36 also targets TNF-α and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor to regulate inflammatory response.38,39

Cytokine storm syndromes include a variety of inflammatory etiologies with a final common result of overwhelming systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, multi-organ dysfunction, and potentially death.40 Dysregulated macrophage activation has been implicated in these disorders.41,42 Therefore, how to control or contain the cytokine storm is a significant question for the disorders with systemic inflammation. At present, the most commonly used method to treat sepsis is antibiotics; other treatment options include hemodynamic support with fluid administration and vasoactive agents to maintain blood pressure in patients with associated circulatory shock.24,43 Currently, no drugs are available to directly suppress systemic inflammation.31 Although antibiotics are the fundamental therapeutic strategy in sepsis management, it can deplete the commensal anaerobic gut microbes leading to unintended consequences.44 Here we showed that a small molecule FTO inhibitor, FB23-2, was able to effectively block pro-inflammatory cytokine production in LPS-induced macrophage cultures and to markedly attenuate systemic inflammation and reduce mortality in sepsis models. FB23-2 directly binds to FTO and has been used to treat acute myeloid leukemia, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, and diabetic retinopathy.25,45,46 To our knowledge, this is the first report that an FTO inhibitor can curb systemic inflammation in animals that are developing severe sepsis. This exciting result may have paved the way for the evaluation of the anti-sepsis activity of FTO inhibitors in the clinical settings.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations in this study. Our study strongly suggests that, in the negative feedback loop required to properly control macrophage inflammatory response to bacterial infection, ZFP36 up-regulation is a critical event to promote Fto mRNA degradation so as to keep the m6A status of Scos1 transcript hence an appropriate SOCS1 induction. However, how Zfp36 mRNA transcription is up-regulated upon bacterial infection or LPS challenge is unclear, and we did not address this key question in this study. Another limitation is that we do not know whether the ZFP36-FTO axis has additional targets in the regulation of macrophage activation, and this question was not addressed in this work either.

Experimentals model and subject details

Animals

The generation of Zfp36flox/flox mice has been published.17 Ftoflox/flox mice (B6.129-Ftotm1.1Pzg/YxtJ; JAX:027830) and LysM-Cre transgenic mice (B6.129P2-Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo/J, JAX: 004781) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Zfp36flox/flox;LysM-Cre (mZfp36−/−) and Ftoflox/flox;LysM-Cre (mFto−/−) mice were generated by crossing these two strains of mice. In all experiments 6- to 8-week old mice, both male and female in approximately equal numbers, were used. Animal genders were not found to influence the results. All mice were housed at 25°C and maintained in a 12h/12h light/dark cycle. All animal study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Chicago.

Cell lines

RAW264.7 mouse macrophage cell line (ATCC TIB-71) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C and 5% CO2. L929 mouse fibroblast line (ATCC CCL-1) was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C and 5% CO2. L929 conditioned media were harvested 3 days after confluency, filtrated through 0.22 μm filters and stored at −80°C. The cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat analysis and were tested for mycoplasma contamination with negative results.

Bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs)

BMDM cultures and lentiviral transduction of BMDMs were performed as described previously.23 Briefly, mouse bone marrow cells were flushed out of the femur and tibia with 10 mL RPMI using a syringe attached with a 26-G needle, and red blood cells were lysed with 10 mM NH4Cl (pH8.0). The bone marrow cells were plated in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. After incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 5 h, the unattached cells were collected, replated in RPMI1640 containing 10% FBS and 30% L929 conditioned media and cultured for 7 days before use. For LPS treatment, macrophages were incubated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at 100 ng/mL for various times, and then cellular RNAs and/or cell lysates were prepared for analyses.

Method details

LPS-induced sepsis

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with LPS (E. Coli 0111:B4; L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in PBS (pH 7.0) at 20 mg/kg. Control mice were injected with 0.05 mL PBS. Mouse core body temperature was measured using a Digi-Sense digital thermometer (Model No. WD-20250-91). To study the effect of FB23-2 on systemic inflammation, mice were treated with one dose of FB23-2 at 2.5 mg/kg i.p. 1 h before LPS challenge. Mouse sera were collected through retra-orbital bleeding under anesthesia and stored at −80°C for later ELISA analysis of blood cytokines.

Cecum ligation and puncture (CLP) polymicrobial infection model

CLP procedure was performed based on a published protocol.47 In brief, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isofluorane mixed with 100% oxygen. When the mice failed to respond to paw pinch, buprenophine was administered subcutaneously at 0.05 mg/kg prior to proper sterilization of the skin with 10% providone iodine, and then a midline abdominal incision was made. The cecum was exposed and ligated with a 3-0 silk tie 1 cm from the tip and the cecal wall perforated with a 20-gauge needle. The cecum was squeezed lightly to expose a small amount of stool to ensure complete perforation. Then the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was closed. Immediately following the procedure, 0.5 mL of warmed normal saline was administered subcutaneously. Control mice underwent anesthesia, laparotomy and wound closure but not the cecal ligation and puncture procedures. Mouse core body temperature was measured using a Digi-Sense digital thermometer. Mouse sera were collected through retra-orbital bleeding under anesthesia and stored at −80°C for later ELISA analysis of blood cytokines.

Lung histology

The lung was harvested immediately after mice were killed and fixed overnight in 4% formaldehyde made in PBS (pH 7.2) at room temperature. The tissue was processed, embedded in paraffin wax, cut into 4 μm sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Cytokine quantification

Mouse TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β and IFN-γ concentrations in the sera were quantified using ELISA kits purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lentiviral construction

Fto shRNA-lentiviruses were constructed in pLV[shRNA]-EGFP:T2A:Neo-U6 lentiviral vector (VectorBuilder). The shRNA target sequences are TAGTCTGACTTGGTGTTAAAT (#1) or TTGAAAGAGGAGCCCTATTTC (#3). The pLV-Neo-EF1A-hFTO construct has been reported previously.12

BMDM transduction

Lentiviral transduction of BMDMs was performed as reported previously.12,23 Briefly, freshly prepared bone marrow cells were plated in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. After incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 5 h, the unattached cells were harvested and then incubated with a lentivirus at a MOI of 10 in RPMI1640 containing 10% FBS and 6 μg/mL polybrene for 48 h before being cultured in the differentiation medium for 7 days. The transduced BMDMs were treated with LPS at 100 ng/mL and cellular RNAs and/or cell lysates were isolated for analysis.

RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). First-strand cDNAs were synthesized using a 5X All-in-one RT Plus MasterMix kit (Lamda Biotech). Cytokine transcripts were quantified by real-time PCR in a Roche Light Cycler 480II Real-Time PCR System, using Universal SYBR Green qPCR MasterMix 2X (Lamda Biotech). The relative amounts of transcripts were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt formula,48 normalized to Gapdh or beta-actin transcript as an internal control. PCR primers have been reported in our previous publication.12

Western blotting

Detailed Western blot procedures were described previously.49 Briefly, tissue and cell samples were homogenized in Laemmli buffer. Protein concentration was determined using a Bio-Rad DC RC protein assay kit. Protein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and electroblotted onto Immobilon-P membranes. The membranes were blotted with a primary antibody, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence using a Bio-Rad Chemi-Doc imager.

m6A RIP-qPCR

m6A RIP-qPCR was carried out as described previously.12 Poly(A+) mRNAs were fragmented using RNA Fragmentation Reagents (ThermoFisher). A portion of fragmented mRNAs was saved as input control. Fragmented mRNAs were incubated with anti-m6A antibody, and antibody-bound mRNA fragments were purified using the EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment Kit (New England Biolabs). The IP-purified mRNA fragments were reversed-transcribed into cDNA using hexamer random primer, and short sequences (100–150 bp) covering the m6A sites were quantified by real-time PCR. PCR primers have been reported in our previous publication.12 The enrichment of m6A was determined by normalization to the input.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data values were presented as means ± SD. Most experiments were repeated at least twice. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 9.5.1. For two group comparisons unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used, and for three or more group comparisons ordinary one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Animal survival rates were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and groups were analyzed by the log rank test. p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
