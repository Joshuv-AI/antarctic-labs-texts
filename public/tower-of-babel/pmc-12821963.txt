Baicalin affects the progression of diabetic retinopathy through the RAGE/PXDN/PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Yi Gao, Huirui Liu, Tao Yang, Jianwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is the leading cause of blindness-related eye diseases in adults. The pathogenesis of DR is attributed to the excessive proliferation of microvessels, which leads to vitreous haemorrhage and retinal traction, thereby significantly damaging the patient’s vision. In this study, we investigated the role and molecular mechanism of Peroxidasin (PXDN) and the active ingredient baicalin (BAI) of traditional Chinese medicine in regulating the senescence and pathological angiogenesis of human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs) to identify the key genes and signalling pathways involved in the progression of DR.MethodsIn this study, key hub genes were screened using relevant datasets. The expression levels of PXDN, fibronectin (FN1), and PI3K/AKT pathway-related proteins were verified in clinical specimens, animal models, and DR cell models using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), quantitative real-time PCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). The binding ability of BAI to the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) was verified by molecular docking technology analysis and Western blot. Moreover, the direct binding relationship between PXDN and FN1 was verified by protein–protein interaction (PPI) and western blot. By further applying gene knockdown and overexpression techniques in combination with Western blot analysis, the molecular pathways through which BAI affects RAGE/PXDN(FN1)/PI3K/AKT signalling were investigated.ResultsIn the DR model, the expression level of PXDN significantly increased. Direct protein interactions between PXDN and FN1 were verified through PPI and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments. Moreover, knockdown of PXDN in vivo and in vitro significantly reduced the formation of lesions. BAI inhibited the expression of PXDN and FN1 by binding to RAGE, thereby effectively alleviating the senescence and pathological angiogenesis of HRMECs induced by high glucose (HG) conditions through regulation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway.ConclusionsThis study revealed that BAI regulates the progression of DR by regulating the RAGE/PXDN (FN1)/PI3K/AKT signalling pathway. These findings not only provide new molecular insights into the pathogenesis of DR but also lay an important theoretical foundation for the development of new therapeutic strategies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07590-0.Keywords: Baicalin, PXDN, FN1, Cell senescence, Diabetic retinopathy

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common and serious microvascular complication of diabetes and one of the leading causes of low vision and blindness worldwide [1, 2]. Epidemiological data show that the prevalence of DR in the global population aged 40 years and above is approximately 34.6%, and the annual incidence is approximately 2.2%-12.7% [3]. In terms of pathological processes, DR can be divided into nonproliferative (NPDR) and proliferative (PDR) types. NPDR is characterised by vascular endothelial cell loss, disruption of the blood–retinal barrier, and vascular leakage. With the progression of NPDR, retinal ischaemia and hypoxia are aggravated, pathological neovascularization is induced, and the disease enters the PDR stage [4, 5]. These fragile blood vessels are prone to inducing vitreous haemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment, resulting in visual impairment or complete blindness. The occurrence and progression of diabetes and its complications are jointly influenced by multiple factors [6]. Current clinical management of DR primarily involves laser treatment and antiangiogenic interventions, particularly for patients with PDR [7, 8]. However, existing therapies still have obvious limitations, as they neither provide preventive intervention at an early stage nor address the fundamental problems caused by retinal ischaemia. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more selective therapeutic strategies and targets to inhibit abnormal neovascularisation while promoting normal neovascularisation [9].

Cellular senescence, an irreversible state of growth arrest, plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of vascular diseases [10, 11]. In DR, senescent endothelial cells (SECs) accumulate under high glucose (HG) conditions and promote disease progression by influencing the formation and regression of abnormal vascular structures through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [12–15]. The proinflammatory microenvironment established by the SASP promotes senescence, disrupts vascular homeostasis, and directly contributes to the development of proliferative diabetic retinopathy [16–18]. Therefore, targeted clearance of SECs or inhibition of the SASP has emerged as an important strategy for the prevention and treatment of DR [19].

In contrast to current first-line DR therapies, exploring multitarget, low-toxicity natural products has emerged as a promising research direction. Baicalin (BAI), a flavonoid glycoside from Scutellaria baicalensis, exhibits favourable lipid solubility and diverse pharmacological activities [20, 21]. This compound has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiapoptotic effects and protects retinal capillary endothelial cells through the suppression of pathological angiogenesis [22–24]. Additionally, BAI safeguards retinal ganglion cells against glutamate-induced oxidative stress [25, 26]. Notably, BAI counteracts cellular senescence by enhancing SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial function in neurons [27]. Although BAI has the potential to treat diabetic complications and has antiangiogenic effects on the retina, its ability to modulate HG-induced senescence in human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMECs) and the underlying mechanisms connecting this effect to pathological angiogenesis suppression in DR require further investigation.

Peroxidasin (PXDN) is a member of the heme-containing peroxidase superfamily and is highly expressed in cardiovascular tissues [28, 29]. It has attracted significant attention because of its marked dysregulation of diabetic vascular complications. PXDN mutations are associated with various ocular pathologies, including microphthalmia, cataracts, and glaucoma [30]. This protein plays a central role in extracellular matrix homeostasis through type IV collagen cross-linking, with its aberrant expression linked to tissue fibrosis and vascular dysfunction [31, 32]. Notably, PXDN serves as a critical gene of cellular senescence, whose deficiency accelerates senescence in hepatic stellate cells [33]. Fibronectin (FN1), a ubiquitously distributed extracellular matrix (ECM) glycoprotein, is involved in wound healing, haemostasis, and thrombosis and modulates angiogenesis via direct interactions with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [34, 35]. Evidence from myocardial ageing models has demonstrated that senescent signalling significantly downregulates FN1 expression in fibroblasts, consequently attenuating fibrosis [36]. These findings collectively suggest that PXDN and FN1 are pivotal regulators not only in vascular dysfunction but also in cellular senescence. However, whether these genes function cooperatively as hub genes in DR to directly regulate HRMECs senescence and pathological angiogenesis through specific signalling mechanisms remains unclear.

The PI3K/AKT pathway, a critical signalling cascade that regulates cellular senescence, proliferation, and metabolism, is closely associated with the progression of DR when aberrantly activated. This pathway has been established as a key downstream effector of the Advanced Glycation End Product (AGE)-Receptor for AGE (RAGE) signalling axis [37], which is widely recognized as a central pathogenic mechanism in diabetes and its complications, particularly micro- and macrovascular complications. Evidence indicates that the expression of both PXDN and FN1 have been reported as key downstream effectors of the advanced glycation end products (AGEs) [38, 39]. These findings suggest that the PI3K/AKT pathway may serve as a critical bridge linking PXDN/FN1 to cellular senescence and angiogenesis.

This study aims to investigate the roles of BAI and the hub genes PXDN and FN1 in HG-induced HRMECs senescence and pathological angiogenesis, with the goal of delaying DR progression and providing new insights for the development of novel clinical treatments for DR.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HRMECs were maintained in vitro under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in endothelial cell-specific medium (ScienCell, USA). The cells were allocated to two experimental groups: the control group was incubated in medium supplemented with a physiological concentration of glucose (5.5 mM), and the experimental group was exposed to a high-glucose environment (30 mM) to simulate the chronic hyperglycaemic conditions of diabetes, as established in previous studies [40–42].

Human sample collection

This study was approved by the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A cohort of 60 participants was enrolled in this study, each providing informed consent. Vitreous fluid collection was performed under a fully closed perfusion system. The catheter cap of the vitreous cutter was detached and connected to a 5 ml syringe, and gentle aspiration was performed via a 23-gauge minimally invasive vitrectomy setup. Following aspiration, the collected fluid was immediately processed at a freezing station, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at − 80 °C. After unsuitable samples were excluded and statistical adjustments were made, 25 matched sample pairs were included for further experimental procedures [42].

Cell transfection and infection

PXDN and FN1 siRNAs were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, China) (Additional file 1: Table S1). HRMECs were transfected using jetPRIME transfection reagent. Before transfection, the cells were plated and incubated until the appropriate confluence was achieved. The culture medium was then exchanged for a medium devoid of serum and antibiotics. A transfection complex comprising jetPRIME reagent and siRNA was prepared and applied to the cells. Following 6–8 h of incubation, the medium was aspirated and replaced with complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The transfected cells were cultured for 48 h before collection for downstream analyses.

The PXDN overexpression adenovirus was purchased from WZ Biosciences (Shandong, China) (Additional file 1: Table S2). Adenovirus infection was performed when the cells were in the logarithmic growth phase at a density of 50%-70%. On the basis of the viral titre, an appropriate multiplicity of infection (MOI) range was determined through preliminary experiments. Before infection, the virus was thawed on ice, and fresh medium containing the diluted virus was added to the wells at half of the final volume. After 4 h of infection, the remaining half of the medium was added. The virus-containing medium was aspirated 10–16 h post-infection and replaced with regular complete medium. The cells were then further cultured for 24–48 h before being collected for subsequent experiments.

Construction of mouse models

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the ARVO statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. A DR mouse model was generated by the administration of a high-fat diet (HFD) in combination with low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) delivered via intraperitoneal injection to mimic the metabolic conditions associated with DR. Male C57BL/6J mice, four weeks old and weighing 20 ± 2 g, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Centre of Harbin Medical University. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the same institution. The animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions, with the ambient temperature controlled at 23 ± 2 °C, the humidity at 50 ± 5%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. After one week of acclimation, the mice were transitioned to an HFD regimen. Four weeks later, following a 12-hour fast, the animals received an intraperitoneal injection of freshly prepared STZ solution (50 mg/kg) in 0.1 mol/l sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5). Blood glucose levels were measured randomly one week post-STZ injection, and levels exceeding 16.7 mmol/l were considered to indicate successful model induction. The mice in the model group continued on the HFD after hyperglycaemia was successfully established [43, 44].

Animal adeno-associated virus and drug injection

For intravitreal administration, anaesthesia was induced in mice via intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (Aladdin, USA) at a dosage of 0.1 ml per 10 g body weight. Pupillary dilation was achieved using 1% tropicamide eye drops. Under microscopic guidance, a 5 µl microsyringe equipped with a 33-gauge needle (Hamilton, USA) was introduced perpendicularly into the vitreous chamber, with the insertion site positioned approximately 1.5 mm posterior to the corneal limbus. A total of 1.5 µl of the corresponding AAV9 viral suspension (titre: 5.44 × 10¹³ vg/ml; WZ Biosciences Inc., Shandong, China) was injected slowly into the vitreous cavity. The needle was retained in situ for 30 s post-injection to minimize reflux. An antibiotic eye cream (such as erythromycin) was applied immediately after surgery to prevent infection. All the animals were housed until the predetermined study endpoint, after which they were euthanised by cervical dislocation under deep anaesthesia. The eyeballs were completely enucleated and promptly placed in a specialised eye fixation solution for subsequent experiments. Intraperitoneal injections of BAI and 740Y-P were administered, with additional components injected in volumes equivalent to 0.9% NS for each group.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tissue specimens and cultured cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The purified RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a commercial reverse transcription kit (Sevenbio, Beijing, China). qRT–PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland) for amplification and fluorescence-based quantification. The resulting expression data were analysed via the 2 − ΔΔCt method to determine relative gene expression levels. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 2: Table S3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)

The concentration of PXDN protein in human vitreous fluid samples was quantified via ELISA. All procedures were performed in strict compliance with the protocol provided by the commercial ELISA kit manufacturer (UpingBIO, Cat No. SYP-H2366).

Western blot

The experimental procedure for Western blotting was the same as that in a previous study [35]. Following rinsing with ice-cold PBS, tissue and cellular samples were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors to extract total protein. Equal quantities of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for one hour, followed by incubation with appropriate primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight (details of the antibodies used are included in Additional file 3: Table S4). The next day, the PVDF membrane was subjected to three washes with TBST buffer. The membrane was then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for two hours at room temperature. After three additional washes with TBST, the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Haematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining of retinas

Mouse retinal tissues were fixed with a FAS eye fixator (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 24 h at room temperature, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. All the sections were cut through the optic nerve head in the same direction to ensure sample comparability. After being embedded in paraffin and rehydrated, the retinal sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin and observed under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Retinal tissue sections were incubated at 60 °C for one hour and then subjected to xylene dewaxing for 30 min. Gradient ethanol solutions (95%, 80%, and 75%) were used for dehydration, with each concentration applied for one minute. The sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at 37 °C for 30 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidases. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min, after which the samples were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. After blocking with normal serum for 5 min at room temperature, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody against PXDN (ABclonal, Cat# A17929) at 4 °C overnight. The following day, a secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, Product No. SA5-10255) was applied, and the samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. A 30-minute incubation with a streptavidin-biotin-peroxidase complex was then performed. Colour development was performed using 3 ml of diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 7 min. Finally, the sections were counterstained with haematoxylin, mounted with neutral balsam, and imaged under a microscope. Three randomly selected fields per sample were captured for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Fluorescein fundus angiography (FFA)

Following anaesthesia, the murine pupils were fully dilated using mydriatic agents. Ocular surface moisture was maintained by the application of gatifloxacin eye gel (China). Subsequently, sodium fluorescein (Beijing, China) was administered via intraperitoneal injection, and the retinal vasculature was examined using a digital fundus camera (Canon, Japan). The presence of indistinct vascular margins and diffuse fluorescent signals indicated fluorescein leakage.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

After the cells were washed, lysis was performed using cell lysis buffer (P0013; Beyotime, China). The cells were scraped with a cell scraper and subjected to continuous lysis at 4 °C for 1 h. Following complete lysis, the samples were ultrasonicated and centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Primary antibodies were added to the protein lysates, which were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MCE, USA) were introduced into each coincubation system, followed by incubation with rotation at 4 °C. The bead–antibody complexes were then collected. The beads were subsequently washed three times with ice-cold wash buffer. Finally, the beads were eluted by adding 2×SDS loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Since the optimal concentration of BAI for stimulating HRMECs remains unclear, CCK-8 assays were used to screen and determine the appropriate treatment concentrations. BAI was administered at various concentrations, and subsequent experimental concentrations were selected on the basis of the detection results. After the cells were counted, the cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 0.5–1 × 10⁴ cells per well, with control, experimental, and negative control groups. Following treatment with the corresponding concentrations of BAI, CCK-8 reagent (Sevenbio, Beijing, China) was added to each well. The absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability analysis was performed to determine the optimal working concentration of BAI.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and cultured for 24 h until they reached 90% confluence. A straight scratch was generated on the monolayer using a 200 µl pipette tip, followed by washing with PBS to remove dislodged cells and debris. The cells were subsequently maintained in serum-free medium for 24 h. Wound closure was monitored and imaged at 0 h and 24 h post-scratching under a microscope. The width of the wounds was quantified using ImageJ software to assess cell migration ability.

Transwell assay

After 24 h of starvation treatment, the cells were digested and resuspended in serum-free medium. Next, 100 µl of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell, and 500 µl of complete medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum was added to the lower chamber. After being cultured in an incubator at 37 °C for 48 h, the Transwell chamber was removed, the culture medium was discarded, the cells were washed three times with PBS buffer, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with crystal violet for 30 min, washed three times with PBS buffer again, and observed and photographed under a microscope.

5-Ethynyl-2’ deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

A total of 1.25 × 106 cells were seeded in six-well plates. After the culture conditions were completed, the cells were subjected to EdU staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EdU reagent (10 µM; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After the cells were stained with azide 555 (red fluorescence) and Hoechst 33,342 (blue fluorescence), a fluorescence assay was performed, and the cell proliferation rate was calculated as the ratio of EdU-positive cells (red fluorescence) to Hoechst 33,342-positive cells (blue fluorescence).

Senescence β-galactosidase staining (SA-β-gal)

The activity of SA-β-gal, a biomarker of cellular senescence, was assessed with a commercial histochemical staining kit (C0602; Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. HRMECs exhibiting positive staining were identified by blue precipitate under light microscopic observation (Vert. A1, Zeiss, Germany).

Tube formation assay

A volume of 50 µl of Matrigel (Corning, USA) was added to each well of a 96-well plate and allowed to polymerize by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁵ cells were seeded into each coated well, and the plate was returned to the 37 °C incubator. Cellular morphology and tube formation were monitored under a microscope, with images captured after 4–6 h of culture.

Molecular Docking

Drugs and key target protein structures were derived from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org). The protein structure was prepared by removing water molecules and ligands using PyMol. Molecular docking was then performed with AutoDock Vina, and the resulting complexes were visualized using PyMol for structural analysis.

Bioinformatic analysis

Microarray data preprocessing

We downloaded the GSE94019 profile from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), which is based on the GPL11154 platform of the Affymetrix Rat Genome 230 2.0 Array. Download of GSE94019-related data and microarray data preprocessing were performed as follows: Data were converted to expression, followed by background correction, quantile normalization, log2 transformation, batch correction, and probe summarization. Finally, the gene expression matrix file was constructed.

Data processing and standardization

We downloaded GEL format files (GEO data format). We used ActivePerl 5.24.2 to process matrix files of the GSE94019 series to convert gene probe IDs into expression matrix data annotated with official gene symbols. After excluding samples with missing gene symbols, the average expression of gene symbols in all samples was calculated. Use of Oligo software (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/oligo.html) by Bioconductor (v1.41.1) in R language (v3.4.1) processed sequencing data, MAS background correction, quantile normalization, and expression level calculation.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted with the GEO2R web tool (accessible at http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r), employing the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for multiple testing correction to compute the false discovery rate (FDR) from original p-values. Significant DEGs (|log2FC|>1.0 and FDR < 0.05) were identified by applying thresholds for both the absolute log2 fold change and the false discovery rate. Graphical representations of these DEGs, including heatmaps and volcano plots, were subsequently generated utilizing the R statistical computing environment.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis is commonly employed in large-scale genomic studies to categorize gene functions across three domains: cellular components (CC), biological processes (BP), and molecular functions (MF). Concurrently, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to elucidate signaling and metabolic pathways associated with DEGs. Together, GO and KEGG analyses facilitate the assessment of the biological relevance of DEGs. The DAVID database (Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery) offers a suite of tools for systematic functional annotation, enabling the identification of statistically significant pathways linked to DEGs through p-value computation.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Network Analysis

The STRING database (Search Tool for Interacting Genes, https://cn.string-db.org/) is an online database containing confirmed and predicted PPI. DEGs were integrated into the PPI network, with a high confidence interval (CI) threshold set at 0.700 to define statistically significant interactions. The importance of nodes within this preconstructed PPI network was subsequently evaluated using CytoHubba, a Cytoscape plugin, which uses multiple topological algorithms to rank nodes according to their network centrality.

Identification of hub genes

The top 10 hub genes were identified from multiple candidate genes using the Cytohubba plugin, which ranks genes according to their maximal clique centrality (MCC) scores. The “degree” metric within the Cytohubba algorithm was applied to prioritize these hub genes within the interaction network. Genes exhibiting higher degree values are considered more central to the network and are thus more likely to function as critical hubs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8 software. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normality of the data distribution. Unpaired Student’s t tests were used when the variances were equal for comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data. In contrast, Welch’s t test was used in cases of variance heterogeneity, as determined by Levene’s test. For multigroup comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for nonnormally distributed data. Post hoc analyses included the Bonferroni correction after ANOVA and Dunn’s test following the Kruskal–Wallis test. All the experiments were repeated at least three times using different tissue samples and different batches of cells. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Hub gene screening and PXDN/FN1 PPI verification

To identify the core genes involved in diabetic retinopathy, we retrieved the transcriptomic expression profiles from the GSE94019 dataset (platform GPL11154) in the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), which includes retinal tissue samples from healthy controls and patients with PDR. Through bioinformatics analysis of the GSE94019 dataset, 7,115 DEGs were identified, including 4,929 upregulated genes and 2,186 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A, B). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs are involved primarily in cell–matrix junctions, structural constituents of the ECM, and the AGE–RAGE signalling pathway in DR (Fig. 1C). The PPI network showed genes with protein–protein interactions, along with the top 10 genes ranked by the MCC score (Fig. 1D, E).Given that hyperglycaemia-driven cellular senescence is a key mechanism of vascular dysfunction in DR, we aimed to identify novel hub genes in this pathway [45]. Among the top 10 genes ranked by the MCC score, we prioritized PXDN and FN1 on the basis of the literature analysis. Protein–protein docking analysis supported this hypothesis by predicting specific binding interfaces between PXDN and FN1 (Fig. 1F). This prediction was subsequently empirically validated through Co-IP experiments (Fig. 1G, H).

The expression of PXDN significantly increases under HG/DR conditions

After confirming the interaction between PXDN and FN1, we further investigated the potential role of these genes in DR by systematically evaluating their expression patterns across clinical specimens, animal models, and cellular experiments. At the clinical level, ELISA analysis of vitreous fluid from DR patients revealed significantly upregulated PXDN expression (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis of retinal tissues from DR model mice revealed markedly increased protein levels of both PXDN and FN1 (Fig. 2B-E), which was further supported by the results of IHC staining, which revealed increased PXDN expression intensity in DR mouse retinas (Fig. 2F). At the cellular level, experiments using HG-treated HRMECs revealed significant increases in both PXDN mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 2G-I) and substantially increased FN1 protein levels (Fig. 2J, K). Above, the in vitro/vivo results show that PXDN and FN1 proteins expression levels increased significantly, suggesting that both may play important roles in the pathogenesis and progression of DR.

BAI binding to RAGE inhibits the expression of PXDN under HG conditions

Through the docking simulation of small molecule BAI-protein RAGE, we found that BAI binds to the ligand-binding domain of RAGE by forming multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, with a binding energy of -7.3 kcal/mol, indicating strong binding affinity and a stable binding conformation (Fig. 3A). We further validated this interaction through in vivo and in vitro experiments. Western blot analysis revealed that BAI effectively reduced RAGE protein expression levels in a DR mouse model (Fig. 3B) and under HG stimulation (Fig. 3C). These results corroborated the molecular docking findings and strongly suggested that BAI is a potential RAGE inhibitor.

After confirming the interaction between BAI and RAGE, we further investigated the effects of BAI on downstream signalling pathways and cellular functions. First, a CCK-8 assay was conducted to determine the optimal treatment concentration, and 50 µM BAI was selected for all subsequent functional studies (Fig. 3D). BAI treatment significantly reduced the protein expression levels of the downstream molecules PXDN and FN1 (Fig. 3E-G). Concurrently, BAI exhibited multifaceted protective effects. In terms of cellular senescence, BAI treatment markedly downregulated the expression of the senescence-associated proteins p21 and p53 (Fig. 3H) and significantly reduced the number of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 3I, J), indicating effective mitigation of HG-induced senescence in HRMECs. BAI treatment led to a significant decrease in the percentage of EdU-positive cells (Fig. 3K, L), suggesting the suppression of HRMEC proliferation. Furthermore, BAI impaired cell migration, as evidenced by the downregulation of MMP2 and MMP9 expression (Fig. 3M, N), delayed scratch wound closure (Fig. 3O, P), and reduced numbers of migrating cells in Transwell assays (Fig. 3Q, R). In terms of angiogenic capacity, BAI downregulated the expression of the key angiogenic markers CD31 and VEGFA (Fig. 3S, T) and significantly inhibited the tube-forming ability of HRMECs (Fig. 3U-W), demonstrating a potent suppressive effect on pathological angiogenesis under HG conditions. Mechanistic studies revealed that BAI treatment significantly decreased the protein levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT (Fig. 3X, Y).These results collectively suggest that BAI likely binds to RAGE, subsequently regulating PXDN/FN1 expression and the activity of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, thereby mitigating senescence, migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis in HRMECs under HG stimulation.

PXDN may affect senescence and angiogenesis in HRMECs through the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway in vitro

We evaluated the knockdown efficiency of different siRNAs targeting PXDN by western blot and found that siRNA #4 had the highest knockdown efficiency (Fig. 4A). Based on this, siRNA #4 was selected for subsequent experiments. Knockdown of PXDN significantly decreased the expression of PXDN and FN1 under HG conditions (Fig. 4B, C). Western blot analysis revealed that PXDN knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of key senescence-associated proteins, p21 and p53, during cellular senescence. BAI regulates the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway through PXDN to affect senescence and angiogenesis in vitro

To determine whether BAI mitigates HG-induced senescence and angiogenesis via the PXDN/PI3K/AKT pathway, we first assessed the effects of BAI on key functional and signalling molecules. We found that BAI significantly downregulated the expression of senescence-related proteins (p21 and p53), migration-associated metalloproteinases (MMP2 and MMP9), and angiogenesis markers (CD31 and VEGFA) (Fig. 5A–F) and concurrently suppressed the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT (Fig. 5G, H). To functionally validate the centrality of this pathway, we used the PI3K-specific agonist 740Y-P in combination with BAI. The addition of 740Y-P largely reversed the inhibitory effects of BAI on p-PI3K and p-AKT (Fig. 5G, H) and restored the expression levels of p21, p53, MMP2, MMP9, CD31, and VEGFA (Fig. 5A–F). These results suggest that BAI regulates key downstream effector molecules by inhibiting the PXDN/PI3K/AKT signalling axis, ultimately ameliorating the senescence, angiogenesis, migration, and proliferation of HRMECs in DR.

PXDN and FN1 affect senescence and angiogenesis by regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway in vitro

To determine whether PXDN and FN1 affect cellular senescence and angiogenesis by regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway, we conducted a series of experiments. In terms of cellular senescence, PXDN overexpression induced a senescent phenotype, as evidenced by increased numbers of SA-β-gal-positive cells and increased p53 and p21 protein levels, whereas FN1 knockdown or BAI treatment effectively reversed this process (Fig. 7A–C). Furthermore, with respect to cell proliferation and migration, PXDN overexpression significantly increased these abilities, as consistently confirmed by EdU, scratch wound, and Transwell assays, and was associated with changes in MMP2 and MMP9 protein levels. In contrast, FN1 knockdown and BAI treatment had opposite inhibitory effects (Fig. 7D–J). Finally, PXDN overexpression markedly increased tube formation and the expression of the key markers CD31 and VEGFA, whereas FN1 knockdown and BAI treatment similarly significantly inhibited these processes (Fig. 7K–O). Crucially, all the protective effects mediated by BAI could be reversed by the PI3K agonist 740Y-P, strongly suggesting that the actions of PXDN/FN1 depend on downstream signalling.

To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we further confirmed that the PI3K/AKT pathway serves as the common molecular hub mediating the aforementioned phenotypes. Western blot analysis revealed that both FN1 knockdown and BAI treatment significantly suppressed PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, whereas PXDN overexpression activated this pathway. Similarly, the inhibitory effect of BAI on the pathway activation induced by PXDN overexpression was reversed by 740Y-P (Fig. 7P, Q).

BAI affects cellular senescence and angiogenesis by regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway via PXDN in vivo

To investigate whether BAI treats DR through the PXDN/PI3K/AKT axis, we used AAV-mediated PXDN knockdown in a DR mouse model and found that it significantly alleviated DR pathology, an effect further enhanced by BAI treatment.

At the molecular level, Western blot results revealed that PXDN knockdown significantly reduced p21, p53, MMP2, MMP9, CD31, and VEGFA protein expression levels. BAI treatment further reduced the expression levels of these genes, and 740Y-P completely reversed the inhibitory effects of the combination therapy (Fig. 8A-C). Additionally, PXDN knockdown significantly inhibited the protein expression of p-PI3K and p-AKT. BAI treatment further reduced the expression levels of these genes, and 740Y-P completely reversed the inhibitory effects of the combination therapy (Fig. 8D). These in vivo results indicate that PXDN knockdown effectively alleviates DR progression by inhibiting the activity of the PI3K/AKT pathway, which manifests as reduced cellular senescence and angiogenesis. BAI enhanced this protective effect, whereas 740Y-P completely counteracted it. In terms of pathological manifestations, FFA revealed significant microvascular leakage in the fundus of DR mice, which markedly improved following intravitreal injection of AAV-shPXDN and intraperitoneal injection of BAI (Fig. 8E). In the DR mouse model, H&E staining revealed vascular proliferation in the inner nuclear layer, disorganized cell arrangement in the outer nuclear layer, and sparse retinal ganglion cells with a morphological absence or blurring in the model group. These pathological changes were significantly ameliorated after intravitreal injection of AAV-shPXDN or intraperitoneal injection of BAI (Fig. 8F).

Discussion

In this study, the transcriptome data of the GEO database were first integrated for bioinformatics analysis, and finally, PXDN and FN1, two core hub genes that can link HG stress and cell senescence, were screened out. Co-IP assays were performed to elucidate the functional interaction between PXDN and FN1, which verified a direct PPI and established a molecular basis for further mechanistic investigation. The protein expression levels of PXDN and FN1 are significantly increased in the vitreous of DR patients, DR animal models, and high-glucose-treated HRMECs, suggesting that these proteins are closely related to the occurrence and development of DR. The knockout of PXDN effectively alleviated cell senescence, inhibited abnormal microvascular formation, and significantly alleviated retinal tissue damage and vascular leakage in mice in vivo. Subsequent investigations revealed that BAI potentially acts as a ligand capable of binding to the membrane receptor RAGE. Treatment with BAI suppressed the protein expression of both PXDN and FN1, thereby attenuating cellular senescence and pathological angiogenesis. Furthermore, mechanistic analyses revealed that PXDN and FN1 modulate the senescence of HRMECs via the PI3K/AKT pathway, contributing to the regulation of aberrant retinal microvascular formation and ultimately inhibiting the progression of DR.

This study explored on the therapeutic potential and mechanistic basis of BAI, a natural flavonoid compound derived from the botanical compound Scutellaria baicalensis. BAI exhibits various pharmacological properties, including anti-apoptotic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities, underscoring its promise as a candidate for medicinal applications [24]. In recent years, its role in delaying cell senescence has attracted increasing attention, and it has shown good anti-aging potential in various models [46, 47]. For example, in diabetic nephropathy, BAI has been shown to reduce renal fibrosis and oxidative stress and alleviate the ageing process [48]. Combined analysis of the microbiome and metabolomics revealed a protective effect of BAI on ageing-related alveolar bone inflammatory bone resorption. Additionally, BAI protects against UVA-induced senescence in human fibroblasts by mitigating oxidative stress and modulating the expression of key senescence-associated genes such as p53 and p16 [49, 50].The anti-aging effect of BAI in the present study is consistent with the anti-aging effect of BAI in these diseases.

Cellular senescence and abnormal angiogenesis are not isolated events in DR but constitute a mutually reinforcing vicious cycle. On the one hand, senescent HRMECs destroy the homeostasis of the blood–retinal barrier and drive pathological neovascularization through the secretion of SASP components. On the other hand, abnormally generated neovascularization is not functional enough to effectively perfuse tissue but instead aggravates retinal ischaemia and hypoxia. Continuous metabolic stress further induces and accelerates the senescence of more HRMECs [9, 51]. BAI concurrently suppresses both processes, indicating its potential to disrupt this vicious cycle and exert multifaceted protective effects. Recent studies have demonstrated that BAI inhibits pathological angiogenesis and effectively attenuates laser-induced choroidal neovascularization through blocking VEGFR2/ERK signalling. Moreover, it alleviates inflammatory responses and oxidative stress by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby protecting retinal capillary endothelial cells [32]. Our study revealed that BAI, a natural compound, could effectively alleviate cellular senescence and reduce abnormal angiogenesis by inhibiting the expression of PXDN/FN1 and the phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT.

RAGE, a cell surface receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily, acts as a signal transduction receptor for AGEs [52–55]. There is increasing evidence that the binding of RAGE to AGEs induces oxidative stress, inflammation, and thrombosis in various cells and is involved in the occurrence and development of the vascular complications of diabetes [56]. This study revealed that BAI functions as a ligand capable of directly binding to RAGE in DR, thereby conferring protective effects through RAGE targeting. Mechanistically, BAI suppresses the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, thereby attenuating cellular senescence and pathological angiogenesis. Concurrently, it significantly downregulated the expression of the interacting proteins PXDN and FN1. These findings suggest that BAI may serve as a natural RAGE antagonist, potentially interrupting the initiation of HG-mediated signalling cascades. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing the protective effect of RAGE inhibitors in DR [57].

The hub genes PXDN and FN1 play important roles in various diseases. PXDN can promote AGES-induced endothelial dysfunction in diabetes by attenuating eNOS phosphorylation at Ser1177 through the NOX2/HOCl/Akt pathway [39]. Moreover, serum response factors activate peroxisome transcription to block hepatic stellate cell senescence [33]. In addition, PXDN can alleviate adriamycin-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and myocardial senescence [58]. FN1 is a key molecular mediator and can effectively block VEGF-induced angiogenesis in vitro [59]. Our study revealed that the hub genes PXDN and FN1, which are key molecules in DR, affect the senescence and pathological angiogenesis of HRMECs.

In the process of the occurrence and development of DR, chronic metabolic abnormalities, namely hyperglycaemia, will continue to produce toxic effects on retinal tissue through the vascular system, eventually leading to neurovascular unit damage [60–62]. During this pathological progression, endothelial cells within a high-glucose milieu undergo metabolic reprogramming, marked predominantly by dysregulation of glucose metabolism pathways. Such alterations are strongly associated with the perturbation of multiple signalling cascades. A key mechanism underlying this dysregulation involves the accumulation of AGEs and the subsequent activation of their receptor, RAGE. The engagement of AGEs with RAGE promotes inflammatory responses and facilitates the development of SASP through activation of the downstream PI3K/AKT signalling axis [63, 64], which establishes a positive feedback loop that promotes chronic inflammation and tissue damage [65, 66]. Its dysfunction has been confirmed to be the core link of diabetic microangiopathy [67, 68]. In the present study, we found that knockdown of PXDN or FN1 significantly inhibited the phosphorylation level of the PI3K/AKT pathway and simultaneously downregulated the expression of cell senescence markers, SASP-related angiogenic factors, and cell migration-related proteins. These results suggest that PXDN and FN1 regulate HG-induced retinal microvascular endothelial cell dysfunction by activating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway.

This study has several limitations. First of all, although the binding of BAI to RAGE has been confirmed, the specific binding site of BAI in vivo and the precise effect of binding on RAGE conformation and signalling still need to be elucidated by point mutagenesis and other techniques. Secondly, the specific domain of PXDN that interacts with FN1 and the precise mechanism underlying its regulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway need to be further studied. In addition, validating the efficacy and safety of BAI in large animal models will be an important step in promoting its clinical translation.

By exploring the molecular mechanism of HRMECs in DR, this study aimed to explore the transformation of the phenotype of HRMECs by targeting intracellular and extracellular pathways. We constructed a docking model of BAI and RAGE and further elucidated the interaction between BAI and RAGE through experimental verification. To verify the interaction between PXDN and FN1, a Co-IP experiment was used to dock with macromolecular proteins, and the model confirmed the binding relationship between them, suggesting the possibility of direct interaction between PXDN and FN1. Therefore, this study suggests that BAI interacts with RAGE to mediate the interaction between PXDN and FN1 to regulate cell senescence and angiogenesis in DR through the PI3K/AKT pathway, which protects retinal microvascular endothelial cells and alleviates the progression of DR. This study deepens the understanding of the pathogenesis of DR and provides a solid theoretical basis and experimental support for the development of novel DR treatment strategies targeting PXDN/FN1 and using BAI as the lead compound.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that BAI and RAGE mediate the interaction of proteins PXDN and FN1 to regulate the occurrence and progression of DR through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. The key genes PXDN/FN1 and small molecule compound BAI, which control HRMEC senescence and angiogenesis, were investigated, and the related mechanisms underlying HRMEC senescence and angiogenesis in DR were further elucidated, which has important theoretical significance.These findings highlight the potential of BAI as a therapeutic candidate for targeting the RAGE/PXDN/FN1 axis in the treatment of DR.
