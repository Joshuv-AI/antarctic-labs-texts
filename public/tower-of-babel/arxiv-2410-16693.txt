Learning shallow quantum circuits with many-qubit gates
Francisca Vasconcelos, Hsin-Yuan Huang

Abstract
The seminal work of [LMN93] established a cornerstone result for classical complexity, with profound implications for learning theory. By proving low-degree Fourier concentration of 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} , the work demonstrated that Boolean functions computed by constant-depth circuits can be efficiently PAC-learned via low-degree Fourier sampling. This breakthrough provided the first sample- and time-efficient (quasi-polynomial) algorithm for learning 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} . Proposed by [Moore99] as a natural quantum analog of 𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{AC}^{0} , 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} is the class of constant-depth quantum circuits composed of arbitrary single-qubit gates and polynomial C ​ Z CZ gates of unbounded width. In this work, we present the first algorithm for efficient average-case learning of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits with logarithmic ancilla. Namely, our algorithm achieves quasi-polynomial sample- and time-complexity for learning unknown 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} unitaries to inverse-polynomially small error. We further show that these learned unitaries can be efficiently synthesized via poly-logarithmic depth circuits, making progress towards proper learning of 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} . Since in finite-dimensional circuit geometries 𝖰𝖠𝖢 0 \mathsf{QAC}^{0} circuits require polynomial depth to implement, this result significantly expands the family of efficiently learnable quantum circuits.

Efficient Learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-Evolved Observables.

Although efficient,

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}=\sum_{|Q|\leq\ell}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\cdot Q

Efficient Sewing of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-Evolved Observables.

This procedure is used to efficiently learn all
3n

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heinsenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables.
HLB+ (24)
’s light-cone sewing procedure (with a modified projection operator) is then used to sew all
3n
learned observables
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
into approximate unitary
C_{\text{sew}}
, close to
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
in average-case distance.

Lemma 4

(Sewing error bound)
.

Suppose
C
is an
n
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, which has a set of Heisenberg-evolved observables
\left\{O_{P_{i}}\right\}_{i,P}
, corresponding to each of the
3n
single-qubit Paulis
P_{i}
. Let
\left\{\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}\right\}_{i,P}
denote the set of learned observables, which are at most
\epsilon_{P_{i}}
-far from the true observables. By “sewing” the learned observables, construct the unitary

\displaystyle C_{\text{sew}}\left(\{\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\}_{i,P}\right):=\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right],

(16)

where
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
is the projection onto the unitary minimizing the Frobenius norm distance and
\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}
swaps the first and last
n
qubits. The average-case distance between
C_{\text{sew}}
and
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
is at most

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\frac{1}{2}\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\epsilon_{P_{i}}.

(17)

Since our result leverages average-case distance measures, we had to re-prove most of the learning guarantees of
HLB+ (24)
, which were only proven for worst-case distance measures. Furthermore, to ensure that
C_{\text{sew}}
is unitary, the average-case version of the sewing procedure leverages the
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
projection onto the unitary minimizing the Frobenius norm distance. Solving for this unitary is the solution to the well-established Orthogonal Procrustes Problem, which requires computing a singular value decomposition (SVD) and setting all singular values to one. In general, computing the SVD of a
2n
-qubit unitary requires exponential time. However, we imposed that the learned observables
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}
are supported on only
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits. This implies that each matrix,
\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}

\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
support. Since we only need to compute the SVD of the non-trivial subsystem within the support, the computational complexity of the sewing procedure is only quasi-polynomial, i.e.
\mathcal{O}(2^{\textnormal{poly}\log n})
. This is the central reason that we impose that the learned observables are not only low-degree, but also low-support.

By plugging our
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
error bound for
\epsilon_{P_{i}}
into
Lemma

4
, as well as summing the sample and time complexities of the learning and sewing procedures, we can guarantee that our algorithm efficiently learns
n
-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
unitaries, thereby proving
Theorem

1
. For a more detailed description of these sewing results and proofs, we refer the readers to
Section

D.2

Learning
\mathsf{QAC}
Circuits with Improved Depth.

With our efficient procedure for learning the unitary corresponding to an
n
-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, one could ask whether we could learn a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit synthesizing this unitary, also known as “proper learning” of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. In this work, while we do not achieve a proper learning algorithm for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, we do make progress towards one. A naive attempt at implementing the learned
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
unitary would require a
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit of quasi-polynomial depth. However, in
Proposition

1
we establish that the circuit depth can be reduced to poly-logarithmic.

Begin by noticing that the learned
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
unitary
C_{\text{sew}}
, as given in
Equation

16
, sews the learned unitaries
W_{i}=\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)

via SWAP gate operations, which are implementable in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. To achieve a poly-logarithmic depth
\mathsf{QAC}
implementation of
C_{\text{sew}}
, we prove the following two key results: 1) each unitary
W_{i}
can be implemented to high accuracy via a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit and 2) there exists a sewing order with parallelization such that all
n
of the
W_{i}
unitaries can be implemented and sewed in worst-case poly-logarithmic depth.

To prove the first result, we decompose the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit structure into two key parts: the
CZ_{k}
gates and the arbitrary single-qubit rotation gates. Since
W_{i}
is supported on only
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits, a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit operating on
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits has at most quasi-polynomial configurations of
CZ_{k}
gates with
2\leq k\leq\mathcal{O}(\log n)
. For each such configuration, we show that it is possible to construct a polynomial sized
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-net over the polynomial number of arbitrary single-qubit rotation gates. Therefore the
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-net over all such
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit architectures has quasi-polynomial size and a simple brute-force search can be leveraged to find the circuit closest to
W_{i}
.

To prove the second result, demonstrating that the learned
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits can be sewn into a poly-logarithmic depth
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit, we leverage a graph-coloring argument similar to
(
HLB+, 24
, Lemma 13)
. Note that the poly-logarithmic depth arises due to the poly-logarithmic support of the
W_{i}
unitaries, resulting in a poly-logarithmic degree and, thus, worst-case coloring of the graph. For more details about this unitary synthesis procedure, we refer the reader to
Section

D.3
.

Concentration and Learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with Ancilla.

In
Appendix

E

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

, we only consider circuits where the ancillas are initialized to the

\ket{0^a}
by the end of the computation). This implies that the action of

on the
(n+a)
-qubit system is equivalent to the action of another unitary

on just the
n

C(I\otimes\ket{0^a})=A\otimes\ket{0^a}
. Thus, we define the Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observables of this system “without ancilla restriction” as
O_{P_{i},n+a}=C(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})C^{\dagger}

and “with ancilla restriction” as
O_{P_{i},n}=(I\otimes\bra{0^a})\cdot O_{P_{i},n+a}\cdot(I\otimes\ket{0^a})=AP_{i}A^{\dagger}

and

are related by a

Lemma 5

(Effect of ancilla)

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{n}
be a subset of the set of
n

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2^{a}\cdot\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n+a}].

(18)

We also show that the distance between
O_{P_{i},n}
and
O_{P_{i},n}^{*}
(the circuit with large
CZ

O_{P_{i},n+a}
and
O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*}
, but that a
2^{a}
multiplicative blow-up factor also arises relative to the distance in the ancilla-free case.

Lemma 6

(Large
CZ_{k}
removal error with ancillas)
.

The average-case distance between observables of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C
and
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
\widetilde{C}
(
C
with all
m

CZ_{k}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|O_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|O_{P_{i},n+a}-O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*}\|^{2}_{F}\leq 2^{a}\cdot\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}.

Therefore, by using

to learn the

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+2^{a}\cdot\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}

5
Discussion and Future Work

There are several interesting directions for future work that would strengthen our understanding of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
and improve the applicability of this learning procedure.

Ancilla.

Although our learning procedure handles logarithmic ancilla, many quantum algorithms require far more ancilla to, e.g., perform block-encodings and error correction. We proved that our algorithm works for polynomial ancilla under
Conjecture

1
, but proving this conjecture remains an important and challenging open problem. Concurrent work by
ADOY (24)
proved that
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
projectors are well-approximated by low-degree projectors according to the spectral norm, with guarantees for slightly superlinear ancilla, but it is not clear if this result extends to our setting.
Furthermore, in this work, we prove algorithmic guarantees for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits that perform clean computation on their ancillas. However,
NPVY (24)
also demonstrated that their low-degree
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
concentration results hold for circuits which do not perform clean computation and even for circuits with dirty ancilla qubits. Therefore, it could be interesting to explore the feasbility of our learning algorithm in these different ancilla settings.

Proper Learning.

While our unitary synthesis procedure guarantees a poly-logarithmic depth circuit implementation, we believe that proper learning of the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit should be possible. Our current circuit synthesis approach is via brute-force search and an
\varepsilon
-net, which we believe leaves room for improvement:

Open Problem 1

(Quasi-polynomial proper learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
)
.

Given the learned unitary
C_{\text{sew}}
, which is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C
, does there exist a quasi-polynomial time algorithm to learn a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit implementing unitary
C_{\text{sew}}^{*}
such that

\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq 1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
?

Although it is widely believed that efficient
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
proper learners do not exist,
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
and
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with sublinear ancilla are fundamentally incomparable
7
7

7

To see why:
ADOY (24)
showed that Parity
\notin
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
[sublinear]. Since Parity
=
Fanout quantumly, so Fanout
\notin

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
[sublinear]. However, Fanout
\in
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
by definition.
.
Thus, efficient proper learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with sublinear ancilla would not contradict the classical belief.

Time Complexity.

Classically,
Kha (93)
showed that
LMN (93)
’s quasi-polynomial complexity for learning
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
is optimal, under the standard classical cryptographic hardness-of-factoring assumption. Since factoring is not quantumly hard,
AGS (21)
established a quasi-polynomial time lower-bound for
quantum
learning of
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
via a reduction to Ring Learning with Errors (
\mathsf{RLWE}
). Although there are fundamental differences between
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
and
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, we believe that it should be possible (potentially through similar crypotgraphic reductions) to prove a quasi-polynomial lower-bound for learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. This would imply optimality of our algorithm.

Sample Complexity.

Our learning algorithm’s core subroutine can be interpreted as learning a junta approximation of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observables. To this end, there is a growing literature of sophisticated algorithms for quantum junta learning
(
CNY, 23
;
BY, 23
;
Gut, 24
)
.
While these results do not directly apply to learning the entire
n
-qubit unitary of a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, they open up intriguing possibilities for improving our algorithm’s sample complexity with respect to parameters such as circuit size.

Beyond
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
.

Finally, our work contributes to the burgeoning field of research seeking to identify provably trainable and learnable quantum circuit families.
Our results extend
HLB+ (24)
’s efficient learnability of
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
to the broader class of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. This advance raises intriguing questions for future exploration.
For instance, since
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
requires linear depth to implement in 1D geometry, can we efficiently learn other polynomial-depth circuit families in the 1D geometry? Furthermore, since
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
\subseteq

\mathsf{QNC}^{1}
\subseteq\mathsf{QNC}
, a natural progression would be to investigate efficient learnability of polylog-depth
\mathsf{QNC}
circuits or other superclasses of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. By uncovering rigorous algorithms for training and learning quantum circuits, we may enable automated design of quantum circuits, protocols, and algorithms, in addition to characterization of experimental quantum devices.

42
]
. To begin, a standard notion of distance for quantum states is the fidelity measure.

Definition 1

(Fidelity)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma
the fidelity of the states is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\Tr(\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\sigma\sqrt{\rho}})^{2}.

(23)

If one of the states is a pure state, e.g.
\sigma=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, then the fidelity expression reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}.

(24)

Note that for any input
(\rho,\sigma)
, the fidelity is bounded as
\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, the maximal value of 1 is obtained if and only if the states are identical, i.e.
\mathcal{F}(\rho,\rho)=1
.

For quantum channels, we consider the standard Haar distance measure, which is the same notion of average-case distance for comparing quantum channels as utilized in
[
33
, Definition 3]
.

Definition 2

(Average-Case Distance)
.

The average-case distance between two
n
-qubit CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})=\underset{\ket{\psi}\sim\textnormal{Haar}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[1-\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})\right)\right],

(25)

where
\ket{\psi}
is sampled from the Haar (uniform) measure and
\mathcal{F}
is the fidelity.

Intuitively, this notion of average-case distance measures how distinct the two channel outputs are, for pure input states averaged over the Haar (uniform) measure. In the case of unitary channels the Haar distance measure simplifies to the average gate fidelity measure. As such, we will often abuse notation for unitary channels and write
\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(U_{1},U_{2})
to mean
\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})
.

Fact 1

(Average Gate Fidelity -
[
42
]
)
.

For unitaries
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, with corresponding unitary channels
\mathcal{U}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}
, the average-case distance satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{n}}\left|\Tr(U_1^\dag U_2)\right|^{2}\right).

(26)

In this work we will also leverage properties of the well-established normalized Frobenius and global phase-invariant distance measures for unitaries.

Definition 3

(Normalized Frobenius Distance)
.

The normalized Frobenius distance between two
n
-qubit unitaries
U
and
V
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(U,V)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|U-V\|_{F}^{2}

(27)

Definition 4

(Global Phase-Invariant Distance)
.

The global phase-invariant distance between two
n
-qubit unitaries
U
and
V
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{P}(U,V)=\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\penalty\ \frac{1}{2^{n}}\|e^{i\phi}U-V\|_{F}^{2}

(28)

As distance measures, both satisfy the triangle inequality. Furthermore, from these definitions, it trivially follows that the normalized Frobenius distance is lower-bounded by the global phase-invariant distance.

Fact 2
.

For unitaries
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, the global phase-invariant distance is upper-bounded by the normalized Frobenius distance,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{P}(U_{1},U_{2})\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(U_{1},U_{2}).

(29)

[
33
]
also showed that this average-case distance can be upper-bounded by the Frobenius norm distance between the unitaries.

Fact 3
.

For unitaries
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, with corresponding unitary channels
\mathcal{U}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}
, the average-case distance is upper-bounded by the global phase-invariant distance,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2})\leq\mathcal{D}_{P}(U_{1},U_{2}).

(30)

A.3
Pauli Analysis

In this work, we will make frequent use of concepts from so-called “Pauli analysis” or “quantum Boolean functions”, as originally defined by
[
39
]
, which is a quantum analog of classical analysis of Boolean functions
[
44
]
.

Central to Pauli analysis are the four standard Pauli operators:

\displaystyle I:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(31)

Note that the identity matrix is often thought of as the “trivial” Pauli operator, as it has no effect on a quantum system. We will denote the set of single-qubit Pauli operators as
\mathcal{P}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{n}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
denote the set of all
n
-qubit Pauli strings.

In general, given an
n
-qubit Pauli string
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
, we use
Q_{i}
to refer to the
i^{\text{th}}
Pauli of Pauli string
Q
. Similarly, given a set of indices
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
,
Q_{\mathcal{S}}
refers to the Pauli sub-string

\displaystyle Q_{\mathcal{S}}=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}Q_{i}^{\delta\{i\in\mathcal{S}\}},

(32)

with the convention that
Q_{i}^{0}=I
. Note, however, that we will abuse notation and, in the case of single-qubit Pauli
P\in\mathcal{P}
use
P_{i}
to refer to the “single-qubit Pauli”

\displaystyle P_{i}:=I^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes P\otimes I^{\otimes(n-i)},

(33)

which applies
P
to the
i^{\text{th}}
qubit in an
n
-qubit system. We define the support of a Pauli string
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
to be the set of qubit indices upon which the Pauli acts non-trivially, that is

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp}(Q)=\{i\in[n]:Q_{i}\neq I\}.

(34)

The degree of a Pauli string
Q
is the size of its support,

\displaystyle|Q|=|\textnormal{supp}(Q)|.

(35)

Central to Pauli analysis is the observation that the set of Pauli strings
\mathcal{P}^{n}
forms an orthonormal basis for any
n
-qubit quantum unitary
U\in U(2^{n})
,

\displaystyle U=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\widehat{U}(P)\cdot P,\quad\text{ where }\widehat{U}(P)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\Tr(U^\dagger P).

(36)

Note that
\widehat{U}(P)
are typically referred to as the Pauli coefficients of
U
.

For a subset of Pauli strings,
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{n}
, we define the unitary’s Pauli weight on that subset as

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[U]=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}|\widehat{U}(Q)|^{2}.

(37)

For notational convenience, we will use the notation
\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}}
to refer to the weight in subset
\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{P}^{n}\backslash\mathcal{S}
and the notation
\textbf{W}^{>k}
to refer to the weight of Paulis with degree greater than
k
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|>k\}
. Finally, note that Parseval’s formula holds for the Pauli decomposition.

Fact 4

(Parseval’s Formula)
.

For unitary
U\in U(2^{n})
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\|U\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}|\widehat{U}(P)|^{2}=\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}[U]=1

(38)

Finally, important to this work, we will distinguish between the support and degree of a unitary. The support of a unitary is the set of all qubits upon which the unitary acts non-trivially. Formally, for the
n
-qubit unitary
U
defined in
Equation

36
, its support is the union of the supports of all the Pauli strings
P
for which
\widehat{U}(P)\neq 0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp}(U)=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:\hat{U}(P)\neq 0}\{i\in[n]:P_{i}\neq I\}.

(39)

If
n
-qubit unitary has support
k
, where
k
is small relative to
n
, we refer to
U
as low-support. In analogy to the classical setting, this is similar to a
k
-junta. Meanwhile, the degree of a unitary is equal to the maximum degree of any Pauli string
P
for which
\widehat{U}(P)\neq 0
. Formally, for the
n
-qubit unitary
U
defined in
Equation

36
, its degree is

\displaystyle|U|=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:\hat{U}(P)\neq 0}|P|.

(40)

If
n
-qubit unitary has degree
k
, where
k
is small relative to
n
, we refer to
U
as low-degree. It is clear that any unitary with support
k
has degree
k
. However, a unitary with degree
k
could have, for example, non-zero Pauli coefficients on all Pauli strings of degree
k
and, thus, support
n
. Therefore, a unitary of degree
k
does not necessarily have support
k
. This implies that any low-support unitary is neccesarily a low-degree unitary, but any low-degree unitary is not neccesarily a low-support unitary. Note that this is analogous to how, in the classical setting, all
k
-juntas necessarily have degree-
k
, but a degree-
k
Boolean function is not necessarily a
k
-junta.

A.4
Classical Shadow Tomography

Central to our learning results will be the classical shadow tomography procedure of
[
31
]
, which enables efficient learning of
\Tr(O_i \rho)
for an arbitrary quantum state
\rho
and a set of observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i}
. In particular, we will leverage the improved sample-complexity achieved by the shadow-norm result of
[
28
]
for Pauli observables.

Lemma 7

(Classical Shadows for Low-Degree Pauli Observables -
[
31
,
28
]
)
.

Assume we are given error parameter
\epsilon>0
, failure probability
\delta\in[0,1]
, degree
\ell\geq 1
, and

\displaystyle N=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{3^{\ell}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right)

(41)

copies of unknown
n
-qubit state
\rho
that we can make random Pauli measurements on. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a set of
n
-qubit Pauli matrices of degree
\leq\ell
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\subseteq\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|\leq\ell\}.

(42)

With probability at least
1-\delta
, we can output an estimate
\widetilde{s}(P)
for each
P\in\mathcal{M}
such that

\displaystyle|\widetilde{s}(P)-\Tr(P\rho)|\leq\epsilon.

(43)

The computational complexity of this procedure scales as
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{M}|\cdot N)
.

A.5
Unitary Projection

Our sewing procedure will require an efficient procedure for projecting non-unitary matrices onto unitary matrices. Specifically, we require a projection which minimizes the Frobenius norm distance. As such, for an arbitrary matrix
A
, we will define
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}(A)
to be the projection of
A
onto the unitary matrix that minimizes Frobenius norm distance,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Proj}_{U}(A)=\underset{B\in U(2^{n})}{\min}\|A-B\|^{2}_{F}.

(44)

Note that this minimization task is the well-established “Orthogonal Procrustes Problem” and has an efficiently computable, simple solution.

Fact 5

(Orthogonal Procrustes Problem)
.

For
m\times m
matrix
A
with singular value decomposition
A=U\Sigma V^{\dagger}
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Proj}_{U}(A)=UV^{\dagger},

(45)

which can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
time.

A.6
The
CZ_{k}
Gate

In this work, we will use
CZ_{k}
to refer to a control-
Z
gate acting on
k
-qubits. Note that, unlike other quantum “controlled” operations, the
CZ_{k}
does not need to distinguish between target and control qubits, since it only applies a phase when activated by the all ones state. Mathematically, the
CZ_{k}
gate can be described by its action on the computational basis states
\ket{i}\in\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}^{\otimes k}
, as

\displaystyle CZ_{k}\ket{i}=\begin{cases}-\ket{i},&\textnormal{ if }\ket{i}=\ket{1_k}\\
\ket{i},&\textnormal{ otherwise}\end{cases},

(46)

where we use
\ket{1_k}=\ket{111...1}
to denote the all ones state. Thus, the
CZ_{k}
unitary can be decomposed in terms of projectors as

\displaystyle CZ_{k}=-\outerproduct{1_k}{1_k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
j\neq 1_{k}\end{subarray}}\outerproduct{j}{j}.

(47)

Alternatively, the
CZ_{k}
unitary can be expressed in terms of its Pauli decomposition.

Lemma 8

(
CZ_{k}
Pauli Decomposition)
.

The Pauli decomposition of the
CZ_{k}
gate acting on
k
qubits is given by

\displaystyle CZ_{k}=\sum_{P_{Z}\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes k}}\widehat{\alpha}_{CZ_{k}}(P_{Z})\cdot P_{Z},

(48)

with Pauli coefficients

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{CZ_{k}}(P_{Z})=\begin{cases}1-2^{-k+1},&\textnormal{ if }\deg(P_{Z})=0\\
2^{-k+1},&\textnormal{ if }\deg(P_{Z})=\textnormal{odd},\\
-2^{-k+1},&\textnormal{ if }\deg(P_{Z})\neq 0\textnormal{ and }\deg(P_{Z})=\textnormal{even}.\end{cases}

(49)

Note that we use notation
P_{Z}
to emphasize that Pauli strings consisting of any
X
or
Y
Paulis have zero Fourier mass.

Proof of
Lemma

8
.

Using the
CZ_{k}
projector decomposition given in
Equation

47
, we can calculate the
CZ_{k}
Pauli coefficient of for any Pauli string
P\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes k}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{CZ_{k}}(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\Tr\left(P^{\dagger}CZ_{k}\right)

(50)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\Tr\left(P\outerproduct{1_k}{1_k}\right)+\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
j\neq 1_{k}\end{subarray}}\Tr\left(P\outerproduct{j}{j}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\bra{1_k}P\ket{1_k}+\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
j\neq 1_{k}\end{subarray}}\bra{j}P\ket{j}

(52)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\bra{1_k}P\ket{1_k}+\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\Tr(P)-\bra{1_k}P\ket{1_k}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\Tr(P)-\frac{2}{2^{k}}\bra{1_k}P\ket{1_k}

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot 2^{k}\cdot\delta(P=I_{k})-\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\cdot(-1)^{\delta(\textnormal{deg}(P)=\textnormal{odd})}

(55)

\displaystyle=\delta(P=I_{k})+\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\cdot(-1)^{\delta(\textnormal{deg}(P)=\textnormal{even})}.

(56)

∎

Note that this
k
-qubit decomposition can straightforwardly be extended to a
CZ_{k}
gate acting on a
k
-qubit subset of an
n
-qubit system, as

\displaystyle CZ_{k}\otimes I_{n-k}=\sum_{P_{Z}\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes k}}\widehat{\alpha}_{CZ_{k}}(P_{Z})\cdot P_{Z}\otimes I_{n-k},

(57)

where it is assumed without loss of generality that the
CZ_{k}
gate acts on the first
k
qubits of the system. From hereon, when we write
CZ_{k}
, the identity on the remaining
n-k
qubits, i.e.
I_{n-k}
, will be assumed.

Appendix B
Choi Representations and Heisenberg-Evolved Observables

The work of
[
43
]
studied the Choi representation of quantum channels, while the work of
[
33
]
considered the Heisenberg-evolved observables of quantum circuits. Similar to
[
33
]
, in this work, we consider
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables. However, we will now establish that they are in fact closely related to the single-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channel Choi representations of
[
43
]
.

In particular, the work of
[
43
]
studied channels of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C}(\rho)=\Tr_{n-1}(C\rho C^{\dagger}),

(58)

where
C
is the unitary implemented by a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit and
\rho
is a density matrix. Their results were presented in terms of the channel’s Choi representation, given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\outerproduct{x}{y}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{C}\left(\outerproduct{x}{y}\right).

(59)

Note that
\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}
operates on
n+1
-qubits, where we denote the
n
-qubit register corresponding to the channel input as the “in” register and the single qubit corresponding to the channel output as the “out” register. From the definition of the Choi representation, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C}(\rho)=\Tr_{\text{in}}\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}\left(I_{\text{out}}\otimes\rho^{\top}_{\text{in}}\right)\right).

(60)

Measuring the output of this channel with respect to a single-qubit observable
O
results in the expectation value
\Tr(O \calE_C(\rho))
. Via algebraic manipulation of this expectation, we can solve for the dual channel
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}_{C}(O)
,

\displaystyle\Tr(O \calE_C(\rho))

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(O\cdot\Tr_{n-1}(C\rho C^{\dagger})\right)=\Tr\left((I_{n-1}\otimes O)C\rho C^{\dagger}\right)=\Tr(\underbrace{(C^\dag(I_{n-1}\otimes O) C)}_{\calE_C^\dag(O)}\rho).

(61)

Therefore, the Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit observable of this single-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C}^{\dagger}(O)=C^{\dagger}(I_{n-1}\otimes O)C.

(62)

Alternatively, leveraging
Equation

60
,

\displaystyle\Tr\left(O\mathcal{E}_{C}(\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(O\Tr_{\text{in}}\left(\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}(I_{\text{out}}\otimes\rho^{\top}_{\text{in}})\right)\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((O_{\text{out}}\otimes I_{\text{in}})\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}(I_{\text{out}}\otimes\rho^{\top}_{\text{in}})\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\Tr_{\text{out}}\left((O_{\text{out}}\otimes I_{\text{in}})\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}\right)\rho^{\top}_{\text{in}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\Tr\bigg(\underbrace{\Tr_\text{out}\left((O_\text{out}\otimes I_\text{in}) \Phi_{\calE_C}\right)^\top}_{\calE_C^\dag(O)} \rho_\text{in}\bigg),

(66)

we can also express the Heisenberg-evolved observable in terms of the Choi representation, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{C}^{\dagger}(O)=\Tr_{\text{out}}\left((O_{\text{out}}\otimes I_{\text{in}})\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}\right)^{\top}.

(67)

Thus,
Equation

62
and
Equation

67
directly establish the link between
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit observables and single-qubit output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Choi representations.

Intuitively, the Choi representation contains a full description of the channel and can be used to calculate the channel expectation for any input state and observable pair
(\rho,O)
. Meanwhile, the Heisenberg-evolved observable restricts the channel output to a single measurement observable
O
, but can be used to compute the expectation for any input state
\rho
. This relationship is illustrated by the tensor network diagrams in
Figure

2
.

is the

Finally, we can explicitly relate the Pauli coefficients of each
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observable to a Fourier coefficient of the single-output

Proposition 4
.

For an
n
-qubit Pauli
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
, the Pauli coefficient of the Heisenberg-evolved observable

is related to that of the single-output Choi representation

as

\displaystyle\widehat{O}_{P_{\text{out}}}(Q)=2\cdot\widehat{\Phi}_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}(P_{\text{out}}\otimes Q)\cdot(-1)^{\delta\{Q\text{ has an odd \# of Pauli }Ys\}}.

Proof of
Proposition

.

For an
n
-qubit Pauli
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
, leveraging

,

\displaystyle\widehat{O}_{P_{\text{out}}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\Tr(O_{P_\text{out}} \cdot Q)

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\Tr\left(\Tr_{\text{out}}((P_{\text{out}}\otimes I_{\text{in}})\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}})^{\top}Q\right)

Suppose

Analogous to
[
43

, we begin by showing that the weight spectrum of the

Lemma 9
.

Let
O_{P_{i}}
be an observable corresponding to a circuit

P_{i}

>\ell

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>\ell}\left[O_{P_{i}}\right]=0.

(85)

Proof of
Lemma

9
.

Given the circuit
C
and an observable
P_{i}
, we will decompose the circuit as

\displaystyle C=D_{P_{i}}L_{P_{i}},

(86)

where

is the unitary corresponding to all gates in the circuit that are in the backwards light-cone of

is the unitary corresponding to the gates which are not. Thus, by the standard light-cone argument,

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}P_{i}C=D_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}L_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}P_{i}L_{P_{i}}D_{P_{i}}=L_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}P_{i}L_{P_{i}}.

Plugging this into our expression for the weight of observable

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i}}]

(89)

we see that the weight only depends on gates in the backwards light-cone of
P_{i}
. If

Analogous to
[
43

, we can also upper-bound the distance between the true observable,

Lemma 10

Let

Proof of
Lemma

Let us define the sub-circuits

\displaystyle C_{j}

\displaystyle=U_{j}\prod_{i=j}^{m}\left(CZ_{k_{i}}U_{i+1}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{j}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=j}^{m}U_{i}.

(92)

Via unitary-invariance of the Frobenius norm and a hybrid argument,

\displaystyle\left\|O_{P_{i}}-O_{P_{i}}^{*}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|C_{1}^{\dagger}P_{i}C_{1}-\widetilde{C}_{1}^{\dagger}P_{i}\widetilde{C}_{1}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|CZ_{k_{1}}^{\dagger}C_{2}^{\dagger}P_{i}C_{2}CZ_{k_{1}}-\widetilde{C}_{2}^{\dagger}P_{i}\widetilde{C}_{2}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\vdots

C.2
Low-Support Concentration

For the computational efficiency of the learning algorithm to be presented in this work, it is crucial to establish that, beyond low-degree concentrated, the
O_{P_{i}}
observables’ weight spectrum is concentrated on a set of Paulis with small support, i.e. low-support concentrated.

To begin, we modify
Lemma

9
to show that the weight of a Heisenberg-evolved observable is zero for all Paulis that lie outside of its support.

Lemma 12
.

Let
O_{P_{i}}
be an observable corresponding to a circuit
C
measured with respect to
P_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:P_{i}=I,\penalty\ \forall i\notin\textnormal{supp}(O_{P_{i}})\}
denote the set of Pauli strings in
O_{P_{i}}
’s support. Then
O_{P_{i}}
’s weight is zero for all Paulis acting non-trivially outside of its support, i.e.

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}}\left[O_{P_{i}}\right]=0.

(114)

Proof of
Lemma

12
.

Given the circuit
C
and an observable
P_{i}
, we will decompose the circuit as

\displaystyle C=D_{P_{i}}L_{P_{i}},

(115)

where
L_{P_{i}}
is the unitary corresponding to all gates in the circuit that are in the backwards light-cone of
P_{i}
and
D_{P_{i}}
is the unitary corresponding to the gates which are not. Thus, by the standard light-cone argument,

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}P_{i}C=D_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}L_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}P_{i}L_{P_{i}}D_{P_{i}}=L_{P_{i}}^{\dagger}P_{i}L_{P_{i}}.

(116)

Plugging this into our expression for the weight of observable
O_{P_{i}}
for Paulis outside the support,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\notin\mathcal{S}}\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)^{2}=\sum_{Q\notin\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\Tr(O_{P_i} Q)^{2}=\sum_{Q\notin\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\Tr(C^\dagger P_i C Q)^{2}

(117)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\notin\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\Tr(L_{P_i}^\dagger P_i L_{P_i} Q)^{2},

(118)

we see that the weight only depends on gates in the backwards light-cone of
P_{i}
, and thus in the support of
O_{P_{i}}
. If
Q
acts non-trivially outside
\mathcal{S}=\textnormal{supp}(O_{P_{i}})
, this implies that there must exist at least one non-identity term in
Q
which corresponds to an identity term in
O_{P_{i}}
, meaning
\Tr(L_{P_i}^\dagger P_i L_{P_i} Q)=0
. Therefore, for all
Q\notin\mathcal{S}
,
\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)^{2}=0
, which implies that
\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i}}]=0
.
∎

Leveraging this result and a proof similar to that of
Lemma

11
, we achieve a low-support concentration result. In particular, we show that the weight of
O_{P_{i}}
outside the set of Paulis in the support of
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
is at most the distance between
O_{P_{i}}
and
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
, which we proved in
Lemma

10
to decay with respect to the size of the support of
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
.

Lemma 13
.

For
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\textnormal{supp}(O^{*}_{P_{i}})
, the weight of
O_{P_{i}}
outside the support of
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
is upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i}}]\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(O_{P_{i}},O^{*}_{P_{i}})\leq\epsilon^{*}.

(119)

Proof of
Lemma

11
.

Note that the weight expression satisfies the triangle inequality. Thus, the weight of
O_{P_{i}}
can be decomposed with respect to
O^{*}_{P_{i}}
as follows.

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i}}]

\displaystyle=\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O^{*}_{P_{i}}+(O_{P_{i}}-O^{*}_{P_{i}})\right]

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O^{*}_{P_{i}}\right]^{1/2}+\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i}}-O^{*}_{P_{i}}\right]^{1/2}\right)^{2}

(121)

By
Lemma

12
, we have that
\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O^{*}_{P_{i}}\right]=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i}}]

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i}}-O^{*}_{P_{i}}\right]\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(O_{P_{i}},O^{*}_{P_{i}}).

(122)

This concludes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Efficient Learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Circuit Unitaries

Leveraging the low-support concentration of Heisenberg-evolved
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
observables, we will prove the main result of this work – a sample and time efficient algorithm for learning
n
-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
unitaries.

Theorem 2

(Learning shallow circuits with many-qubit gates)
.

Consider an unknown
n
-qubit, depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit governed by unitary
C
. For error parameter
\epsilon=1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, we can learn a
2n
-qubit unitary
C_{\text{sew}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\varepsilon,

(123)

with high probability
1-\delta
.
C_{\text{sew}}
can be learned with quasi-polynomial sample and time complexity.

At a high-level, the proof of this theorem consists of two main parts:

1.

In
Section

D.1
, we demonstrate that the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit’s Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables can be efficiently learned to high accuracy. This section is where most of the algorithm’s novelty lies. Via classical shadow tomography, we show that an observable with
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
-support can be learned in quasi-polynomial sample and time complexity. Then, leveraging the previously-established concentration results, we prove that this low-support learned observable is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the true Heisenberg-evolved observable.

2.

In
Section

D.2
, we leverage
[
33
, Section 5.2.2]
’s procedure for “sewing” these learned Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observables into a unitary description of the circuit. Note that novel work is done to guarantee that the sewed unitary is close to the true unitary under the
average-case
distance measure. (In
[
33
]
, guarantees were given according to the worst-case measure).

In
Section

D.3
, we also offer an efficient procedure to synthesize an explicit poly-logarithmic depth
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit that implements a unitary
\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}
-close to
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
.

D.1
Approximate Learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-Evolved Observables

In this section, we propose an efficient algorithm,
Algorithm

1
, for learning an observable
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
, supported on
\ell=\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits. The majority of the section will focus on proving the following learning guarantee, which establishes that
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the true
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observable
O_{P_{i}}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
b\geq 2
and
c\geq 3d
be constants. Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a failure probability. For observable
O_{P_{i}}
, with high probability,
1-\delta
, we can learn an approximate observable
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\right)\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}\leq\frac{2}{n^{b}}+\frac{9d^{2}}{c^{2}\cdot n^{c-2}}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(124)

The sample and time complexity of this procedure are quasi-polynomial,
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{\textnormal{poly}\log n}\cdot\log\left(1/\delta\right)\right)
.

Importantly, our proposed algorithm fundamentally differs from those of
[
33
]
and
[
43
]
. In particular, we cannot simply apply
[
33
, Lemma 10]
because that result assumes that the true observable to be learned is low-degree. However, in our setting, the true
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
observable is not low-degree (just
close to
a low-degree observable in average-case distance), meaning that the procedure’s guarantees no longer hold. Meanwhile, the learning algorithm of
[
43
]
simply leverages the low-degree concentration result to efficiently learn a degree-
\ell
Choi representation, which is close to the true single-output
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
channel Choi representation. However, as will become apparent in the next section, to achieve an efficient computational complexity for the observable sewing procedure, it is critical that we learn an observable which has an
\ell
-qubit
support
. That is, we need to learn an observable which is not only low-degree, but also low-support. We cannot simply sample from the low-degree support, because apriori we do not actually know which qubits the observable is supported on.

D.1.1
The Low-Support Observable Learning Algorithm

We will now describe
Algorithm

1
– our proposed procedure for learning the Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables of a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit. With this algorithm, we aim to learn, for each observable
O_{P_{i}}
, an approximation,

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\cdot Q,

(125)

such that the normalized Frobenius distance is guaranteed to be small,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}\right)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}.

(126)

Note that
O_{P_{i}}
could be supported on all
n
qubits, meaning
\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)
could be non-zero for all
4^{n}
possible Paulis
Q
. Thus, if we were simply to try and learn all the Pauli coefficients of
O_{P_{i}}
, we would require a learning algorithm with exponential complexity.

However, in
Appendix

C
, we saw that
O_{P_{i}}
is close in distance to
O^{*}_{P_{i}}
, where
|\textnormal{supp}(O^{*}_{P_{i}})|\leq\ell=O(\log^{d}n)
. This implies that
O_{P_{i}}
is concentrated on the set of Paulis
S^{*}_{\ell}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:P_{i}=I,\penalty\ \forall i\notin\textnormal{supp}(O^{*}_{P_{i}})\}
. Therefore, we should be able to learn a decent approximation of
O_{P_{i}}
simply by learning the
S_{\ell}^{*}
-truncated approximation,

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}^{*}_{P_{i}}=\sum_{Q\in S_{\ell}^{*}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\cdot Q.

(127)

Note, however, that we will be learning the coefficients directly from
O_{P_{i}}
and do not apriori know which qubits are contained in
\textnormal{supp}(O^{*}_{P_{i}})
. Therefore, we will first need to learn approximations of all the degree-
\ell
Pauli coefficients of
O_{P_{i}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q),\quad\forall\penalty\ Q\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|\leq\ell\}.

(128)

Then, we will select the learned
\ell
-qubit support with maximal weight.

Formally, to describe this we will need to introduce a bit of notation. Let
S=\{L\subset[n]:|L|=\ell\}
denote the set of all possible
\ell
-qubit subsets of the
n
total qubits. For an
\ell
-qubit subset
s\in S
, define the set of all Paulis supported on that set as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\{s\}}=\left\{P_{s}\otimes I_{\bar{s}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \forall P\in\mathcal{P}^{\ell}\right\}.

(129)

Finally, denote the set of all possible
\ell
-qubit supports as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\ell}=\left\{\mathcal{F}_{\{s\}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \forall s\in S\right\}.

(130)

Thus, the set of Paulis in an
\ell
-qubit support with maximal weight is defined as

\displaystyle T_{\ell}=\underset{\mathcal{F}_{\{s\}}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\ell}}{\arg\max}\sum_{Q\in S}\left|\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}.

(131)

We will set any learned coefficients outside this set to zero, i.e.

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q),&\text{ if }Q\in T_{\ell},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

(132)

Thus,
Algorithm

1
will learn the observable

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}=\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\cdot Q.

(133)

D.1.2
Observable Learning Guarantees

We will now prove guarantees for
Algorithm

1
– namely, that the learned observable
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the true observable
O_{P_{i}}
.

Lemma 15

(Observable Learning Guarantees)
.

Let
O_{P_{i}}
be a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observable, which is
\epsilon^{*}
-close to the observable
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
, with all gates of width
\geq\kappa
removed. Furthermore, suppose that we can learn all the degree-
\ell
Pauli coefficients of

to precision
\eta

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|\leq\eta,\quad\forall Q\in\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|\leq\ell\}.

(134)

Leveraging these learned coefficients,
Algorithm

1
will produce a learned observable
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}},O_{P_{i}}\right)\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\epsilon^{*}.

(135)

Proof of
Lemma

15
.

The error can be decomposed as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}},O_{P_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(136)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(137)

\displaystyle\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}.

(138)

In order to establish the desired error bound, we will upper-bound
\sum_{Q\notin T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}
. Leveraging
Equation

131
and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in S_{\ell}^{*}}\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(139)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left(\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}+\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}\right)

(140)

\displaystyle\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2},

(141)

which implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{Q\in S_{\ell}^{*}}\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}\right)-|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}.

(142)

Leveraging Parseval’s (
Fact

4
) and the fact that
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
only has non-zero coefficients for Paulis in
T_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\notin T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin S_{\ell}^{*}}\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}=|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin S_{\ell}^{*},Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(143)

Using the triangle inequality and bound from
Lemma

13
, we obtain an upper-bound in terms of
\eta
and
\epsilon^{*}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\notin T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin S_{\ell}^{*},Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin S_{\ell}^{*},Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(144)

\displaystyle\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\sum_{Q\in T_{\ell}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)-\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}+\sum_{Q\notin S_{\ell}^{*}}\left|\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(145)

\displaystyle\leq|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\epsilon^{*}

(146)

\displaystyle=2\cdot|T_{\ell}|\cdot\eta^{2}+\epsilon^{*}

(147)

Noting that
T_{\ell}
is the set of Pauli coefficients in the support of
\ell
-qubits,
|T_{\ell}|=4^{\ell}
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Leveraging
Lemma

15
, classical shadow tomography
[
31
]
(as described in
Lemma

7
), and some of our prior concentration results, we can now prove
Lemma

14
. In particular, we will now prove that the learning procedure requires quasi-polynomial sample and time complexity.

Proof of
Lemma

14
.

Leveraging our bound on the distance between
O_{P_{i}}
and
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
from
Lemma

15
, our goal is to show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}\right)\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}=2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\epsilon^{*}\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(148)

Setting
\ell=C\cdot\log^{d}n
(where
C=c^{d}
and
c\geq 3d
), this implies that
m=d\cdot\lfloor\frac{n}{\kappa}\rfloor
gates of width at least
\kappa=c\cdot\log n
are removed from
O_{P_{i}}
to obtain
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
. Plugging this into
Lemma

10
implies that the distance between
O_{P_{i}}
and
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}\leq\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}\leq\frac{9\cdot d^{2}\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\kappa}\right\rfloor^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}\leq\frac{9\cdot d^{2}\cdot n^{2}}{\kappa^{2}\cdot 2^{\kappa}}=\frac{9\cdot d^{2}\cdot n^{2}}{c^{2}\log^{2}n\cdot 2^{\log(n^c)}}\leq\frac{9d^{2}}{c^{2}n^{c-2}}

(149)

Therefore, setting the observable learning accuracy to

\displaystyle\eta^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{b}\cdot 4^{\ell}},

(150)

for some constant
b\geq 2
, achieves the desired error bound:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{P_{i}}=2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\epsilon^{*}\leq\frac{2}{n^{b}}+\frac{9d^{2}}{c^{2}\cdot n^{c-2}}\leq\frac{2}{n^{b}}+\frac{1}{n^{3d-2}}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(151)

Now all that remains is to prove the sample and computational complexity. Denote the set of
n
-qubit Paulis of degree
\leq\ell
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\ell}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|\leq\ell\}.

(152)

Via classical shadow tomography, with the state
\rho=O_{P_{i}}
and the set of bounded-degree Pauli observables
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, we can produce an
\eta
-estimate
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)
of

\displaystyle\widehat{O}_{P_{i}}(Q)=\Tr(Q \cdot O_{P_i})

(153)

for each
Q\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
. Since the Pauli observables are all of degree at most
\ell
, by
Lemma

7
, the sample complexity of this classical shadow tomography procedure is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{3^{\ell}}{\eta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(3^{\ell}\cdot n^{b}\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{b}\cdot 4^{2\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right).

(154)

Furthermore, since the size of
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, or the total number of Pauli of degree
\leq\ell
, is upper-bounded as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}|=\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}3^{k}\cdot{n\choose k}\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{\ell}),

(155)

the computational complexity is

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(n^{\ell+b}\cdot 4^{2\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right).

(156)

As described in
Algorithm

1
, to produce the approximation
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}
, we also need to find the set of Paulis supported on
\ell
qubits with maximal weight and set all other Pauli coefficients to zero. Since there are
{n\choose\ell}
possible supports of size
\ell
and calculating the weight of each support involves summing over
3^{\ell}
different Pauli coefficients, the computational complexity of finding the set
T_{\ell}
is quasi-polynomial, i.e.

\displaystyle{n\choose\ell}\cdot 3^{\ell}\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{\ell}).

(157)

To achieve the final, explicit sample and computational complexity, plug in the value
\ell=C\cdot\log^{d}n
, where
C=c^{d}
and
c\geq 3d
. Thus, the overall sample complexity is quasi-polynomial

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(n^{b}\cdot 4^{2\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{b}\cdot 2^{4C\log^{d}n}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{C\log^{d}n}}{\delta}\right)\right)

(158)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{b}\cdot 2^{4C\log^{d}n}\cdot\left(C\log^{d}n\cdot\log n-\log\delta\right)\right)

(159)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{b}\cdot 2^{4c^{d}\log^{d}n}\cdot\left(\log^{d+1}n+\log(1/\delta)\right)\right)

(160)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(2^{\textnormal{poly}\log n}\cdot\log\left(1/\delta\right)\right),

(161)

and the computational complexity is also quasi-polynomial

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(n^{\ell+b}\cdot 4^{2\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)+n^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{\ell+b}\cdot 4^{2\ell}\cdot\log\left(\frac{n^{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right)

(162)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{c^{d}\log^{d}n+b}\cdot 2^{4c^{d}\log^{d}n}\cdot\left(\log^{d+1}n+\log(1/\delta)\right)\right)

(163)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{\textnormal{poly}\log n}\cdot\log\left(1/\delta\right)\right).

(164)

∎

D.2
Sewing
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-Evolved Observables

We will now use a procedure analogous to that of
[
33
, Section 5.2.2]
to sew these Heisenberg-evolved Pauli observables and project them onto a unitary which is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the true
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit unitary, with respect to the average-case distance.

Analogous to
[
33
, Lemma 9]
, we begin by showing that the error in the sewing procedure can be upper-bounded by the sum of the learned observables’ learning error (from the last section). Importantly, note that our proof differs from that of
[
33
]
, since we are leveraging an average-case instead of a worst-case distance measure.

Lemma 16

(Observable Sewing Guarantees)
.

Suppose
C
is an
n
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, which has a set of Heisenberg-evolved observables
\left\{O_{P_{i}}\right\}_{i,P}
, corresponding to each of the
3n
possible single-qubit Paulis
P_{i}
. Let
\left\{\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}\right\}_{i,P}
denote the set of learned observables, which are each at most
\epsilon_{P_{i}}
-far from the respective true observable. Construct the unitary

\displaystyle C_{\text{sew}}:=\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right],

(165)

by “sewing” the learned observables, where
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
is the projection onto the unitary minimizing Frobenius norm, as defined in
Equation

44
, and
\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}
swaps the first and last
n
qubits. The average-case distance between
C_{\text{sew}}
and
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
is at most

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\frac{1}{2}\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\epsilon_{P_{i}}.

(166)

Proof of
Lemma

16
.

To begin, note that
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
, can be decomposed in terms of the true observables as

\displaystyle C\otimes C^{\dagger}=\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}\right].

(167)

Also, define the following three matrices:

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}

(168)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}

(169)

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i}\right).

(170)

Noting that
C_{\text{sew}}
and
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
implement unitary channels, we can leverage
Fact

3
and
Fact

2
to upper-bound the average gate fidelity of the channels by the normalized Frobenius distance of the corresponding unitaries,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\mathcal{D}_{P}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger}).

(171)

Leveraging the facts that the Frobenius norm is unitary invariant and satisfies the triangle inequality, we perform the following hybrid argument:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{sew}},\mathcal{C}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})

(172)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(S\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{i},S\prod_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\right)

(173)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\right)

(174)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}W_{i}V_{n}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}W_{i}V_{n},\prod_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\right)

(175)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{n},V_{n}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}W_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}V_{i}\right)

(176)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\vdots

(177)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}\right).

(178)

Upper-bounding each
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}\right)
term as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},\widetilde{W}_{i}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right),

(179)

we are now interested in the values of
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},\widetilde{W}_{i}\right)
and
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)
. However, leveraging the definition of
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
,
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},\widetilde{W}_{i}\right)
can be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left\|\widetilde{W}_{i}-W_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}(2^{2n})}\left\|\widetilde{W}_{i}-U\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left\|\widetilde{W}_{i}-V_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right).

(180)

Therefore,
Equation

179
simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}\right)\leq 2\cdot\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right).

(181)

Furthermore, we can upper-bound
\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left\|\widetilde{W}_{i}-V_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left\|\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}

(183)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)\otimes P_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(184)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\Tr\left(\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)^{\dagger}\otimes P_{i}\cdot\sum_{Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{Q_{i}}\right)\otimes Q_{i}\right)

(185)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P,Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\Tr\left(\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{Q_{i}}\right)\otimes P_{i}Q_{i}\right)

(186)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P,Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\Tr\left(\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{Q_{i}}\right)\right)\Tr\left(P_{i}Q_{i}\right)

(187)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P,Q\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\Tr\left(\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{Q_{i}}\right)\right)\cdot 2^{n}\delta(P_{i}=Q_{i})

(188)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\Tr\left(\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right)\right)

(189)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}-O_{P_{i}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(190)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\epsilon_{P_{i}}.

(191)

Therefore, combining our bounds from
Equation

178
,
Equation

181
, and
Equation

191
, we obtain the desired upper-bound:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\cdot\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\epsilon_{P_{i}}.

(192)

∎

With these results establishing efficient learning and sewing of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables, we can now prove the main result of the section,
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Leveraging
Algorithm

1
and
Lemma

14
, we can learn the set of observables
\{\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\}_{i,P}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}}\right)\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}\leq\frac{2}{n^{b}}+\frac{9d^{2}}{c^{2}\cdot n^{c-2}}

(193)

for all
3n
single-qubit Paulis
P_{i}
, with quasi-polynomial sample and computational complexity. Leveraging the result of
Lemma

16
, we can then “sew” these learned observables into the unitary

\displaystyle C_{\text{sew}}:=\textnormal{SWAP}^{\otimes n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right],

(194)

which, for constants
b\geq 2
and
c\geq 3d
, satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{sew}},\mathcal{C}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\dagger})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\epsilon_{P_{i}}\leq\frac{3n}{2}\cdot\underset{P_{i}}{\arg\max}\penalty\ \epsilon_{P_{i}}\leq\frac{3}{n^{b-1}}+\frac{9d^{2}}{c^{2}\cdot n^{c-3}}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)},

(195)

meaning that
C_{\text{sew}}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
in average-case distance.

All that remains is to verify that the computational complexity of the sewing procedure of
Equation

194
is in fact quasi-polynomial. Note that the construction of
C_{\text{sew}}
requires computing

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{i}=\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}\right)

(196)

n
times. As described in
Fact

5
, computing
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
reduces to computing the singular value decomposition of the matrix. In general, computing the SVD of a
2n
-qubit matrix has exponential complexity
\mathcal{O}(2^{6n})
. However, in
Algorithm

1
we specifically imposed that the learned observable
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}
only have support on
\ell=\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits. Since
P_{i}
only has support on 1 qubit, the total support of the matrix
\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}
is
\ell+1
qubits. Therefore, we only need to compute the SVD of the sub-matrix corresponding to the non-trivial support, which has quasi-polynomial computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(2^{\textnormal{poly}\log n})
. Therefore, the total sewing procedure involves performing
n
of these SVDs, which is still quasi-polynomial complexity.
∎

D.3
Learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with Optimized Depth

Now that we have learned a unitary
C_{\text{sew}}
which is close in average-case distance to
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
, we will explore circuit-synthesis procedures that implement unitaries close to
C_{\text{sew}}
. In other words, we will look into
proper learning
of the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit. We will show that, while a naive compilation procedure would produce a worst-case quasi-polynomial-depth circuit, we can generate in quasi-polynomial time an explicit (poly-logarithmic depth)
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit
C^{*}_{\text{sew}}
that implements a unitary
\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}
-close to
C_{\text{sew}}
. In particular, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Given a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C
, there exists a quasi-polynomial time algorithm to learn a
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit implementing unitary
C_{\text{sew}}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C\otimes C^{\dagger}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(197)

Throughout this section, we will use the following notation:

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i},

(198)

\displaystyle V^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O^{*}_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i},

(199)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i},

(200)

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Proj}_{U}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i}\right).

(201)

Furthermore, by the sewing procedure of
[
33
]
, if
O_{P_{i}}
corresponds to the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C
and
L_{i}
is the light-cone of
C
with respect to measurement qubit
i
, then

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}

(202)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}C^{\dagger}P_{i}C\otimes P_{i}

(203)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}L_{i}^{\dagger}P_{i}L_{i}\otimes P_{i}

(204)

\displaystyle=(L_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes I)\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}P_{i}\otimes P_{i}\right)(L_{i}\otimes I)

(205)

\displaystyle=L_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}L_{i},

(206)

where
S_{i}
denotes the SWAP operation between the
i^{\text{i}}
and
(n+i)^{\text{th}}
qubits.
Similarly, if
O_{P_{i}}^{*}
corresponds to the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
\widetilde{C}
(with large
CZ
gates removed) and
\widetilde{L}_{i}
is the light-cone of
\widetilde{C}
with respect to measurement qubit
i
, then

\displaystyle V^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}\widetilde{L}_{i}.

(207)

D.3.1
Naive Implementation

We will begin by evaluating the worst-case circuit depth of a naive compilation procedure for our learned unitary. Our naive implementation will leverage the following standard fact about the complexity of unitary synthesis.

Fact 6

(
[
33
]
Fact 4)
.

Given a unitary
U
, which acts on
k
qubits, there is an algorithm that outputs a circuit (acting on
k
qubits) that consists of at most
4^{k}
two-qubit gates, which exactly implements the unitary
U
, in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.

Because any two-qubit gate can be generated by a constant number of single-qubit gates and
CZ
gates, we can immediately obtain the following fact from the above.

Fact 7

(Adapted from
[
33
]
Fact 4)
.

Given a unitary
U
, which acts on
k
qubits, there is an algorithm that outputs a circuit (acting on
k
qubits) that consists of at most
4^{k}
single-qubit gates or many-qubit
CZ
gates, which exactly implements the unitary
U
, in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.

For each qubit measurement index
i
, we will consider a naive circuit-synthesis procedure that simply compiles the individual projected unitaries
W_{i}
into a circuit with gates acting on at most 2 qubits and then sews these compiled circuits into
C_{\text{sew}}
, as specified in
Equation

194
, via some arbitrary ordering. Since the support of each unitary
W_{i}
is
\ell=\textnormal{poly}\log(n)
, by
Fact

6
, it would be compiled into a circuit acting on
\ell=\textnormal{poly}\log(n)
qubits consisting of up to
4^{\textnormal{poly}\log(n)}
gates.

Stitching together all
3n
of these circuits in an arbitrary order results in a circuit with quasi-polynomial depth.
This is substantially deeper than the constant depth of the true
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit that we aimed to learn. Thus, we will now show how improved compilation and ordering of the unitaries in the sewing procedure can reduce this depth down to poly-logarithmic, while still only requiring quasi-polynomial computational complexity.

D.3.2
Improved Compilation

We will now describe an improved compilation procedure, that reduces the circuit-synthesis depth for unitaries
W_{i}
from
\mathcal{O}(4^{\textnormal{poly}\log n})
to constant-depth
d
.

Theorem 4
.

The learned unitary
W_{i}
can be compiled into a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit, governed by unitary
C_{i}^{*}
, that is supported on
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits and such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},C_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(208)

The computational complexity of this compilation procedure is quasi-polynomial.

In order to prove this result, we will first show that the learned unitaries
W_{i}
are close to the
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
-support
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits governed by unitary
V_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}\widetilde{L}_{i}
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
O_{P_{i}}
be a Heisenberg-evolved observable of the
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C
and
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}
be a Heisenberg-evolved observable of some other circuit
\widetilde{C}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}\right)\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall i\in[n],\penalty\ P\in\{X,Y,Z\}.

(209)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i},\penalty\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}\right)\leq\epsilon

(210)

Proof.

Leveraging Cauchy-Schwarz and the fact that
\|A\otimes B\|_{F}^{2}=\|A\|_{F}^{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i},\penalty\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}\right)

(211)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}(O_{P_{i}}-\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}})\otimes P_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(212)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|(O_{P_{i}}-\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}})\otimes P_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(213)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|(O_{P_{i}}-\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}})\right\|_{F}^{2}\left\|P_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(214)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i}},\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}\right)

(215)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{4}\cdot\epsilon.

(216)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The learned unitaries
W_{i}
are
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the unitaries
V_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}\widetilde{L}_{i}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(217)

Proof.

By
Lemma

14
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i}},O_{P_{i}}\right)\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)},

(218)

and, by
Lemma

10
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{*}_{P_{i}},O_{P_{i}}\right)\leq\epsilon^{*}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(219)

By triangle inequality and
Lemma

17
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V^{*}_{i}\right)

(220)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V_{i}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(V_{i},V_{i}^{*}\right)

(221)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\otimes P_{i}-O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\sum_{P\in\{X,Y,Z\}}\left(O_{P_{i}}\otimes P_{i}-O_{P_{i}}^{*}\otimes P_{i}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}

(222)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{P_{i}}+\epsilon^{*}\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(223)

Furthemore, since
\textnormal{Proj}_{U}
is the projection onto the unitary minimizing the Frobenius norm, for
W_{i}=\textnormal{Proj}_{U}(\widetilde{W}_{i})
, it must be true that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},W_{i}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V^{*}_{i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(224)

Therefore, by triangle inequality we obtain the desired result,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},W_{i}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{W}_{i},V^{*}_{i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(225)

∎

This implies that there must exist a circuit of the form
V_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}\widetilde{L}_{i}
that is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to each of the learned unitaries
W_{i}
. Note that since the swap gate
S_{i}
can be implemented in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, the circuit
V_{i}^{*}
is contained
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. This implies that, to find a circuit
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
W_{i}
, rather than searching over all possible
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
architectures, we can restrict our search to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits of the form of
V_{i}^{*}
, or more precisely
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits in the lightcone
\widetilde{L}_{i}
. Thus, we will now show how to efficiently construct an
\epsilon
-net over
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits of depth-
d
, with
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
support, that is guaranteed to contain a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to the learned unitary
W_{i}
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{*}
be the class of all depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with
CZ
gates acting on at most
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
qubits and supported on
\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits.
\mathcal{C}^{*}
has a
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-net, denoted
\mathcal{N}_{1/\textnormal{poly}(n)}(\mathcal{C}^{*})
, of quasi-polynomial size that can be constructed in quasi-polynomial time.

Proof.

Recall that the general structure of a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit is alternating layers of
CZ
gates and layers of arbitrary single-qubit gates. Therefore, we can construct an
\epsilon
-net for
\mathcal{C}^{*}
by first enumerating all possible architectures (i.e. placements of
CZ
gates) and then, for each architecture, creating an
\epsilon^{\prime}
-net for each possible SU(2) gate.

We will begin by enumerating all possible
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
architectures, i.e. configurations of
CZ
gates of width at most
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
acting on
\ell=\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
qubits across
d
layers. We begin thinking of a given layer of the architecture as a graph with
\ell
vertices, corresponding to each of the qubits in the support. Within this graph framework, if a set of vertices are contained in a
k
-clique this means the corresponding qubits are acted upon by a
CZ_{k}
gate. Therefore, to enumerate the total number of distinct
CZ
configurations in the layer, we simply need to enumerate the number of distinct graphs comprised of cliques of size at most
\kappa
. This is trivially upper-bounded by the number of distinct subgraphs of the complete graph on
\ell
nodes, which is quasi-polynomial, i.e.

\displaystyle 2^{{\ell\choose 2}}\leq 2^{\ell^{2}}=2^{\textnormal{poly}\log(n)}.

(226)

Since the circuits are depth-
d
, where
d
is constant, the total number of
CZ
configurations across the whole circuit is
d\cdot 2^{\textnormal{poly}\log(n)}
, which is also quasi-polynomial.

As previously mentioned, between the layers of
CZ
gates are layers of arbitrary single-qubit gates. In total, there are at most
d\cdot\ell\leq\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
single-qubit gates. By a standard hybrid argument, it can be shown that the error propagation of the SU(2)
\epsilon^{\prime}
-net is additive both within and across layers of the single qubit gates. Therefore, to achieve an overall
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-net,

\displaystyle d\cdot\ell\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\textnormal{poly}\log(n)\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)},

(227)

which implies that
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq 1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
. An
\epsilon^{\prime}
-net for
SU(2)
can be constructed with
\left(\frac{c_{0}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)^{c_{1}}
elements, which for
\epsilon^{\prime}=1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
is polynomial size.

Therefore, the total size of the net is the size of the
SU(2)

\epsilon^{\prime}
-net times the total number of single-qubit gates times the total number of architectures, which is bounded as

\displaystyle\textnormal{poly}(n)\cdot d\cdot\textnormal{poly}\log(n)\cdot 2^{\textnormal{poly}\log(n)}\leq\mathcal{O}(2^{\textnormal{poly}\log(n)}),

(228)

and therefore quasi-polynomial.
∎

We will now show that we can efficiently find an element of the
\epsilon
-net that is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
W_{i}
. Combining this with the prior results of the section, we achieve a simple proof of
Theorem

4
.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

By using a brute-force search procedure, we can iterate through the quasi-polynomial elements of the
\epsilon
-net described in
Lemma

18
to find the element

\displaystyle L^{*}_{i}=\underset{L\in\mathcal{N}_{1/\textnormal{poly}(n)}(\mathcal{C}^{*})}{\arg\min}\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},L^{\dagger}S_{i}L\right).

(229)

Since the swap gate
S_{i}
can be implemented in constant-depth in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, we thus have a constant-depth circuit implementation of the unitary
C_{i}^{*}=(L^{*}_{i})^{\dagger}S_{i}L^{*}_{i}
.
By
Corollary

2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},C_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}\widetilde{L}_{i}\right)=\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},V_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)},

(230)

meaning that
C_{i}^{*}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
W_{i}
, as desired.
∎

D.3.3
Improved Ordering

Leveraging the improved compilation result, we will now propose an improved ordering for the sewing procedure. This will enable us to construct a
\mathsf{QAC}
circuit which is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
C_{\text{sew}}
, thereby proving
Theorem

3
.

To begin, we show how the constant-depth learned circuits for
C_{i}^{*}
can be sewed into a worst-case poly-logarithmic depth circuit. Note that our proof approach is similar to that of
[
33
, Lemma 13]
. However, the circuit for each
C_{i}^{*}
has poly-logarithmic support (whereas those of
[
28
]
have constant support), meaning we only achieve poly-logarithmic depth (instead of constant depth).

Lemma 19
.

(Sewing into a poly-logarithmic depth circuit) Given
3n
learned observables
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}
, such that for any qubit
i
,
\left|\bigcup_{P}\textnormal{supp}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}(\textnormal{poly}\log(n))
and there are only
\textnormal{poly}\log(n)
qubits
j
such that

\displaystyle\textnormal{supp}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\right)\cap\textnormal{supp}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{j}}^{(\ell)}\right)\neq\varnothing.

(231)

There exists a sewing ordering for
C_{\text{sew}}
, as defined in
Equation

194
, such that it can be implemented by a
\textnormal{poly}\log(n)
-depth quantum circuit. The computational complexity for finding this sewing order is polynomial, i.e.
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{d}n)
.

Proof of
Lemma

19
.

Defining
A(i)=\bigcup_{P}\textnormal{supp}\left(\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}\right)
, then
\textnormal{supp}\left(C^{*}_{i}\right)\subseteq A(i)\cup\{n+i\}
, where
C_{i}^{*}
is the learned constant-depth circuit from
Theorem

4
.

Now, consider an
n
-node graph (where each node represents one of the
n
qubits), such that each pair
(i,j)
of nodes is connected by an edge if

\displaystyle A(i)\cap A(j)\neq\varnothing.

(232)

The graph only has
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{d}n)
many edges and can be constructed as an adjacency list in time
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{d}n)
. Since the size of the support
A(i)
is poly-logarithmic, the graph has poly-logarithmic degree. Thus, we can use a
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{d}n)
-time greedy graph coloring algorithm to color the graph using
\chi=\mathcal{O}(\log^{d}n)
colors. For each node
i
, let
c(i)
denote the color labeled from 1 to
\chi
.

We can modify the arbitrary sewing order of the
3n
observables
\widetilde{O}_{P_{i}}^{(\ell)}
in
Equation

194
to the ordering given by this greedy graph coloring, where we order from the smallest to the largest color. By the definition of graph coloring, for any pair
(i,j)
of qubits with the same color,

\displaystyle A(i)\cap A(j)=\varnothing.

(233)

Therefore, for each color
c^{\prime}
, we can implement the
2n
-qubit unitary

\displaystyle\prod_{i:c(i)=c^{\prime}}C^{*}_{i}

(234)

via the constant-depth quantum circuits
C^{*}_{i}
. Since there are at most a poly-logarithmic number of colors, with the color-based ordering,
C_{\text{sew}}
will be poly-logarithmic depth in the worst-case.
∎

Combining this improved ordering result with the improved compilation result of the last section, we can now prove
Theorem

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

For each qubit
i
, by
Theorem

4
we can find a constant-depth
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit
C_{i}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{i},C_{i}^{*}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(235)

Performing this for all
n
measurement qubits requires quasi-polynomial computational complexity. By
Lemma

19
, we can find a sewing order for all the
C_{i}^{*}
circuits, in polynomial time, which sews them into the poly-logarithmic depth circuit
C_{\text{sew}}^{*}
.

To conclude, we will prove that
C_{\text{sew}}^{*}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
C\otimes C^{\dagger}
. By triangle inequality and
Theorem

2

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C\otimes C^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C_{\text{sew}})+\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}},C\otimes C^{\dagger})\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C_{\text{sew}})+\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}

(236)

Therefore, all that remains is to show that
C_{\text{sew}}^{*}
is
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
-close to
C_{\text{sew}}
. This can be achieved by leveraging the bound of
Theorem

4
and a simple hybrid argument,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(C_{\text{sew}}^{*},C_{\text{sew}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i}C^{*}_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)

(237)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}C^{*}_{i},W_{1}\prod_{i=2}^{n}C^{*}_{i}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(W_{1}\prod_{i=2}^{n}C^{*}_{i},\prod_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)

(238)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(C^{*}_{1},W_{1}\right)+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=2}^{n}C^{*}_{i},\prod_{i=2}^{n}W_{i}\right)

(239)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}+\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\prod_{i=2}^{n}C^{*}_{i},\prod_{i=2}^{n}W_{i}\right)

(240)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\vdots

(241)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)}.

(242)

This concludes the proof.
∎

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

E.1
Concentration of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

C

Lemma 20
.

Let
C
be an
(n+a)
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}(O_{P_{i},n+a},O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*})\leq 2^{a}\cdot\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}.

\displaystyle\|O_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|(I\otimes\bra{0^a})\left(C(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})C^{\dagger}-\widetilde{C}(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})\widetilde{C}^{\dagger}\right)(I\otimes\ket{0^a})\|_{F}

(249)

\displaystyle=1\cdot\|C(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})C^{\dagger}-\widetilde{C}(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})\widetilde{C}^{\dagger}\|_{F}\cdot 1

(250)

\displaystyle=\|C(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})C^{\dagger}-\widetilde{C}(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})\widetilde{C}^{\dagger}\|_{F}

(251)

\displaystyle=\|O_{P_{i},n+a}-O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*}\|_{F}.

(252)

Therefore, if

has

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(O_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}3\cdot 2^{(n+a-k_{j})/2}\right)^{2}

∎

We will now demonstrate that the Pauli weight of the observable with ancilla restriction

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{n}
be a subset of the set of

-qubit Paulis, then

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2^{a}\cdot\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n+a}].

Proof of
Lemma

We will begin by assuming that

\displaystyle O_{P_{i},n+a}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},R\in\mathcal{P}^{a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\cdot Q\otimes R

We can relate the Pauli coefficients of

\displaystyle\widehat{O_{P_{i},n}}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\Tr\left((I\otimes\bra{0^a})\cdot O_{P_{i},n+a}\cdot(I\otimes\ket{0^a})\cdot S\right)

(259)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\Tr\left((I\otimes\bra{0^a})\left(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},R\in\mathcal{P}^{a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\cdot Q\otimes R\right)(I\otimes\ket{0^a})\cdot(S\otimes I^{a})\right)

(260)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},R\in\mathcal{P}^{a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\cdot\Tr\left(QS\otimes\bra{0^a}R\ket{0^a}\right)

(261)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},R\in\mathcal{P}^{a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\cdot\Tr(QS)\cdot\bra{0^a}R\ket{0^a}

(262)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},R\in\mathcal{P}^{a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\cdot 2^{n}\cdot\delta\{Q=S\}\cdot\delta\{R\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes a}\}

(263)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(S\otimes R).

(264)

Leveraging this result and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can prove the desired result as

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q:Q\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\widehat{O_{P_{i},n}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(265)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q:Q\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\sum_{R\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes a}}\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\right|^{2}

(266)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a}\cdot\sum_{Q:Q\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{R\in\{I,Z\}^{\otimes a}}\left|\widehat{O_{P_{i},n+a}}(Q\otimes R)\right|^{2}

(267)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a}\cdot\textbf{W}^{\in\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n+a}].

(268)

∎

Note that this result is similar to that of
[
43
, Proposition 27]
, but we prove the bound for the weight over arbitrary subsets of Paulis
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
, as opposed to just the weight above a certain degree. This enables us to use the result to achieve both the low-degree and low-support concentration bounds as simple corollaries.

Corollary 3

(Low-Degree Concentration with Ancillas)
.

Suppose
C
is a depth-
d
, size-
s

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit performing clean computation on
n+a
qubits, where
a
is the number of ancilla. Let
O_{P_{i},n}=(I\otimes\bra{0^a})C(P_{i}\otimes I^{a})C^{\dagger}(I\otimes\ket{0^a})
be a Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observable with ancilla restriction. Then for every degree
k\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(s^{2}2^{-k^{1/d}}\right)\cdot 2^{a}.

(269)

Proof of
Corollary

3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of Paulis of degree
>k
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|P|>k\}
. By
Lemma

21
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2^{a}\cdot\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n+a}].

(270)

Since
O_{P_{i},n+a}
is an observable defined over
n+a
qubits without ancilla restriction, by
Proposition

5
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n+a}]\leq\mathcal{O}\left(s^{2}2^{-k^{1/d}}\right).

(271)

Plugging this into
Equation

270
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Corollary 4

(Low-Support Concentration with Ancillas)
.

For
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\textnormal{supp}(O_{P_{i},n}^{*})
, the weight of
O_{P_{i},n}
outside the support of
O_{P_{i},n}^{*}
is upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i},n+a},O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*}\right)\leq 2^{a}\cdot\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}}

(272)

Proof of
Corollary

4
.

Note that the weight expression satisfies the triangle inequality. Thus, the weight of
O_{P_{i},n}
can be decomposed with respect to
O_{P_{i},n}^{*}
as follows.

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]

\displaystyle=\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i},n}^{*}+(O_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}^{*})\right]

(273)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right]^{1/2}+\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right]^{1/2}\right)^{2}

(274)

Since
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\textnormal{supp}(O_{P_{i},n}^{*})
, by
Lemma

12
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right]=0.

(275)

Furthermore, by
Lemma

20

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}\left[O_{P_{i},n}-O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right]\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(O_{P_{i},n+a},O_{P_{i},n+a}^{*}\right).

(276)

Plugging these results into
Equation

274
and leveraging our upper-bound on the distance from
Lemma

20
obtains the desired expression.
∎

E.2
Learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
Heisenberg-Evolved Observables with Ancillas

In order to learn the unitary of an
(n+a)
-qubit
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit with ancillas, it turns out that we can use the procedure of
Appendix

D
with some slight modifications.

Proposition 6

(Learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with Logarithmic Ancillas)
.

Suppose we are given an
(n+a)
-qubit depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit governed by unitary
C
, performing clean computation

\displaystyle C(I\otimes\ket{0^a})=A\otimes\ket{0^a}

(277)

on a logarithmic number of ancilla qubits, i.e.
a=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.
For error parameter
\varepsilon=1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, we can learn a
2n
-qubit unitary
A_{\text{sew}}
which is
\varepsilon
-close to the unitary
A\otimes A^{\dagger}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(A_{\text{sew}},A\otimes A^{\dagger})\leq\varepsilon,

(278)

with high probability
1-\delta
, as well as quasi-polynomial sample and computational complexity.

Proof of
Proposition

6
.

As expressed in
Equation

277
, since the circuit
C
performs clean computation, the ancillas return to their starting state by end of the computation. Therefore, we can measure out the ancillas at the end of the computation, to obtain the unitary
A
which performs the same computation as
C
, but only acts on the
n
computation qubits. Thus, in the learning procedure, we will be interested in learning and sewing the
n
-qubit Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Paulis
with
ancilla restriction, i.e.
O_{P_{i},n}
.

Since these observables with ancilla restriction only act on
n
qubits, we can straightforwardly apply
Algorithm

1
for learning the Heisenberg-evolved single-qubit Pauli observables of the circuit. However, the learning guarantees in this case differ from those of the ancilla-free case. In particular, to show that the learned observable
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n}
is close to the desired observable with ancilla restriction
O_{P_{i},n}
, we need to bound the distance between these operators. Plugging
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n}
and
O_{P_{i},n}
into
Lemma

15
, we get that their distance is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}\right)\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+2^{a}\cdot\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa}},

(279)

where
\eta
is the learning accuracy and the second inequality leveraged our low-support concentration result from
Corollary

4
. Notice that the key difference to the ancilla-free case is a
2^{a}
factor amplifying the error in removing large
CZ
gates.

As in the ancilla-free case, we need to perform a balancing act to ensure that this learning error is small while ensuring that the algorithm has efficient sample and computational complexity. Since the sample and time complexity are directly related to the supports of the learned observables, to achieve quasi-polynomial complexity, we will want to learn observables with
\textnormal{poly}\log n
support. However, if we were to remove all
CZ
gates of width
\geq\kappa=c\cdot\log(n+a)
in this circuit of size
s=\textnormal{poly}(n)
, then

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9m^{2}}{2^{\kappa-a}}\leq\frac{9\cdot s^{2}\cdot 2^{a}}{2^{\log((n+a)^c)}}=\frac{9\cdot s^{2}\cdot 2^{a}}{(n+a)^{c}}.

(280)

Due to the
2^{a}
factor in the numerator of
Equation

280
,
\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i}}]
is guaranteed to be
\leq 1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
only if the number of ancillas is logarithmic, i.e.
a=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.
∎

Note that the reason our algorithm is restricted to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with a logarithmic number of ancillas is the ancilla-dependence in the low-support concentration result of
Corollary

4
. However, we do not believe this bound to be tight. If
[
43
, Conjecture 1]
were proven true, it would imply ancilla-independent low-degree concentration of the Heisenberg-evolved observables.

Conjecture 2

(
[
43
]
Conjecture 1)
.

For a size-
s
, depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit acting on
n
-qubits and
\textnormal{poly}(n)
ancilla qubits, then for all
k\in[n+1]
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}]\leq\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})}.

(281)

Corollary 5
.

For a size-
s
, depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit acting on
n
-qubits and
\textnormal{poly}(n)
ancilla qubits, then for all degrees
k\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})}.

(282)

Proof.

By
Proposition

4
and
Conjecture

2
, for
k\in[n]

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{>k}[O_{P_{i},n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|Q|>k}\left|\widehat{O_{P_{i},n}}(Q)\right|^{2}

(283)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}:|Q|>k}\left|\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}}(Q\otimes P)\right|^{2}

(284)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{R\in\mathcal{P}^{n+1}:|R|>k+1}\left|\widehat{\Phi_{\mathcal{E}_{C}}}(R)\right|^{2}

(285)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega((k+1)^{1/d})}

(286)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})}

(287)

∎

However, for the purposes of our learning algorithm, it does not suffice to have low-degree concentration. Instead, we need low-support concentration. Thus, we conjecture that the ancilla-dependence of the low-support concentration result of
Corollary

4
can be eliminated.

Conjecture 3

(Ancilla-Independent Low-Support Concentration)
.

For a size-
s
, depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit acting on
n
-qubits and
\textnormal{poly}(n)
ancilla qubits and support
\mathcal{S}
such that
|\mathcal{S}|=k^{d}
,

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})}.

(288)

If this conjecture were proven true, it would imply that our learning algorithm works for
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits with polynomially many ancilla qubits.

Corollary 6

(Efficient learning of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
with Polynomial Ancillas)
.

Suppose we are given an
(n+a)
-qubit depth-
d

\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit governed by unitary
C
, performing clean computation

\displaystyle C(I\otimes\ket{0^a})=A\otimes\ket{0^a}

(289)

on polynomially many ancilla qubits, i.e.
a=\textnormal{poly}(n)
.
For failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, we can learn a
2n
-qubit unitary
A_{\text{sew}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{avg}}(A_{\text{sew}},A\otimes A^{\dagger})\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{poly}(n)},

(290)

with high probability
1-\delta
. The sample and computational complexity of this procedure are quasi-polynomial.

Proof.

Following from the proof of
Proposition

6
, in this case, the distance between
\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n}
and
O_{P_{i},n}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}\left(\widetilde{O}^{(\ell)}_{P_{i},n},O_{P_{i},n}\right)\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 2\cdot 4^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}+\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})},

(291)

where
\eta
is the learning accuracy. Since the sample and time complexity are directly related to the supports of the learned observables, to achieve quasi-polynomial complexity, we will want to learn observables with
\textnormal{poly}\log n
support. In this case, if we remove all
CZ
gates of width
\geq\kappa=c\cdot\log(n+a)
where
a=n^{b}
in this circuit of size
s=\textnormal{poly}(n)
, then

\displaystyle\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{poly}(s)\cdot 2^{-\Omega(k^{1/d})}\leq\frac{\textnormal{poly}(s)}{2^{c\cdot\log(n+n^b)}}\leq\frac{\textnormal{poly}(s)}{n^{bc}}.

(292)

Thus, if
c
is chosen to be a constant sufficiently large such that
n^{bc}>\textnormal{poly}(s)
, then
\textbf{W}^{\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}}[O_{P_{i},n}]\leq 1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
and we obtain the desired learning guarantee.
∎

Appendix F
Hardness of Learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}

We will conclude with a result on the hardness of learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
. This result follows straightforwardly from
[
33
, Proposition 3]
, which showed that it is exponentially-hard to learn
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
according to the diamond-norm distance. In particular, the proof leverages a specific worst-case logarithmic-depth circuit
U_{x}
, which via a Grover lower-bound is shown to require exponential queries to learn according to the worst-case measure. We simply observe that
U_{x}
is in and of itself a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit to extend the hardness result to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits. For the sake of completeness, we re-write the theorem statement of
[
33
, Proposition 3]
in the context of
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuits, as well as the proof.

Proposition 7

(Hardness of learning
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
)
.

Consider an unknown
n
-qubit unitary
U
generated by a
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
circuit. Then,

1.

Learning
U
to
\frac{1}{3}
dimond distance with high probability requires
\exp(\Omega(n))
queries.

2.

Distinguishing whether
U
equals the identity matrix
I
or is
\frac{1}{3}
-far from the identity matrix in diamond distance with high probability requires
\exp(\Omega(n))
queries.

Proof.

For
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
U_{x}
be the unitary,

\displaystyle U_{x}\ket{y}=\begin{cases}1,&x=y,\\
-1&x\neq y,\end{cases}

(293)

which can be constructed as

\displaystyle U_{x}=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]:\\
x_{i}=0\end{subarray}}X_{i}\right)CZ_{[n]}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]:\\
x_{i}=0\end{subarray}}X_{i}\right).

(294)

[
33
]
used
U_{x}
to prove a learning lower-bound for logarithmic depth circuits comprised of constant-width gates. To begin, they showed that, in the class of circuits comprised solely of constant-width gates,
U_{x}
could be synthesized in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-depth. Core to the argument is that, if one can learn a unitary
U
up to
\frac{1}{3}
error in diamond distance with high probability or distinguish whether
U
equals the identity
I
or is
\frac{1}{3}
-far from
I
in diamond distance with high probability, then one can successfully distinguish
I
from
U_{x}
. However, distinguishing
I
from one of
U_{x}
,
\forall x\in\{0,1\}
is the Grover search problem. Therefore, by the Grover lower bound
[
4
]
, the number of queries must be at least
\Omega(2^{n/2})=\exp(\Omega(n))
.

Note that the unitary
U_{x}
is not only contained in the class of logarithmic-depth constant-width circuits, but also is contained in
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, since it is comprised solely of single-qubit gates and an
n
-qubit
CZ
gate. Therefore, the argument by and lower-bound of
[
33
]
also applies to
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}
, concluding the proof.
∎
